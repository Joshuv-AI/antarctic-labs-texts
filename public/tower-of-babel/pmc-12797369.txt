Factors associated with the variation in drug prescription of antipsychotics in long-term care facilities: a systematic review
Authors: Rabia Bibi, Massimiliano Panella, Samer Saada, Anil Babu Payedimarri, Andrea Conti, Sophia Russotto, Francesco Barone-Adesi, Alice Masini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite warnings and strict guidelines, the use of antipsychotics in long-term care facilities (LTCFs) remains problematic worldwide. Several factors, including facility and resident characteristics, could influence prescription practices. Given the vulnerability of the older people, this systematic review aims to analyze the key factors leading to variations in antipsychotic prescribing practices in LTCFs.MethodFollowing PRISMA guidelines we searched PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Library for primary research published between 2013 and 2023. Eligibility criteria included: population—LTCF residents aged 60 years or older with mental disorders; intervention—factors influencing variability in antipsychotic prescribing. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the JBI and STROBE checklists. Both data extraction and quality assessment were performed by two independent reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer.ResultOf 6572 records, 6394 were screened by title and abstract, resulting in 14 studies included in our analysis. We found that better staff training and the availability of more registered and licensed nurses were associated with lower antipsychotic medication (APM) use. Nonprofit and government facilities exhibited lower APM prevalence and better quality of care.ConclusionAddressing facility factors and promoting collaboration between health professionals is crucial to reducing unnecessary antipsychotic use. This approach highlights that antipsychotic use is influenced by diverse prescribing cultures and healthcare environments, extending beyond the patient’s condition. Implementing guidelines and legislative actions can help reduce antipsychotic prescribing variation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06558-5.

Introduction

Antipsychotic medications (APMs) are among the most commonly prescribed medications in long-term care facilities (LTCFs) to treat the symptoms of mental health disorders such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder [1, 2]. However, their prescription and use in LTCFs for older adults with dementia has become a concern due to their limited effectiveness and potential adverse effects [3]. For this reason, in 2005 the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a box warning for the use of APMs in residents with dementia. Yet, between 2009 and 2014, more than 20% of nursing home residents in the United States received APMs [4, 5].

Despite the efforts made to reduce the unnecessary APM use (e.g., implementation of specific training and educational programs), the rate of potentially inappropriate prescriptions remains high [6]. According to the most recent report by the Office of Inspector General (US Department of Health and Health Services), the persistent use of APMs in nursing homes was found to be around 80% between 2011 and 2019 [7]. Similarly, the Canadian Institute for Health Information reported an inappropriate use rate of approximately 27.5% in 2016 [8].

The inappropriate use of APMs, namely the prescription of these medications to manage behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), remains remarkably high in Western Europe and the US, with a prevalence up to 50% and 20%, respectively [9]. The Canadian Institute for Health Information reported an 3.8% increase from 2019 to 2023 of inappropriate use of APM in LTCFs [10]. However, despite APMs not being an approved treatment for dementia, they are largely used to treat behavioral symptoms associated with this disease and to calm residents with behavioral symptoms [11]. One of the major concerns associated with increased or unnecessary use of APMs is high mortality. Indeed, a recent study by Gou et al., reported people older than 64 years are 2.1 times more likely to experience severe adverse reactions and death compared to the people aged 18 to 35 [12]. These give rise to considerable concern about the safety of APM for older people. In fact, Rochon et al. also highlighted that facilities with a high prevalence of APM use are three times more likely to maintain such prescribing patterns, indicating that facility prescribing habits play a significant role in perpetuating inappropriate use [13].

In addition to prescribing culture and practices, scientific literature has shown that several factors contribute to variations in APM prescribing practices in LTCFs [13, 14]. These include facility characteristics (e.g., size, ownership, and staffing); resident characteristics (e.g., age, gender, and severity of cognitive impairment); and the background of the prescribing physician, including their area of expertise and years of experience [11, 15]. For instance, Falvey et al. observed that LTCFs situated in deprived neighborhoods have inadequate nursing staff levels, and often resort to APMs as a mean to manage challenging behaviors among residents [16]. In response to concerns about the overuse of APMs, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) launched a nationwide program called National Partnership to improve dementia care in LCTFs. CMS reported quarterly data from 2011 to 2017, that showed geographic variation, with a consistent increase in APM use across different states [17].

In order to examine the factors associated with the unnecessary prescription of APMs in LTCFs, it is important to identify the areas where prescribing practices need improvement. This involves identifying the gaps that link between increased APMs use and the contributing factors. Although the recent systematic review by Cioltan et al. has focused on the issue of variation in APMs used in LTCFs located in the United States, our work aims to broaden that research including studies from around the world [18].

Therefore, the present systematic review aimed to investigate the impact of several factors (i.e., facility characteristics, staffing, geographical location, etc.) contributing to the variation in APMs prescribing practices in LTCF. We believe that conducting a comprehensive analysis of existing evidence on antipsychotic use/prescription in LTCFs could help enhance the overall quality of life for residents and simultaneously minimize potential adverse effects associated with these medications. This analysis may also shed light on gaps in knowledge regarding APM prescribing practices in LTCFs, providing valuable insights for policymakers, healthcare providers, and researchers.

Methods

The present systematic review followed the reporting guidelines and criteria of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA) 2020 Statement. The PRISMA Checklist is available in the Supplementary S1 [19]. The Protocol for the present systematic review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), registration number CRD42023422329.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search using the specific Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) and specific keywords in the following electronic databases: Medline, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and Scopus. The search was completed in October 2023. Search strings are available in Supplementary Material Table S2. To find further relevant papers, grey literature and freehand searches of specific journals, organization websites and Google Scholar were also conducted. In addition, we snowballed and manually searched the reference list of included studies and systematic reviews to examine and extract any potentially eligible papers that may have been missed by the database searches.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included studies that examine the variability in APM use as the difference in APM prescription pattern across different NHs reflecting both geographic and temporal differences during the inclusion period. This allowed us to identify the diverse contexts and conditions under which APM use may vary. The included studies focused on older adults (average 65 years of age) in LTCFs suffering from dementia. We excluded patients diagnosed with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder or other mental health conditions for which APMs are clinically indicated. We included all types of APMs prescribed, however, we specifically excluded studies focusing only on psychotropic medications without antipsychotic use in LTCFs. We considered studies based on primary data, including cross-sectional studies, cohort studies (prospective and retrospective), experimental, and quasi-experimental studies. Study protocols, studies without original data, and opinion letters were excluded. We only considered articles written in English and published in the last ten years (2013–2023). We excluded the studies relating to the inpatient and home-based older population, as we are interested in facility-based factors that cannot be observed in these settings. The full PICOST (Participants, Interventions, Comparators, Outcomes, Study design, and Timing) criteria for the inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table S3 (supplementary material).

Selection criteria

Search results from each database were imported into an Excel spreadsheet (CSV format), duplicates were removed, and two researchers (RB, SS) independently screened titles and abstracts to assess eligibility for inclusion. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were obtained to assist in screening for final inclusion. Disagreements between reviewers during the screening process regarding the inclusion or exclusion of studies are resolved by consensus and/or discussion with a third reviewer (AM). Reasons for the exclusion of studies are recorded as part of the screening process.

Data extraction & synthesis

Two researchers (RB, SS) independently extracted data from the included studies using a predetermined form. Inconsistencies in extracted data were resolved after a discussion between the two researchers. When necessary, a third researcher (AM) was consulted to ensure accuracy and consistency. Extracted data included the following information: author, year of publication, country, study design, participants characteristics (e.g., gender, average age, total number), type of antipsychotics prescribed; prescription rate or use rate of antipsychotics; facility characteristics including occupancy, staffing level, ownership, staff-related factors; variation based on geographical location; and main findings. We perform the qualitative synthesis of the included studies in tabular and narrative description to report the variations and its associated factors and where available, the quantitative data (e.g. mean, ranges and percentages) were also reported. For qualitative studies, relevant data were extracted and organized according to key themes and categories that aligned with our research question. This approach allowed for a structured presentation and interpretation of relevant findings.

Risk of bias assessment

Two researchers (RB, SS) independently assessed the quality of the included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool and the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist [20, 21]. Disagreements were resolved by a third researcher (AM). The STROBE checklist was used for assessing the quality of the observational study. Since the STROBE statement does not provide a predefined scoring system, the cut-off was established as follows: a score of 0-14 represented high risk of bias, 15-25 represented medium risk of bias, and 26-33 represented low risk of bias. We used the JBI checklists for randomized controlled trials and qualitative studies. The following cut-off values were given to the JBI tool to assess the quality of the studies: If studies scored 49% or less, they were considered high risk of bias; studies scoring between 50% and 69% were considered medium risk of bias; and studies with positive responses greater than 70% were considered low risk of bias.

Results

A total of 6554 records were retrieved via the electronic databases, and a further 18 records were identified by freehand search. After removing the duplicates, the dataset was refined to 6394 records. All records were then thoroughly screened, and 6210 were excluded at the title and abstract level. Therefore, 51 full-text were screened (Fig. 1). Finally, 14 studies were included. The PRISMA flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

The studies showed remarkable diversity in terms of sample size, mean age of participants, and type of APMs used. Indeed, the sample size varied from 98 to 1,156,875 patients [6, 34], while the average age ranged from 75.4 to 86.7 years [34]. The highest age distribution is observed in the 75–89 age group, which accounts for 46.7% of the population, while those aged 85 and over were 53% [6, 33].

APMs variability

The prevalence of APM use in LTCFs varied significantly among the studies, with the highest use of 37.3% in the total of 44 NHs in Tel Aviv, followed by Terada et al. who reported an overall prevalence of 4.4% for a total of 50 NHs in Japan [32, 34]. The study conducted by Busch et al. reported a low prevalence of APMs in the highest performing quintile of institutions (7.0%) and a high prevalence in the lowest performing quintile (33.7%) [6]. Another study found an APM prevalence of 25% in the highest quintile and a prevalence of 5% in the lowest quintile [28]. Both cRCTs were based on the impact of educational interventions for the use of APMs in NHs [26, 30].

Occupancy

Four studies reported occupancy rates and their association with APM utilization in LTCFs [24, 26, 30, 32]. Szczepura et al. categorized occupancy rates according to the size of the facility: small facilities ≤ 24 residents with 6.5%, medium facilities with 25–39 residents with 34.6%, and large facilities with ≥ 40 residents with 58.9%, with the lowest prescription rate [32]. Stocks et al. reported an occupancy rate of 32.2% in DAL facilities and 33.7% in LTCFs, with the occupancy rate in LTCFs being slightly higher than in DAL facilities [29]. Thomas et al. and Candon et al. reported occupancy rates of 46% for 250 facilities in various seven US states and 81.2% for 13,151 facilities in Virginia, respectively, both studies did not report association of occupancy with antipsychotic use [23, 26]. Two studies reported the association based on occupancy rate (number of beds). Terada et al. reported the number of beds available in the NHs and group homes (GHs), across Japan in GHs (9) and NHs (50), in South Korea in GHs (10) and NHs (37), in the other Asian countries only the NHs occupancy was reported, including China (290), Taiwan (40) and Thailand (65) [34]. The lowest prescription rate was observed in Chinese LTCFs and the highest in Japanese GHs [34]. Winter et al. reported a bed count of 104 in the highest quintile in APM use and 114 in the lowest quintile [27].

Staffing level

Four studies reported staffing levels [27, 30, 33, 35]. Terada et al. reported no doctor in Japan GH, only one doctor per NHs, similarly in Thailand and South Korea one part-time doctor and one doctor visit every two months respectively, while in China 6 psycho-geriatricians were available and in Taiwan 3 full-time and 2 part-time doctors which shows lower APM use [34]. Tjia et al. reported the staffing level in each arm of the study, with the distribution of medical staff varying: Arm 1 included 13% Certified Nursing Assistants (CNAs), 40% Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs), and 75% Registered Nurses (RNs); Arm 2 consisted of 5% CNAs, 27% LPNs, and 50% RNs; and Arm 3 included 49% CNAs, 69% LPNs, and 94% RNs, but no significant difference was found for APM use based on different staffing level in each arm [26]. Frankenthal reported a proportion of 15.9% physicians and 38.6% nurses (with and without degrees), and 38.6% of the specialized geriatricians for the total 44 LTCFs, the facilities with the more specialized geriatricians showed lower prevalence [32]. Stock et al. reported that facilities with LPN/RN coverage at LTCFs were at 100%, while pharmacist and physician availability were at 34.8%, indicating higher APM utilization than at DAL, where LPN/RN and pharmacist availability were lowest [29].

Staff-related factors

Eight studies reported staff-related factors that influenced APM utilization rates [6, 22, 24, 27, 28, 31, 33]. Kleijer et al. reported that lower staffing levels and nurses’ lack of qualifications were associated with higher APM utilization [33]. Candon et al. and Chappell et al. both reported a positive association between the ratio of Registered Nurses (RNs) to Licensed Practice Nurses (LPNs) and APM prescription [22, 23]. Frankenthal et al. reported that inadequate staffing, including occupational therapists and geriatricians, is associated with increased APM use [32]. Simmons et al. and Szczepura et al. both reported that high staff workload (including physicians and nurses) was associated with high levels of inappropriate prescriptions [24, 31]. Both Tjia et al. and Tadrous et al. emphasized the importance of LTCF staff involvement and familiarity with the intervention, feedback and audit in reducing APM use, suggesting that staff training and involvement is important in optimizing medication use [26, 30].

Ownership

Five studies reported on the ownership of LTCFs for profit, nonprofit, and government-operated facilities reimbursed by Medicaid and Medicare [6, 23, 27, 29, 30]. Busch et al. reported facilities with increased APM use rate were for profit (80%) compared with non-profit facilities 13.9% and for government owned facilities 5.9% [6]. Chappell reported for-profit (74.73%), non-profit (16.3%), government (4.45%) and unidentified (4.49%) facilities; they found for-profit facilities were associated with lower staffing levels and increased APM rate [23]. Stock et al. reported the ownership patterns for Dementia Assisted Living (DAL): profit (39.5%) and non-profit (60.5%) and LTCFs profit (28.1%) and (78.9%), it showed a slight increase of APM use for profit facilities in both DAL and LTC [29]. Winter et al. reported the APM use in quintiles and found privately owned NHs in the highest quintile (60%) and lowest quintile (80%) [27]. Tadrous et al. reported ownership in the intervention group for private NHs (72.2%) and non-profit facilities (27.8%) and in the control group for private and non-profit facilities (72.7% and 27.3%, respectively) and no association was reported for APM use rate [30].

Geographical location and APM use

Six studies reported the geographical location of LTCFs s [6, 25, 27, 29, 31, 33]. The study with the highest notable variation in APM use between countries is observed in East Asian countries, with the lowest APM use observed in Thailand (16.3%) and the highest in South Korea (48%) [34]. However, in other studies, we observed different prevalence rates depending on the location of the facilities. The study by Szczepura et al. from the UK showed that the greatest variation in APM utilization was observed in 26 different regional areas, with the highest area having 37.5% and the lowest 5.7% APM prescription rates [31]. Frankenthal et al. observed that APM utilization in the 44 facilities in Tel Aviv varied between 14.8% and 70.6% [32]. Winter et al. observed a difference of approximately 20% in APM utilization in the state of Virginia, with the highest performing quintile showing 25.30% compared to the lowest performing quintile at 5.00% [27]. They also reported the highest APM prevalence in urban facilities at 34% and 67% in the facilities with the lowest APM use, while conversely, Busch et al. reported that 29% of rural facilities had the highest APM prescription while 19% of rural facilities had the lowest APM prescription [6].

Risk of bias assessment

Table 3 illustrates the quality of the included studies. Three studies were assessed using the JBI tool: two cRCTs (one scored low risk of bias and one scored medium risk of bias), and one qualitative study scored low risk of bias [24, 26, 30]. Both cRCTs did not explain in detail the blindness of the participants, of those delivering treatment to the treatment group, and of the outcome assessors (Item no 3, 4, and 5, respectively) [26, 30]. Moreover, Tjia et al. did not clearly define the concealment of the allocation of the treatment group while Tadrous et al., reported the impossibility of performing the allocation concealment of the treatment group due to the nature of the intervention of the study [26, 30]. Simmons et al. scored low risk of bias, however, the authors did not provide information about the influence of researchers on the research, and ethical approval of the research [24]. Following STROBE criteria, 10 out of 11 studies scored medium risk of bias and one scored high risk of bias, the reason for the low scoring among all the studies assessed was the lack of information about the statistical methods used [6, 22, 23, 25, 27–29, 32–34]. Moreover, 7 studies out of 11 did not provide detailed information about descriptive data for the results in the study (Items no 14 and 16) [6, 22, 23, 27, 29, 32, 34]. The scoring of JBI and STROBE checklist for the individual study is reported in the supplementary material Table S4 a and b, S5 a and b.

Discussion

The purpose of this systematic review was to investigate the impact of factors contributing to APM prescribing in LTCFs. The main focus was the relationship between antipsychotics and facility factors, as these medications are typically used in response to patient behavioral problems. In addition, examining facility factors allows for an assessment of the broader influence of the environment and can examine prescribing practices in LTCFs.

The current systematic review highlights several factors associated with the use of APM in different healthcare settings worldwide. We identified different facility-related characteristics such as occupancy rates, staffing levels, staff-related factors, and ownership structures. Examining these facility characteristics in the LTCF population is important for several reasons, since variation in APM use may indicate inconsistencies in prescribing practices and a lack of standardized approaches. We looked for the factors driving increased APM use and potential areas of intervention to promote appropriate prescribing practices.

Occupancy

Contrasting findings have been observed in previous research where low occupancy rates were associated with high APM use, likely due to lower funding, and lower numbers of trained staff [11, 37]. However, Szczepura et al. reported that facilities with larger sizes are associated with lower antipsychotic prescriptions. These findings could be partly due to the fact that many facilities were located in deprived neighborhoods (based on the English Indices of Deprivation 2010), which could explain the lack of resources in these areas [31]. Interestingly, we found an inverse relationship between occupancy rate and for-profit ownership, whereas for-profit facilities are commonly thought to have a greater number of beds and resources [23, 24]. Although, our findings suggest that for-profit facilities were associated with higher APM utilization, in line with previous studies [38]. Among the East Asian countries, China has the highest number of beds per unit than the other East Asian countries. However, the prevalence of APM was also the lowest there, as China has specialized dementia wards in NHs. In addition, psychogeriatrics were available there, which may explain the lower need for medication [34].

Staff-related factors and staffing levels

Staff-related factors proved to be decisive factors for APM prescribing practice. Factors such as staff qualifications, skills, hours spent per patient and staffing levels were positively associated with APM prescribing. Patient behavior has always been a challenge for nursing staff to deal with [25]. The sedative effect of APMsis commonly used to manage the agitated and aggressive patients in a short time and relieves the burden on nursing staff. However, this form of behavioural control comes with significant risks such as falls and aspiration in older patients [39]. Simmons et al. observed that nurses usually opt for pharmacological approaches, as they believe that non-pharmacological interventions may not be sufficient to control the severe challenging behavior [24]. Furthermore, nurses are often conditioned by patients’ family members to prescribe APM in order to become more involved in their relatives’ therapy in nursing homes [24, 27, 34]. However, the benefits of APM for the treatment of behavioral problems are still unclear, as it is repeatedly used off-label for dementia patients in NHs [35–38]. Higher staffing levels have been associated with lower prescribing of APMs, but this is not only related to the number of staff but rather the type of staff available, the licensed and registered nursing staff and their training [27, 29]. Conversely, Szczepura et al. reported that facilities where more general practitioners were available were associated with higher APM prescriptions, likely due to inconsistencies in the treatment plan that ultimately contribute to the different prescribing cultures and practices [31]. In contrast, in China where more geriatric (psycho-geriatrician) specialists are available, the use of APM is lower. The authors suggested that this could be partly due to a preference for behavioral therapies over pharmacological ones [35]. Our findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that variations in practice can be observed where there is debate about the appropriateness of treatment, particularly in relation to vulnerable populations [13].

The availability of RNs, LNs, and mixed-skill nurses, rather than staffing ratios, has been shown to be beneficial in reducing the use of APM [6, 23, 26]. Candon et al. explained this phenomenon with the use of non-pharmacological interventions by RNs, which allow for more personalized treatment to the patients [22]. Similarly, Hughes et al. also reported that the ratio of assistants to nurses was associated with higher APM use and analyzed the need for professional nurses to provide specialized care to residents [14]. Training of the nursing staff, educational interventions and increased recreational activities have been shown to be strategic tools for implementing more non-pharmacological interventions [14, 39, 40]. However, it is important to note that studies outside the US did not provide data on LNs, RNs, or skill mix, but only on the availability of geriatricians in NHs [34]. Outside of our scope, we found a racial disparity in APM prescribing in the US, where African American males were more likely to be prescribed APMs than males of other races [28]. Although it is plausible that staff-related factors and prescribing practices contribute to this disparity, it is important to recognize that multiple interrelated factors may be at play [6, 13, 28, 29].

Geographical location

Geographical location significantly influenced APM use, with variations observed across countries and even within cities. In the USA, southern states showed an upward trend in APM use, in contrast with the findings from recent systematic reviews conducted in the USA by Cioltan at el [18, 25, 26]. The most recent work by Crystal et al. reported a lower prevalence of APM use in Texas following the implementation of a national campaign [37]. However, Thomas et al. study showed the continued trend of increased APM use despite the use of “chemical restraints” being banned in Texas and increased initiatives and regulations being proposed to reduce inappropriate APM use [25]. We also observed differences in prescribing practices and cultures, with much of this variation is likely due to differences in country policies and prescribing cultures [33, 35]. However, since our work aimed to summarize this topic using a worldwide approach, we were not able to draw any additional robust consideration beyond these possible reasons. Finally we compared APM use between urban and rural facilities. Consistently with previous work [13, 30, 37] our results suggested that APM use was higher in urban facilities. A potential explanation can be that facilities in metropolitan areas may have greater availability of resources, nursing staff and better equipment. However, it is better to assume that facility characteristics, including ownership, payer mix and NHs crowding, also play a role in this regard [27]. Conversely, our findings suggested an high APM prevalence in rural areas in those communities with lower population density. Although we recognize the significance of location, we cannot provide a clear explanation for the two different outcomes or the differences observed, except that the other facility factors also contribute to such disparity [27, 37, 41].

Ownership

As regards as ownership, for-profit facilities were associated with increased APM use rates [6, 23, 25]. Frankenthal et al. observed facilities with reimbursement from the Ministry of Health had performed better than the for-profit and self-pay residents because of the rigorous monitoring by the Health Ministry, which is a plausible explanation for similar studies findings. However, several studies have also highlighted that for-profit facilities are also prone to the lower staffing levels and the pressure from family members and other residents to stabilize the agitated residents as main reasons, Further investigation is warranted [32]. In general, the governmental-owned and non-profit facilities have performed better in the reduction of inappropriate use of APMs [42, 43]. In the US studies showed higher prevalence of APMs in Medicaid facilities as compared to Medicare-reimbursed facilities. Residents with Medicaid reimbursement in the facilities regardless of the size of the facility have a higher risk of receiving an inappropriate APM prescription [6, 22, 23]. The increase in APM use in Medicaid facilities has also been positively associated with lower staffing levels, which is unexpected given that the Center for Medicare and Medicaid launched a nationwide initiative in 2012 to reduce the inappropriate use of APMs. Despite these efforts, the prevalence of APMs in the US has continued to increase a decade later, even within the facilities where they are used [44]. One possible explanation for the Medicaid reimbursed residents could be the coverage of low-income residents, so Medicaid facilities may have fewer nursing staff and equipment available [45].

Strength & limitations

In our systematic review, we have endeavored to include data to pool the findings and the association of different factors. One of the strengths of our review is its global perspective, as we included studies from various countries. This allowed us to observe general trends and differences in APM prescribing practices across different settings. Moreover, we explored how certain facility characteristics can influence the APM use more than the other and are crucial in prescription decision making. Future research, like scoping review, would complement our systematic review by potentially revealing areas that require further detailed investigation.

There are some limitations in our systematic review. First, variability in study designs, settings, and data collection methods may affect the generalizability of findings. The retrieved studies do not always provide specific details, such as the occupancy rate, ownership, and geographical location. Additionally, we cannot identify any specific association of facility size with the use of APM similarly, geographical location also does not exhibit a consistent pattern in APM usage, as variations exist within countries and across continents. We believe that there is a severe scarcity of evidence outside of the US, as the US alone accounts for half of the study included in our review. In contrast, the remaining studies were distributed sparsely across multiple countries, limiting country-level insights. This highlights a gap in high-quality, country-specific evidence outside the US. Future research should aim to address these limitations and explore the long-term impacts of interventions on APM use in diverse healthcare settings. Moreover, we included mental health disorders because of ambiguity surrounding the generic term used in the studies such as ‘mental health’. In addition, we found numerous studies to be of intermediate quality, therefore future studies should use more rigorous methodologies. This includes using standardized and validated measures, and robust statistical analyses.

Policy implications

Our findings collectively implicate the necessity for informed policies aimed at reducing inappropriate APM use. Improving staff education and training, enhancing workforce qualifications, and implementing evidence-based practices are crucial steps identified by multiple studies [27, 41, 44]. Policy changes should also consider regional differences, to ensure that interventions are contextually and regionally relevant.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that variability in APM prescription patterns is associated with several contributing factors in LTCFs. By examining factors such as occupancy rates, staffing levels, staff-related factors, ownership, and geographical locations, we were able to identify factors that might contribute to the variations in the APM prescription. Identification of increased APM use based on facility characteristics provides a foundation for targeted interventions and quality improvement initiatives. Moreover, collaborative efforts involving healthcare professionals, facility administrators, policymakers, and regulatory agencies are essential to implement and maintain the interventions and foster a culture of evidence-based prescribing practices in nursing homes. By prioritizing resident-centered care and promoting medication safety, we can provide a better quality of life for residents in LTCFs.
