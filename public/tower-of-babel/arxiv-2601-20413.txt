Schadenfreude in the Digital Public Sphere: A cross-national and decade-long analysis of Facebook news engagement
Nouar Aldahoul, Hazem Ibrahim, Majd Mahmutoglu, Hajra Tarar, Muhammad Fareed Zaffar, Talal Rahwan, Yasir Zaki

Abstract
Schadenfreude, or the pleasure derived from others’ misfortunes, has become a visible and performative feature of online news engagement, yet little is known about its prevalence, dynamics, or social patterning. We examine schadenfreude on Facebook over a ten-year period across nine major news publishers in the United States, the United Kingdom, and India (one left-leaning, one right-leaning, and one centrist per country). Using a combination of human annotation and machine-learning classification, we identify posts describing misfortune and detect schadenfreude in nearly one million associated comments. We find that while sadness and anger dominate reactions to misfortune posts, laughter and amusement form a substantial and patterned minority. Schadenfreude is most frequent in moralized and political contexts, higher among right-leaning audiences, and more pronounced in India than in the United States or United Kingdom. Temporal and regression analyses further reveal that schadenfreude generally increases when groups are politically out of power, but these patterns differ across party-lines. Together, our findings move beyond anecdotal accounts to map schadenfreude as a dynamic, context-dependent feature of digital discourse, revealing how it evolves over time and across ideological and cultural divides.

2.1
What is Schadenfreude?

The term
schadenfreude
originates from German, combining “Schaden” (harm) and “Freude” (joy), meaning “joy at another’s misfortune.” Philosophical reflections on the concept date back centuries, with philosophers such as Aristotle and Nietzsche remarking on its moral significance
[
21
,
31
,

In modern psychology, schadenfreude is theorized as a multidimensional construct shaped by social comparison (reducing status asymmetries with envied targets), justice considerations (pleasure at the deserved suffering of wrongdoers), and intergroup rivalry (outgroup setbacks that elevate the ingroup)
[
40
,
46
,
17
,
25
]
.

Beginning with social comparison, Smith et al.
[
40
]
showed that setbacks for superior or envied targets can elicit pleasure by narrowing status asymmetries. Subsequent experiments indicate that downward comparison and self-affirming processes help explain this response: when an envied target “falls from grace,” observers’ threatened self-views are restored, increasing schadenfreude
[
46
,
47
]
. From this perspective, schadenfreude functions as a coping mechanism that reframes another’s loss as a relative psychological gain. A second perspective argues for
justice-based
schadenfreude, where pleasure emerges when perceived wrongdoers suffer, aligning with intuitions about moral desert and norm enforcement
[
17
]
. Experimental evidence shows that norm violations and unfairness reliably increase schadenfreude toward the offending target
[
46
]
, and that such responses are embedded in wider social appraisals of deservingness rather than indiscriminate hostility
[
25
]
. In this view, schadenfreude is not merely antisocial glee but often a morally framed reaction to violations of expected conduct. The third perspective emphasizes
intergroup dynamics
. In competitive or adversarial contexts, such as politics, sports, or identity-based conflict, the misfortunes of an outgroup can enhance the relative standing of the ingroup, thereby intensifying schadenfreude
[
25
]
. Classic social identity research shows how ingroup favoritism and outgroup derogation structure emotional responses to intergroup outcomes
[
43
,
11
]
. From this perspective, schadenfreude is not merely an interpersonal reaction but a group-based emotion, rooted in comparative evaluations and boundary maintenance between “us” and “them”
[
35
,
25
]
.

Across these perspectives, schadenfreude emerges as a socially situated emotion reflecting and often amplifying social hierarchies, moral judgments, and group boundaries
[
25
]
. Neuroscientific evidence further supports this view: observing rivals’ setbacks activates reward-related regions (e.g., ventral striatum), indicating that schadenfreude is both psychologically and biologically embedded
[
44
]
. This grounding helps explain why schadenfreude may thrive on platforms like Facebook, where social comparison, moralized discourse, and intergroup contestation are endemic features of interaction
[

3
Methodology

Building on prior work that conceptualizes social media as an affective arena where emotion and identity shape engagement, we combine large-scale data collection with human annotation and language model–based classification to examine how schadenfreude manifests in Facebook discourse. The following sections describe our data sources, annotation protocols, and classification pipeline.

3.1
Data Collection

To observe schadenfreude behavior among news audiences, we collected posts, comments, and reactions from verified Facebook pages of major news outlets across three countries—the United States, the United Kingdom, and India—each represented by one left-leaning, one centrist, and one right-leaning source. For each news source, we collect up to 1000 posts per month, for each year between 2015 and 2024.

From the United Kingdom, we included The Guardian (left), Reuters (center), and Metro (right). From India, we sampled Indian Express (left), Hindustan Times (center), and OpIndia.com (right). From the United States, we included NBC News (left), CNBC (center), and Fox News (right). This stratified sampling strategy enabled comparison across both ideological orientation and national context, capturing variation in how audiences emotionally engage with politically or culturally adversarial events.

For each post, we collected the text content, timestamp, as well as all associated public comments, and available reaction metadata (e.g., “Haha,” “Sad,” “Angry”). Personally identifying information was removed, and only publicly visible content was analyzed.

3.2
Human Annotation Protocols

We employed human annotation to generate ground-truth labels for two key components of the analysis: (1) identifying whether a post describes a misfortune, and (2) classifying the emotional tone of comments responding to those posts.

3.2.1
Misfortune Annotation

We randomly sampled 500 posts and instructed three trained annotators to determine whether each post described a misfortune (e.g., harm, scandal, failure, loss) or was neutral/normal. Annotators were provided with written guidelines and examples. Inter-annotator agreement was high (
\kappa=0.81
), indicating strong reliability. The resulting annotations were used to refine the prompt for large-scale misfortune detection using GPT-4o Mini.

3.2.2
Comment-Level Emotion Annotation

To identify how audiences emotionally responded to misfortunes, we next annotated a randomly sampled set of 1,000 post–comment pairs. Annotators labeled each comment as toxic, sympathetic, neutral, or unknown. Annotators were instructed to follow the following annotation guide:

•

Toxic: comments contain insults, harassment, hate, or harmful language.

•

Sympathetic: comments express empathy, support, or kindness toward the post or its author.

•

Neutral: Comments that are too neither toxic nor sympathetic; factual, indifferent, or unrelated.

•

Unknown: Comments that are too unclear, ambiguous, or insufficient information to classify.

We also provided two examples for each category.
Since every post involves some form of misfortune, annotators were instructed to focus on how the commenter reacts. In other words, annotators are asked to judge the comment’s tone in response to the post, while considering subtler aspects of the comment such as sarcasm or emoji usage. If the intent was not obvious, annotators were instructed to choose the “unknown” category.

Each pair was initially annotated by three independent raters; disagreements were resolved by two additional annotators using majority voting. Of the 1000 post-comment pairs, 900 reached a majority consensus. These pairs were then used for the training, validation, and testing of our classifier. The fine-tuned classifier was utilized for classifying all collected pairs of post-comment across three countries and over ten years.
Informed consent was obtained from all human annotators.

3.4
Topic Analysis

To examine how the type of misfortune shapes emotional responses, we categorized each misfortune-related post into one of eight topical domains: moral or ideological, political, religious, and institutional, social and group-level, economic and corporate, celebrity and cultural, personal or everyday, sports and competitive, and natural and environmental.

These categories were selected to reflect the principal contexts in which prior research suggests schadenfreude arises. Psychological and sociological studies identify three broad antecedents of schadenfreude—moral deservingness, status and comparison, and intergroup rivalry. Our topical scheme mirrors these dimensions while ensuring coverage of the most common misfortunes encountered in public discourse. Specifically, moral or ideological and political, religious, and institutional topics capture situations of moral transgression, hypocrisy, or ideological conflict, where justice-based schadenfreude is most likely. Social and group-level and economic and corporate categories reflect collective and competitive dynamics in which status and hierarchy are salient. Celebrity and cultural, personal or everyday, and sports and competitive misfortunes represent lower-stakes or entertainment-driven contexts, where amusement may stem from social comparison rather than moral judgment. Finally, natural and environmental events serve as a contrast category, representing non-agentic or accidental harms that typically elicit empathy rather than pleasure.

Each post was classified into one of these eight categories using GPT-5.2 and a structured prompt provided in Appendix Figure

.

Results

To characterize how audiences engage emotionally with others’ misfortunes, we proceed in several steps. We first examine surface-level emotional reactions expressed through Facebook’s reaction icons, before turning to the content of user comments to directly quantify schadenfreude. We then explore how these expressions vary across ideological and national contexts, topic domains, and over time, culminating in a regression analysis that isolates the political and contextual predictors of schadenfreude.

4.2
Schadenfreude by Country and Ideological Leaning

While reaction icons provide a useful first glimpse into how users respond emotionally to others’ misfortunes, they capture only the most superficial layer of engagement. Next, we look to understand how audiences verbalize their pleasure or amusement. Specifically, we quantify schadenfreude directly from the content of user comments, rather than from the choice of reaction icons (see the Methods section for more details on this process). Figure
2
presents the average proportion of comments exhibiting schadenfreude across both countries and ideological leanings. This measure captures explicit expressions of amusement, mockery, or satisfaction at others’ misfortunes, offering a richer and more deliberate behavioral indicator than reaction-based metrics.

Across all contexts, the prevalence of schadenfreude in comments far exceeds that inferred from “Haha” reactions. Whereas laughter reactions provide a visible but minimal signal, textual commentary reveals a much deeper and more frequent engagement with others’ misfortunes. In India, comments exhibiting schadenfreude constitute roughly 42% of all responses to misfortune-related posts, nearly doubling the rate implied by Haha reactions. The United States follows at about 32%, and the United Kingdom at 28%. These cross-national differences mirror the relative expressive patterns see in reactions observed earlier, but their magnitude suggests that laughter reactions alone may underestimate the frequency at which users engage in schadenfreude.

One interpretation is that Haha reactions serve as a lower-bound estimate of schadenfreude on the platform. Clicking an emoji is a fast, socially visible act—one that may be constrained by norms of decorum or the ambiguity of humor. Commenting, by contrast, allows for explicit verbalization, where users can gloat, ridicule, or moralize in detail, often amplifying the affective tone of the post. The discrepancy between the two measures thus points to the layered nature of emotional expression online. Schadenfreude can manifest both subtly (through laughter icons) and overtly (through language). While these modes are not mutually exclusive, a user can both react and comment, they may reflect different thresholds of emotional investment. Turning to ideological differences, the same general asymmetry persists. Topics of misfortune and the contexts that elicit schadenfreude

To this end, we examined the proportion of comments expressing pleasure or amusement at others’ suffering across eight topical categories, which is illustrated in Figure

Figure
3
visualizes the rate of schadenfreude across eight topical categories, split by both country and ideological leaning. The heatmap reveals that amusement at others’ misfortunes is not evenly distributed but varies sharply depending on both the moral framing of the event and the socio-political context of the audience. Across all countries, moral or ideological and political, religious, and institutional misfortunes consistently evoke the highest levels of schadenfreude. In these domains, where wrongdoing, hypocrisy, or partisan conflict are salient, the mean schadenfreude rate often exceeds 0.40 and reaches above 0.50 among right-leaning outlets in India. Such cases likely trigger both justice-based and intergroup forms of pleasure, as audiences interpret harm to opponents or elites as morally deserved or symbolically rewarding. By contrast, natural and environmental and sports and competitive misfortunes produce the lowest levels of schadenfreude across all contexts, rarely exceeding 0.20. These events tend to lack moral agency or group antagonism, potentially leading audiences to respond with sympathy or neutrality rather than amusement. Personal or everyday and celebrity and cultural misfortunes occupy an intermediate range (0.20–0.30), reflecting lighter, often humorous engagement rather than moralized gloating.

Cross-nationally, India stands out for both its higher overall levels and its sharper ideological contrasts. Right-leaning Indian outlets exhibit especially elevated schadenfreude in nearly every topic—surpassing 0.40 in most domains and reaching 0.54 for moral or ideological misfortunes—suggesting that laughter and mockery are more culturally permissible and politically instrumental forms of reaction. The United States shows moderate differentiation by leaning, where right-leaning outlets record more frequent amusement in moral and political topics (0.46 and 0.33, respectively), while left- and center-leaning outlets remain closer to the 0.30–0.35 range. The United Kingdom displays the narrowest range of variation overall, with schadenfreude rarely exceeding 0.35 and converging across ideological lines, particularly in centrist and left-leaning sources.

4.4
Schadenfreude over time

Figure 4

After identifying who expresses schadenfreude and in response to what types of events, we next examine how these patterns have evolved over time. Tracing temporal dynamics allows us to assess whether schadenfreude represents a stable feature of online discourse or one that fluctuates with broader political and cultural shifts. To this end, we examine how the rate of schadenfreude in comments evolved over time, between 2015 and 2024, across the three countries and ideological orientations (Figure
4
). Each line represents the yearly average schadenfreude rate for left-, center-, and right-leaning outlets (blue, green, and red lines, respectively), with the dashed black line indicating the overall country average.

In India, schadenfreude levels are consistently higher than in the other two countries across the entire period. From 2015 through the late 2010s, all ideological groups exhibit relatively elevated and stable rates. Beginning around 2019, however, schadenfreude declines across outlets, with substantial variation by ideology. Right-leaning outlets maintain the highest levels throughout the period, peaking around 2018–2020 before gradually decreasing. Centrist outlets follow a similar but lower trajectory, with a steady decline after 2019. In contrast, left-leaning outlets experience a pronounced and continuous drop after 2019, reaching markedly lower levels by 2023. The national average reflects this overall downward trend while remaining higher than the corresponding averages in the UK and USA.

In the United Kingdom, schadenfreude exhibits a dip followed by a partial rebound. Between 2015 and 2019, all ideological groups show declining rates, converging near a common low point. After 2020, schadenfreude begins to increase, driven primarily by right-leaning outlets, which rise steadily and separate from the other ideological groupings by the end of the period. Left-leaning and centrist outlets remain comparatively low and closely aligned, showing only modest increases. The national average mirrors this trajectory, indicating a mid-period trough followed by renewed growth.

In the United States, schadenfreude levels display moderate variation over time with clear ideological differentiation. Right-leaning outlets consistently show the highest rates, followed by centrist and then left-leaning outlets for much of the period. After approximately 2020, schadenfreude slightly declines across right-leaning and centrist outlets, with an increase in schadenfreude rates in the comments of the left-leaning source. As a result, the national average remains relatively steady, despite these within-country ideological shifts. Overall, these results indicate that schadenfreude expression varies systematically by country and ideological orientation.

The results reveal clear and systematic differences by ideological leaning. Relative to centrist outlets, both left- and right-leaning outlets exhibit significantly higher rates of schadenfreude. However, the effect for left-leaning outlets is an order of magnitude larger (
+0.016
) than that for right-leaning outlets (
+0.0015
). Yet, when considering the governing party at the time when a post is made, we see that posts from outlets aligned with the ruling government exhibit significantly lower schadenfreude rates (
-0.0052
), suggesting that the experience of political opposition amplifies the likelihood of expressing pleasure at others failures. However, this aggregate effect conceals striking asymmetries across the ideological spectrum. Among left-leaning outlets, the alignment penalty is especially strong (
-0.016
). In other words, when there is a Democratic president currently in office, schadenfreude in the comments of left-leaning news outlets drops sharply. In contrast, right-leaning outlets display a small but significant increase (
+0.0021
) when there is a Republican president in office. These findings provide quantitative evidence suggesting that schadenfreude is not merely an incidental byproduct of online polarization but a structured, context-dependent emotional signal that fluctuates with both the topic and power status of the group expressing it.

5
Discussion

Our results provide one of the most comprehensive empirical portraits to date of how schadenfreude, an emotion once largely confined to private experience, has become a visible, measurable, and socially patterned feature of online political communication. Drawing on over a decade of Facebook activity across the United States, the United Kingdom, and India, we find that posts describing misfortunes evoke a mix of empathy, outrage, fascination, and amusement. Beneath this diversity, however, lie consistent structures: expressions of schadenfreude vary systematically by ideology, national context, and topic. Together, these findings reveal how pleasure at others’ suffering functions as a form of moralized and identity-driven commentary in the digital public sphere.

At a broad level, our findings show that online schadenfreude is not an occasional or fringe response but a recurring mode of affective engagement. While sadness and anger dominate reactions to misfortune posts, laughter and amusement account for a substantial share of engagement, appearing in roughly one out of every five reaction clicks and an even higher proportion of user comments. When we compare across national contexts, we find meaningful variation in emotional norms. Indian audiences exhibit the highest overall rates of schadenfreude, both in reactions and comments. In contrast, audiences in the United States and the United Kingdom show more restrained amusement, consistent with contexts where moral outrage dominates political discourse. These differences demonstrate that the same platform affordances interact with distinct cultural and media traditions to shape the emotional tenor of public communication.

Ideological patterns further clarify how schadenfreude becomes embedded in political behavior. Right-leaning audiences and outlets consistently express more amusement at others’ misfortunes, in line with prior work showing that conservative communication styles often rely on ridicule or irony. Yet our regression analyses reveal a deeper asymmetry. Left-leaning outlets display what could be termed “power-compensatory” schadenfreude, where their expressions of schadenfreude intensify when politically out of power and diminish when governing. Right-leaning outlets, by contrast, exhibit “power-licensed” schadenfreude, maintaining or even increasing when their ideological allies hold office. These patterns suggest that schadenfreude serves distinct psychological and political functions across ideological orientations—either as a coping mechanism or as a reinforcement of dominance.

Our temporal analyses suggest that schadenfreude has remained a stable, though evolving, feature of online discourse. Although national trajectories differ, rising in India, declining in the United Kingdom, and fluctuating in the United States, its persistence over time suggests that moralized amusement has become a routine emotional register through which users process public events. Rather than emerging only during moments of scandal or crisis, schadenfreude constitutes a habitual affective lens in digital communication.

Our findings carry important implications for understanding affective polarization, moral communication, and the emotional architectures of social media. First, they show that polarization operates not only through cognitive mechanisms such as selective exposure or motivated reasoning but also through emotion. Users do not merely oppose their outgroups, they often derive pleasure from those groups’ failures. This helps explain why moralized discourse online tends to escalate rather than resolve division, as the emotional gratification of witnessing an opponent’s misfortune reinforces group boundaries and inhibits empathy. Second, our results highlight how moral psychology interacts with platform design. Features such as reaction buttons, engagement-based ranking, and algorithmic amplification convert fleeting feelings into public, quantifiable signals. When laughter or mockery are rewarded through likes, shares, or visibility, derision becomes a socially legitimate form of political participation.

Third, our cross-national analysis reveals that while schadenfreude is a universal emotional capacity, its public expression is culturally mediated. In geopolitical contexts such as India, amusement at others’ misfortunes may serve as a normative form of critique. In contrast, in countries where moral outrage dominates political culture, such expressions are more likely to be stigmatized. Future research could examine how local media systems, regulatory environments, and linguistic norms shape these emotional boundaries.

5.1
Limitations

As with any large-scale analysis of social media discourse, our study is subject to several limitations. First, not all relevant user comments may have been captured. Platform moderation, user deletions, and automated activity can remove or distort portions of the comment stream. While these omissions likely introduce random noise rather than systematic bias, they may slightly reduce our estimates of overall engagement. Second, although our text classifier performs strongly in identifying schadenfreude, current large language models still struggle to fully capture the subtleties of irony, sarcasm, or culture-specific humor. Differences in language use across India, the United States, and the United Kingdom may lead some expressions of amusement or empathy to be misclassified. Our measures should therefore be interpreted as behavioral indicators of emotional intent rather than direct reflections of psychological states.

Third, because our data are observational, we cannot infer causality. While we find robust associations between ideology, power status, and emotional tone, we cannot determine whether exposure to certain content causes schadenfreude or whether individuals predisposed to such emotions seek out particular media environments. Experimental approaches would be needed to establish causal relationships, which we leave as an avenue for future work. Fourth, our analysis focuses on public Facebook pages, which may not represent the broader audience. Users who comment on political news tend to be more engaged and polarized than average news consumers, meaning that our results likely capture the expressive core of the online public rather than its entirety. Finally, cross-national comparisons must be interpreted with caution. Differences in media regulation, language, and platform penetration can influence both the frequency and meaning of emotional expression. Future research combining multilingual annotation, qualitative contextualization, and cross-platform data would strengthen the generalizability of our findings.

Despite these limitations, the consistency of our results across multiple measures, such as reaction icons, comment text, topical domains, and regression analyses, supports our central conclusion that schadenfreude has become a structured, context-dependent feature of online news engagement. Rather than being an incidental byproduct of online hostility, it constitutes a measurable and enduring mode of affective participation that reflects deeper moral and political dynamics in the digital public sphere.
