From Confounding to Learning: Dynamic Service Fee Pricing on Third-Party Platforms
Rui Ai, David Simchi-Levi, Feng Zhu

Abstract
We study the pricing behavior of third-party platforms facing strategic agents. Assuming the platform is a revenue maximizer, it observes market features that generally affect demand. Since only the equilibrium price and quantity are observable, this presents a general demand learning problem under confounding. Mathematically, we develop an algorithm with optimal regret of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T ∧ σ S − 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T}\wedge\sigma_{S}^{-2}) . Our results reveal that supply-side noise fundamentally affects the learnability of demand, leading to a phase transition in regret. Technically, we show that non-i.i.d. actions can serve as instrumental variables for learning demand. We also propose a novel homeomorphic construction that allows us to establish estimation bounds without assuming star-shapedness, providing the first efficiency guarantee for learning demand with deep neural networks. Finally, we demonstrate the practical applicability of our approach through simulations and real-world data from Zomato and Lyft.

Introduction

Nowadays, millions of transactions occur daily on third-party platforms such as food delivery services (e.g., DoorDash
(
37
)
) and ride-hailing apps (e.g., Uber
(
43
)
). These platforms earn revenue by charging a service fee on each transaction — such as the booking fee on Uber or the service fee added by DoorDash. Setting this fee appropriately is critical, as it directly influences the final price seen by consumers. For example, if DoorDash sets its service fee too high, price-sensitive consumers may leave the platform or even switch to alternatives like Uber Eats. Conversely, if the fee is too low, the platform risks eroding its profit margins. In this work, we study how a platform can learn to set its optimal service fee
(
39
,
44
,
61
)
in a market with strategic consumers and confounding factors.

Example 1.1

As a ride-hailing platform, Uber charges a booking fee for each reservation, as shown in
Figure

1
. Over a certain time period, Uber’s booking fee remains approximately the same. However, over a longer period of time, an exploratory dynamic pricing strategy can be beneficial to acquire more demand information.

Figure 1:
Uber ride receipts from the same city: one from February 2024 (left) and two from March 2024 (right)

However, to set a reasonable service fee, the platform faces several key challenges:

\bullet

Unobserved demand curve:
While the platform can observe the supply side — for example, DoorDash knows which restaurants are available and their menu prices — it lacks direct access to the demand curve. Although it may observe certain market features (e.g., increased demand on rainy days), the relationship between these features and actual consumer demand is often complex and unknown.

\bullet

Confounded observations:
The platform can only observe the equilibrium price
P_{t}^{e}
and quantity
Q_{t}^{e}
, which are influenced by supply, the service fee, and market conditions. Some confounding factors can bias demand estimation. For instance, naively regressing
Q_{t}^{e}
on
P_{t}^{e}
may result in spurious positive correlations when supply fluctuations are minimal — an outcome that contradicts economic intuition. This highlights the need for valid, yet often scarce, instrumental variables (IVs).

\bullet

Strategic consumer behavior:
Consumers often act strategically. For example, on Uber, if a customer checks the price and finds it too high, the customer may choose to wait until the price goes down. Similarly, consumers may delay purchases until sales events like Black Friday. Prior work
(
5
,
4
)
shows that when buyers and sellers are similarly patient, no-regret learning becomes impossible. This makes optimal pricing under strategic behavior particularly difficult.

\bullet

Nonstandard function learning with DNNs:
To model the complex relationship between demand and market features, deep neural networks (DNNs) are often leveraged. However, standard statistical learning theory often relies on structural assumptions like star-shapedness
(
87
,
76
,
91
)
, which DNNs typically violate. This creates a significant theoretical gap between the empirical success of DNNs and their formal understanding. Bridging this gap is essential for developing interpretable and reliable machine learning methods in pricing applications
(
82
,
92
)
.

In this paper, we tackle the challenges outlined above through a series of methodological and theoretical contributions.

\bullet

To address the limited availability of instrumental variables in market settings, we propose using endogenous, non-i.i.d. actions as approximate instruments. This approach provides a practical workaround for the scarcity of valid instruments in real-world applications.

\bullet

Incorporating techniques from doubly robust machine learning, we design an algorithm that attains
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
regret in noise-free supply settings, and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret when supply noise is present. Both bounds are tight and match the corresponding information-theoretic lower bounds. Moreover, we identify a phase transition in the regret behavior as the noise variance crosses the critical threshold of
\Theta(1/\sqrt{T})
: below this level, the regret remains constant, while above it, it decreases along a hyperbolic trajectory.

\bullet

To accommodate strategic buyer behavior, we develop a low-switching-cost algorithm that updates the pricing policy only
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
times over
T
rounds. This design ensures sublinear regret while remaining adaptive and robust, without requiring prior knowledge of buyers’ discount factors.

\bullet

For the setting involving deep neural networks, we construct a homeomorphic transformation and show that empirical risk minimization (ERM), when combined with instrumental variable regression, achieves asymptotically optimal estimation even in the absence of the standard star-shapeness assumption. Interestingly, the effective dimension of the resulting function class increases by at most one, preserving statistical efficiency.

We validate our approach through comprehensive simulations and empirical studies on real-world datasets. Our algorithm consistently delivers accurate demand estimation and leads to substantial revenue improvements for third-party service platforms.

2.1
The Model

We study the revenue management problem from the perspective of a civilian-operated online platform that facilitates transactions between buyers and sellers.

Sellers

Over a horiozon of
T
rounds, sellers first announce their supply before buyers or the platform taking actions — for instance, in a ride-hailing context, platforms such as Uber are aware of the number and types of available vehicles (e.g., UberX, UberXL) at any given moment; similarly, in ticket marketplaces such as Ticketmaster, sellers disclose the number of tickets they wish to offer, along with their ask prices. Motivated by these examples, we model the total supply curve at time
t
as:

P_{St}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}Q+\epsilon_{St},

where
\epsilon_{St}
represents zero-mean,
\eta_{S}
-sub-Gaussian noise with variance
\sigma_{S}^{2}
. We assume sellers report their supply curves truthfully. Since the platform’s pricing decision and buyers’ purchasing behavior occur after the supply being revealed, sellers have no strategic incentive to misrepresent their offers, and their actions do not confer any additional advantage through strategic manipulation.
For instance, on Ticketmaster, sellers must decide their ask price before the tickets enter the market, and when a buyer is willing to pay that price, the platform automatically matches and completes the transaction.

The Platform

At the same time, the platform collects revenue by charging a service fee — for example, Uber collects a booking fee, and Ticketmaster charges a service fee and an order processing fee. In this paper, we collectively refer to these as service fees, denoted by
a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. We assume that the platform announces its pricing structure before transactions occur, as previous behavior-based pricing literature
(
45
,
79
,
12
,
13
)
has suggested that committing to a pricing strategy can help the platform earn more revenue.

Buyers

The platform does not have access to the buyers’ demand curve. For simplicity, we assume there is a single representative buyer in the market. In reality, some online platforms are legally restricted from implementing personalized pricing
(
80
)
. When there are multiple buyers, we can interpret the quantity
Q
as the total purchase aggregated from all buyers. Although the platform cannot observe the demand curve, which represents the private type of the representative buyer, it can observe some public feature
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
before setting the service fee. For example, Uber can use weather conditions and traffic congestion levels to predict demand, and Ticketmaster can estimate consumer interest based on metrics such as click-through rates and page dwell time. We assume the demand curve is

P_{Dt}=\beta Q+f(x_{t})+\epsilon_{Dt},

where the external zero-mean
strongly-log-concave noise
\epsilon_{D}
with variance
\sigma_{D}^{2}
captures unobservable demand information beyond
x_{t}
. To avoid nuisance from market feasibility, we assume that
\alpha_{1}\geq 0>\beta
, and all realized prices and quantities are non-negative — otherwise, we can truncate those negative numbers to zero, and such truncation does not affect our analysis and conclusions (see also
Section

B.1
).

rationale for assuming a linear relationship between price and quantity

\alpha_{1}
and
\beta

19
show that under certain conditions, the consequences of model misspecification due to assuming linearity are significantly less severe than commonly expected. Third, the linear form is widely adopted in the literature on demand learning
(

14

, instrumental variables
(

(

Strategic Behavior

Unlike sellers, since the platform cannot observe demand, the representative buyer has room for strategic behavior. For example, a common practice taken by many Uber passengers is to wait and save. Another example is shopping on Amazon — even if a buyer’s willingness to pay is higher than the current price, many buyers may wait until Black Friday to make a purchase because they anticipate that the price will drop on Black Friday. We use
P_{Dt}^{\prime}(Q,x_{t})
to denote the realized demand, possibly tweaked by strategic behavior.

The platform cannot observe the entire demand curve but can only observe the equilibrium price and quantity denoted by
(P_{t}^{e},Q_{t}^{e})
, determined by the following equation:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:equ}P_{t}^{e}:=P_{St}(Q_{t}^{e})+a_{t}=P^{\prime}_{Dt}(Q_{t}^{e},x_{t}).

(1)

The buyer’s strategic behavior aims to maximize cumulative consumer surplus. Since we consider a civilian-run online platform, please refer to
69

(

For instance, auction platforms continuously auction off a large volume of ad slots, whereas buyers often have immediate advertising needs. Platforms are generally less focused on short-term fluctuations and more prioritized on long-term user engagement and retention. 2.2
Our Methodology

In this section, we provide a high-level description of our methodology. To begin, we abstract away from buyers’ strategic behavior and focus exclusively on the problem of demand learning.

For the platform, the only observable variables are the equilibrium price
P_{t}^{e}
and quantity
Q_{t}^{e}
, both of which are influenced by market noise. As a result, simple estimation methods — such as nonparametric regression — are susceptible to confounding, leading to biased estimates. Specifically, we have the moment condition

{\mathbb{E}}[(P_{t}^{e}-\beta Q_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t}))Q_{t}^{e}]\neq 0,

because
\epsilon_{D_{t}}
is correlated with
Q_{t}^{e}
. Note that a standard non-parametric regression tries to solve

\min_{\beta,f}\sum_{t=1}(P_{t}^{e}-\beta Q_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t}))^{2}.

Taking the gradient of
\beta
as
0
leads to

\sum_{t=1}^{T}(P_{t}^{e}-\beta Q_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t}))Q_{t}^{e}=0

which is inconsistent with the moment condition. Instead, by noticing that
x_{t}
is independent of the noise term
\epsilon_{Dt}
, we can obtain the following structural equation:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:structural}{\mathbb{E}}[(P_{t}^{e}-\beta Q_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t}))\tilde{f}(x_{t})]={\mathbb{E}}[\epsilon_{D_{t}}\tilde{f}(x_{t})]=0,\ \forall\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{F}.

(2)

Since the effective dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is
dim
, intuitively Equation (
2
) provides a set of
dim
essential equations. However, because of the unknown
\beta
, there are
\textit{dim}+1
unknowns. Recall that
a_{t}
, the service fee, is chosen before the realization of
\epsilon_{Dt}
. Consequently, we can use the service fee
a_{t}
as an instrumental variable —

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:IV}{\mathbb{E}}[(P_{t}^{e}-\beta Q_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t}))a_{t}]=0,

(3)

Note that
a_{t}
is not necessarily an independent variable and can be dependent on the past history, but still can be used as an instrumental variable. The non i.i.d. challenge can be resolved using martingale concentration analysis. Together, (
2
) and (
3
) provide a sufficient number of equations to estimate the demand curve.

Building on the development of doubly-robust machine learning techniques
(
50
,
29
)
, we can enhance Equation (
3
) via a robust structural equation to reduce variance:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:IV_{r}obust}{\mathbb{E}}[(P_{t}^{e}-\beta Q_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t}))(a_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}[a_{t}{\,|\,}x_{t}])]=0.

(4)

As long as either
P_{t}^{e}-\beta Q_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t})
or
a_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}[a_{t}\mid x_{t}]
is accurately estimated, Equation (
4
) holds, thereby compensating for first-order errors. In supplementary material, we use ablation experiments to demonstrate that the doubly-robust approach produces estimators with smaller variance.

We therefore combine empirical risk minimization and instrumental-variable regression to estimate demand. Specifically, our estimators are given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[(P_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t}))(a_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}[a_{t}{\,|\,}x_{t}])]}{{\mathbb{E}}[Q_{t}^{e}(a_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}[a_{t}{\,|\,}x_{t}])]},

\displaystyle\widehat{f}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}{\mathbb{E}}[(P_{t}^{e}-\beta Q_{t}^{e}-f(x_{t}))^{2}].

In practice, we start by setting
\widehat{f}=0
, and then iterate over the following updates using empirical expectations.

However, our estimation method can be vulnerable to the weak instrumental variable problem when the market noise is insufficient. From an online learning perspective, this corresponds to inadequate exploration. To mitigate this issue, we inject additional noise into the system. Notably, a naive constant noise injection would increase regret linearly, so the noise level must be carefully tuned. This tuning requires balancing the high uncertainty caused by weak instrumental variables against the loss caused by artificial exploration. Our theory and experiments show that setting the noise magnitude to
t^{-1/4}
effectively balances these two factors, minimizing regret and enabling near-optimal algorithm design.

Finally, we address the challenge of the buyer’s untruthful demand reporting. Misreporting leads to an immediate loss for the buyer but can be compensated for by a higher future surplus. To counter this incentive, we design a low-switching algorithm that reduces the frequency of parameter re-estimation and algorithm updates, thereby discouraging strategic manipulation by the buyer.

These insights form the foundation of our algorithm design. In the following sections, we present the detailed algorithms and their corresponding regret bounds.

3.2
Regret Bound without Supply Volatility

We now turn to the case where the supply noise
\sigma_{S}^{2}=0

On the other hand, this situation implies that all observed equilibria lie on the supply curve, rendering the task of demand estimation particularly challenging.

To address this issue, we introduce artificial randomness into the service fee

to promote exploration. However, the level of injected noise must be carefully calibrated, since excessive randomness increases regret. This naturally leads to a trade-off between exploration and exploitation. We inject Gaussian noise into

. Specifically, we set the variance to

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{size}(\mathcal{D})+1}}.

This design ensures that the injected noise diminishes as the dataset grows. Intuitively, as more data accumulate, the extent of buyer misreporting also gradually increases. The coupling between dataset size and noise variance helps mitigate the overall impact on regret. We present the formal result in the following theorem.

3.4
Technical Challenges

In this section, we briefly introduce our technical innovations in proving the upper and lower bounds.

Bypassing the star-shaped assumption.
In the statistical learning literature, it is common to assume that function class
\mathcal{F}-f
is star-shaped
(
87
,
91
)
. However, this assumption is often difficult to satisfy in real-world scenarios. Typically, the star-shaped property holds only for
\mathcal{F}
itself. Note that when we say
\mathcal{F}
is star-shaped, it means that for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have
\lambda f\in\mathcal{F}
. When we use a deep learning network (such as an MLP, CNN, RNN, or Transformer) to learn the demand curve—which is very common in industry—
\mathcal{F}
is star-shaped, since we can obtain
\lambda\mathcal{F}
by rescaling the last layer (i.e., multiplying it entirely by
\lambda
). However, we have no reason to believe that the entire function class remains star-shaped after centering around a specific
f
. An alternative approach is to assume the convexity of

\mathcal{F}
, but this would rule out powerful tools like deep learning, making it difficult to build a bridge between theoretical results and practical applications.

Figure 3:
\mathcal{F}
is only star-shaped around 0.

We developed a new analytical approach that goes beyond the star-shaped assumption. Specifically, we construct an auxiliary function class that is homeomorphic to

\lambda\mathcal{F}+(1-\lambda)f
. Note that this function class is not directly accessible since
f
is unknown. We then show that the effective dimension of this new function class increases only slightly compared to the original
\mathcal{F}
. In fact, it increases from
dim
to
dim+1
. Intuitively, we can duplicate the network
\mathcal{F}
, i.e., double the width, and then add a new connecting layer with weights
\lambda
and
1-\lambda
, respectively. However, this new function class is star-shaped with respect to
f
. Importantly, we do not even require
\mathcal{F}
itself to be star-shaped, thereby extending the boundaries of traditional statistical learning—at the cost of only a one-dimensional increase in complexity. The final step is to observe that our algorithm does not rely on access to the new function class; we use it merely as a bridge to establish statistical efficiency. What we need is to compute the complexity of
\mathcal{F}-f
, which is no greater than that of the new function class since it is homomorphically contained within it. Thus, by constructing this auxiliary function class, we obtain an upper bound that bypasses the unrealistic star-shaped assumption. This provides a theoretical explanation for why deep learning can perform well in dynamic pricing.

Demand noise in hard-to-differentiate instances
: We know that supply noise can help learning. From the proof of the upper bounds, we see that when
\sigma_{S}^{2}
is smaller than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}
, the regret scales as
\sqrt{T}
; when it is larger than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}
, the regret scales as
\frac{1}{\sigma_{S}^{2}}
. This suggests that noise also carries some information. So, does the noise in demand play an equally important role? The answer is
yes
. The magnitude of noise in demand is crucial for the proof of the lower bound. When we construct hard-to-differentiate instances, we find that if we only modify the form of the expected demand while keeping the demand noise unchanged, the demand noise will transmit information about the demand curve by affecting the variance of the equilibrium price and quantity. Therefore, traditional methods for constructing lower bounds, whether based on KL divergence or the Van Trees inequality, cannot eliminate the information leakage caused by demand noise simply by adjusting the value of
\beta
. As a result, existing approaches for constructing hard-to-differentiate instances are not applicable in this setting.

We isolate the information leakage from demand noise by adjusting both the parameters and the magnitude of the demand noise. In other words, we construct two instances whose first-order differences are zero, leaving only second-order differences. As a result, all algorithms struggle to distinguish between them, effectively establishing the lower bound. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time in the literature that the information contained in demand noise has been considered in the context of proving lower bounds. In summary, supply noise plays a central role in our proof of the upper bounds, while demand noise plays a crucial role in establishing the lower bounds. Our approach not only offers a new perspective for future lower bound proofs, but also provides fresh insights into how noise influences machine learning.

4
Practical Considerations and Extensions

4.1
Economic Insights and Examples

There are several components in our regret bounds. We now provide economic insights into how these parameters shape the regret behavior.

\bullet

\gamma
(Discount rate): The discount factor
\gamma
captures the difference in patience between the platform and the consumers. As discussed earlier, when
\gamma=1
, the regret becomes unavoidably linear. In this case, consumers can misreport their demand and wait for future price reductions without incurring any cost, since future services have the same value as present ones. When
\gamma<1
, however, the value of future services decays exponentially, which is reflected in the regret bounds — due to strategic behavior, regret scales polynomially with
\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
.

\bullet

\sigma_{S}^{2}
(Supply volatility): The supply volatility
\sigma_{S}^{2}
characterizes noise in the supply process. Since the platform makes decisions after observing the supply, the direct impact of supply noise on revenue management is limited. Nevertheless, supply noise plays a crucial role in facilitating exploration on the demand side. In the extreme case where
\sigma_{S}^{2}=0
, all equilibrium points lie on the supply curve, making it difficult to infer demand. To address this, we introduce artificial perturbations to the service fee. This mechanism also intuitively explains why regret decreases as
\sigma_{S}^{2}
increases.

\bullet

dim
(Effective dimension): The effective dimension
dim
represents the complexity of the relationship between demand and market features. Mathematically, highly irregular demand functions are unlearnable, but in practice, demand typically depends on features in a structured and smooth way. Here, we use
dim
to quantify model complexity. Apart from the unavoidable approximation error,
dim
effectively captures the linear relationship between model complexity and regret: a larger
dim
reduces model bias but increases variance given finite data. In Section
5
, we will employ neural networks to model and predict market behavior.

Beyond these economic interpretations, we next examine some commonly used function classes and their effective dimensions.

4.3
Extensions: General Demand Learning

In this section, we leave a practical recipe for general demand learning, where we abandon all assumptions and only assume

Ignoring concerns about whether

will be influenced by the previous service fee.
Second, we need extra artificial randomness to explore, as
Theorem

3.3
suggests. However, aiming for sublinear regret, the size of artificial noise should vanish after obtaining enough information on demand. This scheme brings confounders as well.
Finally, the strategic behavior also relates to

and affects equilibria. In other words, strategic behavior serves as a mediator. Nonetheless, our approach of using actions as instruments remains plausible. Using the low-switching cost design, we only need to update policies

. Given the average quantity (29.61) and price (—113.28) in the training set, a 1% price increase implies approximately a 0.53% decrease in demand, consistent with the relatively low price elasticity expected for essential goods such as meal deliveries.

Revenue Evaluation and Counterfactual Analysis

We next evaluate the impact of our algorithm on platform revenue using the test set. Because supply data are unavailable, we fix the
mean service fee
(service fee averaged over time) at its observed level (—4.90) and examine how redistributing it across time affects total revenue. The results show a substantial increase in average hourly revenue — from —110.65 to —611.45 (Figure
8
) — without altering the mean service fee or equilibrium quantity. The improvement arises entirely from dynamically adjusting service fees in response to estimated demand elasticities.

Economically, the algorithm increases service fees during peak-demand hours (e.g., dinnertime) and lowers them in off-peak periods, consistent with the theoretical framework. This suggests that actual platform pricing may prioritize market share and traffic acquisition over short-term profit maximization, potentially explaining the relatively low baseline service-fee share.

Robustness and Discussion

We test the robustness of the results by varying the training-test split between 70% and 90%, observing consistent revenue improvements across all configurations (Figure
8
). Two caveats are noteworthy. First, the service fee definition includes both the platform’s commission and restaurant-related charges, which may overstate the algorithm’s revenue contribution. Second, unobserved supply-side variations — such as increased delivery capacity during dinner hours — could also influence equilibrium quantities and service fees, potentially biasing our estimates upward.

Overall, the findings provide strong empirical evidence that employing
actions as instruments
enables more accurate demand learning and that dynamic service-fee optimization can generate substantial revenue gains for the platform.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the problem of dynamic service fee pricing on third-party platforms. We show that the actions themselves can serve as instruments to address confounding in equilibrium. By combining adaptive randomness injection with a low-switching design, we develop an approximately optimal
Algorithm

1
. Moreover, we find that the platform’s regret is closely tied to the level of noise in supply. When the noise variance is 0, an
\Omega(\sqrt{T})
regret is unavoidable, whereas
Algorithm

1
achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
regret with constant supply noise.
Besides, we observe a phase transition around the order of
\Theta(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
. Finally, using data from food delivery and ride-hailing platforms, we demonstrate that our algorithm significantly improves platform revenue.

This work opens up several promising directions for future research. Is the dependence of our algorithm on
\gamma
optimal? While we provide theoretical guarantees under a separable model, we propose an extension and demonstrate empirical performance for non-separable models in
Sections

4.3
and
E.2
. However, establishing theoretical guarantees for pricing with deep neural networks remains challenging due to the lack of a solid theoretical foundation in deep learning. Bridging the gap between empirical success and theoretical understanding under more general models is a valuable avenue for future exploration. We hope our work offers useful insights into the pricing problem faced by third-party platforms.

We now consider two situations when

and

During the whole
T

On the one hand, when

The first inequality holds due to the positivity of regret while the second inequality holds because when

On the other hand, when

\frac{T}{2}

A.11

Therefore, it holds that by combining both two cases

\displaystyle\quad\max\{{\mathbb{E}}\text{Regret}_{0}(T),{\mathbb{E}}\text{Regret}_{\epsilon}(T)\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}({\mathbb{E}}\text{Regret}_{0}(T)+{\mathbb{E}}\text{Regret}_{\epsilon}(T))

\displaystyle\geq\min\{{\mathbb{E}}_{[\cdot|T_{0}\geq\frac{T}{2}]}\frac{\text{Regret}_{0}(T)+\text{Regret}_{\epsilon}(T)}{2},{\mathbb{E}}_{[\cdot|T_{0}<\frac{T}{2}]}\frac{\text{Regret}_{0}(T)+\text{Regret}_{\epsilon}(T)}{2}\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\{\frac{25\sqrt{T}}{4},\frac{25\sqrt{T}}{384}e^{-\frac{25}{18}}\}

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T}\log^{2}T)&\text{ when }\sigma_{S}^{2}\in[\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}},\frac{5}{\sqrt{T}}]\text{ and }\mathbb{H}_{0}\\
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T}\log^{3}T)&\text{ when }\sigma_{S}^{2}\in[\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}},\frac{5}{\sqrt{T}}]\text{ and }\mathbb{H}_{1}\\
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log^{3}T}{\sigma_{S}^{2}})&\text{ when }\sigma_{S}^{2}\in[\frac{5}{\sqrt{T}},\infty).\end{array}\right.

Notice that when
\sigma_{S}^{2}\in[\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}},\frac{5}{\sqrt{T}}]
, adding artificial randomness or not results in slightly different results. It may inspire us to change the threshold of hypothesis testing from
\Theta(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
to
\Theta(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}})

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t-1}(\widetilde{\beta})=\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(Q_{i}^{e}-\frac{\beta_{0}-a_{i}-\alpha_{0}-\epsilon_{Si}}{\alpha_{1}-\beta}\right)^{2}+C,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{t-1}^{\pi}(\widetilde{\beta})=\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{\pi}\left[-\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{t-1}(\widetilde{\beta})/\partial\tilde{\beta}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\beta}}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{(\alpha_{1}-\beta)^{2}}&-\frac{\beta_{0}-a_{i}-\alpha_{0}-\epsilon_{Si}}{(\alpha_{1}-\beta)^{2}}\\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{\pi}\left[(a_{t}-a_{t}^{*}(\widetilde{\beta}))^{2}\right]\right]\geq\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[C(\widetilde{\beta})(\partial a_{t}^{*}(\widetilde{\beta})/\partial\widetilde{\beta})^{\top}\right]\right)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[C(\widetilde{\beta})\mathcal{I}_{t-1}^{\pi}(\widetilde{\beta})C(\widetilde{\beta})^{\top}\right]+{\mathcal{I}}(\lambda)},

\displaystyle C(\widetilde{\beta})\mathcal{I}_{t-1}^{\pi}(\widetilde{\beta})C(\widetilde{\beta})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(\alpha_{1}-\beta)^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\left(\frac{\beta_{0}-\alpha_{0}}{2}-(\beta_{0}-a_{i}-\alpha_{0}-\epsilon_{Si})\right)^{2}\right]

D.2
Empirical Study Setup

The Zomato dataset contains individual orders in 21 Indian cities, including Ahmedabad, Bengaluru, Chennai, Coimbatore, Delhi, Gurgaon, Hyderabad, Jaipur, Jodhpur, Kochi, Kolkata,
Lucknow, Manali, Mumbai, Mysore, Nagpur, Ooty, Pune, Rourkela,
Trivandrum and Vadodara. The dataset contains not only the successful orders, but also the failed orders. We gather over hours the successful orders to simulate the market equilibrium
Q_{t}^{e}
. We use the amount a customer pays for an order as the equilibrium price and the difference between it and the total value of the goods included in the order, including taxes, before any discounts, as the service fee. Note that the service fee includes a platform fee (as of May 2025, 10 per order), delivery partner fee, restaurant charges, and any applicable coupons. We use simple averages to construct features. For example, if in one hour, 30% orders are in Delhi while 70% are in Mumbai, the Delhi dummy will be 0.3. Although Zomato is a food delivery app, it actually contains seven kinds of items, including pharmacy, cosmetics, electronics, flowers, grocery, pet shop and food (constituting 83.47%). We also include dummy variables indicating discounts and first-time customers. Besides, we control for the delivery time and dropoff distance. Finally, we remove observations with missing values.

We use the service fee as an instrument to estimate the demand curve. For ordinary least squares, the estimated
\widehat{\beta}
is -0.02 with p-value 0.81. It is unreliable, highlighting the importance of using actions as instrumental variables in demand learning. Moreover, to calibrate the influence of the service fee on quantity, we conduct a linear regression using the test set. It serves as a robustness check for our algorithm under a potential distribution shift between the training and test sets. Since, for example, the platform fee is fixed, we regard the confounding to be negligible, as little strategic pricing is observed in the dataset between service fees and equilibrium quantities. The estimated result is
Q_{t}^{e}=-0.12a_{t}+\text{controls}
, and we also perform a calibration using the residuals. This aligns with the economic fact that higher service fees are shared between sellers and buyers.

Since we don’t have access to the supply data, we study the revenue if the average service fee is fixed, consequently, the average quantity is also fixed at 28.92. Since the platform must consider not only revenue but also competition for customer traffic, we can isolate the additional revenue attributable to our demand learning, holding the average service fee constant. Therefore, we estimate the supply intercept satisfying equal average value and get counterfactual service fees.

We find that
Algorithm

1
significantly increases revenue, with the average hourly revenue rising from approximately 100 to around 600. More specifically, our algorithm raises the service fee during peak demand hours such as dinnertime, which is consistent with our theoretical framework and thereby improves overall revenue. One potential explanation is that Zomato might be competing for customer traffic, so revenue may not be the platform’s primary concern during that period. This may also account for the relatively low proportion of the service fee.
Moreover, variations in supply, such as increased availability during dinner hours, may affect service fees; ignoring such factors could lead to an overestimation of our algorithm’s impact.
Also, our definition of service fee includes both the platform’s revenue and a portion of the restaurant’s income, which may lead to an overestimation of the algorithm’s performance due to the underlying allocation.

Besides the concern raised in the Zomato example, there are two additional concerns in this experiment. First, since the number of parameters far exceeds the number of samples, training may suffer from overfitting or get stuck in local optima. Second, the surge may not be a valid instrumental variable. For example, when there are sporting events in Boston, such as games by the Celtics or the Red Sox, certain areas experience not only a reduction in driver supply due to traffic congestion but also an increase in rider demand. As a result, the service fee we use may act as a confounder, which could explain the limited improvement in revenue.

We have a total of 23,540 data points. We use the first 80% of the data as the training set and the remaining 20% as the test set. We experiment with various network architectures, with the number of attention blocks ranging from 2 to 8, and the number of attention heads ranging from 2 to 16. The total number of parameters ranges from 41.7 K to 624 K. For a fair comparison, we also fix the average service fee. Please refer to

Among the 16 network architectures tested, 15 increase the platform’s revenue, with relative changes ranging from -0.65% to 9.37%. Compared to the Zomato case, the improvement in Lyft’s revenue with respect to surge is relatively limited. This may be due to the maturity of the ride-hailing market in the U.S. and the widespread adoption of personalized pricing.

Thus, a typical dynamic service fee pricing workflow involves first encoding various types of market information, such as images, text, and structured data, followed by using the service fee as an instrument to model the market demand curve with a neural network. Finally, based on the observed supply-side information, the service fee is adjusted to maximize revenue.
