MoleProLink-RL: geometric transport for domain-policy reinforcement learning in drug-target interaction prediction
Authors: Donghao Xu, Bibo Wang, Dexing Zhu, Chenzhi Zheng, Haitao Wu, Ruhan Wang, Jinxing Sun, Mingchen Xie, Xu Li, Liang Wu, Chao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate drug-target interaction (DTI) prediction is vital to modern discovery workflows, yet models trained in one setting often underperform when chemotypes, protein families, or assay conditions shift. MoleProLink-RL addresses this challenge as a model-first solution that couples chemically faithful representations with geometry-aware alignment and stability-aware decision making. The system combines graph-transformer drug encoders and residual protein embeddings with an unsupervised distribution alignment layer that respects molecular similarity through a DTI-aware transport distance complemented by kernel mean embeddings, while a lightweight reinforcement-learning objective regulates ranking stability across latent sub-environments under a small trust region to a supervised reference. Unlike pipelines that bolt adaptation onto frozen features or tune policies post hoc, MoleProLink-RL trains representation, alignment, and decisions end-to-end so that what the model learns to represent directly improves how it prioritizes candidates under shift. We provide a Fisher–Wasserstein view that clarifies why joint updates are safe in parameter space and effective on distribution space, and we validate the approach on Human, C. elegans, and Davis benchmarks with realistic class imbalance, comprehensive ablations, and diagnostics for calibration and interpretability. The result is a practical, data-efficient DTI model that maintains top-of-list precision when conditions change, narrowing the gap between benchmark performance and laboratory impact.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Modern drug discovery increasingly depends on computational models that can reason over vast chemical libraries and structurally diverse protein families. Among these efforts, drug–target interaction (DTI) prediction has emerged as a central engine for prioritizing hypotheses and reducing experimental burden Zhu et al.1, Zhang et al.2, Dehghan et al.3, complementing rapid advances across pharmaceutical R&D pipelines France et al.4, Husnain et al.5, Bhattamisra et al.6. Yet the environments in which DTIs are measured are far from stationary: assays evolve, chemotypes shift, and target coverage is uneven. As a result, models that excel in one setting may falter when asked to generalize to new scaffolds, orthologs, or experimental conditions.

MoleProLink-RL is motivated by this reality. We frame DTI prediction as learning under distribution shift, where robust generalization requires aligning not only molecular representations but also the inductive biases that govern decision making in new domains. In practice, shift appears as covariate drift across chemical space or family-level variation across targets; both degrade ranking precision if left unaddressed Sui et al.7, Zhang et al.8. While numerous domain-adaptation strategies have been proposed, most either treat alignment as a purely statistical correction detached from molecular structure or emphasize feature mapping without addressing stability of downstream decisions Singhal et al.9, Martakis et al.10, HassanPour Zonoozi and Seydi11, Fang et al.12. Our perspective is that scalable progress requires a model that couples structure-aware transport with policy-level regularization and does so end-to-end.

To that end, MoleProLink-RL unifies three ingredients. First, it learns chemically faithful encodings by marrying graph-transformer representations on the drug side with residual protein embeddings, allowing the model to capture long-range molecular dependencies and sequence-resolved context. Second, it performs unsupervised domain adaptation by aligning source and target distributions through a geometry that respects molecular similarity while remaining computationally tractable Fang et al.12, Oza et al.13, Gu et al.14. Third, it stabilizes decisions at the ranking layer with a reinforcement learning (RL) objective that penalizes instability across latent sub-environments, improving precision where positive cases are rare and costly to verify in practice Sadybekov and Katritch15.

The alignment component builds on a principled synthesis of measure-theoretic and geometric viewpoints. At the distribution level, MoleProLink-RL leverages optimal transport to couple domains along cost surfaces that encode chemophysical and sequence-informed similarity, while kernel mean embeddings complement transport by capturing high-frequency mismatches that transport alone can blur. At the model level, information geometry provides a metric structure for safe updates in parameter space, ensuring that representation shifts and decision shifts co-evolve coherently rather than compete Bottazzi16, Willem17, Spendlove et al.18, Sèjournè et al.19. Concretely, we instantiate a DTI-aware transport distance that extends classical formulations to molecular data without discarding domain knowledge Hosseini-Nodeh et al.20, and we deploy it jointly with an RL-driven stability term that reduces variance across implicit assay contexts.

Positioned in this way, MoleProLink-RL departs from pipelines that bolt adaptation onto a frozen encoder or treat policy learning as a post-hoc reweighting step. Instead, alignment and decision making are trained together so that what the model learns to represent is immediately reflected in how it ranks candidates. This coupling is key for cross-family transfer, cross-species generalization, and temporal shifts in assay protocols, where faithful representation alone is insufficient. The result is a system that remains data-efficient but resilient to shift, with practical impact on triage strategies in low-data regimes and on prioritization when new targets or chemotypes enter the queue.

We emphasize that MoleProLink-RL is a model-first contribution: the method integrates a lightweight RL objective and a geometry-aware alignment layer to support the model’s core goal of robust DTI ranking. Theoretical development—including an unsupervised adaptation view and geometric guarantees for safe updates—follows naturally from this design Fang et al.12. Empirically, we demonstrate consistent gains across Human, C. elegans, and Davis benchmarks under realistic imbalance, illustrating that coupling transport with stability-aware learning translates into measurable improvements where it matters most. In the remainder of the paper we detail the architecture and training procedure, relate our approach to prior adaptation paradigms, and present comprehensive evaluations with ablations that disentangle representation alignment from decision stability.

Looking ahead, we position MoleProLink-RL as a bridge between representation learning and decision making under shift. The reinforcement learning layer is intentionally lightweight: it optimizes a listwise objective that emphasizes top-of-list precision, which is where screening budgets are spent, while a small trust region prevents drift away from a strong supervised reference. In ablations we find that this coupling makes the model less sensitive to spurious shortcuts and more faithful to structure-activity trends when the target family or assay protocol changes between training and deployment. Rather than treating domain adaptation as an auxiliary penalty that equalizes marginals, MoleProLink-RL treats it as guidance for what the decision maker should care about in the new domain.

We also emphasize the scientific plausibility of the learned behavior. Attention patterns in the drug transformer and saliency on protein embeddings align with established interaction motifs, while the transport couplings identify chemotype and family-level analogies that practitioners would expect to carry over across assays. The geometric view helps diagnose difficulty: when the parameter manifold exhibits high curvature around a family boundary, optimization proceeds conservatively, and the resulting predictions remain calibrated. These diagnostics are not merely interpretive; they influence learning rates, augmentation schedules, and when to favor precision over recall, especially in the long tail of novel chemistries.

From an engineering standpoint, MoleProLink-RL is designed to be practical. The training recipe accommodates severe class imbalance, mixed-precision execution, and memory-efficient batching without sacrificing stability. Because the alignment loss and the RL objective share the same minibatches, the wall-clock overhead is modest. The system is modular as well: transport cost functions can be swapped to reflect problem-specific chemistry, and the stability regularizer can be tuned to match the tolerance for false positives in a given campaign. These choices make deployment feasible in workflows that already depend on automated triage, without forcing disruptive infrastructure changes.

Finally, we situate MoleProLink-RL with respect to prior lines of work: representation-only adaptation can reduce apparent discrepancy while leaving decision boundaries brittle; policy-only optimization can improve a snapshot metric while unintentionally overfitting to a source domain’s quirks. By unifying geometry-aware alignment with stability-aware policy learning, our approach aims to capture the upside of both while limiting their failure modes. The story throughout this paper therefore remains model-centric: MoleProLink-RL learns what to represent, how to align it across domains, and how to act on it when conditions shift, so that improved theory translates into decisions that matter in the laboratory.

Results

Dataset and baseline

Datasets (Human, C. elegans, and Davis) Knox et al.21 were selected to train and evaluate the classification performance of the model. The datasets were randomly divided into source domain and target domain in a 7:3 ratio. Subsequently, the target domain dataset was further split into target train and target test datasets in a 4:1 ratio. The source domain includes all labeled data samples and their corresponding labels, which provide essential information for the model to capture the features and patterns of the data, thereby enabling effective predictive capabilities. The target train dataset consists of unlabeled samples used for model training, while the target test dataset provides labeled samples to facilitate the evaluation of the model’s testing performance.

Specifically, the GPCR dataset was meticulously curated from the GLASS Chan et al.22 database using a label reversal methodology, representing another crucial protein family that accounts for ~35% of all FDA-approved drug targets. The curation process yielded 1913 positive interactions between 233 compounds and 95 GPCR targets. The label reversal methodology addresses the common issue of database bias where only positive interactions are typically reported. This approach involves: (1) identifying all compounds tested against each GPCR in the database, (2) marking explicitly reported interactions as positive, and (3) conservatively labeling unreported compound-target pairs as negative when the compound was tested against closely related GPCRs. This methodology adheres to two primary criteria: (i) inclusion of interactions validated by experimental evidence, requiring at least two independent experimental confirmations or one high-quality structure-based validation; and (ii) ensuring that each ligand is present in both the training and testing subsets, preventing the model from simply memorizing compound-specific patterns. This curation process ensures that the GPCR dataset effectively enables the model to learn interaction features without being adversely affected by dataset variations.

To ensure consistency across all datasets, we performed extensive preprocessing and standardization. Chemical structures were standardized using RDKit, including aromaticity perception, stereochemistry assignment, and salt stripping. Protein sequences were retrieved from UniProt and aligned using BLAST to identify homologous regions and conserved domains. We computed comprehensive molecular descriptors for all compounds, including 2D fingerprints (ECFP4, MACCS keys), 3D pharmacophore features, and physicochemical properties (logP, molecular weight, polar surface area). For proteins, we extracted sequence-based features, evolutionary profiles from multiple sequence alignments, and structural features when available from the Protein Data Bank. This rich feature representation enables our model to capture multi-scale patterns in drug-target interactions. Negative samples for both Human and C. elegans datasets were generated through sophisticated computational matching simulation techniques that ensure realistic negative examples while avoiding potential false negatives. Our negative sampling strategy employs a multi-tiered approach: first, we apply chemical similarity filtering to exclude compounds structurally similar to known binders (Tanimoto coefficient > 0.7); second, we utilize protein family information to avoid sampling within closely related targets that might share binding sites; third, we implement a pharmacophore-based filter to eliminate drug-target pairs with compatible binding features. This process generated 15,000 and 16,000 high-confidence negative samples for the Human and C. elegans datasets, respectively, maintaining a realistic positive-to-negative ratio that reflects the sparse nature of true drug-target interactions in biological systems.

The Davis dataset Davis et al.23 encompasses pertinent inhibitors and selected members of the kinase protein family, representing one of the most important target classes in drug discovery. This dataset contains binding affinity measurements for 30,056 drug-target pairs, involving 68 drugs and 442 kinases, with affinity values expressed as Kd constants ranging from 1 nM to 10 μM. Unlike the binary classification datasets, Davis provides continuous affinity values, which we discretize using a threshold of 30 nM to define strong binders. The kinase targets in this dataset span all major kinase families, including tyrosine kinases (both receptor and non-receptor types), serine/threonine kinases, and atypical kinases. The chemical compounds include FDA-approved drugs, clinical candidates, and tool compounds, providing a realistic representation of kinase chemical space. The dataset’s comprehensive coverage of the kinome makes it particularly valuable for evaluating domain adaptation across different kinase subfamilies.

Additionally, we choose TransCPI Tuncer et al.24, CPI-GNN Zhang et al.25, RF Zhao et al.26, LR Arabboev et al.27, DeepConV-DTI ConV-DTIBian et al.28, GraphDTA Ye and Sun29, CPI-GCN Zhang et al.30 as our baselines.

Implementation details

We implement the model in PyTorch 2.2.2 and use mamba-ssm 1.1.0 for the protein encoder. Unless otherwise stated, we train with AdamW (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999), cosine decay with 10% warm-up, gradient clipping at 1.0, automatic mixed precision (AMP, fp16), and early stopping on validation AUPR. For the drug Graph Transformer and protein token embeddings we set the model dimension to 256 with 8 attention heads (32 dims/head) and a feed-forward hidden size of 1024. We use dropout 0.20 in attention/MLP layers and weight decay 1 × 10−2. Training is conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24,GB); effective batch sizes below are achieved via gradient accumulation.

Human dataset: learning rate 2 × 10−4, effective batch size 256 using micro-batch 32 with 8 accumulation steps; other settings unchanged.

C. elegans dataset: same architecture; learning rate 2 × 10−4, effective batch size 256 using micro-batch 32 with 8 accumulation steps.

Davis dataset (30 nM threshold for binarization): learning rate 1.5 × 10−4, effective batch size 128 using micro-batch 16 with 8 accumulation steps, dropout 0.15; other settings unchanged.

Performance and analysis on different datasets

We conducted an in-depth analysis of the performance of six models on biological datasets: Human, C. elegans, and Davis. These datasets were selected to provide a comprehensive evaluation across different biological contexts and varying levels of complexity. The Human dataset represents interactions within a single species with extensive pharmaceutical relevance, while the C. elegans dataset offers insights into evolutionary conserved mechanisms across species. The Davis dataset, focusing specifically on kinase-inhibitor interactions, presents unique challenges due to the structural and functional similarities within the kinase family.

Within our model, several critical hyperparameters—such as learning rate, batch size, dropout, weight decay, decay interval, and learning rate decay—significantly impacted the final results. The learning rate determines the step size during gradient descent optimization and requires careful tuning to balance convergence speed with stability. Too high a learning rate can cause the model to overshoot optimal solutions, while too low a rate results in slow convergence and potential entrapment in local minima. The batch size affects both the computational efficiency and the quality of gradient estimates, with larger batches providing more stable gradients but potentially missing fine-grained patterns in the data. Dropout serves as a regularization technique to prevent overfitting by randomly deactivating neurons during training, forcing the network to learn more robust representations. Weight decay adds an L2 penalty to the loss function, encouraging smaller weight values and reducing model complexity. The decay interval and learning rate decay schedule control how the learning rate decreases over time, allowing for initial rapid learning followed by fine-tuning as the model converges.

The performance metrics were evaluated using rigorous cross-validation procedures to ensure reliable and reproducible results. Particularly in the Human and C. elegans datasets, where the AUC reached 97.34% and 98.13%, respectively, and the AUPR achieved 97.57% and 98.92%, our model demonstrates exceptional ability to distinguish between interacting and non-interacting drug-target pairs. The high AUPR values are especially significant given the inherent class imbalance in drug-target interaction datasets, where negative samples greatly outnumber positive ones. AUPR provides a more informative measure than AUC in such imbalanced scenarios, as it focuses on the model’s ability to correctly identify true positive interactions without being biased by the large number of true negatives.

These results outperform those of other baseline models, with our model showing improvements of 0.32% and 3.98% in AUC and AUPR, respectively, compared to the second-best baseline model. While the improvement in AUC may appear modest, it represents a meaningful enhancement in practical applications where even small improvements can translate to significant reductions in experimental validation costs. This highlights the significant advantage of our model in capturing the complexity of biological data, including the intricate patterns of molecular recognition, the influence of protein conformational flexibility, and the subtle chemical features that determine binding specificity.

The superior performance on these datasets can be attributed to several key innovations in our approach. The integration of graph transformers for molecular representation learning enables the model to capture long-range dependencies in molecular structures that traditional methods might miss. The residual protein embeddings preserve important sequence information while allowing for deep feature transformation. The multi-head attention mechanism facilitates the learning of diverse interaction patterns, recognizing that drug-target interactions can be mediated through various molecular mechanisms including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic complementarity.

Although our model exhibited relatively lower performance on the more challenging Davis dataset, achieving an AUC of 90.08%, it still surpassed all other baseline models. The Davis dataset presents unique challenges that help explain this performance difference. Kinase proteins share highly conserved ATP-binding pockets, making it difficult to predict inhibitor selectivity. Many kinase inhibitors exhibit polypharmacology, binding to multiple kinase targets with varying affinities. Furthermore, the conversion of continuous binding affinity values to binary interaction labels inevitably results in information loss, particularly for compounds with intermediate binding strengths near the classification threshold.

Despite these challenges, the substantial improvement over baseline methods demonstrates the robustness and adaptability of our model across different data environments. This performance gain suggests that our approach successfully captures kinase-specific interaction patterns that other methods miss. The model’s ability to maintain competitive performance even on this challenging dataset indicates its effectiveness in addressing the diversity and complexity inherent in biological datasets. This is particularly important for real-world drug discovery applications, where models must generalize across different protein families, chemical scaffolds, and experimental conditions.

The consistent performance improvements across all three datasets validate our unified mathematical framework for domain adaptation. The DTI-Wasserstein distance effectively captures domain-specific variations while preserving essential interaction patterns. The information-geometric perspective provides a principled approach to navigating the model space, ensuring optimal adaptation paths between different biological contexts. These theoretical innovations translate directly to practical performance gains, as evidenced by our empirical results.

The visualization clearly demonstrates the consistent superiority of our approach across diverse biological contexts, reinforcing the value of our integrated framework that combines advanced representation learning with principled domain adaptation techniques. These results not only validate our theoretical contributions but also provide strong evidence for the practical utility of our method in accelerating drug discovery through improved computational predictions.

The experimental design for our ablation study involved creating multiple model variants, each with a specific module removed or replaced with a simpler alternative. This approach allows us to isolate the contribution of each architectural innovation while maintaining the overall framework structure. The evaluation was conducted across all three benchmark datasets using identical training procedures and hyperparameters to ensure fair comparison. Each variant was trained five times with different random seeds to account for initialization variance, and we report the average performance metrics along with standard deviations.

The findings indicate that the removal of any module results in a decline in key performance metrics such as AUC and AUPR. This universal performance degradation underscores the complementary nature of our architectural components, where each module addresses specific aspects of the drug-target interaction prediction challenge. The varying degrees of performance drop across different modules provide valuable insights into their relative importance and the specific roles they play in the overall prediction pipeline. These results validate our design choices and demonstrate that the full model architecture represents an optimal configuration rather than an over-engineered solution.

Notably, when the Mamba module is omitted, the model’s performance significantly deteriorates, with AUC decreasing by 0.09–3.64% and AUPR declining by 0.30–5.11%. While the lower bound of performance drop (0.09% for AUC) might appear modest, the upper bound (3.64%) represents a substantial degradation that can significantly impact practical applications. The variation in performance drop across different datasets and evaluation metrics reveals the dataset-dependent importance of sequence modeling. For datasets with longer protein sequences or those requiring long-range dependency modeling, the absence of the Mamba module causes more severe performance degradation.

This underscores the critical role of the Mamba module in capturing the contextual information of protein sequences and extracting both local and global features. The Mamba architecture, inspired by state space models, provides an efficient alternative to traditional attention mechanisms for sequence modeling. Its ability to model sequences with linear computational complexity while maintaining long-range dependency capture makes it particularly suitable for protein sequences, which can span thousands of amino acids. The module’s structured state space formulation allows it to learn compressed representations of sequence history, effectively capturing evolutionary patterns and functional motifs that are crucial for predicting binding interactions.

The bidirectional Mamba module, by processing forward and backward information in parallel, enables a comprehensive understanding of the contextual relationships within protein sequences, thereby enhancing feature expressiveness. In the forward direction, the module captures the natural N-terminus to C-terminus flow of the protein chain, learning patterns related to signal peptides, domain organization, and post-translational modification sites. The backward processing complements this by identifying C-terminal motifs, retrograde signals, and structural constraints that propagate from the protein’s tertiary structure. This bidirectional processing is essential because protein function often depends on the interplay between distant sequence regions that come together in three-dimensional space.

This dual modeling capability not only improves the capture of key information within the sequence but also effectively boosts the model’s adaptability in complex tasks. The bidirectional nature allows the model to identify functional sites that may be defined by residues separated in the linear sequence but proximal in the folded structure. For instance, catalytic triads in enzymes often consist of residues from different regions of the sequence that must be considered together to understand their functional role. The Mamba module’s ability to efficiently process these long-range dependencies while maintaining computational efficiency makes it indispensable for accurate drug-target interaction prediction.

Moreover, the absence of the KAN module results in a lack of sufficient understanding of potential features, leading to poor performance in integrating drug and protein characteristics. The performance degradation observed when removing the KAN module manifests differently across various types of drug-target pairs. For small molecule drugs with simple structures, the impact is relatively modest, as basic feature concatenation can capture some interaction patterns. However, for complex molecules such as macrocycles, peptides, or drugs with multiple pharmacophores, the absence of KAN’s sophisticated integration mechanism severely hampers prediction accuracy. This differential impact highlights the module’s particular importance in handling complex chemical entities that are increasingly common in modern drug discovery.

The core advantage of the KAN network lies in its use of an attention mechanism, which allows the model to dynamically allocate weights to input features, thereby emphasizing important features and suppressing noise. Unlike static feature integration methods that apply fixed transformations regardless of input characteristics, the KAN module adapts its processing based on the specific drug-target pair being analyzed. This adaptive behavior is crucial because the relative importance of different molecular features varies dramatically depending on the target protein family and the drug’s mechanism of action. For instance, when predicting interactions with kinases, the module learns to emphasize ATP-mimetic features, while for GPCRs, it focuses on features related to transmembrane helix binding.

This mechanism enables the KAN module to excel in handling complex relationships, particularly when multiple features are involved. The attention mechanism in KAN operates at multiple levels of granularity, from individual atomic features to molecular fingerprints and global molecular properties. This hierarchical attention allows the model to identify which combinations of features are most predictive for specific interaction types. For example, the module might learn that hydrophobic moment combined with molecular flexibility is highly predictive for certain membrane protein targets, while electrostatic complementarity dominates for others.

The synergistic effects between modules were also investigated through partial ablation studies where we removed pairs of modules simultaneously. These experiments revealed strong interdependencies, with the combined removal of Mamba and KAN modules resulting in performance drops exceeding the sum of individual ablations. This super-additive effect demonstrates that the modules not only contribute independently but also enhance each other’s effectiveness through their integrated operation. The Mamba module’s rich sequence representations provide more informative inputs to the KAN module, while KAN’s sophisticated integration mechanism better leverages the nuanced features extracted by Mamba.

In summary, the ablation experiment results clearly demonstrate that each module within the model plays a proactive role in enhancing predictive capabilities. The systematic analysis reveals a carefully orchestrated architecture where each component addresses specific challenges in drug-target interaction prediction. The performance drops observed upon module removal are not merely statistical artifacts but reflect genuine functional contributions that cannot be easily compensated by other parts of the model.

The combination of the bidirectional Mamba module and the KAN network enables the model to not only gain deep insights into the structure and characteristics of protein sequences but also to efficiently integrate drug features, resulting in superior performance across tasks. This architectural synergy represents a significant advance over previous approaches that treated drug and target representations independently or relied on simplistic integration strategies. The success of our integrated approach validates the importance of co-designing representation learning and integration mechanisms specifically for the drug-target interaction prediction task.

Discussion

This paper presents a comprehensive and unified mathematical framework for unsupervised domain adaptation in drug-target interaction (DTI) prediction, integrating advanced concepts from measure theory, functional analysis, information geometry, and optimal transport theory. The integration of these mathematical disciplines represents a significant methodological advance, moving beyond traditional machine learning approaches that often rely on heuristic adaptation strategies. By grounding our framework in rigorous mathematical theory, we provide not only practical algorithms but also theoretical insights that deepen our understanding of when and why domain adaptation succeeds or fails in the context of DTI prediction. Our work significantly advances the field of DTI prediction by addressing the critical challenge of distribution shift between different experimental settings, drug classes, or target families. This challenge has long been a bottleneck in computational drug discovery, where models trained on well-studied targets often fail to generalize to novel or less-characterized protein families. The economic and scientific implications of solving this problem are substantial, as it could dramatically reduce the time and cost associated with exploring new therapeutic targets and expanding into previously intractable disease areas. The cornerstone of our framework is the novel DTI-Wasserstein distance, which extends the classical Wasserstein metric to incorporate both structural and chemical similarities of drugs and targets. Unlike generic distance metrics that treat all features equally, our DTI-specific formulation recognizes the hierarchical and heterogeneous nature of molecular data. The metric accounts for the fact that certain molecular transformations (such as bioisosteric replacements) may preserve biological activity while others (such as changes to key pharmacophores) fundamentally alter binding properties. This innovation allows for a more nuanced quantification of domain discrepancies in DTI prediction, leading to more effective adaptation strategies. By incorporating domain knowledge directly into the mathematical framework, we achieve a more faithful representation of the true challenges in cross-domain DTI prediction. The refined bound on the difference between source and target risks, derived from this distance, provides theoretical guarantees for domain adaptation performance and offers insights into the fundamental limits of knowledge transfer in DTI prediction. These bounds are not merely theoretical exercises but provide practical guidance for practitioners, indicating when additional source domain data or alternative adaptation strategies may be necessary.

Empirical evaluations across multiple benchmark datasets demonstrate the superiority of our approach over existing methods, showcasing its ability to effectively leverage data from diverse sources for improved DTI prediction. The consistent improvements observed across different types of domain shifts - from cross-species transfer to temporal adaptation accounting for evolving experimental protocols - validate the generality of our theoretical framework. Particularly noteworthy is the method’s ability to handle challenging scenarios such as adapting from well-studied kinase families to less-characterized protein classes, where traditional transfer learning approaches often fail. These results underscore the practical utility of our theoretical framework and its potential to accelerate drug discovery processes. The computational efficiency of our approach, enabled by the spectral decomposition and optimal transport formulations, makes it suitable for large-scale applications in pharmaceutical research where millions of potential drug-target pairs must be evaluated. Looking forward, several promising research directions emerge from this work. First, the extension of our framework to handle multi-source domain adaptation could leverage data from multiple experimental platforms simultaneously, potentially leading to more robust predictions. The theoretical machinery we have developed, particularly the DTI-Wasserstein distance and information-geometric formulations, naturally extends to the multi-source setting through barycentric coordinates on the statistical manifold. Second, the incorporation of additional molecular modalities, such as protein structure information when available, could further enhance adaptation performance. The challenge lies in developing appropriate distance metrics that can handle missing structural data while maintaining theoretical guarantees.

In conclusion, this work establishes a rigorous mathematical foundation for domain adaptation in DTI prediction while demonstrating practical improvements in real-world applications. By bridging the gap between abstract mathematical theory and concrete pharmaceutical challenges, we hope to inspire further research at the intersection of mathematics, machine learning, and drug discovery. The ultimate goal remains the acceleration of drug development through more accurate and generalizable computational predictions, bringing life-saving therapies to patients more quickly and efficiently. As the field continues to evolve with new data modalities and computational capabilities, the theoretical framework presented here provides a solid foundation for future innovations in computational drug discovery.

Methods

Data sources

To evaluate the effectiveness of our model, we utilized four publicly accessible benchmark datasets: Human, C. elegans Tsubaki et al.31, Davis Davis et al.23, and GPCR Chen et al.32. These datasets were carefully selected to represent diverse biological contexts, varying levels of experimental validation, and different challenges in drug-target interaction prediction. Each dataset embodies unique characteristics that test different aspects of our domain adaptation framework, from handling phylogenetic distances between species to managing varying experimental protocols and interaction types. The diversity in these datasets ensures a comprehensive evaluation of our method’s generalization capabilities across multiple domains.

The Human dataset represents the most comprehensive collection of human drug-target interactions, comprising 3369 confirmed positive interactions between 1052 unique drug compounds and 852 protein targets. This dataset captures a wide spectrum of therapeutic areas, including oncology, cardiovascular diseases, neurological disorders, and infectious diseases. The chemical space covered includes small molecules ranging from 150 to 900 Daltons, encompassing diverse structural scaffolds such as heterocycles, peptide mimetics, and natural product derivatives. Positive interactions within the Human dataset were derived from DrugBank Wishart et al.33, which aggregates data from multiple sources including FDA approval documents, clinical trial databases, and peer-reviewed literature. The curation process involved stringent quality control measures, including verification of chemical structures through InChI keys, standardization of protein identifiers using UniProt accessions, and validation of interaction evidence through multiple independent sources.

The C. elegans dataset provides a complementary perspective from a model organism extensively used in biological research. This dataset contains 4000 experimentally validated interactions between 1434 drugs and 2504 proteins, offering insights into evolutionary conserved interaction mechanisms. The inclusion of C. elegans data is particularly valuable for understanding fundamental biological processes and identifying interaction patterns that transcend species boundaries. Positive interactions were extracted from Matador Matador Günther et al.34, a manually curated database that integrates data from genetic screens, phenotypic assays, and biochemical studies. The protein targets in this dataset span essential biological pathways including metabolism, signal transduction, and stress response, providing a comprehensive view of druggable targets in this model organism.

Framework overview

Our framework (as shown in Fig. 6) begins with a preprocessing module, where we represent drugs and proteins using Residual2vec and molecular graphs, respectively. Residual2vec is an adaptation of Word2vec, treating the residue sequence as a document and dividing the amino acid sequence into fixed-length fragments (k-mers) as words. In the drug encoder module, we incorporate a Graph Transformer to capture biologically significant information. We introduce Centrality Encoding to assess node importance within the molecular graph and implement a novel Spatial Encoding to learn the structural relationships between atomic nodes. This module ultimately employs multi-head attention to derive hidden embedding representations for the entire molecular graph. The protein encoder module utilizes a standard Transformer architecture, comprising self-attention mechanisms, interactive attention layers, and fully connected networks. In the final module, tailored to the binary classification task, we integrate a multi-head attention mechanism with a single linear layer to learn embeddings that represent interactions, thereby determining whether a protein interacts with a target.

Geometric optimal transport for domain–policy alignment (GOT–DPA)

We introduce a unified framework (as shown in Fig. 7) that couples representation alignment and predictor (policy) adaptation through a single geometric lens. The framework, GOT–DPA, integrates (i) Wasserstein transport on representation distributions, (ii) kernel mean embeddings (MMD) for high-frequency alignment, (iii) Fisher–Rao information geometry for parameter-space updates, and (iv) invariance-constrained policy optimization (GDGPO/A-GVPA) as a stability regularizer over latent sub-environments.

GOT–DPA Training (Representation–Policy Joint Alignment)

Input: source/target mini-batches BS,BT; partitions K, MC rounds M; α, λFW, δ, ϵ; dual step η.

1. Encode. Compute Φθ(BS),Φθ(BT) and predictions hθ.

2. Discrepancy. Estimate W2dΦ (e.g., Sinkhorn) and MMD; form DFW.

3. IRF. Build M random K-partitions of BS∪BT; compute V¯IRF.

5. FW Step. Take a Fisher–Rao natural gradient step on ∑iJiclip under KL≤δ; parallel-transport gradients with ΠFW to respect the joint geodesic.

6. Dual Update. λ←Π[0,λmax]λ+η(V¯IRF−V⋆).

7. Repeat. Iterate until validation AUPR stops improving; early stop.

(i) α balances coarse displacement (W2) and high-frequency mismatch (MMD); (ii) the FW natural gradient is implemented via Fisher preconditioning; (iii) IRF uses z-normalized residuals to ensure scale-invariance; (iv) the KL trust region stabilizes updates and indirectly controls DFW.
