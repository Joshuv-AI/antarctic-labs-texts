Stochasticity dominated adaptive assembly of benthic microbiota across oasis-to-desert sections of an arid inland river
Authors: Yongcheng Chen, Yingchao Sun, Rongzheng Huang, Shuangming Li, Yuhang Liu, Xingke Yuan, Xinyi Chen, Junfeng Li, Lihe Yin, Chunhui Ma, Fanfan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySediment microbial communities in arid inland rivers are important for biogeochemical cycling, but the assembly mechanisms and network responses across the transition from irrigated oases to desert margins remain unclear. We investigated bacterial and fungal communities in Yarkand River sediments along salinity and nutrient gradients using 16S rRNA and ITS sequencing, sediment chemistry, iCAMP null-model analysis, and co-occurrence networks. Downstream increases in electrical conductivity, total nitrogen, and pH were associated with reduced bacterial α-diversity and turnover from nutrient-responsive Pseudomonadota in the oasis midstream to stress-tolerant Chloroflexota and Actinomycetota in the desert downstream. Fungal diversity showed high inter-site variability without a linear decline. Despite strong physicochemical gradients, ecological drift dominated assembly in both kingdoms, while desert networks showed higher connectivity. These findings support a hierarchical assembly model in which salinity filters community composition, but stochastic processes and network densification shape benthic microbiota in arid river sediments.

Introduction

Riverbed sediments represent the most biogeochemically active compartments of aquatic ecosystems, serving as critical hotspots for microbial activity that facilitates nutrient cycling, organic matter mineralization, and the attenuation of anthropogenic pollutants.1,2 These sedimentary environments are not merely passive accumulation zones but are dynamic interfaces where microbial communities drive the transformation of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus, thereby maintaining the metabolic balance and overall health of the riverine system.3,4,5 Within these habitats, microbial community structure is highly sensitive to fluctuations in the physicochemical environment, including pH, ionic strength, and nutrient availability, thereby leading to pronounced spatial heterogeneity in community composition, diversity, and assembly mechanisms along the river continuum.6,7,8

In arid and semi-arid regions, inland rivers are increasingly subjected to severe ecological pressures arising from water diversion, intensive runoff regulation, and climate-driven aridification. These stressors frequently culminate in progressive salinization and alkalinization, particularly in the lower reaches where cumulative downstream effects are most pronounced.9,10 Such environmental gradients exert strong selective pressures on the microbiome, often triggering a shift from freshwater-tolerant taxa toward halotolerant or halophilic lineages.11,12,13 While bacterial responses to these gradients have been extensively documented in humid and temperate systems, the ecological behavior of fungi—which are equally vital for the degradation of complex organic polymers and nutrient mobilization—remains significantly understudied in arid inland river sediments.14,15 Preliminary evidence suggests that fungi may exhibit distinct environmental sensitivities and dispersal patterns compared to bacteria, potentially due to differences in body size, metabolic strategies, and susceptibility to environmental stressors such as salinity and nutrient stoichiometry.16,17

The theoretical framework of microbial community assembly provides a critical lens for interpreting these spatial dynamics, generally conceptualized as a balance between deterministic and stochastic processes.18,19 Deterministic processes, rooted in niche theory, emphasize the role of environmental filtering and biotic interactions in shaping community structure.20 Under this paradigm, species coexist based on their functional traits and fitness under specific abiotic conditions, such as pH or salinity.21 Conversely, stochastic processes, derived from neutral theory, posit that community composition is primarily influenced by random events, including ecological drift, dispersal, and diversification, which are independent of species-specific traits.22,23 Recent studies have highlighted that the relative importance of these processes is not static but varies across environmental gradients and spatial scales.24 Under conditions of high environmental stress or sharp physicochemical transitions, the influence of deterministic selection often intensifies, as only a subset of the regional species pool possesses the traits necessary for survival.25 However, in many lotic systems, stochastic drift and dispersal limitation continue to play a dominant role, especially when hydrological disturbances or geographical barriers restrict the movement of microorganisms.8,26

The Yarkand River, a representative inland river in the Tarim Basin of northwest China, provides an ideal natural laboratory for investigating microbial spatial dynamics under extreme environmental gradients. As the largest tributary of the Tarim River, it supports vast agricultural oases and critical riparian ecosystems, yet it faces significant challenges from runoff reduction and increasing salinity.27,28 Notably, the mid-lower Yarkand River traverses a sharp socio-ecological transition from an alluvial oasis plain with intensive irrigation agriculture and dense settlements to the desert margin with sparse vegetation and strong evaporative salinization.29,30,31 Despite its importance, the longitudinal responses of sedimentary bacterial and fungal communities to this oasis-to-desert transition and to coupled gradients of nutrients and salinity within this system have not been clearly resolved. Furthermore, there is a lack of integrated analysis concerning the coexistence networks and assembly mechanisms of these two major microbial groups in such water-scarce environments.32 Existing research in the region has primarily focused on water quality indices or specific functional groups, often neglecting the broader biogeographic patterns and the underlying ecological rules that maintain microbial diversity.33

Against this backdrop, the present study targets the sedimentary microbiome along the middle to the terminal/lower reaches of the Yarkand River. By integrating high-throughput sequencing of 16S rRNA and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions with detailed sediment indicators and spatial data, this research moves beyond descriptive diversity assessments to a mechanism-based understanding of community turnover. Specifically, we address the following research questions. (1) How do the α- and beta-diversity patterns of bacterial and fungal communities diverge along the longitudinal salinization and nutrient gradient of the Yarkand River? (2) To what extent do deterministic and stochastic processes, analyzed using the phylogenetic-bin-based iCAMP framework, govern the assembly of bacterial and fungal communities along different reaches of the river? (3) How does environmental stress modulate the topological complexity and stability of microbial co-occurrence networks? (4) Which core taxa and environmental factors primarily drive community succession? These insights can provide a microbiological basis for contributing to water quality management and ecological security in arid river regions.34

Results

Establishment of longitudinal physicochemical gradients

Sediment physicochemical properties differed markedly between the oasis and desert sections, reflecting pronounced spatial heterogeneity along the Yarkand River (Figure 2). A general longitudinal enrichment was evident for salinity and nutrients. Total nitrogen (TN) and nitrate-N (NO3−-N), pH, and electrical conductivity (EC), proxies for salinity, were significantly higher in the desert-region sediments than in the oasis region (p < 0.05). Alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen (AN) tended to be higher in the desert zone, reflecting downstream buildup of labile nitrogen. Total organic carbon (TOC), total phosphorus (TP), and AN tend to stabilize or slightly re-enrich in the middle reaches of the oasis river section and the lower reaches of the desert section. Notably, substantial intra-reach heterogeneity was observed within the oasis section. Specifically, site C exhibited anomalously high nutrient loads—specifically, AN reached 124.73 mg⋅kg−1 and TP reached 4.26 g⋅kg−1, which were approximately 5–10 times higher than adjacent sites (Table S1). This suggests localized anthropogenic point-source inputs within the oasis reach, contributing to the high environmental heterogeneity observed upstream. Therefore, by the end of the river, the concentration in the desert section was comparable to (or slightly higher than) that in the oasis section, but the difference was not significant. Regarding stoichiometry, C/N ratio was significantly higher in the oasis region (p < 0.05), indicating a shift in the relative carbon-nitrogen balance between the two landscape units. Divergent diversity patterns and taxonomic turnover

After quality filtering and denoising, a total of 2,589,230 high-quality bacterial reads and 3,621,419 fungal reads were retained. These reads were resolved into 1,753–3,824 bacterial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and 100–1,104 fungal ASVs per sample. Curves based on observed richness (Sobs) and Shannon diversity approached clear plateaus for both bacterial and fungal communities, indicating that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the majority of within-sample diversity (Figure S1). Overall, the α-diversity did not differ significantly between the oasis and desert sediments for either bacterial or fungal communities (Figure 1). One may explain this to be partly due to the high within-group heterogeneity introduced by pooling multiple high-resolution sampling sites within each habitat type (oasis: sites A–J; desert: sites K–R). However, from the perspective of each site, bacterial and fungal communities exhibited divergent ecological strategies in response to these gradients. Bacterial α-diversity (Sobs index and Shannon index) exhibited a sensitive negative response, peaking in the upstream part of the oasis and showing a significant downward trend in the high-salinity desert (Figures S2A and S2B). In contrast, fungal diversity showed a decoupled response with no monotonic decline, instead characterized by strong inter-site variability and localized peaks (Figures S2C and S2D).Figure 1Example map of sampling sites (A–R) in the Yarkand River reach

Spatial turnover and community similarity mechanisms

Beta-diversity analysis (principal coordinate analysis) revealed profound structural segregation between oasis and desert communities for both bacteria (ASV level: R = 0.963, p = 0.001) and fungi (R = 0.246, p = 0.001) (Figure S6). This separation was consistent even when examined at the individual site level without grouping (Figure S7), confirming that spatial turnover is a robust feature of the river continuum.

To mechanistically parse this turnover, we analyzed the relationship between community similarity and the shared species pool (Figure 4). For bacteria, the Bray-Curtis similarity exhibited a strong positive linear dependence on the number of shared ASVs (R2 = 0.923, p < 0.001), indicating that spatial similarity decay was strictly driven by the loss of common taxa (Figure 4A). Incorporating phylogenetic information (weighted UniFrac) maintained this high correlation (R2 = 0.883), suggesting that the turnover of dominant, phylogenetically related lineages governs bacterial spatial dynamics. Conversely, fungal communities displayed a weaker dependency on shared ASVs (R2 = 0.577) and a much lower phylogenetic signal (weighted UniFrac R2 = 0.609) (Figure 4B), implying that fungal assembly is more stochastic or influenced by patch-specific factors rather than a predictable depletion of a shared phylogenetic pool. Stochasticity-dominated assembly and core microbiome persistence

Despite strong environmental gradients, the iCAMP analysis revealed that stochastic processes dominated community assembly (Figures 5A and 5B). Ecological drift (DR) was the primary driver, contributing 54.26%–58.93% for bacteria and 44.05%–52.96% for fungi. However, spatial constraints became critical downstream: dispersal limitation increased significantly in the lower reaches, particularly for fungi (rising from 32.00% to 42.15%) (Figure 5C). The dominance of stochasticity was further reflected in the core microbiome structure. The “Transient-Intermittent-Persistent” model showed that transient taxa dominated both richness (>59%) and abundance across all sections (Figures 5D–5F; Figure S10). Intermittent taxa, which were present in 20%–80% of the sites, contributed moderately to both richness and abundance but were not as predominant as transient taxa. Persistent “core” taxa constituted a negligible fraction (<2% for bacteria, <0.3% for fungi), confirming that the community is sustained by a dynamic turnover of transient immigrants rather than a stable resident core, reinforcing the role of drift and dispersal in maintaining diversity.

Network densification as a stress response

Co-occurrence network analysis indicated a “structural adaptation” strategy. Compared to the oasis section, desert section networks for both kingdoms exhibited markedly denser topologies with increased edge numbers (Figure 6). Positive correlations dominated, suggesting that facilitative interactions or shared niche preferences outweighed competition in these saline environments. Environmental drivers of community succession

Mantel tests and constrained ordinations (redundancy analysis/canonical correspondence analysis) identified salinity and nutrients as the primary abiotic drivers of these shifts. Bacterial community dissimilarity was significantly correlated with river length, EC, and TN (Figures 7A and 7C). Specific dominant genera, such as the desert-enriched Actinomarinales, showed strong positive correlations with nutrient variables (e.g., TP) and tolerance to stoichiometric shifts (Figure S11A). Discussion

Stochasticity-dominated structural adaptation in extreme riverine sediments

Our investigation into the Yarkand River reveals a critical biological response to salinization and nutrient accumulation: mechanistically, the site-level decline in bacterial diversity is consistent with the expectation that increasing salinity imposes osmotic stress and ion toxicity, together with elevated energetic costs of homeostasis (e.g., ion transport, compatible-solute synthesis, and stress-response maintenance), thereby constraining realized community structure under high ionic strength conditions.21,35 By comparison, the absence of a monotonic decline in fungal diversity suggests that fungi may be more strongly shaped by microhabitat-driven resource patchiness and localized environmental conditions (e.g., organic matter microenvironments), which can generate heterogeneous diversity hotspots rather than a uniform downstream suppression. Importantly, the observed diversity shifts along the gradient do not necessarily indicate that community assembly is governed solely by deterministic environmental filtering. Instead, we propose a “Hierarchical Assembly” model to reconcile the paradox between strong gradients and drift dominance. First, high salinity acts as a rigorous deterministic filter, excluding salt-sensitive lineages and reducing the regional species pool. Second, within this constrained, stress-tolerant species pool, the assembly of local communities is largely governed by stochastic ecological drift (>44%). This is likely exacerbated by hydrological fragmentation in the desert reach, which creates semi-isolated benthic “islands” where dispersal limitation overrides local selection. In this context, our results are compatible with the notion of a “stochasticity-dominated structural adaptation,” whereby microbial communities may enhance network connectivity or inter-taxon associations to buffer stress even when compositional turnover is not entirely deterministic.36,37 Collectively, these findings highlight that interpreting salinization effects requires resolving patterns at the site level in addition to pooled habitat comparisons and that bacterial and fungal communities may adopt fundamentally different ecological strategies across the oasis-desert continuum. Concomitantly, the bacterial community underwent a taxonomic turnover from an oasis community dominated by Pseudomonadota (formerly Proteobacteria; commonly associated with fast-growing, nutrient-responsive strategies) toward a desert community dominated by Chloroflexota and Actinobacteria (often characterized by stress tolerance, desiccation resistance, and resource-efficient life-history traits).38,39 Such a succession is coherent with a shift from relatively dynamic, nutrient- and disturbance-responsive assemblages in oasis sediments to a desert assemblage shaped by chronic salinity and reduced connectivity, where persistence may depend on stress-resistant phenotypes (e.g., thick cell walls, spore formation, and efficient substrate scavenging).1,12 Beyond phylum-level replacement, the LEfSe-derived biomarkers further suggest that desert sediments are enriched in lineages often associated with structured or micro-anaerobic niches and complex carbon cycling (e.g., Chloroflexota clades such as Anaerolineae/Dehalococcoidia and other oligotrophic-associated groups), implying that salinization may interact with sediment redox microgradients and organic matter quality to reorganize trophic pathways.40

In addition, the co-occurrence networks were denser in the desert for both bacteria and fungi, and positive associations dominated over negative ones in both regions. And the desert fungal network contained module hub candidates, indicating enhanced within-module organization. Under harsh conditions (e.g., high salinity), increased positive co-occurrence signals should be interpreted as “habitat filtering.”41 As salinity stress restricts available niches, physiologically similar stress-tolerant taxa (e.g., specific Chloroflexota and Ascomycota) are forced to co-inhabit the remaining viable micro-habitats, leading to mathematically positive correlations. Thus, the desert microbiome maintains stability through structural clustering in refugia.42 Critically, this network pattern complements—not contradicts—the stochastic assembly signal. To reconcile the full set of findings, we propose a three-level organization model for the Yarkand River sediment microbiome. Level 1 (species pool constraint): desert salinization and nutrient accumulation narrow viable taxa, producing bacterial richness decline and taxonomic turnover.11 Level 2 (metacommunity operator): increasing spatial isolation and reduced effective dispersal downstream elevate dispersal limitation and sustain drift as the dominant assembly process.36,43 Level 3 (interaction architecture): within the constrained pool and stochastic operator regime, communities increase co-occurrence density and positive associations, which may reflect facilitation, shared refuge tracking, and module-level organization—collectively supporting persistence under extreme stress.41

Deterministic drivers versus stochastic assembly

The dichotomy between our expected environmental correlations and the unexpected dominance of stochasticity warrants a multi-layered interpretation. First, on the physicochemical level, the results align with deterministic paradigms: salinity (EC) and TN were identified as the primary drivers of community shifts. The existence of environmental filters was confirmed by the changes in the abundance of Pseudomonas (typical cotrophic r-strategists) and Chloroflexota and Actinobacteria (stress-tolerant K-strategists) in the oasis and desert,38 conversely, the persistence of stochasticity (drift and dispersal limitation) in such a harsh filter. This apparent mismatch—strong environmental gradients and compositional replacement, yet stochastic-dominated assembly—suggests that “environmental filtering” and “deterministic assembly dominance” are not synonymous in this system.

The reasons for these phenomena may be based on the following mechanisms. First, there is a pre-filtering of the regional species pool followed by drift within a narrowed tolerance set. Under chronic salinity, many lineages may be excluded early from desert section sediments, narrowing the viable pool to salt- and drought-tolerant taxa.12 Within this pre-filtered pool, however, relative abundances can still be strongly shaped by stochastic birth-death dynamics, priority effects, and local demographic fluctuations.16,19 In other words, deterministic forces may set the “membership constraints,” while drift governs “who wins” among those that can persist. Second, spatial structuring and fragmentation intensify dispersal limitation, amplifying stochasticity. The desert reach of an arid inland river is often characterized by reduced flow continuity and stronger channel disconnection, all of which can decrease effective dispersal among benthic patches.6,8 The observed desert section increase in dispersal limitation—most pronounced for fungi—supports the interpretation that desert section sediments function as semi-isolated habitat islands, where limited immigration increases the influence of drift. Importantly, a drift-dominated outcome can emerge even under strong stress when dispersal is sufficiently restricted and demographic stochasticity is high.26 Third, the high stochasticity signal is likely amplified by sediment “legacy effects.” Riverine sediments act as archives, accumulating dormant spores or “relic DNA” (extracellular DNA from dead biomass) transported from upstream.44 These inactive components do not respond to immediate environmental filtering, yet are detected by DNA sequencing, thereby statistically inflating the signal of stochastic drift and masking the strength of deterministic selection.45 Microhabitat heterogeneity dilutes detectable deterministic signals at the sampling scale. Collectively, these pathways point to a central inference: desert section salinization in the Yarkand River drives pronounced compositional and diversity responses, yet the dominant assembly operator remains stochastic—primarily drift, modulated by increasing dispersal limitation. In contrast to bacteria, fungal α-diversity did not exhibit a monotonic decline with increasing salinity; instead, it showed strong inter-site variability with pronounced peaks and troughs. Mechanistically, this “decoupling” can reflect differences in life history, propagule ecology, and substrate dependence between fungi and bacteria.15 Moreover, fungal distributions in sediments may be more strongly governed by patchy organic substrates (plant detritus, particulate organic matter, and localized carbon hotspots) than by bulk porewater chemistry alone, producing a site-specific response that is “less linear” along large-scale gradients.14 Strikingly, fungal assembly also appeared more constrained by spatial processes: dispersal limitation increased desert section more strongly for fungi than for bacteria.

Directions for future research and management

The strong association between salinity/nitrogen gradients and community restructuring suggests that controlling salinization and nitrogen loading is critical for preserving benthic microbial diversity.33 Furthermore, efforts should prioritize restoring hydrological connectivity. Since dispersal limitation is a primary constraint in the desert reach (Figure 7), artificial bioaugmentation is likely to be ineffective due to washout and drift. Instead, implementing periodic ecological water conveyance to reconnect isolated benthic pools would be the most effective strategy to alleviate dispersal barriers, replenish the benthic seed bank, and sustain microbial functional resilience.46

Longitudinal salinization and nitrogen accumulation drove a deterministic decline in bacterial α-diversity and a succession from nutrient-responsive Pseudomonadota to stress-tolerant Chloroflexota and Actinomycetota. In contrast, fungal diversity remained decoupled from linear gradients, driven by local heterogeneity. Contrary to the expectation that extreme stress enforces strict deterministic filtering, community assembly was primarily governed by stochastic processes (44%), specifically ecological drift. This is sustained by a high turnover of transient taxa rather than a stable resident core. The transition to the desert reach significantly intensified dispersal limitation, particularly for fungi. This hydrological fragmentation effectively isolates benthic communities, reinforcing the dominance of stochastic assembly. To counteract osmotic stress and isolation, microbial networks exhibited a structural densification strategy. This topology likely reflects strong niche filtering and shared habitat preference rather than active cooperation. Furthermore, given the prominence of dispersal limitation in the lower reaches, restoration strategies should not rely solely on natural recolonization; instead, enhancing hydrological connectivity is essential to alleviate dispersal barriers and sustain the natural resilience of the benthic microbiome.

Limitations of the study

While our study design is robust, it is limited by the use of DNA-based sequencing, which does not distinguish active cells from dormant spores. In addition, the river suffers from severe water scarcity in terms of seasonality, with intermittent and uneven flow. Many river sections may partially or completely dry up during the dry season, making it impossible to collect standardized benthic sediments at different locations. Therefore, we conducted sampling during high flow (humid) seasons, during which moisture and connectivity were restored, although this may not fully reflect the community status and combination processes during the drought phase and the alternation between drought and humidity. Future research should employ RNA-based transcriptomics to definitively separate the active community from the dormant seed bank and utilize time-series sampling to test whether drift dominance and network densification are temporally stable.2 Nevertheless, the identified stochasticity-dominated structural adaptation framework provides a testable basis for future work in sustaining benthic microbial resilience under ongoing aridification.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study used environmental riverbed sediment samples collected from the Yarkand River in Xinjiang, China. No human participants, clinical samples, experimental animals, cell lines, or model organisms were used. Ethics approval and consent to participate are not applicable.

Method details

Study site and sample collection

The Yarkand River, located in the Tarim Basin of Xinjiang, China, is a major inland river system characterized by arid conditions, high salinity gradients, and significant anthropogenic influences from agriculture and urbanization. The river has a total length of 1,289 km. Sampling was conducted along a longitudinal transect from the midstream section (site A, Altash Reservoir, coordinates: 76.48°E, 37.98°N, river distance: 554 km) to the downstream terminus (site R, Tarim River confluence, coordinates: 79.59°E, 39.86°N, river distance: 1289 km). Along this transect, sites A–J are located within the irrigated oasis zone, where the river splits into multiple distributaries and irrigates a large oasis—one of the largest in Xinjiang, encompassing the town of Yarkand.30 Whereas sites K–R are situated at the desert margin, where the river traverses the arid Taklamakan Desert fringe and the landscape shifts to barren desert with sparse natural vegetation.31 Therefore, based on geomorphic setting, land use/human environment, and climatic-ecological characteristics, we define A–J as the river-oasis reach and K–R as the river-desert reach.29,48 A total of 18 sampling sites (A–R) were selected at intervals reflecting key hydrological and land-use transitions, though these sites were not equidistant. This longitudinal, multi-replicate design improves representativeness of benthic sediments.49 The specific latitude and longitude coordinates of all sites can be found in Table S2. Sediment samples were collected in triplicate at each site in July 2023 to capture cross-sectional variability: one from each riverbank and one from the mid-channel. At each replicate point, surface sediments (0–10 cm depth) were retrieved using a Peterson grab sampler, homogenized in sterile bags, and subsampled for physicochemical and microbial analyzes.50 Subsamples for DNA extraction were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until processing. Subsamples for physicochemical measurements were air-dried, sieved through a 2-mm mesh, and stored at 4 °C. All sampling adhered to aseptic techniques to minimize contamination.

Physicochemical analyses

Sediment pH was measured in a 1:2.5 (w/v) sediment: water slurry using a calibrated pH meter (PHS-3C, INESA, China). Electrical conductivity (EC) was determined in a 1:5 (w/v) sediment: water extract using a conductivity meter (DDS-307A, INESA, China). Total nitrogen (TN) was quantified by the Kjeldahl method following sulfuric acid digestion, with distillation and titration. Alkaline-hydrolyzable nitrogen (AN) was assessed via alkaline hydrolysis diffusion, involving incubation with 1.8 M NaOH at 40 °C for 24 h, followed by boric acid absorption and titration. Nitrate nitrogen (NO3--N) was extracted with 2 M KCl and measured spectrophotometrically using the phenol disulfonic acid method at 490 nm.51

Total phosphorus (TP) was determined by the molybdenum blue colorimetric method after perchloric acid digestion, with absorbance read at 700 nm. Total organic carbon (TOC) was analyzed using the potassium dichromate oxidation-external heating method, followed by titration with ferrous ammonium sulfate. Elemental stoichiometric ratios (C/N, C/P, N/P) were calculated on a molar basis from TOC, TN, and Tp values.52,53 All analyzes were performed in triplicate, and results are reported as means ± standard deviation. River length was measured as cumulative distance from the upstream reference point (site A) using GPS coordinates and ArcGIS software (Esri, USA).

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and high-throughput sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of each sediment subsample using the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with bead-beating for 10 min to ensure efficient lysis of microbial cells. DNA quality and concentration were verified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Extracts were stored at −20 °C until amplification. For bacterial communities, the V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). For fungal communities, the ITS1 region was targeted with primers ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). PCR reactions (25 μL) contained 12.5 μL of 2× Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, USA), 0.5 μM of each primer, 10 ng template DNA, and nuclease-free water. Amplification conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplicons were purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen, USA) and quantified with a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).54 Equimolar pooled libraries were barcoded with unique indices and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), generating 250 bp paired-end reads. Sequencing targeted >50,000 reads per sample to ensure adequate coverage. Sequence processing and downstream statistical analyses were conducted using the Majorbio Cloud Platform of Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. and publicly available software/packages described in the Methods (https://v.majorbio.com/).

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing data were demultiplexed and quality-filtered using fastp v0.20.0, with removal of adapters, low-quality reads (Q < 20), and ambiguous bases. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH v1.2.11, and chimeric sequences were identified and removed with UCHIME. Denoising was performed using DADA2 in QIIME2 (v2022.(8) to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) at 100% similarity. Taxonomic assignment was conducted against the SILVA v138 database for bacteria and the UNITE v8.0 database for fungi, with a confidence threshold of 0.7. ASV tables were rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth (∼30,000 reads/sample) to normalize for uneven sampling effort. Core microbiota were classified based on occupancy: transient (present in <20% of sites), intermittent (20–80%), and persistent (>80%). Community assembly processes were inferred using the iCAMP package in R.47 The rarefied ASV abundance table and corresponding rooted phylogenetic tree were used as inputs, and bacterial and fungal communities were analyzed separately. ASVs were assigned to phylogenetic bins, within which pairwise community turnover was evaluated using the beta Net Relatedness Index (βNRI) and the Bray–Curtis-based modified Raup–Crick metric (RCbray). Following the standard iCAMP thresholds, βNRI > +1.96 and βNRI < −1.96 were interpreted as heterogeneous and homogeneous selection, respectively. For comparisons with |βNRI| ≤ 1.96, RCbray > +0.95 indicated dispersal limitation, RCbray < −0.95 indicated homogenizing dispersal, and |RCbray| ≤ 0.95 was assigned to drift and other undominated stochastic processes. The relative contribution of each ecological process was calculated as the abundance-weighted proportion of pairwise turnovers assigned to that process across all phylogenetic bins, and was summarized separately for bacterial and fungal communities as well as for oasis and desert sections.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Alpha diversity was estimated using the Sobs (observed ASVs) and Shannon indices in QIIME2. Differences among sites were tested with the Kruskal-Wallis H test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc multiple comparisons (p < 0.05) in R v4.2.1. Beta diversity was assessed via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities, with group separation evaluated by analysis of similarities (ANOSIM, 999 permutations). Differential abundance of taxa between oasis (A–J) and desert (K–R) sections was identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with an LDA score threshold of 4.0 and Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 0.05). Spearman rank correlations were computed between dominant genera (top 20 by relative abundance) and environmental variables (river length, TN, AN, TOC, pH, EC, C/N, C/P, N/P, NO3--N, TP) using the psych package in R. Occupancy–abundance relationships were fitted with linear regression models. All visualizations were generated using ggplot2 and vegan packages in R.
