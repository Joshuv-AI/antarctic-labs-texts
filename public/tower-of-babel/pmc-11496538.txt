Knowledge, attitude, and practice toward nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs among osteoarthritis patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dongliang Gong, Xiao Wu, Minghu Wu, Fuyong Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are common drugs in patients with osteoarthritis (OA). NSAIDs are generally used at home by patients, without supervision, requiring proper knowledge and attitudes for correct practice. This study investigated the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of patients with OA toward NSAIDs. Methods This cross-sectional study enrolled patients with OA at the Qingpu Branch of Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University between January and March 2024. The KAP scores and demographic information of respondents were collected through a self-designed questionnaire. Results There were 645 participants, with 579 (89.8%) over 45 years old and 394 (61.1%) females. The average scores for knowledge, attitude, and practice were 16.26 ± 3.79 (possible range: 0–24), 18.12 ± 1.99 (possible range: 5–35), and 29.20 ± 5.52 (possible range: 10–50), respectively. The structural equation model (SEM) found that for individuals currently using NSAIDs, the attitude had a direct effect on practice (β = 0.978, P < 0.001). For individuals not using NSAIDs, the attitude had a direct effect on practice (β = 0.936, P < 0.001). Conclusion This study suggested that adequate NSAID knowledge is the prerequisite for correct NSAID-related medical decisions, while attitude has a crucial intermediary effect. Healthcare professionals and society should strengthen education regarding the relevant knowledge of NSAIDs and guide the cultivation of positive attitudes toward NSAIDs. Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common degenerative joint disease that significantly affects the quality of life of millions of people worldwide and is ranked second as a cause of disability by the World Health Organization (WHO)1,2. Possible symptoms of OA include pain, swelling, stiffness, and impaired mobility of the affected joint3–6. In 2020, 7.6% of the global population (595 million people all over the world) was suffering from OA, and this proportion is growing rapidly due to the increase of the aging population trend5,7,8 posing one of the global public health challenges7,9. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are the main pharmacological treatments for OA and have demonstrated significant efficacy in relieving pain and reducing inflammation10. Topical NSAIDs are strongly recommended by OA Research Society International (OARSI), especially for OA patients with other diseases combined, such as gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and frailty morbidities11,12. Oral NSAIDs are more commonly recommended for patients experiencing continuous, moderate-to-severe pain13. However, patients’ cognition, attitude, and actual application may profoundly influence the success of treatment. Indeed, NSAIDs are generally used at home, without medical supervision. Hence, proper knowledge of their use and a positive attitude toward their effectiveness is necessary to use them optimally and achieve the best outcomes possible. In addition, the patients must be aware of the precautions, risks, and adverse effects associated with the use of NSAIDs and how to react when such events occur to ensure the safe use of NSAIDs. Hence, a lack of proper knowledge of and negative attitudes toward NSAIDs can lead to failure of timely pain relief, affecting physical and psychological functions, increasing the risk of negative emotions (such as anxiety, stress, and depression), and impairing quality of life14–16. Furthermore, inadequate knowledge may lead to drug abuse, resulting in unwanted side effects17–19. Thus, it is essential to assess the knowledge, beliefs, and actions of OA patients regarding NSAIDs. Such data would allow more targeted interventions aiming at improving proper knowledge and cultivating positive attitudes.

Knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) study is a structured survey method that assesses participants’ status of knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding a specific topic through questionnaires20. The KAP theory holds that only when individuals possess a thorough understanding of knowledge and keep a positive attitude they can make the right choice and practice21. The present study used a questionnaire survey to collect data on the KAP of patients with OA toward NSAIDs. Furthermore, a structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis is a comprehensive statistical approach to testing hypotheses about relations among observed and latent variables22. Given the ability to handle complex relationships, latent variables, and measurement error, SEM can test hypothesized models grounded in a theoretical framework. Using a SEM analysis, a KAP study can explore the potential issues influencing the KAP status, providing a basis for further optimization of health education and disease management strategies23.

Previous studies examined the KAP of medical workers or common people toward OA or NSAIDs24–30, but data from patients with OA are scarce. In 2007, it was reported that less than a third of the patients with OA knew that analgesics could be taken prophylactically, and 34% did not know that NSAIDs should be taken with or following food31, suggesting suboptimal knowledge of NSAIDs among patients with OA. Even healthcare providers appear to have limited KAP toward NSAIDs for OA management32,33.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitude, and practice of OA patients toward NSAIDs. The results could help identify the gaps, misconceptions, and misunderstandings that would limit the optimal use of NSAIDs in patients with OA and that could be targeted in eventual educational and motivational interventions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study enrolled patients with OA from the Qingpu Branch of Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University between November 1, 2023, and March 1, 2024.

The inclusion criteria were (1) persistent joint pain lasting more than 1 month, (2) X-ray evidence of joint space narrowing, subchondral bone sclerosis, and/or cystic changes, along with osteophyte formation, (3) limited joint mobility, with morning stiffness ≤ 30 min, (4) joint deformity or swelling, and (5) audible joint crepitus during movement. The exclusion criteria were (1) unwillingness to participate in the study or (2) presence of cognitive impairment.

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Qingpu Branch of Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The participants were assured that their data would be handled confidentially and that any identifiers would be removed or encrypted to ensure anonymity. Furthermore, during data collection, all questionnaires and interviews were conducted in a private and secure setting to minimize the risk of unauthorized access to sensitive information. After data collection, strict data management protocols were implemented, and rigorous measures were employed to prevent any unauthorized sharing or dissemination of the data.

Questionnaire introduction

The questionnaire was designed based on the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of OA (2021 edition), with modifications based on the feedback from two experts (specialists in the field of OA) (content value). A pilot study involving 154 participants provided additional feedback on the questionnaire (face value), with data from 49 participants with response times less than 90 s and four participants with incorrect answers to the trap questions being excluded, resulting in 101 valid questionnaires. From these, 50 cases were randomly selected for reliability testing. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.893, with the knowledge, attitude, and practice sections scoring 0.937, 0.805, and 0.737, respectively, indicating good internal consistency for each section and the questionnaire as a whole34–36.

The final questionnaire, in Chinese, comprised four sections: (1) demographic characteristics (age, gender, residence, education level, employment status, income, marital status, duration of OA, current use of NSAIDs (e.g., celecoxib, meloxicam, ibuprofen, aspirin, paracetamol, indomethacin, naproxen, diclofenac, nimesulide, etoricoxib, etc.), previous use of NSAIDs topical patches (e.g., flurbiprofen gel patches, loxoprofen gel patches, loxoprofen sodium patches, diclofenac sodium patches, etc.), history of intra-articular injections (e.g., corticosteroids), history of surgical interventions (e.g., arthroscopy, joint replacement, osteotomy, etc.)); (2) knowledge dimension (communication with physicians about OA, awareness of OA manifestations and risks, understanding of NSAIDs definition, types, main effects, efficacy, administration methods, adverse reactions, precautions, common drug names, etc.); (3) attitude dimension (perceived effectiveness of NSAIDs, willingness to use, attitude towards adverse reactions, ability to tolerate adverse reactions, attitude towards administration methods, etc.); (4) practice dimension (proactive seeking of OA and NSAIDs-related information and educational activities, maintaining communication with physicians; for patients currently using NSAIDs, adherence to medical advice, attention to package inserts and precautions, etc.) (Appendix 1). The knowledge dimension consisted of 12 questions, scored as follows: very knowledgeable = 2 points, heard about = 1 point, unclear = 0 points, with scores ranging from 0 to 24 points. The attitude dimension comprised seven questions, using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1 point) to strongly agree (5 points), with scores ranging from 5 to 35 points. The practice dimension included 10 questions, also using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from always (5 points) to never (1 point), with scores ranging from 10 to 50 points. The questionnaires were distributed to the participants through both online and offline methods.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional studies: where =1.96 when α = 0.05, the assumed degree of variability of p=0.5 (to maximize the required sample size), and δ is the admissible error (which was 5% here). The theoretical sample size was 480, including an extra 20% to allow for subjects lost during the study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Stata 18.0 software. The distribution of the scores for each dimension was assessed for normality. If the data followed a normal distribution, mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used to present the data; otherwise, median, 25th percentile, and 75th percentile were used. The response status for each question based on different demographic characteristics was expressed as n (%) of participants. For continuous variables following a normal distribution, the t-test was employed for comparisons between two groups, while the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test was used for non-normally distributed data. For three or more groups with continuous variables following a normal distribution and equal variance, ANOVA was utilized, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis analysis of variance was applied for non-normally distributed data. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the association of knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions37,38. SEM was used to verify whether attitude served as a mediator between knowledge and behavioral practice. The size of indirect and direct effects was calculated and compared. The threshold standards for goodness of fit indicators in the SEM model were set as ANOVA < 0.08, SRMR < 0.08, TLI > 0.8, and CFI > 0.8. If the goodness of fit thresholds were not met, a mediation analysis was performed, utilizing path analysis39–41.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 645 individuals participated in this study, of whom 458 (71.01%) were using NSAIDs when they answered the questionnaire. Among the participants, there were 251 males (38.9%) and 394 females (61.1%). Only 10.2% of the participants were under 45 years old, 12.1% were from rural areas, 53.6% achieved high school education or above, and 60.6% were employed. Individuals with a monthly income between 2000 and 5000 CNY comprised the largest group, accounting for 42.6%, and with monthly income above 20,000 CNY being the least, accounting for only 2.8%. In addition, 60.8% of the participants were married. Besides, participants having both social and commercial insurance accounted for 60.9%. Disease duration was less than 3 years in 45.1% of the participants and 3–5 years in 36.0%. Meanwhile, 507 participants (78.6%) expressed that they had used NSAIDs topical patches, 379 (58.8%) had undergone intra-articular injections, and 463 (71.8%) had undergone surgical interventions (such as arthroscopy, joint replacement, osteotomy, etc.) (Table 1).

Knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions

The mean knowledge score was 16.26 ± 3.79. The mean attitude score was 18.12 ± 1.99. The mean practice score for questions 1–3 was 11.45 ± 2.83. Only the participants who answered “yes” to “Currently using NSAIDs” (n = 458) completed all eight practice questions, with a mean score of 29.20 ± 5.52 (Table 1).

In the knowledge dimension, the highest score was observed for the item “Osteoarthritis is a chronic disease that causes damage to cartilage and surrounding tissues”, with an accuracy rate of 69.0%, while the lowest score was observed for the item “It is recommended to take gastroprotective medications in conjunction with NSAIDs to prevent gastrointestinal ulcers”, with an accuracy rate of only 27.6%. Besides, the item “NSAIDs can be used in combination with other types of pain relievers” also showed a low correctness rate of 28.1%. Less than half of patients (43.1%) were clearly sure that “NSAIDs can alleviate the pain and swelling caused by osteoarthritis”, and only 36.7% of the patients were very familiar with the item “NSAIDs can be administered orally or topically”. For potential side effects such as “NSAIDs may potentially cause gastrointestinal bleeding”, only 34.9% of patients expressed being very familiar with it, and 56% had just heard of it. In summary, more than half of OA patients possessed basic knowledge about OA and NSAIDs. Nearly half of them knew that NSAIDs could help them with pain and swelling. However, most patients were not very familiar with deeper knowledge, such as the usage of NSAIDs, combined drugs, and the side effects and how to prevent them.

About 60-70% of patients chose “strongly agree” and “agree” to the questions in the attitude dimension, which demonstrated that more than half of patients with OA believed in the effects of NSAIDs and were willing to use NSAIDs to treat their discomfort. Meanwhile, most patients were also concerned about the possible side effects NSAIDs might bring (68.0%, question 3), but they were willing to follow doctors’ advice (70.4%, question 5). Besides, more than half (65.8%, question 7) of the participants were agreeing with that “the topical NSAIDs were safer than oral administration”.

About 60-70% of patients chose “Always” and “Often” in the practice part, which proved that this percentage of people were able to make the right choices. In addition, 63.8% of patients would proactively seek information about osteoarthritis and NSAIDs medication (question 2), 69.2% of them were willing to participate in relevant educational activities (question 3), 68.6% of patients would follow the doctors’ advice and prescriptions when using NSAIDs (question 4), and 67.5% regularly communicated feedback on medication use with their doctors (question 8). Still, the remaining 30-40% of patients were not motivated enough, possibly due to a lack of knowledge or a negative attitude (Supplementary Table S1).

Pearson correlation analysis

The Pearson correlation analysis showed that for all participants, knowledge was correlated with practice (r = 0.080, P = 0.042). For individuals currently using NSAIDs, knowledge was correlated with attitude (r = 0.095, P = 0.043). For individuals not using NSAIDs, knowledge was correlated with practice (r = 0.150, P = 0.041) (Table 2).

For individuals currently using NSAIDs, the attitude had a direct effect on practice (β = 0.978, P < 0.001). Discussion

The knowledge and attitudes of patients with OA toward NSAIDs both influenced their practice toward NSAIDs. Knowledge influenced practice mainly through indirect effects through attitude. The results provided specific knowledge and attitude items that would warrant interventions for improvements. It is recommended that healthcare professionals and society should strengthen the education of relevant knowledge and cultivate positive attitudes toward NSAIDs of patients with OA. Although the OA outcomes were not assessed in the present study, a better KAP toward NSAIDs could translate into better OA outcomes. A future interventional study could examine that point.

OA is the most common form of arthritis and is more likely to affect older people, reducing their quality of life. NSAIDs, when correctly used, can relieve the suffering of patients with OA, including pain, stiffness, and mobility impairments, and also prevent possible side effects like gastrointestinal discomfort42,43. It is noteworthy that patients with knee OA require a more sustained use of NSAIDs compared with those with OA in other parts of the body. For long-term patients, compared with newly diagnosed patients, it is essential to develop individualized NSAID treatment plans based on factors such as the patient’s pain level, gastrointestinal risk, cardiovascular risk, and so on. Therefore, for patients with OA, especially for long-term and severely ill patients with knee OA, exploring their KAP towards NSAIDs and proposing targeted strategies are crucial for improving patient management44.

This study collected relevant information about the KAP of OA and NSAIDs from 645 patients with OA. In addition, a comprehensive SEM analysis was developed to analyze the relationship between KAP dimensions. The results indicated that over 60% of patients with OA possessed basic knowledge about OA and NSAIDs, but less than half of them were familiar with deeper knowledge, such as the usage of NSAIDs, combined drugs, and side effects and how to prevent them. The item with the lowest score was “It is recommended to take gastroprotective medications in conjunction with NSAIDs to prevent gastrointestinal ulcers”, which was concerning because not taking gastroprotective medications during NSAID treatment increases the risk of possible gastric side effects that could be easily avoided. In order to address the knowledge gaps, it is necessary to organize relevant educational activities focusing on the side effects of NSAIDs and recommended usage. In addition, it is advisable for physicians to provide more information about NSAIDs to their patients rather than simple prescribing. The present study did not examine the KAP of healthcare providers, but previous studies reported limited KAP32,33. Since healthcare providers are considered by the patients as reliable sources of health-related information45, that point should be investigated in future studies and put in relation to the patients’ KAP. A previous study from America also indicated that adults with knee and/or hip OA were more likely to use NSAIDs properly if they were more familiar with, had an increased perceived benefit of, and had a decreased perceived risk of these drugs46. In the present study, patients with OA showed suboptimal knowledge yet maintained a positive attitude and proactive approach toward NSAIDs. About 60-70% of the participants kept positive attitudes toward OA and NSAIDs, and a similar percentage demonstrated good practice. There was a weak correlation between knowledge and practice scores, with a correlation coefficient of 0.080 (p < 0.05). Most patients were aware that NSAIDs were not entirely safe, but they were willing to use NSAIDs under the guidance of physicians, which, once again, emphasizes the significance of professional guidance from physicians and healthcare providers.

The SEM analysis revealed a positive relationship among the KAP dimensions. The mastering of knowledge significantly and positively influenced the attitude and practice of patients with OA on NSAIDs. Meanwhile, their practices were influenced by knowledge and attitude. As an important basis, knowledge not only has a significant effect on practice directly but also affects practice indirectly through attitude. The latter path was more significant than the former, indicating the vital role that attitude played in the relationship between knowledge and practice. These results reveal the significance of knowledge education on OA and NSAID usage, which further develops positive beliefs and eventually promotes the correct decisions regarding NSAIDs. Moreover, similar findings have been reported in a self-completed knowledge questionnaire research in the UK targeting patients with OA31, in a cross-sectional study focused on common people in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia29, and in a cross-sectional study evaluating the KAP of community pharmacists towards adverse effects of NSAIDs in Egypt24. Despite the different types of participants, all these studies demonstrated the positive effect of knowledge towards practice through attitude.

This study reveals the importance of knowledge popularization of OA and NSAIDs, especially the usage, side effects, and how to prevent them. Over 60% of the patients with OA were willing to master more relevant knowledge from media, educational activities, and physicians, but most of them only possessed basic knowledge. It is essential to strengthen education and propaganda of information about OA and NSAIDs in communities, hospitals, and social media, not only targeting patients but also their families. Knowledge about the usage (e.g., orally or topically), side effects (e.g., the occurrence rate and how to prevent them), and combined drugs need to be specially reinforced.

This study has several limitations. First, the research was conducted at a single center, resulting in a limited sample size and potential sampling bias, impacting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the data collected through questionnaires may introduce response bias and social desirability bias, affecting the accuracy and reliability of the results. Third, there was a deficiency in comparing KAP scores among patients with OA and different demographic characteristics, as data regarding site, severity, and symptom duration were not collected. In order to address these issues, future research should include a broader range of demographic and disease-specific characteristics, expand to multi-center settings to enhance sample size and representation and incorporate more objective measures alongside questionnaires to mitigate biases. Such improvements would enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings, providing more comprehensive insights into KAP among patients with OA.

Conclusion

The attitudes of patients with OA toward NSAIDs have a mediating role between knowledge and practice, exerting a more significant indirect positive effect than the direct effect of knowledge. Physicians should attach importance to the dissemination of relevant knowledge and the development of positive beliefs toward NSAIDs. The study identified gaps, misconceptions, and misunderstandings that could limit the optimal use of NSAIDs in patients with OA and that could be targeted in eventual educational and motivational interventions. Meanwhile, communities and social media should also be fully utilized to strengthen the dissemination of relevant knowledge. These efforts could translate into better OA outcomes, including negative emotions and quality of life, but they will have to be examined in future studies.
