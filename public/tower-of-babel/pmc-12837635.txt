Quality assessment of clinical practice guidelines for sepsis and variations in recommendations
Authors: Hong-Yan Li, Shan-Ling Jiang, Jing Wang, Hai-Shan Wang, Li-Hong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo provide a clinical reference, we assessed the quality of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for sepsis management in adults and compared their key recommendations.MethodsRelevant CPGs for adult sepsis management were identified through searches of electronic databases, guideline repositories, and professional societies from January 1, 2018, to March 1, 2025. The Appraisal of Guidelines Research and Evaluation (AGREE) II instrument and the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation-Recommendation Excellence (AGREE-REX) were used to evaluate the methodological quality of CPGs and the quality of recommendations. Key recommendations were comprehensively extracted and compared.ResultsA total of 11 CPGs were included. The median (interquartile range [IQR]) scores for AGREE II domain were: scope and purpose, 88.89% (77.78–94.44%); stakeholder involvement, 83.33% (50.00–88.89%); rigor of development, 58.33% (50.00–83.33%); clarity of presentation, 94.40% (88.89–94.44%); applicability, 45.83% (33.33–66.67%); and editorial independence, 66.67% (58.33–91.67%). The AGREE-REX assessment indicated clinical applicability at 70.83% (63.89–83.33%), implementability at 58.33% (47.92–64.58%), and values and preferences at 30.21% (25.00–39.58%). In total, 552 key recommendations were extracted. Among guidelines utilizing the GRADE system, the proportion of evidence categorized as low (Grade C; n = 97, 40.93%) and very low quality (Grade D; n = 66, 27.85%) was substantially higher than that of high (Grade A; n = 15, 6.33%) and moderate-quality (Grade B; n = 59, 24.89%) evidence. Furthermore, weak recommendations (n = 168, 70.89%) were markedly more prevalent than strong recommendations (n = 69, 29.11%). Although guidelines for sepsis diagnosis were generally consistent, evidence gaps in hemodynamic management and antibiotic use led to controversial recommendations.ConclusionTreatment strategies for sepsis vary across guidelines and remain inconsistent. The quality of CPGs for sepsis management requires improvement, particularly in applicability and values and preferences. Future CPG developers should adhere strictly to standardized methodologies and focus on enhancing the dissemination and implementation of guidelines. In this study, we lay the foundation for future guideline development, aiming to synthesize evidence-based insights for clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12434-9.Keywords: Sepsis, Clinical practice guidelines, AGREE II, AGREE-REX, Fluid resuscitation, Antibiotic therapy

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. It is a critical clinical condition and the leading cause of death among critically ill patients worldwide [1]. The incidence of sepsis continues to rise owing to an aging population, an increasing prevalence of tumors, and frequent use of invasive medical treatments, with millions of new cases reported globally each year [2, 3]. In 2017, an estimated 48.9 million cases of sepsis were reported worldwide, resulting in 11.0 million deaths and accounting for 19.7% of all global deaths [2]. A 2020 epidemiological survey in mainland China revealed that the incidence of sepsis in intensive care units was 20.6%, with intensive care units and inpatient mortality rates of 29.6% and 32.1%, respectively [4]. Sepsis mortality arises from non-modifiable (e.g., advanced age, genetics) and modifiable (e.g., delayed recognition, inappropriate therapy) factors [5]. Substantial heterogeneity in patient risk profiles, pathogens, and host responses further hampers timely recognition and contributes to the high mortality burden [6]. Recently, the overall prognosis of patients with sepsis has not substantially improved owing to the complexity and nonspecific nature of the disease [7]. Once sepsis develops, the condition often worsens dramatically and affects multiple organs and systems. Clinicians require a high-quality clinical practice guideline (CPGs) to provide evidence-based guidance for managing this critical condition effectively.

CPGs are the best guidance for patient-specific clinical problems, based on evidence obtained through systematic evaluation, and are developed after a comprehensive evaluation of various alternative interventions, making them an important guiding documents in medical practice [8]. With the deepening of evidence-based medical research, CPGs for sepsis have shown rapid growth. However, methodological approaches and reporting practices can vary considerably across guidelines, potentially resulting in inconsistencies in their quality and adding complexities to clinical decision-making [9]. A consensus on the recommendation for management of sepsis has not yet been established, leading to clinical decision-making dilemmas. These limitations collectively underscore the need for robust evaluations to ensure guidelines translate effectively into improved patient outcomes.

To address these challenges, we employed a dual-tool appraisal framework. The Appraisal of Guidelines for Research & Evaluation (AGREE) II instrument, widely endorsed as a gold standard by numerous healthcare organizations, assesses the methodological quality of guidelines and reflect the rigor and scientific integrity of their development process [10–12]. Complementing this, the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation-Recommendation Excellence (AGREE-REX) instrument certified by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) was introduced to focus on assessing the clinical applicability, values and preferences, and feasibility of implementation of recommendations, thereby overcoming AGREE II’s limitations in determining the validity of recommendations [13]. This comprehensive strategy helps reduce assessment bias and effectively reconciles discrepancies between evaluations of methodological rigor and clinical relevance. We aimed to reveal the deficiencies in the standardization of current guidelines for the management of sepsis through methodological quality assessment, providing a basis for optimizing the guideline development process. Additionally, it sought to integrate the consensus and discrepancies across different guidelines, and evaluate the quality of the recommendations to promote a patient-centered, individualized therapeutic decision-making system.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [14]. The AGREE II and AGREE-REX instruments were used to assess the methodological quality and recommendation quality of the included sepsis guidelines.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, and three major Chinese academic databases—Wanfang Data, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and SinoMed—from January 1, 2018, to March 1, 2025. Moreover, we systematically reviewed the official websites of key international organizations and guideline repositories, including the World Health Organization (WHO), Guidelines International Network, Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network, NICE, National Guideline Clearinghouse, and relevant professional societies, to identify additional eligible guidelines. Three search terms were used to identify relevant literature: “sepsis,” “septic shock,” and “clinical practice guidelines.” Specific details of the PubMed search strategy are provided in Table S1.

Identification of guidelines

By definitions provided by the Institute of Medicine (IOM) and WHO [15], guidelines were identified based on the following criteria: (1) published in English or Chinese with clear recommendations for managing sepsis in adults, including all related supporting materials and documents; (2) classified as evidence-based CPGs, developed using systematic methodologies, such as the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) Evidence to Decision framework or other structured tools to inform recommendations, and meeting predefined methodological standards that include the classification of level of evidence (LOE) and strength of recommendation (SOR); and (3) included the most recent version of each guideline, if updates were available.

Exclusion criteria: (1) guidelines focusing on specific complications of sepsis (e.g., sepsis-included coagulopathy, acute kidney injury); (2) guidelines targeting sepsis management in children or pregnant women; and (3) documents, such as consensus-based CPG and consensus statements, interpretations, translations, or documents lacking clear recommendations.

Data collection

Two reviewers (H-Y.L. and J.W.) independently conducted a two-stage screening process: first, titles and abstracts were screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria to identify potentially relevant studies; second, full-text articles of selected studies were systematically reviewed to confirm eligibility. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Data were extracted using a pre-designed form, which included essential documents and methodological characteristics. Recommendations for managing sepsis, along with their LOE and SOR, were collected, and the proportions of different LOE levels were calculated.

Quality assessment

The CPGs were independently evaluated using the AGREE II and AGREE-REX instruments by four assessors (H-S.W., H-Y.L., J.W., and L-H.W.) with varying backgrounds and experience in CPG evaluation. The AGREE II instrument comprises 23 items organized across six domains: scope and purpose, stakeholder involvement, rigor of development, clarity of presentation, applicability, and editorial independence [12]. Each item was rated on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), with domain scores calculated by summing individual item scores. The overall quality of the guidelines was classified as: A (strongly recommended, all domains ≥ 60%), B (recommended with some modification, ≥ 3 domains between 30 and 60%), and C (not recommended, ≥ 3 domains < 30%) [16]. In contrast, the AGREE-REX instrument evaluates recommendation quality through nine items grouped into three implementation-focused domains: clinical applicability, values and preferences, and implementability [17]. Domain scores were categorized as: low quality (< 30%), moderate quality (30–70%), and high quality (> 70%) [18].

Comparison of recommendations

We systematically extracted all recommendations related to sepsis management from the included guidelines. For interventions addressed in multiple CPGs, such as hemodynamic management or antibiotic therapy, we performed a comparative analysis focusing on two key aspects: (1) direction of the recommendations (i.e., whether they supported or opposed a specific intervention), and (2) strength of the recommendations (e.g., strong vs. weak).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized AGREE II and AGREE-REX domain scores as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). According to the Fleiss classification system [19], ICC values were interpreted as: excellent (0.75–1.00), acceptable to good (0.41–0.75), and poor (0.00–0.40) agreement. Data summarization and visualization of CPG quality assessment results were performed using Microsoft Office 2024 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Scope and purpose

The median score for the “scope and purpose” domain was 88.89% (77.78–94.44%), with all guidelines scoring above 60%. Although all the included guidelines clearly described the objective, three [27–29] did not adequately describe the health problems or clinical issues they aimed to address. Additionally, three guidelines [22, 25, 27] did not clearly describe the target population to which they should be applied.

Stakeholder involvement

The median score for the “stakeholder involvement” domain was 88.33% (50.00–88.89%), with six guidelines (54.55%) scoring above 60%. All guidelines specified the target users, and two [21, 26] considered the viewpoints of patients and family representatives. Methodologists were involved in the synthesis of evidence and preparation of five guidelines [20–22, 25, 28].

Rigor of development

The median score for the “rigor of development” domain was 58.33% (50.00–83.33%), with five guidelines (45.45%) scoring above 60% [20–23, 26]. All the guidelines explicitly linked recommendations to evidence, explaining how the recommendations were developed.

Clarity of presentation

The median score for the “clarity of presentation” domain was 94.40% (88.89–94.44%), with all guidelines scoring above 60%. Recommendations were clear, and options for specific scenarios or health issues were outlined.

Applicability

The “applicability” domain had the lowest median score among the six domains, at 45.83% (33.33–66.67%), with four guidelines (36.36%) scoring over 60% [21–23, 26]. Five guidelines [21, 22, 24, 26, 28] included suggestions or tools (e.g., charts) for applying recommendations in practice, and one [21] provided monitoring and review criteria.

Editorial independence

The median score for the “editorial independence” domain was 66.67% (58.33–91.67%), with eight guidelines (72.73%) scoring above 60% [20–23, 25, 26, 28, 29]. One guideline [30] did not disclose any conflicts of interest, and three [21, 22, 29] explicitly stated that sponsor opinions did not influence recommendations.

Quality of recommendations

The ICC was 0.845 (95% CI: 0.787–0.894), indicating high consistency. Among 11 included guidelines, the median (IQR) AGREE-REX domain scores were 70.83% (63.89–83.33%) for clinical applicability, 30.21% (25.00–39.58%) for values and preferences, and 58.33% (47.92–64.58%) for implementability. Overall, all 11 guidelines achieved scores ≥ 30%, indicating moderate methodological quality according to AGREE-REX criteria. The LOE and SOR of the recommendations in CPGs

The 11 guidelines collectively provided 552 key recommendations for sepsis management. Eight guidelines [20–22, 24–26, 28, 30] evaluated the SOR and LOE using the GRADE approach, whereas the other three used self-modified methodologies. Among the 237 recommendations assessed using the GRADE system, most were weak recommendations (n = 168, 70.89%), with the remaining classified as strong (n = 69, 29.11%). The proportion of evidence categorized as low (Grade C; n = 97, 40.93%) and very low quality (Grade D; n = 66, 27.85%) was substantially higher than that of high (Grade A; n = 15, 6.33%) and moderate-quality (Grade B; n = 59, 24.89%) evidence. Hemodynamic management of sepsis

Six guidelines addressed fluid resuscitation in sepsis [21–23, 26, 29, 30]. Although a broad agreement exists on most recommendations, notable differences were observed. Fluid resuscitation of sepsis

Five guidelines [21, 22, 26, 29, 30] recommended that patients with sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock receive at least 30 mL/kg of IV crystalloid fluid within the first 3 h of resuscitation. However, the NICE 2024 guideline [23] states that individuals with confirmed or suspected sepsis should receive an intravenous (IV) fluid bolus within an hour of being diagnosed as high-risk. Crystalloids are recommended for initial fluid resuscitation and subsequent volume replacement therapy [26, 27, 30]. However, preferences for crystalloids varied. The KSCCM 2024 guideline [22] and AGIHO 2018 guideline [29] recommended using either balanced crystalloids or saline, whereas the SSC 2021 guideline [26] favored balanced crystalloids over saline for resuscitation, citing a network meta-analysis of 14 studies which revealed that balanced crystalloids were associated with reduced mortality compared to saline [31].

Use of vasopressors

Norepinephrine was consistently recommended as the first-line vasopressor [21, 22, 26, 29, 30]. The J-SSCG-2024 guideline [21] advised initiating vasopressors either concomitantly or early in fluid resuscitation for patients with sepsis or septic shock who struggle to maintain hemodynamics.

Antibiotic treatment of sepsis

Eight guidelines [21–24, 26, 27, 29, 30] explicitly provided recommendations on antibiotic treatment for sepsis, whereas the remaining guidelines did not include specific recommendations on antibiotic therapy. The recommendations can be broadly categorized into two key aspects: the timing of the initial dose and the choice of empirical agents. Timing of initial antibiotic administration

Recommendations on the timing of initial antibiotic therapy varied owing to limited direct evidence. The China 2018 [30] guidelines recommended antibiotic administration as soon as possible after hospital admission or sepsis diagnosis, within 1 h and no later than 3 h. The J-SSCG 2024 guideline [21] advised immediate antibiotic administration upon detecting sepsis or septic shock, however using a target time of less than 1 h is not recommended. The KSCCM 2024 guideline [22] recommended administering antibiotics within 1 h of septic shock recognition and within 3 h of sepsis recognition. The SSC 2021 guideline [26] recommends administering antibiotics immediately, ideally within 1 h, for adults with possible septic shock or a high likelihood of sepsis. For adults with possible sepsis without shock, it recommends a rapid investigation and the administration of antibiotics within 3 h if infection concern persists. The NICE 2024 guideline [23] recommended managing current infections based on the National Early Warning Score (NEWS2), categorizing patients as low, moderate, or high risk. High-risk individuals should receive broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 h of the initial assessment. For moderate- and low-risk patients, broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment may be delayed up to 3 and 6 h, respectively.

Choice of empirical antibiotics

Regarding the choice of empirical antibiotics, several guidelines [23, 24, 30] recommend initiating broad-spectrum therapy to cover all likely pathogens, with subsequent de-escalation to source-specific antibiotics once the causative pathogen is identified. Beyond this general principle, three guidelines specifically incorporated considerations of local microbial ecology and multidrug-resistant (MDR) organism risk into their recommendations [24, 26, 27]. The SWAB 2022 guideline [24] advocates for tailoring the empirical choice based on prior resistance data from clinical cultures. The SSC 2021 guideline [26] offers targeted strategies for at-risk patients, recommending empiric coverage for methicillin-resistant staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and suggesting dual therapy for MDR gram-negative organisms. Providing the most granular guidance, the JAID/JSC 2021 guideline [27] specifies agent selection, including the use of carbapenems for Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamases risk, based on detailed patient risk profiles for MDR infections.

Visceral support therapy for sepsis

Mechanical ventilation

Three guidelines [26, 29, 30] provided recommendations on mechanical ventilation. The SSC 2021 guideline [26] recommended transnasal high-flow oxygen therapy over noninvasive ventilation for adult patients with sepsis-induced severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). For patients with ARDS, a low tidal volume ventilation strategy (6 mL/kg) was recommended. In addition, for patients with respiratory failure and sepsis-induced non-ARDS, low tidal volume ventilation was recommended. For moderate-to-severe ARDS, the guidelines advocated a plateau pressure upper limit of 30 cmH2O (1 cmH2O = 0.098 kPa) and daily prone ventilation for > 12 h [26, 29, 30].

Renal replacement techniques

Five guidelines [21, 26, 28–30] addressed renal replacement therapy for sepsis. In adults with sepsis, septic shock, and acute kidney injury requiring renal replacement therapy, either continuous renal replacement therapy or intermittent therapy was recommended. Continuous renal replacement therapy was preferred for hemodynamically unstable patients to manage fluid balance. Renal replacement therapy should be avoided in cases of sepsis or septic shock with acute kidney injury unless clear indications are present. Long-term outcome and goals of care

The SSC 2021 [26], J-SSCG 2024 [21], and AGIHO 2019 [29] guidelines collectively emphasize structured, evidence-based strategies to address long-term outcomes and care goals for sepsis survivors. The J-SSCG 2024 guideline [21] recommends early rehabilitation to mitigate post-intensive care syndrome, alongside conducting follow-up and rehabilitation to improve physical, cognitive, and mental impairments. The AGIHO 2019 guideline [29] complements this by prioritizing patient-centered care, including psychological support, structured communication, and palliative care consultations to align therapy goals with prognosis. However, the SSC 2021 guideline [26] emphasizes early discussion of treatment goals and prognosis with patients/families (within 72 h) for sepsis survivors, integrating palliative care when appropriate. They further stress the importance of structured discharge plans, including medication coordination, written summaries, and follow-up for physical, cognitive, and emotional issues post-discharge.

Other supporting treatments

Vitamin C

Two guidelines provided recommendations for vitamin C intake. In adults with sepsis or septic shock, the SSC 2021 guideline [26] advised against intravenous administration of vitamin C. The J-SSCJ 2025 guideline [21] advised against the high dose of vitamin C.

Artificial intelligence

The China 2022 guideline [25] highlighted the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in sepsis management. AI tools can enhance clinical outcomes by providing early warning signs and developing optimized treatment plans.

Discussion

CPG quality improvement needs

Developing CPGs is crucial for enhancing care quality, optimizing resource allocation, and safeguarding patient rights. The effectiveness of CPGs depend on their quality, as evidence-based guidelines significantly influence clinicians’ decisions and indirectly impact patient outcomes [32]. Thus, guideline development must adhere to rigorous and standardized methodologies. Our use of AGREE II and AGREE-REX highlights methodological strengths and gaps, offering insights into improving future guideline development processes.

Using the AGREE II tool, we evaluated 11 CPGs, revealing substantial room for improvement. High scores were observed in the domains of “scope and purpose,” “stakeholder investment,” and “clarity of presentation,” whereas “applicability” scored low. This gap likely stems from insufficient attention to clinical implementation and guideline promotion during development. Enhancing applicability requires understanding the target audience, incorporating their feedback, and using visual aids to simplify guideline comprehension and adherence [33]. Greater emphasis on training and multi-channel promotion can further facilitate guideline adoption. Future development should prioritize content quality and practical application. The methodological limitations of CPGs should be addressed, with improvements made in accordance with AGREE II standards to ensure that each item is optimized to the greatest extent possible [34, 35].

AGREE-REX assessments identified significant deficiencies in the “Values and Preferences” domain, with scores generally below 50%. This was primarily owing to inadequate or absent reporting on the process of identifying and integrating the values and preferences of key stakeholders, including patients, healthcare providers, guideline developers, and policymakers, during the formulation of recommendations. For instance, patient priorities (e.g., trade-offs between quality of life and survival in sepsis management) and clinician workflow constraints were often overlooked, whereas policymakers’ resource allocation goals and the methodological transparency requirements of guideline developers were inadequately addressed. The lack of such data may hinder the acceptability and real-world implementation of sepsis-related guidelines. These findings are consistent with existing evidence demonstrating that early and meaningful stakeholder engagement is closely linked to improved guideline implementability [17, 36]. Notably, the AGREE-REX evaluation highlighted deficiencies in “local application and adoption”. This item evaluates whether guideline recommendations are appropriate for specific healthcare settings, target populations, and system contexts. Notably, most guidelines lack actionable strategies for tailoring recommendations to address resource limitations or population-specific needs. Implementation science experts emphasize that this gap compromises the practicality of even rigorously developed guidelines in resource-limited settings. Integrating diverse stakeholder perspectives into the guideline development process and considering local resource can enhance the relevance, feasibility, and uptake of recommendations in clinical practice.

Evidence of recommendations in sepsis

Sepsis, a heterogeneous condition caused by diverse pathogens, presents unique challenges for clinical research. Variations in underlying disease, immune status, and infection sites complicate the creation of uniform clinical trial protocols. In our study, the quality of evidence need to be improved. Many large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in sepsis have primarily focused on mortality as the main outcome; however, this measure may not fully reflect the long-term functional recovery of survivors. Owing to the severity of the condition, certain study designs—such as placebo-controlled trials—are often ethically challenging, further shaping the landscape of sepsis research. As a result, observational studies are frequently utilized, offering insights into real-world clinical scenarios. Future clinical studies should consider alternative outcome metrics, including organ dysfunction scores and complication rates, in addition to mortality. Flexible and innovative clinical trial designs are needed alongside multicenter RCTs to produce high-quality evidence, thereby strengthening management and improving patient outcomes.

Hemodynamic management of sepsis

Fluid resuscitation

Fluid resuscitation plays a central role in the early management of sepsis and septic shock by restoring intravascular volume, improving tissue perfusion, and optimizing oxygen delivery. The concept of early goal-directed therapy (EGDT), introduced by Rivers et al. [37], emphasized aggressive hemodynamic optimization within the first 6 h of sepsis recognition and was associated with improved survival. However, subsequent trials—namely ProCESS [38], ARISE [39], and ProMISe [40]—failed to replicate the mortality benefit of protocolized EGDT, prompting a shift toward a more pragmatic approach. This led to the 2016 SSC guideline [41], which recommended 30 mL/kg of crystalloid within 3 h of admission, guided by ongoing hemodynamic assessment. The 2018 SSC guideline [42] further condensed this timeframe into a 1 h bundle, emphasizing immediate initiation of fluid resuscitation upon sepsis recognition. Although this change sparked debate owing to limited high-quality evidence supporting its impact on hard outcomes, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis has provided supportive evidence, demonstrating that adherence to the 1 h bundle was associated with a significant reduction in short-term mortality (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.69–0.84), particularly in ICU settings [43]. Nonetheless, critics raised concerns about potential unintended consequences, including overhydration, unnecessary antimicrobial use, and increased workload for frontline clinicians. In response, the 2021 SSC guideline [21] maintained the core principles of early resuscitation while de-emphasizing rigid time targets, reflecting the evolving understanding of sepsis heterogeneity and the importance of individualized care.

For the optimal fluid volume for sepsis resuscitation, most guidelines recommended the 30 mL/kg (ideal body weight) crystalloid dose, and this fixed volume of initial resuscitation was based on observational evidence [44]. Notably, no RCT has directly compared different fluid volumes, including the commonly recommended 30 mL/kg dose, limiting definitive conclusions about optimal dosing strategies. Furthermore, differences in resource availability and patient characteristics across settings may influence the appropriateness of this standardized approach. Findings from a large-scale study conducted in Africa suggested potential harm in administering 30 mL/kg of crystalloid early in sepsis [45]. Observational studies report varying results of different individuals underscoring the need for further research. Current trials, including FRESHLY (NCT05453565) and LOFT (NCT03754799), are expected to provide higher-quality comparative data to guide clinical decision-making and refine resuscitation protocols.

Application of vasopressors

Vasopressors increase blood pressure by enhancing preload, improving cardiac contractility, and raising systemic vascular resistance [46, 47]. According to the 2021 SSC guideline [26], the initial target mean arterial pressure for patients with septic shock is 65 mmHg. Norepinephrine is recommended as the first-line vasopressor, with vasopressin (arginine vasopressin or antidiuretic hormone) serving as a second-line therapy when additional support is needed. Current guidelines support the early initiation of vasopressors in patients who remain hypotensive despite initial fluid administration. The timing of vasopressor therapy has been shown to influence outcomes, with evidence suggesting that earlier use is associated with improved survival [48, 49]. For instance, a 2014 observational study in China found that each hour of delay in initiating norepinephrine during the first 6 h of septic shock was associated with a 5.3% increase in 28-day mortality [50]. Similarly, a retrospective analysis in the US reported lower 30-day mortality when vasopressors were started within 6 h of hypotension onset compared with later administration [51]. Nevertheless, clinical experience indicates that it should be integrated with adequate fluid resuscitation. Combining early vasopressor administration with aggressive fluid resuscitation (≥ 1000 mL) was associated with lower mortality, suggesting that a balanced approach may optimize outcomes [52]. Ongoing research, such as the Early Vasopressors in Sepsis Trial (NCT05179499), aims to further clarify the optimal timing and integration of vasopressor therapy with fluid resuscitation in the management of septic shock. However, fluid resuscitation strategies continue to evolve from standardized, fixed-volume approaches toward more individualized, dynamic, and physiology-guided methods, reflecting the increasing recognition of patient heterogeneity in sepsis care.

Antibiotic treatment for sepsis

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition resulting from the body’s dysregulated response to infection. Prompt identification and targeted anti-infective therapy are fundamental components of effective sepsis management. The relationship between the timing of antimicrobial administration and patient outcomes has been extensively studied, with evidence consistently suggesting that earlier treatment is associated with improved survival.

A landmark observational study involving 2,731 patients with septic shock reported that mortality increased by 7.6% for each hour of delay in administering effective antibiotics after the onset of persistent hypotension [53]. Additional large-scale studies involving tens of thousands of patients have similarly reported an approximate 1% increase in mortality per hour of delay [54].These findings contributed to the 2016 SSC guideline recommendation that intravenous antibiotics be administered within 1 h of diagnosing sepsis or septic shock [41]. This guidance aimed to standardize care and emphasize the urgency of intervention in critically ill patients. In 2021, the SSC updated its recommendations to better reflect clinical complexity, advising that antibiotics be administered within 1 h for adults with suspected septic shock or a high likelihood of sepsis. For patients with suspected sepsis but without shock, rapid diagnostic evaluation is encouraged, with antibiotics to be administered within 3 h if infection remains a concern [26]. The evolution of these guidelines highlights the ongoing effort to integrate emerging evidence with practical clinical considerations. Although most supporting data come from observational studies, which carry inherent limitations such as potential confounding, the consistent associations observed have informed valuable clinical standards.

Beyond timely intervention, the strategic selection and optimization of empirical antibiotics are paramount in sepsis management. Effective empirical therapy must ensure broad-spectrum coverage against all potential pathogens, with agent selection meticulously informed by three pillars: infection source identification, local microbiological epidemiology, and individualized patient risk factors for MDR organisms. Furthermore, for adult patients with sepsis or septic shock at high risk for MRSA, empirical antimicrobial regimens incorporating MRSA coverage are strongly recommended over non-covered alternatives [26]. In cases involving high-risk MDR organisms, dual-agent gram-negative coverage (e.g., carbapenem + aminoglycoside) is advised for initial empiric treatment over monotherapy, although supporting evidence remains weak [26]. This precision-driven approach ensures that time-sensitive antibiotic administration is rapid and appropriately targeted, maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing collateral damage from broad-spectrum agents. By aligning initial therapy with evolving microbiological data and patient-specific vulnerabilities, clinicians can optimize outcomes in sepsis management.

The Infectious Diseases Society of America has also emphasized the importance of balancing timely antimicrobial use with responsible prescribing practices, noting that premature or unnecessary use may contribute to antimicrobial resistance and adverse effects [55]. These perspectives support a broader movement toward precision in antibiotic stewardship.

Future research should focus on refining antimicrobial strategies, identifying patient subgroups that benefit most from early administration, and developing tools to guide timely yet appropriate treatment decisions. Such efforts will help further improve outcomes in sepsis while preserving the effectiveness of available antimicrobial agents.

Vitamin C and sepsis

Recently, the use of vitamin C for treating sepsis has attracted widespread attention. A 2017 study by Marik et al. reported that a “cocktail regimen” (vitamin C, vitamin B1, and hydrocortisone) significantly reduced mortality in patients with sepsis [56]. Studies have suggested that vitamin C may play an important role in attenuating the inflammatory response in sepsis, protecting organ function, and improving patient prognosis [57, 58]. However, the LOVIT study on patients treated with vasopressor drugs reported higher mortality or persistent organ dysfunction within 28 days in the vitamin C group [59]. A meta-analysis published in 2024 [60] indicated that the available evidence is insufficient to support the notion that vitamin C improves the prognosis of patients with sepsis, although it significantly shortens the duration of vasoactive drug use. Given the conflicting findings in recent years, the effectiveness of vitamin C in sepsis management remains an area worth investigating. Its low cost and good safety profile make it an attractive therapeutic candidate. Therefore, additional large-scale RCTs are required to provide more robust evidence for its clinical benefits in managing sepsis.

Long-term outcome and goals of care

The integration of long-term care strategies for sepsis survivors reflects a shift toward holistic, patient-centered models, emphasizing early (within 72 h) goal-setting discussions, palliative care integration when clinically appropriate, and multidisciplinary follow-up frameworks to address physical, cognitive, and psychosocial recovery. Shared decision-making is central to intensive care unit discharge planning, medication coordination, and functional monitoring, whereas socioeconomic and psychosocial support (e.g., peer groups, housing, financial assistance) is recommended to address broader recovery needs. However, evidence gaps persist in interventions, such as structured handover tools, early rehabilitation, cognitive therapy, or routine formal palliative care consultations, highlighting the need for robust validation across diverse populations. To optimize outcomes, future research should prioritize harmonizing care pathways through large-scale trials, developing cost-effective post-discharge rehabilitation and telehealth-enabled follow-up models, and validating patient-reported outcomes alongside socioeconomic determinants. These efforts must balance evidence-based practices with individualized risk-benefit assessments, multidisciplinary collaboration, and ethical considerations, while addressing challenges in standardization, resource disparities, and long-term efficacy validation.

Considerations for resource-limited settings

The management of sepsis in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) presents distinct challenges that are not fully addressed by current international guidelines. Although evidence-based recommendations such as those from the SSC are optimized for well-resourced settings, their direct applicability in LMICs is constrained by systemic barriers, including infrastructural deficits, workforce shortages, and limited access to essential diagnostics and therapeutics [26]. Recent survey underscore that implementing resource-intensive care bundles is substantially hampered in LMICs by these structural and material limitations [61, 62]. For example, foundational components of sepsis management such as early warning systems, lactate testing, and microbiological culture capabilities are frequently unavailable or subject to significant delays, making strict time-bound protocols difficult to sustain [61]. A cross-sectional study by Williams et al. reported that lactate testing and culture supplies were unavailable in 19% and 11% of hospitals, respectively, with delays exceeding 3 h commonly reported for initiating fluids, vasopressors, and antibiotics [61]. Furthermore, the etiological spectrum of sepsis in these regions often includes tropical and neglected pathogens, which are seldom reflected in guidelines derived primarily from high-income settings [62]. Chronic understaffing contributes to moral distress and burnout among healthcare workers, further undermining consistent adherence to guideline recommendations [61]. In response, recent global consensus initiatives have called for context-sensitive, pragmatic strategies, notably a Pareto-inspired emphasis on high-impact interventions alongside the development and validation of resource-adjusted management protocols specifically tailored to LMIC settings [62]. Future guideline updates should integrate evidence from and actively account for the realities of sepsis care in resource-limited environments, aiming to advance global equity in sepsis outcomes.

AI and future direction

Sepsis presents significant challenges owing to its complexity and variability in etiology, pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and treatment response. The advent of AI technology offers new prospects for personalized management, enabling early warnings and individualized interventions that reduce morbidity, mortality rates, and hospital stays [25]. A 2018 study in Nature Medicine introduced an AI model using reinforcement learning to make optimal treatment decisions for patients with sepsis, thereby improving outcomes [63]. AI-driven fluid recommendations have effectively predicted fluid responsiveness [64], and AI applications in antibiotic therapy have shortened antimicrobial use, thereby reducing hospitalization-related complications and mortality [65]. Future research should explore multimodal data integration and interdisciplinary collaboration to promote AI application in sepsis care. Precision and personalized treatment supported by AI are anticipated to play a critical role in future sepsis management.

Reasons for changes in guideline recommendations

The guidelines included in this study exhibit similarities and differences in their recommendations, with observable trends over time. These variations are primarily owing to advancements in medical research and the continuous emergence of new evidence, which lead to evolving recommendations. Differences in healthcare resources, policy directions, and technological capabilities across countries influence the feasibility and accessibility of specific treatments. Additionally, diverging expert opinions and varying degrees of international academic collaboration affect the consistency of guidelines. Some countries follow fixed update schedules for their guidelines, regularly revising recommendations, whereas others adjust them as needed based on emerging evidence. Overall, these factors collectively contribute to the diversity seen in clinical practice guidelines across different times and regions. Understanding these underlying reasons can aid in the better interpretation and application of guidelines, facilitating the sharing and standardization of best practices globally.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents several notable strengths. First, the four assessors demonstrated strong inter-rater agreement in evaluating CPG quality, thereby enhancing the reliability and generalizability of the findings. Second, it represents the first comprehensive assessment of CPGs specifically for adult sepsis, addressing a critical gap in the literature. Third, the systematic extraction and comparative analysis of recommendations from multiple guidelines provide a robust foundation for future research on sepsis management and guideline harmonization.

However, some limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the guideline selection process was confined to peer-reviewed journals, professional association websites, and institutional repositories, potentially excluding relevant CPGs disseminated through other resources. The inherent bias in manual search methodologies compounds this limitation. Second, the inclusion criteria restricted analysis to English and Chinese language CPGs, thereby limiting the global applicability of our findings. Third, the subjective nature of AGREE II and AGREE-REX tools introduces risks of interpretational variability among reviewers, despite standardized training protocols. Finally, we focused exclusively on methodological rigor and recommendation quality, without evaluating the real-world implementation of these guidelines or their direct impact on patient outcomes—a gap that future research should address through longitudinal studies or implementation science frameworks.

Conclusions

The methodological quality of current CPGs for sepsis needs improvement, particularly in application and values and preferences. Future CPG developers should strictly follow standardized methodologies and focus on improving guideline dissemination and application. The treatment strategies of different guidelines are slightly controversial for sepsis. Recommendations should be revised periodically as new evidence emerges. In this study, we systematically evaluated existing CPGs, laying the foundation for future high-quality guideline development and synthesizing evidence-based insights to support clinical decision-making, and propose directions for future studies.
