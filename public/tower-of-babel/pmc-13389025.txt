Nonlinear temperature change responses shape soil organic carbon loss-gain transitions in global Mollisol croplands
Authors: Xiangtian Meng, Yilin Bao, Susan L Ustin, Chong Luo, Xinle Zhang, Mikhail Semenov, Huanjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMollisol croplands are important for global food production and soil carbon storage, yet it remains unclear how climate warming affects transitions between soil organic carbon loss and gain. Here we show that surface soil organic carbon across global Mollisol croplands declined by 5.59% from 1990 to 2020, although the rate of loss slowed over time. Using 5,535 soil samples and satellite observations, we identified a nonlinear relationship between soil carbon change and the rate of warming, with a threshold at 0.0309 °C/yr above which net soil carbon change shifted from loss toward gain. This threshold increased toward lower latitudes, indicating that warmer regions require stronger warming to reach a similar transition. If recent climate-soil relationships persist, about 65% of Mollisol cropland may exceed this threshold by 2050 under a moderate emissions scenario. These results highlight the importance of nonlinear threshold responses in predicting soil carbon dynamics under climate change.Subject terms: Carbon cycle, Climate-change impacts, Agroecology

Introduction

Soil carbon pools account for 80% of terrestrial carbon in Earth’s carbon cycle1 and play a crucial role in the long-term storage and release of carbon globally2–4. The high soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks in Mollisol are crucial for global food security. However, SOC stocks in Mollisol show significant spatial and temporal variation, with declining trends mainly attributed to climate change and agricultural activities5–8. Predictions of SOC stock dynamics in Mollisol vary significantly due to differences in temporal and spatial scales, technological factors, and modeling approaches9–11, resulting in substantial uncertainties and leaving their global dynamics insufficiently constrained12–17. Despite numerous studies analyzing the causes of declining SOC stocks18–20, the responses of SOC stocks to multiple drivers in global Mollisol remain poorly understood. This limits our ability to generalize impact patterns across different temporal and regional conditions, hindering efforts to restore dynamic equilibrium in SOC stocks.

In recent decades, heterogeneous changes in global Mollisol SOC stocks have created a pressing need for continuous, high-accuracy spatial predictions. However, most large-scale predictions rely on statistical relationships with environmental variables and are constrained by existing SOC data, which suffer from limited spatial resolution, incomplete spatiotemporal coverage, and consequently inconsistent estimates of baseline stocks and their key drivers12. Satellite remote sensing, through the continuous monitoring of soil spectral reflectance9–11, offers a powerful alternative to track SOC variations. This approach directly addresses the critical limitations of sparse data points, coarse spatial resolution9,12–15, and static modeling frameworks inherent in traditional methods21–23. The capacity of satellite remote sensing to provide long-term time series with greatly improved spatial detail fundamentally enhances our ability to characterize and quantify the dynamics of SOC stocks across global Mollisol croplands.

Several factors, including temperature increases19, change in precipitation20, cultivation duration24, and crop type7, have been repeatedly identified as important controls on cropland SOC stocks in Mollisol regions. Recent syntheses and meta-analyses have examined how SOC responds to multiple global-change drivers across climates and soil types, suggesting that warming can amplify SOC responses to elevated CO2, nitrogen addition, and altered moisture regimes, that increases in plant productivity only partly compensate warming-induced SOC losses, and that deeper, mineral-associated SOC pools can also be vulnerable to sustained warming25–27. These studies represent important progress by explicitly considering multiple drivers, soil depth, and environmental context. Nevertheless, because these studies typically aggregate data across soil orders, include relatively few Mollisol cropland observations, and do not explicitly resolve how SOC responses vary along gradients of warming rate at high spatial resolution, they offer limited insight into whether, and at what warming rates, SOC losses might be reversed in the global Mollisol region.

Against this background, Mollisol regions provide a critical setting for examining how soils and climate interact under intensive agricultural use. Existing studies have shown that the mechanisms by which climate change affects SOC stocks in Mollisol vary across climates and spatial-temporal scales28,29. For example, increased temperatures may enhance microbial activity and accelerate SOC losses in temperate climates8,30, whereas warming-induced soil freeze-thaw cycles may, under some conditions, promote carbon accumulation in cold regions31. We therefore hypothesize that soil-climate feedbacks in Mollisol croplands are inherently nonlinear, and that sufficiently strong warming may, in some regimes, enhance plant productivity and organic inputs enough to offset SOC losses. Such threshold-like responses are likely to vary across latitudinal gradients, driven by differences in baseline climate, SOC pools and ecosystem-level thermal adaptation. In addition, increased nitrogen fertilizer use and atmospheric deposition can promote plant growth and enhance SOC stocks in the short term32, whereas water stress and elevated temperatures may suppress carbon accumulation by inhibiting photosynthesis and altering microbial communities, ultimately reducing SOC stocks33. The relative importance of climatic, biological, anthropogenic and soil property drivers in Mollisol has therefore been shown to vary from broad latitudinal zones to fine-scale regional microclimates12,34, but the existence, location and magnitude of warming-rate thresholds, as well as the ways in which the dominant drivers shift across these thresholds, remain poorly quantified in Mollisol regions.

To address these knowledge gaps, we integrate 5535 soil samples (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1) with 1,086,202 Landsat images to construct a global database of surface (0–20 cm) SOC stocks in Mollisol croplands at 30 m resolution for the period 1990–2020. Using a hybrid deep-learning framework that combines field observations with multi-decadal satellite reflectance, we generate four decadal SOC stock maps (1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020) and validate their spatial and temporal dynamics through independent datasets and meta-analysis. We then analyze how changes in SOC stocks relate to the rate of change in annual mean temperature (ΔTavg) and to key climatic, biological, anthropogenic, soil property, and topographic factors. Specifically, we identify two distinct temperature change thresholds in the ΔSOC-ΔTavg relationship, namely a segmented response threshold and an SOC gain-loss crossover threshold. Finally, we use these thresholds to delineate regimes in which different drivers dominate SOC changes, thereby elucidating the mechanisms by which warming rate and co-varying factors jointly shape SOC dynamics in Mollisol croplands. Our results challenge simple linear assumptions in soil-climate feedbacks and provide an observation-based framework for assessing the sustainability of SOC stocks under ongoing soil degradation and declining agricultural soil quality.

Results

Spatiotemporal dynamics of SOC stocks in global Mollisol croplands

Surface SOC stocks in croplands across the global Mollisol region have been significantly affected by global warming and intensive agricultural activities over recent decades (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). We used Landsat image, field-collected soil samples, and a hybrid probability deep learning model (Supplementary Fig. 2) to generate 30 m spatial resolution global estimates of Mollisol cropland surface SOC stocks (root mean square error (RMSE), 17.83 MgC/ha; coefficient of determination (R2), 0.68) across multiple time periods (Supplementary Fig. 3a) (Verification results for the spatial and temporal patterns of SOC stock changes are detailed in the Supplementary Note 1) and the spatial distribution of uncertainty in SOC stock estimations (Supplementary Fig. 4). From 1990 to 2020, global Mollisol cropland surface SOC stocks declined by 5.59%, with average values decreasing from 59.37 ± 21.30 MgC/ha to 56.05 ± 21.17 MgC/ha (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 2). The decadal decreases were 2.98% (1990–2000), 1.35% (2000–2010), and 1.36% (2010–2020), respectively.

Significant spatial heterogeneity is observed in the spatial distribution of SOC stocks in the global Mollisol (Fig. 1a, the spatial distribution of SOC stocks in the global Mollisol for other periods are provided in Supplementary Fig. 5). SOC stocks exhibited a clear latitudinal gradient across global Mollisol croplands, with higher values at higher latitudes and a decrease of 0.26 MgC/ha for each 1° decrease in latitude toward the equator, highlighting the strong influence of annual average temperature gradients on SOC stocks (Supplementary Fig. 6). Among continents, Siberia (SI) and Europe (EU) had the highest SOC stocks, with 71.44 ± 12.32 MgC/ha and 60.15 ± 21.15 MgC/ha, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). Asian (AS) and North American (NA) Mollisol had the second highest SOC stock, at 59.76 ± 16.68 MgC/ha and 45.60 ± 16.72 MgC/ha, respectively. South American (SA) Mollisol showed the lowest SOC stock, at 33.51 ± 8.17 MgC/ha.

Global Mollisol SOC stocks are still declining, but the rate of decline has slowed from 0.18 MgC/ha/yr (1990–2000) to 0.08 MgC/ha/yr (2010–2020) (Fig. 1d). The change in global Mollisol SOC stocks varied significantly across continents (Fig. 1b). The largest declines in SOC stocks over the past 30 years occurred in the SI (a decline of 8.47 MgC/ha) and SA Mollisol regions (a decline of 7.69 MgC/ha). The AS (a decline of 4.23 MgC/ha), NA (a decline of 3.46 MgC/ha), and EU (a decline of 1.84 MgC/ha) Mollisol regions followed closely with declines. From 1990 to 2020, SOC stocks decreased in 60.84% of the global Mollisol region (Fig. 1e), with declines concentrated in eastern NA, SA, SI, and northeastern AS (Fig. 1b). In the remaining 39.16% of the area, SOC stocks increased, particularly in western NA and eastern EU. The distribution of SOC stock changes indicates that new areas of decline are mainly in the southern EU, southeastern NA, and the entire SA Mollisol region (Supplementary Fig. 7). The proportion of regions with declining SOC stocks from 1990 to 2020 varies across continents, in addition to the general pattern described above. The area of declining SOC stocks in the EU Mollisol region showed no significant change (Fig. 1m). In contrast, the area of SOC stock decline is increasing annually in other regions: the NA Mollisol region (up by 9.02%), SA Mollisol region (up by 22.54%), SI Mollisol region (up by 10.06%), and AS Mollisol region (up by 7.42%). However, the rate of increase in declining SOC stock areas is slowing.

Climatic and anthropogenic controls on SOC stocks spatial heterogeneity

We investigated the spatial heterogeneity of SOC stocks across global Mollisol croplands over four time periods and assessed the influence of multiple environmental factors. These environmental factors were selected because they are key determinants of SOC stocks and are among the most widely used indicators in SOC-related studies (Supplementary Table 3). A boosted regression tree (BRT) algorithm was employed to assess the effects of climatic, anthropogenic, biological, soil, and topographic factors on SOC stocks (Supplementary Fig. 8). The BRT model explained 82% of the spatial heterogeneity in SOC stocks (Fig. 2a). Annual average temperature (Tavg) was the most influential factor (53.26%), followed by nitrogen (N) (19.42%). Other factors contributed less than 10%, including farm machinery data (FMD) (5.73%), minimum normalized difference tillage index (minNDTI) (5.07%), elevation (ELE) (4.41%), annual precipitation (PR) (1.89%), gross primary productivity (GPP) (1.75%), consecutive wet days (CWD) (1.63%), consecutive dry days (CDD) (1.27%), and livestock manure (1.11%). When examining the individual effects of each factor, Tavg exhibited a strong negative effect on SOC stocks below 14 °C, which plateaued above this threshold (Fig. 2b). N below 59 g/kg had a significant positive effect on SOC stocks, which tended to saturate above this threshold (Fig. 2c). FMD showed a significant negative effect on SOC stocks below 2.2 Ps/1000 ha, which saturated beyond this level (Fig. 2d). minNDTI exhibited a significant negative effect on SOC stocks, which saturated at a threshold of 0.5 (Fig. 2e). ELE below 160 m had a significant positive effect on SOC stocks, whereas between 160–450 m, it had a significant negative effect (Fig. 2f). PR exhibited a significant positive effect up to 55 mm, after which it saturated (Fig. 2g). Similar partial response patterns were observed across all time periods (Supplementary Figs. 6–9). These partial response patterns highlight the complex nature of SOC responses to key climatic and management-related factors across global Mollisol croplands.

We compared the environmental factors influencing the spatial heterogeneity of SOC stocks across different continents. Tavg was the most important factor on all four continents, except in SA, where nitrogen (N) was the dominant factor (Supplementary Fig. 8c). In all cases, the factor importance exceeded 45%. The response of SOC stocks to Tavg varied across regions. SOC stocks in NA and AS were the most sensitive, showing the greatest decline with a 1 °C increase in Tavg (Supplementary Figs. 13, 17). In contrast, SA was the least sensitive (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Nonlinear response of SOC stock change to temperature change rate

We analyzed temporal changes in cropland SOC stocks across global Mollisol and evaluated the effects of environmental factors change over the past three decades. Climate change was the dominant factor of change in SOC (ΔSOC) (Fig. 3b), with change rate in Tavg (ΔTavg) exerting the strongest influence (Fig. 3c). Using a generalized additive model (GAM), we identified and corroborated a nonlinear relationship between ΔSOC and ΔTavg (Supplementary Fig. 18), characterized by a segmented response (Fig. 4a). When ΔTavg was below 0.0086 °C/yr (95% CI: 0.0077–0.0102 °C/yr), ΔSOC was negatively correlated with ΔTavg (r = − 0.48, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4b), indicating that faster cooling was associated with larger SOC gains. When ΔTavg exceeded 0.0086 °C/yr, the relationship became positive (r = 0.42, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4b), implying that faster warming was associated with weaker SOC losses. Notably, at ΔTavg > 0.0309 °C/yr (95% CI: 0.0302–0.0316 °C/yr) (Fig. 4b), ΔSOC crossed from negative to positive, suggesting that higher warming rates can shift SOC from loss to accumulation. We term this threshold the SOC gain-loss crossover threshold. This response was most pronounced in Mollisol regions of Southwest AS, Northern SA, and Eastern EU (Fig. 3a). We also detected a clear latitudinal gradient in the gain-loss crossover, increasing from high to low latitudes: 0.0261 °C/yr (95% CI: 0.0170–0.0352 °C/yr) for > 55°N, 0.0307 °C/yr (95% CI: 0.0299–0.0314 °C/yr) for 40°–55°N, and 0.0893 °C/yr (95% CI: 0.0113–0.1595 °C/yr) for 40°S–40°N (Supplementary Table 4). To further assess this gradient, we conducted supplementary analyses by 5° latitude bands, which corroborated these trends (Supplementary Table 4). These results indicate that stronger warming is required to trigger positive SOC responses in low-latitude croplands. In addition, ΔSOC showed a stronger positive association with changes in CDD (ΔCDD) (Supplementary Fig. 19a). Under low N, ΔSOC was generally negative, whereas at higher N levels, ΔSOC increased with N (Supplementary Fig. 19b).

Based on the segmented response threshold (0.0086 °C/yr) and the SOC gain-loss crossover threshold (0.0309 °C/yr), we further examined the relationship between ΔSOC and change in environmental factors across different stages.

During the stage where ΔTavg was below 0.0086 °C/yr, ΔSOC correlated with multiple environmental factors, including ΔPR, ΔTavg, ΔFMD, ΔGPP, N, and others (Fig. 4b). Structural equation modeling (SEM) indicated that this stage was dominated by soil property constraints (Fig. 4d): N had the strongest direct negative effect on ΔSOC (path coefficient, PC = − 0.41) and also negatively affected ΔGPP (PC = − 0.39). Because ΔGPP had a positive effect on ΔSOC (PC = 0.18), N indirectly inhibited SOC accumulation by suppressing ΔGPP, thereby weakening carbon inputs and amplifying its net negative effect on ΔSOC. ELE indirectly effected ΔSOC (indirect effect = 0.16; Supplementary Fig. 20) via its negative effect on N (PC = − 0.43). ΔTavg and ΔPR had negative effects on ΔSOC (PC = − 0.27 and − 0.15, respectively).

During the stage with ΔTavg between 0.0086 and 0.0309 °C/yr, ΔSOC was correlated with ΔTavg, ΔYield, ΔGPP, clay, N, and SLO (Fig. 4c). ΔSOC was positively affected by ΔTavg, N, clay, and SLO (PCs = 0.28, 0.16, 0.13, and 0.13, respectively). The negative effect of N on ΔGPP persisted (PC = − 0.29; Fig. 4e) but was significantly weaker than in the < 0.0086 °C/yr stage. SLO had a direct positive effect on ΔSOC (PC = 0.13) and indirect negative effects via ΔYield and N (PCs = − 0.27 and − 0.09, respectively; Fig. 4e). The negative effects of ΔYield and ΔGPP on ΔSOC were weaker (PCs = − 0.08 and − 0.11, respectively). Compared with the previous stage, ΔTavg had a stronger direct effect on ΔSOC (PC = 0.28; Supplementary Fig. 20), whereas the effect of N weakened (PC = 0.16).

In the stage with ΔTavg > 0.0309 °C/yr, SEM indicated that ΔPR, ΔTavg, ΔGPP, and N were the primary drivers (Fig. 4f). PCs from ΔPR and ΔTavg to ΔSOC were 0.24 and 0.16, respectively. Concurrently, ΔTavg positively affected ΔGPP (PC = 0.31) and N (PC = 0.27), suggesting that strong warming activated both biological and nutrient processes. However, N had a stronger negative effect on ΔGPP (PC = − 0.61) than in earlier stages. Furthermore, N mediated the relationship between ΔGPP and ΔSOC (PC = − 0.19), partly offsetting the direct positive climate effect. Overall, this stage featured stronger direct positive climate effects on ΔSOC, partially counteracted by negative effects from N and ΔGPP.

Using the SOC gain-loss crossover threshold estimated for each 5° latitudinal band (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 4), we project that, under the SSP2-4.5 scenario, 64.76% of Mollisol cropland areas will experience annual warming rates exceeding this threshold by 2050 (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 5). This projected spatial pattern is particularly pronounced in southeastern NA, southern SA, and extensive parts of the EU, SI, and AS Mollisol regions. In these regions, SOC trends may shift from net losses to net gains. We hereafter refer to these regions as warming-enabled carbon reversal zones.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a data-driven framework integrating remote sensing, a hybrid probability deep learning model, and field-collected soil samples to generate high-resolution (30 × 30 m) SOC stock maps for croplands in the global Mollisol region across four periods (1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Our maps reliably capture fine-scale spatial and temporal variability of SOC stocks (Supplementary Fig. 21), as confirmed by multiple independent validations (Supplementary Note 1). The success of high-accuracy SOC stock prediction can be attributed to three key factors: (1) leveraging spectral heterogeneity in visible, near-infrared, and shortwave infrared bands to detect SOC-related reflectance signatures35–38 (Supplementary Fig. 22). (2) using a bare soil pixel selecting strategy to generate bare soil composite image (Supplementary Fig. 23), eliminating the influence of surface coverings such as vegetation, snow cover, and crop residues21,38,39. (3) implementing a hybrid probability deep learning model (Supplementary Fig. 24) that partitions the study area into homogeneous subregions (Supplementary Fig. 25), effectively reducing spatial heterogeneity and boundary artifacts24,40 (Supplementary Fig. 26). In addition, the deep learning component of the model effectively learns the nonlinear relationships between SOC stocks and remote sensing variables using large-scale field-collected data23,41, resulting in improved prediction performance (Supplementary Fig. 3), with an R2 increase of 0.16 compared to a conventional global model.

Building on these robust spatial and temporal predictions, we further investigated how ΔSOC responds to climate change, with a particular focus on different stages of temperature change. During the stage with ΔTavg < 0.0086 °C/yr (cooling or weak warming), the association between ΔSOC and ΔTavg likely reflects decomposition constrained by temperature, while ΔGPP remains at a moderate level. This is consistent with the negative effect of ΔTavg on ΔSOC and the positive effect on ΔGPP in Fig. 4c, as well as the high temperature sensitivity of soil decomposition8,42. It also accords with evidence that lower decomposition rates promote increases in SOC stocks43,44. Cooling reduces enzyme activity and diffusivity, and ΔPR can weaken aggregate-mineral associations via erosion and leaching, exposing protected substrates and fostering decomposition and respiration under moist conditions, thereby reducing stable SOC retention45. Concurrently, soil pathways did not translate into proportional input gains: higher N tended to intensify mineralization by accelerating turnover of soluble substrates and inducing positive feedbacks, thereby suppressing net carbon sequestration. This aligns with the negative path from N to ΔSOC identified in this stage39,46. Collectively, these findings support a regulatory sequence at this stage, in which cooling leads to decomposition limitation and ultimately enhances SOC accumulation.

During the stage where ΔTavg ranged from 0.0086 to 0.0309 °C/yr, the mean ΔSOC remained negative (Fig. 4a). However, the overall influence of ΔTavg on ΔSOC shifted from negative to positive compared to the previous stage, though this effect was partially offset through the ΔGPP pathway. Slow warming without concurrent hydrological relief increases vapor-pressure deficit and thermal stress, suppressing photosynthesis and stomatal conductance and thereby reducing ΔGPP. This is consistent with reports of warming-induced productivity declines47–49. The positive path from clay to ΔSOC indicates that mineral phases began to play a larger storage role in this stage. Higher clay content and active mineral surfaces promote conversion of new inputs to mineral-associated organic carbon and reduce respiratory losses45,50. The negative effect of N on ΔGPP, together with its weak positive effect on ΔSOC (Fig. 4e), indicates a dual role: accelerating decomposition while offering limited support for stabilization. Under warming, additional N can amplify turnover via enhanced decomposition, reduced microbial carbon-use efficiency, and priming, without necessarily increasing effective inputs46,51–54. Conversely, higher N may also reflect legacy organic matter and fertilization in regions with ample clay and active mineral surfaces, providing some capacity for carbon sequestration. This helps explain why N becomes a net negative driver of ΔSOC in the ΔTavg < 0.0086 °C/yr stage. Although SLO had a weak direct positive effect on ΔSOC, it indirectly signaled erosion-driven losses of long-term inputs on sloping land by attenuating the pathway from N to Δyield55. Overall, this stage reflects a trade-off between input limitation from temperature-inhibited ΔGPP and stabilization provided by clay and aggregates: when the former dominates, the system tends toward an input-constrained, turnover-dominated deficit; when mineral stabilization partly compensates for input deficits, SOC approaches relative stability.

When ΔTavg exceeds 0.0309 °C/yr, the mean ΔSOC shifted to positive values, and the positive correlation between ΔSOC and ΔTavg persisted (Fig. 4a). This occurred because the direct positive effects of ΔPR and ΔTavg on ΔSOC became dominant. Concurrently, climate established a critical indirect pathway by influencing ΔGPP, enabling both hydrotemperate and biogeochemical processes to jointly govern ΔSOC dynamics56,57 (Fig. 4f). Warming and humidification generally enhance photosynthesis and root turnover, thereby increasing the potential for carbon inputs. However, the negative effect of ΔGPP on ΔSOC and the strong negative effect of N on ΔGPP indicate that the pathway linking N, ΔGPP and ΔSOC acts as an inhibitory loop that accelerates carbon turnover at this stage. This pattern is consistent with mechanisms in which warming and moistening increase substrate availability and mineralization, reduce microbial carbon use efficiency, and amplify priming effects42,58,59. Consequently, additional carbon inputs are more readily lost via respiration rather than stabilized in longer-lived pools60,61. Thus, this stage essentially represents a competition between hydrothermal forcing–driven increases in carbon inputs and nitrogen-regulated acceleration of turnover, on the one hand, and the capacity of soils to stabilize additional carbon, on the other. When increased inputs are balanced by stabilization capacity, SOC is more likely to show net accumulation; conversely, when nitrogen-driven turnover dominates, even enhanced inputs are unlikely to be retained over the long term46. This net gain state could be partly transient, because under continued rapid warming, the acceleration of turnover may eventually outpace input gains, especially if stabilization capacity becomes limiting.

Overall, the above phased mechanism provides a conceptual basis for explaining the latitudinal dependence of the SOC gain-loss crossover threshold. In cold, energy-limited high-latitude regions (Supplementary Fig. 27), weak warming (ΔTavg < 0.0086 °C/yr) mainly manifests as a partial release of temperature constraints on decomposition, accompanied by relatively moderate changes in ΔGPP. As plant inputs and mineral protection gradually begin to compensate for enhanced decomposition, ΔSOC can shift from net loss to net accumulation even at relatively low warming rates. By contrast, in warmer, more water- and nutrient-limited low-latitude regions (Supplementary Fig. 27), observed ΔTavg more frequently and persistently falls within the moderate and strong warming stages identified here (0.0086–0.0309 and > 0.0309 °C/yr; Fig. 3a). Under these conditions, in the absence of concurrent improvements in hydrological status, warming often maintains an input-constrained, turnover-dominated carbon deficit state. Additional nitrogen is then more likely to reinforce the inhibitory pathway involving N, ΔGPP and ΔSOC than to translate into effective additional carbon inputs. Only when warming is sufficiently strong and is accompanied by adequate moisture and clay-mediated stabilization can these constraints be relaxed, leading to net SOC gains. This is consistent with the observed spatial pattern in which SOC loss-gain crossover thresholds increase monotonically toward lower latitudes.

A key advancement of this study is the empirical identification of a temperature change threshold that governs changes in SOC stocks (segmented response threshold, and SOC gain-loss crossover threshold). To our knowledge, this critical threshold and its potential drivers have not been explicitly examined in global, observation-based studies of SOC-climate feedbacks. Existing global soil carbon assessments primarily rely on two approaches. First, spatial statistical products such as SoilGrids 2.0 provide high-resolution, static maps of SOC stocks but cannot directly reveal their dynamic relationships with climate change10,62. Second, process-based models, including Earth system models and the RothC model, can simulate SOC stock responses under future scenarios. However, their predictions remain sensitive to assumptions about model structure and parameterization and are not constrained by empirically derived thresholds in temperature change63,64. Building on these advances, our study provides an observation-based estimate of the warming-rate threshold at which SOC transitions from continuous loss with warming to a reversal from loss to gain. This reveals that soil-climate feedbacks are not linear but instead exhibit critical inflection points. It moves beyond traditional empirical correlations toward a testable threshold framework, providing new observational constraints for improving SOC-climate models and informing climate-adaptation and carbon-sequestration strategies. Our analytical approach complements controlled incubation experiments, which offer irreplaceable advantages for elucidating specific mechanisms8 (e.g., temperature sensitivity) but struggle to capture the synergistic effects of multiple drivers (climatic, biogiocal, anthropogenic, etc.) in large-scale agricultural ecosystems. By reconstructing multi-decadal SOC stock time series across global Mollisol croplands, we quantitatively characterized the nonlinear relationship between ΔSOC and ΔTavg and precisely identified the SOC gain-loss crossover threshold. More importantly, using SEM, we revealed a systematic succession in the dominant controlling factors across different temperature change stages. This integrated empirical framework, which links thresholds, stages and mechanisms, moves SOC-climate feedback research beyond traditional statistical correlations and unconstrained model extrapolation. It enables systematic identification of real-world driving mechanisms and provides critical theoretical foundations and data support for developing soil carbon-climate models with stronger observational constraints.

However, several limitations and sources of uncertainty should be acknowledged. First, our prediction of SOC reversal zones under the SSP2-4.5 scenario was derived from statistical inference of the empirical relationship between ΔSOC and ΔTavg, implicitly assuming that other drivers (land use, crops, and management practices) remain within their historical ranges. Therefore, these projections should be regarded as first-order estimates of potential net SOC gains driven by warming alone, rather than as fully coupled scenario simulations. Second, our analysis is confined to the 0–20 cm surface soil layer and does not capture SOC stock dynamics in deeper horizons, where slower turnover and stronger physical and mineralogical protection may dampen or delay responses to warming. In addition, the geographic coverage of independent validation remains imperfect in a few key Mollisol cropland regions (e.g., South America/Pampas), which may leave some residual uncertainty in transferability. Third, our analysis is based on observational data and statistical models and therefore primarily reveals correlations between ΔSOC and its potential drivers rather than establishing explicit causal relationships. Finally, the AR variable, which approximates tillage duration, was calculated using land-use data from 1990 to 2020 and therefore does not fully capture pre-1990 tillage history. This limitation may partially confound the estimated influence of the AR variable on ΔSOC. To address these limitations, future research should aim to reconstruct pre-1990 land use and soil management histories by integrating historical literature and long-term statistics. Furthermore, integrating remote sensing data with process-based models such as CENTURY or RothC will help predict SOC stocks across multiple soil depths and provide a means to test the causal mechanisms implied by the identified temperature change rate thresholds.

Methods

Data sources

Global Mollisol region

Mollisol are one of the 12 soil orders in the USDA Soil Taxonomy and are characterized by a black, organic-rich diagnostic topsoil horizon with high base saturation65. These data were obtained from the Web Soil Survey (https://websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov/). The soil map was resampled to a regular 30 m grid, and isolated patches smaller than a single grid cell were removed.

Cropland dataset

Land-cover datasets for 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020 were acquired from the Data Sharing and Service Portal66 (https://data.casearth.cn/). The data have a spatial resolution of 30 m. For this study, only rainfed cropland (ID = 10) and irrigated cropland (ID = 20) were selected to delineate the cropland extent.

We used the spatial extent of Mollisol to clip cropland areas for each period, thereby defining the Mollisol cropland regions. All subsequent analyses were restricted to these Mollisol cropland regions.

SOC stock dataset

In this study, we collected a global SOC stock database comprising 5535 soil samples from the topsoil layer of cropland of Mollisol from different periods (Supplementary Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 1). To ensure depth consistency, we retained only records explicitly reported as topsoil within the 0–20 cm interval (e.g., 0–10, 0–15, 0–20 cm) and excluded samples outside this range. When multiple sublayers within 0–20 cm were available for the same site, we aggregated them to a single 0–20 cm value using thickness-weighted averaging for SOC concentration, thereby ensuring that all samples correspond to a consistent 0–20 cm topsoil definition. This includes a comprehensive dataset of 4345 soil samples from the World Soil Information Service (WoSIS), which were collected between 1986 and 2015. Another set of 113 soil samples was taken from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Discipline Data Center for Ecosystems67 (EDDC) during 2000–2016. These samples were predominantly concentrated in the eastern part of Northeast China, with some being continuously monitored for more than five years. An additional 1077 soil samples were collected from croplands within Northeast China between 2016 and 2024. These samples included specific details such as the latitude and longitude coordinates of each sampling site, SOC content, and the date of soil sample collection. Detailed information on each dataset, including SOC content data standardization and comparability procedures, is provided in the Supplementary Note 2. The SOC stock was calculated using Eq. (1), and the bulk density was computed using Eq. (2), which was derived from 1630 soil profiles and 358,772 soil samples from cropland soils68,69.

There was significant spatial heterogeneity in global Mollisol SOC stock (Supplementary Table 6), with the following average SOC content across various Mollisol regions within croplands: Europe (73.64 MgC/ha) > Asian (52.95 MgC/ha) > North American (52.06 MgC/ha) > South American (40.63 MgC/ha).

Satellite-based remote sensing image datasets

We harnessed the power of the Landsat Thematic Mapper/Operational Land Imager (TM/OLI) image to cover the Mollisol region from January 1986 to December 2024. This entailed a vast dataset of 1,086,202 images accessible via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform (Supplementary Fig. 28). To ensure the quality and accuracy of our analysis, we employed the ‘pixel_qa’ bit mask band for each Landsat TM/OLI image, allowing us to effectively mask out low-quality observations caused by cloud and snow cover. Six optical bands from the Landsat TM/OLI plus the thermal band were selected for analysis (Supplementary Table 7). Variations in the parameters of Landsat TM and OLI sensors result in differing reflectance of the same feature, complicating the temporal analysis of soil properties70. To resolve this issue, we obtained Landsat Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (ETM + ) images to calibrate Landsat TM to the OLI level (Specific step is provided in the Supplementary Note 2). Considering the relatively stable nature of SOC stock, which tends to exhibit minimal changes over ten-year intervals11, we divided the timeframe spanning from 1986 to 2024 into four distinct periods: 1986–1995, 1996–2005, 2006–2015, and 2016–2024. This segmentation allowed us to create composite images of bare soil for each of these four periods. The process for generating these bare soil composite images involved the following steps: we established specific criteria for each pixel, ensuring to meet certain conditions, namely, a normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) of less than 0.35, a normalized burn ratio 2 (NBR2) of less than 0.125, and a bare soil index (BSI) exceeding 0.021 (refs. 38,39.) (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Table 8). In instances where a single observation location contained multiple image elements, we performed an average calculation of these elements. This methodology was implemented to mitigate the potential influence of soil moisture on our prediction results of SOC stock, aligning with established practices71. The bare soil composite images for the four periods are shown in Supplementary Fig. 23.

Climatic datasets

For climatic conditions, we collected total precipitation, minimum and maximum temperature data from the European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) atmospheric reanalysis of the global climate (ERA5, https://www.ecmwf.int/) dataset for the period from 1986 to 2024. In this study, we calculated the annual total precipitation (PR) and annual average temperature (Tavg). The annual mean temperature data were derived by averaging the annual minimum and maximum temperatures. For example, for the period 1986–1995, the annual mean temperature is the average of the annual mean temperatures for those ten years. Consecutive dry days (CDD), consecutive wet days (CWD), coldwave frequency (CWF), and heatwave frequency (HWF) were downloaded from the historical dataset of climate extreme indices72 at a resolution of 0.25° × 0.25° (Supplementary Table 9). Finally, we obtained the PR, Tavg, CDD, CWD, CWF, and HWF for four periods: 1986–1995, 1996–2005, 2006–2015, and 2016–2024.

Topographic datasets

For topographic features, elevation, slope and aspect were selected. The elevation data were obtained from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission-Digital Elevation Model (SRTM-DEM) data with a spatial resolution of 30 m. Slope and aspect data were derived from the elevation data with the SAGA-GIS software.

Anthropogenic activities datasets

For the anthropogenic activity data, N fertilizer application intensity (Nrate), reclamation age (AR), livestock manure (Manure), yield (Yield), farm machinery data (FMD), and crop residue cover were acquired.

We obtained Nrate data for the period from 1986 to 2020 with 5-arc-min spatial resolution73.

The AR was calculated based on land use data obtained for the years 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020. The specific calculation process for the reclamation ages is described as follows. The age of reclamation was categorized into three periods: 1) 10 years (2010–2020), 2) 20 years (2000–2020), and 3) 30 years (1990–2020). The land cover classification for regions that were cropland in 2020 but not cropland in 2010 was designated as the reclamation period for years 10 (ref. 24). Other reclamation periods were identified by this approach. It needs to be emphasized that these land use data were utilized to delineate the cultivated land regions for four distinct periods.

Livestock manure data were obtained from https://www.fao.org/home/en/, where data from 1986 to 2020 for all countries within the Mollisol region were downloaded and processed into raster data for analysis.

Yield data were sourced from 10.1038/s41597-020-0433-7, with a grid resolution of 0.5-degree grid-cell.

FMD data were obtained from https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/machinery-per-agricultural-land?tab=table, where data from 1986 to 2019 for all countries within the Mollisol region were downloaded and processed into raster data for analysis.

Crop residue cover was represented using the minimum normalized difference tillage index (minNDTI), an index designed to minimize the confounding effects of green vegetation independent of moisture62. The index was calculated based on bare soil composite images.

Biological datasets

For the biological data, we obtained the gross primary production (GPP) from 1986 to 2024 using the Landsat GPP CONUS dataset on the GEE platform, with a spatial resolution of 30 × 30 m. In addition, we obtained the annual average NDVI from 1986 to 2024 on the GEE platform.

Soil properties dataset

For the soil properties data, we downloaded the clay content data and nitrogen (N) data with a spatial resolution of 250 m via https://data.isric.org/.

To maintain the consistency of the spatial analysis, all raster data were resampled in ArcGIS 10.8 software employing the nearest-neighbor method to 30 m spatial resolution.

Determination of input variables for SOC stock prediction

The input variables specifically include (Supplementary Table 10): the six raw bands of Landsat TM/OLI (B, G, R, NIR, SWIR1, SWIR2), the normalized difference index (NDI), the ratio index (RI), and the difference index (DI), which are the most sensitive to SOC stock (Supplementary Fig. 29), and the soil-related soil adjusted vegetation index (SAVI) and modified soil adjusted vegetation index (MSAVI) indices74. According to the information provided on the official website of the USGS, the BT band of Landsat TM/OLI was utilized to calculate the soil temperature (land surface temperature, LST), since temperature is typically a key driver for change in SOC content, with elevated temperatures expedite the decomposition of SOC17,75. In addition, land surface wetness (LSW) and the normalized difference moisture index (NDMI) reflect soil moisture status, which can indirectly affect soil carbon stocks by reducing total plant primary productivity under water stress and increasing vegetation mortality76. Land surface brightness (LSB) and brightness index (BI) can reflect the brightness of the soil, which, in general, is usually lower in areas with high SOC content, whereas the reflectance is usually higher in areas with low SOC content (Supplementary Fig. 22). Lower SOC content causes a brighter soil color and this indicates lower light absorption and higher reflectance77. The clay index (CI) reflects the soil texture78, which is considered to be one of the most promising indicators for SOC content79. Consequently, we selected a total of 17 input variables for SOC stock predicting (Supplementary Table 10).

Determination of the factor affecting the spatial distribution of SOC stock and its changes

The factors affecting the spatial distribution of SOC stock include: climatic factors (CDD, CWD, CWF, HWF, PR, and Tavg), anthropogenic factors (AR, FMD, Manure, minNDTI, Nrate, and Yield), biological factors (GPP, and NDVI), soil properties (Clay, and N), and topographic factors (ASP, ELE, and SLO) (Supplementary Table 9).

The factors affecting the changes in SOC stock include: change in climatic factors (ΔCDD, ΔCWD, ΔCWF, ΔHWF, ΔPR, and ΔTavg), change in anthropogenic factors (ΔAR, ΔFMD, ΔManure, ΔminNDTI, ΔNrate, and ΔYield), change in biological factors (ΔGPP, and ΔNDVI), soil properties (Clay, and N), and topographic factors (ASP, ELE, and SLO). In this study, changes in SOC stock, climatic factors, anthropogenic factors, and biological factors are calculated by comparing the periods 1990 and 2020.

To assess the validity and reliability of the environmental factors used to interpret the spatial distribution and temporal change of SOC stock, we applied three complementary multicollinearity diagnostics: (i) computed the Pearson correlation coefficient matrix among environmental factors (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31) to preliminarily identify highly correlated pairs, using |r | ≥ 0.7 as the reference threshold80; (ii) Calculated the variance inflation factors (VIF) (Supplementary Tables 11 and 12), using a threshold of VIF ≤ 5 to indicate the absence of significant multicollinearity, and reported the maximum VIF value81; (iii) Applied the Belsley diagnostic method, reporting the condition index (CI) values (Supplementary Tables 13 and 14). Moderate and severe multicollinearity were defined as CI > 15 and CI > 30, respectively82. Based on the diagnostic results, no significant multicollinearity was detected among environmental factors explaining either the spatial distribution or the temporal change of SOC stock.

Modeling SOC stock dynamics

To link SOC stock observations with time-varying input variables, each soil sample was first assigned to a period according to its sampling year (for example, 1986–1995, 1996–2005, 2006–2015, and 2016–2024). For every soil sample, we then extracted the values of all raster covariates from the input variable datasets corresponding to the same period (Supplementary Fig. 32). Although some positional uncertainty is inevitable, we used accurate geographic coordinates and a 30 m pixel size to ensure a close match between sampling locations and selected pixels.

After calibrating the SOC stock prediction model on all samples from all regions and periods jointly, we sequentially applied the fitted model to the raster covariates for each target period. This procedure yields spatially explicit SOC stock maps through time, including for regions and periods where no soil samples were available, by relying on the learned relationships between SOC stocks and input variables within the observed range of covariate conditions.

Hybrid probability deep learning model

The hybrid probability deep learning model employed in this study integrates the C-means model with the convolutional neural network model (CNN). In the hybrid probability deep learning model, the soil samples were grouped into several clusters using the C-means model (Supplementary Fig. 24). Next, the CNN model was employed to train in different clusters separately. Then, the probability that each image pixel is grouped into different clusters was calculated using RStudio software. Finally, the predicted SOC stock for a pixel is calculated by summing the product of the probability of that pixel in each cluster and the predicted SOC stock of that cluster.

where SOC represents the SOC stock, CNN is the convolutional neural network model, n represents the number of clusters; and C1 represents the probability that a soil sample or image pixel is classified as cluster 1. The C-means clustering associates each data point to the nearest cluster center while maintaining the greatest possible distance between cluster centers83. The algorithm groups soil samples based on the similarity of the input quantities and performs iterative calculations to minimize the value of the objective function (J) (Eq. (4)). The advantage of this algorithm is the introduction of the fuzzy performance index (FPI), modified packet entropy (MPE), and cluster independence index (S) to determine the optimal number of clusters. The FPI index was employed to evaluate the misclassification of soil samples, while the MPE index assesses the differences among samples within the same cluster, with S denotes the differences among different clusters. The optimal number of clusters is obtained when the values of all three indexes are minimized (Supplementary Fig. 33). In this study, the number of clusters ranged from a minimum of 2 to a maximum of 12. In addition, the number of soil samples in each cluster had to be more than 30 to meet the minimum number of sample points required for modeling. Finally, the optimum number of clusters was determined to be five (Supplementary Fig. 33 and Supplementary Table 15).

Here, c represents the number of principal components, n denotes the number of clusters, and m signifies the number of soil samples, uij (1 ≤ i ≤ c, 1 ≤ j ≤ n) indicates the membership of soil sample ci to cluster center xj, while (xj−ci)2 is the squared distance between xj and ci in the eigenvector space.

The CNN algorithm consists of multiple layers, each of which processes the input differently84. The convolutional layer serves as the initial layer of the CNN, which extracts local features of the input using a filter (Supplementary Fig. 34), and then passes these features through a ReLU function that handles the nonlinear relationship between these features and the SOC stock85. In the subsequent layers of the CNN, convolutional layers are generally alternated with pooling layers. This structure reduces the spatial dimensionality of local features, enhancing computational efficiency86. Finally, the fully connected layer performs SOC stock prediction based on the features extracted in the previous layer. It is worth noting that different neighborhood sizes play different roles in SOC stock prediction results when the input is processed in the input layer87. Therefore, to validate the effect of spatial neighborhood, we tested different neighborhood sizes. The results of SOC stock prediction for various neighborhood sizes are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 35, with a final neighborhood size of 9*9 chosen for SOC stock prediction.

In this study, we developed a CNN model using the TensorFlow framework, comprising three convolutional layers for feature extraction, three max pooling layers for spatial dimensionality reduction, and three fully connected layers for final prediction (Supplementary Table 16). A 3D convolutional layer with 3*3*3 pixel filters and a max pooling layer with 2*2 pixel filters were employed. To prevent overfitting and enhance network generalization, we utilized a dropout layer85 and monitored model performance on the validation dataset during training. In practice, we first carried out preliminary experiments with different combinations of learning rate, number of training epochs and dropout rate and selected the configuration that minimized the validation RMSE. An Adam optimizer was employed to determine the optimal parameters of the model. The learning rate, stochastic gradient descent momentum, number of training sessions, and dropout rate were finally established at 1e-5, 0.5, 50 and 0.5, respectively. All hyperparameter choices were made based only on the calibration and validation dataset, while the independent test dataset was reserved for final evaluation.

To validate the benefits of the proposed hybrid probability model, a global CNN and a random forest (RF) model constructed directly using the entire soil samples were established for comparison with the hybrid probability model (Supplementary Fig. 3). The process of clustering and calculating the probability of dividing into different clusters using the C-means model is implemented in GEE platform, and the SOC stock prediction process using the RF and CNN model were conducted in Python software.

Model evaluation and uncertainty analysis

The soil data, comprising 5535 samples in total, were divided into calibration (2767 samples), validation (1384 samples), and test (1384 samples) datasets at a 2:1:1 ratio. The validation dataset is used to optimize model parameters. The calibration model’s accuracy in predicting SOC stock was assessed using RMSE (Eq. (5)), R2 (Eq. (6)), and RPIQ (Eq. (7)) using the test dataset88. In this study, we used the standard deviation (SD) of the 10-fold cross-validation results to assess the uncertainty of the predictive model in SOC stock mapping. The SD metric is critical for evaluating the robustness of our model and the confidence in our predictions89. Firstly, we generated spatial distribution maps of SOC stock for each period using cross-validation, we then averaged over the 10 cross-validation iterations to produce the final spatiotemporal distribution maps of SOC stock. Finally, the prediction uncertainty was quantified by calculating the SD of the 10 SOC stock spatial distribution maps for each period. A lower uncertainty indicates higher confidence in the predictions90.

SOC stock dataset accuracy validation

To verify the spatial accuracy of the SOC stock dataset, we obtained SoilGrids250m data9, combined it with lab-measured sample points, and compared them for accuracy of fit and spatial resolution. In addition, we compiled and recalculated 63 soil samples to independently validate the accuracy of the predicted spatial distribution of SOC stocks (Supplementary Fig. 36a). These samples were obtained from the Russian Soil Register and represent SOC stocks in the top 20 cm of soil across Russia’s Mollisol regions.

To verify the SOC stock dataset trend accuracy from 1990–2020, we obtained literature from Web of Science, Science direct, Google Scholar, and Scopus using keywords Organic carbon, Organic matter, Mollisol, and Chernozem. We examined the primary texts and supplementary materials of these literatures to locate SOC stock databases. Raw data were obtained directly from tables when available, or alternatively extracted by digitizing graphs using Origin (version 2021b; OriginLab Corporation)91. After collecting data related to SOC stock, a meta-analysis was performed on these data, a process that mainly involves data standardization. Due to variations in SOC metrics reported across the literature (e.g., depth intervals and measurement units), we employed a mass-preserving profile function for depth standardization. This method uniformly converts all records to SOC stocks within the 0–20 cm depth range (MgC/ha)24,92, ensuring comparability between existing literature SOC stocks and those predicted in this study. In addition, we simulated annual SOC stocks from 1990 onward using the RothC model, which represents ecosystem carbon cycling through input, decomposition, transformation, and output processes. The rates and fluxes of these processes are influenced by environmental conditions, soil properties, vegetation types, land use, and management practices, making RothC well suited for characterizing SOC stock changes93.

Statistical analysis

Drivers of spatial and temporal variation in SOC stocks

To eliminate the effects of spatial autocorrelation, we calculated the minimum distances to avoid spatial autocorrelation based on the global Moran’s I indices among different environmental factors (Supplementary Table 17). Ultimately, we re-aggregated the data into 50 km × 50 km grids94, generating 5253 cells for analyzing the drivers of spatial and temporal variation in SOC stocks.

We used BRT analysis to quantify the relative contributions of environmental factors, including climatic, anthropogenic, biological, soil property, and topographic factors, to the spatial variation in SOC stocks. This approach has been widely applied in ecological research to explore complex relationships between predictors and response variables95,96. BRT is a machine learning ensemble method that iteratively fits and combines multiple regression trees to enhance predictive performance97. To minimize random variation, we performed 10 iterations of the BRT model. Partial dependence plots (PDP) derived from BRT analysis were used to illustrate the marginal effects of individual environmental factors on SOC stocks, isolating the influence of each variable from others. To identify, for each environmental factor, the value at which SOC stock is most sensitive, we located the point on its PDP where the slope was maximal and defined this as the value exerting the greatest influence on SOC stocks.

We quantified ΔSOC in croplands across the global Mollisol region by calculating the difference in SOC stocks between 1990 and 2020 at the pixel level. Multiple linear regression models were constructed to examine the response of ΔSOC to changes in environmental factors, with ΔSOC as the dependent variable and topography, soil properties, changes in climatic, changes in anthropogenic, and changes in biological factors as predictors. All predictor variables were standardized prior to modeling to eliminate the influence of differing units and magnitudes. Statistical significance was evaluated at the 95% confidence level. In addition, GAM was used to test whether a nonlinear relationship exists between ΔSOC and ΔTavg. Using the first- and second-order derivatives of the smoothing function, together with a residual minimization criterion, we identified segmented response thresholds. A 95% confidence interval for these thresholds was estimated using 1000 bootstrap iterations (Supplementary Note 3).

SEM was used to analyze the direct and indirect relationships between ΔSOC and changes in environmental factors. Building on previous studies98–100, we specified an a priori SEM that hypothesized causal relationships among these factors (Supplementary Fig. 37). Prior to modeling, we assessed correlations between ΔSOC and changes in environmental factors to identify key influencing factors. Model fit was evaluated using the root mean square error of approximation (RMEA; acceptable fit: 0.00 ≤ RMSEA ≤ 0.05), chi-squared test (X2), and probability level (P; acceptable fit: P > 0.05)101.

Data analysis and visualization were conducted using the following R packages: caret102, gbm103, piecewiseSEM, lme4 (ref. 104) and ggplot2 (ref. 105.).

Potential areas for reversal of ΔSOC stocks in 2050

To identify areas with a potential reversal in ΔSOC stock by 2050, we first obtained projections of Tavg for 2020–2050 under the SSP2-4.5 scenario from seven CMIP6 models (AWI-CM-1-1-MR, BCC-CSM2-MR, CMCC-ESM2, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0, NorESM2-MM and TaiESM1) via the ESGF data portal (https://esgf.github.io/nodes.html). To reduce inter-model uncertainty, we calculated the multi-model ΔTavg over 2020–2050 for each grid cell. We then applied the empirically derived thresholds linking ΔSOC to ΔTavg, including latitude-specific SOC gain-loss crossover thresholds estimated in 5° latitudinal bands, to statistically extrapolate the spatial distribution of potential SOC stock reversals in global Mollisol croplands by 2050 under SSP2-4.5.

To evaluate the robustness of the identified segmented response threshold, SOC gain-loss crossover threshold, and potential ΔSOC reversal areas, we excluded regions with high prediction uncertainty and recalculated these thresholds and reversal areas. We then compared the two sets of results. Detailed findings are presented in Supplementary Note 4.
