Effective coverage of maternal and neonatal healthcare services in low-and middle-income countries: a scoping review
Authors: Ayelign Mengesha Kassie, Elizabeth Eakin, Aklilu Endalamaw, Anteneh Zewdie, Eskinder Wolka, Yibeltal Assefa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is no consistent operationalization of effective coverage (EC) across studies. Therefore, this scoping review synthesized evidence on the definitions and measurement approaches, outcomes reported, and the factors that are associated with variations in quality-adjusted EC estimates of maternal and neonatal healthcare services in low- and middle-income countries.MethodsArticle search was conducted using PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and other databases. Then, title, abstract, and full text screenings for inclusion were performed by two authors independently and disagreements were resolved through discussion. In case of duplication, the full-text published articles were retained, and the results are presented using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis extension for Scoping Reviews checklist as a guide.ResultsOverall, 36 articles were included in this scoping review. In most articles, EC has been defined from the perspectives of people in need utilizing healthcare services in well-equipped health facilities and from actual receipt of quality services. In addition, usage, instead of need, has been used as a domain in estimating EC in some instances. Effective coverage ranged from 0% for different services including for post-partum care, to 84% for antenatal care. Moreover, different socio-demographic factors including wealth index, education, and residence are found to have an association with variations in EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services with the wealthiest, most educated, and those living in urban areas having higher estimates. From the supply side, numerous factors, particularly health facility capacity-related constraints, have been reported to be associated with low EC of those services.ConclusionsThe variability in the definition and measurement approaches of EC across studies emphasizes the need for standardization for better comparison in future research. We recommend that quality-adjusted EC be defined in two ways: intervention-specific service delivery EC and general service delivery EC. In addition, disparities in EC are observed among women with different socioeconomic status including educational level and wealth index. Organizational capacity constraints and other health system and societal factors also contributed to variations in EC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12085-7.Keywords: Antenatal care, Delivery care, Effective coverage, Quality-adjusted coverage, Postnatal care

Background

Maternal and infant mortality rates are still high around the world, particularly in resource-limited settings [1, 2]. For instance, about 810 women died every day from preventable causes related to pregnancy and childbirth in 2017 and around 94% of all maternal deaths were recorded in low-resource settings, primarily in Sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia [3]. Approximately 75% of all maternal deaths are associated with preventable and treatable causes including severe bleeding, infections, pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, unsafe abortion, delivery complications, and other causes [3, 4].

These causes of mortality could have been prevented by providing good quality healthcare services [4]. Cognizant of the highlighted gaps, and subsequent national and global initiatives, significant improvements in maternal and child healthcare service coverage have been reported around the world [5]. For example, first-trimester antenatal visits for pregnancy care increased by 43% between 1990 and 2013, to 58.6%, at the global level [6]. Four or more antenatal care visit coverage has also been reported ranging from 51 to 97% in developing and developed countries respectively [7]. In addition, the proportion of women who received any antenatal care has increased from 64 to 83% between 1990 and 2014 in developing countries [8]. Furthermore, institutional delivery and skilled birth attendance coverage has reached 80% in several low- and middle-income countries in 2018 [9].

Nevertheless, there has been an increasing recognition that a high coverage rate by itself may not be sufficient to lower mortality. This is because, while achieving a high health facility visit coverage is commendable, it does not guarantee positive outcomes and could be potentially misleading as it does not show whether interventions are given according to standards and guidelines, or not [10]. Thus, improving the quality of care and working towards eliminating preventable causes of maternal and neonatal deaths has become paramount [11, 12]. As a result, a new approach called "EC" has been introduced as a measure of the quality of healthcare services provided [13]. In estimating EC, the dimension of quality is added to the measurement of intervention coverage to better capture the potential health benefits of an intervention/utilization/contact coverage [14, 15].

A combination of three terms; need, use, and quality are critical in EC. Need refers to the target population, and use is for the utilization of services. Thus, when the quality of care is multiplied by the utilization of services and then divided by the population in need, it provides EC [15, 16]. EC has been proposed to be seen in three different ways recently: quality-adjusted coverage, user-adherence-adjusted coverage, and outcome-adjusted coverage. However, the usage of quality-adjusted coverage is recommended for routine maternal and neonatal healthcare services as estimating outcome-adjusted coverage using maternal and neonatal mortality can pose a challenge in attributing the reasons of mortality to the healthcare system and be influenced by different external factors to the healthcare system [17].

Furthermore, the EC rates in key maternal and neonatal health services are low and substantially different across the world. For instance, a study in Cambodia has indicated that the EC for antenatal and facility delivery care was 56.4%, in 2014 [18]. On the other hand, a very low EC, 30.1%, is reported for maternal healthcare services (average of antenatal and delivery care) in Rwanda in 2015 [19]. Similarly, a 35% EC has been reported for antenatal care (ANC) despite having a very high, 92%, 4+ANC visit coverage rate in Pakistan, showing how coverage is compromised from the quality-of-care perspective [20]. Moreover, a study conducted in six countries: Nepal, Bangladesh, Haiti, Malawi, Senegal, and Tanzania, has reported that EC of facility delivery varies significantly ranging from 24% in Haiti to 66% in Malawi indicating the need for considering disparities across countries in addressing maternal and neonatal healthcare EC inequity [21].

Significant within-country inequalities in EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services have also been reported in several studies [22–24]. Despite the existence of such substantial variations in EC of those services across low- and middle-income countries, evidence on the factors that are associated with the variations is not well documented especially in resource-limited settings [25, 26]. In addition, the definition of quality-adjusted EC and its measurement approaches in antenatal, delivery, and postnatal care services are not consistent across studies making interpretation of the results and comparisons difficult. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to synthesize literature and learn how EC is defined and measured from quality adjustment perspectives, assess what outcomes have been reported, identify the factors that are associated with variations in the EC rates of maternal and neonatal healthcare services across low- and middle-income countries, and make recommendations towards enhancing EC.

Methods

Protocol development and research questions

Firstly, databases such as the Joanna Briggs Institute and Open Science Framework were searched to determine the existence of scoping review studies or ongoing projects related to the topic. After confirming that no ongoing projects were found, a scoping review protocol was developed following The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist. The Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework was employed to assess the research question's appropriateness and make amendments [27]. The research questions that guided this scoping review are: 1) How is EC defined in antenatal, delivery, and PNC services?; 2) How are EC of antenatal, delivery, and PNC services measured?; 3) What are the EC rates of reported maternal and neonatal healthcare services in low- and middle-income countries?; 4) Which countries are more successful in EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services among the low- and middle-income countries category? and 5) What factors can explain variations in EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services from a quality perspective among the low- and middle-income countries category?

Search strategy

PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, and Google Scholar were explored to identify relevant literature. We also examined the reference lists of pertinent studies for additional articles. The search was conducted from October 15 to November 20, 2023. Duplicates were automatically removed using the Covidence online data extraction tool, with any remaining duplicates eliminated manually. The search strategy applied to all major databases included: ("Effective coverage" OR "Quality-adjusted coverage" OR "Quality adjusted coverage" OR "Quality adjusted effective coverage" OR "Quality-adjusted effective coverage") AND ("Antenatal care" OR "Skilled delivery care" OR "Labor and delivery care" OR "Facility delivery" OR "Institutional delivery" OR "Intrapartum care" OR "Postnatal care" OR "Postpartum care" OR "Neonatal care" OR "Newborn care" OR "Perinatal care" OR "Obstetric care"). The complete search strategy is provided in the supplementary file (see Additional file 1).

Eligibility criteria and screening

The scoping review included all published literature reporting quality-adjusted EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services, with no restrictions on publication date. Only articles published in English were considered due to feasibility issues. Conference proceedings, oral and poster presentations, abstracts, correspondence articles, comments to the editor, brief communications, and systematic and literature review articles were excluded, as a detailed exploration of full-text results is necessary for comprehensive review and data extraction. All article citations identified through database and manual searches were imported into the Covidence online tool, where each citation was critically examined by two independent reviewers through title and abstract reviews. Studies that passed this initial screening were further assessed by reading the full texts. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion, and in cases of duplication, only the full-text published articles were retained.

Data extraction, analysis and presentation

The data extraction platform was developed on an Excel sheet, organized by key themes, including authors' names, publication year, study design, study topic, WHO geographic category/region, World Bank income classification, reported EC rates, and factors associated with variations or low EC in maternal and neonatal healthcare services across low- and middle-income countries. Modifications to the data extraction sheet were made after piloting it with five randomly selected papers, and the template was then customized into the Covidence online data extraction tool format 2.0. The principal investigator extracted all necessary data from each article using the customized format in Covidence, followed by a second review after completion of extraction. A scoping review of the literature and narrative data synthesis were conducted to present findings in text, figure, and table formats, guided by the PRISMA-ScR checklist.

Results

Search results

We identified a total of 360 articles: 84 from Embase, 65 from Web of Science, 64 from Scopus, 63 from PubMed, 60 from Google Scholar, 20 from CINAHL, and 4 from citation searching. After removing 214 duplicates using the Covidence online tool and 5 additional duplicates manually, 141 studies were screened, leading to the exclusion of 74 articles based on title and abstract reviews. Then, 67 full-text studies were assessed for eligibility resulting in further exclusion of 31 articles. Finaly, 36 studies were included in the scoping review. Definition, measurement approach, and reported EC rates

Effective coverage of ANC services

Effective coverage of ANC services has been defined in different ways across studies. Some of the articles reported their findings in terms of EC of single interventions and/or separately for different interventions. For instance, a study conducted by Baker U, et al. has indicated that EC of screening tests was 15% for syphilis, 65% for HIV, and 51% for anaemia in Tanzania; and, in Uganda, EC of the screening tests was estimated at 3% for syphilis, 37% for HIV, and 19% for anaemia. In this study, EC of the ANC services for syphilis, and HIV was conditional on mothers having attended ANC in a health facility where the screening tests are available and who reported having had the test. In the case of anaemia, no specific indicator was available and mothers’ reports of having blood drawn for any test were considered as a proxy indicator for the outcome [31]. However, in most of the articles, EC has been estimated from composite ANC services. An example of this is the study conducted by Lindberg C, et al. in which blood pressure and weight measurements, urine and blood samples taken at least once, tetanus vaccination, and receipt of information about pregnancy danger signs from a health worker were considered as indicators of ANC visits service quality. Then, the authors used full coverage of those six listed ANC quality indicators as a measure of effective ANC coverage. Accordingly, the EC of ANC was estimated at 38% in Eastern Uganda [41] (Supplementary Table 1).

Effective coverage of delivery care services

Like ANC services, EC for delivery care is defined and measured in diverse ways. In some of the studies, it has been reported from structural readiness/quality adjustment perspectives and in others, from the process of care/quality adjustment perspectives or a combination of both structural and process of care quality perspectives. In addition, no consistent measurement tools have been utilized across studies. Keeping in mind these variations, EC of health facility delivery care has been reported ranging from 0% in Tanzania (where EC is assessed based on a combination of various items with 90% completion of required elements) [47] to 93.3% in Mexico (where EC is measured by the proportion of births occurring in a hospital attended by a skilled birth attendant) [56] (Supplementary Table 2).

Effective coverage of PNC services

There was no similarity in the definition and measurement methods of EC of PNC services across studies as it was found for ANC and delivery care. Regarding the outcomes, EC of PNC for mothers was reported ranging from 0% in Nigeria, Ethiopia, and India, each, [28] to 3.6% in Myanmar [42] (Supplementary Table 3). Effective coverage has been presented in different ways for newborns as well. Particularly, locally tested models of care that can enhance EC of Kangaroo mother care have been developed in India and Ethiopia. Accordingly, significant outcomes have been reported. For instance, in India, an EC rate of 52.7% at discharge and 64.8%7 days post-discharge were reported after implementing a program to enhance the EC of Kangaroo mother care for newborns weighing < 2000 g [50] (Supplementary Table 4). Moreover, EC was reported for two or more maternal and neonatal health care service domains combined in several of the included articles. In addition, with methods of measurement and definitions being variable across studies as for the other domains discussed, EC of maternal and neonatal health care services has been reported ranging from 0.1% in Myanmar (EC for ANC, Delivery, and PNC combined) [42] to 76% in Uganda (EC of PNC for mothers and newborns combined) [41] (Supplementary Table 5).

Performance of countries on EC of ANC services

Country performance on EC of ANC services was assessed using Hodgins et al.'s study, which analysed Demographic and Health Survey data from 41 countries. In this study, the authors proposed adopting the simple average of a set of available indicators for receipt of specific services as a summary measure of antenatal program performance (EC) at the population level. The EC estimated using the simple average score of different interventions in this article was found to be justifiable for comparison purposes and identify positive deviant countries in their performance for two main reasons: 1) usage of the same assessment tool across countries and 2) relative closeness of surveys in period across the included articles [30] (Table 3).

Factors associated with variations in EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services

Several factors have been cited as having an association with high and low EC in different maternal and neonatal healthcare services. These factors are presented using two themes: individual, household, and community level factors and health system and societal level factors.

Individual, household, and community factors

Socioeconomic status is one of the most influential determinants of EC. However, the effect size varies across studies. For instance, a study conducted across 39 low-income and middle-income countries has revealed that the wealthiest group reported greater coverage compared with the least wealthy groups. The study also noted that there was a difference of 6.9% and a ratio of 1.11 in the upper-middle income, a difference of 45.6% and a ratio of 3.41 in the lower-middle-income and a difference of 31% and a ratio of 3.38 in the low-income country categories in the effective ANC service coverage between the wealthiest and the least wealthy groups of pregnant women [24]. Education has also been revealed as a main reason for differences in EC [18, 20, 24, 51]. For example, in Pakistan, the odds of women having formal education was 4.8 times higher to be effectively covered with ANC services than those who have no formal education [20]. In addition, in the study conducted across 39 low-income and middle-income countries, the most educated group reported greater ANC EC compared with the least educated group [24]. Parity [20, 51], age at delivery, marital status, women’s occupation, urban–rural [51], and region of residence have also been reported to be associated with variations in EC of ANC services among pregnant women across some literature [36, 51]. In Indonesia, older, married, white-collar, and urban-dwelling women were more likely to have higher EC of ANC services compared to their counter parts. The study further added that EC was variable among women living in different regions of the country [51].

Health system and societal level factors

Different factors have also been reported to be associated with variations in EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services at the health system and societal level factor’s theme. For instance, facility readiness constraints have been found as important reasons for low EC in some studies. In this case, a study that was conducted in Uganda in two districts has reported that human resource shortage was the biggest bottleneck for EC of HIV counselling and testing services and for intermittent presumptive treatment for malaria. In addition, the practice of the service providers was reported to be another bottleneck. The authors justified this by adding that pregnant women were not consistently offered drugs during the ANC visits despite the drugs being available at the health facility. Another bottleneck was the unavailability of test kits for HIV and syphilis screening services [40]. In Tanzania, an estimated EC of syphilis screening in one district (Tandahimba) was found to be 12% despite near universal accessibility coverage with health facility readiness being the largest bottleneck. It further added that in another district (Newala), EC was 20% despite 100% accessibility coverage and clinical practice was the largest bottleneck. In addition, EC of pre-eclampsia screening in Tandahimba was reported at 22%, with health facility readiness being the largest bottleneck, and in Newala, EC was 28%, with access being the largest bottleneck [54].

On the other hand, the introduction of a national health insurance program was found to be associated with an adds of 1.66 times increment in EC of ANC services in Indonesia [51]. Furthermore, one study assessed the impact of results-based financing aimed at enhancing both access to and the quality of facility-based obstetric care for women and newborns in Malawi. The intervention involved implementing a combination of performance-based payments to facilities and district health management teams and conditional cash transfers to pregnant women within specific catchment areas. In the end, the findings demonstrated a notable 7.1% net increase in the EC of obstetric care following the implementation of this intervention [38]. Increment in EC of kangaroo mother care has also been reported after implementation-based research works. For instance, a model was co-created with mothers and health-care partners employing human-centered design over several cycles of deployment, learning, and data-driven modification and outfitting high delivery load facilities with a kangaroo mother care Lounge to assure mothers' comfort, respectful care, and high-quality care for extended periods in Uttar Pradesh, India. Accordingly, 52.7% and 64.8% EC of kangaroo mother care were achieved at discharge and 7 days later, respectively [50] (Supplementary Table 6).

Conceptual framework for the multilevel factors in effective coverage

The factors influencing EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services are complex and operate across multiple levels, from individual to societal and policy factors. Thus, utilizing the socio-ecological model is essential to identify these factors for designing EC enhancement programs. For instance, individual factors such as inadequate knowledge, unfavourable attitudes, and low income can negatively impact women’s health-seeking behaviour. Household decision-making dynamics, as well as community-level misconceptions, also play significant roles in influencing service utilization [57, 58]. At the health facility level, capacity constraints like shortages of human resources, equipment, diagnostics, and medicines can affect the quality of care thereby reducing EC [31, 40, 54]. However, unlike health facility capacity-related factors, socio-demographic factors can affect EC by reducing utilization coverage rates and also contributing to low-quality care services once women arrive at health facilities for several reasons. For instance, Fink et al.'s study found that, even though women visited the same health facilities, wealthier women received better quality care than their counterparts. The authors suggest several reasons for this disparity. One possibility is that facilities might charge separately for components of care, such as consultations, diagnostic tests, and consumables, which may only be affordable for wealthier women. Another possible reason is that wealthier women might choose to receive a larger package of services, as they may be more informed about the different ANC procedures [22] (Fig. 2).

Discussion

There is a huge variability in how the quality-adjusted EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services is measured. The predominant approach in most articles involves employing composite measures to gauge the EC of these services [18–24, 28–30, 33, 35–39, 41–47, 49, 51, 52, 55, 56]. However, some studies lack clear explanations of their EC measurement methodologies, potentially undermining the reliability of their findings. For instance, Lozano et al. measured EC of skilled birth attendance solely by the number of hospital births attended by skilled professionals, without considering the actual service quality [56]. Such ambiguous and insufficient measures may hinder the credibility of reported EC estimates and limit their usefulness in informing policy decisions. Regarding the formula, most studies estimate EC using the population in need, but some focus only on those who use healthcare services. For example, Venkateswaran et al.’s study in Palestine measured EC based on the proportion of women receiving recommended services among health facility visitors, excluding those who did not visit [48].

Effective coverage has been estimated not only from the actual provision of care but also from the structural or health facility readiness perspectives (contact coverage adjusted to the process of care quality vs. structural quality). For instance, a study in Bangladesh linked Demographic and Health Survey data with Service Provision Assessment data to combine contact coverage with service readiness, creating an input-adjusted coverage measure. Service readiness was evaluated by the availability of nutrition-specific inputs such as equipment, diagnostics, medications, and trained staff [52]. Given such discrepancies in the definition and measurement of EC, making comparisons across studies was not practical. However, multinational studies provided uniform measurement, allowing for justifiable country comparisons. Our analysis of one such study found Rwanda, Burkina Faso, and Malawi (low-income), Honduras, Nepal, and Ghana (lower-middle income), and the Dominican Republic (upper-middle income) to be high performers in ANC EC within their category. Conversely, Ethiopia and the Democratic Republic of Congo (low-income), Pakistan and Nigeria (lower-middle income), and Namibia (upper-middle income) ranked among the lowest performers [30].

Moreover, only few studies have identified socio-demographic factors explaining variations in EC. Wealth and education are the prominent variables that are found to be a source of disparity in EC with the wealthiest and educated groups having higher EC of ANC services [18, 23, 43, 47, 51, 52]. The disparity may be due to wealthier women being able to afford healthcare costs, including transportation. A systematic review supports this, showing that poor women in many low-income African countries lack access to maternal healthcare due to transportation expenses [60]. In addition, the most educated women are more likely to have better-paying jobs and afford healthcare service-related expenditures than those who have no education [61]. Educated women may also better understand the importance of maternal and neonatal healthcare. For instance, a study in Ghana found that women with tertiary education were 10 times more likely to use 4+ ANC services than those without education [62]. These findings are supported by other studies across the literature [63–65].

Other socio-demographic factors including parity [22, 36], age at delivery, marital status, women’s occupation, and urban–rural residence [36] have also been reported to be associated with variations in EC of those services. This could be due to various reasons, including exposure to health education and awareness programs. For example, women living in urban areas are more likely to have better access to mass media than rural women. In this context, research has found that exposure to radio and television is positively associated with ANC service usage among women in Sub-Saharan Africa [66]. Concerning age and parity, it is obvious that women who had more than one child and those who are older are more likely to have better knowledge from their past experiences and a positive attitude toward attending maternal and neonatal healthcare [67]. In addition, married women are more likely to receive financial and emotional support from their spouses which increases the likelihood of timely healthcare access [68]. For instance, a study in Indonesia has indicated that married women had about 2 times higher odds of completing all recommended ANC visits than their counterparts [69]. Similarly, a systematic review reported that women whose husbands had positive attitudes toward ANC were more likely to utilize ANC services compared to women whose husbands had negative attitudes [70].

On the supply side, factors such as lack of access and organizational capacity constraints are linked to low EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services. A pertinent example is the study conducted by Baker U, et al. in Tanzania and Uganda, which found that shortages of test kits were associated with lower EC estimates for ANC services. The study noted that these shortages were linked to procurement inadequacies and a lack of coordination among various stakeholders [31]. A similar study identified shortages of healthcare workers and their incompetency, alongside a lack of equipment and commodities, as major bottlenecks for low ANC EC [40]. This is concerning as antenatal screening is critical for early detection and treatment of health issues and prevent complications for both mother and foetus. For example, undiagnosed anaemia can lead to miscarriages, foetal growth restrictions, stillbirths, preterm births, and maternal mortality [71].

On the other hand, interventional programs have resulted in substantial increases in EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services. For example, three studies, 2 from India and 1 from Ethiopia, reported significant improvements in EC of kangaroo mother care [34, 50, 53]. These findings provide an important clue to consider the positive deviance approach for maternal and neonatal healthcare service quality and EC enhancement programs [72, 73]. We propose the question of what factors can explain high EC of antenatal, skilled delivery, and postnatal care in low- and middle-income countries as a research priority. By utilizing the positive deviance and/or realist approach, along with national health survey data and document reviews, the health system, societal and other contextual factors that make high performers be successful in achieving high EC in those services can be identified. This offers significant potential for low-performing countries to learn from high performers and develop their own evidence-based strategies for EC enhancement.

Strength and limitations

This scoping review encompasses a wide range of articles with varying methodologies and approaches for measuring EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services, which makes direct comparisons and synthesis of findings challenging. In addition, careful interpretation of the results is needed, as no quality appraisal was performed to assess the rigor of included studies. Limiting the review to English-language publications may also exclude valuable studies in other languages, while the exclusion of conference proceedings, abstracts, and non-peer-reviewed literature could further reduce its comprehensiveness. Despite these limitations, the review makes significant contributions by addressing broader research questions related to the EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services, emphasizing the need for standardized EC measurement approaches, and classifying countries by EC scores to identify high-, medium-, and low-performing nations within income categories for comparative learning. This approach provides a foundation for future research and policy efforts aimed at improving the quality and accessibility of maternal and neonatal healthcare worldwide.

Conclusion

We found that quality-adjusted EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services is very low in several low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia. The inconsistent definitions and methods used to measure EC across studies made making comparisons complicated. There is also an inadequate explanation of the quality of care considered in estimating EC in several studies. To address this, we propose defining quality-adjusted EC in two distinct ways: intervention-specific service delivery EC and general service delivery EC. This distinction clarifies how EC is adjusted based on the actual quality of care provided (process quality). Standardizing these definitions can enhance the rigor and utility of EC estimates in healthcare decision-making, resource allocation, and policy formulation. Furthermore, several socio-demographic factors, including wealth index, education and residence have been found to be associated with disparities in EC of maternal and neonatal healthcare services. In addition, health facility readiness limitations, are reported to contribute to low EC. However, several critical health system factors, including the role of leadership, technology, information sharing, and communication systems, remain underexplored. Therefore, more research is needed to address unexplored societal and health system-related factors that can explain variations in EC across different groups and to identify why some countries are more successful in achieving high EC and draw lessons.
