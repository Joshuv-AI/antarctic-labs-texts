Advancing General-Purpose Reasoning Models with Modular Gradient Surgery
Min Cai, Yu Liang, Longzheng Wang, Yan Wang, Yueyang Zhang, Long Xia, Zhiyuan Sun, Xi Ye, Daiting Shi

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) has played a central role in recent advances in large reasoning models (LRMs), yielding strong gains in verifiable and open-ended reasoning. However, training a single general-purpose LRM across diverse domains remains challenging due to pronounced domain heterogeneity. Through a systematic study of two widely used strategies, Sequential RL and Mixed RL, we find that both incur substantial cross-domain interference at the behavioral and gradient levels, resulting in limited overall gains. To address these challenges, we introduce M odular G radient S urgery ( MGS ), which resolves gradient conflicts at the module level within the transformer. When applied to Llama and Qwen models, MGS achieves average improvements of 4.3 (16.6%) and 4.5 (11.1%) points, respectively, over standard multi-task RL across three representative domains (math, general chat, and instruction following). Further analysis demonstrates that MGS remains effective under prolonged training. Overall, our study clarifies the sources of interference in multi-domain RL and presents an effective solution for training general-purpose LRMs.

Introduction

Building large reasoning models (LRMs) with long chain-of-thought capabilities has become a central goal in recent language model development
(
34
;
25
;
73
)
. Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a key driver of this progress. In particular, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has demonstrated substantial gains in domains with objective correctness signals, such as mathematics
(
67
;
39
)
and code
(
56
;
76
)
, where RL not only improves final accuracy but also incentivizes advanced reasoning such as branching and reflection
(
44
;
69
;
64
)
. Motivated by these successes, recent efforts have extended RL beyond strictly verifiable settings, applying RL to more general tasks such as instruction following
(
22
;
6
)
and open-ended chat
(
5
;
28
)
using model-based rewards.

Despite this progress, training a single general-purpose reasoning model that performs well across diverse domains remains challenging. Building such a model requires combining heterogeneous rewards and domains (e.g., rule-based rewards for math, preference-based rewards for chat). Practitioners consistently observe difficulties when mixing these objectives: learning on one domain often degrades performance on another, transfer across domains is weak, and naively mixing rewards can lead to unstable or brittle training dynamics
(
28
;
5
;
55
)
.

In this work, we systematically study how to perform RL on diverse domains to build broadly capable reasoning models. As illustrated in Figure
1
, we begin by analyzing two natural and widely used strategies: (1)
Sequential
RL, where a model is trained on different domains one after another; and (2)
Mixed
(multi-task) RL, where data from multiple domains are combined within each training batch. Through controlled experiments on representative domains—math reasoning, general chat, and instruction following—we show that both strategies suffer from limitations in multi-domain settings.
Sequential
RL exhibits mode interference, where optimizing strongly for one domain erases or constrains behaviors needed for others, while
Mixed
RL suffers from gradient conflicts between domains. As a result, both approaches exhibit a substantial gap compared to single-domain experts, as shown in Figure
1
.

To address these limitations, we propose
M
odular
G
radient
S
urgery (
MGS
), an effective method for mitigating cross-domain interference in
Mixed
RL training. As pretrained transformers are highly modular, with different components specializing in distinct functionalities, and gradient conflicts are often localized to specific modules rather than uniformly distributed across the model. MGS resolves conflicting gradients at the module level using gradient surgery
(
68
)
, allowing compatible updates to be preserved while selectively mitigating destructive interference.

Empirically, we show that MGS consistently improves
Mixed
RL training across model families and task combinations. Applied to Llama and Qwen models over
Math
,
Chat
, and Instruction-Following (
IF
) domains, MGS substantially expands the capability frontier, achieving stronger and more balanced performance than
Sequential
training, Naïve Mixing, and Global Gradient Surgery. Further analysis demonstrates that MGS scales with extended training, yielding an additional 3% relative improvement on the Math benchmark. Furthermore, MGS generalizes well to additional tasks, achieving a 19.4% relative average improvement on
Llama-3.1-8B
. These gains are achieved with negligible overhead with high-performance parallel training frameworks like FSDP.

Overall, our findings clarify why multi-domain RL post-training remains difficult in practice and provide a principled approach for advancing general-purpose reasoning models. To summarize, our contributions include:
(1) A systematic study of multi-domain RL for reasoning, revealing the limitations of
Sequential
and
Mixed
training strategies;
(2) A new approach, Modular Gradient Surgery, which resolves cross-domain interference at modular levels; and
(3) An extensive evaluation across models and domains, establishing stong and consistent empirical gains of MGS.

Background and Experimental Setup

Research question.

Our research goal focuses on how to effectively train a general-purpose reasoning model across diverse domains via RL. Previous works have shown strong success in training reasoning models with a single type of reward over a set of relatively similar domains. Notably, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains reasoning models using rule-based rewards in domains such as mathematics and code
(
59
;
69
;
50
;
23

(
5

Formally, let

We consider a set of training domains denoted as

Our goal is to optimize

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{(\mathcal{D}_{k},R_{k})\sim\mathcal{T}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{k},\,(y,z)\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}R_{k}(x,y)\right].

3.2
Analysis: Mode Interference in
Sequential
RL

Our results in §
3.1
reveal the limitation of
Sequential
RL training, referred to as Mode Interference, which emphasizes that different domain objectives compete during RL optimization rather than composing additively.

Mode Interference limits the effectiveness of scaling
Sequential
training.

Increasing the amount of training on a single domain may not reliably translate into improved multi-domain performance, as gains in one stage can be offset by interference in previous or subsequent stages.

Properly ordering training domains mitigates mode interference.

As shown in Figure
2
and
3
, Chat
\to
Math generally has better performance. These results suggest that, in sequential RL, domains that preserve higher entropy and flexible reasoning behaviors should be trained earlier, as they form a more permissive foundation for later, more structured domains.

3.3
Experiment 2:
Mixed
RL

We also evaluate
Mixed
RL training. Unlike
Sequential
RL, which optimizes domains sequentially,
Mixed

Setting.

Our primary setting uses a 1:1 mixture of general chat (

Unlike

remains fixed as the initial Base model throughout training, following standard practice in multi-task RL
(
28
;
70
)
. In addition to the balanced mixture, we evaluate different mixing ratios, including 1:9 and 9:1 for Math versus Chat, to study how different mixing portions affect cross-domain trade-offs.

4
Modular Gradient Surgery

To mitigate gradient conflicts in
Mixed
training, we introduce a new technique that resolves interference at the module level. Our approach addresses gradient conflicts within individual transformer modules by applying gradient surgery
(
68
)
locally to each module. We present our method in the following paragraphs.

Background: Gradient Surgery.

Negative interference between tasks has been a long-standing challenge in multi-task learning. A representative line of research attributes this to conflicting task gradients, leading to the development of gradient surgery methods. These methods address conflicting objectives in multi-task optimization by modifying gradients to avoid destructive interference. A representative approach is PCGrad
(
68
)
, which detects conflicts between task gradients (e.g., via negative cosine similarity) and projects gradients to remove components that harm other tasks.
Suppose there are two tasks, let
\mathbf{g}_{i}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}
and
\mathbf{g}_{j}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{j}
denote the gradients for tasks
i
and
j
. PCGrad identifies a conflict when the inner product is negative, i.e.,
\mathbf{g}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{j}<0
. To resolve this,
\mathbf{g}_{i}
is replaced by its projection onto the orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{g}_{j}
, and vice versa:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\text{proj}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{i}-\frac{\mathbf{g}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{j}\|^{2}}\mathbf{g}_{j}

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{j}^{\text{proj}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{j}-\frac{\mathbf{g}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}}\mathbf{g}_{i}

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}^{\text{new}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\text{proj}}+\mathbf{g}_{j}^{\text{proj}}.

We propose Modular Gradient Surgery (MGS), which extends the PCGrad framework by resolving gradient conflicts at the module level. Our approach is motivated by the observation that pretrained transformers are inherently modular, with distinct components specializing in different functional domains. Prior work shows that MLP layers are closely associated with parametric memory and knowledge storage
(
43
;
18
;
15
)
, while attention layers primarily handle information routing and reasoning
(

;
53
;

;
62
;
45
)

(
3
)
.
This modularity implies that gradient conflicts are not uniform across the network. In practice, we observe substantial variation in gradient conflict frequency and magnitude across modules during multi-domain RL (Figure
5
and
10

Some modules may experience severe cross-domain interference, while others exhibit largely compatible gradients.
This further motivates module-aware conflict resolution, whereas applying global gradient surgery can therefore be overly conservative.

Effect of gradient norm.

We conduct another ablation study based on gradient norms. In this experiment, we apply MGS solely to the modules whose gradient norms fall within the range of top-
\mu\%
(
\mu\in\{1,10\}
). Because for each module, we have
K

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{g}[\phi]\|_{\text{candidate}}=\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|_{2},

(4)

where
\mathbf{g}[\phi]
refers to the gradients w.r.t parameter
\phi
.
Results are also shown in Figure

, where we find that while applying MGS to modules of Top 10% Norm exhibits a similar trend to excluding different modules, applying MGS to modules of Top 1% Norms shows a distinct trade-off in model capability. With Top 1% Norm, the model has a performance boost in

Math
performance drops, with only marginal improvements compared to the Base model. This suggests that while high-norm modules are critical for
Chat
,
Math
relies on the coordinated alignment of gradients across a broader range of modules, not just those with the largest magnitude. This observation also suggests that gradient norm magnitude may serve as an effective heuristic for identifying target modules for gradient surgery and warrants further investigation.

Effect of different modules.

We observe that the cosine similarity between the tasks has different patterns across different modules (Fig.

). Therefore, we try to answer the question of
“How addressing gradient conflict of certain modules contributes to the final performance”
. Specifically, we focus on
MLP
,
Attention Head
(

), and we also consider
LayerNorm
as a type of transformer module. We selectively exclude gradient surgery for specific module families in this experiment. For example, as shown in Figure

and

Among these, although

Related Work

Our work is closely related to LLM post-training and multi-task RL.
LLM post-training has evolved from standard RLHF for alignment
(
46
)
to Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for complex reasoning
(
52
;
23
)
. While multi-task RL is well-studied in classical contexts, focusing on gradient manipulation
(
68
)
and weighting
(
9
)
to mitigate task interference, its application to LLM RL post-training remains nascent
(
61
)
. Recent efforts in multi-domain LLM RL
(
28
;
34
)
often rely on complex pipelines or distillation, frequently overlooking the inherent gradient imbalances identified in mixed-objective training
(
61
)
. Our work bridges this gap by introducing Modular Gradient Surgery, an approach that resolves inter-task conflicts at the module level, providing a stable and efficient recipe for general-purpose RL. Our complete related works are shown in Appendix
K
.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we conduct a systematic study on how to perform RL
on diverse domains to build broadly capable reasoning models. We study two basic training strategies, i.e.,
Sequential
training and
Mixed
mixed training, and find that 1) for
Sequential
RL, Mode Interference manifests as Forgetting and Rigidity; 2) for
Mixed
RL, we observe gradient conflicts which pose negative interference on different tasks. To advance general-purpose reasoning, we propose Modular Gradient Surgery, which addresses the conflicts at the module level. Empirical results show that MGS consistently leads to better performance.

In addition, our work is the first to apply gradient manipulation in LLM RL post-training.
We hope our work can serve as an initiative toward more effective and efficient LLM Post-training for general-purpose reasoning.

Impact Statement

This work aims at studying and advancing general-purpose reasoning LLM, which mainly focuses on Math, Chat and Instruction Following. The deployment of such models may have broader social impacts, while none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

\displaystyle S_{\text{tags}}=(\texttt{<think>},\texttt{</think>},\texttt{<response>},\texttt{</response>})

(8)

Objective with KL divergence and entropy constraints.

In our preliminary study, we explored an objective function for
Math
tasks that incorporates both Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and entropy regularization to maintain policy stability and exploration:

\displaystyle J_{\textsc{Sequential}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(q,a)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\end{subarray}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{G}{|o_{i}|}}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\Big(\min\Big(r_{i,t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{i,t},\operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{i,t}\Big)

\displaystyle-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}}\big)_{i,t}+\gamma\,\operatorname{H}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}\big)_{i,t}\Big)\Bigg],

(10)

where the token-level KL divergence and entropy are defined as:

\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\cdot)_{i,t}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\theta}(v|\cdot)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v|\cdot)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(v|\cdot)},\text{~and~}\operatorname{H}(\pi_{\theta})_{i,t}=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\theta}(v|\cdot)\log\pi_{\theta}(v|\cdot).

(11)

Policy entropy analysis.

To study the entropy dynamics during training, we measure the average token-level policy entropy across the training dataset
\mathcal{D}
. For a policy
\pi_{\theta}
, the mean entropy
\mathcal{H}
is defined as:

\mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{y}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\boldsymbol{y}|}\operatorname{H}(\pi_{\theta})_{t}\right]=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{y}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\boldsymbol{y}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\theta}(v|\cdot)\log\pi_{\theta}(v|\cdot).

(12)

In our experiments, we compute this metric over each batch of prompts used for training to monitor the evolution of policy diversity during training.

Additional Analysis: Parallel training amortizes extra memory cost.

Gradient surgery methods were known for their high memory overhead. However, this overhead can be significantly amortized in parallel training. Here we analyze the extra cost on time and memory, brought by MGS. For memory, the overhead is
\mathcal{O}(T\times N)
, where
T
is the number of tasks and
N
is the number of parameters. We need to store all the gradients and apply PCGrad before updating the model parameters. For time, the theoretical overhead is
\mathcal{O}(M\cdot T^{2})
, where
M
is the number of modules when using
Module
PCGrad, other
M=1
, and
T
is the number of tasks. In practice, the time cost in applying PCGrad is
\sim 163
seconds in a
\sim 30
hours training, which is negligible. As for GPU memory overhead, the peak memory cost is
Memory_{\text{peak}}\approx(T_{\text{buffer}}+T_{\text{projected}})\times N\times Bytes
. However, due to the use of Fully-Sharded Data Parallel training, the actual cost for a single GPU is
\mathcal{O}(T\times\frac{N}{world\_size})
. Thereby, the peak memory overhead for a single GPU is
Memory_{\text{peak}}\approx(T_{\text{buffer}}+T_{\text{projected}})\times\frac{N}{world\_size}\times Bytes
, where
Bytes
is determined by the training precision.

LLM RL Post-training

LLM post-training typically refers to the sequence of alignment and specialization stages applied to a base model before deployment. Reinforcement learning has become a central component of this process, most notably through reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)
(
11
;
46
;
4
)
and more recently reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). RLVR has been shown to significantly improve reasoning performance in domains with objective correctness signals such as mathematics and coding
(

;
52
)
.
Other works such as rubric-based
(

and checklist-based
(

(
6
;
71
;
24
)
.
Recent work demonstrates that RL can also improve open-ended chat and instruction following.
5
show that applying RL on top of reasoning traces leads to stronger conversational ability, while
70
demonstrate that zero-RL can generalize beyond strictly verifiable domains. Large-scale systems such as DeepSeek-R1
(
23
)
further validate the effectiveness of RL for reasoning, though they rely on complex multi-stage pipelines involving cold-start SFT
(
50
)
, rejection sampling
(
63
)
, and multiple rounds of RL.
Despite these advances, existing RLHF and RLVR approaches typically focus on a single target domain at a time. How to jointly train a model that is strong at both verifiable reasoning (e.g., Math) and open-ended interaction (e.g., Chat) using a simple RL recipe remains under-explored. Our work addresses this gap by studying RL post-training with multiple heterogeneous rewards simultaneously.

General Multi-task RL

Our
Mixed
training setting is closely related to classical multi-task reinforcement learning, where a single policy is optimized over multiple tasks. A central challenge in this literature is negative interference between tasks, especially when gradients are imbalanced.
A line of work addresses this issue through Gradient Manipulation methods
(
8
)
, including GradVec
(
58
)
, PCGrad
(
68
)
, and GradDrop
(
10
)
, which aim to find Pareto-improving or conflict-averse updates.
Another line of work addresses gradient imbalance through Gradient Weighting. This line of work aims at finding a common update direction by adaptively aggregating gradients from different objectives/tasks
(
27
;
57
;
35
;
51
;
9
)
.
PCGrad, in particular, performs gradient surgery by projecting conflicting task gradients to remove destructive components, and has been shown effective in both supervised learning and RL settings. However, these methods treat model parameters as a monolithic vector.
In contrast, large language models exhibit strong modular structure across attention, MLP, and normalization components. Our work extends classical gradient conflict resolution to LLM RL by introducing Modular Gradient Surgery, which resolves conflicts at the module level rather than globally. This design better aligns with transformer architectures and leads to improved stability and performance in multi-domain RL post-training.

However, in LLM RL post-training, few tryouts have been taken to address gradient imbalance.
61

They find that the imbalance can hardly be explained by a series of metrics, e.g., learning gains, advantages, etc. Our work serves as the first to address this imbalance through Gradient Manipulation and showcase that resolving the conflicts/imbalances in this way leads to substantial and consistent gains.
