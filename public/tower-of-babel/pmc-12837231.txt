Multimodal artificial intelligence in retinal vascular and neovascular macular diseases: a systematic review of diagnostic and prognostic applications
Authors: Abdulmajeed Al Khathami, Mohamed Baklola, Meshari Ahmed Alalyani, Amal Turki Aljuaid, Rehab Alsaeed, Amani Al Ghramah, Orjuwan Turki Aljuaid, Reema Hasan AlGarni, Raghad Sayyaf Alshahrani, Rawan Saud Aldreweash, Razan Saleh Alshehri, Safiyah Abdullah Albishi, Ghadah Mohammed Almutairi, Naji Al-bawah, Khaled Abdelkhalique Abdelshafi Ghaith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRetinal vascular diseases, including diabetic retinopathy (DR) and retinal vein occlusion (RVO), and neovascular macular diseases such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) are leading causes of vision loss worldwide. With the rapid growth of artificial intelligence (AI), multimodal approaches that integrate diverse imaging modalities and clinical data have emerged as powerful tools for improving diagnosis, prognosis, and risk stratification.MethodsThis systematic review, conducted according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines, synthesized evidence on the diagnostic and prognostic applications of multimodal AI in retinal vascular diseases. Six databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, and Cochrane Library) were searched for English-language studies published between 1 January 2019 and 1 November 2025. Eligible studies applied AI or machine learning models integrating two or more data modalities for diagnosis, prognosis, or prediction in DR, RVO, or AMD. Data extraction, quality appraisal, and narrative synthesis were performed.ResultsFrom 11,659 identified records, 12 studies met the eligibility criteria. Multimodal AI systems consistently outperformed unimodal models and, in several cases, exceeded expert ophthalmologist performance. Diagnostic accuracy for AMD and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) ranged from 87% to 96%, with fusion-based approaches achieving area under the curve (AUC) values up to 0.989. Prognostic models predicting treatment response or recurrence in nAMD and RVO achieved AUCs between 0.972 and 0.980, surpassing both clinician and single-modality baselines. Hybrid and foundation models integrating imaging, clinical, and textual data demonstrated promising results but variable robustness. Most studies were retrospective, single-center, and exhibited moderate-to-high risk of bias, emphasizing the need for larger, prospective, multicenter validation to establish clinical applicability and generalizability.ConclusionMultimodal AI demonstrates superior diagnostic and prognostic performance compared to unimodal models and, in some cases, outperforms expert clinicians in managing retinal vascular diseases. Integrating complementary data sources, such as OCT, fundus imaging, and clinical information, enhances model accuracy and generalizability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04561-3.

Background

Retinal vascular diseases such as diabetic retinopathy (DR), diabetic macular edema (DME), retinal vein occlusion (RVO), and neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) are among the leading causes of irreversible visual impairment worldwide, severely affecting patients’ functional independence and quality of life [1–3]. Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) alone is expected to affect more than 14% of middle-aged to older adults over the next 15 years, with choroidal neovascularization (CNV) representing a critical biomarker of progression to the late neovascular stage, which can rapidly lead to blindness if untreated [4]. Although intravitreal anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy has significantly improved outcomes in patients with nAMD and other retinal vascular diseases, treatment decisions remain heavily dependent on imaging-based evaluation of disease activity and response [5]. In this review, we use the term “retinal vascular and neovascular macular diseases” to encompass both canonical retinal vascular disorders (such as DR and RVO) and neovascular macular disease (nAMD). We made this choice because the clinical evaluation and management of nAMD depend critically on vascular activity (choroidal neovascularization) assessed with multimodal vascular imaging (OCT, OCTA, FA/ICGA), making nAMD directly relevant to multimodal vascular AI research.

Various imaging modalities are central to the diagnosis and monitoring of retinal diseases. Fundus photography is widely used for population-level screening, whereas fluorescein angiography (FA) and indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) provide vascular details for disease characterization [6]. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has become indispensable for detecting intraretinal and subretinal fluid, retinal thickness changes, and structural biomarkers of progression, whereas OCT angiography (OCTA) enables the noninvasive visualization of perfusion abnormalities and neovascular complexes [7, 8]. However, these modalities generate vast amounts of data and their interpretation relies on the expertise of trained ophthalmologists. Manual assessment is time-consuming, subject to inter-observer variability, and may overlook subtle disease signatures, underscoring the need for supportive technologies that can efficiently process multimodal data [9].

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, has emerged as a powerful ophthalmological tool. Early efforts focused largely on single-modality analysis, most often fundus photography, to achieve a high accuracy in detecting DR, AMD, and glaucoma [10–12]. Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) applied to OCT have further enabled tasks such as distinguishing exudative from nonexudative AMD, identifying CNV activity, and recommending referral urgency [13, 14]. Nevertheless, single-modality approaches face important limitations: they do not capture the multidimensional nature of retinal vascular diseases and thus, may underperform when applied to complex clinical scenarios that require the integration of structural, functional, and demographic information.

To address these challenges, multimodal AI approaches have been developed that combine diverse data sources such as OCT, OCTA, FA, fundus photography, clinical records, and electrophysiological measures [9, 15]. Studies have demonstrated that multimodal fusion consistently improves the sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic accuracy compared to unimodal models [9]. For example, integrating OCTA with OCT features improves AMD subtype classification and CNV activity detection, whereas ensemble networks that leverage multiple modalities outperform expert ophthalmologists in certain diagnostic tasks [16, 17]. Beyond diagnosis, multimodal AI has also been applied to prognosis, including the prediction of macular edema recurrence following anti-VEGF therapy and the identification of quantitative imaging biomarkers associated with treatment outcomes [18].

Despite these promising advances, research on multimodal AI for retinal vascular and neovascular macular diseases remains limited and heterogenous. Many studies used small retrospective cohorts, often lacked external validation, and the literature is currently concentrated on a narrow set of tasks, particularly CNV and activity detection and treatment-response prediction in nAMD, rather than a broad representation of retinal vascular pathologies. As a result, it is unclear how generalizable reported performance gains are across diseases, populations, and clinical settings, and whether multimodal approaches reliably outperform single-modality AI or clinician assessment in routine practice. Therefore, a comprehensive and methodical review is warranted to consolidate the existing evidence, highlight methodological strengths and weaknesses such as disease composition and risk of bias, and outline priorities for future clinically translatable studies that evaluate multimodal AI across a wider spectrum of retinal vascular and neovascular macular conditions.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines [19]. The methodological procedures, including eligibility criteria, search strategy, screening, data extraction, and quality appraisal, were prespecified in an internal protocol. This review was not registered in PROSPERO.

Because the research questions relate to diagnostic and prognostic accuracy, the review was structured using a PIRT framework rather than the PICO framework typically used for interventional studies. The components were defined as follows:

Search strategy

A comprehensive search of six electronic databases, PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, IEEE Xplore, and the Cochrane Library, was conducted to identify eligible studies published from 1 January 2019 to 1 November 2025. The start date of 2019 was selected to capture the emergence of multimodal deep learning approaches and the rapid advancement of OCT- and OCTA-based AI systems during this period.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (MeSH/Emtree) and free-text keywords related to retinal vascular and neovascular macular diseases and artificial intelligence. Disease-related terms included “retinal vein occlusion,” “branch retinal vein occlusion,” “central retinal vein occlusion,” “diabetic retinopathy,” “proliferative diabetic retinopathy,” “diabetic macular edema,” and “neovascular age-related macular degeneration.” Imaging-related terms included “fundus photography,” “optical coherence tomography,” “OCT,” “OCT angiography,” “OCTA,” “fluorescein angiography,” “FA,” “autofluorescence,” and “multimodal imaging.” AI-related terms included “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “deep learning,” “neural networks,” “convolutional neural network,” “ensemble learning,” “multimodal learning,” “fusion model,” and “predictive modeling.” Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to combine terms and maximize search sensitivity.

The search was restricted to studies published in English, as English is the predominant language of indexed biomedical literature in ophthalmic AI research, and the likelihood of missing substantial non-English AI work was considered low. The full database-specific search strings are provided in Supplementary File S1. The search was last updated on 1 November 2025, immediately prior to final manuscript revision. Reference lists of all eligible studies were also screened, and no additional eligible studies were identified through this process.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) they investigated patients with retinal vascular and neovascular macular diseases, specifically diabetic retinopathy (DR), retinal vein occlusion (RVO), or neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). nAMD was included because its diagnosis and management depend on vascular activity such as choroidal neovascularization assessed through multimodal vascular imaging, making it directly relevant to multimodal AI systems; (2) they applied artificial intelligence or machine learning methods using multimodal data integration, defined as the combination of two or more complementary data sources such as fundus photography with OCT, OCT with OCTA, fluorescein angiography with OCTA, or imaging combined with clinical, demographic, laboratory, or textual variables; (3) they reported quantitative diagnostic, prognostic, or predictive performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, or area under the ROC curve (AUC), or provided sufficient information to derive these metrics; (4) they used a retrospective or prospective study design; and (5) they evaluated diagnostic, prognostic, disease-activity assessment, or treatment-response prediction tasks relevant to retinal vascular or neovascular macular pathology.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if: (1) they evaluated single-modality AI or ML models without multimodal data fusion; (2) they did not primarily investigate retinal vascular or neovascular macular diseases; (3) they were not original research articles, including reviews, editorials, commentaries, or case reports; (4) they were conference abstracts without full-text availability, or were animal, preclinical, or simulation-based studies; (5) they used only conventional image analysis or statistical modeling methods without AI or ML techniques; (6) they focused on non-vascular retinal conditions such as inherited retinal dystrophies or other degenerative disorders without a vascular component; or (7) they were not available in full text or were published in languages other than English.

Study selection and screening

All the identified records were imported into Rayyan, a web-based platform for systematic review and screening [20], where duplicate records were automatically removed. Title and abstract screening were conducted independently by AAK, MB, MAA, ATA, RSA, and RA using the predefined eligibility criteria. Inter-rater agreement between primary screeners was assessed using Cohen’s kappa, which demonstrated substantial concordance (>0.75) [21]. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the screening team, and unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by the senior corresponding author NA.

Full-text screening was performed independently by AAK, MB, AAG, GMA, and OTA to confirm eligibility. Conflicts were resolved through consensus, with arbitration by NA when required. References and full texts were managed using EndNote. Screening of the reference lists of all included studies was also conducted; however, no additional eligible studies were identified.

Quality and risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using design-appropriate tools. For studies that evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of artificial intelligence or imaging biomarkers, we applied the QUADAS-2 tool, which evaluates four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing [22]. To develop or validate the prognostic or prediction models, we used the PROBAST tool, which assesses the risk of bias across participants, predictors, outcomes, and analyses [23].

Risk-of-bias assessments were independently performed by RHA, RSA, RA, and AAG, with methodological oversight provided by MB and NA. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, adjudication by NA. This multilayered approach ensured consistency and rigor across studies with varied designs.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data from all eligible studies were extracted using a standardized extraction form developed for this review. Extraction included: study authors and year, country and setting, study design, participant characteristics, sample size, imaging modality, AI or machine learning method, reference standard, and performance metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and AUC. Where applicable, details concerning external validation, comparator groups, calibration reporting, and multimodal fusion strategies were also collected.

Data extraction was performed independently by AAK, MB, MAA, ATA, RA, AAG, and OTA, with all extracted data cross-checked by GMA, RHA, and RSA to ensure consistency and completeness. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion, and unresolved issues were adjudicated by NA. Given the heterogeneity across included studies in disease populations, imaging modalities, AI architectures, outcome measures, and validation strategies, a meta-analysis was not feasible. Findings were synthesized narratively and are presented in comparative tables. Descriptive statistics, such as ranges of performance metrics, were used to highlight overall trends and variability.

Results

Search results

Our systematic search identified a total of 11,659 records from six databases: PubMed (n = 1586), Embase (n = 2078), Scopus (n = 4591), Web of Sciences (n = 2924), IEEE (n = 388), and the Cochrane Library (n = 92). After 7,890 duplicate records were removed, 3,769 records remained for screening. During the title and abstract screening phase, 3,710 records were excluded. The primary reasons for exclusion at this stage were non-relevant disease (n = 1320), studies on single-modality AI (n = 910), or being a review/editorial article (n = 640). This left 59 reports sought for retrieval, all of which were successfully retrieved (n = 0 not retrieved). Following a full-text assessment of these 59 reports, 47 were excluded. The main reasons for exclusion at this stage were again being single-modality AI only (n = 16), lacking specific diagnostic/prognostic performance data (n = 12), or not focusing on retinal vascular disease (n = 11).

An additional screening of reference lists of included studies was performed, but no further eligible records were identified. Ultimately, 12 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in this systematic review. The complete study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

A total of twelve studies published between 2021 and 2025 met the inclusion criteria. The characteristics of these studies are summarized in Table 1. The included studies were geographically diverse, originating from China, Taiwan, the United States, Korea, Brazil, Russia, and Canada, with one study utilizing publicly available datasets [24]. Most studies (11 of 12) adopted retrospective designs, while only one study, conducted by Huang and colleagues, followed a prospective, observational approach [25].

The studies addressed a broad range of retinal vascular diseases. Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) was the most frequently investigated condition, reported in six studies [16, 17, 25–28]. Retinal vein occlusion (RVO) was also commonly examined, as in the studies by Kang et al. and Zhang et al. [18, 29]. Other studies focused on diabetic retinopathy (DR) and diabetic macular edema (DME) [24, 29, 30], or investigated broader retinal and systemic associations. The nature of multimodal data varied considerably across studies. Some combined paired imaging modalities, such as color fundus photography and optical coherence tomography (OCT), while others incorporated clinical variables, text-based metadata, or non-imaging signals such as electroretinography (ERG).

Analysis of AI models and fusion strategies

The AI architectures and multimodal fusion strategies employed in the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The analysis revealed substantial methodological diversity in how multimodal data were combined for diagnostic or predictive modeling. Late-stage (feature) fusion was the most commonly used approach, applied in four studies, including those conducted by Xu et al., Thakoor et al., Zhang et al., and Zedadra et al. [17, 18, 24, 26]. In these studies, each modality was processed through separate feature extractors, and the resulting feature vectors were concatenated before classification. Intermediate (deep) fusion, which integrates cross-modal information at multiple layers within the neural network, was employed by Chen et al. and Jin et al. to enable more nuanced feature interactions [16, 27]. A data-level fusion approach was reported by Kang et al., who combined raw inputs from multiple imaging modalities (fundus, OCT, FA/ICGA) into a single structured “image package” before training [29].

Temporal fusion, demonstrated by Han et al., concatenated features derived from sequential OCT scans to model treatment progression over time [28]. Hybrid and ensemble strategies were utilized by Orlova et al., who integrated outputs from gradient boosting and rule-based classifiers through a weighted ensemble mechanism [30]. Finally, two studies, conducted by Ghalibafan et al. and Carrillo-Larco et al., employed foundation models such as GPT-4 V and MedCLIP that natively support joint processing of multiple data types, including images and text, reflecting the latest trends in multimodal AI research [31, 32].

AMD/PCV diagnosis and subtyping

Three studies evaluated multimodal AI for the classification of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV). Across all studies, multimodal fusion consistently outperformed unimodal comparators and often matched or exceeded expert-level performance. Xu et al. reported that their Fundus + OCT model achieved an accuracy of 87.4%, surpassing both the OCT-only (83.2%) and Fundus-only (75.0%) models, and slightly outperforming the best human expert (86.0%) [26]. Similarly, Chen et al. demonstrated strong external generalizability, with their IR + OCT model achieving 91.6% accuracy compared to 74.8% for OCT-only and 80.4% for IR-only inputs, closely matching senior clinician performance (92.5%) [27]. Thakoor et al. also achieved superior results using OCTA + OCT fusion for three-class AMD classification, significantly outperforming all single-modality baselines [17].

CNV activity assessment

In Jin et al. (2022), multimodal fusion substantially enhanced the assessment of choroidal neovascularization (CNV) activity [16]. Their OCT + OCTA fusion model achieved an AUC of 0.980, markedly outperforming the OCT-only baseline (AUC: 0.739).

Broad disease screening

Three studies applied multimodal AI to the concurrent detection or classification of multiple retinal diseases. Kang et al. employed a data-level fusion strategy that combined Fundus, OCT, and FA images, achieving outstanding diagnostic performance (AUC up to 0.987) and high accuracy in predicting treatment needs (AUC: 0.969) [29]. Zedadra et al. developed a hybrid multimodal model integrating images, clinical data, and text, which reached an accuracy of 98.9%, substantially outperforming the unimodal baseline of 71.0% [24]. Similarly, Orlova et al. demonstrated that incorporating non-imaging modalities such as ERG data, demographics, and patient history improved model performance, achieving a higher MAUC of 0.912 compared to 0.670 for a rule-based system alone [30].

Prognostic modeling and treatment response prediction

Two studies focused on prognostic applications, demonstrating that multimodal AI can surpass clinician performance in predicting treatment outcomes. Zhang et al. integrated OCTA images with clinical variables to predict macular edema recurrence in retinal vein occlusion (RVO), achieving an AUC of 0.972, which was higher than both the image-only model (AUC: 0.913) and clinician performance [18]. Similarly, Han et al. utilized temporal fusion of sequential OCT scans to predict treatment response in neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). Their model achieved an accuracy of 82.0%, outperforming the average accuracy of six ophthalmologists (76.1%) [28].

Exploratory and novel applications

Three studies investigated emerging or unconventional applications of multimodal AI in retinal research. Huang et al. applied AI-based analysis of intravenous fluorescein angiography (IVFA) for quantitative biomarker extraction, finding a significant correlation with structural OCT metrics (p = 0.026) [25]. Carrillo-Larco et al. used a MedCLIP foundation model to predict diabetes duration from combined Fundus images and textual data, achieving moderate predictive performance (F1 = 57%) [31]. Meanwhile, Ghalibafan et al. assessed the zero-shot diagnostic capability of GPT-4 V, which demonstrated limited accuracy (31.3% for multiple-choice and 13.7% for open-ended questions), highlighting that current vision-language foundation models still face substantial challenges in structured clinical reasoning [32].

Discussion

This systematic review demonstrates that multimodal artificial intelligence consistently outperforms unimodal models in the diagnosis and prognosis of retinal vascular and neovascular macular diseases. Across the included studies, integrating complementary imaging and clinical modalities led to meaningful improvements in diagnostic accuracy, prognostic prediction, and treatment-response modeling. These findings support the premise that combining heterogeneous data streams, such as structural OCT with vascular OCTA or color fundus imaging with clinical variables, provides a more comprehensive and biologically grounded analytical framework [16].

By simulating the integrative reasoning process of clinicians, multimodal AI systems can capture the multidimensional features of retinal pathology more effectively than single-modality models [33]. This superiority was consistent across disease categories, including age-related macular degeneration (AMD), polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV), diabetic retinopathy (DR), and retinal vein occlusion (RVO), and across both diagnostic and prognostic applications [34, 35]. Although AMD and nAMD comprised a substantial proportion of the included studies, the performance benefits observed across varied modalities and disease categories reinforce the general potential of multimodal AI.

Several multimodal AI models demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to or exceeding that of experienced ophthalmologists. Xu et al. and Chen et al. reported that integrating fundus or infrared images with OCT enhanced AMD and PCV classification, surpassing expert-level accuracy [26, 27]. Similarly, Jin et al. showed that combining OCT with OCTA substantially improved the detection of choroidal neovascularization (AUC 0.980 vs. 0.739 for OCT-only), highlighting the critical contribution of vascular information to disease activity assessment [36]. These findings suggest that multimodal approaches can leverage complementary imaging information to enhance diagnostic precision and may support clinical decision-making in complex retinal diseases.

The prognostic applications of multimodal AI highlight a shift toward predictive, personalized ophthalmology. Zhang et al. and Han et al. demonstrated that multimodal models can accurately predict macular edema recurrence and treatment response in neovascular AMD, achieving AUCs above 0.97 and outperforming clinician-based assessments [18, 28]. These findings indicate that such tools could facilitate data-driven treatment planning and patient counseling, supporting the implementation of precision medicine in retinal care.

Beyond imaging, studies incorporating non-imaging data such as demographics, electrophysiological measurements, and text-based metadata highlight the expanding potential of multimodal AI. Hybrid models that integrate imaging with structured and unstructured data achieved substantial performance gains, as demonstrated by Zedadra et al. and Orlova et al. [24, 30]. Although early results from foundation models such as GPT-4 V show limited diagnostic accuracy (31.3%) [32], these approaches illustrate the emerging potential for scalable, general-purpose ophthalmic AI systems capable of integrating diverse data modalities.

A deeper analysis of AI architectures reveals several key trends in multimodal fusion strategies. Late-stage, or feature fusion remains the most widely adopted and reliable approach, combining features extracted from each modality prior to final classification, as demonstrated by Xu et al. and Thakoor et al. [17, 26]. Intermediate, or deep, fusion, employed by Chen et al. and Jin et al., enables intermodal feature learning within network layers, improving performance when modalities are closely related, such as infrared imaging and OCT [16, 27]. Hybrid and ensemble fusion approaches represent a more advanced paradigm, integrating imaging data with clinical, demographic, and textual information through diverse architectures, including graph neural networks and large language models, as shown by Zedadra et al. and Orlova et al. [24, 30]. This strategy supports the shift toward comprehensive patient modeling and precision analytics. Foundation and zero-shot models, such as GPT-4 V, remain experimental but conceptually promising, although they require domain-specific fine-tuning to achieve clinically reliable performance [32]. Collectively, these developments suggest that multimodal AI is progressing from simple feature concatenation toward semantically aligned, context-aware integration, representing an essential step for scalable implementation in clinical practice.

For multimodal AI systems to progress toward routine clinical use, several steps are essential. Future research should emphasize standardized multimodal datasets, transparent model documentation, robust interpretability tools, and prospective multicenter external validation [37]. Regulatory alignment, assessment of model fairness, and integration with clinical workflows will also be necessary to support clinician trust and ensure equitable adoption across populations [38]. With these foundations in place, multimodal AI has the potential to enhance diagnostic precision, optimize treatment planning, and ultimately improve visual outcomes for patients with retinal vascular and neovascular macular diseases [39].

Strengths and limitations

This review has several notable strengths. It employed a comprehensive and systematically developed search strategy across six major databases, supplemented by citation screening, and was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 reporting standards. The review provides a detailed synthesis of multimodal artificial intelligence approaches, with particular attention to the types of data fusion, model architectures, and clinical applications. By comparing multimodal systems with unimodal baselines and clinician performance, the review offers valuable insights into current capabilities and emerging trends in AI-driven diagnosis and prognosis. Furthermore, the inclusion of both retinal vascular diseases and neovascular macular disease (nAMD) reflects the clinical reality that vascular imaging and choroidal neovascularization assessment are central to the interpretation of modern retinal imaging modalities and multimodal AI systems.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only twelve studies met the eligibility criteria, reflecting the early stage of multimodal AI research in this field. The disease distribution was uneven, with many studies focused on nAMD, which may limit the representativeness of findings across other retinal vascular conditions such as DR and RVO. Although nAMD was intentionally included due to its reliance on vascular imaging and relevance to multimodal AI, this imbalance reduces the generalizability of the overall results.

Second, most included studies were retrospective, single-center investigations with relatively small sample sizes. These features increase the risk of overfitting and limit external validity. Risk-of-bias assessments also identified concerns related to patient selection, index-test masking, reference-standard robustness, and insufficient validation. All prognostic studies were rated as high risk of bias, suggesting that many reported accuracy and AUC values may be overly optimistic and not fully applicable to routine clinical practice.

Third, substantial heterogeneity existed across studies in imaging modalities, fusion strategies, AI architectures, outcome definitions, and evaluation metrics. This variability prevented quantitative synthesis and restricted direct comparisons. Variation in modality combinations, such as OCT with OCTA, fundus with OCT, or imaging with clinical data, also makes it difficult to determine which components consistently improve performance.

Additional limitations include the exclusion of non-English publications, which may introduce language bias, and the reliance on published data without access to individual patient-level information, which limited the ability to perform subgroup or sensitivity analyses. Nevertheless, the consistent pattern of multimodal models outperforming unimodal baselines across diverse settings supports the reliability of the overall conclusions and underscores the need for larger, prospective, multicenter studies with robust external validation.

Conclusion

Multimodal artificial intelligence demonstrates clear advantages over single-modality approaches in the diagnosis and prognosis of retinal vascular and neovascular macular diseases. By integrating complementary structural, vascular, and clinical information, these models achieve higher diagnostic accuracy, improved prognostic performance, and greater potential to support individualized treatment planning. However, most available studies are limited by retrospective single-center designs, small sample sizes, and insufficient external validation, which restricts the generalizability of current evidence and highlight the gap between research-level performance and real-world applicability. To advance toward clinical translation, future research should prioritize standardized multimodal datasets, transparent and reproducible reporting, robust external benchmarking, and prospective multicenter evaluation. Strengthening interpretability and workflow integration will also be essential to ensure safe, equitable, and effective deployment of multimodal AI systems aimed at improving visual outcomes in patients with retinal vascular and neovascular macular conditions.
