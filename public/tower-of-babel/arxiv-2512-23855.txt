Gedanken Experiments of Entanglement in Particle Physics: Interactions, Operators and Bell Inequalities in Flavor Space Preprint: WSU-HEP-2504
Corbin Pacheco, Nausheen R. Shah

Abstract
In this article we explore ideas from quantum entanglement which can be meaningfully formulated and tested in the collider environment. We propose Bell-type inequalities as operator-level diagnostics of quantum incompatibility in particle-physics systems. We construct flavor operators associated with mass identification, flavor change, and charged-current weak mixing which arise from fundamental interactions in the Standard Model. We treat these interactions as alternative measurement settings in a Gendanken experiment. For entangled two-particle states, these operators generate nontrivial correlations that violate Bell-type bounds, excluding non-contextual local descriptions under the stated assumptions. These violations arise from the algebraic structure of the operators rather than from kinematic correlations or exotic dynamics. We discuss how the predicted correlation patterns may be probed with experimental data, clarifying the scope and limitations of Bell-type reasoning in particle physics.



I
Introduction:

Particle physics seeks to understand the smallest constituents of matter and the fundamental interactions that govern them. From the vastness of cosmological scales to the minuteness of subatomic particles, nature presents us with a striking hierarchy of sizes, energies, and timescales. At the forefront of experimental exploration lies the Large Hadron Collider, which probes these scales directly by accelerating particles to near-light speeds and studying their collisions. Through such experiments, we have tested the Standard Model (SM) of particle physics to remarkable precision.

Yet, despite its empirical success, the SM remains conceptually puzzling—beautiful, symmetric, and deeply unnatural.

The SM is based on the gauge symmetry
SU(3)_{C}\times SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
, and contains three generations of fermions with seemingly arbitrary masses and mixings. Fermion and gauge boson masses are forbidden by symmetry and arise only through the Higgs mechanism, whose origin remains unexplained. This leads to an unsettling conclusion: the SM is simultaneously
highly symmetric and highly random, with arbitrary parameters and unexplained hierarchies.

The interplay between symmetry and apparent arbitrariness in the SM invites deeper scrutiny. Concepts such as entanglement and entropy, central to quantum information theory, offer a promising lens through which to examine this structure. Recent work has explored theoretical interactions as sources of entanglement and entropy, as well as the use of quantum-information–theoretic tools to formulate constraining principles within the SM and beyond, see for e.g. Refs.

We emphasize that Bell-type inequalities provide a general criterion for distinguishing classical from quantum correlations in bipartite systems, and test entanglement through correlations between binary outcomes. While the original proposal was concerning spin

This motivates reformulating Bell-type reasoning in a setting where measurement settings are defined by physical interactions.

We propose using fundamental SM interactions as probes of entanglement in particle physics. Such interactions will be shown to be completely analogous to spin operators in quantum mechanics, performing binary measurements of inherent particle quantum numbers.

We formulate Bell-type inequalities at the operator level, in the spirit of a Gedanken experiment. In each run, a definite pair of interaction-defined measurement settings acts simultaneously on the two subsystems, so that the correlator

E(a,b)

Possible experimental validation of our Gedanken experiments is also discussed. We reserve Sec.

II
Binary Observables and Bell-Type Reasoning

Bell in his seminal paper
Bell 1964
discussed the implications of quantum versus classical correlations on the spin singlet state. Here, for completeness, we present his argument for both correlated and anti-correlated states. Consider the maximally entangled spin states,

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle+|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle\right)\;,

(1)

or

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\right)\;.~

(2)

Assume a local hidden variable theory (LHVT) characterized by a hidden variable
\lambda
, distributed according to a density
\rho(\lambda)
. Given locality and realism as encoded in the LHVT, if Alice measures the first qubit and Bob (spacelike separated from Alice) measures the second qubit, measurement outcomes are determined by functions

A(a,\lambda)
and

respectively,
corresponding to measurement choices
a
and

A(a,\lambda)=\pm 1,\qquad B(b,\lambda)=\pm 1.

(3)

Perfect correlations, Eq. (
1

2

A(a,\lambda)B(a,\lambda)=\pm 1\;,

(4)

where the

After a series of measurements performed on identically prepared initial states by Alice and Bob, the expectation value of the product of their measurements predicted by the LHVT is

E(a,b)=\int d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\,A(a,\lambda)\,B(b,\lambda).

(5)

Given a minimum of three measurement choices

\bigl|E(a,b)-E(a,c)\bigr|\;\leq\;1\pm E(b,c)\;,

where the + ( - ) on the right hand side corresponds to the initial state being anti-correlated (correlated). This bound, often referred to as Bell’s Inequality, limits the maximum possible correlation in measurements that could be obtained in a classical system. We emphasize that for a LHVT Eq. (

, and
c

any
choice of
a,b

In quantum mechanics, the corresponding expectation values for the given states
|\psi\rangle_{\pm}
are

E(a,b)_{\rm QM}=\langle\hat{S}_{\theta_{b}}\otimes\hat{S}_{\theta_{a}}\rangle_{\Psi_{\pm}}=\pm c_{\theta_{ab}},

(7)

where

denotes the relative angle between spin measurement directions

and
b

refers to
\cos\theta_{ab}

Choosing spin measurement directions

along the
z
-axis
(\hat{S}_{z})

along the
x
-axis
(\hat{S}_{x})
, and

at some arbitrary angle

(\hat{S}_{\theta})
with respect to the

6
) and
(
7
) together predict for both the correlated and anti-correlated states,

\cos\theta_{ac}+\sin\theta_{ac}\leq 1,

(8)

which is violated for

0<\theta_{ac}<\frac{\pi}{2}.

(9)

Fundamentally, at the operator level, Bell-type inequalities test whether a LHVT can consistently assign predetermined values to incompatible observables so as to reproduce all correlators, independent of the details of any experimental implementation
Bell 1964
;
Clauser and Shimony 1978
;
Abramsky and Hardy 2012
;
Fine 1982
;
Abramsky and Brandenburger 2011
;
Brunner et al. 2014
. Hence the explicit violation of Eq. (

Further, the analysis above is independent of the physical realization of the observables and only depends on the binary nature of the system. Hence, any 2-state system where we can identify operators/measurements corresponding to the spin
identification
(
\hat{S}_{z}

III
Single-particle Fundamental 2-Level Systems in Particle Physics

Spin-based Bell tests may be impractical in collider environments due to the lack of controllable spin analyzers, rapidly varying reference frames, and
dependence on momentum reconstruction
Abel et al. 2025
;
Bechtle et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Abel et al. 1992
. However, the SM contains other intrinsic two-level quantum degrees of freedom at the single-particle level: chirality and flavor.
We construct explicit binary operators associated with
them in particle-physics processes. These operators are defined through the structure of weak interactions and decay channels, coupled with direct projective mass measurements, and are therefore interpreted as effective observables at the operator level. This construction provides the ingredients needed for the Bell-type analyses developed in the following section.

III.1
Chirality

Unlike spin or polarization, chirality is not an externally chosen observable but is dynamically selected by the weak interaction itself. Thus, chirality provides a natural and experimentally relevant two-level system for high-energy Bell-type inequalities.

A relativistic fermion field is described by a Dirac spinor
\psi
, which may be decomposed into left- and right-handed components using the chiral projection operators

P_{L,R}=\frac{1}{2}(1\mp\gamma^{5}),

(10)

satisfying

P_{L}^{2}=P_{L},\quad P_{R}^{2}=P_{R},\quad P_{L}P_{R}=0.

(11)

The field decomposes as

\psi=\psi_{L}+\psi_{R},\qquad\psi_{L,R}=P_{L,R}\psi.

(12)

Chirality is therefore a fundamental operator-defined property of relativistic fermions.

It is important to distinguish chirality from helicity. Helicity is defined as the projection of spin onto the direction of momentum and is frame dependent for massive particles. Chirality, by contrast, is Lorentz covariant.
Of course in the massless limit,

m\to 0,~\text{chirality}=\text{helicity},

which is an excellent approximation for many collider processes where
E\gg m
. Nevertheless, it is chirality,
not helicity
, that enters the weak interaction.
From the perspective of quantum measurement theory, weak interactions themselves function as built-in analyzers of a binary observable.

The single-particle chirality Hilbert space is,

\{|L\rangle,|R\rangle\},

(13)

which is mathematically identical to that of the familiar two-level spin quantum system
\{|\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\rangle\}
.

Considering SM interactions, while QED and QCD interactions act as identity operators on this chirality space, the weak interactions are more interesting.
One may define a chirality identification operator, associated with the
Z\bar{\psi}\psi
interactions,

\hat{\mathcal{Z}_{C}}=|L\rangle\langle L|-|R\rangle\langle R|,

(14)

which distinguishes left and right handed fermion fields due to their different coupling strengths to the
Z
. Hence this operator has eigenstates and eigenvalues,

\hat{\mathcal{Z}_{C}}|L\rangle=+|L\rangle,\qquad\hat{\mathcal{Z}_{C}}|R\rangle=-|R\rangle\;.

(15)

Similarly, considering the Higgs-fermion interactions, we can define a chirality flip operator,

\hat{\mathcal{H}_{C}}=|L\rangle\langle R|+|R\rangle\langle L|,

(16)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}_{C}}(|L\rangle+|R\rangle)=+(|L\rangle+|R\rangle),

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}_{C}}(|L\rangle-|R\rangle)=-(|L\rangle-|R\rangle).

(18)

Note the similarity of Eqs. (
14
)-(
16
) with the action of the Pauli matrices
\sigma_{z}
and
\sigma_{x}
on a 2-state system. Hence these SM interactions precisely satisfy the requirements for Bell-type reasoning: binary observables with outcomes
\pm 1
.

On the other hand, charged weak interactions couple exclusively to left-handed fermions.
Right-handed fermions are completely inert under this interaction. Hence, charged weak processes act as effective projective measurements onto the left-handed subspace.

While chirality is not directly observable for asymptotic states, given the above, weak decays provide experimentally accessible observables that act as effective chirality analyzers. This follows for example from the purely left-handed structure of charged weak interactions.
Consider a generic fermion decay mediated by a charged weak current,

f\to f^{\prime}W^{(*)}\to f^{\prime}\ell\bar{\nu}_{\ell}\;.

(19)

The decay amplitude depends explicitly on the left-handed chiral component of the parent fermion. As a result, the angular distribution of the charged lepton in the parent rest frame takes the form
Group 2024

\frac{1}{\Gamma}\frac{d\Gamma}{d\cos\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\alpha\,\cos\theta\right),

(20)

where
\theta
is the angle between the charged lepton momentum and a chosen spin quantization axis, and
\alpha
is the analyzing power.
For purely left-handed couplings,
\alpha
is maximal (or near-maximal), implying that the charged lepton direction serves as a near-optimal analyzer of the parent fermion chirality.

From the measured angular distribution, one may define a binary observable

\mathcal{O}_{\chi}=\begin{cases}+1&\cos\theta>0,\\
-1&\cos\theta<0\;.\end{cases}

(21)

Operationally, this measurement realizes an effective binary observable correlated with the chiral coupling structure via polarization (helicity) analyzing power, rather than a direct measurement of chirality as an operator on asymptotic states.
Thus, decay kinematics may provide an experimental handle on the intrinsic two-level chirality degree of freedom.

Although the discussion above concerns single-particle wave functions, the implications extend immediately to multi-particle systems. A (properly normalized) generic single-particle state may be written as

|\psi\rangle=\alpha|L\rangle+\beta|R\rangle,

(22)

while a two-particle state takes the form

|\Psi\rangle=\alpha|LL\rangle+\beta|RR\rangle+\gamma|LR\rangle+\delta|RL\rangle.

(23)

Weak production processes naturally generate correlated or entangled chirality states, particularly favoring left-handed configurations for charged current interactions. This makes chirality an interesting property for entanglement studies in particle physics. However, as far as we are aware, there are no additional SM interactions that could be interpreted as operators for chirality that play the role of the spin mixing operator. As Bell showed
Bell 1964
, two orthogonal sets of measurements can always be due to a LHVT and therefore no violation can be observed of Eq. (
6
) in such a system.

III.2
Fermion Generations: Flavor and Mass

Similar to chirality, flavor (or generation) is not a conserved quantum number of the weak interactions in the SM. While the SM contains three generations of fermions, we restrict our attention here to the first two generations. The restriction to two generations is not an approximation to the SM but a deliberate projection onto a two-dimensional subspace. Hence, effective operators acting on a restricted flavor Hilbert space can be defined in a precise analogy with spin operators
in quantum mechanics. This mirrors the use of polarization qubits in quantum optics
Fabre and Treps 2020
;
Romero and Milburn 2025
and provides the minimal Hilbert space required for constructing Bell inequalities.

In the SM, flavor eigenstates diagonalize the interaction basis, while mass eigenstates
|m_{a}\rangle
diagonalize propagation. We do not distinguish between particle/anti-particle or up- vs. down-type fermions in each generation for defining our Hilbert space. Hence, for example, in the quark sector,
|m_{1}\rangle
labels any of the first generation quarks:
\{u,\bar{u},d,\bar{d}\}

Thus, while operators (interactions) are defined in flavor space, measurements project onto mass eigenstates. Restricting attention to two flavors, denoted
|f_{1}\rangle

|f_{2}\rangle

\left\{|f_{1}\rangle,|f_{2}\rangle\right\}\;,

Although flavor eigenstates are not directly observed asymptotically, decay channels provide effective projections onto flavor states. Importantly, these projections do not erase quantum correlations; they merely rotate
the basis in which they are observed. Correlations reconstructed from decay products retain
sensitivity to the underlying flavor operators.
Hence, in practice, flavor is identified through characteristic decay products and branching patterns.
For example, different generations of quarks and leptons decay into distinct final states with identifiable kinematic and particle-content signatures. Observation of a specific decay mode therefore corresponds to a projection onto a particular flavor subspace. Another example of this type of flavor subspace projections are neutrino oscillation experiments, in which flavor probabilities are reconstructed despite propagation in the mass basis.

Having defined the relevant single-particle Hilbert space, we now define

We define the Hermitian mass identification operator

\hat{F}_{ID}=\hat{\sigma}_{z}=|m_{1}\rangle\langle m_{1}|-|m_{2}\rangle\langle m_{2}|,

IV
Two-Particle Wave Functions

The minimal setting in which quantum entanglement can arise is a composite system consisting of two subsystems with internal degrees of freedom. As discussed above, in collider environments, natural and experimentally meaningful choices for such degrees of freedom could be chirality or particle flavor.

Chirality and flavor are distinct quantum numbers in the SM, but weak interactions correlate them in a nontrivial way. Hence they provide a natural mechanism by which entanglement could be generated, alleviating the need for external entangling operations.

Below we construct an explicit example of Bell-type inequalities using internal quantum numbers relevant to particle physics. Rather than relying on actively chosen detector settings, the observables considered here are defined through interaction contexts, i.e. the physical processes by which the system is probed. This formulation allows Bell-type inequalities to be used as operator-level diagnostics of local hidden-variable descriptions, while keeping the assumptions and limitations of such constructions explicit.

We will focus on the flavor entangled states, since as discussed in Sec.
III
we are not aware of a third chirality mixing operator in the SM. We reemphasize that even though measurements project onto mass eigenstates, correlations inherited from the initial flavor structure persist in the joint decay distributions. These correlations are precisely what enter Bell-type observables constructed in flavor space.

The Bell-type inequalities derived here rely on the assumption that such pair-wise measurements, collected across different interaction contexts, all originate from the same underlying quantum state prepared at production.

IV.2
Experimental Validation

Although formulated as a Gedanken experiment, the scenario considered here admits meaningful experimental validation. The essential requirement is not a loophole-free Bell test, but experimental access to the distinct interaction-defined observables entering the correlators—mass identification, flavor change, and charged-current mixing—on ensembles prepared in the same underlying quantum state. Experimentally, the correlations described in the previous section are obtained by pair-wise observations in which both subsystems are measured in the same event, with the interaction channel inferred from the observed decay and mass signatures.

As a concrete example, for the state
|\chi\rangle
discussed above, different runs which result in the pairwise observation of kaon decays with down-type quarks indicate measurements due to the
(\hat{F}_{F}\otimes\hat{F}_{ID})
operator. On the other hand, observations of kaon decays with up-type quarks indicate measurements due to the
(\hat{F}_{F}\otimes\hat{F}_{W})
operator, and observations of up- vs. down-type quarks indicate measurements due to the
(\hat{F}_{ID}\otimes\hat{F}_{W})
operator.
We emphasize that present experiments may not realize all assumptions of the idealized setting choices envisioned here; nevertheless, the Bell-type inequalities proposed here provide consistency conditions that any LHVT description of flavor observables must satisfy. Agreement with the predicted correlation pattern would support the operator-level description and entanglement structure assumed in this work, while significant deviations would indicate either experimental limitations or the need for an alternative description of flavor correlations beyond noncontextual local models.

Finally, we note that while we have constructed an explicit realization of Bell-type inequalities for the first and second generation of quarks, an analogous analysis may be undertaken for the first and second lepton generations, with a straightforward replacement of relevant parameters:
f_{1}:\{u,\bar{u},d,\bar{d}\}\rightarrow\{e^{-},e^{+},\nu_{e},\bar{\nu}_{e}\}
, etc. However, experimentally probing the lepton sector for Bell inequalities may be more challenging than for the quarks. Neutrinos are notoriously difficult to detect, muonium-antimuonium oscillations (while possible in the SM due to non-zero neutrino masses) have yet to be observed, and neutrinos don’t form bound states.

V
Discussion

The Standard Model stands as one of the most successful theories in physics, yet its unnatural features continue to challenge our understanding. Exploring ideas such as entanglement, operators, and information-theoretic concepts may provide new ways of thinking about long-standing puzzles.
Entanglement in particle physics remains largely unexplored, and the collider environment offer a unique laboratory for testing quantum correlations at the highest accessible energies.

The framework we have presented demonstrates that Bell-type inequalities can be meaningfully formulated in particle physics without direct spin measurements. Flavor and chirality provide natural binary observables intrinsic to the SM, even though measurements are performed on mass eigenstates.

The Bell-type inequalities proposed in this work are formulated in the spirit of a Gedanken experiment, intended to probe the incompatibility of flavor measurements rather than to realize a loophole-free test of spacetime non-locality. Measurement settings correspond to distinct SM interaction-defined operators—mass identification, flavor change, and charged-current weak mixing—that act as alternative measurement contexts on a common two-particle quantum state. Correlators are defined at the operator level prior to decay and mapped onto observable channels only afterward. Within this idealized framework, violations of Bell-type inequalities certify the necessity of entanglement and contextuality in the flavor sector, while remaining agnostic about experimental limitations.

Whether or not entanglement ultimately plays a fundamental role, asking these questions pushes us to re-examine what we mean by measurement, observables, and quantum structure in particle physics.
