Ischaemic strokes as an initial manifestation of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis with eosinophilic myocarditis: a case report
Authors: Lukas Ley, Christian Kirsch, Dirk Bandorski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEosinophilic myocarditis (EM) is a rare form of myocarditis which may be caused by eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), a rare disease which frequently affects the heart. The case of a young woman with a rare initial manifestation of EGPA as a cause of EM is reported.Case summaryA 39-year-old woman presented with diffuse neurological symptoms. She was diagnosed with multiple, bilateral, simultaneous ischaemic strokes. No obvious stroke origin was detectable. However, significantly elevated troponin levels and eosinophilia were noted. Cardiac computed tomography ruled out coronary artery disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) proved myocarditis. However, endomyocardial biopsy, did not confirm EM. Nevertheless, EGPA with primary cardiac involvement was diagnosed due to eosinophilia, history of asthma, evidence of pANCA and matching CMR findings. Treatment with benralizumab was initiated. Follow-up CMR after about 6 months showed a significant reduction in inflammatory activity.DiscussionEosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis and EM associated with EGPA are rare but in patients with EGPA, cardiac involvement is frequent. Ischaemic strokes as an initial manifestation of EGPA is particularly rare. Other potential causes of stroke were ruled out. In the presence of CMR-proven myocarditis associated with peripheral eosinophilia, EM may be diagnosed without histological confirmation. Endomyocardial biopsy may be false negative in a relevant number of cases but CMR is usually abnormal. However, there are no specific CMR findings that occur exclusively in EM.

Introduction

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a rare, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)-associated, small-vessel vasculitis. It is typically characterized by asthma, eosinophilia, and potential involvement of several organs.1 Cardiac involvement occurs in ∼25%–40% of patients and can manifest in a wide variety of forms, including eosinophilic myocarditis (EM), which is associated with a significantly worse prognosis than other forms of cardiac involvement.1–5 In general, EM is a rare form of myocarditis with a rate of in-hospital death of up to 22%.5 Besides EGPA, other causes such as hypersensitivity and allergic reactions, parasitic infections, hypereosinophilic syndrome, and cancer can also lead to EM.5

Case presentation

A 39-year-old woman presented with diffuse neurological symptoms (new-onset gait disorder, instability, cephalgia, and photopsia) for more than 72 h. Except for slowed speech and impaired orientation, and an unstable gait, the physical examination was unremarkable. Her medical history included arterial hypertension, hypothyroidism, ulcerative colitis, chronic pancreatitis, psoriasis, polyarthralgia, and borderline personality disorder. Significantly elevated troponin T levels (261.0 and 254.0 ng/L) and eosinophilia (23.1%) were noted, with normal kidney function (estimated glomerular filtration rate: 80.2 mL/min/1.73 m2). The subsequent cranial magnetic resonance imaging study showed multiple bilateral simultaneous ischaemic strokes. Significant stenoses of supra-aortic arteries, cardiac arrhythmias, cardiac thrombi, endocarditis, and patent foramen ovale were ruled out. Because of atypical chest pain, dyspnoea on exertion (New York Heart Association II), high troponin levels, and new-onset T-wave inversions in II, III, aVF, and V4–V6, coronary artery disease was considered despite a low risk factor-weighted clinical likelihood. After rule out by cardiac computed tomography, myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries was diagnosed. However, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed echogenic thickening of the left ventricular apical endomyocardium and a conspicuous non-contrasted area in projection onto the apical endomyocardium after contrast medium injection (Figure 1). Left ventricular systolic function was preserved [left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF): 62%] without any wall motion abnormalities or significant valvular heart disease.

The subsequent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) study showed subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and subendocardial oedema, with apical predominance (Figure 2). Left ventricular volumes (end-diastolic: 117.9 mL, end-systolic: 47.4 mL, stroke volume: 70.5 mL), LVEF (59.8%), and myocardial mass (68.3 g/m2) were within normal ranges. Native T1 and T2 mapping data of the left ventricle showed diffusely elevated myocardial T1 and T2 values across all 16 myocardial segments consistent with diffuse myocardial injury and oedema (see Supplementary material online, Figures S1 and S2), while extracellular volume calculations were not available.

Based on these findings and the presence of eosinophilia, EM was suspected. Accordingly, right ventricular endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) was performed. However, histological examination did not confirm EM. Meanwhile, asthma was diagnosed because of dyspnoea on exertion, bronchial hyperresponsiveness and eosinophilia. Due to the absence of toxic influences, pronounced eosinophilia (23.1%), asthma, evidence of pANCA (1:320) and MPO-ANCA (43 IU/mL), and matching CMR findings, EGPA with cardiac involvement, in the form of EM, was diagnosed. Therefore, treatment with benralizumab (30 mg, subcutaneously, every 4 weeks) was initiated. Follow-up CMR after about 6 months showed a significant reduction in inflammatory activity (Figures 3 and 4, Supplementary material online, Figures S3 and S4).

Discussion

The present report describes the case of a 39-year-old woman who presented with diffuse neurological symptoms and multiple, bilateral, simultaneous strokes. A thorough diagnostic process led to the rare diagnosis of EM in the context of cardiac involvement in EGPA. However, ischaemic strokes as the initial manifestation leading to EGPA diagnosis are particularly rare.1,6,7

The mechanism underlying the ischaemic strokes in the present case cannot be definitively proven and may have been heterogeneous. A cardioembolic mechanism was initially considered because cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed multiple, bilateral, simultaneous ischaemic lesions in the white matter of both cerebral hemispheres not confined to a single vascular territory. Moreover, EM may cause endomyocardial injury and mural thrombus formation with subsequent embolization.7,8 However, extensive diagnostic work-up did not reveal a definite cardioembolic source: no intracardiac thrombus was detected, left ventricular systolic function was preserved, no wall motion abnormalities were present, and arrhythmias, endocarditis, and a patent foramen ovale were excluded. Nevertheless, a transient embolization from an undetected small endomyocardial thrombus cannot be entirely excluded. Cerebral vasculitic involvement represents another potential stroke mechanism, as central nervous system involvement in EGPA, although rare, may manifest as ischaemic stroke.6 However, in the present patient, the distribution of ischaemic lesions was not suggestive of a primarily vasculitic aetiology. Furthermore, the cranial MRI did not reveal imaging features suggestive of cerebral vasculitic involvement. A prothrombotic thromboinflammatory state related to eosinophilia may provide another plausible explanation.9 In this context, the simultaneous occurrence of marked eosinophilia and multifocal bilateral ischaemic lesions support this mechanism, potentially in combination with transient cardiac embolization. Accordingly, the underlying stroke mechanism was considered multifactorial rather than attributable to a single proven mechanism.

Anticoagulation was not initiated, as extensive diagnostic work-up did not identify a persistent cardioembolic substrate. In particular, no ventricular thrombi were detected, left ventricular systolic function was preserved, and there were no regional wall motion abnormalities. Moreover, no recurrent ischaemic strokes occurred after the initial events, and severe eosinophilia was only transient. Given the limited evidence supporting anticoagulation in this particular setting, the multidisciplinary team decided against anticoagulation.

In the present case EM associated with EGPA was diagnosed in the absence of histological confirmation, based on eosinophilia, asthma, evidence of pANCA, and matching CMR findings. Current ESC guidelines on inflammatory myopericardial syndrome state that ‘the diagnosis of EM can be established by either the EMB-proven presence of eosinophilic infiltration of the myocardium, or the presence of CMR-proven myocarditis with pathognomonic findings associated with peripheral eosinophilia’.5 This is due to the focal distribution and the diffuse patchy nature of EM that may result in a relevant number of false negatives with EMB.8 This may also have been the case with this patient, as only five biopsies were taken only from the right ventricle. However, CMR shows abnormalities in almost all patients with EGPA and cardiac involvement.3 In the present patient CMR showed subendocardial LGE and oedema. Therefore, myocarditis was CMR-proven according to updated Lake Louise criteria.5 However, a predominantly subendocardial pattern is not typical for myocarditis and more commonly suggests acute myocardial ischaemia. Nevertheless, relevant coronary artery disease was excluded, and the distribution of LGE and myocardial oedema did not correspond to a specific coronary artery territory. Most of the patients with EGPA and EM present with myocardial oedema and LGE. Late gadolinium enhancement is usually located subendocardial and multifocal but may involve any myocardial layer.3,8,10 Therefore, CMR findings were considered consistent with EM rather than ischaemic myocardial injury.

Because there are no specific CMR findings that occur exclusively in EM, diagnosis of EM associated with EGPA still relies on clinical findings, laboratory tests, and/or EMB in addition to CMR-proven myocarditis. In the present case, LGE was located in the typical region. Moreover, TTE (Figure 1) revealed echogenic thickening of the left ventricular apical endomyocardium and a conspicuous non-contrasted area in projection onto the apical endomyocardium after contrast medium injection. This corresponded well to the CMR-proven myocardial oedema (Figure 2). Furthermore, follow-up CMR after about 6 months showed a significant reduction in inflammatory activity (Figures 3 and 4).

No specific heart failure therapy was initiated, as the patient did not meet diagnostic criteria for heart failure. Furthermore, corticosteroids were not initiated during the acute phase, as the diagnosis of EGPA-associated EM had not yet been established and an infectious aetiology could not be reliably excluded before the biopsy results were available. Benralizumab was later recommended by rheumatology specialists as long-term treatment for EGPA and is currently being continued.

In conclusion, EM is a rare form of myocarditis that may be caused by EGPA, a rare disease that nevertheless frequently affects the heart. Rarely, EGPA-associated EM may be diagnosed through untypical manifestations, such as ischaemic strokes. In the presence of CMR-proven myocarditis associated with peripheral eosinophilia and typical clinical findings, EM may be diagnosed without histological confirmation.
