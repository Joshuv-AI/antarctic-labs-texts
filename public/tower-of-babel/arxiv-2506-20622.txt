Behavioral Traits as Dynamical Systems: Utilizing Entropy to Analyze Longitudinal Psychometric Data in Coupled Ordinary Differential Equations
Anderson M. Rodriguez

Abstract
Traits such as neuroticism persist across species despite exhibiting characteristics typically regarded as maladaptive. This project presents an alternative model for understanding the stability of such traits by integrating findings from the Swedish Adoption/Twin Study on Aging [ 1 ] (SATSA) with a system of recursive, biologically inspired ordinary differential equations (ODEs). To utilize the ODEs analytically, Shannon entropy is extracted from longitudinal Likert-scale psychometric data, enabling the translation of high-dimensional behavioral responses into continuous-time dynamical systems. The model incorporates principles from evolutionary biology, including mutation-selection balance, genetic pleiotropy and metabolic constraints, and embeds environmental feedback as a recursive driver of phenotypic expression. The argument is presented that traits such as neuroticism exist not stochastically, but as emergent multistable attractors within a biologically-constrained system. This paper shows that entropy extracted from longitudinal psychometric data can be meaningfully modeled using recursive ordinary differential equations, revealing stable dynamical attractors and biologically and environmentally grounded constraints in traits often deemed maladaptive. This framework offers a scalable, mathematically grounded foundation for analyzing phenotypic expression, with the ultimate goal of biological extension for eventual multi-omic modeling of behavioral traits.

Introduction

Evolutionary processes operate through cycles of variation and selection, constrained by genetic architecture, energetic trade-offs, and environmental limitations
[
2
]
. While this principle is widely accepted in population genetics and systems biology
[
3
]
, it has less frequently
[
4
,
5
]
been applied to the modeling of behavioral and psychometric traits as formal dynamical systems, and indeed it has sometimes been argued that population level data obscures the complexities of individual behavior when analyzing psychometric data at the group-level
[
6
]
. This paper introduces a mathematical framework that treats psychometric traits, such as neuroticism, as emergent features of recursive evolutionary dynamics, observable at the population-level through information-theoretic translation of longitudinal psychological data.

While previous studies have applied entropy metrics to psychometric data, literature review found no comparable biologically-constrained system of differential equations yet developed for the continuous, mechanistic analysis of population-level psychometric flux.

The longitudinal psychometric data is explored through a constructed set of biologically inspired, coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations (ODEs), informed by mutation-selection balance, pleiotropy, metabolic constraints, and environmental feedback. To translate high-dimensional behavioral data into a form compatible with continuous-time modeling, Shannon entropy was extracted from longitudinal Likert-scale psychometric responses. This extraction process and compression process transforms categorical behavioral data into an entropic informational metric, enabling the construction of trait dynamics as a dynamical system.

Here, the term “recursive” is used in the dynamical systems sense: trait expression both influences, and is influenced by, genetic, metabolic, and environmental factors across time. While recursion is often treated as a computational or algorithmic property, in this context it reflects self-reinforcing feedback mechanisms that stabilize traits as multistable attractors within an evolving system.

Although the present work focuses on longitudinal psychometric data drawn from the Swedish Adoption/Twin Study of Aging (SATSA), the underlying framework is generalizable. By treating entropy as a structural feature of trait expression, this paper proposes a system in which behavioral traits can be analyzed within a unified dynamical model. This mirrors arguments made in epigenetics and disease modeling (e.g., Haslberger et al., 2006), but extends them to trait evolution at both individual and population levels.

While this paper focuses on an initial application of the process to longitudinal psychometric data, it does so in service of investigating general evolutionary dynamics as a recursive process, bound by self-and-environmentally-imposed structural constraints and mutation-selection balance. Although the focus is on psychometric data, the argument is developed that the concepts presented here are generally and biologically relevant and that traits such a neuroticism, emotional responses, and stress resilience have known genetic components but are also expressed not just on the individual level, but on a collective psychological level in a coherent system.

Ultimately, this paper argues that evolutionary dynamics are not merely nonlinear but structurally recursive, and that the integration of entropy-based modeling with biological constraint terms provides a scalable, predictive framework for understanding the evolution and persistence of complex traits.

2
Methods for Data Cleaning and Comparative Approach Analysis

Data used in this study were gathered from the Inter-University Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR). The data originates from a publicly available program of research in gerontological genetics known as the Swedish Adoption/Twin Study of Aging (SATSA, or, ICPSR 3483), which began in 1984. SATSA is a longitudinal study exploring genetic and environmental influences on aging, especially cognitive and personality traits. It does so by comparing twins reared apart (TRA) with those reared together (TRT). Data were gathered from mail-in questionnaires as well as through in person cognitive and physical testing. Only results from the questionnaires are under consideration for the models presented. Respondents were surveyed on questions including health status, personal histories, drug/alcohol intake, dietary habits, and questions regarding personality, habits, and attitudes.

For this presentation, data up until 2007 was analyzed. Participants at trial start were n
\sim
1,700, while that number reduced to roughly one-third of that initial cohort by the end of the survey period. Considering that the survey took place over 20 years with no additional members being added to the survey, the decrease in responding population is expected as natural attrition due to age.

Survey samples were collected in six distinct waves (1984, 1987, 1993, 2003, 2007). Survey questions were sampled by the author to act as empirical phenotypic and epigenetic psychological examples for a toy model generated in service of refining the general model. Future iterations of the model will further analyze both psychological data sets as depicted here, as well more targeted genomic data. After genomic iterative testing, an ultimate vision is for multi-omic integration.

Author selected measures were taken across traits generalizable as:
Worry–”I often worry” ;
Curiosity–”I like to think about theories and/or profound ideas” ;
Fulfillment–”When I look back on my life I realize that the most
important expectations have not been fulfilled” ;
Hot-Temperedness–”People think I am hot-tempered and temperamental” ;
Satisfaction–”I often feel satisfied” ;
Indignancy–”I get worried and indignant from everyday events” ;
Depressive Tendencies–”I feel ”blue” or depressed more often than others”.

Each of the above traits was measured via a structured psychometric instrument (the original Likert-scale survey in the study) with five sub-items per trait which explored the gradient to which the subject felt they agreed with the presented question tied to an associated emotional state, characteristic, or phenomena (’exactly right’; ’almost right’; ’neither’; ’not quite right’; ’not right at all’).

Utilizing Shannon Entropy was deemed appropriate as an investigate tool as a higher order extension of the ‘complex system’ of: the relationship the respondent had to the survey itself, over many years, regarding intimate details entangled with fallible memory, potential distortions in self-assessment; as well as the nature of the initial composition of the questions and survey themselves by a team of researchers.

A central justification for the use of Shannon entropy in this framework follows directly from the maximum entropy principle formulated by E. T. Jaynes
[
8
]
. In this interpretation, entropy is not merely a metaphorical stand-in for disorder, but a principled inference tool reflecting the most unbiased distribution consistent with known constraints. Rather than assuming the psychological trait system is fully observable, the entropy values here are treated as compressed, population-level state descriptors encoding uncertainty under biological and environmental constraints.

This aligns the present framework with the foundational logic of statistical mechanics and information theory, recasting trait entropy not as an after-the-fact summary, but as a governing component of the system’s dynamics. Consequently, this approach establishes a rigorous link between population-level trait data and information-theoretic concepts, providing a robust framework for examining the evolving dynamics of the psychometric ecosystem from the SATSA data, which spanned from 1984 to 2007. The implications of this framework suggest that it reflects broader patterns within Sweden’s psychometric ecosystem during that period.

2.2
A Note on Entropy: Classical vs Information-Theoretic Perspectives

It is worth noting that classical thermodynamics is, in many respects, a limiting special case in the broader landscape of entropy-based modeling.

Rooted in equilibrium assumptions and linear approximations, classical thermodynamics was developed under conditions optimized for closed, tractable systems, such as heat engines or ideal gases. This perspective historically constrained entropy to represent decay, disorder, or inevitable equilibrium
[
9
,
10
]
. However, in the broader contexts of biological, cognitive, and ecological systems, these assumptions often fail
[
11
]
, and as Jørgensen et al. (2000) argue, even frameworks like Maximum Entropy Production (MEP) fail to match empirical ecological scaling laws, indicating that entropy-driven models must be integrated with system-specific constraints and organizational dynamics
[
12
]
.

As Jaynes and others have emphasized, entropy can be more appropriately interpreted as a measure of uncertainty or diversity in possible system configurations, rather than as a synonym for disorder
[
8
]
. From this perspective, classical thermodynamics emerges not as a general law of nature, but as a special, highly constrained domain within a much richer framework of entropy-driven dynamics—especially when applied to adaptive, nonlinear, or informationally open systems like those modeled in this study.

Although sharing formal similarities, Shannon entropy and Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy arise from different foundational contexts and imply distinct system dynamics
[
13
,
8
]
. Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy originates in classical statistical mechanics and quantifies the number of microstates consistent with a given macrostate, typically under assumptions of energy conservation and equilibrium. In contrast, Shannon entropy was developed in the context of information theory to measure the expected uncertainty (or information content) of a probabilistic distribution, without requiring physical constraints. While Boltzmann entropy is often treated as an extensive thermodynamic quantity, Shannon entropy is agnostic to underlying physical substance and is instead concerned with state probabilities themselves. As Jaynes (1957) demonstrated, both can be unified under the principle of maximum entropy inference, but their interpretations diverge when applied to open, adaptive systems.

In the present framework, Shannon entropy is used to track the compression of behavioral diversity in psychometric distributions over time. This interpretation reflects not decay into equilibrium, but rather a dynamic reconfiguration of informational complexity, allowing for biological and cognitive modeling that extends beyond the closed-world assumptions of classical thermodynamics.

2.3
Calculating Shannon Entropy

Shannon Entropy is calculated in the standard manner
[
13
]
:

To quantify the distributional uncertainty of trait responses over time, we compute the Shannon entropy of each trait’s categorical frequency distribution. Let
\vec{x}_{t}=[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
denote the raw category counts for a trait at time
t
, where
n=5
response categories (e.g., ”Very Low” to ”Very High”). We first normalize
\vec{x}_{t}
to obtain a probability distribution:

\vec{p}_{t}=\left[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\right],\quad\text{where }p_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}}

The Shannon entropy
H(\vec{p}_{t})
is then calculated as:

H(\vec{p}_{t})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\log_{2}p_{i}

Entropy is measured in bits. The summation is defined only over nonzero
p_{i}
(i.e.,
p_{i}>0
), since
p_{i}\log_{2}p_{i}\to 0
as
p_{i}\to 0
by continuity.

2.4
Theoretical Range and Meaning

The Shannon entropy
H
of a discrete distribution with
n
categories lies in the range:

0\leq H\leq\log_{2}(n)

H=0
: Perfect order: all responses fall into a single category (no uncertainty).

H=\log_{2}(n)
: Maximum disorder: responses are evenly distributed across all categories.

For
n=5
, the theoretical maximum is:

H_{\text{max}}=\log_{2}(5)\approx 2.32\text{ bits}

2.5
Interpretation in Psychological and Behavioral Genetics Contexts

In the context of psychological trait assessment, the entropy
H(\vec{p}_{t})
quantifies the distributional complexity or ambiguity of how individuals in the population endorse a trait:

•

High Entropy (
\approx 2.3
bits): Indicates divergent or ambiguous population-level expression of the trait. Suggests no clear consensus and may reflect trait heterogeneity, cultural polarization, or measurement noise.

•

Moderate Entropy (
\approx 1.5
bits): Indicates a spread of opinion or distribution, but with some central tendency. This is typical of traits under polygenic influence with environmental modulation.

•

Low Entropy (
\lesssim 1.0
bits): Indicates strong convergence or polarization in the population. Most respondents choose similar trait levels. This may reflect:

–

True trait convergence (e.g., normative shifts)

–

Reduced environmental variance

–

Strong heritability and/or strong selection pressure

Figure 3:
Shannon entropy of three traits isolated from the set under review: Worry, Curiosity, and Fulfillment—across six SATSA survey years (1984–2007). While all traits show slight convergence, Worry displays transient re-expansion in the early 1990s, followed by a downward trend. Curiosity remains the most stable across the period.

It is important to note that while low entropy may reflect population-level convergence on a trait, this does not necessarily indicate psychological homogeneity at the individual level. Rather, it reflects a compression of response diversity which may result from cultural, developmental, or ecological influences. Phenomenologically, this is in line with what would be expected from a dynamic system.

2.6
Relevance of Shannon Entropy to Behavioral Trait Expression in a Population

Entropy provides a complementary tool to variance decomposition. While heritability estimates describe the proportion of variance explained by genetics, entropy characterizes the structure of that variance across categorical trait levels. Entropy dynamics over time can detect the emergence of trait stratification, or adaptive convergence. Sudden shifts or non-monotonic entropy trends may indicate environmentally-driven phase transitions (such as bifurcations) or the breakdown of prior genetic-environmental equilibria, all of which work in favor of dynamical systems understandings
[
14
]
.

By translating the population-level psychometric Likert-scale data into Shannon entropy, the investigator gains a robust, interpretable, and mathematically principled way to monitor the collective distributional dynamics of traits over time. This serves as a useful companion to statistical tools in psychology and behavioral genetics, especially when applied in conjunction with nonlinear dynamics, stability analysis, and entropy-based modeling frameworks.

The method and approach of the ODEs were devised as a way of modeling personality trait dynamics within a ’psychometric ecosystem’. The argument is that if psychological traits are considered as dynamical states with nested relative dynamical points of chaotic stability (multistable attractors points where systems tend), then traits like neuroticism, which are currently treated as maladaptive, may more accurately be considered as dynamically evolving states nested within a complex system. This model sets up future inquiry of the genomic concept by starting at the ‘layer’ of pyschological data. With this consideration, information compression is arguably justified in that, while a relatively small sample size, n=7, each data point represents a high density of information as each node consists of a subject pool n 1,700 at survey start, with five associated variations per question, all of which were considered six times over more than twenty years. This is rich information density per point.

2.7
Comparable Approaches

Several approaches have been proposed to model the evolution or persistence of psychological traits, but leveraging formal dynamical systems rooted in systems biology is relatively rare. Traditional behavioral approaches, such as those found behavioral genetics or psychology toolsets focus on variance decomposition and heritability estimates derived from twin or family designs, as well as on qualitative psychological assessments, respectively. These models can be statistically powerful, as well as analytically informative, but typically lack recursive temporal dynamics which embed the system-under-review in a realistic environment. A lack of such environmental considerations can at times lead to replicative difficulties
[
15
,
16
]
, particularly in behavioral and psychiatric research, where trait expression is highly context-sensitive.

In psychology, dynamic systems theory has occasionally been used to describe developmental trajectories or emotional regulation patterns
[
4
,
5
]
, but these implementations typically rely on qualitative descriptions, symbolic representations, or low-dimensional visualizations rather than formal differential modeling frameworks grounded in biological constraints.

Additionally, psychophysics, which, ”usually leads to some mathematical representation of mapping from inputs to outputs, in which the environment is given and the behavior is dependent and the mapping is written outside time, being static and not dynamic,” has seen approaches dating back to at least 1860, with Gustav Fechner’s ”Elements of Psychophysics’
[
17
]
. Gregson, 2008, gives a good overview on psychophysics, as well as their own approach developed beginning in 1988. Gregson’s approach uses a discrete-time, complex cubic recursion but in contrast to the approach developed in this paper, their approach is phenomenological, not biologically driven, with chaotic structure emerging from iteration in the complex plane (the real part of the equation mapping to observable response; the imaginary part of the equation maps to internal fluctuation). Additionally, Gregson’s approach can be considered as more theoretical than empirical.

While traditional psychophysics approaches are subject to classic arguments between physicists and psychologists ”about the measurability of sensation… and about the additivity or nonadditivity of levels of reported sensation… what is actually done in psychophysics is to match judgments, of magnitude or probability, made in various forms of choice or discrimination against increments of stimulus activity levels, usually of intensity or duration,”
[
17
]
. This is on a different scale of domain size and rigor than the approach which this paper develops.

Likewise, a conceptually similar but ontologically distinct avenue of approach can also be found in frameworks for collective intelligence, such as Sulis, 2008
[
18
]
. While considering the population level domain, the approach of Sulis (2008) is focused more on emergent behavior exhibited by social insects rather than human or animal psychological traits.

Regarding the use of Shannon entropy, entropy and dynamical systems have been explored in behavioral science using approaches such as state space grids
[
19
,
20
]
, Markovian transition models
[
21
]
, and orbital decomposition
[
22
]
. These methods provide valuable insights into real-time interaction patterns, symbolic dynamics, and entropy-based measures of coherence and Pincus gives a solid overview of these approaches while addressing the philosophical question of ”what is psychotherapy?”
[
23
]
and addressing ’metaorder’ in psychotherapeutic systems
[
24
]
. However, the aforementioned entropy and meta-system acknowledging approaches lack a formal biological or evolutionary basis, and do not integrate entropy as a functional input into continuous-time models. In contrast, the present framework treats entropy not only as a descriptive measure, but as a compressed state variable embedded within a system of biologically grounded differential equations.

In terms of biological systems modeling, work by Kaneko and collaborators
[
2
,
25
]
, applies nonlinear ODEs to gene regulatory networks and cell differentiation. These models demonstrate how stability and bifurcation emerge at the biological level from feedback and constraint structures–the same principles this paper analyzes in the population level domain of behavioral traits.

To the author’s knowledge, no published model has unified these approaches by treating psychometric trait evolution as a recursive, entropy-driven dynamical system informed by biological constraint terms. The following presented framework seeks to bridge this gap.

To sum up: ’Psychophysics’-style models typically focus on sensory-cognitive dynamics within abstract or complex-valued recursive maps (Gregson); collective intelligence approaches work on population-level domains but with differing analytical focus; Shannon entropy-assessing models have been developed but limited to the realm of discrete-analysis, or nonbiological, theoretic-minded models, or psychotherapeutic models; and existing biological systems modeling is currently focused in the realms of gene regulatory networks
[
25
]
, as well as bifurcation-driven transitions and slow-control variables in pattern formation and developmental trajectories
[
26

.

This positions the following approach developed in this paper as intellectually adjacent, but more biologically integrated, empirically grounded, more general, and more extendable than similar but ontologically distinct approaches. It can be considered that the approached developed here is a natural follow up to the preceding approaches, as qualitative visualization and symbolic entropy, while both previously utilized, are here extended to nonlinear differential modeling grounded in dynamical biological considerations.

3
Methods for the Proposed Ordinary Differential Equation System

The following section proposes and defines a system of coupled nonlinear differential equations to model the evolution of psychometric traits in a population, incorporating mutation, selection, pleiotropy, and environmental feedback. The variables, in current form, represent population-level entropy-derived metrics of trait expression over time. The models are set up, currently, to assess a phenotypic behavioral trait as it may be expressed in the entropy of the ’psychometric ecosystem’.

This system introduces an emergent feedback mechanism: traits influence stress, which feeds back into metabolic cost, which constrains trait persistence. The system is recursive, entropy-driven, and sensitive to parameter variation. This enables analysis of bifurcation, stability, and long-term trait evolution under realistic biological constraint conditions.

3.1
Selection Pressure Dynamics

\frac{dN}{dt}=\mu N-s(N)\cdot N,\quad\text{where}\quad s(N)=\alpha\cdot N

(1)

This equation models the dynamics of neuroticism-like traits
N
, where the trait increases via mutation (
\mu N
) but is constrained by a selection cost that scales with trait expression amounting, essentially, to: (
\alpha N^{2}
) but left in general form, above.

This portion of the model creates a natural self-limiting mechanism similar to logistic growth but biologically interpreted as fitness cost accumulation. This argues that a trait such as neuroticism grows via mutation but may stabilize in a population as part of a self-limiting process due to selection-cost.

3.2
Pleiotropic Trait Dynamics

\frac{dP}{dt}=\mu P-\beta P\cdot\left(\frac{E_{\text{metabolic}}}{G}\right)

(2)

E_{\text{metabolic}}=c_{1}P+c_{2}N+c_{3}E_{\text{stress}}

(3)

Here,
P
represents pleiotropic traits that carry nontrivial metabolic costs. The term
\mu P
represents mutational introduction of new traits, while the second term represents suppression via metabolic constraints. The effective energy cost
E_{\text{metabolic}}
is composed of additive burdens from pleiotropy, neuroticism, and stress exposure, normalized by total metabolic capacity
G
. This is interpretable as: more traits = more energy costs = more suppression of trait expression.

The inclusion of metabolic factors is inspired by ecological systems approaches, including (but ontologically distinct from): Dynamical Energy Budget theory (DEB) and the West, Brown, Enquist model
[
27
]
, as well as by systems genetic approaches such as Civelek & Lusis (2014)
[
28
]

This pleiotropic term is here modeled heuristically at the level of population level psychometric data, so traits such as ’hot-headedness’ may be treated by the ODE system as a putative ’Neurotic’ trait while a trait such as ’tendency for worry’ may be heuristically assessed as an associated pleiotropic trait. This approach is based on behavioral genetical analytic approaches, such as Blizard et al. (2023) who utilize Maudsley rat and Genome Wide Associated Studies (GWAS) to link neuroticism with conditions like Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) and anxiety
[
29
]
. While the approach put forth in this paper is heuristic and currently limited to population level psychometric data, the chosen data points for Neurotic-like traits were carefully associated with biologically realistic potential pleiotropic traits.

While Blizard et al. do not directly frame generational neurotic recurrence as ’stability’, per se, their findings suggest that the persistence of neuroticism is regulated by both genetic and environmental factors, which in turn maintains the trait’s expression across generations.

3.3
Environmental Stress Dynamics

\frac{dE_{\text{stress}}}{dt}=\gamma E_{\text{stress}}\cdot\left(\frac{N}{N+K}\right)

(4)

Stress levels
E_{\text{stress}}
are modeled as increasing due to neuroticism expression, modulated by a saturation constant
K
to prevent unbounded growth. This form reflects a positive feedback loop: as neurotic traits accumulate, individuals may generate or perceive more environmental stress, reinforcing trait expression indirectly via pleiotropic energy cost coupling. While typically it is expected that environment influences the trait, this system argues that both influence each other in an emergent, recursive feedback loop dynamic.

Core Dynamical System Overview

Selection Pressure Dynamics (Neurotic-like Trait):

\frac{dN}{dt}=\mu N-s(N)\cdot N,\quad\text{where}\quad s(N)=\alpha\cdot N

(5)

Trait
N
increases via mutation and is constrained by an endogenous selection cost proportional to its own magnitude, producing a self-limiting dynamic analogous to logistic growth with biological interpretation as fitness cost accumulation.

Pleiotropic Trait Dynamics:

\frac{dP}{dt}=\mu P-\beta P\cdot\left(\frac{E_{\text{metabolic}}}{G}\right)

(6)

E_{\text{metabolic}}=c_{1}P+c_{2}N+c_{3}E_{\text{stress}}

(7)

Trait
P
represents pleiotropic expressions that are suppressed by increasing energetic burden. These burdens arise from interactions with neuroticism (
N

P

\frac{dE_{\text{stress}}}{dt}=\gamma E_{\text{stress}}\cdot\left(\frac{N}{N+K}\right)

Environmental stress increases as a function of neuroticism, with saturation introduced via the constant

4
Results

4.1
Shannon Entropy Profiles of Trait Dynamics

Trait-wise, of the initially analyzed 7 traits, entropy values revealed non-random but nonlinear evolution across the measured decades. With the notable exception of ’satisfaction’, entropy decreased for all measured traits from 1984 through 2007, implying a transient phase of convergence for most of the measured traits, but with notable divergence in the satisfaction measures of the population-level distribution. While these psychometric traits are not biological data, per se, the measured entropy was here assessed as representative of a sort of biologically-determined global system ’curvature’ (drawing to mind the geometry of dynamical systems), and the entropy measures were utilized in service of analyzing and tuning the ODE model.

4.4
Interpretation of Model Validation Scores and Comparative Significance

The evaluation metrics obtained from the present model—particularly the RMSE,
R^{2}
, and Pearson correlation coefficients—demonstrate a level of predictive alignment between simulated and empirical dynamics that, while preliminary, is highly atypical within the behavioral sciences. Given that the present model is a first-generation system of coupled differential equations derived from biologically inspired dynamics, and applied to empirical entropy-processed longitudinal data without overfitting, this raises key implications for the modeling of psychological traits.

To contextualize these results, it is necessary to briefly review standard expectations for model performance in behavioral science.

Unlike many traditional longitudinal models in personality psychology, which often focus on parameter estimation without emphasizing predictive accuracy
[
32
]
, the present framework explicitly reports out-of-sample validation metrics, including
R^{2}
, RMSE, and Pearson correlation. In much of the behavioral sciences, there is no consensus or standardized threshold for what constitutes a “good” predictive fit; many studies report only inferential statistics or treat model fit qualitatively. This lack of normed benchmarks makes quantitative comparisons difficult.

By contrast, the coupled ODE system presented here demonstrates stable, interpretable dynamics with notably high
R^{2}
values (demonstrated up to 0.76 for neuroticism entropy).

While the absence of field-wide standards complicates direct benchmarking, this work contributes to establishing such norms by introducing physically interpretable, biologically grounded modeling with explicit fit evaluation as a core design feature.

Why These Scores Matter

The primary claim is not simply that the present model achieves some metric of score, but that it does so while demonstrating a generative biological mechanism, which: embeds mutation, selection, pleiotropic cost, and environmental stress into a dynamical framework; maps continuous-time differential changes to empirically-derived entropy values; and aligns the predicted time trajectory with observed macro-scale trait ecosystem patterns.

These features distinguish the present model from black-box forecasting techniques and from descriptive-only psychological entropy studies. While further empirical refinement is ongoing, these results suggest that the structure of psychometric variation may follow lawful dynamics governed by constraint structures, rather than random fluctuation.

4.7
Assessment of Overfitting Risk

Given the small sample size and high model performance (especially for the neuroticism proxy), it is necessary to address the possibility of overfitting.

The risk of overfitting in empirical dynamical systems is typically associated with (a) high parameter count relative to data points, (b) flexible or non-deterministic model architectures (e.g., neural nets), and (c) absence of theoretical constraints guiding model structure
[
35
]
. The current work mitigates these risks through the following design principles:

Low Degrees of Freedom: The system comprises a minimal, interpretable set of coupled ordinary differential equations (ODEs), constrained by biological and metabolic reasoning. All terms (e.g., pleiotropic cost, mutation rate, stress amplification) are modeled with fixed functional forms and biological motivation, which ultimately reduces parameter arbitrariness.

Physically-Informed Parameter Bounds: The parameter search space was bounded within biologically plausible ranges for mutation rates, metabolic costs, and stress dynamics, informed by literature from evolutionary biology, behavioral ecology, and population genetics. The parameter space was not exhaustively searched or tuned through brute-force optimization as the L-BFGS-B algorithm is efficient and minimizes the objective function given the bounds (biologically motivated) and constraints specified. No machine learning pipeline was used as the biologically motivated bounds offer advantage to standard ML approaches here,
[
36
,
37
]
, and no automated hyper-parameter optimization (e.g., grid search, Bayesian optimization) was performed.

Independent Trait Targets: The system simulates trait entropy trajectories using only the internal state variables of the model. No direct fitting was performed on the final time series beyond computation of validation metrics (e.g., RMSE,
R^{2}
, Pearson
r
). The entropy values used for evaluation were computed independently from the raw trait distribution data and were not part of the ODE model formulation.

Lack of Post-Hoc Tuning: Once the core model form was established, parameter adjustments were made conservatively and manually, with the goal of producing plausible biological behavior, not maximizing scores. Several parameter sets are reported, including weaker ones, to illustrate model robustness across a local neighborhood of parameter values. Future work will explore further modes of parameterization.

Cross-Validation in Future Work: Though the present dataset size (the entropy of seven behavioral traits over six time points) limits cross-validation procedures, future work will incorporate rolling validation windows and apply the model to other longitudinal datasets. The ultimate test of generalizability will come from reusing the same functional structure on entirely different trait time series (e.g., different countries, traits, or age groups).

5
Discussion and Future Work

The results presented here demonstrate that even relatively simple biologically inspired differential systems can yield predictive fits for longitudinal psychometric entropy data, particularly for traits such as neuroticism. The coupled dynamics of pleiotropy, metabolic constraint, and environmental stress reveal emergent attractor behaviors that appear consistent with trait stabilization over time. These results support the hypothesis that behavioral traits may be governed by feedback mechanisms akin to those found in regulatory biological networks.

Notably, the nonlinear interaction terms in the model allow for bounded yet flexible responses to environmental pressures. In particular, the strong fit observed under parameter set 4 (with the inclusion of an environmental oscillation term) suggests that context sensitivity plays a critical role in trait persistence, and that even coarse-grained environmental dynamics can meaningfully influence system trajectories. This aligns with the view that biological and behavioral traits co-evolve with fluctuating ecological and social landscapes.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The model currently assumes population-level homogeneity (data from a single country), lacks real-time adaptive feedback from the environment (it has only been modeled as an oscillatory system), and does not yet integrate networked or multi-trait coupling in a truly general form (associations between
N
and
P
terms was speculative, here).

Additionally, while the fit metrics are promising, they are limited by the availability and resolution of longitudinal entropy data.

As stated previously, discovering generalizations regarding optimal parameterization patterns for the coupled differential equations is the current focus of ongoing work. Emerging evidence implies stable attractor regimes may correspond to biologically meaningful parameter ranges, implying possible evolutionary regularities across species and contexts.

As the system scales in complexity, the computational burden increases nonlinearly. However, the framework is being designed with geometric tractability in mind, with the ultimate goal of integrating with machine learning architectures. If achievable, this approach would enable the exploration of high-dimensional attractor landscapes, the use of variational inference techniques, and the testing of robustness across simulated and empirical datasets. Such integration may also facilitate the detection of critical transitions in trait evolution under shifting environmental pressures.

Taken together, these results suggest a promising pathway for bridging behavioral science, information science, and systems biology. By embedding the entropy of psychometric trait evolution in a dynamical, biologically and environmentally constrained framework, this approach may offer a new lens for assessment of longstanding questions in personality theory, behavioral genetics, evolutionary psychology, and evolutionary dynamics.

5.1
Future Directions

Several extensions of the current model are underway. One key direction involves investigation of the incorporation of hierarchical population structure, allowing for subpopulation-level dynamics, assortative mating, migration effects, and population bottlenecks. Such extensions could help bridge micro- and macroevolutionary timescales, capturing how environmental and social changes propagate through structured populations.

Additionally, eventual integration of datasets from other spaces in the same time frame as the SATSA data are desired so as to explore the potential significance of measurements in space as well as time.

The framework will also be tested under stochastic perturbations, both intrinsic (e.g., mutation rate variability) and extrinsic (e.g., random environmental shocks), with a view toward identifying stable attractor basins under noisy conditions. This may support eventual integration with stochastic differential equation (SDE) frameworks.

Lastly, further empirical validation efforts are underway to assess the model’s predictive capacity on real-world longitudinal psychological data beyond the current entropy-derived inputs.

These directions together aim to evolve the current model into a generalized, scalable tool for analyzing behavioral evolution across biological and cognitive domains.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel modeling framework for Information-Theoretic Behavioral Dynamics that treats trait evolution as a biologically grounded, recursive dynamical system. By embedding entropy as a state-dependent constraint within a system of nonlinear differential equations, the approach bridges the gap between traditional psychological theory, information-theory, symbolic dynamics, and systems biology.

Even in its first-generation form, the model demonstrates predictive alignment with entropy-derived psychometric data and highlights how energetic and environmental constraints shape trait persistence and pleiotropic tradeoffs. By extending the domain of formal dynamical systems to behavioral evolution, the model positions psychological traits not as static outcomes or statistical abstractions, but as evolving state variables in constrained, entropy-sensitive populations.

While the model as currently presented is early-stage and limited in empirical scope, it provides a testable framework grounded in established physical and biological principles, and meets the goal of establishing a viable minimal formalism for future interdisciplinary development. This project ultimately aims to open new directions for interdisciplinary research (from psychometric data to multi-omics), inviting further mathematical development, empirical testing, and eventual integration with machine learning frameworks.

Although this first-generation model is intentionally minimalistic, it demonstrates the feasibility of bridging entropy-based behavioral data with biologically plausible dynamic systems. Future iterations will continue to build toward a generalizable theory of trait evolution integrating environment, energy, and cognition.

If the initial success of this model holds, there has been an underlying tiered-argument being made by this paper:

1. The coupled nonlinear ODEs are able to approximate the time evolution of the entropy of psychometric trait distributions from Swedish longitudinal data with relatively strong fit metrics.

2.This entropy-measuring approach (when embedded in biologically inspired dynamic systems) may offer a pathway to modeling trait evolution and constraint in population-level psychometrics, with potential future extensions to more fine-grained trait expression data.

3. Entropy computed from longitudinal psychometric Likert-scale data can be interpreted as a meaningful proxy for a culture’s collective psychological state or stability. While entropy is not a direct measure of psychological state, its stability or volatility across time may capture the systemic flexibility, constraint, or tension within a cultural behavioral substrate.

In sum, this paper makes a three-tiered contribution. First, it demonstrates that a system of biologically inspired ordinary differential equations (ODEs) can approximate the empirical entropy dynamics of psychometric trait distributions in longitudinal Swedish data. Second, it proposes a generalizable framework for modeling the evolutionary dynamics of psychological traits, integrating entropy as a compressed state variable subject to metabolic, genetic, and environmental constraint. Finally, it opens the door to a broader interpretation: that entropy derived from Likert-scale psychometric data may reflect systemic psychological stability or flexibility within a population over time. While speculative, this third proposition invites further research at the intersection of behavioral science, systems biology, and dynamical systems theory.
