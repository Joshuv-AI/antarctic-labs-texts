Long-term evolution of mid-channel forms in gravel-bed rivers of the Polish Carpathians
Authors: Karol Witkowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMid-channel forms are key components of fluvial morphology, particularly in gravel-bed rivers where their development reflects sediment transport dynamics, vegetation establishment, and human impacts. This study investigates the long-term changes in mid-channel forms, including bars, islands, and inter-channel areas (ICAs) in mountain and foreland rivers of the Polish Carpathians over the past 150 years. Based on five historical and contemporary maps from 1869 to 1887, 1906–1913, 1924–1939, 1976–1989, and 2019, a spatial database of nearly 3,000 forms was developed and analyzed using morphometric methods and multivariate statistics, specifically Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA). The classification of forms into bars, islands, and ICAs was based on land cover and geometric relationships to channel width. Statistical analyses confirmed that ICAs and islands represent two morphometrically distinct types of persistent mid-channel features. A decline in the surface area and shape compactness of these forms was observed over time, followed by a recent resurgence in their number, especially in reaches where river training structures have deteriorated. The findings highlight the dynamic nature of mid-channel forms under long-term anthropogenic and hydromorphological pressures. By offering a standardized morphometric approach and a publicly available vector dataset, this study contributes to the broader understanding of channel pattern evolution in regulated gravel-bed rivers and supports future comparative analyses in fluvial geomorphology and river management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25726-6.Keywords: Mid-channel form, Mid-channel bar, Island, Inter-channel area, Gravel-bed rivers, Polish CarpathiansSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Mid-channel forms occur in various channel pattern types, either as key features that define the pattern, such as braiding, or as forms resulting from local processes1. Vegetation-stabilized forms are referred to as islands. Islands may appear as occasional elements supplementing a braided channel pattern, but they can also become stable, multi-decadal features that form anabranching channels2. The distinction between bars and islands is straightforward and primarily based on the presence or absence of vegetation cover on the gravel or sand surface3. Once plant succession begins on the surface of a mid-channel form, the sedimentation conditions change. The deposition of finer sediment acts as a stabilizing factor, leading to the formation of an island4. The transformation of a bar into an island is also indirectly influenced by declining water levels, which may be caused by channel incision, regulation, damming, or hydroclimatic changes, resulting for example in a reduction in the number of floods5. As the island stabilizes, it becomes part of the valley floor, reaching the level of the floodplain. Consequently, it no longer divides the flow of a single channel, but separates two distinct channels6. This dichotomy results in the classification of the perpetuated islands into two distinct groups: islands that are part of the channel (ISL) and islands that reach the level of the floodplain (ICA)7.

The term inter-channel area (ICA) was popularized in the literature by Brice and Blodgett8. To be classified as an ICA, a landform must be at least 50% covered by vegetation, arable land, or built-up areas and must meet specific morphometric criteria, with a minimum width of three times the channel width and a minimum length of seven times the channel width. Nevertheless, the term island is still commonly used to describe the dominant form in anabranching rivers9. The function of islands as persistent geomorphic features is further emphasized by the use of the term long-lived island10. Heidari et al.11 propose the use of the term mid-channel island. Ádám and Malatinszky12, on the other hand, introduce the term real island. They also classify as islands those landforms that are already partially attached to the floodplain. However, their approach appears to broaden the category of islands to include forms that have lost their hydraulic function. Due to the varying terminology used in the literature, this article adopts the following abbreviations: MCFs – mid-channel forms – all forms surrounded by flowing water within the channel; MCBs – mid-channel bars; ISLs – islands; ICAs – inter-channel areas.

Anabranching rivers and ICAs are generally associated with lowland environments11. The preserved islands identified by researchers in mountainous river reaches are typically interpreted as part of a braided channel with islands13. This classification is justified provided that the islands within braided channels are inundated during most flood events9. When ICAs dominate river morphology as higher-order forms (i.e., valley-bottom elements), they give rise to an anabranching channel pattern, within which lower-order forms – such as islands and mid-channel bars – may also occur. This implies the hydraulic possibility of an anabranching river coexisting with a braided channel pattern within a single channel reach2.

A river typically develops an anabranching channel pattern in order to increase its transport capacity. Consequently, anabranching rivers composed of only two or three channels are the most common14. ICAs often resembles the floodplain in terms of lithological structure. This results in permanently separated channels and limited morphological change within the system during flood events15. Model-based analyses indicate that sediment deposited on large islands (ICAs) and in non-mainstream channels is particularly resistant to remobilization16. In geomorphology, long-lived islands (ICAs) are typically associated with large lowland anabranching rivers17. However, studies of gravel-bed mountain rivers demonstrate that islands have also functioned, and in some cases still function, within their valleys7,13,18. This was the case in river valleys draining the northern part of the Carpathian Mountains, where numerous ISLs and ICAs were removed as a result of channelization during the 20th century19. At present, ICAs are also forming in this region. Outside of Poland, anabranching channels have also been studied in the Morávka Valley in northern Bohemia20 and in the Western Romanian Carpathians21. Numerous studies in the Alpine region have examined the issue of MCFs and related types of channel patterns13,22,23. One important issue analyzed in the context of ISL development in European mountain rivers was the role of large wood24,25. A significant achievement in European channel pattern research was creating a new riverbed classification that considers numerous types containing MCF9. This highlights the significance of the classification of mid-channel forms as ISLs and ICAs in mountain rivers. For this reason, the principal rivers of the Polish Outer Carpathians were selected for the study of mid-channel forms.

Research area

MCFs functioning within the valleys of seven rivers in the Polish Carpathians and the Subcarpathian Basin were examined. These rivers primarily drain mid-mountain ranges of the Beskids and their foothills. All of them are tributaries of the Vistula River. Additionally, the Dunajec River originates in the Tatra Mountains, which are classified as alpine-type mountains. Four intramontane basins are located within the studied catchments (Fig. 1; Table S1).

The study area, with the exception of the upper course of the Dunajec River, is composed of flysch rocks. The western part of the catchment is characterized by higher relief energy than the eastern part26. From west to east, the influence of the continental climate increases over the oceanic one. Annual precipitation in the catchment averages between 800 and 1200 mm, with maximum values recorded during the summer months. Rivers in the western part of the study area exhibit a pluvial-nival regime, with peak discharges occurring between June and August. In contrast, in the eastern part, the highest discharges are observed between March and May as a result of snowmelt. Currently, summer floods predominate in the Carpathians. The upper courses of the rivers are also susceptible to flash floods27.

Results

Through the digitization of historical maps and a contemporary orthophotomap, a database of MCFs consisting of 2,981 records was created. The data were organized by time period and saved in.shp format to enable immediate visualization within a GIS environment. The attribute table contains the results of measurements and morphometric analyses, as well as spatial characteristics. The dataset is provided as Supplemenary Data.

Testing the classification of MCFs

To verify the geomorphological distinction between ICAs and ISLs, a statistical analysis was conducted to compare their morphometric and environmental characteristics. MCBs were excluded from the analysis, as their identification is unambiguous and based on the complete coverage of the bar by gravel.

A multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) showed that ICAs and ISLs form two statistically distinct groups. All tests yielded highly significant results (Table S2). This indicates that, taken together, all analyzed variables differentiate between ICA and ISL types in a statistically highly significant manner.

Subsequently, each variable was analyzed individually using either Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test (Table S3). To determine the appropriate test, Levene’s test was applied to assess the equality of variances between ICAs and ISLs. If p (Levene) < 0.05, the Mann–Whitney U test was used; if p (Levene) ≥ 0.05, Student’s t-test was applied.

Most variables showed statistically significant differences between the groups (p < 0.001). Particularly strong differences were observed in geometric dimensions. These results indicate that ICAs are significantly larger and more elongated than ISLs, which reflects their geomorphological character as stable, vegetated forms that divide the main river channels. In contrast, ISLs are smaller forms, often associated with local flow divergence rather than a complete division into two channels. These differences are illustrated in Figure S1, which presents the distribution of the most discriminating variables (length, perimeter, length/width of the channel, width/width of the channel) as boxplots. ICAs exhibit not only higher mean values but also greater variability in geometric metrics.

The Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) model achieved an overall 10-fold cross-validated accuracy of 89.3% (1,227/1,374 correctly classified; balanced accuracy 86.0%, κ = 0.754; Table S4). Performance was asymmetric across classes: ISLs were identified very well (recall 97.1%), whereas ICAs were more often misclassified as ISLs (recall 74.9%, 121 ICA→ISL vs. 26 ISL→ICA). These errors are consistent with the intermediate morphometric characteristics of ICAs. LOOCV produced similar results (accuracy 89.9%, balanced accuracy 86.6%, κ = 0.767).

The analysis of LDA coefficients indicated that the variables with the greatest importance for separating island types were: length, length-to-channel-width ratio, width, area, perimeter, sphericity, and altitude. These are primarily measures of size, proportion, and shape, which is consistent with the earlier results from the Mann–Whitney U tests and the MANOVA.

The results are presented graphically (Fig. S2) as the distribution of the discriminant function values (LDA1) for each group. The plot displays histograms overlaid with Kernel Density Estimate (KDE) curves, which illustrate the estimated continuous distribution of LDA values for the ICA and ISL groups. The KDE curves show a clear separation between the two groups, although some overlap is present. The curve for ISLs displays a distinct peak at higher LDA values, whereas the distribution for ICAs is shifted toward lower values.

The relatively minor overlap between the two curves suggests that the LDA function effectively separates ICAs and ISLs in feature space, although an intermediate group exists in which classification may be less definitive.

General variation in the abundance and size of MCFs

The overall abundance of MCFs in the successive study periods reflects catchment settings well. In rivers with higher energy, located in the western part of the study area (from the Soła to the Dunajec), there were significantly more MCFs between the 19th and 21 st centuries than in the eastern part (San and Wisłok). The Wisłoka represents a transitional river, as clearly illustrated in Fig. 2. Absolute counts of MCFs are not fully informative – for example, the maximum number of ISLs (228) was recorded on the San in 2019. However, an analysis of the number of ISLs per river kilometer shows that there were only 0.49.

Temporal variation in the number and density of MCFs (forms per river kilometer) across all studied rivers.

The overall temporal variation in the number of MCFs across the studied rivers shows a declining trend during the 19th and early 20th centuries, followed by a rebound and the attainment of the highest recorded values in 2019. Differences in the timing of minimum values reflect the varying pace and extent of river training. During the period under study, it is worth noting that the average annual river discharges were stable. However, fluctuations in the magnitude and distribution of minimum and maximum discharges were observed. However, according to hydrological analyses, river training structures and the reservoirs played a significant role in shaping the regime28,29. The differing scope of river training is also highlighted by the MCF density curve for the Dunajec in 1930 (Fig. 2). The abrupt increase in the number and density of MCFs in the 1930 s resulted from spontaneous restoration following river training works carried out in the Orawa–Nowy Targ Basin and the Sącz Basin in the early 20th century. In contrast, increases in the second half of the 20th century were caused by widespread spontaneous restoration, particularly during major flood events. Spontaneous restoration involved destroying river training structures during floods and widening the channel30. The magnitude of the increases, reflected in the 2019 maxima, is disproportionate to values recorded in earlier periods. Because the 2019 data are based on high-resolution orthophotomaps, they are reliable. In contrast, historical map data likely underestimate the true extent of mid-channel forms. However, comparable insights are provided by the analysis of MCF surface area.

The change in MCF surface area over time across all rivers follows a decreasing trend (Fig. 3). In the Dunajec, a one-time spike in 1930 corresponds to the abrupt increase in MCF number (see Fig. 2). In contrast, the spike observed on the San resulted from river training works during which a meander was cut off and an artificially created, large ICA (over 3.3 km²) persisted for several years. The density of MCFs, expressed in square meters per river kilometer, illustrates the historical dominance of ICAs and the scale of their occurrence in the Soła and Dunajec rivers. This indicates that multi-kilometer anabranching reaches once existed in these rivers.

Temporal changes in the surface area and density of MCFs (form area per river kilometer) across all studied rivers.

The shifts in the position and size of the boxes in Figure S3 clearly indicate periods dominated by channels with large MCFs, particularly ICAs. The most stable distribution of ICA size was observed in the Dunajec, where the highest number of forms was also recorded – visible in Figure S3 as outliers. In the 19th century and the first half of the 20th century, large MCBs were also present; in later periods, their surface area significantly decreased. Although a direct comparison of MCB numbers between 2019 and earlier periods is not possible, the substantial reduction in the surface area of the largest MCBs is undeniable. The third quartile calculated for the MCB area has decreased on all rivers, dropping from 14,19 m² in 1880 to 413 m² in 2019. This trend also applies to ISLs (from 46,076 m2 to 1,344 m2) and ICAs (from 166,618 m2 to 13,248 m2).

Regional variation in mid-channel forms

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, MCFs occupied the largest total surface area in intramontane basins and the Subcarpathian Basin (Fig. 4). In the second half of the 20th century and in the 21 st century, this pattern reversed. The largest valley-floor areas occupied by MCFs are now found in foothill and mountainous zones. In the latter part of the study period, general catchment settings became less important for the development of MCFs. Their formation is now determined primarily by local conditions, which are often associated with the deterioration or destruction of river training structures.

(A) Total surface area and number of ICA, ISL, and MCB classified by region (mountains, foothills, intramontane basins, Subcarpathian Basin). (B) Total surface area and number of ICA, ISL, and MCB in four intramontane basins.

Correlation analysis between river segment slopes and the geometric parameters of MCFs revealed generally weak statistical relationships. Slope variability showed a slight association with MCF elongation (r = −0.08) and sphericity (r = −0.061), which may indicate a tendency toward more elongated and irregular shapes under steeper terrain conditions. Other morphometric parameters – such as length (r = 0.03), width (r = −0.02), area (r = −0.02), perimeter (r = 0.019), and equivalent circular diameter (r = 0.001) – exhibited correlations close to zero. Correlation analysis performed exclusively for the year 2019 (the only period for which slope data were available) yielded even lower correlation coefficients, ranging from − 0.06 to 0.05. While the directions of the observed relationships are consistent with the known characteristics of MCFs in different geomorphic settings23, the strength of these correlations is too low to support any general conclusions regarding the influence of slope on morphometry.

There are differences in the number and surface area of MCFs between the courses of individual rivers. The most prominent examples are intramontane basins, where MCFs historically dominated. The Orawa–Nowy Targ Basin is located at the piedmont of the highest range of the Carpathians, the Tatra Mountains, which results in a substantial supply of bedload to this area. To this day, the Orawa–Nowy Targ Basin contains the highest number and the largest total area of MCFs. In contrast, temporal changes followed a different trajectory in the lower-lying Sącz Basin (Fig. 4). By 1910, as a result of progressive river training, few MCFs remained in the basin (Fig. 5). A rebound occurred through spontaneous channel restoration in formerly regulated reaches. Notably, in 1970 only MCBs were present in the Sącz Basin, with no stabilized forms – a consequence of continued river training and the gradual onset of spontaneous restoration that began with the formation of MCBs. By 2019, the number of MCBs had remained relatively stable, but numerous ISLs and several ICAs had appeared. In the remaining basins, MCFs play a minor role. In the Żywiec Basin, the current scarcity of MCFs is largely due to the inundation of a significant portion of the Soła valley by a reservoir. The Jasło–Krosno Basin, on the other hand, separates foothill zones in the eastern part of the study area, and local conditions are generally unfavorable for MCF development.

Variation in the shape of MCFs

When analyzing temporal changes in the shape of MCFs, it is essential to focus on indicators that are least susceptible to underestimation resulting from differences in map scale. Therefore, shape variability over time was assessed based on changes in length and width, as well as the resulting elongation index (Table S5).

For ICAs, regression analyses revealed a statistically significant decline in both length (slope = −4.223) and width (−1.374). The elongation index also showed a slight but significant downward trend (−0.0005), indicating that these forms became smaller over time while simultaneously becoming more elongated. In the case of ISLs, there was a clear and statistically significant decrease in both length (−1.812) and width (−0.487). However, elongation did not exhibit any significant change (slope ≈ 0), suggesting that these are transitional forms that do not evolve in a consistent direction. For MCBs, both length and width also showed statistically significant declines, but elongation did not display a meaningful trend. This implies that, despite changes in size, the shape of these unstable forms remained variable and irregular.

Linear regression analyses conducted separately for mountain regions, foothills, intramontane basins, and the Subcarpathian Basin revealed clear differences in the intensity and consistency of morphometric changes over time. Although the share of statistically significant regressions was the same in all regions (77.8%), the R² values varied considerably, reflecting differences in the strength of the temporal relationships. The highest mean and median R² values were observed in the foothills (mean R² = 0.245; median R² = 0.314) and in the mountains (mean R² = 0.213; median R² = 0.242). This indicates that, in these regions, temporal variation in length, width, and elongation was more predictable and better explained by the time variable. In contrast, in intramontane basins (mean R² = 0.171) and in the Subcarpathian Basin (mean R² = 0.154), this variability was less strongly correlated with time.

Values of the P/A index (perimeter-to-area ratio) exhibit a clear upward trend over time, particularly after 1930 (Fig. 6). This increase indicates a progressive development of the shoreline relative to the surface area of MCFs, which can be interpreted as a sign of increasing shape irregularity – either through elongation and/or more irregular shorelines. This phenomenon also reflects the overall reduction in MCF size, as the P/A index is indirectly dependent on the scale of the form. This interpretation is supported by statistical tests. A Kruskal–Wallis test showed that differences in P/A values between the years 1880 and 2019 are statistically significant (test statistic = 1741.89; p < 0.0001). This confirms that the changes in shoreline complexity of MCFs over the study period are not random but reflect a real and significant morphological evolution of these forms within river valleys.

Discussion

The use of maps in morphometric analyses involves the risk of underestimation; therefore, such analyses require caution and a preference for qualitative over quantitative approaches31. In line with this principle, island morphometry has already been studied using maps of varying scales32–34. In the case of historical maps, inaccurate georeferencing also poses a challenge35. Contemporary orthophotomaps provide a realistic representation of the river channel. In contrast, older maps did not consistently record all small channel forms, as these may have been considered irrelevant from the perspective of practical cartography34. As emphasized by Hohensinner et al.36, the key to long-term analyses is the identification of persistent forms that were sufficiently large or significant to be mapped.

The division of MCFs into three types is of considerable importance for the classification of channel patterns37. Failure to distinguish between islands functioning as channel features (ISLs) and inter-channel areas (ICAs) functioning as valley-floor features complicates the differentiation between anabranching patterns and single-channel, multi-thread systems containing vegetated islands8. The method applied in this study8 as modified by Teisseyre38, enables such classification, although it is not without limitations. First, this classification does not account for geological structure, which is essential for determining the presence of overbank deposition within ICAs6. Second, it does not consider water levels. During flood events, much of the ICA remains above the water surface, similar to a floodplain. This has been confirmed by observations on the Yangtze River39. Third, the classification does not address the origin of the island. The conceptual model of island development in gravel-bed rivers4 assumes that island formation requires sufficiently long intervals between flood events to allow vegetation to establish. Under such conditions, bars can transform into islands40. Teisseyre’s7 research demonstrates that ICAs can also originate through avulsion in gravel-bed rivers. In large-scale studies such as this one, simplifications are necessary22. The classification used here produces results consistent with detailed analyses of channel patterns conducted along selected Carpathian river reaches, such as the Raba41 and Skawa42. Moreover, the applied classification is supported by statistical evidence.

The application of multivariate methods such as MANOVA and LDA has proven useful in geomorphology, particularly in bar-related analyses. For example, Sutfin et al.43 proposed a classification of mountain river channels based on geometric and environmental variables using MANOVA, which resulted in a clear separation of forms. Similarly, Moore et al.44 demonstrated that in the lower Mississippi River, changes in the width and shape of MCBs occur dynamically, and that their morphometry can be effectively classified using MANOVA. The results of the MANOVA and LDA analyses in this study clearly support the geomorphological distinction between ICA and ISL island types. Statistically significant differences in 17 variables indicate that these MCF types form distinct groups in morphometric space. It is worth noting the partial overlap of ICA and ISL distributions in LDA space, which results from the presence of transitional forms. This is consistent with the approach of Kleinhans and van den Berg45, who described a morphological continuum between various types of bars and channels in systems where boundaries between categories are not sharply defined.

The results of the analysis of channel pattern changes in selected rivers of the Polish Carpathians indicate substantial transformations in channel morphology over the past approximately 150 years. The clear difference between the MCF-rich rivers of the western Carpathians and the morphologically less dynamic rivers of the eastern part confirms previous findings46. The analysis of morphometric changes revealed a systematic decline in the number and surface area of MCFs up to World War II, followed by an increase in their number, though with smaller individual areas. This is further supported by the observed increase in the perimeter-to-area ratio of these forms. The trends observed here corroborate previous case studies conducted along Carpathian river reaches, which documented channel transformations resulting from intensive river training47,48. Similar changes have also been observed in other regions of the world49.

This study proves that the narrowing of channels over time is the result of river regulation rather than reduced river discharge. This finding is supported by case studies of the Soła, Dunajec, and San rivers33. Studies of Carpathian river discharges in the 20th century reveal no evident trends in average annual discharges. However, changes in extreme flows are significant29. Minimum discharges show a downward trend, while flood peaks demonstrate significant decadal variability. The highest values occurred in 1934, 1960, and 201028,50. Additionally, Soja28 emphasizes that river training structures have significantly altered Carpathian river discharges.

It is also noteworthy that these changes were not spatially uniform. In the 19th century, river reaches within intramontane basins and the Subcarpathian Basin were primarily dominated by various types of MCFs. Following river training, these differences became less pronounced, yet MCFs remain the most numerous in intramontane basins relative to their surface area. These findings confirm that intramontane basins are areas predisposed to the accumulation of transported sediment22. This is particularly favored by the absence of natural confinement, which allows for the development of wide channels9. In channel functioning models, the formation of MCFs is also associated with river slope6. However, this study demonstrated that slope analyses based on long channel segments do not explain MCF variability. It is not possible to clearly associate river slope values with specific MCF types.

The timing of minimum MCF occurrence varied between rivers, reflecting differences in the pace and extent of river training. The Dunajec and San rivers underwent regulation along long reaches as early as the second half of the 19th century. In contrast, river training works on the remaining rivers began only in the 20th century51. Increases in the number of MCFs in the second half of the 20th century were the result of spontaneous restoration, particularly during flood events. This has also been confirmed by the findings of Picco et al.52.

The above discussion of the results of the research on MCF in the Polish Carpathians highlights the research gaps and future directions. To fully understand changes in MCF location and size, a vector database should be created using additional orthophotomaps and aerial photographs from the second half of the 20th century and the 21st century. This database would allow for research to be conducted in short time steps. Another important direction for future research is correlating the variability of flows, both long-term and event-related, with MCF functioning. This research should be conducted on a regional scale, implying the need to work with large amounts of data. The vector object database provided with this article can serve as a starting point for further research.

Methods

Vectorization of mid-channel forms

Five maps covering the entire study area were selected for the analysis of mid-channel forms (MCFs, Table 1). The oldest map, dating from 1869 to 1887, was used for research in the form of a Web Map Tile Service (WMTS) available at https://maps.arcanum.com/. Two subsequent maps, from 1906 to 1913 and 1924–1939, were downloaded from http://igrek.amzp.pl/. Georeferencing using ground control points (GCPs) was required for these maps. The georeferencing process relied primarily on old churches that existed both at the time the map was created and in the present day. In some cases, road intersections were used. The topographic maps from 1976 to 1989 and the 2019 orthophotomaps already contained georeferencing information in their base files.

In the Third Military Survey of the Habsburg Empire (1869–1887), the authors of the digitized version of the map declared an overall error of 200 m (root mean square error, or RMSE)53. This error encompasses the entire 19th-century Habsburg Empire mosaic and stems from errors in connecting individual map sheets. Actual errors within a single sheet reach 0.5 mm on the map, translating to 35–40 m in the field. An RMSE of 38 m was obtained for the entire set of maps from 1906 to 1913 and 21.6 m for maps from 1924 to 1939 (Table 1). Table S6 provides information on the RMSE obtained when georeferencing each river separately. Differences in RMSE among rivers are due to map quality, e.g., the undulating surface of the scanned map. It should be emphasized that, for small-scale historical maps, errors of 100 m indicate very good positioning in the coordinate system54.

On each map, the banks of the active channel of the studied rivers were vectorized. The active channel includes the water surface and unvegetated point bars along the banks. In the case of maps, bars were identified based on gravel or sand symbols; in the orthophotomap, bars were vectorized up to the vegetation line, which is a standard procedure55. All MCFs were vectorized regardless of their size. Surface cover was identified using the same approach as for marginal bars. For MCFs, the object attributes included the type of land cover (G – gravel, V – vegetation, F – field, B – buildings) and the proportion of stabilized surface, defined as the area covered by non-gravel surfaces. All vectorized features for each of the five time periods were saved in a spatial database (Supplementary Data).

The project, entitled “Transformation of Channel Patterns of Carpathian Rivers in the 19th-21st Centuries,” aimed to georeference maps from 1906 to 1913 and 1924–1939, as well as vectorize MCFs on all maps. The result of the project is a vector database made available with this article. Three people were hired to assign georeferences and vectorize data. The contractors worked independently on assigned map sheets. The author of the study verified the results of their work and, in the event of discrepancies, sent them back to the contractors for review. This approach ensured that the bias that could arise from performing these tasks by a single person was avoided.

Morphometric analysis of mid-channel forms

For each MCF, measurements were taken (width, length, channel width, perimeter, area) to calculate shape indices used in correlation analyses and for the classification of forms. To measure the length and width of MCFs, the Minimum Bounding Geometry tool in QGIS was used. This tool generates a rectangle based on each polygon. Under Geometry Type, the option oriented minimum bounding box was selected, which positions the rectangle so that its sides are as short as possible. As a result, the long side corresponds to the form’s length, and the short side to its width. In the case of highly irregular shapes (e.g., crescent-shaped forms), manual correction of the rectangle’s width was necessary. All generated rectangles were reviewed to ensure accuracy. Without manual correction, the width would be overestimated, resulting in misclassification. Using the field calculator, the Elongation parameter was calculated as the ratio of the form’s width to its length. MCFs with regular shapes have values close to 1, while elongation increases as the index decreases.

Channel width was measured manually. Channel width is a key parameter in the classification of MCFs, particularly in distinguishing between islands (ISLs) and inter-channel areas (ICAs). For this reason, measurements were conducted only in the vicinity of MCFs with land cover other than gravel.

Classification of mid-channel forms

In this study, a classification scheme was applied to distinguish three types of mid-channel forms (MCFs): mid-channel bars (MCBs), islands (ISLs), and inter-channel areas (ICAs) (Table 2). MCBs were identified solely based on land cover. If the entire surface of a mid-channel form was covered with gravel or sand, it was classified as a bar. This approach treats MCBs as non-permanent features that remain exposed only during low and medium flows and are reshaped or relocated by the river during high-flow events.

All remaining MCFs that showed at least partial stabilization by vegetation or land use were classified as either ISLs or ICAs. Contemporary Carpathian islands are typically vegetated with trees and/or shrubs, while historical islands also hosted croplands or even buildings. All such land cover types indicate long-term persistence of the landform and the presence of areas that remain unsubmerged even during high flows. Thus, the presence of even a few trees or shrubs was treated as evidence of long-term stability of the vegetated part of an island. The classification distinguished typical islands (ISLs) as forms partially or entirely stabilized by vegetation or other land use, yet relatively small in relation to channel width. Large forms occupying a substantial portion of the channel and at least half-stabilized were classified as inter-channel areas (ICAs). These forms, due to their size, divide not only flow paths but entire channels. According to Brice and Blodgett8, the distinction between ISLs and ICAs can be made by analysing the ratios of form length and width to channel width. Teisseyre38 confirmed this observation and proposed threshold values adapted to gravel-bed rivers (Table 2).

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA)

To identify which morphometric and environmental variables best differentiate between ICAs and ISLs, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) was applied. This method allows for determining the combination of variables that best separates predefined groups and for assessing classification performance59.

The analysis included only MCFs classified as ICA or ISL (excluding MCBs) and used the same 17 quantitative variables as in the MANOVA. Variables were analysed in their original units because classical LDA relies on the pooled within-class scatter and assumes equal covariance matrices. To quantify out-of-sample performance and avoid optimism from evaluating the model on the training data, we applied stratified 10-fold cross-validation and, as a robustness check, leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV).
