When Is a Bulge Not a Bulge? Revealing the Satellite Nature of NGC 5474’s Bulge
Ray Garner, III, J. Christopher Mihos, F. Fabián Rosales-Ortega

Abstract
A satellite galaxy of the nearby spiral M101, NGC 5474 has a prominent bulge offset from the kinematic center of the underlying star-forming disk that has gained attention in recent years. Recent studies have proposed that this putative offset bulge is not a classical bulge within the plane of the disk but instead a dwarf companion galaxy along the line-of-sight. Using integral field spectroscopy data taken as part of the PPak IFS Nearby Galaxies Survey (PINGS), we perform the first analysis of the stellar and gas kinematics of this putative bulge and portions of the disk. We find a radial velocity offset of ∼ \sim 24 km s − 1 24\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1} between the emission lines produced by the disk H II regions and the absorption lines produced by the putative bulge stellar component. We interpret this velocity offset as evidence that the putative bulge and disk are two separate objects, the former orbiting around the latter, supporting simulations and observations of this peculiar system. We attempt to place this external companion into the context of the M101 Group and the M101-NGC 5474 interaction.

III
Data Analysis

In order to determine the nature of the putative bulge of NGC 5474, we relied on the distinct stellar populations that make up the disk and putative bulge and their resulting spectra. Old and intermediate-age stars primarily produce the light of the offset putative bulge, while young stellar populations and gaseous H II regions contribute to the disk light
[
6
,
10
]
. Thus, where the putative bulge dominates the light profile, the absorption lines present in the stellar continuum should trace its kinematics. In contrast, emission lines produced by the H II regions trace the disk kinematics. If the putative bulge is kinematically separate from the disk, the two spectral components should be offset according to their radial velocities. This is the technique proposed (and attempted) by
[
6
]
.

We used two methods to measure the radial velocity offset between the disk and putative bulge. The first method sums the total light within a series of circular apertures centered on the putative bulge to measure the radial velocities of the absorption and emission lines. This technique has the benefit of increasing the S/N of the data. The second method utilized our entire FOV to produce two-dimensional spatially-resolved velocity maps. This method necessarily reduces the signal-to-noise but puts the putative bulge into dynamical context with the rest of the disk. However, both techniques give the same answer: a radial velocity offset of
\simeq
24\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
between the disk and putative bulge. We detail both methods in the following subsections.

Since both methods rely on the Python version of the penalized pixel-fitting code
ppxf
(v9.1.1;
Cappellari & Emsellem 14
,
Cappellari 12
,
Cappellari 13
) to fit the spectra and extract radial velocities, we detail our setup here. The
ppxf
code requires the user to select a base set of stellar population templates to construct the final model. We adopted the
[
11
]
high-resolution model templates for this analysis. These models adopt a
[
57
]
IMF and the Padova isochrones
[
41

ppxf

ppxf

. As we shall see in the next section, this is consistent within the scatter seen in the spatially resolved velocity maps.

III.2
2D Velocity Maps

The second method for measuring the radial velocity uses our full integral field spectroscopy coverage to produce 2D velocity maps. This technique complements and extends the aperture method, allowing us to investigate spatial variations in the disk and putative bulge. Other 2D kinematic studies have found that typical spiral galaxies show no abrupt change in the stellar kinematics across a central bulge beyond what is expected in a rotating system (see many of the velocity maps in, e.g.,
Falcón-Barroso et al. 19
,
García-Lorenzo et al. 23
,
Falcón-Barroso et al. 20
,
Oh et al. 51
). Additionally, there is usually less than a
\sim
2\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
velocity difference between the stellar and gas velocities within the bulge
[
43
]
. Therefore, any significant variations in the stellar kinematics alone or between the gas and stellar kinematics in the disk and putative bulge should provide key insights into the nature of the putative bulge.

Again, we use
ppxf
, as described at the beginning of this section, to extract physical properties from the 2D integral field spectroscopy data. In order to speed up computation time and improve the S/N in each pixel, we spatially bin our data into
3\text{\times}3\text{\,}\mathrm{pixel}
(
$$\times$$
) bins. We do not split up the stellar continuum and emission line fitting; instead, we opt to fit them simultaneously in one run of
ppxf
. Note that we still mask the emission lines while fitting the stellar continuum. Finally, we do not bootstrap the model solutions. In its place, we apply regularization, which reduces the noise in the model fit and physical parameters (see Section 3.5 of
Cappellari 12
for details).

As with the circular apertures, we extract the stellar and gas kinematics and the mass-weighted ages and metallicities in each binned pixel. We checked the reduced
\chi^{2}
values of each model fit, and they were all approximately 1 to 2, indicating that the models are well-fit. Similarly, the S/N of each binned pixel was estimated. As expected, given our pointings and light distribution of NGC 5474, the S/N is highest in the putative bulge with a median of
\sim
35 and decreases further into the disk. Thus, the properties located near or at the putative bulge are likely well-determined and have lower uncertainties than those measured in the disk. We subsequently applied a S/N cut on the data and only kept data with a
\text{S/N}>5
. This reduces the total coverage to the putative bulge and some portions of the disk north and south of the putative bulge as seen in the following discussion.

IV
Summary & Implications

Using data from the PPak IFS Nearby Galaxies Survey, we have successfully and robustly measured a kinematic offset of
\simeq
24\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
between the disk and putative bulge of NGC 5474. This measurement, combined with a wealth of observations
[
56
,
46
,
6
,
10
, e.g.,]
, strongly suggests that the putative bulge of NGC 5474 is not a bulge in the same plane as the disk, but instead a separate object seen along the line-of-sight. In the following, we attempt to put the puzzle pieces together, putting the velocity fields, photometry, and simulations in the same context.

Our interpretation that the disk and the putative bulge are separate objects draws on two key observations. The first is the striking velocity offset between the gas and stars in the region of the putative bulge. In a survey of 30 isolated spiral galaxies,
[
43
]
compared the ionized gas and stellar velocity offsets. They found a median difference of
V_{\text{gas}}-V_{\ast}=$-2.06\pm\pm 0.20\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
such that the gas velocity is slightly blueshifted compared to the stars which
[
43
]
interpret as due to the near-side expansion of H II regions. The velocity difference in NGC 5474 is an order of magnitude larger than that seen in typical spiral galaxies and in the opposite sense–the gas is redshifted with respect to the stars rather than blueshifted. Furthermore, NGC 5474 is not a strongly star-bursting galaxy (
\text{SFR}\simeq$0.08\text{\,}\mathrm{\mbox{$\mathcal{M}_{\odot}$}}\text{\,}{\mathrm{yr}}^{-1}$
;
Kennicutt et al. 35
), lacks an AGN, and the H I disk shows no velocity distortions near the putative bulge
[
56
]
. Thus, it is very unlikely that the velocity difference observed is caused by an outflow.

The second observation is the apparent blueshift of the putative bulge compared to the surrounding disk (Figure
5
). For isolated spiral galaxies, stellar velocity maps show smooth, regular rotation with no kinematic offset of the bulge (see many of the velocity maps in, e.g.,
Falcón-Barroso et al. 19
,
Ganda et al. 22
,
Martinsson et al. 43
,
García-Lorenzo et al. 23
,
Falcón-Barroso et al. 20
,
Guérou et al. 28
,
Oh et al. 51
) unlike what is observed for NGC 5474. Could the velocity offset be a sign that the putative bulge is currently merging with the disk of NGC 5474? Galaxies in the late stages of merging do show distortions to their stellar kinematics
[
4
,
9
,
50
,
48
, e.g.,]
, but these result in stronger H I kinematic irregularities than is observed for NGC 5474
[
56
,
39
]
. Meanwhile, galaxies in the earliest pre-merger stages show regular velocity fields with one galaxy being redshifted from the other, such as the optically superimposed galaxy pair VV488 (see Figure B2 in
Barrera-Ballesteros et al. 4
), which is qualitatively similar to NGC 5474. Thus, it is more likely that these two objects, the putative bulge and disk of NGC 5474, are physically separate.

While likely separate, the disk and putative bulge are probably physically associated with each other since the velocity difference between the disk (gas) and putative bulge (stellar) velocities is less than the circular speed of NGC 5474 (
\simeq
44\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
, assuming
i=$$
;
Rownd et al. 56
,
Kornreich et al. 39
). Thus, the putative bulge is likely an external satellite galaxy moving around the disk of NGC 5474 rather than a chance projection of distant objects
[
52
]
. Given the lack of optical tidal features
[
46
,
26
]
and distorted velocity fields characteristic of galaxies in the process of merging (e.g.,
Barrera-Ballesteros et al. 4
and references therein), this is probably a weak interaction rather than an actively merging system. Models suggest that NGC 5474 had a previous interaction with M101
\sim
300\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}
ago
[
40
]
, and clearly that close passage did not unbind the putative bulge-disk pair. Thus this is likely a somewhat bound pair undergoing a weak interaction.

These findings provide a compelling case that the putative bulge of NGC 5474 is not an intrinsic bulge but rather a distinct satellite galaxy in the early stages of interaction with the disk. This interpretation sheds light on the peculiar dynamics and structure of NGC 5474 and highlights the complex evolutionary pathways that galaxies can undergo. Future work, including higher-resolution spectroscopy of the disk and bulge, as well as simulations and dynamical studies incorporating these two separate bodies into the M101-NGC 5474 interaction
[
40
, e.g.,]
, will be essential to confirm the nature of this interaction and explore its role in shaping the observed properties of NGC 5474 and M101. Ultimately, unraveling the nature of this interaction will deepen our understanding of satellite-disk dynamics and the broader processes that drive galaxy evolution in group environments.
