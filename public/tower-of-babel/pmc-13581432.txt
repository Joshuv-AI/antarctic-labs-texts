Cervical Radiculopathy Caused by Epidural Venous Varix Associated with a Dural Arteriovenous Fistula in the Anterior Condylar Confluence: A Case Report
Authors: Takanori Fukunaga, Shingo Toyota, Tomoaki Murakami, Hiroya Matsumoto, Koichi Nakashima, Motoki Nakamura, Shuhei Yamada, Koji Takano, Maki Kobayashi, Haruhiko Kishima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWhile degenerative disorders are the typical cause of cervical radiculopathy, vascular etiologies are exceedingly rare. Dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) in the anterior condylar confluence (ACC) generally manifest with cranial neuropathies or pulsatile tinnitus. This report presents a unique case of ACC dAVF producing an epidural venous varix and cervical radiculopathy and describes the staged therapeutic strategy leading to complete recovery.Case PresentationA man in his 70s presented with progressive right upper limb weakness. MRI detected an extradural mass compressing the C5 and C6 nerve roots, initially mimicking a tumor. DSA revealed an ACC dAVF with venous drainage into the cervical epidural venous plexus, forming a varix compressing the nerve roots. Through staged embolization (transarterial and transvenous), the fistula was successfully obliterated, resulting in partial neurological improvement. Through endovascular embolization for shunt flow reduction, followed by surgical decompression, both the hemodynamic abnormality and mass effect were resolved.

Introduction

Cervical radiculopathy is most commonly caused by degenerative disorders, including intervertebral disc herniation and foraminal stenosis. However, its vascular etiologies are exceedingly rare. An example is venous varices of the epidural venous plexus, often mimicking spinal tumors or degenerative lesions.1–4) Another is spinal epidural arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs), which present with nerve root compression and radicular symptoms.1–4)

The anterior condylar confluence (ACC), located adjacent to the hypoglossal canal, is a major venous crossroad connecting the internal jugular vein, inferior petrosal sinus, cavernous sinus, and vertebral venous plexus. The clinical manifestations of ACC/hypoglossal canal dural AVFs (dAVFs) depend largely on the venous drainage pattern.5–8) Most lesions drain into the internal jugular vein, inferior petrosal sinus, or vertebral venous plexus, typically manifesting as pulsatile tinnitus or lower cranial neuropathies.7,8)

In this report, we present an exceptionally rare case of ACC dAVF with venous drainage into the cervical epidural venous plexus, resulting in varix formation and C5–C6 nerve root compression. To our knowledge, ACC dAVF presenting with cervical radiculopathy caused by an epidural venous varix has not yet been reported.

Case Presentation

A man in his 70s presented with a 2-month history of progressive weakness of the right upper limb. Initially, he noticed difficulty elevating his arm, and this gradually worsened over several weeks. He had no history of trauma, infection, or prior cervical spine disease. Regarding medical history, he had common vascular risk factors, with no prior neurological illness.

On examination, he was alert and oriented, without cranial nerve deficits. Neurologically, he demonstrated marked persistent weakness of the right deltoid (manual muscle testing [MMT] 2/5) and biceps (MMT 3/5) without any muscle atrophy or fasciculations; other muscle groups were preserved at full strength. Sensory function was intact, and there were no signs of myelopathy, including segmental signs such as impairment of fine motor skills, hyperreflexia, or gait disturbance. These findings indicated right C5 and C6 radiculopathy.

Contrast-enhanced CT showed dilation of the right hypoglossal canal and venous dilation at the C4/C5 and C5/C6 levels (Fig. 1A). Cervical MRI revealed an enhancing extradural lesion extending into the cervical spinal canal and the right C4/C5 and C5/C6 foramina, compressing the corresponding nerve roots. No abnormal intramedullary signal intensity was observed (Fig. 1B–1E). CT demonstrated no signs of calcification or mass to indicate a spinal tumor; however, its serpiginous morphology suggested a vascular origin.

For diagnosis confirmation, the patient underwent cerebral angiography (Fig. 2), which revealed a dAVF located at the ACC. This lesion was supplied by branches of the external carotid circulation, mainly including the ascending pharyngeal artery (APA), middle meningeal artery, occipital artery, meningohypophyseal trunk from the internal carotid artery, and a branch from the vertebral artery. The exact shunt point was at the anterior condylar vein (ACV), with venous drainage directed into the internal/external epidural venous plexus. Additionally, the inferior petrosal sinus was not visualized, indicating intracranial venous flow restriction. Owing to abnormal shunting, a venous varix was formed within the cervical spinal canal and neural foramina, directly compressing the right C5 and C6 roots. These findings led to the diagnosis of cervical radiculopathy secondary to an epidural venous varix caused by an ACC dAVF.

Based on the hemodynamic characteristics of the lesion, an initial treatment strategy was established consisting of endovascular flow reduction, followed by surgical intervention if neurological improvement was insufficient. Given the high vascularity and the risk of significant intraoperative bleeding, a staged endovascular-first approach was selected. Initially, arterial flow was reduced through transarterial embolization (TAE; Fig. 3). Under local anesthesia, a 9-Fr long sheath was inserted into the right femoral artery. An 8-Fr, 80-cm guiding catheter (FUBUKI XF; Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) was advanced coaxially over a 6-Fr, 125-cm diagnostic catheter (JB-2; Silux, Saitama, Japan) with a 0.035-inch, 150-cm guidewire (Radifocus; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) and subsequently positioned in the right common carotid artery. Next, a 6-Fr, 105-cm guiding catheter (Benchmark; Penumbra, Alameda, CA, USA) was placed in the external carotid artery. Using a 130-cm catheter (Guidepost; Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) as a distal access catheter, we advanced a 150-cm microcatheter (Excelsior SL-10 STR; Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) over a 0.014-inch, 200-cm guidewire (CHIKAI; Asahi Intecc) and positioned it in the APA. After the hypoglossal branch was selected, the Excelsior SL-10 STR was carefully advanced across the hypoglossal canal into the distal feeder (Fig. 3A). Liquid embolic agents such as n-butyl cyanoacrylate or Onyx (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) were intentionally avoided, because embolization through the hypoglossal branch of the neuromeningeal trunk of the APA carries a substantial risk of hypoglossal nerve palsy due to potential reflux into the vasa nervorum. Thereafter, the feeder underwent coil embolization (Fig. 3B). Consequently, shunt flow was significantly reduced according to the final APA angiography (Fig. 3C).

Although this procedure achieved partial reduction of shunt flow, the venous varix remained opacified, with no clear neurological improvement, and the deltoid and biceps strengths persisted at 2/5 and 3/5, respectively. Hence, the patient underwent transvenous embolization (TVE) to achieve complete obliteration of the shunt (Fig. 4). Under general anesthesia, 5- and 8-Fr long sheaths were introduced into the left femoral artery and right femoral vein, respectively. Through the left femoral arterial sheath, a 5-Fr, 90-cm catheter (Envoy; Johnson & Johnson, Miami, FL, USA) was advanced into the right external carotid artery using a 4-Fr, 125-cm JB-2 catheter and a 0.035-inch, 150-cm Radifocus guidewire for selective angiography. External carotid angiography demonstrated that the shunt flow converged into the ACC. From the right femoral vein, an 8-Fr, 90-cm FUBUKI XF catheter was advanced coaxially with a 6-Fr, 125-cm JB-2 catheter and a 0.035-inch, 150-cm Radifocus guidewire. The catheter was navigated retrogradely through the right subclavian vein, deep cervical vein, and posterior vertebral venous plexus; then, the 8-Fr FUBUKI XF’s distal tip was positioned.

External carotid angiography performed via the 5-Fr ENVOY catheter confirmed the fistulous point, associated venous varix, and drainage into the posterior vertebral venous plexus. Under roadmap guidance, a 130-cm distal access Guidepost catheter, a 150-cm Excelsior SL-10 STR microcatheter, and a 0.014-inch, 200-cm CHIKAI guidewire were retrogradely advanced through the venous system, sequentially navigating the ACV into the ACC (Fig. 4A; top). The coils were deployed within the ACC (Fig. 4A; bottom). Post-embolization external carotid (Fig. 4B) and internal carotid angiography (Fig. 4C) confirmed that the shunt was completely obliterated. Additionally, the patient did not receive postoperative anticoagulation therapy following TVE.

Consequently, the patient’s motor function partially recovered. Although embolization eliminated the hemodynamic abnormality, the patient’s motor strength did not recover sufficiently after a month. Therefore, posterior cervical foraminotomy with direct coagulation of the venous varices was performed (Fig. 5). Intraoperatively, dilated venous channels were identified overlying the right C5 and C6 roots. The walls of the varicose veins were not firm, but were somewhat thick. After careful coagulation, these nerve roots were sufficiently decompressed, as confirmed by postoperative MRI (Fig. 6).

Postoperative radiological images. (A) CT showing the bone area reduced sufficiently from the upper pedicle to the lower pedicle for each intervertebral foramen. (B) T2-weighted MRI showing decompressed C5 and C6 nerve roots. (C) Contrast-enhanced MRI showing decreased foraminal varix volume and a disappearing foraminal varix.

The postoperative course was favorable, with the patient’s motor function improving steadily. Deltoid strength recovered to 4+/5 and biceps to 5−/5 on postoperative day 1. By postoperative day 8, both muscle groups had fully recovered to 5/5.

Discussion

DAVFs in the ACC often present with pulsatile tinnitus, orbital congestion, or cranial neuropathies, reflecting their typical venous drainage patterns.5–8) Typically, the venous outflow involves the internal jugular vein, inferior petrosal sinus, or cavernous sinus, whereas posterior drainage into the vertebral venous plexus leads to lower cranial nerve deficits.8) In our case, the pathway was atypical, with venous drainage extending caudally into the cervical epidural venous plexus, forming foraminal varices that compressed the C5–C6 nerve roots and caused radiculopathy. Venous varices and epidural AVFs are uncommon causes of radiculopathy. Kohno et al. described a cervical dAVF presenting as radiculopathy,1) while Lai et al. reported cervical radiculopathy caused by epidural venous varicosity.2) In the lumbar region, similar cases, including symptomatic epidural varices compressing nerve roots, have been described.9–12) Slin’ko and Al-Qashqish further highlighted the challenges in diagnosing and managing intraspinal epidural varices surgically.13) While these reports confirmed that venous engorgement within the epidural plexus can mimic degenerative disease, none have linked this mechanism to an intracranial dAVF, as seen in our case. The marked enlargement and varix formation of the cervical internal vertebral venous plexus (IVVP) in our case likely resulted from combined anatomical and hemodynamic factors. According to recent anatomical and review studies, the vertebral venous plexus is a valveless, large-capacity network with considerable interindividual variability.14–16) In our patient, several factors likely redirected shunt flow into the IVVP. These factors included relative restriction of the usual cranial outlets such as the inferior petrosal sinus, an anatomic predisposition favoring drainage into the IVVP, a high-flow shunt supplied by multiple arterial feeders, and the confined anatomy of the neural foramen. The resulting sustained venous hypertension surpassed the normal buffering capacity of the epidural plexus, causing passive venous dilation and focal varix formation.

Endovascular embolization is considered the first-line treatment for ACC dAVFs, and both transarterial and transvenous routes have been effective.5–8) In our patient, TAE was selected as the initial treatment to achieve partial flow reduction and to better define the angioarchitecture of the shunt prior to TVE. TAE through the neuromeningeal branches effectively reduced shunt inflow and provided important information regarding venous drainage patterns, which guided further treatment planning for TVE. Consequently, staged embolization achieved angiographic cure and partial neurological recovery. In this context, intraoperative hypoglossal nerve monitoring during TVE has been reported as a strategy to prevent postoperative hypoglossal nerve palsy.17) Based on these reports, hypoglossal nerve monitoring may serve as a useful adjunct to enhance the safety of TVE in future cases. At this stage, observation versus surgery was debated. While some recovery had already occurred, the deficit had persisted for almost 3 months, with imaging showing continuous nerve compression. After 3 weeks of observation following embolization, the patient underwent direct decompression, leading to full neurological recovery. Anticoagulation therapy could be considered as a treatment option to address progressive venous thrombosis. However, given the potential risk of hemorrhagic complications during subsequent surgical decompression, anticoagulation was not administered in this case.

Another important issue is whether decompression alone would have been sufficient. With active shunt flow, however, engorged arterialized varices posed a high risk of intraoperative bleeding. By first reducing the shunt flow via embolization, decompression was made safer. Embolization also served as a therapeutic trial; if symptoms had resolved completely, surgery might have been avoided. Additionally, initial surgical decompression without prior shunt control may lead to recurrence or redistribution of venous congestion. Therefore, our staged approach minimized risk while maximizing recovery.

An additional point of interest in the present case is the persistence of venous dilatation despite apparent angiographic resolution of the shunt. Several mechanisms may account for this finding. First, residual or recurrent shunting, including angiographically occult spinal or epidural shunts, cannot be entirely excluded. Second, although thrombosis within the varix may have partially contributed to the residual mass effect, the intraoperative finding that the varicose venous wall was not firm but rather somewhat thick did not strongly support the presence of a fully organized thrombus as the primary mechanism. Instead, persistent structural changes of the epidural venous plexus, including phlebosclerotic change or compensatory venous wall hypertrophy secondary to chronic venous hypertension, may have played a more important role.

Notably, the partial neurological improvement observed after embolization suggests that venous hypertension was at least partially relieved. However, once markedly dilated, epidural venous structures may not completely regress, and residual enlargement may continue to exert mechanical compression on adjacent nerve roots.

Conclusion

This report highlights an exceptionally rare case of cervical radiculopathy caused by an epidural venous varix secondary to an ACC dAVF. Unlike typical ACC dAVFs, which typically exhibit pulsatile tinnitus or cranial neuropathies, this lesion drained into the cervical epidural venous plexus and compressed the adjacent nerve roots. The staged therapeutic strategy—endovascular embolization for eliminating shunt flow and minimizing intraoperative bleeding risk, followed by direct surgical decompression of residual varices—resulted in complete neurological recovery. By recognizing this mechanism early and providing timely individualized, staged treatment, irreversible neurological deficits can be prevented.
