Abstract
. The Rankin–Cohen brackets provide a basic example of “non-elementary” differential symmetry breaking operators. They can be interpreted as bi-differential operators remarkable for reflecting the structure of fusion rules for holomorphic discrete series representations of the Lie group S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) SL(2,\mathbb{R}) and are intimately connected to classical special polynomials. In this introductory article, we explore the combinatorial structure of these operators and discuss a general framework for constructing their higher-dimensional analogues from the representation-theoretic perspective on branching problems. The exposition is based on lectures delivered by the authors during the thematic semester “Representation Theory and Noncommutative Geometry”, held in Spring 2025 at the Henri Poincaré Institute in Paris.

Symmetry breaking operator,
Rankin–Cohen bracket, orthogonal polynomial, branching rule.

1.
Introduction

The Rankin–Cohen brackets made their first implicit appearance in the late 1880s in the work of P. Gordan
[
10
]
and his student S. Gundelfinger
[
11
]
on invariant theory. Since then, they have been rediscovered on several occasions in different mathematical contexts, a fact that clearly attests to their enduring relevance.

Renewed interest in this family of operators largely stems from the role they play in number theory. In a landmark paper
[
4
]
, H. Cohen explored various properties of what were later called

the Rankin–Cohen brackets

by D. Zagier
[
40
]
,
including an ingenious proof of their covariance, and applied them to the study of special values of
L
-functions.

We therefore introduce these operators through modular forms.

1.1.
Holomorphic modular forms

Consider the complex upper half-plane

\mathbb{H}:=\{z=x+iy~|~x\in\mathbb{R},y>0\}

endowed with the Poincaré metric

(ds)^{2}=y^{-2}(dx^{2}+dy^{2})=(\mathrm{Im}(z))^{-2}|dz|^{2}

that makes
\mathbb{H}
into a hyperbolic plane; that is, a real two-dimensional Riemannian manifold of constant negative curvature
-1
.
Moreover, the Poincaré metric defines a Hermitian metric compatible with the complex structure.

The Lie group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
acts on
\mathbb{H}
by fractional linear transformations

(1.1)

g:z\mapsto g.z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\quad z\in\mathbb{H},\,g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL(2,\mathbb{R}).

The action of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathbb{H}
is transitive; in fact, already the action of its subgroup
P
of upper triangular matrices is transitive. Indeed,

P\ni\begin{pmatrix}y^{\frac{1}{2}}&xy^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
0&y^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}:i\mapsto x+iy.

Moreover, the stabilizer of the point
i\in\mathbb{H}
is the subgroup
SO(2)
, which is a maximal compact subgroup of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
. Thus
\mathbb{H}
can be viewed as a Hermitian symmetric space isomorphic to
SL(2,\mathbb{R})/SO(2)
.

The geometric action (
1.1
) naturally extends to a family of actions on spaces of functions on
\mathbb{H}
, parametrized by
weight

\lambda
.

Consider a particular class of holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{H}
that
satisfy, for a fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, the following functional equation:

(1.2)

f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)=(cz+d)^{\lambda}f(z),\quad\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z}).

Since the group
\Gamma=SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
contains the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

any function
f
satisfying (
1.2
) is necessarily periodic,
f(z+1)=f(z)
, and thus admits a Fourier series expansion:

f(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n}(f)e^{2\pi inz}.

One says that such a function
f
is holomorphic at infinity if
c_{n}(f)=0
for all
n<0
. If, furthermore,
c_{0}(f)=0
, then
f
is said to
vanish at infinity
or to
be cuspidal at infinity
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\lambda
be a non-negative even integer. A modular form of weight
\lambda
with respect to the group
\Gamma=SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
is a holomorphic function
on
\mathbb{H}
, which satisfies (
1.2
) and is holomorphic at infinity.

The vector space of such functions is usually denoted by
M_{\lambda}(SL(2,\mathbb{Z}))
or simply by
M_{\lambda}(\Gamma)
if there is no ambiguity.

Modular forms exist and form a finite-dimensional vector space
(see, for instance,
[
1
]
for an elegant introduction to the theory of modular forms).
For instance, Ramanujan’s delta function

\Delta(z)=e^{2\pi iz}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-e^{2\pi inz}\right)^{24}

is a modular form of weight 12. Moreover, it is cuspidal at infinity.

Given
f\in M_{\lambda}(\Gamma)
of weight
\lambda
, it is straightforward to obtain a modular form of weight
2\lambda
by taking
f^{2}\in M_{2\lambda}(\Gamma)
, whereas constructing a modular form of
weigh
2\lambda+2
is less immediate. However, the following holds:

(1.3)

\lambda f\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial z^{2}}-(\lambda+1)\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)^{2}\in M_{2\lambda+2}(\Gamma).

It is natural to ask whether there are further formulas like (
1.3
) to produce modular forms of other higher weights. The answer is affirmative: there exists a countable family of such operators.

To see this, let us polarize the non-linear operators into bilinear operators.

We begin with an observation that the holomorphic derivative
\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}
of a modular form
f
of weight
\lambda
is no longer modular, as the second term below constitutes an obstruction :

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left((cz+d)^{-\lambda}f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(cz+d)^{-\lambda-2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)-\lambda c(cz+d)^{-\lambda-1}f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right).

However, an appropriate combination of two such holomorphic derivatives of modular forms can cancel the extra terms and produce a
bi-differential operator
that preserves
modularity. For example, let
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
be two modular forms of weight
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
, respectively. Then, using the formula above, one observes that the following combination of first-order derivatives:

F(z):=\lambda^{\prime}f_{1}(z)\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial z}(z)-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial z}(z)f_{2}(z)\\

is a modular form of weight
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2
. In case where
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda^{\prime\prime}
and
f_{1}=f_{2}
, this
expession reduces to
(
1.3
)

up to a scalar multiple, and can be viewed as its polarization.

As in the previous observation, this construction is neither
ad hoc
nor isolated.
Rather, it belongs to an infinite family of such bi-differential “combinations” which
preserve the modularity property (
1.2
).

As a further illustration, consider the following second-order bi-differential operator:

(f_{1},f_{2})\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(\lambda^{\prime\prime}+1)f_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{2}}{\partial z^{2}}-(\lambda^{\prime}+1)(\lambda^{\prime\prime}+1)\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial z}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial z}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}+1)\frac{\partial^{2}f_{1}}{\partial z^{2}}f_{2}.

When applied to two modular forms
f_{1}\in M_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\Gamma)
and
f_{2}\in M_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}(\Gamma)
,
this formula produces a modular form of weight
{\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+4}
.

Let us now give a general formula for such transformations.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{N},\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}:=\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a
.
The
a
-th Rankin–Cohen bracket

\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}\equiv\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a}:\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H})\times\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H})

is defined by

(1.4)

\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}(f_{1},f_{2})(z):=\\
\mathrm{Rest}_{z_{1}=z_{2}=z}\circ\sum_{\ell=0}^{a}(-1)^{\ell}\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda^{\prime}+a-1\\
\ell\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda^{\prime\prime}+a-1\\
a-\ell\end{matrix}\right)\frac{\partial^{a-\ell}f_{1}}{\partial z_{1}^{a-\ell}}(z_{1})\frac{\partial^{\ell}f_{2}}{\partial z_{2}^{\ell}}(z_{2}).

Here,
\mathrm{Rest}_{z_{1}=z_{2}=z}
denotes the restriction map that simultaneously sets
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
equal to
z
.

For
\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}=\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a
with
a=0,1
and
2
, the Rankin–Cohen brackets

\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}(f_{1},f_{2})
take the following form:

\displaystyle a=0:

\displaystyle f_{1}f_{2},

\displaystyle a=1:

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime\prime}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial z_{1}}f_{2}-\lambda^{\prime}f_{1}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial z_{1}},

\displaystyle a=2:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(\lambda^{\prime\prime}+1)f_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{2}}{\partial z^{2}}-(\lambda^{\prime}+1)(\lambda^{\prime\prime}+1)\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial z}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial z}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}+1)\frac{\partial^{2}f_{1}}{\partial z^{2}}f_{2}.

In particular, the cases
a=0,1,2
recover the examples discussed earlier.

The Rankin–Cohen brackets satisfy the following fundamental property.

Fact 1.3

(Cohen
[
4
]
)
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{N},\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}:=\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a
.
If
f_{1}\in M_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\Gamma)
and
f_{2}\in M_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}(\Gamma)
, then
\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}(f_{1},f_{2})\in M_{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}(\Gamma)
.

This property reflects the original role of the Rankin–Cohen brackets as an explicit algorithm for constructing modular forms of higher weight from modular forms of lower weight.

Once the explicit expression for the Rankin–Cohen brackets is known, the proof of Fact
1.3
reduces to an elementary, though lengthy, direct computation generalizing the preceding observation.

Beyond H. Cohen’s original proof
[
4
]
of the covariance property in Fact

1.3
, there exist at least seven alternatives proofs based on different ideas, as follows.

•

Recursion relations for the lowest-weight vectors of corresponding representations
[
8
,
13
,
36
]
;

•

Taylor coefficients of Jacobi forms
[
9
]
;

•

Reproducing kernels of Bergman spaces
[
3
,
35
]
;

•

Dual pair correspondence
[
14
]
;

•

Source operators
[
2
]
;

•

Generating operators
[
27
]
;

•

The F-method
[
25
]
.

In Section
1.3
, we will illustrate the first method, which is based on recurrence relations. Within the context of symmetry breaking operators (SBOs), this method is the most elementary.

On the other hand, to understand the structure of the Rankin–Cohen brackets, we must not only explain the reason why they satisfy the covariance property underlying Fact
1.3
, but also how they can be discovered anew and why, up to a scalar, no other such bi-differential operators exist provided
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. To achieve this, we appeal to the representation theory of the ambient Lie group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
, rather than viewing the covariance as a purely arithmetic feature of the modular group
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

The last method, referred to as the F-method, derives the explicit form of the Rankin–Cohen brackets from scratch by revealing that the seemingly complicated alternating sum coefficients arise naturally from the Jacobi polynomials. Moreover, it provides “exhaustion-type” theorems in the classification theory of SBOs, see Corollary
2.12
.

The F-method will be discussed in a more general framework in Section
2
, based on the “algebraic
Fourier transform of Verma modules”. Meanwhile, we introduce some basic notions that will be used later for this purpose.

1.2.
Holomorphic discrete series representations

Among irreducible unitary representations of a real simple Lie group
G
, there exists a distinguished family of infinite-dimensional representations, that occurs as subrepresentations of the left regular representation of
G
on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(G)
. These representations are called
square-integrable representations
, because they are characterized by the square-integrability of their matrix coefficients. They are also referred to as
discrete series representations
of
G
.
Harish-Chandra initiated a systematic study of these representations and provided both a criterion for their existence in terms of the rank condition, as well as a parametrization of discrete series representations. See Duflo
[
7
]
for a survey of the developments of the theory of discrete series during the 1970s.

The group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
possesses a non-empty discrete series. In this case, the discrete series exhibits a particularly rich structure: the corresponding
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-modules are either lowest or highest-weight modules.
These discrete series representations can be realized concretely in Hilbert spaces of holomorphic or anti-holomorphic functions respectively.

More precisely, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
, consider the space

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{2}(\mathbb{H}):=\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{H},y^{\lambda-2}dxdy),

consisting of holomorphic functions on the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
that are square integrable with respect to the weighted measure
y^{\lambda-2}dxdy
. If
\lambda>1
, then
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{2}(\mathbb{H})\neq\{0\}
and is infinite-dimensional. Moreover, since pointwise evaluation map is a continuous linear functional on this Hilbert space, it admits a reproducing kernel. The space
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{2}(\mathbb{H})
is referred to as a
weighted Bergman space
.

The fractional linear action (
1.1
) of the group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathbb{H}
induces a unitary representation

\pi_{\lambda}
on
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{2}(\mathbb{H})
, given by:

(1.5)

(\pi_{\lambda}(g)f)(z)=(cz+d)^{-\lambda}f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right),\,{\mathrm{for}}\,g^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL(2,\mathbb{R}).

For
\lambda>1
such representations
\pi_{\lambda}
are irreducible and pairwise inequivalent. The fact that these representations can be realized in the kernel of the Cauchy–Riemann operator is a special feature of the group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
;
representations arising in this manner are referred to as
holomorphic discrete series
representations. More generally, this phenomenon persists in the broader framework of Hermitian Lie groups.

We note that the classical Paley–Wiener theorem provides yet another realization of the
holomorphic discrete series representations

of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, the Fourier–Laplace transform maps the weighted Bergman space
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{H})
into the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},x^{1-\lambda}dx)
, thus
giving rise to an

L^{2}
-
model
of the same representation
\pi_{\lambda}
. Although explicit formulas for the group action are more complicated than in
(
1.5
), this model turns out to be appropriate for the analysis of Rankin–Cohen brackets and the dual notion of symmetry breaking operators, namely the
holographic operators

[
26
]
, through the F-method.

We also note that the action (
1.5
) admits a more conceptual interpretation. Indeed, there exists a countable family of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-equivariant holomorphic line bundles
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
over
\mathbb{H}
,
parametrized by
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
, associated with a character of
SO(2)
, which together exhaust all such bundles. The regular representation of the group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
on the space of holomorphic sections
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H},\mathcal{L}_{\lambda})
can be identified with
\pi_{\lambda}
after the trivialization of this line bundle via the Bruhat decomposition.

The covariance property of the Rankin–Cohen brackets in Definition
1.2
is formulated in terms of the representations

\pi_{\lambda}
given in (
1.5
) as follows.

Fact 1.4
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{N},\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}:=\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a
.
Then for every
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H})
and any
g\in SL(2,\mathbb{R})
one has

\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}(\pi_{\lambda^{\prime}}(g)f_{1},\pi_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}(g)f_{2})(z)=\left(\pi_{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}(g)\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}(f_{1},f_{2})\right)(z).

This result extends Fact
1.3
to a larger group of transformations—namely, from

SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
to
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
—and shows that the covariance property is fundamentally geometric rather than arithmetic holding over the entire Lie group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
.
Later, we allow the
parameter
\lambda
to be a complex number, and one observes that this statement remains valid for the universal covering group of
{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

Moreover, it admits a representation-theoretic interpretation, as the Rankin–Cohen brackets can be regarded as intertwining operators, see for instance
[
3
]
. We adopt this perspective and demonstrate how it explains the structure of these operators, why they take this specific form, and how to construct, in an exhaustive and systematic way, their analogues for different families of real semisimple Lie groups.

1.3.
Weight Watchers

One somewhat handcrafted way to construct such intertwining operators relies on the fact that the holomorphic discrete series representations are lowest-weight modules satisfying weight-multiplicity-free property. This property provides an elementary method for
{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, yet it is not easily applicable for higher-rank groups.

Consider the real Lie algebra of
2\times 2
traceless matrices

\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R}):=\{X\in\mathrm{Mat}(2,\mathbb{R}):\mathrm{tr}(X)=0\}.

As a vector space, it is three -dimensional, and we choose the following basis elements

e^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad h=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad e^{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

which are subject to the commutation relations:

[h,e^{\pm}]=\pm 2e^{\pm},\quad[e^{+},e^{-}]=h.

The center
\mathcal{Z}
of the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
of the complexification of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
is generated, as a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra, by the single element:

c=h^{2}+2(e^{+}e^{-}+e^{-}e^{+})=h^{2}+2h+4e^{-}e^{+}.

The infinitesimal action
d\pi_{\lambda}
of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
on the space of holomorphic functions
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H})
corresponding to the holomorphic discrete series representation
\pi_{\lambda}
of the Lie group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
is defined by

d\pi_{\lambda}(X)f(z):=\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\pi_{\lambda}(\exp(tX))f(z),\,X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R}),f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H}).

Then, according to the formula (
1.5
) we have

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda}(e^{-})f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda zf(z)+z^{2}f^{\prime}(z),

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda}(e^{+})f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f^{\prime}(z),

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda}(h)f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda f(z)-2zf^{\prime}(z).

Furthermore, consider the particular element

(1.6)

{}^{\lambda}v_{\ell}\equiv v_{\ell}:=\frac{(\lambda+\ell-1)!}{(\lambda-1)!}z^{-\lambda-\ell}

and
denote by
V_{\lambda}=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\langle{}^{\lambda}v_{\ell}\rangle_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
the vector space spanned by these functions. Then,

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda}(e^{+})({}^{\lambda}v_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{}^{\lambda}v_{\ell+1},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda}(e^{-})({}^{\lambda}v_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ell(\lambda+\ell-1)\,{}^{\lambda}v_{\ell-1},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\},

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda}(e^{-})({}^{\lambda}v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda}(h)({}^{\lambda}v_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda+2\ell){\,}^{\lambda}v_{\ell},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda}(c)({}v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda^{2}-2\lambda)v,\quad\forall v\in V_{\lambda},

where in the last identity, we abuse notation slightly by using the same symbol for the extension of the Lie algebra representation
d\pi_{\lambda}
to its universal enveloping algebra.

Thus, the space
V_{\lambda}
can be seen as a lowest-weight module for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, the maximal compact subgroup
SO(2)
of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
acts on
V_{\lambda}
in such a way that every
element of
V_{\lambda}
is
SO(2)
-finite in the sense that

\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\langle\pi_{\lambda}(g)v:g\in SO(2)\rangle<\infty
for all
v\in V_{\lambda}
.
The space
V_{\lambda}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{H})
, and
V_{\lambda}
is the underlying
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-module for the holomorphic discrete series representation
\pi_{\lambda}
.
Since
d\pi_{\lambda}(e^{-})({}^{\lambda}v_{0})=0
, the Harish-Chandra module
V_{\lambda}
is a lowest-weight module.

For two given integers
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}>1
, consider the tensor product
V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
of two such modules as an
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
-module via the diagonal embedding of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
into
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
. Then this module is no longer irreducible and decomposes discretely and multiplicity-freely into a direct sum of lowest-weight Harish-Chandra modules:

(1.7)

V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}=\bigoplus_{a\in\mathbb{N}}V_{\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a}.

The
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
-case was proved by Molchanov
[
32
]
and Repka
[
38
]
. We refer to
[
17
, Theorem 8.4]
for a more conceptual explanation of this phenomenon in a broader framework.

As a refinement of the abstract branching rule (
1.7
), one can explicitly determine the lowest-weight vectors of the irreducible components appearing in the decomposition (
1.7
) (see
[
8
,
13
]
, for example).

We now carry out this computation. The element
\left({}^{\lambda^{\prime}}v_{\ell}\right)\otimes\left({}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}v_{m}\right)\in V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
is an eigenvector for the diagonal action of
h\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
, with eigenvalue
\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2(\ell+m)
.

Therefore, the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue

\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}:=\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a

is generated, as a vector space, by elements
of the form
{}^{\lambda^{\prime}}v_{\ell}\otimes{}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}v_{a-\ell}
.

To identify irreducible components in the decomposition (
1.7
), we thus need to find a family of constants
\kappa_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}
such that the element

w_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{a}(z_{1},z_{2}):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{a}\kappa_{\ell}({}^{\lambda^{\prime}}v_{\ell}\otimes{}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}v_{a-\ell})\in V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}

is annihilated by the diagonal action of the element
e^{-}\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

Thus, in order to determine these coefficients, we describe the kernel of the differential operator

\displaystyle d\pi_{\lambda^{\prime}}(e^{-})\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathrm{Id}_{V_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\otimes d\pi_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}(e^{-})

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda^{\prime}z_{1}+z_{1}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}\right)\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathrm{Id}_{V_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\otimes\left(\lambda^{\prime\prime}z_{2}+z_{2}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}}\right),

where the variables
z_{1},z_{2}
refer to the realization of the modules
V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
and
V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
, respectively.

If
w_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{a}
is annihilated by this operator, the comparison of the coefficients of

{}^{\lambda^{\prime}}v_{\ell}\otimes{}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}v_{a-\ell}
yields the following equality for every
\ell
:

(1.8)

(\ell+1)(\lambda^{\prime}+\ell)\kappa_{\ell+1}+(a-\ell)(\lambda^{\prime\prime}+a-\ell-1)\kappa_{\ell}=0,

Interpreting this as a recurrence relation, one has

(1.9)

\kappa_{\ell}=\frac{a!}{(\lambda^{\prime\prime})_{a}}(-1)^{\ell}\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda^{\prime}+a-1\\
\ell\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda^{\prime\prime}+a-1\\
a-\ell\end{matrix}\right)\kappa_{0},

where
(x)_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(x+n)}{\Gamma(x)}
is the rising Pochhammer symbol and the initial condition
\kappa_{0}
can be chosen freely.

The element
w_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{a}
is, up to a scalar multiple, the lowest-weight vector in
V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}=V_{\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a}
, viewed as an irreducible submodule of
V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
.

Since
d\pi_{\lambda}(e^{+})({}^{\lambda}v_{\ell})={}^{\lambda}v_{\ell+1}
, the element
w_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{a}
can also be viewed as the image of the tensor product of the two lowest-weight vectors

\left({}^{\lambda^{\prime}}v_{0}\right)\otimes\left({\,}{}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}v_{0}\right)
under the action of the element

\mathfrak{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}:=\sum_{\ell=0}^{a}\kappa_{\ell}(e^{+})^{\ell}\otimes(e^{+})^{a-\ell}\in U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})\otimes U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}).

Duality Theorem
2.1
in Section
2
asserts that finding a singular vector (
i.e.
a lowest-weight vector in the tensor product representation) is equivalent to constructing a differential symmetry breaking operator.
In the present setting,
the corresponding bi-differential operator is obtained by formally replacing

e^{+}
with
dR(e^{+})=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
(see Example
2.2
).
Consequently, the element

\mathfrak{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}

in the enveloping algebra induces a bi-differential operator
on
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H})
given by

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{a}\kappa_{\ell}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}}\right)^{a-\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}\frac{(-1)^{a}a!}{(\lambda^{\prime\prime})_{a}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{a}(-1)^{a-\ell}\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda^{\prime}+a-1\\
\ell\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda^{\prime\prime}+a-1\\
a-\ell\end{matrix}\right)\frac{\partial^{a}}{\partial z_{1}^{\ell}\partial z_{2}^{a-\ell}}

by (
1.9
). Restricting this operator to
z_{1}=z_{2}(=z)
,
and choosing the normalizing constant

\kappa_{0}=\frac{(-1)^{a}(\lambda^{\prime\prime})_{a}}{a!},

we recover precisely the
a
-th Rankin–Cohen bracket
\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}
.
Therefore, by construction, we verify that
\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})}(V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}},V_{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}})
.

1.4.
Connection to orthogonal polynomials

Far from being an isolated instance, this example fits into a broader framework, developed in
Section
2
, which clarifies the combinatorial structure of such homomorphisms and opens the door to far-reaching generalizations.

At a first glance, one might expect that taking normal derivatives with respect to equivariant embeddings of homogeneous spaces would yield intertwining operators (symmetry breaking operators) valued in representations of the corresponding subgroups acting on functions defined on these submanifolds. Although this occurs only under restrictive conditions, one encounters phenomena in which
intertwining operators appearing in branching rules are realized by holomorphic differential operators that cannot be expressed as normal derivatives (see
[
25
]
, Section 5).

The specific geometric structure of the upper half-plane and of its higher-rank analogues constrains
the class of intertwining operators under consideration to be local operators, as formulated by Localness Theorem
2.6
below.

Rankin–Cohen brackets (
1.4
) are holomorphic differential operators with constant coefficients. As seen in (
1.9
), the coefficients obtained from the recurrence relation,
coincides, up to normalization, exactly with those of certain orthogonal polynomials.
Indeed, using the notation introduced in
[
25
]
,
the Rankin–Cohen brackets may be expressed as

(1.10)

\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}=(-1)^{a}\mathrm{Rest}_{z=z_{1}=z_{2}}\circ P_{a}^{\lambda^{\prime}-1,1-\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}},\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}}\right),

where
P_{a}^{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)
is a homogeneous polynomial in two variables of degree
a
,
defined by

(1.11)

P_{a}^{\alpha,\beta}(x,y):=y^{a}P_{a}^{\alpha,\beta}\left(2\frac{x}{y}+1\right),

and obtained by “inflation” (see (
2.18
), Section 2) from the classical Jacobi polynomial, which is given by the
Rodrigues formula

(1.12)

P_{a}^{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{(-1)^{a}}{2^{a}a!}(1-t)^{-\alpha}(1+t)^{-\beta}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{a}\left((1-t)^{a+\alpha}(1+t)^{a+\beta}\right).

This precise correspondence between the Rankin–Cohen brackets and the Jacobi polynomials is not accidental, but can be explained systematically by the F-method discussed in Section
2
. Within this framework, an important aspect is
that the Jacobi polynomial
P_{a}^{\alpha,\beta}(t)
arises as a polynomial solution of the Gauss hypergeometric differential equation (but not vice versa, see Theorem
2.10
).
To make this explicit,
expressing the Jacobi polynomials in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric function

P_{a}^{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{(\alpha+1)_{a}}{a!}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-a;\alpha+\beta+a+1,\alpha+1;\frac{1-t}{2}\right),

one finds that they satisfy the following second-order differential equation, known as
the
Jacobi differential equation
:

(1.13)

\left((1-t^{2})\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+(\beta-\alpha-(\alpha+\beta+2)t)\frac{d}{dt}+a(a+\alpha+\beta+1)\right)y=0.

The
Rodrigues formula
(
1.12
) provides a recursive structure for the entire family of Jacobi polynomials and ensures that they form a complete orthonormal system of the Hilbert space
L^{2}([-1,1],(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}dt)
. These three key features of Jacobi polynomials—differential equation, recursion, and orthogonality—acquire a profound significance in the analysis of branching rules.

While the role of orthogonality in the analysis of symmetry breaking operators, or their dual notion of holographic operators was first addressed in
[
26
]
, and will not be pursued here, we shall show in the next section that the differential equation aspect arises naturally via the “algebraic Fourier transform of Verma modules”.

Before delving further into the representation-theoretic perspective, let us note that beyond their role in the theory of modular forms, the Rankin–Cohen brackets also appear in a variety of other settings, including equivariant deformation quantization, as well as non-commutative and conformal geometry
[
5
,
6
,
15
,
39
]
.

2.
Symmetry breaking Paradigm

Symmetry breaking in branching problems
offers a natural and conceptually transparent framework for understanding Rankin–Cohen brackets and their higher-dimensional analogues.
In this section, we outline this perspective and explain why it is both natural and effective.
Our exposition follows the approach developed in
[
24
,
25
]
.

Let

p:Y\longrightarrow X
be a smooth map between two manifolds
Y
and
X
. Let
\mathcal{W}\longrightarrow Y
and
\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow X
be vector bundles over
Y
and
X
, respectively.
A central object in this framework is a linear operator relating sections over
X
to those over
Y
.
Following
[
24
]
, we say that a continuous linear map
T:C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V})\to C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W})
is a
differential
operator between two manifolds
if it satisfies the natural support condition

(2.1)

p\left(\operatorname{Supp}Tf\right)\subset{\operatorname{Supp}f}

for all smooth sections

f\in C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V})
.

The support condition (
2.1
) implies
the
localness
of the operator
T
along the map
p

in the following sense: for any open subset
U
of
X
, the operator

T
induces a continuous linear map:

T_{U}:C^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{V}|_{U})\longrightarrow C^{\infty}\left(p^{-1}(U),\mathcal{W}|_{p^{-1}(U)}\right).

If
X=Y

and
p
is the identity map,
then the condition (
2.1
) is equivalent
to
T
being a differential operator
in the usual sense,
by Peetre’s theorem
[
34
]
.

In the general case, any operator
T
satisfying (
2.1
) can be expressed as a finite linear
combination of operators of the form
F_{Y}\circ p^{*}\circ D_{X}
, where
F_{Y}
denotes
multiplication by a smooth function on
Y
, and
D_{X}
is a differential operator on
X
.
A characterization of such operators in terms of their Schwartz distribution kernels was given in
[
24
, Lemma 2.3]
.

We denote by
\operatorname{Diff}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y})
the vector space
of differential operators
from
C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V})
to
C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W})
.

This framework plays a fundamental role in the formulation of differential symmetry breaking operators. With this in mind, we now incorporate symmetries into the setting
by considering a pair of Lie groups
G^{\prime}\subset G
.
We assume that
G^{\prime}
acts on
Y
, that
G
acts on
X
, and
that the map
p:Y\longrightarrow X
is
G^{\prime}
-equivariant. Moreover, we assume that vector bundles
\mathcal{W}\longrightarrow Y
and
\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow X
are equivariant with respect to the actions of
G^{\prime}
and
G
, respectively.

The Lie group
G^{\prime}
acts on the vector bundle
\mathcal{V}\to X
by restriction of the action of
G
.
This action induces natural actions of
G^{\prime}
on the Fréchet spaces

C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V})

and
C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W})
by translations.
Denote by
\operatorname{Hom}_{G^{\prime}}(C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V}),C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W}))

the space of continuous
G^{\prime}
-homomorphisms and introduce the main object of our study:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y}):=\operatorname{Diff}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y})\cap\operatorname{Hom}_{G^{\prime}}(C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V}),C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W})).

We refer to elements of
\operatorname{Hom}_{G^{\prime}}(C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V}),C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W}))
as
symmetry breaking operators
, and to elements of
\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y})
as

differential symmetry breaking operators
.

To illustrate these notions, consider the following example. If
X
and
Y
are both Euclidean vector spaces
with an injective linear map
p:Y\hookrightarrow X
and if

G^{\prime}
contains the subgroup of all translations of
Y
, then

\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y})
is a subspace of the space of
differential operators with constant coefficients.

If the vector bundle
\mathcal{W}\longrightarrow Y
is isomorphic to the pullback
p^{*}\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{V}
, then the restriction map
f\mapsto f|_{Y}
is a
G^{\prime}
-equivariant differential operator of order zero.

An analogous notion can be defined
in the holomorphic setting.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}\to X
and
{\mathcal{W}}\to Y

be two holomorphic vector bundles with a holomorphic map
p:Y\to X
between the complex
manifolds
X
and
Y
.
We say that a differential operator
T:C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V})\to C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W})
is
holomorphic
if

T_{U}(\mathcal{O}(U,\mathcal{V}|_{U}))\subset\mathcal{O}(p^{-1}(U),\mathcal{W}|_{p^{-1}(U)})

for any open subset
U
of
X
. We denote by
\operatorname{Diff}^{\mathrm{hol}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y})
the vector space of holomorphic differential operators.
When a Lie group
G^{\prime}
acts biholomorphically on the two holomorphic vector bundles
\mathcal{V}\to X
and
\mathcal{W}\to Y
, we set

\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{hol}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y}):=\operatorname{Diff}^{\mathrm{hol}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y})\cap\operatorname{Hom}_{G^{\prime}}(C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V}),C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W})).

Symmetry breaking operators arising from concrete geometric models provide both a guiding framework and powerful tools for
the detailed study of branching problems.

A new method, the F-method, based on the
algebraic Fourier transform of generalized Verma modules
, was developed in
[
24
]
to characterize the covariance condition in
\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y})
in terms of systems of higher-order differential equations.
It provides both an explicit construction and an effective tool for proving the exhaustion
of differential symmetry breaking operators. This approach is based on three key results, which we now describe.

2.1.
The big three

Suppose that the groups
G
and
G^{\prime}
act transitively on
X
and
Y
, respectively, so that we may write
X=G/H
and
Y=G^{\prime}/H^{\prime}
.

The first result, referred to as the
Duality Theorem
(
[
24
, Theorem 2.9]
), provides an algebraic description of symmetry breaking operators in terms of generalized Verma modules:

Theorem 2.1

(Duality Theorem)
.

Let
H^{\prime}\subset H
be (possibly disconnected) closed subgroups
of a Lie group
G
with Lie algebras

\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{h}
,
respectively.
Suppose that

V
and
W
are finite-dimensional representations of

H
and
H^{\prime}
, respectively, and denote by
V^{\vee}
and
W^{\vee}
corresponding contragredient representations.

Let
G^{\prime}
be any subgroup of
G
containing
H^{\prime}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{X}:=G\times_{H}V
and

\mathcal{W}_{Y}:=G^{\prime}\times_{H^{\prime}}W
be the corresponding
homogeneous
vector bundles.
Let
\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee})
be the
\mathfrak{g}
-module defined by

\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee}):=U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes_{U(\mathfrak{h})}V^{\vee}.
Notice that this is a generalized Verma module if
\mathfrak{h}
is a parabolic subalgebra.
Define
dR\colon\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathrm{End}(C^{\infty}(G,V))
by

\left(dR(X)F\right)(g)=\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}F(ge^{tX}).

Then there is a natural linear isomorphism:

(2.2)

D_{X\to Y}:\operatorname{Hom}_{H^{\prime}}(W^{\vee},\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y}\right),

or equivalently,

D_{X\to Y}:\operatorname{Hom}_{(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime},H^{\prime})}(\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}^{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}(W^{\vee}),\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y}\right).

For
\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{H^{\prime}}(W^{\vee},\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee}))

and
F\in C^{\infty}(X,{\mathcal{V}})\simeq C^{\infty}(G,V)^{H}
,
the corresponding section

D_{X\to Y}(\varphi)F\in C^{\infty}(Y,{\mathcal{W}})\simeq C^{\infty}(G^{\prime},W)^{H^{\prime}}

is defined by the following formula:

(2.3)

\langle D_{X\to Y}(\varphi)F,w^{\vee}\rangle=\sum_{j}\langle dR(u_{j})F,v_{j}^{\vee}\rangle|_{G^{\prime}}\quad\text{for $w^{\vee}\in W^{\vee}$},

where
u_{j}\in U({\mathfrak{g}})
and
v_{j}^{\vee}\in V^{\vee}
are chosen in such a way that

\varphi(w^{\vee})=\sum_{j}u_{j}v_{j}^{\vee}

\in\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee})
.

Example 2.2
.

In the setting where
V=W=\mathbb{C}
, the homomorphism
\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{H^{\prime}}(W^{\vee},\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee}))

is represented by
u\in U(\mathfrak{g})
such that
\varphi(1)=u\cdot 1
. Consequently, the formula
(
2.3
) reduces to

D_{X\to Y}(\varphi)F=dR(u)F\big|_{G^{\prime}}.

When
H^{\prime}
is connected,
the left-hand side of (
2.2
) can be expressed in terms of Lie algebras.

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose we are in the setting of
Theorem
2.1
and
that
H^{\prime}
is connected.
Then the morphism (
2.2
) is bijective :

(2.4)

\displaystyle D_{X\to Y}:\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}(W^{\vee},\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y}),

or equivalently,

[formula]

D_{X\to Y}:\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}(\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}^{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}(W^{\vee}),\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y}).

This result was previously known in the special setting where
X=Y
and both are complex flag varieties,

i.e.

G^{\prime}=G
and
H^{\prime}=H
are Borel subgroups, see
e.g.

[
31
,
12
]
.

When
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
is a reductive subalgebra
and
{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}
is a parabolic subalgebra,
the existence
of an
{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}
-module
W

for which the left-hand side of (
2.4
)
is non-zero,
is closely
related to the “algebraic discrete decomposability” of the
{\mathfrak{g}}
-module

\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee})

upon restriction to the subalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}

(
[
16
, Part III]
,
[
18
]
). This relationship
plays a crucial role in the proof of the Localness Theorem (Theorem
2.6
below), which asserts that any continuous
symmetry breaking operator in a holomorphic setting is necessarily realized as
a differential operator.

An analogue of
Theorem
2.1
holds in a holomorphic setting as well.
More precisely,
let
G_{\mathbb{C}}
be a complex Lie group,
G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
,
H_{\mathbb{C}}

and
H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
be closed complex subgroups
such that
H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}\subset H_{\mathbb{C}}\cap G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
.
We write
{\mathfrak{g}}
,
{\mathfrak{h}}
,

\ldots

for the Lie algebras
of the complex Lie groups
G_{\mathbb{C}}
,
H_{\mathbb{C}}
,
\ldots
, respectively.
Given finite-dimensional holomorphic representations
V
of
H_{\mathbb{C}}

and
W
of
H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
,
we form the associated equivariant holomorphic vector bundles

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=G_{\mathbb{C}}\times_{H_{\mathbb{C}}}V

\displaystyle\mathrm{over}

\displaystyle X_{\mathbb{C}}=G_{\mathbb{C}}/H_{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}:=G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}\times_{H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}}W

\displaystyle\mathrm{over}

\displaystyle Y_{\mathbb{C}}=G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}/H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}.

For simplicity,
we assume
that
H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
is connected.
(This is always the case
if
G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
is a connected complex reductive Lie group
and
H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
is a parabolic subgroup of
G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
.)
Then we have:

Theorem 2.4

(Duality theorem in the holomorphic setting)
.

There exists a canonical linear isomorphism:

D_{X\to Y}:\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}\left(\mathrm{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}^{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}(W^{\vee}),\mathrm{ind}_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(V^{\vee})\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Diff}_{G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{hol}}(\mathcal{V}_{X_{\mathbb{C}}},\mathcal{W}_{Y_{\mathbb{C}}}).

Suppose furthermore
that
G
,
G^{\prime}
,
H
and
H^{\prime}
are real forms
of the complex Lie groups

G_{\mathbb{C}}
,
G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
,
H_{\mathbb{C}}

and
H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
,
respectively.
By restriction, we regard
V
and
W
as
H
- and
H^{\prime}
-modules, and form the vector bundles

{\mathcal{V}}=G\times_{H}V\,\mathrm{over}\,\,X=G/H,\quad{\mathcal{W}}=G^{\prime}\times_{H^{\prime}}W\,\mathrm{over}\,\,Y=G^{\prime}/H^{\prime}.

One may then ask whether all symmetry breaking operators
admit holomorphic extensions.
Here is a simple sufficient condition:

Corollary 2.5

(
[
24
, Corollary 2.13]
)
.

If
H^{\prime}
is contained
in the connected complexification
H_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
,
then there exists a natural bijection:

\operatorname{Diff}_{G_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{hol}}({\mathcal{V}}_{X_{\mathbb{C}}},{\mathcal{W}}_{Y_{\mathbb{C}}})\overset{\sim}{\to}\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}({\mathcal{V}}_{X},{\mathcal{W}}_{Y}).

In general, symmetry breaking operators between two principal series representations of real reductive Lie groups
G^{\prime}\subset G

are given by integro-differential operators and their meromorphic continuations
in geometric models.
Such operators were highlighted by Knapp and Stein in the case
G^{\prime}=G
and further developed by Kobayashi and Speh
[
29
,
30
]
in the broader setting
G^{\prime}\subsetneqq G
.
Among all symmetry breaking operators, those given by differential operators are quite rare, as evidenced by the complete classification results for certain pairs of real reductive groups
G^{\prime}\subset G
.

However, when
X
is a Hermitian symmetric space,

Y
is a subsymmetric space, and
G^{\prime}\subset G
are the groups of biholomorphic transformations of
Y\hookrightarrow X
,
respectively, a remarkable “inverse” phenomenon occurs. Namely, any continuous
G^{\prime}
-intertwining operator between two representation spaces
consisting of holomorphic sections of holomorphic vector bundles over Hermitian symmetric spaces is necessarily realized as a differential operator. This is the second key result, which we call the
Localness Theorem
.

Let
G
be a connected reductive Lie group,
\theta
a Cartan involution, and
G/K
the associated Riemannian symmetric space.
Let
{\mathfrak{c}}({\mathfrak{k}})

denote the center
of the complexified Lie algebra

\mathfrak{k}:=\operatorname{Lie}(K)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{C}}
.
Suppose that
G/K
is a Hermitian symmetric space. This means that
there exists a characteristic element
Z\in{\mathfrak{c}}({\mathfrak{k}})

such that the eigenvalues
of
{\operatorname{ad}}(Z)\in{\operatorname{End}}({\mathfrak{g}})

are 0 or
\pm 1
and, that
\mathfrak{g}
admits an eigenspace decomposition

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{k}}+{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}+{\mathfrak{n}}_{-}

with respect to
{\operatorname{ad}}(Z)
, corresponding to the
eigenvalues
0
,
1
, and
-1
, respectively.
We note that
{\mathfrak{c}}({\mathfrak{k}})
is one-dimensional if
G
is simple.

Let
G_{\mathbb{C}}
be a complex reductive Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and
P_{\mathbb{C}}
the maximal parabolic subgroup with Lie algebra

{\mathfrak{p}}:={\mathfrak{k}}+{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
,
with abelian nilradical
{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
. The complex structure
of the homogeneous space
G/K
is induced from
the open embedding

G/K\subset G_{\mathbb{C}}/K_{\mathbb{C}}\exp\mathfrak{n}_{+}=G_{\mathbb{C}}/P_{\mathbb{C}}.

Let
G^{\prime}
be a connected reductive subgroup of
G
.
Without loss of generality we assume
that
G^{\prime}
is
\theta
-stable. We set

K^{\prime}:=K\cap G^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, we assume that the characteristic element
Z\in\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{k})
satisfies

(2.5)

Z\in\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}.

If the condition (
2.5
) holds, then the parabolic subalgebra
\mathfrak{p}
is
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
-compatible
and the homogeneous space

G^{\prime}/K^{\prime}
is a Hermitian sub-symmetric space of
G/K
such that the embedding

G^{\prime}/K^{\prime}\hookrightarrow G/K

is holomorphic.

Consider a finite-dimensional representation of
K
on a complex vector space
V
. We extend it to a holomorphic representation of
P_{\mathbb{C}}

by letting the unipotent subgroup
\exp(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
act trivially,
and form a holomorphic
vector bundle
\mathcal{V}_{X_{\mathbb{C}}}=G_{\mathbb{C}}\times_{P_{\mathbb{C}}}V
over
X_{\mathbb{C}}=G_{\mathbb{C}}/P_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Restricting to the open subset
G/K\subset X_{\mathbb{C}},
we obtain a
G
-equivariant holomorphic vector bundle
\mathcal{V}:=G\times_{K}V
. We thus have
a natural representation of
G
on the vector space
\mathcal{O}(G/K,\mathcal{V})
of global holomorphic sections, endowed with
the Fréchet topology of uniform convergence on compact sets.

Likewise, given a finite-dimensional representation
W
of
K^{\prime}
, we form
the
G^{\prime}
-equivariant holomorphic vector bundle
\mathcal{W}=G^{\prime}\times_{K^{\prime}}W
over
G^{\prime}/K^{\prime}
and consider
the representation of
G^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{O}(G^{\prime}/K^{\prime},\mathcal{W})
.
By definition, it is clear that

(2.6)

\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{hol}}(\mathcal{V}_{X},\mathcal{W}_{Y})\subset\operatorname{Hom}_{G^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{O}\left(G/K,\mathcal{V}\right),\mathcal{O}(G^{\prime}/K^{\prime},\mathcal{W})\right).

The Localness Theorem below shows that these two spaces in fact coincide.

Theorem 2.6

(Localness Theorem)
.

Let
G^{\prime}
be a reductive
subgroup of

2.2.
F-method

Let

Let us make a few comments on these examples.

In cases (1) and (2), both

and

are one-dimensional. Consequently, the symbols of the differential SBOs are given by scalar-valued polynomials, which are characterized via the F-method as polynomial solutions to certain second-order differential equations. By contrast, case (4) provides

In the remainder of this section, we focus on cases (1) and (5) in detail.

In Section

2.4.
Blow-up of Multiplicities

In this section, we explain why and how the multiplicity-free property of fusion rules of Verma modules fails for highly singular values of spectral parameters, and we emphasize the role played by symmetry breaking operators with holomorphic parameters in this phenomenon.
In order to avoid unnecessary technicalities, we restrict our attention to the group
G=SU(1,1)
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
, we write
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
for the
G
-equivariant holomorphic line bundle over the unit disk

D=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}.

Using the Bruhat decomposition, we trivialize the line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
and identify
the regular representation of
G
on

\mathcal{O}(D,\mathcal{L}_{\lambda})
with the following multiplier representation on
\mathcal{O}(D)
, as in (
1.5
) for
the action of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{H})
:

\left(\pi_{\lambda}(g)F\right)(z)=(cz+d)^{-\lambda}F\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right),\,\mathrm{for}\,g^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},F\in\mathcal{O}(D).

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, the representation
\pi_{\lambda}
, originally defined for the discrete parameter

\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
, admits an extension to complex values of
\lambda
. In order to make sense of
this extension, it is necessary to pass from
G=SU(1,1)
to the universal covering group
\widetilde{G}=\widetilde{SU}(1,1)
.

Let
\mathfrak{b}
be a Borel subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
consisting of lower triangular matrices. The induced
module
\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{b}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(\nu)=U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes_{U(\mathfrak{b})}\mathbb{C}_{\nu}

is a Verma module of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

In our parametrization, if
\lambda=1-k
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
the
k
-dimensional irreducible representation occurs both as a subrepresentation of

(\pi_{\lambda},\mathcal{O}(D))
and as a quotient of
\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{b}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(-\lambda)
.

We consider symmetry breaking operators from the tensor product representation

\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime}})\,\widehat{\otimes}\,\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}})=\mathcal{O}(D\times D,\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\boxtimes\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}})
to
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}})
.
Here,
\widehat{\otimes}
denote the completion of the tensor product of two nuclear spaces.
Theorem
2.13
asserts that the Rankin–Cohen bracket
\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}
provides an example of such an operator whenever

\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}-\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in 2\mathbb{N}.

For generic values of the parameters, these operators exhaust all symmetry
breaking operators.
However, for certain highly singular values of spectral parameters
(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})
, the Rankin–Cohen brackets vanish, whereas other differential symmetry breaking operators emerge.
In what follows, we examine this phenomenon in detail.

For
(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
, we define

H(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}):=\mathrm{Hom}_{\widetilde{G}}(\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime}})\widehat{\otimes}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}),\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}})).

By the Localness Theorem
2.6
, this space coincides with the space of differential symmetry breaking operators:

\displaystyle H(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Diff}_{\widetilde{G}}(\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime}})\widehat{\otimes}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}),\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}))

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{b}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(-\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}),\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{b}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(-\lambda^{\prime})\otimes\operatorname{ind}_{\mathfrak{b}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(-\lambda^{\prime\prime})),

where the second isomorphism follows from the Duality Theorem
2.1
.

The general
theory of multiplicity-freeness of branching rules
[
17
]
implies that the dimension of
H(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})
is
generically
equal to 0 or 1. However, finer properties of symmetry breaking operators allow us to control the behavior of multiplicities even beyond the generic case.
The following result gives a precise dimension formula
[
25
, Theorem 9.1]
:

Theorem 2.9
.

The dimension of the vector space
H(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})
is uniformly bounded for all

(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
. More precisely,

(1)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\{0,1,2\}
.

(2)

H(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})\neq\{0\}
if and only if

(2.9)

\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}-\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in 2\mathbb{N}.

(3)

Suppose (
2.9
) is satisfied. Then the following three conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})=2
.

(ii)

(2.10)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z},\quad 2\geq\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime},\quad\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}\geq|\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda^{\prime\prime}|+2.

(iii)

\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}=0
.

A purely algebraic characterization of the multiplicity blow-up phenomenon has recently been formulated by R. Murakami
[
33

, from the viewpoint of the extent to which complete reducibility fails for tensor product of Verma modules. More precisely, in the notations of Theorem

, the condition
\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}=0
is equivalent to

(\mathrm{iv})\quad 2\leq\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}\,\mathrm{and}\,\left(M(-\lambda^{\prime})\otimes M(-\lambda^{\prime\prime})\right)^{\chi_{-\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}+1}}=M(-\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime})\oplus M(\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}+2).

Here,
\chi_{\nu}:\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
denotes the
\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})
-infinitesimal
character via the Harish-Chandra isomorphism, and

M^{\chi_{\nu}}
denotes the
\mathfrak{g}
-submodule of a module
M
consisting of elements annihilated by a power of the ideal
\mathrm{Ker}(\chi_{\nu}:\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathbb{C})
, that is the
\chi_{\nu}
-primary component of a locally
\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})
-finite

\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{IGr}_{n-1}(\mathbb{C}^{2n-2})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathrm{IGr}_{n}(\mathbb{C}^{2n}),

where
\mathrm{Gr}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{k})
denotes the complex Grassmanian of
p
-planes in
\mathbb{C}^{k}
,

\mathrm{Q}^{m}\mathbb{C}:=\{z\in\mathbb{P}^{m+1}\mathbb{C}\,:\,z_{0}^{2}+\cdots+z_{m+1}^{2}=0\}
is the complex quadric,
and
\mathrm{IGr}_{n}(\mathbb{C}^{2n}):=\{V\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n}\,:\,\mathrm{dim}\,V=n,\,Q|_{V}\equiv 0\}
is the Grassmanian of isotropic subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
with respect to a non-degenerate quadratic form
Q
.
The corresponding three pairs of complexified reductive groups
(G_{\mathbb{C}},G^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}})
are

\displaystyle(GL(p+q,\mathbb{C}),GL(p+q-1,\mathbb{C})\times GL(1,\mathbb{C})),

\displaystyle(SO(2n+2,\mathbb{C}),GL(n+1,\mathbb{C}))

\displaystyle(SO(2n+2,\mathbb{C}),SO(2n,\mathbb{C})\times SO(2,\mathbb{C})).

On the other hand, differential symmetry breaking operators arising from the other three geometries include higher-dimensional analogues of Rankin–Cohen brackets, which we now explain.

From now on, as a representative case, we consider the pair

(G_{\mathbb{C}},G^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}})=(GL(n+1,\mathbb{C})\times GL(n+1,\mathbb{C}),GL(n+1,\mathbb{C})),

where the real form
G^{\prime}
is taken to be the Hermitian Lie group
U(n,1)
.
The case
n=1
was essentially treated in the previous section.
Our method provides a conceptual understanding of higher-dimensional generalization of Rankin–Cohen brackets as follows.

Consider the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, defined by

D:=\{Z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\,:\,\|Z\|<1\},

where

\|Z\|^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|z_{j}|^{2}

for
Z=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n})
.
This is the Hermitian symmetric domain of type AIII in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
in the É. Cartan classification.
As in the
n=1
case treated in Section
2.4
, the Lie group
G^{\prime}=U(n,1)
acts biholomorphically on
D
by linear fractional transformations:

g\cdot Z=(aZ+b)(cZ+d)^{-1}\quad{\mathrm{for}}\quad g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in U(n,1),\,Z\in D.

The isotropy subgroup at the origin is isomorphic to

K^{\prime}=U(n)\times U(1)
.

For
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we form
a
U(n,1)
-equivariant holomorphic line bundle
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}=U(n,1)\times_{U(n)\times U(1)}\mathbb{C}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}
over
D
associated with the holomorphic character of the complexified isotropy group
K^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}}=GL(n,\mathbb{C})\times GL(1,\mathbb{C})
:

GL(n,\mathbb{C})\times GL(1,\mathbb{C})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times},\quad(A,d)\mapsto(\det A)^{-\lambda_{1}}d^{-\lambda_{2}}.

The representation of
U(n,1)
on
\mathcal{O}(D,\mathcal{L}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})})
can be identified, via the Bruhat open cell trivialization, with the multiplier representation, denoted simply by
\pi_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}
, of
U(n,1)
on
\mathcal{O}(D)
given by

\left(\pi_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(g)F\right)(Z)=(cZ+d)^{-\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}(\det g)^{-\lambda_{1}}F\left(({aZ+b})({cZ+d})^{-1}\right).

Next, recall from (
1.11
) the definition of the homogeneous polynomial in two variables obtained by inflating the classical Jacobi polynomial:

(2.18)

P_{\ell}^{\alpha,\beta}(x,y):=y^{\ell}P_{\ell}^{\alpha,\beta}\left(2\frac{x}{y}+1\right).

For instance,
P_{0}^{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=1
,
P_{1}^{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=(2+\alpha+\beta)x+(\alpha+1)y
, etc.

Let
\widetilde{U}(n,1)
denote the universal covering of the group
U(n,1)
. Then, for
all
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
one defines a

\widetilde{U}(n,1)
-equivariant holomorphic line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}
over
D
, and the corresponding representation of
\widetilde{U}(n,1)
on
\mathcal{O}(D,\mathcal{L}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})})
.

In contrast to the
n=1
case, representations on the spaces of holomorphic sections of line bundles are no longer closed under tensor product decompositions; that is, most of the irreducible components that appear in the decomposition cannot be realized as spaces of sections of line bundles (see
[
17
]
for explicit branching laws in the general setting). This naturally leads one to consider holomorphic vector bundles, rather than line bundles alone, as the target of symmetry breaking operators.

For this purpose, let
a\in\mathbb{N},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let
W_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}

denote the irreducible representation of
K^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}}=GL(n,\mathbb{C})\times GL(1,\mathbb{C})
having the highest weight

(-\lambda_{1},\cdots,-\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{1}-a;-\lambda_{2}+a)
. Then
W_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}

can be realized on the space
\operatorname{Pol}^{a}[v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}]
of homogeneous polynomials of degree
a
, where
(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})
are the standard coordinates on

(\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n})/\mathrm{diag}\mathbb{C}^{n}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Differential operators on
D\times D
with values in the vector space
W_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}

can be written as elements in

\mathbb{C}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\prime}_{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\prime}_{n}},\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\prime\prime}_{1}},\cdots\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\prime\prime}_{n}}\right]\otimes\operatorname{Pol}^{a}[v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}].

Associated with the
K_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
-module
W_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}
, we form a
G^{\prime}
-equivariant holomorphic vector bundle
\mathcal{W}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}
over
D=G^{\prime}/K^{\prime}
.

As in the line bundle case, for all
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
, one can define a corresponding

\widetilde{U}(n,1)
-equivariant vector bundle, which we also denote
\mathcal{W}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}
. Similarly to
\pi_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}
for the line
bundle
\mathcal{L}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}
, we define the representation

\pi_{W_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}}
of
\widetilde{G^{\prime}}
on
\mathcal{O}(D,\mathcal{W}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a})\simeq\mathcal{O}(D)\otimes\mathcal{W}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}
.

We are now ready to describe symmetry breaking operators in the higher-dimensional case.

Theorem 2.13

(
[
25
]
, Theorem 8.1)
.

Let
\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime},\lambda_{1}^{\prime\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{C},a\in\mathbb{N}
, and

\lambda^{\prime}:=\lambda_{1}^{\prime}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime}

and
\lambda^{\prime\prime}:=\lambda_{1}^{\prime\prime}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime\prime}
.

(1)

The space

\mathrm{Hom}_{\widetilde{U}(n,1)}\left({\mathcal{O}}(D,{\mathcal{L}}_{(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})})\,\widehat{\otimes}\,\mathcal{O}(D,{\mathcal{L}}_{(\lambda_{1}^{\prime\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime\prime})}),{\mathcal{O}}(D,{\mathcal{W}}_{(\lambda_{1}^{\prime}+\lambda_{1}^{\prime\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime}+\lambda_{2}^{\prime\prime})}^{a})\right)

has dimension either one or two. It is equal to two if and only if

(2.19)

\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\{-1,-2,\cdots\}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad a\geq\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2a-1\geq|\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda^{\prime\prime}|.

(2)

The vector-valued differential
operator

(2.20)

D_{X\to Y,a}:=P_{a}^{\lambda^{\prime}-1,-\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda^{\prime\prime}-2a+1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}},\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}\right)

intertwines
\pi_{(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})}\boxtimes\pi_{(\lambda_{1}^{\prime\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime\prime})}\Big|_{G^{\prime}}
with
\pi_{W}
, where
W\simeq W^{a}_{(\lambda_{1}^{\prime}+\lambda_{1}^{\prime\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime}+\lambda_{2}^{\prime\prime})}
.

(3)

If the parameters
(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},a)
satisfy the condition (
2.19
), then
D_{X\to Y,a}=0
. Otherwise,
any symmetry breaking operator
is proportional to
D_{X\to Y,a}
.

Remark 2.14
.

(1)

Part (1) of Theorem
2.13
shows that, as in the
n=1
case, the blow-up phenomenon of multiplicities can also occur in higher dimensions. The condition (
2.19
) specifies precisely when this occurs. As noted in the previous section, this phenomenon exhibits a rich interplay not only within representation-theoretic framework but also with other areas of mathematics, such as special solutions of differential equations.

(2)

The vector bundle

{\mathcal{W}}_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{a}

is a line bundle
if and only if
a=0

or
n=1
.
In the case
n=1
,
the formula (
2.20
) reduces to
the classical Rankin–Cohen bidifferential operators (see (
1.4
)) with an appropriate
choice of spectral parameters, namely, for
a:=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}-\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{N}
, the following identity holds:

(2.21)

\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}=(-1)^{a}P^{\lambda^{\prime}-1,1-\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}}_{a}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}},\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}}\right)\big|_{z_{1}=z_{2}=z}.

Remark 2.15
.

Let
X=D\times D\supset Y=D
.

(1)

If
\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime},\lambda_{1}^{\prime\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a\in\mathbb{N}

G
and

(2)

If
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}>n

remain true

3.
Further Perspectives

Building on a detailed understanding of a broad range of symmetry breaking operators, we initiated in
[
28
]
a new line of investigation
into branching problems
by introducing the concept of
generating operator
. This operator is defined
as a formal power series in
t
,

(3.1)

T(t)\equiv T(\{R_{\ell}\};t):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{R_{\ell}}{\ell!}t^{\ell}\in\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma(X),\Gamma(Y))\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[[t]],

for a family of operators
R_{\ell}:\Gamma(X)\longrightarrow\Gamma(Y)
, where
X
and
Y
are smooth manifolds, and
\Gamma(X)
and
\Gamma(Y)
denote appropriate function (or section) spaces defined on them.

In the case when
X=\{\text{point}\}
, each

R_{\ell}
may be identified with an element of
\Gamma(Y)
,
and the generating operator reduces to the classical notion of a
generating function
.
Such functions have played a prominent role
in the classical theory of orthogonal polynomials, especially
when
\Gamma(Y)={\mathbb{C}}[y]
.

When
X=Y
, the space

\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma(X),\Gamma(Y))\simeq{\operatorname{End}}(\Gamma(X))

carries a natural ring structure,
and one may take
R_{\ell}

to be the
\ell
-th power of a single operator
R

on
X
.
In this case,
the operator
T(t)
in (
3.1
)
may be written as
e^{tR}
,
provided that the series converges.
We note that even if
R
is a differential operator
on a manifold
X
,
the resulting operator
T(t)=e^{tR}
is generally no longer differential.
For example,
if
R=\frac{d}{dz}
acts on
{\mathcal{O}}({\mathbb{C}})
,
then
T(t)=e^{t\frac{d}{dz}}
is the shift operator

f(z)\mapsto f(z+t)
.

In the very special setting where
X=Y
and
R
is a self-adjoint operator
with spectrum bounded from above,
the generating operator
T
reduces to the classical

semigroup

e^{tR}
, which is
generated by
R
for
\operatorname{Re}t>0
and has been extensively studied.
Typical examples include
the heat kernel
for
R=\Delta
,
the Hermite semigroup
for
R=\frac{1}{4}(\Delta-|x|^{2})
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
or
the Laguerre semigroup
for
R=|x|(\frac{\Delta}{4}-1)
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\frac{1}{|x|}dx)
.

We propose to consider a more general setting
where we allow
X\neq\{\text{point}\}
and
X\neq Y
.
In this broader context,
we refer to
T
in (
3.1
)
as the
generating operator

for the family of operators

R_{\ell}\colon\Gamma(X)\to\Gamma(Y)
.

More specifically, when
(X,Y)=(D\times D,\mathrm{diag}\,D)
, we
found, after choosing an appropriate normalization, an explicit closed formula for the generating operator
associated with the family of Rankin–Cohen operators
R_{\ell}=\mathcal{RC}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}+2\ell}
(
[
27
,
28
]
). This concept aims to control branching rules in an integrated manner through a global analytic object.

As an application of the concept of generating operator,
we proposed in
[
19
]
a method
for transferring
discrete data

into
continuous data
via the generating operator.
This is accomplished by solving a Mittag-Leffler type problem for operator-valued meromorphic functions. We
illustrated this method with an example involving
SL_{2}
demonstrating that
the
generating operator

T

associated with the Rankin–Cohen brackets
\{R_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}}

yields various families
of non-local intertwining operators with continuous parameters, which emerge
in geometric settings distinct from the original domains of the operators.

Specifically, the countable family of holomorphic differential operators

\{R_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}}
initially defined
on the Poincaré upper half-plane, leads to a generating operator for which the formal power series converges near
t=0
.
Although the generating operator itself is non-local, its generator can be extended to the entire complex projective space via the Extension Theorem
2.7
. Consequently, the generating operator is also well-defined on the complex projective space. To recover the original discrete data (the Rankin–Cohen brackets), one differentiate with respect to
t
. Alternatively, by applying
a
fractional derivative
in
t
, one can obtain continuous data—specifically, symmetry breaking operators with continuous parameters.

When restricted to a different real form, it produces a family of covariant operators
in various geometric settings, including symmetry breaking for tensor products of principal series representations of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
, Poisson transforms for
the de Sitter space
d\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and concrete embedding of discrete series representations into principal series representations.

This strategy is also relevant for higher-rank groups: extensions of generating operators associated with families of normal derivatives on Hermitian symmetric spaces give rise to new differential symmetry breaking operators on real Grassmannians.

We believe that this represents a challenging and promising research direction.
We expect it to enhance our understanding of
branching problems for infinite-dimensional representations of higher-rank reductive Lie groups.
