Challenges and Opportunities in Advancing Global Craniofacial Surgery: A Framework for Ethical, Sustainable, and Effective Collaboration
Authors: Diego A. Gomez, Skyler K. Palmer, Raymond Harshbarger, Amanda A. Gosman, Fatima Arif, Greta L. Davis, David K. Chong, Christopher M. Runyan, Phuong D. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Global disparities in access to craniofacial surgery remain profound, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where delayed or absent surgical intervention can lead to severe functional impairment, psychosocial distress, and preventable mortality. These inequities stem from a complex interplay of limited surgical infrastructure, workforce shortages, financial barriers, and inadequate public awareness. This article outlines a framework for ethical, sustainable, and effective collaboration in global craniofacial surgery. We first characterized the scope of surgical need in low- and middle-income countries and examined the systemic obstacles to timely, safe, and affordable care. Through a review of current initiatives and models of global surgical partnership, we identified key areas of opportunity: (1) investment in local training and retention of surgical providers; (2) integration of craniofacial care within the national health systems; (3) reduction of out-of-pocket costs through policy reform and philanthropic support; and (4) culturally tailored efforts to improve health literacy and early diagnosis. We emphasized the importance of bidirectional knowledge exchange, long-term partnerships, and the avoidance of vertical, short-term interventions that fail to build local capacity. Ethical global engagement must move beyond charity-based paradigms to embrace principles of mutual respect, capacity-building, and long-term partnerships. By adopting strategies that are context-sensitive and locally led, the global craniofacial surgery field can foster resilient systems of care and reduce the burden of untreated surgical conditions in underserved regions.

Takeaways

Question: How can global craniofacial partnerships move beyond short-term missions toward ethical, sustainable, locally led care in low- and middle-income countries?

Findings: This review outlines major barriers to care—including clinical complexity, infrastructure limitations, ethical challenges, and financial constraints—and highlights strategies that promote enduring impact. Long-term, bidirectional partnerships, progressive case complexity, co-surgeon models, and context-appropriate training were identified as essential. Case studies from Vietnam, Moldova, El Salvador, Mercy Ships, and SHARE (Surgeons in Humanitarian Alliance for Reconstruction, Research, and Education) demonstrate capacity growth, strengthening of local systems, and transition to regional training hubs.

Meaning: Ethical, sustainable global craniofacial surgery hinges on locally driven, long-term partnerships that build durable capacity and prioritize patient safety.

INTRODUCTION

Craniofacial anomalies (CFAs) are congenital conditions affecting the soft tissues and skeletal structures of the face and skull, occurring in approximately 0.5%–2% of births, depending on the population and diagnostic criteria.1,2 Beyond cleft lip and palate, CFAs include craniosynostosis, jaw deformities, dental anomalies, and auricular or orbital malformations. These conditions can impair feeding, speech, breathing, and vision, and they contribute to stigma and social isolation.

Globally, an estimated 5 billion people lack access to safe surgical and anesthesia care.3 Much of this burden falls on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where up to 30% of disease requires surgical treatment and many congenital anomalies are amenable to timely intervention.3,4 Barriers to surgical care include geographic isolation, high out-of-pocket expenditures, limited insurance coverage, and shortages of providers trained to care for children with complex craniofacial conditions.5,6

This article outlines the global landscape of craniofacial inequity and proposes a framework for ethical, sustainable, and locally integrated solutions. We highlight systems-level challenges, emphasize principles of partnership and long-term engagement, and illustrate these concepts through case examples from Vietnam, Moldova, El Salvador, Mercy Ships, and the Surgeons in Humanitarian Alliance for Reconstruction, Research, and Education (SHARE) program.

CHALLENGES IN GLOBAL CRANIOFACIAL SURGERY

Clinical and Surgical Complexity

Craniofacial surgery is technically demanding and requires coordinated multidisciplinary care. Procedures such as orthognathic surgery, midface advancement, and craniosynostosis repair demand expertise in complex airway management, blood loss mitigation, and postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) monitoring, which can be difficult to provide in resource-constrained settings.7,8

Training pathways and certification standards vary widely across countries, leading to heterogeneity in local expertise. In some settings, a small number of surgeons may be responsible for both trauma and elective craniofacial reconstruction, with limited opportunities for subspecialty training. Effective global partnerships must therefore be tailored to local clinical capacity, training infrastructure, and institutional priorities, rather than assuming a uniform baseline.

Logistical and Infrastructural Barriers

Diagnosis and surgical planning for craniosynostosis, hemifacial microsomia, and midface hypoplasia often rely on computed tomography (CT) imaging and, occasionally, 3-dimensional modeling.9 In many LMICs, CT access may be limited by geographic distribution, equipment downtime, or out-of-pocket cost. Advanced implants, such as distractors, resorbable plates, and patient-specific devices, are frequently expensive, subject to import challenges, and sometimes reused out of necessity.9 Sterilization capacity, maintenance, and supply chain interruptions further complicate safe and consistent practice.

Craniofacial services are typically centralized in large cities or national referral hospitals. Families traveling from remote regions may face long distances, unreliable transportation, and lost income. Even when they reach tertiary centers for index operations, many cannot afford return visits for postoperative assessment, speech therapy, orthodontic care, or secondary procedures.10,11 These barriers contribute to delayed presentation and suboptimal long-term outcomes.

In conflict-affected regions, additional constraints—including safety risks, disrupted referral pathways, unreliable anesthesia and blood supply, evacuation logistics, and severely limited follow-up capacity—may further limit care delivery. Emerging models from countries affected by active conflict demonstrate adaptive solutions, including mobile craniofacial surgical units,12 national capacity assessments,13 and augmented-reality remote surgical support.14

Ethical and Sociocultural Sensitivities

Short-term surgical missions have long been a common model of global surgical engagement. However, they can carry unintended consequences when not integrated into local systems.15,16 Local surgeons have reported that visiting teams may inadvertently divert operating room time or staff, interrupt longitudinal care for complex conditions, or operate outside the norms of local practice and follow-up capacity.16,17

Differences in expectations and communication around postoperative care can also affect community trust. Patients treated by foreign teams may later present to local providers with complications but report that “the foreign doctors” promised different outcomes or timelines.16

Ethical global craniofacial engagement requires respect for local standards, shared decision-making about case selection, realistic discussion of risks and benefits, and explicit planning for postoperative follow-up within the local system. It also requires attention to language, cultural norms, and power dynamics between visiting and host teams.

Financial Constraints

Financial limitations remain a major barrier to the expansion and sustainability of global craniofacial surgery. Mission-based work requires funding for travel, lodging, equipment transport, and consumables, often supported by nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), professional societies, or philanthropy. Long-term capacity-building requires investment in local workforce training, infrastructure development, and acquisition or maintenance of specialized equipment.

Funding streams are often fragmented and short-term, which can lead to interruptions in service, inability to maintain donated equipment, and difficulty retaining trained staff. Patients and families face additional financial stressors—including transportation, accommodation, and lost wages—that may delay or prevent care even when surgery is nominally “free.”18 Addressing these financial barriers requires deliberate integration of craniofacial services into national health plans, partnership with local organizations and insurers, and diversified funding models that prioritize continuity over 1-time events.

OPPORTUNITIES FOR EFFECTIVE AND SUSTAINABLE IMPACT

Building Local Partnerships

Sustainable capacity-building depends on identifying and supporting committed local partners, including plastic, neurosurgical, maxillofacial, or otolaryngology specialists with pediatric interest and institutional backing. Given the congenital nature of many CFAs, affiliation with a children’s hospital or access to pediatric anesthesia and ICU support is essential for safe care.

Effective partnerships are built on mutual trust, transparent communication, and clearly defined shared goals. They require an understanding of local health system constraints, workforce dynamics, and patient priorities. Programs that are codesigned with local leaders and incorporate locally defined metrics of success are more likely to endure and adapt over time. Trainee involvement should likewise be intentional and prealigned with local leadership to support workforce development, sustainability, and systems-oriented training.

Optimizing Communication With Global Partners

Regular, open communication helps align expectations, refine case selection, and ensure continuity of care between visits. Cultural competence—respect for local customs, values, and practices—and cultural humility—ongoing self-reflection about one’s limitations and power—are both crucial.19,20 Together, they help mitigate misunderstandings, avoid paternalism, and promote bidirectional learning.21

Technology can extend the reach of partnerships via virtual case conferences, secure image sharing, asynchronous consultation, and postoperative review. When used thoughtfully and in compliance with local regulations, these tools can help maintain momentum, support real-time problem-solving, and reduce the isolation of surgeons working in low-resource settings.

Progressive Complexity in Case Selection

Case selection should follow a deliberate, staged approach that begins with simpler procedures to foster trust, assess capacity, and develop shared experience between visiting and local teams. As collaboration strengthens, progressively more complex interventions may be introduced in a manner that aligns with local capabilities and promotes surgical education. Importantly, care must never exceed the visiting team’s scope of practice or local system capacity based on the assumption that “some treatment is better than no treatment.”17 Instead, a gradual escalation in case complexity—coordinated alongside local surgeons—supports skill development and patient safety while building sustainable perioperative and follow-up pathways.

Complication Avoidance Strategies

Complication avoidance begins with structured preoperative planning and extends through long-term follow-up. Preoperative “huddles” clarify roles, review airway and blood loss plans, and anticipate potential challenges based on available resources. Training should emphasize protocols that are feasible within the local context rather than importing practices that depend on unavailable equipment or staffing.

Barriers to follow-up—travel distance, cost, lost wages, and competing responsibilities—must be anticipated. Programs have improved follow-up through transportation support, appointment clustering, and small incentive models for long-term outcome visits.11,22,23 Electronic registries, shared databases, and photographic documentation allow local teams to track outcomes and consult with partners asynchronously, strengthening shared accountability and iterative improvement.

FINANCIAL MODELS FOR SUSTAINABILITY

Financial sustainability requires diversification. Successful programs often combine support from NGOs, faith-based organizations, philanthropic donors, local charities, and academic institutions, alongside engagement with ministries of health. Governmental buy-in is particularly important to incorporate craniofacial care into essential health benefits packages, support workforce training, and allocate operating room and ICU resources.

Academic institutions may contribute faculty time, educational materials, research support, and equipment donations even when direct financial contributions are limited. Regardless of funder mix, partners should emphasize long-term local capacity rather than episodic “mission-based” care, and ensure that financial decisions do not undermine local providers or distort existing health priorities. Sustainable financing supports equipment maintenance, allied health services, and ongoing mentorship through telehealth or secure messaging platforms, maintaining local surgeon continuity between visiting team engagements.

PRESERVING AND STRENGTHENING LOCAL SURGICAL ECOSYSTEMS

Global craniofacial efforts should reinforce local health systems rather than creating parallel structures. Co-surgeon models, shared operative planning, and joint authorship of academic work recognize local expertise and build professional ownership. Whenever possible, care should be delivered within local hospitals rather than stand-alone or temporary facilities that may be difficult to integrate or sustain. Collaborations may also be challenged by political instability and shifting priorities within local health systems, underscoring the need for sustained engagement, integration of local leadership, and flexible program design.

Transparent engagement with patients and communities is also critical. Educational efforts that highlight local surgical capabilities and clearly define postoperative care pathways can counter the perception that foreign teams offer inherently superior care. Association with national surgical plans further strengthens long-term integration and equitable resource allocation.

CASE STUDIES AND PILOT PROGRAM OUTCOMES

Vietnam

Reconstructive surgery in Vietnam gained early structure with the establishment of the Barsky Unit in Saigon in 1969, which provided care for trauma-related injuries and congenital anomalies.24 In subsequent decades, NGOs such as Operation Smile (1989) and Smile Train (2007) expanded cleft services; however, complex craniofacial surgery remained limited in scope.

In 2000, the Reconstructive International Cooperation Exchange partnered with Ministry of Health hospitals in Hanoi to build local capacity in microsurgery, hand surgery, and craniofacial reconstruction. The program emphasized co-surgeon models and progressive autonomy. Capacity for intracranial surgery initially lagged due to limited ICU resources, equipment, and experience.

In 2020, the Reconstructive International Cooperation Exchange evolved into Nuoy Reconstructive International, emphasizing longitudinal growth, structured educational courses, and collaborative research. Regular in-country engagements since 2018 have led to substantial growth in craniofacial capability at Vietnam National Children’s Hospital, supported by ongoing case exchange and didactics across plastic, craniofacial, and oral–maxillofacial departments (Fig. 1).

Multidisciplinary partnership team at Vietnam National Children’s Hospital, consisting of ear, nose, and throat; oral maxillofacial surgery; and plastic surgery collaborators from Nuoy International and Vietnam National Children’s Hospital, reflecting a sustained partnership since 2018.

Early initiatives included feasibility studies to establish craniofacial databases in the setting of nascent electronic medical records.25 Clinically, one of the first major milestones was the adoption of mandibular distraction for Pierre Robin sequence using donated devices, co-surgeon cases, and iterative feedback, culminating in independent local practice.26 Craniosynostosis care progressed from open fronto-orbital advancement and total vault remodeling to dynamic techniques such as cranial distraction and spring-assisted cranioplasty, allowing earlier, age-appropriate intervention.

As local proficiency increased and responsibility shifted from visiting to local teams, case complexity expanded to midface advancements (Le Fort III/II with zygomatic repositioning) and hypertelorism correction (facial bipartition). Complications, including cerebrospinal fluid leaks, were comanaged through frequent messaging and real-time videoconferencing. Partnerships later extended to Children’s Hospital 1 and Children’s Hospital 2 in Ho Chi Minh City—centers that have now adopted distraction osteogenesis of the craniofacial skeleton—broadening Vietnam’s national craniofacial capacity.27

Moldova

Before 2000, craniofacial surgery in Moldova was largely limited to basic care for congenital facial anomalies. A 1999 partnership between the Republic of Moldova and the State of North Carolina, facilitated by the U.S. State Department, created a framework for economic, cultural, and scientific collaboration.28 In 2001, craniofacial surgeons from Wake Forest University began annual visits with maxillofacial surgeons at the Nicolae Testemitanu State University of Medicine and Pharmacy in Chișinău, initially focusing on secondary speech surgery and alveolar bone grafting.

As the partnership matured, efforts expanded to include craniosynostosis and syndromic craniofacial conditions, with increasing involvement of pediatric neurology and neurosurgery. Early practice was constrained by the lack of powered drills and saws, absorbable plating systems, and orthotic helmets. Nonetheless, yearly bilateral exchanges continued, and in 2011 cranial springs were introduced as a cost-effective technique for sagittal craniosynostosis.29 By the mid-2010s, a dedicated Moldovan craniofacial team—comprising a maxillofacial surgeon, pediatric neurosurgeon, pediatric neurologist, and anesthesiologist—began performing craniosynostosis repairs independently. On 1 occasion, asymmetrical parietal expansion required a midvault parietal switch cranioplasty at the time of spring removal; this was performed collaboratively with visiting experts.

In 2018, powered craniotomes and saws donated through Project C.U.R.E. enabled more complex procedures, including Le Fort I osteotomy, bilateral sagittal split osteotomy, genioplasty, subcranial Le Fort III distraction, cranial vault remodeling, and posterior vault distraction. Local trauma expertise allowed mini-fixation plates to be sourced from existing suppliers, whereas distraction hardware was safely reprocessed from previous cases. Since 2024, the Moldovan team has expanded its role to training Romanian maxillofacial and pediatric neurosurgeons at Marie Curie Children’s Hospital in Bucharest, demonstrating evolution from learner to regional educator and highlighting the multiplier effect of sustained partnership.

El Salvador

For more than 4 decades, Operation Smile and Austin Smiles conducted cleft missions in San Salvador using a vertical, short-term model in which families relied on visiting teams for primary cleft surgery (Fig. 2). Local capacity for comprehensive cleft and craniofacial care was limited. Over time, the establishment of Bloom Children’s Hospital, a nationwide children’s hospital staffed by pediatric plastic surgeons, and the global shift toward multidisciplinary cleft and craniofacial care changed this landscape.

Approaches to international outreach.

A long-term partnership emerged between Austin-based surgeons (including Austin Smiles) and Bloom Children’s Hospital, with Rotary International serving as a key local support organization. The shared goal was to build a sustainable cleft and craniofacial center at Bloom, grounded in relationship-building, excellence, and the delivery of “Global North–level” care in the Global South.

Baseline assets included in-house orthodontics, speech therapy, neurosurgery, ICU capacity, a blood bank, and multiple pediatric subspecialists. Using a “simple-to-complex” framework, maxillofacial surgery was introduced and has developed into a program that now provides orthognathic surgery, alveolar bone grafting, and maxillary and mandibular distraction osteogenesis.30 Patients are being evaluated for upper midface osteotomies, reflecting growing capability.

A second initiative focuses on developing intracranial surgery capacity. In partnership with local neurosurgeons, the team is establishing treatment pathways for nonsyndromic and syndromic craniosynostosis and for patients requiring frontofacial advancement. Education and financial sustainability have emerged as the 2 main pillars of long-term success. Mentorship occurs both in-country and at the home center in Austin, and 1 local plastic surgeon has completed a craniofacial fellowship abroad. An American Society of Maxillofacial Surgeons lecture and laboratory course, supported by Rotary, helped build skills among local surgeons. Ongoing work aims to establish an independent foundation to support prorated care and ensure the financial viability of the craniofacial center.

Mercy Ships

Mercy Ships is a faith-based organization providing free surgical care aboard floating hospitals that dock in partner countries for approximately 1 year at a time. Each ship includes CT imaging, 5 operating rooms, multiple wards with ICU capabilities, and infrastructure that allows volunteers to live onboard for extended periods.

Since 2015, a consistent craniofacial surgical and anesthesia team has been volunteering across Madagascar, Benin, Cameroon, Guinea, and Senegal. Trips typically last 3 weeks, with return visits at 6 months for postoperative follow-up. A local neurosurgeon plays a vital role in both capacity-building and ongoing patient care.

This hybrid model—combining medium-term presence with advanced onboard resources—has proven highly effective for providing complex craniofacial surgery in resource-limited settings. Recent studies highlight the model’s capacity-building potential and favorable clinical outcomes.31,32

The SHARE Program

The Plastic Surgery Foundation’s SHARE program is a global initiative dedicated to expanding surgical capacity through mentorship, education, and research collaboration. SHARE operates a 2-year fellowship that integrates global fellows, global educators, and global researchers in a pod-based mentorship structure. Each fellow is paired with 1 faculty educator and collaborating residents who support continuous learning, shared case discussions, and project development.

Since its inception, SHARE has trained 97 global fellows across 3 cycles, expanding from primarily East African partners to collaborations across sub-Saharan Africa, Europe, Southeast Asia, and South America. Resident and medical student participation has grown from 34 to more than 100, reflecting increasing interest in structured global surgery education.

The curriculum combines access to ASPS EdNet (the online corriculum of educational content through ASPS); Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery; and research education in grant writing, study design, and data analysis. SHARE-affiliated trainees and faculty have produced 59 peer-reviewed publications and received 13 pilot microgrants, strengthening local research ecosystems.

Cleft and craniofacial surgery have been integral to the curriculum since its inception. The program launched a Craniofacial Subspecialty Track to provide focused workshops on multidisciplinary care and context-adapted techniques such as spring-assisted craniosynostosis surgery. In a preworkshop survey of 27 participants from Kenya, Nigeria, Ethiopia, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Ghana, barriers to craniosynostosis treatment included lack of training (82%), limited equipment, insufficient ICU access, patient cost, and restricted pediatric anesthesia capacity. After participation, 94% reported improved confidence and perceived feasibility of initiating or expanding craniosynostosis care locally.

SHARE emphasizes sustained partnerships and faculty exchange, including visiting professorships coordinated with the American College of Surgeons and collaboration with institutions such as the University of Rwanda. Notably, 27 of 47 SHARE Global Educators (57.4%) are former fellows, illustrating the program’s ability to develop independent educators and regional champions who drive sustained capacity-building. The long-term objective is to support formal craniofacial fellowships within the African continent, ensuring continuity and accessibility of advanced reconstructive care. With growth in trainee volume, research output, and faculty retention, SHARE is building a scalable, regional model for craniofacial and reconstructive workforce development in LMICs.

CONCLUSIONS

Global craniofacial surgery faces significant challenges, including clinical complexity, limited infrastructure, ethical dilemmas, and financial constraints that disproportionately affect care delivery in LMICs. Addressing these barriers requires strategic, sustainable, and ethical approaches grounded in long-term partnerships. Central to this effort is the empowerment of local surgical ecosystems through capacity-building, integration with national health systems, and shared decision-making rooted in cultural humility. Sustainable progress depends not on episodic missions but on bidirectional knowledge exchange, collaborative case planning, and resource-sensitive program design. Despite substantial NGO investment in cleft and craniofacial care, the current landscape can be fragmented, with competition for patients, pressure toward exclusive alignment, and limited transparency regarding outcomes and cost-effectiveness. Greater coordination with national surgical plans and shared reporting standards may improve accountability while minimizing unintended harm. We call on the global surgical community to move beyond short-term interventions and commit to thoughtful, enduring partnerships that prioritize local leadership, patient safety, and equity.
