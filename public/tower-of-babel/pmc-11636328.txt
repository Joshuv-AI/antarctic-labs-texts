Student-led discussions of landmark discovery articles: a foothold in teaching primary virology literature
Authors: Camilla E. Hippee, Aloysius Klingelhutz, Li Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroducing students to primary scientific literature is essential for establishing scientific literacy; however, students can feel overwhelmed by the amount of information within a research article. In our virology discussion courses, we address this issue by creating a student-centered and active learning environment. Students present and guide discussions on the rationales, background, methods, results, and conclusions from research literature in class. This approach has been applied in both undergraduate and graduate settings for students studying microbiology, with different expectations and criteria between the two groups. Student evaluations collected from three semesters were positive toward the teaching methods. Students praised the inclusion of “landmark discovery” articles, which examined paradigm-shifting concepts in virology. Undergraduates expressed their increased confidence in comprehending scientific literature and highlighted the impact of group work. In a critique of the course, students suggested more variety in the topics covered and inclusion of recent publications. Overall, our enhanced approach and methods improved student experiences with primary scientific literature and promoted student learning.

INTRODUCTION

Primary science literature can be daunting for students at the undergraduate and early graduate levels. Research articles often contain techniques, abbreviations, and other vocabulary that are unfamiliar to students. Even so, reading primary literature is important for developing critical thinking skills and science literacy (1–3). As such, a considerable amount of research is focused on improving student experiences with primary science literature (2, 4–7).

Virology is a fundamental and rapidly evolving subdiscipline that is often underrepresented in general microbiology courses (8). To improve scientific literacy in the field of virology, we offer primary literature-based virology discussion courses to undergraduate and graduate students in the microbiology major. Here, we discuss our procedure and successes from three semesters of undergraduate and graduate virology literature discussion courses. We also share the students’ evaluations and discuss future improvements to the courses. These methods can be useful for instructors in classrooms with few students.

PROCEDURE

While this course is adapted for undergraduate or graduate students, the goals of the course remain the same. We aim to (i) introduce students to virology primary literature and teach skills necessary for interpretation, (ii) introduce students to current problems and approaches in virology, and (iii) reinforce and expand on concepts that were introduced in virology lectures and laboratories. Our goals are achieved by assigning students primary virology literature to read and prepare for a class discussion. This course uses a student-active teaching method as faculty instructors only contribute when necessary to prompt discussion, eliminate confusion, and provide context. Over the semester, the students cover a wide variety of topics and articles published between 1970 and the present day. Notably, some of these articles cover “landmark discoveries” such as the discovery of an important viral receptor or the key function of the unique retroviral reverse transcriptase (Table 1).

Undergraduate course overview

The prerequisite for students in the course is completion of a 3-credit undergraduate course of Viruses and Human Disease with a minimum grade of C. This course has roughly 8–12 senior, microbiology major students and meets once a week for 110 min. One research article is assigned per class period. Articles are carefully selected to reflect what students have learned in other courses and match the level of student understanding. Notably, this course is split by 2–3 virology faculty instructors, with each taking a block of the semester.

The instructor first teaches students how to read, understand, present, and discuss a research article (see supplemental information of the figure presentation rubric). Students are assigned to groups of 3–4 for the entirety of the semester. Groups are then assigned to articles on a rotating schedule. Students must work together to present, understand, and interpret their assigned article. They then prepare PowerPoint slides to present during class, which explain their article’s rationale, the results, and the main conclusions. Students who are not in the presenting group must read the article, complete a homework assignment with questions about the article (see supplemental information of the seven generic questions) before class, and contribute to the classroom discussion. Faculty instructors give feedback and provide grades on student homework and presentations.

Graduate course overview

The prerequisite for students in the course is successful completion of a 3-credit undergraduate virology course or a 4-credit graduate course of Viruses and Human Disease. Graduate students taking this course are in their first two years of various biomedical graduate programs and have research interests in virology. Differences in class structure from the undergraduate course are as follows: (i) 2–3 articles are assigned for each class period, (ii) students are individually assigned articles, (iii) there are no homework assignments, and (iv) at the end of a faculty’s block, the students turn in an abridged research proposal that is inspired from the assigned articles.

CONCLUSION

At the end of each semester, student evaluations were collected anonymously through a university-wide, standardized questionnaire, called Assessing the Classroom Environment (ACE). Students were asked to evaluate two statements concerning the learning methods: (i) “Learning focused—the instructor’s teaching methods helped students learn” and (ii) “Learning materials—the assignments, readings, and activities facilitated student learning”. Students responded with a rating from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), indicating how much they agree with the statement (Table 2). Student response rates to the survey averaged 52.3%, with an average number of five responses per semester. The average scores for the “learning focused” and “learning materials” statements were 5.95 and 5.96, respectively. Students scored other instructor blocks similarly (data not shown). Overall, the student evaluation scores of both courses were higher than the average scores of virology courses in the department, indicating positive feedback for the active learning approach.

ACE evaluations also give students three open-ended questions to which they can leave comments, critiques, and suggestions (Table 3). Notably, many students expressed relief. Undergraduate students commented that they were nervous or unsure of their skills in primary science literature when beginning this course. Both undergraduate and graduate students emphasized their increased confidence and skills in reading and interpreting science literature by course completion. Student comments suggest that the depth of the discussion was essential for these improvements. One student commented, “I felt that pausing presentations after each section of a figure [to have] a class discussion and [ask] questions made it easier for me to understand the material.” Students also asked for more class time or fewer articles to discuss, suggesting that instructors should be cautious when assigning material and allow time for in-depth discussion.

When students were asked what was most helpful to their learning, landmark discovery articles were specifically praised for their helpfulness in understanding the progression of scientific discovery. However, these articles were also subject to criticism as they did not offer graduate students a comprehensive platform for developing their novel research proposals. Landmark discovery articles may have been particularly influential because students often fear that they will not understand a research article (6). The findings of these landmark discovery articles had been previously introduced to students in other courses. Potentially, their familiarity with the topic eased their fears and allowed them to focus on the content. Students also suggested more variety in the viruses covered in this block. Thus, these courses could be improved by including paradigm-shifting publications from a wide breadth of viruses, but special attention should be paid to include recent publications for graduate student proposals.

In summary, undergraduate and graduate students in our virology literature discussion courses benefited from reading, presenting, and discussing discovery articles. In this student-led environment, there was stimulating discussion among students and faculty. Additionally, students were more likely to ask questions compared with other lecture-based didactic courses. Encouraging a relationship between students and primary literature is an important cause for increasing scientific literacy and combating disinformation and misinformation.
