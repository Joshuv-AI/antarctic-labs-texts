Adaptive graph-evolutionary framework for dynamic feature refinement in multi-label learning
Authors: Hossein Movahed, Tina Toosi, Shamim Nael
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multi-label learning in high-dimensional environments is fundamentally challenged by complex interdependencies among features and labels, as well as redundancy and noise that degrade predictive performance and scalability. Conventional feature selection techniques typically rely on static optimization or one-shot subset identification, limiting their ability to adapt to evolving feature relevance during learning. To address these limitations, this study introduces an adaptive graph-evolutionary framework for dynamic feature refinement in multi-label learning. Instead of treating feature selection as a fixed optimization problem, the proposed approach models feature reduction as a sequential decision process guided by structural relationships and uncertainty estimation. A heterogeneous graph is constructed to capture feature–label–instance interactions, while a graph neural network dynamically evaluates feature reliability through confidence and uncertainty modeling. These signals are integrated into an evolutionary optimization mechanism that adaptively refines the feature space through staged elimination, enabling continuous adjustment to changing label dependencies. The proposed framework is evaluated on multiple benchmark datasets using standard multi-label classifiers. Experimental results demonstrate consistent improvements over state-of-the-art methods, achieving significant reductions in Hamming Loss and Ranking Loss, alongside notable gains in Average Precision and F1-score. Statistical analysis confirms the robustness and significance of the improvements across varying data distributions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56255-5.

Introduction

The rapid growth of high-dimensional data across modern applications has significantly increased the complexity of machine learning tasks, particularly in scenarios where instances are associated with multiple labels simultaneously. Recent advances in multi-graph embedding techniques have demonstrated the importance of modeling complex structural relationships in partially labeled environments1. At the same time, uncertainty-aware graph-guided frameworks have highlighted the critical role of incorporating ambiguity and incomplete supervision into learning processes2. In this context, multi-label learning has emerged as a fundamental paradigm, where traditional problem transformation methods such as Binary Relevance provide a baseline yet often fail to capture inter-label dependencies effectively3. More comprehensive studies have emphasized the growing importance of modeling label correlations and structural dependencies to improve predictive performance in multi-label settings4.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have recently become a powerful tool for capturing complex relational structures, particularly with advancements such as flexible message-passing mechanisms that extend beyond homophily assumptions5. In parallel, the increasing prevalence of streaming and imbalanced data has introduced additional challenges, motivating adaptive learning mechanisms capable of handling evolving distributions6. Furthermore, uncertainty and imprecision in multi-view and high-dimensional data representations have been identified as critical factors affecting model robustness and generalization7. To address scalability concerns, topology-aware graph construction frameworks and efficient graph storage and processing techniques have been proposed, enabling large-scale graph-based learning8,9.

Despite these advancements, multi-label feature selection remains a challenging problem, especially in the presence of missing labels and noisy data10. Recent surveys on deep learning for multi-label learning have highlighted the need for integrating structural modeling, uncertainty estimation, and adaptive optimization within a unified framework11. In this direction, reinforcement learning and sequential decision-making approaches on graphs have been introduced to model complex interactions and dynamic optimization processes12. Additionally, heterogeneous graph-based message passing frameworks have demonstrated the effectiveness of capturing multi-type relationships among features, labels, and instances13. Complementary advances in representation learning, including transformer-based architectures, further enhance the ability to extract discriminative features from complex data modalities14.

Traditional multi-label feature selection methods, as extensively reviewed in earlier studies, often rely on static evaluation criteria and fail to adapt to evolving feature relevance15. Approaches that incorporate label-specific feature modeling partially address inter-label dependencies but still lack dynamic adaptability16. Recent graph-based techniques have shown promise in capturing structural information through clustering and relational modeling, particularly in high-dimensional data representations17. Adaptive graph partitioning strategies further improve the modeling of heterogeneous data distributions by dynamically restructuring relationships18. Moreover, combining local and global structural information has been shown to enhance representation quality and stability in graph-based learning frameworks19.

Evolutionary optimization methods have been widely used to address the combinatorial nature of feature selection, with multi-objective differential evolution algorithms enabling the simultaneous optimization of relevance and redundancy20. However, these methods often operate independently of structural learning mechanisms, limiting their ability to fully exploit relational information. Recent developments in GNN architectures and integration strategies have emphasized the need for unified frameworks that combine structural learning with adaptive optimization21. In addition, graph kernel methods provide an alternative perspective for capturing complex dependencies in structured data, further reinforcing the importance of graph-based modeling in multi-label learning22.

More recently, deep reinforcement learning approaches have been explored for sequential decision-making and adaptive scheduling in dynamic environments, demonstrating their effectiveness in handling uncertainty and optimizing long-term objectives23. Similarly, the integration of GNNs with advanced control and optimization strategies in complex systems highlights the potential of combining graph-based reasoning with adaptive decision processes24.

Expanding upon this concept, we suggest a new framework called PG-GFE. The fundamental concept is integrating evolutionary optimization with graph-based reasoning in a progressive elimination loop. A graph neural network is used to evaluate feature confidence and uncertainty at each step of elimination, while a heterogeneous graph is built to explicitly characterize feature–label–instance relationships. These confidence estimates function as dynamic guiding signals for an evolutionary optimization technique that determines when and how features should be removed, rather than only being employed for feature ranking. The suggested method strikes a principled compromise between compactness, stability, and performance preservation by maximizing the elimination trajectory rather than a static subset.

A key component of the suggested paradigm is the interplay between evolutionary optimization and graph reasoning. The graph structure and confidence distributions are changed when features are gradually eliminated, enabling the model to adjust to changing label dependencies. The system can react to intricate, non-linear interactions seen in multi-label data thanks to its closed-loop architecture, which also reduces the possibility of making snap or irrevocable elimination judgments. As a result, PG-GFE provides a strong substitute for traditional feature selection techniques, especially in high-dimensional and high-label-cardinality scenarios. Related work

Multi-label learning has attracted significant attention due to its ability to model complex relationships between instances and multiple target labels. Early approaches primarily relied on kernel-based frameworks, where advanced kernel learning systems were employed to capture nonlinear feature–label interactions and improve classification performance25. Similarly, multiple kernel learning combined with support vector machines has demonstrated effectiveness in specialized domains such as bioinformatics, enabling the integration of heterogeneous feature representations for accurate prediction26. Despite their flexibility, kernel-based approaches often struggle with scalability and fail to explicitly model structural dependencies among features and labels.

To address high-dimensional feature spaces, evolutionary optimization techniques have been widely explored. Many-objective differential evolution methods enhance diversity and optimize multiple criteria such as relevance and redundancy simultaneously, making them suitable for complex multi-label feature selection tasks27. However, these approaches typically operate in a static optimization framework, where feature subsets are determined in a single stage, limiting adaptability to evolving feature importance during learning.

Recent advances have emphasized the role of graph-based learning in capturing structural relationships. Multi-granularity graph convolutional networks have been proposed to model hierarchical feature–label interactions, enabling improved representation learning in multi-label information systems28. Dual graph neural network architectures further extend this idea by incorporating label-aware mechanisms, allowing more precise modeling of label dependencies in domain-specific applications29. In extreme multi-label scenarios, graph neural networks have also been applied to handle large-scale label spaces by leveraging relational structures among labels and features30. While these methods effectively capture structural dependencies, they generally assume a fixed graph representation and lack mechanisms for dynamic feature refinement.

Feature selection strategies tailored to multi-label settings have also been investigated. Class-dependent feature selection approaches consider label-specific characteristics to improve classification performance, providing more refined feature subsets compared to global selection methods31. Swarm intelligence-based algorithms, such as ant colony optimization, explore feature spaces through heuristic search processes that balance relevance and redundancy32. Active learning techniques have been introduced to reduce annotation costs by selecting informative instances based on uncertainty, thereby improving model efficiency in data-scarce environments33. Nevertheless, these approaches often overlook the dynamic interplay between feature relevance and structural dependencies over time.

Graph-based feature selection methods have shown promising results by explicitly modeling feature–label relationships. Subgraph association techniques combined with graph representation learning enable the identification of informative features through structural patterns, improving selection accuracy34. Additionally, uncertainty-aware mechanisms have been incorporated into graph-based models to enhance robustness, particularly by considering feature-space confusability and model uncertainty during evaluation35. Semantic-sensitive graph convolutional networks further improve representation quality by integrating contextual information, especially in text-based multi-label classification tasks36. Despite these improvements, most graph-based approaches rely on static evaluation criteria and do not account for progressive updates in feature importance.

More recent studies have explored advanced graph learning paradigms to enhance knowledge transfer and representation learning. Bi-directional knowledge transfer mechanisms in graph neural networks have demonstrated the ability to improve learning efficiency by sharing information across different levels of representation37. In parallel, link prediction methods based on hyperspherical embeddings provide alternative ways to model complex relationships in structured data, contributing to improved relational learning38. Semi-supervised approaches have also been proposed to address label imbalance by leveraging label propagation techniques, enabling better generalization in scenarios with limited labeled data39.

Despite these advancements, several limitations remain. First, most existing methods treat feature selection as a static or one-shot optimization problem, failing to capture the progressive nature of feature relevance. Second, graph-based models often assume fixed structures, limiting their ability to adapt to evolving feature–label relationships. Third, evolutionary and swarm-based methods provide strong exploration capabilities but lack integration with structural learning mechanisms, resulting in suboptimal performance in highly dynamic environments. Finally, uncertainty and stability considerations are often treated as auxiliary factors rather than being explicitly integrated into the optimization process.

Proposed method

The PG-GFE framework for multi-label learning is presented in this section. PG-GFE formulates feature reduction as a progressive elimination problem, where features are gradually removed based on their evolving relevance, confidence, and stability within the multi-label learning process. This is in contrast to traditional feature selection approaches, which seek to directly identify an optimal subset of features in a single optimization process. The main driving force behind this formulation is the fact that feature relevance in multi-label data is dynamic and varies as label dependencies become more apparent and redundant or noisy features are eliminated.

The suggested methodology combines evolutionary optimization with graph-based relational modeling in a closed-loop elimination approach. To explicitly record interactions between features, labels, and instances, a heterogeneous graph is built. Then, feature-level confidence ratings that account for both structural relevance and uncertainty are estimated using a graph neural network. These ratings are integrated into an evolutionary optimization process that adaptively defines the elimination trajectory over many stages, as opposed to being used just for ranking. The graph representation and confidence estimations are updated in accordance with the removal of a subset of features with high instability and low confidence at each step. There are three main stages to the whole PG-GFE framework:

Graph-based feature confidence modeling, which estimates feature uncertainty and encodes relational relationships.Evolutionary elimination schedule, which establishes the sequence and degree of feature removal.Progressive stabilization and termination, in which measurements of confidence and performance are eliminated until they coincide.

The proposed PG-GFE framework’s general process is shown in Fig. 1. A heterogeneous graph that explicitly describes feature-label-instance interactions is first created using a multi-label dataset. Node embeddings are taught to identify interconnections and capture structural dependencies based on this network. An elimination mask, the decision variable in the evolutionary scheduling step, is then encoded using these embeddings. Alternative elimination procedures are investigated using mutation and crossover operators, and potential solutions are assessed using a fitness function that strikes a compromise between confidence stability and performance preservation. Until the convergence requirements are met, the evolutionary process is repeated. The best elimination mask is chosen and used to gradually eliminate low-confidence features when convergence is reached.

Progressive graph-guided feature elimination framework

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D=\left\{\right({x}_{i},{Y}_{i}){\}}_{i=1}^{N}$$\end{document} denote a multi-label dataset, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$$\end{document} is a feature vector with d features and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}\subseteq\:L$$\end{document} is the associated label set. The goal of PG-GFE is to progressively reduce the original feature space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F=\{{f}_{1},{f}_{2},\dots\:,{f}_{d}\}$$\end{document} by eliminating features that contribute marginal or unstable information, while preserving predictive performance and label dependency structure. To achieve this goal, PG-GFE operates over a sequence of elimination stages indexed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t=\mathrm{1,2},\dots\:,T$$\end{document}. Graph-based feature confidence estimation

The created heterogeneous graph G (t) is utilized to transmit relational information and learn node representations using a graph neural network. Let ℎi(t) ∈ ℝd represent the feature node fi’s learnt embedding at elimination step t. To measure each feature’s dependability under the present limited feature space and changing label dependencies, a feature confidence score is calculated based on these embeddings.

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:cos(\cdot\:,\cdot\:)$$\end{document} stands for cosine similarity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Ui\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is an uncertainty term related to feature i, α and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} are balancing coefficients, and Ni is the neighborhood of feature node fi in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:G\left(t\right)$$\end{document}. Confidence levels are normalized into the range (0,1) using the sigmoid function σ(⋅). Instability in feature-label interactions is captured by the uncertainty term Ui(t), which is calculated as follows:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{i}\left(t\right)={\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{l}\in\:L}\left(\phi\:\right({h}_{i}\left(t\right),{h}_{\mathcal{l}}\left(t\right)\left)\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\phi\:\left(\cdot\:\right)$$\end{document} quantifies the degree of feature label connection and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:h\mathcal{l}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} indicates the embedding of label node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{l}$$\end{document}. Lower confidence levels result from inconsistent or unstable interactions with labels, which are indicated by a greater variance.

The suggested PG-GFE framework’s graph-based feature trust modeling stage is shown in Fig. 3. First, a heterogeneous graph representation of the input data is created using a multi-labeled dataset that includes instances, features, and related label sets. Redundancy and connectedness under multi-label dependencies are captured by the framework thanks to the built graph, which clearly represents feature-feature dependencies, feature-label linkages, and instance-label interactions. Then, a graph neural network is used to train feature-level embeddings that represent structural consistency and label interactions, as well as to transport relational information across diverse node types. Each feature’s stability and dependability in the present feature space are measured using a trust score that is computed based on these embeddings.

Evolutionary elimination scheduling

To establish the elimination schedule, PG-GFE uses an evolutionary optimization technique rather than choosing or rejecting features based on a predetermined threshold. Every member of the population encodes an elimination mask that indicates the percentage of low-confidence characteristics that should be eliminated at this point. A composite aim that strikes a compromise between elimination efficiency, confidence stability, and performance preservation is optimized via the evolutionary process. While selection promotes individuals who attain consistent performance with little feature retention, mutation and crossover operators are used to investigate various elimination techniques.

Progressive update and termination

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}^{\left(t+1\right)}$$\end{document} is obtained by updating the feature set after the application of the chosen elimination mask. The decreased feature space is then used to rebuild the graph, and confidence ratings are recalculated. Until convergence requirements are satisfied, such as a little gain in performance measurements or confidence stability, this progressive loop keeps going. A controlled elimination trajectory produces a compact, stable, and interpretable feature representation as the end product of PG-GFE, rather than just a chosen feature subset.

Evolutionary elimination scheduling

Based on the confidence information gathered from the graph-based modeling stage, an evolutionary optimization method is used in this phase to plan the gradual deletion of features. The suggested framework does not provide features static weights or pheromone values, in contrast to ant colony optimization employed in traditional weighting-based techniques. Rather, it approaches feature reduction as an adaptive elimination scheduling issue, where the goal is to decide which low-confidence features should be eliminated at each elimination step while maintaining stability and predictive performance.

Instead of representing a feature subset, each potential solution in the evolutionary population at elimination stage t represents an elimination mask. The percentage and identity of features to be removed from the current feature set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document} are controlled by this mask. The search procedure avoids hasty or irreversible selections and dynamically adjusts to shifts in feature importance by developing exclusion techniques rather than explicitly choosing features.

Evolutionary operators for elimination scheduling

Through mutation and crossover operators created especially for progressive feature reduction, the evolutionary process improves elimination tactics. By flipping mask entries with a stage-dependent probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:r\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, which is defined as follows, mutation adaptively modifies the elimination rate.6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:r\left( t \right) = r_{{{\mathrm{min}}}} + (r_{{{\mathrm{max}}}} - r_{{{\mathrm{min}}}} ) \cdot \left( {1 - \frac{t}{T}} \right)$$\end{document}

By trading elimination choices across feature indices, crossover integrates complimentary elimination patterns between two parent masks to produce child masks.

Progressive feature elimination

The suggested PG-GFE framework updates the feature space in a controlled and adaptable way by performing progressive feature elimination after identifying the best elimination technique at stage t using evolutionary scheduling. The suggested method eliminates features gradually over many phases, enabling ongoing reevaluation of feature relevance, confidence stability, and structural dependencies, in contrast to traditional feature selection techniques that eliminate a set number of features in a single step. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}^{\left(t\right)}=\{{f}_{1},{f}_{2},\dots\:,{f}_{{d}_{t}}\}$$\end{document} denote the feature set at elimination stage t, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{m}^{\left(t\right)}\in\:\{\mathrm{0,1}{\}}^{{d}_{t}}$$\end{document} be the optimal elimination mask obtained from Step 2. Feature elimination is performed as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}^{\left(t+1\right)}={F}^{\left(t\right)}\setminus\:\{{f}_{i}\mid\:{m}_{i}^{\left(t\right)}=1\}.$$\end{document}

This process keeps the features that provide consistent and discriminative information while eliminating a subset of low-confidence features. Crucially, the elimination mask is adaptive and stage-dependent, allowing for adjustable lowering rates all along the way.

Re-estimation of feature confidence

With the updated graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{G}^{\left(t+1\right)}$$\end{document}, the graph neural network is reapplied to compute new feature embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{i}^{\left(t+1\right)}$$\end{document}. Feature confidence scores are then recomputed using the formulation introduced in Sect. 3.1.2:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{i}^{\left(t+1\right)}=\sigma\:\left(\alpha\:\cdot\:\frac{1}{\mid\:{N}_{i}^{\left(t+1\right)}\mid\:}\sum\:_{j\in\:{N}_{i}^{\left(t+1\right)}}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{s}({h}_{i}^{\left(t+1\right)},{h}_{j}^{\left(t+1\right)})-\beta\:\cdot\:{U}_{i}^{\left(t+1\right)}\right)$$\end{document}

The evolution of feature relevance when redundant or noisy features are eliminated is captured by this re-estimation process. Robust and label-consistent information is more likely to be represented by features whose confidence stays constant or rises over time.

Progressive stability control

PG-GFE keeps an eye on confidence stability across stages to avoid aggressive removal that might deteriorate predictive performance. The following is a measure of feature confidence stability:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\Delta C^{{\left( t \right)}} = \frac{1}{{\left| {F^{{\left( t \right)}} } \right|}}\sum\limits_{{f_{i} \in F^{{\left( t \right)}} }} {\left| {c_{i}^{{\left( t \right)}} - c_{i}^{{\left( {t - 1} \right)}} } \right|}$$\end{document}

Convergence toward a stable feature representation is indicated by a diminishing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\Delta\:}{C}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document}, whilst significant oscillations highlight the need of careful deletion in later phases. The convergence requirements of the overall framework and the fitness assessment in Step 2 both directly utilize this stability metric.

Termination of progressive elimination

Until one or more convergence conditions are met, such as: Confidence variation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\Delta\:}{C}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document} drops below a predetermined threshold, predictive performance degradation stays within allowable bounds, or a target sparsity level is reached, the progressive elimination process iteratively continues. Instead of a single-shot selection, a controlled elimination trajectory yields a compact and stable subset of the remaining feature set if termination criteria are satisfied.

The whole process of the suggested PG-GFE framework is summed up in Algorithm 1. The method works step-by-step, modeling changing feature–label–instance associations by building a heterogeneous graph at each elimination stage. In order to estimate feature confidence scores that simultaneously incorporate structural relevance and uncertainty, graph neural representations are first trained. Instead of choosing a fixed feature subset directly, these confidence ratings are then used to steer an evolutionary optimization process that creates an adaptive removal mask. Low-confidence features are gradually eliminated using the chosen elimination approach, and the graph structure and confidence estimations are then updated appropriately. Until convergence conditions based on performance preservation and confidence stability are met, this iterative process keeps going.

Experiments

A number of experiments were conducted to evaluate the proposed Progressive Graph Guided Feature Elimination (PG-GFE) approach. This section conducts extensive experiments on benchmark multi-label datasets to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method. The Mulan repository and eight popular real-world datasets are used in this research. The aim of the experiment is to evaluate the flexibility, feature reduction capabilities, and classification performance in high-dimensional and correlated labeling situations. Using common evaluation criteria, the proposed strategy is compared with current state-of-the-art multi-label learning and feature selection techniques.

Evaluation in the Multi-Label K-Nearest Neighbor (MLkNN)

To assess the efficacy of the proposed PG-GFE architecture, extensive experiments were performed on several real-world multi-label datasets. Each stated result signifies the mean performance over 10 separate executions for each technique on a specified dataset, guaranteeing resilience against fluctuations in initialization and stochastic factors. A stratified 10-fold cross-validation technique was used for data partitioning, ensuring that each fold preserves the label distribution to address the class imbalance characteristic of multi-label scenarios. This method offers a more dependable evaluation than basic train-test divisions, especially for datasets with differing label cardinalities.

The tests used MLkNN with k equal to 10 and a smoothing parameter of 1, alongside BR with SVM (linear kernel) as the foundational learner, to evaluate generalization across several categorization paradigms. All implementations were executed in Python 3.10 on a machine including an Intel Core i9 CPU and 128 GB of RAM, using modules such as PyTorch for graph neural networks and DEAP for evolutionary optimization. The parameters for PG-GFE were optimized by grid search on a validation subset: a population size of 50, a maximum of 100 iterations, elimination rates between 0.1 and 0.3, and confidence balance coefficients α and β set at 0.5. Default settings from the original publications of competing techniques were used to guarantee a fair comparison.

The evaluation process systematically removes features until around 50% of the initial feature set is retained, achieving a balance between compactness and informativeness. The next categorization is conducted in this reduced space. Tables 4, 5 and 6 show the comparative outcomes using MLkNN across the parameters of Hamming loss, Average accuracy, and Ranking loss. PG-GFE regularly attains better results due to its adaptive elimination trajectory, which dynamically considers changing feature-label dependencies and confidence stability. This results in more discriminative subsets, especially in high-dimensional or sparse datasets such as Bibtex and Delicious, where conventional static selection often fails. Significantly, although competitors may occasionally achieve comparable performance on smaller datasets, PG-GFE’s incorporation of graph reasoning and evolutionary scheduling results in the lowest error rates and highest precision in most instances, exhibiting minimal decline in sparse scenarios that mirror real-world applications.

If the P-value is below the significance threshold, the test suggests the null hypothesis is rejected. In conclusion, the findings in Table 11 indicate that the suggested strategy is statistically superior than the alternative methods in most instances.

Conclusion

This research introduces PG-GFE, an innovative framework for multi-label feature selection that combines graph neural networks for dynamic confidence evaluation with evolutionary optimization for adaptive elimination scheduling. PG-GFE tackles significant issues in high-dimensional multi-label data, including redundancy, noise, and complex label correlations, by creating heterogeneous graphs to represent feature-label-instance interactions and systematically eliminating less informative features while maintaining relational stability. Comprehensive trials on eight benchmark datasets with MLkNN and BR classifiers show their superiority over leading algorithms such as AKLS25, MOD-DE27, MG-GCN28, JobXMLC30, MLACO32, and FLSA-GRL34. PG-GFE regularly demonstrates reduced Hamming Loss and Ranking Loss, with enhanced Average Precision and F1-Measure, as seen in Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9; Figs. 4, 5 and 6.

Notwithstanding these advancements, PG-GFE has limits. It depends on graph neural networks, which may result in increased memory consumption in very big datasets (> 100k instances), thus constraining scalability without distributed computing. The evolutionary optimization presupposes balanced label distributions, which may lead to suboptimal performance in highly unbalanced situations often seen in real-world applications such as medical diagnostics. Moreover, hyperparameter sensitivity (e.g., elimination rates α, β) need meticulous adjustment, potentially varying each dataset.

Future endeavors include expanding PG-GFE to semi-supervised or streamed multi-label contexts, integrating deep reinforcement learning for enhanced scheduling adaptability, and investigating federated learning for privacy-preserving feature selection in dispersed situations. Incorporating explainability modules, such as SHAP for graph attributions, would improve interpretability. Ultimately, empirical validation in new fields such as social media sentiment analysis or genetic data might further illustrate its adaptability.
