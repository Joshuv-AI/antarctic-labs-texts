Transarterial embolization in musculoskeletal care: an indication-stratified narrative review
Authors: Jean-Pierre Pelage, Thunayan Aldalmani, Ruben Geevarghese, Norbert Zhang, Marc-Antoine Jegonday, Gregory Berry, Anthony Dohan, Audrey Fohlen, Sylvain Grange
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransarterial embolization (TAE) is increasingly used across a broad spectrum of musculoskeletal conditions, ranging from established hemostatic and tumor-related indications to emerging pain-directed procedures for degenerative joint disease and chronic inflammatory tendinopathies. Because these applications differ substantially in clinical maturity, methodological quality, durability of outcomes, and comparative evidence, an indication-stratified synthesis is needed.Main bodyThis narrative review summarizes technical principles, vascular anatomy considerations, embolic agent selection, clinical indications, and evidence maturity in musculoskeletal TAE. Hemostatic and selected tumor-related applications are established components of multidisciplinary care, whereas pain-directed embolization, including Genicular artery embolization for knee osteoarthritis and embolization for tendinopathies, is generally performed as a stand-alone minimally invasive procedure targeting pathological neovascularity. Current evidence remains heterogeneous, with variable study designs, follow-up, endpoints, and definitions of technical and clinical success.ConclusionsMusculoskeletal TAE is an expanding field with established, intermediate, and exploratory indications. Its evidence base is strongest for hemostatic and selected tumor-related applications, whereas stand-alone pain-directed embolization requires careful patient selection, standardized outcome assessment, and further comparative trials to define durability and clinical value.

Introduction

Transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) involves the deliberate occlusion of selected blood vessels to control hemorrhage or treat conditions such as hypervascular tumors by delivering embolic agents through a catheter-based procedure. Since its first reported use in 1972 in the management of pelvic hemorrhage [1] and life-threatening pelvic trauma [2], the role of TAE has progressively expanded to include the treatment of hypervascular tumors, postoperative complications such as recurrent hemarthrosis, and inflammatory or degenerative arthropathies. Pre-operative embolization of hypervascular bone tumors can effectively achieve tumor devascularization and significantly reduce intraoperative blood loss [3]. Spontaneous or postoperative recurrent hemarthrosis, particularly in patients with hemophilia, may also be successfully treated with TAE [4]. In addition, patients presenting with intractable pain related to bone lesions may benefit from embolization as a palliative strategy to reduce pain [5]. Abnormal neovascularization, along with associated nerve ingrowth, is believed to play a key role in the pain seen in chronic inflammatory and degenerative arthropathies [6]. As a result, TAE has emerged as a potential therapeutic option for chronic tendinopathies and has been evaluated across multiple anatomical sites [7].

Musculoskeletal TAE encompasses six broad applications: emergency and postoperative hemostasis; tumor-related embolization; adjunctive embolization before or during percutaneous treatment; vascular malformations and aneurysmal bone cysts; recurrent hemarthrosis; and stand-alone pain-directed embolization for osteoarthritis and tendinopathies. Existing reviews often discuss these applications together despite major differences in therapeutic intent, evidence quality, follow-up, and clinical maturity. This narrative review therefore provides an indication-stratified synthesis, distinguishing established hemostatic and selected tumor-related uses from less mature pain-directed procedures.

Methods

Literature search and article selection

This article was designed as a narrative review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis. A structured literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar, with the final search conducted on June 24, 2026. Search concepts combined embolization-related terms with indication-specific terms covering trauma and postoperative hemorrhage, bone and soft-tissue tumors, aneurysmal bone cysts, vascular malformations, hemarthrosis, osteoarthritis, tendinopathies, adhesive capsulitis, and other musculoskeletal applications. Reference lists of selected articles and major reviews were also screened.

English-language clinical studies, systematic reviews, narrative reviews, technical reports, and consensus or guideline documents were considered. Case reports and small case series were included for rare or emerging indications when higher-level evidence was unavailable. Article selection was performed collaboratively by the authors rather than through formal duplicate independent screening, and disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus.

Consistent with the narrative-review design, no formal PRISMA-based study-selection process, risk-of-bias assessment, or quantitative pooling was performed. Evidence maturity was characterized qualitatively according to study design, sample size, comparative evidence, consistency of reported outcomes, follow-up duration, safety data, and current clinical use. These categories were developed by the authors specifically for this narrative review and do not represent a formally validated evidence-grading system or a guideline/consensus classification. Because study identification and selection were not conducted as a formal systematic screening process, we did not report an approximate included-study count, as this could imply a degree of systematic exhaustiveness that was not intended for this narrative review.

Vascular anatomy of major joints: key considerations for embolization

A thorough understanding of MSK vascular anatomy is essential for the safe and effective performance of TAE. This includes knowledge of arterial origins, branching patterns, collateral pathways, and potential cutaneous, muscular, or neural branches at risk of nontarget embolization. The knee is discussed in greater detail because Genicular artery embolization is the most extensively studied stand-alone pain-directed musculoskeletal embolization procedure and because genicular arterial anatomy is complex [8], variable, and directly relevant to both efficacy and safety (Fig. 1). Other territories are summarized more concisely to preserve the focus of this narrative review. The elbow is supplied by a dense periarticular collateral network connecting branches of the brachial, profunda brachii, radial, ulnar, and interosseous arterial systems. Relevant contributors include the radial collateral and middle collateral arteries, the superior and inferior ulnar collateral arteries, and the radial, ulnar, and interosseous recurrent arteries. In lateral epicondylitis, pathological hypervascularity is typically sought around the common extensor origin. Superselective catheterization and careful angiographic assessment are required to target abnormal neovessels while avoiding nontarget embolization of muscular, cutaneous, or neural branches [8]. Embolic-agent selection should be tailored to the indication, target-vessel caliber, desired level and duration of occlusion, collateral circulation, and risk of nontarget embolization (Table 1) [9–19]. Temporary agents, including gelatin sponge, imipenem/cilastatin slurry, and Lipiodol-based approaches, may be preferred when transient reduction of pathological hyperemia is intended, particularly in selected pain-directed indications. Permanent particles, coils, plugs, NBCA, or EVOH are more appropriate when durable occlusion is required, such as in active bleeding, pseudoaneurysm treatment, tumor devascularization, or vascular malformations. Because embolic materials are not interchangeable, future studies should report agent type, size, dilution, embolization endpoint, and safety outcomes in a standardized manner.

Embolization for hemostasis

Hemostatic TAE is an established component of musculoskeletal trauma and postoperative bleeding management. It is used in pelvic ring injuries, particularly in hemodynamically unstable patients, periarticular hemorrhage associated with long-bone fractures, and postoperative bleeding around major joints. Embolization should be integrated into multidisciplinary care pathways combining resuscitation, correction of coagulopathy, imaging triage, surgery when required, and postprocedural monitoring. Contemporary management should be driven by hemodynamic status, bleeding site, associated injuries, local expertise, and the expected speed of hemorrhage control. The goal is not only technical vessel occlusion but also rapid clinical stabilization while preserving tissue perfusion through superselective embolization whenever feasible. Agent selection depends on bleeding pattern, vessel caliber, coagulation status, and the need for temporary versus durable occlusion; this is summarized in Table 1.

Acute hemorrhage in pelvic ring and periarticular trauma

Pelvic ring injuries, usually resulting from high-energy trauma, are associated with high morbidity and mortality due to massive hemorrhage [2, 20]. Exsanguination from intrapelvic bleeding is a leading cause of early death [21]. Initial management includes application of a pelvic binder or external fixation to control venous sources, followed by angiography and embolization to address arterial hemorrhage. Arterial bleeding is identified in approximately 5–20% of cases—more frequently in hemodynamically unstable patients—and embolization achieves technical success rates ranging from 81 to 100% [22, 23].

Current management protocols emphasize contrast-enhanced whole-body CT in hemodynamically stable or transient responder patients to identify active extravasation, pseudoaneurysms, or potential target vessels [24]. In hemodynamically unstable patients with suspected pelvic arterial hemorrhage, angiography and embolization should be rapidly integrated into the center’s trauma protocol; their sequence relative to surgery, preperitoneal packing, resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta, or other hemorrhage-control measures depends on the bleeding pattern, associated injuries, and local resources.

During angiography, contrast extravasation is identified in 61–100% of cases but may be absent due to spasm [25] (Fig. 2), low pressure [21], intermittent bleeding, or nonselective injection [9]. Commonly injured arteries include the superior gluteal artery (Fig. 3) and the lateral sacral arteries. The internal pudendal arteries (Fig. 4) may be injured in butterfly fractures. Injury to the internal iliac arteries (Fig. 5), as well as the obturator and inferior gluteal arteries, is typically associated with posterior dislocation of the pelvic ring (open-book fractures). The external iliac (Fig. 5) or femoral arteries may also be involved in case of anterior compression injury [9]. When a bleeding source is identified, superselective embolization is preferred. Resorbable gelatin sponge is the most commonly used agent. Large particles (> 700 µm) are suitable for proximal occlusion. In combined embolization strategies—often employed to achieve durable hemostasis—distal particulate embolization is performed first, followed by proximal coils to protect normal vasculature [12]. In the presence of pseudoaneurysms, “sandwich” technique, with coils placed both distal and proximal to the lesion, is recommended [9]. Liquid embolic agents, such as NBCA, may be used in selected cases, with low rebleeding rates reported [13]. Covered stent placement is less frequently performed in MSK trauma but may be considered in selected vascular injuries (Fig. 5).

Reported rates of recurrent bleeding range from 0 to 19%, with identified risk factors including initial nonselective embolization, massive transfusion requirements, and multivessel arterial involvement [23, 26]. Repeat embolization may be considered depending on the patient’s clinical status [25]. Complications related to embolization, such as skin necrosis or nerve injury, are uncommon when selective techniques are employed [21, 27].

Embolization for postoperative hemorrhage

Postoperative hemorrhage is an uncommon but potentially severe complication of major orthopedic surgery, and TAE can provide minimally invasive hemorrhage control in selected patients. After hip surgery, bleeding or pseudoaneurysm (Figs. 6 and 7) may present with hemodynamic instability, transfusion requirements, increased surgical-drain output, or an expanding thigh hematoma [28]. Transcatheter angiography plays a central role in identifying the bleeding source, and TAE has been shown to be safe and effective in the management of postoperative hemorrhage after hip surgery [28]. Resorbable gelatin sponge (Gelfoam) can be used for proximal occlusion. Endovascular treatment of postoperative pseudoaneurysms may use coils, NBCA, or, in selected complex peripheral vascular injuries, EVOH-based liquid embolics; agent choice depends on anatomy, flow, collateral supply, and operator experience [13, 15].

Tumor-related embolization

Tumor-related embolization should be interpreted according to therapeutic intent: pre-operative devascularization, symptom palliation, treatment of selected benign hypervascular lesions, or adjunctive use before or during percutaneous ablation or consolidation. The available evidence remains heterogeneous, with variation in tumor type, vascularity, embolization endpoint, timing before surgery, associated treatments, and outcome definitions. Therefore, the magnitude of benefit should be interpreted according to indication and treatment intent rather than generalized across all musculoskeletal tumors.

Pre-operative embolization of hypervascular bone tumors to reduce bleeding risk

TAE has been used since 1974 for the pre-operative devascularization of hypervascular bone tumors [29]. In particular, TAE of hypervascular spinal bone tumors reduces estimated intraoperative blood loss [3]. Indications for TAE should be discussed in a multidisciplinary setting, taking into account the risk–benefit balance, tumor location, and the arterial territories involved. Reported rates include 93% technical success and 72–100% of post-procedural angiograms demonstrating > 80% tumor devascularization [12, 29].

Hypervascular bone lesions include benign aggressive tumors, primary bone tumors, and metastases, particularly from renal cell carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, sarcoma, melanoma, and neuroendocrine tumors [12]. Clinical presentation and expected benefit vary according to tumor biology, location, vascularity, and treatment intent.

Pre-operative embolization aims to reduce tumor vascularity, decrease intraoperative blood loss, and facilitate safer resection, usually within 72 h before surgery [3]. An example in vertebral metastases from renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is shown in Fig. 8. Complete evaluation of feeding arteries, collateral pathways, and potential anastomoses to spinal cord, neural, muscular, or cutaneous branches is essential. Embolic-agent selection depends on vascular anatomy, desired depth of devascularization, and safety constraints. Calibrated particles may be used for distal tumor devascularization, whereas coils, plugs, or liquid embolics may be useful for larger feeders or selected high-flow situations. Embolization as an adjunct to percutaneous tumor therapy

Beyond pre-operative devascularization to reduce intraoperative bleeding, another important role of TAE in oncologic musculoskeletal (MSK) practice is as an adjunct or bridging technique to percutaneous therapies such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA), cryoablation, sclerotherapy, and cementoplasty [30]. By reducing tumor vascularity, TAE can improve the safety and effectiveness of these procedures, particularly in painful hypervascular primary or metastatic bone disease. This strategy is commonly implemented as a staged, two-step approach [3, 11, 30]. The main objective is selective embolization of tumor arterial feeders, thereby lowering the risk of procedural bleeding and reducing the heat-sink effect during RFA. The scope of TAE as part of combination therapy is broad.

In a retrospective single-center study by Sundararajan et al. [33], patients with symptomatic osseous neoplasms underwent embolization followed by cryoablation, with adjunct osteoplasty when mechanical stabilization was required. The authors reported additional improvement in pain scores after sequential treatment; all patients receiving oral analgesics reduced their use, and no complications occurred during embolization, cryoablation, or osteoplasty.

Another reported strategy is the combination of transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) with osteoplasty/cementoplasty. In pelvic bone metastases, Zhang et al. compared percutaneous osteoplasty alone with osteoplasty plus TACE (POPTACE) in 100 patients [5]. Pain and functional outcomes were similar between groups, but early tumor response favored the combined strategy (partial response at 1 month: 74% with POPTACE vs 52% with osteoplasty alone). Finally, Grange et al. [30] proposed a combined approach for aggressive vertebral hemangioma in which staged embolization and percutaneous radiopaque gelified ethanol sclerotherapy, followed by vertebroplasty, was technically feasible and generally effective for pain relief. These studies suggest that TAE can serve as a practical enabler of multimodality percutaneous MSK tumor care—particularly for hypervascular tumors and complex pelvic/axial disease—while underscoring the need for meticulous technique in sensitive territories such as spinal embolization. TAE may therefore facilitate broader multimodal strategies.

Palliative embolization for pain

In nonsurgical candidates, TAE may also be considered for symptomatic patients (Fig. 11). The goals of TAE are to control intractable pain refractory to major analgesics, improve quality of life, and facilitate combined approaches with thermoablation and consolidative procedures (e.g., screw fixation and cementoplasty). Embolization of primary or secondary bone tumors should be as selective as possible, typically using nonresorbable particles such as calibrated microspheres. Smaller particles may be more effective but increase the risk of ischemia and necrosis in adjacent normal tissues. In a retrospective series of 164 patients with spinal metastases, Facchini et al. reported a reduction of at least 50% in pain scores and analgesic requirements in 159 patients (97%), with a mean duration of pain relief of 9.2 months [34]. Because this evidence derives from a single retrospective cohort, it should not be generalized to all musculoskeletal tumors. Transarterial chemoembolization has also been used for osteosarcoma, with histologic tumor devascularization reported in 70–94% of specimens [35]. Reported adverse events include post-embolization syndrome, skin or soft-tissue necrosis, neurologic complications, and arterial injury; their frequency varies by tumor location, vascular anatomy, embolic agent, and treatment intent [3, 12].

Expanding non-oncologic indications

Embolization in recurrent hemarthrosis or chronic joint bleeding

Hemarthrosis has multiple etiologies, including trauma, anticoagulant therapy, bleeding disorders (e.g., hemophilia [4]), inflammatory or degenerative arthritis, and iatrogenic causes. Recurrent spontaneous hemarthrosis after joint replacement is rare, with a reported incidence of 0.17–1.6% [36]. It is typically related to hypervascular hypertrophic synovitis and may resolve spontaneously. Recurrence after conservative management is low; conservative measures include joint immobilization, ice application, analgesics/anti-inflammatory drugs, selective aspiration, and adjustment or temporary cessation of anticoagulation. In the event of recurrence, arthroscopic synovectomy or open surgical management (e.g., vessel ligation/clipping or thermoablation) can be effective. However, these procedures may be time-consuming, carry an infectious risk, and may be associated with perioperative bleeding if the culprit arterial pedicles cannot be identified. Radionuclide synoviorthesis using intra-articular yttrium-90 may be considered, although long-term risks remain uncertain.

Genicular artery embolization (GAE) is a safe and effective treatment for spontaneous hemarthrosis of the knee after arthroplasty [36]. A detailed understanding of genicular arterial anatomy is essential for safe and successful procedures [8] (Figs. 1, 12, and 13). In a recent review of non-hemophilic recurrent hemarthrosis, technical success was achieved in 99% of patients; the postembolization recurrent bleeding rate was 10%, with a mean time to recurrence of 8.1 months [36]. Major complications occurred in 2% of cases (mainly joint infections), and minor complications occurred in 6%, including transient skin ischemia, ulceration, or pain. Notably, all transient skin complications occurred after use of spherical embolic particles < 300 µm or when n-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) glue was used; therefore, spherical particles > 300 µm are recommended. However, proximal coil placement should be avoided because distal revascularization may occur and subsequent arterial access can be compromised in the event of recurrence. In patients with hemophilia, TAE can significantly reduce the number of bleeding episodes over the following year [4].

Stand-alone pain-directed embolization for degenerative joint disease and chronic inflammatory tendinopathies

In contrast to hemostatic or tumor-related indications, pain-directed embolization for osteoarthritis and chronic inflammatory tendinopathies is generally performed as a stand-alone minimally invasive therapeutic procedure. The therapeutic rationale is to reduce pathological neovascularity and associated neo-innervation rather than to serve as an adjunct to surgery, ablation, or another percutaneous intervention. This distinguishes TAE from traditional multimodal management, which typically combines analgesics, physiotherapy, activity modification, intra-articular or peritendinous injections, and, in selected advanced cases, surgery. In this setting, embolization is proposed for carefully selected refractory patients as a targeted vascular intervention rather than as a component of an operative pathway.

TAE for chronic degenerative and inflammatory tendinopathies is based on the concept that these conditions are driven, at least in part, by pathologic neovascularization and persistent synovial or peritendinous hyperemia within inflamed tissues [8]. These newly formed, fragile vessels may sustain inflammatory signaling and serve as conduits for nociceptive nerve ingrowth, thereby amplifying pain and promoting chronicity [8]. By selectively catheterizing and embolizing these abnormal neovessels, TAE aims to reduce regional hypervascularity, decrease the delivery of inflammatory mediators, and promote regression of associated perivascular nerves. This targeted vascular approach is intended to alleviate pain and improve function while preserving normal tissue perfusion. On angiography, these abnormal vascular networks may appear as a focal hyperemic “blush” (sometimes described as a tumor-like blush). Building on this rationale, we discuss the clinical applications investigated to date, including GAE, embolization for adhesive capsulitis, and targeted tendinous embolization.

Genicular artery embolization (GAE) in knee osteoarthritis (OA) and persistent pain or hemarthrosis after arthroplasty

GAE is a more recent application of TAE, based on the premise that embolization of abnormal periarticular neovessels may lead to regression of accompanying nociceptive nerve fibers [8].

Typical inclusion criteria include symptomatic knee osteoarthritis with persistent pain despite optimized conservative treatment, concordance between symptoms, clinical examination, and imaging findings, and angiographic evidence of abnormal periarticular hypervascularity corresponding to the painful compartment. Most studies have focused on mild-to-moderate radiographic osteoarthritis rather than end-stage disease. Potential exclusion criteria include advanced end-stage osteoarthritis requiring arthroplasty, severe malalignment or mechanical instability as the dominant pain driver, active infection, inflammatory arthropathy not controlled medically, severe peripheral arterial disease, uncorrectable coagulopathy, renal dysfunction limiting contrast administration, pregnancy, and inability to complete standardized clinical follow-up.

Technical success should be defined as superselective catheterization of the target genicular or periarticular branches and embolization of angiographically abnormal hypervascularity without major non-target embolization. Clinical success should be defined using prespecified patient-reported outcomes, such as improvement in pain and function scores, reduction in analgesic use, and patient global assessment at standardized follow-up intervals. Patient evaluation should include baseline pain assessment, functional scoring, radiographic grading, assessment of prior conservative treatments, documentation of analgesic use, and follow-up at standardized intervals, commonly 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. Longer-term follow-up is needed to determine durability and the potential need for repeat embolization or subsequent arthroplasty. Access is typically radial or femoral, followed by selective catheterization of the genicular arteries. Targeted embolization is performed at sites of abnormal hyperemic blush on angiography (Fig. 14). Embolic agents reported in the literature include 100–300 µm microspheres, embozene particles, imipenem–cilastatin (IMP/CIS) [7] slurry, and lipiodol-based [19] preparations, delivered into genicular branches to reduce synovial hypervascularity while sparing normal periarticular flow. The LipioJoint-1 trial, a prospective multicenter single-arm study, reported meaningful pain relief and functional improvement with a favorable safety profile, supporting Lipiodol-based GAE as a minimally invasive option for symptomatic knee OA [19]. Several prospective cohorts and single-arm trials have reported high technical success and improvements in pain and function after GAE, sometimes lasting 12–24 months [19, 38, 39]. These findings are also summarized in recent technical and systematic reviews [8, 37]. However, sham-controlled evidence is inconsistent. Van Zadelhoff et al. found no significant benefit over sham at 4 months [40]; in the subsequent 12-month report, both groups had sustained pain improvement, with no significant between-group differences in pain, synovitis, or other clinical outcomes, leading the authors to conclude that the findings did not support routine clinical implementation of GAE [41].

In contrast, the 2026 Society of Interventional Radiology position statement supports GAE as a viable joint-preserving option for appropriately selected patients with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis who have failed conservative treatment and are not candidates for, or wish to delay, arthroplasty, while also calling for larger randomized trials [42]. Taken together, these data support a cautious, individualized interpretation rather than routine use across all patients.

In a prospective single-arm study, Taslakian et al. [39] evaluated GAE in symptomatic knee OA and reported sustained clinical improvement along with associated biologic changes. At 12-month follow-up, significant reductions in circulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra) were observed, supporting a potential role of GAE in modulating neoangiogenesis and inflammatory pathways implicated in OA-related pain. Jin-Woo Choi et al. [43] assessed the value of pre-procedural MRI in patients undergoing GAE for knee OA pain. MRI findings—particularly synovitis, bone marrow lesions, and contrast-enhancing synovial hypertrophy—were associated with better clinical response after GAE, whereas advanced structural degeneration appeared to derive less benefit. These findings support MRI as a tool for patient selection and prognostication, consistent with the concept that GAE may be most effective in knees with active inflammatory and hypervascular features rather than end-stage degenerative disease.

GAE has also been investigated for chronic pain after total knee arthroplasty. Early clinical studies suggest technical feasibility and potential improvement in selected refractory patients, but the evidence remains limited and should be interpreted cautiously [44, 45].

Beyond the knee, pain-directed embolization has also been considered for other joints, including the hip, temporomandibular joint, interphalangeal joints, thumb base, and facet joints. These applications remain early and hypothesis-generating. They may broaden the concept of joint embolization, but each vascular territory has specific safety constraints and requires anatomical validation, standardized imaging criteria, and prospective clinical assessment before routine clinical use.

Epicondylitis

Lateral and medial epicondylitis are overuse tendinopathies related to repetitive forearm strain and are a common cause of elbow pain. Both entities are primarily degenerative and are characterized histologically by fibroblastic degeneration and pathologic neovascularization with accompanying perivascular nerve ingrowth [46, 47]. Standard management is similar to that of other chronic tendinopathies and may include physical therapy, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroid injections, and, in selected refractory cases, surgical treatment. More recently, TAE has emerged as an alternative approach aimed at embolizing these abnormal neovessels [48] (Fig. 15). Target vessels are selected according to the symptomatic compartment and angiographic hyperemic blush, with careful attention to muscular, cutaneous, and neural branches. In the prospective 24-patient pilot study by Iwamoto et al., mean maximum-pain VAS decreased from 77± 15 mm at baseline to 16 ± 13 mm at 6 months, 9 ± 13 mm at 12 months, and 11 ± 14 mm at 24 months [48]. Postprocedural MRI findings also showed improvement in tendinosis without evidence of muscle loss or cartilage or bone necrosis [48]. These findings are encouraging but derive from a small uncontrolled cohort and require confirmation in comparative trials. No serious adverse events have been reported in the available small series; transient postprocedural pain, generally resolving within 48–72 h, has been described [48, 49]. Overall, early uncontrolled studies suggest that TAE may provide pain relief in selected refractory patients, but comparative effectiveness and recurrence rates relative to conservative treatment remain uncertain. Data for medial epicondylitis remain limited. Lee et al. evaluated TAE in 10 patients with refractory medial epicondylitis and reported significant pain reduction without major adverse events [50].

Plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendinopathy

Plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendinopathy have also been investigated using embolization, based on the rationale of pathological neovascularity and nociceptive nerve ingrowth [51, 52]. However, the evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. For plantar fasciitis, causal interpretation is particularly challenging because spontaneous improvement may occur within several months in many patients. In the largest reported plantar fasciitis cohort, Sasaki et al. [53] described 66 cases with up to 4 years of follow-up, but most procedures were ultrasound-guided and patients underwent a mean of 2.3 reinterventions. Future studies should focus on clearly refractory cases, standardized imaging criteria, and comparative outcomes.

Adhesive capsulitis

Adhesive capsulitis is an idiopathic inflammatory condition classically described as progressing through three phases: freezing, frozen, and recovery. It is thought to be triggered by soft-tissue injury, initiating an inflammatory cytokine cascade that promotes neovascularization and accompanying nociceptive nerve ingrowth [54]. Traditional management includes oral analgesics, physical therapy, intra-articular injections, and, in selected cases, arthroscopic capsular release [55]. More recently, TAE has been investigated as an alternative treatment [56]. The goal of TAE is to embolize hyperemic areas harboring abnormal neovessels, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response and targeting associated perivascular nerves. Understanding the arterial supply of the shoulder is essential for safe TAE. The main contributors include the suprascapular, anterior and posterior circumflex humeral, thoracoacromial, and subscapular arteries. In adhesive capsulitis, abnormal neovessels have been described as newly developed periarticular microvessels arising in addition to the normal capsular supply [57]. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by imaging [58]. Selected patients may undergo angiography to identify abnormal hyperemic “blush”-type enhancement prior to targeted embolization [59, 60].

The most commonly reported embolic agents for adhesive capsulitis are resorbable materials and imipenem–cilastatin (IMP/CIS) slurry [56]. A recent multicenter study [61] found that TAE for shoulder musculoskeletal pain using an ethiodized oil-based emulsion appeared feasible and safe, supporting further evaluation in larger prospective cohorts. Nevertheless, embolization for adhesive capsulitis remains exploratory. Early studies suggest potential improvements in pain and range of motion, but the natural history of the disease, small sample sizes, and limited comparative data prevent firm conclusions regarding treatment effect.

Discussion

The main finding of this narrative review is that musculoskeletal transarterial embolization (TAE) should not be considered a single homogeneous field. Its indications differ markedly in clinical maturity, evidence quality, therapeutic intent, and expected durability. Hemostatic and selected tumor-related applications are established within multidisciplinary pathways, whereas stand-alone pain-directed procedures for osteoarthritis and tendinopathies remain promising but less mature and require stricter patient selection and standardized outcome assessment (Table 2). In acute hemorrhage, the clinical value of TAE depends on rapid integration with resuscitation, imaging, correction of coagulopathy, surgery or packing when required, and post-operative monitoring. Relevant outcomes therefore extend beyond angiographic occlusion to hemodynamic stabilization, transfusion requirements, repeat intervention, ischemic complications, and mortality. For tumor-related indications, benefit is similarly context-dependent and should be interpreted according to tumor vascularity, treatment intent, embolization endpoint, and integration with surgery, ablation, cementoplasty, radiotherapy, or systemic therapy.

Pain-directed embolization differs because it is used as the principal therapeutic intervention. Although observational studies report improvements in selected patients, chronic pain is influenced by structural disease, natural history, concomitant therapy, central sensitization, and placebo effects. For GAE, uncontrolled cohort data must be balanced against conflicting sham-controlled trials, including 12-month evidence showing no superiority over sham. The 2026 Society of Interventional Radiology position statement nevertheless considers GAE a viable option for appropriately selected patients after failed conservative therapy, while calling for larger randomized trials. Other pain-directed indications remain supported mainly by small uncontrolled series.

Across emerging indications, the main limitations are small samples, heterogeneous selection criteria and embolic agents, inconsistent outcome definitions, and limited long-term comparative data. Future studies should use indication-specific technical, clinical, and safety endpoints, with particular emphasis on patient-centered outcomes, durability, analgesic use, and subsequent surgery.

Future development should remain indication-specific: pragmatic pathway-based evaluation for established hemostatic and selected oncologic uses, and rigorous comparative assessment for pain-directed procedures.

This review has limitations. It was designed as a narrative review and not as a systematic review or meta-analysis. Although a structured literature search was performed, no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative synthesis was undertaken. The level of evidence was therefore summarized qualitatively, with emphasis on clinical maturity, study design, follow-up, comparative data, and current clinical status.

Conclusion

Transarterial embolization has an expanding but indication-dependent role in musculoskeletal care. Hemostatic and selected tumor-related applications are established components of multidisciplinary management. For knee osteoarthritis, uncontrolled prospective cohorts have reported clinically meaningful improvements, but randomized sham-controlled evidence remains inconsistent, including 12-month data showing no sustained superiority over sham. Other stand-alone pain-directed applications, including tendinopathies and adhesive capsulitis, remain exploratory. Future studies should prioritize standardized patient selection, procedural reporting, comparative outcomes, safety assessment, and long-term durability.
