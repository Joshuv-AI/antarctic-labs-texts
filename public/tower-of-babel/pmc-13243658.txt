ABSTRACT
Industrial chemical manufacturing relies heavily on process analytical technology for quality assurance and efficiency. However, many critical assays remain constrained by slow, off-line laboratory methods, creating significant latency in process control. Here we demonstrate that Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS), a form of atomic emission spectroscopy, can overcome these limitations for direct in-line analysis of industrial liquids. Using LIBS as the measurement platform, we develop and validate a comprehensive chemometric framework for quantifying phosphorus pentoxide (P\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_5$$\end{document}) equivalence in concentrated phosphoric acid, a key parameter in fertilizer and chemical production. The LIBS analysis requires less than ten seconds, representing a greater than 180 fold decrease in analysis time compared to classical ICP-OES analysis or chromatographic procedures. The core of this work lies in the systematic evaluation of data processing strategies: twelve regression algorithms spanning linear, regularized, ensemble, and non-linear paradigms were benchmarked across four preprocessing conditions (raw data, variable-selected, SNV-corrected, and SNV/Savitzky–Golay smoothed spectra), providing a rigorous and reproducible comparison of chemometric approaches for LIBS-based liquid analysis. A strategic variable selection strategy, targeting chemically significant emission wavelengths combined with variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis. Using a carefully designed experimental setup with laser characteristics that were customized (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 1064\,\textrm{nm}$$\end{document}, pulse energy around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$25\,\textrm{mJ}$$\end{document}), the optimized partial least squares (PLS) regression model, built on SNV + Savitzky–Golay preprocessed spectra in the 200–900 nm range, achieves a cross-validated coefficient of determination \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.96$$\end{document} and a root mean square error of prediction (RMSEP) of 1.81 % P\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_5$$\end{document}. This methodology makes in situ, real-time feedback for closed-loop process controls possible, which can translate to dramatically increased product consistency, resource utilization, and sustainability in production. Beyond the specific application to phosphoric acid, this work establishes a transferable methodological template, integrating spectral preprocessing, data-driven feature selection, and systematic model benchmarking, that can be extended to various liquid process streams in chemical production.

Introduction

Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) has transitioned from a specialized laboratory technique to a versatile analytical tool with considerable industrial and scientific value1,2. The technique exploits optical emissions from laser-induced micro plasmas to determine elemental composition with minimal sample preparation. LIBS is now widely applied in planetary science3,4, cultural heritage diagnostics5, environmental monitoring6, and biomedical imaging7.

Of particular relevance to the present work, advances in laser and spectrometer technologies have enabled the successful deployment of LIBS for real-time, in-line process control in metallurgy8, additive manufacturing9, alloy recycling10, and pharmaceutical production11,12, addressing the pressing industrial need for immediate, in-situ measurements to replace sluggish off-line analyses. Notwithstanding these advances in solid-phase applications, extending LIBS to liquid-phase analysis introduces distinct challenges. Direct ablation of bulk liquids is hampered by splashing, aerosol formation, and plasma quenching, which severely reduce sensitivity and reproducibility13. The flowing liquid jet configuration is particularly attractive for process analytics, as it provides a continuously renewed ablation surface, eliminates memory effects, and integrates readily into closed loop circuits14. Quantitative LIBS determination of phosphorus has been reported for solid matrices such as soils and mineral ores6; however, to the best of our knowledge, no study has yet addressed the direct quantification of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {P_2O_5}$$\end{document} in concentrated phosphoric acid. The combination of high acid strength, a corrosive matrix, and the inherent difficulties of liquid-phase plasma generation renders this a demanding, hitherto unexplored analytical scenario. The high dimensionality of broadband LIBS spectra has motivated the adoption of multivariate calibration, with Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression being the most established method15. Machine learning approaches, including Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and neural networks, have also been tested. Preprocessing strategies such as Standard Normal Variate (SNV) and Savitzky–Golay smoothing are essential for removing non-informative variance, while Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) analysis enables principled feature selection16–18. Despite these individual advances, systematic comparisons of multiple algorithm families under controlled preprocessing conditions remain scarce and virtually absent for liquid-phase applications.

In the industrial wet-process production of phosphoric acid, rigorous control of reactant concentrations is essential for optimizing yield and minimizing waste19. The acid concentration, expressed as % \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {P_2O_5}$$\end{document}, is the primary quality parameter. Conventional titration or ion chromatography requires manual sampling and 30 to 60 minutes of laboratory time, severely limiting real-time process correction. There exists a compelling need for analytical technology capable of delivering precise concentration data within seconds to enable dynamic, closed-loop control.

The present study addresses these gaps by developing and validating a LIBS-based methodology for real-time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {P_2O_5}$$\end{document} quantification in industrial phosphoric acid. A systematic benchmarking of regression algorithms across four progressively refined preprocessing conditions provides a comprehensive chemometric comparison for liquid LIBS calibration. Furthermore, a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP)-guided wavelength selection strategy is introduced, reducing the spectral feature space while improving model generalizability and spectroscopic interpretability. The optimized calibration model achieves prediction accuracy fully suitable for industrial process monitoring, with analysis times under ten seconds, representing a greater than 180-fold improvement over conventional ICP-OES procedures, and directly compatible with the requirements of real-time closed-loop process control.

Materials and methods

LIBS technology

The interaction of high-intensity laser pulses with target materials has attracted significant attention in numerous fields of research and analysis13. When a high-powered laser pulse is focused onto a sample a small amount of material is evaporated, thereby triggering avalanche ionization of the constituent elements and leading to the formation of a microplasma. The use of lasers to vaporizing, dissociating, exciting, or ionizing species at material surfaces has shown great potential as a powerful analytical technique20,21.Given that only nanogram- to microgram-level quantities of material are ablated over femtosecond to nanosecond timescales (depending on the laser pulse duration), the entire process can be deemed minimally destructive22–24. The technique that exploits the spectroscopic analysis of optical emissions from these laser-induced plasmas to determine the composition of the target material is known as Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS). LIBS is a rapid spectral detection methodology capable of analyzing substances in various states (solid, liquid, and gas)14,25–27. It offers several advantages, including non-destructive (or micro-destructive) measurement, synchronous detection of multiple elements, and rapid, real-time analysis. Consequently, LIBS has been applied extensively across diverse sectors, such as industry, environmental science, agriculture, and food quality control7,28–30. Owing to continuous technological advancements the number and range of LIBS applications for elemental analysis have expanded rapidly, and numerous studies have employed LIBS for the quantitative assessment of elemental content in various samples30–32.Within a LIBS experiment, a high-intensity pulsed laser beam is focused onto the surface of a sample, generating a plasma with temperatures on the order of 10,000 to 20,000 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{K}$$\end{document} through a multi-stage breakdown process wherein the laser energy couples to the material. Typically, a tiny quantity of the substance is initially ablated and vaporized by both thermal and non-thermal mechanisms under a laser pulse energy density reaching tens of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{GW}\,\textrm{cm}^{-2}$$\end{document}15,32–35. It subsequently undergoes dissociation in the high-temperature plasma, yielding free electrons along with weakly ionized molecular, atomic, and ionic species36–44. Since expanding plasma travels at supersonic velocities, it quickly cools down, allowing species recombination and de-excitation to happen, giving rise to discrete light emissions at specific wavelengths. This is due to the energy being released in the form of photons synchronous with electrons moving down to lower energy states45,46. Atoms therefore emit specific spectral signatures that are harvested using optical instruments. Emission peaks for individual elements have characteristic wave lengths such that several elements are detectable at a go from a single LIBS spectrum47–49. Because most elements possess multiple electron orbital transitions, a single LIBS emission intensity spectrum typically contains numerous peaks for each element, often yielding hundreds or even thousands of spectral lines for many materials. In fact, a broadband LIBS spectrum, covering the ultraviolet to near-infrared range (approximately 200\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{nm}$$\end{document} to 900\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{nm}$$\end{document}), records the full elemental composition of a sample. As the intensity of the emitted light for a given element is proportional to the concentration of the corresponding species in the plasma. Furthermore, LIBS offers high-volume chemical analysis, in situ and in real time, whether using bench-top systems in a conventional laboratory setting in the field50–60.LIBS data, however, are highly multivariate, encompassing several thousand to tens of thousands of variables per spectrum61. In addition to information on the elemental composition of samples, numerous other factors contribute to the overall spectral fingerprint such as molecular fragments in the plasma62, variations in laser coupling across different sample types, and other matrix effects inherent to LIBS spectra63. The extensive and intricate chemical information contained in each spectrum poses a substantial challenge in terms of rapid and effective data processing.

Phosphoric acid

A major part of phosphoric acid production (90%) is dedicated to chemical fertilizers64, the rest being used as raw materials to produce detergents, animal feed, additives in toothpastes, ingredients of non-alcoholic drinks like soda or cola and in the surface treatment of stainless metals65. The main industrial way generally applied to make phosphoric acid from this rock, known as the “wet process,”includes combining phosphate rock with sulfuric acid (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {H_2SO_4}$$\end{document}). Widely used in the fertilizer business, this method gives an acid of high concentration but may contain various unwanted elements (heavy metals, fluorides, etc.) that need to be reduced or removed based on the final application66. The phenomenon can be described by the following two-stage reaction:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm {Ca_3(PO_4)_2 \cdot CaF_2 + 6H_3PO_4}&\rightarrow \mathrm {4Ca(H_2PO_4)_2 + 2HF} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm {Ca(H_2PO_4)_2 + H_2SO_4 + nH_2O}&\rightarrow \mathrm {2H_3PO_4 + CaSO_4 \cdot nH_2O} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the gypsum product can be dihydrate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 2$$\end{document}), hemihydrate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 0.5$$\end{document}) or anhydrite (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 0$$\end{document}).

Overall, worldwide production of phosphoric acid comes to several tens of millions of tons per year, showing its critical importance in the chemical industry and for food security. To ensure that the product meets these standards, makers and labs commonly employ reference methods such as ICP-OES, ICP-MS, titration, or absorption spectroscopy. These methods offer high sensitivity and selectivity, allowing exact measurement of crucial parameters such as the P2O5 content (a sign of acid concentration) or trace-level toxins. However, they often require physical sampling, preparation of the sample (e.g., dilution, hardening), and shipment to a faraway laboratory67, the time that elapses between sample collection and the release of data can thus extend to several hours or even days, reducing the capacity to act quickly to changes in the process. In an industrial setting increasingly directed toward digitalization and“Industry 4.0,”there is a growing need for live analysis systems capable of giving ongoing data or frequent updates. Although these systems may exhibit slightly lower precision or sensitivity compared to laboratory-based methods, their chief advantage is substantially increased responsiveness: any drift in concentration or rise in impurities can be detected within a matter of seconds or minutes, allowing near real-time adjustments to operational parameters (flow rate, temperature, pH, etc.). Such a method improves product quality, optimizes returns, and reduces energy consumption, all while combining into a larger strategy of process automation and control. Phosphoric acid, whose content and purity can vary due to the variety of raw materials or changing process conditions, is particularly well suited to constant tracking. This study is fully placed within the setting of online research of phosphoric acid. Our attention is especially to the measurement of phosphorus pentoxide (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {P_2O_5}$$\end{document}), the percentage of which serves as a key sign of acid strength. In an industrial setting, our goal is to assess the possibility of applying a fast, process-integrated analysis method capable of sending data in near real time.

Data preprocessing: spectral correction and enhancement

The raw LIBS spectra, as much as they contain a wealth of information, necessarily harbor numerous sources of systematics and noise variations that do not relate to the chemical composition under investigation. These sources of confounding can strongly undermine quantitative calibration models’ performance if not corrected for. Thus, raw spectral datasets underwent a specially conceived series of preprocessing that was intended to correct instrumental variations, remove physical interference effects, improve the signal-to-noise ratio, yet maintain the inherent chemical information.

Standard Normal Variate (SNV)

Standard Normal Variate (SNV) normalization was applied to each spectrum individually to remove multiplicative intensity variations. These variations refer to systematic, whole-spectrum proportional scaling effects where the entire spectrum is multiplied by a sample or shot-dependent factor devoid of chemical information. In the LIBS analysis of dynamic liquid jets, these multiplicative effects arise primarily from: (1) pulse-to-pulse fluctuations in the ablated mass and laser-liquid coupling efficiency caused by the dynamic surface hydrodynamics of the fluid jet; (2) variations in plasma temperature and electron density; and (3) changes in optical collection efficiency due to slight geometric shifts of the plasma position relative to the collection optics. By standardizing each spectrum to a zero mean and unit variance, SNV effectively eliminates these inter-sample intensity differences while preserving the underlying relative emission line patterns that encode the critical compositional information16. Savitzky-Golay smoothing

The Savitzky-Golay smoothing was then applied as an auxiliary preprocessing after SNV normalization for further enhanced spectral quality by selectively diminishing high-frequency noise without sacrificing the significant peak shapes carrying chemical information. Unlike simple moving average filters that tend to increase broadening of peak shapes and reduce peak heights with attendant loss of chemical information, the Savitzky-Golay filter uses local polynomial fitting to maintain spectral fidelity while effectively reducing noise. This filter operates by applying a pre-specified-degree polynomial to successive contiguous data points (defined by a moving window) by least-squares regression, then replacing the central point by the regressed value of the polynomial in that spot. This digital filter procedure achieves noise reduction by virtue of local regression by polynomials while concurrently preserving the geometrical attributes of spectral features like positions, heights, widths, as well as shapes of peaks17. In this investigation, a Savitzky-Golay filter with an 11-point window and first-order polynomial was applied using the savgol filter function from the SciPy library in a Python/Jupyter Notebook computing environment. These parameters were optimized through comparative testing of multiple window sizes and polynomial orders against model performance metrics:Window Size (11 points): We set the window width such that it is large enough to effectively average over multiple spectral points for noise reduction, yet small enough to preserve sharp spectral features characteristic of LIBS emission lines. Model development and variable selection strategy

Instead of utilizing the full spectral range (comprising thousands of points in terms of wavelengths), a strategic variable selection method was adopted where only the analytically most important wavelengths were identified and kept. The selective method has some merits over the full range approach: (1) Simplification of the model structure and computational burden, (2) Removal of uninformative spectral areas that contribute mostly towards to noise, (3) Increase in model interpretability by selecting chemically significant emission lines that will give more focus on chemically interpretable models, (4) Enhancement in model generalizability by diminishing the overfitting chance towards spurious spectral features.

Eleven particular wavelengths were selected on dual criteria: their inherent analytical significance for phosphate calibration in spectra by LIBS (on the basis of their atom physics as well as typical characteristics of emission lines) as well as their demonstrated superior performance for preliminary calibration models.

This prevents model performance statistics calculated on the test set from being biased estimates of the model’s performance on novel data during a prediction task that is so vital for determining the real world usefulness of the calibration models built.

Comprehensive model evaluation across preprocessing conditions

To investigate comprehensively the effect of the preprocessing methods as well as variable selection on predictive power systematically, twelve regression algorithms were deliberately selected in this study to represent the major families of supervised learning methods, ensuring a comprehensive and unbiased assessment of the chemometric landscape for LIBS-based calibration. Specifically, three regularized linear models (Ridge, Lasso, and ElasticNet) were included to probe the linearity of the spectral–concentration relationship under L2, L1, and combined L1/L2 penalty structures, respectively. PLS regression was included as the established reference method in spectroscopic chemometrics, designed to extract latent variables from collinear predictor sets. Five tree-based ensemble methods (Decision Tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and LightGBM) were selected to evaluate whether non-linear relationships or complex feature interactions could improve predictive accuracy beyond what linear models achieve. Finally, two fundamentally different non-linear paradigms were included: Support Vector Regression (SVR), a kernel-based method, and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), an instance-based learner. This deliberate diversity of algorithmic paradigms follows recommended practice in chemometrics, where empirical comparison across model families is preferred over a priori selection of a single algorithm, as the optimal model complexity for a given dataset cannot be reliably predicted from theoretical considerations alone. Variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis: identifying critical wavelengths

In order to achieve further mechanistic understanding on the specific spectral features responsible for precise prediction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {P_2O_5}$$\end{document} as well as for optimizing the model based on data-driven selection of features, Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) was calculated on the PLS regression model. VIP values give a holistic quantitative estimate on the importance of each variable by considering the extent of magnitude in the PLS regression coefficients (due to strength in the relationship between each wavelength and concentration of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {P_2O_5}$$\end{document}) as well as the extent of response variance (Y-variance) attributed by each PLS component. The variable importance in projection (VIP) score for the j-th variable is defined as:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{VIP}_j = \sqrt{ p \cdot \frac{ \displaystyle \sum _{k=1}^{A} w_{jk}^{2}\, s_k }{ \displaystyle \sum _{k=1}^{A} s_k } } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where p is the total number of predictor variables (e.g., wavelengths), A is the number of PLS components, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{jk}$$\end{document} is the weight of variable j in component k, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k = t_k^{2} q_k^{2}$$\end{document} denotes the contribution of component k, computed from the squared X-scores (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_k^2$$\end{document}) and squared Y-loadings (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_k^2$$\end{document}).

The VIP measure for each variable reflects its total contribution towards all PLS latent components considering the amount of Y-variance explained by each component. Generally speaking, variables with a VIP value more than 1.0 can be regarded as being strongly important, while variables with a VIP value more than 0.5 can be regarded as moderately important and potentially retained. Variables with a VIP value below 0.5 contribute minimally towards the model and might be targeted for removal18.

In this study, we set a conservative threshold of VIP > 0.5 (Fig. 13) in order to retain all moderately to highly significant wavelengths and reject truly non-informative spectral regions. Practical considerations for industrial deployment and matrix effects

While the current study establishes a strong calibration baseline with volumetrically diluted industrial stock solutions, the use of the LIBS analytical system in a real-world production setting will necessarily involve dynamic matrix effects associated with the wet process. It should be noted that the use of commercial industrial-grade phosphoric acid (75% H\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}PO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}) as the stock solution ensures that the calibration matrix already contains a representative set of typical industrial impurities (Fe, Al, Mg, silicates), providing a first-order approximation of the real analytical matrix rather than a simplified synthetic system. In active industrial streams, fluctuations in acid viscosity, the presence of suspended solids such as calcium sulphate (gypsum), and varying concentrations of co-extracted impurities can significantly perturb laser–target coupling and plasma optical emission. Physical matrix variations typically manifest as multiplicative intensity fluctuations due to inconsistent ablated masses. Our methodological choice to apply Standard Normal Variate (SNV) normalization specifically counteracts these physical variations by standardizing the spectral intensity independent of the absolute ablated volume. Furthermore, chemical matrix effects, such as shifts in plasma temperature or electron density induced by varying concentrations of easily ionizable elements, are inherently mitigated by our Partial Least Squares approach. By utilizing a multivariate feature space that includes matrix-associated emission lines rather than relying solely on isolated phosphorus peaks, the PLS model captures and mathematically compensates for these complex matrix interferences. An additional consideration concerns the effect of temperature on measurement performance, all measurements in this study were conducted at controlled laboratory temperature (20 ± 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C), whereas industrial phosphoric acid streams typically operate at elevated temperatures (60–85 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C), such temperatures affect both the physical properties of the liquid (viscosity, surface tension, jet flow behavior) and the plasma characteristics (initial temperature of the ablated material, plasma expansion dynamics, electron density and excitation temperature), potentially altering laser–liquid coupling efficiency and emission line intensities. The laminar jet configuration employed here is well suited to accommodate these variations, since the continuously renewed liquid surface mitigates thermal accumulation effects and surface degradation. In conclusion, while the controlled conditions of this study do not perfectly simulate the characteristics of a true industrial process stream, the methodology employed in this investigation using industrial-grade acid and impurities, a liquid jet system, and a multivariate calibration approach provides a solid foundation for future work in validating this approach. The challenges outlined in the above sections are well known in the LIBS field and can be overcome by using engineering and chemometric techniques. The future work in this investigation will be focused on validating and adapting this approach to real process streams.

Conclusion

The central contribution of this work is a comprehensive chemometric framework encompassing systematic preprocessing optimization, data-driven feature selection, and rigorous model benchmarking, for the quantification of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {P_2O_5}$$\end{document} in industrial phosphoric acid by LIBS spectroscopy. By evaluating twelve regression algorithms, spanning linear, regularized, tree-based ensemble, and non-linear paradigms, across four progressively refined preprocessing conditions, we provide a reproducible and unbiased assessment of the chemometric landscape for LIBS-based calibration in liquid matrices. The optimally operating calibration model based on PLS regression assisted by VIP-driven feature selection and full-spectrum spectral preprocessing (SNV + Savitzky-Golay) attains remarkable prediction capability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}CV = 0.96, RMSECV = 1.81% \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {P_2O_5}$$\end{document}) that can be applied in industrial process monitoring procedures. The systematic comparison of preprocessing conditions demonstrates that scatter correction (SNV) and noise reduction (Savitzky–Golay) address independent sources of spectral variability, and their combination yields synergistic gains across nearly all model families tested. Importantly, the strategic selection of eleven emission wavelengths grounded in atomic physics reduced model complexity while preserving predictive performance, demonstrating that domain-informed feature engineering outperforms naive full-spectrum approaches for LIBS data. From a measurement perspective, the custom LIBS platform, employing a liquid jet sampling configuration with sub-ten-second analysis time, provides the rapid, in-situ analytical capability that, when coupled with the validated chemometric pipeline, enables real-time closed-loop process control. These results provide a transferable template applicable to other LIBS-based quantification challenges in liquid process streams across the chemical industries.
