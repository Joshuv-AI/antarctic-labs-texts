Stability and turbulence in illegal drug markets
Authors: Beau Kilmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceIllegal drug markets generate enormous harms and involve large amounts of money. This article provides a primer on illegal drug markets, with an emphasis on supply chains. While some features of illegal drug markets have been quite stable over time (e.g., quality of the product is often very difficult to determine from visual inspection), there have been major changes over the past 15 y (e.g., explosion of fentanyl use in North America, increasing use of cryptocurrencies in illegal drug transactions). The article highlights the need for monitoring the price and purity of drugs along the supply chain to better understand supply changes and evaluate policy interventions.

The use of substances to create a sense of euphoria or alter one’s state of consciousness dates back millennia (1, 2). Some of these substances were used for medicinal or spiritual purposes while others were used for pleasure. That is still the case today, with many of these drugs now being subject to laws prohibiting them, or to regulations governing their supply, possession, and use.

The markets for illegal drugs are large, although credible and recent global estimates do not exist. In the United States alone, annual retail expenditures likely exceed $100 billion (3). Many drugs are easy to produce, and their high value-to-weight ratios make them attractive to smugglers. The barriers to entering these markets are minimal at many points along the supply chain, and the main risks to participants are arrest and violence. Demand in major drug markets is driven by those who use frequently and often spend a large share of their income on drugs (in affluent countries, it can be on the order of $10,000 to $20,000 annually for daily or near daily users of cocaine, heroin, and/or methamphetamine) (4). Depending on the drug, consumers typically do not buy in bulk and sometimes make multiple purchases a day. The resulting large number of retail transactions has important implications for the structure of these markets and the conduct of suppliers.

There are also illegal markets for drugs that are legally produced and diverted from medical use. Indeed, diverted prescription opioids played an important role in creating dependence for many people who are now suffering fatal drug overdoses from illegal opioids in North America (5). However, this essay focuses on substances which are illegal throughout the supply chain—from production to final sale. It mostly focuses on illegally produced cocaine, opioids, amphetamine-type substances (including methamphetamine), and cannabis as they account for the largest share of expenditures and harms associated with illegal drug markets. Frequent use of these substances can create problems for people who use them, their loved ones, and others (6–10), but these drugs can also produce benefits. For example, opioids can dull pain and stimulants can provide energy. Even the pure hedonic pleasure of use could enhance social welfare, although pleasure is usually excluded in policy analyses (11, 12).

This article is a primer on illegal drug markets, not a cost–benefit analysis of drug prohibition or legalization. The next section provides an overview of drug prohibition and its enforcement. Section 2 describes research on the size of illegal drug markets and the harms associated with production and trafficking. Section 3 presents some general observations about the structure and nature of drug trafficking enterprises, and Section 4 describes two large markets with different supply chains that have undergone important changes over the past 15 y: The fentanyl trade to the United States and the cocaine trade to Europe. Section 5 describes some recent technological innovations in illegal drug markets, and Section 6 concludes with some ideas for future research.

Overview of Prohibition and Drug Law Enforcement

Many, but not all, mind-altering substances are prohibited because of the harms related to being under the influence (e.g., health, crime) and/or the risks of overdose and addiction, which can be loosely defined as ongoing use despite negative consequences. There are also other reasons that some jurisdictions have prohibited some of these substances [e.g., moral and/or religious opposition to intoxication, prejudice against certain racial groups; (13)].

Potential Mechanisms of Action.

There are at least four mechanisms by which prohibition can discourage illegal drug use.* First, there is the codification and/or encouragement of social disapproval, sending a message about what is acceptable (14). Second, there is the legal deterrent effect (15). Third, prohibition raises prices in two ways: i) It imposes a risk of arrest and can lead to market violence against suppliers by competitors and others involved in criminal activity. Those risks lead suppliers and their workers to get paid more than people doing comparable tasks with legal products, and those costs get passed on to customers (16); and ii) There are major inefficiencies associated with operating businesses in the shadow of the law (17). Fourth, prohibition also increases the time and effort it takes to find a supplier, thus increasing the nonmonetary costs for drug consumers (18).

Prohibition increases the money price for illegal drugs, although the effect of intensifying enforcement on prices generally diminishes as the drug becomes more entrenched and supply networks become more robust. There are examples of major enforcement actions leading to price increases and/or purity declines in established drug markets, but these shocks tend to be temporary [e.g., (19)]. People who use drugs are sensitive to the price (i.e., they consume less when price goes up), although the demand is relatively inelastic (20, 21). Gallet’s (20) review finds that demand for cannabis is less price sensitive than it is for cocaine and heroin, which makes sense since many of those who drive the market for more addictive drugs spend a larger share of their income on these drugs (22). However, an important limitation to much of this literature is the focus on the raw price for drugs vs. the purity-adjusted price. Indeed, a dime bag will usually be $10 but what is inside that bag can vary across time and place (23).

Laws.

Volumes have been written about the origins of drug prohibition, with the specifics generally depending on the substance and place [e.g., (24–29)]. Except for cannabis, today’s dominant illegal drugs did not exist 160 y ago.

There were efforts to reduce nonmedical access to some intoxicating substances before the 1900s, perhaps most notably with bans on alcohol in many Muslim countries, but prohibitions of the “modern drugs” at the national and international levels did not really begin to take shape until the first decades of the 20th Century (27, 29).† Additional drugs were banned in various countries as they emerged throughout the 20th Century. In the 1960s and 1970s, the patchwork of drug-specific bans was made more systematic via three major international conventions on drug control which provide the architecture for current drug laws in many countries (30).

After World War II, there was remarkable international consensus on illegal drugs with the West, the Warsaw Pact countries, nonaligned nations, and newly independent former colonies nearly all being signatories to the various drug control conventions. A noteworthy break from that consensus was in 2011 when Bolivia announced it would withdraw from one of these conventions and reaccede with a reservation to the ban on growing and using coca leaf for traditional purposes (31). A much larger break involves the increasing number of countries which have legalized the supply and possession of cannabis for nonmedical and nonscientific reasons.‡

There are many ways to legalize the supply of a drug and they will have different consequences, especially on price reductions and the size of the illegal market (32–36). Near one end of the cannabis legalization spectrum, almost half of United States states allow profit-maximizing firms to produce, advertise, and sell cannabis to any adult (37).§ Near the other end is Germany which only allows home production and noncommercial cannabis clubs/collectives (38). Canada falls somewhere in the middle: Licensed producers are for-profit businesses, provincial authorities typically serve as the wholesaler, and retail cannabis can be sold by businesses or government stores (36). Canada implemented this model in late 2018 and one study finds that within 4 y the legal market accounted for approximately 75% of total cannabis spending (39).

But recent changes to drug supply policies are not all in the same direction. A major example is the Taliban’s ban on the cultivation, production, and trade of drugs in Afghanistan beginning in April 2022 (40). Traditionally, Afghanistan produces most of the opium used to supply heroin markets in Europe and many other countries (41). While this ban has been “inconsistently enforced” (42), it led to a very large reduction in the total area under opium poppy cultivation [from approximately 230,000 ha in 2022 to closer to 10,000 ha in 2024; (43)]. There are concerns that a heroin shortage could lead to a proliferation of synthetic opioids as seen in North America [e.g., (44); further discussed in Section 4], but many report that the ban has not had much of an effect in Europe, so far [e.g., (42)]. Newly released United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) data show large increases in purity-adjusted heroin prices in Europe from 2022–2024 (43), suggesting this is not an idle fear—especially with increased mentions of nitazene (a synthetic opioid) seizures and overdoses in Europe (45, 46). One possible explanation for the delay is that these production disruptions have been buffered by large opium stockpiles (47).¶ Another nonmutually exclusive possibility is that other countries have increased their opium production (42, 43). The duration of the Taliban ban and whether Europe can prevent a major shift to synthetic opioids remain to be seen, but this experience demonstrates how some drug markets may be more resilient to supply shocks than others, at least in the short run.

Enforcement.

Just because there are laws on the books does not mean they are consistently enforced across drugs, geography, and populations. An individual’s expected sanction for possessing/using or supplying a drug is largely a function of three factors: the probability of getting caught multiplied by the probability of being convicted multiplied by the likely sanction for the offense [which can include the collateral consequences of being convicted of a drug offense, such as losing access to some social services in some jurisdictions; (37)]. That said, one should not assume that everyone knows the penalties for various drug offenses (48).

Many people who consumed illegal opioids or stimulants in the past year did so only infrequently, but the minority of high-frequency consumers dominate consumption, and since many of them buy multiple times per week, and sometimes multiple times per day, there are a staggering number of drug transactions (further discussed in Section 2). A corollary is that for any given retail purchase, the expected sanction is extremely low (13).

The sanctions often depend on the substance(s) and amounts involved, the extent to which the individual is exposed to policing for other reasons, and other relevant circumstances. In some countries, penalties for personal possession can lead to years of incarceration; in others it is not a criminal offense. The sentences tend to be longer for those caught selling, particularly those trafficking larger quantities or if a weapon or violence was involved—and in some countries the punishment can be execution (49).

Depending on the country, criminal justice resources spent addressing drug law violations can be substantial. Between 2010 and 2019, drug arrests accounted for the largest category of arrests made by state and local law enforcement officials in the United States—12 to 16%, or about 1.3 to 1.6 million arrests a year, with a large portion attributable to cannabis and approximately 80% involving possession, not supply (50). Data from Europe and Australia also suggest that possession accounts for the bulk of drug arrests (51, 52).

Since most countries prohibit these drugs and only a small subset employ aggressive, zero-tolerance enforcement, there are clearly other policy options under prohibition—especially within the United States. For example, some cities have passed deprioritization measures which make the enforcement of certain drug laws a low or the lowest priority for law enforcement [e.g., (53)]. The United States Congress has gone further and prohibited the United States Department of Justice from using “taxpayer funds to prevent states from ‘implementing their own laws that authorize the use, distribution, possession, or cultivation of medical marijuana.’” (54). Law enforcement efforts sometimes prioritize activities that reduce harms associated with trafficking and open-air drug markets vs. trying to decrease supply (55, 56).

Scale of Markets and Harms

Illegal markets for drugs are large in terms of customers, transactions, and expenditures, but small in terms of the weight or volume of products sold (3, 4). This section focuses on expenditures—which is useful when trying to understand how much money is being earned by criminal organizations—and some of the harms associated with production and supply.

Expenditures.

There have been various efforts over the years to estimate retail expenditures on illegal drugs. One estimate from UNODC suggested that $322 billion was spent on illegal drugs in 2003 (~$570 billion in 2025 dollars), with roughly 75% of that being spent in Europe and North America (57). That geographic concentration in sales revenue reflects at least some concentration in numbers of users, but even more in disparities in retail prices.

A detailed analysis for the United States put the total retail spending on cannabis, cocaine, heroin, and methamphetamine at approximately $150 billion in 2016, which was on par with the value of the United States alcohol industry at the time (3). Roughly two-thirds of that amount was for cocaine, heroin, and methamphetamine. If one assumes that a typical purchase for these three drugs ranges from $20 to $50 (3, 58), that puts the annual number of retail transactions between 2 billion and 5 billion—and that is just for retail and just for these three substances in the United States.

Estimates produced by the European Union Drugs Agency (EUDA) and Europol (59) suggest the total amount spent on cannabis, cocaine, heroin, amphetamines, and ecstasy in the EU, Norway, and Türkiye totaled at least 30 billion euros in 2017. Given that the population of EU/Norway/Türkiye is nearly 200 million people larger than the US population, the difference in estimates is striking. This speaks not only to higher levels of spending on illegal drugs in the United States, but also to methodological differences which likely underestimate spending in Europe. Much less is known about expenditures in Africa, Asia, and Latin America [e.g., (60)].

Harms Associated with Supply.

An important tension with drug prohibition is that it raises the price of drugs (in hopes that it will reduce consumption), which makes it more profitable for organized crime organizations and other groups to get involved. For example, it would typically cost approximately €250 to send a 1-kg package of legal material from Bogota to London. But under prohibition and the inefficiencies it creates in the supply chain, a $2,000 kg of cocaine in South America can sell for more than €25,000 when it enters Europe [(61); SI Appendix].

Table 1 presents approximate price and purity values for 1 kg of fentanyl pills destined for United States markets and 1 kg of cocaine powder destined for E.U. markets along their supply chains circa 2023 (Sources and justification for these estimates are presented in the SI Appendix). A kilogram of counterfeit pills with fentanyl [approximately 10,000 pills; (62)] costs about $3,000 in Mexico and can generate $30,000 in gross revenue at the retail level in the United States, and sometimes much more. Considering that it can cost less than $20 to acquire the precursor chemicals to make 10,000 pills (SI Appendix), this is a substantial markup, even within Mexico. Similar calculations for cocaine require purity adjustments as the powder gets diluted along the supply chain, but there appears to be about a 100-fold increase between the price for a pure kilogram of cocaine in Colombia and what it could generate at retail level in Europe.

Given the amount of money involved, violence, intimidation, and corruption play important roles in these markets. Those involved cannot settle disputes in courts, so conflicts over payments, quality, trafficking routes, and market share are not uncommon. In some places, those involved in trafficking have actively battled against law enforcement officials attempting to disrupt the trade. The deadliest confrontations have occurred in Latin America where trafficking groups sometimes fought both competitors and government actors (e.g., law enforcement officials, members of the military, politicians). Between 2006–2018, conflicts involving organized crime led to 125,000 to 150,000 deaths in Mexico involving members of these groups, journalists, politicians, and others—and that excludes the more than 70,000 people who went missing or “disappeared”(63). Colombia also saw a staggering amount of violence and death in the 1980s and 1990s related to groups deeply involved in the drug trade (64). Of course, the entities involved in these battles were sometimes struggling over political power and other illegal activities (e.g., extortion), but the prominent role of the drug trade in this violence is undeniable.

Related to this is the frequency of corruption and other threats to the rule of law. Given the relatively low wages paid to law enforcement and border officials in many producer and trafficking countries (compared to the revenues involved), it often makes financial sense to bribe them and politicians to look the other way. Those who will not accept bribes may be persuaded with threats of violence to them and their families (colloquially referred to as the “silver or lead” question). Corruption also occurs in consumer countries. For example, there are multiple examples of United States border guards and other law enforcement officers either being paid or blackmailed to “assist” with drug shipments into the country (65, 66).

There are also well-documented environmental harms related to the illegal drug trade (67). These range from clearing rainforests and other socioenvironmentally important ecosystems to grow illegal crops, to illegally dumping highly toxic chemicals used to produce synthetic drugs or convert plant material into more intoxicating substances (68). Of course, legal businesses and people damage the environment, too, and the effects of drug policy changes on these harms will be shaped by the regulations and, more importantly, monitoring and enforcement of those regulations.

At the retail level, overt or open-air drug markets can increase crime and disorder, creating real problems for those who live and work in these neighborhoods. It is not just the loss of public spaces (e.g., parks), risk of victimization, and general inconvenience that drive the harms associated with these markets. It is also the frustration and resentment that this activity would not be tolerated in higher income neighborhoods [e.g., (69)].

There are larger debates about whether legalizing the production and sales of these substances would reduce these consequences and pass a cost–benefit test (11, 13, 32, 70). While this article does not attempt to answer that question, it is worth noting that prohibition makes it difficult for consumers to know exactly what they are using. Variation in purity and mixtures including dangerous adulterants can lead to health problems and even death (71).

Structure, Nature, and Characteristics of Drug Trafficking Enterprises

It is often useful to distinguish synthetic drugs that can be produced in illegal labs (e.g., fentanyl, lysergic acid diethylamide) from agricultural-based drugs. The cannabis plant produces a large quantity of psychoactive material per unit area, so it can profitably be grown indoors or outdoors, including in final market countries where it is banned. By contrast, opium poppies and coca bushes are essentially always grown outdoors, which is typically only done in areas with weak state control.

This can create a positive feedback loop: Low state control facilitates drug production which in turn supports criminal organizations that undermine state control. As a result, cocaine and heroin production tend to concentrate in certain parts of the world from which they are then distributed via international trafficking. This does not mean that there are not examples of drugs being produced in the country where they are consumed, but the supply chains for most illegal drugs cross at least one national border.

There are multiple frameworks and typologies attempting to classify actors involved at different levels of the drug supply chain. For example, while most trafficking groups are essentially profit-maximizing businesses preferring to keep a low profile to avoid conflict and detection [“trade-oriented” in the literature’s lexicon; (72, 73)], at the higher levels of the markets, where large quantities are supplied, organizations can be more “governance-oriented,” where the focus is on controlling a territory or market, the structure is more hierarchical, and there is a higher propensity for violence (73).# At the other end of the chain, consumers can obtain drugs from multiple sources. There are full-time sellers vying for promotions, part-time sellers supplementing other income sources, and friends/acquaintances willing to share drugs or sell them slightly above cost (74–76).

The remainder of this section provides general observations on the structure and nature of drug trafficking enterprises, with a focus on four areas.

Levels of the Market and Size of Enterprises.

Because most illegal drugs cross international borders, there are typically multiple entities involved across the supply chain. Indeed, there are often multiple links within the final market country. The number of links in the chain will depend on the substance being considered, physical distance between the producer and consumer, and number of border crossings. To generalize, most agricultural-based illegal drugs often involve at least six types of organizations in the supply chain: Producers, exporters, importers, wholesale distributors, retail sellers, and consumers.

The total number of people participating in the supply chain for agricultural-based drugs likely resembles an hourglass (77–79). But once the product makes it to the country of final destination, the number of actors begins to increase, as one person or organization at one market level supplies many people at the next lower level. In big markets like the United Kingdom, there are tens of thousands of retail drug sellers (80). In contrast, the right side of Fig. 1 represents the supply chain for synthetic opioids like fentanyl. It is more like a triangle, with very few producers and chemists involved, and a growing number of actors as the product moves down the supply chain (81).

Within each level there are multiple enterprises involved. Often, they coexist relatively peacefully, either unaware of each other’s activities or not finding it profitable to attack rivals. However, the top and bottom of the distribution chains tend to be more place-based, and so more prone to violent competition. Indeed, there can be multiple street gangs involved in retail drug selling in just one city, and this competition can lead to violence. There may also be other retailers that are not members of gangs or criminal organizations. Closer to the top of the supply chain, there are often just a few organizations that control most of the drugs flowing out of producer countries. For example, it is believed that the Sinaloa and the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG) organizations in Mexico are responsible for exporting most of the fentanyl consumed in the United States (82).

The great majority of drug distribution in final market countries is conducted by small organizations or sole proprietors. Even international smuggling operators would not be considered “big businesses” akin to many Fortune 500 companies. But particularly where production requires compromising state control, there can be large organizations; however, it would generally be wrong to assume that all employees of the high-level organizations are involved in the drug trade.

For example, one study estimates there were on the order of 175,000 “cartel” members in Mexico in 2022, working for roughly 150 organizations [CJNG and Sinaloa were the two largest, accounting for 18% and 9% of members, respectively (83)]. These larger Mexican organizations are often involved in multiple activities (e.g., drugs, extortion, kidnapping, human smuggling). Indeed, some employees could focus on corruption or surveillance, which may not be specific to a certain class of activities. But given the role of drugs in many of these Mexican organizations, it does not seem controversial to assert that tens of thousands of Mexicans are involved in the production, packaging, and trafficking of drugs.

At the retail level, street gangs likely play a smaller role in distribution today than in the 1980s, and a similar caveat applies: Gangs exist to serve many functions for their members (e.g., physical protection, sense of identity), and not all members of these gangs are exclusively involved in the drug trade. Focusing on just one drug selling gang in Chicago in the 1990s, Levitt and Venkatesh (84) found there were roughly 30 individuals responsible for 12-square blocks of territory in Chicago. There were at least twice as many other “rank-and-file” gang members who did not sell drugs in this area; however, some obtained drugs from the gang to sell elsewhere.

For this example and the estimates presented for the Mexican organizations, one should not assume these figures are for full-time equivalent employees. There are important questions about how representative this Chicago organization is of other gangs primarily focused on selling drugs, particularly today. Levitt and Venkatesh (84) argue that the structure of this organization is “simple compared with most firms of comparable size,” and that it is similar to a franchise model. The local gang leaders pay a fee to central leadership and keep the net revenues from what they sell. The extent to which this franchise model is used in today’s drug markets is unknown; however, multilayer systems still insulate “kingpins” from consumers and lower-level sellers (who will sometimes provide information to legal authorities in return for a lesser sentence).

Online supply chains can be an exception because the absence of in-person transactions reduces the need for there to be many layers separating producers from consumers. For example, one infamous early fentanyl pill pressing organization imported fentanyl directly from China and sold to individual consumers, not just bulk sales to retailers (85).

Vertical Integration.

It is not unheard of for illegal drug producers to also serve as the supplier to the final consumer (e.g., there have always been some small-scale cannabis growers who also sell). However, these producer-to-consumer transactions likely account for a small share of today’s market. The supply chains highlighted in Fig. 1 represent the majority of illegal drug market activity.

With respect to the conduct of firms along the supply chain, some of the larger trafficking organizations may have affiliations or long-term relationships with smaller criminal organizations involved in wholesale and/or retail trade (86). This provides a constant supply source for the smaller organizations and can disincentivize them from doing business with other trafficking organizations. There are also cases where there is no formal relationship among suppliers; interactions are much more transactional. Transactions with strangers could increase risk of a rip-off or interaction with an undercover law enforcement official, but it could also make investigations more difficult given the lack of information participants have about each other.

Understanding of actor relationships across and within these groups has been a focus of social network analysis (SNA). Bichler and colleagues (87) argue that before the emergence of SNA, the general consensus was that most drug trafficking operations involved loosely connected networks that can quickly react to shifting market conditions. Their systematic review of SNA research on drug trafficking groups provided additional support for this hypothesis and found that while several structures exist, these networks tend to be sparse (i.e., loosely connected) with “central individuals connecting the group and linking different groups, suggesting an operation preference for security.” A recent analysis focused on the networks of 1,800 individuals involved in drug distribution in Western Canada found that 9% of them could be classified as “community brokers” who made connections across groups and/or market levels (88).

Competitiveness.

Drug markets are generally competitive, and prices tend to get bid down to largely compensate for the costs of doing business; however, compensation for the risk of violence and arrest are large, contributing to the large markups as drugs move down the supply chain [(89); Table 1]. Drug suppliers not only compete with other illegal enterprises; they must also contend with government enforcement entities (e.g., police, border control, and sometimes the military).

Retail prices are high and can be highly variable, in part because of poor information flows; it is expensive to “comparison shop” for the best price when any given potential seller may be a law enforcement agent conducting a reverse sting (“sell and bust”) operation. Analyzing data from the Drug Enforcement Administration, Reuter and Caulkins (90) studied price dispersion for heroin and cocaine in the United States. They found that the average coefficient of variation in drug retail prices (>=0.48, depending on product) exceeded the coefficient of variation for the 15 legal goods they examined, ranging from beer (0.19) to bacon (0.06). Caulkins and Baker (91) provide another plausible explanation for the dispersion: Some suppliers’ aversion to holding inventory combined with their imperfect insights about future price fluctuations. Another factor influencing prices is distance from the point of import (92).

Barriers to Entry and Characteristics of Participants.

Putting cannabis aside, converting agricultural products into street drugs requires access to chemicals, but it is generally not a difficult task. There are also few barriers to acquiring the precursor chemicals (which are often used for legal purposes, too) and equipment needed to produce fentanyl pills. To show how easy it is to enter the fentanyl trade, a recent investigation by Reuters involved reporters purchasing $3,600 worth of precursors and equipment online (e.g., pill press) from Chinese vendors, enough to produce at least 3 million counterfeit oxycodone pills containing fentanyl (93).

The Reuters reporters did not produce the fentanyl, but it would not have been too difficult. They quoted one Mexican fentanyl cook as saying, “It’s like making chicken soup.” Another investigative reporting team noted that “(L)earning to produce fentanyl required little more than access to a recipe, with no need for advanced knowledge of chemistry. These recipes could be purchased directly from suppliers in China or taught by amateur chemists”(94). These authors also noted it costs around $60,000 to set up a fentanyl production lab in Mexico. That amount of start-up capital is easy to amass for trafficking organizations that collectively already had net revenues in the billions per year before fentanyl.

Wholesale distribution is mostly brokerage and requires even less technical knowledge (95). One buys a large package of drugs, sometimes adds adulterants, divides it into smaller packages, and sells to the next layer down the chain. The only important scarce resource may be connections (i.e., knowing from whom to buy and to whom to sell). That limits the effectiveness of eliminating such organizations, because others can generally easily expand to replace them.

It is even easier to become a retail dealer if someone wants to risk arrest (and its subsequent consequences), violence, and possibly reputational damage. Indeed, some of those addicted to substances will get involved in selling or brokering, or serving as lookouts in return for free product (96).

That barriers to entry are low does not mean that just anyone can easily enter the market. Social networks matter. Knowing someone in the market who can vouch for a new entrant—to suppliers and customers—can make it much easier to excel in the drug trade. There is rarely a shortage of potential sellers who know people in the market (e.g., young individuals who serve as lookouts for dealers), so a limited pool of labor does not limit drug distribution. Given the risk of arrest and violence, drug supply can be especially attractive to those with limited opportunities in the legal labor market, e.g., because of a prior criminal record, exclusion from conventional career paths, or coming from an impoverished area.

In the United States, drug supply arrests can be made by law enforcement officials at the federal, state, and local levels. To generalize, the federal cases tend to focus on the higher levels of the market while state/local agencies focus on lower offenses (e.g., street-level dealers). For those convicted of a federal drug trafficking offenses and sentenced in fiscal year 2023 (N = 18,939), the mean age was 38 y, 83.6% were male, 43.5% were Hispanic, 27.6% were Black, 25.8% were White, 81.9% were United States citizens, and 6.3% had their sentence increased because they held a leadership or supervisory role in the offense (97). This latter statistic is consistent with previous research on those in federal and state prisons for drug offenses which found that 6.6% and 4% of these individuals, respectively, reported being high- to mid-level drug ring participants (98). As the authors note, most of these incarcerated individuals were neither kingpins nor mules; they are largely in the “ambiguous middle of that spectrum.”

While there are exceptions, many street-level dealers do not make a lot of money; however, some tolerate the low incomes in hopes of getting promoted and earning higher incomes (84, 99). In fact, many of these street-level dealers also work in the legal sector. For the drug gang studied by Levitt and Venkatesh (84), the authors note that the street level drug dealers probably worked for the gang an average of 20 h a week and many of them also had jobs in the legitimate sector. This is somewhat consistent with MacCoun and Reuter (99) who found that among 186 Washington DC probationers who had reported earning income from drug selling, the median time spent selling per day was 3 h and median monthly income from drug selling and legal sources was similar.

Case Studies in Market Developments and Innovations

The section focuses on two specific supply chains that i) represent important drug markets that have undergone significant changes in the past 15 y, ii) highlight two different product types (a synthetic opioid and an agricultural-based stimulant), iii) focus on two different locations of consumers (United States and Europe, which vary in terms of location from production and intensity of law enforcement), and iv) have been the subject of empirical analyses.

Illegally Manufactured Fentanyl Supply to the United States.

Fentanyl plays an important role in medicine, especially as an anesthetic and pain reliever; however, the United States and Canadian problems with this drug have largely come from illegally manufactured products vs. diverted pharmaceuticals. Before the current wave of the fentanyl crisis which began circa 2013, there had been multiple brief illegally manufactured fentanyl outbreaks, some of which killed hundreds of people; however, these outbreaks could typically be linked to a single enterprising chemist, and they were eventually shut down (71).

The first major change in the 2010s was technological: It became much easier for those with minimal expertise to synthetize fentanyl. New, easier synthesis techniques proliferated online, and China’s large and poorly regulated chemical industry was ripe for producing and exporting it to populations transitioning from diverted prescription pills to illegal opioids (60, 71).

Multiple factors led to the fall of China’s dominance in the illegal fentanyl market, including increased attention from United States law enforcement and China banning production of fentanyl in 2019 (but not precursors). Fentanyl production for the United States market transitioned to Mexico using precursor chemicals from China (93, 100). The move to Mexico also expanded the product form from mostly powder (essentially fentanyl replacing heroin) to also counterfeit pills (which appeal to people who would not use via injection). While there had always been some counterfeit pills containing fentanyl in the United States. (85), this appeared to ramp up when fentanyl production moved to Mexico. Indeed, the number of fentanyl pills seized in the United States increased from roughly 50,000 in 2017 to more than 115 million in 2023 (62).

The economics of fentanyl make it very attractive for drug trafficking organizations (and hence also for people who use opioids). After adjusting for potency by conversion to morphine equivalent doses (MED), fentanyl cost only 1% as much as heroin for producers of illegal opioids in Mexico circa 2019 (71). Indeed, the raw materials for making fentanyl are so extraordinarily cheap that producers make relatively little from them. Researchers estimate that the total annual revenues for selling fentanyl precursors needed to supply the United States in the early 2020s was likely in the low tens of millions of dollars (101).

The weight of pure fentanyl consumed in the United States is quite small because the drug is so potent. The best estimates available circa 2021 put the total pure amount consumed in the single-digit metric tons (MT) (81, 101). To help put this in perspective, the United States consumed about 145 MT of pure cocaine in 2016 (3) and imports about 1,000,000 tons of avocados from Mexico each year (56, 102).

Even though fentanyl collapsed production costs for illegal opioids, retail prices did not fall proportionately. The reason is that when a person who uses drugs buys illegal opioids, they are purchasing not just the physical product, but also the service of moving that product from the point of production to the point of retail sale. The costs and risks of distributing illegal fentanyl are not all that different than the costs and risks of distributing heroin, and—as noted above—most of the increase in illegal opioid prices along the supply chain is from domestic distribution.

Nonetheless, the fentanyl era does appear to have depressed prices per MED throughout the supply chain. One estimate focused on the lower-level wholesale markets for fentanyl powder in the United States suggests that the purity-adjusted prices likely halved between 2016 and 2021 (103).

But something has changed in recent years as there has been a large and rapid decline in the number of United States overdose deaths mentioning fentanyl. In April 2023, the number of people who died from a drug overdose involving synthetic opioids other than methadone (the category that includes fentanyl) in the past year was ~78,000; the comparable figure for April 2025 was ~44,000—roughly a 44% decrease (104). Several hypotheses have been offered, ranging from waning of the impact of the COVID-19 epidemic, to increases in treatment availability and naloxone, to changes by Mexican suppliers (105, 106). But few explanations are consistent with the quick and simultaneous decline in fentanyl deaths observed in the United States and Canada—which is believed to get most of its fentanyl from domestic synthesis, not Mexico (107). One plausible explanation is that actions were taken in China which reduced access to precursor chemicals for both markets (107). Because changes in fentanyl deaths were not uniform across United States states (104), more research is needed on the likely combination of factors driving these changes, and how they may differ across jurisdictions.

Cocaine Supply to Europe.

Cocaine is produced from the leaves of the coca bush which is primarily grown in Bolivia, Colombia, and Peru. The leaves are mixed with solvents (gasoline and other chemicals) to extract the cocaine into a pasty material known as cocaine base. The base, being more compact, can more easily be transported to labs where hydrochloric acid is used to convert the cocaine base into cocaine hydrochloride, the powder we typically think of as cocaine. Crack is easy to produce from powder cocaine just by mixing it with water and baking soda and heating.

Coca had been used for centuries in South America, mostly in unconcentrated form (e.g., chewing leaves or steeping them in teas), but the first large export market for cocaine was the United States. Cocaine was always available in Europe, but it was much more expensive, and the purity tended to be lower; not surprising given the proximity of the United States to the producer countries. But the situation has changed, with the UNODC noting that “flows of cocaine to Europe have increased dramatically in recent years” (73).

Indeed, the average purity of cocaine at the retail level—an important indicator of supply—for 23 European countries increased from less than 40% in 2010 to more than 60% in 2020 (108). More recent figures from France suggest the average cocaine purity at the retail level in 2023 exceeded 70% (109). Data from the wastewater testing network in Europe suggest that cocaine use was generally flat from 2011–2015, but started increasing in 2016 and in 2024 there were “further increases in cocaine residues in most cities when compared to 2023” (110) The EUDA reports that cocaine “is now the most frequently reported substance in acute drug-toxicity presentations to sentinel hospital emergency departments” (111).

Since 2010, there have been at least two significant changes to the cocaine supply chain to Europe: i) A massive increase in coca production in South America, and ii) The emergence of Ecuador as a major cocaine exporter. This is not meant to suggest that these changes have not affected other countries, but they have had a particularly significant impact on European markets.

The massive increase in coca production in Latin America.

Data published by UNODC show a fourfold increase in the number of hectares dedicated to coca production from 2010 to 2022, with the bulk of the increase happening in Colombia (112). The UNODC also generates estimates of the potential amount of pure cocaine produced, and they show a larger increase over this period for Colombia, from roughly 400 MT in 2010 to 1,700 in 2022. Data for 2023 show a 1-y increase of nearly 50%, to more than 2,600 MT (112).

There are multiple hypotheses as to what caused the increase, which really seemed to pick up circa 2014. One focuses on incentives. During the peace negotiations in Colombia (2012–2016) there was an announcement that coca farmers would be compensated for substituting their coca crops with something else. This incentivized farmers to start growing (or grow more) coca (113), and the government struggled to compensate these farmers (114). Indeed, Prem et al. (113) concluded that this announcement “generated an unprecedented escalation in cocaine production in Colombia, offsetting almost 20 y and billions of dollars of United States-backed efforts to stop drug production and cartel action.” Also, with the peace process leading to the demobilization of the main armed group (Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia, FARC), this created an opportunity for smaller criminal organizations to get involved in the trade and look for new markets (115, 116).||

Another potential explanation is that governmental efforts to destroy and/or eradicate coca plants in Colombia started to decline circa 2008, falling from a peak of more than 200,000 ha of coca a year to less than 70,000 by 2014 (117). Most of the reduction was in aerial spraying (vs. manual eradication), and in 2015, citing health concerns with glyphosate, Colombia discontinued aerial spraying (118). Interestingly, Prem et al.’s analysis (113) does not find that this ban—or other changes to enforcement in Colombia around that time—challenges their conclusion that the announcement dramatically increased production from 2014 to 2017.** Coca bushes between years 2 to 4 produce higher yields (115); thus, with fewer bushes being destroyed, the longer they can live and the higher the amount of coca alkaloids that can be extracted per bush.

A final explanation could be an improvement in coca yields. While it is common to refer to the hectares of land used for growing coca and the average yield, there are multiple species of coca (119, 120). There have been several mentions of coca farmers using hybrid coca crops that generate higher cocaine alkaloid yields [e.g., (116)]. Whereas the UNODC estimated that the average annual cocaine leaf yield was 4.7 MT per hectare in 2014, this increased 36% to 6.4 MT by 2020. There was a larger increase in the estimates of average potential cocaine hydrochloride per hectare—5.6 kg in 2014 to 7.9 kg in 2020. Some of this could be attributable to the type of coca grown, but it is also influenced by farming happening in places where the coca fields are more productive (115) and/or the use of better fertilizers (118).

Emergence of Ecuador as a major cocaine exporter.

Around 2010, the UNODC claimed that Colombian organized crime groups dominated the cocaine trafficking trade to Europe. Today, the amount of cocaine leaving the Americas from Colombia is declining, with traffickers choosing exit points in other parts of Latin America, especially Ecuador (108, 121). While it is difficult to precisely estimate how much is exported from each country, Ecuador’s president estimates that as much as 70% of the world’s cocaine supply now passes through the country (122).

Multiple sources have documented the increasing role of Albanian cocaine traffickers in Ecuador (123, 124), with The Economist noting “criminal networks led by Albanians infiltrated Ecuador’s ports, judiciary, prison system, and security forces to gain control of key parts of the cocaine supply chain and trigger a deluge of the drug in Europe”(125). These Albanian traffickers have very strong connections with distributors throughout Europe (123, 125, 126).††

Synthesis.

30 y ago, one could generalize that North America largely had a problem with stimulants and Europe largely had a problem with opioids. While both are still dealing with these issues, the drugs of major concern have flipped. The major problem facing the United States is opioids, and Europe is dealing with a “deluge” of cocaine. Barriers to producing fentanyl declined dramatically with advances in synthesis methods using relatively easy to access precursor chemicals. While there were also technological advances in cocaine production, the major European expansion likely had more to do with policy announcements and changes in power structures in Latin America. The announcement of a plan to compensate Colombia farmers to not grow coca backfired. At the same time there was also an increase in the number of criminal organizations involved in the trade (some with strong European ties), partially because of the vacuum created by the Peace Accords and the diminished role of the FARC. Some of these groups were able to capitalize on—and in some cases facilitate via corruption and intimidation—a weak rule of law in other Latin American countries.

Technological Change in Illegal Drug Markets

While advances in production of synthetic drugs and changes in trafficking patterns and organizations involved account for some of the adaptation in illegal drug markets over the past two decades, these are not the only changes. The modal retail transaction is still in person involving cash, but technological changes are increasingly affecting illegal drug markets.

Dark net marketplaces use “both encryption and anonymizing communication technologies, which are designed to promote anonymity and frustrate organized tracking efforts,” to facilitate the sale of illegal drugs and many other prohibited goods (127). There is little evidence that dark net marketplaces account for a sizable share of illegal drug transactions; the percentage of people who use drugs and made at least one purchase via the dark web is likely in the single digits (128).

But sellers and buyers do not have to use the dark net to make internet transactions—there are many opportunities for making these purchases on the regular “surface” web. There were many examples of Chinese sellers marketing fentanyl online when they dominated the trade (81), and the proliferation of social media and encrypted texting platforms that automatically delete messages has reduced search times and risk for some consumers. Social media makes it easy for sellers to advertise—especially to young people—and, depending on the source, purchased goods can be delivered in person or via post (129, 130). In some countries, there appears to be an increase in Russian-style “dead drops” where drugs are hidden in public places for customers to retrieve after they make an online purchase (131).

To the extent these deliveries from dark net or social media sellers reduce the size of overt drug markets (and the disorder and violence often associated with them), this could generate some benefits over traditional street selling. Of course, this must be weighed against the increased access it provides to youth and other vulnerable populations.

The use of cryptocurrencies to pay for drugs may be a relatively small part of the retail market, but there is also the opportunity to use crypto to launder money back to the major drug trafficking organizations. For example, a federal indictment made public in November 2024 claims that defendants “worked together to pick up bulk cash, derived from drug sales in various cities throughout the United States. They would then coordinate to exchange the cash for cryptocurrency that was sent to wallets controlled by the defendants or their coconspirators. The cryptocurrency would then be converted into cash and delivered to cartel leaders in Mexico and Colombia”(132). It is unclear how many other operations like this exist, but this technology creates additional challenges to those seeking to address money laundering.

There have also been improvements in the ability to determine what is in the powders and pills being consumed—which can potentially save lives and mitigate the “lemons” problem that plagues consumers (and downstream suppliers) who do not know exactly what they are purchasing (90, 133). Law enforcement officials typically send seized drugs to labs where the samples are often tested in expensive and stationary machines. Not only have there been important advances in technologies that allow police and people who use drugs to determine if a sample contains a certain substance in real-time (e.g., binary fentanyl test strips), there have also been new efforts to provide more detailed information about substances in a sample and, in some cases, their amounts. For example, in an increasing number of United States cities [and in some parts of Europe; (134)], people can test a small sample with drug checkers using mobile testing units and get a report in less than 10 min [via Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy; (135)]. There are also an increasing number of initiatives to create phone apps and other devices that provide drug content information instantaneously [e.g., (136)].

The extent to which suppliers are using these drug checking services is unknown, but it is generally believed that most of the participants are the final consumers. But as these technologies get better, cheaper, and more available, transactions at the retail and wholesale levels could become more transparent. This may not only improve health outcomes for some individuals, but it could also reduce rip-offs and the violence they sometimes provoke.

Conclusions

Drug markets have many distinctive features. The supply chains are almost always international and sometimes intercontinental, which creates opportunities for organized crime groups in cross-border transactions—albeit those who often have portfolios of income-generating illegal activities.

Suppliers in these markets constantly innovate, adopting the latest technologies, and collectively they are among the largest illegal markets in terms of expenditures. And yet, some features of illegal drug markets have been quite stable over time, in part because most sales are to heavy users who find it difficult to buy in bulk. As a result, the supply chains must continue to i) Support tens of billions of in-person retail sales per year, which ii) Demands labor from people who can tolerate high arrest risk and physically deter robbery and nonpayment (which in practice may mean young men from low-income communities), and iii) Insulate “kingpins” from the retail sellers who are more vulnerable to police enforcement.

Another consistent feature of these markets is that the quality of the product is often very difficult—if not impossible—to determine from inspection, and consuming poor-quality products can be fatal. The potency of the product being purchased is unknown (often to the sellers, too) and there are no labels indicating what other chemicals or compounds are included in the package.

But there has also been turbulence in these markets over the past 15 y. While illegally manufactured synthetic drugs are not new, the proliferation of fentanyl, nitazenes, and other synthetic opioids in illegal markets has created an overdose crisis in North America and has other countries on high alert (137). The internet has also made producing and accessing a wide range of synthetic drugs and their precursor chemicals easier, and the emergence of cryptocurrencies has created new opportunities to hide transactions and launder the proceeds (138). The fact that Ecuador has gone from being a Latin American success story to being labeled the “world’s newest narco-state” by The Economist (125) is a troubling reminder of how quickly illegal drug trafficking—and the corruption and violence it generates and/or accelerates—can change a country or region.

The types and amounts of drugs flowing through these markets will change, and improving monitoring efforts to track and understand fluctuations could save lives and provide data critical for policy. The recommendations are straightforward for the United States, but some of them also apply to other countries (139, 140): i) Resurrect data programs that provided rich insights about drug market participants, transactions, and revenues (e.g., Arrestee Drug Abuse Monitoring Program); ii) Create a wastewater testing network for monitoring consumption of illegal drugs (this would serve as an early warning system and allow researchers to assess trends and consumption levels); and iii) Return to allowing vetted researchers access to incident-level data from law enforcement seizures that allow for the analysis of purity and (when available) price.

Indeed, the United States used to lead the world in generating estimates of the purity-adjusted prices—which is arguably the most important measure for understanding and evaluating market changes—but these data are no longer regularly reported by the federal government (140). There are multiple approaches to generating these prices from law enforcement data (103, 141, 142), but there are other ways that do not involve law enforcement data. Indeed, as drug checking services become more prevalent and accurate, asking people who test their drugs about the total amount they most recently purchased and how much they spent—and then merging this with the purity information from the sample being tested—could provide rich data about these markets.

Of course, the research opportunities in this field extend far beyond (re)building the data infrastructure. While the vast majority of drug policy research focuses on the demand reduction, a considerable share of drug-control spending is focused on supply (143). Learning more about the decision-making processes of current and former higher-level traffickers could yield great insights into market fluctuations and help predict future ones (95, 140). There is also important research to be done to help law enforcement agencies and other decisionmakers cost-effectively allocate resources related to reducing harms from drug trafficking (56)—especially given the dynamics of drug epidemics (144). If government resources are going to be used to influence these markets, taxpayers need to make sure they are getting the most “bang for the buck.”
