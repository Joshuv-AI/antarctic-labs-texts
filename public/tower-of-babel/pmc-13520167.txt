A multi-systemic ethical dilemma model in hemodialysis patients: a qualitative study
Authors: Huiqing Zhu, Anjie Zhao, Yixing Zhou, Farong Zhang, Yuxiu Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn hemodialysis, patients navigate ethical tensions arising from the intersection of clinical demands, personal values, and systemic constraints. While existing research has focused largely on clinician perspectives, limited attention has been paid to how patients themselves experience and interpret these ethical dilemmas. This study aims to identify and analyze the ethical dilemmas experienced by hemodialysis patients, and to construct a multi-level conceptual model that articulates how these ethical issues manifest across different systemic dimensions.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative descriptive design. 35 hemodialysis patients were purposely selected from four traditional Chinese medicine hospitals in Shandong Province, China. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews between September and December 2025 and analyzed using inductive thematic analysis. The framework of Ecological Systems Theory was applied to determine how multilevel systemic interactions give rise to ethical dilemmas, leading to the construction of a multidimensional conceptual model.ResultsEthical dilemmas experienced by hemodialysis patients were grouped into three themes with 12 subthemes and 19 subcategories. A multi-systemic ethical dilemma model was developed, encompassing macro-level dilemmas involving policy, organization, culture, and community dimensions; mezzo-level interactions among healthcare providers, family members, and other patients; and micro-level individual struggles. Furthermore, we illustrate how dilemmas at one level can directly generate, or indirectly exacerbate dilemmas at other levels through dynamic interplay.ConclusionThis study introduces a multi-systemic ethical dilemma model grounded in patients’ experiences, revealing how ethical challenges in hemodialysis operate across macro, mezzo, and micro levels of the care ecosystem. By reframing dilemmas as embedded rather than isolated, the model underscores the need for systemic solutions that address structural and policy-level factors alongside individual support. These findings call for holistic approaches in research, practice, and policy to foster ethically sustainable environments for patients.Keywords: Ethical dilemma, Hemodialysis, Patients, Qualitative study, Ecological Systems Theory

Introduction

Hemodialysis serves as a cornerstone life-sustaining therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease, necessitating complex and lifelong disease management. This includes strict adherence to hemodialysis regimens (typically thrice-weekly sessions lasting approximately four hours), meticulous control of medications, dietary intake, and fluid balance. In addition to these treatment demands, patients frequently contend with a range of complications and comorbidities, such as peripheral vascular disease and debilitating symptoms including fatigue, pruritus, anxiety, and insomnia [1]. These interconnected factors collectively contribute to substantial physical suffering and psychological distress [2]. In recent years, health authorities in China have actively promoted the integration of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) techniques into medical institutions at all levels. This integration reflects a broader international trend, as some countries have incorporated TCM therapies into their hemodialysis care protocols [3]. Furthermore, the financial implications are profound; the annual expenditure on dialysis treatment accounts for approximately 1% to 3% of the total national health expenditure, imposing a considerable burden on the public health insurance system [4]. While China has implemented reforms to its relevant medical insurance policies, these changes have also introduced new ethical tensions related to resource allocation and coverage equity. For example, patients enrolled in different insurance schemes face significantly different reimbursement rates for the same dialysis services, creating an inequity. This disparity, which violates the principle of distributive justice, places hemodialysis patients in an ethically vulnerable position.

Building directly on this clinical and policy context, hemodialysis places patients within a complex web of clinical, relational, and systemic factors that frequently give rise to ethical issues [5]. Aligning with the core aim, this study adopts and justifies a broadened, patient-centered conceptualization of ethical dilemmas for hemodialysis populations. Traditional philosophical definitions frame ethical dilemmas as irreconcilable moral conflicts involving unavoidable tragic trade-offs where any choice necessarily violates an equally important moral principle [6]. However, contemporary medical ethics research advocates for an expanded scope of ethical analysis in chronic disease care, demonstrating that patient-experienced ethical challenges extend beyond individual moral quandaries to include systemic, remediable injustices and long-term care burdens shaped by healthcare institutions and social structures [7, 8]. A strict philosophical definition would exclude many of the most pressing moral problems reported by patients—such as insurance disparities or inadequate community support—thereby limiting the comprehensiveness of any multi-systemic model. Therefore, rather than abandoning the traditional concept, we propose a complementary layered vocabulary to capture the full spectrum of patient-reported ethical struggles. This vocabulary distinguishes between two interrelated but conceptually distinct categories: (1) Individual moral dilemmas, which refer to intrapersonal, irreconcilable conflicts between competing moral principles (e.g., trade-offs between treatment adherence and quality of life, or between prolonging life and preserving autonomy). These fit the strict philosophical definition of a dilemma. (2) Systemic ethical dilemmas, which refer to patient-perceived structural injustices, violations, and predicaments stemming from inequitable healthcare policies, resource allocation gaps, social stigma, and inadequate social support [9, 10]. While patients may experience these phenomena as oppressive constraints, they are not tragic choices but rather remediable wrongs. This distinction matters because individual dilemmas call for support in navigating trade-offs, whereas systemic dilemmas demand structural remedies through policy and organizational change. This layered conceptualization enables systematic multi-level analysis to capture the full scope of patient-reported ethical struggles in chronic dialysis care without conflating analytically distinct phenomena.

Ethical dilemmas experienced by hemodialysis patients are distinguished from mere clinical uncertainty or practical challenges precisely because they involve irreconcilable moral values at stake, rather than resolvable informational or logistical difficulties. Such ethical dilemmas encompass tensions between treatment adherence and quality of life, between medical advice and patient preferences, and between finite healthcare resources and the imperative to provide equitable care [11]. Hemodialysis patients, who face high dependence on life-sustaining treatment and multifaceted care needs [12], are especially vulnerable to the ethical tensions because their survival depends on a rigid medical regimen that often conflicts with their personal values, dignity, and life goals—forcing them to repeatedly choose between competing moral goods, such as prolonging life versus preserving autonomy or avoiding family burden.

Regarding TCM, its integration into hemodialysis raises distinct ethical questions [11]. Patients who choose TCM based on cultural affinity may face uncertain efficacy, potential risks, and financial burdens, engaging core principles such as informed consent, non-maleficence, and justice. For instance, TCM’s holistic philosophy—emphasizing balance, herbal remedies, and dietary adjustments [13]—may conflict with the rigid biomedical requirements of hemodialysis, such as strict fluid restriction, fixed dialysis schedules, and avoidance of potentially nephrotoxic herbs. A patient wishing to follow TCM principles to improve overall well-being might consume herbal decoctions that inadvertently increase fluid load, forcing a choice between cultural alignment and clinical safety. This tension pits cultural congruence and patient autonomy against clinical beneficence and non-maleficence. Thus, TCM exemplifies how cultural factors generate ethical dilemmas at the intersection of patient values, clinical evidence, and healthcare systems [14].

While the ethical dimensions of hemodialysis have received increasing attention—particularly regarding clinician moral distress arising from systemic constraints or conflicts between patients and families over end-of-life decisions [15, 16]—existing research exhibits a significant limitation. Studies have predominantly focused on the perspectives of healthcare professionals, with considerably less attention given to how patients themselves experience, interpret, and navigate ethical dilemmas. Moreover, the limited research addressing ethical issues from the patient perspective has largely been cataloged in isolation [17], lacking a coherent theoretical framework to explain how these issues interconnect.

Patients are not merely passive recipients of ethically complex care; they are active moral agents [18] whose values, goals, and lived experiences shape how they navigate such challenges. This view aligns with relational autonomy, which recognizes that decision-making in chronic illness is embedded within social and relational contexts [19]. Therefore, understanding which ethical dilemmas are most salient from their perspective is of critical clinical and ethical importance for developing supportive interventions that align with their values.

To provide a structured analytical lens, we adopt Ecological Systems Theory as the analytical framework. Originally formulated by Bronfenbrenner as Ecological Systems Theory and subsequently adapted by Zastrow for application in the social and behavioral sciences under the name Ecological Systems Theory, this framework is epistemologically founded on the premise that human development and behavior are most accurately understood within the context of multiple, interrelated environmental systems [20, 21]. This study adopts Zastrow’s adapted version of Bronfenbrenner’s framework, which simplifies the original five nested systems into three actionable levels (micro, mezzo, macro). The theory posits that individuals operate as adaptive systems, shaped by dynamic and reciprocal interactions with their surrounding environments. This framework offers a comprehensive multi-level analytical framework, particularly well-suited for examining complex issues such as ethical practice [22] and has proven instrumental in our prior studies. Its analytical scope encompasses three levels: the micro-system, addressing individual-level factors; the mezzo-system, encompassing group-level dynamics; and the macro-system, incorporating broader institutional, cultural, and societal influences. By applying this multi-level lens, we systematically examine how factors at different hierarchical levels interact to shape ethical dilemmas in hemodialysis patients.

By positioning patient voices at the core of inquiry, this study aims to systematically identify and analyze the ethical dilemmas encountered by hemodialysis patients as personally experienced. Drawing on Ecological Systems Theory, we will construct a multi-level conceptual model to elucidate how these dilemmas manifest across individual, relational, and systemic dimensions. The proposed model not only elucidates the inherent complexity of ethical challenges in this clinical context but also facilitates the development of targeted, multi-dimensional support strategies, thereby providing a more holistic foundation for ethical practice in hemodialysis.

Aim

The study aims to identify and categorize the ethical dilemmas unique to hemodialysis patients and to construct a multidimensional model for systematically analyzing these ethical issues across various levels of interaction.

Methods

Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design to explore the multi-layered factors inherent in the ethical dilemmas of hemodialysis patients. This approach is well-suited for obtaining direct and comprehensive descriptions of complex real-world phenomena [23]. The study adhered to the Consolidated Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [24].

Participants

Participants were recruited from four TCM hospitals across four cities in Shandong Province, China. These hospitals were purposefully selected because the integration of TCM into hemodialysis care creates a unique context for examining ethical dilemmas at the intersection of cultural values, clinical evidence, and healthcare policy. Using purposive sampling, researchers recruited patients of varying genders, ages, education levels, marital status, medical insurance, hemodialysis vintage, vascular access, and living and occupational status to capture a wide range of perspectives. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients who had undergone hemodialysis for more than three months; and (2) patients who voluntarily agreed to participate. Recruitment proceeded until data saturation was attained, as determined by continuous iterative analysis. Saturation was considered achieved when three successive interviews produced no new themes or substantial additions to the established codebook [25]. Ultimately, 35 hemodialysis patients (P1-P35) participated in the study.

Data collection

We employed semi-structured in-depth individual interviews to obtain comprehensive data. Although the interview guideline was deductively developed based on the Ecological Systems Theory’s three-level framework to ensure coverage of relevant systemic dimensions, the actual questioning remained open-ended, and participants were actively encouraged to introduce issues and experiences beyond these predefined categories. The interviews utilized a guideline that was initially developed from relevant literature [5, 6, 20, 21] and subsequently refined through consultations with experts in nephrology, hemodialysis, nursing, and medical ethics. To assess the clarity and appropriateness of the guideline, we conducted a pilot interview with three patients and revised it based on their feedback. These three patients were not included in the final sample. The final version is presented in Table 1.

Interview guideline

The first, second, and third authors, all experienced in qualitative interviewing, conducted all interviews between September and December 2025. The format and specific timing of the interviews were determined based on the preferences of the participants. Each interview began with the researchers explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, and the need for audio-recording. This was followed by an operational definition of “ethical dilemma”, accompanied by illustrative examples, to ensure a shared understanding of the concept. The operational definition provided to participants was: “An ethical dilemma is a situation where you face a choice between two morally important values, and choosing one means you cannot fully honor the other – for example, you may face a choice between strictly adhering to medical advice on fluid and dietary restrictions, and meeting your own basic physiological needs.” Open-ended and probing questions, such as “Could you elaborate further on this situation?” and “Have you encountered similar dilemmas in other contexts?” were used to encourage participants to articulate ethical challenges encountered. A total of 35 interviews were completed, at which point thematic saturation was achieved. Based on participants’ preferences, 33 face-to-face interviews took place at the bedside during hemodialysis, while two were held via telephone. All interviews, averaging 32.5 min in duration, were audio-recorded. During the 33 face-to-face interviews, we also documented nonverbal cues, such as tone of voice, facial expressions, and body language. No participants refused or withdrew from the interviews.

Bedside interviews during dialysis treatment enhanced ecological validity by capturing participants’ real-time care experiences and subtle non-verbal cues, strengthening data authenticity [26]. Nevertheless, this setting introduced inherent methodological limitations. Physical immobility and treatment-related fatigue may have restricted participants’ detailed elaboration of complex experiences, while the open ward environment and participants’ ongoing reliance on institutional care could limit full disclosure of critical or negative perceptions, potentially causing response bias. To mitigate these risks, we reassured participants of confidentiality and adopted a neutral interview stance to facilitate authentic expression and conducted member checking throughout data analysis to resolve ambiguous or sensitive content, ensuring overall data trustworthiness.

Data analysis

Within 24 h of each interview, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and verified through repeated listening. We employed a hybrid inductive-deductive thematic analysis approach [27], which integrates bottom-up inductive coding with top-down deductive application of a pre-existing theoretical framework.

The analysis proceeded in two sequential phases. In Phase I (inductive), the first and fifth authors (a seasoned qualitative researcher) independently engaged in repeated, in-depth readings of the transcripts to achieve familiarization. They then generated initial codes and systematically identified patterns pertaining to the perception of ethical dilemmas or their reported consequences. These codes were iteratively discussed, refined, and consolidated into preliminary themes.

In Phase II (deductive), the preliminary themes were deductively mapped onto the Ecological Systems Theory framework, which provided a conceptual structure for illustrating interactions across micro-, mezzo-, and macro-level systems. This theory-driven deductive mapping was performed after the inductive coding was completed, thereby maintaining fidelity to participants’ accounts while allowing theoretical interpretation. This post-hoc theoretical integration allowed for a structured presentation of the complex findings while remaining grounded in participants’ original accounts. The mapping process involved inherent analytical ambiguity, as several emergent themes spanned multiple systemic levels and resisted rigid single-level classification. For example, stigma, categorized as a macro-level cultural factor in the final model, originates from societal prejudice (macro-level), manifests in interpersonal ward interactions (mezzo-level), and exacerbates individual psychological distress (micro-level). To resolve such cross-level ambiguity and ensure rigor, all themes were classified based on their primary root cause rather than secondary expressive manifestations. Ambiguous codes and thematic boundaries were resolved through repeated team discussion and consensus negotiation. This approach avoided forced alignment between raw data and the theoretical framework, enhancing the transparency and credibility of the multi-system dilemma model.

Throughout the analytical process, we engaged in ongoing reflexivity to critically examine how our professional backgrounds in nephrology might influence data interpretation in accordance with established guidance on reflexivity in qualitative research [28]. The first and second authors maintained reflexive journals to document their assumptions, reactions, and potential biases during coding and theme development. Regular team meetings included explicit discussions of how our disciplinary perspectives shaped the emerging findings, with the fourth author—who was not involved in direct patient care—serving as a critical interlocutor to challenge taken-for-granted clinical assumptions. Any ambiguous or potentially contentious points were clarified with participants in a timely manner. The fourth author also verified the final themes against the original transcripts and coded data, and regular team discussions were convened to establish consensus, thereby minimizing individual bias. These reflexive and verification practices were integrated throughout to ensure both interpretive awareness and analytical accuracy.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number: (2024) Lun Shen No. (168)-YJS). All participants were fully informed of the study’s purpose, methodology, and significance. Written or electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants. Their personal information and interview records were kept strictly confidential.

Rigor

To establish rigor, we employed Lincoln and Guba’s four-dimensional criteria [29]. Credibility was enhanced through pilot interviews to refine the interview guide, participant-determined interview formats to foster authentic responses, and member checking to clarify ambiguous points. Transferability was supported by purposive sampling that ensured diverse participant characteristics across four hospitals, providing rich contextual information for readers to assess applicability to other settings. Dependability was established through meticulous documentation of the research process and regular team discussions to maintain consistency in data interpretation. Confirmability was achieved by having the fourth author verify emergent themes against original transcripts and coded data, ensuring findings reflected participant perspectives rather than researcher preconceptions.

Results

The 35 participants had a mean age of 52.2 years (range: 22.0–72.0 years), with an average of 5.2 years on hemodialysis (range: 4.0 months–19.0 years). The sample was predominantly male (80.0%), and 77.1% had not attained a full-time bachelor’s degree or higher. A majority (82.9%) utilized an arteriovenous fistula for vascular access. Regarding health insurance, 62.9% were enrolled in the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance. However, a substantial proportion (72.2%) were unemployed, primarily due to health-related reasons associated with their hemodialysis treatment. Analysis of the data revealed systemic and multidimensional ethical dilemmas in hemodialysis patients, which could be mapped onto a three-tiered model (Fig. 1) comprising macro- (societal/organizational), mezzo- (interpersonal), and micro-system (individual) levels. Three main themes, comprising 12 subthemes and 19 subcategories, were identified, each situated within and across these levels. Furthermore, we illustrate how dilemmas at one level can directly generate—or indirectly exacerbate—dilemmas at other levels through dynamic interplay.

Ethical dilemma model for hemodialysis patients

Figure 1 provides a dedicated representation of this three-tiered model, with explicit directional and reciprocal relationships. The macro level (socio‑environmental) includes policy, organizational, cultural, and community factors (Theme 1). The mezzo level (interpersonal) encompasses healthcare provider‑patient, friendship, family, and patient‑patient relationships (Theme 2). The micro level (individual) comprises physiological/psychological, agency, role/responsibility, and medical knowledge factors (Theme 3). Directional arrows indicate that macro‑level dilemmas (e.g., limited insurance policies, inadequate community support) directly generate or exacerbate dilemmas at the mezzo level (e.g., family caregiving burden, provider‑patient power asymmetry) and the micro level (e.g., loss of autonomy, identity crisis). Conversely, reciprocal arrows (feedback loops) show that micro‑level dilemmas—such as patients’ psychological distress, self‑withdrawal, or perceived loss of social role—can intensify mezzo‑level conflicts (e.g., communication imbalance, family alienation) and, through collective patient experiences, may indirectly influence macro‑level structures over time (e.g., policy advocacy, organizational change, or cultural shifts in stigmatization). This bidirectional interplay, depicted by arrows running both top‑down and bottom‑up across all three levels, demonstrates how ethical dilemmas propagate and reinforce each other, creating cycles that can either entrench or alleviate patient suffering.

Theme 1: Macro-system dilemmas (Socio-environmental Level)

Participants identified ethical dilemmas associated with policies, organizational structures, cultural norms, and community contexts, which collectively shape the ethical landscape navigated by hemodialysis patients.

Subtheme 1: Policy factors

Subcategory 1: Limited insurance and inadequate assistance policies

Interviews revealed that hemodialysis insurance policies deviated from health equity values. Variations in reimbursement rates across insurance types imposed unequal financial burdens on patients.This disparity created “same disease, different support” inequity. For unemployed patients, high out-of-pocket costs exposed inadequate assistance, placing them in a dilemma between treatment access and survival dignity. This disparity violates distributive justice, as patients with identical needs receive unequal support.

P5: “TCM head therapy and acupressure are quite effective for post-dialysis headaches, but neither treatment is covered by medical insurance.”

P18: “Patients with different insurance types receive different reimbursement rates, but we are all hemodialysis patients—this is extremely unfair!”

P34: “For those like me who have lost their jobs due to hemodialysis, there is a lack of appropriate government financial assistance.”

P15: “My biggest pressure is financial. The reimbursement rate for rural medical insurance is very low, and there is no additional assistance. Even affording food has become a problem.”

Subcategory 2: Conflict between occupational fairness and survival needs

Participants experienced a persistent tension between their right to equitable employment and their need for life-sustaining treatment. Existing occupational policies failed to accommodate essential dialysis, compelling patients to choose between “work” and “life”. This structural dilemma constituted an ethical challenge regarding social integration and full citizenship, that patients were forced to withdraw from or become marginalized in the workforce to survive, thereby perpetuating new inequities. This violates the ethical principle of distributive justice.

P2: “I used to work in catering, but was dismissed after starting hemodialysis because I couldn’t provide a health certificate.”

P23: “I kept asking for leave to attend regular hemodialysis, and eventually lost my job. What employer would accept an employee who constantly asks for time off?”

P29: “Although I still have a job, I was transferred to a less demanding position, and my income has decreased considerably.”

Subcategory 3: Regional and socioeconomic inequalities in dialysis provision

Hemodialysis patients faced dilemmas rooted in unequal access to healthcare resources. Dialysis allocation was markedly uneven across geographic and socioeconomic dimensions, challenging the need-based principle of medical justice. Rural patients endured long commutes and physical strain due to transportation barriers, while financial constraints led many to forgo necessary tests or medications—highlighting the compounded struggles of vulnerable populations.

P24: “I live in a rural area, so going to the city for hemodialysis is very inconvenient. The one-way trip takes two hours, and each session takes a whole day.”

P15: “For a financially struggling family like mine, we have to skip certain tests and avoid some medications whenever possible.”

P27: “I have to transfer buses three times to get to this hospital. I already have poor physical strength, and by the time I arrive, I am completely exhausted.”

Subtheme 2: Organizational factors

Subcategory 1: Management-induced safety dilemmas

Interviews revealed systemic management failures in hemodialysis units, including inadequate infection control, insufficient staffing, and equipment delays. These deficiencies compromised patient safety and dignity, exposing patients to infection risks and disrupting care continuity, thereby violating the ethical principles of non-maleficence and beneficence. Noise disturbances and high staff turnover further eroded patient dignity and respect, posing ethical challenges to patients’ right to life and physical and mental integrity.

P 23: “Family members freely enter the hemodialysis unit, talking and laughing loudly, which disturbs our rest and may introduce pathogens”.

P 27: “The nurses assigned to my care change frequently, so there is no consistent nurse who knows my condition well. Also, the machine failure during my dialysis caused blood to clot in the tubing, leading to waste.”

P16: “During lunch hours, only one doctor and one nurse are on duty, which is far from sufficient for so many hemodialysis patients.”

Subcategory 2: Lack of humanization in the medical environment

Hemodialysis patients experienced sustained harm from dehumanized clinical environments and procedures. Workflows, unit layouts, and amenities failed to accommodate their vulnerabilities, forcing undue physical strain, discomfort, and dignity loss. This violated patient-centered ethics and neglected patients’ rights to bodily integrity and freedom from suffering, causing secondary harm and conflicting with beneficence.

P16: “The distances from the consultation room to the payment counter and then to the pharmacy are very long, requiring several rests during the process.”

P25: “The dialysis unit is cramped, causing crowding during treatment initiation and termination. This offers no privacy and increases infection risk.”

P28: “For me, the biggest problem is that the hemodialysis beds are too short. At nearly two meters tall, my feet hang off the end of the bed.”

Subtheme 3: Cultural factors

Subcategory 1: Expectations of TCM culture versus therapeutic reality

Patients choosing adjunctive TCM based on cultural affinity faced disappointing realities—limited efficacy, unpleasant taste, or exacerbated complications—resulting in financial and physical-psychological burdens. This implicated informed consent and non-maleficence principles, revealing tensions between cultural expectations and medical standards.

P2: “I prepared herbal decoctions myself, which was very troublesome. I took them for three months; they were extremely bitter but ineffective.”

P31: “The decocted TCM formula was so bitter that I vomited immediately upon ingestion, especially problematic given that we are already prone to nausea.”

P3: “Hemodialysis causes pruritus, and TCM physiotherapy exacerbated these symptoms.”

P28: “I had high expectations for TCM, but its effect on improving renal function was minimal.”

Subcategory 2: Stigmatization and social exclusion in hemodialysis

Participants experienced severe stigmatization associated with both their underlying disease and its treatment. The illness itself was framed through catastrophic narrative, intensifying psychosocial distress. This stigma extended to dialysis patients through public misconceptions about transmission risks, engendering “contagion fears” that marginalized individuals from social participation. Such social prejudice exacerbated vulnerability, violating the principles of non-maleficence and justice.

P5: “It is often said that this disease is like a protracted cancer, with rumors that it will mentally and physically drain both patients and their families.”

P19: “During gatherings, some people dared not sit with me because they feared being infected.”

P26: “I have experienced discrimination due to hemodialysis. Former neighbors who used to play cards with me now avoid me when we meet.”

Subtheme 4: Community factors

Subcategory: Inadequate community support systems

Patients encountered support deficits at the community level, implicating the principle of justice. Gaps in community-based medical resources, accessible facilities, employment assistance, and psychosocial support forced already vulnerable patients to bear social isolation. This exacerbated their vulnerability and deprived them of social integration and developmental rights, constituting an invisible impairment of social personhood integrity.

P28: “There is no dialysis center within our community. We patients already have poor physical strength, making frequent hospital visits highly inconvenient.”

P33: “Our community offers no assistance whatsoever for patients like us, let alone psychological guidance or rehabilitation support.”

P30: “We hope to find suitable work close to home, but the community has not provided such opportunities. This leaves me without a sense of social belonging.”

Theme 2: Mezzo-system dilemmas (Interpersonal relationship level)

Ethical issues emerged from strained dynamics with social contacts, healthcare providers, family members, and other patients, creating relational challenges.

Subtheme 1: Healthcare provider-patient relationships

Subcategory 1: Relational fairness dilemmas

Patients perceived differential interpersonal treatment from healthcare providers, manifesting as neglect, preferential attention, or inconsistent responsiveness. Patients’ advocacy for individualized care and nurses’ preferential attention to certain individuals, driven by clinical judgment or emotional inclination, both raised fairness concerns among the patient group. These dynamics challenged the principle of justice, causing secondary harm to those feeling overlooked.

P1: “I must protect my blood vessels, so I request experienced nurses and avoid young, inexperienced ones.”

P12: “I prefer a fixed hemodialysis bed to get used to the layout, but nurses disagree.”

P32: “A patient without family often receives meals from doctors and nurses, making the rest of us feel overlooked.”

Subcategory 2: Competence-related trust dilemmas

Deficits in both patient-provider communication and clinical competency threatened patients’ safety and eroded trust in the patient-provider relationship. Lack of explanation after adverse events and insufficient notification before treatment changes violated informed consent. Unstable cannulation and perfunctory education breached non-maleficence and beneficence, causing distress and anxiety.

P2: “The doctor reduced the anticoagulant dose, causing clotting in the dialysis circuit. No explanation or apology was given afterward, making me anxious for every session.”

P34: “Some young nurses lack proficiency in cannulation, often requiring multiple attempts, which damages blood vessels.”

P33: “Health education sessions are too infrequent. Nurses conduct them once to fulfill a task, but we cannot remember the information.”

Subcategory 3: Communication imbalance and power asymmetry dilemmas

Participants encountered difficulties due to knowledge gaps, communication barriers, and provider attitudes. Patients with limited medical knowledge often forwent informed participation; they also suffered psychological distress from healthcare providers’ poor communication, such as jargon use, indifference, or impatience. This violated the principles of respect for autonomy and non-maleficence, reinforcing the power imbalance and leaving patients passive and disempowered.

P34: “Doctors often use medical jargon when explaining conditions. I don’t understand but am afraid to ask.”

P4: “Some doctors are indifferent. They pretend not to hear my questions and don’t look up when speaking to me.”

P16: “Some nurses always speak to us in a commanding tone, which makes me very uncomfortable.”

Subtheme 2: Friendship ties

Subcategory: Weakening and fragmentation of friendships

The social predicament of the patients was characterized by dual deprivation: passive exclusion and active withdrawal. Disease-related stigma and physical limitations led to their exclusion from existing social networks, while a diminished sense of self-worth prompted voluntary severance of social ties. This weakening and fragmentation of relationships infringed upon patients’ relational autonomy and belonging, and increased psychological vulnerability, contravening the principles of beneficence and non-maleficence.

P33: “Many friends no longer stay in touch with me because, to them, I no longer hold any social value.”

P2: “I used to be very socially active and enjoyed attending gatherings, but now, due to dietary restrictions, I no longer participate in such activities.”

Subtheme 3: Family relationships

Subcategory: Family alienation and caregiving burdens

Interviews revealed dilemmas arising from the erosion of family bonds among the patients and their caregivers. Patients faced abandonment or marital dissolution due to lost economic contribution and self-care ability, while family caregivers experienced exhaustion and emotional conflict. This reciprocal distress intensified patients’ sense of isolation, leaving them to endure not only physical suffering but also the dual burden of guilt over being a “family burden” and diminished dignity.

P5: “I lost my income and couldn’t even do housework; I needed my wife to take care of me, so she eventually divorced me.”

P34: “After years of caring for me, I could see my wife’s helplessness and resentment, but she had no other choice.”

P11: “Due to my illness, I often vomited, and even my children showed signs of disgust.”

Subtheme 4: Patient-patient relationships

Subcategory: Tensions in the hemodialysis unit and lapses in public ethics

In the shared dialysis space, ethical tensions arose from conflicting patient behaviors, needs, and emotions. Divergent expectations regarding conduct led to interpersonal conflicts and psychological distress, disrupting the therapeutic environment. Without adequate behavioral guidelines or emotional support, patients struggled to maintain dignity and mutual respect, undermining the principles of non-maleficence and harmonious coexistence.

P3: “In the dialysis room, some people cough and spit, and some even can’t control their bowels—it’s really hard to bear.”

P31: “Some patients argue with the nurses just because their hemodialysis starts a bit late, making the environment very noisy.”

P32: “The patient next to me is very pessimistic. I always have to listen to him repeat, ‘Life is meaningless’—it makes me feel terrible.”

Theme 3: Micro-system dilemmas (Individual level)

Ethical concerns stemmed from patients’ physiological and psychological distress, dilemmas regarding roles and responsibilities, and pressures tied to medical knowledge, creating individual challenges.

Subtheme 1: Physiological and psychological factors

Subcategory 1: Ethical rupture between life extension and life dignity

Patients on lifelong hemodialysis endured profound distress: fear of complications, guilt over family burden, meaninglessness, and suicidal ideation. The life-sustaining interventions conflicted with the patients’ psychological resilience and dignity, trapping them in “forced survival”. Disease progression also fostered their self-blame, reflecting the individualized ethical burden of health responsibility, which intensified their psychological suffering. Prolonging life at the cost of dignity is an irreconcilable conflict between beneficence and autonomy.

P25: “The itching makes it impossible to sleep more than two hours a day—I’ve become terrified of sleep itself.”

P4: “I often wish to die. At my age, I’m nothing but a burden to my children—I truly don’t want to live anymore.”

P7: “I resent myself—for staying up late, for reckless eating and drinking that ruined my health, led to kidney disease, and condemned me to a lifetime of dialysis.”

Subcategory 2: Self-identity crisis

Treatment-induced physical changes and diminished strength prompted patients to withdraw socially and conceal their condition. This withdrawal was not merely a response to physical limitation, but a strategy to protect a crumbling self-image. Unable to fulfill traditional roles, such as the family labor provider or an independent social being, patients experienced a profound rupture in their identity. They were trapped in the ethical dilemma of “being alive, yet unable to be oneself”. This violates the ethical principle of respect for human dignity.

P11: “The fistula in my left arm prevents me from lifting heavy objects. As a man, being unable to do physical work is deeply humiliating.”

P34: “I can’t stand for long due to physical exhaustion, so I dread running into acquaintances.”

P29: “I don’t want my friends to know I’m on hemodialysis, nor do I wish to share my feelings with my family. I want to forget that I’m a patient.”

Subtheme 2: Agency factors

Subcategory: Systemic deprivation of life autonomy

Interviews revealed that hemodialysis systematically deprives patients of life autonomy. From physiological needs to personal hobbies, patients were compelled to abandon these due to the rigid constraints of treatment, reducing life to a mechanical cycle centered around hemodialysis machines and hospital schedules. They must sustain life at the cost of sacrificing autonomy, confronting an alienated state of “survival for survival’s sake,” which led to a loss of meaning in life and existential distress.

P11: “I dare not drink water. No matter how hot it is in summer, I endure thirst.”

P33: “I used to love eating meat. Now, being told to eat less meat and more vegetables—I just can’t accept it.”

P1: “I used to enjoy fitness and exercise. Now I lack the strength to work out, and I worry that an elevated heart rate might trigger complications.”

P29: “I am tied to a regular hemodialysis schedule every week—otherwise, I wouldn’t survive. I feel completely controlled.”

Subtheme 3: Role and responsibility factors

Subcategory: Social role disintegration and loss of responsibility

Patients’ established familial and social roles were stripped by illness and hemodialysis, forcing a transition from active responsibility-bearers to passive dependents. This loss was twofold: they were no longer able to fulfill their expected duties toward others, while involuntarily becoming objects of care themselves. The reversal of responsibility, from giving care to receiving it, fundamentally altered their position within the family and society, generating profound feelings of guilt, uselessness, and moral failure, thereby undermining their dignity and sense of moral agency.

P19: “I’m fatigued all day and I’ve become a useless person.”

P28: “I used to carry my child on my shoulders for fun, but now, because of the fistula, I can’t even hold my child. I just can’t accept it.”

P2: “My father passed away from cancer six months ago. During his illness, I was unable to care for him due to my own health condition—this is a lifelong regret.”

P30: “Now I can’t take care of myself, nor can I care for my parents. Instead, I need them to take care of me.”

Subtheme 4: Medical knowledge factors

Subcategory: Technological dependence and knowledge gaps

Patients’ limited understanding of core treatments, particularly vascular access, created dual challenges in physical management and psychological safety. As a critical life-sustaining access point, the fistula’s complex care and inherent fragility alienated patients from their bodies, transforming this vital pathway into a source of daily anxiety and behavioral restriction. This alienation left them living under the constant threat that their ignorance might trigger a life-threatening crisis. This violates the ethical principles of informed consent and non-maleficence.

P25: “The central venous catheter pulsates so noticeably that I constantly worry it might burst.”

P31: “Every day, I have to be extremely careful with my fistula arm—avoid any bumps, avoid any pressure. It’s terribly inconvenient.”

P17: “Regarding my dialysis, I simply don’t know what to do—what medications to take, how to care for my fistula.”

Discussion

Grounded in Ecological Systems Theory, this multi-systemic model captures the layered ethical dilemmas encountered by hemodialysis patients. The model conceptualizes these dilemmas as products of a dynamic, interconnected system. It illustrates how macro-systemic factors create the foundational conditions for ethical challenges, which then become pronounced within the mezzo-system through interpersonal interactions and are ultimately internalized at the micro-system level by individuals. The core analytical power of this model lies in its focus on how ethical stress permeates across different levels, whereby dilemmas at one level invariably exacerbate and compound challenges at another.

Specifically, to map themes to the ethical dilemma definition, we systematically re-examined each theme against the operational definition adopted: an ethical dilemma requires a conflict between two or more moral claims (e.g., autonomy vs. beneficence, justice vs. utility) where no course of action can fully satisfy all competing principles. In this study, macro-system themes (e.g., limited insurance, management-induced safety dilemmas, TCM expectation-reality gaps, inadequate community support) all involve conflicts between distributive justice and utility, or between between cultural respect for patient choice and non-maleficence. Mezzo-system themes (e.g., relational fairness, friendship fragmentation, family alienation, patient-patient tensions) involve conflicts between autonomy and beneficence, or between justice and compassion. Micro-system themes (e.g., ethical rupture between life extension and dignity, systemic deprivation of autonomy, social role disintegration, technological dependence and knowledge gaps) involve conflicts between beneficence and respect for autonomy, or between the duty to preserve life and the principle of human dignity. Two micro-level themes might appear borderline at first glance: the knowledge gap reflects a conflict between informed consent and practical communication constraints, while social role disintegration embodies a tension between dignity and care dependency. However, both are genuine ethical dilemmas—not merely challenges of disease adaptation.

At the macro-level, our findings reveal that ethical dilemmas are often structurally embedded within policies, organizational designs, cultural norms, and community contexts—what we conceptualize as ‘system-induced ethical dilemmas’. Each of these dilemmas involves a conflict between fundamental ethical principles. For instance, the “same disease, different support” inequity arising from medical insurance reimbursement variations reflects a violation of the principle of distributive justice, where patients with identical clinical needs receive disparate treatment based on insurance type [11]. This is not merely a policy inefficiency but a structural ethical failure that forces patients to choose between treatment access and economic dignity—an irreconcilable choice. This builds upon prior research documenting financial catastrophe in hemodialysis patients [30] by pinpointing how policy-level structural inequalities undermine health equity. Similarly, the conflict between occupational fairness and survival needs represents not merely an administrative issue but a profound ethical dilemma. This dilemma is rooted in policies that fail to accommodate essential medical care. Regional and socioeconomic inequalities in hemodialysis provision further violate the need-based principle of medical justice, forcing rural and economically disadvantaged patients to endure disproportionate physical and financial burdens. These macro-level dilemmas establish systemic barriers that compromise patients’ fundamental rights to equitable care and social participation.

Critically, the TCM expectation-reality gap identified in this study constitutes a unique, culturally specific ethical dilemma underexplored in global dialysis ethics research [14]. Patient decisions to pursue adjunctive TCM are not fully autonomous, rationally informed clinical choices. Instead, they are socially and culturally conditioned, driven by ingrained societal beliefs in TCM’s holistic health regulation, implicit family therapeutic expectations [31], and widespread patient distrust of the purely symptomatic treatment logic of biomedicine. In the Chinese healthcare system, TCM possesses unique institutional legitimacy, cultural symbolism, and policy-endorsed status, creating persistent inter-authority tension within dialysis units between standardized, evidence-based biomedical dialysis protocols and culturally popular but dialysis-unvalidated TCM practices [11]. This dual-structure conflict creates a critical informed consent dilemma: patients adopt TCM based on cultural familiarity rather than comprehensive awareness of potential risks and uncertain efficacy for ESKD dialysis management, while clinicians commonly permit unregulated TCM use out of cultural respect rather than rigorous risk assessment. This substantially dilutes the authenticity of patient autonomous choice and violates standard informed consent norms, forming a unique contextual ethical conflict between cultural respect, patient autonomy, and clinical non-maleficence that distinguishes Chinese dialysis ethics from Western practice frameworks.

The manifestation of these ethical dilemmas was notably shaped by variations in participants’ backgrounds and regional socioeconomic contexts. Regarding insurance type, patients with URRBMI (37.1%) consistently reported greater financial distress and stronger perceptions of this “same disease, different support” inequity compared to those with UEBMI. This pattern aligns with previous study showing that rural insurance scheme patients face significantly higher out-of-pocket expenses compared to UEBMI patients, reflecting a structural injustice embedded in the insurance system itself [32]. In terms of geographic residency, rural patients endured long commutes and physical exhaustion, which not only exacerbated their micro-level loss of autonomy but also amplified mezzo-level caregiving tensions with family members.

More broadly, socioeconomic disparities across different regions—such as uneven dialysis facility distribution, varying local reimbursement implementation, and lack of community support in less developed areas—directly shaped the severity of the macro-level dilemmas described above. These disparities are consistent with some evidence that hemodialysis prevalence and healthcare resource allocation vary significantly by regional socioeconomic development [33]. Within the same hospital setting, variations in unit-level practices—such as frequent nurse turnover or lack of fixed dialysis beds—influenced the severity of competence-related trust dilemmas and relational fairness concerns. These observations confirm that ethical dilemmas are not uniform but are modulated by patients’ structural locations within the healthcare system and broader regional socioeconomics.

Serving as the key intermediary, the mezzo-system translates macro-level pressures into immediate interpersonal ethical tensions. Our model captures this dynamic, revealing how structural constraints become manifest as relational strains. At this level, ethical dilemmas manifest as conflicts between justice and compassion, autonomy and beneficence, or non-maleficence and respect for dignity. Competence-related trust dilemmas reflect not merely individual provider failings but are exacerbated by macro-level issues such as inadequate staffing policies [11]. This finding aligns with Talson’s work on provider-patient interactions [34] and extends it by conceptually linking communication breakdowns and mistrust to systemic organizational deficits. Communication imbalance and power asymmetry dilemmas further illustrate how macro-level constraints on clinical time and organizational priorities that devalue patient engagement translate into interpersonal dynamics that violate respect for patient autonomy and cause psychological distress. The weakening and fragmentation of friendships, family alienation, and tensions in the dialysis unit all represent the relational embodiment of a healthcare system that focuses narrowly on biological survival while neglecting the social dimensions of personhood.

At the micro-level, systemic and interpersonal conflicts become internalized, manifesting as profound personal ethical struggles. Our model reframes these individual-level dilemmas not as personal failings but as the embodied consequences of systemic failure. Each micro-level dilemma represents the internalization of macro- and mezzo-level ethical tensions into the patient’s sense of self, dignity, and moral agency. This is similar to Farmer’s concept of “structural violence”—that is, social arrangements embedded in the political and economic organisation of society that place specific individuals and groups at risk and cause injury [35]. In this study, insurance disparities, inadequate community support, and organizational safety dilemmas—all macro-level factors—operate as forms of structural violence that cause direct harm to patients’ physical, psychological, and social well-being. The ethical rupture between life extension and life dignity represents the ultimate personal toll of a system that sustains biological life while systematically eroding psychological well-being and autonomy. This finding resonates with Walker’s work on psychological distress in dialysis patients [36], but reframes such distress as the logical outcome of cumulative ethical stress from higher system levels. Self-identity crisis, systemic deprivation of life autonomy, social role disintegration, and technological dependence with knowledge gaps all represent how macro-level policy inadequacies and mezzo-level relational failures become inscribed on patients’ bodies and psyches. The transformation of patients from autonomous “bearers of responsibility” to passive “dependents” reflects how treatment, the very intervention sustaining life, simultaneously becomes the source of dignity loss and self-alienation [37]. Cluley’s post-human reading of hemodialysis [17] captures precisely this alienation: the fistula as both life-source and anxiety-source, an assemblage that patients cannot escape.

While previous studies on dialysis ethics have largely treated ethical challenges as discrete issues [29, 36, 37], our multi‑systemic model extends this literature by demonstrating how dilemmas co‑constitute each other across macro‑, mezzo‑, and micro‑levels. Unlike prior work that has mostly focused on individual‑level ethical distress [36] or isolated relational tensions [38], our framework reveals bidirectional ethical stress pathways (bottom‑up and top‑down), a dynamic underexplored in existing ecosystem‑based models of dialysis ethics [39]. Extending the moral injury literature beyond clinicians [40], our findings suggest that patients experience an analogous form of moral suffering—persistent violation of autonomy, dignity, and social belonging through systemic constraints beyond their control.

Consequently, recommendations from our model focus on the cross‑level pathways identified. Given the exploratory scope of this single‑site qualitative study, the following suggestions are presented as tentative directions rather than definitive prescriptions. At the macro‑level, policy reforms must extend beyond cost‑containment to build ethical infrastructure—targeting insurance disparities (P18, P34), occupational conflicts (P2, P23), and rural transportation barriers (P15, P27). Organizational interventions should address staffing policies that ensure adequate training and supervision, as reflected in P27’s account of frequent nurse turnover and P16’s report of understaffing. At the mezzo‑level, interventions should promote ethical culture‑building, including team‑based care and support for family caregivers—exemplified by P34’s narrative of spousal resentment and P5’s experience of marital dissolution. At the micro‑level, psychological support and health literacy are valuable [41, 42] (e.g., P4’s suicidal ideation, P17’s knowledge gaps about fistula care), but only as buffers against systemic limitations rather than individual solutions. Only by addressing macro‑level injustices can the micro‑level suffering documented here be meaningfully alleviated.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the sample’s lack of diversity, as all participants were recruited exclusively from TCM hospitals within a single province in China. Second, while this study provides an in-depth exploration of the patient perspective, its singular focus means that the findings are not yet integrated with the perspectives of nurses and physicians. Third, the transferability of the results may be influenced by the distinct socio-cultural and institutional context of the Chinese healthcare system, which differs from those in other countries. Fourth, the professional backgrounds of the research team in nephrology may have shaped the interpretation of data, a potential bias that, despite rigorous reflexivity practices, cannot be entirely dismissed. Finally, our sample had a male predominance (80.0%) that exceeds the typical gender distribution in Chinese hemodialysis populations (approximately 55–61% male) [43]. This imbalance may limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly regarding gender-specific ethical dilemmas.

Conclusions

This study advances the understanding of ethical challenges in hemodialysis by proposing a multi-systemic ethical dilemma model grounded in the lived experiences of patients. Moving beyond prior research that has largely cataloged ethical issues in isolation, our framework provides theoretical coherence by illustrating how ethical dilemmas permeate the macro-, mezzo-, and micro-levels of the care ecosystem. In doing so, the model’s key contribution lies in explicating the interconnected pathways through which societal factors, institutional policies, and interpersonal dynamics converge to shape patients’ ethical worlds, offering a novel lens for interpreting the complexity of their moral experiences.

A central implication of this model is its reframing of ethical dilemmas not as isolated incidents, but as embedded phenomena requiring systemic solutions. The findings underscore that interventions targeting only individual patient coping mechanisms are unlikely to suffice without concurrent reforms in healthcare structures and policy frameworks. Consequently, we advocate for future scholarly inquiry, clinical practice guidelines, and administrative strategies to embrace this holistic viewpoint. By adopting such an integrated approach, healthcare stakeholders can move beyond merely identifying recurrent ethical tensions toward developing comprehensive, multi-tiered interventions that address root causes, ultimately fostering a more ethically supportive environment for patients navigating long-term hemodialysis.
