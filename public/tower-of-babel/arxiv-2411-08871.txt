Restriction estimates using decoupling theorems and two-ends Furstenberg inequalities
Hong Wang, Shukun Wu

Abstract
. We propose to study the restriction conjecture using decoupling theorems and two-ends Furstenberg inequalities. Specifically, we pose a two-ends Furstenberg conjecture, which implies the restriction conjecture. As evidence, we prove this conjecture in the plane by using the Furstenberg set estimate. Moreover, we use this planar result to prove a restriction estimate for p > 22 / 7 p>22/7 in three dimensions, which implies Wolff’s 5 / 2 5/2 -hairbrush bound for Kakeya sets in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . Our approach also makes improvements for the restriction conjecture in higher dimensions.



In this paper, we propose to study the Fourier restriction conjecture via decoupling theorems and two-ends Furstenberg inequalities. For a
C^{2}
hypersurface
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, the Fourier extension operator
E_{S}
is defined as

(0.1)

E_{S}f(x):=\int_{S}e^{ix\cdot\xi}f(\xi)d\sigma_{S}(\xi).

Here
f:S\to\mathbb{C}
and
\sigma_{S}
is the surface measure on
S
.

Regarding the extension operator, Stein
[
Ste79
]
made the following conjecture.

Conjecture 0.1
.

Suppose that
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a compact
C^{2}
hypersurface (maybe with boundary) with a strictly positive second fundamental form.
Then when
p>\frac{2n}{n-1}
,

(0.2)

\|E_{S}f\|_{p}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{L^{p}(d\sigma_{S})}.

Our main theorem is the following:

Theorem 0.2
.

Conjecture
0.1
is true when
n=3
and
p>22/7
.

Our approach can also make progress for the restriction conjecture in higher dimensions.
See Theorem
0.17
in Section
0.6
.

0.1.
Overview

Fefferman
[
Fef70
]
solved Conjecture
0.1
in the plane and provided partial results in higher dimensions.
After Fefferman, Tomas
[
Tom75
]
showed that (
0.2
) holds for
p>\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
by a
TT^{\ast}
method.
In fact, Tomas proved the
L^{2}
estimate
\|E_{S}f\|_{p}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{2}
for
p>\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
, which is a quite complete result since this estimate fails if
p<\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
.
The endpoint case
p=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
was later settled by Stein, so this
(L^{2},L^{\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}})
estimate is known as the Stein-Tomas estimate.

The modern era of the restriction conjecture started in 1991, when Bourgain published his first article
[
Bou91
]
in this field.
His idea is to study
E_{S}f
by decomposing it into wave packets: For a large
R
, we first decompose the function
f
as
f=\sum_{\theta}f{\bf 1}_{\theta}
.
Each
\theta\subset S
is a cap of radius
R^{-1/2}
, and
\{\theta\}
forms a covering of
S
.
An important observation is that
|E_{S}f_{\theta}|
is essentially constant in any tube
T
of dimensions
R^{1/2}\times\cdots\times R^{1/2}\times R
, which we call an
R
-tube, pointing in direction
\theta
.
Given a
\theta
, let
\mathbb{T}_{\theta}=\{T\}
be a family of
R
-tubes pointing in direction
\theta
that forms a finite-overlapping covering of
B^{n}(0,R)
.
We then break each function
f_{\theta}=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}}f_{T}
for some functions
\{f_{T}\}
so that inside
B^{n}(0,R)
,
E_{S}f_{T}
is essentially supported on
T
, and
|E_{S}f_{T}|
is essentially constant on
T
as well.
Each
f_{T}
is called a “wave packet”, and this gives us the “wave packet decomposition” of
f
:
f=\sum_{\mathbb{T}}f_{T}
with
\mathbb{T}=\bigcup_{\theta}\mathbb{T}_{\theta}
.

For each wave packet
f_{T}
,
E_{S}f_{T}
has two key attributes: its oscillation, which maintains roughly the same amplitude on
T
; its support, which is
T
essentially.
Thus, armed with the wave packet decomposition, the study of the function
E_{S}f
can be divided into two main parts:

(1)

For each
x
, study the oscillation among all
\{E_{S}f_{T}(x):x\in T\}
.

(2)

For a given collection of
R
-tubes
\mathbb{T}
, study the geometric interference among
T\in\mathbb{T}
, which is commonly referred to as Kakeya-type problems.

In Bourgain’s original work
[
Bou91
]
, the interference between different wave packets is studied locally via the Stein-Tomas estimate, and the global geometric interaction among the thin tubes of wave packets is studied by using Kakeya-type inequalities.

An important milestone towards the restriction conjecture was the work of Wolff
[
Wol01
]
and Tao
[
Tao03
]
, where a multi-scale method known as “induction on scales” was initiated.
Broadly speaking, this method provides the following dichotomy:
Either we obtain the desired result by induction, or we can observe extra geometric information regarding the thin tubes associated to wave packets.

In 2014, there were two breakthroughs in the field of restriction theory: the application of polynomial partitioning to Fourier analysis by Guth
[
Gut16
]
, and the proof of the
\ell^{2}
-decoupling theorem by Bourgain and Demeter
[
BD15
]
.
The polynomial method is an algebraic tool that is powerful in studying Kakeya-type inequalities, whereas the decoupling theorems are powerful in studying interference between wave packets.

In this paper, the tools to study oscillation are the method of induction on scales and decoupling theorems, and the tools to study geometric interference between wave packets (incidence problems between balls and tubes) are two-ends Furstenberg inequalities.

0.2.
Tools for oscillation.

The decoupling phenomenon was first observed by Wolff
[
Wol00
]
.
It culminated in Bourgain-Demeter’s resolution of the
\ell^{2}
-decoupling theorem
[
BD15
]
.
What we are using here is an influential refinement known as the “refined decoupling theorem”.
This result (stated below) was presented in
[
GIOW20
]
and observed independently by Du-Zhang.

Theorem 0.3
.

Let
E_{S}
be the extension operator for a strictly convex
C^{2}
hypersurface
S
with a Gaussian curvature
\sim 1
, and let
p=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
. Suppose
f
is a sum of wave packets
f=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}f_{T}
so that
\|E_{S}f_{T}\|_{L^{p}(w_{B_{R}})}^{2}
are the same up to a constant multiple for all
T\in\mathbb{T}
.
Let
X
be a union of
R^{1/2}
-balls in
B_{R}
such that each
R^{1/2}
-ball
Q\subset X
intersects to at most
M
tubes
\mathbb{T}
.
Then

(0.3)

\|E_{S}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}^{p}\lessapprox M^{\frac{2}{n-1}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}\|E_{S}f_{T}\|_{L^{p}(w_{B_{R}})}^{p}.

Here
w_{B_{R}}
is a weight that is
\sim 1
on
B_{R}
and decreases rapidly outside
B_{R}
.

If each
\mathbb{T}_{\theta}
contains
\sim m
many
R
-tubes, then we have
\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}}\|E_{S}f_{T}\|_{L^{p}(w_{B_{R}})}^{p}\sim(mR^{\frac{n+1}{2}})^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\big(\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}}\|E_{S}f_{T}\|_{L^{2}(w_{B_{R}})}^{2}\big)^{\frac{p}{2}}
. This is because each
E_{S}f_{T}
is essentially constant on
T
and because
|T|\sim R^{\frac{n+1}{2}}
.
Note that each
f_{\theta}
is supported in a cap
\theta
with measure
\sim R^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}
.
By Plancherel and Hölder’s inequality, we get
\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}}\|E_{S}f_{T}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim R\|f_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim R(R^{-\frac{n-1}{2}})^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\|f_{\theta}\|_{p}^{2}
.
These calculations show that when
p=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
,

(0.4)

\|E_{S}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}^{p}\lessapprox(Mm^{-1})^{\frac{2}{n-1}}R^{-1}\|f\|_{p}^{p}.

Therefore, the refined decoupling theorem suggests that one can control
\|E_{S}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}
by the multiplicity factor
M
and
m
.
Estimating these factors is indeed a Kakeya-type problem, that is, a tube-ball incidence problem.

To set up this problem, we first make the following simple but crucial observation: Suppose that each
R
-tube
T\in\mathbb{T}
intersects
\sim\lambda R^{1/2}
many
R^{1/2}
-balls in
X
, then via
L^{2}
-orthogonality on each
R^{1/2}
-balls in
X
,

(0.5)

\|E_{S}f\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lesssim\lambda R\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

In other words, the smaller the
\lambda
, the better estimate we have in the
L^{2}
-space.

Now we set up the incidence problem as follows:

Problem 0.4
.

Let
M(Q)=\#\{T\in\mathbb{T}:T\cap Q\not=\varnothing\}
be the multiplicity on an
R^{1/2}
-ball
Q\subset X
.
Suppose that each
\mathbb{T}_{\theta}
contains
\sim m
many
R
-tubes, and for each
R
-tube
T\in\mathbb{T}
, the shading
Y(T)=T\cap X
contains
\sim\lambda R^{1/2}
many
R^{1/2}-
balls.
We want an upper bound for the multiplicity factor
M(Q)
for a generic
Q\subset X
using the information of
\lambda
and
m
.

One strong tool to study this incidence problem is the Kakeya maximal inequality.
Specifically, the Kakeya maximal conjecture asserts that for a generic
Q\subset X
, the multiplicity factor
M(Q)
can be bounded by
m
and the density
\lambda
as

(0.6)

M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{1-n}.

The loss of
m
in (
0.6
) agrees with the gain in (
0.4
) perfectly.
However, even with the full strength of the Kakeya maximal inequality, the loss of
\lambda
in (
0.6
) is too big, and cannot be compensated by the gain in (
0.5
).

The tool that allows us to improve upon (
0.6
) is the method of induction on scales.
In our setting, roughly speaking, the induction on scales provides the following dichotomy:
Either we prove the desired restriction estimate by induction, or the shading
Y(T)
is distributed on both ends of the tube
T
.
With this extra two-ends spacing information on the shading
Y
, the exponent on
\lambda
in (
0.6
) is expected to improve significantly.
Similar observations were also made in
[
Wol95
,
Wol98
,
KT02
]
.

For example, in Bourgain’s work
[
Bou91
]
, he proved a Kakeya maximal inequality
M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-4/3}R^{1/3}
when
n=3
.
With little effort, this bound can be improved to
M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-2/3}R^{1/3}
if there is a two-ends assumption on the shading
Y
.
Plugging the improved bound back to (
0.4
) with
n=3
, we end up with

(0.7)

\|E_{S}f\|_{L^{4}(X)}^{4}\lessapprox(\lambda R)^{-2/3}\|f\|_{4}^{4}.

Interpolate this estimate with the
L^{2}
estimate (
0.5
), we have
\|E_{S}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}^{p}\lessapprox\|f\|_{p}^{p}
when
p=3.2
.
Via a dyadic pigeonholing argument and a global-to-local reduction (known as an
\epsilon
-removal argument), this implies
\|E_{S}f\|_{p}^{p}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{p}^{p}
when
p>3.2
.
In other words, we prove Conjecture
0.1
when
n=3
and
p>3.2
.

Remark 0.5
.

Combining Wolff’s hairbrush structure with Bourgain’s bush structure, one can improve
M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-2/3}R^{1/3}
to
M(Q)\lessapprox m\cdot\min\{\lambda^{-1}R^{1/4},R^{1/2}\}
through a simple application of the two-ends assumption.
This bound still yields
p>3.2
for Conjecture
0.1
when
n=3
.

0.3.
Tools for incidence

The calculation in the last subsection gives a strong restriction inequality (it covers all prior findings in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
), and, surprisingly, the tool for the incidence problem is the “bush” structure discovered by Bourgain back in 1991.
It is therefore intriguing to explore the strongest incidence estimates under a two-ends assumption.

Let us first take a look at the lowest dimension
n=2
. A powerful incidence result known as the Furstenberg set estimate was obtained recently in
[
OS23
,
RW23
]
.
Before stating this result, we quickly go through some necessary notations.
Suppose
\delta\in(0,1)
is a small number.
For a set
E\subset[0,1]^{n}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(E)
be the smallest family of
\delta
-balls that forms a covering of
E
.
Given an
s\in(0,n]
, the set
E
is called a
(\delta,s)
-set if
\#\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(E\cap B)\lesssim r^{s}\#\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(E)
for all
r\in[\delta,1]
and all
r
-balls
B\subset[0,1]
.
Given a family
{\mathcal{T}}
of non-horizontal
\delta\times 1
-tubes in the unit ball, by the point-line duality (Definition
1.13
), we can identify
{\mathcal{T}}
as a family
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
of
\delta
-balls inside the unit ball.
We say
{\mathcal{T}}
is a
(\delta,s)
-set if
\cup_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}}
is a
(\delta,s)
-set.

Now we state the Furstenberg set estimate.

Theorem 0.6

(
[
RW23
]
)
.

Let
t\in(0,2]
,
s\in(0,1]
, and
\lambda\in[\delta,1]
.
Suppose that
{\mathcal{T}}
is
(\delta,t)
-set of
\delta\times 1
-tubes in the unit ball, and for each
T\in{\mathcal{T}}
, there is a shading
Y(T)\subset T
so that
Y(T)
is a
(\delta,s)
-set and
|Y(T)|\sim\lambda|T|
.
Then

(0.8)

\big|\bigcup_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}}Y(T)\big|\gtrapprox\lambda\delta\cdot\delta^{-\min\{t,\frac{s+t}{2},1\}}.

Note that when
\#{\mathcal{T}}\lesssim\delta^{-t}
,
t=1
, and
\lambda\sim\delta^{1-s}
, Theorem
0.6
implies that a generic
\delta
-ball contained in
\bigcup_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}}Y(T)
intersects
\lessapprox\delta^{-\frac{1-s}{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{-1/2}
tubes in
{\mathcal{T}}
.

Let us see how this theorem and the calculation can help Problem
0.4
when
n=2
.
Assume that

(1)

For all
T\in\mathbb{T}
, the
R^{-1}
-dilate of
Y(T)
is an
(R^{-1/2},s)
-set with
\lambda=R^{\frac{s-1}{2}}
;

(2)

\mathbb{T}_{\theta}
is not empty for
\sim R^{1/2}
caps
\theta
.

Then, by the triangle inequality, the calculation above shows that for a generic
Q\subset X
, the multiplicity factor
M(Q)
can be bounded above by
m\lambda^{-1/2}
.
Plug this back to (
0.4
) with
n=2
so that

(0.9)

\|E_{S}f\|_{L^{6}(X)}^{6}\lessapprox(\lambda R)^{-1}\|f\|_{6}^{6}.

An interpolation with the
L^{2}
estimate (
0.5
) shows
\|E_{S}f\|_{L^{4}(X)}^{4}\lessapprox\|f\|_{4}^{4}
when
n=2
.
That is, we prove the restriction conjecture in the plane using decoupling and the Furstenberg set estimate, however, with two additional assumptions.

The real challenge is the first assumption “the
R^{-1}
-dilate of
Y(T)
is an
(R^{-1/2},s)
-set with
\lambda=R^{\frac{s-1}{2}}
”.
Being a
(R^{-1/2},s)
-set means that the shading
Y(T)
possesses a strong spacing condition.
In particular, it suggests that the
R^{-1}
-dilate of
Y(T)
behaves like an
s
-dimensional set.
However, in Problem
0.4
, the spacing condition on
Y(T)
is merely two-ends, which, roughly speaking, only gives “the
R^{-1}
-dilate of
Y(T)
is an
(R^{-1/2},s)
-set with some
s
obeying
R^{-s}\approx 1
”.
This statement is much weaker than the first assumption, as generally
\lambda
is much smaller than
1
, and it can be as small as
R^{-1/2}
.

In
[
DW24
, Corollary 1.5]
, the
s
-dimensional spacing assumption on
Y(T)
was removed and replaced by a product spacing assumption on
{\mathcal{T}}
.
The main result in
[
DW24
]
does not quite give what we need here, but the arguments and proof scheme inspire us to make the following crucial and perhaps surprising observation:

With merely the two-ends spacing condition, we can obtain
M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-1/2}
for Problem
0.4
in the plane.

Theorem 0.7

(c.f. Theorem
2.1
)
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{T}
is a collection of
R^{1/2}\times R
-tubes with
\#\mathbb{T}_{\theta}\leq m
.
Suppose also that there exists a
\lambda\in[R^{-1/2},1]
such that
|Y(T)|\sim\lambda|T|
, and
Y(T)
is two-ends for each
T\in\mathbb{T}
.
Then

(0.10)

\big|\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}Y(T)\big|\gtrapprox\lambda^{3/2}(R^{3/2}m^{-1}\#\mathbb{T}).

In particular, for a typical
Q\subset\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}Y(T)
,
M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-1/2}
.

The bound
M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-1/2}
matches what one can get from the Furstenberg set estimate.
Especially, it shows that we indeed can prove the restriction conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
using decoupling and (
0.10
), which we call a two-ends Furstenberg inequality.

0.4.
Two-ends Furstenberg inequality: consequence and conjecture

By using Wolff’s hairbrush structure and the two-ends Furstenberg inequality in the plane, we prove
M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-3/4}R^{1/4}
for Problem
0.4
when
n=3
(c.f. Lemma
3.1
). Consequently, we get Theorem
0.2
.

When
n=3
, the exponent
p>22/7
corresponds to the exponent
5/2
for the Kakeya conjecture.
To compare, the exponent
p>3.2
corresponds to the exponent
7/3
for the Kakeya conjecture, which was proved by Bourgain
[
Bou91
]
in 1991. We will discuss the numerologies in Section
6
.

On the other hand, the exponent
5/2
is almost the best-known result for the Kakeya conjecture.
It was obtained by Wolff
[
Wol95
]
in 1995, and improved to
5/2+\epsilon_{0}
in 2017 by Katz-Zahl
[
KZ19
]
(see also
[
Wu24
]
).

Let us return to general dimensions.
As mentioned at the beginning of the previous subsection, we would like to find out the strongest two-ends Furstenberg inequality.
Given that the two-ends Furstenberg inequality and the Furstenberg set estimate have the same numerology for the restriction problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we first explore examples and evidence for the higher-dimensional Furstenberg set estimate.

In
[
GSW19
]
, the authors studied the Furstenberg set estimate under a “well-spaced” assumption in all dimensions. We refer to their paper for the precise definition of well-spaced tubes.

Theorem 0.8

(
[
GSW19
]
Theorem 4.1)
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{T}}
is a collection of “well-spaced”
\delta
-tubes with length 1 and cross-section radius
\delta
.
Then for
n=2
and
3
,

(0.11)

\#\mathcal{P}_{r}({\mathcal{T}})\lessapprox\frac{(\#{\mathcal{T}})^{\frac{n}{n-1}}}{r^{\frac{n+1}{n-1}}},

where
\mathcal{P}_{r}({\mathcal{T}}):=\{B\subset\cup_{\mathcal{T}}:B\cap T\not=\varnothing\text{ for $\geq r$ tubes $T\in{\mathcal{T}}$}\}
denotes the set of
r
-rich
\delta
-balls of
{\mathcal{T}}
.

In our notation, Theorem
0.8
shows that if
\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}Y(T)
is a union of well spaced
R^{1/2}
-balls, then when
n=2
and
3
,

(0.12)

\big|\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}Y(T)\big|\gtrapprox\lambda^{\frac{n+1}{2}}(R^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\#T).

In particular, we have

(0.13)

\#M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}

for Problem
0.4
under the well-spaced assumption on balls when
n=2
and
3
.

Notice that (
0.13
) is stronger than (
0.6
), which is predicted by the Kakeya maximal inequality.
Moreover, the following discrete example shows that (
0.13
) is likely the best possible bound:

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
n
-dimensional lattice points
[0,N]\times[0,k_{1}N]\times\cdots[0,k_{n-1}N]
for some
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n-1}\geq 1
. Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be the set of lines
\ell_{a,b}:(a,0)+\mathbb{R}(1,b):a\in[1,k_{1}N]\times\cdots\times[1,k_{n-1}N]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n-1},b\in[1,k_{1}]\times\cdots\times[1,k_{n-1}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
.
Then
\#\mathcal{P}\sim N^{n}\prod k_{j}
,
\#{\mathcal{L}}=N^{n-1}\prod k_{j}^{2}
. Moreover, the incidence
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{P},{\mathcal{L}})=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P},\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}}\chi(p,\ell)\sim N^{n}\prod k_{j}^{2}
.
Therefore, we have

(0.14)

\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{P},{\mathcal{L}})\sim(\#\mathcal{P})^{\frac{2}{n+1}}(\#{\mathcal{L}})^{\frac{n}{n+1}},

which matches the numerology in (
0.11
) and hence in (
0.13
).

In the absence of unanticipated phenomena, we might hazard a guess on the best possible estimate for the two-ends Furstenberg inequality.
Here we use the notations in Definitions
1.15
,
1.16
,
1.17
, and
1.21
.

Conjecture 0.9
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Let
(L,Y)_{\delta}
be a set of directional
\delta
-separated lines in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with an
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
two-ends,
\lambda
-dense shading.
Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(0.15)

|E_{L}|\geq c_{\varepsilon}\delta^{\varepsilon}\delta^{O(\varepsilon_{1})}\lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\sum_{\ell\in L}|Y(\ell)|.

Apply an
R
-dilate version of Conjecture
0.9
to Problem
0.4
to have
M(Q)\lessapprox m\lambda^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}
.
Thus, plugging this back to (
0.4
) and interpolating with (
0.5
), we get

Theorem 0.10
.

Conjecture
0.9
implies Conjecture
0.1
.

Remark 0.11
.

There might be an analog of Conjecture
0.9
if directional separation is replaced by a more general assumption.
See Remark
2.2
.
We do not pursue it here due to the lack of knowledge in higher dimensions.

0.5.
Analogues of Furstenberg sets conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Conjecture
0.9
is the higher dimensional version of Theorem
2.1
.
Here we include higher dimensional versions of the Furstenberg sets conjecture, which might serve as a (difficult) intermediate step towards Conjecture
0.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a set of distinct
\delta
-tubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set, define

(0.16)

{\mathcal{T}}[U]:=\{T\in{\mathcal{T}}:T\subset U\}.

Definition 0.12
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
t\in(0,2)
.
We say that a family of
\delta
-tubes
{\mathcal{T}}
satisfies the
t
-Frostman Convex Wolff axiom with error
C
if for any convex set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(0.17)

Let

Definition 1.1

Definition 1.5

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
0<\delta<\rho<1
.
Suppose

is a Katz-Tao
(\delta,s,C)
-set, where

.
Then there exists

\rho^{s}\#E^{\prime}\gtrapprox\delta^{s}\#E

Let

Then with high probability,

(1.5)

\Big\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\#(E_{1}\cap B(x,r))\leq C_{1}(r/\rho)^{s},&\hskip 8.5359pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,r\in[\rho|\log\delta|^{2},1],\\[4.30554pt]

Let

be a maximal

By the second line of (

Let

Let

Lemma 1.8

(Uniformization)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a small number.
If

is a union of dyadic
\delta
-balls, then there is a
\gtrsim(\log|\log\delta|)^{-\frac{|\log\delta|}{\log|\log\delta|}}
-refinement

of

such that

is uniform.

This lemma is standard, which follows from dyadic pigeonholing on

at each scale

It shows that for an arbitrary set, we can always find a refinement that is also uniform.
The next definition serves as a preliminary tool to study a uniform set.

Definition 1.9

(Branching function)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
M=|\log\delta|
.
Let
\rho_{j}=M^{-j},\,j=1,\dots,\lceil\log_{M}\delta^{-1}\rceil=:N
.
Let

be a union of
\delta
-balls that is uniform.
Define a

\beta_{E}:[0,N]\to[0,n]
as

(1.6)

\beta_{E}(j)=\frac{\log(|E|_{\rho_{j}})}{|\log\delta|},\hskip 8.5359pt0\leq j\leq N

and interpolate linearly between
\beta_{E}(j)
and
\beta_{E}(j+1)
.

Note that the branching function
\beta_{E}
characterizes the distribution of
\delta
-balls in
E
.

Lemma 1.10
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
,
M=|\log\delta|
, and
N=\lceil\log_{M}\delta^{-1}\rceil
.
Suppose
\mathcal{E}
is a finite family of uniform sets of
\delta
-balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then there is subset
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{E}
with
\#\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\gtrsim(n\log M)^{-N}\#\mathcal{E}

and a
uniform branching function
\beta_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}
of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
such that for any

,
|\beta_{E}-\beta_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}|\leq|\log\delta|^{-1}
.
In particular,
\#\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\gtrapprox\#\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

For any branching function
\beta:[0,N]\rightarrow[0,n]
, define an “
\varepsilon
-ball”
D(\beta,\varepsilon)=\{\beta^{\prime}:[0,N]\rightarrow[0,n]
,
|\beta(j)-\beta^{\prime}(j)|\leq\varepsilon\text{ for all }0\leq j\leq N\}
.

Cover
\{\beta_{E}\}_{E\in\mathcal{E}}
by

. Since
\beta_{E}(j+1)-\beta_{E}(j)\in[0,n\frac{\log M}{|\log\delta|}]
for all
0\leq j\leq N
and since
\beta_{E}(0)=0
, by pigeonholing, there exists

such that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}:=\{E^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}:\beta_{E^{\prime}}\in D(\beta_{E},\varepsilon)\}
has cardinality
\#\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\geq(n\frac{\log M}{|\log\delta|}\varepsilon^{-1})^{-N}\#\mathcal{E}
. Take
\varepsilon=|\log\delta|^{-1}
.
∎

After rescaling, the branching function of a uniform set becomes a
1
-Lipschitz function.
What follows is a powerful tool to analyze a

-Lipschitz function.
It was first introduced by Shmerkin

]

The precise statement below can be found in

Lemma 1.11
.

Let
\eta>0
be a small number and let
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}(\eta)=\eta^{2\eta^{-1}}

\beta:[0,1]\to[0,1]

(1.7)

0=A_{1}<A_{2}<\cdots<A_{H+1}=1

and a sequence

(1.8)

0\leq s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots<s_{H}\leq 1

such that for each

(1.9)

A_{h+1}-A_{h}\geq\eta_{0}\eta^{-1};

(1.10)

\displaystyle f(x)\geq

\displaystyle f(A_{h})+s_{l}(x-A_{h})-\eta(A_{h+1}-A_{h}),\text{ for all }x\in[A_{h},A_{h+1}],

(1.11)

\displaystyle f(A_{h+1})\leq f(A_{h})+(s_{h}+3\eta)(A_{h+1}-A_{h});

(1.12)

Suppose

\delta
-balls that is uniform.

Proposition 1.12

(Multi-scale decomposition)

Let
\eta>0

\eta_{0}(\eta)=\eta^{2\eta^{-1}}

Suppose

(1.13)

and a sequence

A_{h+1}-A_{h}\geq\eta_{0}\eta^{-1}

(2)

\log_{1/\delta}\big(\frac{|E|_{\delta^{A_{h+1}}}}{|E|_{\delta^{A_{h}}}}\big)\leq(s_{h}+4\eta)(A_{h+1}-A_{h})

(3)

For each

-ball

-dilate of the set

Definition 1.19
.

Let

Let
\ell
be a line, and let

\delta

Definition 1.20
.

Let

.

Lemma 1.22

Lemma 1.26
.

Lemma 1.27
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
, and let
(L,Y)_{\delta}
be a set of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and shading.
For all

, there exists a

(1)

(2)

There are two disjoint subsets

of lines such that

, and
\rho\geq\measuredangle(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\gtrapprox\rho
for all
\ell_{1}\in L_{1},\ell_{2}\in L_{2}
.

Proof.

Let

.
Let

with
\rho_{n}\approx\delta
.
Then

.
Consider the following algorithm, starting at the scale
\rho_{0}
:

\rho_{j}
, there is a set of lines

with

such that the directions of the lines in

is contained in a

For each

Otherwise, the algorithm stops.
In this case, note that there are

2.
A two-ends Furstenberg inequality in the plane

The main goal of this section is to establish the two-ends Furstenberg inequality.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Let
(L,Y)_{\delta}
be a set of
1
-parallel,
\delta
-separated lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with an
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-two-ends,
\lambda

(2.1)

|E_{L}|\geq c_{\varepsilon}\delta^{\varepsilon}\delta^{\varepsilon_{1}/2}\lambda^{1/2}\sum_{\ell\in L}|Y(\ell)|.

Remark 2.2
.

If a two-ends assumption is added to
[
DW24
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
DW24
, Proposition 4.2]
, the same proof might work for a larger range

The assumption

However, the condition

To ensure that the proof in

The proof of Theorem

The wave packet decomposition satisfies the following properties.

With the wave packet decomposition, we state the refined decoupling theorem.

Theorem 4.4

(
[

]

.

be the extension operator (

\|Ef\|_{L^{p}(X)}^{p}\lessapprox R^{\varepsilon_{0}}M^{\frac{2}{n-1}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}\|Ef_{T}\|_{L^{p}(w_{B_{R}})}^{p}.

What follows is an

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

be a union of

-balls, and let

Suppose for each

\int_{X}\big|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}Ef_{T}{\bf 1}_{Y(T)}\big|^{2}\lesssim(\lambda R)\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

For each

-ball

, let

Then by

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\big|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}Ef_{T}{\bf 1}_{Y(T)}\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{2Q}\big|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}(Q)}Ef_{T}\big|^{2}+R^{-C}\|f\|_{2}^{2}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}(Q)}\big|Ef_{T}\,\phi_{2Q}\big|^{2}+R^{-C}\|f\|_{2}^{2}

(4.9)
