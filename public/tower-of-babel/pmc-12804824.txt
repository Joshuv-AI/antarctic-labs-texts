Subtypes of cocaine use disorder and their neurobehavioral profiles
Authors: Leyla R. Brucar, Gunner Drossel, Eduardo A. Garza-Villarreal, Anna Zilverstand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cocaine Use Disorder (CUD) afflicts over 1.4 million individuals in the United States alone. However, current treatments are largely ineffective, with up to 85% returning to use long-term. We posit that addressing functional heterogeneity underlying CUD is crucial for improving treatment success. Based on prior work, we hypothesized heterogeneity in function across three domains: approach-related behavior, executive function, and negative emotionality. We analyzed data from 109 participants (ages 22-39, 15% female, N = 61 CUD) from the SUDMEX CONN dataset. To test if distinct ‘mechanism-based’ subtypes exist within CUD, we conducted a Latent Profile Analysis using phenotypic data as input. The discovered subtypes were characterized regarding their use characteristics, psychiatric diagnostics, and resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC). Three distinct subtypes emerged (p < 0.05, partial eta squared: 0.19–0.51): a ‘Relief Type’ with high negative emotionality (N = 22); a ‘Cognitive Type’ with lower executive function (N = 15); and an ‘Undefined Type’ with no apparent impairments (N = 24). Consistent with this profile, the Relief Type had more comorbid psychiatric diagnoses. Furthermore, each subtype demonstrated a unique neurobiological profile underlying their current cocaine use (pFDR < 0.05): the Relief Type showed aberrant rsFC in Limbic/Memory and Salience networks; the Cognitive Type in Frontoparietal, higher visual, Motor Planning, Salience, and Parietal Association networks; and the ‘Undefined Type’, in Motor Planning, Ventral Frontoparietal, Salience, and Default-Mode networks. Importantly, despite these distinct profiles, CUD severity was equivalent between all three subtypes. Results highlight significant heterogeneity in the mechanisms underlying CUD, emphasizing the importance of mechanism-based subtyping to inform the development of targeted treatments.

Introduction

Cocaine use in the United States has been steadily escalating, with approximately 5.0 million individuals aged 12 or older (1.8% of US population) reporting past-year use, a 3-fold increase since 2010 (1.5 million, 0.6% of US population) [1]. Among these users, an estimated 1.4 million people meet criteria for past-year Cocaine Use Disorder (CUD) [1]. Despite the growing prevalence, both treatment seeking and treatment rates remain low, which has, in part, been attributed to a lack of confidence in available treatment options [2–4]. Compounding this issue, rates for return to use after treatment are high, ranging from to 75–80% [5, 6], indeed justifying a lack of confidence in treatment. We and others have argued that the considerable heterogeneity in the mechanisms underlying CUD, and a lack of targeted treatments to address this heterogeneity, may be a key factor contributing to poor treatment outcomes [7–13]. We therefore posit that parsing the mechanistic heterogeneity in CUD is essential to effectively guide the development of targeted, personalized treatments, and ultimately alleviate this ongoing ‘treatment crisis’ [7–13].

Current theories of addiction emphasize the complexity of neurobehavioral mechanisms underlying addiction, suggesting an importance of at least three broad mechanisms or functional domains: 1) altered approach-related behavior (also reward-related behavior or incentive salience), 2) higher negative emotionality and 3) lower executive function [14–18]. Recent empirical studies, including our own, support the significance of these three mechanisms in Substance Use Disorder (SUD) [13, 19]. Importantly, these studies also affirmed the functional independence of these three mechanism [13, 20–22]. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that distinct brain systems underlie each of these three mechanisms, further emphasizing their functional independence [23–26]. Moreover, we have previously demonstrated with a ‘mechanism-based’ subtyping approach that at least three subtypes exist in Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder, each with a primary impairment in only one of the three functional domains. Specifically, we found three ‘mechanism-based’ addiction subtypes: 1) a ‘Reward Type’ with high approach-related behavior, 2) a ‘Relief Type’ with high negative emotionality, and 3) a ‘Cognitive Type’ with lower executive function [13]. Each of these three subtypes was further characterized by distinct neurobiological profiles underlying their current substance use that aligned with their phenotypic profile [13]. However, such ‘mechanism-based’ subtypes and their neurobiological profiles have not yet been demonstrated in individuals with CUD.

Previous investigations into CUD heterogeneity have focused on ‘symptom-based’ subtyping, primarily using measures of CUD symptom severity and occasionally psychiatric comorbidity, rather than data that assesses underlying neurobehavioral impairments. Specifically, among the roughly dozen published CUD subtyping studies, the majority relied on symptom scales (e.g., Addiction Severity Index, craving scales), identifying between 2–6 CUD subtypes typically differentiated by variations in CUD severity (e.g., cocaine use frequency, use duration, age of onset) and comorbid psychopathology [27–33]. In contrast, only two previous subtyping studies have explored heterogeneity in the underlying functional mechanisms of cocaine use, focusing on one functional domain only. Both identified ‘impulsive’ and ‘non-impulsive’ subtypes, indicating substantial heterogeneity for this underlying mechanism [34, 35]. However, to our knowledge, no research has investigated the heterogeneity in CUD using assessments across all three functional domains.

The current study aims to address this gap by investigating whether ‘mechanism-based’ subtypes exist among individuals with current CUD, extending our prior work in Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder. Based on our prior work, we hypothesized considerable heterogeneity exists within the three functional domains relevant in addiction, and that we would identify three separable ‘mechanism-based’ subtypes in CUD, similar to those we previously identified: 1) a ‘Reward Type’ with high approach-related behavior, 2) a ‘Relief Type’ with high negative emotionality, and 3) a ‘Cognitive Type’ with lower executive function [13]. Additionally, we expected that, as previously shown, each of these phenotypically-derived subtypes would exhibit a corresponding neurobiological profile. To evaluate neurobiological function, we examined whole-brain resting-state functional connectivity using graph theory analysis, consistent with our previous work. This allowed us to assess network-level differences in large-scale brain organization by subtype and evaluate the consistency with our previously identified subtype-specific neurobiological profiles.

Methods

Participants

We analyzed data from the Substance Use Disorder Connectivity study from Mexico City (SUDMEX CONN) dataset, a comprehensive Mexican open-access dataset with a broad phenotypic assessment, good clinical characterization, and inclusion of functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) data from both patients with current CUD and controls [36]. All study procedures and informed consent, including consent to share de-identified data, were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Instituto Nacional de Psiquiatría “Ramón de la Fuente Muñiz” according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Recruitment criteria and full sample details are described in [36], control participants were matched on age, gender and education level to CUD participants. Cocaine dependence was diagnosed using the MINI International Neuropsychiatric Interview – Plus Spanish version 5.0.0 [37], administered by trained psychiatrists and psychologists. CUD participants were required to be active cocaine users or with an abstinence period shorter than 60 days prior to the scan; frequency of cocaine use had to be at least 3 days per week with no more than 60 continued days of abstinence during the last 12 months [36]. After excluding participants with > = 25% missing data, our sample included a total of 109 participants (15% Female; CUD: N = 61, Control: N = 48). Psychiatric comorbidity was assessed with DSM Axis I symptom scales [see [36] for details] and a Structured Clinical Interview to assess DSM-IV Axis 2 Personality Disorders (SCID-II) [38] and comorbid substance use. Phenotypic measures

The SUDMEX dataset phenotyped participants using clinical interviews, self-report and task-based measures of behavior, affect, and cognition. All self-report, task-based measures, DSM Axis I and Axis II symptom scores, but not the diagnostic psychiatric data (mental health diagnoses) were included in the subtyping analysis. Only measures with <10% missing data and that showed significant differences between Controls and participants with CUD were used as input for the subtyping analysis. By focusing on measures that distinguish the CUD group from Controls, we ensured that the subsequent subtyping approach captured meaningful disorder-related variability in the underlying mechanisms, rather than unrelated variation. In total, we included 20 standardized subscale scores or single item measures derived from 9 different assessments (Table S1). The included measures broadly encompassed the three functional domains hypothesized to underlie addiction – Negative Emotionality, Executive Function, and Approach-Related Behaviors. However, assessments of the approach-related behavior domain were notably limited in the study design, with only two available single item measures assessing promiscuity and reckless driving (Table S1).

Subtyping analysis

To recover distinct subtypes within individuals with current CUD (N = 61), we conducted a latent profile analysis (LPA) using the 20 phenotypic measures described above (Table S1). LPA, a form of gaussian-mixture modeling, identifies subgroups within multi-dimensional data by modeling patterns across multiple variables to reveal latent profiles or subtypes [39]. LPA was performed in R (4.0.5) using the “mclust” package (version 5.7.4) [40]. The model was initialized by model-based hierarchical clustering and fitted using expectation-maximization to assign posterior probabilities to the distribution of the variables [41]. To ensure the data supported a class- or profile-based structure, several model evaluation metrics were used including the Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC), Integrated Complete-data Likelihood (ICL), and non-negative BIC difference [42]. The model with the lowest BIC, ICL, and non-negative BIC difference was selected for further analysis. The power for this analysis (N = 61; 20 continuous input variables; N = 15 in the smallest observed class; observed effect size for profile differentiation: partial-eta squared = 0.25–0.96) was adequate according to simulations. Specifically, adequate power can be ascertained because class sizes in the population were hypothesized to be equal based on our previous work [22], because a large number of input variables (all continuous) were used, and because substantial variation in the individual scores for the selected measures was demonstrated (post-hoc) by the large effect sizes [42–44].

Characterization of recovered subtypes

Control group assignment and statistical analyses

To minimize bias and skew in statistical comparisons due to slightly unbalanced sample sizes for the discovered subtypes, we matched a control group to each subtype. We used the ‘matchControls’ function from the R “e1071” library [45], creating a distinct age- and gender-matched control group for each subtype. All subsequent comparisons between subtypes and control participants were conducted using these matched control groups. ANOVAs, ANCOVAs, independent samples t-tests and chi-squared analyses (two-sided where appropriate) were used for the statical analyses described below, with Holm-Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons on post hoc analyses. All analyses were conducted in R using the ‘rstatix’ package and the ‘emmeans’ package.

Phenotypic profiles

To characterize the phenotypic profiles of each of the identified subtypes across the variables used in the LPA, we statistically tested for differences on the LPA variables between the recovered subtypes for descriptive purposes only. Additionally, we compared each subtype to its matched controls on the same variables, to establish if the recovered phenotypic profiles indicated ‘impairments’ in function.

Clinical validation

Additional measures, not included in the subtype construction through the LPA, were used for independent validation of the recovered subtypes. These included demographics, psychiatric diagnostic data, drug use characteristics, and treatment history. As for the phenotypic profiles, we tested both for differences between subtypes, as well as for differences between subtypes and controls.

Neurobiological characterization

To capture meaningful disorder-related variability in neurobiological function, rather than unrelated variation, we tested if each of the phenotypically derived subtypes had a unique neurobiological profile linked to their current cocaine use. Specifically, we investigated if the neurobiological correlates of current cocaine use (predictor: cocaine use in past 30 days) were subtype-specific. We employed resting-state Functional Connectivity (rsFC) graph theory analysis to assess whole-brain neurobiological function.

Neuroimaging data was acquired using a Philips Ingenia 3 T MR system (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands, and Boston, MA, USA), with a 32-channel dS Head coil. Each participant underwent a single MRI session, with blood alcohol levels measured prior to the scan. For each participant, 10 minutes of resting-state fMRI data was acquired. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes open, focus on a fixation cross (white cross with black background), and to relax and not think about anything in particular. See [36] for a full description of all acquisition parameters. Further detailed information on the MRI quality control, preprocessing, and the rsFC analysis is provided in the Supporting Information. In short, after assessing data quality using the MRI Quality Control tool (MRIQC) [46], fMRI preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep v. 20.2.1 [47]. fMRI denoising and the rsFC analysis were conducted in MATLAB (version 2021a; The MathWorks, Inc.). Following denoising, the whole brain was parcellated using the Glasser multimodal parcellation (360 cortical areas [48], in addition to the 19 anatomically defined subcortical areas from the HCP preprocessing pipeline [49]. Subsequently, each participant’s rsFC matrix (379×379) was constructed by extracting the mean BOLD time series for these 379 areas and computing the z-transformed Pearson correlation coefficients between the time-courses of all areas. The resulting matrices were binarized with a proportional threshold of 15%, reflecting the median of an ideal cost range (approximately 0.01–0.30). This provides for optimized sparsity in the thresholded rsFC graph [50] and improved stability of measures as compared to absolute thresholds [51].

Using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (brain-connectivity-toolbox.net), we calculated graph theory metrics based on these rsFC graphs to characterize resting-state brain function for each subtype. We used the following graph theory measures to assess the function of brain areas (nodes): a) global and local efficiency, which capture functional integration; b) betweenness centrality to assess information transfer; and c) participation coefficient to capture integration/segregation within brain ‘modules’. Global and local efficiency quantify the efficiency of information transfer between a specific node and all other nodes in the brain (global efficiency) or its neighboring nodes (local efficiency), by calculating the average length of the shortest paths between the node of interest and the other nodes [52, 53]. Betweenness centrality quantifies the number of paths that pass through a given node and thus assesses that node’s importance as an information hub in the network [52, 53]. Lastly, participation coefficient quantifies a region’s functional integration within its own (versus other) brain ‘modules’ [52, 54]. The assignment of brain areas to modules for computing participation coefficient were defined based on Glasser’s multimodal parcellation and corresponding network assignment [48].

Finally, for each graph theory metric, a generalized linear model (GLM) regression analysis was conducted to investigate the subtype-specific neurobiological correlates of current cocaine use (predictor: cocaine use in past 30 days). Multiple comparisons were corrected using an FDR-threshold of p < 0.05. Results were visualized using Connectome Workbench (version 1.5.0) [55]. To aid the discussion and interpretation of results, the 360 cortical areas were additionally assigned to large scale resting-state networks by first matching each area to its Brodmann Area and then determining the corresponding Intrinsic Connectivity Network based on Laird et al., 2011 (see Supporting Information; Figure S1) [56].

Results

Recovered subtypes and their phenotypic profiles

Among all available phenotypic data, 20 variables showed significant differences between controls and participants with current CUD (p < 0.05) (Table S2), indicating functional impairments, and were thus selected as input to the LPA. The LPA identified three subtypes as the optimal solution, confirmed by the lowest BIC, ICL, and the non-negative BIC difference values (Table S3). Subtype-to-subtype comparisons of the LPA input variables indicated that each subtype had a unique phenotypic profile, with very large between-subtype effect sizes, as expected (p < 0.05 corrected; partial eta squared = 0.25–0.96; Fig. 1, Table S4). The comparison to matched controls demonstrated significant impairments, also with very large effect sizes (p < 0.05 corrected; partial eta squared = 0.20–0.51; Figure S2, Table S5). Both analyses converged to show that each discovered subtype was either characterized by a main impairment in one of the three hypothesized functional domains or was relatively un-impaired based on the available assessments (Table S4, Table S5). Specifically, we identified three subtypes, which we labeled according to the domain in which each demonstrated the largest effect (Fig. 1). A ‘Relief Type’ (N = 22, mean age = 32.3 years, male = 82%), exhibiting higher negative affect (depressive symptoms, anxiety, anger and neuroticism), neuroticism and higher levels of psychiatric symptoms (symptom scales: paranoia, aggression, borderline, narcissistic, antisocial, dependent and somaticizing). A ‘Cognitive Type’ (N = 15, mean age = 29.7 years, male = 87%), characterized by lower executive function (Barratt Impulsiveness Scale: higher ‘non-planning’ impulsivity). Clinical validation

Clinical validation measures that were not included in the LPA for subtype identification, further revealed that the ‘Relief Type’, in addition to its greater psychiatric symptom scores, demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of SCID-II AXIS-II Personality disorder diagnoses than the control group for eleven of the twelve assessed personality disorders (Table S6). In contrast, the ‘Cognitive Type’ had a significantly higher proportion of diagnoses than the control group for only one of twelve assessed personality disorders, specifically Borderline Personality Disorder (Table S6). Meanwhile, the ‘Undefined Type’ did not exhibit a significantly higher or lower proportion of diagnoses for any of the twelve assessed personality disorders compared to the control group (Table S6). Lastly, there were no significant differences between any of the three subtypes for age, gender, education level, socio-economic status, or any of the indicators of cocaine use, including age of cocaine use onset, years of cocaine consumption, weekly cocaine dose, self-reported problems related to current drug or alcohol use, or treatment history (Table S7).

Neurobiological characterization

Each identified subtype demonstrated distinct rsFC patterns linked to their current cocaine use (Fig. 2; Table 2). For individuals in the Relief Type, current cocaine use was associated with altered rsFC in two regions associated with the Limbic/Memory (entorhinal cortex) and Salience (parainsular) networks. For individuals in the Cognitive Type, current cocaine use was largely associated with altered rsFC in the Frontoparietal (inferior parietal, superior parietal, dlPFC, posterior cingulate), Higher Visual (lateral temporal), Motor Planning (premotor cortex, lateral temporal), Salience (insula, frontal opercular, ACC/mPFC, Caudate, Putamen), and Parietal Association (paracentral lobular/mid-cingulate) networks. For individuals in the Undefined Type, current cocaine use was linked to altered rsFC in the Motor Planning (premotor cortex), Ventral Frontoparietal (inferior frontal), Salience (insula, frontal opercular), and Default (posterior cingulate) networks. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate heterogeneity across the three functional domains of addiction to determine whether distinct mechanism-based subtypes exist in individuals with current CUD, similar to those identified in prior research in Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder [13]. Our results revealed three phenotypically distinct CUD subtypes, two of which had a main impairment in one of the three hypothesized functional domains and another with no apparent impairment based on the available assessments. Specifically, we identified a ‘Relief Type’ with high negative emotionality, a ‘Cognitive Type’ with lower executive function, and an ‘Undefined Type’ with no apparent impairments. Further, and as hypothesized, all three subtypes exhibited unique neurobiological profiles underlying their current cocaine use.

Individuals in the Relief Type exhibited higher levels of negative affect (depressive symptoms, anxiety, anger), neuroticism, and comorbid psychiatric symptoms, and a higher proportion of diagnoses for all but one personality disorder, indicating significant functional impairment on the domain of Negative Emotionality. As hypothesized, the phenotypic profile of this ‘Relief Type’ with its high levels of negative affect and psychiatric symptoms, closely aligned with the Relief Type previously identified in individuals with Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder [11, 13, 57]. Regarding their neurobiological profiles, the Relief Type identified here demonstrated rsFC patters linked to current cocaine use in two regions associated with the Limbic/Memory and Salience networks. Although only two regions were implicated, these findings also align with the network disruptions observed in the previously identified Relief Type in Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder [13]. The observed neurobiological profile of this subtype also aligns with its phenotypic profile by implicating brain networks underlying affective processing [58, 59]. Additionally, while this Relief Type did not differ from the other subtypes with regards to age of onset or severity of cocaine use, its markedly higher levels of psychiatric comorbidity resemble previously reported subtypes endorsing higher comorbid psychopathology [27, 29, 31]. This suggests a link between the recovered ‘mechanism-based’ subtypes and the more clinically defined ‘symptom-based’ CUD subtypes in the literature. Substantial research supports the role of negative emotionality in driving SUDs and their comorbidities [14–17, 60, 61]. In CUD specifically, a vast body of literature highlights negative emotionality as it pertains to emotion regulation [62–65] and stress [66, 67]. However, importantly, our findings demonstrate that impaired negative emotionality and high levels of psychiatric comorbidity significantly impacted only a subgroup of individuals with CUD. Specifically, consistent with our previous analysis on subtypes in Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder in an independent dataset [13], only about one-third of individuals with CUD demonstrated significantly higher levels of negative emotionality and psychiatric comorbidity. This highlights the need for targeted treatment approaches that address negative affect and psychiatric comorbidity specifically for this subgroup.

Individuals in the ‘Cognitive Type’ demonstrated challenges in executive function, being characterized by higher non-planning impulsivity. Further reflecting these challenges, this subtype also demonstrated altered rsFC in the Frontoparietal, Higher Visual, Motor Planning, Salience, and Parietal Association networks - regions highly associated with executive dysfunction in addiction [68–70]. Overall, this subtype aligned very well with the previously identified Cognitive subtype in individuals with Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder [13]. Although the phenotypic profile of the identified CUD Cognitive Type may initially appear somewhat different, the lack of overlapping assessments between these two independent datasets prevents direct comparison of their phenotypic profiles. However, the altered rsFC patterns observed in the CUD Cognitive Type closely mirrored those in the previously identified Cognitive Type [13], suggesting similar challenges in cognitive function across both groups. Additionally, the CUD Cognitive Type we identified resembled the highly impulsive CUD subtypes reported in previous literature [34]. Its profile was also consistent with the broader literature indicating that impaired cognitive control – specifically impulsivity - is a key feature of CUD [35, 71]. Overall, these findings clearly demonstrate the need for more targeted interventions in CUD. Similarly, as in our previous analysis of individuals with Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder, the ‘Cognitive Type’ represented approximately a third of individuals, underscoring the importance of the development of targeted treatment options for this type.

The third recovered subtype, the ‘Undefined Type’, exhibited no apparent phenotypic impairments compared to controls, but rather exhibited very low levels of anti-social and borderline symptoms indicating high social functioning. However, CUD in this subtype was as severe as in the two other types, with no significant differences identified among any of the three subtypes regarding current cocaine use, age of onset, or treatment utilization. Crucially, this finding suggests that this ‘Undefined Type’ may not merely indicate a ‘less severe’ form of CUD, but rather reflect our inability to capture its underlying functional impairment, given the limitations of the available assessments in the dataset. Moreover, we had hypothesized that a subtype characterized by higher approach-related behaviors, similar to the ‘Reward Type’ identified in Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder, would emerge. Given the lack of assessments of the approach-related functional domain, it was not possible to fully evaluate whether the ‘Undefined Type’ may be the hypothesized Reward Type. However, this type does seem to have high social function, supporting that it may be a reward seeking type. Additionally, this type ‘demonstrated distinct rsFC patterns in the Motor Planning, Ventral Frontoparietal, Salience, and Default networks—regions highly implicated in reward neurocircuitry [68, 72]. Moreover, three of these four networks were also implicated in the previously identified Reward Type [13], suggesting a striking resemblance between the two types. Given the clinical profile indicating high social function and similarity in neurobiological profiles between the ‘Undefined Type’ and the previously identified Reward Type, we speculate that the ‘Undefined Type’ may indeed be the (un-recovered) Reward Type. Importantly, while this idea certainly needs further evaluation, the notion of a Reward subtype in CUD is novel. Prior CUD subtyping analyses have not assessed the heterogeneity across reward-seeking and approach behaviors beyond substance use itself, preventing the identification of a distinct subtype characterized by these tendencies. Overall, this finding underscores the need for future research to more comprehensively assess heterogeneity in approach-related behaviors in CUD, extending beyond substance use itself. Finally, as in our previous analysis, this third recovered subtype comprised roughly a third of the individuals with CUD.

There were also shared characteristics of the three recovered CUD subtypes. Although the recovered CUD subtypes were phenotypically distinct and largely displayed differential rsFC patterns, brain regions within the salience network were consistently implicated across all three identified subtypes. The salience network has been reliably implicated in prominent addiction theories and the neurocircuitry of addiction [68, 72–74], particularly within the CUD literature [75–80]. It comprises brain regions such as the dorsal ACC, anterior insula, and inferior parietal lobule, which are involved in (re)directing attentional resources toward salient stimuli (i.e., motivational/incentive salience) [73, 74]. In individuals with substance use disorder, this motivational/incentive salience system is thought to be impaired, leading to a redirection of attention from adaptive stimuli to the more salient drug-related cues [72]. The involvement of the salience network across all identified CUD subtypes underscores its critical role in CUD. A dose-response effect on the salience network dependent on the severity of cocaine use history has been previously reported for CUD specifically [68]. It remains to be investigated if this is a shared biomarker is particularly important in individuals with stimulants as a primary drug of choice.

There are some limitations to the current study, most notably the insufficient assessment of reward/approach behaviors which hindered our ability to evaluate whether a ‘Reward Type’, as identified in Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder, could be replicated in individuals with current CUD. Additionally, this was the first study to investigate mechanism-based subtypes in individuals with Cocaine Use Disorder, and data for an a priori power analysis was hence not available. We therefore performed a power analysis post-hoc to estimate the sensitivity of our analyses based on observed effect sizes. This post-hoc analysis demonstrated adequate power for our primary analysis for the discovery of subtypes. While the resting-state neuroimaging analysis had a small sample size, the validity of the neurobiological characterization was supported by their close replication from our previous work [13]. Nonetheless, future studies should aim to replicate these analyses in larger samples to further validate and refine our understanding of these subtype characteristics. In conclusion, overall, our findings support the generalizability of our previously reported addiction subtypes to current CUD and demonstrate the robustness of this mechanism-based subtyping framework.

In summary, the results of this study demonstrate substantial neural and behavioral heterogeneity in the recovered CUD subtypes, adding to the growing literature supporting the need for the development of targeted or ‘personalized’ treatment approaches [7–12]. Based on our prior work [13] and the current results, we further hypothesize that the demonstrated ‘mechanism-based’ subtypes reflect stable, or “trait-like” vulnerability profiles that are addiction general. The current study indeed adds empirical evidence that ‘mechanism-based’ subtypes may be independent of the primary drug of choice. A future clinical application could therefore include the development of a robust and quick screening tool to identify these subtypes across substance use disorders in the clinic. The long-term goal would be the development of personalized treatments which directly target the discovered subtype-specific distinct functional impairments. Prior research in individuals with Alcohol Use Disorder indeed indicates that targeted treatments often outperform non-targeted, standard, approaches [11, 57, 81]. For example, a pharmacological treatment that blocks the brain’s “pleasure response” was effective for individuals whose primary drinking motive was pleasure seeking but less effective for those who drank to cope with negative affect [8, 11, 57]. However, the exploration and development of personalized, targeted treatments in addiction is still emerging. The current study thus primarily establishes a preliminary framework for future research. For instance, individuals in the Relief Type may particularly benefit from treatments targeting affective dysregulation. Potential approaches include, for example, supplemental pharmacotherapy for anxiety and depression [82], neuromodulation approaches that directly target the brain circuits underlying affective dysregulation [83], or cognitive-behavioral therapies designed to integrate substance use treatment with comorbid mental health treatment [81]. In contrast, individuals from the Cognitive Type may benefit more from interventions targeting executive function, such as cognitive trainings [84, 85], pharmacological cognitive enhancers [84, 86], or neuromodulation aimed at improving prefrontal cortex function and cognitive control [87, 88].

Conclusion

The current results provide empirical evidence for significant heterogeneity in the mechanisms underlying CUD. We identified three ‘mechanism-based’ subtypes with distinct neurobehavioral profiles that largely aligned with our previously identified subtypes in Alcohol and Cannabis Use Disorder [13]. Importantly, measures of cocaine use severity, age of onset, use duration, or dose did not differ among these subtypes, demonstrating that the recovered subtypes differ from previously reported ‘symptom-based’ subtypes, which vary in symptom severity but show no differences in functional profiles. Overall, these findings underscore the need for targeted treatments in CUD to improve treatment success.
