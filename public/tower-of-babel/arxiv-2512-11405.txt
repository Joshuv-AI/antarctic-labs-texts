A new expansion of the Riemann zeta function
B. Candelpergher

Abstract
After a brief introduction to Ramanujan’s method of summation, we give an expansion of the Riemann Zeta function in the critical strip as a convergent series ∑ m ≥ 0 x m ​ P m ​ ( s ) \sum_{m\geq 0}x_{m}P_{m}(s) where the functions P m P_{m} are polynomials with their roots on the line { ℜ ( s ) = 1 / 2 } \{\Re(s)=1/2\} , the coefficients x m x_{m} being finite linear combinations of the Euler constant γ \gamma and the values ζ ⁡ ( 2 ) , ζ ⁡ ( 3 ) , … , ζ ⁡ ( m + 1 ) . \zeta(2),\zeta(3),\dots,\zeta(m+1).



1
Introduction

There are many polynomial expansions of the Riemann zeta function.
Some of them (cf.[E-K-K], [F-V],[B],[M], [V])
are based on the Newton interpolation formula for functions related to Zeta. For example

\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{m}c_{m}\binom{s-1}{m},

with

c_{m}=\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{k}\big(\zeta(k+1)-\frac{1}{k}\big).

Others are linked to the well known expansion

\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{m}\gamma_{m}\frac{(s-1)^{m}}{m!}

where
\gamma_{m}
are the Stieltjes constants. This gives (cf.[E-G])

\zeta(s)-\frac{s}{s-1}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{m}l_{m}(\frac{s-1}{s})^{m},

with

l_{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m-1}{k-1}(-1)^{m-k}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{k!}.

and for
s=\frac{1}{2}+it

(t\in\mathbb{R}
) one obtain an expansion of
\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)
on the orthonormal basis
(e_{m})
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\frac{dt}{2\pi(t^{2}+1/4)})
given by

e_{m}(t)=\Big(\frac{1-2it}{1+2it}\Big)^{m}.

There is also (cf. [K]) an expansion of the function

\Xi(t)=\frac{s(s-1)}{2}\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s),\ \ \ s=\frac{1}{2}+it,\ (t\in\mathbb{R}),

which takes the form

\Xi(t)=e^{\pi t/4}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}b_{m}p_{m}(t),

where
p_{m}(t)
are polynomials related to the Mellin transform of odd Hermite functions. These polynomials are orthogonal in
(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},|\Gamma(3/4+it/2)|^{2}dt)
, but it seems that the coefficients
b_{m}
does not have a simple form.

The polynomial expansion we give in this paper is related to the Mellin transform of the functions
x\mapsto e^{-x}L_{m}(2x)
where

(L_{m})_{m\geq 0}
are the Laguerre polynomials defined by

L_{m}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{k}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.

The generating function of the Laguerre polynomials
(cf. [Leb]) gives for
x\in[0,+\infty[

e^{-xz}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}2e^{-x}L_{m}(2x)\psi_{m}(z).

where
(\psi_{m})_{m\geq 0}
the sequence of the functions defined in the half-plane
\{Re(z)>0\}
by

\psi_{m}(z)=\frac{1}{1+z}\big(\frac{z-1}{z+1}\big)^{m}.

By Mellin transform of the function
x\mapsto e^{-xz}
, we obtain for
\Re(z)>0
and
\Re(s)>0

\Gamma(s)z^{-s}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\Big(2\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-x}L_{m}(2x)x^{s-1}\ dx\Big)\psi_{m}(z),

Since we can write

2\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-x}L_{m}(2x)x^{s-1}\ dx={\Gamma(s)}P_{m}(s),

where
P_{m}
is the polynomial

P_{m}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{k}2^{k+1}\ \frac{\Gamma(s+k)}{\Gamma(s)}\frac{1}{k!},

then we have, for
\Re(s)>0
, the expansion

z^{-s}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\psi_{m}(z)P_{m}(s).

Take
z=1,2,3,\dots
, then we get an infinite sequence of relations that it’s tempting to sum up, to obtain

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1}\psi_{m}(n)\Big)P_{m}(s).

In section 3, we prove that, for
0<\Re(s)<1
, this last formula is correct if we replace the divergent series
\sum_{n\geq 1}
by their Ramanujan’s summations
\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}
, and then we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}z_{m}P_{m}(s)\ \text{ where }\ z_{m}=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}.

In section 2 we give a short introduction to Ramanujan’s summation of divergent series. Since we will see that, for
s\neq 1
, we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1},

then, after an explicit evaluation of
z_{n}
, we obtain, for
0<\Re(s)<1
, the following expansion

\zeta(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}P_{m}(s)

with

x_{m}=\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-2)^{k}\zeta(k+1)+r_{m}

and
r_{m}
are rational numbers explicitely given.
For
s=\frac{1}{2}+it
with
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the preceding relation gives the expansion

\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)=2\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}Q_{m}(t)

where the polynomials

Q_{m}:t\mapsto\frac{1}{2}P_{m}(\frac{1}{2}+it)

form complete orthonormal system in
\big(L^{2}(-\infty,+\infty),\frac{dt}{\cosh(\pi t)}\big)
.
Thus, for every
m\geq 1
, the roots of
Q_{m}
are real, and this implies that the roots of

P_{m}
are on the line
\{\Re(s)=\frac{1}{2}\}
.

2
Ramanujan’s summation

2.1
Ramanujan’s constant of a series

Let a function
f\in C^{1}(]0,+\infty[)
, the first order Euler-MacLaurin formula says that, for every integer
n\geq 1

{\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(k)=\int_{1}^{n}f(x)\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\big(f(n)+f(1)\big)+\int_{1}^{n}\big(x-[x]-\frac{1}{2}\big)f^{\prime}(x)\ dx},

(1)

where
[x]
denote the integer part of
x
.
For
x\geq 1
, let

b(x)=x-[x]-\frac{1}{2}

and let’s assume that the integral
\int_{1}^{+\infty}b(x)f^{\prime}(x)\ dx
is convergent. The previous formula gives

{\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(k)=\int_{1}^{n}f(x)\,dx+C(f)+\frac{1}{2}f(n)-\int_{n}^{+\infty}b(x)f^{\prime}(x)\ dx},

(2)

\text{ with }\ \ C(f)=\frac{1}{2}f(1)+\int_{1}^{+\infty}b(x)f^{\prime}(x)\ dx.

The constant
C(f)
is called
Ramanujan’s constant of the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}f(n)
(cf. [H1]).

Clearly
C(f)
depends linearly of
f
.
And if the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}f(n)
is convergent, then by (2) we have

C(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}f(k)-\int_{1}^{+\infty}f(x)\ dx.

We observe that
C(f)
is also well defined for some divergent series.
We have immediately
C(1)=1/2.
And applying (2) to the function
f:x\mapsto 1/x
,
we have for every integer
n\geq 1

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}=\log n+C(f)+\frac{1}{2n}+\int_{n}^{+\infty}b(x)\frac{1}{x^{2}}\ dx,

which gives that the Ramanujan’s constant of the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}
is the
Euler constant
:

C(f)=\gamma=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}-\log n\Big).

2.2
Plana’s formula

The following summation formula was discovered by Plana in 1820 (and also given by Abel in 1823) (cf. [Lin]).

Theorem 1
.

Let
f
be an analytic function in the half-plane
\{\Re(z)>0\}
such that there is a constant
{a}<2\pi
and, for any interval
[c,d]\subset]0,+\infty[
, a constant
C_{c,d}>0
with

\left|f(x+it)\right|\leq C_{c,d}\ e^{a|t|},\ \text{ for }x\in[c,d]\ \text{ and all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Then we have Plana’s formula : for any integer
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(1)+f(n)}{2}+\!\int_{1}^{n}f(u)\ du

\displaystyle+i\!\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(1+it)-f(1-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt-i\!\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(n+it)-f(n-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt\,.

(3)

If we now compare Euler-MacLaurin formula (1)
and Plana’s (3), then we get, for
n\geq 1
,

\int_{1}^{n}b(x){f^{\prime}(x)}\ dx=i\!\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(1+it)-f(1-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\ dt-i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(n+it)-f(n-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt\,.

If the integral
\int_{1}^{+\infty}b(x)f^{\prime}(x)\,dx
is convergent and if

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(n+it)-f(n-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\ dt=0,

then we have

\int_{1}^{+\infty}b(t){f^{\prime}(t)}\ dt=i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(1+it)-f(1-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt.

Thus, in this case the Ramanujan’s constant
C(f)
, previously defined by (2), is also

C(f)=\frac{1}{2}f(1)+i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{{f(1+it)-f(1-it)}}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt.

(4)

Take now this last formula as the definition of Ramanujan’s constant
C(f)
for a function
f
verifying the hypotheses of theorem 1.

Remark 1
.

Let
f
and
g
two functions verifying the assumptions of theorem 1 and such that

f(n)=g(n)
for every integer
n\geq 1
. Then Ramanujan’s constants
C(f)
and
C(g)
, defined by
(4), need not necessarily be the same.
For example, if
f\equiv 0
and
g:z\mapsto\sin(\pi z)
, then
C(f)=0
, but

\displaystyle C(g)=i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin\big(\pi(1+it)\big)-\sin\big(\pi(1-it)\big)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{\pi t}-e^{-\pi t}}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt\;\;=\;\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\pi t}\ dt=\frac{1}{\pi}.

To avoid functions like
z\mapsto\sin(\pi z)
, we will consider a space of analytic functions such that Ramanujan’s constant, defined by (4), depends
only on the values
f(n)
for all integers
{n\geq 1}
.

2.3
The space
\mathcal{E}^{\pi}

Definition 1
.

Let
{\alpha}>0
and
{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}
the space of analytic functions
f
in the half plane
\{\Re(z)>0\}
such that there is some constants
\emph{a}<\alpha
and
\emph{b}>0
with :

for every
{\varepsilon}>0
, there is a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
such that

\left|f(z)\right|\;\;\leq\;\;C_{\varepsilon}\ e^{b\,\Re(z)}\ e^{a\,|\Im(z)|},\ \text{ if }\ \Re(z)>{\varepsilon}.

(5)

Example 1
.

Let
f_{s}\;:\;z\mapsto e^{sz}
, with
s=\sigma+i\tau
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. For
\Re(z)>0
, we have

|f_{s}(z)|\;\;=\,\;e^{\sigma\Re(z)-\tau\Im(z)}.

Thus
f_{s}\in\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}
if and only if
-\alpha<\Im(s)<\alpha
.

Properties

a) If
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}
and
g\in\mathcal{E}^{\beta}
, alors
fg\in E^{\alpha+\beta}
.

b) If
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}
and if
F(z)=\int_{1}^{z}f(u)du
then
F\in\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}
.

An important theorem for the rest of this section is the following.

Theorem 2
.

Carlson’s theorem
(cf. [H2])

If
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
is such that
f(n)=0
for all
n\;=\;1,\,2,\,\ldots
, then
f\equiv 0
.

Thus a function
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
is only determined by the values
f(1),f(2),f(3),\dots
.

Remark 2
.

The function
g\;:\;z\mapsto\sin(\pi z)
is such that
g(n)=0
for every integer
{n\geq 1}
, but
g\notin\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
since
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}

|g(x+it)|\sim\frac{1}{2}e^{\pi|t|}\text{ when }t\to\infty

2.4
Ramanujan’s method of summation

Let
f
and
g
two functions verifying the hypothesis of theorem 1.
By Carlson’s theorem we know that if
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
and
g\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
verify
{f(n)=g(n)}
for
n=1,2,3,\dots
, then for
{x>0}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

{f(x+it)-f(x-it)}={g(x+it)-g(x-it)},

thus, by (4), we have

C(f)=C(g).

This leads to the following definition.

Definition 2
.

For
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
, we define
the Ramanujan’s summation of
the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}f(n)
by

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)=\frac{f(1)}{2}+i\!\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(1+it)-f(1-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt\,.

(6)

It is clear that
\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)
depends linearly on
f
.

Remark 3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
then by (3), we have for every integer
n\geq 1

\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(k)=\!\int_{1}^{n}f(u)\ du+\frac{f(n)}{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)-i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(n+it)-f(n-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt\,.

(7)

If

\lim_{n\to+\infty}f(n+it)=0
, for every
\ t\in\mathbb{R}
,
then, by dominated convergence, we get

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(n+it)-f(n-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt\,=0,

and if in addition, the integral

\int_{1}^{+\infty}f(u)\ du
is convergent
then the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}f(n)
is convergent.
In this case we have by (7)

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)\;\;=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}f(n)-\!\!\int_{1}^{+\infty}\!\!f(x)\ dx.

Example 2
.

For
f:x\mapsto\frac{1}{x^{s}}
, with
\Re(s)>1
, we get

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\;\;=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}-\!\!\int_{1}^{+\infty}\!\!\frac{1}{x^{s}}\ dx,

thus, for
\Re(s)>1,
we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\;\;=\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}.

(8)

2.5
Interpolation of partial sums

To evaluate Ramanujan’s summation of a divergent series
\sum_{n\geq 1}f(n)
, a simpler way than the formula (6) can be obtained using an interpolation function of partial sums of this series .
In chapter VI of his Notebooks, Ramanujan uses, without any definition, such a function
\varphi

which satisfies

{\varphi(x)-\varphi(x-1)=f(x)}\ \text{ with }\varphi(0)=0.

The following theorem clarifies this point.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
.
There is a unique function
\varphi_{f}\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
, such that

\varphi_{f}(x+1)-\varphi_{f}(x)=f(x+1)\ \text{ for all }x>0\text{ and }\varphi_{f}(1)=f(1).

(9)

Proof.

a)
Existence.

Since
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
then the function
\varphi_{f}
, defined for
\Re(z)>0
, by

\varphi_{f}(z)=\int_{1}^{z}f(u)\ du+\frac{f(z)}{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)-i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(z+it)-f(z-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\ dt,

(10)

is also in

\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
.
By (3)
we have
\varphi_{f}(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(k)
for every integer
n\geq 1
, thus the function
\varphi_{f}
verifies

\varphi_{f}(n+1)-\varphi_{f}(n)\,=f(n+1),\text{ for }n=1,2,3,\dots.

Since the functions
z\mapsto\varphi_{f}(z+1)-\varphi_{f}(z)
and
z\mapsto f(z+1)
are in
\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
, then by Carlson’s theorem, we get

\varphi_{f}(z+1)-\varphi_{f}(z)=f(z+1),\quad\!\!\text{ for all }z\text{ such that }\Re(z)>0.\

(11)

The condition
\varphi_{f}(1)=f(1)
is simply a consequence of (6) and (10).

b)
Uniqueness.

If
\varphi\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
is such that

\varphi(x+1)-\varphi(x)=f(x+1)\ \text{ for }x>0,\ \ \text{ with }\varphi(1)=f(1),

then, for every integer
n\geq 1
, we get

\varphi(n)-f(1)=\varphi(n)-\varphi(1)\;=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\big(\varphi(k+1)-\varphi(k)\big)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}f(k+1),

thus
\varphi(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(k)=\varphi_{f}(n)
for every integer
n\geq 1
.
Since
\varphi
and
\varphi_{f}
are in the space
\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
, then by Carlson’s theorem we get
\varphi=\varphi_{f}.

∎

Remark 4
.

The preceding proof asserts that if
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
then

\varphi_{f}(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(k)\text{ for every integer }n\geq 1.

The function
\varphi_{f}
given by (10) is the unique function in
\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
wich interpolates the partial sums of the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}f(n)
.
Note that, by (11), we have for
\Re(z)>0

\varphi_{f}(z)=\varphi_{f}(z+1)-f(z+1),

and since the right-hand side defines an analytic function in the half-plane
\{\Re(z+1)>0\}
, this relation gives an analytic continuation of
\varphi_{f}
in the half-plane
\{\Re(z)>-1\}.
Let’s denote again by
\varphi_{f}
this continuation, then the relation
{\varphi_{f}(1)=f(1)}
provides the condition required by Ramanujan

{\varphi_{f}(0)=\varphi_{f}(1)-f(1)=0.}

Example 3
.

Let
f:z\mapsto 1/z
, then
\varphi_{f}
is related to
digamma function
which is defined, for
\Re(z)>0
, by

\Psi(z)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(z)}{\Gamma(z)}.

This function is analytic in
\{\Re(z)>0\}
,
and by logarithmic differentiation of the classical product formula for
\Gamma
(cf. [G-R])
we get for
\Re(z)>0

\Psi(z)=-\frac{1}{z}-\gamma+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\big(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+z}\big)=-\frac{1}{z}-\gamma+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{z}{n(n+z)}

(12)

Then it is easily verified that
\Psi\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
and
that

\Psi(z+2)\;-\;\Psi(z+1)=\frac{1}{z+1}

Since
\Psi(1)=-\gamma
,
then, by the previous theorem, we get for
\Re(z)>0

\varphi_{f}(z)=\Psi(z+1)+\gamma.

A very useful formula for Ramanujan’s summation is provided by the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
, and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
be a solution of

\displaystyle\varphi(x+1)-\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x+1),\ \text{ for all }\ x>0,

\displaystyle\varphi(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(1).

Then we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)\;\;=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(x)dx.

Proof.

By theorem 3, we have
\varphi=\varphi_{f}
.
Let
F(z)=\int_{1}^{z}f(u)\ du
, then integration of (10)
gives

\int_{1}^{2}\varphi_{f}(x)\ dx\;\;=\int_{1}^{2}F(u)\ du\,+\,\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{2}f(x)\ dx\,+\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)-iD(f),

(13)

where

D(f)=\int_{1}^{2}\Big(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{f(x+it)-f(x-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt\Big)\ dx.

By Fubini’s theorem we have

D(f)\;\;=\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\Big(\int_{1}^{2}{f(x+it)-f(x-it)}\ dx\Big)}\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt,

thus

D(f)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{F(2+it)-F(2-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt-\!\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{F(1+it)-F(1-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt.

If we apply Plana’s formula to the function
F
, with
n=2
, then we get

\displaystyle-\big(\frac{1}{2}F(1)+\frac{1}{2}F(2)\big)+\!\int_{1}^{2}F(t)dt

\displaystyle=i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{F(2+it)-F(2-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt

\displaystyle-i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{F(1+it)-F(1-it)}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt,

which is

iD(f)=-\frac{F(2)}{2}+\int_{1}^{2}F(t)\ dt.

Then (13) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{2}\varphi_{f}(x)\ dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{2}f(x)\ dx+\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)+\frac{F(2)}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{2}f(x)\ dx+\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n).

from which we deduce that

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)=\int_{0}^{1}\big(\varphi_{f}(x+1)-f(x+1)\big)\ dx=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{f}(x)\ dx.

∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
, then the function
x\mapsto f(x+1)
belongs to
\mathcal{E}^{\pi}
and we have
the shift formula

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n+1)\;\;=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}f(n)-f(1)+\int_{0}^{1}f(x+1)\ dx.

Proof.

Let
g:x\mapsto f(x+1)
, then it is easily verified, by theorem 3, that we have for
x>0

\varphi_{g}(x)=\varphi_{f}(x+1)-f(1).

Thus we have

\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{g}(x)dx=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{f}(x+1)dx-f(1)=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{f}(x)dx+\int_{0}^{1}f(x+1)dx-f(1),

and the conclusion follows from theorem 4.
∎

Example 4
.

Let
f:x\mapsto 1/x
. By example 3, we have

\varphi_{f}(x)=\Psi(x+1)+\gamma,

thus we get

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n}=\int_{0}^{1}(\Psi(x+1)+\gamma)dx=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}(x+1)dx+\gamma,

thus we verify again that

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n}=\gamma,

(14)

Observe that, by corollary 1, we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}=\gamma-1+\log(2).

Example 5
.

Since, fro any
x>0
, we have

\log(\Gamma(x+2))-\log(\Gamma(x+1))=\log(x+1)

and
\log(\Gamma(2))=\log(1)
, we deduce that

\varphi_{\log}(x)=\log(\Gamma(x+1))
.
Now if we use the
Raabe’s formula, that is :

\int_{a}^{a+1}\log(\Gamma(x))dx=a\log(a)-a+\log(\sqrt{2\pi}),

then we get

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\log(n)=\log(\sqrt{2\pi})-1.

Example 6
.

Let
f(x)=\Psi(x)
and
\varphi(x)=x\Psi(x)-x+1
. We have
f(1)=\Psi(1)=\varphi(1)

and

\varphi(x+1)-\varphi(x)=\Psi(x+1)=f(x+1).

Thus, by the previous theorem, we get

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\Psi(n)=\int_{0}^{1}x\Psi(x)dx+\frac{1}{2}=-\int_{0}^{1}\log(\Gamma(x))dx+\frac{1}{2},

then by
Raabe’s formula

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\Psi(n)=-\log(\sqrt{2\pi})+\frac{1}{2}.

Example 7
.

We can evaluate
\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}n^{k}
for
k=1,2,3,\dots
by means of Bernoulli numbers.
Let the
Bernoulli polynomials

B_{n}(x)
be defined by

B_{0}(x)=1
and, for
k\geq 1,

B^{\prime}_{k}(x)=nB_{k-1}(x)\ \text{ with }\ \int_{0}^{1}B_{k}(x)dx=0.

The
Bernoulli numbers
are the numbers
B_{k}=B_{k}(0)
.
These polynomials verify (cf. [G-R])

B_{k+1}\big((x+1)+1\big)-B_{k+1}(x+1)=(k+1)(x+1)^{k}.

For
k\geq 1
, we have
B_{k+1}(1)=B_{k+1}
.
Let the function
f:x\mapsto x^{k}
with an integer
k\geq 1
, then
by theorem 3, we obtain

\varphi_{f}(x)=\frac{B_{k+1}(x+1)}{k+1}-\frac{B_{k+1}}{k+1}.

Since

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{B_{k+1}(x+1)}{k+1}dx\;\;=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{B_{k+1}(x)}{k+1}dx\,+\int_{0}^{1}x^{k}dx=\frac{1}{k+1},

then theorem 4 gives

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}n^{k}=\frac{1-B_{k+1}}{k+1}.

(15)

Remark 5
.

Relations (14) and (15) can be written in terms of the Zeta function :

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 1}\Big(\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}\Big),

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}n^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta(-k)-\frac{1}{-k-1}\text{ for every integer }k\geq 1.

Note also that

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}n^{0}=\frac{1}{2}=\zeta(0)+1,

and by (9) we have in the half-plane
\{\Re(s)>1\}

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}.

(16)

All these formulae are special cases the following general result.

Theorem 5
.

The function

s\mapsto\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\;\;

is analytic on
\mathbb{C}
and we have for every

s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{1\}

\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{s-1}

(17)

Proof.

First, it is easily seen that, for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the function
z\mapsto 1/{z^{s}}

is in the space
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha>0.
Since,
if
s=\sigma+i\tau
(
\sigma\in\mathbb{R},\ \tau\in\mathbb{R}
), we can write

\Big|\frac{1}{z^{s}}\Big|=\Big|\frac{1}{(x+it)^{s}}\Big|=\Big|e^{-s\log(x+it)}\Big|=e^{-{\sigma}\log\sqrt{x^{2}+t^{2}}+\tau\arctan(\frac{t}{x})}\leq e^{|\tau|\pi/2}(x^{2}+t^{2})^{-\sigma/2}

where
z=x+it
, with
x>\varepsilon>0
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Thus the sum
\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}
is well defined and, by (6), we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\;\;\frac{1}{2}+i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\Big({\frac{1}{(1+it)^{s}}-\frac{1}{(1-it)^{s}}}\big)\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\,\ dt\,

=\frac{1}{2}+2\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin(s\arctan(t))}{(1+t^{2})^{s/2}}\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt.

This last integral depends analyticaly of
s\in\mathbb{C}
so the function
s\mapsto\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}
.
Since the function
s\mapsto\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{1\}
then, by analytic continuation of (16), we get

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}\text{ for any }s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{1\}.

∎

3
An expansion of Zeta in the critical strip

We are now going to use expression (17) to obtain, in the strip
\{0<\Re(s)<1\}
, a polynomial expansion of Zeta

\zeta(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}P_{m}(s).

As we have seen in the Introduction the polynomials
P_{m}
are related to the Laguerre polynomials by Mellin transform. We now give another approach to these polynomials.

3.1
The sequence of polynomials
(P_{m})

We start with the observation that
for
s\in\mathbb{C}
,
and for every
n>0
, we have

\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\frac{1}{n+1}G_{s}(\frac{n-1}{n+1}),

(18)

where
G_{s}
is the function defined on
D(0,1)=\{z\in\mathbb{C};|z|<1\}
by

G_{s}:z\mapsto\frac{2}{1-z}\Big({\frac{1-z}{1+z}}\Big)^{s}

(19)

Since
\Re\big({\frac{1-z}{1+z}}\big)>0
for
z\in D(0,1)
, we see that the function
G_{s}
is analytic on
D(0,1)
.
Let
P_{m}(s)
denote the coefficients of its Taylor expansion

\frac{2}{1-z}\Big({\frac{1-z}{1+z}}\Big)^{s}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}P_{m}(s)z^{m}\ \text{ for }\ \ \ z\in D(0,1)

(20)

Let
z=\frac{x}{1+x}
, with
x\in]0,1[
, then, using (20), we get

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}P_{m}(s)\Big(\frac{x}{1+x}\Big)^{m+1}=2x(1+2x)^{-s}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{k}2^{k+1}\frac{(s)_{k}}{k!}x^{k+1},

(21)

where
(s)_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(s+k)}{\Gamma(s)}=s(s+1)\cdots(s+k-1)
if
k\geq 1
and
(s)_{0}=1.

We use now the well-known
equivalence related to Euler summation (cf.[H1])

\sum_{m\geq 0}b_{m}(\frac{x}{1+x})^{m+1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}x^{k+1}\ \Leftrightarrow\ b_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\ a_{k},

which gives, for
P_{m}(s),
the following explicit expression

P_{m}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{k}2^{k+1}\ \frac{(s)_{k}}{k!}.

(22)

We also have an integral expression for
P_{m}(s)
when
0<\Re(s)<1
, since for
k\geq 0
, we can write

\frac{(s)_{k}}{k!}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)}\int_{0}^{1}t^{s-1+k}(1-t)^{-s}dt,

thus, by (22), we get for
0<\Re(s)<1

P_{m}(s)=2\frac{\sin(\pi s)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}t^{s-1}(1-t)^{-s}(1-2t)^{m}dt.

(23)

This integral expression allows us to obtain the following lemma which will be useful later.

Lemma 1
.

For
0<\Re(s)<1
, we have, for every
m\geq 1

|P_{m}(s)|\leq e^{\pi|Im(s)|}\Big(\frac{\Gamma(\Re(s))}{m^{\Re(s)}}+\frac{\Gamma(1-\Re(s))}{m^{1-\Re(s)}}\Big).

Proof.

Using (23) we get

\displaystyle|P_{m}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{|\sin(\pi s)|}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}t^{\Re(s)-1}(1-t)^{-\Re(s)}|1-2t|^{m}dt,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{|\sin(\pi s)|}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u)^{\Re(s)-1}(1+u)^{-\Re(s)}|u|^{m}du,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\pi|Im(s)|}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u)^{\Re(s)-1}(1+u)^{-\Re(s)}|u|^{m}du.

Let
a=\Re(s)
, this last integral is :

I_{a}=\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{a-1}(1+u)^{-a}u^{m}du+\int_{-1}^{0}(1-u)^{a-1}(1+u)^{-a}(-u)^{m}du.

Since
0<a<1
, we have

\displaystyle I_{a}\leq\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{a-1}u^{m}du+\int_{-1}^{0}(1+u)^{-a}(-u)^{m}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{a-1}u^{m}du+\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{-a}u^{m}du.

Now if
0<\alpha<1
we have, for every
x>0

\int_{0}^{1}{(1-u)^{\alpha-1}}u^{x}du=\int_{0}^{+\infty}{e^{-t}}{(1-e^{-t})^{\alpha-1}}e^{-xt}dt\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-xt}dt=\Gamma(\alpha)x^{-\alpha},

(the last inequality is justified by
1\leq\frac{t}{1-e^{-t}}\leq{e^{t}}
, for every
t>0
).
Finally we obtain

I_{a}\leq\frac{\Gamma(a)}{m^{a}}+\frac{\Gamma(1-a)}{m^{1-a}}.

∎

Remark 6
.

It is easily verified (cf. [G-R]) that, for
m\geq 1
, we have

P_{m}(s)=2F(-m,s,1,2),

where
F
is the Gauss hypergeometric function

F(a,b,c,z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}}\frac{z^{k}}{k!},

By classical properties of hypergeometric function (cf. [G-R]), we get the relations

P_{m}(1-s)=(-1)^{m}P_{m}(s),

(24)

sP_{m}(s+1)-(s-1)P_{m}(s-1)=(2m+1)P_{m}(s).

(25)

3.2
Orthogonality relations

Let
(\varphi_{m})_{m\geq 0}
the sequence of functions

\varphi_{m}:x\mapsto\sqrt{2}\ e^{-x}L_{m}(2x).

It is well known (cf. [Leb]) that
the sequence
(\varphi_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is an orthonormal system in
(L^{2}(0,+\infty),dx)
.
We verify as in the introduction that, by Mellin transform, we have for
\Re(s)>0

\mathcal{M}\varphi_{m}(s)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\varphi_{m}(x)x^{s-1}\ dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\Gamma(s)}P_{m}(s).

(26)

Thus the
Mellin-Parseval formula (cf. [T]), gives

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\overline{\mathcal{M}(\varphi_{m})(\frac{1}{2}+it)}\ \mathcal{M}(\varphi_{n})(\frac{1}{2}+it)\ dt=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\overline{\varphi_{m}(x)}\varphi_{n}(x)dx=\delta_{(m,n)},

with
\delta
the usual Kronecker symbol. That is

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\overline{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+it)}\overline{P_{m}(\frac{1}{2}+it)}\ \Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+it)P_{n}(\frac{1}{2}+it)dt=\delta_{(m,n)}.

Since

\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+it)\overline{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+it)}=\frac{\pi}{\cosh\pi t},

then

\frac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\overline{P_{m}(\frac{1}{2}+it)}\ P_{n}(\frac{1}{2}+it)\frac{dt}{\cosh\pi t}=\delta_{(m,n)}.

This proves that the sequence
(Q_{m})_{m\geq 0}
of polynomials

Q_{m}:t\mapsto\frac{1}{2}P_{m}(\frac{1}{2}+it)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{k}2^{k}\ \frac{(\frac{1}{2}+it)_{k}}{k!}

(27)

is an orthonormal system in
\big(L^{2}(-\infty,+\infty),\frac{dt}{\cosh(\pi t)}\big)
. It is also an orthonormal basis of this space since for
|a|<\pi
, we have

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{a|t|}\frac{dt}{\cosh(\pi t)}<+\infty

By a classical result on orthogonal polynomials we deduce that, for every
m\geq 1
, the roots of
Q_{m}
are real. This implies that the roots of

P_{m}
are on the line
\{\Re(s)=\frac{1}{2}\}
.

For the first values of
m
we get

\displaystyle Q_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle Q_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i(-2t),

\displaystyle Q_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2t^{2}+\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle Q_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i(\frac{4}{3}t^{3}-\frac{5}{3}t),

\displaystyle Q_{4}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}t^{4}-\frac{7}{3}t^{2}+\frac{3}{8}.

Remark 7
.

By (24) and (25) we have,
for every
m\geq 0
, the relations

\overline{Q_{m}(t)}=Q_{m}(-t)=(-1)^{m}Q_{m}(t),

\big(\frac{1}{2}+it\big)Q_{m}(t-i)+\big(\frac{1}{2}-it\big)Q_{m}(t+i)=(2m+1)Q_{m}(t).

3.3
Expansion of
\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}

We now apply Ramanujan’s summation on both sides of (18). Using (20),
we get

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}G_{s}(\frac{n-1}{n+1})=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}P_{m}(s).

The following theorem says that, for
0<\Re(s)<1
, these last two sums can be inverted.

Theorem 6
.

For
0<\Re(s)<1

we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}z_{m}P_{m}(s)\ \text{ where }\ z_{m}=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}.

And we obtain the expansion

\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}z_{m}P_{m}(s).

(28)

Proof.

By definition 2, we can write

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}=c_{m}+i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{2+it}\big(\frac{it}{2+it}\big)^{m}-\frac{1}{2-it}\big(\frac{-it}{2-it}\big)^{m}\Big)\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\ dt

with
c_{0}=1/4
if
m=0
and
c_{m}=0
if
m\geq 1
.
We will now prove that the series

\sigma(s)=\sum_{m\geq 0}\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}P_{m}(s)

is convergent for every
0<\Re(s)<1
, and that its sum is equal to

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}P_{m}(s).

Since
c_{0}P_{0}(s)=1/2
, then, by definition 2, the series
\sigma(s)
is also

\displaystyle\sigma(s)=\frac{1}{2}+i\sum_{m\geq 0}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{2+it}\big(\frac{it}{2+it}\big)^{m}-\frac{1}{2-it}\big(\frac{-it}{2-it}\big)^{m}\Big)\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\ dt\ P_{m}(s).

(29)

To prove the convergence of this series and
invert signs
\sum
and
\int
we use the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

For
0<\Re(s)<1
, the series

\sum_{m\geq 0}\Big|\Big(\frac{1}{2+it}\big(\frac{it}{2+it}\big)^{m}-\frac{1}{2-it}\big(\frac{-it}{2-it}\big)^{m}\Big)\ P_{m}(s)\Big|

(30)

is convergent and

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\Big|\Big(\frac{1}{2+it}\big(\frac{it}{2+it}\big)^{m}-\frac{1}{2-it}\big(\frac{-it}{2-it}\big)^{m}\Big)\ P_{m}(s)\Big|\ \frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt<+\infty.

(31)

Proof of the lemma
.

We have

\Big|\frac{1}{2+it}\big(\frac{it}{2+it}\big)^{m}-\frac{1}{2-it}\big(\frac{-it}{2-it}\big)^{m}\Big|\leq 2\Big|\frac{1}{2+it}\big(\frac{it}{2+it}\big)^{m}\Big|=\frac{2}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\big)^{m}

Using lemma 1, we see that the terms of the series (30) are, for
m\geq 1
, dominated by the terms

\frac{4}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}e^{\pi|Im(s)|}\big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\big)^{m}\Big(\frac{\Gamma(\Re(s))}{m^{\Re(s)}}+\frac{\Gamma(1-\Re(s))}{m^{1-\Re(s)}}\Big)

Consequently, the series (30) is convergent, and we have

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\Big|\Big(\frac{1}{2+it}\big(\frac{it}{2+it}\big)^{m}-\frac{1}{2-it}\big(\frac{-it}{2-it}\big)^{m}\Big)\ P_{m}(s)\Big|\ \leq M(t)

with

M(t)=\frac{4t}{4+t^{2}}+e^{\pi|Im(s)|}\frac{4}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\big)^{m}\Big(\frac{\Gamma(\Re(s))}{m^{\Re(s)}}+\frac{\Gamma(1-\Re(s))}{m^{1-\Re(s)}}\Big)

It remains to prove that

\int_{0}^{+\infty}M(t)\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt<+\infty.

This follows from the convergence of the integral

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{4t}{4+t^{2}}\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt,

and the fact that, for
0<\alpha<1
, we have

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\big)^{m}\frac{1}{m^{\alpha}}\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}dt<+\infty,

as a consequence of the following inequalities

\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\sum_{m=2}^{+\infty}\big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\big)^{m}\frac{1}{m^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}\big)^{m}\leq\frac{t}{{t^{2}+4}}\Big(\frac{1}{1-\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}}\Big)\leq\frac{t^{2}}{4}.

End of the proof of the lemma
.

By the preceding lemma the series
\sigma(s)
is convergent for every
0<\Re(s)<1
, and we get

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}P_{m}(s)

=\frac{1}{2}+i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{2+it}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\big(\frac{it}{2+it}\big)^{m}P_{m}(s)-\frac{1}{2-it}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\big(\frac{-it}{2-it}\big)^{m}P_{m}(s)\Big)\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\ dt.

Using (20) with

z=\frac{\pm it}{2\pm it}
, this last expression is equal to

\frac{1}{2}+i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\Big(\big(\frac{1}{1+it}\big)^{s}-\big(\frac{1}{1-it}\big)^{s}\Big)\frac{1}{e^{2\pi t}-1}\ dt,

which is by definition 2, also equal to

\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}-\frac{1}{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}.

The relation (28) follows immediately from (17).

∎

3.4
Evaluation of the sum
z_{m}=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}

If we apply the shift formula of corollary 1 to the function
f:x\mapsto\frac{1}{x}\big(1-\frac{2}{x}\big)^{m}
, we get

\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n+1}\Big(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\Big)^{m}=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n}\Big(1-\frac{2}{n}\Big)^{m}-(-1)^{m}+y_{m}

with

y_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\Big)^{m}\frac{1}{x+1}dx=\int_{1}^{2}\Big(1-\frac{2}{x}\Big)^{m}\frac{1}{x}dx.

Now the binomial expansion

\Big(1-\frac{2}{x}\Big)^{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-2)^{k}\frac{1}{x^{k}}

gives

z_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-2)^{k}\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{k+1}}-(-1)^{m}+\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-2)^{k}\int_{1}^{2}\frac{1}{x^{k+1}}dx.

Thus we obtain
z_{m}=x_{m}+y_{m}
, where

x_{0}=\gamma-1,\ \ y_{0}=\log(2),

and for
m\geq 1

\displaystyle x_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-2)^{k}\Big(\zeta(k+1)-\frac{1}{k}\Big)-(-1)^{m},

(32)

\displaystyle y_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(2)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-2)^{k}\frac{1}{k}\Big(1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\Big).

For the first values of
m
we get

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma-1,

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma-2\zeta(2)+3,

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma-4\zeta(2)+4\zeta(3)+1,

\displaystyle x_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma-6\zeta(2)+12\zeta(3)-8\zeta(4)+11/3,

\displaystyle x_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma-8\zeta(2)+24\zeta(3)-32\zeta(4)+16\zeta(5)+5/3.

Theorem 7
.

For
0<\Re(s)<1
, we have

\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}z_{m}P_{m}(s),

(33)

where

z_{m}=\gamma+\log(2)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-2)^{k}\zeta(k+1)+(-1)^{m+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k}.

Proof.

This follows from the expansion (28) of theorem 6, and
the combinatorial identity

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k}.

∎

3.5
An expansion of Zeta

The previous theorem will enable us to get an expansion of Zeta in the strip
\{0<\Re(s)<1\}
. Indeed, the polar part
1/(s-1)
of (33) has the simple expansion given by the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

For
0<\Re(s)<1
we have

\frac{1}{s-1}=-\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}y_{m}P_{m}(s).

(34)

Proof.

For
0<\Re(s)<1
, we write

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}y_{m}P_{m}(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\Big)^{m}\frac{1}{x+1}dx\Big)P_{m}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\Big(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\Big)^{m}P_{m}(s)\frac{1}{x+1}dx.

(35)

This interchanging of signs
\sum
and
\int
is justified by

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|\frac{x-1}{x+1}|^{m}\ |P_{m}(s)|\frac{1}{x+1}dx\leq\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(x+1)^{m+1}}dx\ |P_{m}(s)|\leq\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{m}|P_{m}(s)|,

and we immediately check, with the lemma 1, that the latter series is convergent.

The relations (18) and (20) allows us to write for every
x>0

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{x+1}\Big(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\Big)^{m}P_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{x^{s}},

and by (35) we have

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}y_{m}P_{m}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{x^{s}}dx=-\frac{1}{s-1}.

∎

Theorem 8
.

For
0<\Re(s)<1
, we have

\zeta(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}P_{m}(s)

(36)

with

x_{m}=\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-2)^{k}\zeta(k+1)+r_{m}

(37)

and
r_{m}
are the rational numbers

r_{m}=(-1)^{m+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1+(-1)^{k-1}}{k}

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the preceding lemma and theorem 7 and the combinatorial identity

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\frac{(-2)^{k-1}}{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1+(-1)^{k-1}}{k}.

∎

3.6
An orthonormal expansion

Let
X(0)=\gamma-1
and for
k\geq 1

X(k)=\zeta(k+1)-1-\frac{1}{k}.

Then formula (32) can be written as a binomial transform

x_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{k}2^{k}X(k).

Let

f_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}-\frac{1}{t},

then we have for
k\geq 0

X(k)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-t}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\ f_{0}(t)dt

By definition of Laguerre polynomials, we obtain for the coefficients
x_{m}
the integral expression

x_{m}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-t}L_{m}(2t)f_{0}(t)dt=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\varphi_{m}(t)f_{0}(t)dt=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\varphi_{m},f_{0}).

(38)

Theorem 9
.

We have for any integer
m\geq 0

x_{m}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\overline{Q_{m}(t)}\ \zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)\frac{dt}{\cosh(\pi t)}.

(39)

Proof.

By Mellin-Parseval formula (cf. [T]), we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\varphi_{m}(t)}f_{0}(t)dt=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\overline{\mathcal{M}(\varphi_{m})(\frac{1}{2}+it)}\ \mathcal{M}(f_{0})(\frac{1}{2}+it)\ dt

Since (cf. [T]) we have
\mathcal{M}f(s)=\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)
for
0<\Re(s)<1
, then, using (26), we get

x_{m}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\overline{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+it)\frac{1}{2}P_{m}(\frac{1}{2}+it)}\Gamma((\frac{1}{2}+it))\frac{1}{2}\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)\ dt,

since
Q_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{2}P_{m}(\frac{1}{2}+it)
, this is the integral expression (39).

∎

The previous theorem means that the pointwise convergent expansion

\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)=2\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}Q_{m}(t)

is also an orthonormal expansion in the space
\big(L^{2}(-\infty,+\infty),\frac{dt}{\cosh(\pi t)}\big)
.

Then we have

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Big|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)\Big|^{2}\frac{dt}{\cosh(\pi t)}=4\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}^{2}.

(40)

We give next a formal evaluation of this last sum using Ramanujan’s summation.

3.7
A generating function

Let
\mathcal{L}
the Laplace transform

\mathcal{L}f(z)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-xz}f(x)\ dx=(e^{-xz},f).

By Paley-Wiener theorem (cf. [Ho], [P]) , the transformation
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathcal{L}
maps isometrically the space
L^{2}(]0,+\infty[,dx)
to the Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})

of analytic functions
g
in the half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}=\{z|\Re(z>0\}
, such that :
there exists
M_{g}>0
with

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(x+iy)|^{2}\ dy\leq M_{g}\text{ for all }x>0.

Each such
g
has non-tangential limits
g(iy)=\lim_{x\to 0}g(x+iy)
at almost every point of the imaginary axis.
The space

H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
is an Hilbert space with the inner product

(g,h)_{H^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{g(iy)}{h(iy)}dy,

and the functions

\psi_{m}

(m\geq 0)
, form an orthonormal basis of
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
.
If
f\in L^{2}(]0,+\infty[,dx)
.
From

e^{-xz}=\sqrt{2}\ \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\varphi_{k}(x)\psi_{k}(z).

we get

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathcal{L}\varphi_{m}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(e^{-xz},\varphi_{m})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\psi_{m}(z)

The system
(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\psi_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis of
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
.
From the expansion

f_{0}=\sum_{m\geq 0}(\varphi_{m},f_{0})\varphi_{m}

we get the expansion

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathcal{L}f_{0}(z)=\sum_{m\geq 0}(\varphi_{m},f_{0})\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\psi_{m}(z).

and, by (38) we deduce

\mathcal{L}f_{0}(z)=2\sum_{m\geq 0}x_{m}\psi_{m}(z).

Since it is well known (cf. [G-R]) that

\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-xz}f_{0}(x)dx=\log(z)-\Psi(z)-\frac{1}{z}

then we get

2\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}\psi_{m}(z)=\log(z)-\Psi(z)-\frac{1}{z}.

(41)

Equivalently this gives the generating function

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}u^{m}=\frac{1}{1-u}\Big(\log(\frac{1+u}{1-u})-\Psi(\frac{1+u}{1-u})\Big)-\frac{1}{1+u}.

(42)

Remark 8
.

Now, if we apply (41) with
z=1,2,3,\dots
and take Ramanujan’s summation, we get

2\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\psi_{m}(n)=2\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}\psi_{m}(n)=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\log(n)-\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\Psi(n)-\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n}.

Then, with the results of examples 4-6, this relation gives

2\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}(x_{m}+y_{n})=\log(2\pi)-\gamma-\frac{3}{2}.

Since

y_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{m}(x)dx

then by (41) we have

2\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}y_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}2\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}\psi_{m}(x)dx=\int_{0}^{1}(\log(x)-\Psi(1+x))dx=-1.

Finally we get

\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}x_{m}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma-\frac{1}{4}.

Then (40) gives us the relation

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Big|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)\Big|^{2}\frac{dt}{\cosh(\pi t)}=2\log(2\pi)-2\gamma-1,

(43)
