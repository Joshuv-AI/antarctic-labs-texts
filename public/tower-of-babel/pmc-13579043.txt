Hypoparathyroidism with a TBX1 variant: reconsidering genetic etiologies in the absence of 22q11.2 deletions
Authors: Marah Alsayed Hasan, Rahaf Sultan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypoparathyroidism is most commonly postsurgical, but autoimmune and genetic causes should be considered in the absence of prior neck surgery. T-box transcription factor 1 (TBX1) is a dosage-sensitive regulator of pharyngeal pouch and parathyroid development. While DiGeorge syndrome classically results from a 22q11.2 deletion, emerging evidence suggests that heterozygous TBX1 variants may cause deletion-negative, partial phenotypes with variable penetrance. We report a 25-year-old man with congenital sensorineural hearing loss who presented with chronic hypoparathyroidism after an initial hypocalcemic seizure at age 15. Autoimmune evaluation, chromosomal microarray analysis, and fluorescence in situ hybridization for 22q11.2 deletion were unremarkable. A multigene hypoparathyroidism panel identified a TBX1 c.1009G>C (p.Asp337His) heterozygous variant of uncertain significance (VUS) in exon 8 within the consensus splice site, distinct from previously reported pathogenic splice-site mutations. This variant has been variably classified as likely pathogenic and as a VUS, reflecting differences in laboratory interpretation frameworks. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of TBX1-associated disease, demonstrating isolated hypoparathyroidism with hearing loss in the absence of 22q11.2 deletion. Identification of TBX1 variants should prompt phenotype-directed screening for associated cardiac, renal, immunologic, and audiologic defects. Family segregation analysis can provide critical evidence to support the reclassification of VUS.Keywords: TBX1, hypoparathyroidism, variant of uncertain significance, DiGeorge syndrome

Introduction

Hypoparathyroidism is an endocrine disorder characterized by impairment or deficiency in parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion, leading to disruptions in calcium and phosphate homeostasis [1, 2]. Clinical manifestations of hypocalcemia include neuromuscular excitability, seizures, paresthesia, ectopic calcifications, and cardiac arrhythmias [1, 2]. Although postsurgical hypoparathyroidism is the most common etiology, nonsurgical causes include autoimmune disease, metabolic derangements, infiltrative processes, and genetic disorders [1, 2]. In individuals presenting in childhood or without a history of neck surgery, genetic etiologies should be considered.

DiGeorge syndrome is a well-recognized developmental disorder caused by 22q11.2 deletions and is characterized by parathyroid and thymic hypoplasia, congenital heart disease, palatal anomalies, and immune dysfunction [3-5]. T-box transcription factor 1 (TBX1) is a dosage-sensitive transcription factor essential for pharyngeal pouch, inner ear, and parathyroid development and is considered a major contributor to the phenotypic features of 22q11.2-associated syndromes [3-5]. Emerging evidence suggests that heterozygous TBX1 variants may result in partial or isolated phenotypes without the full syndromic presentation [6-13]. However, reported cases remain limited and genotype–phenotype relationships are incompletely defined.

We describe a young adult with long-standing hypoparathyroidism and congenital sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) in whom a heterozygous TBX1 c.1009G>C (p.Asp337His) variant was identified. The variant occurs at the terminal nucleotide of exon 8 within a consensus splice site previously implicated in TBX1-related pathogenicity. Although the functional consequence of this substitution is unknown, its proximity to known splice-site mutations raises the possibility of altered TBX1 transcript processing. This case adds to the limited reports of TBX1 variants presenting with isolated hypoparathyroidism and highlights the challenges of variant interpretation in patients with unexplained hypoparathyroidism.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old man with congenital SNHL presented for the management of long-standing hypoparathyroidism. At age 7 and 8, he was diagnosed with mild bilateral hearing loss and audiologic testing demonstrated mild SNHL in the right ear and moderate to severe mixed hearing loss in the left ear. Computed tomography of the head revealed a congenital inner ear anomaly with dilation of the lateral semicircular canals and partial assimilation to the vestibules. At age 15, he presented to the emergency department with hypocalcemic seizures, paresthesias, and carpopedal spasms. Laboratory studies revealed a serum calcium of 5.8 mg/dL (International System of Units [SI]: 1.45 mmol/L) (reference range, 8.5–10 mg/dL [SI: 2.12-2.5 mmol/L]) with an inappropriately normal PTH level. The inadequate compensatory PTH response for the degree of hypocalcemia is consistent with the diagnosis of hypoparathyroidism. He was started on standard therapy with calcium carbonate and calcitriol with resolution of his symptoms.

Evaluation for the etiology of the hypoparathyroidism included negative autoimmune testing, normal chromosomal microarray analysis, and a negative fluorescence in situ hybridization for 22q11.2 deletion. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed no intracranial calcifications, and renal imaging was unremarkable without nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis. Family history was notable for autoimmune thyroid disease and type 1 diabetes, but no known parathyroid disease or features of a syndromic disorder including developmental delay, craniofacial abnormalities, cardiac abnormalities, or immune deficiency. He was followed by pediatric endocrinology during his adolescence. Growth and pubertal development were normal for age. His hearing deficits have remained stable, and he has not required hearing aids.

Diagnostic assessment

Physical examination was unremarkable, without craniofacial anomalies, cardiac findings, or clinical signs of hypocalcemia. He has been maintained on conventional therapy with calcium carbonate, calcitriol, and vitamin D with stable serum calcium levels of 8.2-8.9 mg/dL (SI: 2.05-2.22 mmol/L) (reference range, 8.6-10.2 mg/dL [SI: 2.15-2.54 mmol/L]), low PTH of 6-8 pg/mL (SI: 0.64-0.85 pmol/L) (reference range, 16-77 pg/mL [SI: 1.7-8.2 pmol/])L, and hyperphosphatemia ranging from 4.3-5.6 mg/dL (SI: 1.39-1.81 mmol/L) (reference range, 2.8-4.1 mg/dL [SI: 0.9-1.32 mmol/L]). Twenty-four-hour urine calcium remained elevated > 400 mg/24 hours (SI: >10 mmol/24 hours) (reference range <250 mg/24 hours [SI: <6.25 mmol/24 hours]), although kidney function was normal, and there was no evidence of nephrocalcinosis or nephrolithiasis.

Given his persistent hypoparathyroidism and the history of childhood-onset SNHL, a comprehensive multigene hypoparathyroidism panel (Invitae) was performed. No 22q11.2 deletion or known pathogenic variants were identified. However, a heterozygous TBX1 c.1009G>C (p.Asp337His) variant of uncertain significance (VUS) was detected. The missense variant is located at the terminal nucleotide of exon 8 within the consensus splice site.

Notably, prior genetic testing performed in 2020 (GeneDx) identified the same TBX1 c.1009G>C (p.Asp337His) variant, which was classified as likely pathogenic and consistent with a TBX1-related DiGeorge-like phenotype. The discrepancy likely reflects differences in laboratory-specific variant interpretation frameworks, available population and functional databases, and evolving variant classification criteria.

The patient was referred for genetic counseling and family segregation analysis. Phenotype-directed screening for syndromic features with renal ultrasound and a transthoracic echocardiogram were unremarkable except for left ventricular hypertrophy. Given the absence of recurrent infections, immunodeficiency, or intolerance to live vaccines, clinically significant thymic dysfunction was considered unlikely, and T-cell phenotyping was deferred.

Treatment

The patient has been maintained on calcium carbonate (500 mg elemental calcium) twice daily, calcitriol 0.5 mcg twice daily, cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) 2000IU daily, and magnesium oxide 240 mg daily. Dietary counseling focused on reducing sodium intake and taking calcium with meals to reduce hyperphosphatemia and urinary calcium excretion.

Outcome and follow-up

At follow-up approximately 10 years after diagnosis, the patient remained clinically stable without recurrent severe hypocalcemia requiring hospitalization. Serum calcium remained within the low-normal range on conventional therapy and renal function was preserved. Hypercalciuria persisted despite dietary modifications. Recombinant PTH therapy (palopegteriparatide) will be initiated to address the persistent hypercalciuria and hyperphosphatemia.

His hearing loss remained stable, and no new cardiac, craniofacial, or immunologic abnormalities have been identified. He experienced no adverse effects related to calcium or calcitriol therapy. He was evaluated by genetics and family segregation analysis is pending.

Discussion

We describe a young adult with chronic hypoparathyroidism and congenital SNHL in whom a heterozygous TBX1 c.1009G>C (p.Asp337His) variant was identified in the absence of a 22q11.2 deletion. Although classic DiGeorge syndrome results from 22q11.2 deletion, increasing evidence suggests that heterozygous TBX1 variants can result in deletion-negative DiGeorge-like phenotypes [1, 4-13]. This case expands the growing spectrum of partial or isolated TBX1-associated phenotypes and highlights the challenges of interpreting rare variants in patients with unexplained hypoparathyroidism.

The phenotypic variability reported likely reflects tissue-specific sensitivity to TBX1 dosage, developmental timing, functional consequences of different variants and the genetic context [6, 14-17]. During early embryogenesis, TBX1 regulates epithelial proliferation and pharyngeal pouch patterning of the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches, from which the parathyroid glands arise [4, 6, 14-17]. TBX1 functions within a complex spatiotemporal transcriptional network to establish the developmental environment required for lineage-specifying factors like glial cells missing 2, which promotes parathyroid progenitor cell differentiation and survival [4, 6, 14, 17]. Different pharyngeal pouch derivatives appear to have distinct thresholds for TBX1 activity and variable capacity for developmental compensation [15, 16]. Partial disruptions in TBX1 function may selectively impair parathyroid organogenesis while preserving the development of other TBX1-dependent structures. Additional phenotypic variability may be influenced by epigenetic factors, genetic modifiers, stochastic developmental events, and environmental exposures [4, 6, 14-17]. TBX1 also contributes to inner ear morphogenesis, providing biological plausibility for the co-occurrence of hypoparathyroidism and congenital hearing loss in this patient [18]. While these developmental roles support a mechanistic link between TBX1 dysfunction and the observed phenotype, they do not establish causality.

Consistent with prior reports, intragenic TBX1 variants have been associated with a broad phenotypic spectrum that extends beyond the classic 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, with some individuals exhibiting partial or isolated manifestations (Table 1) [3, 6-13]. The TBX1 c.1009G>C (p.Asp337His) variant identified in this patient is located at the terminal nucleotide of exon 8, adjacent to a splice donor site. The proximity of this variant to previously reported pathogenic splice-site variants raises the possibility of altered TBX1 expression or function. However, in the absence of functional studies or confirmatory RNA analyses, the available evidence remains insufficient to establish pathogenicity.

An important aspect of this case is the discordant classification of the same TBX1 variant across laboratories and time. Genetic testing performed in 2020 classified the variant as likely pathogenic, whereas subsequent testing through a different laboratory classified it as a VUS. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in laboratory-specific interpretation frameworks, larger population frequency databases, and progressive refinement of the criteria used in the classification of variants [19-21]. This case illustrates both interlaboratory variability and temporal evolution in variant classification, emphasizing that genetic result should not be considered static and may warrant periodic re-evaluation.

This case has several implications for endocrinology practice. First, idiopathic hypoparathyroidism in the absence of prior neck surgery should prompt consideration of genetic etiologies. Second, TBX1 variants should be considered even in the absence of a 22q11.2 deletion given the variable penetrance and expressivity. Third, the identification of a TBX1 variant should prompt phenotype-directed screening for additional DiGeorge-like features including cardiac, renal, immunologic, and audiologic abnormalities. Finally, family segregation analysis may provide critical evidence, particularly if the variant co-segregates with disease or is absent in affected relatives, to clarify variant pathogenicity and support reclassification.

This case has several important limitations. The small sample size limits the ability to draw definitive genotype–phenotype associations. Causality cannot be established between the TBX1 variant and hypoparathyroidism given the lack of functional validation and family segregation analysis. Nevertheless, detailed case reports such as this remain valuable for refining genotype–phenotype correlations and informing future variant interpretation.

In conclusion, this case expands on the phenotypic spectrum of TBX1-associated disease, demonstrating that deletion-negative TBX1 variants can present with isolated or partial DiGeorge-like features, including hypoparathyroidism and congenital hearing loss. It highlights the importance of genetic evaluation in unexplained hypoparathyroidism, phenotype-directed screening for associated features, and ongoing variant reinterpretation with segregation analysis to guide diagnosis, counseling, and management.
