Dimethyl itaconate suppresses dendritic cell and CD8+ T cell responses to halt vitiligo
Authors: Yinghan Wang, Yuhan Chen, Jingjing Ma, Linxuan Wu, Pan Kang, Jianru Chen, Pengran Du, Wei Wu, Xinju Wang, Kaiqiao He, Yuqi Yang, Sen Guo, Weinan Guo, Ling Liu, Zhe Jian, Tianwen Gao, Shuli Li, Chunying Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough dendritic cell (DC)- and CD8+ T cell-mediated autoimmunity is critical for destroying melanocytes in vitiligo, treatment options remain limited by the absence of therapies that cotarget both cell types.MethodsWe first evaluated the association between the immunoregulatory metabolite itaconate and disease development, by determining human vitiligo serum itaconate levels and monitoring depigmentation progression in Acod1 knockout (KO) mice with endogenous itaconate deficiency. We further evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of the itaconate derivative, dimethyl itaconate (DI) in mice and assessed its effects on cutaneous infiltration and the functional properties of DCs and CD8+ T cells in vivo and ex vivo. The gene signatures and signaling pathways involved in DI-treated CD8+ T cells were also assessed.ResultsWe observed an elevation of circulating itaconate in vitiligo patients, whereas itaconate deficiency accelerated depigmentation in Acod1 KO mice after vitiligo induction. The administration of DI halted vitiligo development and promoted repigmentation, with elevated circulating itaconate levels, increased melanocyte counts, and decreased cutaneous CD8+ T cell densities. Mechanistically, DI dampened CD8+ T cell activation (CD69), effector function (Interferon-γ, IFN-γ), cytotoxicity (Gzmb), proliferation, and proinflammatory gene expression (Csf1, Ifitm1, CD49a, NKG2D, and NKG2A), partly by suppressing the Janus kinase (JAK)‒STAT pathway. Moreover, DI-treated mice exhibited reduced cutaneous DC infiltration, as well as fewer DCs with mature and migratory phenotypes.ConclusionsOur findings identify DI as a metabolite-derived small molecule that protects against autoimmune injury by cotargeting DC and CD8+ T cell responses, thereby demonstrating a promising therapeutic strategy and providing a foundation for treating vitiligo and other cell-specific autoimmune diseases.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04512-1.Keywords: Autoimmune disease, CD8+ T cell, Dendritic cell, Dimethyl itaconate, JAK-STAT pathway, Vitiligo

Background

Vitiligo is a prototypical autoimmune depigmenting skin disease characterized by disfiguring and refractory features, affecting 0.5%−2% of the population worldwide [1–4]. It is well established that autoreactive CD8+ T cells are the central drivers of vitiligo pathogenesis [1–3]. This mechanistic understanding was inspired by early observations: that perilesional skin presents with dense CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in both the dermis and epidermis, and that melanocyte-specific cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells can eradicate melanocytes ex vivo [1, 5, 6]. In vitiligo, CD8⁺ T cell-derived interferon-γ (IFN-γ) triggers local keratinocytes and fibroblasts to secrete chemokines via the IFN-γ receptor-Janus kinase (JAK) pathway, thereby fueling a self-amplifying inflammatory loop that leads to further CD8+ T cell recruitment [1]. A series of recent clinical trials have demonstrated that JAK inhibitors (JAKi)—which block the pathological IFN-γ‒chemokine axis, thereby impeding CD8+ T cell aggregation—have favorable efficacy [7, 8], highlighting the importance of targeting the CD8+ T cell-mediated autoimmune response in vitiligo therapy.

Dendritic cells (DCs) serve as upstream regulators that orchestrate melanocyte-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses in vitiligo by cross-presenting antigens to prime CD8⁺ T cells in the dermis [9]. In addition, elevated numbers of DCs and increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines produced by DCs have been found in the circulation or perilesional skin of vitiligo patients [10–12]. More evidence on the contribution of DCs was obtained from mouse models where vitiligo develops after the adoptive transfer of premelanosome protein (PMEL)-pulsed dendritic cells and PMEL-specific CD8+ T cells [13, 14]. Interfering with DC function [12] has shown great efficacy in a mouse model of vitiligo. However, DC inhibition alone cannot halt preactivated CD8+ T cells, and suppressing CD8+ T cells directly also fails to prevent DC-driven activation, which sustains T cell responses. Thus, strategies targeting DC and CD8+ T cell-directed adaptive immunity are critical for more efficiently inhibiting the immune response, but these strategies have not been established in vitiligo.

The recognition of intermediate metabolites with immunomodulatory functions may reshape the treatment of autoimmune diseases [15]. The metabolite itaconate is synthesized in stimulated macrophages that undergo metabolic reprogramming, derived from the decarboxylation of cis-aconitate by the enzyme cis-aconitate decarboxylase 1 (Acod1, also known as immune-responsive gene 1, Irg1) [16, 17]. Clinically, endogenous itaconate levels are elevated in early sepsis [18], suggesting that it may be a metabolic regulator of autoimmune/inflammatory diseases and serve as a biomarker for monitoring clinical status. Itaconate is known for its potent anti-inflammatory effects. To dissect its mechanisms, researchers commonly use cell-permeable esterified derivatives, such as dimethyl itaconate (DI) or 4-octyl itaconate (OI) [17, 19]. Studies utilizing these derivatives have demonstrated that itaconate suppresses inflammation by activating the nuclear factor erythroid-2 related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway [20], inhibiting inflammasome activation [21] and the NF-κB pathway [22] in macrophages. Treatment with itaconate or its derivatives has been shown to attenuate immune responses in macrophage-driven autoimmune/inflammatory disease models [17, 23]. Notably, although itaconate derivatives resemble itaconate, recent studies highlight that they may not fully recapitulate its effects, underscoring the need for an explicit distinction [19]. Given these potent anti-inflammatory properties, we sought to determine whether itaconate or its derivatives could play a suppressive role in autoimmune disease progression by simultaneously targeting DC and CD8+ T cell responses.

To investigate the role of itaconate and its derivative, DI, in vitiligo development and treatment, we combined models of endogenous Acod1 deficiency and exogenous DI supplementation in mice and then evaluated the effects of DI on the cutaneous infiltration and function of dendritic cells and CD8+ T cells in vivo and ex vivo.

Methods

Participant enrollment

Patients clinically diagnosed with either active or stable vitiligo were enrolled in this study from the Department of Dermatology, Xijing Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University. Patients meeting at least one of the following criteria were classified into the active vitiligo group: 1) new lesion appearance or noticeable lesion expansion within the past 6 months [24], 2) Koebner’s phenomenon, 3) confetti depigmentation, or 4) trichrome sign [25, 26]. Conversely, patients whose lesions had remained unchanged for more than 6 months were enrolled in the stable group to ensure their clinical stability [24]. The exclusion criteria included a history of additional inflammatory, autoimmune, or infectious diseases, as well as any topical or systemic therapy or narrow band-UVB phototherapy within the 3 months preceding sample collection. Age- and sex-matched healthy individuals were recruited from the Health Management Center of Xijing Hospital. The detailed characteristics of the patients and healthy volunteers are provided in Tables S1 and S2.

Animals and animal care

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Hunan Slaccas Laboratory Animal Company. Homozygous Acod1-knockout (Acod1 KO) mice were generated by mating C57BL/6 N-Acod1em1cyagen (Acod1±, stock no. KOCMP-16365-Acod1-B6N-VA) mice purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (https://www.cyagen.com/cn/zh-cn/sperm-bank-live/16365). The Acod1 KO mice and littermate wild-type controls were genotyped with the following primers: F1-TGTTACAGTCAGAGATGGAGAGG, R1-CACACACAGCAGCTCACAGTAG, R2-TGTGTCAGGTACGGTAATGAGTG using the following cycling conditions: 94 °C, 3 min; 94 °C 30 s, 62 °C 35 s, 72 °C 35 s (33 cycles); 72 °C, 5 min. Reactants were isolated on 2% agarose (Sigma-Aldrich) gels, yielding a 670 bp band for homozygotes and 464 bp for the wild-type allele and two bands with 670 bp and a 464 bp band for heterozygotes. KRT14-Kitl*4XTG2Bjl mice (Krt14-Kitl*, also referred to as stem cell factor (SCF) transgenic mice) and PMEL T cell receptor (TCR) transgenic mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. All the mice were housed at the laboratory animal center of Fourth Military Medical University and were fed and watered freely under pathogen-free and 12-h light‒dark cycle conditions at 22 °C‒25 °C and 50‒55% humidity. Littermates of the same sex or the purchased mice in the same batch were randomly assigned to experimental groups.

Induction of vitiligo in mice

Two different vitiligo induction models were used to determine the role of itaconate derivatives in controlling vitiligo development and inducing repigmentation. A melanoma-associated vitiligo mouse model was established as previously described [4, 27, 28]. This model recapitulates human vitiligo by employing a combination of B16F10 melanoma inoculation, surgical incision, and CD4+ Treg cell depletion to stimulate endogenous autoreactive cytotoxic CD8+ T cells that target melanocytes. On day 0, the dorsal skin in the left flank of 6–8-week-old mice was intradermally inoculated with 2 × 105 B16F10 melanoma cells. On days 4 and 10, CD4 neutralization antibodies (200 μg of CD4 antibody per 20 g of mouse) were intraperitoneally injected. The developed primary tumors were surgically removed on day 12. Spontaneous metastases were not observed during the experiments, and mice with recurrent primary tumors after surgery (< 2%) were excluded. At 5 weeks post-induction, the development of vitiligo in the mice was monitored via whole-mount immunofluorescence to quantify the number of melanocytes and CD8+ T cells in the tail skin epidermis. The mice that developed vitiligo (approximately 75%, as previously described [28]), which are infiltrated with CD8+ T cells in the tail epidermis [4, 28, 29], were used for subsequent studies. Mice without vitiligo induction were used as a negative control, and mice with established vitiligo were randomly assigned to the treatment or vehicle group. In the tail skin, melanocytes are positioned in both the epidermis and the hair follicle, which resemble human skin, while in mouse dorsal skin melanocytes are positioned in hair follicles, not within the epidermis [4]. Thus, tail skin depigmentation was used as the hallmark of vitiligo development.

The adoptive-transfer-based vitiligo mouse model was induced as previously reported [30]. Naïve PMEL TCR transgenic CD8+ T cells from the spleen were magnetically separated through negative selection (Miltenyi Biotec). Purified CD8+ T cells were injected retro-orbitally into 10–12-week-old SCF hosts at 106 cells per mouse. Recipient mice were sublethally γ irradiated (5.192 Gy) 1 day before transfer and received an intraperitoneal injection of 1 × 106 pfu. recombinant vaccinia virus expressing the cognate antigen PMEL (Genechem) on the same day as adoptive T cell transfer.

In vivo treatment experiments

The vitiligo model was administered intraperitoneally mostly at 20 mg DI (Sigma Aldrich) in 400 μl of sterile PBS per mouse or 50 mg/kg 4-octyl itaconate (OI) (MedChemExpress) in 40% hydroxypropyl-beta-cyclodextrin in PBS or PBS alone at 5 weeks post-tumor implantation. The mice were treated with DI/OI/PBS 5 times each week for 5 consecutive weeks. Upon treatment cessation, the mice were euthanized. The spleen, lymph node, blood, and tail skin were harvested for flow cytometry analysis, and the serum was collected to determine the itaconate concentration. We ensured blinding during sample collection and data analysis: both the experimenters and the data analysts were blinded to group identities. For repigmentation analysis, after 24 weeks of vitiligo induction, SCF-based vitiligo model mice were treated intraperitoneally 5 times per week with 20 mg DI per mouse for 24 consecutive weeks. Changes in the skin color of the tail were photographed at the indicated times. The area of depigmented tail skin was calculated with ImageJ.

Preparation of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and serum samples

Human PBMCs were isolated from 30 mL of peripheral blood drawn from patients in active state or healthy volunteers using Lymphoprep (Dakewe), according to the manufacturer's instructions. To obtain serum, fresh whole blood from humans and mice was centrifuged at 2500 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min and immediately quenched in liquid nitrogen. Serum samples were stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Prior to the experiments, the serum samples were transferred to −20 °C for 20 min and then thawed in an ice bath to decrease degradation.

Preparation of murine single cell suspensions

Tail skin was first incubated with Dispase II (2.5 mg/ml, Sigma Aldrich) for 1 h at 37℃. The epidermis was then removed and physically dissociated using a 40 μm filter (BD Falcon). Afterwards, the dermis was incubated with collagenase IV (1 mg/ml, Sigma Aldrich) and deoxyribonuclease I (2 mg/ml, Sigma Aldrich) for 1 h at 37℃, followed by mechanical dissociation using a 40 μm filter. Mouse skin-draining lymph nodes and spleens were mechanically crushed through 70 μm filters. Red blood cells from splenocytes were removed using ACK buffer (Thermo Fisher). All samples were filtered twice to obtain the remaining cells.

Cell culture, stimulation, and treatment

B16F10 melanoma cells (ATCC, CRL-6475) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Pricella) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Solarbio LIFE SCIENCES) for transient inoculation in mice. All remaining cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin but were used for different experimental purposes. Specifically, human PBMCs, mouse splenocytes, lymph node cells, and mouse skin-derived cells were seeded at (1–2) × 10⁶ cells/mL in 24- or 96-well plates for functional assays. THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages and seeded in a Transwell system for coculture experiments with T cells.

To validate the suitable concentration of DI in ex vivo [22] experiments, PBMCs isolated from vitiligo patient blood and vitiligo mouse splenocyte samples were treated with the different DI concentrations for 3 days, after which cell viability was determined via the CCK8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions (7 Sea Biotech). The cells were treated with 250 μM DI for subsequent experiments unless otherwise stated.

To detect DC maturation, whole blood from vitiligo patients was pretreated with DI for 18 h, followed by stimulation with 1 μg/mL LPS (Thermo Fisher) for 8 h. The red blood cells were then removed with ACK buffer (Thermo Fisher) followed by flow cytometry.

To identify the production of IL-12 by DCs, lymph node cells were pretreated with DI for 18 h in 24-well plates at 2 × 106 cells/500 μL culture medium and then were stimulated with 10 ng/ml IFN-γ (BioLegend) and 1 μg/mL LPS for an additional 16 h. Brefeldin A (eBioscience) was added to the cells, which were incubated for 6 h before sample collection for flow cytometry.

For analysis of activation, cytokines produced by CD8+ T cells in PBMCs, skin single cells, and mouse splenocytes were treated with DI for 18 h and then stimulated with cell activation cocktail (Cat#423,304, BioLegend) for 6 h or activated with anti-human CD3/CD28 beads (T&L Biological Technology) for 24 h. Notably, to investigate the direct role of DI in CD8+ T cells, CD8+ T cells were isolated from the PBMCs of patients with vitiligo using a CD8a+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For comparative evaluation, 0.25/5 mM itaconate (GLPBIO), the electrophilic compounds 0.05 mM dimethyl fumarate (GLPBIO) and 7.5 mM sulforaphane (GLPBIO) were diluted into culture media and prepared as previously described [19, 22, 31]. PBMCs were treated with this panel of compounds for 18 h and then stimulated with cell activation cocktail for 6 h followed by flow cytometry.

Human CD8⁺ T cells were isolated from PBMCs via magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec). To assess STAT phosphorylation, the cells were pretreated with DI for 16–24 h, stimulated with anti-human CD3/CD28 beads and IL-2 (100 ng/mL; PeproTech) for 30 min, and then collected. For Western blot analysis of STATs, the stimulation was extended to 1 or 2 h. To assess JAK phosphorylation, DI-pretreated cells were stimulated for 10 min with anti-human CD3/CD28 beads and IL-2 before collection for Western blot analysis.

Flow cytometry

Zombie Fixable Viability Dye (BioLegend) was used to exclude dead cells. Prior to staining for CD8⁺ T cells and DCs, the samples were blocked with TruStain FcX™ (anti-mouse CD16/32 or anti-human CD16/32; BioLegend) for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (purchased from BioLegend unless stated otherwise) for 30 min at 4 °C. For analysis of surface markers of DCs, CD8+ T cells and macrophages, the antibodies used were mouse-specific for CD45 (I3/2.3), CD3 (145-2C11), CD11c (N418), CD11b (M1/70), CCR7 (4B12), MHCII (M5/114.15.2, eBioscience), CD80 (16-10A1), CD86 (GL-1), CD8a (53–6.7), CD69 (H1.2F3), NKG2AB6 (16A11), CD49a (HMα1), and F4/80 (QA17A29); and human-specific for CD11c (3.9), CD11b (M1/70), CD14 (HCD14), CD15 (W6D3),CD16 (3G8), CD56 (MEM188), CD68 (Y1/82A), CD80 (W17149D), CD86 (BU63), HLA-DR (L243), CD8a (RPA-T8), and CD69 (FN50).

For intracellular staining, surface-stained cells were fixed and permeabilized with the FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience), followed by incubation with anti-mouse IFN-γ (XMG1.2), anti-human IFN-γ (4S.B3), anti-human/mouse Gzmb (QA16A02), and CD68(Y1/82A, Elabscience) antibodies. Data was acquired on a LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star).

For the detection of phosphoproteins, the cells were fixed for 15 min at 37 °C with 2% (wt/vol) paraformaldehyde (PFA), permeabilized for 30 min with 90% (vol/vol) methanol on ice, and then stained for CD8 and phospho-STAT1 (A15158B), phospho-STAT3 (13A3-1), and phospho-STAT5 (SRBCZX, eBioscience). Analyses were gated on live single cells. Isotype controls were used for gating. The gating strategies are detailed in Figures S3, S5, S8, and S9.

Macrophage and T-cell coculture

Using a 24-well Transwell coculture system (0.4 μm pore; Corning), we differentiated THP-1 cells into M0 macrophages in the lower chamber. Specifically, 1 × 10⁶ wild-type (WT) or Acod1 KO THP-1 cells (Cyagen, SKO-1356-1B12) were seeded and treated with 100 ng/mL PMA for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. The macrophages were subsequently stimulated for 24 h with 20 ng/mL IFN-γ and 100 ng/mL LPS to polarize them to the M1 phenotype and induce itaconate production. For the rescue experiment, Acod1 KO macrophages were supplemented with exogenous 5 mM itaconate during this polarization step. Following macrophage polarization, 2 × 10⁶ sorted CD8⁺ T cells, which had been prestimulated with CD3/CD28 antibodies for 24 h, were added to the upper chamber of the Transwell insert to coculture with the macrophages for an additional 24 h. After coculture, CD8⁺ T cells were harvested from the upper chamber for further cytometric analysis.

Whole-mount immunofluorescence

Tail skins were collected from the different experimental groups of vitiligo mice both before and after treatment. Briefly, whole-mount tail skins were treated with 20 mM EDTA to separate the epidermis from the dermis. Sebaceous glands in the epidermis were removed under a stereomicroscope. Epidermal tissues were fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min, washed, fixed with 0.3% H2O2 in methanol for 20 min at −20℃, and washed with PBS, and blocked for 1 h in blocking buffer (2% normal donkey serum, 1% BSA, and 0.3% Triton in PBS). Primary antibodies against CD8 (Abcam, Cat#ab22378, dilution 1:200), and MelanA (Abcam, Cat#ab210546, dilution 1:200), were incubated with the epidermis at 4 °C overnight. The samples were washed and incubated with secondary antibody and DAPI (dilution 1:1000) for 8 h at 4 °C. The samples were then washed and mounted in 50% glycerol for imaging. Immunofluorescence images were taken with confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880; Carl Zeiss). Images were obtained via a 10 × 0.5 objective lens for quantitative analysis. Z-stacks were obtained at a 512 × 512 resolution. Analysis of the immunofluorescence data was performed as previously described [4] using Imaris software with the 3D visualization module (Bitplane).

Determination of itaconate by liquid chromatograph‒mass spectrometer (LC‒MS)

Detailed information about the itaconate concentration determination protocol can be found in Additional file 1: Supplementary methods.

RNA-seq analysis

CD8+ T cells were purified for bulk RNA-seq analysis. The cells were magnetically sorted (Cat#130–117-044, Miltenyi Biotec) from the skin-draining lymph nodes of wild-type and PBS/DI-treated vitiligo mice at week 11. Total RNA was isolated from CD8+ T cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen). Library construction was performed on the DNBSEQ platform (BGI-NGS-JK-RNA-007 A0, BGI Genomics). The data were analyzed via the BGI online system Dr.Tom (https://biosys.bgi.com/#/report/login). Differential gene expressions were performed via the DEGseq method with a threshold of log2 fold change (log2FC) > 1 and an FDR < 0.05.

scRNA-seq data from a dataset of vitiligo-affected skin [4, 32] and peripheral blood [33, 34] were reanalyzed via the interactive online platform Omicsmart (http://www.omicsmart.com).

Western blot analysis

Protein samples from human purified CD8+ T cells were lysed with RIPA buffer with 2% protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), followed by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 r.p.m. for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for SDS‒PAGE. Total protein samples were resolved on 8% SDS–PAGE gels and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes via a wet transfer system. The membranes were blocked in 5% (w/v) fat-free dried milk diluted in TBS-Tween for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at a 1:1000 dilution at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. The primary antibodies used included anti-p-JAK1 (Abcam, Cat#138,005), anti-JAK1 (Proteintech, Cat#66,466–1-Ig), anti-p-JAK2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#3776), anti-JAK2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#3230), anti-p-JAK3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#5031), anti-JAK3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 8827), anti-p-STAT1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#7649), anti-STAT1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#14,994), anti-p-STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#9145), anti-STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#9139), and anti-β-actin (Proteintech, Cat# 66,009–1-Ig, dilution 1:20,000). After detecting the phosphorylated protein, the membrane was stripped with Stripping Buffer for polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (CoWin Biosciences, Cat#CW0056M) and reprobed for the corresponding total protein. In all cases, β-actin was detected on the same membrane as a loading control for both the phosphorylated and total forms of each target. Bands were visualized via the enhanced chemiluminescence reagent western blotting detection system (Bio-Rad).

CFSE cell proliferation assay

Freshly collected human PBMCs were resuspended at 106 cells/ml in PBS and then stained with CFSE working solution (BioLegend). The CFSE-stained cells were subsequently washed and seeded into 96-well round bottom plates (2 × 105 cells/200 μl) and were treated with DI and/or stimulated with anti-human CD3/CD28 beads at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After incubation for 5 days, the cells were harvested and stained with an anti-CD8 antibody (BioLegend) and Zombie Fixable Viability (BioLegend) at 4 °C for 30 min and then used for flow cytometry analyses.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of murine serum IL-12 were measured via Quantikine ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Elabscience). The optical density values were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm via a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher).

Statistics

At least three independent experiments were performed for all ex vivo studies and two were performed for in vivo studies. All the statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 8. The data are expressed as median (25th and 75th percentiles), mean ± SEM, or mean ± SD. Dual comparisons were tested with unpaired Student’s t test. Three or more groups were analyzed by Kruskal‒Wallis H test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance is denoted by * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, and **** P < 0.0001.

Results

Endogenous itaconate deficiency worsens vitiligo development in mice

To understand the association between itaconate and vitiligo, we first evaluated the circulatory abundance of itaconate in a cohort of 38 vitiligo patients and found that it was markedly increased in both active and stable disease compared to healthy controls [0.057 (0.014–0.083) μM and 0.047 (0.032–0.058) μM vs. 0.006 (0.005–0.010) μM, respectively] (Fig. 1A; Additional file 1: Table 1 and 2). Endogenous serum itaconate levels were not significantly correlated with disease extent, as measured by Vitiligo Area Scoring Index (VASI) score (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A) or disease duration (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B), and no significant difference was observed across different disease activity stages (Additional file 1: Fig. S1C). Next, we established a vitiligo mouse model that recapitulates the pathology of human vitiligo by implanting B16F10 melanoma cells, depleting Tregs, and surgically excising the tumor (Fig. 1B). The level of circulating itaconate was also higher in vitiligo mice [0.016 (0.012–0.021) μM] than in uninduced mice [0.011 (0.006–0.014) μM] when visible skin depigmentation occurred (Fig. 1C). Since the production of itaconate is predominantly induced in stimulated macrophages by the enzyme Acod1 [17] (Additional file 1: Fig. S1D), we therefore examined whether upregulated itaconate is derived from macrophages in vitiligo. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) [4] revealed upregulated expression of proinflammatory genes—including STAT1, CXCL10, CCL18, and S100A9 [35]—in skin macrophages, indicating their activated state (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). While scRNA-seq failed to detect Acod1 in patient lesions, Acod1 was exclusively expressed at low levels in CD14+ monocytes (macrophage precursors) among peripheral immune cells, albeit without a significant difference between patients and controls (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B and C). In line with this observation, we detected a significant increase in both CD14++CD16− classical (cMo) and CD14++CD16+ intermediate (intMo) monocytes in patients (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A and B). Furthermore, we observed an increase in CD14+CD68+ CD80+ macrophages (classical activated M1-like proinflammatory macrophages) [36, 37] in the peripheral blood of human vitiligo patients (Additional file 1: Figure S3C), as well as in the skin-draining lymph nodes (F4/80+CD11b+) of mice (Additional file 1: Fig. S3D). Collectively, these data suggest that itaconate is probably macrophage-derived in vitiligo.

To determine the role of itaconate in vitiligo pathogenesis in vivo, we generated itaconate-deficient mice by globally deleting Acod1 [38] and established a vitiligo model in these mice (Fig. 1D). PCR confirmed the efficient Acod1 deletion in the mice (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A). Acod1 KO mice developed more rapid and severe tail skin depigmentation than wild-type (WT) controls (Fig. 1E and Additional file 1: Fig. S4B). Furthermore, more extensive depigmentation was observed on the dorsal and ventral skin of Acod1 KO mice at weeks 8 and 11 postinduction (Fig. 1F). These results demonstrate that itaconate exerts endogenous beneficial effects after vitiligo induction.

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that macrophage-derived itaconate mediates the protective effect by suppressing the activation of CD8+ T cells. To test this, we cocultured WT or Acod1 KO macrophages with CD8+ T cells. CD8+ T cells cocultured with Acod1 KO macrophages produced significantly more IFN-γ than those co-cultured with WT macrophages. This enhanced production was reversed by itaconate supplementation (Fig. 1G). Thus, our data demonstrates that macrophage-derived itaconate suppresses CD8+ T cell function.

Exogenous itaconate derivatives control and reverse vitiligo in mice

Itaconate's conjugated unsaturated double bond structure confers high polarity and low electrophilicity, leading to poor cell permeability [19]. Researchers therefore widely use itaconate derivatives to investigate its biological roles [19] (Additional file 1: Fig. S1D). To investigate whether exogenous itaconate derivatives inhibit the vitiligo development, we administered DI, OI, or vehicle PBS to WT vitiligo mice at the onset of epidermal CD8+ T cell infiltration (Fig. 2A and B). Compared with that in PBS-treated mice, tail skin depigmentation in DI/OI-treated mice was slower and milder (Fig. 2C). After 6 weeks of treatment, treatment with DI and OI dramatically attenuated depigmentation, resulting in tail pigmentation levels of 77.30 ± 5.43% and 75.1 ± 6.67%, respectively, compared to 52.94 ± 11.64% in the vehicle-treated mice. (Fig. 2D and Additional file 1: Fig. S4C). DI and OI administration attenuated the vitiligo pathology, as whole-mount immunofluorescent images revealed attenuated melanocyte death and decreased CD8+ T cell densities in tail skin (Fig. 2E). This may be partly because OI can directly hydrolyze into itaconate [21] and exogenous DI increases the biosynthesis of itaconate [39]. As expected, supplementation with DI and OI led to a significant increase in serum itaconate concentrations compared to the vehicle control [0.057 (0.036–0.101) μM and 0.067 (0.030–0.083) μM vs. 0.017 (0.010–0.018) μM, respectively] (Fig. 2F).

In addition to thwarting depigmentation, inducing repigmentation is also the primary concern for vitiligo care. To better observe the repigmentation phenomenon, we established an adoptive transfer-based vitiligo model in SCF transgenic mice [30], where melanocytes reside throughout the epidermis and hair follicles (Fig. 2G). We only used DI for the subsequent experiments because of its better solubility in PBS. We intraperitoneally treated mice with stable vitiligo (> 12 weeks following vitiligo induction) [30] with DI 3 times weekly for 24 consecutive weeks and observed visually apparent repigmentation in DI-treated mice compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 2H and I). Together, these findings suggest that treatment with itaconate derivatives, especially DI, shortens white patch expansion and promotes repigmentation.

DI dampens vitiligo-induced CD8+ T cell responses in vivo

To test whether DI influences the CD8+ T cell function, we evaluated the frequencies and cytokine production of CD8+ T cells from the vitiligo mice following DI treatment. After treatment cessation, DI reduced the number of CD8+ T cells in the tail epidermis, while the counts in the dermis, lymph nodes, spleen and blood remained unaffected (Fig. 3A and B, Additional file 1: Fig. S5A-D). Importantly, the expression of the CD8+ T-cell activation marker CD69 decreased after DI treatment in the tail skin epidermis, dermis, spleen, and blood, despite being unaffected in the lymph nodes (Fig. 3C-F and Additional file 1: Fig. S5E-H). Furthermore, in DI-treated vitiligo mice, CD8+ T cells produced less IFN-γ in the epidermis and skin-draining lymph nodes but showed unchanged levels in the dermis and spleen compared with those in controls (Fig. 3G and H, Additional file 1: Fig. S5I and J). These results suggest DI suppresses epidermal CD8+ T cell infiltration and alleviates the activation of CD8+ T cells systemically and locally.

DI inhibits effector function, cytotoxicity, and proliferation in vitiligo CD8+ T cells ex vivo

To further ascertain the effect of DI on CD8+ T cell functionality, ex vivo lymphocytes isolated from mice and humans with vitiligo were cultured with or without DI. CD3/CD28 bead-activated mouse skin and splenic CD8+ T cells were incubated with 250 μM DI—the highest concentration without affecting cell viability [22] (Additional file 1: Figure S6A) —and this treatment repressed IFN-γ production (P = 0.034 for skin, P = 0.009 for spleen) (Fig. 4A, B). Although DI did not alter skin CD8+ T cell Gzmb production (Fig. 4C), it reduced the number of IFN-γ+Gzmb+ skin cells and splenic Gzmb+ CD8+ T cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S6B and Fig. 4D). In parallel with the response of murine CD8+ T cells, DI dose-dependently reduced IFN-γ expression in human vitiligo CD8+ T cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S6C). Particularly, we found that IFN-γ production was also suppressed in sorted DI-treated CD8+ T cells, indicating that DI can directly impact CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4E). Likewise, human activated vitiligo DI-treated CD8+T cells also exhibited lower expression of Gzmb (Fig. 4F). In addition, DI reduced CD69 expression and the proliferation of activated human vitiligo CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4G and H). These data further confirmed that DI represses CD8+ T cell effector function, cytotoxic activity, activation, and proliferation.

Since DI is not metabolized into itaconate intracellularly [39], and the biological effects of itaconate occur at millimolar [19], we next assessed whether DI-mediated inhibition of CD8⁺ T cells is independent of itaconate but dependent on electrophilicity. Notably, treatment with 250 μM DI and 250 μM itaconate resulted in comparable intracellular itaconate levels in CD8⁺ T cells, both of which were lower than those observed under 5 mM itaconate treatment (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, parallel treatment with 250 μM DI, 5 mM itaconate, the classical electrophile dimethyl fumarate and sulforaphane significantly suppressed IFN-γ production—with the exception of 250 μM itaconate (Fig. 4J). These results demonstrate that DI acts primarily through its electrophilic properties rather than via itaconate.

DI suppresses the JAK-STAT pathway, which disrupts CD8+ T cell function

We next explored how DI regulates CD8+ T cells. Itaconate has been shown to activate Nrf2 while inhibiting NF-κB signaling in macrophages [20, 22]. Both the Nrf2 pathway and the NF-κB pathway can regulate T cell function [40, 41]. This prompted us to test the effect of DI on the Nrf2 and NF-κB pathways in CD8+ T cells from vitiligo patients. However, Nrf2 expression in vitiligo-affected CD8+ T cells was higher than that in healthy controls (Additional file 1: Fig. S7A). Furthermore, Furthermore, DI pretreatment reduced Nrf2 expression in activated vitiligo CD8+ T cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B). Consistently, the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385, the NF-κB pathway agonist TNF-α, or the nonclassical NF-κB pathway agonist AZD5582 failed to reverse DI-induced suppression of IFN-γ in vitiligo CD8+ T cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S7C-E). These results suggest that DI suppresses CD8+ T cell function independently of Nrf2 or NF-κB pathway inhibition.

Therefore, we performed bulk RNA-seq on DI/vehicle-treated CD8+ T cells from the lymph nodes of vitiligo model mice (Fig. 3A). Differential global transcriptomes were observed: (1) control vs vehicle-treated vitiligo and (2) vehicle- vs DI-treated vitiligo mice (Fig. 5A and B). Compared with control mice, vehicle-treated vitiligo mice presented predominantly upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 5 A and B). The Venn diagram identified a subset of 992 aberrantly highly expressed genes that were significantly downregulated following DI treatment (Fig. 5C). Gene set enrichment analysis revealed significant suppression of “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” and “T cell receptor signaling pathway” in the DI groups (Fig. 5D and Additional file 1: Fig. S7F). As the JAK-STAT pathway controls T cell cytokine release via STAT phosphorylation [7, 42], we hypothesized that DI might inhibit this pathway. Consistent with these findings, flow cytometry revealed that DI suppressed the phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT3, but not STAT5, in CD8+ T cells from human vitiligo patients (Fig. 5E). Western blotting verified that DI reduced p-STAT1/3 in sorted vitiligo CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5F, G). Upon cytokine-receptor engagement, the associated upstream JAKs are tyrosine-phosphorylated and subsequently phosphorylate and activate downstream STATs [43]. We next investigated the upstream mechanism and found that DI pretreatment reduced the phosphorylation of JAK2 (p-JAK2) (Fig. 5H), while the levels of p-JAK1 and p-JAK3 remained unchanged (Additional file 1: Fig. S7G, H). Together, these data suggest that DI inhibits CD8+ T cell function at least in part through the JAK2-STAT1/3 signaling axis.

Importantly, CD49a, Gzmb, and NKG2D expression defines specific subsets of CD8+ T cells executing cytotoxic and proinflammatory functions in vitiligo skin [44–46]. NKG2A is a CD8+ T cell inhibitory receptor that constrains overactivation while maintaining virus-specific responses [47]. In vitiligo mice, CD8+ T cells exhibited a cytotoxic and proinflammatory phenotype that was more pronounced than that in control or DI-treated vitiligo mice, as indicated by elevated expression of Csf1, Ifitm1, CD49a, Gzmb, NKG2D, and NKG2A (Fig. 5I). We further confirmed decreased CD49a, NKG2A, and Gzmb expression in DI- vs vehicle- treated CD8+ T cells at the protein level (Fig. 5 J-L). Collectively, these data suggest that DI suppresses the expression of cytotoxic/proinflammatory genes, impairing CD8+ T cell function.

DI governs DC infiltration and function in vitiligo both in vivo and ex vivo

Since DCs are essential for T cell activation and expansion, we asked whether DI inhibits the DC response. Vitiligo mice displayed significantly expanded population of CD11c+ cells in both the epidermis, and this population was declined after DI treatment (Fig. 6A and B, Additional file 1: Fig. S8A and B). In contrast, the frequency of DCs in skin-draining lymph nodes was lower in vehicle-treated vitiligo mice than in negative control and DI-treated vitiligo mice (Fig. 6C and Additional file 1: Fig. S8C). To explain this variation in DCs in the lymph nodes, we analyzed migratory DCs (CD11cintMHCIIhi) and resident DCs (CD11chiMHCIIint) in the lymph nodes, and observed a decrease in the number of migratory DCs in mice treated with DI (Fig. 6D), and comparable resident DCs between these groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S9A), suggesting that these two subsets of DCs cannot account for the decreased percentage of CD11c+ DCs in vehicle-treated vitiligo mice. Within the spleen, conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) constitute two subsets: cDC1s (CD11c+CD11b−CD8a+) and cDC2s (CD11c+CD11b+CD8a−) [48]. cDC1s work in priming Th1 responses, whereas cDC2s induce follicular helper T cell responses [48]. However, the percentages of splenic cDC1s and cDC2s were similar in vehicle- and DI-treated vitiligo mice (Additional file 1: Fig. S9B-D). These results suggest that DI alters DC infiltration in the epidermis, spleen, and lymph nodes and inhibits subsets of migratory DCs in the lymph nodes.

We further monitored the role of DI in DC mobilization, function, and maturation. In the inflamed environment, DCs migrate from peripheral tissues to lymph nodes in a manner dependent on chemokine receptor CCR7 signaling [49]. In vitiligo lymph nodes, we found that DI treatment prominently abrogated the CCR7 expression in CD11c+ cells, suggesting that chemotaxis of DCs was suppressed (Fig. 6E and Additional file 1: Fig. S8C). DC-derived IL-12 is indispensable for Th1 differentiation [50]. Although lymph node DCs expressed similar levels of IL-12 between the vehicle and DI-treated groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S9E), we observed that DI treatment downregulated the elevated serum IL-12 levels in vitiligo mice (Fig. 6F). In addition, culture of vitiligo lymph node cells exposed to DI mitigated the expression of IL-12 (Fig. 6G). Although the expression of CD80 and CD86 was similar in DCs among vehicle- and DI-treated vitiligo lymph nodes at the treatment endpoint (Additional file 1: Fig. S9F and G), the expression of the costimulatory molecules CD80 and HLA-DR, but not that of CD86, was inhibited in DI-treated human vitiligo DCs ex vivo (Fig. 6H, Additional file 1: Fig. S9H and I). Together, our results demonstrate that DI suppresses CCR7-dependent DC migration, IL-12 production, and maturation, suppressing DC-triggered immunity.

Discussion

Metabolite-derived small molecules show promise for targeting immune cells in autoimmune therapy [15, 16]. Here, we uncovered a previously unknown role of the itaconate derivative DI in the inhibition of autoimmunity via the coordinated mitigation of DC and CD8+ T cell responses (Additional file 1: Graphical Abstract). Our findings indicate that DI suppresses CD8+ T cell effector function via the JAK-STAT pathway, in contrast to its previously shown role in modulating macrophage state through the Nrf2 and NF-κB pathways. These collective findings highlight that suppressing DC and CD8+ T cell responses is an essential strategy to control vitiligo and related autoimmune diseases.

Itaconate, a metabolic product of reprogrammed macrophages, plays critical roles in infection [16], fibrosis [17], and inflammation [16, 22]. In contrast to the low physiological levels observed in healthy controls, we found that itaconate was upregulated in both humans and mice with vitiligo. Since macrophages are the major source of itaconate production, we observed an increase in macrophages not only in circulation of patients but also in the lymph nodes of mice during the early stages of disease induction. This observation suggests that the upregulation of itaconate might be associated with macrophage activation [18] in vitiligo pathogenesis. Furthermore, we showed that serum itaconate levels are comparable between patients with active and stable vitiligo. The elevated level of itaconate is likely a result of prolonged macrophage activation, given that inflammation is perpetuated in stable vitiligo [51]. A substantial sample size is needed to further validate this phenomenon. Interestingly, the itaconate levels in DI-treated mice (~ 0.06 μM, Fig. 2F) were comparable to those in vitiligo patients (Fig. 1A). This could be attributed to interspecies differences in itaconate pharmacokinetics, along with physiological factors such as body weight, surface area, and itaconate distribution [52, 53].

We demonstrated the beneficial effects of itaconate and its derivatives in a mouse model of vitiligo. The findings from Acod1 KO mice revealed that deficiency of itaconate exacerbated vitiligo development. Treatment with itaconate derivatives increased circulating itaconate levels and suppressed immunogenic melanocyte death. Our findings are supported by observations in other disease models. For example, itaconate upregulation is also observed during the acute phases of intracerebral hemorrhage and plays beneficial roles [54], indicating its role as a protective defense mechanism against broad pathophysiological insults. The immune compensatory response of itaconate is reminiscent of the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 [55, 56], which is also elevated under inflammatory or steady-state conditions to balance immune activity and prevent self-tissue damage [56–59].

In a vitiligo mouse model mediated by endogenous autoreactive CD8+ T cells, we found that DI suppressed aberrant CD8+ T cell responses, expanding its known anti-inflammatory effects. DI suppresses CD8+ T cell’s aggregation, cytokine production, and activation in the epidermis, suggesting that DI attenuates the local cutaneous immune response which is the most important event for direct melanocyte death. While in the dermis and other lymphoid organs, DI inhibits CD8+ T cell activation and effector function without altering their proportion. These results demonstrated that DI suppresses CD8+ T cell functional integrity in these tissues but has slight effects on T cell populations. Ex vivo DI supplementation analysis further confirmed that DI directly impairs the effector function, cytotoxicity, and proliferation of CD8⁺ T cells derived from both vitiligo model mice and patients. Zhao et al. reported that myeloid-derived suppressor cells-derived itaconate inhibits the ability of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells to attenuate antitumor immunity [60], suggesting that DI and itaconate share broader similarities in their capacity to impair CD8⁺ T cell functional integrity. Notably, we demonstrate here that the effect of DI on CD8⁺ T cells is independent of itaconate but instead attributable to its electrophilic properties. Recent studies further emphasize that the characteristics of DI and itaconate are distinct and should be explicitly differentiated [19, 61].

DI or OI have been reported to suppress stimulated macrophages through Nrf2 activation and NF-κB suppression to attenuate macrophage-driven diseases [22, 23, 62]. Unlike prior findings, we identified a novel mechanism by which DI dampened the JAK-STAT pathway in vitiligo CD8+ T cells. The divergent mechanisms of DI on CD8+ T cells and macrophages may be partly due to distinct activation signals and downstream effectors, as macrophages use the LPS/TLR4 pathway [63], whereas CD8+ T cell activation requires TCR/CD3-mediated and costimulation signals [64]. Another plausible explanation is that immune cells activate distinct pathways in various inflammatory contexts, which are controlled by distinct transcriptional profiles or metabolic states [65–67]. Moreover, itaconate and its derivatives bind to specific sites on target proteins or modify cysteine residues to modulate the activity of functional proteins [21, 68, 69]. Thus, besides inhibiting the phosphorylation of JAK2, DI may also modify STAT1 and STAT3 in CD8+ T cells. These modifications provide an in-depth understanding of the mechanisms underlying JAK-STAT pathway inhibition. Together, these findings highlight DI’s specific anti-inflammatory effects across diseases and cell types.

Our study identified DI as a JAK2 inhibitor. Unlike conventional JAKi, which cannot prevent vitiligo relapse due to their inability to deplete cutaneous resident memory T (Trm) cells [70], DI may have unique immunomodulatory properties. This speculation is supported by findings that itaconate and OI reduce liver CD103+ Trm cell numbers in murine models [71]. We therefore hypothesize that DI could directly target pathogenic Trm cells in the skin, thereby offering a superior strategy for preventing recurrence. Further investigations are needed to determine whether DI is superior to direct JAKi and achieves durable efficacy.

DCs prime CD8+ T cells. We identified the altered DCs (CD11c+ cells) in the epidermis, spleen, and lymph nodes of vitiligo model mice, demonstrating that DCs are involved in the melanocyte-specific immune responses. Within the spleen, the cDC1 and cDC2 subsets were comparable between the groups, indicating that splenic DCs are probably not linked to T-cell response stimulation in the mouse model of vitiligo. During inflammation, DCs seeded in peripheral tissues such as the skin acquire maturation phenotype with high expression of MHCII and migrate to draining lymph nodes in a CCR7-dependent manner [72]. These DC subsets are migratory DCs, and they present peptides that interact with T cells in the lymph nodes [72]. In our study, while CD80/CD86 expression was unaffected in vivo, DI impaired DC migration via reduced CCR7 and migratory DC numbers. This may reduce the degree of crosstalk between DCs and CD8+ T cells in the lymph nodes and thus inhibit elicited CD8+ T cell migration to the skin. Moreover, we observed that the IL-12 serum level was alleviated by DI in vitiligo mice, which is in accordance with our ex vivo findings that DI suppresses lymph node DC-derived IL-12 release. Our findings suggest that DI suppresses the DC response, especially in lymph nodes, by dampening DC infiltration, migration, and cytokine production. Intriguingly, the percentage of DCs in the skin-draining lymph nodes decreased in vehicle-treated vitiligo model mice. This reduction may occur because DCs undergo apoptosis following interaction with T cells [73, 74] or because the total number of lymphocytes increases, resulting in a relative decrease in the DC proportion.

Several limitations of this study need to be mentioned. Recently, Yang et al. reported that CGRP signaling enhances DC‒CD8⁺ T-cell interactions in vitiligo-affected skin and that blockade of this axis alleviates the disease, highlighting the critical role of the interaction in vitiligo pathogenesis [9]. Although our study focused on the individual responses of DCs and CD8+ T cells, we cannot exclude the possibility that DI may also suppress CD8+ T cells indirectly—either by inhibiting DC maturation/cytokine secretion (and thereafter suppressing CD8+ T cell clonal expansion and activation) or by disrupting DC-CD8+ T cell interactions—in addition to its direct inhibitory effect. Concurrent direct and indirect inhibition may allow DI to achieve more robust suppression of both the magnitude and quality of CD8+ T cell immune responses. Our work highlights the divergence between DI and itaconate but focuses on the effects of DI in treating vitiligo. It will be also crucial to conduct more comparative experiments to evaluate the superiority/inferiority of DI and itaconate in vitiligo. Furthermore, our findings indicate favorable roles of DI in inducing repigmentation. While adoptive-transfer-based vitiligo mice may rarely experience spontaneous repigmentation by week 12 postinduction [30], we administered DI in a stable model at 24 weeks postinduction to preclude this possibility. Nonetheless, the underlying mechanisms of DI-mediated repigmentation remain to be fully elucidated.

Conclusions

Overall, we demonstrated a novel strategy attenuating DC and CD8+ T cell responses to suppress the initiation/effector phase of antigen-specific autoimmune responses shown in vitiligo. Our data suggests that DI represents a promising agent to halt vitiligo development by coregulating DCs and CD8+ T cells. DI has emerged as a promising candidate to optimize vitiligo therapy. Since vitiligo is a paradigm of a cell-specific autoimmune disease with a well-defined mechanism involving DCs and CD8+ T cells, the therapeutic strategy of cotargeting these cells warrant further investigation in other autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis.
