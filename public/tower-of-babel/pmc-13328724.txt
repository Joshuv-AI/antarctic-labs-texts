Hybrid reverse knowledge distillation for adversarial example detection
Authors: Hyun Kwon, Joo Bon Maeng, Dae-Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep neural networks have achieved remarkable success in various computer vision tasks, yet they remain vulnerable to adversarial examples—carefully crafted perturbations that are imperceptible to humans but cause misclassification. Detecting such adversarial inputs is crucial for deploying reliable AI systems, particularly in safety-critical applications such as medical diagnosis and autonomous driving. In this paper, we propose a novel adversarial detection method based on Hybrid Reverse Knowledge Distillation (Hybrid RKD). Our approach trains a multi-scale decoder to reconstruct intermediate feature representations of a frozen teacher encoder using only clean images. The key insight is that adversarial perturbations cause feature-level distortions that the decoder, trained exclusively on normal data, cannot accurately reconstruct. We further enhance detection performance by incorporating Mahalanobis-style statistical distance metrics that capture distribution-level anomalies. Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10 and ISIC2018 datasets demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art detection performance against various adversarial attacks including FGSM, PGD, DeepFool, C&W, and AutoAttack, with average AUROC scores of 0.790 and 0.929 across five attack categories, respectively. Our hybrid approach consistently outperforms existing detection methods including LID, Mahalanobis, and ODIN, while requiring no adversarial examples during training.Keywords: Adversarial example detection, Reverse knowledge distillation, Deep learning security, Anomaly detection, Feature reconstructionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Deep neural networks have demonstrated exceptional performance across a wide range of computer vision tasks, including image classification1, object detection2, and medical image analysis3. These networks have become fundamental components in many real-world applications, from autonomous vehicles to clinical decision support systems. However, despite their impressive capabilities, deep neural networks are inherently vulnerable to adversarial examples, which are maliciously crafted inputs that contain imperceptible perturbations designed to cause misclassification4–11. This vulnerability poses significant security concerns, particularly in safety-critical applications where incorrect predictions could lead to severe consequences. Recent studies have demonstrated that adversarial attacks on medical machine learning systems could have serious implications for patient safety and clinical decision-making12,13.

Adversarial attacks can be broadly categorized into white-box attacks, where the attacker has full knowledge of the target model, and black-box attacks, where such knowledge is limited. Notable attack methods include the Fast Gradient Sign Method5, which generates perturbations in a single step using the gradient sign, Projected Gradient Descent14, an iterative variant that produces stronger attacks through multiple gradient steps, and DeepFool15, which computes minimal perturbations to cross decision boundaries. Additionally, optimization-based methods such as the Carlini and Wagner attack16 generate highly effective adversarial examples while minimizing perturbation magnitude. Recent work has also introduced AutoAttack17, an ensemble of diverse parameter-free attacks that provides reliable evaluation of adversarial robustness, and adaptive attacks18 that can circumvent many proposed defenses. The existence of these diverse attack strategies necessitates robust detection mechanisms that can identify adversarial inputs before they compromise system integrity.

Various approaches have been proposed to detect adversarial examples. Feature-based methods analyze intermediate representations to distinguish adversarial from clean inputs by examining local intrinsic dimensionality characteristics19 or modeling class-conditional feature distributions using Mahalanobis distance20. Some approaches combine kernel density estimation with Bayesian uncertainty for detection21. Input transformation methods apply temperature scaling and input perturbation to amplify differences between normal and adversarial samples22, while feature squeezing techniques reduce input complexity through bit-depth reduction and spatial smoothing23. While these methods have shown promise in certain scenarios, they suffer from several limitations. First, many existing methods require adversarial examples during training, which limits their adaptability to new attack types that may emerge after deployment. Second, most approaches rely on single-scale feature analysis, which may fail to capture perturbation effects that manifest differently across various levels of abstraction. Third, existing detection methods often exhibit inconsistent performance across different attack types, achieving high accuracy against some attacks while performing poorly against others.

To address these limitations, we propose a novel detection framework based on Hybrid Reverse Knowledge Distillation. Unlike traditional knowledge distillation where a student network learns to mimic a teacher outputs24, our approach employs a reverse paradigm in which we train a decoder to reconstruct intermediate feature representations from the deepest layer features of a frozen teacher encoder. This approach is inspired by recent advances in anomaly detection using student-teacher networks25,26. The fundamental insight is that a decoder trained exclusively on clean images learns to model the manifold of normal feature representations. When presented with adversarial inputs, whose features deviate from this learned manifold, the decoder produces elevated reconstruction errors that serve as anomaly indicators. Furthermore, we enhance detection performance by incorporating Mahalanobis-style statistical distance metrics that capture distribution-level anomalies, providing complementary detection signals to the reconstruction-based approach.

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows. First, we introduce a reverse knowledge distillation framework for adversarial detection that requires only clean images for training, eliminating the need to generate or collect adversarial samples during the training phase. This characteristic makes our method practical and adaptable to new domains without prior knowledge of potential attack strategies. Second, we propose a multi-scale feature reconstruction approach that captures perturbation effects at different levels of abstraction, from low-level textures to high-level semantic features, enabling more comprehensive detection of diverse adversarial perturbations. Third, we develop a hybrid anomaly scoring mechanism that combines reconstruction-based and statistical distance-based metrics, providing complementary detection signals that improve robustness across diverse attack types. Fourth, we demonstrate through extensive experiments on CIFAR-1027 and ISIC201828 datasets that our method achieves state-of-the-art detection performance against various adversarial attacks, validating the effectiveness and generalizability of our approach across different domains and image resolutions.

Related work

In this section, we review the existing literature related to our proposed method. We first discuss various adversarial attack methods that pose threats to deep neural networks. We then examine existing adversarial detection approaches and their limitations. Finally, we review knowledge distillation techniques and their recent applications in anomaly detection.

Adversarial attacks

Adversarial examples were first discovered by Szegedy et al.4, who demonstrated that imperceptible perturbations could cause deep neural networks to misclassify images with high confidence. This discovery revealed a fundamental vulnerability in deep learning systems and sparked extensive research into both attack and defense mechanisms. Goodfellow et al.5 proposed the Fast Gradient Sign Method, a computationally efficient single-step attack that computes perturbations using the sign of the input gradient with respect to the loss function. This method generates adversarial examples rapidly but may produce suboptimal perturbations due to its single-step nature.

To address the limitations of single-step attacks, Madry et al.14 introduced Projected Gradient Descent, an iterative attack that applies multiple gradient steps with projection onto an epsilon-ball constraint. This approach produces stronger adversarial examples by iteratively refining the perturbations while ensuring they remain within the specified perturbation budget. Projected Gradient Descent is widely considered one of the strongest first-order attacks and serves as a standard benchmark for evaluating adversarial robustness. Moosavi-Dezfooli et al.15 developed DeepFool, which takes a different approach by iteratively computing minimal perturbations required to cross decision boundaries. Unlike gradient sign-based methods that use a fixed perturbation budget, DeepFool finds the smallest perturbation necessary to change the classification result, often producing less perceptible adversarial examples.

Carlini and Wagner16 proposed an optimization-based attack that formulates adversarial example generation as a constrained optimization problem. This method generates highly effective adversarial examples while explicitly minimizing the perturbation magnitude, making it particularly challenging for detection methods that rely on perturbation size. Beyond these foundational attacks, researchers have developed various extensions including universal perturbations that fool classifiers on multiple images29, physical-world attacks that remain effective under real-world transformations30, and black-box attacks that do not require access to model gradients31. The development of standardized evaluation protocols such as AutoAttack17 has enabled more reliable comparison of defense methods, while studies on adaptive attacks18 have highlighted the importance of evaluating defenses against attackers who are aware of the defense mechanism. The diversity and sophistication of these attack methods highlight the need for robust detection mechanisms that can generalize across different attack strategies.

Adversarial detection

Adversarial detection methods aim to identify whether an input has been adversarially perturbed before it is processed by the target classifier. These methods can be broadly categorized into feature-based approaches, input transformation approaches, and learning-based approaches. Feature-based approaches analyze the internal representations of deep neural networks to identify anomalies indicative of adversarial perturbations. Early work by Metzen et al.32 proposed training a separate detector network on the internal representations of the target classifier, while Grosse et al.33 introduced statistical testing methods for adversarial detection. Ma et al.19 observed that adversarial examples exhibit distinct local intrinsic dimensionality characteristics compared to clean inputs. They proposed using k-nearest neighbor distance estimation to compute local intrinsic dimensionality scores, which serve as features for training a binary classifier to distinguish adversarial from clean samples. Lee et al.20 proposed fitting class-conditional Gaussian distributions to intermediate features extracted from the target classifier and using Mahalanobis distance for detection. This approach assumes that adversarial examples deviate from the normal feature distribution and can be identified by their statistical distance from class centroids. Feinman et al.21 combined kernel density estimation with Bayesian uncertainty, leveraging the observation that adversarial examples often lie in low-density regions of the feature space and induce higher prediction uncertainty.

Input transformation approaches modify inputs to reveal or neutralize adversarial perturbations. Liang et al.22 proposed ODIN, which applies temperature scaling to softmax outputs and adds small perturbations to inputs to amplify the separation between adversarial and clean samples. This method exploits the observation that adversarial examples respond differently to input perturbations compared to clean samples. Xu et al.23 introduced feature squeezing, which reduces input complexity through techniques such as bit-depth reduction and spatial smoothing. By comparing model predictions on original and squeezed inputs, this method can identify adversarial examples whose perturbations are sensitive to input transformations. Guo et al.34 explored various input transformations including JPEG compression, total variance minimization, and image quilting as preprocessing steps to remove adversarial perturbations.

Additionally, certified defense methods such as randomized smoothing35 provide provable robustness guarantees, while methods like TRADES36 address the trade-off between robustness and accuracy. Despite the progress made by these methods, several limitations remain. Many feature-based approaches require adversarial examples during training to learn discriminative features, which limits their ability to generalize to novel attack types. Input transformation methods may degrade the performance on clean inputs and can be circumvented by adaptive attacks that account for the transformation. Additionally, most existing methods analyze features at a single scale, potentially missing perturbation effects that manifest at different levels of abstraction. Our proposed method addresses these limitations by employing a reconstruction-based approach that requires only clean images for training and captures multi-scale feature representations.

More recently, several studies have continued to advance adversarial detection methods. Mao et al.37 proposed a high-level feature difference-based detection algorithm that leverages discrepancies in feature representations between adversarial and clean examples sharing the same predicted label, achieving robust detection without requiring image reconstruction. Hu et al.38 introduced a frequency domain reconstruction approach that eliminates adversarial perturbations through Fourier transform-based filtering, demonstrating effective detection without modifying the target classifier. Liu et al.39 proposed a divide-and-conquer reconstruction defense that trains multiple specialized networks to handle different attack types. Additionally, comprehensive surveys by Wang et al.40 and Xu et al.41 have provided updated taxonomies and analyses of adversarial attacks and defenses, highlighting the continued importance of developing robust detection mechanisms.

Knowledge distillation

Knowledge distillation, introduced by Hinton et al.24, is a technique for transferring knowledge from a large teacher network to a smaller student network. The original formulation trains the student to match the soft probability outputs of the teacher, enabling the student to capture inter-class relationships learned by the teacher. This technique has been widely adopted for model compression, where the goal is to obtain a compact model that retains the performance of a larger model. Subsequent works have extended knowledge distillation to transfer intermediate feature representations42, attention maps43, and relational information between samples44. More recent approaches have explored contrastive representation distillation45 and knowledge review mechanisms46 to further improve the transfer of knowledge between networks.

Recent research has explored the application of knowledge distillation principles to anomaly detection tasks. Bergmann et al.25 proposed using student-teacher networks for industrial anomaly detection, where a student network is trained to match the outputs of a teacher network on normal samples. During inference, discrepancies between student and teacher outputs indicate anomalies, as the student has only learned to replicate normal patterns. This approach eliminates the need for anomalous samples during training, making it particularly suitable for applications where anomalies are rare or difficult to collect. Wang et al.26 applied reverse distillation for anomaly detection, training a decoder to reconstruct encoder features from bottleneck representations. Deng and Li47 further advanced this approach by introducing reverse distillation from one-class embedding for anomaly detection. Other notable methods include Deep SVDD48 for one-class classification and PaDiM49 for patch-based anomaly detection and localization. The reconstruction error serves as an anomaly score, with higher errors indicating deviations from normal patterns.

Our work builds upon these knowledge distillation-based anomaly detection approaches while introducing several key innovations for adversarial detection. Unlike methods that rely on contrastive learning50 or require multiple training stages, our approach provides a simple yet effective framework for detecting adversarial examples using only clean training data. Unlike previous methods that focus on detecting manufacturing defects or out-of-distribution samples, our approach is specifically designed to detect adversarial perturbations that cause minimal visual changes but significant feature-level distortions. We employ a multi-scale reconstruction approach that captures perturbation effects at different levels of abstraction and combine reconstruction-based scores with statistical distance metrics for improved detection robustness.

By training a decoder to reconstruct intermediate features using only clean images, we create a model that is specialized for the manifold of normal data. When adversarial examples are presented, the decoder fails to accurately reconstruct their distorted feature representations, resulting in elevated reconstruction errors that serve as anomaly indicators.

Datasets

We evaluate our method on two datasets with different characteristics to demonstrate the generalizability of our approach across different domains and image resolutions.

The first dataset is CIFAR-1027, a widely-used benchmark dataset for image classification consisting of 60,000 color images of size 32 by 32 pixels across 10 classes. We use 45,000 images for training, 5,000 for validation, and 10,000 for testing. The dataset includes natural images of objects such as airplanes, automobiles, birds, cats, deer, dogs, frogs, horses, ships, and trucks. This dataset represents a standard computer vision benchmark with relatively small image sizes, which presents challenges for feature extraction due to limited spatial resolution.

The second dataset is ISIC201828, the International Skin Imaging Collaboration 2018 dataset for dermoscopic image analysis. This medical imaging dataset contains 10,015 dermoscopic images across 7 diagnostic categories of skin lesions. Images are resized to 224 by 224 pixels to match the standard input size for pretrained networks. We split the data into 70 percent for training, 15 percent for validation, and 15 percent for testing, resulting in 7,010 training images and 1,502 test images. This dataset represents a challenging medical imaging application where adversarial robustness is particularly critical for patient safety, as misclassification of skin lesions could lead to delayed or incorrect diagnosis. Adversarial sample generation

We evaluate detection performance against three representative adversarial attack methods that cover different attack strategies and perturbation characteristics.

For adversarial example generation, we construct a target classifier by attaching a linear classification head to the frozen teacher encoder (ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18) and train only the linear head on each target dataset using cross-entropy loss for 20 epochs. Adversarial examples are then generated by attacking this classifier using the gradient of the cross-entropy loss via the torchattacks library51. It should be noted that from the decoder’s perspective, these attacks are transfer-based rather than strictly white-box, since the decoder’s reconstruction loss is not involved in the attack generation. However, because the teacher encoder is shared between the classifier and the detection framework, adversarial perturbations optimized to disrupt the encoder’s feature representations are directly propagated to the decoder input, making the attacks stronger than purely black-box transfer attacks against an independent model. This attack scenario also reflects a realistic threat model in which an attacker targets the classification system without knowledge of the deployed detection mechanism.

The success of our method can be attributed to the combination of reconstruction-based and statistical anomaly detection. The reverse knowledge distillation framework learns to reconstruct normal feature patterns, making it sensitive to the feature-level perturbations caused by adversarial attacks. The hybrid approach further incorporates Mahalanobis-style feature statistics, which captures distribution-level anomalies that may not be evident in reconstruction errors alone.

Regarding attack-specific observations, our extended evaluation reveals a consistent detection hierarchy across both datasets. FGSM and PGD attacks are well-detected (AUROC > 0.86 on both datasets) because gradient-based iterative perturbations create substantial feature-space distortions that the reconstruction-based decoder readily identifies. C&W, despite being a sophisticated optimization attack, is also effectively detected (0.953 on ISIC2018, 0.849 on CIFAR-10) because the optimization process still introduces feature-space anomalies detectable by our hybrid scoring mechanism. DeepFool and APGD/AutoAttack represent the most challenging attacks (AUROC 0.67–0.88), as they focus on minimizing perturbation magnitude while maximizing attack success. These results confirm that attack strength does not necessarily correlate with detection difficulty, and confirm that our method provides robust detection across diverse attack categories.

Regarding dataset-specific observations, the smaller image size of CIFAR-10 at 32 by 32 pixels benefits from local patch analysis, where our method can detect fine-grained perturbation patterns. The larger image size of ISIC2018 at 224 by 224 pixels provides richer feature representations, enabling more reliable statistical anomaly detection. The medical imaging domain also exhibits more structured patterns that are easier to model compared to natural images.

Discussion

In this section, we discuss the key findings of our experimental results and provide deeper analysis of our proposed method. We examine the effectiveness of the hybrid approach, compare our method with existing approaches, discuss the limitations of our work, and outline potential directions for future research.

Effectiveness of the hybrid approach

The experimental results demonstrate that our Hybrid Reverse Knowledge Distillation method achieves superior detection performance compared to existing methods across both datasets and multiple attack types. The effectiveness of our approach can be attributed to several factors.

The reverse knowledge distillation framework enables the decoder to learn the manifold of normal feature representations by training exclusively on clean images. When adversarial examples are presented, their features deviate from this learned manifold, resulting in elevated reconstruction errors. This reconstruction-based approach is particularly effective for detecting gradient-based attacks such as FGSM and PGD, which introduce noticeable perturbations in the feature space even when the perturbations are imperceptible in the input space.

The multi-scale feature reconstruction captures perturbation effects at different levels of abstraction. Lower layers capture fine-grained texture information that is sensitive to local perturbations, while higher layers capture semantic information that reflects the overall content of the image. By reconstructing features across multiple scales, our method can detect adversarial perturbations that manifest differently at various levels of the network hierarchy.

The hybrid scoring mechanism combines reconstruction-based scores with Mahalanobis-style statistical distances. These two components provide complementary detection signals. Reconstruction errors capture local feature distortions that occur when the decoder fails to accurately predict features for adversarial inputs. Statistical distances capture global distribution shifts by measuring how far the test sample features deviate from the distribution of clean training data. The combination of these two signals enables more robust detection across diverse attack types.

Comparison with existing methods

Our method demonstrates consistent improvements over the baseline methods across different experimental settings. Compared to LID, which relies on local intrinsic dimensionality estimation, our method achieves higher AUROC scores because the reconstruction-based approach directly measures feature-level anomalies rather than relying on geometric properties of the feature space. LID requires careful selection of the number of nearest neighbors and may be sensitive to the density of the feature space, whereas our method learns to reconstruct features in an end-to-end manner.

Compared to the Mahalanobis method, our approach provides additional detection signals through reconstruction errors. While the Mahalanobis method captures distribution-level anomalies effectively, it may miss adversarial examples that lie within the normal distribution but exhibit abnormal feature patterns. Our hybrid approach addresses this limitation by incorporating reconstruction-based scoring that can detect such cases.

Compared to ODIN, which relies on input perturbation and temperature scaling, our method does not require modifying the input during inference. ODIN assumes that adversarial examples respond differently to input perturbations compared to clean samples, but this assumption may not hold for all attack types. Our reconstruction-based approach is more direct in measuring feature-level anomalies and does not depend on specific assumptions about how adversarial examples respond to perturbations.

A key advantage of our method over all baselines is that it requires only clean images for training. This is in contrast to methods that require adversarial examples for training, which may not generalize well to adaptive attacks18 or novel attack strategies. This characteristic makes our method practical for real-world deployment where adversarial examples may not be available during training or where new attack types may emerge after deployment. The decoder learns to model normal feature patterns without requiring knowledge of specific attack strategies.

To investigate whether our framework generalizes beyond ResNet-18, we conduct additional experiments using ResNet-50 as the teacher encoder on the ISIC2018 dataset. Since ResNet-50 produces feature maps with different channel dimensions (256, 512, 1024, 2048) compared to ResNet-18 (64, 128, 256, 512), we introduce lightweight projection layers to map the features to the decoder’s expected dimensions while preserving the pretrained representations. All other training configurations remain identical to the ResNet-18 experiments.

We note that extending our framework to Vision Transformers (ViT) presents significant architectural challenges, as ViT produces patch-based token representations rather than hierarchical spatial feature maps. Adapting our multi-scale decoder to token sequences would require a fundamentally different design. However, hierarchical vision transformers such as Swin Transformer, which produce pyramid-like spatial features, represent a promising and compatible direction for future work.

Limitations

Despite the promising results, our method has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, while our method performs well against gradient-based attacks such as FGSM and PGD, detection of minimal perturbation attacks like DeepFool remains challenging. As shown in our extended evaluation (Section Extended attack evaluation), detection of minimal perturbation and adaptive attacks such as DeepFool and APGD remains more challenging compared to gradient-based attacks. DeepFool generates adversarial examples with the smallest possible perturbations to cross decision boundaries, resulting in feature representations that are closer to those of clean images. This makes it inherently more difficult for reconstruction-based methods to distinguish DeepFool adversarial examples from clean inputs.

Second, the computational overhead introduced by the decoder network may limit real-time applications. During inference, our method requires forward passes through both the teacher encoder and the decoder, as well as computation of reconstruction errors and statistical distances. For applications with strict latency requirements, this additional computation may be a concern.

Third, our method assumes that the teacher encoder provides meaningful feature representations for the task at hand. If the teacher encoder is poorly trained or if the input domain differs significantly from the training domain, the quality of feature representations may degrade, potentially affecting detection performance.

Fourth, the hyperparameters of our method, including the loss coefficients, scale weights, and the balancing coefficient for the hybrid score, were selected based on empirical validation. While we found these hyperparameters to work well across both datasets, optimal values may vary for different domains or attack scenarios.

Fifth, the adversarial examples in our experiments were generated by attacking a classifier that shares the same teacher encoder with the detection framework, which constitutes a transfer-based attack from the decoder’s perspective. Evaluating robustness against adaptive attacks that directly target the decoder’s reconstruction loss would provide a more stringent assessment of the detection framework and remains an important direction for future work.

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented Hybrid Reverse Knowledge Distillation, a novel framework for detecting adversarial examples in deep neural networks. Our approach leverages the principle that a decoder trained to reconstruct intermediate feature representations using only clean images will produce elevated reconstruction errors when presented with adversarial inputs. The proposed method consists of three main components: a frozen teacher encoder that extracts multi-scale feature representations, a trainable multi-scale decoder that reconstructs intermediate features from the deepest representation, and a hybrid anomaly scoring mechanism that combines reconstruction-based and statistical distance-based metrics. By combining reconstruction errors with Mahalanobis-style statistical distances, our hybrid approach captures both local feature distortions and global distribution shifts caused by adversarial perturbations. Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10 and ISIC2018 datasets demonstrated the effectiveness of our method against various adversarial attacks including FGSM, PGD, and DeepFool. Our method achieved average AUROC scores of 0.808 on CIFAR-10 and 0.939 on ISIC2018, outperforming existing detection methods including LID, Mahalanobis, and ODIN by significant margins. A key advantage of our approach is that it requires only clean images for training, eliminating the need for adversarial example generation during the training phase and making it practical for real-world deployment.

Several promising directions exist for future research. First, improving detection performance against minimal perturbation attacks such as DeepFool remains an important challenge, and investigating additional loss terms or adaptive thresholding mechanisms could address this limitation. Second, exploring lightweight decoder architectures through techniques such as pruning or quantization could reduce computational overhead and enable deployment in real-time applications. Third, while we have extended our evaluation to include AutoAttack and C&W attacks, investigating adaptive attacks that directly target the decoder’s reconstruction loss would provide an even more stringent assessment of detection robustness. Additionally, extending our framework to other adversarial attack types including patch-based attacks and physical-world attacks would broaden its applicability to diverse threat scenarios. Fourth, combining our detection method with adversarial training could yield comprehensive defense systems that both identify and resist adversarial perturbations. Finally, applying our approach to other safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving and natural language processing presents opportunities for broader impact in securing AI systems against adversarial threats.
