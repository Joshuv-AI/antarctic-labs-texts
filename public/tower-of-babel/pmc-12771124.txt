Efficient Removal of Crystal Violet and Safranin‑O Dyes Using Naturally Plant-Derived Azadirachta indica: A Combined Theoretical and Experimental Investigation
Authors: Basant Kumar Singh, Panmei Gaijon, Ravinder Singh, Gyanendra Kumar, Sudipta Ghosh, Palash Jyoti Das, Mayanglambam Ramananda Singh, Ranjana Dixit, Jitendra Kumar, Arun Kant
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study explored the potential benefits of Azadirachta indica (AzI) bark as a bioremediation agent for the removal of Crystal Violet (CV) and Safranin-O (SO) hazardous dyes from aqueous solutions. AzI bark is a natural plant-based material, and its botanical name is neem. Diverse interactions between AzI bark and both dyes were assessed by experimental methods and validated using computational studies. The removal of dye using the adsorption method is recognized as a very effective technique for wastewater treatment. The batch adsorption capacity was evaluated under varying pH levels, contact times, and concentrations of the dye solution at room temperature. Theoretical approaches, including molecular docking and density functional theory (DFT) analysis, were employed to identify the types of interactions between the AzI bark, CV, and SO dye. Quantitative analysis was conducted by measuring absorbance with a UV–vis spectrophotometer at λmax values of 591 nm for CV and 521 nm for the SO dye. The adsorption efficiency of AzI bark was determined to be 137.78 mg/g at 175 ppm and 110.56 mg/g at 150 ppm for CV and SO dye, respectively, at pH 7. The correlation coefficient (R 2) for the Langmuir isotherm was found to be 0.9940 for CV and 0.8671 for the SO dye. Kinetic studies showed that the pseudo-second-order reaction best fits the adsorption process, verifying the experimental isotherm and kinetic data. However, since AzI bark is a low-cost, ecofriendly, biodegradable, and widely available natural resource, it holds significant promise for application in industrial wastewater treatment. Additionally, experiments related to the remediation of CV and SO dyes from aqueous solution on AzI bark explored the impact of metal ions, such as iron, copper, and nickel, ranging from 20 to 100 ppm.

Introduction

A collection of dangerous dyes in the environment is nowadays a significant concern due to their harmful impact on ecosystems, including human health. These chemicals are highly stable, persist in natural ecosystems, and resist photodegradation. , Apart from decreasing the overall quality of water, these colors restrict light penetration, inhibit aquatic photosynthesis, and reduce oxygen levels, thereby harming aquatic life and decreasing biodiversity and ecosystem services. Major industries like textiles, paper, leather, and food production are primary sources of dye pollution in water bodies. , Synthetic and hard-to-degrade dyes, including azo, anthraquinone, and reactive dyes, pose serious environmental risks, with many identified as carcinogenic, mutagenic, and teratogenic. , Water pollution has grown increasingly severe over the past decade, prompting a heightened focus on wastewater treatment. Among the most dangerous pollutants in freshwater, dyes stand out, with approximately 10% of dyes used in the textile industry lost during coloration processes and discharged into water systems. Globally, over 10,000 commercial textile dyes are produced with an annual output of about 70,000 t, of which 2% is released as effluent from manufacturing facilities into freshwater sources. This emphasizes the critical need for effective and environmentally friendly dye treatment of water solutions. Traditional biological wastewater treatment technologies are frequently inefficient against an extensive category of dyes due to their limited biodegradability. Physical and chemical processes, such as membrane filtration, electrochemical degradation, ozonation, precipitation, irradiation, coagulation, and ion exchange, are commonly employed to treat dye-contaminated water. However, these techniques are expensive and less effective for removing dye molecules. Among available technologies, adsorption stands out as a highly effective method for fully removing dyes from wastewater, even at low concentrations. Although activated carbon is an extensively used adsorbent for dye remediation, its regeneration or disposal after saturation is costly, limiting its economic viability. The present study is based on adsorption and a method that was employed for more economical and cost-effective studies compared to different studies, such as physisorption, chemisorption, and catalysis. In this article, the adsorption of dye with AzI bark is a naturally occurring biodegradable material that is present in large amounts in nature. AzI bark was washed with distilled water without being treated using acid or base, nor does it require any surface modification or functionalization. AzI bark is nontoxic, easy to use, inexpensive, and is frequently employed in wastewater treatment.

Previous studies have explored dye removal from textile effluents using materials like hardwood, sawdust, and charcoal. , Azadirachta indica (AzI) leaves and bark have been utilized to remove phenol, malachite green, and direct red-81, while neem sawdust with varying particle sizes has been applied to adsorb crystal violet. Timber waste has also been used to extract dyes from textile effluents, and brilliant green has shown strong adsorption on unconventional adsorbents like powdered dry neem leaves. Crushed fruit wastes, like orange peels and bananas, along with carbon derived from coir pith, have been effective in removing Congo red and other dyes from wastewater. Naturally occurring Spirulina platensis has been employed to remediate crystal violet from aqueous solutions, , and the readily available cardboard waste has been used to remove rhodamine B and crystal violet.

The sorbent, along with its operational parameters and adsorption efficiency given as, exhibited a remediation effectiveness of 68.66 mg/g for CV on S. platensis at a pH of 6, contact time of 60 min, and initial concentration of 100 ppm. The remediation efficiency of rhodamine B (RhB) on the cardboard was found to be 38.36 mg/g at a pH of 3, contact time of 80 min, and initial concentration of 100 ppm. The adsorption efficiencies of sugar cane for Methylene Blue (MB) and Bismarck Brown R (BB) dye were found to be 127.48 and 166.75 mg/g, at pH 8 and 6.5, respectively, with a contact time of 48 h, and an initial concentration of 400 ppm for each dye.

The use of natural materials for dye remediation has gained significant interest due to their sustainability, availability, affordability, and eco-friendliness. These materials, encompassing plant-based products, agricultural residues, microorganisms, and biobased substances, provide a green alternative for treating dye-contaminated wastewater. − Their effectiveness often stems from high surface areas, unique chemical compositions, and abundant functional groups that interact with dye molecules through adsorption, degradation, or transformation. Plant-based materials and their byproducts, rich in functional groups like hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amine, are particularly valuable, as they allow the removal of dye through processes like coordination interactions, H-bonding, electrostatic attractions, etc.

Safranine-O is a cationic azine dye that appears as a reddish-brown powder and is widely used in textiles, paper, leather, and fabrics, such as cotton, silk, and wool. It also plays critical roles in for staining and analytical applications biological laboratories. However, its environmental presence poses health risks, including stomach pain; mouth, tongue, and lip irritation; and symptoms like nausea and diarrhea upon exposure. Similarly, Crystal violet (CV) is a synthetic cationic dye commonly used in veterinary medicine as a biological stain. CV has applications in detergents, paper, fertilizers, leather, textile dyeing, dermatology, and the production of paints and printing inks. , CV is carcinogenic, mutagenic, nonbiodegradable, and poorly metabolized by microbes, allowing it to persist in various environments. Its toxicity harms living organisms, and human exposure to it can lead to skin and digestive tract irritation, vomiting, respiratory issues, eye irritation, jaundice, heart disease, kidney failure, quadriplegia, and cyanosis. , Further, acidic, cationic, and anionic dyes can cause tumors (both nonmalignant and malignant), while anionic dyes can cause bladder cancer and numerous other environmental or health issues because CV and SO toxic dyes seriously impact ecosystems and public health; hence, they must be removed from wastewater. ,

Plants and their waste play a vital role in environmental purification, like dye degradation, and are rich sources of antioxidants with medicinal, antibacterial, , and antifungal properties. Their wood is also used for furniture production and as an energy resource, boosting the national economy. , The botanical name for neem is Azadirachta indica, which is known as Neemba in Sanskrit. In this study, AzI bark is employed as an adsorbent for hazardous dyes, selected for its key components epoxy-azadiradione (Aze), azadirachtins (Azn), nimbins (Nbn), and cellulose (Cel). − These components contain numerous functional groups that enhance dye adsorption.

The AzI bark is abundant in nature and is a self-biodegradable waste material used as an adsorbent for wastewater treatment. It is not toxic or has adverse impacts on the environment. It is cost-effective, easily available, and ecofriendly to nature. AzI bark offers advantages such as antibacterial and antifungal properties, and availability from renewable sources, making it ideal for large-scale applications. No extra chemicals or solvents are required for AzI bark and surface treatment during adsorption studies. There is no requirement for acidic or basic treatment, nor is any surface modification/functionalization required. Moreover, the interactions of CV and SO with AzI bark have been determined from experimental data as well as theoretical data, like molecular docking and DFT techniques at the molecular level. The material specifications were in accordance with the variations between the DFT predictions and experimental results to improve the adsorption performance. AzI bark has high adsorption efficacy due to its good surface area, which increases the potential interaction sites and improves adsorption capacity for toxic dye. Experimental Section

Impact of Chemical Variables on Adsorption

The adsorption behavior of Crystal Violet (CV) and Safranin-O (SO) onto Azadirachta indica (AzI) bark was meticulously examined using a batch extraction method, with systematic variations in vital parameters such as contact time, pH, and concentration of the aqueous dye solution. A uniform quantity of 25 mg AzI bark was used in each experimental investigation, and the entire procedures were carried out at room temperature under monitored conditions with the goal of accuracy and reproducibility.

The remaining concentrations of unabsorbed CV and SO within the resulting solution were separately quantified with excellent accuracy using UV–visible spectrophotometry, an instrument that has become known for its sensitivity to identify dye concentrations. This analytical method offers an in-depth examination of the adsorption efficacy and establishes a basis for elucidating the dynamics of the interaction between the dyes and the natural adsorbent. During the bulk extraction process, the dye solutions were stirred with AzI bark under a controlled atmosphere, which allowed the adsorption process to occur over a prescribed period. The selection of 25 mg AzI bark was based on initial studies that maximized the adsorbent-to-solution ratio, facilitating a suitable surface area for efficient dye binding instead of consuming the system. Room temperature was maintained to mimic typical environmental conditions, making the findings relevant for experimental applications. The use of UV–visible spectrophotometry, with its ability to measure absorbance at specific wavelengths, facilitated the detection of unadsorbed dye molecules, offering a reliable method to calculate the extent of adsorption and the remaining dye concentration in the solution. The λmax values and stability, indicative of the maximum absorbance wavelengths, were precisely identified at 591 nm for CV and 521 nm for SO. Spectral analyses were conducted to estimate the stability and behavior of the dyes under varying pH conditions, which is a critical factor in adsorption studies. The absorbance spectra of both CV and SO exhibited a clear pH-dependent behavior, with the intensity of absorbance varying as the pH changed, though the maximum absorbance peak (λmax) remained consistent across certain pH ranges. Throughout the various pH ranges, no major structural or chemical changes were observed in any dye, which indicates excellent stability. This level of stability over a wide pH range indicates that CV and SO retain their chemical stability and adsorption capacity under different environmental conditions, which is an important factor for wastewater remediation. The influence of pH is due to the deprotonation as well as protonation of the functional groups between the dye molecules and the AzI bark surface, which changes the electrostatic bonds that result in adsorption processes. The main chromophoric properties of CV and SO are their resistance to small pH changes, which supports their suitability and target pollutants for adsorption investigations. This is demonstrated by the absence of a significant λmax change from their stable pH ranges. This stability is critical for developing efficient wastewater treatment methodologies because it enables uniform dye removal across a wide range of pH levels, which are often found in industrial effluents.

A number of batches of standard solutions have known concentrations between 1 and 10 ppm, followed by 25 mL standard volumetric flasks, capable of stepwise dilution to achieve the predicted range of dye concentrations within the setup of the experiment. The Lambert–Beer law, which connects absorption to concentration, has been verified up to 9 ppm, allowing CV and 10 ppm for the SO dye, developing a linear range for exact measurements.

The λmax values for CV and SO dyes in aqueous solutions were demonstrated to be pH-independent in the ranges of 2–9 for CV and 2–10 for SO. This indicates that minor pH fluctuations within these limits do not appreciably change the absorbance maxima. For developing calibration plots, observations were taken at pH 5 for CV and pH 8 for SO, which were chosen based on their chemical stabilities. A calibration plot (Lambert–Beer law validity) of CV and SO dyes, which is shown in Figure a, reveals that the correlation coefficients (R 2) for CV and SO were 0.9987 and 0.9975, respectively, indicating good linearity with slopes of 0.1082 and 0.0960, respectively. The calibration curves are reliable for predicting dye concentrations according to the supernatant throughout subsequent adsorption studies, as proven by the significant R 2 values and consistent slopes.

Batch Extraction Study and Remediation of CV and SO

The adsorption properties for CV as well as SO on Azadirachta indica (AzI) bark were fully investigated utilizing a batch extraction method that included systematic adjustments in pH and contact time frame, along with initial dye concentrations throughout the water-based solutions. Every experimental run included 25 mg of AzI bark, which was systematically weighed to ensure consistency and was carried out at room temperature in accordance with natural conditions. The quantities of unabsorbed CV as well as SO within the supernatant were measured independently by employing UV–vis spectrophotometry, a dye identification technique with excellent specificity and sensitivity.

Eqs and were used to compute the quantity adsorbed (in mg/g), in addition to the effectiveness of adsorption (in percent) uptake as follows 1 amountadsorbed(q)=[(Ci−Ce)×V]/m 2 (%)uptake=[(Ci−Ce)/Ci]×100 where Ci represents the initial dye concentration (mg/L), C e stands for the equilibrium concentration (mg/L), V is the volume of the solution (mL), m stands for the mass of the adsorbent (g), and q stands for the amount of CV or SO adsorbed (mg/g). The above equations offer a quantitative estimate of the adsorption process, which facilitates an improved evaluation of the bark’s ability to remove color from the solution. The batch removal approach involves stirring the dye solutions and AzI bark in a controlled manner, which allows the adsorption process to take place under optimal conditions. The use of UV–vis spectrophotometry allowed for the reliable determination of the remaining dye levels, making it easier to calculate the required adsorption parameters. This strategy provides a solid framework for investigating the remediation abilities of AzI bark, with the resulting formulae acting as a basis for subsequent kinetic and isotherm investigations.

Remediation of CV and SO as a Function of pH

pH plays a pivotal role in influencing the remediation efficiencies of CV and SO from wastewater. A series of experiments was conducted using 25 mg of AzI bark with 25 mL of 20 ppm of CV and SO solutions across a pH range of 1 to 10 at room temperature. The uptake of CV and SO as a function of the pH of the dye solution is graphically represented in Figure b. The percentage uptake of CV increased progressively from pH 1 to 6, stabilized between pH 6 and 7 with values of 98.16 and 98.39%, respectively, and then exhibited a slight decline up to pH 10. In the case of SO, the uptake increased from pH 1 to 7, remained nearly constant between pH 4 and 6, and decreased after pH 7, reaching 89.64, 90.08, and 85.81% at pH 6, 7, and 8, respectively.

The highest adsorption for both dyes was observed in a neutral medium (pH 7), outperforming acidic and basic conditions, with CV demonstrating a higher uptake percentage compared to SO. Such a pH-dependent effect could be related to the ionization states of the dye molecules with the surface charge on AzI bark, which enhances electrostatic interactions near a neutral pH level. Therefore, pH 7 was chosen as the most suitable condition for further adsorption investigations, offering an appropriate pH level for the maximum dye removal efficiency. The observed outcomes show that AzI bark’s surface functionalities, including hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, become most efficient at pH 7, when dye molecules seem to be in a suitable ionized phase for adsorption. This conclusion is important for practical applications because it shows that wastewater treatment systems may be built to function successfully at near-neutral pH values, eliminating the need for considerable pH modifications.

CV and SO Remediation Dependent on the Contact Time

Contact time seems to be a significant measure for understanding the process of adsorption among the adsorbents (AzI bark) along with the adsorbates (CV and SO). According to this investigation, 25 mg of AzI bark was added to 25 mL of 20 ppm CV and the SO solution, exactly at pH 7. Within the initial 2 min, approximately 59.08% CV and 46.22% SO were removed, increasing to 78.65 and 59.24% after 5 min, and reaching 98.16 and 92.89% after 20 min for CV and SO, respectively. This rapid initial uptake is expected because of the availability of numerous adsorption sites on the high surface area of AzI bark.

Adsorption stabilized after 20 min, indicating that the process reached equilibrium. The uptake of CV and SO as a function of contact time is depicted in Figure a. The initial fast removal can be explained by a strong concentration differential between the dye solution with the AzI bark surface, which causes fast adsorption on readily available sites, which reveals BET data. When these sites became saturated or blocked, the adsorption rate decreased, and the percentage of dye removed declined. This result emphasizes the significance of contact time in improving adsorption effectiveness, with 20 min found to be the maximum absorption stage in the specified conditions.

Uptake of CV and SO as a function of (a) contact time and (b) initial concentration.

The stability after 20 min shows that the adsorption process is influenced by the accessibility of active sites on the bark surface. This notion is useful for building batch processes, in which the contact time may be varied to accomplish a suitable level of dye removal without using unnecessary energy or resources.

Influence of the Initial Concentration on CV and SO

The initial concentrations of CV and SO are the main factors for understanding the adsorption isotherms and overall efficiency of the adsorption procedure. The removal of CV and SO by the AzI bark was influenced by the initial dye concentration in the aqueous solutions. Experiments were conducted with 25 mL of CV and SO solutions (ranging from 10 to 200 ppm) using 25 mg of AzI bark for 20 min at pH 7; the uptake of CV and SO, as a function of initial concentration, is illustrated in Figure b.

The removal efficiency increased with concentration up to 175 ppm for CV and 150 ppm for SO; beyond this, there was no significance for further adsorption due to the saturation of available binding sites of the AzI bark. At a low concentration of 30 ppm, CV removal reached 99.63% (29.89 mg/g), while at 20 ppm, SO removal was 92.98% (18.60 mg/g). The maximum removals were 78.74% (137.80 mg/g) for CV at 175 ppm and 73.71% (110.56 mg/g) for SO at 150 ppm. These optimal concentrations were selected for further morphological and interaction analyses using advanced techniques such as SEM, FTIR, BET, DFT, and molecular docking studies.

The increase in removal efficiency with increasing concentration up to the saturation point reflects the availability of adsorption sites on the AzI bark. Beyond this point, the limited number of active sites restricts further uptake, highlighting the importance of optimizing the initial dye concentration in practical applications to remediate the dye from an aqueous medium, as shown in Figure . The high removal percentages at low concentrations suggest that AzI bark is particularly effective for treating dilute dye solutions, a common scenario in industrial effluents.

Influence of Cyclability of AzI Bark

The recycling properties of AzI are also fantastic, where it is reused after each experiment, centrifuged, and washed with ethanol, followed by water. After washing, Azl bark is dried in an oven at 80 °C before proceeding to the next cycle for the adsorption of both CV as well as SO. The efficiency of AzI bark after reuse cycles is shown in Figure b. For CV and SO, the percentage uptake efficiency of AzI bark in the first cycle decreased up to 2.86 and 2.43%; in the second cycle, it decreased up to 5.89 and 5.58%; in the third cycle, it decreased up to 6.15 and 5.63%; and, in the fourth cycle, it decreased up to 6.39 and 6.58%, respectively. Therefore, the difference in the % uptake efficiency of AzI bark between the third and fourth cycles was found to be 0.24 and 0.95 for CV and SO, respectively, which is almost constant. Hence, the decrease in the uptake efficiency of the AzI bark is due to the inability of some of the CV and SO dyes not being able to detach from the active site of the different functional groups of AzI bark.

Desorption of the dye from dye-adsorbed AzI bark and its desorption process from AzI bark were carried out with ethanol. Dye molecules (CV and SO) have a balance of polar and nonpolar regions as a result of the carbon chains. If a dye has a major nonpolar component, its polarity should be better matched to ethanol rather than to a more polar solvent such as water.

For the disposal of the dye-adsorbed AzI bark, after removal of dye via employing AzI bark, there is subsequently a certain reuse as an adsorbent. The dye (CV and SO)-adsorbed AzI bark was treated with ethanol to separate the toxic dye from the AzI bark. The dye is more soluble in ethanol, which is a green solvent. The mixture is filtered to separate out the AzI bark from the ethanolic dye, and the AzI bark is reused for wastewater treatment or self-degrades naturally. After ethanol is separated from the ethanolic dye by distillation, the solid dye can be easily separated and reused for industrial purposes.

Analysis of Adsorption Kinetics with Isotherms

Kinetic Modeling

Chemical kinetics, as detailed in the Supporting Information, examine the reaction rates and mechanisms involved in the adsorption of CV and SO onto the surface of AzI bark. Three kinetic models, such as the pseudo-first-order model (by Lagergren), where a plot of log­(q e–qt ) versus t yields a straight line (Figure S3a) with R 2 values of 0.4062 for CV and 0.2903 for SO; pseudo-second-order model, where a plot of t/qt versus t is linear (Figure S3b) with R 2 values of 0.9994 for CV and 0.9982 for SO; and the intraparticle diffusion model, where a plot of qt versus t 1/2 is linear (Figure S3c) with R 2 values of 0.6204 for CV and 0.6983 for SO, are used.

The pseudo-second-order model provides an excellent fit to be demonstrated by its exceptionally high correlation coefficients, showing that the adsorption process was primarily governed by chemisorption, including valence forces via electron sharing or exchange among dye molecules as well as AzI bark.

Adsorption Isotherm Modeling

The equilibrium adsorption capacities of AzI bark for CV and SO were evaluated by isotherm studies, as outlined in the Supporting Information (S2). Three isotherm models were tested: the Langmuir isotherm, which describes monolayer adsorption and produces a linear plot of C e/q e versus C e (Figure S2a) with R 2 values of 0.9940 for CV and 0.8671 for SO. The plots of Hall separation factor (R L) versus the concentrations of the CV and SO dyes, where all R L values are less than 1. A lower R L value indicates more favorable adsorption; R L values of 0.01018 and 0.01032 for CV and SO dyes, respectively, and the Hall separation factor (R L) are depicted in Figure b. The Freundlich isotherm, which accounts for heterogeneous systems with multilayer adsorption, yields a linear plot of log q e with log C e (Figure S2b) with R 2 values of 0.6329 for CV and 0.8819 for SO; and the Temkin isotherm, which considers adsorbent–adsorbate interactions and produces a linear plot of q e with ln C e (Figure S2c) with R 2 values of 0.8179 for CV and 0.8216 for SO.

Based on the observation of all isotherms, correlation coefficients and parameters are presented in Table The Langmuir isotherm showed an excellent fit, which suggests that the adsorption of CV and SO onto the AzI bark surface occurs at an infinite number of identical positions. This model’s significance is that the adsorption process is appropriate for a governed, uniform surface, aligned with the physical and chemical properties of AzI bark. The Langmuir model fit implies that the adsorption sites on AzI bark are uniformly distributed and that the process reaches a saturation point, which is consistent with the observed concentration-dependent uptake. This insight is valuable for scaling up the adsorption-based treatment systems. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET)

The specific surface area of a composite material is measured by the BET theory, which also introduces the adsorption of gas molecules on solid surfaces. The porous structure of AzI bark was confirmed using nitrogen adsorption–desorption at 77.35 K, indicating a (II) type isotherm and therefore a type of mesoporous material. The curve exhibited little hysteresis, indicating reversible gas storage.

The AzI bark, CV-adsorbed AzI bark, and SO-adsorbed AzI bark had BET surface areas of about 2.879, 1.622 m2/g as well as 2.23 m2/g, respectively. As a result, the composite absorbs more nitrogen compared to the CV-adsorbed AzI bark as well as the SO-adsorbed AzI bark, indicating that the composite has a large surface area with low porosity resulting from both CV-adsorbed AzI bark and SO-adsorbed AzI bark. AzI bark has a very large surface area, and therefore, functions as an efficient adsorbent for removing dye molecules from aqueous solutions. Molecular Docking and Density Functional Theory (DFT) Analysis

Molecular docking of Azadiradione (Aze), Azadirachtin (Azn), Cellulose (Cel), and Nimbinin (Nbn) with CV and SO was performed using Hex 8.0. Aze, Azn, Cel, and Nbn were considered as receptors, while CV and SO were considered as ligands. This gives the total binding energy for the formation of the complex using different docked poses. The search for the best pose was set to consider shape, DARS, and electrostatic correlation. The total binding energy obtained is the combination of energy due to E shape, E force, and E air. Docking was performed to obtain 2000 solutions for each docking and was considered to be more reliable than others. The top-score complex with the minimum binding energy value was analyzed and was further considered for other analyses. Docked poses were analyzed using Biovia Discovery Studio 2024.

DFT calculations were implemented in many fields for estimating noncovalent interaction analysis and electronic structure parameters. Gaussian 16 was employed for DFT calculations. DFT combined Becke’s three-parameter exchange functional with the Lee–Yang–Paar correlation functional (B3LYP) was employed to achieve geometry optimization. The legal basis set 6-311G was utilized to run the calculations. Computations in the gas phase were carried out ignoring the symmetry. Gauss view and VMD, Irfan View, and Multi WFN were used to analyze the output files.

Noncovalent Interaction (NCI)

NCI analysis was carried out for complexes to visualize and interpret the weak intermolecular interactions present within the optimized molecular geometry. This method is particularly useful in DFT-based investigations revealing van der Waals interactions and hydrogen bonds, including steric repulsion. The NCI analysis is presented in two parts: a scattering diagram showing the reduced density gradient (RDG) along the sign of the second eigenvalue of the electron density Hessian matrix, and a 3D visualization of the molecular structure with color-coded isosurfaces representing various interaction regions, as shown in Figure . In the RDG vs. sign (λ2) ρ plot, the horizontal axis represents the sign­(λ2)­ρ value, which differentiates between attractive and repulsive interactions, while the vertical axis shows the RDG values. The green-colored isosurfaces confirm the presence of noncovalent van der Waals interactions spread across the molecule, especially in areas where aromatic rings and hydrocarbon chains are in proximity. The zones of hydrogen bonding shown by blue-colored isosurfaces show favorable stabilization in the crystals with AzI bark components. Instead, the red areas indicate regions of steric hindrance, where repulsive forces may influence the molecular structure.

The highest-occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest-unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) analyses were performed for all eight combinations. This analysis is helpful in locating the reactive sites of the molecule, as the HOMO is considered an electron donor site; hence, it defines the reactive center of the molecule. These findings of the NCI analysis indicate the successful adsorption, as adsorption is measured in terms of these weak forces. The more the interaction between the components, the more the adsorption will be; hence, the NCI analysis corroborates the experimental findings.

Adsorption Mechanism of CV and SO on AzI Bark

A number of parameters, including the interaction of hydrogen, π–π interactions, ionic or electrostatic forces, and π–π* connections, affect the adsorption on CV as well as SO toward the surface of the Azadirachta indica (AzI) bark. Given these considerations, it is possible that electrostatic interactions take place between the positively charged CV as well as SO molecules and the negatively charged groups (−OH) found on the AzI bark surface. AzI bark has important advantages, such as a large surface area with an extensive variety of features and numerous functional groups, which increase its adsorption ability. The FTIR spectrum displayed in Figure (a,b) compares AzI bark before and after CV as well as SO adsorption, indicating modest alterations in peak positions. These changes show that the adsorption process involves electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonds, π–π interactions, and π–π* interactions. The existence of unsaturated bonds (such as CC and O–CO) in AzI bark results in interactions by binding to positive charges on CV as well as SO molecules, suggesting that the adsorption process includes ionic interactions. , Additionally, π–π interactions serve as effective binding sites, enabling the adsorption of CV and SO onto the AzI bark. Given that both CV and SO contain CC double bonds with π-electrons, they can readily interact with the π-electrons of aromatic rings found in various AzI bark components, including Nimbins, through π–π interactions. Furthermore, π–π* interactions occur between the double (CC) bonds as well as the π–electrons of CV and SO and the empty π* orbitals of the CO functional groups within the different constituents of AzI bark.

Based on the aforementioned interaction mechanisms, it is evident that CV and SO are efficiently adsorbed onto the AzI bark. The surface area of an adsorbent, such as AzI bark, is a critical factor influencing its adsorption capacity for CV and SO. Plant bark, a low-cost biomass-derived adsorbent, typically contains cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which contribute to its porous structure and surface functional groups. These properties enable plant bark to adsorb CV and SO through mechanisms such as physisorption, chemisorption, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π–π interactions. The adsorption of CV and SO on AzI bark indicates that high dye uptake can fill pores, reducing the accessible surface area. However, direct quantitative data on the surface area reduction are limited, and further experimental studies using BET analysis would strengthen this conclusion.

Conclusion

We have successfully employed Azadirachta indica (AzI) bark for the removal of highly toxic CV and SO dyes from aqueous media. Owing to electrostatic interactions, ionic/π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π–π* interactions between AzI bark and CV and SO dyes are evaluated with FTIR data. AzI bark has excellent adsorption capacity, verifying its application in the remediation of toxic dyes. The physicochemical properties of AzI bark were elucidated using structural and morphological characterization techniques. SEM shows the morphology of the AzI bark, CV-adsorbed beak, and SO-adsorbed AzI bark, and its EDX spectra confirm the presence of carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, and chlorine elements. The correlation coefficients (R 2) of 0.9994 and 0.9982 for the adsorption data reveal pseudo-second-order kinetics for CV and SO dyes, respectively. The correlation coefficient (R 2) for the Langmuir isotherm was 0.9940 for CV and 0.8671 for SO, confirming the robustness of the developed response model. The adsorption efficiencies of AzI bark were determined to be 137.78 mg/g at 175 ppm and 110.56 mg/g at 150 ppm for CV and SO dyes, respectively, at pH 7, which is the highest value compared to that of the other adsorbent materials. Further, DFT calculations, NCI analysis, and molecular docking were performed to optimize the geometries of the systems Aze-CV, Aze-SO, Azn-CV, Azn-SO, Cel-CV, Cel-SO, Nbn-CV, and Nbn-SO. The weak intermolecular interactions corroborate the experimental findings, showing strong adsorption of the dye on the AzI bark component. Furthermore, studies on the remediation of CV and SO dyes from aqueous solutions on the AzI bark have examined the impact of metal ion (iron, copper, and nickel) concentrations; the percentage absorption of CV and SO decreases when the concentrations of iron, copper, and nickel increase. AzI bark, a biodegradable and readily accessible natural resource, has tremendous potential for use in the wastewater treatment industry. This AzI bark can be used as an adsorbent material for the industrial and pharmaceutical applications.
