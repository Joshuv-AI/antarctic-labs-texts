Evaluation of Bianchi rigid meromorphic cocycles at big ATR points
Lennart Gehrmann, Xavier Guitart, Marc Masdeu

Abstract
. We develop the tools required to effectively evaluate the Bianchi rigid meromorphic cocycles introduced by Darmon–Gehrmann–Lipnowski at big ATR points, and use them to obtain the first numerical verification of the conjectured algebraicity of these special values. Moreover, our computations suggest that these special values exhibit behaviour analogous to that of the special values of Borcherds products on Hilbert modular surfaces at big CM points.



1.
Introduction

Singular moduli are the values of the classical
j
-function at CM points on the modular curve
X_{0}(1)
. These complex numbers are in fact algebraic and generate abelian extensions of the corresponding imaginary quadratic fields, and their norms down to
\mathbb{Q}
exhibit remarkable and highly structured factorizations. Several of these properties of the
j
-function are known to hold in a more general setting, namely for the values of Borcherds products on Shimura varieties attached to orthogonal groups of real signature
(r,2)
with
r\geq 1
at special points.
Most notably, special values of Borcherds products on Hilbert modular surfaces at big CM points were studied by Bruinier and Yang in
[
BY06
]
.

The theory of
rigid meromorphic cocycles for orthogonal groups
was introduced in
[
DGL25
]
as a conjectural analogue for the case of groups
G
of arbitrary real signature
(r,s)
, even in cases where no associated Shimura variety exists. This is a
p
-adic theory, rather than complex meromorphic, in the sense that rigid meromorphic cocycles are elements in cohomology groups of the form
H^{s}(\Gamma,\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{rq}}^{\times}),
where
\Gamma
is a
p
-arithmetic group and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{rq}}^{\times}
is the multiplicative group of rigid meromorphic functions on a certain
p
-adic symmetric space
X_{p}
attached to
G
. These cocycles can be evaluated at suitable special points on
X_{p}
, and the resulting
p
-adic values are conjectured to be algebraic, lying in abelian extensions of the reflex fields of the special points.

An important piece of evidence for the validity of the conjectures formulated in
[
DGL25
]
on the algebraicity of the values of rigid meromorphic cocycles at special points comes from numerical calculations for groups of signatures
(2,1)
and
(3,1)
. In case of signature
(2,1)
, the orthogonal group is just an inner form of
\SL_{2}
and
X_{p}=\mathcal{H}_{p}
is the
p
-adic upper half plane.
Thus, one recovers the rigid meromorphic cocycles introduced by Darmon–Vonk in
[
DV21
]
, which have been extensively studied in recent articles such as
[
DV25
]
,
[
DPV24
]
,
[
DV22b
]
,
[
DG22
]
,
[
DV22a
]
,
[
Geh22
]
, and
[
GMX21
]
.

The first genuinely new instance of the construction arises in signature
(3,1)
, where
\Gamma
is a Bianchi congruence subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/p])
with
K
an imaginary quadratic field, in which
p
is split, and
X_{p}=\mathcal{H}_{p}\times\mathcal{H}_{p}
is the product of two copies of the
p
-adic upper half plane. In this setting, experimental evidence supporting the algebraicity conjecture was obtained in
[
DGL25
]
when
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
for two types of special points: the so-called
small RM points
, which can be in fact regarded as “images under the diagonal embedding” of the special points considered in
[
DV21
]
, and the so-called
small CM points
, for which a few examples were calculated and found to be very close
p
-adically to algebraic numbers over the predicted fields of definition, with norms exhibiting remarkably small and concentrated support.

However, there is a third type of special points at which one can evaluate these Bianchi meromorphic cocycles, called
big special points
in
[
DGL25
]
. The reflex field of such points is an almost totally real (ATR) field, and their associated values were not computed numerically in
[
DGL25
]
. Therefore, the rationality conjecture remained untested for this type of points, which we will refer to as
big ATR points
in this article.

The main goal of this article is to explain how the big ATR points can be computed in the case
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
, and to present extensive numerical verifications of the algebraicity conjecture formulated in
[
DGL25
]
for these points. For completeness, we also extend the available numerical evidence in the cases of small RM and small CM points.

A bit more precisely, in §
2
we set the stage by explaining how quadratic number fields give rise to four-dimensional quadratic spaces. Moreover, we determine the fields of definition of the nascent rigid meromorphic cocycles predicted by the conjectures of
[
DGL25
]
.
In §
3
we recall the construction of a family of rigid meromorphic cocycles introduced in
[
DGL25
, §5.2]
, and we extend it in a slight but crucial way that enables evaluation at big ATR points. In §
4
we recall the construction of small RM points, small CM points, and big ATR points and describe how to perform this evaluation
Finally, in §
5
we present the numerical results and discuss the supporting evidence for the algebraicity conjecture.
Furthermore, we exhibit a divisibility criterion for the primes in the support of these values that resembles the criterion for the special values of Borcherds products on Hilbert modular surfaces proven in
[
BY06
]
.

2.
Spinor norms and the field of definition

The algebraicity conjecture for special values of rigid meromorphic cocycles of
[
DGL25
]
involves the compositum of two number fields: one is the field of definition of the point one is evaluating the rigid meromorphic cocycle at and the other is the field of definition of the rigid meromorphic cocycle itself.
The latter is a polyquadratic extension, whose definition involves certain local spinor groups.
In the following we will compute the local spinor groups in question and thus determine the field of definition for a large class of rigid meromorphic cocycles of four-dimensional quadratic spaces.

2.1.
The spinor norm

Let
F
be a field of characteristic
\mathrm{char}(F)\neq 2
and
(V,q)
a finite-dimensional non-degenerate quadratic space over
F
.
Write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the associated bilinear form, that is,
\langle v,v\rangle=2q(v)
for all
v\in V
.
For any
a\in V
with
q(a)\neq 0
write
\tau_{a}\in\Ort(V)
for the reflection

\tau_{a}\colon V\longrightarrow V,\ v\longmapsto v-2\frac{\langle a,v\rangle}{\langle a,a\rangle}a.

Recall that the
spinor norm
is the unique homomorphism

\sn_{F}\colon\Ort(V)\longrightarrow F^{\times}/(F^{\times})^{2}

such that for any
a\in V
with
q(a)\neq 0
the reflection
\tau_{a}
is mapped to
q(a)\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}
.
Let
W\subseteq V
be a non-degenerate subspace.
One can extend any
g\in\Ort(W)
to an element in
\Ort(V)
by letting it act as the identity on the orthogonal complement of
W
.
Under this embedding, the spinor norm on
\Ort(V)
restricts to the spinor norm on
\Ort(W)
.
Given a non-zero isotropic vector
w\in V
, that is
q(w)=0
, and an element
a\in V
with
\langle a,w\rangle=0
, one defines the
Eichler transformation
via

E(w,a)(v)\coloneqq v-\langle v,w\rangle a+\langle v,a\rangle w-q(a)\langle v,w\rangle w\quad\forall v\in V.

Straightforward calculations show that
E(w,a)\in\SO(V)
,
E(w,w)=\text{Id}_{V}
and that
E(w,a+b)=E(w,a)\circ E(w,b)
for all
a,b\in V
orthogonal to
w
.
As
E(w,a)=E(w,a/2)^{2}
, it follows that

\sn_{F}(E(w,a))=1.

Suppose that
R
is a principal ideal domain with field of fractions equal to
F
.
An
R
-lattice
L
in a quadratic
F
-vector space
(V,q)
is called
even
if
q(L)\subseteq R
.
It is called
self-dual
if it agrees with its
dual lattice

L^{\sharp}\coloneq\{v\in V\,|\,\langle v,L\rangle\subseteq R\}.

For an even lattice we consider the subgroups
\Ort(L)\subseteq\Ort(V)
and
\SO(L)\subseteq\SO(V)
of elements that respect the lattice
L
.
For an isotropic vector
w\in L
and
a\in L
orthogonal to
w
, the Eichler transformation
E(w,a)
belongs to
\SO(L)
.
The
discriminant module
of an even lattice is the quotient

D_{L}\coloneq L^{\sharp}/L,

which is a torsion
R
-module.
The quadratic form
q
descends to a quadratic map

D_{L}\longrightarrow F/R,\quad x\longmapsto q(x)\bmod R.

By a slight abuse of notation, we denote the quadratic map and the associated non-degenerate bilinear map by
q
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, respectively.
The group of
R
-linear transformations of
D_{L}
preserving
q
is denoted
\Ort(D_{L}).

The natural action of
\Ort(L)
on
L^{\sharp}
yields a homomorphism

(1)

\Ort(L)\longrightarrow\Ort(D_{L}),

which is surjective in case
R=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
by
[
Nik80
, Theorem 1.9.5]
.
If
L
is a self-dual even lattice, one may define the integral spinor norm

\sn_{R}\colon\Ort(L)\longrightarrow R^{\times}/(R^{\times})^{2}

as in
[
Con14
, Appendix C]
.
Since the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Ort(L)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R^{\times}/(R^{\times})^{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Ort(V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F^{\times}/(F^{\times})^{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\subset}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sn_{R}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sn_{F}}

is commutative, it follows that

(2)

\sn_{R}(\Ort(L))\subseteq R^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}.

We want to study certain four-dimensional quadratic spaces attached to quadratic étale extensions.
To that end, let
K
be an étale
F
-algebra of dimension
2
, i.e.,
K
is either a quadratic field extension or isomorphic to
F\times F
.
Given
\alpha\in K
write
\alpha^{\prime}
for its Galois conjugate and
\No_{K/F}(\alpha)=\alpha\alpha^{\prime}\in F
for its norm.
Consider the quadratic space
V^{\prime}_{K}
whose underlying vector space is
K
with quadratic form given by
\No_{K/F}
.
If
K
is a field,
V^{\prime}_{K}
is anisotropic.
On the other hand,
V^{\prime}_{K}
is a hyperbolic plane in case
K=F\times F
.
Letting
a\in K^{\times}
act on
V^{\prime}_{K}
by multiplying with
a/a^{\prime}
defines a homomorphism from
K^{\times}
to
\SO(V^{\prime}_{K})
.
In fact, multiplication with
a/a^{\prime}
is equal to the composition
\tau_{a}\circ\tau_{1}
(see for example
[
DGL25
, Lemma 4.3]
).
One easily checks that this assignment defines an isomorphism

(3)

K^{\times}/F^{\times}\xlongrightarrow{\sim}\SO(V^{\prime}_{K}).

By definition, the spinor norm of multiplication with
a/a^{\prime}
is equal to
\No_{K/F}(a)\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}
.
Moreover,
\tau_{1}(a)=-a^{\prime}
for every
a\in K
and, in particular, the equalities
\det(\tau_{1})=-1
and
\sn_{F}(\tau_{1})=1
hold.
In case
K=F\times F
, we will identify
K^{\times}/F^{\times}
with
F^{\times}
via the map
(a,b)\mapsto ab^{-1}
.

Consider the quadratic space
V_{K}\coloneq K\oplus F\oplus F
with quadratic form
q
given by
q(\alpha,b,c)=\No_{K/F}(\alpha)-bc
.
In other words,
V_{K}
is the orthogonal direct sum of
V^{\prime}_{K}
and a hyperbolic plane.
We identify
V_{K}
with a subspace of
M_{2}(K)
by sending
(\alpha,b,c)
to the matrix

[\alpha;b,c]\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-b\\
c&-\alpha^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Under this identification the quadratic form
q
is given by the negative of the determinant.
The group
G\coloneq\{g\in\GL_{2}(K)\ |\ \det(g)\in F^{\times}\}
acts on
V
by isometries via

g[\alpha;b,c]=g\cdot[\alpha;b,c]\cdot(g^{\prime})^{-1}

yielding a surjective homomorphism
G\rightarrow\SO(V_{K})
.
The kernel of this homomorphism is given by the subgroup of scalar diagonal matrices with entries in
F^{\times}
.
Thus, the determinant
\det\colon G\rightarrow F^{\times}
descends to a homomorphism

\SO(V_{K})\longrightarrow F^{\times}/(F^{\times})^{2},

which can be shown to agree with the spinor norm.
In particular, the subgroup of
\SO(V_{K})
of elements with trivial spinor norm is identified with the group
\PSL_{2}(K)
.

2.2.
The field of definition

Let
(V,q)
be a quadratic
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space of dimension
n\geq 3
and real signature
(r,s)
.
Moreover, for any subgroup
G\subseteq\Ort(V\otimes\mathbb{R})
we write
G^{+}\subseteq G
for the subgroup of elements of trivial real spinor norm.
Fix a prime
p\geq 3
such that
V\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}
admits a self-dual
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-lattice.
Let
X_{p}
be the
p
-adic symmetric space attached to
V
in
[
DGL25
, Section 1.3]
(see also Section
3.1
below).
This rigid analytic space carries a natural action by the group
\SO(V\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p})
.
Moreover, consider the group
\Div_{\mathrm{rq}}^{\dagger}(X_{p})
of locally finite, rational quadratic divisors on
X_{p}
(see
[
DGL25
, Section 2.3]
).
Finally, let
\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}
be the restricted product of the non-Archimedean fields
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
,
\ell\neq p
,
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^{p}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}
the maximal compact subring, and consider the space

S^{p}_{f}(V)\coloneq\{\Phi\colon V\otimes\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\,|\,\Phi\,\mbox{is locally constant with compact support}\}

of integer-valued Schwartz–Bruhat functions on
V\otimes\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}
.
Given
\Phi\in S^{p}_{f}(V)
we let
U_{\Phi}
be the stabilizer of
\Phi
in
\SO(V)
and
\Gamma_{\Phi}=U_{\Phi}\cap\SO(V)^{+}
the associated
p
-arithmetic subgroup.
Then, Section 2.4 of
[
DGL25
]
attaches to every positive rational number
d\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
the so-called
Kudla–Millson
divisor

\mathscr{D}_{d,\Phi}\in\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Gamma_{\Phi},\Div_{\mathrm{rq}}^{\dagger}(X_{p})).

The construction of Kudla–Millson divisors in special cases will be recalled in Section
3.2
below.
These Kudla–Millson divisors are
p
-adic analogues of Heegner divisors on connected components of orthogonal Shimura varieties.
Write
\mathbb{I}
for the idele group, which is the restricted product over the multiplicative groups
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}^{\times}
for all primes
\ell
and
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
.
Taking the product of the local spinor norms defines a homomorphism

\sn_{\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}}\colon\SO(V_{\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}})\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}.

By analogy with fields of definition of connected components of orthogonal Shimura varieties we make the following definition (cf.
[
DGL25
, Section 4.3.8]
):

Definition 2.1
.

The field of definition
E_{\Phi}
of
\Phi
is the polyquadratic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
whose Galois group is identified with

C(\Phi)\coloneq\mathbb{Q}^{\times}\backslash\mathbb{I}/\mathbb{I}^{2}\sn_{\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}}(U_{\Phi})\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\times}.

via the Artin reciprocity map.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that, by construction, the prime
p
is split in
E_{\Phi}
for every
\Phi\in S^{p}_{f}(V)
.

Let
K
be an étale
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra of degree
2
with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
In case
K=\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}
is the split étale algebra,
\mathcal{O}_{K}
denotes the order
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
.
The quadratic space
V_{K}
has signature
(3,1)
if
K
is imaginary quadratic and signature
(2,2)
otherwise.
The base change of
V_{K}
to
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
admits a self-dual lattice if and only if
p
is not ramified in
K
, which we will assume in the following.

Fix an integer
N\geq 1
coprime to
p
and put
\mathbb{Z}_{N}\coloneq\prod_{\ell\mid N}\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
.
Moreover, let
\chi\colon(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\to\{\pm 1\}
be a quadratic Dirichlet character,
which we also view as a character on
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{\times}
via pullback.
Denote by
K_{\chi}
the extension of
\mathbb{Q}
cut out by
\chi
, which is at most of degree
2
.
Consider the
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L_{K}\coloneq\mathcal{O}_{K}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\subseteq V_{K}
and its closure
\hat{L}^{p}_{K}
in
V_{K}\otimes\mathbb{A}_{f}^{p}
.
The Schwartz–Bruhat function
\Phi_{\chi}\in S^{p}_{f}(V_{K})
is defined by

\Phi_{\chi}(\alpha,b,c)=\begin{cases}\chi(c_{N})&\mbox{if}\,(\alpha,b,c)\in\hat{L}^{p}_{K},\,c_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{\times},\\
0&\mbox{else},\end{cases}

where
c_{N}
denotes the projection from
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^{p}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The field of definition
E_{\Phi_{\chi}}
of
\Phi_{\chi}
is equal to
\mathbb{Q}
unless
\chi
is non-trivial,
K=K_{\chi}
, and
p
is split in
K
, in which case
E_{\Phi_{\chi}}=K_{\chi}
.

We will prove Proposition
2.3
in Section
2.4
.

2.3.
Local spinor norms

From now on
F
denotes a non-Archimedean local field with
\mathrm{char}(F)\neq 2
and
K
is an étale
F
-algebra of degree
2
.
Let
L^{\prime}_{K}\subseteq V^{\prime}_{K}
be the even lattice given by the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of
K
.
Analogously to the case of the rational numbers,
\mathcal{O}_{K}
denotes the order
\mathcal{O}_{F}\times\mathcal{O}_{F}
in case
K=F\times F
is split.
The lattice
L^{\prime}_{K}
is self-dual if and only if
K/F
is unramified.

Lemma 2.4
.

(a)

We have:

\sn_{F}(\Ort(L^{\prime}_{K}))=\sn_{F}(\SO(L^{\prime}_{K}))=\begin{cases}\No_{K/F}(K^{\times})\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}&\text{if $K$ is a field},\\
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}&\text{if $K=K\times K$.}\end{cases}

(b)

Assume that
F=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
for some prime
p
.
Then the homomorphism

\SO(L^{\prime}_{K})\longrightarrow D_{L^{\prime}_{K}}

is surjective.

Proof.

The reflection
\tau_{1}\in\Ort(L^{\prime}_{K})
has trivial spinor norm but non-trivial determinant and, hence,
\sn_{F}(\Ort(L^{\prime}_{K}))=\sn_{F}(\SO(L^{\prime}_{K}))
.
In case
K
is a field,
a/a^{\prime}
belongs to
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
and, thus, defines an automorphism of the lattice
L^{\prime}_{K}
.
If
K=F\times F
, we may assume that
a
is of the form
(b,1)
,
b\in F^{\times}
.
Since
a/a^{\prime}=(b,b^{-1})
, this defines an automorphism of the lattice
L^{\prime}_{F\times F}
if and only if
b\in\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}
.

Part (
b
) is trivial in case
K/\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is unramified.
So assume that
K/\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is a ramified quadratic extension.
In that case
\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}+\mathbb{Z}_{p}\sqrt{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
In particular,
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with the quadratic form
x\mapsto x^{2}
splits off as an orthogonal direct summand of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
This lattice is self-dual for
p\neq 2
, while for
p=2
its discriminant module is one-dimensional over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
In any case, the reflection
\tau_{1}
or in other words, multiplication by
-1
on
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, acts trivially on
D_{L^{\prime}_{K}}
.
Thus, the claim follows from the surjectivity of (
1
).
∎

Next we compute the group of spinor norms for the lattice
L_{K}\coloneq\mathcal{O}_{K}\times\mathcal{O}_{F}\times\mathcal{O}_{F}\subseteq V_{K}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

We have:

\sn_{F}(\Ort(L_{K}))=\sn_{F}(\SO(L_{K}))=\begin{cases}F^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}&\mbox{if}\,K\,\mbox{is ramified},\\
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}&\mbox{else}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The first equality follows by considering the reflection attached to
1\in K
as in the proof of Lemma
2.4
.
As the lattice
L_{K}
splits of a hyperbolic plane, we have
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}\subseteq\sn_{F}(\Ort(L_{K}))
in any case by Lemma
2.4
.
If
K
is ramified, Lemma
2.4
also implies that a uniformizer of
F
lies in
\sn_{F}(\Ort(L_{K}))
.
So assume that
K
is not ramified.
Then the lattice
L_{K}
is self-dual and we may conclude by invoking (
2
).
∎

Let
\chi\colon\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}\to\{\pm 1\}
be a continuous character.
If
\chi
is trivial, we put
K_{\chi}=F
.
Otherwise,
K_{\chi}
denotes the unique quadratic extension of
F
with
\No_{K_{\chi}/F}(K_{\chi}^{\times})\cap\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}=\ker(\chi)
, which is necessarily ramified.
Similarly as above, consider the Schwartz–Bruhat function
\varphi_{\chi}\colon V_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
given by

\varphi_{\chi}(\alpha,b,c)=\begin{cases}\chi(c)&\mbox{if}\,(\alpha,b,c)\in L_{K},\,c\in\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times},\\
0&\mbox{else},\end{cases}

and its stabilizer
U_{\chi}\subseteq\SO(V_{K})
.
Note that
U_{\chi}
sends the set
\{v\in V_{K}\,|\,\varphi_{\chi}(v)=1\}
to itself.
As this set generates
L_{K}
, we see that
U_{\chi}
is a subgroup of
\SO(L_{K})
.

Lemma 2.6
.

We have:

\sn_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(U_{\chi})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}&\mbox{if}\,K\,\mbox{is unramified},\\
F^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}&\mbox{if}\,K\,\mbox{is ramified and}\,K\ncong K_{\chi}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Assume first that
K
is unramified.
Then by Lemma
2.4
,
\sn_{F}(U_{\chi})
contains
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}
, while it is contained in the latter by Lemma
2.5
.
In the ramified case, Lemma
2.4
again implies that
\sn_{F}(U_{\chi})
contains
\No_{K/F}(K^{\times})\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}
.
The action of
\ker(\chi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}
on the hyperbolic plane spanned by the coordinates
b
and
c
clearly stabilizes
\Phi_{\chi}
.
In case
K\neq K_{\chi}
one easily sees that
\ker(\chi)
and
\No_{K/F}(K^{\times})
together generate
F^{\times}\bmod(F^{\times})^{2}
.
∎

2.4.
Proof of Proposition
2.3

By (
3
), the orthogonal group of the hyperbolic plane spanned by the coordinates
b
and
c
can be identified with the multiplicative group.
Under this identification
\ker(\chi)\subseteq(\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^{p})^{\times}
clearly stabilizes
\Phi_{\chi}
.
In particular,
\ker(\chi)
is contained in
\sn_{\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}}(U_{\Phi_{\chi}})
, which implies that
E_{\Phi_{\chi}}\subseteq K_{\chi}
.
From Remark
2.2
and Lemma
2.6
it follows that
E_{\Phi_{\chi}}=\mathbb{Q}
unless
p
is split in
K_{\chi}
and
K=K_{\chi}
.
So let us assume that this is the case.
Consider the even lattice
L_{K}(N)\coloneq\{(\alpha,b,c)\in L_{K}\,|\,N\mid C\}
.
Its discriminant module splits into the orthogonal direct sum
D_{L_{K}(N)}=D_{L^{\prime}_{K}}\oplus H
, where
H
is the free
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
2
with generators
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
having representatives
b/N
and
c
,respectively, and therefore satisfying

q(e_{1})=q(e_{2})=0\bmod\mathbb{Z}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle=-1/N\bmod\mathbb{Z}.

One easily checks that the group
U_{\Phi_{\chi}}
stabilizes the completion of the lattice
L_{K}(N)
in
V_{K}\otimes\mathbb{A}^{p}_{f}
and, hence, it acts on
D_{L_{K}(N)}
.
Since it also stabilizes the completion of the lattice
L_{K}
, it stabilizes the
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
-line generated by
e_{2}
.
By multiplying with an element of
\ker(\chi)
(viewed as a subgroup of the automorphisms of the hyperbolic plane spanned by
b
and
c
), it is enough to consider those
g\in U_{\Phi_{\chi}}
that stabilize
e_{2}
.
For such
g
we compute

\langle g\alpha,e_{2}\rangle=\langle g\alpha,ge_{2}\rangle=\langle\alpha,e_{2}\rangle=0\quad\forall\alpha\in D_{L^{\prime}_{K}}.

In particular,
g\alpha
is of the form
\alpha_{g}+g_{\alpha}e_{2}
for some
\alpha_{g}\in D_{L^{\prime}_{K}}
and
g_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
.
As
q(\alpha)=q(g\alpha)=q(\alpha_{g}+g_{\alpha}e_{2})=q(\alpha_{g})
, the assignment
\alpha\mapsto\alpha_{g}
defines an element of
\Ort(D_{L^{\prime}_{K}})
.
Using Lemma
2.4
, we may reduce to the case that
\alpha_{g}=\alpha
for all
\alpha\in D_{L^{\prime}_{K}}
.
By a similar computation as above, we see that
ge_{1}=e_{1}+g_{e_{1}}e_{2}+\beta
for some
g_{e_{1}}\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta\in D_{L^{\prime}_{K}}
.
As
ge_{1}
is isotropic and orthogonal to
g\alpha
, one deduces that

q(\beta)=-g_{\beta}/N\quad\mbox{and}\quad\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=-g_{\alpha}/N.

Let
\widetilde{\beta}\in L_{K}(N)^{\sharp}
be a lift of
\beta
.
Since
N\widetilde{\beta}\in L_{K}(N)
, the Eichler transformation
E(c,N\widetilde{\beta})
belongs to
\SO(L_{K}(N))
.
One readily checks that
E(c,N\widetilde{\beta})
and
g
induce the same action on
D
.
Since Eichler transformations have trivial spinor norm, we may assume that
g
acts trivially on
D
.
But every
g
that acts trivially on
D
satisfies
\sn_{\mathbb{A}_{f}^{p}}(g)\in\ker(\chi)
:
in case
p\neq 2
, this follows from
[
MM09
, Theorem 9.3]
, while in case
p=2
, it follows from Theorem 10.7 and Theorem 10.8 of
[
MM09
]
.
Thus, the assertion follows.

3.
Rigid meromorphic cocycles for the Gaussian rationals

Let
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
be a prime, and let
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
be the field of Gaussian rationals. In this section we recall the construction and algorithmic computation of a family of rigid meromorphic cocycles or, more precisely, rigid meromorphic modular symbols, for a congruence subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/p])
as described in
[
DGL25
, §5.2]
, and we complement it in one essential aspect. In
loc.cit.
only the evaluation of these modular symbols at the pair of cusps
(0,\infty)
was provided. While this suffices for evaluating at small CM and small RM points, evaluation at big ATR points requires the values of the modular symbol at other pairs of cusps. This is precisely the extension that we develop here.

Let
V=V_{K}
be the four dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space

\displaystyle V=\left\{[\alpha,b,c]=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&-b\\
c&-\alpha^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{M}_{2}(K)\ \middle|\ \alpha\in K,b,c\in\mathbb{Q}\right\},

where
\alpha^{\prime}
denotes the
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
-conjugate of
\alpha
.
Regard
V
as a quadratic space over
\mathbb{Q}
with quadratic form
q
given by
q([\alpha,b,c])=-\det([\alpha,b,c])=\No_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)-bc
.
Recall from Section
2.1
that the group
\SL_{2}(K)
acts on
V
by means of

\displaystyle\gamma*M=\gamma M(\gamma^{\prime})^{-1}

and this action identifies
\PSL_{2}(K)
with the subgroup of elements in
\SO(V)
with trivial spinor norm.

3.1.
Symmetric spaces

The symmetric space
X_{\infty}
of the real Lie group
\SO(V\otimes\mathbb{R})
is given by the space of negative definite lines in
V\otimes\mathbb{R}
.
It carries a natural action of
\SO(V\otimes\mathbb{R})
and, therefore, it also carries an action of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
For an element
M\in V
with
q(M)>0
we write
\Delta_{M,\infty}
for the subspace of
X_{\infty}
of all lines orthogonal to
M
.
We identify
X_{\infty}
with hyperbolic
3
-space via the map

X_{\infty}\xlongrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0},\quad[\alpha,b,c]\longmapsto(\alpha/c,-q([\alpha,b,c])/c^{2}).

Under this identification
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
acts on
(z,t)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
via

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}.(z,t)=\left(\frac{(az+b)\overline{(cz+d)}+act^{2})}{|cz+d|^{2}+|c|^{2}t^{2}},\frac{t}{|cz+d|^{2}+|c|^{2}t^{2}}\right).

Let
\overline{X}_{\infty}
be the space obtained by attaching
\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)
to
X_{\infty}
by mapping
\alpha\in K
to
(\alpha,0)
and
\infty
to
(0,\infty)
.
The action of
\SL_{2}(K)
on
X_{\infty}
extends continuously to an action on
\overline{X}_{\infty}
.
On the boundary the action is given by fractional linear transformations.
Given
r,s\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)
, we denote by
(r,s)
the open hyperbolic geodesic path from
r
to
s
in
X_{\infty}
.
Let
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)]_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)]
the subgroup of divisors of degree
0
.
Given an abelian group
M
, an
M
-valued modular symbol
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear homomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)]_{0}
to
M
.
In case
M
carries a
\Gamma
-action for some subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\GL_{2}(K)
, we endow the space
\mathcal{MS}(M)
of
M
-valued modular symbols with the
\Gamma
-action given by
(\gamma F)(D)=\gamma(F(\gamma^{-1}D))
.
Write

(4)

\delta\colon\mathcal{MS}^{\Gamma}(M)\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Gamma,M)

for the first boundary map in the long exact sequence in group cohomology induced by the short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)]_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)]\xlongrightarrow{\deg}\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow 0.

A modular symbol is uniquely characterized by its values on elements of the form
[s]-[r]
, which we identify with the geodesic
(r,s)
.
A function
F
on these open hyperbolic geodesics comes from a modular symbol if and only if

F(r,s)=-F(s,r)\quad\mbox{\quad}F(r,s)+F(s,t)+F(t,r)=0\ \forall r,s,t\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(K).

Let
Q
be the zero locus of
q
inside the projective space of
V
.
In other words,
Q
parametrizes isotropic lines in
V
.
The
p
-adic symmetric space
X_{p}
attached to
V
is the connected rigid analytic subspace of the base change of
Q
to
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, whose
\mathbb{C}_{p}
-valued points are given by

X_{p}(\mathbb{C}_{p})=\{\xi\in X_{p}\,|\,\langle\xi,w\rangle=0\ \forall w\in Q(\mathbb{Q}_{p})\}.

See
[
DGL25
, Section 1.3]
for more details on the construction.
As in the Archimedean case, for
M\in V
with
q(M)>0
we consider the divisor
\Delta_{M,p}\subseteq X_{p}
given by all isotropic lines orthogonal to
M
.
Now fix once and for all an embedding
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{p}
.
As the prime
p
is split in
K
, this embedding induces the isomorphism

K\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\xlongrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Q}_{p}\times\mathbb{Q}_{p},\quad\alpha\otimes x\longmapsto(\alpha x,\alpha^{\prime}x).

Utilizing this isomorphism we identify
X_{p}
with the square of the
p
-adic upper half space
\mathcal{H}_{p}
via the assignment

\mathcal{H}_{p}\times\mathcal{H}_{p}\xlongrightarrow{\sim}X_{p},\quad(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\longmapsto[(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}),\tau_{1}\tau_{2},1].

Under this identification the divisor
\Delta_{M,p}
of a matrix
M=[\alpha,b,c]\in V
with
q(M)>0
is given by the vanishing locus of the function

F_{M}\colon\mathcal{H}_{p}\times\mathcal{H}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{p},\quad(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\longmapsto c\tau_{1}\tau_{2}-\alpha\tau_{1}-\alpha^{\prime}\tau_{2}+b.

3.2.
Modular symbols

Denote by
L
the
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
V\cap\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
and similarly write
L[1/p]
for the
\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
-lattice
V\cap\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/p])
.
Let
\chi_{4}
be the odd Dirichlet character modulo 4, and for
M=[\alpha,b,c]\in L[1/p]
put
\chi_{4}(M)=\chi_{4}(c)
.
Observe that
\chi_{4}(p)=1
, so the quantity
\chi_{4}(c)
makes sense even if
c
has denominators, simply by applying
\chi_{4}
to the numerator of
c
.
Moreover, let
\Gamma\subseteq\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/p])
be the subgroup of matrices that are upper triangular modulo
2
.
Denote by
\Div^{\dagger}(X_{p})
the group of locally finite, rational quadratic divisors on
X_{p}
as defined in
[
DGL25
, Section 2.3.3]
.
For every integer
d>0
that is not a norm of an element in
K
, it is proved in Section 2.6 of
loc.cit.
that the assignment

(5)

(r,s)\longmapsto\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in L[1/p]\\
q(M)=d\end{subarray}}\chi_{4}(M)(\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s))\cdot\Delta_{M,p}

defines a
\Gamma
-invariant modular symbol with values in
\Div^{\dagger}(X_{p})
.
Here,
\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)
is the intersection product between the cycle
\Delta_{M,\infty}
and the hyperbolic geodesic path
(r,s)
joining
r
and
s
in
X_{\infty}
as defined in
[
DGL25
, Section 2.6]
.
The image of the modular symbol (
5
) under the boundary map (
4
) is equal to the Kudla–Millson divisor
\mathscr{D}_{d,\Phi_{\chi_{4}}}
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{\times}
be the group of multiplicative rigid meromorphic functions on
X_{p}=\mathcal{H}_{p}\times\mathcal{H}_{p}
.
For every positive integer
d
that is not a norm from
K
, we consider the formal modular symbol
J_{d}
for
\Gamma
with values in
\mathcal{M}^{\times}
defined in
[
DGL25
, §5.2]
, which evaluated at a pair
r,s\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)
is given by

(6)

\displaystyle J_{d}(r,s)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in L[1/p]\\
q(M)=d\end{subarray}}F_{M}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})^{\chi_{4}(M)(\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s))}.

Its image under the divisor map is clearly equal to (
5
).
For
{\mathscr{D}}=\sum n_{i}(d_{i})
a formal sum of positive integers each not a norm from
K
, define

(7)

\displaystyle J_{\mathscr{D}}(r,s)=\prod_{i}J_{d_{i}}(r,s)^{n_{i}}.

As explained in
[
DGL25
, §5.2]
, if the assignment
(r,s)\mapsto J_{{\mathscr{D}}}(r,s)
is
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
-equivariant, then the theory of modular symbols reduces the calculation of
J_{\mathscr{D}}
to that of
J_{d}(0,\infty)
. However, due to the presence of the character
\chi_{4}
in the exponent of (
6
) one can not expect equivariance by the full
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
, but only by the congruence subgroup

\Gamma_{0}(2)=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}x&y\\
z&t\end{matrix}\right)\in\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\colon 2\mid z\right\}.

Therefore, the theory of modular symbols reduces the calculation of
J_{\mathscr{D}}
to that of
J_{\mathscr{D}}(g_{j}0,g_{j}\infty)
for some system of representatives
g_{1},\dots,g_{h}
of
\Gamma_{0}(2)\backslash\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
.

In
[
DGL25
, §5.2]
, conditions on
{\mathscr{D}}
are given in order that the formal product (
7
) converges when evaluated at the pair of cusps
(0,\infty)
. This is enough for computing values at small RM or small CM points. Indeed, for these points one needs to evaluate
J_{\mathscr{D}}(r,s)
where the pair
(r,s)
has the special feature that the path
(r,s)
can be written as

\displaystyle(r,s)=\sum_{j}(\gamma_{j}0,\gamma_{j}\infty)=\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}(0,\infty)

for some matrices
\gamma_{j}
that belong to
\Gamma_{0}(2)
. But this is not true in general for an arbitrary pair of cusps
(r,s)
and, in particular, for the cusps that appear when evaluating at big ATR points.

In order to evaluate the cocycle at an arbitrary pair of cusps
(r,s)
, one can proceed as follows. First of all, use the Manin trick to write

\displaystyle(r,s)=\sum_{j}(\gamma_{j}0,\gamma_{j}\infty)

for some
\gamma_{j}\in\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
. Let
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{h}\}
be a set of representatives for
\Gamma_{0}(2)\backslash\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
and write
\gamma_{j}=\gamma_{j}^{\prime}g_{k(j)}
, with
\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{0}(2)
and some
k(j)\in\{1,\dots,h\}
.
By
\Gamma_{0}(2)
-equivariance we have that

\displaystyle J_{\mathscr{D}}(\gamma_{j}^{\prime}g_{k(j)}0,\gamma_{j}^{\prime}g_{k(j)}\infty)=(\gamma_{j}^{\prime})^{-1}.J_{\mathscr{D}}(g_{k(j)}0,g_{k(j)}\infty).

Therefore, knowing the values
J_{\mathscr{D}}(g_{j}0,g_{j}\infty)
for
j=1,\dots,h
suffices to compute
J_{\mathscr{D}}(r,s)
. In fact, even less information is required, a fact that is crucial for accelerating the computations. Indeed, since
[\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\colon\Gamma_{0}(2)]=6
, one might expect that the values at six pair of cusps are required; however, as we show in the next result, it is enough to know
J_{\mathscr{D}}
at only two pairs of elements in
\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)
instead of six. For this, it will be important to consider also the opposite path to
(r,s)
, that we denote by
-(r,s)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\
i+1&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
and let
r,s\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)
. The path
(r,s)
can be written as

(8)

\displaystyle(r,s)=\sum_{j}\pm\gamma_{j}(0,\infty)+\sum_{k}\pm\gamma_{k}(g0,g\infty)=\sum_{j}\pm\gamma_{j}(0,\infty)+\sum_{k}\pm\gamma_{k}(0,\frac{1+i}{2}),

for some matrices
\gamma_{j},\gamma_{k}\in\Gamma_{0}(2)
; we remark that the
\pm
sign in the above expression refers to the fact that each of the paths in the sum can be either as it appears or its opposite.

Proof.

A direct computation shows that a system of representatives for
\Gamma_{0}(2)\backslash\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
is given by:

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),\,g_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),\,g_{3}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\
i+1&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle g_{4}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&-i\\
-2i+1&i-1\end{smallmatrix}\right),\,g_{5}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\
i+2&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right),\,g_{6}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\
2i-1&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right).

By the Manin trick, it is enough to find an expression of the form (
8
) for the cusps of the form
(g_{j}0,g_{j}\infty)
. This is obviously true for
j=1,3
. For the rest, one can directly check that

\displaystyle(g_{2}0,g_{2}\infty)

\displaystyle=-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right)(0,\infty)

\displaystyle(g_{4}0,g_{4}\infty)

\displaystyle=-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i-2&1\\
4&-i-2\end{smallmatrix}\right)(0,\frac{1+i}{2})

\displaystyle(g_{5}0,g_{5}\infty)

\displaystyle=-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-i&i\\
-2&i+2\end{smallmatrix}\right)(0,\infty)+\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\
2&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right)(0,\infty)

\displaystyle(g_{6}0,g_{6}\infty)

\displaystyle=-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&i\\
2i+2&2i-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)(0,\infty)+\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\
2i-2&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right)(0,\infty).

∎

Following
[
DGL25
, §5.2]
, it is convenient for the algorithmic calculation to decompose

J_{d}(r,s)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\Phi_{d,j}(r,s),

where the
\Phi_{d,j}(r,s)
are given by

(9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{d,j}(r,s)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in L\\
q(M)=dp^{2j}\end{subarray}}F_{M}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})^{\chi_{4}(M)\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)}.

The set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{d,j}(0,\infty)=\{M\in L\,|\,q(M)=dp^{2j}\ \text{and}\ \Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(0,\infty)\neq 0\}

can be computed algorithmically. Indeed, by
[
DGL25
, Lemma 5.1]
we have that

(10)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{d,j}(0,\infty)=\{M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&-b\\
c&-\alpha\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\colon\alpha\alpha^{\prime}-bc=p^{2j},\,bc<0\},

which is obviously finite and effectively computable since there are only finitely many possibilities for
b
and
c
, and for every choice of
b
and
c
there are finitely many possibilities for
\alpha
.
For any pair of elements
r,s\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)
, the set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{d,j}(r,s)=\{M\in M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\,|\,q(M)=dp^{2j}\ \text{and}\ \Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)\neq 0\}

can also be computed, using the following two properties of the intersection product:

\displaystyle\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)=\Delta_{\gamma M,\infty}\cap(\gamma r,\gamma s)\text{ for all }\gamma\in\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\ \mbox{and}

\displaystyle\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,t)=\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)+\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(s,t),\ \mbox{for all}\ r,s,t\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(K).

Indeed, using the Manin trick, we write

\displaystyle(r,s)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{j}(0,\infty)\text{ with }\gamma_{j}\in\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K}).

Using the two properties above, we see that

(11)

\displaystyle\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)=\sum\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(\gamma_{j}0,\gamma_{j}\infty)=\sum\Delta_{\gamma_{j}^{-1}M,\infty}\cap(0,\infty).

From this, we deduce that
\gamma_{j}^{-1}M=M_{0}
for some
M_{0}\in\Sigma_{d,j}(0,\infty)
if
\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)\neq 0
. Therefore, we can compute
\Sigma_{d,j}(r,s)
running over matrices
M\in\cup_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{j}\Sigma_{d,j}(0,\infty)
(there are finitely many of these and we can compute them since we can compute
\Sigma_{d,j}(0,\infty)
), and for each
M
we compute
\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)
using (
11
); if
\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)\neq 0
we put
M
in
\Sigma_{d,j}(r,s)
and if
\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(r,s)=0
we discard it.

By the Manin trick and
\Gamma_{0}(2)
-equivariance, it is therefore enough to compute
J_{\mathscr{D}}(0,\infty)
and
J_{\mathscr{D}}(g0,g\infty)
for
g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\
i+1&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right)
in order to compute the cocycle
J_{\mathscr{D}}
.

3.3.
Convergence criterion

In
[
DGL25
, §5.2]
, conditions on the divisors
{\mathscr{D}}
are found to ensure that
J_{\mathscr{D}}(0,\infty)
converges. We next show that one extra condition is needed to guarantee that
J_{\mathscr{D}}(g0,g\infty)
also converges. Define the weight
\operatorname{wt}(\Phi_{d,j}(r,s))
of
\Phi_{d,j}(r,s)
to be the sum of the exponents that appear in (
9
).
The following lemma compares the weights of
\Phi_{d,j}(0,\infty)
and
\Phi_{d,j}(g0,g\infty)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have:

\operatorname{wt}(\Phi_{d,j}(0,\infty))=(-1)^{d}\operatorname{wt}(\Phi_{d,j}(g0,g\infty)).

Proof.

On the one hand, as in the proof of
[
DGL25
, Lemma 5.2]
we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{wt}(\Phi_{d,j}(0,\infty))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\alpha^{\prime}-bc=dp^{2j}\\
bc<0\end{subarray}}\chi_{4}(c)\operatorname{sign}(c).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\operatorname{wt}(\Phi_{d,j}(g0,g\infty))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\Sigma_{d,j}(r,s)\\
q(M)=dp^{2j}\end{subarray}}\chi_{4}(M)(\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(g0,g\infty))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\Sigma_{d,j}(0,\infty)\\
q(M)=dp^{2j}\end{subarray}}\chi_{4}(gM)(\Delta_{gM,\infty}\cap(g0,g\infty))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\Sigma_{d,j}(0,\infty)\\
q=dp^{2j}\end{subarray}}\chi_{4}(gM)(\Delta_{M,\infty}\cap(0,\infty))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\alpha^{\prime}-bc=dp^{2j}\\
bc<0\end{subarray}}\chi_{4}(c^{\prime})\operatorname{sign}(c),

where for a matrix
M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&-b\\
c&-\alpha^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}r+is&-b\\
c&-r+is\end{smallmatrix}\right)
we denote by
c^{\prime}
the lower left entry of
gM
. A direct computation shows that

(12)

\displaystyle c^{\prime}=-2(r+s)+2b+c.

We next show that
\chi_{4}(c^{\prime})=\chi_{4}(c)
if
d
is even and that
\chi_{4}(c^{\prime})=-\chi_{4}(c)
if
d
is odd, from which the result follows immediately. To begin with, clearly
c
is even if and only if
c^{\prime}
is even, so that
\chi_{4}(c)=0
if and only if
\chi_{4}(c^{\prime})=0
. So we can assume from now on that
c
is odd.

Suppose now that
d
is even. If
b
is even, the fact that

(13)

\displaystyle r^{2}+s^{2}-bc=dp^{2j}

implies that
r
and
s
have the same parity, and then (
12
) shows that
c^{\prime}\equiv c\pmod{4}
. If

Finally, suppose that

∎

Let
{\mathscr{D}}=\sum_{i}n_{i}(d_{i})
be a divisor and put
\Phi_{{\mathscr{D}},j}=\prod_{i}\Phi_{d_{i},j}^{n_{i}}
. By

, Lemma 5.2]
, we have that

\mathrm{wt}(\Phi_{d,j}(0,\infty))=16\sigma(dp^{2j}),\text{ where }\sigma(n)=\sum_{4\nmid e\mid n}e.

Combining this with Lemma

, we see that if

\displaystyle\sum_{i}n_{i}\sigma(d_{i})=0\text{ and }\sum_{i}(-1)^{d_{i}}n_{i}\sigma(d_{i})=0,

then both

have weight 0 for all

\displaystyle\sum_{i}n_{i}a_{d_{i}}(f)=0

We have numerically tested the convergence of the products defining

3.4.
Overconvergent algorithm

Observe that computing

by running over all possible

and

in (

) results in an algorithm of exponential complexity on

-adic accuracy to which

, and hence

, are computed. Therefore, this is only feasible for small values of

. To compute

we use the same iterative method of
[

]
, which is similar to those of
[

]
,
[
DP06
]
, or
[

]
. With this method, it is enough to compute

for

to compute

and

by the formula

where

Observe that, by (

only matrices whose determinant has even

As we will see in §

and

4.
Evaluation at special points

In this section we explain how to evaluate the rigid meromorphic cocycle
J_{\mathscr{D}}
of Section
3
at arguments coming from certain ATR fields, the so-called
big ATR points
. This section is inspired by
[
DL03
]
, which evaluates Hilbert modular forms at analogous quantities arising from ATR fields.
But first, let us briefly recall how to evaluate rigid meromorphic cocycles at small RM points and small CM points.

4.1.
Evaluation at small RM and CM points

Let
E
a real quadratic number field, in which the prime
p
is inert.
Fix a generator
u
of the group of norm-one units of
\mathcal{O}_{E}
.
By choosing a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\mathcal{O}_{E}
, the element
u
gives rise to a matrix
\gamma_{u}\in\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Since
p
is inert in
E
, it follows that
\gamma_{u}
has exactly two fixed points
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
,
which can be computed by solving quadratic equations.
The matrix
\gamma_{u}
also acts on the
p
-adic symmetric space

via the embedding
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\SL_{2}(K)
.
The four fixed points of this action are given by
(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})
,

.
The
reflex field

of such a fixed point

is a quadratic number field contained in the compositum
EK
.
More precisely, it is equal to

if

, in which case we call
\tau
a
small RM point
.
Otherwise,
\tau
is a
small CM point
, and

is the imaginary quadratic subfield of

in which

is inert.
In any case, we define the evaluation of

at

One can similarly evaluate the cocycle

by means of

J_{\mathscr{D}}^{\mathrm{odd}}[\tau]\coloneq J_{\mathscr{D}}^{\mathrm{odd}}(\infty,\gamma_{u}\infty)(\tau).

4.2.1.
Algebraicity conjecture

The main conjecture of
[
DGL25
]
predicts that the quantity
j=J_{\mathscr{D}}[\tau]
, which in principle belongs to
\mathbb{C}_{p}
, is in fact an algebraic number for every special point
x\in X_{p}
as described above.
More precisely, by the conjectural reciprocity law of
[
DGL25
, Section 4.4]
it should be contained in the compositum of

•

the field of definition of the rigid meromorphic cocycle, which by Proposition
2.3
is equal to
\mathbb{Q}(i)
, and

•

an abelian extension
A
of
E
, which is unramified outside
2
.
Moreover, let
F_{\tau}\subseteq E_{\tau}
denote the unique subfield of the reflex field of
\tau
with
[E_{\tau}:F_{\tau}]=2
.
Then
A/F_{\tau}
is Galois and
\Gal(E_{\tau},F_{\tau})
acts on
\Gal(A/E_{\tau})
via inversion.

In other words,
E_{\tau}(j)/E_{\tau}
is conjectured to be an abelian extension unramified outside
2
.
Moreover, the only subextensions of the Galois closure of
E_{\tau}(j)/F_{\tau}
that are abelian over
F_{\tau}
are polyquadratic.
Since the field of definition of the rigid meromorphic cocycle is
\mathbb{Q}(i)
, we expect
\mathbb{Q}(i)
to be a subfield of that Galois closure frequently.

Tables of small CM and small RM points

In addition to computing the values at big ATR points, we have also evaluated all cocycles at several small CM and small RM points, and we have recognized many of these values as probably algebraic. Although the first such experiments were already carried out in
[

]
, our computations provide further numerical evidence for the rationality conjecture in these cases, as we have found 56 new probably algebraic non-trivial small CM points and 591 small RM points. The complete data for these computations can be found in table

For the case of small RM, as we explained in the introduction, they are related to the special values of the rigid meromorphic cocycles of Darmon–Vonk

As for the case of small CM points, perhaps the most remarkable novelty is that we have found some special values defined over number fields strictly larger than
