Critical Assessment of Fellowship Director Gender and Ethnic Diversity across the Five Major Plastic Surgery Fellowships
Authors: Sam Boroumand, Sasha Stogniy, Beatrice Katsnelson, Omar Allam, Aliyar Zahedi Vafa, Janessa Sullivan, Bohdan Pomahac, Siba Haykal, Paris D. Butler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Subspecialty fellowship directors are essential to the selection and mentorship of trainees. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the gender and racial/ethnic diversity of fellowship directors across fellowship programs in plastic and reconstructive surgery (PRS).Methods:In December 2023, listings of fellowship programs/directors were surveyed from respective professional society websites across the 5 primary PRS fellowships: hand (American Society for Surgery of the Hand), craniofacial (American Society of Craniofacial Surgeons), microsurgery (American Society for Reconstructive Microsurgery), aesthetic (The Aesthetic Society), and burn (American Burn Association). Fellowship director demographics from each program were researched and identified.Results:In total, 248 fellowship directors were identified: 94 hand, 34 craniofacial, 62 microsurgery, 38 aesthetic, and 20 burn. There was a significantly greater percentage of male versus female directors across all fellowships (87.5% versus 12.5%; P < 0.01). Men were noted to have a significantly higher average h-index score (20.4 versus 15.0; P = 0.045) but constituted a lower proportion of assistant professorships (32.3% versus 12.9%; P < 0.01). Relative to directors from racial/ethnic minority backgrounds, White directors constituted a significantly greater percentage of fellowship directors (72.9% versus 27.1%; P < 0.01) and had a greater number of years passed since completing their most recent training (21.7 versus 17.1; P = 0.030).Conclusions:Across PRS fellowship programs, women and racial/ethnic minorities are disproportionately underrepresented in leadership. Targeted initiatives to promote diversity in PRS fellowship directors should be considered as medicine works toward a surgical workforce more reflective of the patient population.

Takeaways

Question: What is the gender and racial/ethnic composition of leadership among the 5 major plastic surgery fellowships, comparatively?

Findings: Across all fellowships there was a significantly greater percentage of male (87.5%) versus female directors (12.5%), with White directors constituting a significantly greater percentage (72.9%) relative to directors from racial/ethnic minority backgrounds (27.1%). In particular, Black and Latino directors (historically underrepresented in medicine) constituted 2% and 6%, respectively, of fellowship director positions.

Meaning: Across plastic surgery fellowship programs, women and racial/ethnic minorities are disproportionately underrepresented in leadership.

INTRODUCTION

To meet the healthcare needs of an increasingly diverse patient population, while mitigating longstanding healthcare disparities, it is critical to foster a medical workforce that reflects the diversity of the population it serves.1–7 Although strides have been made to prioritize the recruitment of racially, ethnically, and gender-diverse students into medical schools, there has been no significant change in the matriculation of racially/ethnically underrepresented applicants to surgical residency programs in the past decade.8,9 Plastic surgery, in particular, continues to exhibit substantial underrepresentation of Black, Latino, and female surgeons compared with other surgical specialties.9–12 A 2009 four-decade demographic analysis revealed that Black and Latino physicians represented only 1.4% and 3.6% of academic plastic surgeons, respectively.1 More recent assessments have found similar challenges in ethnic and racial representation with no substantive increases over the last decade.13,14 In a similar vein, despite an increase in female plastic surgery residents from 22% in 2007 to 39% in 2017, disparities in academic and leadership roles remain pronounced.11,15–19 Although the etiology of these disparities are multifactorial, the scarcity of demographic concordant mentors serving in leadership roles is thought to play a significant role in enabling the current status quo.1,7,9,12,20

Although past studies have characterized the lack of diversity at the trainee and attending level, the current state of leadership among plastic and reconstructive surgery (PRS) fellowship program directors has not been assessed.21,22 By bridging the gap between resident training and attending practice, fellowship directors play a crucial role in the clinical and academic development of trainees. Many fellowship directors also serve as sponsors helping to pave the way for future employment; thus, evaluating inclusivity at the fellowship director level deserves consideration.15,22–24 This study serves as the first comprehensive exploration and comparison of the racial, ethnic, and gender composition of fellowship directors across the 5 primary PRS fellowships. We hypothesize that female fellowship directors and fellowship directors from racial/ethnic backgrounds underrepresented in medicine (URiM) would be disproportionately represented in fellowship directorship positions relative to their male or White director counterparts, respectively.

METHODS

Data Collection

A cross-sectional analysis was performed in December 2023 of the US fellowship directories from the professional society websites of the 5 primary fellowship programs (hand, craniofacial, microsurgery, aesthetic, and burn) in PRS. This included the American Society for Surgery of the Hand, the American Society of Craniofacial Surgeons, the American Society for Reconstructive Microsurgery, the Aesthetic Society, and the American Burn Association. The fellowship director from each fellowship program listing was identified. A comprehensive web search was conducted on each fellowship director to identify demographic and educational background information by reviewing institutional faculty profiles, Doximity, Scopus, private practice websites, and public records. Data points collected included academic position (assistant professor, associate professor, and professor), Hirsch index (h-index) score, years since completing most recent formal training, completion of advanced degrees outside of medicine (eg, MPH, MBA, and PhD), completion of fellowships, and whether their fellowship directorship was at the same institution they received residency training at. The h-index is a citation-based measure of publication output widely used to assess a researcher’s scholarly impact.25 Race/ethnicity (White, Black, Latino, and Asian) and sex (male and female) for each fellowship director was independently determined by 2 separate reviewers (S.S. and A.Z.V.) through examination of institutional faculty profile pictures as well as surname origin analysis through ancestry.com. Any discordant race/ethnicity or gender determinations between the 2 independent reviewers was settled by a third reviewer (S.B.). URiM was defined as physicians from Black, Latino, or indigenous (American Indian/Alaska Native/Native Hawaiian) backgrounds consistent with the Association of American Medical Colleges definition.26 Of note, no American Indian, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, or Pacific Islander fellowship directors were identified; thus, these racial categories were excluded from the results tables.

Statistical Analysis

The percentage distribution of fellowship directors by gender and ethnicity for each fellowship was calculated. Two-proportion z-tests were used to compare the proportion of male versus female directors as well as White versus racial/ethnic minority directors across categorical variables examined for each fellowship. Two-tailed Student t tests were used to compare the average h-index scores and average years since completing most recent training for each demographic comparison. For all analyses, P values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Fellowship Director Overview

In total, 248 fellowship directors were identified across all US programs: 94 hand, 34 craniofacial, 62 microsurgery, 38 aesthetic, and 20 burn (Table 1). The majority of fellowship directors held the academic title of professor (93, 37.5%), followed by associate professor (65, 26.2%), and then assistant professor (38, 15.3%). In terms of demographic regions, most fellowship directors resided in the South (83, 33.4%) followed by the Northeast (54, 21.8%), the West (51, 20.6%), and the Midwest (50, 20.2%).

Fellowship Director Gender Diversity

As highlighted in Table 1, there was a greater percentage of male versus female directors across all fellowships (87.5% versus 12.5%; P < 0.01). This trend was noted within all 5 fellowship categories. Burn had the greatest proportion of female directors (6, 30.0%) followed by hand (13, 13.8%), craniofacial (4, 11.8%), and microsurgery (7, 11.3%), whereas aesthetic fellowship had the lowest proportion (1, 2.6%). In comparing academic positions among these fellowship directors, there was no significant gender difference in the proportion of professors (39.2% versus 25.8%; P = 0.151) or associate professors (25.3% versus 32.3%; P = 0.413) (Table 2). However, there was a significantly greater proportion of assistant professor female fellowship directors relative to male directors (32.3% versus 12.9%; P = 0.005). When evaluating research productivity/impact, male fellowship directors had a significantly higher average h-index score relative to female fellowship directors (20.4 ± 14.4 versus 15.0 ± 9.1; P = 0.044). There was no significant difference between male versus female fellowship directors when comparing average years passed since completing most recent training (20.6 ± 10 versus 17.1 ± 8.8; P = 0.066), proportion attaining other advanced degrees (27.6% versus 22.6%; P = 0.552), completing a fellowship (87.6% versus 93.5%; P = 0.332), and completing a fellowship at the same institution they served as a fellowship director of (35.9% versus 41.9%; P = 0.517). Fellowship Director Ethnicity Diversity

Upon evaluating racial/ethnic diversity across fellowship leadership, directors identified as White comprised a greater proportion of fellowship directors relative to all other racial/ethnic minorities combined (73.0% versus 27.0%; P < 0.01) (Table 1). More specifically, out of all fellowship directors evaluated, 5 were Black (2.0%), 10 were Latino (4.0%), and 52 were Asian (21.1%). In total, URiM fellowship directors comprised 6% of the cohort. The greatest proportion of White fellowship directors was in aesthetic fellowship (30, 78.9%), followed by hand (73, 77.6%), microsurgery (43, 69.4%), burn (13, 65.0%), and craniofacial (22, 64.7%). The greatest proportion of Black fellowship directors was in burn (1, 5.0%), followed by aesthetic (1, 2.6%), hand (2, 2.1%), and microsurgery (1, 1.6%), with none in craniofacial (0%). The greatest proportion of Latino fellowship directors was in aesthetic (4, 10.5%), followed by craniofacial (2, 5.9%), microsurgery (2, 3.2%), and hand (2, 2.1%), with none in burn (0%). Finally, the greatest proportion of Asian fellowship directors was in burn (6, 30.0%), followed by craniofacial (10, 29.4%), microsurgery (16, 25.8%), hand (17, 18.1%), and aesthetic (3, 7.9%).

When comparing the proportion of fellowship directors from racial/ethnic minority backgrounds relative to White directors across different academic positions, there was no significant difference in the proportion of professors (37.3% versus 37.6%; P = 0.968), associate professors (31.3% versus 24.3%; P = 0.262), or assistant professors (17.9% versus 14.9%; P = 0.569) (Table 3). Further breakdown of the racial/ethnic minority category revealed that of the 5 Black fellowship directors, 2 (40%) were professors, 1 (20%) was an associate professor, and 2 (40%) had other/nonacademic titles. Of the 10 Latino fellowship directors, 1 (10%) was a professor, 6 (60%) were associate professors, and 3 (30%) had other/nonacademic titles. Of the 52 Asian fellowship directors, 22 (42.3%) were professors, 14 (26.9%) were associate professors, 12 (23.1%) were assistant professors, and 4 (7.6%) had other/nonacademic titles. When comparing h-index scores between fellowship directors from racial/ethnic minority backgrounds to White directors, there was no significant difference noted (18.8 ± 12.2 versus 20.1 ± 14.6; P = 0.517). Similarly, there was no significant difference identified between the 2 demographic groups when comparing the proportion of directors that attained other advanced degrees (32.8% versus 24.9%; P = 0.218), completed a fellowship as part of their training (85.1% versus 89.0%; P = 0.343), and completed a fellowship at the same institution they served as fellowship director of (34.3% versus 37.6%; P = 0.617). However, when evaluating the number of years passed since completing most recent residency training, White fellowship directors had a significantly greater number of years than fellowship directors from racial/ethnic minority backgrounds (21.0 ± 10.1 versus 17.9 ± 9.2; P = 0.029) (Supplemental Digital Contents 1–5, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D600).

DISCUSSION

To meet the healthcare needs of a growing US population that is increasingly more racially, ethnically, and culturally diverse, it is essential to develop a healthcare workforce that is representative of the patients they care for. Although modest strides have been made over the past few decades to attain this ideal, there remain stark disparities by sex, race, and ethnicity particularly in subspecialties such as PRS.27,28 PRS fellowship programs serve a vital role in transitioning residents into the demands of subspecialty practice, making the leadership and mentorship provided at this stage paramount.21,22 Through providing the first comprehensive overview of leadership diversity across the five primary PRS fellowship programs, this study effectively spotlights significant gender and racial/ethnic disparities.

Fellowship Director Gender Diversity

Across all fellowships, female fellowship directors were significantly less represented relative to their male counterparts. A 2021 cross-sectional study by Keane et al identified that women similarly only account for 20% of faculty and 8% of chairs/chiefs in PRS.15 Additionally, female representation among many prominent national PRS associations (eg, American Society of Plastic Surgeons and American Society for Aesthetic Plastic Surgery) was noted to be less than 20%.29–32 However, at the trainee level, this discrepancy is not as apparent considering the most recent 2023 Association of American Medical Colleges report that highlighted that, out of 1064 active integrated plastic surgery residents, 51.8% identified as men and 48.1% as women.33 This progressive decline in female representation at each successive level of academic professional advancement following residency (fellows, faculty, residency/fellowship program directors, and division/department chiefs or chairs) has been described as the “leaky pipeline.”30–32,34 This phenomenon stems from a variety of factors, including concerns over how family responsibilities and pregnancy impact careers in academic PRS, overt gender discrimination, and persistent gender pay gaps, among other challenges.15,30,35–37

Undeniably, one of the most significant drivers of this dwindling gender representation along the academic hierarchy is the lack of female mentors within leadership positions in PRS. Studies have shown that female residents’ choice of programs have been strongly associated with the degree of female faculty at a program and that female role models are the most influential factor for female medical students interested in pursuing the specialty.30,32,38–40 Thus, the clear diminution of female plastic surgeons progressing from residency to faculty to leadership in academic PRS only highlights the critical need for adequate representation of female mentors within fellowship programs. There remains a significant gender disparity among subspecialty fellowship programs, as only 12.5% of all fellowship directors were identified as women in this study. This composite figure is comparable to those reported for similar appointments within PRS, as women make up only 13% of residency program directors.15 A 10-year review (2012–2021) of microsurgery and craniofacial fellows identified that 36.7% and 27.9%, respectively, were women.41 The proportions of female microsurgery and craniofacial fellowship directors identified in this study were only 11.3% and 11.8%, respectively. The dearth of female fellowship directors available to serve as mentors relative to the considerably larger representation of female fellowship trainees may highlight this “mentorship gap” that then limits the ability for female plastic surgeons to advance professionally within faculty and leadership positions posttraining.

This same “mentorship gap” likely perpetuates the cycle of underrepresentation among female fellowship directors. Compared with male directors, there was no significant difference in factors such as advanced degrees or years of experience postresidency, suggesting that inherent differences in qualifications/academic experience do not adequately explain the gender disparity. Even when considering directors who obtained their positions at the same institutions where they trained—where their candidacy may be more favorably viewed as an internal/alumnus applicant—gender differences were negligible. It should be noted that although male fellowship directors had a significantly higher h-index score, this has been a previously documented finding in part confounded by on average older authors present among male faculty and the greater prominence for female surgeons to dedicate their time to other pursuits including education and community engagements.42–45 Interestingly, although there was no significant difference in the proportion of professor and associate professor positions among male versus female fellowship directors, female fellowship directors held a higher proportion of assistant professorships. This finding may be a product of the downstream progress made thus far by an increased of women entering PRS over the past decade.19,42,46 Ultimately, these findings underscore the necessity of ongoing efforts to enhance female leadership in PRS, which include significant investments in formal mentorship, strategic sponsorship, and career advancement preparatory opportunities.15

Fellowship Director Racial/Ethnic Diversity

Across fellowship program leadership, our results demonstrate that disparities in racial/ethnic diversity are highly prevalent, maintaining a pervasive consistency within the plastic surgery workforce continuum.1,7,11,14 As a whole, racial/ethnic minorities were significantly less represented as fellowship directors relative to their White counterparts. More specifically, Asians constituted 21.1% of fellowship directors, whereas Black and Latino directors accounted for merely 2% and 4%, respectively, across all 5 fellowships.11,41 Although it has been well-established that URiM groups are represented in medicine far below the 12.1% Black, 18.7% Latino, and 1.5% indigenous US population, even more concerning is their attenuation along the educational progression into academic surgery.14,47

Based on the 2023 Association of American Medical Colleges report, the collective racial/ethnic breakdown of integrated plastic surgery residents consisted of 62.8% White, 22% Asian, 7.6% Latino, and 4% Black.48 Especially for URiM groups, these proportions have largely remained stagnant over the past decade.7,12 After residency training, the number of Black PRS faculty drops to less than 2.8% with only 2% of program directors and department heads/division chiefs identified as Black, in line with the fellowship director composition highlighted in this study, and only recently in 2023 marked the first time a Black plastic surgeon was named president of the American Society of Plastic Surgeons in its near century of existence.11,14,49 Along similar lines, Latino plastic surgeons comprise 4.8% of academic faculty, 2% PRS academic society board members, and 3% of editorial board members.11,14 This “broken pathway” from residency training into academic practice underscores the critical necessity for a diverse cohort of fellowship directors who can effectively support URiM trainees’ advancement into faculty and leadership roles.

Not only have studies highlighted that Black and Latino residents actively seek out mentors of the same race/ethnicity but that racial/ethnic discordance can hinder effective mentorship as mentors that do not share the same background may not fully understand the challenges faced by URiM trainees.40,50,51 The effect of having a diverse leadership body in PRS also encourages a diverse recruitment of trainees and entry-level surgeons. Through cross-sectional studies, Benaroch et al52 identified that racial diversity of a residency cohort is positively associated with racial diversity of its program directors, whereas Smith et al53 found that department chairs from ethnic minority backgrounds used significantly more URiM faculty. Thus, the current underrepresentation of minority fellowship directors may not only blunt the availability of racial/ethnic concordant mentorship opportunities for URiM PRS trainees, but also reduce their recruitment and pursuit of fellowship training, contributing to the “broken pathway.”

Of note, although the number of racial/ethnic minority fellowship directors was significantly lower than White directors, there were no significant differences between the 2 groups across several key metrics such as level of professorship, presence of advanced degrees, and h-index score. This suggests that the disparity among fellowship directors from racial/ethnic minority backgrounds is not due to a lack of qualifications or academic aptitude among those selected, but likely flaws in the selection process of those faculty appointed to these important leadership roles. These flaws are encompassed by a multitude of factors captured in the literature, inclusive of gender/racial discrimination, implicit bias, lack of opportunity, and deficits in career mentorship, among many others.1,14,50,53–55 Although several models for improving access to mentorship in PRS for URiM medical students and trainees have been utilized, including mentorship programs like PREPPED56 or virtual distance telementoring,57 there are no formal mentorship programs for junior faculty.58 Junior faculty mentorship programs are especially pertinent, considering this study found that URiM fellowship directors had significantly fewer years in practice since the completion of training relative to White directors, suggesting this younger population may have greater opportunity to benefit from more peer-to-peer faculty mentorships.

LIMITATIONS

This study has notable limitations. First, all data on fellowship directors were collected from publicly available academic society websites which run the risk of not being the most up-to-date. However, with the increasing use of online platforms for medical trainees and physicians, the authors expect most academic society websites to maintain accurate and current information. Additionally, self-identification of gender and race/ethnicity was not available on the fellowship directory listings or most faculty profiles, necessitating the evaluation of institutional profile pictures and surname origin analysis for determination of these demographics among fellowship directors. Although this methodology has been utilized in prior studies,11,34,59,60 it is susceptible to bias and erroneous assignments to what individual fellowship directors may have self-identified. This effect was mitigated, however, by the rigorous employment of 3 independent reviewers to provide a final determination of fellowship director race/ethnicity and sex.

CONCLUSIONS

Women and racial/ethnic minorities, especially those from URiM backgrounds, are significantly less represented among PRS fellowship directors. Given the significant transitionary role these fellowships play for resident trainees entering practice, the lack of diversity at this level can thwart the mentorship available to women and racial/ethnic minorities for advancing into faculty and leadership positions within PRS. Thus, it is essential that we continue to evaluate the factors driving this disparity and seek effective measures to ameliorate them.
