Transcatheter treatment of pure native aortic insufficiency (AI) represents a significant challenge in structural cardiology, primarily given the lack of commercially approved transcatheter heart valves (THVs) specifically designed for this indication. Current management is guided by patient risk stratification: surgical aortic valve replacement remains the standard for low- and intermediate-risk patients, while high-risk patients may be considered for investigational devices (eg, JenaValve or J Valve).1 The latest guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology and the European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery have recently indicated that off-label transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with currently available valves can be considered in patients who are at prohibitive surgical risk and are ineligible for device trials (Class IIb recommendations).2

Contemporary THVs were developed for aortic stenosis. They anchor at the aortic annulus by 2 mechanisms: outward radial force and interaction with leaflet calcification. The principal technical barrier to TAVR in severe pure native AI is the absence of annular and leaflet calcification. This deficiency increases the risk of valve embolization, malposition, and residual regurgitation.3,4 Registry data, such as from the PANTHEON international registry, underscore the high-risk nature of off-label TAVR in pure AI, with notable rates of valve embolization or migration (12.4%), need for permanent pacemaker implantation (22%), and moderate to severe residual AI (9.2%).3 The registry included 201 patients undergoing TAVR, of whom 132 (66%) had self-expanding valves and 69 (34%) had balloon-expandable valves. Although strategies such as device oversizing can enhance anchoring by increasing radial force, they also introduce risks of conduction disturbances and annular trauma.3,5

The Navitor transcatheter aortic valve (Abbott) is a self-expanding intra-annular valve. It is an upgrade of the Portico valve, with a fabric cuff to minimize paravalvular regurgitation and a more flexible delivery catheter to reduce vascular injury. Importantly, the valve cells at the outflow flare outward, rendering it the largest aortic diameter of commercially available valves (Figure 1). For example, a 35-mm Navitor valve provides a maximum aortic diameter of 46 mm, compared with 38 mm for a comparable Medtronic Evolut valve, which may be advantageous when anchoring in the ascending aorta.6 This specific mechanical utility of the Navitor platform's extensive outflow flare in noncalcified, high-risk structural environments has been further supported in recent clinical literature, including its successful off-label deployment to treat severe continuous-flow left ventricular assist device–associated aortic regurgitation.7

The unique design of the Navitor valve provides an important benefit in these patients by allowing for anchoring at the annulus and in the ascending aorta in patients without aortic dilation. The AURORA anatomical classification operationalizes this concept by grading pure AI anatomies according to their ability to provide anchoring in 3 regions (left ventricular outflow tract [LVOT], annulus, and ascending aorta) with at least 10% oversizing-derived radial support. Patients in whom a transcatheter valve engages all 3 “anchors” demonstrate higher device success and lower late failure than those with only 1 or no reliable anchoring zones.8 In this context, while many centers, including ours, have traditionally relied on oversizing balloon-expandable valves, we have increasingly leveraged the large aortic diameter of the Navitor valve to obtain dual- or triple-level anchoring (annulus and ascending aorta, with or without LVOT), which can potentially reduce embolization risk and improve procedural success in carefully selected patients with nondilated ascending aortas.

Potential Pitfalls

Although the Navitor valve offers an advantage of additional anchoring in the ascending aorta in select patients, this strategy has not been widely tested. The main pitfalls of TAVR in pure native AI include unreliable anchoring, the need for aggressive oversizing, and challenging anatomy, which together drive high rates of valve embolization, residual AI, and conduction injury.

In the absence of leaflet or LVOT calcification, there is often no effective landing zone; therefore, oversizing contemporary TAVR devices to generate sufficient radial force can still fail.

Anatomical geometry, specifically the ratio of the LVOT area to the aortic annulus area, serves as a critical surrogate for embolization risk. A larger LVOT relative to the annulus removes native mechanical support, and a more flared anatomy demands a higher landing strategy. Deeper implantation removes the crucial geometric support of the system, where the outward radial forces of the frame can paradoxically pull the prosthesis downward, precipitating instantaneous ventricular embolization.

A principal technical limitation of this approach is that the flared outflow section of the self-expanding frame cannot provide mechanical stabilization until it is completely expanded. Consequently, during the “final release” phase—immediately before full expansion allows the outflow cells to contact the ascending aortic wall—the THV remains highly vulnerable and can instantaneously embolize ventricularly given the absence of calcific anchoring.

Conclusions

These 2 cases demonstrate the feasibility of the Navitor valve as a novel solution for severe AI in native valves because of its unique design, which enables additional anchoring within the ascending aorta, unlike other self-expanding valves, which are more cylindrical in shape. The Navitor valve may offer bridge therapy in select patients until a dedicated transcatheter valve is approved and widely available for the treatment of pure native AI.
