Low-energy enhancement in the magnetic dipole radiation of actinide nuclei
C. Rodgers, D. DeMartini, and Y. Alhassid

Abstract
We present the first theoretical results of the magnetic dipole (M1) γ \gamma -ray strength function ( γ \gamma SF) for actinide nuclei within the shell-model Monte Carlo (SMMC) method. We observe a low-energy enhancement (LEE) in the M1 γ \gamma SFs of the six nuclei studied here, which serves as the first evidence, theoretical or experimental, that the LEE persists in the actinides. We also identify a scissors mode resonance in all six nuclei, which we compare with recent Oslo-method experiments.



Introduction
.—
The
\gamma
-ray strength function (
\gamma
SF) of a nucleus, which describes the probability of
\gamma
-ray emission and absorption, is a critical input to neutron-capture rate calculations within the Hauser-Feshbach theory
Hauser and Feshbach 1952
. These reaction rates are important for determining elemental abundances from r-process nucleosynthesis
Mumpower et al. 2016
. Furthermore, structures seen in
\gamma
SFs provide insight into the collective behavior of nucleons
Heyde et al. 2010
. Thus, accurate modeling of nuclear
\gamma
SFs is important for developing more precise predictions of elemental abundances in stars as well as improving our understanding of nuclear structure.

The giant dipole resonance, which is an electric dipole (E1) excitation
Goldhaber and Teller 1948
, dominates the
\gamma
SF at energies
\sim 15
MeV. Nuclei also exhibit resonances at lower energies (below the neutron separation energy
S_{n}
), some of which are magnetic dipole (M1) in nature. The most prominent of these are the scissors mode resonance (SR)
Heyde et al. 2010
;
Lo Iudice and Palumbo 1979
, seen in deformed nuclei, and the spin-flip mode
Heyde et al. 2010
. Accounting for these low-energy structures in the
\gamma
SF of neutron-rich nuclei significantly improve calculations of neutron radiative capture rates
Mumpower et al. 2017
.

There have been extensive efforts to calculate the
\gamma
SF of neutron-rich nuclei at low transition energies. Most experimental measurements of the
\gamma
SF rely on either nuclear resonance fluorescence (NRF) reactions
Berg and Kneissl 1987
or the “Oslo method”
Schiller et al. 2000
, with the latter allowing for simultaneous determination of both the
\gamma
SF and the nuclear level density. Oslo method experiments on light- and medium-mass nuclei observed a then unexpected ’upbend’ in the
\gamma
SF at the lowest
\gamma
-ray energies
Guttormsen et al. 2005
;
Voinov et al. 2004
, now known as the low-energy enhancement (LEE). Subsequent configuration-interaction (CI) shell-model calculations suggested that the LEE is magnetic dipole (M1) in nature
Schwengner et al. 2013
.

More recently, a LEE has been observed experimentally
Naqvi et al. 2019
;
Simon et al. 2016
and calculated theoretically
Fanto and Alhassid 2024
;
Mercenne et al.
;
DeMartini and Alhassid
in the M1
\gamma
SF in chains of neodymium and samarium isotopes. Due to the large shell model spaces associated with these heavy open-shell nuclei, CI shell-model calculations are only possible with the shell-model Monte Carlo method (SMMC) method
Johnson et al. 1992
;
Lang et al. 1993
;
Alhassid et al. 1994
;
Koonin et al. 1997
;
Alhassid 2001
;
Alhassid 2017
. SMMC has been used extensively for calculating properties of lanthanide nuclei at finite temperature
Alhassid et al. 2008
;
Özen et al. 2013
;
Alhassid et al. 2014
;
Guttormsen et al. 2021
. Recently, the SMMC has been extended to calculate statistical properties of actinide nuclei
DeMartini and Alhassid 2025
.

The possible existence of a LEE in actinide nuclei is especially important, as neutron capture rates in these actinides are relevant not only for the astrophysical r-process, but also in processes occurring inside of nuclear reactors
Chadwick et al. 2011
. If the LEE persists in neutron-rich heavy nuclei, this would have profound effects on
r
-process nucleosynthesis through the enhancement of neutron-capture rates near the neutron drip line
Larsen and Goriely 2010
.

While various experimental measurements were made of the
\gamma
SF in actinide nuclei, many of these experiments utilized NRF reactions
Adekola et al. 2011
;
Heil et al. 1988
;
Margraf et al. 1990
;
Yevetska et al. 2010
. The LEE has been shown to only exist in the
\gamma
SF built on top of excited states. Thus, it cannot be measured in NRF experiments, and has been seen in Oslo-method experiments measuring the decay of excited nuclei. Oslo-method experiments, however, are currently limited to studying actinides down to
\gamma
-ray energies of
E_{\gamma}\gtrapprox 1\,
MeV
Guttormsen et al. 2012
;
Guttormsen et al. 2014
;
Laplace et al. 2016
;
Tornyi et al. 2014
;
Bello Garrote et al. 2022
;
Zeiser et al. 2019
. While this is sufficient for observing the scissors mode, probing the LEE may require measurements of the
\gamma
SF at even lower
\gamma
-ray energies. Thus, the existence of a LEE in the actinides has yet to be experimentally confirmed.

Here we present the first SMMC calculations for the M1
\gamma
SF near the neutron-separation energy of six actinides that have recently been studied with the Olso method
Guttormsen et al. 2014
;
Laplace et al. 2016
;
Zeiser et al. 2019
:
232
Th,
237-239
U,
240
Pu and
243
Pu. We clearly identify a LEE and observe both the scissors and spin-flip modes in all six nuclei.

In this work, we provide an overview of the computational methods we use to calculate the imaginary-time M1 response function and convert it into a
\gamma
SF. Then we present results of our calculations in six actinide nuclei and compare them with experimental data
Guttormsen et al. 2014
;
Laplace et al. 2016
;
Zeiser et al. 2019
.

Strength and response functions
.—
For a nucleus at temperature
T
, we define the strength function of the M1 spherical vector operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M1}
as

\displaystyle S_{M1}(T;\omega)=\sum_{\alpha_{i},J_{i}\alpha_{f},J_{f}}

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\beta E_{\alpha_{i},J_{i}}}}{Z}|(\alpha_{f}J_{f}||\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M1}||\alpha_{i}J_{i})|^{2}

(1)

\displaystyle\times\delta(E_{\alpha_{f},J_{f}}-E_{\alpha_{i},J_{i}}-\omega)

where
Z=\sum_{\alpha_{i},J_{i}}(2J_{i}+1)e^{-\beta E_{\alpha_{i},J_{i}}}
is the canonical partition function and the magnetic dipole transition operator is defined as

\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M1}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\frac{\mu_{N}}{\hbar c}\left(g_{l}\hat{\mathbf{l}}+g_{s}\hat{\mathbf{s}}\right)

(2)

where
\hat{\mathbf{l}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
are, respectively, the orbital and spin angular momentum operators. We use the free-nucleon
g
-factors
g_{l,p}=1
,
g_{l,n}=0
,
g_{s,p}=5.5857
, and
g_{s,n}=-3.8263
.

Experiments using the Olso method do not measure
S_{M1}
; rather, they measure the de-excitation M1
\gamma
SF,
f_{M1}
, which is defined by
Bartholomew et al. 1973

f_{M1}(E_{i};E_{\gamma})=\tilde{\rho}(E_{i})E_{\gamma}^{-3}\braket{\Gamma_{M1}(E_{i};E_{\gamma})}

(3)

where
\braket{\Gamma_{M1}(E_{i};E_{\gamma})}
is the average partial width for a nucleus at energy
E_{i}
to emit M1 radiation with energy
E_{\gamma}
, and
\tilde{\rho}
is the average level density.

It can be shown (see, e.g., Ref.
DeMartini and Alhassid
) that Eq. (
3
) can be written as

\displaystyle f_{M1}(E_{i};E_{\gamma})=a\frac{\tilde{\rho}(E_{i})}{3\tilde{\rho}(E_{f})}\bar{S}_{M1}(T;\omega=-E_{\gamma})

(4)

where
\bar{S}_{M1}(T)
is the smoothed analog of Eq. (
1
), obtained by integrating over a finite energy resolution
\Delta\omega
, and
a=\frac{16\pi}{9(\hbar c)^{3}}
.

The
\gamma
SF cannot be directly calculated using the SMMC. Instead, we can calculate the imaginary-time response function

\displaystyle R_{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}(T;\tau)=\braket{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\dagger}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{O}}(0)}

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{O}}(\tau)=e^{-\tau\hat{H}}\hat{\mathcal{O}}e^{\tau\hat{H}}

(6)

where
\braket{\cdot}
is a thermal average at temperature
T
and
\hat{H}
is the nuclear Hamiltonian.

The strength function in Eq. (
1
) can be shown to satisfy

R_{M1}(T;\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega K(\tau,\omega)S_{M1}(T;\omega)

(7)

where
K(\tau,\omega)=e^{-\tau\omega}+e^{-(\beta-\tau)\omega}
and we used the symmetry relation
S_{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}(T;-\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}S_{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}(T;\omega)
. Calculating the
\gamma
SF thus amounts to inverting Eq. (
7
).

Computational Methods
.—
To calculate the response function (
5
) we use the SMMC
Ormand et al. 1994
;
Alhassid 2017
, which relies on the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation
Stratonovich 1957
;
Hubbard 1959
to express thermal averages as an integral over axillary fields
\sigma_{\alpha}
. However, the SMMC calculates such averages within the grand canonical ensemble. To obtain observables for states with fixed number of nucleons we use a particle projection procedure
Ormand et al. 1994
, with the projection being done onto both protons and neutrons separately. This particle projection results in a sign-problem at low temperatures for the odd-mass nuclei
Alhassid et al. 2024
;
DeMartini and Alhassid
. In this letter, however, we will be considering temperatures high enough such that the Monte-Carlo sign remains close to 1.

In the SMMC, the imaginary-time response function (
5
) is given by

R_{M1}(T;\tau)=\frac{\int D[\sigma]W_{\sigma}\Phi_{\sigma}\braket{\hat{O}_{M1}(\tau)\cdot\hat{O}_{M1}(0)}_{\sigma}}{\int D[\sigma]D[\sigma]W_{\sigma}\Phi_{\sigma}}

(8)

where
\hat{O}_{M1}(\tau)\cdot\hat{O}_{M1}(0)=\sum_{\mu}\hat{O}_{M1}^{\mu}(\tau)\hat{O}_{M1}^{-\mu}(0)
and
\hat{O}_{M1}(\tau)=\hat{U}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\tau)\hat{O}_{M1}\hat{U}_{\sigma}(\tau)
. Explicit forms for these quantities can be found in Ref.
Alhassid 2017
. The integral in Eq. (
8
) is calculated using a Metropolis Monte Carlo algorithm.

For our calculations of the actinides, we use a valence space comprised of the
0h_{9/2}
,
1f_{7/2}
,
1f_{5/2}
,
2p_{3/2}
,
2p_{1/2}
,
0i_{13/2}
and
1g_{9/2}
orbitals for protons and
1g_{9/2},2d_{5/2},0i_{11/2},1g_{7/2},3s_{1/2},2d_{3/2},0i_{15/2}
and
1h_{11/2}
orbitals for neutrons. These were determined by considering orbital occupation numbers, just as in previous SMMC analyses
Alhassid et al. 2008
.

The effective residual interactions, which consist of monopole pairing and multipole terms, are the same as those used in Ref.
DeMartini and Alhassid 2025
.

The SMMC is not capable of calculating the M1
\gamma
SF directly. Rather, one must compute the imaginary-time response function
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\dagger}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{O}}(0)}
via Eq. (
8
) and then invert Eq. (
7
). This is equivalent to numerically inverting a bilateral Laplace transform, which is known to be an ill-posed problem. While a number of methods exist to perform this task
Tripolt et al. 2019
, we use the maximum entropy method (MEM)
Jarrell and Gubernatis 1996
, which has been used previously in SMMC calculations of strength functions for heavy nuclei
DeMartini and Alhassid
;
Fanto and Alhassid 2024
;
Mercenne et al.
.

The MEM is a Bayesian approach to maximizing the objective function

Q(S_{M1},\alpha)=\alpha\mathcal{S}-\frac{1}{2}\chi^{2}

(9)

with respect to
S_{M1}
. Here,
\chi^{2}
is the likelihood function given by

\chi^{2}=(R^{\mathrm{SMMC}}_{M1}-R_{M1})^{T}C^{-1}(R^{\mathrm{SMMC}}_{M1}-R_{M1})

(10)

where
C
is the covariance matrix of the SMMC response function data
R^{\mathrm{SMMC}}_{M1}
, and
R_{M1}
is calculated using Eq. (
7
) for a given choice of
S_{M1}
. Furthermore, we have the entropy function defined by

\mathcal{S}=\int d\omega\left(S_{M1}-S^{\mathrm{prior}}_{M1}-S_{M1}\log{\left(S_{M1}/S_{M1}^{\mathrm{prior}}\right)}\right).

(11)

The quantity
S^{\mathrm{prior}}
is an approximation of the strength function that serves as a prior distribution.

To account for the free parameter
\alpha
we use Bryan’s method
Bryan 1990
which averages the solutions to Eq. (
9
) over all values of
\alpha
.

The success of the MEM is dependent on our choice of prior distribution. For this analysis, we will take our prior to be the static-phase approximation (SPA)
Puddu et al. 1991
;
Lauritzen et al. 1988
;
Alhassid and Bush 1992
;
Rossignoli and Ring 1998
;
Rossignoli et al. 1999
, as previous theoretical studies of the lanthanides demonstrated that the SPA provides a good approximation for the M1 response function at temperatures near the neutron separation energy
Fanto and Alhassid 2024
;
Mercenne et al.
;
DeMartini and Alhassid
.

Guttormsen et al. 2014

(13)

where
\sigma
,
\omega

To make sense of the accuracy of our theoretical value for
B(M1)
compared to the experimental measurements, we must note several key facts. First, the experimental measurements don’t distinguish the parity of the
\gamma
-rays, meaning the strengths contain contributions from both E1 and M1 radiation. While the experimental results do account for the presence of the E1 giant dipole resonance by subtracting an extrapolated tail, theoretical calculations have suggested that thermal excitations in the continuum may contribute to an enhancement of low-energy E1 transitions
Litvinova and Belov 2013
. Thus, we expect there to be residual E1 radiation in the experimental SR results. Second, our calculations are performed at a fixed temperature
T
(canonical ensemble) whereas the experiment is performed at an initial energy
E_{i}
(microcanonical ensemble). Finally, the integrated strength in our calculations is sensitive to the window used in our Lorentzian fitting, leading to additional systematic uncertainties in the fit parameters.

Accounting for all of these factors, we conclude that the integrated strengths of our scissors modes are in reasonable agreement with those measured by Refs.
Guttormsen et al. 2014
;
Laplace et al. 2016
.

The experimental measurements of the SR for the actinides considered here contain two peaks, rather than the single peak we observe in our calculations. Further, the experimental SR peaks are located at a higher energy than our theoretical calculations suggest, as was observed in previous SMMC calculations of the lanthanides
DeMartini and Alhassid
.

It has been suggested that
\gamma
(triaxial)-deformation can lead to a splitting of M1 modes by an amount proportional to
\sin{\gamma}

Lipparini and Stringari 1989
. Further, because the SR is the counter-rotation of the deformed proton and neutron densities, the energy of the SR mode is positively correlated to the nucleus’s
\beta
and
\gamma
deformation.

In previous work studying nuclear deformation in the lanthanides
Mustonen et al. 2018
it was found that in restricted model spaces a phenomenological factor accounting for core polarization must be included to generate sufficient deformation in the SMMC. Thus, the SMMC calculations presented here under-predict the deformation of the six nuclei, which could potentially result in both the SR having a lower energy than expected and the two SR peaks being too closely split to be resolved.

Therefore, both of these observed discrepancies may be the result of our SMMC calculations under-predicting the deformation of these nuclei due to the finite model space. As the LEE is only weakly dependent on deformation
Mercenne et al.
, we expect our results for the LEE to be robust.

Summary and discussion
.—
The
\gamma
SF of actinide nuclei serves as important input for calculations relevant to astrophysical processes and nuclear technology, and provides insight into collective modes of nuclei. Here, we have performed SMMC calculations of the M1
\gamma
SF for the actinide nuclei
232
Th,
237-239
U,
240
Pu, and
243
Pu near the neutron separation energy. To perform the necessary analytic continuation we used the MEM, with a prior strength calculated in the SPA.

These results are the first theoretical prediction of a LEE in the M1
\gamma
SF of actinides. We also observe the scissors mode in all six actinides.

Improvements to the results presented here could be made by studying the spin dependence of the
\gamma
SF. This would allow us to more directly compare our theoretically calculated
f_{M1}
to experimental measurements, which populate spins differently from the intrinsic spin distribution of the nucleus.
