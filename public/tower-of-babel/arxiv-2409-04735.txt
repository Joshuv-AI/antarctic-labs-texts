Counting points on generic character varieties
Stefano Giannini 1, Masoud Kamgarpour 2, GyeongHyeon Nam 3, Bailey Whitbread 4

Abstract
. We count points on character varieties associated with punctured surfaces and regular semisimple generic conjugacy classes in reductive groups. We find that the number of points are palindromic polynomials. This suggests a P = W P=W conjecture for these varieties. We also count points on the corresponding additive character varieties and find that the number of points are also polynomials, which we conjecture have non-negative coefficients. These polynomials can be considered as the reductive analogues of the Kac polynomials of comet-shaped quivers.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Character varieties of surface groups play a central role in diverse areas of mathematics and physics, such as gauge theory
[
AtiyahBott83
,
HitchinProceedingofLMS87
]
, mirror symmetry
[
HauselThaddeusInventiones2003
]
, non-abelian Hodge theory
[
Simpson92
,
Simpson94i
]
, and the geometric Langlands program
[
BeilinsonDrinfeld
,
BenZviNadler
]
. Consequently, the topology and geometry of these varieties have been the focus of active research for decades. In their groundbreaking works
[
HRV08
,
HLRV11
]
, Hausel, Letellier, and Rodriguez-Villegas counted points on
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-character varieties, leading to deep conjectures about the geometry of these varieties.

Many of these conjectures have since been proven. Notable achievements include the determination of the Betti numbers
[
SchiffmannAnnals2016
,
MellitInventiones21
,
Mellit20
]
, the establishment of curious Poincaré duality and curious Hard Lefschetz
[
MellitDecomposition
]
, and the recent resolution of the
P=W
conjecture
[
hausel2022p
,
maulik
,
HoskinPW
]
.

In
[
HLRV11
]
, the authors also considered the
additive
version of character varieties, where the natural commutation relation is replaced by its Lie algebraic analogue. According to the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence, points on the usual (multiplicative) character varieties correspond to flat connections on vector bundles. When
g=0
, the points on the additive character variety correspond to flat connections on the
trivial
bundle. Thus, when
g=0
, the additive character variety is a dense open subvariety of the usual character variety, sometimes known as the
open de Rham space

[
HWW23
]
.

This direct relationship between additive and multiplicative character varieties is lost in higher genus; nevertheless, it was conjectured in
[
HLRV11
]
that the cohomology of the additive character variety captures the pure part of the cohomology of the multiplicative character variety in all genus. As evidence, the authors proved that additive character varieties are pure, i.e., their cohomology carries a pure Hodge structure. This result relied on the beautiful bridge built by Crawley-Boevey between additive character varieties and quiver varieties associated to comet-shaped quivers
[
CBIndecomposable
]
. Under this correspondence, the number of points on the additive character variety matches the Kac polynomials of the corresponding quiver. Utilising these ideas, Hausel, Letellier, and Rodriguez-Villegas proved Kac’s conjecture for arbitrary quivers
[
HLVKacAnnals2013
]
.

Despite significant advances in understanding the geometry of character varieties associated with
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
(and more generally, groups of type
A
), there is limited understanding of character varieties associated with arbitrary reductive groups
G
. Understanding these general cases is crucial for applications in fields such as Langlands duality and mirror symmetry, where the Langlands dual group plays a central role.

In this paper, we count points on both multiplicative and additive character varieties associated with punctured surface groups and regular semisimple conjugacy classes in arbitrary reductive groups. Our work builds upon and generalises the contributions of Cambò
[
Cambo17
]
, who studied
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
-character varieties;
[
BK22
]
, who investigated character stacks associated with compact surface groups; and
[
KNP25
]
, who examined character varieties in presence of regular unipotent conjugacy classes at the punctures.

One of our main results is that the number of points of multiplicative character varieties over finite fields are palindromic polynomials. This hints at a
P=W
conjecture, a curious Poincaré duality, and a curious Hard Lefschetz property for these varieties.

1.2.
Generic character varieties

Let
g
and
n
be integers satisfying
g\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
. Let
\Gamma=\Gamma_{g,n}
be the fundamental group of an orientable surface with genus
g
and
n
punctures:

\Gamma={\langle a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\,|\,{[a_{1},b_{1}]\cdots[a_{g},b_{g}]c_{1}\cdots c_{n}}=1\rangle}.

Let
G
be a connected split reductive group over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with split maximal torus
T
and centre
Z
. Let
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
be a tuple of conjugacy classes of
G
.
The
representation variety
associated to
(\Gamma,G,C)
is defined by

R:=\bigg\{(a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in G^{2g}\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}\ \bigg|\ \prod_{i=1}^{g}[a_{i},b_{i}]\prod_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=1\bigg\}.

This is an affine scheme of finite type over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with an action of
G
by conjugation. The associated character variety is the GIT quotient

{X}:=R/\!\!/G.

We also have the corresponding character stack

[{X}]:=[R/(G/Z)].

Assumption 1
.

We will be working under the following assumptions:

•

The centre
Z=Z(G)
is connected and the ground characteristic is very good for
G
.

•

\prod_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}\subseteq[G,G]
, otherwise
R
would be empty.

•

Each conjugacy class
C_{i}
contains an element
S_{i}\in T(\mathbb{F}_{q})
which is strongly regular; i.e.,
C_{G}(S_{i})=T
(cf.
[
Steinberg65
,
Humphreys95
]
).

•

The tuple
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
is
generic
in the sense of §
3.1
.

•

2g+n\geq 3
, otherwise the generic assumption implies that
X
is empty.

As explained in §
3.2
,
the generic assumption ensures that the action of
G/Z
on
R
has finite étale stabilisers and that
R
is smooth and equidimensional. Thus,
[X]
is a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack. Moreover,
X
and
[X]
have the same number of points over a finite field, and are equidimensional of pure dimension

\dim({X})=(2g-2+n)\dim(G)+2\dim(Z)-n\cdot\dim(T).

Before stating our main theorem, we need to recall some facts about polynomial count varieties and
E
-polynomials. We refer the reader to
[
HRV08
]
or
[
LetellierVillegas
]
for further details. A variety
S
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is said to be
polynomial count
if there exists a polynomial
f_{S}(t)\in\mathbb{C}[t]
such that

|S(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})|=f_{S}(q^{m}),\qquad\forall m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

In this case,
f_{S}
is known as the
counting polynomial
of
S
. An important fact is that if
S
is polynomial count then its counting polynomial equals the
E
-polynomial
of
S
. The latter is a certain specialisation of the mixed Poincaré polynomial of
S
(defined using étale cohomology). Since the
E
-polynomial has integer coefficients, it follows that the counting polynomial must belong to
\mathbb{Z}[t]
.

The
E
-polynomial gives useful topological information about the variety. For instance, the leading coefficients of the
E
-polynomial equals the number of irreducible components of top dimension, and that the value of the
E
-polynomial at
1
equals the Euler characteristic.

Our main result states that generic character varieties are, after a finite base change, polynomial count:

Theorem 2
.

There exists a polynomial
f_{X}\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
(given explicitly in §
4.6
) and a positive integer
r
such that

|X(\mathbb{F}_{q^{rm}})|=f_{X}(q^{rm}),\qquad\forall m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

The starting point is the observation that since
X
is the coarse moduli space of the Deligne-Mumford stack
[X]
, we have
|X(\mathbb{F}_{q})|=|[X](\mathbb{F}_{q})|
. An application of Lang’s theorem then implies

\displaystyle|[X](\mathbb{F}_{q})|=\frac{|R(\mathbb{F}_{q})||Z(\mathbb{F}_{q})|}{|G(\mathbb{F}_{q})|}.

Next, the Frobenius Mass Formula allows one to write
|R(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
in terms of irreducible complex representations of the finite group
G(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. We then use results of Deligne and Lusztig
[
DL76
]
to analyse this formula and prove the theorem; see §
4
for details.

Let
\overline{X}
denote the base change of
X
to
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
and
E(\overline{X})
the
E
-polynomial of
\overline{X}
. The previous theorem implies that
E(\overline{X})=f_{X}
. By analysing the counting polynomial
f_{X}
, we will obtain:

Corollary 3
.

(a)

E(\overline{X})
is palindromic.

(b)

|\pi_{0}(\overline{X})|=|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{G}))|
, where
\check{G}
is the Langlands dual group.

(c)

If
g>1
, or
g=1
and
Z
is non-trivial, then
\chi(\overline{X})=0
.

Expressions for the Euler characteristic in the case
g=0
or
g=1
and
Z=\{1\}
is given in §
5.3
.
The first part of the above corollary provides evidence for the following:

Conjecture 4
.

The analogue of
{X}
over the complex numbers is Hodge–Tate, and satisfies the curious Poincaré duality and curious hard Lefschetz. Moreover, there is a version of the
P=W
conjecture for
{X}
.

When
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, Mellit constructed a cell decomposition for
{X}
, thus providing a conceptual explanation of the fact that
{X}
is polynomial count and proving in addition that it is Hodge–Tate and satisfies curious Hard Lefschetz and curious Poincaré duality
[
MellitDecomposition
]
. We expect that
{X}
has an analogous cell decomposition for arbitrary reductive group
G
.

Remark 5
.

(i)

The polynomial
f_{X}
depends only on
(G,g,n)
; i.e., it is independent of the conjugacy classes
C_{i}
(provided they satisfy Assumption
1
).

(ii)

We expect that the theorem holds without assuming that the centre
Z(G)
is connected; however, the expression for
|X(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
becomes more complicated due to disconnected centralisers in the dual group.

(iii)

Our analysis shows that the stacky count
|[{X}](\mathbb{F}_{q})|
is a polynomial in
q
even if we drop the generic assumption. We expect that the regularity assumption is also not required for the polynomial property. We emphasise, however, that without the generic assumption, the variety
X
is quite different from the stack
[X]
.

(iv)

There are two reasons for the finite base change in the main theorem. First, the inclusion
[G(\mathbb{F}_{q}),G(\mathbb{F}_{q})]\subseteq[G,G](\mathbb{F}_{q})
may be proper (e.g. for
G=\mathrm{PGL}_{n}
). However, given
S\in[G,G](\mathbb{F}_{q})
, there exists a positive integer
m
, such that
S\in[G(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}),G(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})]
.
Second, if
W^{\prime}
is a reflection subgroup of
W
, then
T^{W^{\prime}}
may be disconnected. The group of components
\pi_{0}=\pi_{0}(T^{W^{\prime}})
is a finite étale group which may be non-split (i.e. the action of Galois group may be non-trivial). This implies that
|\pi_{0}(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
depends on the residue of
q
modulo
|\pi_{0}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})|
. However, as we shall see, the fact that
\mathrm{char}(k)
is good for
G
implies that
q
is co-prime to
|\pi_{0}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})|
. Thus, there exists a positive integer
n
such that

q^{n}\equiv 1\mod|\pi_{0}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})|.

It follows that
|\pi_{0}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}})|=|\pi_{0}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})|
.

1.3.
The additive analogues

We now consider the additive (or Lie-algebraic) analogues of the above discussions. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra of
G
and
{\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{n})}
a tuple of adjoint
G
-orbits on
\mathfrak{g}
. Define the
additive representation variety
by

{A}:=\bigg\{(a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathfrak{g}^{2g}\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{i}\ \bigg|\ \sum_{i=1}^{g}[a_{i},b_{i}]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=0\bigg\}.

The group
G
acts on
{A}
by the adjoint action.
We call the resulting GIT quotient

{Y}:={A}/\!\!/G

the
additive character variety
associated to
(\Gamma,G,\mathcal{O})
.

We will be working under the obvious additive analogues of Assumption
1
. Then the action of
G/Z
on
{A}
has finite étale stabilisers,
{A}
is smooth and equidimensional, and
{Y}
is also equidimensional of the same dimension as
{X}
.

Our main result states that the variety
Y
is, after a quadratic base change, polynomial count:

Theorem 6
.

There exists a polynomial
f_{Y}\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
(given explicitly in §
6.6
) such that

|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2m}})|=f_{Y}(q^{2m}),\qquad\forall m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

The starting point of the proof is an additive analogue of the Frobenius formula which gives an expression for
|{Y}(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
in terms of the Fourier transforms of
G
-invariant orbits on
\mathfrak{g}
. We then use a result of Kazhdan and Letellier to evaluate these Fourier transforms and compute
|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
; see §
6
for details.

Remark 7
.

The reason for the quadratic base change is that computing
|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
involves the size of a Springer fibre over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. It was shown by Springer that this is a polynomial if
q
is large enough. This was refined in
[
GoodwinRohrle
, Theorem 3.10]
where it was shown that (under the assumption that
Z
is connected and
p
is good for
G
) the size of the Springer fibre is a polynomial in
q
, unless
G
has a factor of type
E_{8}
, in which case a quadratic base change may be necessary.

Let
\overline{Y}
be the base change of
Y
to
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. By analysing the counting polynomial
f_{Y}
, we find:

Corollary 8
.

(a)

The variety
\overline{Y}
is connected.

(b)

If
G
is simple of rank less than
7
,
0\leq g\leq 10
and
1\leq n\leq 1000
, then the coefficients of the counting polynomial
f_{Y}
are non-negative.

Part (b) of this corollary is established with code written using CHEVIE
[
GHLMP96
,
Michel15
]
; see
[
Bailey
]
for the relevant code.
The counting polynomial can also be used to give expressions for the Euler characteristic; see §
7.4
.

When
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, the additive character variety
Y
is isomorphic to a quiver variety and
|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
is a polynomial with non-negative coefficients
[
HLRV11
]
. Combined with the previous corollary, this provides evidence for the following:

Conjecture 9
.

The cohomology
H_{c}^{*}(\overline{{Y}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
is pure; thus,
|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
has non-negative coefficients.

If
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
then
Y
is isomorphic to a quiver variety and the coefficients of
|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
were recently shown to be the graded dimensions of the associated graded Borcherd algebra
[
Lucien
]
, proving a conjecture of Schiffmann
[
Schiffmann18
]
. It would be interesting to find a similar interpretation for the coefficients of
|{Y}(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
for general reductive groups.

2.
Recollections on root systems and reductive groups

In this section, we recall basic facts and notations about root systems and reductive groups. The material here is standard, though perhaps the facts about pseudo-Levi subgroups and quasi-isolated semisimple elements are not as widely known. For these notions, we refer the reader to
[
MS03
]
,
[
Bonnafe05
]
, and
[
Taylor22
]
.

2.1.
Root systems

Let
\Phi
be a (reduced crystallographic) root system in a finite dimensional Euclidean vector space
(V,(.\ ,.))
. We assume that
\Phi
spans
V
and fix a base
\Delta
of
\Phi
. Then
\Delta
is a basis of the vector space
V
. Let
W
be the Weyl group generated by the simple reflections
s_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\Delta
.

2.1.1.
Subsystems

A root subsystem of
\Phi
is a subset
\Psi\subseteq\Phi
which is itself a root system. This is equivalent to the requirement that for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
,
s_{\alpha}(\beta)\in\Psi
. A subsystem
\Psi\subseteq\Phi
is
closed
if

\alpha,\beta\in\Psi\quad\textrm{and}\quad\alpha+\beta\in\Phi\quad\implies\quad\alpha+\beta\in\Psi.

Given a subset
S\subseteq\Phi
, let
\langle S\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the subgroup of
V
generated by
S
. The subsystem of
\Phi
generated by
S
is defined by

\langle S\rangle:=\langle S\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap\Phi.

Note that
\langle S\rangle
is a closed subsystem of
\Phi
.

2.1.2.
Dual root systems

For each root
\alpha\in\Phi
, define the coroot

\check{\alpha}:=\frac{2}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha\in V.

The set of coroots forms the dual root system
\check{\Phi}\subset V
. If
\Psi
is a subsystem of
\Phi
, then
\check{\Psi}
is a subsystem of
\check{\Phi}
. However, if
\Psi
is closed in
\Phi
, then
\check{\Psi}
need not be closed in
\check{\Phi}
. For instance,
C_{n}\times C_{n}
is a closed subsystem of
C_{2n}
but if
n>1
, then
B_{n}\times B_{n}
is not closed in
B_{2n}
. Indeed,
B_{n}\times B_{n}
does not arise in the Borel–de Siebenthal algorithm applied to
B_{2n}
.

2.1.3.
Levi subsystems

A
Levi subsystem
(aka parabolic subsystem)
of
\Phi
is a subsystem of the form
\Phi\cap E
where
E\subseteq V
is a subspace.
A Levi subsystem of
\Phi
is closed in
\Phi
. Moreover, every Levi subsystem of
\Phi
is of the form
w\cdot\langle S\rangle
, where
S
is a subset of
\Delta
and
w\in W
. Note that
\Psi
is a Levi subsystem of
\Phi
if and only if
\check{\Psi}
is a Levi subsystem of
\check{\Phi}
.

2.1.4.
Isolated subsystems

A subset
S\subseteq\Phi
is called
isolated in
\Phi
if
S
is not contained in a proper Levi subsystem of
\Phi
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

S
is isolated in
\Phi
.

(2)

\check{S}
is isolated in
\check{\Phi}
.

(3)

S
does not lie in a proper subspace of
V
.

(4)

\langle S\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}\otimes\mathbb{R}=V
.

(5)

The root systems
\langle S\rangle
and
\Phi
have the same rank.

(6)

\displaystyle\bigcap_{\alpha\in S}\mathrm{ker}(\alpha)=0
.

2.1.5.
Pseudo-Levi subsystems

Assume
\Phi
is irreducible. Let
\theta
be the highest root of
\Phi
, and

\widetilde{\Delta}:=\Delta\sqcup\{-\theta\}.

A pseudo-Levi subsystem is a subsystem of
\Phi
of the form
w\cdot\langle S\rangle
, where
w\in W
and
S
is a subset of
\widetilde{\Delta}
. More generally, if
\Phi=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Phi_{r}
with
\Phi_{i}
irreducible, then a subsystem
\Psi\subseteq\Phi
is said to be a pseudo-Levi if
\Psi_{i}:=\Psi\cap\Phi_{i}
is a pseudo-Levi inside
\Phi_{i}
for all
i
. It is clear that every Levi is a pseudo-Levi. The converse is true in type
A
but false otherwise. For instance,
C_{2}
has a pseudo-Levi
A_{1}\times A_{1}
which is not a Levi.

2.1.6.
Isolated pseudo-Levis

Let
\Psi
be a pseudo-Levi subsystem of an irreducible root system
\Phi

(1)

\Psi
is isolated in
\Phi

(2)

\Psi=w\cdot\langle S\rangle
where
S\subset\tilde{\Delta}
has the same size as
\Delta

W

2.1.7.
Endoscopy subsystems

Let
\check{\Psi}
be a pseudo-Levi subsystem of
\check{\Phi}
. Then the dual root system
\Psi
is called an
endoscopy subsystem
of
\Phi
. For instance,
B_{n}\times B_{n}
is an endoscopy subsystem of
B_{2n}
. Note that this subsystem is not closed.

2.1.8.
Very good primes

We say the prime
p
is good for
\Phi
if
p
does not divide the coefficients of
the highest root of
\Phi
. Otherwise
p
is bad for
G
. Bad characteristics are
p=2
if the root system is of type
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
or
D_{n}
,
p=2,3
in type
G_{2}
,
F_{4}
,
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
and
p=2,3,5
in type
E_{8}
. We say
p
is very good for
\Phi
if
p
is good for
\Phi
and
p
does not divide
n+1
for every irreducible component of type
A_{n}
of
\Phi
. Note that
p
is (very) good for
\Phi
if and only if it is (very) good for
\check{\Phi}
.

2.2.
Reductive groups

Let
G
be a connected reductive group over an algebraically closed field
k
. Let
T
be a maximal torus of
G
,
B
a Borel containing
T
,
U
the unipotent radical of
B
,
W
the Weyl group,
(X,\Phi,\check{X},\check{\Phi})
the root datum,
\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
root lattice, and
\langle\check{\Phi}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
the coroot lattice. Let
\Delta\subseteq\Phi
(resp.
\check{\Delta}\subseteq\check{\Phi}
) denote the base determined by
B
and
V:=X\otimes\mathbb{R}
.

2.2.1.
Dual group

Let
\check{T}:=\mathrm{Spec}\,k[X]
be the dual torus and
\check{G}
the Langlands dual group of
G
over
k
. In other words,
\check{G}
is the connected reductive group over
k
with maximal torus
\check{T}
and root datum
(\check{X},\check{\Phi},X,\Phi)
.

2.2.2.
Levi subgroups

A Levi subgroup of
G
is defined to be the centraliser
L=C_{G}(S)
of a torus
S\subseteq G
. If
S\subseteq T
(in other words, if
L
contains
T
), then
L
is said to be a
standard
Levi subgroup. In this case, the root system of
L
is a Levi subsystem of
\Phi
. Every Levi subsystem arises in this manner.

If
x\in\mathfrak{g}
is a semisimple element, then the connected centraliser
C_{G}(x)^{\circ}
is a Levi subgroup of
G
. Moreover, every Levi subgroup of
G
arises in this manner. Note that if the characteristic of
k
is very good for
G
, then
C_{G}(x)
is connected
[
Steinberg75
, Theorem 3.14]
.

2.2.3.
Parabolic subgroups

A connected subgroup
P\subseteq G
is called parabolic if the quotient
G/P
is proper. Every parabolic subgroup has a Levi decomposition
P=L\ltimes N
, where
N
is the maximal closed connected normal unipotent subgroup of
P
and
L
is a Levi subgroup of
G
. Every Levi subgroup of
G
arises in this manner.

2.2.4.

In what follows, we will be using the following facts:

(1)

If
s\in P
is semisimple, then
s
is
P
-conjugate to an element of
L
. Indeed,
s
lies in some maximal torus
T^{\prime}
of
P
and since all maximal tori of
P
are
P
-conjugate, we can choose a
P
-conjugate of
T^{\prime}
which lies in
L
.

(2)

If
l\in L
, then
l\in[L,L]
if and only if
l\in[P,P]
. This follows from the fact that
[P,P]=[L,L]\ltimes N
.

2.2.5.
Pseudo-Levi subgroups

Let
x\in G
be a semisimple element. The connected centraliser
G_{x}^{\circ}=C_{G}(x)^{\circ}
is called a pseudo-Levi subgroup of
G
. The dual group is called an endoscopy group for
G
. If
x\in T
, then
G_{x}^{\circ}
is called a
standard
pseudo-Levi subgroup of
G
and its dual is called a
standard
endoscopy group for
\check{G}
.

2.2.6.

If
x\in T
, then the root system of
G_{x}^{\circ}
is given by

{\Phi}_{x}:=\{{\alpha}\in{\Phi}\,|\,{\alpha}(x)=1\}.

This is a pseudo-Levi subsystem of
\Phi
. Moreover, every pseudo-Levi subsystem arises in this manner
[
Deriziotis
]
. By a theorem of Steinberg,
\pi_{0}(G_{x})
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\pi_{1}([G,G])
; thus, if
[G,G]
is simply connected, then
G_{x}
is connected
[
Steinberg75
, §2]
.

2.2.7.
Quasi-isolated elements

A semisimple element
x\in G
is called
quasi-isolated
if
G_{x}
is not in a proper Levi subgroup of
G
. It is called isolated if
G_{x}^{\circ}
is not in any proper Levi subgroup of
G
. Thus,
x\in T
is isolated if and only if
\Phi_{x}
is an isolated (pseudo-Levi) subsystem of
\Phi
. If
x
is quasi-isolated, then
G_{x}
is an irreducible subgroup of
G
; i.e., it does not belong to a proper parabolic. By Schur’s lemma (cf.
[
Sikora
, Proposition 15]
)
Z(G_{x})/Z(G)
is finite.

2.2.8.
Pretty good primes

Following
[
Herpel
]
, we say a prime
p
is pretty good for
G
if for every subset
A\subseteq\Phi
, the finitely generated abelian groups
X/\mathbb{Z}A
and
\check{X}/\mathbb{Z}\check{A}
have no
p
-torsion.

Theorem 10

(
[
Herpel
]
)
.

(i)

If
p
is very good for
G
, then it is pretty good for
G
.

(ii)

Suppose
\tilde{G}
is a possibly disconnected reductive group with connected component
G
, the prime
p=\mathrm{char}(k)
is pretty good for
G
, and
\pi_{0}(\widetilde{G})
is étale. Then the centraliser
C_{\tilde{G}}(H)
of every closed subgroup scheme
H\subseteq\tilde{G}
is smooth.

Corollary 11
.

If
p=\mathrm{char}(k)
is very good for
G
, then for every
x\in T
, the centre
Z(G_{x})
is smooth.

Proof.

Indeed,
p
being pretty good for
G
implies that it is pretty good for
G_{x}^{\circ}
and that
G_{x}=C_{G}(x)
is smooth; thus,
\pi_{0}(G_{x})
is étale. By the previous theorem,
Z(G_{x})=C_{G_{x}}(G_{x})
is smooth.
∎

3.
Generic character varieties

Let
G
be a connected reductive group over an algebraically closed field
k
. In this section, we define the notion generic tuples of conjugacy classes of
G
and study the corresponding character varieties. Most of this material appears in
[
HLRV11
]
for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
and
[
Boalch14
]
for general
G
over complex numbers. We work with reductive groups over arbitrary fields.

3.1.
Generic classes

Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
be conjugacy classes of
G
.

Definition 12
.

The tuple
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
is called
generic
if whenever there exists a proper parabolic subgroup
P\subset G
and
x_{i}\in P\cap C_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, then
\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\notin[P,P]
.

3.1.1.
Maximal parabolics

Clearly it is enough to check the above condition on maximal (proper) parabolic subgroups. This implies that if
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, then being generic is equivalent to requiring that whenever a proper non-zero subspace
V\subset k^{n}
is preserved by
x_{i}\in C_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, we have
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{det}(x_{i}|_{V})\neq 1
. Thus, we recover the notion of generic tuples of
[
HLRV11
, §2]
.

3.1.2.
Standard parabolics

It is enough to check the genericity condition for
standard
(proper) parabolic subgroups. (By definition, standard parabolic subgroups are those containing a chosen Borel subgroup
B\subset G
.) Indeed, suppose
P
is an arbitrary parabolic and choose
g\in G
such that the conjugate
g\cdot P
is standard. Then

x_{i}\in P\cap C_{i}\qquad\iff\qquad g\cdot x_{i}\in g\cdot P\cap C_{i}

and

\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\in[P,P]\qquad\iff\qquad g\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\in g\cdot[P,P]=[g\cdot P,g\cdot P].

3.1.3.
Semisimple part

For each conjugacy class
X
, write
X^{s}
for the corresponding semisimple class, obtained by taking the semisimple parts of the elements of
X
.
Then one can show that
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
is generic if and only if
C^{s}=(C_{1}^{s},\ldots,C_{n}^{s})
is generic. This is the reductive analogue of the fact that in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, being generic depends only on the eigenvalues.

3.1.4.
Levis instead of parabolics

For semisimple conjugacy classes, one can check genericity using Levi subgroups instead of parabolics. More precisely, it follows from discussions of §
2.2.3
that a tuple of semisimple conjugacy classes
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
is generic if and only if whenever there exists a proper standard Levi subgroup
L\subset G
and
s_{i}\in L\cap C_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, then
\prod_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\notin[L,L]
.

3.1.5.
Generic elements

We now explain that deciding whether a tuple of (semisimple) conjugacy classes is generic involves checking finitely many conditions. To this end, we need a definition.

Definition 13
.

A tuple
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})\in T^{n}
is said to be generic if for every
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in W^{n}
and every proper standard Levi subgroup
L\subset G
, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot S_{i}\notin T\cap[L,L].

Let
C_{i}:=G\cdot S_{i}
be the conjugacy class corresponding to
S_{i}
.

Lemma 14
.

The element
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})\in T^{n}
is generic if and only if the tuple of conjugacy classes
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
is generic.

Proof.

It is clear that if
C
is generic then so is
S
. For the converse, let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in C_{1}\times\ldots\times C_{n}
. Let
L\subset G
be a proper standard Levi subgroup containing
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. We need to show
\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\notin[L,L]
. We have
x_{i}=l_{i}\cdot t_{i}
for some
l_{i}\in L
and
t_{i}\in T
. Now
t_{i}
and
S_{i}
are elements of
T
which are
G
-conjugate; thus, they must actually be conjugate under
W
; i.e., there exists
w_{i}\in W
such that
t_{i}=w_{i}\cdot S_{i}
, cf. proof of Proposition 3.7.1 in
[
Carter93
]
. By assumption,
t_{1}\cdots t_{n}\notin[L,L]
which implies that
x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\notin[L,L]
. Thus,
C
is generic.
∎

3.1.6.
Existence

The above lemma shows that generic conjugacy classes exist. Indeed, if
L
is a proper standard levi subgroup, then
T\cap[L,L]
is a proper closed subvariety of
T
. (At the level of Lie algebras,
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{t}\cap[\mathfrak{l},\mathfrak{l}]\oplus Z(\mathfrak{l})
for
\mathfrak{t}=\mathrm{Lie}(T)
and the centre
Z(\mathfrak{l})
is non-trivial if
\mathfrak{l}
is a proper Levi subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.) Since there are only finitely many standard Levis
L
and finitely many
w
’s, we conclude that the generic locus of
T^{n}
is open dense.

3.1.7.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{n}\subseteq T^{n}
denote the subvariety consisting of those tuples
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})\in T^{n}
satisfying
\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}\in[G,G]
. Then the above argument also shows that the generic locus of
\mathbf{T}_{n}
is open dense. Since the (strongly) regular locus is also open dense in
T^{n}
, it follows that conjugacy classes satisfying Assumption
1
exist.

3.2.
Generic character varieties

In this section, we assume that
p=\mathrm{char}(k)
is a very good prime for
G
and
Z=Z(G)
is connected.
Let
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
be a generic tuple of conjugacy classes of
G
satisfying
\prod_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}\subseteq[G,G]
. For ease of notation, let
M\colon G^{2g}\times C_{1}\times\cdots\times C_{n}\rightarrow[G,G]
be the map

M(a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}):=[a_{1},b_{1}]\cdots[a_{g},b_{g}]c_{1}\cdots c_{n}.

The representation variety associated to
(\Gamma_{g,n},G,C)
is defined by
R:=M^{-1}(1)
. This is an affine scheme of finite type over
k
. We assume that
R
is non-empty (this is guaranteed if
g>0
).

Theorem 15
.

Every element of
R
is irreducible and stable and the action of
G/Z
on
R
has finite étale stabilisers. Moreover,

R
is smooth and equidimensional with

\dim(R)=2g\dim(G)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dim(C_{i})-\dim([G,G]).

Proof.

Let
{r=(a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in R}
and suppose
P
is a parabolic subgroup of
G
containing all
a_{i},b_{i},
and
c_{i}
’s. Then,
[P,P]
contains
[a_{1},b_{1}]\cdots[a_{g},b_{g}]
; thus,
[P,P]
also contains
\prod_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}
. As
C
is generic, it follows that
P=G
. Thus,
r
is irreducible and Schur’s lemma (cf.
[
Sikora
, §4]
) implies that
\mathrm{Stab}_{G/Z}(r)
is finite. Note that this stabiliser is the same as the centraliser
C_{G}(H)
, where
H:=\langle a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\rangle\subseteq G
. By Theorem
10
, this group is smooth. Since we already have shown that it is finite, it follows that it is finite and étale.

Now stability follows from the fact that whenever a connected reductive group acts on an affine scheme of finite type with finite stabilisers, then every point is stable, cf.
[
Mumford
, Proposition 0.8]
. (Alternatively, one can use the Hilbert–Mumford criterion as in
[
Boalch14
]
.) The statements about
R
follow from the fact that
1
is a regular value for
M
, see
[
KNP25
, §2]
for details.
∎

3.2.1.

Next, let
{X}:=R/\!\!/G
and
[{X}]:=[R/(G/Z)]
denote, respectively, the (generic) character variety and character stack associated to
(\Gamma_{g,n},G,C)
. The previous theorem implies:

Proposition 16
.

[{X}]
is a smooth Deligne-Mumford stack with coarse moduli space
{X}
.

3.3.
Frobenius Mass Formula

We now work over
k=\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
and assume that
G
is equipped with an untwisted Frobenius automorphism
F:G\rightarrow G
.
We also assume that the conjugacy classes
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
are
F
-stable. Then the action of the Frobenius
F
on
G
and
C_{i}
’s, gives an action of
F
on
R
,
{X}
and
[{X}]
. We can therefore speak about the Frobenius fixed points
R^{F}
,
{X}^{F}
, and
[{X}]^{F}
. The first two are finite sets while the last one is a finite groupoid.
By definition,

R^{F}=\Big\{(a_{1},\ldots,b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in(G^{F})^{2g}\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}^{F}\,\Big|\,M(a_{1},\ldots,b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})=1\Big\}.

3.3.1.
Stable conjugacy

Definition 17
.

A conjugacy class
C\subseteq G
is called
stable
if the stabiliser in
G
of each element of
C
is connected.

If
C
is stable, then
C^{F}
is a unique
G^{F}
-conjugacy class, cf.
[
GM20
, §2.7.1]
. Thus, it makes sense to evaluate irreducible complex characters of
G^{F}
on
C_{i}^{F}
.

3.3.2.
Frobenius Mass Formula

The following result goes back to the work of Frobenius; see e.g.
[
HLRV11
, §3]
for a proof:

Theorem 18
.

If
C_{i}
’s are stable, then

{|R^{F}|}=|G^{F}|\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})}\left(\frac{|G^{F}|}{\chi(1)}\right)^{2g-2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\chi(C_{i}^{F})|C_{i}^{F}|}{\chi(1)}.

3.3.3.
Points of the character stack

An application of Lang’s theorem implies that

|[{X}]^{F}|=\frac{|R^{F}|}{|(G/Z)^{F}|},

cf.
[
Behrend91
, Lemma 3.5.6]
.
Thus, the Frobenius Mass Formula also provides an expression for the number of points of the character stack.

3.3.4.
Points of character variety

We now assume that the tuple
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
is generic. We saw above that
{X}
is a coarse moduli space for the smooth Deligne-Mumford stack
[{X}]
. This implies that
[{X}]
and
X
have the same number of points over finite fields, cf.
[
Behrend91
, §2]
or
[
BogaartEdixhoven
]
. Thus, we conclude:

Corollary 19
.

If
C_{i}
’s are generic and stable, then

|{X}^{F}|=|[{X}]^{F}|=|Z^{F}|\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})}\left(\frac{|G^{F}|}{\chi(1)}\right)^{2g-2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\chi(C_{i}^{F})|C_{i}^{F}|}{\chi(1)}.

3.4.
Additive character varieties

The above considerations have additive analogues which we leave to the reader to formulate. The only non-trivial modification is the additive analogue of the Frobenius Mass Formula, which we now discuss.

3.4.1.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{n}
be
F
-stable adjoint
G
-orbits in
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
A
denote the corresponding additive representation variety. For ease of notation, let
m\colon\mathfrak{g}^{2g}\times\mathcal{O}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{O}_{n}\rightarrow[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
be the map

m(a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}):=\sum_{i=1}^{g}[a_{i},b_{i}]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}.

Then, we have

{A}^{F}=\Big\{(a_{1},\ldots,b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in(\mathfrak{g}^{F})^{2g}\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{F}\,\Big|\,m(a_{1},\ldots,b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})=0\Big\}.

3.4.2.

Let
1_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}^{G}
denote the characteristic function of the
G^{F}
-orbit
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{F}
. Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the Fourier transform on the space of
G^{F}
-invariant functions on
\mathfrak{g}^{F}
(see §
6.1
for the definition). For each
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, let
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
denote its centraliser in
\mathfrak{g}
. Then we have (cf.
[
HLRV11
, §3]
):

Theorem 20

(Additive Frobenius Mass Formula)
.

If
\mathcal{O}_{i}
’s are stable, then

{|{A}^{F}|}=|\mathfrak{g}^{F}|^{g-1}\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}}|\mathfrak{g}_{x}^{F}|^{g}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{F}(1_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}^{G})(x).

Corollary 21
.

If
\mathcal{O}_{i}
’s are generic and stable, then

|Y^{F}|=|[Y]^{F}|=\frac{|{A}^{F}||Z^{F}|}{|G^{F}|}=\frac{|Z^{F}||\mathfrak{g}^{F}|^{g-1}}{|G^{F}|}\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}}|\mathfrak{g}_{x}^{F}|^{g}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{F}(1_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}^{G})(x).

4.
Counting points on character varieties

In this section, we count points on the character varieties introduced in §
1.2
and prove Theorem
2
.
We treat the case of a once-punctured surface first and mention the modifications required in the multi-punctured case at the end.

Let
G
be a connected split reductive group over
k=\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
equipped with an untwisted Frobenius
F
, giving
G
an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-structure. We assume
Z(G)
is connected and
p=\mathrm{char}(k)
is very good for
G
.
Let
T
be a maximal split
F
-stable torus of
G
. Let
S\in T^{F}
be a strongly regular generic element and
C=G\cdot S
the corresponding conjugacy class in
G
. Let
X
denote the character variety associated to the once-punctured Riemann surface and the conjugacy class
C
.

By the Frobenius Mass Formula (Theorem
18
), we have

(1)

|X^{F}|=\frac{|R^{F}|}{|(G/Z)^{F}|}=\frac{|Z^{F}|}{|T^{F}|}\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})}\left(\frac{|G^{F}|}{\chi(1)}\right)^{2g-1}\chi(S).

Note that the equality continues to hold if
F
is replaced by
F^{m}
for a positive integer
m
.
In what follows, we use a crucial result of Deligne and Lusztig to rewrite the above sum in a more tractable form.

4.1.
A theorem of Deligne and Lusztig

Given a finite abelian group
A
, write
A^{\vee}
for the Pontryagin dual
\mathrm{Hom}(A,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
.
Recall that a principal series representation is an irreducible constituent of

R_{T}^{G}\,\theta:=\mathrm{Ind}_{B^{F}}^{G^{F}}\,\theta,\qquad\theta\in(T^{F})^{\vee}.

Theorem 22

(Corollary 7.6 of
[
DL76
]
)
.

Let
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})
be an arbitrary character and
S\in T^{F}
a strongly regular element. Then

\chi(S)=\sum_{\theta\in(T^{F})^{\vee}}\langle\chi,R_{T}^{G}\,\theta\rangle\,\theta(S).

Corollary 23
.

If
S\in T^{F}
is a strongly regular element and
\chi(S)\neq 0
, then
\chi
is a principal series representation.

The above corollary implies that only principal series representations contribute to the sum in the expression (
1
) for
|X^{F}|
.
Note that one does not need Deligne–Lusztig theory to formulate the above theorem and its corollary. However, the only proof we know of uses the full power of the Deligne–Lusztig theory.

4.1.1.

As we have assumed that
G
has connected centre, the induced representations
R_{T}^{G}\,\theta
and
R_{T}^{G}\,\theta^{\prime}
have a common constituent if and only if
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
are
W
-conjugate, cf.
[
DL76
, Corollary 6.3]
.
Thus, we can associate to a principal series representation
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})
a character
\theta=\theta_{\chi}\in(T^{F})^{\vee}
, well-defined up to
W
-conjugacy.

4.1.2.
Interlude on Pontryagin duality

Let
\mu_{\infty,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
denote the set of roots of unity in
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
whose order is prime to
p
. We choose, once and for all, group isomorphisms

(2)

k^{\times}\simeq(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})_{p^{\prime}}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})_{p^{\prime}}\simeq\mu_{\infty,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C}).

As noted in
[
DL76
, §5]
, this induces a group isomorphism

(3)

(T^{F})^{\vee}\simeq\check{T}^{F}

4.1.3.

Using the above identification, we can think of
\theta\in(T^{F})^{\vee}
as an element of
\check{T}^{F}\subseteq\check{G}^{F}
.
Let
\check{G}_{\theta}
denote the centraliser of
\theta
in
\check{G}
. The fact that
Z(G)
is connected implies that
\check{G}
has simply connected derived subgroup. By Steinberg’s theorem, the stabiliser
\check{G}_{\theta}
is a
connected
standard pseudo-Levi subgroup of
\check{G}
. The Weyl group of
\check{G}_{\theta}
equals

W_{\theta}=\{w\in W\,|\,w\cdot\theta=\theta\}.

The Langlands dual group
G_{\theta}:=(\check{G}_{\theta})^{\check{}}
is a standard endoscopy group for
G
with Weyl group
W(G_{\theta})=W_{\theta}
.

4.1.4.

Since
G
is assumed to have connected centre, the constituents of
R_{T}^{G}\,\theta
are in canonical bijection with
\mathrm{Irr}(W_{\theta})
, cf.
[
Kilmoyer78
, Corollary 4.20]
or
[
DL76
, Theorem 6.8]
. Let
\chi_{\theta,\rho}\in R_{T}^{G}\,\theta
be the irreducible character corresponding to
\rho\in\mathrm{Irr}(W_{\theta})
. Then, the double centraliser theorem (cf.
[
Etingof11
, Theorem 5.18.1]
) gives

\langle\chi_{\theta,\rho},R_{T}^{G}\,\theta\rangle=\dim(\rho).

4.1.5.

Consider the set of pairs
(L,\rho)
, where
L
is a standard endoscopy group for
G
and
\rho
is an irreducible character of
W(L)
. Note
W
acts on this set by conjugation. Let
\mathcal{T}(G)
denote the set of
W
-orbits. We call the elements of
\mathcal{T}(G)

G
-types
and denote them by
\tau=[L,\rho]
. Note that
\mathcal{T}(G)
is a finite set which depends only on the root system
\Phi
of
G
; in particular, it is independent of the ground field.

By the above discussions, we can associate a type to every principal series representation. (This is a reductive analogue of the notion of type used in
[
HRV08
,
HLRV11
,
Cambo17
]
.) In other words, we have a map

(4)

\textrm{Principal series representations of $G^{F}$}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}(G).

4.1.6.
Unipotent characters

Now let
L
be a standard endoscopy subgroup of
G
. The irreducible constituents of
R_{T}^{L}1
(which are known as unipotent principal series representations of
L^{F}
) are in bijection with irreducible characters of
W(L)
. Let
\tilde{\rho}
denote the unipotent principal series character of
L^{F}
corresponding to
\rho\in\mathrm{Irr}(W(L))
.

Definition 24
.

We define the
q
-mass
of
\tau=[L,\rho]\in\mathcal{T}(G)
by

m_{\tau}(q):=q^{|\Phi^{+}(G)|-|\Phi^{+}(L)|}\frac{|L^{F}|}{\tilde{\rho}(1)}.

Here,
\Phi^{+}(L)
denotes the set of positive roots of
L
.
Note that
\tilde{\rho}(1)
is a polynomial in
q
which divides
|L^{F}|
, cf.
[
GM20
, Remark 2.3.27]
.
Thus,
m_{\tau}(q)
is a polynomial in
q
.

4.1.7.

The relevance of
m_{\tau}(q)
emerges in the following proposition which can be found in, for instance,
[
GM20
, Corollary 2.6.6]
:

Proposition 25
.

Suppose
\chi
is a principal series character of
G^{F}
of type
\tau=[L,\rho]
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{|G^{F}|}{\chi(1)}=m_{\tau}(q).

Remark 26
.

As noted in
[
GM20
, Remark 2.3.7]
, for all irreducible characters
\chi
, we have that

|T^{F}|
divides
m_{\tau}(q)
(as polynomials in
q
).

4.1.8.

We can now rewrite the Frobenius Mass Formula (
1
) in terms of types. To this end, let
\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})_{\tau}
denote the set of irreducible principal series characters of type
\tau
.
Then, we have

|{X}^{F}|=\frac{|Z^{F}|}{|T^{F}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(G)}m_{\tau}(q)^{2g-1}\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})_{\tau}}\chi(S).

4.1.9.

For ease of notation, define

\boxed{S_{\tau}(q):=\displaystyle\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})_{\tau}}\chi(S).}

Then, we find

|{X}^{F}|=\frac{|Z^{F}|}{|T^{F}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(G)}m_{\tau}(q)^{2g-1}S_{\tau}(q).

The importance of the above formula is that the sum is over a parameter which does not depend on
q
. Moreover, it implies that computing
|{X}^{F}|
is reduced to computing the character sums
S_{\tau}(q)
.

Remark 27
.

(i)

In obtaining the above formula, we used the assumption that
S
is strongly regular, but not the assumption that
S
is generic.

(ii)

The above formula holds if
F
is replaced by
F^{m}
for a positive integer
m
.

4.2.
Evaluating the character sum
S_{\tau}

In this subsection, we rewrite the expression for
S_{\tau}
in terms of sums of characters of
T^{F}
, thus turning the problem of computing
S_{\tau}
into a “commutative” problem.

Suppose
\tau=[L,\rho]\in\mathcal{T}(G)
where
L
is a standard endoscopy group for
G
with Weyl group
W(L)
and
\rho
is an irreducible character of
W(L)
. Let
[L]
denote the
W
-orbit of
L
in
G
and
[W(L)]
the
W
-orbit of
W(L)
in
W
.

4.2.1.

We start by elucidating the nature of
\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})_{\tau}
.
It consists of characters
\chi_{\theta,\rho}
as
\theta
runs over elements of
(T^{F})^{\vee}
satisfying
[G_{\theta}]=[L]
. Equivalently,

\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})_{\tau}=\{\chi_{\theta,\rho}\,|\,\theta\in(T^{F})^{\vee},\,[W_{\theta}]=[W(L)]\}.

We refer the reader to §
4.1.4
for the definition of
\chi_{\theta,\rho}
.

4.2.2.

The above discussion implies

S_{\tau}(q)=\displaystyle\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})_{\tau}}\chi(S)=\frac{|W(L)|}{|W|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
[W_{\theta}]=[W(L)]\end{subarray}}\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S)=\frac{|W(L)|}{|N_{W}(W(L))|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
W_{\theta}=W(L)\end{subarray}}\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S).

The second equality follows from the fact that
\chi_{\theta,\rho}
depends only on the
W
-orbit of
\theta
.

4.2.3.

Recall that
[L]
denotes the orbit of
L
under the action of
W
on subgroups of
G
. According to
[
Carter72
, Lemma 34]
, we have
{|W/N_{W}(W(L))|}=|[L]|
. Thus, we obtain

S_{\tau}(q)=\frac{|W(L)||[L]|}{|W|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
W_{\theta}=W(L)\end{subarray}}\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S).

Remark 28
.

In
[
Carter72
, Proposition 28]
, one finds the following formula for
|[L]|
. Let
W^{\perp}(L)
be the Weyl group generated by the roots orthogonal to
\Phi(L)
. Let
A_{W}(L)
denote the group of symmetries of the Dynkin diagram of
L
induced by elements of
W
. Then

|[L]|=\frac{|W|}{|W(L)||W^{\perp}(L)||A_{W}(L)|}.

4.2.4.

Next, Theorem
22
implies

\displaystyle\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S)=\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\check{T}^{F}}\langle\chi_{\theta,\rho},R_{T}^{G}\,\theta^{\prime}\rangle\,\theta^{\prime}(S).

Note that a summand is zero unless
\theta^{\prime}
is in the same
W
-orbit as
\theta
, in which case it equals
\dim(\rho)\,\theta^{\prime}(S)
; thus,

\displaystyle\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S)=\dim(\rho)\sum_{w\in W/W_{\theta}}(w\cdot\theta)(S)=\frac{\dim(\rho)}{|W_{\theta}|}\sum_{w\in W}\theta(w\cdot S).

Note that the last sum depends only on the
W
-orbit of
\theta
.

4.2.5.

We now plug in the explicit formula for
\chi_{\theta,\rho}
into the formula for
S_{\tau}
to obtain

S_{\tau}(q)=\frac{|W(L)||[L]|}{|W|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
W_{\theta}=W(L)\end{subarray}}\frac{\dim(\rho)}{|W_{\theta}|}\sum_{w\in W}\theta(w\cdot S)=\frac{\dim(\rho)|[L]|}{|W|}\sum_{w\in W}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
W_{\theta}=W(L)\end{subarray}}\theta(w\cdot S).

4.2.6.

To alleviate notation, let

\boxed{\alpha_{L,S}(q):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
W_{\theta}=W(L)\end{subarray}}\theta(S).}

Thus, we have shown

S_{\tau}(q)=\frac{\dim(\rho)|[L]|}{|W|}\sum_{w\in W}\alpha_{L,w\cdot S}(q).

This formula continues to hold if
q
is replaced by
q^{m}
for a positive integer
m
.

The above equality implies that the problem of computing
S_{\tau}
reduces to evaluating the character sum
\alpha_{L,S}
. To compute the latter, we first consider an auxiliary sum
\alpha_{L,S}^{\supseteq}
which is easier to handle.

4.3.
The auxiliary sum
\alpha_{L,S}^{\supseteq}

Given a standard endoscopy group
L
for
G
and
S\in T^{F}
, define

\alpha_{L,S}^{\supseteq}(q):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
W_{\theta}\supseteq W(L)\end{subarray}}\theta(S)=\sum_{\theta\in(\check{T}^{F})^{W(L)}}\theta(S).

Lemma 29
.

We have

\alpha^{\supseteq}_{L,S}(q)=\begin{cases}|(\check{T}^{F})^{W(L)}|&\textrm{if $S\in[L^{F},L^{F}]$}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This holds if
q
and
F
are replaced by
q^{m}
and
F^{m}
, for any positive integer
m
.

Proof.

The lemma follows from the fact (proved in
[
KNP25
, §5.2]
) that the Pontryagin dual of the embedding of finite abelian groups
(\check{T}^{F})^{W(L)}\hookrightarrow\check{T}^{F}
is the canonical quotient map

T^{F}\twoheadrightarrow\frac{T^{F}}{T^{F}\cap[L^{F},L^{F}]}.\qed

4.3.1.

Now we simplify the above expression using the fact
S\in[G,G]
is assumed to be generic. This implies that
S\in[L,L]
if and only if the root system
\Phi(L)
is isolated in
\Phi=\Phi(G)
; i.e.,
\Phi(L)
is not contained in any proper Levi subsystem of
\Phi
.

Note that the containment
[L^{F},L^{F}]\subseteq[L,L]
may be proper (e.g.
L=G=\mathrm{PGL}_{n}
). Thus, it could happen that
S\in[L,L]\setminus[L^{F},L^{F}]
. However, this subtlety goes away after a finite base change. Indeed,
there are finitely many isolated standard endoscopic groups for
G
; thus, given
S\in T^{F}
, there exists a positive integer
m
, such that
S\in[L^{F^{m}},L^{F^{m}}]
for all isolated standard endoscopy groups. We conclude:

Corollary 30
.

There exists a positive integer
m
such that

\alpha^{\supseteq}_{L,S}(q^{m})=\begin{cases}|(\check{T}^{F^{m}})^{W(L)}|&\textrm{if $L$ is isolated}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Moreover, the same formula holds if
m
is replaced by
mx
for a positive integer
x
.

4.3.2.
Description of
\check{T}^{W(L)}

To proceed further, we need a description of
T^{W(L)}
. It is proved in
[
KNP25
, §5]
that, under the assumption that
G
has connected centre, we have:

\check{T}^{W(L)}\simeq Z(\check{L}).

Thus,

|(\check{T}^{F})^{W(L)}|=|Z(\check{L})^{F}|=|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{L}))^{F}|\times|(Z(\check{L})^{\circ})^{F}|.

By Corollary
11
, the prime
p=\mathrm{char}(k)
does not divide
|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{L}))|
. Thus, there exists a positive integer
n
such that
q^{n}\equiv 1\mod|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{L}))|
. This, in turn, implies

\pi_{0}(Z(\check{L}))^{F^{n}}=\pi_{0}(Z(\check{L})).

Combining this with the previous corollary, we obtain:

Corollary 31
.

There exists a positive integer
r
such that for every standard endoscopy
L
of
G
, we have

\alpha^{\supseteq}_{L,S}(q^{r})=\begin{cases}|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{L}))|\times|(Z(\check{L})^{\circ})^{F^{r}}|&\textrm{if $L$ is isolated}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The same formula holds if
r
is replaced by
rx
for any positive integer
x
.

4.4.
Evaluating
\alpha_{L,S}

We now give an expression for
\alpha_{L,S}
using Möbius inversion.

4.4.1.

Let
\mu
denote the Möbius function on the (finite) poset of the standard endoscopy groups of
G
, ordered by inclusion of their root systems (Note that these root systems all live in
\Phi(G)
). Möbius inversion implies

\alpha_{L,S}=\sum_{L^{\prime}}\mu(L,L^{\prime})\alpha^{\supseteq}_{L^{\prime},S},

where the sum is over standard endoscopy groups
L^{\prime}
satisfying
\Phi(L^{\prime})\supseteq\Phi(L)
.

4.4.2.

We have seen that
\alpha_{L^{\prime},S}^{\supseteq}
is
0
unless
L^{\prime}
is isolated. In this case, our assumption that
G
has connected centre implies

Z(\check{L^{\prime}})^{\circ}=Z(\check{G})^{\circ}\simeq Z(G)^{\circ}=Z(G).

4.4.3.

To simplify notation, let

\nu(L):=\sum_{L^{\prime}}|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{L^{\prime}}))|\,\mu(L,L^{\prime}).

Here, the sum runs over isolated standard endoscopy groups
L^{\prime}
of
G
whose root systems contain
\Phi(L)
. Thus,
\nu(L)
is an integer depending only on the root data of
L
and
G
; in particular, it is independent of the ground field
k
(of very good characteristic). In fact,
\nu(L)
depends only on the
W
-orbit of
L
. In view of the above discussions, we conclude:

Proposition 32
.

There exists a positive integer
r
such that

\alpha_{L,S}(q^{r})=|Z^{F^{r}}|\,\nu(L).

The equality continues to hold if
r
is replaced by
rx
for any positive integer
x
.

Observe that
\alpha_{L,S}
is independent of
S
(provided
S
is generic).

4.5.
Proof of Theorem
2
in the once-punctured case

The above discussions imply that there exists a positive integer
r
such that

|{X}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})|=\frac{|Z(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})|}{|T(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})|}\sum_{\tau=[L,\rho]\in\mathcal{T}(G)}S_{\tau}(q^{r})\,m_{\tau}(q^{r})^{2g-1},

where

S_{\tau}(q^{r})=\frac{\dim(\rho)\,|[L]|}{|W|}\sum_{w\in W}\alpha_{L,w\cdot S}(q^{r})=\dim(\rho)\,|[L]|\,\nu(L)\,|Z(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})|.

Moreover, the above formulas continue to hold if
r
is replaced by a positive multiple.

4.5.1.

To proceed further, we need some notation.
Let
f_{Z}
,
f_{T}
,
f_{L}
be counting polynomial for
Z
,
T
,
L
. Thus,

f_{Z}(t)=(t-1)^{\dim(Z)},\quad f_{T}(t)=(t-1)^{\dim(T)},\quad f_{L}(t)=f_{T}(t)t^{|\Phi^{+}(L)|}P_{L}(t),

where
P_{L}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
is the Poincaré polynomial of the flag variety of
L
.
Recall that for a type
\tau=[L,\rho]
, the polynomial
m_{\tau}(t)
is defined by

m_{\tau}(t):=t^{|\Phi^{+}(G)|-|\Phi^{+}(L)|}\frac{|f_{L}(t)|}{\tilde{\rho}(1)}\in\mathbb{Q}[t].

Definition 33
.

Define the rational function

f_{X}(t):=\frac{f_{Z}(t)^{2}}{f_{T}(t)}\sum_{\tau=[L,\rho]\in\mathcal{T}(G)}\dim(\rho)|[L]|\,m_{\tau}(t)^{2g-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(t).

4.5.2.
Conclusion of the proof

We have seen that there exists a positive integer
r
such that

|X(\mathbb{F}_{q^{rm}})|=f_{X}(q^{rm}),\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

Thus,
f_{X}(q^{rm})
is an integer for all
m>0
. An elementary argument (cf.
[
GoodwinRohrle
, Lemma 2.11]
) then implies that
f_{X}(t)
is a polynomial. Thus,
X\times_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}}
is polynomial count with counting polynomial
f_{X}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
2
in the single punctured case.

4.6.
Proof of Theorem
2
in the multi-punctured case

Let
{X}
be a character variety associated to an
n
-punctured surface group satisfying Assumption
1
. Define the rational function

(5)

f_{X}(t):=\frac{|W|^{n-1}f_{Z}(t)^{2}}{f_{T}(t)^{n}}\sum_{\tau=[L,\rho]\in\mathcal{T}(G)}\frac{\dim(\rho)^{n}|[L]|\,m_{\tau}(t)^{2g-2+n}}{|W(L)|^{n-1}}\in\mathbb{Q}(t).

Our goal is to show that

|X(\mathbb{F}_{q^{rm}})|=f_{X}(q^{rm}),\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

4.6.1.

As in the once-punctured case, we have

|X^{F}|=\frac{|Z^{F}|}{|T^{F}|^{n}}\sum_{\tau=[L,\rho]\in\mathcal{T}(G)}m_{\tau}(q)^{2g-2+n}S_{\tau}(q),

where
S_{\tau}
is now defined as

S_{\tau}(q):=\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})_{\tau}}\chi(S_{1})\cdots\chi(S_{n}).

4.6.2.

Proceeding as in the once-punctured case, we compute

S_{\tau}(q)=\frac{|W(L)|}{|N_{W}(W(L))|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
W_{\theta}=W(L)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S_{i})=\frac{|W(L)|}{|W|}|[L]|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\check{T}^{F}\\
W_{\theta}=W(L)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S_{i}).

We already computed

\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S_{i})=\frac{\dim(\rho)}{|W_{\theta}|}\sum_{w\in W}\theta(w\cdot S_{i}).

Plugging this expression for
\chi_{\theta,\rho}(S_{i})
into the one for
S_{\tau}
yields

S_{\tau}(q)=\frac{\dim(\rho)^{n}\,|[L]|}{|W|\,|W(L)|^{n-1}}\sum_{\underline{w}\in W^{n}}\alpha_{L,\underline{w}\cdot\underline{S}}(q),

where
\underline{w}:=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in W^{n}
and
\underline{w}\cdot\underline{S}:=(w_{1}\cdot S_{1})\cdots(w_{n}\cdot S_{n})\in T^{F}
.

4.6.3.

We have seen that there exists a positive integer
r
such that

S_{\tau}(q^{r})=\frac{|W|^{n-1}}{|W(L)|^{n-1}}\dim(\rho)^{n}\,|[L]|\,\nu(L)\,|Z^{F^{r}}|.

This means we have

(6)

|X^{F^{r}}|=\frac{|W|^{n-1}|Z^{F^{r}}|^{2}}{|T^{F^{r}}|^{n}}\sum_{\tau=[L,\rho]\in\mathcal{T}(G)}\frac{\dim(\rho)^{n}\,|[L]|\,m_{\tau}(q^{r})^{2g-2+n}}{|W(L)|^{n-1}}=f_{X}(q^{r}).

This continues to hold if
r
is replaced by
rm
for any positive integer
m
. The same reasoning as in the single punctured case completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

5.
Topological implications

The aim of this section is discuss the topological implications of our expression for the counting polynomial of generic character varieties and prove Corollary
3
.
We continue using the notation of the previous section.

5.1.
Palindromic property

We start by recalling some facts about Alvis–Curtis duality; see
[
DM20
, §7.2]
for further details.

5.1.1.

Alvis–Curtis duality is (up to a sign) an involution

\mathfrak{D}:\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F})\rightarrow\mathrm{Irr}(G^{F}).

To compute the dimension of the irreducible character
\mathfrak{D}(\chi)
, we need to replace
t
by
t^{-1}
in the polynomial
d_{\chi}(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
encoding the dimension of
\chi
. More precisely,

\dim\mathfrak{D}(\chi)=t^{|\Phi^{+}|}d_{\chi}(t^{-1}).

5.1.2.

It is known that
\mathfrak{D}
restricts to an involution on the set of irreducible constituents of
R_{T}^{G}\,\theta
; namely, it sends the irreducible constituent corresponding to
\rho\in\mathrm{Irr}(W_{\theta})
to the constituent corresponding to
\epsilon\rho
, where
\epsilon
is the sign character of
W_{\theta}
. In particular, we see that
\mathfrak{D}
sends an irreducible character of type
[L,\rho]
to one of type
[L,\epsilon\rho]
.

5.1.3.

Now, observe that

f_{Z}(t^{-1})=(-1)^{\dim(Z)}t^{-\dim(Z)}f_{Z}(t),\qquad f_{T}(t^{-1})=(-1)^{\dim(T)}t^{-\dim(T)}f_{T}(t),

and

f_{L}(t^{-1})=(-1)^{\dim(T)}t^{-\dim(T)-3|\Phi^{+}(L)|}f_{L}(t).

Moreover, we have

m_{\tau}(t^{-1})=(-1)^{\dim(T)}t^{-\dim(T)-|\Phi(G)|}m_{\tau^{\prime}}(t),

where
\tau^{\prime}=[L,\epsilon\rho]
. Thus, we conclude:

f_{X}(t^{-1})=t^{-\deg(f_{X})}\frac{|W|^{n-1}f_{Z}(t)^{2}}{f_{T}(t)^{n}}\sum_{\tau=[L,\rho]\in\mathcal{T}(G)}\frac{\dim(\rho)^{n}|[L]|\,m_{\tau^{\prime}}(t)^{2g-2+n}}{|W(L)|^{n-1}}.

Since the map
\tau=[L,\rho]\mapsto\tau^{\prime}=[L,\epsilon\rho]
is an involution, we conclude
t^{\deg(f_{X})}f_{X}(t^{-1})
equals
f_{X}(t)
; i.e.,
f_{X}
is a palindrome. ∎

5.2.
Number of connected components

The approach here is very similar to
[
BK22
, Corollary 5]
, where it is shown that the number of components of the character stack associated to a
compact
surface group is
|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{G}))|
. Thus, we only sketch the main idea.

Since
X
is smooth and equidimensional, connected components and irreducible components coincide. Thus, the number of connected components equals the leading coefficient of the
E
-polynomial. By the above discussions, the
E
-polynomial of
\overline{X}
equals
f_{X}
. Thus, our goal is to determine the leading term of
f_{X}
.

Following the same analysis as
[
BK22
, Corollary 5]
, one can show that the only type contributing to the leading term of
f_{X}
is the
[G,1]
. This is the type corresponding to one-dimensional (principal series) representations of
G(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})
; i.e. representations of the form

G(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})\rightarrow G(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})^{\mathrm{ab}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}.

The number of such representations is

|G(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})^{\mathrm{ab}}|=|Z(\check{G}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}}))|=|\pi_{0}(\check{Z}(G))(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})|\times q^{r\dim(Z)}.

As
q
is coprime to
|\pi_{0}(\check{Z}(G))|
and
r
is chosen so that
|\pi_{0}(\check{Z}(G))(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})|=|\pi_{0}(\check{Z}(G))|
, we conclude that the leading coefficient of
f_{X}
is

|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{G}))(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})|=|\pi_{0}(Z(\check{G}))|.

5.3.
Euler characteristic

Note that
E(\overline{X})=f_{X}(1)
.
We have three cases:

5.3.1.
Genus
g=0

The formula
6
implies

f_{X}(1)=\frac{1}{(2r)!}\frac{d^{2r}}{dq^{2r}}\bigg|_{q=1}\xi(q),

where
r:=\dim(T)-\dim(Z)
is the semisimple rank of
G
and
\xi
is the rational function

\xi(q):=q^{|\Phi^{+}|(n-2)}\sum_{L}\bigg(\frac{|W|}{|W(L)|}\bigg)^{n-1}\nu(L)\sum_{\rho}\dim(\rho)^{n}\bigg(\frac{P_{W(L)}(q)}{\dim(\tilde{\rho})}\bigg)^{n-2}.

Here, the first sum is over all standard endoscopy groups of
G
, and the second sum is over all irreducible characters of
W(L)
.

5.3.2.
Genus
g=1
and trivial centre

The formula (
6
) implies

f_{X}(1)=|W|^{n-1}\sum_{L}|W(L)|\,|\mathrm{Irr}(W(L))|\,\nu(L),

where the sum is over all standard endoscopy groups of
G
.

6.1.
Recollections on invariant functions

Fix a non-trivial additive character

6.1.1.
Green function

The Green function

Here,

Since

6.1.3.

Let

We write

6.2.1.

6.2.5.

Let

Clearly, this is independent of the choice of

Lemma 38

Let

Proof.

Observe that

Thus, we have

6.2.6.

In view of the above discussions, we can rewrite the formula for

|{Y}^{F}|=\frac{|Z^{F}||\mathfrak{g}^{F}|^{g-1}}{|G^{F}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{g})}q^{d(\tau)g}\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}}\mathcal{F}(1_{H}^{G})(x),

\boxed{H_{\tau}(q):=\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}}\mathcal{F}(1_{H}^{G})(x).}

6.3.
Evaluating the sum
H_{\tau}

First, observe that
\mathcal{F}(1_{H}^{G})
is
G^{F}
-invariant; thus, instead of summing over all elements in
\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}
, we can sum over
G^{F}
-orbits
\mathfrak{O}_{x}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{F}
of elements of
x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}
. In other words,

H_{\tau}(q)=\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{O}\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}/G^{F}}|\mathfrak{O}|\mathcal{F}(1_{H}^{G})(\mathfrak{O}).

Next, we show that the size of the orbit
|\mathfrak{O}|
depends only on the type of
\mathfrak{O}
:

Lemma 39
.

If
\mathfrak{O}
has type
\tau=[L,\mathcal{N}]
, then

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{O}|=\frac{|G^{F}||\mathcal{N}|}{|L^{F}|}
.

Proof.

Let
x=x_{s}+x_{n}\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}
. Jordan decomposition is preserved under adjoint action; thus, for
g\in G
,
g\cdot x=g\cdot x_{s}+g\cdot x_{n}
is the Jordan decomposition of
g\cdot x
. It follows that
g\cdot x=x
if and only if
g\cdot x_{s}=x_{s}
and
g\cdot x_{n}=x_{n}
. In other words,

G_{x}=G_{x_{s}}\cap G_{x_{n}}=C_{G_{x_{s}}}(x_{n}).

Now if
x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}
has type
\tau=[L,\mathcal{N}]
, then

C_{G_{x_{s}}}(x_{n})=|L^{F}|/|\mathcal{N}|
. Thus, if
\mathfrak{O}
is the
G^{F}
-orbit of
x
, we conclude

|\mathfrak{O}|=\frac{|G^{F}|}{|G_{x}^{F}|}=\frac{|G^{F}|}{|C_{G_{x_{s}}}(x_{n})^{F}|}=\frac{|G^{F}||\mathcal{N}|}{|L^{F}|}.\qed

The above lemma implies

H_{\tau}(q)=\frac{|G^{F}||\mathcal{N}|}{|L^{F}|}\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{O}\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}/G^{F}}\mathcal{F}(1_{H}^{G})(\mathfrak{O}).

To proceed further, we need to better understand the values of the Fourier transform.

6.3.1.

Recall that Theorem
35
states
\mathcal{F}(1_{H}^{G})(x)=q^{|\Phi^{+}|}R_{\mathfrak{t}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(f_{H})(x)
and that
R_{\mathfrak{t}}^{\mathfrak{g}}
is defined via a sum over the set

\mathcal{A}(x):=\{g\in G^{F}\,|\,g\cdot x_{s}\in\mathfrak{t}^{F}\}.

If we assume
x_{s}\in\mathfrak{t}^{F}
, then

\mathcal{A}(x)=\bigcup_{w\in W}\dot{w}G_{x_{s}}^{F}=\bigsqcup_{w\in W/W_{x_{s}}}\dot{w}G_{x_{s}}^{F}.

Therefore, under this assumption, we have:

R_{\mathfrak{t}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(f_{H})(x)=\frac{Q_{T}^{G_{x_{s}}}(\varpi(x_{n}))}{|G_{x_{s}}^{F}|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{A}(x)}f_{H}(g\cdot x_{s})=\frac{Q_{T}^{G_{x_{s}}}(\varpi(x_{n}))}{|W_{x_{s}}|}\sum_{w\in W}f_{H}(w\cdot x_{s}).

6.3.2.

The above discussion implies that for a type
\tau=[L,\mathcal{N}]
, we have

H_{\tau}=\frac{|G^{F}||\mathcal{N}|}{|L^{F}|}\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}/G^{F}}{q^{|\Phi^{+}|}}R_{\mathfrak{t}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(f_{H})(x)=\frac{|G^{F}||\mathcal{N}|}{|L^{F}|}\frac{q^{|\Phi^{+}|}Q_{T}^{L}(\varpi(\mathcal{N}))}{|W(L)|}\sum_{w\in W}\,\,\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}/G^{F}}f_{H}(w\cdot x_{s}).

Here,
x
is an orbit representative satisfying
x_{s}\in\mathfrak{t}^{F}
.

6.3.3.

The element
x_{s}
is well-defined up to
W/W_{x_{s}}
-conjugacy, thus, we can now rewrite the above double sum as:

\sum_{w\in W}\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{g}^{F}_{\tau}/G^{F}}f_{H}(w\cdot x_{s})=\frac{|W(L)|}{|W|}\sum_{w\in W}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathfrak{t}^{F}\\
[W_{y}]=[W(L)]\end{subarray}}}f_{H}(w\cdot y)=\frac{|W(L)|}{|N_{W}(W(L))|}\sum_{w\in W}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathfrak{t}^{F}\\
W_{y}=W(L)\end{subarray}}}f_{H}(w\cdot y).

6.3.4.

For ease of notation, let

\beta_{L,H}(q):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathfrak{t}^{F}\\
W_{y}=W(L)\end{subarray}}f_{H}(y).

In view of the fact that
|[L]|=|W/N_{W}(W(L))|
(see §
4.2.3
), we conclude:

\boxed{H_{\tau}(q)=\frac{q^{|\Phi^{+}|}\,Q_{T}^{L}(\varpi(\mathcal{N}))\,|\mathcal{N}|\,|G^{F}|\,|[L]|}{|L^{F}|\,|W|}\sum_{w\in W}\beta_{L,w\cdot H}(q).}

Thus, the problem of computing
H_{\tau}
reduces to evaluating
\beta_{L,H}
. (Note that
\beta_{L,H}
is the additive analogue of
\alpha_{L,S}
introduced in §
4.2
.)

6.4.
Computing
\beta_{L,H}

Let

\beta_{L,H}^{\supseteq}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathfrak{t}^{F}\\
W_{t}\supseteq W(L)\end{subarray}}f_{H}(t)=\sum_{t\in(\mathfrak{t}^{F})^{W(L)}}f_{H}(t).

Let
\mu
denote the Möbius function on the poset of standard Levi subgroups of
G
ordered by inclusion.
4
4

4

Note that this poset is not the same as the one used in the previous section, but we have abused notation and denoted their Möbius functions by the same symbol.
Möbius inversion gives

\beta_{L,H}=\sum_{L^{\prime}\supseteq L}\mu(L,L^{\prime})\beta_{L^{\prime},H}^{\supseteq}

6.4.1.

We are reduced to computing the Möbius function and the functions
\beta_{L,H}^{\supseteq}
. The latter can be computed as follows. Consider the canonical quotient map from
\mathfrak{t}^{F}
to the group of

-coinvariants

f_{\mathfrak{l}}:\mathfrak{t}^{F}\rightarrow(\mathfrak{t}^{F})^{W(L)}=\mathfrak{t}^{F}/\mathfrak{t}^{F}\cap[\mathfrak{l}^{F},\mathfrak{l}^{F}].

Then Pontryagin duality implies

\beta_{L,H}^{\supseteq}=\begin{cases}|\mathfrak{t}^{F}/\mathfrak{t}^{F}\cap[\mathfrak{l}^{F},\mathfrak{l}^{F}]|&\textrm{if $H\in[\mathfrak{l}^{F},\mathfrak{l}^{F}]$;}\\

6.4.2.

Since

is generic, we have that

|\mathfrak{t}^{F}/\mathfrak{t}^{F}\cap[\mathfrak{g}^{F},\mathfrak{g}^{F}]|=|Z(\mathfrak{g})^{F}|.

Thus,

\boxed{\beta_{L,H}=q^{\dim(Z)}\mu(L,G).}

6.5.
Conclusion of the point count for once-punctured case

We have seen that

|{Y}^{F}|=\frac{|Z^{F}||\mathfrak{g}^{F}|^{g-1}}{|G^{F}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{g})}q^{gd(\tau)}H_{\tau}(q),

where

H_{\tau}(q)=q^{|\Phi^{+}|+\dim(Z)}Q_{T}^{L}(\varpi(\mathcal{N}))|\mathcal{N}|\mu(L,G)|[L]|\frac{|G^{F}|}{|L^{F}|}.

6.5.1.

It is shown in
[

that (under the assumption that

is good for

is a polynomial in

has a factor of type

such that the size of the Springer fibre

over the finite field

6.5.2.

Let

denote the counting polynomial for

. Let

\gamma_{G}:=\dim(Z)+(g-1)\dim(\mathfrak{g})+|\Phi^{+}|.

Define the rational function

f_{Y}(t):=f_{Z}(t)t^{\gamma_{G}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{g})}t^{gd(\tau)}\frac{f_{\tau}(t)}{s_{\tau}(t)}\mu(L,G)|[L]|\in\mathbb{Q}(t),

is the counting polynomial for

The above discussions imply

|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2r}})|=f_{Y}(q^{2r}),\qquad\forall r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

6.5.3.

By
[

, Lemma 2.11]
,
f_{Y}\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
.
Thus,

is polynomial count with counting polynomial

. This concludes the proof of Theorem
6
in the single punctured case.

6.6.
Multi-punctured case

Let
{Y}
be the additive character variety associated to an

-punctured surface group and
(H_{1},\ldots,H_{n})\in\mathfrak{t}^{n}
where each

is regular and the tuple is generic. Then a similar analysis as in the once-punctured case gives

|{Y}^{F}|=\frac{|Z^{F}||\mathfrak{g}^{F}|^{g-1}}{|G^{F}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{g})}q^{gd(\tau)}H_{\tau}(q),

where

H_{\tau}(q)=q^{n|\Phi^{+}|+\dim(Z)}\frac{|G^{F}|}{|L^{F}|}|\mathcal{N}|\Big(Q_{T}^{L}(\varpi(\mathcal{N}))\Big)^{n}\bigg(\frac{|W|}{|W(L)|}\bigg)^{n-1}|[L]|\,\mu(L,G).

6.6.1.

Now, let

\gamma_{G}:=\dim(Z)+(g-1)\dim(\mathfrak{g})+n|\Phi^{+}|.

Define the rational function
f_{Y}
by

f_{Y}(t):=f_{Z}(t)t^{\gamma_{G}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{g})}t^{gd(\tau)}\bigg(\frac{|W|}{|W(L)|}\bigg)^{n-1}\frac{(f_{\tau}(t))^{n}}{s_{\tau}(t)}\mu(L,G)|[L]|\in\mathbb{Q}(t).

Then

|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2r}})|=f_{Y}(q^{2r}),\qquad\forall r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

6.6.2.

The same argument as the previous subsection implies
Y\times_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
is polynomial count with counting polynomial
f_{Y}
.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
6
.

6.7.
Connectedness

Our aim now is to show that
\overline{Y}
is connected. We have shown that
\overline{Y}
has
E
-polynomial equal to
f_{Y}
. Thus, it suffices to show that
f_{Y}
is monic. The only
\mathfrak{g}
-type contributing to the leading term of
f_{Y}
is
\tau_{1}=[G,0]
. The coefficient of this term is determined by the Green polynomial
Q_{T}^{G}(1)
because

H_{\tau_{1}}=q^{n|\Phi^{+}|+\dim(Z)}Q_{T}^{G}(\varpi(0)).

The term
Q_{T}^{G}(\varpi(0))
is the Poincaré polynomial of
G
and is known to be monic. Thus, the polynomial
c(q):=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{g})}q^{gd(\tau)}H_{\tau}(q)
is monic. Now,

|Y(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2r}})|=\frac{|Z(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2r}})||\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2r}})|}{|G(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2r}})|}c(q)

is the quotient of two monic polynomials, therefore it is also monic.∎

6.8.
Euler characteristic

It follows from the explicit description of
f_{Y}
that

f_{Y}(1)=\frac{1}{r!}\frac{d^{r}}{dq^{r}}\bigg|_{q=1}\zeta(q),

where

is the semisimple rank of
G
and
\zeta
is the rational function

\zeta(q):=\frac{|\mathfrak{g}(k)|^{g-1}}{q^{|\Phi^{+}|}P_{W}(q)}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{g})}q^{gd(\tau)}H_{\tau}(q),

where
P_{W}(q)
is the Poincaré polynomial of
W
.
We use this formula to give examples of
\chi(\overline{Y})
in the next section.
