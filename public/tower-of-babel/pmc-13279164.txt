Social Disconnection, Genetic Risk, and the Incidence of Degenerative Valvular Heart Disease: A Population‐Based Cohort Study
Authors: Chenghui Cao, Cheng Wei, Wen Gong, Bilian Yu, Zhenfei Fang, Shenghua Zhou, Zhaowei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDegenerative valvular heart disease (VHD) is increasingly recognized as a major public health concern in aging populations. Although traditional cardiovascular risk factors have been extensively investigated, the role of loneliness and social isolation has remained largely overlooked in the context of valvular pathology.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study involving 462 917 participants from the UK Biobank, free of VHDs at baseline. Loneliness and social isolation were assessed using validated questionnaires.ResultsDuring a median follow‐up of 13.9 years, 11 003 cases of degenerative VHD were documented. Among them, there were 4280 cases of aortic valve stenosis and 4693 cases of mitral valve regurgitation. In fully adjusted models using no loneliness as reference, the highest loneliness level was associated with significantly increased risks of degenerative VHD (hazard ratio [HR], 1.19 [95% CI, 1.09–1.28]), aortic valve stenosis (HR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.06–1.37]) and mitral valve regurgitation (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.09–1.39]) (all P for trend <0.001). This association was consistently observed for degenerative VHD‐related events. In contrast, no significant associations were observed for social isolation. Additionally, loneliness was associated with an increased risk of degenerative VHD, independently of genetic background, but the highest risk occurs in individuals with both high genetic risk and elevated loneliness (P<0.001). In mediation analyses, unhealthy lifestyles were identified as significant mediators in the association between loneliness and incident degenerative VHD.ConclusionsLoneliness, rather than social isolation, was significantly associated with an increased risk of degenerative VHD. Our findings highlight novel opportunities for nonpharmacological interventions aimed at managing degenerative VHD.Keywords: aortic valve stenosis, loneliness, mitral valve regurgitation, social isolation, valvular heart diseaseSubject Categories: Mental Health, Risk Factors

Valvular heart disease (VHD) is becoming an increasingly significant global public health challenge. With the aging of the population, degenerative changes have overtaken other causes to become the predominant cause of VHD. 1 Epidemiological studies indicate that aortic valve stenosis (AS) and mitral valve regurgitation (MR) account for ∼50% and 25% of VHD cases, respectively. 2 However, the progression of degenerative VHD is insidious and often underrecognized in its early stages, resulting in delayed diagnosis. 3 Patients may develop heart failure (HF) or experience sudden cardiac death, severely compromising prognosis if not treated promptly. 4 Therefore, identifying key risk factors and accurately stratifying high‐risk populations are of critical clinical importance for facilitating early detection, slowing disease progression, and optimizing intervention strategies.

Although the clinical and lifestyle determinants of degenerative VHD have been extensively studied, 1 , 5 emerging evidence highlights the impact of psychosocial factors on cardiovascular outcomes, 6 , 7 drawing growing attention to loneliness and social isolation as potential contributors to degenerative VHD risk. Loneliness reflects the subjective, distressing perception of being socially disconnected, whereas social isolation refers to an objective deficiency in social connections and interactions. 8 In the United States, approximately one quarter of adults aged 65 years and older are socially isolated, and 43% of those aged 60 years and older report feeling lonely. 9 A substantial body of research has linked both loneliness and social isolation to increased incidence and mortality of various cardiovascular diseases (CVD), 6 , 8 potentially mediated through physiological mechanisms such as sympathetic nervous system activation, elevated inflammatory responses, and heightened oxidative stress. 10 , 11 Despite broad recognition of these psychosocial influences on cardiovascular health, their relationship with degenerative VHD risk remains poorly understood.

Emerging evidence suggests that loneliness may inhibit central oxytocin release, potentially promoting hepatic steatosis and atherosclerosis via brain‐liver axis, 12 pathologies potentially involved in the onset and progression of degenerative VHD. Importantly, the impact of loneliness and social isolation across the life course of degenerative VHD has not been systematically elucidated, hindering our understanding of the role of social relationships in its prevention and comprehensive management. Moreover, degenerative VHD results from a complex interplay between environmental exposures and genetic predisposition. However, it remains unclear whether loneliness and social isolation interact with genetic risk to influence susceptibility to degenerative VHD.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a population‐based cohort study using data from the UK Biobank to investigate the associations between loneliness, social isolation, and the risk and prognosis of degenerative VHD. This study aimed to characterize the relationship between social disconnection (loneliness and social isolation) and degenerative VHD and expand current understanding of potential intervention strategies. Additionally, we assessed the potential effects of adverse lifestyle factors and genetic risk on these associations.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from UK Biobank. These data are subject to access restrictions and cannot be shared by the authors. However, the UK Biobank resource is available to all eligible researchers upon application and approval.

Study Design and Population

The UK Biobank is a large, population‐based prospective cohort study that recruited >500 000 adult participants from 22 assessment centers across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. At enrollment, all participants provided written informed consent, completed touchscreen questionnaires, underwent physical measurements, and provided biological samples. The study was approved by the North West Multi‐Center Research Ethics Committee, and detailed information is publicly available on the UK Biobank website.

Of the 502 409 participants with available data, we excluded 1298 individuals who had withdrawn from the UK Biobank, as well as 34 565 participants with missing data on loneliness and social isolation assessments. An additional 3629 participants with VHD (including rheumatic, nonrheumatic, congenital, infective endocarditis, or Marfan syndrome‐related disease) at baseline were also excluded. The final analytical cohort comprised 462 917 adults. A flow chart with inclusion and exclusion criteria is shown in Figure S1.

Assessment of Loneliness and Social Isolation

In this study, the loneliness and social isolation scales were developed based on self‐reported questionnaire items from the UK Biobank at baseline. 6 Loneliness was assessed using 2 items derived from the revised University of California, Los Angeles loneliness scale 13 : (1) “Do you often feel lonely?” and (2) “How often are you able to confide in someone close to you?” Social isolation was assessed using 3 items that are similar to Berkman–Syme social network index 14 : (1) “Including yourself, how many people are living in your household?” (2) “How often do you visit friends or family, or have them visit you?” and (3) “Which of the following do you attend once a week or more often?” Scores for loneliness and social isolation ranged from 0 to 2 and 0 to 3, respectively, and were calculated by summing responses to the relevant items. High‐risk loneliness was defined as reporting feeling loneliness and never confiding in someone. High‐risk social isolation was defined as living alone, having social contact with friends or family less than once a month, and not participating in any social or leisure activity. A score of 2 points or greater indicates the presence of loneliness and social isolation. The loneliness and social isolation scales used in this study have been previously validated in multiple UK Biobank‐based investigations. 6 , 15 , 16 Further details on the scoring criteria for loneliness and social isolation are provided in Table S1.

Assessment of Degenerative Valvular Heart Disease

The primary outcome of interest comprised the incidence of any degenerative VHD, along with its 2 common subtypes: AS and MR. Secondary outcomes of interest included incidence of VHD and VHD‐related events, which defined as (1) valve replacement or cardiovascular death; and (2) major valvular or cardiovascular events comprising, valve replacement, cardiovascular death, HF, stroke, myocardial infarction, or coronary artery bypass grafting, whichever occurred first. 17 , 18 Deaths among patients with degenerative VHD were ascertained through linkage with the death registry. Follow‐up time was calculated from the baseline assessment date until the first occurrence of any of the following: diagnosis of degenerative VHD, death, or the censoring date (December 31, 2022). Prevalent cases of VHD (with onset at or before baseline) were identified based on these criteria as well as on relevant self‐reported history. Diagnoses were ascertained using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10): degenerative VHD (I34‐I37), AS (I35.0 and I35.2), and MR (I34.0). Detailed definitions and adjudication criteria for outcomes are provided in Table S2.

Assessment of Genetic Risk

Polygenic risk scores (PRS) 19 and self‐reported family history of CVD 5 were employed, based on previous studies, to evaluate participants' genetic risk. Genetic information and family history were obtained from blood samples and questionnaire data collected from participants during their first visit to a UK Biobank assessment center. The PRS for degenerative VHD and AS was constructed using genome‐wide sequencing data from Icelandic individuals who participated in various disease‐specific projects conducted by deCODE Genetics. 20 Details of the PRS construction are provided in Data S1. Due to limitations in the existing literature, we were unable to construct a PRS for MR. This analysis was restricted to participants who self‐identified as being of White racial background. A total of 15 independent genetic variants were selected for inclusion in the PRS, as detailed in Table S3. Participants were categorized into high and low genetic risk groups based on PRS distribution. 5 Information on family history of CVD was obtained by asking participants whether their parents or siblings had ever been diagnosed with the condition. Individuals with a positive family history were classified as having a high genetic risk. 21

Assessment of Covariates

To comprehensively evaluate the association between loneliness, social isolation, and degenerative VHD, we adjusted for 3 major categories of potential confounders: demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. 6 , 18 Demographic characteristics included age, sex, race or ethnicity, the Townsend Deprivation Index, and education. Lifestyle factors encompassed body mass index (BMI), alcohol intake frequency, smoking status, physical activity, sleep duration, and diet score. The diet score was derived from self‐reported dietary habits and reflected adherence to a healthy eating pattern, including intake frequencies of fruits, vegetables, fish, red meat, and processed meat products. Comorbidities included hypertension, atrial fibrillation (AF), dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes (T2D), all of which are cardiometabolic risk factors. These conditions were ascertained through a combination of self‐reported data and linked primary care and hospital inpatient electronic health records to enhance diagnostic accuracy and completeness. Covariates with <10% missing data were imputed with the median, and those with >10% missingness were converted to a categorical “Unknown” group. A detailed summary of the number and proportion of participants with missing covariates is provided in Table S4.

Statistical Analysis

Participants were categorized based on their loneliness and social isolation scores. In each analysis, individuals with a score of 0 on the respective scale were used as the reference group. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means ± SD or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate.

Cumulative risk curves were plotted by the category of social disconnection using Kaplan–Meier methods. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for the association between loneliness or social isolation phenotypes and incident degenerative VHDs. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violation was found (global test P>0.05). Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and race or ethnicity; Model 2 was further adjusted for Townsend Deprivation Index, education, BMI, alcohol intake frequency, smoking status, physical activity, sleep duration, and diet score. Model 3 included additional adjustments for hypertension, AF, dyslipidemia, and T2D. To assess linear trends, loneliness or social isolation scores were treated as continuous variables in multivariable models. To investigate the potential role of genetic predisposition in the relationship between psychosocial factors and degenerative VHD, joint analyses were conducted using PRS and family history of CVD.

We further performed stratified analyses based on the main models to examine potential effect modifications by sex, age, BMI, education, Townsend Deprivation Index, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and T2D. A series of sensitivity analyses were also conducted to test the robustness of our findings: (1) to minimize reverse causality, we excluded degenerative VHD cases diagnosed within the first 2 years of follow‐up; (2) to address potential bias from missing data, participants with incomplete covariate information were excluded; (3) to further test the robustness of results, missing covariates were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations; (4) Fine–Gray competing risk model treating death as a competing event was used to reassess the associations; and (5) propensity score matching based on covariates from the multivariable Cox models was performed to balance baseline characteristics between groups (ratio=1:4, caliper=0.2). As a further step, mediation analyses were conducted to identify potential mediators 22 of the associations between loneliness and degenerative VHD. We assessed several lifestyle and inflammatory factors as potential mediators of the association between loneliness and the risk of degenerative VHD. Mediation analyses were performed using the regression‐based approach implemented in the CMAverse R package to decompose the total observed effect of loneliness into direct and indirect effects. This method is well established and has been widely applied in mediation analyses of survival data. 23 , 24 The specific methodological procedures are described in Data S1.

All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2‐sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data preparation and transformation were carried out using the “tidyverse” suite (v2.0.0), and the “gtsummary” package (v2.4.0) was employed to generate the baseline table. We fitted Cox proportional hazards models using the “survival” package (v3.8–3). For supplementary analyses, we used multiple packages: “MatchIt” (v4.7.2) for propensity score matching; the “cmprsk” package (v2.2–12) to account for competing risks; “interaction” (v0.1.7) for additive interaction analysis; “jstable” (v1.3.19) for subgroup analyses; “mice” (v3.19.0) for multiple imputation; and the “CMAverse” package (v0.1.0) for mediation analyses.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

This study included 462 917 participants with a median age of 58 years (interquartile range, 50–63). Of these, 209 830 (45%) were male and 440 211 (95%) were White. Based on loneliness scale scores, 331 946 (72%) scored 0, indicating minimal loneliness; 108 822 (23%) scored 1, and 22 149 (5%) scored 2, showing higher loneliness levels (Table 1). Participants with higher loneliness scores tended to be male and to have lower educational attainment. They also exhibited less favorable lifestyle profiles, including high rates of current smoking, physical inactivity, unhealthy sleep patterns, and poor dietary scores. Additionally, these individuals had a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and T2D (Table 1).

During a median follow‐up period of 13.9 years, we identified 11 003 incident cases of degenerative VHD, including 4280 cases of AS and 4693 cases of MR (Figure S1). Compared with participants without degenerative VHD, AS, or MR, affected individuals were older, more likely to be male, and had a higher BMI and lower educational attainment. They also exhibited a higher prevalence of previous smoking, irregular sleep patterns, and comorbidities such as hypertension, AF, dyslipidemia, and T2D (Table S5).

Associations Between Social Disconnection and Degenerative Valvular Heart Disease

The Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated significant separation in cumulative risk by social disconnection (Figure S2). Compared with participants with the lowest loneliness score (index=0), those with the highest loneliness score (index=2) had a significantly increased risk of degenerative VHD (HR, 1.19 [95% CI, 1.09–1.28]), AS (HR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.06–1.37]), and MR (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.09–1.39]) (Table 2). When analyzed as a binary variable, the associations remained consistent, with significantly increased risks of incident degenerative VHD (HR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.05–1.25]), AS (HR, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.04–1.33]), and MR (HR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.07–1.36]) (Table S7). A similar dose–response relationship was observed when loneliness was analyzed as a continuous variable (P for trend <0.001) (Table 2). However, after full adjustment for covariates, social isolation was not significantly associated with incident degenerative VHD, AS, or MR (Tables S7 and S8). Furthermore, joint analysis of loneliness and social isolation revealed no significant additive interaction effect (Table S9). In separate analyses of the 5 social disconnection components, feeling loneliness and inability to confide were associated with a higher risk of VHD, AS, and MR. The results were as follows: for feeling loneliness, degenerative VHD (HR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.05–1.16]), AS (HR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.02–1.19]), and MR (HR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.04–1.21]). For inability to confide, degenerative VHD (HR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.05–1.15]), AS (HR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.03–1.20]), and MR (HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.00–1.16]) (Table S10). In contrast, living alone, lack of social support, and lack of social activity showed no significant associations (Table S10).

Impact of Loneliness on Subsequent Valvular and Cardiovascular Outcomes

During follow‐up, clinical outcomes were observed across different valvular conditions. Among patients with degenerative VHD, 26% (2899) experienced valve replacement or cardiovascular death, and 46% (5064) developed major valvular or cardiovascular events (Table 3). Similarly, for patients with AS, the rates of valve replacement or cardiovascular death and major valvular or cardiovascular events were 42% (1811) and 55% (2338) respectively, and for patients with MR, these rates were 19% (896) and 45% (2130) respectively (Table 3). Importantly, Cox proportional hazards models showed that higher loneliness severity was associated with an increased risk of clinical outcomes. After full adjustment, patients with the highest loneliness scores (index=2) were associated with higher risks compared with those with minimal loneliness, including degenerative VHD (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.09–1.38]), AS (HR, 1.19 [95% CI, 1.01–1.42]), and MR (HR, 1.30 [95% CI, 1.09–1.55]) (Table 3).

Associations Between Loneliness and Valvular Heart Disease in Different Genetic Risk Backgrounds

Based on established methods, 5 we evaluated genetic risk using 2 approaches: PRS and family history of CVD. The PRS showed significant associations with risk of incident degenerative VHD (HR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.08–1.15]) and AS (HR, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.22–1.36]), and family history was associated with degenerative VHD (HR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.17–1.25]), AS (HR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.17–1.31]), and MR (HR, 1.19 [95% CI, 1.13–1.26]) (Tables S11 and S12). The association between loneliness levels and VHD risk remained significant across all genetic risk strata, with no significant interactions observed (PRS interaction P=0.252; family history interaction P=0.778). For AS, loneliness‐associated risk was primarily evident in individuals with low genetic predisposition (low PRS or no family history of CVD), though no significant interactions were detected (PRS interaction P=0.383; family history interaction P=0.122). The association between loneliness and MR was apparent only in those with family history, and the interaction did not reach statistical significance (P=0.099). These results demonstrated the association between loneliness and incident degenerative VHD was not modified by genetic susceptibility. Nevertheless, joint analyses revealed that individuals with both high genetic risk and elevated loneliness levels had a substantially higher disease risk compared with those with low genetic risk and low loneliness levels (Figure 1, Figures S3 and S4).

Subgroup, Sensitivity, and Mediation Analyses

No statistically significant interactions were observed between loneliness and the stratification variables (sex, age, BMI, education level, Townsend Deprivation Index, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or T2D) in subgroup analyses (all P for interaction >0.05; Table S13). To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses. First, exclusion of participants diagnosed with degenerative VHD, AS, or MR during the initial 2 years of follow‐up did not substantially alter the associations for either main analyses or clinical outcomes (Table S14). Second, after removing participants with missing covariates instead of imputing the missing values, the results remained consistent (Table S15). Third, the results remained consistent after multiple imputation (Table S16). Fourth, Fine–Gray competing risk analyses accounting for mortality demonstrated persistent significant associations between loneliness and incident degenerative VHD, AS, and MR (Table S17). Fifth, propensity score matching effectively balanced baseline characteristics (standardized mean difference<0.1; Table S6), and loneliness maintaining significant associations (Table S18). Additionally, our mediation analysis revealed that adverse lifestyle factors, including high BMI, current smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, unhealthy sleep patterns, and physical inactivity, significantly mediated the observed relationship between loneliness and degenerative VHD, accounting for 1.6% to 15.4% of the association (Figure 2). DISCUSSION

Our analysis of this prospective UK Biobank cohort (N>400 000) demonstrates several important associations. First, loneliness was independently associated with an increased risk of incident degenerative VHD, AS, and MR, irrespective of genetic susceptibility. Furthermore, loneliness was linked to poorer clinical outcomes in patients with established valvular disease. Notably, although loneliness exhibited significant effects, our data did not support similar associations for social isolation, whether examined independently or in combination with loneliness through joint analyses. Mediation analyses revealed that adverse lifestyle factors partially explained the observed associations between loneliness and degenerative VHD.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Social isolation and loneliness have emerged as pressing public health concerns, with well‐documented associations with various adverse health outcomes. Robust epidemiological evidence links these conditions to increased mortality, 25 , 26 CVD incidence, 7 , 27 T2D incidence, 28 and cognitive impairment development. 29 , 30 Within cardiovascular research specifically, social disconnection has been independently associated with higher incidence and worse prognosis of coronary heart disease, stroke, HF, AF, and venous thromboembolism. 15 , 16 , 31 , 32 , 33 Despite this substantial evidence, the relationship among social isolation, loneliness, and degenerative VHD remains poorly understood. Our study addresses this critical knowledge gap as the first large‐scale prospective cohort investigation to demonstrate a significant association between loneliness and increased risk of degenerative VHD, AS, and MR. In this study, participants with the highest loneliness scores showed 19%, 21%, and 23% greater risks of developing degenerative VHD, AS, and MR, respectively. Furthermore, among patients with existing valvular disease, more severe loneliness was associated with elevated risks of a subsequent valvular or cardiovascular event: 23% for degenerative VHD, 19% for AS, and 30% for MR.

Interestingly, we found no significant association between social isolation and degenerative VHD in this study. This differential pattern aligns with existing literature on cardiovascular outcomes. For instance, data from the English Longitudinal Study of Aging demonstrated that loneliness independently predicted the risk of CVD, with an odds ratio of 1.27, whereas social isolation showed no significant association. 34 It is important to recognize that, although loneliness and social isolation are interrelated, they represent distinct constructs with potentially divergent health impacts. 32 Loneliness reflects the subjective, distressing perception of being socially disconnected, whereas social isolation refers to an objective deficiency in social connections and interactions. 8 Importantly, loneliness may exert more profound psychological stress effects than social isolation, as it can persist independently of one's actual social network size or duration of isolation. 35 , 36 Our component‐specific analysis revealed that among the 5 examined items, feelings of loneliness and inability to confide showed the strongest associations with incident VHD, AS, and MR. However, findings across different cardiovascular diseases are not entirely consistent. Liang et al. found that both loneliness and social isolation were associated with an increased risk of HF. 15 This difference may reflect those diseases vary in their sensitivity to psychosocial factors. 37 Case ascertainment may similarly be a critical factor underlying these discrepancies. For instance, VHD identified through ICD‐10 codes mainly includes moderate‐to‐severe cases, 2 which limits the number of events and reduces the ability to detect associations. Further research is needed to explore how loneliness and social isolation affect different cardiovascular outcomes.

Previous studies have reported that genetic predisposition and loneliness are independently associated with an increased incidence of HF, 15 AF, 33 and T2D. 38 However, the interplay between genetic risk and loneliness in degenerative VHD remains unexplored. Our findings first demonstrate that loneliness and genetic susceptibility are independently associated with an elevated risk of degenerative VHD, with no significant interaction observed. Nevertheless, compared with individuals with both low genetic risk and low loneliness levels, those with both high genetic risk and high loneliness levels show significantly increased risk of degenerative VHD. The results emphasize that psychosocial well‐being, particularly loneliness, represents an important modifiable risk factor that should be addressed, particularly in individuals at high genetic risk for degenerative VHD.

Possible Mechanisms

We propose several plausible mechanisms that may explain the observed association between loneliness and incident degenerative VHDs. First, loneliness predisposes individuals to depression, 39 , 40 creating a psychological barrier that reduces both social support‐seeking behaviors and health care use. This maladaptive pattern ultimately leads to suboptimal management of risk factors and chronic conditions that contribute to degenerative VHD development. Second, chronic low‐grade inflammation plays a well‐established role in degenerative VHD pathogenesis. 41 , 42 Loneliness has been shown to promote hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation and sympathetic nervous system activation, leading to elevated proinflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress. 11 , 43 However, our mediation analysis found no significant mediation effects for the assessed inflammatory factors, including C‐reactive protein, white blood cell count, and neutrophil count. These biomarkers primarily reflect acute or systemic inflammation, which may not capture the chronic, low‐grade inflammatory processes characteristic of degenerative VHD and implicated in loneliness. Therefore, future studies should employ more sensitive and specific inflammatory panels to elucidate this potential link. Third, valvular calcification has been recognized as a marker of systemic atherosclerosis. 44 Ko et al. recently identified that social disconnection induces dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis in mice through a brain‐liver axis mechanism involving hypothalamic oxytocin signaling to hepatocytes. 12 In line with this, we observed a higher prevalence of dyslipidemia among individuals with loneliness (18% versus 14%). Metabolic disorders, including dyslipidemia and T2D, are recognized as independent risk factors for degenerative VHD. 45 , 46 These metabolic issues may represent key mechanistic links between loneliness and degenerative VHD within a broader framework of metabolic comorbidities. Finally, adverse lifestyle factors may function as potential mediators in this association. Epidemiological evidence consistently shows that adverse lifestyle behaviors promote chronic systemic inflammation, 47 and Jia et al observed individuals with healthier lifestyle patterns exhibited a lower risk of developing VHD. 5 Our mediation analyses indicate that modifiable lifestyle factors (including smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep patterns, and physical activity) partially account for the observed associations between loneliness and degenerative VHD risk. These findings suggest that targeted interventions addressing lifestyle behaviors may be particularly beneficial for VHD prevention, especially in individuals experiencing loneliness.

Clinical Implications

Our findings underscore the urgent need to adopt a biopsychosocial framework for the prevention and management of degenerative VHD. Although traditionally regarded as a mechanical and age‐related degenerative process, degenerative VHD may also be shaped by psychosocial stressors that act through systemic inflammatory, neuroendocrine, and metabolic pathways. Our results suggest that addressing social disconnection could help delay disease progression, postpone surgical interventions such as valve replacement, and ultimately reduce the long‐term clinical and economic burden of VHD. Clinicians and policymakers must recognize that alleviating social disconnection is not only a societal imperative but also a degenerative VHD necessity.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this study represents the first large‐scale prospective cohort study to comprehensively examine the relationships between social disconnection and incident degenerative VHD. The strengths of our study include its substantial sample size, extended follow‐up duration, prospective design, rigorous covariate adjustment, and extensive sensitivity analyses. All these factors enhance the validity of our conclusions. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the observational nature of our study precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between social connection and degenerative VHD. Second, the brief measures of social isolation and loneliness used in UK Biobank lack formal validation, although they have demonstrated utility in prior research. 38 Third, as >90% of participants were of White European ancestry, future studies should validate these findings in more diverse populations. Fourth, ICD‐10 codes alone cannot assess the severity of valvular disorders (stenosis/regurgitation), 2 restricting our analysis of how loneliness affects disease progression. Finally, given that loneliness and most mediators were measured concurrently, our findings should be interpreted as supportive of theoretical pathways linking loneliness to degenerative VHD via lifestyle factors rather than as definitive evidence of mediation. Future longitudinal studies incorporating repeated assessments, more diverse populations, and multiethnic genetic analyses are needed to address these limitations and to further validate the temporal dynamics and causality of these mechanisms.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study indicates that loneliness, rather than social isolation, was significantly associated with a higher risk of degenerative VHD. These associations were independent of genetic predisposition and were partially mediated by adverse lifestyles. These findings underscore the importance of screening for degenerative VHD in individuals experiencing loneliness.
