Multi-omics analysis reveals circadian disruption of rumen microbiota and serum metabolites in Tibetan sheep under transport stress
Authors: Wenhui Tang, Penghui Wang, Ting Jiang, Ningna Chen, Maisui Xue, Yale Chen, Rui Pang, Juanxiang Zhang, Ru Wang, Qiangjun Wang, Jiahong Chen, Chunhuan Ren, Yafeng Huang, Zijun Zhang, Xiao Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOff-site fattening of Tibetan sheep is a key strategy to mitigate the effects of high-altitude grassland degradation and winter forage scarcity, promoting sustainable development in plateau animal husbandry. However, transport stress (TS) presents a significant challenge to realizing its benefits. The mechanism by which TS affects the health of Tibetan sheep by regulating rumen microbial and serum metabolite rhythmic changes remains unclear.MethodsThis study selected six healthy male Tibetan sheep, aged seven months and of comparable body weight, for the transport experiment. Blood and rumen fluid samples were collected at four-hour intervals during 24-hour periods pre-transport (CON) and post-transport (TS) for serum indicators, serum metabolome, and rumen microbiome analyses.ResultThe results showed that TS significantly increased serum concentrations of cortisol (COR), melatonin (MT), lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP), serum amyloid A (SAA), and non-esterified fatty acid (NEFA), while significantly decreasing glucose (GLU), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the circadian rhythms of COR, MT, LBP, SAA, NEFA, and GPx were significantly disrupted (ADJ.P < 0.05). TS reduced the proportion of rumen microbial circadian rhythms from 3.46% to 1.99%, with Prevotella, Butyrivibrio, and Ruminococcus losing their circadian rhythmicity in the TS phase (ADJ.P < 0.05). Additionally, TS decreased the proportion of circadian rhythm-regulated serum metabolites from 51.74% to 29.51%. In the TS phase, rhythmically regulated metabolites, including 3’,5’-cyclic AMP, fumarate, dopamine, glutathione, and angiotensin (1–7), were enriched in pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, retinol metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism. Multi-omics analyses demonstrated significant correlations between Ruminococcus and energy metabolites (malic acid, 3’,5’-cyclic AMP, fumarate, NEFA), and between Butyrivibrio, Anaeroplasma, and inflammatory/antioxidant markers (glutathione, SAA, LBP). In conclusion, this study reveals that TS induces a homeostatic imbalance in Tibetan sheep by disrupting the circadian rhythms of both the rumen microbiota and host metabolism. These findings provide a theoretical basis and molecular targets for developing interventions to alleviate TS in livestock.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05109-1.

Introduction

Tibetan sheep (Ovis aries), a keystone species in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau’s pastoral ecosystem, play an essential role in sustaining alpine grassland ecology and supporting herder livelihoods [1]. However, traditional livestock practices coupled with harsh environmental conditions have created a dual challenge of seasonal nutritional deficiencies and ecological overload, hindering the industry’s sustainable development [2, 3]. In this context, off-site fattening has emerged as a promising strategy. By facilitating regional coordination and reallocating spatial resources, this model enables cross-latitude integration of ecological conservation and economic benefits, thereby improving overall production efficiency. Previous studies by our team have demonstrated that winter fattening of Tibetan sheep in southern areas, such as the Jianghuai watershed region, resulted in excellent adaptation, with average daily gains up to 220 g/day and significant improvements in carcass meat quality [4–6]. Nevertheless, this system inevitably involves long-distance transport, during which stress responses can negatively affect animal physiology and health.

Transport stress (TS) refers to the stress response in animals during transportation due to multiple factors such as hunger, vibration, crowding, and noise [7, 8]. TS activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which triggers a surge in the secretion of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and cortisol (COR) [9]. Studies have demonstrated that TS significantly increases stress biomarkers such as COR and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) in chickens, while decreasing total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) [10]. In steers, TS elevates concentrations of serum inflammatory markers like lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) and serum amyloid A (SAA), suggesting that TS induces systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [11]. Furthermore, TS has been shown to increase serum glucose (GLU) and non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) in dromedary camels, triggering energy mobilization during transport [12]. Beyond physiological changes, TS also significantly impacts the gastrointestinal microbiota. For example, in weaned piglets, TS has been shown to increase COR levels and decrease the relative abundance of Lactobacillus [13]. In cattle, TS increases COR levels and alters the rumen microbiome, increasing the abundance of Firmicutes and decreasing Bacteroidetes and Prevotella [14]. A reduction in rumen microbial diversity has also been observed in sheep under TS, marked by a decrease in cellulolytic bacteria and an increase in potential pathogens [15]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that TS profoundly influences hormone secretion, triggers inflammation and oxidative stress, and disrupts rumen ecology and microbial composition. Although the effects of TS have been investigated in various species, including pigs, chickens, cattle, sheep, and camels, most research has focused on static comparisons of pre- and post-transport conditions. Consequently, the dynamic fluctuations in these physiological and microbial parameters during the critical 24-hour period surrounding transport remain poorly understood.

The body’s stress system and circadian rhythm system interact through the HPA axis, enabling adaptive responses to environmental changes [16, 17]. Glucocorticoids, which are secreted upon activation of the HPA axis, are pivotal in coordinating the functions of both the circadian and stress systems [18]. Evidence has demonstrated that stress can disrupt cortisol concentration and circadian rhythm, which has been corroborated by relevant studies in growing pigs [19], mice [20], and Hu sheep [21]. Furthermore, there are significant interactions between the host’s endogenous circadian clock and the rumen microbial ecosystem, which are critical for maintaining rumen metabolic homeostasis [22]. Studies show that approximately 15% or more of ruminal microbial taxa exhibit diurnal fluctuations regulated by host circadian hormones, such as COR and melatonin (MT) [23, 24]. Research reveals that the abundance of bacteria exhibiting diurnal rhythms, such as Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes in the dairy cow rumen, is closely linked to L-Tyrosine levels [25]. Sudo et al. [26] demonstrated that germ-free mice exhibited increased glucocorticoid levels after stimulation, and these levels returned to baseline following colonization with Bifidobacterium infantis. This indicates that external stressors disrupt normal circadian hormone secretion, thereby disturbing the composition and circadian rhythms of rumen microorganisms [27, 28].

While existing research has explored the complex interrelationships among stress responses, circadian rhythms, and rumen microbiota, these systems have often been studied in isolation. Comprehensive investigations have been predominantly conducted in model animals such as mice. Currently, the effects of long-distance TS on serum metabolites and rumen microbial circadian rhythms in sheep, particularly Tibetan sheep, are not fully elucidated. Therefore, the present study sought to characterize the impact of long-distance transport (30 h, 2000 km) on the serum metabolome and rumen microbial circadian rhythms in Tibetan sheep. The findings will provide a novel circadian perspective on TS-induced alterations in host serum and microbiota, thereby offering new insights and a theoretical framework for developing strategies to mitigate TS.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

A cohort of 150 healthy Tibetan sheep was transported from Qinghai Bakatai Farming and Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd. to the Jianghuai Watershed Comprehensive Experiment Station of Anhui Agricultural University. From this cohort, six healthy 7-month-old Tibetan sheep, with an average initial body weight of 22.25 ± 0.5 kg, were randomly selected as experimental subjects. A within-subject (repeated-measures) design was employed, comprising pre-transport (CON) and post-transport (TS) phases. All Tibetan sheep were housed under 12 h light/dark conditions, with zeitgeber time (ZT; ZT0 set at 06:00). The transport procedure involved continuous 30-hour long-distance road transport, during which feed and water were withheld, and no anti-stress medications were administered. Upon arrival, the sheep were given free access to water but were withheld from feed until sample collection was completed. Blood and rumen fluid samples were collected over a 24-hour time course at 4-hour intervals during both the pre-transport phase and on the first day after arrival (Fig. 1).

Measurement of core physiological indicators

The rectal temperature of Tibetan sheep was measured using an electronic thermometer (Delta Track, Pleasanton, CA, USA) in a quiet state both pre-transport and approximately 30 min after arrival at the destination. Heart rate was determined via cardiac auscultation, recording the number of beats within 60 s. Blood oxygen saturation was monitored using a pulse oximeter.

Tibetan sheep sampling of serum and ruminal fluid

Blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4℃ to obtain serum. Rumen fluid was collected using a rumen fluid sampler (Colibri Pastoral, Wuhan, China) with a syringe [29]. After filtration through sterile gauze, the pH was immediately measured using a portable pH meter (Model PHBJ-260, Shanghai Ruike Co., Ltd.). The remaining rumen fluid was transferred to sterile 50 mL centrifuge tubes and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent analysis.

Measurement of serum physiological and biochemical indicators

Serum concentrations of COR, MT, LBP, and SAA were determined using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd). Serum levels of GLU, NEFA, T-AOC, and GPx were measured using standard kits provided by Jiangsu Aidisheng Biological Technology Co., Ltd. All measurements were performed according to the respective kit instructions.

Extraction and sequencing of ruminal microbial 16S rRNA

All sequencing procedures were conducted by BioMarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). DNA was extracted from rumen fluid samples using the Magnetic Soil and Fecal DNA Kit (Tianxing Biochemical Technology, Beijing, China). DNA quality and concentration were assessed by 1.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and a Thermo NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), respectively. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16 S rRNA gene were amplified with primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) in a 10 µL PCR system [29]. Purified PCR products underwent paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., USA). FLASH (v1.2.11) was employed for read assembly, and chimeras were removed using UCHIME (v8.1). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% similarity using USEARCH (v10.0) and taxonomically annotated against the SILVA database. Alpha- and beta-diversity analyses were performed with QIIME2 (v2023.9) on the BMK Cloud platform (www.biocloud.net).

Metabolomic analysis of serum

A 100 µL aliquot of serum was processed by adding 500 µL of a methanol: acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) extraction solvent. After vortexing for 30 s and ice-water bath sonication for 10 min, the mixture was kept at -20 °C for 1 h, then centrifuged (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 15 min). A 500 µL portion of the supernatant was transferred, dried, and reconstituted in 160 µL of acetonitrile: water (1:1, v/v). The sample was again vortexed, sonicated, and centrifuged; 120 µL of the final supernatant was transferred to a vial for LC-MS/MS analysis (injection volume: 10 µL). Non-targeted metabolomic profiling was performed on a Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS UPLC system coupled to a Waters Xevo G2-XS QToF mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, USA). Data were acquired using MassLynx V4.2 software (Waters) and processed with Progenesis QI (Waters) for peak alignment, normalization, and identification. Metabolites were annotated using the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net) and public databases such as KEGG. Differential metabolites were defined by fold change (FC) > 2, FDR < 0.05, and variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.

Statistical analysis

For physiological parameters, intergroup differences were assessed using Student’s t-test, while diurnal variations in serum biochemical indices were examined by repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. Differences in rumen microbial communities at the phylum and genus levels were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by the Wilcoxon test, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons via the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Statistically significant microbial biomarkers were identified through LEfSe analysis (LDA ≥ 3.0, P < 0.05). To evaluate diurnal rhythms in microbes and metabolites, the non-parametric Jonckheere–Terpstra–Kendall (JTK) Cycle algorithm was applied to assess rhythmic significance and amplitude [30]. Temporal expression patterns of rhythmic microbes and metabolites were clustered via the Mfuzz package implementing a fuzzy c-means algorithm [31]. The integrative analysis of multi-omics data was performed utilizing the mixOmics package in R software [32]. Associations between parameters were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation. All figures were generated with GraphPad Prism (v 9.5).

Results

TS alters physiological indicators, circadian rhythms of serum indicators, and rumen fluid pH in Tibetan sheep

Physiological indicators showed that the rectal temperature of Tibetan sheep remained within the normal range (38.5–40 °C) before and after transport, and the values were comparable between the two phases (P > 0.05). Heart rate in the TS phase was significantly lower than in the CON phase, while the blood oxygen saturation was significantly higher than in the CON phase (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A-C). The TS phase exhibited significantly increased rumen fluid pH and serum concentrations of COR, MT, LBP, SAA, and NEFA, while serum GLU, T-AOC, and GPx concentrations were significantly lower than those in the CON phase (P < 0.05). From the perspective of day and night analysis, serum COR, LBP, and NEFA concentrations were significantly lower at night before transport compared to daytime, while MT concentration was significantly higher. After transport, serum MT and NEFA concentrations at night were significantly higher than during the day, while COR, GLU, TAOC, and GPx concentrations were significantly lower. During the daytime phase, post-transport daytime serum concentrations of COR, MT, LBP, SAA, NEFA, and pH were significantly elevated compared to pre-transport levels, while GPx concentration was significantly reduced. During the nighttime phase, post-transport nighttime serum concentrations of COR, MT, LBP, SAA, NEFA, and pH were significantly elevated compared to pre-transport levels, while GLU, TAOC, and GPx concentrations were significantly reduced. Furthermore, serum COR, MT, and LBP concentrations in Tibetan sheep exhibited circadian rhythms before and after TS. Interestingly, SAA and GPx concentrations demonstrated significant circadian rhythms after TS, while disrupting the circadian rhythm of NEFA concentrations (Bonferroni-adjusted P-value, ADJ.P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D-L). Serum COR, LBP, and NEFA concentrations were significantly lower at night before transport compared to daytime, while MT concentration was significantly higher. After transport, serum MT and NEFA concentrations were significantly higher at night compared to daytime, while COR, GLU, TAOC, and GPx concentrations were significantly lower. During the daytime post-transport, serum concentrations of COR, MT, LBP, SAA, NEFA, and pH were significantly elevated compared to pre-transport levels, while GPx concentration was significantly reduced. During the nighttime post-transport, serum concentrations of COR, MT, LBP, SAA, NEFA, and pH were significantly elevated compared to pre-transport levels, while GLU, TAOC, and GPx concentrations were significantly reduced (Fig. 2D-L). Transportation and circadian rhythm significantly affected physiological parameters in Tibetan sheep, with T-AOC concentration being influenced solely by transportation. The interaction between transportation and circadian rhythm significantly affected all physiological parameters except T-AOC and GPx (Table S1).

TS alters the composition and circadian rhythm of rumen microbes in Tibetan sheep

Compared with the CON phase, key alpha-diversity indices (Shannon, Chao1, and ACE) were significantly reduced in the TS phase (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed that 15.06% and 12.26% of the variance were accounted for by PC1 and PC2, respectively. PCoA plots of 16 S rRNA sequences from Tibetan sheep rumen bacteria before and after transport revealed distinct clustering patterns (Fig. 3B). At the phylum level, Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes maintained their status as the predominant phyla throughout the experimental period (pre- and post-transport). In the rumen of the TS phase, the relative abundances of Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Cyanobacteria were significantly lower than in the CON phase, while those of Bacteroidetes and Spirochaetota were significantly higher than in the CON phase (FDR < 0.05). At the genus level, Prevotella was the dominant genus. Compared with the CON phase, TS significantly reduced the relative abundance of Prevotellaceae_UCG_003, Ruminococcus, and NK4A214_group in Tibetan sheep, while significantly increasing the relative abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Butyrivibrio, Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, and Anaeroplasma (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 3C, Table S2-S3). With an LDA score > 3.0 as the threshold, 36 genera exhibited significant enrichment when comparing the CON and TS phases. Among these, 20 genera, including Ruminococcus, Quinella, Streptococcus, and Pseudobutyrivibrio, were significantly enriched in the CON phase, while Butyrivibrio, Saccharofermentans, Lachnospira, the UCG_003 genus of the Prevotellaceae family, and Alloprevotella were significantly enriched in the TS phase (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3D).

Analysis of rhythmically oscillating microbes (ADJ.P < 0.05) revealed that those in the CON phase primarily belonged to the phyla Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes. These rhythmic taxa lost their circadian oscillations in the TS phase (Fig. 4A-B). At the phylum level, rhythmic patterns were distinct between phases. In the CON phase, significant circadian rhythms were observed in Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Synergistota. In contrast, Desulfobacterota exhibited rhythmicity exclusively in the TS phase. Notably, Spirochaetota maintained its circadian rhythm both before and after transport (ADJ.P < 0.05) (Fig. 4E).

Genus-level analysis revealed that the proportions of microbiota exhibiting significant circadian rhythms were 3.46% in the CON phase and 1.99% in the TS phase (ADJ.P < 0.05). The rhythmic microbes in the CON phase peaked predominantly during the daytime, whereas those in the TS phase peaked at night (Fig. 4C-D). TS resulted in the loss of circadian rhythmicity in Prevotella, Butyrivibrio, and Ruminococcus, whereas Anaeroplasma acquired a significant rhythm post-transport. Furthermore, TS disrupted circadian rhythms in Treponema, Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group, Pseudomonas, Candidatus Omnitrophus, and Sphingomonas, which also exhibited significant alterations in their diurnal oscillation patterns and expression levels (ADJ.P < 0.05) (Fig. 4F).

TS alters the diurnal pattern of serum metabolites in Tibetan sheep

PCA and OPLS-DA analysis of serum metabolites revealed significant differences in metabolic profiles between the CON and TS phases (Fig. 5A-B). Volcano plots indicated increased abundance of 265 metabolites and decreased abundance of 711 metabolites in serum (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 5C). KEGG analysis revealed that the CON phase was primarily enriched in pathways including D-amino acid metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism. The TS phase was mainly enriched in pathways related to purine metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, lysine biosynthesis, porphyrin metabolism, and histidine metabolism (Fig. 5D).

Analysis of 3883 serum metabolites via the JTK method indicated that 43.81% (1701 metabolites) in the CON phase and 28.61% (1111 metabolites) in the TS phase exhibited circadian rhythmicity (ADJ.P < 0.05), respectively, with 699 metabolites common to both phases (Fig. 6A). Polar plots combined with a heatmap demonstrated that rhythmic metabolites in both phases were dispersed across the 24 h (Fig. 6B-C). Fuzzy c-means clustering identified three temporal pattern clusters in the CON and TS phases, reflecting distinct kinetic profiles of these metabolites (Fig. 6D). Further examination identified Carboxylic acids and derivatives, Organooxygen compounds, Fatty Acyls, and Prenol lipids grouped as compounds that co-clustered in both conditions (Fig. 6E). Pathway analysis revealed that in the CON phase, clusters 1–3 were primarily associated with Cocaine addiction, D-Amino acid metabolism, and Steroid biosynthesis (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6F). In the TS phase, clusters 1–3 were mainly enriched in Retinol metabolism, Purine metabolism, and Taurine and hypotaurine metabolism (P < 0.05).

JTK analysis was performed on 976 metabolites showing significant intergroup differences. Results revealed that rhythmic metabolites accounted for 51.74% (505 metabolites) in the CON phase and 29.51% (288 metabolites) in the TS phase (ADJ.P < 0.05). A total of 220 metabolites exhibited circadian rhythms across both phases (Fig. 7A). As visualized in polar plots and a heat map, the abundance of these rhythmic metabolites was distributed throughout the day in both the CON and TS phases (Fig. 7B-C). Three distinct temporal pattern clusters were identified from the differential rhythmic metabolites in the CON and TS phases using fuzzy c-means clustering. (Fig. 7D). Following classification, metabolites such as Carboxylic acids and derivatives, Organooxygen compounds and Fatty Acyls were observed to cluster in the CON and TS phases (Fig. 7E). Pathway analysis revealed distinct enrichments: clusters 1–3 in the CON phase were primarily enriched in the glucagon signaling pathway, dopaminergic synapse, and retinol metabolism, while those in the TS phase were enriched in oxidative phosphorylation, retinol metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7F).

Multi-omics integration reveals circadian rhythm alterations in serum parameters, serum metabolites, and rumen microbiota induced by TS in Tibetan sheep

To elucidate the effects of TS on the circadian rhythms of Tibetan sheep, this study analyzed the relationships among rumen microbiota, serum metabolites, and serum physiological and biochemical indicators. Key genera exhibited distinct correlation patterns (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8A). Ruminococcus correlated negatively with COR, MT, SAA, NEFA, rumen pH, and glycerophosphocholine, but positively with 9 metabolites, including malic acid, 3’,5’-cyclic AMP, and dopamine. Butyrivibrio was positively associated with pH and glycerophosphocholine, while being inversely related to 13 metabolites, for instance, malic acid, glutathione, and fumarate. Anaeroplasma was positively linked to SAA, LBP, NEFA, pH, and angiotensin (1–7), and negatively linked to GPx and multiple metabolites, including malic acid and 3’,5’-cyclic AMP. Pseudomonas demonstrated a negative correlation with MT, pH, and angiotensin (1–7), and a positive correlation with 12 metabolites, including malic acid, glutathione, and fumarate. Candidatus Omnitrophus was negatively associated with MT, SAA, LBP, pH, and angiotensin (1–7), and positively associated with GPx and metabolites such as malic acid, retinol, and glutathione. These complex interaction networks were visualized using a Sankey diagram (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

This study found that the heart rate of Tibetan sheep in the TS phase was significantly lower than that in the CON phase, while blood oxygen saturation was significantly higher. Body temperature showed a downward trend, though this was not significant. These changes may be related to the increase in environmental partial pressure of oxygen when migrating from high to low altitudes. At lower altitudes, the higher partial pressure of oxygen can increase blood oxygen saturation and decrease heart rate by enhancing oxygen utilization [33]. Additionally, prolonged fasting and dehydration during transport could also reduce metabolic rates, triggering an energy-conservation mode in the animals. This reduced heat production, leading to decreased body temperature [34]. COR and MT serve as dual markers of circadian rhythms and stress responses. Their dynamic changes reflect the intensity of stress-induced disruptions in the neuroendocrine system [35]. This study demonstrates that both COR and MT maintain significant circadian rhythms in the CON and TS phases, but TS leads to an overall increase in their concentrations and a reduction in rhythmic amplitude. This suggests activation of the HPA axis and pineal system, indicating a high-stress state while disrupting the organism’s intrinsic rhythmic regulatory capacity [36]. This finding aligns with reports of elevated serum COR in transported Iranian Cashmere goats and Karayaka lambs [37, 38]. NEFA is closely associated with energy metabolism. In the TS phase, its concentration was significantly elevated, and the circadian rhythm was completely lost. This reflects substantial fat mobilization for energy under high-stress, fasting conditions, which severely disrupts energy metabolic cycling. This is consistent with studies showing elevated NEFA in transported and Simmental calves and dromedary camels [7, 12]. Moreover, the acute-phase protein, such as SAA, and the antioxidant enzyme, such as GPx, which were initially arrhythmic, exhibited novel rhythmic fluctuations after transport. This suggests a stress-induced remodeling of physiological systems under strong external stimuli. Elevated SAA and LBP indicate that TS induces systemic inflammation, potentially linked to compromised rumen barrier function and the translocation of LPS into the bloodstream [11, 39]. In contrast, the significant decrease in T-AOC and GPx activity suggests that oxidative stress induced by TS overwhelmed the body’s compensatory capacity, impairing its antioxidant defense system. This finding aligns with those reported by Hosseinian et al. [40] and Li et al. [41]. Additionally, transport stress alters pH rhythms and significantly elevates pH levels, though these remain within the normal range of 6.0–8.0 pH. This finding aligns with studies showing markedly increased rumen pH in cattle following 32-hour fasting and transport [42]. The observed increase may result from reduced feed intake during fasting periods, leading to decreased salivary secretion and diminished fermentation activity [43]. Overall, TS disrupts the physiological rhythmic homeostasis of Tibetan sheep by activating the HPA axis and neuroendocrine system, interfering with energy metabolism balance, and inducing systemic inflammation and immune dysfunction.

Rumen microbes play a crucial role in fiber digestion and maintaining host health [44]. Firmicutes dominate fiber degradation [45], Bacteroidetes participate in carbohydrate and protein breakdown [46], Proteobacteria are closely associated with protein and lipid metabolism, and Desulfobacterota engage in sulfur cycling by reducing sulfate to hydrogen sulfide [47]. In this study, TS caused the loss of rhythmicity in core functional phyla such as the Fusobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria. Furthermore, the relative abundance of Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria significantly decreased, while that of Bacteroidetes significantly increased. This suggests that TS may suppress the cellulose degradation capacity of rumen microorganisms [14]. Similar studies on Hu sheep and lactating cows have shown that stress disrupts the circadian rhythms of rumen microbiota [21, 25, 48]. At the genus level, approximately 3.46% of rumen microbial genera in the CON phase exhibited distinct circadian rhythmic fluctuations, while only 1.99% of genera in the TS phase did. This indicates that TS disrupted the endogenous rhythms of rumen microorganisms. Notably, genera such as Prevotella, Butyrivibrio, and Ruminococcus lost their circadian rhythmicity following transport. These genera are closely associated with fiber degradation and short-chain fatty acid production [49]. Fasting or restricted feeding disrupts the composition and rhythmicity of substrate-dependent microbiota like Prevotella and Ruminococcus. This indicates that transportation and fasting are fundamental drivers of rumen microbial composition changes. This occurs because forced fasting during transport interrupts the supply of starchy substrates in the rumen. Insufficient starch availability may consequently fail to effectively drive relevant microbial populations to participate in starch and sucrose metabolism, as well as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways, thereby affecting total volatile fatty acid (TVFA) production [50, 51]. In the TS phase, the significant reduction in Ruminococcus abundance aligns with the observed decrease in fermentation activity reflected by elevated rumen pH. Conversely, the abundance of butyrate-producing genera such as Butyrivibrio and Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group increased. This change may represent a compensatory response to maintain energy substrate supply under stress, though the increase was insufficient to fully offset the decline in fermentation function [52]. Furthermore, TS induced a novel circadian rhythm in Anaeroplasma, accompanied by a significant increase in its abundance. Anaeroplasma is classified as a “multifactorial-responsive” taxon, whose rhythmicity is regulated by multiple factors including feeding time, host physiology, and inflammatory status [51]. The proliferation of this genus may be directly related to alterations in the rumen environment due to negative energy balance under fasting conditions, or it could be a specific inflammatory response triggered by transport stressors such as psychological stress and immune-inflammatory reactions [51, 53]. In summary, both TS and fasting disrupt rumen microbial composition and circadian rhythms.

This study found that TS caused the proportion of metabolites exhibiting significant circadian rhythms to decrease markedly from 51.74% to 29.51%. This reflects impaired coordination of peripheral metabolic organs’ circadian rhythms by the endogenous biological clock, leading to systemic metabolic disorders[]. Fuzzy c-means clustering analysis revealed that the rhythmic metabolites were predominantly enriched in core physiological pathways, including the TCA cycle, dopaminergic synapse, and retinol metabolism. Among these, the downregulation of key intermediates malic acid and fumarate suggests a disruption in the metabolic homeostasis of the TCA cycle [54]. This is because fasting during transport induces an energy deficit in the animals, triggering fat mobilization and shifting the energy metabolism from “glucose-based energy” to “lipid-based energy”, thereby reducing the levels of intermediates in the tricarboxylic acid cycle [55, 56]. Concurrently, reductions in dopamine and its precursor L-Dopa metabolically reveal impaired neurotransmitter synthesis, which may constitute the chemical basis for behavioral inhibition, such as lethargy, observed in animals after transport [57]. Additionally, TS triggered a substantial shift in metabolic pathways from normal physiological processes to stress response, with significant enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation, MAPK signaling pathway, retinol metabolism, and autophagy - animal. Metabolites involved in these pathways include 3’,5’-cyclic AMP, flavin mononucleotide, fumarate, all-trans-4-hydroxyretinoic acid, and retinol. The rhythmic alterations in these differential metabolites indicate that under compromised basal energy metabolism, the organism maintains survival by activating stress signaling and enhancing the recycling of intracellular components [56].

This study aimed to investigate the effects of TS on the circadian rhythms of metabolism in Tibetan sheep. However, the experimental design could not fully distinguish between the independent effects of fasting and TS. Although the sample size was relatively small (n = 6), the intensive time-series sampling (every 4 h within a 24-hour period) effectively enhanced the detection power for circadian rhythm changes. The results demonstrated that TS significantly disrupted the complex circadian interaction network among the rumen microbiota, serum metabolites, and physiological indicators in Tibetan sheep. Specifically, changes in rumen microbial community abundance are closely associated with host stress responses, energy metabolism, and immune status, revealing the central role of microbe-host interactions in stress responses. Genera such as Ruminococcus and Candidatus Omnitrophus, whose abundance decreased post-transport, showed significant negative correlations with stress-related inflammatory markers, including COR, MT, and SAA. Moreover, Ruminococcus has been documented to participate in immune regulation to maintain homeostasis [58, 59]. Furthermore, Ruminococcus positively correlates with key metabolites involved in energy metabolism (e.g., malic acid, 3’,5’-cyclic AMP, fumarate) and negatively correlates with NEFA. This suggests that fasting, dehydration, and physiological stress induced by TS suppress the activity of these microorganisms, reducing the production of primary energy sources such as volatile fatty acids in the rumen. This forces the host to shift from carbohydrate fermentation for energy to fat mobilization, leading to elevated serum NEFA concentrations [60]. Glutathione is a crucial intracellular antioxidant, and its decreased levels are typically associated with heightened oxidative stress [61]. SAA and LBP are key markers of systemic inflammation and immune activation; their elevated levels suggest that TS induces an inflammatory response [62]. Further analysis revealed that the increased abundance of Butyrivibrio and Anaeroplasma, following TS treatment, showed a negative correlation with the antioxidant metabolites (e.g., glutathione) while exhibiting a positive correlation with the acute-phase proteins SAA and LBP. This suggests their potential involvement in inducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. This observation is consistent with the findings of Eom et al. [63]. Increased abundance of Anaeroplasma may be associated with impaired intestinal barrier function and endotoxin translocation, thereby activating the LBP-SAA inflammatory pathway, depleting antioxidants, and exacerbating tissue oxidative damage [64]. Additionally, this study found that several microbial genera (Ruminococcus, Butyrivibrio, Pseudomonas) were significantly correlated with neurotransmitters, including dopamine, L-Dopa, and L-Tyrosine, as well as with MT and angiotensin (1–7). These correlations suggest that stress may alter the rumen microbial community structure, thereby indirectly affecting the host’s neuroendocrine and antioxidant defense systems. Specifically, Pseudomonas was positively correlated with dopamine and its precursors (L-Dopa, L-Tyrosine). Therefore, a stress-induced decrease in Pseudomonas abundance could limit the supply of these precursors for dopaminergic neurotransmission, thereby exacerbating the behavioral and emotional perception of stress in the central nervous system [65]. This is supported by Kannan et al. [57], who observed that prolonged transport led to reduced dopamine levels and increased fatigue in goats. Conversely, Xiang et al. [66] also confirmed that dopamine, functioning as an exogenous quorum-sensing signal or regulator, significantly modulates Pseudomonas growth. Meanwhile, MT and angiotensin (1–7) possess anti-stress, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory functions [67, 68]. The negative correlation between Pseudomonas and these beneficial molecules suggests that this bacterium may, under basal conditions, exert a modest inhibitory influence on their biosynthesis or homeostasis. These correlations suggest that TS may amplify systemic inflammation and oxidative stress by disrupting rumen microbiota abundance and rhythms, thereby compromising intestinal barrier function and peripheral metabolic homeostasis, ultimately exacerbating physiological damage in the host. Future studies should further validate the aforementioned mechanisms through targeted quantification of key metabolites, measurement of the circadian dynamics of ruminal fermentation end-products (e.g., volatile fatty acids), and assessment of intestinal barrier integrity (e.g., lipopolysaccharide and tight junction proteins).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that TS significantly increases serum levels of COR, MT, SAA, LBP, and NEFA while decreasing GPx activity, indicating that Tibetan sheep are in a state of high stress. TS reduces the abundance of Ruminococcus in the rumen while increasing the abundance of Butyrivibrio and Anaeroplasma, disrupting the rhythmicity of key microbial communities and destabilizing rumen ecological balance. Serum metabolomics analysis revealed that TS interferes with energy metabolism, antioxidant, and neuromodulatory metabolites, activating pathways including oxidative phosphorylation, MAPK signaling, purine metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism. Multi-omics correlation analysis further clarified significant associations between rumen microbes (e.g., Ruminococcus, Butyrivibrio, and Pseudomonas) and stress-related inflammatory markers, energy metabolites, and antioxidants. In summary, TS induces a physiological homeostasis imbalance by disrupting the composition and rhythmic changes of rumen microbiota and serum metabolites in Tibetan sheep. These findings provide a theoretical basis and potential intervention targets for alleviating TS in livestock. Future studies should establish control groups to distinguish the independent and interactive effects of fasting and transport stress, investigate the adaptive recovery of the microbiota and host metabolism after transport, and evaluate the efficacy of nutritional or probiotic interventions in alleviating TS and enhancing adaptive capacity.
