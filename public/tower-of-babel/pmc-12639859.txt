Investigation of job stability and influencing factors for graduates of public health-related majors in Jiangxi Province, China
Authors: Cheng Yang, Hongyu Si, Yonghai Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic health graduates play a crucial role in disease prevention, yet China is experiencing significant workforce attrition. Jiangxi Province illustrates this problem, with a public health workforce density of 7.31 per 10,000 population, which falls short of national standards, and ongoing difficulties in recruitment and retention. This study explores turnover intention and its predictors among public health graduates in Jiangxi to provide evidence for improving retention strategies.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 546 graduates, yielding a 91% response rate, using convenience sampling. Multivariable logistic regression and structural equation modeling (SEM) were applied to identify predictors of turnover intention and psychological contract dimensions.ResultsFactors associated with lower turnover intention included being older than 30 years (OR = 0.169), holding senior professional titles (OR = 0.131), working in administrative roles (OR = 0.157), and having permanent employment status (OR = 0.265). SEM analysis showed that all dimensions of the psychological contract (normative, developmental, and relational) were negatively associated with turnover intention, with relational contracts exerting the strongest influence (β = −0.620).ConclusionStrengthening job stability through measures such as career advancement opportunities and fulfillment of psychological contracts is essential for retaining public health professionals in under-resourced provinces like Jiangxi.Keywords: Public health, Graduates, Turnover intention, Structural equation model, Psychological contract

Introduction

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) is a government-established public welfare institution responsible for safeguarding public security and promoting population health [1]. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outbreak in 2019 tested the nation’s governance system and the effectiveness of the public health infrastructure that had been strengthened after the SARS epidemic. While Chinese public health service system demonstrated a degree of effectiveness in crisis management during COVID-19, the pandemic also revealed serious systemic weaknesses, particularly in workforce capacity. One of the most pressing challenges was the inadequate development and retention of skilled disease control professionals. Public health professionals are essential to advancing the principle of prevention in healthcare and play a central role in achieving the Healthy China strategy, which underpins both national security and social stability. In recent years, however, Chinese disease control system has experienced a significant outflow of talent, with recruitment difficulties and retention challenges emerging as critical concerns. According to data from the National Health Commission, from 2009 to 2018, the number of health personnel in hospitals increased by 58.07%, whereas that in the disease control system fell by 4.5%. The density of CDC personnel in China declined from 1.47 per 10,000 population in 2008 to 1.42 in 2017, consistently remaining below the National Health Commission’s recommended minimum threshold of 1.75 [2]. Jiangxi Province illustrates these workforce challenges. As a province in south-central China, with a GDP per capita approximately 20% lower than the national average in 2023 (RMB 71,216 vs. RMB 89,358), Jiangxi’s public health system operates with a workforce density of 7.31 per 10,000 population, far below both national standards and the averages observed in eastern provinces [3]. Moreover, its training infrastructure remains limited, with only two medical universities offering accredited Master of Public Health programs. These disparities in economic resources, workforce distribution, and educational opportunities create a unique setting for examining turnover dynamics among public health graduates.

Employee resignation, particularly voluntary departures, is a process that directly undermines organizational efficiency and productivity [4]. High turnover not only raises costs but also increases management difficulties and undermines staff morale [5]. Health administrative departments in China are especially concerned with this issue, as healthcare professionals often face heavy workloads, low salaries, and unsatisfactory working conditions [6]. Turnover intention, defined as the likelihood that an employee will leave an organization within a given period, is widely recognized as one of the most reliable predictors of actual resignation [7]. Specifically, turnover intention closely reflects an employee’s desire to exit the workplace [8, 9]. Evidence highlights the global magnitude of this problem. A systematic review found the turnover rate among general practitioners worldwide to be 47.0% [10]. In China, nursing staff in hospitals experience a turnover rate of 41.3% [11], and approximately 70.0% of general practitioners have expressed a strong turnover intention [12]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the increasing shortage of healthcare personnel prompted numerous studies in China and abroad to identify key factors influencing turnover to guide workforce retention strategies [13–15]. Furthermore, regions with limited fiscal capacity are more likely to see higher turnover, as inadequate local funding worsens the financial and professional pressures faced by healthcare staff [16, 17]. To date, most studies on turnover have focused on hospital-based employees, while the turnover of public health professionals has received comparatively little attention. However, available data suggest that the problem is equally pressing in this sector. A survey conducted among public health professionals in Shenzhen, a developed coastal city in China, showed that 38.66% of respondents had turnover intentions [18]. Similarly, in developed countries such as the United States, approximately one-third of employees in state and local public health agencies demonstrate turnover intentions [19]. In developed regions, local governments often provide adequate financial support to public health institutions, resulting in better compensation. However, high turnover intentions among public health staff persist even in these areas, indicating that competitive pay alone is insufficient and highlighting the need to investigate other factors influencing turnover intentions.

Psychological contract theory, first introduced by Rousseau [20], suggests that employees hold implicit expectations about mutual obligations between themselves and their employers. The strength of the psychological contract significantly shapes job satisfaction, work engagement, and turnover intention. For example, a survey of bank employees in South Africa found that breaches of the psychological contract reduced engagement and subsequently increased turnover rates [21]. Similar findings were reported in Chile among teachers and public servants [22]. Taken together, these studies confirmed that psychological contract violations and voluntary turnover behavior are significantly positively correlated. When organizations fail to honor their psychological commitments, employees often experience negative emotional reactions, which weaken organizational commitment, reduce job satisfaction, and ultimately heighten both turnover intentions and actual turnover behavior.

Building on the foundational work of Rousseau [20, 23] and Li [24], this study defines psychological contracts as a multidimensional construct with three distinct dimensions: (1) The normative contract captures transactional elements such as satisfaction with pay, benefits, and work systems; (2) The developmental contract reflects opportunities for advancement, training, promotion, and improved social status; and (3) The relational contract includes socioemotional factors such as relationships with supervisors and colleagues, respect from the organization, and a sense of belonging. Breaches in any of these areas—for instance, unmet salary promises (normative), blocked career progression (developmental), or inadequate social support (relational)—have been linked to adverse outcomes, including stronger turnover intention. Despite this evidence, much of the existing literature treats the psychological contract as a single construct, leaving the relative influence of each dimension underexplored. Comparative studies that examine how these dimensions differentially affect turnover remain scarce.

To address this limitation, the present study applies structural equation modeling (SEM), a comprehensive statistical method designed to test complex multivariate relationships. SEM enables simultaneous modeling of latent variables—such as the three psychological contract dimensions—together with their observable indicators and structural pathways. It also estimates both direct and indirect effects, offering a more detailed view of how specific contract breaches shape turnover intention. In addition, SEM provides robust indices of model fit (e.g., comparative fit index, root mean square error of approximation, and standardized root mean square residual) to assess the alignment of hypothesized relationships with the data. By combining measurement and structural components, SEM allows for precise evaluation of the distinct effects of normative, developmental, and relational contracts, thereby offering greater methodological rigor in analyzing their relative contributions.

Current research on turnover intention, both in China and internationally, has focused largely on hospital-based healthcare professionals, while far less attention has been given to staff in disease prevention and control agencies, particularly public health professionals. Studies that use SEM to examine career attractiveness and turnover behavior in this setting are especially limited. In China, psychological contract research in healthcare management is still in its early stages and remains centered on nurses and physicians, with virtually no empirical work on public health professionals in disease control systems. This gap is striking given the ongoing importance of public health as COVID-19 moves into a phase of normalized control. Yet, despite the critical role of public health professionals, little is known about their turnover tendencies, especially among new graduates in provinces such as Jiangxi. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the current status and determinants of turnover intention among public health graduates in Jiangxi, with a particular focus on the role of psychological contract fulfillment. By applying SEM to examine the differential effects of normative, developmental, and relational contract dimensions on turnover intention, this study seeks to generate new evidence and provide a foundation for targeted strategies to improve retention of public health professionals.

Objects and methods

Survey subjects

This study employed a cross-sectional design to recruit graduates from public health-related programs in the universities of Jiangxi Province by using a convenience sampling method. The inclusion criteria were (1) graduates from public health-related programs at universities in Jiangxi Province; (2) individuals who are currently or had previously been employed. The exclusion criterion was individuals unwilling to cooperate with the survey. All research participants provided their informed consent to participate in the study.

The sample size was determined based on both practical feasibility and statistical power considerations. For the logistic regression analysis, a power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. With an anticipated effect size (odds ratio, OR) of 1.40, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and statistical power (1–β) of 0.80, and assuming 10 predictor variables, the minimum required sample size was 442. Considering a 20% dropout rate, the study required a sample size of 530 participants. For SEM, a sample size of 200–400 was considered adequate for models with moderate complexity. We distributed 600 questionnaires and collected 546 valid responses that met the eligibility criteria. The final sample size met the analytical requirements of the study.

Research methods

This study employed a self-designed questionnaire comprising the following three main sections: general demographic characteristics, a psychological contract scale, and a turnover intention scale. The survey was conducted by using an anonymous electronic questionnaire format.

Measurement work

The psychological contract scale developed in this study was based on the theoretical framework established by Rousseau and Li [20, 23, 24]. The turnover intention survey scale draws on the three-dimensional model of turnover intention established by Michaels and Spector [25] by selecting questions that are relevant to both psychological contracts and turnover intention. Enhancements were made to the questionnaire items and dimensions. The original scales were translated into the Chinese language and culturally adapted through a process of forward translation, expert panel review, and back-translation to ensure conceptual equivalence. The expert panel comprised three public health researchers and two senior practitioners who evaluated the relevance, clarity, and appropriateness of each item within the Chinese public health context. Based on their feedback, minor revisions were made to improve the clarity and contextual fit. The survey employed a 5-point Likert scale [26], where the options for the psychological contract scale very dissatisfied, “dissatisfied”, “neutral”, " satisfied”, and “very satisfied” were assigned scores of 1 to 5, respectively. Similarly, in the turnover intention scale, the options “strongly disagree”, “disagree”, “neutral”, “agree”, and " strongly agree” were assigned scores from 1 to 5. A higher score indicates greater satisfaction and stronger turnover intention. The respondents were classified into the high and low turnover intention groups based on the mean of their total turnover intention scores.

Following the initial development of the questionnaire, a pre-survey was conducted with 40 participants. Feedback from this pre-survey was analyzed and discussed to finalize the questionnaire. Exploratory factor analysis was performed to evaluate the scales, resulting in the removal of items with factor loadings < 0.5, and the items were incorrectly classified within the dimensions. Six items were eliminated from the psychological contract scale, leaving a total of 14 items categorized into the following three dimensions: “normative contract”, “relational contract”, and “developmental contract”. Two items were removed from the turnover intention scale, retaining three items within a single dimension. The normative contract encompassed four items, such as satisfaction with benefits and compensation. The developmental contract includes five items, including opportunities for career advancement. The “developmental contract” dimension assessed the organization’s commitment to employee growth and advancement, which included not only tangible opportunities such as promotion and training but also the associated enhancement of professional reputation and social status, which are the recognized outcomes of career development within psychological contract theory. The relational contract consists of five items, for instance, relationships with colleagues. The intention to leave comprises three items, such as “I frequently consider leaving”. Cronbach’s α coefficient was used to measure the internal consistency reliability of the factors, with alpha coefficients for each dimension and the overall scale ranging from 0.868 to 0.905. Factor analysis was used to assess the structural validity of the scales. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) coefficient for the psychological contract scale was 0.809 (P < 0.01), indicating a strong correlation, and the three common factors with eigenvalues > 1 were extracted, which aligns with the intended dimensions, collectively accounting for 72.6% of the total variance. The KMO coefficient for the turnover intention scale was 0.733 (P < 0.01), indicating a strong correlation, with one common factor extracted that had an eigenvalue > 1, which is consistent with the designated dimension and explains 79.3% of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis was employed to evaluate the convergent validity and composite reliability (CR) of the scales, suggesting that each dimension’s average variance extracted (AVE) was > 0.5 and each dimension’s CR was > 0.7, confirming the good reliability and validity of the questionnaire (Table 1).

Quality control

Before the investigation, expert discussions and a pre-survey were conducted to confirm the final content of the questionnaire. Training was provided uniformly to all staff members involved in the survey, and only those who passed the training were allowed to participate in the project. Following the collection of questionnaires, a quality control process was implemented, which involved removing responses with incorrect answers to quality control the questions (e.g., “What is the capital of Jiangxi Province? “) as well as questionnaires in which all scale items were answered with the same option. In this survey, a total of 54 questionnaires were excluded, of which 32 were discarded due to incorrect responses on attention checks and 22 due to patterned answering. The excluded samples represented only 9% of the total sample size, and the demographic characteristics (i.e., age, gender, and education level) of the excluded respondents did not differ significantly from those of the included respondents (all P > 0.05), suggesting that the exclusion process did not affect the representativeness of the sample. Explanations were provided before conducting the survey, and the respondents were required to complete the questionnaire independently; the survey was administered anonymously.

Statistical analysis

Data entry was performed in duplicate using EpiData (v.3.1), with subsequent statistical analyses conducted using IBM SPSS (v.21.0) and AMOS (v.26.0). Unconditional binary logistic regression analysis was employed to examine the factors influencing turnover intention by using a stepwise forward method with the inclusion and exclusion criteria set at 0.05 and 0.10, respectively. The maximum likelihood method was employed to estimate and construct a structural equation model. Model fit was evaluated using a comparative fit index, non-normed fit index, and root mean square error of approximation. All test items in the scale were incorporated as continuous variables in the analysis, with a significance level established at α = 0.05.

Results

Basic information

A total of 546 public health graduates participated in the survey, including 231 men and 315 women (age: 22–38 years; mean age = 28.36 ± 3.52 years). Regarding educational attainment, 395 participants had a bachelor’s degree, 148 had a master’s degree, and 3 had a doctoral degree. In terms of professional titles, 76 respondents had no title, 374 had junior titles, 93 had intermediate titles, and 3 had associate senior titles. Employment settings varied across the cohort. Of the total, 258 graduates were employed in disease prevention and control institutions, 134 in hospitals, and 49 in community or township health service centers. An additional 105 respondents worked in other sectors. Within this subgroup, 11 were employed in government health administration departments, 28 in medical schools, 25 in other health-related institutions, 20 in pharmaceutical companies, 12 in nonpharmaceutical enterprises, and 9 were self-employed. Although these 105 respondents were not currently working in public health institutions, each had prior professional experience in public health roles. All 546 graduates held degrees in fields related to public health.

Scores on the psychological contract scale and turnover intention scale

Analysis of the psychological contract scale showed that the average scores among the 546 graduates were 2.48 ± 0.77 for normative contracts, 2.82 ± 0.68 for developmental contracts, and 3.39 ± 0.82 for relational contracts. The mean score on the turnover intention scale was 3.12 ± 0.94 (Table 2).

Structural equation model of factors influencing turnover intention

Using exploratory factor analysis, this study develops a theoretical model of factors influencing turnover intention, including one endogenous variable, turnover intention, and three exogenous variables: normative, developmental, and relational contracts. It is hypothesized that each of these exogenous variables exerts an effect on turnover intention. The structure of the model is shown in Fig. 1.

Structural equation model of the psychological contract

Testing the hypotheses of the structural equation model requires that the data meet the assumption of multivariate normality. Normality tests indicated violations of this assumption, as the model yielded χ² = 1285.98 with P < 0.001. Given that chi-square statistics are highly sensitive to sample size, the Bollen–Stine bootstrapping method was applied to adjust model fit and parameter estimates [27–29]. A total of 2000 bootstrap iterations were conducted, and the adjusted parameters were used to evaluate overall model fit. As presented in Table 4, the adjusted model demonstrated a strong fit with the observed data. Path coefficients were then calculated for the structural model, and all were statistically significant (P < 0.001), confirming that the three exogenous variables exert measurable effects on turnover intention. Among the absolute values of the coefficients, relational contracts showed the strongest negative association with turnover intention, followed by developmental contracts, while normative contracts had the weakest effect (Table 5).

In addition to assessing their influence on turnover intention, latent variables can be examined as dependent variables to evaluate the correlations among them. In this study, correlation coefficients were calculated for normative, developmental, and relational contracts. The results show that normative and relational contracts have the strongest correlation, followed by developmental and relational contracts, and lastly normative and developmental contracts (Table 6). These findings suggest that, while conceptually distinct, the three dimensions of the psychological contract are closely interconnected in practice. The strongest association was observed between normative and relational contracts (β = 0.411, P < 0.001), indicating that tangible benefits and systemic support (normative) are closely linked with interpersonal trust and organizational respect (relational) in the public health context. This suggests that in resource-constrained environments such as Jiangxi, fair compensation and institutional reliability are often interpreted as signs of organizational care, thereby reinforcing relational bonds. The correlation between developmental and relational contracts was also significant (β = 0.387, P < 0.001), suggesting that opportunities for professional growth and recognition contribute to stronger feelings of belonging and mutual commitment within organizations. Finally, the correlation between normative and developmental contracts, though weaker, remained significant (β = 0.208, P < 0.001). This finding indicates that while job security and basic benefits may provide a foundation for professional development opportunities, they are less directly aligned. Taken together, these results highlight the multidimensional yet interconnected nature of psychological contracts. Fulfillment in one dimension can positively shape perceptions in other dimensions, and collectively these interactions play a critical role in reducing turnover intention among public health professionals.

Discussion

This study represents the first investigation into the psychological contracts and turnover intentions of public health graduates in Jiangxi Province, a south-central Chinese province characterized by slower economic growth and more limited public health resources than the eastern provinces [3]. Of the 546 graduates surveyed, most were employed in public health institutions, yet approximately one-fifth were working in other sectors such as government agencies, educational institutions, private enterprises, and various public organizations. This relatively high proportion of graduates leaving public health practice may signal systemic issues, including limited career advancement, lower salaries, and insufficient training infrastructure within Jiangxi’s public health sector. Among those who remained in the field, the main workplaces included disease prevention and control centers, hospitals, and community health service facilities. The overall turnover intention among these graduates was moderate but strongly shaped by institutional and economic factors specific to Jiangxi. The findings revealed that younger graduates, particularly those under 30 years of age, individuals without permanent employment status, those not holding administrative roles, and those with lower professional titles showed significantly higher turnover intentions. Younger employees expressed a stronger inclination to resign, consistent with previous research documenting a negative association between age and turnover intention [30–32]. For instance, studies in China have shown that emergency physicians older than 37 years had lower turnover intention (OR = 0.64–0.78) [31], and nurses with senior titles reported less inclination to resign (1.87 ± 0.57) compared with those in junior positions (2.44 ± 0.55, P < 0.01) [33]. Similarly, administrative roles were found to lower turnover likelihood (β = −0.084, P = 0.038) [34], and nonregular nursing staff displayed higher turnover intention than permanent employees (OR = 1.62) [35], emphasizing the role of stable employment arrangements.

Although demographic patterns such as age, job title, and administrative status clearly influenced turnover, the major methodological contribution of this study is the application of SEM with Bollen–Stine bootstrapping to account for non-normality in the survey data. Traditional SEM assumes multivariate normality, yet this assumption is often violated in Likert-scale research. In our analysis, the initial SEM produced a significant χ² statistic (χ² = 1285.98, P < 0.001), a result strongly affected by both sample size and the non-normal distribution of variables. To address this, we applied Bollen–Stine bootstrapping with 2000 resamples, which recalibrates fit indices and parameter estimates without relying on normality assumptions. The excellent fit of the adjusted model (e.g., RMSEA = 0.02, CFI = 0.99) confirms the validity of the latent construct relationships despite non-normality. This analytic strategy strengthens the reliability of the findings and establishes a methodological precedent for future workforce research. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies in the public health workforce literature to explicitly apply and report Bollen–Stine bootstrapping within SEM. The approach provides a practical reference for researchers handling non-normal survey data when examining psychological contracts, turnover intention, or other latent constructs in similar contexts.

This study demonstrated that higher fulfillment levels across all dimensions of the psychological contract were linked to lower turnover intention. The standardized path coefficients (β) from the SEM analysis provided a direct comparison of the relative influence of each dimension. Among them, the relational contract exerted the strongest negative effect (β = −0.620), indicating that a one standard deviation increased in relational fulfillment corresponded to a 0.620 standard deviation reduction in turnover intention. The developmental contract also showed a significant, though smaller, effect (β = −0.361). This suggests that improving relational aspects, such as supportive leadership, collegial ties, and organizational respect, is about 1.7 times more effective (|−0.620|/|−0.361|≈1.72) in reducing turnover intention than equivalent improvements in developmental aspects such as training or promotion opportunities. These findings align with psychological contract theory, which highlights relational obligations based on trust, mutual commitment, and socioemotional exchange as critical to employee retention, especially in professions that demand strong dedication [20, 23]. They also resonate with the job embeddedness framework, which emphasizes that employees are anchored by “links” (social connections) and “fit” (perceived alignment) within their workplace and broader community. In resource-constrained settings like Jiangxi, where material incentives may be limited, the social and emotional bonds embedded in relational contracts can serve as powerful substitutes for more tangible rewards.

The importance of interpersonal relationships was further underscored by our results. Collegial harmony, supportive leadership, and a culture of organizational respect foster a sense of security and belonging, which in turn lowers the turnover intention [36–39]. International evidence supports this; for example, a survey among nurses found that strong organizational support significantly reduced turnover intentions [40]. According to the push–pull theory [41], insufficient social support acts as a push factor driving employees away, whereas a positive work environment operates as a pull factor that mitigates negative emotions and encourages retention. Multiple studies confirm that social support consistently predicts lower turnover intention [42–44]. Although relational factors were most influential, normative and developmental dimensions also played important roles. Satisfaction with salary and benefits demonstrated an inverse relationship with turnover intention [45]. Evidence from Chinese emergency physicians showed lower turnover rates among those with higher incomes (OR = 0.75) [12]. Reduced work stress similarly lowered turnover intentions [46], whereas extended working hours and effort–reward imbalance were associated with poor well-being and stronger turnover intentions [47–49]. The absence of incentive rewards further increased turnover likelihood [50]. Importantly, developmental fulfillment emerged as a stabilizing factor: when employees perceived a promising professional future, with opportunities for advancement and meaningful work, they were less inclined to resign [10, 51–53].

Taken together, these findings suggest several targeted strategies for policymakers and organizational leaders working in under-resourced public health systems. To reinforce relational contracts, efforts should concentrate on cultivating supportive leadership and fostering a culture of respect and care. Practical interventions might include mandatory leadership training on empathetic communication, routine supervisory visits focused on staff support, and structured peer mentorship or team-based programs to strengthen workplace relationships. Recognition of employees’ contributions, both formally and informally, can further bolster perceptions of organizational respect. For developmental contracts, establishing transparent and merit-based promotion pathways is essential. This may involve designing structured career ladders with clear advancement criteria, expanding access to continuing education and specialized training in public health competencies, and providing institutional support for advanced degrees or professional certifications. Beyond organizational reforms, enhancing the social visibility and prestige of public health professions through advocacy and public recognition campaigns could also strengthen graduates’ sense of career purpose. Normative contracts, while showing comparatively smaller effects, remain foundational. Competitive compensation and benefits should be prioritized through policy advocacy to secure sustainable funding, particularly for junior staff without permanent civil service positions (bianzhi). Where resources permit, performance-based incentives could supplement base salaries and encourage retention. Overall, a multifaceted approach simultaneously addressing relational, developmental, and normative contracts, with emphasis on relational fulfillment given its stronger effect, is likely to be the most effective way to retain public health graduates within their organizations and reduce turnover intention.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling and online-only surveys may introduce selection bias, potentially overrepresenting graduates with stable internet access and strong organizational ties, while underrepresenting early leavers and those with limited connectivity. This imbalance could lead to an underestimation of leave intention. The geographic restriction to Jiangxi Province and small subgroup sizes (e.g., senior staff) further limit generalizability. Second, information bias may occur due to the cross-sectional design (precluding causal inference) and self-reported data (subject to recall and social desirability biases). Third, considering the potential challenges in accurately collecting data on personal privacy issues such as income and marital status within the Chinese population, this study did not account for the impact of these variables. Cultural and institutional influences on psychological contracts were also not captured. Future multi-center longitudinal studies with stratified sampling are needed to improve validity and generalizability.

Conclusion

This study contributes novel empirical evidence by examining the dimensions of psychological contracts and turnover intentions among public health graduates in an under-resourced Chinese province. The results reveal that most graduates remain engaged in public health roles, though moderate turnover intentions persist. Lower turnover intention was observed among those over the age of 30, those holding senior titles, those having permanent employment status, those working in administrative roles, and those reporting higher levels of psychological contract fulfillment. Future initiatives should aim to refine policies and organizational systems that support the fulfillment of psychological contracts among public health graduates, thereby promoting greater professional stability and long-term retention.
