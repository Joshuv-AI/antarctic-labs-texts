Surgery-first versus neoadjuvant chemotherapy in stage I (cT1c) triple-negative breast cancer: A critical reappraisal
Authors: M Dediu, C Zielinski, V Preoteasa, A Bulzan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of Stage I breast cancer is rising due to the implementation of widespread screening programs. Current ESMO guidelines recommend neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) for Stage I (cT1c) triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a strategy largely extrapolated from trials enrolling Stage II–III patients. This review critically reappraises the NACT paradigm specifically for the cT1c subgroup.Materials and methodsWe analysed emerging real-world data, including large-scale SEER studies and recent meta-analyses, comparing survival outcomes of NACT versus adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) in Stage I TNBC. Additionally, we evaluated the differential efficacy of platinum agents and capecitabine in the neoadjuvant versus adjuvant settings.ResultsRetrospective analyses suggest that for Stage I TNBC, a surgery-first approach followed by ACT is associated with superior breast cancer-specific survival and overall survival compared to NACT. The survival benefit of NACT assumed in higher stages does not consistently translate to Stage I. Furthermore, while the addition of neoadjuvant carboplatin increases pathological complete response (pCR) rates, it yields inconsistent OS benefits. In contrast, adjuvant capecitabine demonstrates a robust long-term survival benefit.ConclusionWe propose a stage-specific therapeutic algorithm for cT1c N0M0 TNBC favouring a surgery-first strategy followed by ACT. This approach optimizes survival outcomes by including adjuvant capecitabine within an anthracycline/taxane backbone while avoiding the potential pitfalls of neoadjuvant undertreatment in early-stage disease.Keywords: Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC), Stage I, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Adjuvant chemotherapy, Surgery-first, Capecitabine

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents the most aggressive breast cancer subtype, accounting for approximately 12%–20% of all diagnoses [1]. With the widespread implementation of screening programs, the incidence of early-stage disease is rising; it is estimated that approximately 50% of newly diagnosed breast cancers are now classified as cT1 [[2], [3], [4]]. While immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with chemotherapy have significantly improved overall survival (OS) in TNBC, the pivotal KEYNOTE-522 trial enrolled only Stage II–III patients, thereby limiting regulatory approval to these higher stages [5]. Consequently, Stage I patients remain dependent on chemotherapy alone for the control of micrometastatic disease.

However, Stage I TNBC is itself a heterogeneous entity: available data show no demonstrable chemotherapy benefit in pT1a tumors and only a limited benefit in pT1b, G1–2 tumors. Chemotherapy omission in patients with favorable biological features — high sTILs or undetectable ctDNA — is increasingly the focus of ongoing prospective trials in this subgroup. We therefore restrict the present analysis to pT1c disease, where a chemotherapy benefit is more consistently observed and the sequencing question we address remains clinically relevant.

For early-stage TNBC, the ESMO guideline represents the best synthesis of available evidence, yet its recommendations are predominantly derived from Stage II–III cohorts [6]. Notably, the guideline draws no biological distinction between Stage I and Stage II TNBC and considers pCR a validated endpoint regardless of stage — an argument that has been used to support de-escalation strategies, such as omitting capecitabine in patients achieving pCR or shortening anthracycline-free regimens, rather than treating all Stage I patients with the same four-compound, relatively toxic regimen.

This review addresses two distinct yet complementary questions structured in a deliberate hierarchy. The primary thesis concerns sequencing: for cT1c N0M0 TNBC, upfront surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) is preferable to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT). The secondary axis examines the optimal chemotherapy composition within that ACT strategy — specifically the role of capecitabine versus carboplatin, taxane selection, and anthracycline integration. Rather than an independent claim, this composition is presented as the operational corollary of the foundational sequencing decision. The most compelling evidence anchoring this secondary analysis is the 15-year follow-up of the FinXX trial [7], in which integrating capecitabine into an anthracycline/taxane adjuvant backbone produced a 10.3% absolute overall survival (OS) gain in the TNBC subgroup (HR 0.59; 95% CI 0.36–0.97). These two questions are addressed sequentially in dedicated sections below.

The efficacy of ACT vs NACT in stage I TNBC

Current clinical guidelines, including those from ESMO [6], classify NACT as a Category IA recommendation for cT1c N0M0 TNBC. This consensus rests on the principle that the timing of chemotherapy—preoperative versus postoperative—does not influence long-term survival. This principle was established by the landmark NSABP-B18 trial [8]and subsequently reinforced by the EBCTCG meta-analysis, which analysed data from 4756 women and found no significant difference between NACT and ACT for breast cancer mortality (5-year breast cancer mortality 34.4% vs 33.7%; rate ratio 1.06, 95% CI 0.95-1.18; P = 0.31) [9] (Supplementary Table S1).

However, applying these historical data to modern Stage I TNBC populations is problematic. The pivotal trials supporting this consensus were conducted prior to contemporary TNBC subtyping and enrolled predominantly Stage II–III cohorts. Notably, although HER2 status was not specifically isolated, data from the ER-negative/PR-negative subset in the EBCTCG meta-analysis actually favored adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) over neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) with respect to breast cancer mortality (RR 1.33, 95% CI 1.00-1.75). This suggests that the preoperative treatment window may carry distinct biological risks in TNBC. Despite this inconsistency, the EBCTCG meta-analysis remains a foundational reference for several international guidelines and expert panels that broadly recommend NACT for early-stage TNBC, including Stage I (cT1c) [[10], [11], [12]]. In contrast, the recent St. Gallen consensus conference established a 2 cm tumor size threshold as the standard cutoff for choosing between adjuvant and neoadjuvant approaches [13].

In addition, emerging real-world data indicate that the “equivalence dogma' may be invalid for Stage I TNBC. Xu et al. utilized the SEER database to compare strategies, generating balanced cohorts of 9025 patients each via propensity score matching (PSM) [14]. After adjusting for age, stage, grade, pathological subtype, surgery and radiation, the analysis revealed that patients treated with surgery-first followed by ACT achieved superior OS compared to those treated with NACT regardless the tumor fenotype (HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.24–1.46; P < 0.001). A similar difference in favour of ACT was also noted specifically in the basal-like molecular subtype (OS HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.03-1.37; P = 0.017). Notably, the most consistent difference was noted in stage I for breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) (HR 2.33, 95% CI 1.56-3.47; P < 0.001) and OS (HR 2.19, 95% CI 1.61-2.96; P < 0.001)(Supplementary Table S1).

These findings were corroborated by Zheng et al., who evaluated cT1c TNBC patients specifically (Supplementary Table S1). After PSM, patients treated with NACT had worse OS compared with ACT. The difference was of borderline significance for both BCSS (HR 1.47; 95% CI 0.94–2.30; P = 0.092) and OS (HR 1.45; 95% CI 0.97–2.18; P = 0.070) [15]. The corresponding 5-year and 10-year survival probabilities of the NACT and ACT groups are shown in Supplementary Table S2. In addition, the analysis demonstrated that the survival deficit in the NACT arm was driven by patients who failed to achieve a pathological complete response (pCR). While achieving pCR yields excellent outcomes, patients with residual disease (non-pCR) experienced significantly worse OS compared with matched patients receiving ACT (Table 1).

In contrast to previous data, Cheng et al., utilizing the same SEER database, claimed that patients with T1cN0M0 TNBC derived an OS benefit from NACT [16] (Supplementary Table S1). However, in the context of the present analysis, this conclusion might be misleading, as the reported benefit was driven by the comparison between NACT and a ‘no treatment’ arm. Moreover, when comparing ACT with NACT, the outcomes appeared to be equivalent (HR 0.83; 95% CI 0.57–1.23), suggesting that survival was improved by the administration of chemotherapy rather than its timing. We consider the finding of ‘equivalence’ likely a statistical artifact of using Cox regression on a heavily unbalanced dataset (4.94% NACT vs 35.53% ACT). In this scenario, the standard Cox model is dominated by the large ACT majority, effectively ‘diluting’ the potentially poor outcome in the small NACT group. This poor outcome became visible only when PSM was used to isolate balanced, high-risk groups, as demonstrated by the findings of inferior OS in the NACT groups by Xu [14] and Zheng [15]. Moreover, the authors failed to fully contextualize their own substantial finding: patients with cT1a disease experienced significantly worse survival with NACT even when compared with the no treatment arm (HR 0.23; 95% CI 0.09–0.60; P < 0.01).

Crucially, the validity of NACT for stage I TNBC relies heavily on extrapolation, as pivotal trials enrolled almost exclusively stage II and III patients. In contrast, ACT trials offer direct evidence, with approximately 30%–40% of participants having stage I disease [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]].

While the conclusion that ACT outperforms NACT in stage I TNBC may appear counterintuitive, it is supported by clinical data and emerging preclinical evidence regarding the dichotomous evolutionary trajectories of tumour cells imposed by NACT stress. The “non-adapter' phenotypes (basal-like 1, immunomodulatory) are characterized by high proliferative indices and profound homologous recombination deficiency [20]. These biologic subtypes respond well to NACT, leading to high pCR rates (a surrogate for excellent long-term survival) [[21], [22], [23]].

Conversely, the mesenchymal (M) and luminal androgen receptor (LAR) subtypes frequently fail to achieve pCR [21,24]. In these subtypes, cytotoxic stress can induce a “drug-tolerant persister' state [25,26]. To maintain viability, these cells undergo metabolic rewiring and upregulate the FSP1-CoQ10 axis to suppress ferroptosis [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Crucially, this survival response is often coupled with epithelial-mesenchymal transition, transforming localized tumour cells into an invasive phenotype [27,30,31]. Upfront surgery abrogates this risk by removing the tumour burden before the acquisition of these stress-induced, resistant phenotypes.

Supplementary arguments for a surgery-first approach may derive from other associated benefits. First, upfront sentinel lymph node dissection ensures the detection of occult pN + disease, identifying candidates for perioperative immunotherapy (as per KEYNOTE- 522 trial) who might otherwise be missed under current NACT algorithms [6,32,33]. Second, it simplifies the integration of radiotherapy (RT). While the optimal sequencing of post-neoadjuvant capecitabine and RT remains debated—with concurrent use showing promise but lacking standardization [34,35]—an adjuvant strategy allows for standard, sequentially delivered RT following ACT as per the FinXX trial [18], avoiding potential toxicity interactions [36,37].

Integration of capecitabine into the ACT backbone in TNBC

The integration of capecitabine is currently defined by the CREATE-X trial, which positioned it as a “rescue' therapy for post-NACT residual disease. In this landmark study, patients with residual disease following NACT were randomized to receive adjuvant capecitabine or no further treatment. In the TNBC subgroup (n = 286), capecitabine demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in both invasive disease-free survival (iDFS) (HR 0.58; 95% CI 0.39–0.87) and OS (HR 0.52; 95% CI 0.30–0.90) [38]. However, extrapolating the benefit of the CREATE-X strategy to cT1c disease is challenging. In CREATE-X, only 4.8% of patients had stage I disease, and the benefit for tumors ≤2 cm was not significant. Additionally, a real-world analysis by de Graaf et al., including 1144 stage I TNBC patients treated with NACT, did not confirm the benefit of capecitabine. Among patients with residual disease post-NACT, those who received capecitabine (57%) did not fare better than those who were observed (OS HR 0.65; 95% CI 0.30–1.44; P = 0.9)39.

Substantial emerging data suggest that restricting capecitabine solely to this “rescue' setting may underutilize its potential. Multiple meta-analyses and randomized trials now indicate a benefit when capecitabine is integrated into the chemotherapy backbone rather than being reserved for high-risk residual disease (Table 2). van Mackelenbergh et al., merging individual patient data from 3854 TNBC patients treated with NACT and ACT, demonstrated that the addition of capecitabine to anthracycline/taxane (A/T) regimens resulted in a significant benefit for DFS (HR 0.86; 95% CI 0.77–0.99; P = 0.046) and OS (HR 0.82; 95% CI 0.69-0.98; P = 0.033). Importantly, this benefit was noted even when CREATE-X patients were excluded, confirming the value of upfront integration of capecitabine [39]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Ye et al. reported significant improvements in survival outcomes in TNBC when capecitabine was added to the A/T regimen (DFS HR 0.76; 95% CI 0.65–0.89 and OS HR 0.76; 95% CI 0.62–0.94). Notably, the benefit for DFS appeared greater in node-negative patients compared with node-positive patients, a finding that further supports the use of this strategy in early-stage disease [40](Table 2).

Critical to our “surgery-first' proposal, recent data suggest that capecitabine is most effective when administered in the adjuvant rather than the neoadjuvant setting. In a network meta-analysis of 3661 TNBC patients, Li et al. identified the addition of capecitabine to adjuvant chemotherapy as the most effective strategy for improving DFS and OS, with a rank probability of approximately 81%. This strategy outperformed both the replacement of other drugs with capecitabine and, crucially, the use of capecitabine in the neoadjuvant setting [41] (Table S2, Table 2). This timing distinction was reinforced by Huo et al., whose analysis found a significant DFS improvement in the adjuvant subgroup (HR 0.73; P < 0.001), whereas the benefit in the neoadjuvant subgroup did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.75; P = 0.056) [42] (Table S2, Table 2).

The most robust evidence for our proposed regimen emerges from the FinXX trial [7,18,43]. Comparing standard adjuvant therapy (T-CEF) against a regimen integrating capecitabine (TX-CEX), the TNBC subgroup (n = 202) showed a clinically meaningful survival advantage after 15 years, with an absolute survival gain of 10.3% (HR 0.59; 95% CI 0.36–0.97). The magnitude of this benefit underscores the value of integration of capecitabine in the adjuvant setting (Table 3).

The FinXX trial reported higher rates of Grade 3/4 diarrhea (6% vs. 3%) and hand-foot syndrome (11% vs. <1%) in the experimental arm. To prevent severe capecitabine-induced diarrhea, DPYD genotyping to rule out dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase deficiency is now mandatory, in accordance with EMA and FDA guidelines [44,45] Furthermore, capecitabine dose reductions may be necessary to manage refractory hand-foot syndrome. It is important to note that the combination of docetaxel and capecitabine can result in cumulative gastrointestinal toxicity, even in patients with a DPYD wild-type genotype. Consequently, we strongly advise caution when prescribing the FinXX regimen to frail or elderly patients.

In conclusion, extensive data from meta-analyses and the long-term results of the FinXX trial provide convincing evidence that adding capecitabine to the adjuvant A/T backbone offers a robust survival benefit. This substantial benefit validates the rationale for a surgery-first, adjuvant-focused approach in Stage I TNBC.

The position of carboplatin (CBDCA) in early-stage TNBC

Despite extensive investigation, the role of adding carboplatin (CBDCA) to the anthracycline/taxane (A/T) backbone in the neoadjuvant setting remains elusive. While the majority of data consistently demonstrate a positive impact on pCR and event-free survival (EFS), the translation of these short-term benefits into an OS advantage remains controversial.

Several meta-analyses have attempted to resolve this issue, yet the data on OS remain equivocal (Supplementary Table S3). For instance, Poggio et al., in a meta-analysis titled “The end of the debate?', reported that platinum-based NACT was associated with significant increases in pCR and EFS. However, the reduction in the risk of death was not statistically significant (HR 0.82; 95% CI 0.64-1.04; P = NS) [46]. These data were confirmed by a recently published meta-analysis by Wu et al., including 2650 TNBC patients, which showed a significant improvement in the pCR and EFS rate but no OS benefit (HR 0.84; 95% CI 0.68–1.05; P = 0.119) [47].

In contrast, other meta-analyses such as those by Mason et al. [48] and Pathak et al. [49] have reported improvements in both DFS and OS. However, methodological limitations warrant caution when extrapolating these findings to Stage I (cT1c) disease. The analysis by Pathak et al. pooled results from trials using CBDCA in both the adjuvant and neoadjuvant settings, thereby obscuring the strategy-specific effects. Furthermore, the analysis relied on extracted aggregate data rather than individual patient records. Similarly, Mason et al. reported an OS benefit by aggregating high-risk Stage II/III populations; consequently, the absolute survival gain for Stage I patients remains hypothetical.

The dissociation between pCR rates and long-term survival is most evident in the recent pooled analysis of the BrighTNess (n = 471), CALGB 40603 (n = 351), and GeparSixto (n = 262) trials communicated by Felsheim et al. [50]. Despite a robust increase in pCR rates with the addition of CBDCA, this comprehensive analysis failed to confirm a significant OS benefit (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.65–1.31; P = 0.662). This finding reinforces the observation that pCR—while a valuable surrogate—does not consistently translate into improved long-term survival [51,52]. Remarkably, the most recent meta-analysis by Taglialatela et al. did not report on the impact of CBDCA addition on OS [53], further highlighting the uncertainty of this endpoint.

Data from the adjuvant setting further complicate the landscape, revealing potential ethnic or regional disparities in carboplatin efficacy (Supplementary Table S4). Trials conducted in Asian populations, such as the CITRINE [17] and RJBC 1501 [19] studies, have reported significant benefits in 5-year DFS and OS when CBDCA was added to the A/T backbone. Conversely, the U.S.-based NRG-BR003 trial found no significant benefit with the addition of CBDCA in 5-year iDFS (77.8% vs 82.9%; HR 0.78; 95% CI 0.58–1.06; P = 0.12) and 5-year OS (84.4% vs 87.7%; HR 0.81; 95% CI 0.57–1.16) [54].Furthermore, the CITRINE trial illustrated the complexity of long-term outcomes: while initially promising, the hazard ratio for DFS shifted in favour of the control arm after year three, raising concerns about the durability of the benefit [17].

A similar ethnic divergence emerges when examining anthracycline-omission strategies. In the predominantly Caucasian ABC trials [55], the non-anthracycline TC6 regimen failed to demonstrate noninferiority to TaxAC, with TaxAC retaining a modest IDFS advantage at 5 years (86.7% vs 85.1%), though without a significant OS difference between arms. By contrast, in the Chinese PATTERN trial [56], the non-anthracycline paclitaxel-carboplatin regimen was actually superior to standard anthracycline-taxane therapy in DFS, again without reaching a significant OS benefit (HR 0.71; 95% CI, 0.42–1.22; P = 0.22). This inconsistency further supports the possibility of population-specific chemosensitivity underlying the broader regional disparities observed with platinum-based strategies.

Current clinical data highlight a distinct divergence in how CBDCA and capecitabine affect patient outcomes. Carboplatin significantly increases the rate of pCR when added to standard neoadjuvant regimens but fails to regularly translate this into an OS benefit [46,47,50]. Given this lack of consistent survival data, the routine addition of CBDCA to the A/T backbone for Stage I TNBC remains controversial.

Conversely, capecitabine generally fails to improve pCR rates when used in the neoadjuvant setting (OR 0.97; 95% CI 0.80–1.17; P = 0.712) [39] but has demonstrated a significant and robust long-term OS improvement, particularly when utilized as adjuvant therapy [43].This suggests a mechanistic dichotomy: while platinum agents are potent at inducing local tumour control (pCR), capecitabine may be more effective in eradicating the chemo-resistant micrometastatic clones that ultimately decide the patient OS.

Reassessing the role of dose-dense chemotherapy

Current ESMO [6] and NCCN [33] guidelines favour a dose-dense (DD) chemotherapy approach for TNBC, a recommendation primarily driven by the 2019 EBCTCG meta-analysis [57]. However, strictly adhering to a DD schedule presents a significant logistical barrier to our proposed strategy, as it precludes the concurrent integration of adjuvant capecitabine, which is typically administered in 3-weekly cycles.

A critical reappraisal suggests that the perceived superiority of DD regimens may stem from trial design artifacts rather than an intrinsic biological advantage of increased density [58]. The EBCTCG meta-analysis frequently compared DD regimens against a control arm of 3-weekly paclitaxel [57]. Because 3-weekly paclitaxel is now recognized as an inferior schedule, this control arm may artificially inflate the perceived benefit of the dose-dense regimens.

This hypothesis is sustained by the PANTHER study [59], which utilized docetaxel rather than paclitaxel (Supplementary Table S5). In this trial, the difference between the dose-dense/tailored regimen and the standard 3-weekly regimen was not significant for breast cancer relapse-free survival in the ER-negative subgroup (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.64–1.35) nor was the OS significantly different for the entire population (P = 0.109). These findings imply that when an optimal taxane like docetaxel is utilized, the theoretical benefit of dose densification diminishes.

Furthermore, compressing the treatment intervals of anthracycline courses specifically has failed to improve outcomes in the majority of randomized trials [[60], [61], [62], [63], [64]].

However, in the randomized ECOG E1199 study [65], the weekly paclitaxel schedule emerged as a potentially superior taxane approach in the adjuvant setting, including for the TNBC subtype. Regarding the potential impact of ECOG E1199 on our proposal, we acknowledge the following: The ECOG E1199 trial utilized a 2x2 factorial design, employing paclitaxel 3-weekly as the standard comparator for all experimental arms. Because there was no direct, head-to-head statistical comparison between weekly paclitaxel and docetaxel 3-weekly, the trial's results cannot be extrapolated to conclude that weekly paclitaxel is superior to docetaxel 3-weekly. Furthermore, the inclusion criteria for E1199 were restricted to patients with node-positive disease (tumor stage T1, T2, or T3; nodal stage N1 or N2) or high-risk, axillary node-negative disease (T2 or T3, N0). Consequently, our specific target population (cT1c N0) was excluded from that analysis, limiting its applicability to our proposal. Of note, for the cT1c N0M0 population, the EBCTCG meta-analysis did not demonstrate a significant benefit for the DD approach in node-negative patients (ratio for annual events: 0.93) [57].

Moreover, our proposal utilizes a combination of docetaxel and capecitabine, which demonstrated a survival benefit compared to docetaxel alone in the metastatic setting [66]. This clinical outcome supports the synergistic activity of these two chemotherapeutic agents, as established in preclinical experiments demonstrating that docetaxel induces thymidine phosphorylase activity, thereby enhancing capecitabine efficacy [67]. Finally, in the FinXX trial, the experimental combination of docetaxel and capecitabine also proved superior to the standard docetaxel-alone arm, particularly in the TNBC subset.

Limitations

Before concluding, we acknowledge several limitations.

First, much of the evidence for ACT superiority in Stage I TNBC comes from retrospective analyses, inherently susceptible to selection bias [68]. However, since pivotal randomized NACT trials enrolled predominantly Stage II–III patients, high-quality RW data remain the only dedicated evidence base for the cT1c N0M0 population, and our key references [14,15] used rigorous PSM methodology to approximate randomized conditions. Even so, these datasets may carry specific limitations as their NACT cohorts pool heterogeneous regimens without stratification by type or intensity; moreover they do not reliably capture whether non-pCR patients received post-neoadjuvant escalation (e.g., capecitabine), now standard since CREATE-X. This last gap is important, as it means the NACT comparator likely mixes optimally and suboptimally treated patients, potentially biasing comparisons against NACT relative to contemporary practice. This limitation is partly offset by a finding within the Zheng et al. meta-analysis itself: although the OS benefit of ACT over NACT in Stage I (cT1c) TNBC reached only borderline significance [15], patients treated with upfront ACT achieved survival equivalent to those who achieved pCR after NACT. This supports the safety of surgery-first as comparable to the “best-case' neoadjuvant outcome, without the risk of progression during NACT.

Second, the FinXX trial's TNBC subset was small (12.4% of 1495 patients), and while 41.9% were pT1, only 11.5% were pN0, leaving no clear data on the stage I (pT1c pN0) cohort specifically; these results are therefore exploratory for our population of interest. We nonetheless consider the long-term survival data robust, as they align with meta-analyses confirming adjuvant capecitabine's benefit [[39], [40], [41], [42]].

Third, certain TNBC histologies (adenoid cystic, secretory, medullary, metaplastic) derive little benefit from adjuvant ChT [6], and our recommendations should not be extended to these subtypes.

Fourth, our algorithm is restricted to patients managed without checkpoint inhibitors, per current ESMO guidance for cT1c N0M0 disease [6]. The rationale shifts fundamentally with immunotherapy: the negative iDFS result of ALEXANDRA/IMpassion030 [69] (adjuvant atezolizumab after surgery) contrasts with the durable benefit of neoadjuvant pembrolizumab in KEYNOTE-5225, a dichotomy also recognized by the 2025 St. Gallen consensus [13]. This suggests chemo-immunotherapy synergy depends on immune priming in the presence of an intact primary tumor, an advantage unavailable in the adjuvant setting; our sequencing principles would need reassessment if checkpoint inhibitors become indicated for stage I disease.

Finally, regarding de-escalation: our concern that 18-week NACT prolongs chemoresistant patients' cytotoxic exposure invites comparison with shorter regimens. The Neo-N trial [70] achieved 53% pCR with 12 weeks of nivolumab plus carboplatin/paclitaxel (anthracycline-free) in stage I–II TNBC, and de Graaf et al. [71] confirm pCR's strong prognostic value in stage I disease (adjusted OS HR 0.23; 95% CI 0.11–0.45). Shorter IO-free regimens have also been prospectively investigated: in WSG-ADAPT-TN, 12 weeks of nab-paclitaxel/carboplatin achieved a 46% pCR, though this pCR advantage was largely attenuated at the survival level once non-responders received adjuvant anthracycline “rescue,' limiting conclusions about the adequacy of a short-term chemotherapy program alone [72]. However, Neo-N relies on a chemo-immunotherapy backbone rather than pure de-escalation, de Graaf et al. is retrospective without a de-escalated-vs-standard comparison, and none of these trials includes an ACT comparator arm. In the absence of a head-to-head trial, surgery-first followed by ACT rests on stronger comparative evidence, while de-escalation remains a promising alternative awaiting mature data.
