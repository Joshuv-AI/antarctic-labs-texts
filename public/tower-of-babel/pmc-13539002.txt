Reclaiming the Human Connection in Clinical Care With AI
Authors: James A. Colbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The patient-provider relationship is becoming increasingly fractured, with many clinicians overwhelmed by administrative tasks and patients often feeling unheard. Often missing from clinical care is what this author has termed “human connection” which refers to the relational quality of encounters characterized by active listening, eye contact, empathic responsiveness, shared decision-making, and the patient’s sense of being seen and understood by their provider. While the electronic health record is often viewed as a cause of this disconnect, artificial intelligence (AI) offers a meaningful opportunity to help restore that bond. By automating clerical tasks such as documentation, inbox management, and care navigation, AI can help providers reclaim the time and cognitive space necessary for empathetic, human-centered care. This article draws on both the published evidence and the author’s own experience to examine the impact ambient and agentic AI can have on the clinical experience for both patients and providers while also acknowledging the risks of AI and the necessary safeguards for using AI in a clinical environment. When deployed with care and oversight, AI can allow clinicians to reclaim the human connection that is at the core of the care experience.

Introduction

As a healthcare provider I know firsthand that medicine is an art as much as it is an objective science. In my own experience I find that the relational experience, or “human connection” between myself and my patients is foundational to the work that I do. For the purposes of this article, “human connection” refers to the relational quality of clinical encounters—characterized by active listening, sustained eye contact, empathic responsiveness, integration of the patient’s values into decision-making, and the patient’s sense of being seen and understood. It is the quality that patients consistently identify as central to trust and therapeutic alliance, and that clinicians identify as the reason they entered the profession.

Over many years of clinical practice I have felt an erosion of that human connection with my patients, often driven by competing demands that draw my focus away from each patient, and that for the purposes of this article I have termed a structural “administrative tax.” This term, used throughout this article, refers to the cumulative burden of documentation, inbox management, billing, prior authorization, and other non-clinical tasks that displace time and attention from direct patient care. Time-based studies have shown that for every hour spent in direct patient care, many physicians now spend two additional hours on documentation and clerical tasks. 1

This administrative burden produces a visible disconnect in the exam room. Providers, tethered to the Electronic Health Record (EHR), often find themselves typing with their backs to the patient, their attention fractured by the need to meet compliance and coding requirements. Providers are measured on productivity, throughput, gap closures and compliance rates, and these outputs drive behavior.

The result for the patient is an experience of often feeling “unseen.” This manifests in recognizable ways: a patient sharing a sensitive mental health concern while the provider is focused on clicking checkboxes; a patient waiting days for a response to a portal message, only to receive a curt, one-sentence reply that lacks context or warmth; a patient feeling abandoned after hospital discharge because they do not know where to seek guidance. These experiences contribute to moral injury for the provider—the distress caused by being unable to deliver the high-quality, empathetic care they were trained to give—and to a sense of transactional coldness for the patient. 2

In this article I will discuss how AI, when deployed thoughtfully and with appropriate safeguards, has the potential to serve as a relational tool to bolster the provider-patient relationship. The sections that follow examine three domains where AI shows particular promise—clinical documentation, inbox and administrative management, and patient navigation—followed by practical recommendations for healthcare organizations seeking to implement these tools responsibly.

Actionable Insights: AI as a Relational Tool

Ambient AI: Restoring the Foundational Clinical Experience

Ambient AI technology creates a recording of the patient-provider encounter and leverages AI to auto-generate a clinical note based on pre-specified prompts and templates. By utilizing ambient listening for documentation, the provider is freed from the keyboard and can shift focus directly to the patient. As a provider I use Commure Ambient AI in my own practice, and I have also helped to support implementation of Commure Ambient AI across thousands of providers. Beyond Commure, there are dozens of other ambient AI products, and they all share the same basic features of taking a recording of a patient encounter and using AI to create a note.

Evidence from the American Medical Association (AMA) suggests that AI scribes are saving up to 15,000 hours of documentation time across large health systems. 3 Providers using Commure Ambient AI are on average saving 3–8 minutes per provider note, representing multiple hours saved per provider each week. However, the true value lies in the restoration of “clinical presence”—a purposeful state of awareness and connection that the administrative tax of the EHR routinely displaces. 4 When a provider no longer needs to worry about capturing every detail of a history, they can return to active listening—maintaining eye contact, observing non-verbal cues, and engaging in deeper diagnostic conversations.

The impact on the human dimension of care is measurable. Efficiency gains in documentation do not automatically translate to improvement in empathy or patient experience, but studies have already found a strong signal that many providers are using that freed time toward relational engagement. According to the AMA study, 47% of patients noted their doctor spent less time looking at the computer; 39% observed their doctor spent more time speaking directly with them, and 56% reported a positive impact on the overall quality of the visit. 3 A 2025 study in JAMA Network Open demonstrated significant reduction in provider burnout as well as improvement in a provider’s “ability to provide undivided attention” while using ambient AI. 5

As ambient AI adoption becomes more routine in clinical encounters, consent and privacy must never be compromised. Ambient listening tools must be strictly opt-in: patients must be informed, in plain language, that a recording or AI-assisted transcription is occurring, and must affirmatively consent before any encounter is captured. Data handling must conform to the highest standards of HIPAA-compliance. Finally, clinicians must continue to maintain accountability for documentation accuracy and completeness, which entails reviewing, approving, and signing off on AI-drafted notes before they become part of the medical record.

Automating the Administrative Tax

Applications of AI in care delivery extend far beyond clinical documentation. Much of the administrative burden for healthcare providers involves tasks outside the exam room: patient communication, triage, chart review, billing, and reimbursement.

AI presents a significant opportunity to mitigate the burnout-inducing workload of inbox management. Providers often receive hundreds of notifications daily; AI-driven triage systems can categorize messages and draft preliminary responses, ensuring urgent clinical needs are prioritized while routine requests are handled efficiently. 6 AI’s ability to conduct rapid chart reviews—synthesizing years of fragmented data into a cohesive clinical narrative—allows providers to gain a deeper understanding of the patient’s history without the cognitive fatigue of manual data mining. AI is also beginning to streamline prior authorization by cross-referencing clinical notes with payer requirements, reducing care delays and the administrative friction that erodes the patient-provider relationship. 7

However, for any use of AI that impacts patient care, it remains imperative that clinicians remain in control of patient care decisions. AI-generated draft responses and summaries must be reviewed and edited by the clinician before use; and provider training should cover not only how to use these tools but how to critically evaluate their outputs. Organizations should build structured onboarding and ongoing competency review into any AI deployment program, particularly for applications that touch patient-facing communication.

AI and Patient Navigation

Additionally, there is a transformative opportunity for AI to act as a digital navigator, addressing the fragmentation and friction patients face outside the clinical visit. Current healthcare systems are often criticized for their opacity; patients frequently feel lost in the transitions between specialists or following hospital discharge, leading to decreased adherence and heightened anxiety. AI-assisted conversational agents and virtual assistants are bridging this gap by providing real-time, 24/7 access to targeted health information and administrative support. Research indicates that the use of these agents is associated with a significant 7.51% increase in patient experience scores related to communication and access. 8 National survey data suggests that nearly 60% of patients are open to the use of AI in these capacities if it translates to more meaningful face-time during their clinical appointments, 9 though this finding reflects survey-reported preferences rather than observed behavior, and should be interpreted accordingly.

The clinical utility of this technology is particularly evident in high-stakes longitudinal care journeys, such as oncology and perioperative care. In these contexts, AI can function as a persistent coach rather than a static set of discharge instructions. In my own work, I have been involved in developing AI-driven tools to support patients through complex clinical preparations and active treatment.

My team worked with a health system to develop an AI conversational agent that helped patients to successfully navigate the colonoscopy preparation process. We tackled this use case because this particular clinical journey is challenging for many patients to navigate. When patients get confused or are unable to complete their preparation, the procedure must be cancelled. We started our project by interviewing patients to understand the top questions and pain points they faced during this journey. Then we spent time with clinicians, schedulers and nurses to map out their workflows. What emerged was an AI assistant that patients could choose to engage with as an additional resource as they prepared for their upcoming procedure. Those patients who opted-in to the service received proactive guidance and education that they could access at home on their phones. Furthermore, patients could text questions to the assistant 24/7 and get a response.

To ensure the safety of this program, we worked with the clinical team to curate a list of questions and validated responses that the assistant would provide. The program operated with a human in the loop such that at any point the patient could ask to be connected with a member of the care team. Furthermore, we built in red flag alerts to ensure that critical symptoms and concerns would trigger a high priority message to a clinician as well as a note to the patient telling them to seek medical care.

Ultimately the assistant was a great success. Thousands of patients were successfully supported through their clinical journey, with a prep completion rate of 96% and a net promoter score of over 90, indicating that more than 9 out of 10 patients who tried the service would strongly recommend it to others. Most importantly, the AI was able to identify hundreds of patients with unmet needs who were connected with resources to help with transportation or medication assistance.

Tools such as this AI conversational agent help to augment the care team and allow patients to feel connected to their providers even when physically distant. This shift from reactive to proactive navigation not only improves clinical outcomes but enhances the patient experience as well. Importantly, all patient-facing AI navigation tools should be designed with the same consent and privacy standards applied to ambient documentation: patients should understand what the AI can and cannot do, how their data is used, and how to reach a human clinician when needed.

Measurement alone is insufficient without a monitoring strategy. Organizations should establish baseline values for each indicator before deployment, define threshold criteria for intervention (e.g., a meaningful decline in patient perception of “undivided attention” should trigger a clinical workflow review), and build in structured review cycles—ideally quarterly—to assess whether AI-driven efficiency gains are being directed toward enhanced relational care or absorbed into increased patient volume.

Listen to Your Providers and Patients, and Use Their Feedback to Guide AI Deployment

Technology decisions should never be made by administrators without meaningful input from the clinicians who will use the tools and the patients who will be affected by them. Ensure that key clinical constituencies are represented on AI evaluation committees and are involved in vendor selection and use-case prioritization. My experience has reinforced that deployment decisions made without frontline clinical input consistently underestimate workflow integration challenges and overestimate adoption rates.

Before, during, and after deployment, mechanisms must be in place to allow providers to voice feedback and enable a continuous optimization cycle. Healthcare organizations should also involve Patient and Family Advisory Councils (PFACs) to evaluate how AI integration affects the care experience—not as a one-time step but as an ongoing iterative process.

Choose AI Technology Partners, Not Vendors

The success of any AI initiative depends heavily on selecting the right technology partners. Begin by convening a multistakeholder group to oversee the evaluation process and define the criteria that reflect the needs of all constituencies. Prioritize partners that demonstrate deep flexibility and a commitment to true partnership over rigid, off-the-shelf offerings.

In addition, organizations must prioritize vendors that demonstrate deep flexibility and a commitment to true partnership rather than offering rigid, “off-the-shelf” solutions. A seamless workflow is only achievable if the technology can achieve native-level integration with the EHR and other critical systems of record, ensuring data continuity and reducing the need for providers to toggle between disparate platforms. Choosing a partner that values collaborative co-development—often involving dedicated boots on the ground support—ensures that the tool is optimized for the specific clinical realities of your institution. Equally important is evaluating a partner’s approach to model versioning and transparency. As AI models are updated, clinical outputs may change. Organizations should require that vendors communicate meaningful model changes proactively and provide mechanisms for clinical re-validation before updated versions are deployed in care settings.

Put in Place Appropriate Guardrails to Mitigate risk—including Protecting Relational Time

The use of AI in healthcare carries real risks that must be actively managed. Known concerns include algorithmic bias, data privacy vulnerabilities, and the potential for AI-generated errors or hallucinations. Bias deserves particular attention: large language models may not be trained on datasets that adequately represent minority and underrepresented populations, with the potential for systematic inequities in AI-assisted care. A “trust but verify” posture is essential—AI outputs must be reviewed by a qualified clinician before influencing clinical decisions, and formal AI governance processes should provide ongoing oversight with a lens toward patient safety and equity.

One risk that receives insufficient attention is the potential for efficiency gains to be redirected toward volume rather than quality. If AI saves a provider 45 minutes per day, that time can be reinvested in deeper patient relationships—or absorbed into an increased patient schedule. Healthcare organizations and their operational leaders must make an intentional, explicit commitment to protect the relational time that AI creates. This may require policy guardrails, scheduling constraints, or performance metrics that reward relationship quality rather than throughput alone. Without this, AI risks becoming a tool for cost extraction rather than care enhancement.

Finally, training is a prerequisite, not an afterthought. Clinicians must be equipped not only to use AI tools but to critically evaluate their outputs, understand their limitations, and maintain appropriate accountability for AI-assisted documentation and communication.

Conclusion

The impact of AI in healthcare settings depends on how healthcare leaders choose to deploy and steward these tools. Based on the evidence reviewed here and my own experience with AI, I remain cautiously optimistic. When AI is deployed with appropriate oversight, genuine partnership, and a clear commitment to human-centered goals, it can restore the relational time and presence that the administrative tax has systematically eroded. That outcome is not guaranteed, but intentionality and attention to governance, safety and transparency can help organizations succeed in expanding the space for human connection in clinical care.
