Growth patterns, sexual dimorphism, and seasonal body mass variation in Plains Zebra of the Serengeti Region, Tanzania
Authors: Saeideh Esmaeili, Peter J P Gogan, Kathryn A Schoenecker, Ronald O Skoog, Neville Pillay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKnowledge of growth patterns and physical properties of animals helps researchers assess population characteristics and health in the field. Information on age-specific body measurements and growth patterns is rare in ungulates because examining a large portion of a population is time-, budget-, and personnel-intensive in the wild. We collected age- and sex-specific body measurements from 442 Plains Zebra (Equus quagga boehmi) culled in the Serengeti region, Tanzania, between 1969 and 1971. The dataset contained 307 males and 133 females. We found that males were on average 2.8% larger than females in body measurements of heart girth, height at shoulder, neck-body length, hind foot length, and the body mass, indicating minimal sexual dimorphism in adult Plains Zebra. We used polynomial regression to explore the relationship between heart girth and body mass and applied third-degree polynomial regression models to predict body mass for 34 females and 115 males with recorded girth but missing body mass records. We applied 5 non-linear models—Gompertz, von Bertalanffy, Bertalanffy-Pütter, logistic, and Richards—to characterize sex-specific growth trajectories for each trait. Our results showed that different traits followed different growth patterns, with size asymptotes reached by ∼2 to 5 yr depending on trait and sex. Seasonal trends in adult body mass were also evaluated using generalized linear models incorporating sex and cyclical effects of day of year. While female body mass showed little seasonal variation, adult males exhibited a 16.9% increase in mass during the wet season, followed by a sharp decline, suggesting greater seasonal plasticity and energetic investment. Our work is one of the few studies on body size and growth in wild equids, which provides new information on age- and sex-specific growth trajectories for comparison across zebra subspecies over time and space.Keywords: Bertalanffy–Pütter, Equidae, Equus quagga, Gompertz, growth model, logistic, morphometrics, Richards, seasonal mass changes, von Bertalanffy

Accurate knowledge of physical and morphological traits, such as sex, age, and body weight of an animal is fundamental to the field of ecology because these traits underpin demographic assessments, individual-based monitoring, and general conservation of populations (Cook et al. 2004; Rowland et al. 2018). The ability to classify animals based on sex and age is key to assessing growth trajectories, population dynamics, health status, and habitat-use patterns (Gaillard et al. 2000; Bonenfant et al. 2009). However, obtaining reliable data on age and body mass in free-ranging populations of large wild herbivores—particularly those that are not subject to hunting or handling—remains challenging. Direct measurements typically require immobilization or carcass recovery, both of which are often logistically difficult and ethically constrained, particularly for populations in protected areas (Fuentes-Allende et al. 2023). As a result, ecologists must often rely on indirect indicators such as photogrammetry, morphometric proxies, or growth models derived from limited culling records or zoo populations (Shrader et al. 2006; Bergeron 2007), although remote methods are recently becoming available (Fuentes-Allende et al. 2023).

Despite these limitations, a range of indirect techniques to estimate age and body condition in wild animals has been developed. Tooth eruption and wear patterns are frequently used to estimate age in ungulates (Spinage 1973), although accuracy can decline in older individuals and may vary across species and environments. Similarly, morphometric measurements such as shoulder height, heart girth, and body length have been used as proxies for body mass or condition (Pérez-Barbería and Gordon 1999; Cook et al. 2004, Shrader et al. 2006). Advances in non-invasive methods, including photogrammetry (Bell et al. 1997; Bergeron 2007; Berger et al. 2012; Leorna et al. 2022) and remote imaging technologies (Rahman et al. 2023; Napoli et al. 2024), provide novel methods for estimating body size and condition without physical capture. However, the development of reliable prediction models that link morphological traits with body mass requires high-quality reference data, often obtained from culled or captive populations where both morphometric and mass data are available. Such reference datasets are especially important for long-lived species like equids, where growth rates and body condition vary with age, sex, and season (Owen-Smith 1988; Rubenstein 2010).

Within Plains Zebra (Equus quagga), variation in growth rate, asymptotic size, and sexual dimorphism has been reported across the 5 extant subspecies (Groves and Bell 2004), but comprehensive datasets from wild populations are rare. Grubb (1981) reviewed available body mass data on E. quagga and reported that E. q. boehmi from East Africa was generally lighter and showed more pronounced sexual dimorphism in adult body mass than did the southern African subspecies (E. q. burchellii). Smuts (1975) also documented larger body size and greater mass in both sexes of E. q. burchellii relative to E. q. boehmi, suggesting ecological or genetic influences on growth trajectories. However, these insights are based on limited data and lack seasonal context for E. q. boehmi (Sachs 1967).

To build on these foundational studies and address gaps in subspecies-level comparisons, we sought to determine sex-specific growth rates and body mass, allometric relationships, and seasonal variation in body mass in E. q. boehmi. To do this, we collected body mass and morphometric measurements from Plains Zebra culled in the Loliondo Game Controlled Area (LGCA), Tanzania, by a private company (Wildlife Services, Pty) in 1969 and morphometric measurements from Plains Zebra culled by the Tanzania Game Department in 1970 and 1971. These data provide a rare opportunity to quantify allometric growth patterns, the extent of sexual dimorphism in a wild population, and seasonal variation in body mass. While opportunistic, such datasets remain among the few available sources for body condition and growth assessments in wild Plains Zebra, absent non-invasive techniques, and can contribute to a broader understanding of intraspecific variation across E. quagga populations.

Methods

Study area

The Serengeti region, encompassing approximately 35,000 km2 across northern Tanzania and southern Kenya (between 1° and 3°30′ South, and 34° to 36° East), is defined by the open grasslands of the southeast and the savannas of the north and west (Supplementary Data SD1). This landscape is seasonally used by Plains Zebra and Wildebeest during their annual migrations (Grzimek and Grzimek 1960; Talbot and Talbot 1963). Much of the Tanzanian portion of this region falls within the boundaries of Serengeti National Park (SNP) and adjacent conservation areas, including the Loliondo Game Controlled Area (LGCA). These seasonal movements are driven by spatial and temporal variation in rainfall. The wet season spans from November to May, peaking in January and February, and accounts for approximately 80% of the region’s mean annual precipitation (Norton-Griffiths et al. 1975; Mahony 2020). During the June to September dry season, the remaining 20% of rainfall is largely restricted to the woodland zones of the north and west. Annual rainfall totals also vary spatially, with the northwestern Serengeti receiving nearly 3 times more precipitation than the southeastern grasslands (Norton-Griffiths et al. 1975; Mahony 2020). This variability in precipitation produces corresponding shifts in primary productivity, resulting in dynamic changes in the distribution of nutritious forage. While green forage is broadly available across the landscape at the end of the wet season in May, it becomes restricted to the northwest by the end of the dry season in September (McNaughton 1985).

Except for approximately 15,000 resident zebra occupying SNP’s western sector year-round, an estimated 100,000 zebra occupying the northwestern SNP and adjacent Kenya during the dry season migrate southward through SNP to access wet-season ranges. An additional 30,000 zebra move southward from dry season ranges through the LGCA, immediately east of SNP, to intermingle with those zebra moving through SNP on the open grasslands of the southeast during the wet season (Skoog 1970; Maddock 1979). In Plains Zebra, foals are born year-round, but births are concentrated between October and March, with a peak during January to March in SNP and the Ngorongoro system (Klingel 1969; Rubenstein 1987). Given a gestation period of 378 to 396 d (Klingel 1969; Smuts 1976), mating occurs throughout the year but peaks during the same calendar window in the preceding year (Nunez et al. 2011).

Zebra body measurements

We used body measurement data from zebra that had been culled to reduce numbers under the authority of the Tanzania Game Department. Culling practices were opportunistic, but adult males were more vulnerable to culling than females and younger males because of their tendency to hang-back behind their family groups. Thus, our sample size is biased toward adult males. Zebra were culled in March through June 1969, and during March through May 1970 and June 1971 (Supplementary Data SD2). We sexed culled zebra by inspection of external genitalia and aged them based on wear and replacement of upper incisors (Klingel 1965; Klingel and Klingel 1966). We assigned age to a year for zebra ≤4 yr old and designated 4 age classes for zebra ≥5 yr old as 5 to 8 yr, 9 to 12 yr, 13 to 16 yr, and 17 to ≥20 yr to account for overlap in the aging criteria age estimates using tooth replacement and wear (Klingel 1965; Klingel and Klingel 1966). We took the following body measurements in millimeters using a flexible steel tape shortly after death in all 3 yr and converted them to centimeters: (i) Heart girth–chest circumference measured immediately behind the forelegs; (ii) height at shoulder, from the withers to the hoof tip of fore leg; (iii) Neck–body, from the anterior atlas joint to the sacro-coccygeal joint along the body contours to the base of the tail; and (iv) Hind foot, from the hock to the tip of the hoof. We recorded the whole weight of zebra using a hanging spring balance to the closest pound in 1969 and then converted the weight to kilograms. To adjust maternal body weight, we subtracted the fetal weight plus an additional 10% to account for placental tissues and fluids—an approach consistent with large herbivore studies that estimate gestational tissues by adding approximately 10% to 15% of fetal weight to total body mass (Robbins and Robbins 1979). All measurements were conducted by R. O. Skoog and P. J. P. Gogan.

Statistical analysis

Our dataset comprised 440 records of body measurements from Plains Zebra. Of these, 133 were females, and 307 were males; 80 of the females were pregnant. For 26 pregnant females, we had corresponding body mass and fetal mass records. We excluded pregnant females without fetal mass records from analyses involving body mass to avoid introducing systematic bias. We assumed that pregnancy did not affect other body measurements following Smuts (1974).

To assess sexual dimorphism in morphology, we restricted comparisons between females and males to adults (n = 399), excluding juveniles (<2 yr old, n = 28) and the oldest individuals (>20 yr old, n = 13) due to small sample sizes. We used Student’s t-test to compare the means between the body measurements of females and males. Where the assumptions of a parametric test were not met, we used Wilcoxon-ranked test for comparisons. Because we did not record all body measurements for every sample, the sample size differs across measurements and statistical tests.

For individuals lacking direct body mass records, we predicted mass using univariate linear regression models based on morphometric traits. We limited analyses to univariate models to avoid multicollinearity, a common issue in morphometric datasets due to high inter-variable correlations (Jakob et al. 1996). Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed between body mass and 3 standard measurements: heart girth, neck-body length, and shoulder height, for males and females separately (Supplementary Data SD3). Heart girth showed the strongest correlation with mass in both sexes (r > 0.90; Supplementary Data SD3), consistent with prior findings in equids and other large mammals (Pérez-Barbería and Gordon 1999; Shrader et al. 2006). Accordingly, we modeled the heart girth–body mass relationship using both linear and non-linear regression models for each sex. The best-fit model was selected based on Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), adjusted R2, and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE; Burnham and Anderson 2004). We predicted mass of females and males with recorded girth but missed mass (n = 34 females, n = 115 males) using the best supported heart girth-mass model with incorporating residual variation into model predictions to use in the growth function described below. Individuals with heart girth values outside the fitted data range were excluded to avoid extrapolation beyond empirical support (n = 9 females, n = 15 males).

To examine growth dynamics, we modeled the relationship between age and each body measurement (measured and predicted body mass, female n = 91 and male n = 193; heart girth, female n = 78 and male n = 173; height at shoulder, female n = 96 and male n = 213; hind foot, female n = 98 and male n = 215; and neck-body length, female n = 114 and male n = 248) using 5 common non-linear growth functions: Gompertz; von Bertalanffy; Bertalanffy-Pütter; logistic; and Richards models. These models have been widely applied to describe growth in large mammals, including equids (Zullinger et al. 1984; Shrader et al. 2006; Pellegrini et al. 2024). For instance, the Gompertz model has been utilized to analyze growth patterns in various animal species due to its flexibility in modeling sigmoidal growth curves (Kirkwood 1985). The von Bertalanffy model has been applied to describe the growth of meat-producing mammals such as bovines, pigs, rabbits, and sheep, as well as wildlife species and other large mammals, including ungulates, carnivores, and marine mammals (Smuts 1974; Suzuki et al. 2001; Bartareau 2019; Fernandes et al. 2019; Agbayani et al. 2020). The Bertalanffy-Pütter model offers a unifying framework for studying growth models and has been employed to identify best-fitting growth curves for mass-at-age data in mammals (Tjørve and Tjørve 2017). Logistic growth models have been effectively used to model mammalian growth (Zullinger et al. 1984). The Richards model, known for its flexibility, has been applied to depict diverse growth courses in mammalian species, accommodating variations in growth patterns (Winship et al. 2001). For each sex, we fitted all 5 models and selected the best-fitting model for each trait based on AIC. Only individuals with both known age and corresponding measurement were included in each model fit.

Finally, to assess seasonal variation in body mass, we modeled adult body mass as a function of sex and time using a generalized linear model (GLM) with a Gamma distribution and a log link. The predictors included sex (as a categorical variable) and the cyclical effect of day of year, modeled using sine and cosine transformations of the day of year, and their interaction. This approach captures intra-annual variation while appropriately accounting for the circular nature of seasonal time (Stolwijk et al. 1999). The inclusion of interaction terms between day of year and sex allowed the model to estimate sex-specific seasonal trends in body mass. We restricted analyses to adult males and females with complete records for body mass and measurement date. Seasonal trends in body mass are expected in large tropical herbivores—including Plains Zebra—due to periodic fluctuations in rainfall, forage availability, and nutritional condition (Fowler 1981; Ogutu et al. 2014).

Results

We found differences in all body measurements between adult male and female E. q. bohmi, confirming minimal sexual dimorphism (Table 1). Adult males were significantly larger than females in heart girth, shoulder height, hind foot length, neck-body length, and mean body mass (P < 0.01). After excluding pregnant females without fetal mass records or adjustment, the recorded body mass of adult females (n = 44) ranged from 198.0 to 320.3 kg (mean ± SD = 273.2 ± 26.9 kg), whereas adult males (n = 61) ranged from 211.4 to 340.2 (mean ± SD = 288.4 ± 29.2 kg), being 4.3% heavier than females (Table 1).

Among the morphometric traits evaluated, heart girth was most strongly correlated with body mass in both sexes (females, r = 0.93, t = 14.21, df = 30, P < 0.001; males, r = 0.97, t = 27.45, df = 38, P < 0.001; Supplementary Data SD3), outperforming neck-body length, shoulder height, and hind foot length (Supplementary Data SD3). The best predictive model for body mass based on heart girth was a third-degree polynomial regression for both sexes (Fig. 1; Supplementary Data SD4). The final model was used to estimate the body mass of 34 females and 115 males lacking direct mass measurements, provided their heart girth fell within the empirical range of the model (Table 1).

Growth analyses identified the Bertalanffy–Pütter model as the best-fitting curve for describing the relationship between body mass and age in both sexes (Fig. 2A and B; Supplementary Data SD5). Among morphometric traits, the best-supported nonlinear models varied by trait and sex (Supplementary Data SD5; Fig. 3A and B). In females (Fig. 3A), the logistic model best described hind foot growth (n = 98), the von Bertalanffy model best fit shoulder height (n = 96) and neck-body length (n = 114), while the Bertalanffy–Pütter model best fit heart girth (n = 78). In males (Fig. 3B), heart girth (n = 173) and neck-body length (n = 248) followed Bertalanffy–Pütter growth, shoulder height (n = 213) was best modeled by von Bertalanffy, and hind foot (n = 215) again followed a logistic growth curve.

We found weak seasonal trends in adult female body mass, with neither the sine term (P = 0.27) nor the cosine term (P = 0.63) showing statistical significance individually. In contrast, male body mass exhibited a seasonal pattern, as indicated by the statistically significant interaction between male sex and the cosine-transformed day of the year (β = –0.23 ± 0.08 SE, P = 0.008), suggesting that seasonal fluctuations in body mass differed by sex (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the positive but marginally non-significant interaction between sex and sine-transformed day of the year (β = 0.31 ± 0.18 SE, P = 0.078) further supports the presence of sex-specific seasonal dynamics. Predicted body mass trends revealed that males experienced greater seasonal amplitude compared to females, with peak male body mass occurring during the mid-to-late wet season. Males gained approximately 45 kg over a 45-d period (21 March to 30 April), averaging 1 kg/d and representing a 16.9% increase in body mass. This gain was followed by an approximate 8% loss in body mass (∼25 kg) over 35 d (30 April 30–early June) at a rate of 0.7 kg/d. In contrast, females exhibited comparatively modest fluctuations, gaining approximately 5 kg over 25 d (15 March to 10 April) at a rate of 0.2 kg/day, equivalent to a 1.8% increase in body mass. They subsequently lost 15 kg over 50 d (April 10 to early June), averaging 0.3 kg/d, which corresponds to a 5.4% reduction in body mass. Sexual dimorphism in body mass became more noticeable after 21 March, with females briefly outweighing males between 11 March and 21 March, potentially indicating a seasonal reversal of sexual dimorphism. However, during the study, female body mass remained relatively stable, exhibiting only mild seasonal fluctuations (Fig. 4). We did not have any recorded or estimated mass between June and March.

Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive analysis of growth patterns, sexual dimorphism, and seasonal body mass dynamics in the Serengeti population of E. q. boehmi, using one of the most extensive morphometric datasets available for this subspecies. By integrating direct and model-predicted body mass with age and multiple body measurements, we quantified sex-specific growth trajectories and identified differences in developmental timing and asymptotic size among traits. This approach allowed us to compare the performance of multiple nonlinear growth models and to distinguish traits that stabilize once skeletal maturity is reached from those that remain responsive to environmental conditions. Although focused on Plains Zebra, these findings are broadly relevant to other equids and large terrestrial herbivores that exhibit prolonged growth, minimal sexual dimorphism, and strong seasonal resource dependence.

Morphometric values for shoulder height, heart girth, and hind foot length were consistent with earlier reports for E. q. boehmi from the Serengeti region (Sachs 1967). Across all traits, males were slightly larger than females, confirming that sexual dimorphism in this subspecies is present but minimal. Both sexes were smaller than reported values for E. q. burchellii (Smuts 1975), which supports morphological differentiation among Plains Zebra subspecies. However, the magnitude of sexual dimorphism observed here is small relative to that documented for many other ungulates, particularly cervids and bovids, in which size divergence between the sexes is common (Lindenfors et al. 2007). Instead, the pattern observed in Plains Zebra is broadly consistent with equids more generally, in which sex differences in size are subtle (Winans 1985; Rubenstein 1987). Such limited dimorphism likely reflects the mating system and social structure of equids, in which extreme size divergence between the sexes may be constrained by the demands of cursorial locomotion and group living (Rubenstein 1987; Ruckstuhl and Neuhaus 2000; Nacarino-Meneses 2023). Moreover, we found that linear traits reached asymptotic values by approximately 2 to 3 yr of age, with no clear separation between late juveniles and adults. As a result, in Plains Zebra, as in other equids, sex and age class are often difficult to distinguish based on morphology alone, particularly at a distance. Reliable field identification, therefore, typically requires additional cues, including behavior, social context, reproductive associations (e.g., harem structure or presence of foals), external genitalia, or individual markings (Smuts 1976; Rubenstein 2010).

Growth trajectories differed among morphometric traits, highlighting trait-specific developmental constraints common to equids and other large mammals. Structurally fixed traits such as shoulder height and hind foot length approached asymptotic skeletal size relatively early, whereas heart girth and body mass continued to change for several years thereafter. Adult shoulder height was typically reached shortly after 3 yr of age, heart girth continued to increase until approximately 4 yr, and body mass did not reach adult levels until around 5 yr, consistent with previous work on Plains Zebra (Smuts 1975) and with broader patterns reported for large herbivores with prolonged juvenile development (Gaillard et al. 2000). When fitting growth models, traits that stabilize once skeletal maturity is reached were best described by simpler sigmoidal functions, whereas environmentally responsive traits such as body mass and heart girth followed more flexible trajectories. Sex differences in growth trajectories for some traits, including neck–body length, may reflect divergent energetic demands associated with reproduction and social competition, as observed across polygynous ungulates (Bergeron et al. 2010).

Some body measurements, such as shoulder height, are relatively stable, but mass fluctuates seasonally. Many temperate zone ungulate taxa exhibit seasonal mass change, with each sex experiencing different patterns of weight gain and loss throughout life (Franzmann et al. 1978; Festa-Bianchet et al. 1996; Loison et al. 1999; Bowyer 2022). Body mass in ungulates typically shows seasonal variation reflecting fluctuations in forage availability, reproductive status, and environmental conditions. In both sexes, body condition tends to decline during the dry season or winter months in temperate regions (Monteith et al. 2013)—when forage quality and quantity are reduced—and improve during the nutritionally favorable season when vegetation is more abundant (Rubenstein 2010). In our study, adult males averaged approximately 12 kg heavier than females overall, but this difference increased substantially during the late wet season (April to May), when male mass exceeded female mass by up to 35 kg. Male body mass increased by 16.9% from mid- to late wet season, whereas female mass remained comparatively stable (Fig. 4). Although we lacked dry-season samples to fully quantify annual change in mass, the observed pattern is consistent with seasonal mass gain in male ungulates during periods of high forage availability and in advance of the breeding season, when energetic reserves enhance competitive ability and mating success (Bergeron et al. 2010). The magnitude of seasonal change observed here aligns with broader patterns reported for East African mammals, in which seasonal mass variation typically does not exceed approximately 15% (Talbot and McCulloch 1965). Comparisons of body mass among subspecies or populations of Plains Zebra further show the need to account for seasonality and reproductive state. Although the mean body mass of E. q. burchellii reported by Smuts (1975) exceeded values observed for E. q. boehmi in our study, the maximum male and female masses recorded here fall well within the reported annual ranges for E. q. burchellii. Similarly, peak wet-season male mass in E. q. boehmi closely matches wet-season values reported for E. q. antiquorum (Myburgh et al. 2022). These overlaps suggest that apparent differences in body mass among Plains Zebra subspecies may partly reflect sampling season and environmental context rather than intrinsic morphological divergence.

Interpretation of seasonal- and sex-specific variation in body mass requires careful distinction among body size, physiological load, and true nutritional condition (Shipley et al. 2020). Body mass is an inherently composite metric that integrates multiple tissue compartments, including fat, lean tissue, gut contents, and reproductive tissues—each of which responds to environmental and physiological drivers differently (Cook et al. 2021). Consequently, changes in body mass may occur without proportional changes in energetic condition, particularly when fat reserves are not measured directly (Barboza and Parker 2008; Shipley et al. 2020; Cook et al. 2021). Reproductive status further complicates interpretation as pregnancy increases body mass through fetal growth, placental tissues, and associated fluid retention, whereas females with foals at foot experience elevated energetic demands associated with lactation that may constrain mass gain or accelerate mass loss (Oftedal 2018). In our study, 70% of the culled females were pregnant, and with such a high pregnancy rate, a substantial proportion of females likely had foals at foot during the sampling period. Although heart girth may partially reflect variation in fat reserves, mass-based metrics cannot distinguish among tissue components or separate true energetic stores from short-term physiological load (Cook et al. 2021). In Plains Zebra, fat reserves are preferentially deposited in the rump and other subcutaneous depots that were not measured in this study, limiting inference about body condition from mass or linear dimensions alone. These limitations are widely recognized in studies of free-ranging large mammals and emphasize that seasonal and sex-specific variation in body mass should be interpreted cautiously as reflecting physiological and reproductive processes rather than direct measures of nutritional condition (Barboza and Parker 2008; Cook et al. 2021).

Overall, our findings emphasize the importance of considering age, sex, season, and reproductive status jointly when interpreting morphometric and body mass data in equids and other large herbivores. Historical datasets derived from culling operations provide rare empirical insight into growth and seasonal physiology that is otherwise difficult to obtain in free-ranging populations. As non-invasive approaches such as photogrammetry, camera trapping, and drone-based measurements continue to advance (Rahman et al. 2023; Napoli et al. 2024), the data presented here offer a valuable benchmark for validating remote estimates of size and mass. More broadly, understanding how body mass and morphology vary through time is essential for interpreting population dynamics in seasonal ecosystems.
