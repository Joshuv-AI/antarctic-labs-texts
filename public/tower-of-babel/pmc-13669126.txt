Challenging bone biopsies: what radiologists needs to know
Authors: Leonor Garbin Savarese, Nicolas Papalexis, Mateus de Andrade Hernandes, Paolo Spinnato, Giancarlo Facchini, Marco Miceli, Nelson Fabricio Gava, Edgard Eduard Engel, Marcello Henrique Nogueira-Barbosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone biopsies play a pivotal role in the diagnostic workup of a wide range of bone pathologies. In many cases, image-guided percutaneous biopsy is favored over surgical approaches because it is minimally invasive, highly accurate, cost-efficient, and associated with lower morbidity. Among the available imaging modalities, CT guidance offers superior spatial resolution and enables precise needle placement, but certain skeletal locations remain technically demanding even under CT guidance. This review focuses on challenging CT-guided bone biopsy scenarios, including lesions adjacent to critical neurovascular or spinal structures, densely sclerotic lesions, and lesions occult on CT, and illustrates practical decision-making through representative clinical cases.

Introduction

Image-guided percutaneous bone biopsy is a well-established, minimally invasive alternative to open surgical biopsy and plays a central role in the diagnostic workup of musculoskeletal lesions. Reported diagnostic accuracy ranges from approximately 68% to 99%, depending on lesion type, location, and biopsy technique [1–6]. CT guidance remains the main imaging modality for most bone biopsies because it provides excellent spatial resolution, precise needle positioning, and safe access to deep or surgically challenging lesions. In addition, percutaneous biopsy has become increasingly important for molecular profiling and personalized oncologic treatment strategies [7–9].

Although many lesions can be safely approached using standard techniques, some biopsies remain technically demanding because of lesion location, density, or limited conspicuity on CT. These include lesions adjacent to critical structures, densely sclerotic lesions, and CT-occult lesions requiring multimodality imaging correlation for accurate targeting. In these situations, careful preprocedural planning, detailed anatomical knowledge, and tailored biopsy strategies are essential to maximize diagnostic yield and minimize complications [2–4, 7, 8, 10–19].

This review discusses practical considerations and representative cases involving (1) lesions adjacent to critical structures, (2) densely sclerotic lesions, and (3) CT-occult lesions. General technical considerations in unusual CT-guided bone biopsies

Indications and decision-making: when should a bone lesion be biopsied?

Before planning a bone biopsy, the radiologist must first determine whether tissue sampling is truly indicated and likely to impact clinical management. Common indications include diagnosis of a primary bone tumor, confirmation of metastatic disease, evaluation of hematologic malignancies, and microbiologic or histopathologic diagnosis in suspected osteomyelitis or spondylodiscitis. In selected cases, biopsy may also be required for molecular profiling and treatment planning in oncologic patients [7–9].

This initial assessment should integrate clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and imaging characteristics. It is equally important to recognize lesions in which biopsy is generally not indicated (“no-touch lesions”), particularly when imaging findings are characteristic and the clinical scenario is appropriate. Examples include typical bone islands, non-ossifying fibromas, fibrous dysplasia, and classic vertebral hemangiomas. In these situations, unnecessary biopsy may expose the patient to avoidable risks and potentially misleading histopathologic findings.

It is crucial to ensure that an adequate imaging workup has been performed and that all available studies have been reviewed, including radiographs, CT, MRI, and nuclear medicine examinations. Correlation among imaging modalities helps refine the differential diagnosis, identify the most appropriate target when multiple lesions are present, and anticipate potential technical challenges [2–4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 13, 16]. In selected lesions with low clinical suspicion or benign imaging features, short-interval imaging follow-up may be a reasonable alternative to immediate biopsy.

Preprocedural planning

Imaging review and target selection

A structured preprocedural plan is essential, particularly for unusual bone lesions. Patient positioning should be considered because anatomical relationships and osseous landmarks may change between supine, prone, and lateral decubitus positions, especially in the pelvis and spine, affecting lesion conspicuity, trajectory, and adjacent critical structures. In suspected primary bone sarcoma, orthopedic oncology may request tattooing or marking of the biopsy tract to facilitate en bloc resection [2–4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 16]. CT remains the mainstay, but fluoroscopy, ultrasound, cone-beam CT, MRI, or PET/CT may be preferred or used adjunctively. Target selection should favor viable tissue and avoid necrotic, cystic, or hemorrhagic areas. Ultrasound can guide selected osteolytic lesions with cortical thinning or disruption or an extraosseous soft-tissue component, providing real-time needle visualization, Doppler assessment, and no ionizing radiation; high diagnostic yield has been reported in appropriately selected lesions [20]. Communication with the referring team is essential to confirm the diagnostic question [1, 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 16, 18]. The workflow is summarized in Table 2. Workflow-oriented planning and technical considerations for CT-guided bone biopsy

Trajectory planning and compartmental considerations

When planning the biopsy trajectory, potential tract seeding should be considered in suspected primary bone tumors because the biopsy route may influence limb-sparing or reconstructive surgery. Safe access may require long intraosseous trajectories, such as trans-iliac or trans-pedicular approaches, to avoid neurovascular structures. The shortest path is not necessarily the safest, and accurate multiplanar planning is essential because trajectory correction becomes limited once the device is advanced within bone [3, 6, 10, 12, 21, 22].

Anesthesia and sedation strategy

Adequate pain control is a critical component of preprocedural planning. Local anesthesia of the skin, subcutaneous tissues, and especially the periosteum is essential to reduce patient discomfort during cortical penetration [8, 23, 24]. In selected cases, such as long procedures, painful sclerotic lesions, pediatric patients, or anatomically constrained approaches (e.g., cervical spine or transoral access), deep sedation or general anesthesia may be considered in collaboration with anesthesiology [10].

Biopsy system selection

Coaxial biopsy systems are preferred in most cases [1, 4, 7, 8, 12, 16, 18]. An outer working cannula traverses the cortex and a removable trephine obtains multiple cores through a single cortical perforation. Maintaining the introducer permits controlled re-angulation or incremental advancement toward different lesion areas, cortical anesthetic injection, and stable needle positioning.

Needle gauge and length should be tailored to the lesion and body habitus. Bone biopsy systems typically range from 10–12 G to 14–16 G. Larger-bore needles provide more substantial cores for primary tumors or molecular testing, at the cost of slightly higher bleeding risk and firmer cortical penetration [2, 7, 8, 11, 12, 18]. Thinner needles may be advantageous near critical structures or in small bones, where minimizing cortical defects and fracture risk is important. Shorter needles provide better torque control in superficial lesions, whereas longer devices are required for deep pelvic, spinal, or sacral targets [4, 7, 10, 12, 13, 18]. Powered systems facilitate access to densely sclerotic lesions and reduce operator fatigue, but brief intermittent drilling and limited torque help reduce heat artifact, needle bending, and fracture [7, 8, 11, 12, 15, 17, 18, 25, 26]. Manual Jamshidi- or Bonopty-type systems remain suitable for lesions with limited sclerosis and when powered devices are unavailable or cost-prohibitive [15, 17, 27]. Spring-assisted or specimen-retaining mechanisms may improve capture of friable bone or marrow [22].

Soft-tissue core needles with cutting trays and spring-loaded mechanisms are useful for extraosseous components, which often yield more cellular material than mineralized bone [2, 7–9, 12, 18, 19, 22]. In very lytic lesions, they may also be advanced intraosseously through a defect created by the outer cannula, avoiding additional cortical trauma. Tip dead space should be considered when sampling small or soft lesions because it may reduce effective tissue capture. Combining a bone trephine for access with a soft-tissue core device allows sampling to be adapted to lesion consistency and anatomy.

Sampling strategy and specimen handling

Core biopsy, FNA, and specimen handling should be planned before the procedure. For suspected lymphoma, plasmacytoma, or other hematologic malignancies, close coordination with pathology is essential so that material is triaged for flow cytometry, immunophenotyping, and possible genomic studies [7, 8, 12, 16, 28]. In metastatic disease or primary tumors, tissue handling should preserve material for molecular profiling [8, 9, 18, 19, 29]. When feasible, a separate blood clot or soft-tissue sample should be submitted without decalcification to preserve nucleic acids for molecular and genetic analyses.

Core samples are generally fixed in formalin; in suspected hematologic malignancies, at least part of the specimen should be submitted fresh in an appropriate medium for ancillary studies. Gentle handling minimizes crush artifact, particularly in lymphoma [18, 30]. FNA may complement core biopsy in metastatic disease, plasma cell neoplasms, and selected hematologic malignancies, where cytology and ancillary studies can be informative.

Factors associated with improved diagnostic yield

Several technical and lesion-related factors influence diagnostic yield in bone biopsy, including lesion selection, sampling strategy, biopsy device, and specimen handling [7, 11, 12, 16–18, 29]. Larger-gauge needles, when safely applicable, may improve yield by providing more substantial tissue cores and facilitating ancillary studies such as immunohistochemistry and molecular profiling [2, 7, 11, 18, 29].

In osteolytic lesions, longer cores are generally desirable because they encompass a greater volume of viable tumor tissue. The “sandwich technique,” in which the biopsy specimen includes the lesion with adjacent normal bone on either side, may help preserve tissue architecture and improve diagnostic yield [28]. Gentle negative pressure applied during sampling may also facilitate tissue retrieval in friable lesions. In lesions with central necrosis, the most informative tissue is typically located at the enhancing peripheral zone and at the interface between viable tumor and bone [1, 12, 16, 18, 19, 22].

When performed coaxially through the same introducer, FNA provides additional cytologic material with minimal incremental risk [7, 8, 12, 16, 18, 22].

In densely sclerotic lesions, powered systems may facilitate cortical penetration, but less dense regions should be targeted whenever feasible to maximize diagnostic tissue [7, 8, 11, 12, 15, 17, 18, 25, 26, 32]. Detailed drilling strategies are discussed below.

Immediate on-site cytologic or adequacy assessment may improve diagnostic yield, particularly in hematologic malignancies, by confirming representative sampling and indicating whether additional cores are needed [31].

Intrinsic lesion-related factors also influence diagnostic success. Larger lesions, cortical destruction, extraosseous soft-tissue extension, and metabolically active tumors generally yield diagnostic samples more frequently [2, 7–9, 11, 12, 17–19, 29]. Conversely, small, purely intraosseous, minimally lytic, or low-grade lesions tend to have lower diagnostic yield and often require meticulous procedural planning.

An important exception is osseous lymphoma, which has been associated with lower diagnostic yield compared with metastatic disease, likely because of patchy marrow infiltration and frequent sclerosis [11]. In these cases, targeting the most lytic or metabolically active portion of the lesion and obtaining multiple representative cores may improve diagnostic performance. Close coordination with hematopathology is also essential, particularly when flow cytometry, immunophenotyping, or molecular studies are anticipated [7, 8, 12, 16, 18].

Challenging scenarios

Densely sclerotic and heavily mineralized lesions

Compared with lytic lesions, osteoblastic targets have lower diagnostic yield, with pooled rates around 74%. A subgroup analysis reported higher yield for battery-powered than manual drill systems (76.7% vs 65.2%) [7, 8, 11, 12, 15, 17, 18, 25, 29]. Technical challenges include difficult entry and slow advancement through compact bone, needle deflection or fracture, and core impaction within the trephine.

When using a battery-powered drill in sclerotic bone (Fig. 1), short cores (approximately 5 mm) are preferable to a single long cylinder because they reduce specimen impaction and allow multiple passes [15, 17]. If a core becomes lodged, a smaller-gauge trephine may be introduced coaxially to “biopsy the biopsy” and retrieve the material. Controlled tapping may also assist specimen release. Soaking the needle in hot water or a decalcifying solution is a last resort because it may impair histologic quality, although it can prevent complete sample loss [12, 16, 18]. Short-interval drilling with frequent pauses limits frictional heat and compressive forces, reducing crush artifact and frictional burn that may compromise histologic interpretation [15, 17, 25, 26]. Core length, drilling pattern, and rescue strategies for impacted tissue should therefore be adapted to the lesion.

Lesions adjacent to critical neurovascular or spinal structures

Lesions in the craniovertebral junction, cervical spine, sacrum, ribs, sternum, and deep pelvis pose substantial technical and safety challenges [7, 10, 12–14, 18, 19, 21, 25]. The route should be planned with the referring surgeon, considering standard surgical approaches, compartmental anatomy, and preservation of future treatment options such as limb-sparing surgery or complex spinal reconstruction [3, 6, 10, 12, 22].

In the cervical spine, vertebral arteries, carotid sheath structures, or the spinal cord may preclude routine posterolateral (Fig. 2) or anterolateral approaches. A transoral route can provide direct access to selected retro-odontoid or C2 lesions, although working angles and maneuverability are limited (Fig. 3) [7, 10, 12–14, 18, 21]. Cross-sectional imaging, and CT angiography when needed, should define carotid and vertebral artery anatomy and variants that may influence the approach [1, 4, 7, 8, 10, 12–14, 18, 33]. Similarly, sacral and pelvic lesions situated close to sacral foramina, the lumbosacral plexus, or iliac vessels often require long transosseous trajectories, such as trans iliac or trans-sacroiliac access, to avoid neurovascular structures and to allow stable anchorage within the bone (Fig. 4) [7, 8, 12, 13, 18, 19, 25]. Long intraosseous paths demand meticulous control of needle orientation in multiple planes and may benefit from CT fluoroscopy (Fig. 5), cone-beam CT, or navigation systems to maintain precision while minimizing cumulative radiation dose and procedure time [1, 4, 7, 8, 18, 19, 25]. Lesions in the sternum, ribs, and upper thoracic spine carry a risk of pleural or mediastinal violation [1, 4, 7, 8, 18, 19, 25]. For rib lesions, a near-parallel trajectory maximizes core length and reduces pneumothorax risk; because of the curved cortex, a more perpendicular initial angle may be needed before flattening the needle along the rib (Fig. 6). For sternal and manubrial targets, a shallow anterior approach with frequent CT checks helps respect bone thickness and mediastinal anatomy (Fig. 7). CT-occult lesions and the role of PET/CT and MRI

A third challenging group comprises lesions that are occult or poorly conspicuous on unenhanced CT, but clearly evident on MRI, bone scintigraphy, or PET/CT. These include early marrow metastases, hematologic malignancies, treated or residual tumor foci, and certain infectious lesions whose conspicuity relies more on marrow signal or metabolic activity than on mineralized matrix change. Robust data specifically reporting diagnostic yield for bone lesions occult on CT are limited, as these cases are typically included within broader biopsy series without dedicated subgroup analysis [1, 4, 5, 14, 18, 34–38].

When PET- or MRI-positive lesions lack a CT correlate, conventional CT guidance may not confidently localize the highest-yield target. Complementary strategies include the following:Anatomic-landmark–based targeting translates the lesion from MRI or PET/CT to CT using fixed osseous landmarks such as cortical contours, joint spaces, endplates, or pedicles. This method can achieve good diagnostic yield (Figs. 8 and 9) [4]. Complications of CT-guided bone biopsy

Complications of CT-guided bone biopsy are uncommon, with overall rates below 5% in most series [7, 10–13, 16, 18, 19, 22, 25, 26, 39, 40]. Nevertheless, the risk profile may be slightly higher in unusual or technically demanding scenarios, and radiologists should be familiar with both the expected spectrum of events and practical management strategies.

Reported complications include bleeding, infection, medication or contrast reactions, injury to adjacent structures, postprocedural pain, pathologic fracture, and needle bending or fracture [7, 10, 12–14, 18, 19, 25, 39, 40]. Fracture is rare but deserves attention in structurally weakened, weight-bearing bones such as the femoral neck. Postprocedural instructions should reflect lesion location, biopsy size, and cortical integrity and may include temporary activity modification or protected weight bearing [7, 10, 12, 13, 15, 17, 18, 25, 41]. Near joints or hardware, contamination or iatrogenic damage should be weighed against diagnostic benefit [3, 6, 10, 12, 18, 19, 22].

In cases of excessive bleeding along the biopsy tract, several measures can be taken. Manual compression and careful postprocedural observation are often sufficient; however, if bleeding persists, an absorbable gelatin sponge (e.g., Gelfoam) can be fragmented, mixed with saline, and injected as a slurry through the outer working cannula at the end of the procedure to promote tract hemostasis [7, 8, 12, 16, 18]. This technique is particularly useful in hypervascular tumors of the pelvis and spine or in patients receiving anticoagulant or antiangiogenic therapies. Only in exceptional situations is endovascular embolization or surgical intervention required [7, 10, 12, 13, 18, 25, 39, 40].

Needle fracture or broken hardware is a rare but characteristic complication, especially in densely sclerotic lesions when aggressive torque is applied or the cortex is traversed at an unfavorable angle (Fig. 10) [7, 8, 12, 15, 17, 18, 25, 26]. Non-diagnostic sampling should be minimized through representative targeting, sufficient cores, and coordination with pathology. When available, intraprocedural frozen section may confirm adequacy and reduce repeat biopsy [7–9, 11, 12, 16–19, 29].

Conclusion

CT-guided percutaneous bone biopsy is a reliable, minimally invasive diagnostic tool. In challenging lesions, success depends on careful target and trajectory selection, appropriate device and sampling strategy, and awareness of complications. Tailoring technique to lesion density, conspicuity, and adjacent anatomy helps maximize diagnostic yield and safety.
