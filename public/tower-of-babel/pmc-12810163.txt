Evaluation of AQP5 in Hypomineralized Teeth in Comparison to Normal Teeth: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Sindhu Subramani, Ramya Sekar, Kavitha Bottu, Sabarinath Balaraman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo quantitatively evaluate aquaporin 5 (AQP5) levels in the saliva of children with molar incisor hypomineralization (MIH) and compare them with those having normally mineralized teeth with dental caries and normally mineralized teeth. Also, to check the role of socioeconomic status (SES) in the incidence pattern.MethodologyA total of 75 children aged 6–12 years from Chennai, India, were divided equally into three groups: MIH, normally mineralized teeth with dental caries, and normally mineralized teeth. Saliva samples were collected using the passive pooling and analyzed for AQP5 levels using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) technique. Data were recorded and analyzed with SPSS software.ResultsAquaporin 5 levels in children with MIH were (0.808 ± 0.997 ng/mL), followed by those with normally mineralized teeth with dental caries (1.252 ± 0.2468 ng/mL) and normally mineralized teeth without dental caries (2.056 ± 0.3489 ng/mL). On intergroup comparison among groups A and B, A and C, and B and C were statistically significant (p < 0.0001). SES showed a significant correlation with the prevalence of MIH and dental caries, with MIH being more common in lower-income groups.ConclusionMolar incisor hypomineralization and dental caries are multifactorial conditions impacting children's oral health. Future research should explore how crystallization of the enamel matrix affects them. Introduction

Enamel is a unique hard tissue in the human body and is composed of 96% inorganic material, 4% organic material, and water. Mineralization of enamel begins at the 14th week of intrauterine life, with enamel matrix proteins secreted by ameloblasts guiding the deposition of minerals to form enamel crystals.1 On tooth eruption, amelogenesis will be complete, and ameloblasts will perish.2 Disturbances that occur in ameloblasts during amelogenesis can lead to developmental defects of enamel (DDE).3 The two major DDEs are enamel hypoplasia, a quantitative defect of enamel, clinically appearing as pits, rows having shallow or deep defects distributed in the overall enamel, and enamel hypomineralization, a qualitative defect with alterations in translucency representing white, brown, or yellow color with normal thickness of enamel.4 In the 1970s, a Swedish dentist noticed widespread hypomineralization in the first permanent molars in a group of children.5 At the congress of the European Academy of Paediatric Dentistry (EAPD) in 2000, four descriptions were given for DDE of first permanent molars, hypomineralized permanent first molars.6 Idiopathic enamel hypomineralization in the permanent first molars,5 nonfluoride hypomineralization in permanent first molars,7 and “cheese molars.”8

Molar incisor hypomineralization (MIH) is the most common developmental defect of enamel. Weerheijm and his team coined the term MIH and defined as “hypomineralization of systemic origin, presenting as demarcated, qualitative defects of enamel of one to four first permanent molars often associated with affected incisors.” Global prevalence of MIH ranges from 2.4% to 14.2%,9 with 10% of prevalence in India,10 and in Chennai city, it is reported to be 9.7%.11 The cause of MIH is attributed to various factors, such as genetics, environmental influences, and both systemic and local elements. Similarly, socioeconomic status (SES) affects dental health through its influence on nutritional status and the utilization of available dental services.12

Aquaporins (AQP) are transmembrane protein channels that facilitate the transport of water and various solutes. AQP5 is involved in the transcellular transport of water, which could be essential during the secretion phase of the ameloblast. It also helps in tissue hydration by regulating the local environment around ameloblasts, influencing the deposition of minerals and the quantity of enamel. Their expression patterns during different stages of tooth development indicate they are crucial for dental structure formation. Polymorphic variations in AQP5 are associated with deregulation of ameloblasts and odontoblasts during tooth formation, resulting in enamel defects.13,14 Thus, this study aims to quantitatively assess proteome AQP5 in saliva samples collected from individuals with MIH and dental caries and compare them with those of individuals with normally mineralized teeth.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This in vitro cross-sectional study was conducted among individuals aged 6–12 years old, during the period of 2022–2023. The study was carried out after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethical Committee, following the Helsinki Declaration protocol (2008). Informed assent was obtained from all participants' parents in English and/or the regional language by explaining the study protocol in detail.

Selection Criteria

The study participants were selected using a convenience consecutive sampling technique, with the age ranging from 6 to 12 years, in Chennai city. The study included participants who were willing to participate in the study, with good to poor oral hygiene index-simplified (OHI-S), and participants who satisfied the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS), decayed, missing, filled teeth (DMFT), and EAPD criteria were included. Participants who were not willing to participate, had a congenital, autoimmune, bleeding disorder, systemic disease, or were differently abled persons were excluded. The OHI-S was employed for assessment.15 Dental caries was evaluated using the DMFT index for permanent teeth and the dmft/def (decayed, extracted, filled teeth) index for deciduous teeth. A DMFT/dmft score of ≥1 indicated the presence of carious lesions.16 Caries severity was scored using the ICDAS II criteria on a scale of 0–6. For MIH, index teeth were assessed using a long charting form developed by Ghanim et al.17 as per EAPD guidelines. Participants aged 6–14 years, with clinically visible crowns showing at least one-third of their length and lesions larger than 1 mm in size, were scored accordingly.

Data Collection

Comprehensive case histories were collected from all study participants, including details such as the child's age, birth region, birth weight, maternal hospitalizations during pregnancy, any child hospitalizations between ages 0 and 3, medication taken during the child's first 3 years, maternal physical activity, sleep patterns during pregnancy, parental education level, and family income. Each participant underwent a dental examination conducted individually in ample daylight conditions. Sterile mouth mirrors and probes were used, and all tooth surfaces were dried and carefully examined.

Sample Collection

Each participant was given information about the protocol for saliva sampling: To refrain from eating or drinking for 1 hour before the collection of saliva, to ensure homogeneity. Samples were collected between 9 am and 12 pm in order to prevent the diurnal variation. Patients were asked to rinse their mouths with water to avoid food particles and impurities. Whole mouth saliva was collected using a passive pooling drooling method for 5 minutes and collected in 10 mL sterile containers. The saliva was centrifuged at 1000 × gm at 2°C–8°C for 15 minutes in a cooling centrifuge, and the supernatants were aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes and stored at −80°C for further analysis.

Estimation of Human AQP5 in Saliva by Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Estimation of AQP5 in saliva samples was carried out by ELISA, with a commercially procured kit from ELK biotechnology. This kit works on the sandwich technique. The microtiter plate is precoated with target antigen (AQP5). Samples were added with biotin conjugated antibody specific to AQP5, after which avidin-conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) was added and incubated. The wells that contain AQP5 have changed in color upon adding 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate. This enzyme-substrate reaction is terminated by adding sulfuric acid, and the color changes were measured spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 450 ± 10 nm.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistical software (Statistical Package for Social Sciences, version 26, BM Corp., Armonk, United States). Descriptive statistics pertaining to the assessed study variables were represented using mean, standard deviation, and standard error of mean. For discrete demographic variables such as age category, gender, and SES, descriptive statistics were represented using frequency and percentage distribution. Intergroup comparison was performed using nonparametric tests. Intergroup comparison by group and SES for the assessed variables was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc analysis. For all comparisons, p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Discussion

Developmental defects of enamel, comprising MIH, have an impact on both esthetic and functional aspects of teeth. Weerhejim first described MIH as a disease of systemic origin with a localized lesion caused by disruption during amelogenesis. Clinically, it has been claimed that MIH affects the molars and incisors predominantly. The affected enamel will be more porous and less hard compared to the normally mineralized enamel.

Hypomineralization of teeth is multifactorial. Environmental factors such as nutritional deficiencies, environmental toxins, maternal smoking during pregnancy, etc., disrupt enamel development. Systemic diseases such as celiac disease, febrile illness, medication like antibiotics in the first three years of life, hypoxia, birth complications, and genetic disturbances are other reasons cited for hypomineralization. There are several studies being conducted to identify their exact role in MIH. Recently, several genes such as Amelogenin (AMELX), Enamelin (ENAM), and Ameloblastin (AMBN) have been reported to relate to MIH.18

Aquaporin 5 is a transmembrane water channel protein permeable to water. The expression of AQP5 was evaluated in salivary glands, and it was demonstrated that AQP5's positive expression was evident in all major salivary glands and in saliva.19 Tooth morphogenesis requires hydrated extracellular matrix, and the tooth germ adapts to the hydrostatic and osmotic pressure of the surrounding tissue. Thus, AQP plays a key role in these processes during the development of the tooth. Interestingly, AQP5 is present in different structures of the developing tooth germ at the early bell stage, where water diffuses through lipid bilayers and allows transcellular water influx in the cervical loop through inner and outer enamel epithelium, establishing accumulation of water surrounding the space of Hertwig's epithelial sheath. Increased expression of AQP5 may change cell hydration and inhibit proteolysis and stimulate increased protein synthesis,20 resulting in hypomineralization of enamel.

In 2010, EAPD had stated that MIH is not caused by a single factor; instead, several determinantal factors in the pre, peri, and postnatal period could interact and increase the risk of MIH. Evidence-based literature also confirms that systemic, along with genetic factors, influence enamel hypomineralization.21–23 However, the duration, timing, and intensity of the etiological factors play a significant role.

There was no molecular study conducted in India on the correlation of AQP5 with MIH. Therefore, the present study aimed to quantify the salivary levels of AQP5 in children with diseased (MIH and Dental Caries) and normally mineralized teeth. The study objectives are to quantify and compare the amount of salivary AQP5 in children with Normally mineralized teeth [group A (n = 25)], normally mineralized teeth with dental caries [group B (n = 25)], and MIH [group C (n = 25)] using ELISA method and to check the role of SES in the incidence pattern of MIH.

The present study was conducted among a total of 75 children from Chennai city, equally distributed among groups A, B, and C based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Age and gender-wise distribution is relatively balanced in all study groups. Salivary AQP5 levels were greater in children with group C, followed by groups A and B in decreasing order, respectively (Fig. 1).

Impact of MIH on oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) based on child perceptions questionnaire in 8–10-year-old group (CPQ8–10) and concluded that the OHRQoL of children with MIH was affected more than that of children who are not affected by MIH20 (Gutiérrez et al.). MIH in permanent teeth showed a lower DMFT index than the teeth that are not affected by MIH. Similarly, in the present study, individuals with MIH showed a low DMFT index score compared with normal teeth dental caries (group B).

Survey conducted among MIH prevalence with SES in Northern England, results in a significant association between increasing deprivation quintile 1 (corresponding to lower SES) up to quintile 4. The correlation between SES and the occurrence of MIH remained notably strong.24 Similarly, in our study, we had a significant correlation with the lower middle and upper lower income group among MIH in group C (Table 4). On the contrary, a study assessed the association between MIH and dental caries with SES and found that the SES did not impact the relationship between MIH and caries in FPMs (first permanent molar).25 MIH is a globally recognized developmental dental condition that affects children's oral health worldwide. AQP5 is a water channel protein that plays a crucial role in the regulation of water transport across the cell membrane, including in ameloblasts. Therefore, AQPs are hypothesized to play a role in MIH. Also, AQPs are expressed in salivary glands and in salivary secretions. From this, it is concluded that the expression of AQP5 is increased in MIH in comparison to individuals with normally mineralized teeth, but there is a slight variation with dental caries. This can be due to a structural defect in the enamel matrix; therefore, AQP5 could play a major role in the pathophysiology of MIH by affecting the ameloblast.

Conclusion

Molar incisor hypomineralization and dental caries have a multifactorial etiology. Factors affecting crystallization (serum albumin, serotonin levels) related to MIH and factors affecting dental caries, such as diet, salivary secretory rate, etc., are to be included in future studies. Gene polymorphisms of AQP5, along with ENAM, AMBN, AMLN, and bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2), can be assessed to understand the genetic basis leading to improved diagnostic methods with a personalized treatment approach. Further studies at a larger scale with more parameters are to be conducted to confirm the role of AQP5 levels in MIH and dental caries.
