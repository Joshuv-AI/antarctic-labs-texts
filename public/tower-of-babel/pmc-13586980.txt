Effects of biotic homogenization on community assembly and biomass production in an α × β‐diversity grassland experiment
Authors: Fons van der Plas, Justus Hennecke, Lea von Sivers, Alexandra Weigelt, Christian Wirth, Kathryn E Barry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVarious studies have shown that local loss of plant species within patches (i.e., α‐diversity loss) hampers plant biomass production, raising concerns about the consequences of biodiversity loss. However, biodiversity loss occurs at multiple spatial scales, and species losses at large spatial scales (γ‐diversity loss) can be either caused by α‐diversity loss, or by the homogenization of species compositions across patches (β‐diversity loss). Yet, we poorly understand the consequences of β‐diversity loss for ecosystem functioning. Here, we present the findings of the first study to experimentally manipulate variation across not only α‐diversity but also β‐diversity. In a grassland experiment, we manipulated plant β‐diversity by setting up 56 plots, each divided into four subplots with two different soil types. Across the subplots within a plot, we sowed either the same plant species combinations (low β‐diversity treatment), partially overlapping species combinations (intermediate β‐diversity), or nonoverlapping species combinations (high β‐diversity treatment). We hypothesized that high β‐diversity could increase biomass production by: (1) enabling species sorting and (2) maintaining high α‐diversity over time. Four years after setting up our experiment, we found that β‐diversity increased biomass production and that these positive effects could not be solely explained by having overall high γ‐diversity. This was because high sown levels of β‐diversity increased realized α‐diversity, which in turn increased biomass production. In contrast, species sorting did not result in increased biomass production. Our results have the potential to inform restoration projects, where sowing different species at different sites could have delayed benefits for both biodiversity and ecosystem functioning, although further studies are needed to explore the benefits of β‐diversity for ecosystem management.Keywords: beta diversity, biodiversity and ecosystem functioning, biodiversity partitioning, biotic homogenization, community assembly, ecosystem functioning, gamma diversity

INTRODUCTION

In the early 1990s, concerns about ongoing biodiversity loss led to studies on the effects of biodiversity on ecosystem functioning (Naeem et al., 1994; Tilman et al., 1996). These studies provided widespread evidence that local species losses can decrease levels of various ecosystem functions, including the production of biomass, litter decomposition rates, soil carbon storage, and ecosystem stability (Balvanera et al., 2006; Cardinale et al., 2012; Hooper et al., 2005; van der Plas, 2019). However, the majority of these studies simulated biodiversity change as a simple loss of species at small spatial scales, that is, α‐diversity loss. This does not fully reflect the reality of biodiversity change, which occurs simultaneously at multiple spatial scales (Chase et al., 2019; McGill et al., 2015). Further, loss of α‐diversity can be accompanied by (or even overshadowed by) biotic homogenization, that is, the loss of β‐diversity, where local species pools become more similar over time (Blowes et al., 2024; Magurran et al., 2015; van der Plas et al., 2024).

β‐diversity forms a bridge between within‐patch (comparatively smaller scale) diversity (α‐diversity) and biodiversity in a comparatively larger area (γ‐diversity). β‐diversity is high when different patches or communities complement each other in their species composition by containing different species, and low when different patches or communities have the same or similar species compositions (Anderson et al., 2011; Whittaker, 1960). Several observational studies have investigated relationships between β‐diversity and biomass production showing both positive (e.g., Grman et al., 2018; Hammill et al., 2018), weak (Burley et al., 2016), and negative (Omidipour et al., 2021) relationships. To make sense of these variable relationships, we need to understand the mechanisms by which β‐diversity can affect biomass production.

β‐diversity may influence biomass production through two different mechanisms (Figure 1). First, we expect that β‐diversity can increase biomass production over time through species sorting (Castillioni & Isbell, 2023; Leibold et al., 2004, 2017; van der Plas et al., 2023). Different plant species are adapted to different abiotic conditions such as different soil types and where they usually also maximize their biomass production. Therefore, high β‐diversity with different species in different patches likely maximizes biomass production in abiotically heterogeneous landscapes (Leibold et al., 2017; Loreau et al., 2003). Second, when β‐diversity is high, species can disperse and colonize patches that they did not previously occupy (Cadotte, 2006; Loreau et al., 2003), increasing and maintaining a high realized α‐diversity. Increases in α‐diversity can then promote local biomass production through local mechanisms such as resource partitioning (Balvanera et al., 2006; Barry et al., 2019; Cardinale et al., 2012; Hooper et al., 2005; Figure 1). Because α‐diversity and β‐diversity can enhance local biomass production through different mechanisms at different scales, we do not expect that their combined effects can simply be summarized through an effect of γ‐diversity. Rather, we expect that partitioning γ‐diversity into α‐diversity and β‐diversity is crucial for understanding biodiversity's multiple effects on biomass production through both local mechanisms as well as species sorting.

Hypothesized mechanisms by which initial levels of β‐diversity may affect biomass production. First, we expect that a high initial β‐diversity can increase biomass production through species sorting. Specifically, in plots with a high initial β‐diversity, subplots with different soils will become dominated by species with different abiotic preferences (Hypothesis 1A or H1A). As subplots in plots with a high initial β‐diversity become increasingly dominated by species that are adapted to local soil conditions, biomass production will increase (H1B). Second, we expect that a high initial β‐diversity can lead to gains in α‐diversity over time, that is, increase realized α‐diversity (H2A). This increase in realized α‐diversity may promote biomass production (H2B), for example, through species complementarity.

Here, we present results from an experiment where we manipulate not only α‐diversity but also β‐diversity and thereby γ‐diversity, for the first time to our knowledge. Specifically, we set up a grassland experiment where each plot was divided into subplots which were sown with different numbers and combinations of vascular plant species, to create an α‐diversity gradient, a β‐diversity gradient, and a γ‐diversity gradient. Further, we created abiotic heterogeneity by filling subplots with two different soil types to enable species sorting. While α‐diversity, β‐diversity, and γ‐diversity were all manipulated, we put focus on the effects of β‐diversity, which acts as a scaling factor between α‐diversity and γ‐diversity and is much less understood (e.g., Cardinale et al., 2011; Hooper et al., 2005; Tilman et al., 2014). Over a period of 4 years, we collected data on both (changing) community compositions and the biomass in these plots to investigate whether, and by which mechanisms, β‐diversity influences biomass production.

In plots with a high initial β‐diversity, where species can colonize new subplots with soil types that match their abiotic preferences, there will be community divergence among subplots, where subplots with different soils become increasingly dominated by species with different abiotic preferences (i.e., species sorting) (H1A).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design and maintenance

We set up our α × β × γ plant biodiversity experiment in the botanical garden of Leipzig University (51°19′47.2″ N, 12°23′30.9″ E) in November 2019. In an unvegetated field, we set up 56 1 × 1 m plots, each divided into 4 subplots of 0.5 × 0.5 m. Plot and subplot borders consisted of 50‐cm tall polyvinyl chloride (PVC) plates, that reached up to 40 cm into the soil, and up to 10 cm above the soil, thereby minimizing root interactions and resource exchange between plants from different (sub‐)plots. In each plot, 2 randomly selected subplots were filled up to 40 cm depth with a clay soil rich in organic matter (“clay soil” hereafter, which is a mixture of topsoil, green compost, and sand [soil type 4 DIN 18915]), while the two remaining subplots were filled with a relatively nutrient‐poor, sandy soil (“sandy soil” hereafter, i.e., a mix of 50% washed and sieved river sand, and 50% of the first soil type).

In each plot, we sowed combinations of plant species in such a way that a full factorial gradient in both plant α‐diversity and β‐diversity was created (Appendix S1: Figure S1). We created two levels of α‐diversity: subplots sown with a richness of one plant species, and subplots sown with a richness of four plant species. In addition, we created three levels of β‐diversity, where β‐diversity was manipulated following three levels of average Jaccard dissimilarity (β j hereafter; Koleff et al., 2003) among subplots within a plot and thereby five levels of gamma diversity. First, there was the β‐diversity = 0 treatment, where all four subplots within a plot were sown with the same plant species, and hence sown β j equals 0. In these plots, γ‐diversity equals α‐diversity, that is, γ‐diversity is either one or four. Second, there was the β‐diversity = 0.5 treatment, whereby at the plot level, two randomly selected subplots were sown with one set of randomly selected plant species, and the two other subplots were sown with another, nonoverlapping set of species (Appendix S1: Figure S1). As a result, half of the subplot pairs were sown with the same species (meaning that their pairwise β j = 0), the other half were sown with a nonoverlapping combination of species, meaning that their pairwise β j = 1, so that the average sown β j at the plot level equals 0.5. In these plots, γ‐diversity is two times α‐diversity, that is, either two or eight. Last, there was the high β‐diversity treatment, where all four subplots within a plot contained a unique set of nonoverlapping species, so that sown β j among each pair of subplots (and hence β‐diversity at the plot level) equals 1. In these plots, γ‐diversity is four times α‐diversity, that is, either four or 16. Note that a γ‐diversity of four occurs at both the highest and lowest levels of β‐diversity. Hence, in total, there were 2 (α‐diversity levels) × 3 (β‐diversity levels) = 6 treatment combinations and five levels of γ‐diversity (1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 species). As we used 16 vascular plant species in our experiment, and we wanted to have plot‐level monocultures for each species, we had 16 replicates for the “low α‐diversity, β‐diversity” treatment. The remaining five treatment combinations were each replicated 8 times, leading to a total of 16 + 5 × 8 = 56 plots.

The treatments were realized by sowing randomly selected combinations of a pool of 16 grassland species that were selected to vary in abiotic preferences (based on Jäger et al., 2013): Bromus hordeaceus L., Cynosurus cristatus L., Festuca pratensis HUDS., Festuca rubra L., Luzula campestris (L.) DC., Campanula patula L., Leucanthemum vulgare LAM., Plantago media L., Prunella vulgaris L., Ranunculus bulbosus L., Veronica chamaedrys L., Lathyrus pratensis L., Lotus corniculatus L., Medicago lupulina L., Trifolium repens L., and Vicia sepium L. In each subplot, 250 viable seeds equally divided by the number of species were sown.

In spring and autumn of the first year after the experiment was set up (i.e., in 2020), in each subplot, species not originally sown there were weeded out. In the second year onward (i.e., 2021–2024), in spring, summer, and autumn we also weeded, but this time in each subplot, we only weeded out those species originally not sown at the associated plot, rather than subplot. By doing so, we deliberately allowed seeds to disperse among subplots within a plot, thereby creating the possibility that sown β‐diversity would influence community assembly, potentially causing changes in the richness and species composition of subplots.

Measurements

In May (“spring” hereafter) 2021–2024, as well as in August (“summer” hereafter) 2020–2023, we measured the living aboveground biomass in each subplot. In a 20 × 50 cm strip, we clipped the vegetation to a height of 10 cm and then separated target species (2020: species sown in the subplot; 2021–2024: species sown in the subplot and/or plot) from nontarget species, and living from dead biomass. In the spring of 2021–2024, we also separated living plant material of the different target species. After that, we dried samples in an oven at 70°C for 72 h, and weighed (1) the total, subplot level aboveground biomass of all target species combined, and (2) the subplot level aboveground biomass of each target species separately (for the spring of 2021–2024).

Data analysis

We first tested the overall effects of sown α‐diversity and β‐diversity on aboveground biomass production, separately for each of the seasons (spring of 2022–2024, summer of 2021–2023) in which β‐diversity could cause species sorting or changes in α‐diversity. For each of the seasons, we ran a linear model with aboveground biomass (summed value across subplots of one plot; then we square‐root‐transformed the raw values to obtain normal model residuals) as the response variable, and sown α‐diversity and β‐diversity as the predictors. In this analysis, we could not analyze soil type, as at the plot‐level, there was no variation in this. We also ran alternative models where we added an interaction effect between sown α‐ and β‐diversity, or where sown α‐ and β‐diversity were replaced by sown γ‐diversity, and we compared the fit of these alternative models using Akaike information criterion (AIC). We also ran separate linear mixed models (LMM) at the subplot—rather than plot‐level, with aboveground biomass (square‐root‐transformed to obtain normal residuals) as the response variable, sown α‐diversity and β‐diversity as well as soil type as the predictors, and plot identity as a random factor. In some plots (on average 3.7 plots per season), aboveground biomass, which was based on clippings at 10 cm above the soil level, values were zero. These were all sown monocultures (with sown α‐diversity = 1 and sown β‐diversity = 1), and the low aboveground biomass values likely reflect one of the known mechanisms causing biodiversity‐biomass production relationships, namely, sampling effects, whereby by chance, some monocultures produce very low biomass because they only contain unproductive species (Tilman et al., 1997). Given that these zero values were correctly measured and reflect relevant ecological processes, these were not omitted from the analyses. LMMs were fitted using a maximum likelihood procedure, using the lmer (Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) packages in R (R Core Team, 2022).

We then investigated whether plant community composition diverged among subplots with sandy versus clay soils (H1A, Figure 1). To do this, we first extracted species indicator values on four abiotic variables known to systematically differ between clay and sandy soils from an external source (Hennekens et al., 2001). Specifically, we used soil Ca, NH4, Mg, and K concentrations which are all generally higher in clay soils (Sparks et al., 2024). We performed a principal component analysis with these (z‐transformed) indicator variables as input and extracted the first principal component (PC1) (explaining 74.1% of all variance, Appendix S1: Table S1) multiplied with −1 as an indicator for clay soils (“clay soil indicator” or CSI hereafter), as it had positive loadings of all original indicator values (Appendix S1: Table S2). These CSI values matched realized differences in aboveground biomass of our species among the different soils as observed in our experiment (see Appendix S1: Supplementary methods). We used the CSI values to test whether especially in plots with high β‐diversity, communities diverged in such a way that subplots with clay soils became more dominated by species with high CSI values, while sandy soils became more dominated by species with low CSI values. We first calculated the community‐weighted mean (CWM hereafter; Garnier, 2004) of CSI for each subplot as CWM=∑i=1Sai×CSIi, where ai is the relative abundance (proportion of total aboveground biomass) of the i‐th species within the subplot, and CSIi is the CSI value of the i‐th species. Within each plot, we quantified average differences in the CWM of CSI values (“ΔCWMCSI” hereafter) between subplots with a clay versus a sandy soil: ΔCWMCSI=CWMCSIclay¯−CWMCSIsand¯. Hence, positive ΔCWMCSI values indicate that subplots with clay soil have a higher dominance of species with high CSI values than subplots with sandy soils. We then analyzed using linear models how ΔCWMCSI (response variable) was driven by sown β‐diversity (H1A), sown α‐diversity, and their interaction, separately for 2022, 2023, and 2024. As we did not have hypotheses on interactive effects between sown α‐ and β‐diversity, we removed this effect if this improved the AIC. We also ran an alternative model, where instead of sown α‐ and β‐diversity, the effect of sown γ‐diversity was tested, and we compared overall model fit of these alternative models using AIC.

We then tested whether plots in which species sorting caused the strongest divergence of plant community composition among subplots with different soil types (i.e., plots with high ΔCWMCSI) also had the highest aboveground biomass production (H1B, Figure 1), separately for 2022, 2023, and 2024. We did so using linear models with plot level aboveground biomass (square‐root‐transformed to adhere to normal residuals) as the response variable, and ΔCWMCSI and sown α‐diversity (and their interaction, if this improved the AIC) as predictors.

To investigate whether sown β‐diversity positively affects realized α‐diversity (H2A, Figure 1), we quantified realized α‐diversity in each subplot, separately for the spring 2022, 2023, and 2024. We did so by counting for each subplot the number of species with an aboveground biomass above 0. We used linear models to analyze how realized α‐diversity (averaged at the plot level) was driven by (1) sown levels of α‐diversity and β‐diversity, including their interaction if this reduced the AIC (predictors), or by (2) sown levels of γ‐diversity. We also ran an alternative model, in which sown β‐diversity was replaced by “colonization potential,” that is, the maximum number of species that could potentially colonize a plot (sown γ‐diversity minus sown α‐diversity). We compared the fit of competing models based on AIC.

We then investigated how realized α‐diversity influenced aboveground biomass production (H2B, Figure 1), separately for data of the spring of 2022, 2023, and 2024. We did so using linear models, with plot‐level aboveground biomass production (square‐root‐transformed) as the response variable, realized α‐diversity as the main predictor of interest, and sown α‐diversity and β‐diversity (removed if this increased the AIC) as additional predictors.

In addition, we also used piecewise structural equation models (SEM), with the “piecewiseSEM” package (Lefcheck, 2016) in R‐4.2.1 (R Core Team, 2022), to have an integrative analysis on how α‐ and β‐diversity affected aboveground biomass production (square‐root‐transformed), both directly, as well as indirectly, through temporal changes in α‐diversity, that is, effects on realized α‐diversity, and through ΔCWMCSI. We did so for 2022, 2023, and 2024 separately, and for each of those years, we first fitted the four separate models described above related to H1A, H1B, H2A, and H2B. We then used the “psem” function to estimate the overall SEM model coefficients, as the “fisherC” function to assess the overall piecewise SEM fit, based on Fisher's C.

RESULTS

Overall effects of sown levels of α‐diversity and β‐diversity on aboveground biomass production

In all seasons, there was a significant, positive effect of sown α‐diversity on aboveground biomass production (Figure 2; Appendix S1: Table S3). Estimated effects of sown β‐diversity were also always positive, although the effects were only significant in the three most recent measurements, that is, in the spring and summer of 2023 and in the spring of 2024 (Appendix S1: Table S3). Interactive effects between sown α‐ and β‐diversity on aboveground biomass production were never significant (Appendix S1: Table S4), and models where sown α‐ and β‐diversity were replaced by sown γ‐diversity (Appendix S1: Figure S2) always had a poorer fit (higher AIC), except in the spring of 2023 (Appendix S1: Table S5). These results were qualitatively similar in analyses at the subplot‐ (rather than plot‐) level, except that this time, (1) sown β‐diversity did not have a significant positive effect on the aboveground biomass in spring 2023 and (2) also in spring 2023, models including sown γ‐diversity had a lower fit than models including sown α‐ and β‐diversity (Appendix S1: Tables S6 and S7).

Hypothesis 1: Effects of β‐diversity on aboveground biomass through species sorting

In 2022 and 2023, neither sown α‐ nor sown β‐diversity were significantly related to ΔCWMCSI, and intercepts of ΔCWMCSI values did not differ significantly from zero, indicating that within plots, subplots with clay versus sandy soils did not differ in CWMs of clay soil indicator values, that is, communities did not diverge among soil types (Figure 3; Appendix S1: Table S8, Figures S3A and S4A). For these years, models with sown γ‐diversity rather than the combination of sown α‐ and sown β‐diversity had a better fit (Appendix S1: Table S9), although also sown γ‐diversity did not have a significant effect on ΔCWMCSI (Appendix S1: Table S10). In contrast and in opposition to our prediction (H1A), in 2022, sown β‐diversity was significantly, negatively related to ΔCWMCSI (Figure 3a; Appendix S1: Table S8). Irrespective of the year (2022, 2023, or 2024), and contrary to our predictions from H1B, there was no significant relationship between ΔCWMCSI and aboveground biomass production (all p > 0.05; Figure 3; Appendix S1: Table S11, Figures S3B and S4B).

Hypothesis 2: Effects of β‐diversity on aboveground biomass through realized α‐diversity

Realized α‐diversity was significantly positively affected by sown levels of both α‐ and β‐diversity, both in the spring of 2022, 2023, and 2024 (Figure 4; Appendix S1: Figure S5, Table S12). Specifically, in 2024, each additional sown species increased realized α‐diversity by 0.5391 (SE = 0.05779) species, while an increase of 1 unit of sown β‐diversity was associated with an increase of realized α‐diversity of on average 1.4728 (SE = 0.2072) (Appendix S1: Table S12). The positive effect of β‐diversity on realized α‐diversity was stronger in 2024 than in 2022 or 2023 (Appendix S1: Table S12). DISCUSSION

In this study, we used a 4‐year‐old experiment to investigate whether, and by which mechanisms, plant β‐diversity affects aboveground biomass production in grasslands. Our experiment suggests that species sorting may not be a strong driver of positive β‐diversity effects. The positive effect of β‐diversity on realized α‐diversity may alternatively be a strong driver of positive β‐diversity–biomass production relationships. In line with this, starting in the third year, we found that sown β‐diversity had a significant, positive effect on aboveground biomass production. Further, we found that α‐diversity and β‐diversity combined better predicted aboveground biomass production than γ‐diversity, indicating the importance of partitioning large‐scale diversity into α‐ and β‐components to fully capture the multiple effects of biodiversity on aboveground biomass production.

Our first hypothesis was that in the abiotically heterogeneous plots of our experiment, plant β‐diversity could increase aboveground biomass production through species sorting processes, if (1) different plant species become dominant in different soil types reflecting their abiotic preferences and (2) these changes in species dominance have positive effects on aboveground biomass production (Isbell et al., 2018; van der Plas et al., 2023). However, we did not find strong evidence for species sorting. In all years, subplots with sandy versus clay soils had on average an equal dominance of clay soil indicating species. Furthermore, contrary to our expectations, in plots with a high sown β‐diversity, differences in the dominance of species with different abiotic preferences among subplots were not higher than in plots with a low sown β‐diversity. In fact, we found the opposite. In the last year of our measurements, ΔCWMCSI became more negative with sown β‐diversity, indicating that in plots with a high sown β‐diversity, the dominance of species indicating sandy soils became relatively higher in subplots with clay soils compared to sandy soils. This may have been caused by mass effects (Leibold et al., 2004), where species that maximize aboveground biomass production in sandy soils also dispersed via seeds to neighboring subplots with clay soils in high β‐diversity treatments, and vice versa. In this scenario, plots with high sown β‐diversity cause species to be relatively common even in subplots where their growing conditions are suboptimal. Given the lack of species sorting, it was no surprise that aboveground biomass production did not increase with ΔCWMCSI. Another study, where plots with different numbers and combinations of plant species were set up in different habitat types, found that those species with highest survival rates in monocultures within a specific habitat type also had highest survival rates in mixed communities within the same habitat type, consistent with the idea that species sorting can mediate positive relationships between β‐diversity and ecosystem functioning (Castillioni & Isbell, 2023). The results of our experiment deviated from this and suggest that in our study system, mass effects may play a larger role in community assembly than species sorting.

In line with our second hypothesis, we found that plots with a high sown β‐diversity gained more species over time when sown α‐diversity was low, or lost fewer species over time when sown α‐diversity was high, leading to increased realized α‐diversity. This is likely because plots with a high sown β‐diversity have access to a larger species pool. A larger number of species increases the potential for at least some species to disperse to and establish in neighboring subplots (Cadotte, 2006; Loreau et al., 2003), leading to higher local realized species richness (Harrison & Cornell, 2008; Ricklefs & Schluter, 1993). We also found, in line with our hypothesis, that in most years when a high β‐diversity resulted in increased realized α‐diversity, communities produced more aboveground biomass. While most classic grassland biodiversity experiments focus on the effects of sown levels of α‐diversity on aboveground biomass production, Rychtecká et al. (2014) reported results from a biodiversity experiment where plots that lost the most species over time had the highest biomass, contrary to our results. However, a key difference to their study was that in our experiment, changes in α‐diversity could be driven not only by local extinctions, but also by the colonization of new species. Local extinctions are often caused by the disappearance of competitively weak species, which tend to produce low amounts of aboveground biomass, so that species losses can have weak, or even positive effects on biomass production. In contrast, species that are gained from nearby plots might be those species that produce high aboveground biomass, so that species gains may also benefit biomass production. From that perspective, our results are partly comparable to observational studies, where observed levels of species richness can be influenced by both local extinctions and local immigration and hence (re‐)colonization. Here, positive relationships between realized grassland plant diversity and biomass production are more common than negative relationships (van der Plas, 2019). These positive relationships can be caused by a combination of “complementarity effects” and “selection effects” (Loreau & Hector, 2001). Here, complementarity effects indicate the situation where plants of a given species produce more biomass when growing in mixed cultures than when growing in a monoculture, for example, due to resource partitioning among species or due to reduced disease spread (Barry et al., 2019; Loreau & Hector, 2001). In contrast, selection effects refer to the situation where those species that are most productive in monoculture plots also become dominant in mixed cultures (Loreau & Hector, 2001). In our experiment, a combination of these processes may have caused positive effects of α‐diversity. For example, in the last year of our experiment, the plant species that was most productive in monoculture plots (F. rubra) also produced >52% of biomass in those four‐species mixture plots where it was sown, suggesting positive selection effects. On the other hand, our experiment contained several legume species, which often cause complementarity effects (Barry et al., 2019). While the design of our experiment does not allow a formal analysis of complementarity and selection effects sensu Loreau and Hector (2001), future studies could dive deeper into the specific mechanisms that have caused our observed positive relationships between α‐diversity and biomass production. In 2024, we did not find a significantly positive relationship between realized α‐diversity and aboveground biomass production when we corrected for sown β‐diversity. This might have been due to the wet spring of 2024 with around 100 mm more precipitation in January–May than the long‐term average (https://www.statista.com/statistics/982744/average-monthly-precipitation-germany/). In general, positive effects of species richness through, for example, resource partitioning are typically weaker in periods when resources are less limiting (Andraczek et al., 2023). Further, our results may underestimate the extent to which having high β‐diversity may rescue communities from species loss or increase α‐diversity. This is because we separated plots belowground using PVC plates to avoid belowground competition between plants of different subplots. This separation means that plants can only reproduce and colonize neighboring subplots via seed. Some of the species in our study can additionally reproduce clonally, so that our experimental design may have led to less positive (or more negative) changes in species richness in high β‐diversity plots.

Given the positive effects of sown β‐diversity on realized α‐diversity, and the positive effects of realized α‐diversity on aboveground biomass production, one would, in line with our second hypothesis, also expect an overall positive relationship between sown β‐diversity and biomass production. In line with this, our results showed that such a positive and significant relationship between β‐diversity and aboveground biomass production was indeed present in the last three seasons of measurements (spring 2023, summer 2023, spring 2024). In the earlier years and seasons, positive trends were also observed, although these were not significant. Our results thereby suggest that thanks to the larger species pools typically associated with a high β‐diversity, β‐diversity can increase aboveground biomass production through enriching effects on local diversity, although these effects take time and are therefore only observable when an experiment has lasted sufficiently long. Importantly, this mechanism by which β‐diversity can increase aboveground biomass production can only be demonstrated in experiments such as the current one, and not in observational studies. The reason for this is that β‐diversity itself decreases when species disperse across patches (Matias et al., 2013), so that realized β‐diversity (the type of β‐diversity that is studied in observational studies) often shows weaker or even negative relationships with realized α‐diversity (van der Plas et al., 2023), masking some of its effects on biomass production.

Finally, we hypothesized that because effects of α‐diversity and β‐diversity on aboveground biomass production operate via different mechanisms, considering their separate effects in a single model was likely to provide a better explanation for aboveground biomass production than only including γ‐diversity. In line with this, we found that models including both α‐diversity and β‐diversity better explained (lower AIC) temporal changes in species richness and aboveground biomass production than models where α‐diversity and β‐diversity were replaced by γ‐diversity, despite the higher number of parameters in the combined α‐diversity and β‐diversity model. This is likely the case because α‐diversity and β‐diversity contribute differently to changes in aboveground biomass production. Increasing α‐diversity can increase biomass production via local mechanisms like resource partitioning, abiotic facilitation, and biotic feedbacks (reviewed by Barry et al., 2019). At the same time, β‐diversity supports the maintenance of high α‐diversity by reducing species losses. Hence, considering these different mechanisms separately driven by high α‐diversity and β‐diversity is more important than simply increasing γ‐diversity and indicates that it is not only the total number of species in a larger area that matters for biomass production, but also the spatial partitioning of this larger scale diversity.

To summarize, we found a positive effect of sown β‐diversity on aboveground biomass production, which was caused by positive effects on realized α‐diversity, but not by species sorting. These results indicate that not only local biodiversity declines, but also biotic homogenization has the risk of impairing ecosystem functioning. Our findings, which are based on sown rather than realized levels of β‐diversity, have potential implications for the management of (semi‐)natural and agricultural systems. For example, our results imply that in restoration projects, sowing different species combinations at different sites can, over time (3 years in our study), maintain or increase levels of α‐diversity and thus increase the likelihood of successful biodiversity conservation. In addition, it can maintain higher levels of aboveground biomass production as a result of these positive effects on realized α‐diversity. While our study could not demonstrate additional positive effects on aboveground biomass production through species sorting, it is possible that these could appear after more time has passed. Obviously, additional studies in restoration settings are required to verify whether positive effects of β‐diversity are also realized in more “real world” settings. Further, our results suggest that there may be significant benefits to increasing β‐diversity in agricultural settings by, for example, planting different crops in neighboring fields. In addition, while the current study focused on the effects of β‐diversity on plant aboveground biomass production various other ecosystem functions, for example, (belowground) carbon storage, the biomass of higher trophic levels can be supported by high levels of biomass production. However, further studies are required to see whether these components of ecosystem functioning also profit from high levels of sown β‐diversity.
