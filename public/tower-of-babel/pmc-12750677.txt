Electrocardiographic manifestations and targeted management of cardiac complications in cardiac-involved DLBCL: a retrospective case series
Authors: Jen-Yu Chuang, Kuan-Ting Chen, Johnson Lin, Ken-Hong Lim, Meng-Ta Sung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac involvement in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is rare but often complicated by life-threatening arrhythmias, conduction disturbances, and heart failure. Electrocardiography (ECG) is essential for early recognition and guiding intervention.ObjectivesTo characterize ECG manifestations of cardiac-involved DLBCL and evaluate strategies for managing associated cardiac complications.MethodsThis study is a retrospective institutional case series conducted at MacKay Memorial Hospital from 2010 to 2025. We reviewed five patients with histologically confirmed primary or secondary cardiac DLBCL. Data on tumor location, ECG and echocardiographic findings, cardioprotective and anti-arrhythmic therapy, chemotherapy regimens, and outcomes were analyzed to correlate electrical abnormalities with anatomical involvement and therapeutic response.ResultsECG patterns varied according to tumor distribution. Background

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) with cardiac involvement is a rare but life-threatening condition that presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Primary cardiac lymphoma (PCL) accounts for approximately 1.3% of all primary cardiac tumors, with DLBCL being the most prevalent histological subtype, comprising around 80% of cases. In contrast, secondary cardiac involvement from extracardiac lymphoma is more common, occurring in up to 20% of patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma, as observed in autopsy studies [1, 2]. Given the rarity of the disease, there are still no standard managements for the patients with cardiac-involved DLBCL.

The patients would present arrhythmias, conduction abnormalities, heart failure, cardiac tamponade, and constitutional complaints of lymphoma [3]. Cardiac manifestations can be promptly detected through electrocardiographic (ECG) and echocardiographic findings, requiring early recognition and management with antiarrhythmic agents, pericardiocentesis, pacemaker implantation, or heart failure medications to stabilize the patient. The most common modality was chemotherapy (70–80%), and other treatment choices included immunotherapy, surgery, and radiation therapy [4]. Although the estimated response rate to chemotherapy ranges from 79% to 87% [3, 5], approximately 60% of patients die within two months due to heart complications, tumor lysis syndrome and hemodynamic collapse [6]. Patients who had arrhythmia, congestive heart failure or older age are worse predictors for their prognosis [1, 4, 7], so it is critical to stabilize their cardiac complications from diagnosis trough treatment.

This case series aims to analyze the electrical and structural cardiac manifestations of DLBCL in our hospital and evaluate the efficacy of different therapeutic approaches in managing cardiac-involved DLBCL and the related cardiac complications.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective case series adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. The study was conducted at MacKay Memorial Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan, and included five patients diagnosed with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) with cardiac involvement between 2010 and 2025.

Case ascertainment and definitions

Cases were identified from institutional databases. Cardiac involvement was confirmed by histopathology of cardiac tumor or pericardial effusion, together with imaging modalities such as echocardiography, cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), and Positron Emission Tomography-Computed Tomography (PET-CT). All patients underwent serial ECG and echocardiographic assessments from the time of diagnosis through the course of treatment.

Results

The five cases presented different ECG abnormalities correlating with tumor location and severity of cardiac involvement (Table 1). Case 1 involved a 69-year-old male diagnosed with primary mobile intra-cardiac DLBCL localized to the right atrium (RA) (Fig. 1-A). ECG revealed right bundle branch block (RBBB) and atrial tachycardia (Fig. 1-B), so propranolol was given. The patient underwent surgical excision of the tumor, followed by three cycles of R-CHOP chemotherapy, consisting of rituximab (375 mg/m²), cyclophosphamide (750 mg/m²), doxorubicin (50 mg/m²), vincristine (2 mg), and prednisone (50 mg twice daily for 5 days), resulting in complete remission. Serial ECG monitoring post-treatment demonstrated persistent RBBB with normal sinus rhythm (Fig. 1-C), suggesting reduced sinus node disruption following tumor resection and systemic therapy.

Cases 2 and 3 involved DLBCL with pericardial involvement and associated pericardial effusion. Case 2 was a 94-year-old female with poor performance status and massive pericardial effusion (Fig. 2-A), presenting with low-voltage QRS complexes in both precordial and limb leads (Fig. 2-B), indicative of significant fluid accumulation. She presented acute heart failure and cardiac tamponade initially that pericardiocentesis was performed and Irbesartan was given. Histopathologic confirmation of primary cardiac DLBCL was established using cell block analysis of pericardial effusion. She received pre-phase chemotherapy to mitigate first cycle effect due to the concern of her performance status [8], consisting of rituximab (375 mg/m²), vincristine (2 mg), and corticosteroids.However, she was unable to tolerate this chemotherapy and subsequently developed profound sepsis. Due to treatment-related toxicity and a decline in functional status, she was transitioned to low-dose cyclophosphamide (50 mg/day) and prednisolone (5 mg/day) as a palliative approach. Given the progressive nature of the disease, her family decided to receive hospice care, and she was ultimately transitioned to comfort-focused management.

Case 3 was a 29-year-old female with minimal pericardial effusion, who demonstrated a normal sinus rhythm on ECG and normal ejection fraction (EF) at diagnosis. DLBCL with secondary pericardial involvement was confirmed by the PET-CT scan. She underwent eight cycles of R-CHOP under standard dose and resulted in long-term complete remission.

Case 4 involved a 68-year-old female diagnosed with DLBCL involving multiple cardiac chambers, accompanied by a moderate pericardial effusion (Fig. 3-A). The initial ECG demonstrated QTc prolongation and a junctional rhythm with escape beats (Fig. 3-B). Additionally, low-voltage QRS complexes were observed in precordial leads, likely associated with the pericardial effusion.

During hospitalization, the patient experienced a sudden cardiac arrest while straining during defecation. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated, and the ECG monitor revealed ventricular fibrillation and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT). Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after 10 min of CPR and a single defibrillation. The suspected etiology of the arrest was an R-on-T phenomenon, likely exacerbated by Valsalva-induced autonomic changes during defecation.

Following admission to the intensive care unit, the patient experienced recurrent episodes of VT, necessitating multiple electrical cardioversions and intravenous lidocaine for rhythm stabilization. She subsequently developed cardiogenic shock, requiring inotropic support with intravenous dopamine (Table 1). Her ECG findings evolved significantly over the course of illness, ultimately progressing to complete atrioventricular (AV) block (Fig. 3-C), which required temporary transvenous pacemaker placement.

Due to the rapidly progressive cardiac involvement, debulking surgery was performed to reduce intracardiac tumor burden. Postoperatively, rituximab (375 mg/m²) and corticosteroids were administered following histopathologic confirmation of DLBCL. Despite aggressive intervention, the patient developed sepsis and tumor lysis syndrome, resulting in death within one week of initiating chemotherapy.

Case 5 was a 71-year-old male with a history of pancreatic DLBCL diagnosed five years prior, for which he had undergone surgical resection followed by six cycles of R-CHOP under standard dose, achieving complete remission. Upon relapse, he was found to have a 5.6*5.1 cm RA mass and pericardial involvement (Fig. 4-A), leading to atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, EF 25%). Cardioprotective medications, including angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs; sacubitril/valsartan), bisoprolol, spironolactone, and sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (empagliflozin), were initiated. Additionally, antiarrhythmic therapy with amiodarone and stroke-preventive oral anticoagulation with edoxaban was administered (Table 1). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, revealing imaging characteristics highly suggestive of cardiac lymphoma, and PET scan showed lymphoma involvement to right upper neck, left axillary region, mediastinum, abdomen, bilateral adrenal glands and retroperitoneum (Fig. 5).The diagnosis with late-relapse DLBCL involving heart was confirmed via endomyocardial biopsy, and we started chemotherapy with Pola-BR, consisting of polatuzumab vedotin (1.8 mg/kg), bendamustine (60 mg/m² for two days), and rituximab (375 mg/m²). During treatment, the patient sometimes developed bradycardia and but sometimes turned to ventricular tachycardia (Fig. 4-BC), necessitating careful titration of antiarrhythmic agents and heart failure management. He successfully completed four cycles of Pola-BR chemotherapy, resulting in tumor regression, with the two RA masses in size of 1.9 × 1.0 cm and 0.7 × 0.5 cm, respectively. Additionally, there was an improvement in cardiac function, with an EF of 52%.

Discussion

Based on findings from previous studies and our case series, arrhythmia and heart failure emerge as life-threatening complications that are often challenging to manage in patients with cardiac lymphoma. Optimal management of these cardiovascular complications is a critical component of comprehensive care for these patients.

While previous studies have predominantly concentrated on describing clinical manifestations and treatment outcomes in patients with cardiac-involved DLBCL [9], our case series aims to explore the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms underlying cardiac complications from the point of diagnosis through the treatments. Additionally, we provide an overview of our clinical strategies for the management and treatment of these patients, highlighting approaches to stabilizing cardiac risks while achieving effective oncologic control.

Pathophysiological considerations in primary and secondary cardiac lymphoma

Primary cardiac lymphoma (PCL), defined as a lymphoma confined to the myocardium and/or pericardium, most frequently involves the right heart, though the underlying reasons remain unclear. The right atrium (RA) is the most common site that accounts for 70–80% of cases, followed by the right ventricle (RV) [10, 11]. These tumors are typically large, localized, and solitary masses that may protrude into the cardiac chambers or extend into the atrioventricular groove or myocardium [12]. They are often difficult to distinguish from atrial myxomas or intracardiac thrombi, and surgical debulking or resection is frequently performed in such cases [13]. Our first case, diagnosed in 2013, was initially suspected to be an intracardiac thrombus and underwent surgical resection, with histopathology finally confirming primary cardiac DLBCL. In the previous research, fewer patients present with congestive heart failure at the time of diagnosis in PCL, which could be related to the more localized nature of the tumor. This presentation appears to be associated with a trend toward better prognosis and longer survival compared with secondary cardiac lymphoma [7].

Secondary cardiac lymphoma refers to systemic lymphoma with metastatic or secondary involvement of the heart. Cardiac involvement typically occurs in the setting of advanced, widespread diseases and is associated with a poor prognosis. The pericardium is the most frequently affected site in secondary cardiac lymphoma, often leading to pericardial effusion [14, 15]. Lymphoma cells may reach the heart through hematogenous spread, lymphatic dissemination, or direct extension from adjacent structures [16]. In some cases, lymphoma directly invades the pericardium or cardiac base; in others, tumor cells seed the epicardium and myocardium via the bloodstream or lymphatic channels [17].

Electrocardiographic characteristics and their correlation with cardiac DLBCL

The ECG presentations of primary or secondary cardiac lymphoma are highly variable and mostly depend on the tumor’s location and extent of myocardial or pericardial involvement. For example, lymphomatous involvement of the epicardium and myocardium may produce ECG changes consistent with localized myocardial injury or irritation, including persistent ST-segment elevation—termed “pseudoinfarction” [18]. Pericardial involvement by lymphoma typically presents with ECG findings related to pericardial effusion and tamponade, most classically characterized by low QRS voltage across all leads and electrical alternans in the setting of a large effusion [15]. In our second case of primary cardiac DLBCL and third case of secondary cardiac DLBCL, both presenting with pericardial effusion, the volume of effusion might be correlated with the electrocardiographic findings: massive pericardial effusion was associated with low-voltage QRS complexes across all leads, whereas minimal pericardial effusion was accompanied by a normal sinus rhythm.

When lymphoma infiltrates into the myocardium or cardiac chambers, ECG abnormalities were found to be closely correlated with tumor burden and anatomical distribution. The risk of developing life-threatening arrhythmia might increase with the extent of cardiac chamber involvement under our observation and the previous research [1, 5]. Prior studies have sought to explore the electrophysiological mechanisms of tumor-associated VT using intraoperative electroanatomical mapping. These investigations demonstrated centrifugal activation patterns originating at the border between tumor and healthy myocardial tissue, resulting in localized reentrant circuits [19]. As suggested in these electrophysiological studies by Murata et al., this mechanism may indicate that tumors involving the right atrium or ventricle could be associated with macro–reentry phenomena, manifesting as atrial fibrillation or VT, respectively. Several reports have documented successful control of tumor-related VT through surgical resection or encircling cryoablation, particularly in cases involving resectable tumors such as fibromas, sarcomas, or lipomas [20]. In our clinical experience, arrhythmias—including VT and atrial fibrillation—were managed pharmacologically using anti-arrhythmic agents during and prior to chemotherapy initiation, enabling patients to proceed with definitive oncologic treatment. This approach might indicate that effective treatment of the underlying malignancy often results in resolution of arrhythmia. Nevertheless, in select patients with refractory arrhythmias or those ineligible for chemotherapy, targeted cryoablation or radiofrequency ablation at the tumor–myocardium interface may represent a promising therapeutic alternative.

Conduction disturbances, particularly AV block and bundle branch block, have been observed in patients with cardiac lymphoma involving different parts of the conduction system. These abnormalities are typically attributed to direct infiltration of the cardiac conduction system by lymphoma cells. In such cases, therapeutic strategies including pacemaker implantation, surgical tumor resection, and systemic chemotherapy may demonstrate clinical benefit, with some patients achieving restoration of sinus rhythm following appropriate treatment interventions [21, 22]. In our series, the first patient presented with RBBB and ectopic atrial tachycardia at diagnosis, potentially secondary to tumor-mediated disruption of sinoatrial (SA) nodal function. Following surgical resection, his ECG returned to sinus rhythm. The fifth patient exhibited sinus bradycardia at presentation, likely due to tumor infiltration of the SA node, resulting in a sick sinus syndrome. Administration of chemotherapy achieved tumor regression and resolution of the conduction disturbance. In contrast, the fourth patient, who demonstrated extensive involvement of multiple cardiac chambers, did not benefit from tumor debulking surgery combined with rituximab. The intervention was complicated by procedural morbidity and ultimately contributed to the patient’s death.

These contrasting outcomes showed the importance of individualized assessment and multidisciplinary planning in the management of conduction system involvement in cardiac lymphoma. Precise therapeutic decisions are essential to optimize outcomes and minimize risk.

Therapeutic role and cardiotoxic considerations of anthracycline-based regimens and emerging approaches for cardiac complications

The primary treatment modality for both primary and secondary cardiac DLBCL remains systemic chemotherapy [9]. The standard first-line regimen is Rituximab combined with cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin (or epirubicin), vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP). However, previous studies have demonstrated that this regimen carries a significant risk of irreversible cardiotoxicity, particularly when the cumulative dose of anthracyclines exceeds 250 mg/m² of doxorubicin or its equivalent [23]. According to the current cardio-oncology guidelines issued by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), R-CHOP is classified as high-risk for inducing cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD). These guidelines recommend early initiation of cardioprotective agents and thorough risk-benefit evaluation prior to anthracycline administration [11].

Although the use of anthracyclines remains uncertain without standard guideline in this specific patient population, our clinical experience and existing literature might suggest that most patients with cardiac involvement from DLBCL who receive R-CHOP achieve significant tumor regression, resolution of arrhythmias, and improved cardiac function along with the use of cardioprotective agents [4, 24, 25]. Furthermore, some patients who received reduced doses of doxorubicin during treatment also demonstrated favorable therapeutic responses [9]. The observed clinical improvement may be attributed to doxorubicin-induced cytotoxicity and subsequent tumor regression, which likely relieved myocardial infiltration and electrical conduction disturbance, thereby reducing arrhythmic events and improving cardiac contractility [26, 27].

With the advent of advanced pharmacologic therapies for heart failure and arrhythmia management—such as ARNIs, selective beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, and modern anti-arrhythmic agents—there is an expanding therapeutic window to stabilize cardiac function [28]. Our case series spans from 2013 to 2025, during which significant advancements in cardioprotective therapies and strategies have been announced. Our patients in recent years have benefited from earlier detection of cardiovascular complications and broader access to cardioprotective agents, which may contribute to improved clinical outcomes. Nonetheless, further studies might be needed to evaluate the risks and benefits of anthracycline use in patients with cardiac lymphoma.

Currently, there is no established standard of care for patients with relapsed or refractory cardiac DLBCL. However, regimens such as polatuzumab vedotin combined with bendamustine and rituximab (Pola-BR), rituximab with gemcitabine and oxaliplatin (R-GemOx), and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy are considered viable options under general clinical conditions [29]. In the case of our fifth patient, who was ineligible for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and was at high risk for developing CTRCD, we opted for Pola-BR over R-GemOx to avoid additional cardiotoxic agents and prescribed anti-arrhythmic drugs and cardioprotective drugs for HFrEF. This patient responded favorably to treatment, with marked tumor regression and improved cardiac function.

The therapeutic outcomes varied significantly based on tumor extent, patient age, and performance status [4]. In our case series, the younger patient could tolerate full-course chemotherapy and achieve complete remission, whereas elderly patients with limited functional reserves may face higher mortality, often due to treatment intolerance and infection-related complications. In our second case, a 94-year-old female presenting with massive pericardial effusion, we administered pre-phase chemotherapy with rituximab, vincristine, and prednisolone to mitigate the risk of first-cycle effects; however, she subsequently developed severe sepsis [8].

Despite chemotherapy being the mainstay of the treatment for cardiac lymphoma, surgical intervention also plays the role in select cases, such as patients who has mobile tumors, or who is hemodynamically unstable, unable to tolerate chemotherapy, or require palliative resection for symptoms [30].

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. First, its retrospective design and small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings. Given the rarity of cardiac-involved DLBCL, the cohort was collected from a single medical center over a 15-year period, which may lead to potential survivorship and treatment-selection bias. Second, treatment strategies were heterogeneous and individualized based on patient condition and therapeutic approaches evolved over time, which makes standardized comparison of treatment efficacy very difficult. Third, advanced diagnostic modalities such as electroanatomical mapping, serial cardiac MRI and systematic biomarker surveillance were not uniformly applied in all cases, potentially limiting the precision of correlating electrocardiographic patterns with anatomical involvement and monitoring the cardiac complications. Finally, long-term follow-up data were not available for all patients, restricting assessment of late cardiotoxic effects.

Conclusions

Cardiac involvement in DLBCL represents a rare but life-threatening clinical entity that demands vigilant cardiovascular surveillance and prompt, multidisciplinary intervention. In this small series, we underscores the importance of individualized treatment approaches that integrate both oncologic and cardiologic expertise to reduce the risks associated with arrhythmias and chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity.

With the advance of pharmacologic therapies for heart failure and arrhythmia management, there are more strategies to stabilize cardiac function. Early initiation of cardioprotective drugs and rhythm control strategies may enable patients to safely undergo potentially curative chemotherapy, particularly in cases where anthracycline-based regimens are otherwise contraindicated due to cardiac risk.

While systemic chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for cardiac-involved DLBCL, clinical outcomes vary significantly depending on tumor burden, functional status, and treatment tolerance. These findings suggest an urgent need for more studies to establish standardized, cardio-oncology–driven treatment algorithms that incorporate cardioprotective strategies to optimize survival and quality of life of these patients. While limited by sample size, this case series provides preliminary insights that could inform future studies on cardiac-involved DLBCL.
