HIV-related Stigma and Alcohol Involvement Among People with HIV Who Engage in Unhealthy Drinking
Authors: Lauren B. Bernier, Bradley J. Anderson, Michael D. Stein, Michael R. Winter, Maya P. L. Kratzer, Ana M. Abrantes, Lisa M. Quintiliani, Scarlett L. Bellamy, Hattie A. Slayton, Tibor P. Palfai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Unhealthy alcohol use is linked to several adverse health outcomes among people with HIV (PWH). HIV-related stigma (HRS) has been identified as a potential risk factor for unhealthy drinking, but research has been limited, and findings have been equivocal. This cross-sectional study examined associations between HRS and alcohol-related indices in a national sample of PWH with unhealthy alcohol use enrolled in an intervention trial for one of two common comorbidities that impact functioning among PWH (i.e., chronic pain or physical inactivity; n = 331). Generalized structural equation modeling tested whether depressive symptoms mediated HRS–alcohol involvement associations, and model invariance was assessed across trials. Participants had a mean age of 47.9 (SD = 11.4) years (16.6% identified as female, 29.6% were White, 51.4% were African American). HRS was directly and indirectly associated with greater alcohol use risk severity (as measured by the AUDIT) and mean drinks per day via depressive symptoms only among participants in the chronic pain trial. In the combined sample, HRS was associated with greater alcohol-related consequences, with significant indirect effects through depressive symptoms, although no trial-specific effects emerged. Depressive symptoms may be a pathway through which HRS influences alcohol involvement among PWH. Clinical comorbidities, such as chronic pain, may amplify HRS–alcohol use associations.

Introduction

Unhealthy alcohol use [1] is common among people with HIV/AIDS (PWH) [2, 3]. It represents a significant risk for mortality and morbidity through its impact across the HIV treatment cascade, including lower rates of testing, suboptimal medication adherence, higher viral load, compromised immune functioning, and more rapid disease progression [3–8].

Investigators have shown increasing interest in the potential role of HIV-related stigma (HRS) and related constructs (e.g., discrimination) as modifiable risk factors for unhealthy alcohol use among PWH, affecting heavy drinking amounts and alcohol use disorder (AUD) [9–11]. HRS refers to the actual or anticipated experiences of social rejection, reduced opportunities, and negative experiences in how others perceive individuals with HIV [12]. HRS represents a salient psychosocial stressor that has been associated with heightened emotional distress [9, 13, 14] and the use of maladaptive or avoidance coping strategies [11, 15]––including alcohol use [16, 17].

Despite these findings, research on the associations between HRS and indices of alcohol involvement among PWH has been relatively limited and findings have been equivocal [17–21]. A number of studies have demonstrated a significant association between HRS and AUD [10, 22] or risk for AUD [17] among PWH, rather than consumption alone, as measured by diagnostic or screening questions (e.g., Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test [AUDIT; [23]). For example, Felker-Kantor et al. (2019) identified that PWH with higher levels of HRS had 1.45 times the risk of reporting hazardous drinking, operationalized dichotomously using an AUDIT cut-point, compared to those with low HRS. Separate studies have demonstrated that PWH with alcohol dependence endorsed greater perceived HRS than those without dependence [10, 22]. Additionally, some constructs associated with HRS, such as discrimination experiences among men who have sex with men (MSM) living with HIV, have been linked with alcohol-related consequences, but not consumption [18, 24].

Other studies have not supported the view that HRS is associated with alcohol outcomes, both in samples of PWH endorsing recent heavy drinking and those with little to no recent alcohol use [19, 20]. Hojilla et al. [19], for example, found that HRS was associated with poorer mental and physical health, but not with AUDIT scores among PWH endorsing recent heavy drinking. In a study specifically examining consumption rather than risk for alcohol dependence, Kalichman et al. [20] found no evidence that cumulative past-year HRS was associated with mean number of drinks per day. The inconsistencies across findings may reflect differences in sample characteristics and in how key variables are operationalized, including levels of alcohol use in each sample [20].

Relatively little is known about the mechanisms linking HRS with alcohol outcomes among PWH. Given the well-established association between HRS and indices of emotional distress or dysregulation [25–27], and the negative influence of maladaptive and alcohol-related coping on alcohol outcomes [11, 28–30], indices of emotional distress may be an important pathway through which HRS affects alcohol outcomes. Depressive symptoms may be particularly salient in this regard [25]. Earnshaw et al. [31], for example, found a significant indirect effect of depressive symptoms in the association between internalized stigma and a combined index of substance use that included alcohol among a sample of PWH [31]. Despite the emerging evidence, depression has not explicitly been tested as a mediator of associations between overall HRS and alcohol-specific outcomes among PWH who engage in unhealthy drinking.

Although most of the extant research has examined the association between global HRS and alcohol outcomes, recent studies have suggested that specific stigma dimensions may show stronger associations with alcohol use behavior [17, 19, 21, 32]. Available findings suggest that negative self-image, in particular, may be linked to hazardous alcohol use, though again, associations have not been consistently observed across alcohol outcomes or samples with different levels of alcohol use [17, 19, 21]. Negative self-image, a marker of internalized HRS, has been associated with symptoms related to depression, including self-blame, hopelessness, and emotion dysregulation [17, 33–35], supporting its relevance to indirect effects involving depression.

Finally, PWH with multiple health conditions may be at greater risk of intersectional health-related stigma [36]. Among PWH who engage in hazardous alcohol use, for instance, prior research has shown that the effects of alcohol use on HIV-related outcomes vary across clinical characteristics (e.g. [37]). Chronic pain is one such factor, with recent estimates suggesting it affects over 50% of PWH [38, 39]. One study identified that only among PWH with moderate-to-severe pain was hazardous alcohol use associated with lower odds of viral suppression [37]. Understanding the role of common comorbidities that impact PWH who engage in unhealthy drinking has become a central research priority [40, 41]. The present study included PWH with unhealthy alcohol use with comorbidities that impacted functioning [42, 43]. Namely, participants were enrolled in a trial for unhealthy drinking and either comorbid chronic pain [44] or physical inactivity [44, 45]. We therefore were able to also examine how associations between stigma and alcohol outcomes differed by trial-defined sample features.

The current study sought to replicate and extend previous research on the association between HRS and alcohol outcomes using a national US sample of PWH who engaged in unhealthy drinking and who were enrolled in a study of chronic pain [44] or physical inactivity [44, 45]. In addition to the AUDIT, participants completed multiple alcohol outcome assessments (e.g., consequences, heavy drinking, total quantity consumed), along with a measure of depressive symptoms. The primary aims of the study were to: (1) examine the associations between HRS and the AUDIT, as well as other secondary alcohol outcomes among a sample of PWH who engaged in unhealthy alcohol use; and (2) test the indirect effect of HRS on alcohol outcomes through depressive symptoms. Through tests of invariance, we were also able to examine whether the association between HRS and alcohol outcomes and the mediational role of depressive symptoms differed by trial. Given previous work suggesting stronger associations with subscale components, in particular negative self-image, exploratory analyses were conducted to examine the indirect effect of this HSS subscale on AUDIT scores via depressive symptoms. Primary hypotheses were that higher HRS ratings would be associated with higher levels of hazardous/harmful drinking, as measured by AUDIT scores, and that this association would be indirectly linked through depressive symptoms.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We performed a cross-sectional analysis with data collected from two ARCHER (Addressing Related Comorbidities for HIV by Employing Remote Technologies) trials funded by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (P01AA029546, PI: Stein). Each trial aimed to address particular HIV-related comorbidities among PWH who engaged in unhealthy drinking, either chronic pain [44] or physical inactivity [45], which can intensify alcohol use among PWH. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Boston University Medical Campus Institutional Review Board.

Participants

Participants included 331 adult (≥ 18 years of age) PWH in the US who, across both trials, were required to confirm their HIV diagnosis either through visual evidence of antiretroviral therapy (ART) medication or medical record documentation. Similarly, across both trials, participants were eligible if they reported unhealthy alcohol use (exceeding NIAAA gender-specific typical standard drinks per week [women ≥ 8, men ≥ 15] or per occasion in the past 30 days [women ≥ 3, men ≥ 4]; [46]). Eligibility for unhealthy alcohol use was first assessed through an online pre-screen and then confirmed during a structured phone screen. Participants were asked to report typical weekly alcohol consumption, and whether they had consumed ≥ 3 drinks (women) or ≥ 4 drinks (men) on at least one occasion in the past month. Participants were required to own a smartphone, reside in the US, be fluent in English, and have a US mailing address. Eligibility criteria for both samples are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Exclusion criteria consisted of self-reported diagnoses of bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, or schizoaffective disorder; history of withdrawal-related seizures or delirium tremens; current engagement in non-pharmacological treatment for alcohol use disorder; inability to provide follow-up contact information for one or more individuals; unwillingness to report sex assigned at birth; and limited or non-reading literacy. Notable differences between the trials include that the chronic Pain trial required participants to report moderate or greater pain (≥ 4 out of 10 on the numerical pain rating scale) for at least three months, while the Physical Activity trial required participants to be physically inactive (≤ 150 min of weekly activity). The Pain trial further excluded individuals with unstable doses of psychoactive medications for pain or alcohol/substance use, current cancer-related pain, current non-pharmacological treatment for chronic pain, and/or plans for pain-related surgery in the next six months. Conversely, the Physical Activity trial excluded individuals with physical or medical limitations preventing safe participation in moderate physical activity. In addition to the self-reported DSM-5 diagnoses listed above, the Physical Activity trial additionally excluded individuals with a history of mania or a current diagnosis of anorexia or bulimia nervosa per self-report, as well as those who demonstrated marked organic impairment according to responses to diagnostic assessments.

Assessments

Assessments consisted of interview-administered instruments conducted at baseline and were completed virtually via a secure, HIPAA-compliant videoconferencing platform or by phone to protect participant confidentiality.

Sociodemographic Characteristics

Demographics, including age, sex, and race/ethnicity, were assessed using a self-report demographic questionnaire.

HIV-related Stigma

An 11-item abbreviated version of the Berger HIV Stigma Scale (HSS) was used to measure HRS [10, 47]. This measure does not specify a time frame and therefore captures general experiences of HRS. The first 10 items of the HSS consist of 4 components: personalized stigma, disclosure concerns, negative self-image, and concern with public attitudes toward people with HIV [12]. This abbreviated format has been assessed in previous work [10, 47, 48]. One additional item asks participants to rate their agreement with the following statement, “I worry that the people I drink or do drugs with will act negatively toward me if I tell them that I am HIV positive” [10]. Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 4 (“Strongly agree”). The current study generated a total composite score between 11 and 44, which was then divided by number of items to create a mean item score, with higher scores indicating greater perceived HRS. The HSS showed high internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.86). Internal consistency from the original 10-item HSS was acceptable-to-good across HSS subscales: personalized stigma (α = 0.86), disclosure concerns (α = 0.68), negative self-image (α = 0.87), and public attitudes (α = 0.75).

This study examined both the overall HSS composite score and negative self-image sub-score as predictors across primary and secondary analyses. Items comprising the negative self-image subscale include, “I feel that I am not as good a person as others because I have HIV,” “Having HIV makes me feel unclean,” and “Having HIV makes me feel that I’m a bad person.”

Alcohol

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) [23] is a 10-item screening tool assessing risk of AUD based on questions on alcohol consumption, alcohol-related consequences, and AUD symptoms during the past one year [23, 49]. Risk scores are calculated based on an individual’s total score from the 10-item AUDIT questionnaire. Each item is scored from 0 to 4, with total scores ranging from 0 to 40. Risk categories are defined by cut-off scores: low risk (0–7), hazardous drinking (8–15), harmful drinking (16–19), and dependence (20–40). Mean number of alcoholic drinks consumed per day (i.e., total drinks in the past 30 days/30) and number of heavy drinking days over the past month were derived from the Alcohol Timeline Followback – 30 days (TLFB-30) [50]. The frequency of alcohol-related consequences in the past three months was assessed using the 17-item Short Inventory of Problems-Revised (SIP-R) [51]. Items on the SIP-R reflect consequences in physical, social, intrapersonal, interpersonal, and impulse control domains. Each item is scored from 0 to 4, with a total score ranging from 17 to 68.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ-8) [52], a widely used self-report measure that evaluates the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks. The PHQ-8 includes eight items corresponding to the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder in the DSM-IV, excluding the item assessing suicidal ideation. Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Not at all”) to 3 (“Nearly every day”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 24. Higher scores indicate greater depressive symptom severity. Severity categories were defined using established PHQ thresholds: minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–24); [53]. The PHQ-8 has demonstrated strong internal consistency, construct validity, and diagnostic accuracy in both clinical samples and in the general population.

Other Measures

Although the two clinical trials had a focus on different comorbidities, pain and physical activity were assessed with the same measures at baseline. Pain was assessed using a three-item composite measure based on past one-week average pain intensity, interference with enjoyment of life, and interference with general activity (PEG-3) [54]. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF) [55] was used to assess frequency and duration of physical activity over the past week, with total scores reflecting MET-minutes across activity types. In the Physical Activity trial, participants were provided with a Fitbit, which informed the primary outcome measure of steps per day across the 6-month study period.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were performed using Stata 18. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the characteristics of the study participants in each trial (Pain vs. Physical Activity), including baseline distributions of HRS, depressive symptoms, and alcohol use severity. T-tests were used to determine differences between the Pain and Physical Activity samples for continuous measures and chi-square tests for categorical measures. Product-moment correlations were calculated for the combined sample and separately for each trial.

General structural equation modeling (GSEM) was used to test the hypothesis that there would be an indirect effect of HRS on AUDIT scores through depressive symptoms as measured by the PHQ-8. Parallel analyses were conducted for secondary outcomes of mean number of drinks per day in the past month, number of heavy drinking days over the past month, and alcohol-related problems. The PHQ-8 equation was specified with a identify link and gaussian family error distribution. The equations for AUDIT and SIP-R were specified with a log link and gamma family error distribution. Mean drinks per day and heavy drinking days were positively skewed and therefore were modeled using a negative binomial distribution with a log link. Apart from indirect and total effects, robust standard errors were used to estimate confidence intervals and test the significance of model coefficients. Indirect and total effects were tested using bootstrapped (5,000 replications) percentile confidence interval estimates; 95% confidence interval estimates that excluded 0 were considered significant at the 0.05 level. Prior to final model specification, Wald chi-squared tests were used to evaluate between-study invariance of the direct effect of HRS on PHQ-8 depression scores, as well as the direct effects of both HRS and PHQ-8 on AUDIT and SIP-R scores.

Exploratory analyses were additionally conducted to examine whether the negative self-image HSS subscale was associated with the primary alcohol outcome (AUDIT scores) and whether depressive symptoms served as an indirect pathway linking negative self-image with AUDIT scores. These analyses are considered exploratory given high intercorrelations observed among HRS subscales in the current sample and are designed to supplement primary models using the full HSS score.

Results

Descriptive Characteristics

At baseline, participants (N = 331) had a mean age of 46.7 (SD = 11.8) years, 16.6% identified as female, 29.6% were White, 51.4% were African American, and 19.0% identified with other or multiple races (Table 1). Latino/a ethnicity varied significantly, X2 (1, 331) = 6.95, p =.008) between studies; 15.0% and 27.0% of participants identified as Latino/a in the Pain and Physical Activity studies, respectively. Ethnicity was therefore included as a covariate in estimated models. As expected, the Pain sample reported significantly greater pain severity on the PEG-3 scale (M = 5.88, SD = 2.13) than the Physical Activity sample (M = 2.40, SD = 2.42, t(329) = 13.77, p <.001) and higher physical activity levels, reflected in greater total METs, t(328) = 6.92, p <.001), as well as a greater likelihood of meeting physical activity criteria, X2 (2, 331) = 40.79, p <.001). Across the two trials, participants did not differ significantly with respect to depressive symptoms (mild), HRS, number of drinking days, mean drinks/day, number of heavy drinking days, AUDIT total scores, or SIP-R scores. Similarly, HSS subscale scores were largely comparable across trials, apart from public attitudes, which was modestly higher in the Pain Sample (M = 2.70, SD = 0.81) than in the Physical Activity sample (M = 2.52, SD = 0.69, t(329) = 2.22, p = 0.027). Descriptive statistics for these measures are reported in Table 1.

Product-Moment Correlations

Zero-order correlations for the combined studies and by trial are provided in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. In the combined sample (Table 2), PHQ-8, HRS, and negative self-image were correlated positively and significantly with AUDIT and SIP-R scores, whereas only PHQ-8 and HRS were significantly associated with other alcohol consumption frequency measures (mean drinks per day, heavy drinking days). In the Pain sample (Table 3, below main diagonal), the pattern for negative self-image mirrored that observed for the combined sample, with significant positive associations observed for AUDIT and SIP-R scores but not frequency measures. However, in the Physical Activity sample (Table 3, above the main diagonal), neither PHQ-8 nor HRS were correlated significantly with AUDIT total scores, and negative self-image was associated only with SIP-R scores.

Tests of Between Trial Invariance

The direct effect of HRS on PHQ-8 depression scores (χ2 = 2.14, df = 1, p =.143) and the effect of PHQ-8 depression scores on AUDIT scores (χ2 = 1.53, df = 1, p =.216) did not significantly differ between studies. However, the direct effect of HRS on AUDIT scores differed significantly across studies (χ2 = 5.37, df = 1, p =.021), indicating non-invariance. Consequently, a two-group GSEM was estimated to evaluate the indirect effect of HRS on AUDIT via PHQ-8 depression scores. With respect to mean drinks per day, the direct effect of HRS on PHQ-8 depression scores did not differ significantly across studies (χ2 = 2.14, df = 1, p =.143), but the direct effect of HRS on mean drinks per day was significantly different (χ2 = 4.25, df = 1, p =.039), warranting the use of a two-group GSEM for this outcome. For SIP-R consequence scores, the direct effects of HRS (χ2 = 2.16, df = 1, p =.141) and PHQ-8 depression scores (χ2 = 1.89, df = 1, p =.170) were statistically invariant; thus, a single-group GSEM was used for these models.

AUDIT Model

HRS had significant direct effects on PHQ-8 depression scores in both the Pain (b = 2.007, 95% CI [0.819; 3.196], z = 3.31, p <.001) and Physical Activity samples (b = 3.322, 95% CI [2.024; 4.620], z = 5.02, p <.001; see Table 4). In the Pain sample, both HRS (b = 0.175, 95% CI [0.021; 0.330], z = 2.22, p =.026) and PHQ-8 depression scores (b = 0.030, 95% CI [0.013; 0.046], z = 3.54, p <.001) were significantly associated with higher AUDIT scores. The indirect effect of HRS on AUDIT scores, through PHQ-8 depression scores, was also significant in the Pain sample (b = 0.060, 95% CI [0.015; 0.121], p <.05), as was the total effect of HRS on AUDIT scores (b = 0.235, 95% CI [0.059; 0.390], p <.05; See Fig. 1). In the Physical Activity sample, neither the direct effect of HRS on AUDIT scores, nor the effect of PHQ-8 depression scores on AUDIT scores reached statistical significance. Notably, the total effect of HRS on AUDIT was significantly stronger in the Pain sample compared to the Physical Activity sample (b = −0.043, 95% CI [−0.186, 0.098], p = ns; see Fig. 2). However, the indirect effect of HRS via depressive symptoms (PHQ-8) did not differ significantly between the two samples.1

Analyses using the negative self-image subscale paralleled the HRS results. In the Pain sample, both negative self-image and PHQ-8 depression scores were significantly associated with higher AUDIT scores, with significant indirect (b = 0.049, 95% CI [0.007, 0.091], p <.05) and total effects (b = 0.179, 95% CI [0.051, 0.205], p <.05). In the Physical Activity sample, the direct effect of negative self-image on AUDIT scores was not significant, nor were PHQ-8 depression scores significantly associated with AUDIT scores. Between-study invariance testing indicated that the total effect of negative self-image on AUDIT scores was significantly stronger in the Pain sample than in the Physical Activity sample (b = 0.235, 95% CI [0.074, 0.393], p <.05), whereas indirect effects did not significantly differ between studies.

Mean Drinks Per Day Model

Stigma did not have a statistically significant direct effect on mean drinks per day in either the Pain (b = 0.165, 95% CI [− 0.089, 0.419], z = 1.27, p = 0.203) or Physical Activity (b = − 0.233, 95% CI [− 0.514, 0.048], z = − 1.63, p = 0.104; see Table 5) samples. However, the direction of effects diverged—positive in the pain sample and negative in the Physical Activity sample—reflecting a disordinal interaction. PHQ-8 depression scores were positively associated with mean drinks per day in the Pain sample (b = 0.030, 95% CI [0.001, 0.058], z = 2.07, p = 0.038), but not in the Physical Activity sample (b = − 0.006, 95% CI [− 0.035, 0.023], z = − 0.41, p = 0.684). In the Pain sample, the indirect effect of HRS on mean drinks per day via PHQ-8 depression scores was significant (b = 0.060, 95% CI [0.022, 0.128], p <.05), as was the total effect (b = 0.225, 95% CI [0.056, 0.422], p < 0.05). Neither effect was statistically significant in the Physical Activity sample. While the indirect effects did not differ significantly across groups, the opposing directions of the direct effects resulted in a significant between-group difference in the total effect of stigma on mean drinks per day (b = 0.478, 95% CI [0.227, 0.756], p < 0.05).

In the Pain sample, the indirect effect of negative self-image on mean drinks per day via depressive symptoms was significant (b = 0.049, 95% CI [0.002, 0.113], p <.05), although the total effect was not significant. In the Physical Activity sample, neither the direct nor indirect effects were statistically significant. However, the total effect of negative self-image on mean number of drinks per day was significant (b = −0.212, 95% CI [−0.462, −0.002], p <.05). Between-study differences were not statistically significant.

SIP-R Model

Since the parameters of substantive interest were statistically invariant across study samples, a one-group model was estimated using the combined dataset, with study included as a covariate (see Table 6). In the combined sample, HRS had a statistically significant direct effect on PHQ-8 depression scores (b = 2.644, 95% CI [1.759; 3.527], z = 5.86, p <.001). HRS (b = 0.110, 95% CI [0.055; 0.165], z = 6.0, p <.001) and PHQ-8 depression scores (b = 0.023, 95% CI [0.016; 0.031], z = 3.92, p <.001) both had statistically significant direct effects on SIP-R scores. In addition, both the indirect effect of HRS on SIP-R via PHQ-8 depressive symptoms (b = 0.060, 95% CI [0.015; 0.121], p <.05) and the total effect (b = 0.235, 95% CI [0.059; 0.390], p <.05) of HRS on SIP-R scores were statistically significant.

In the Pain sample, negative self-image was also associated with higher SIP-R scores (b = 0.030, 95% CI [0.028, 0.159], p =.005), with significant indirect (b = 0.041, 95% CI [0.010, 0.076], p <.05) and total effects (b = 0.137, 95% CI [0.056, 0.213], p <.05). In the Physical Activity sample, negative self-image remained directly associated with SIP-R scores, and the indirect (b = 0.046, 95% CI [0.020, 0.076], p <.05) and total effects (b = 0.077, 95% CI [0.019, 0.135], p <.05) were significant. Between-study differences were not detected.

Heavy Episodic Drinking Model

In the Pain sample, PHQ-8 depression scores were significantly associated with greater numbers of heavy drinking days (b = 0.040, 95% CI [0.001; 0.080], z = 1.99 p =.046; see Table 7), but neither the direct, indirect, nor total effects of HRS were significant. In the Physical Activity sample, neither the direct effect of HRS on heavy drinking days, nor the effect of PHQ-8 depression scores on heavy drinking days were significant. The indirect and total effects similarly did not reach significance. Exploratory analyses further indicated that negative self-image was not directly associated with heavy drinking days across samples; direct and total effects were not significant.

Discussion

This study examined the association between HIV-related stigma (HRS) and alcohol-related behaviors, including AUD risk severity (AUDIT), consumption (mean drinks per day and heavy drinking days), and alcohol-related consequences among PWH engaged in unhealthy alcohol use. Additionally, we examined whether depressive symptoms served as a potential mediator of HRS–alcohol associations. Results showed that associations between HRS and alcohol-related behaviors were significantly different by trial. There were significant associations between HRS and both AUD risk severity and alcohol consumption only among those in the Pain trial. Moreover, among those in the Pain trial, there were significant indirect effects observed between HRS and these alcohol outcomes via depressive symptoms. In contrast, no significant associations emerged in the Physical Activity trial between HRS and either alcohol risk severity or alcohol consumption measures. These findings suggest that the co-occurring experience of chronic pain may exacerbate the effects of HRS on indices of alcohol involvement among PWH engaged in unhealthy alcohol use, a comorbid condition that has been associated with alcohol involvement in previous work among PWH [56, 57].

Interestingly, while trial-specific effects (i.e., Pain vs. Physical Activity) emerged for AUDIT risk categories and for mean drinks per day, this pattern did not extend uniformly across all alcohol outcomes. In particular, the finding that HRS was significantly associated with greater alcohol-related consequences was invariant across trials, and indirect effects of depressive symptoms were also observed across trials. This may suggest that the association between stigma and alcohol-related consequences may be less dependent on the impact of comorbid conditions among PWH who engage in unhealthy drinking in contrast to other indices of alcohol involvement. This should be explored further in future work.

Exploratory findings suggest that HIV-related negative self-image may represent a relevant dimension of HRS in relation to depressive symptoms and alcohol-related consequences across the combined sample. Depressive symptoms partially accounted for associations between negative self-image and alcohol-related outcomes, particularly in the Pain sample where indirect effects were observed for AUDIT, mean drinks per day, and SIP-R scores. Notably, most of the combined sample (72.7%) either denied depressive symptoms or reported mild symptoms on the PHQ-8, raising the possibility that stronger or more consistent indirect effects may have emerged in a sample with greater depressive symptom severity. Nevertheless, findings are consistent with prior work identifying links between negative self-image and both depression and hazardous alcohol use [17, 32], as well as indirect effects of negative self-image on hazardous alcohol use via emotion dysregulation among PWH not selected for hazardous alcohol use or chronic pain [27]. Associations with AUDIT scores and mean alcohol consumption were more evident among participants with chronic pain, suggesting that internalized HRS may relate more strongly to alcohol severity in the context of greater pain burden.

There are limitations to the study that should be considered in the interpretation of these results. First, the study is limited by its cross-sectional design which does not allow for causal inference. Longitudinal studies are needed to further delineate the temporal and potentially reciprocal associations between HRS, depression, and alcohol involvement over time. Indeed, the association between HRS and alcohol use severity may also be bidirectional, such that those endorsing hazardous use or dependence report higher levels of perceived HRS [11, 22, 58]. Second, eligible participants across both trials were those engaged in HIV care who met criteria for unhealthy alcohol use, which may limit generalizability to PWH who show lower risk drinking patterns. Third, due to space limitations and concerns of multiple testing, we did not examine whether other types of HRS captured by subscales of the HIV Stigma Scale (personalized stigma, disclosure concerns, concern with public attitudes) are differentially associated with depression and alcohol outcomes. This may be of interest in future work. Fourth, the present study used an 11-item abbreviated version of the Berger HIV Stigma Scale that included an item assessing anticipated stigma from peers who also use substances. As many prior studies have used the 10-item version, HRS scores in the current sample may differ slightly due to the additional item. It should be noted that mean levels of HRS items in the present sample are still comparable to those observed in other HIV populations characterized by hazardous alcohol use or substance use [10, 48]. Finally, there may be other features of stigma (e.g., related to HIV and intersecting identities) that are not captured with the current measure.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes to our understanding of how HRS may influence alcohol use patterns among PWH. First, it replicates and extends previous research on HRS and alcohol use among those who engage in unhealthy drinking. Most salient is the finding that the impact of HRS on alcohol consumption and AUD risk severity may vary as a function of moderate to severe chronic pain. To our knowledge, the current study is among the first to directly compare HRS–alcohol associations across two clinical trial samples of PWH who engage in unhealthy drinking with differing medical comorbidities (chronic pain or physical inactivity). Importantly, the Pain trial included individuals with moderate to severe chronic pain, whereas the Physical Activity trial excluded individuals with pain-related physical limitations that would prevent or interfere with participation. Participants in the Pain trial reported both greater pain severity, as well as higher physical activity levels and a greater likelihood of meeting physical activity criteria. Substantial variability in activity levels was evident across both samples, suggesting the two trials reflect overlapping yet non-equivalent comorbidity profiles. Findings suggest that HRS interventions to reduce unhealthy drinking may be particularly important for subgroups characterized by chronic pain and perhaps other comorbidity burden.

By examining multiple dimensions of alcohol involvement, such as consumption amounts and alcohol-related consequences, this study provides a more nuanced picture of how stigma is associated with alcohol-related behaviors beyond the more commonly studied AUD risk severity outcome [26, 31]. Finally, this study highlights the potential importance of depressive symptoms as a mediator of the association between HIV-stigma and alcohol involvement, at least among a subset of PWH who engage in unhealthy alcohol use. Previous studies have implicated emotional distress-related constructs as mediators of HRS–alcohol involvement pathways [11, 21, 27], yet few have formally tested the indirect effects of depressive symptomatology in this association. For PWH with unhealthy drinking and chronic pain, alcohol intervention efforts may benefit from identifying and targeting HRS and its emotional sequelae.
