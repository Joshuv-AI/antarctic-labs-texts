Harnessing salt tolerance and C4-like traits from the halophyte wild species Oryza coarctata into cultivated rice (Oryza sativa L.)
Authors: Manas R. Prusty, Sherry L. Henchanova, Jolly Chatterjee, Joie Ramos, Janice Sapin, Young-Chan Cho, Charng-Pei Li, Sung-Ryul Kim, Kshirod K. Jena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing rice varieties suitable for salt-affected coastal regions is critical for ensuring food security in the face of rising sea levels and a growing global population. Oryza coarctata, a halophytic wild species with a KKLL genome, thrives in coastal seawater environments and possesses exceptional traits for salt tolerance and C₄-like photosynthesis. In this study, we successfully hybridized the genomes between AA (O. sativa) and KKLL and further introgressed these unique traits into cultivated rice (Oryza sativa, AA genome) by overcoming hybridization barriers between the two species. Through 36,000 interspecific crosses and optimized embryo-rescue protocols, we generated AKL hybrid and advanced backcross progenies in the indica rice cultivar IR56 background. To confirm the true hybridizations and introgressions, we developed genome-wide polymorphic InDel marker set (132 markers) across 12 chromosomes through direct sequence comparisons. We subsequently developed monosomic alien addition lines (MAALs) and disomic introgression lines (DILs), confirmed using genome-specific InDel markers and SNP genotyping. The F1 and BC1F1 lines exhibited remarkable salinity tolerance, surviving up to EC 24 ds m−1 (240 mM NaCl), whereas the standard salt-tolerant checks (Nonabokra, FL478 and Pokkali) survived only up to EC 12 ds m−1 (120 mM NaCl). This enhanced tolerance is associated with efficient improved Na+/K+ homeostasis, with salt gland-mediated secretion in early hybrids and internal ion regulation in advanced introgression lines. These lines also displayed C₄-like anatomical features, including reduced mesophyll cell rows between bundle sheath cells and enlarged bundle sheath cells traits linked to enhanced carbon assimilation and reduced photorespiration. Furthermore, the disomic introgression lines showed salinity tolerance at EC 12 ds m−1 and retained stable C₄-like leaf characteristics. This work demonstrates the successful transfer of novel salt-tolerance and C₄-like anatomical features from O. coarctata into cultivated rice, offering a promising genetic resource for breeding climate-resilient, high-yielding rice varieties suitable for saline coastal agro-ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54410-6.

Introduction

Rice is the staple food of more than half of the world population. It is cultivated globally in a diverse agro-climatic environment. Global rice production is increasingly threatened by declining arable land, biotic and abiotic stresses and the effect of climate shift1. Increasing population demands sustainable rice production to maintain global food security2. Soil salinity is one of the major abiotic stresses caused by over irrigation of rice land and sea-level rise as well as challenges associated with climate change, which mainly affect rice production in the coastal and deltaic ecosystems3,4. It is broadly estimated that salt affected rice area is nearly 900,000,000 hectares globally5. Cultivation of rice in salt affected soil inhibits uptake of water due to reverse osmosis and damages leaves resulting reduced yield of rice6. Cultivation of rice by enhancing salt tolerance of modern rice cultivars is imperative to safeguard rice production.

The genus Oryza has two cultivated and 22 wild species with 11 distinct genomes belonging to three different gene pools such as primary, secondary and tertiary based on the cross compatibility and cytogenetics of the genomes7. The wild species of AA genome are cross compatible whereas the species of BB, CC, BBCC, CCDD, EE, FF, GG, HHGG are cross incompatible but the species of HHKK and KKLL genomes of the tertiary gene pool are extremely cross incompatible with the O. sativa genome. The six wild species of AA genome are diploids like O. sativa but the 16 wild species of other 10 genomes are either diploids or allotetraploids. Some of the wild species of Oryza are adapted to extreme environmental conditions and harbored valuable genes for abiotic stress tolerance8,9. The Oryza coarctata of the tertiary genepool is an allotetraploid wild species with KKLL genome (2n = 4x = 48) and is native to mangrove and estuarine habitats near the bay of Bengal sea coast of India10. This species exhibits unparalleled tolerance to near-sea water salinity. The extremophilic adaptation of O. coarctata possesses a suite of specialized traits such as salt tolerance by possessing aerial rhizomes, salt-secreting glands, leaf succulence, C₄-like leaf anatomical features and extensive aerenchyma11–14. Recent molecular analyses have revealed that O. coarctata possesses key salt stress-related genes such as HKT1;5, NHX1, and SOS1 along with enriched pathways for osmotic regulation, wax biosynthesis, and ion transport9,15,16. Genomic efforts attempted for O. coarctata have yielded both a draft genome and a chromosome-scale assembly reinforcing it’s potential as a donor for rice improvement17,18.

Alien chromosome addition lines and disomic lines were developed, and some economically important traits/genes for biotic stress tolerance were successfully transferred from wild species of BB, CC, BBCC, CCDD, EE and FF genomes7,19–23. However, the transfer of genes for salt tolerance and C4-like traits from the halophytic wild species, O. coarctata remains elusive. The wild species, O. coarctata naturally grows in brackish water near the coastal saline areas and genetically inaccessible due to its distinct phylogenetic relationship, allotetraploid genome structure and absolute hybrid sterility if combined with O. sativa 14,24. Additionally, O. coarctata has anatomical features analogous with C4 photosynthesis which is essential for restricting photorespiration and increasing yield of rice25. Until this study there is no success towards development of chromosomal introgression lines or breeding populations required for incorporating the most valuable salt tolerant and C4-like anatomical features from O. coarctata.

In this study, we report the development of a cytogenetically confirmed AKL hybrid and subsequent confirmation of F1, BC1 and BC2 progenies by cytogenetic analysis. Advanced backcross progenies derived from O. coarctata were further characterized for the inheritance of value-added traits such as salt tolerance, C4-like anatomical features that were analyzed through molecular and histological analysis using genome-specific InDel and SNP markers spanning all the 12 rice chromosomes. We successfully developed alien chromosome addition lines and produced disomic introgression lines (DILs) with salt tolerance trait into the O. sativa genome. The alien addition lines retained substantial segments of O. coarctata chromosomes and exhibited high degree of salinity tolerance, surpassing the standard salt tolerant checks under EC 24 ds m−1 (240 mM NaCl) concentration. We further identified BC3F3 DILs which were inheriting C4-like anatomical features from O. coarctata.

Results

Production of AKL hybrid and progenies

The allotetraploid wild species Oryza coarctata is highly cross incompatible to O. sativa due to associated severe reproductive barriers. We developed a scheme to produce AKL hybrid and subsequent progenies for the transfer of value-added traits such as salt tolerant, large bundle sheath cells associated with C4 photosynthesis from the halophyte O. coarctata. An interspecific hybrid between the rice variety, IR56 and O. coarctata (Acc. No. IRGC104502) was successfully produced after attempting 36,000 crosses followed by 75 ppm GA3 application and using a modified embryo rescue technique at the Plant Breeding, Genetics, and Biotechnology Division (PBGB) of the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI), Manila, Philippines (Fig. 1). Unlike the standard protocols that we previously used for the production of AB, AC, ABC, ACD, AE hybrids, pollinated spikelets were harvested as early as seven days post-pollination rather than the typical 10–14 days to extract embryo. Of the 36,000 spikelets pollinated, we obtained 9709 spikelets after massive pollination of O. sativa (IR56) stigma with pollen from O. coarctata (Table 1). These spikelets appeared as early seed development but after adopting a modified embryo rescue only one embryo was isolated and implanted on a suitable MS medium that proved critical. Rest of the filled spikelets had no embryo but ovary was filled with watery liquid. Genome-specific InDel marker analysis of O. coarctata chromosome addition lines

To monitor and characterize alien chromosomes (Fig. 3, Table S1) segments inherited from Oryza coarctata, we developed a set of 132 species-specific InDel markers distributed across all 12 rice chromosomes at approximately equal intervals (~ 2.9 Mb intervals). These markers enabled precise identification of introgressed O. coarctata chromosomal segments in the AKL hybrid progenies derived from IR56 × O. coarctata cross. Molecular genotyping of the F1 plants confirmed their true hybrid status, as co-dominant amplification was observed for all designed markers (Fig. 3, Fig. S3). Marker analysis of the BC1F1 progenies revealed clear inheritance profiles, consistent with cytological observations showing retention of one complete set of 22 additional O. coarctata chromosomes (2n + 22 = 46). Enhanced salt tolerance in O. coarctata addition lines under hydroponic culture with salt stress

Under high salt stress, the contrast between cultivated rice and the O. coarctata chromosome addition lines was striking. O. coarctata, F1, and BC1F1 plants remained green and vigorous even at 240 mM NaCl (EC 24), a level at which tolerant checks such as Nonabokra, Pokkali, and FL478 showed high salt sensitivity (Fig. 6a). In contrast, BC2F1 plants exhibited stress tolerance symptoms. The BC2F2 seedlings were screened at EC 6 ds m−1, EC 8 ds m−1, and EC 12 ds m−1, and tolerant plants from EC 12 ds m−1 were advanced to generate BC3F3 seeds, which were further evaluated at EC 12 ds m−1. Twelve tolerant lines were selected at the seedling stage (Fig. S1, Table S5). At EC 12 ds m−1 (120 mM NaCl), several disomic lines exhibited salt tolerance and these plants showed O. coarctata specific SNPs as revealed from 6 K SNP markers (Fig. 4 d,e). Analysis of Na+/K+ ratios in both leaf and root tissues revealed distinct salt tolerance mechanisms among the genotypes (Fig. 6 b,c). O. coarctata and F1 plants maintained low Na+/K+ ratios through salt gland mediated Na+ secretion combined with restricted Na+ translocation to shoots. In contrast, BC1F1 lines, which lack salt glands, exhibited efficient root-level Na+ exclusion or sequestration and better K+ retention, thereby maintaining ionic homeostasis under high salinity. However, BC2 plants showed comparatively higher Na+/K+ ratios, indicating weaker ion regulation, whereas tolerant advanced progenies likely rely on enhanced tissue tolerance mechanisms, including vacuolar Na+ sequestration and improved K+ homeostasis.

Larger bundle sheath cells (BSCs) are essential for achieving the two-celled C4 photosynthetic mechanism in rice, as this trait is necessary to accommodate chloroplasts within the BSCs for a functional C4 cycle. In agreement with previous studies on wild rice leaf anatomy, our observations also confirm that O. coarctata possesses significantly larger bundle sheath cells compared to O. sativa 27 (Fig. 7a). Examination of leaf anatomy in the AKL hybrids revealed the presence of wider BSCs in the F1 and backcrossed lines (Fig. 7b). Specifically, bundle sheath cells were approximately two fold, 1.5-fold, 100%, and 140% wider in the F1, BC1F1, BC2F1, and BC3F1 plants, respectively, while O. coarctata exhibited over a three-fold increase in BSC width compared to O. sativa, as shown in transverse leaf sections (Fig. 7c). Detailed anatomical analyses of the BC3F1 plants were conducted to assess BSC number and size, mesophyll cell (MC) area, vein area, and bulliform cell area (Fig. 7d ). To identify BC3F1 plants with wider BSCs, an initial screening of 119 plants was performed using single-leaf samples to measure BSC size and leaf thickness (Fig. S2 ). From this screening, plants with relatively wider BSCs were selected for detailed anatomical studies using three fully expanded leaves per plant, confirming that 13 plants possessed significantly wider BSCs (Table 2). These plants displayed consistent increase in BSC area, with variable inheritance patterns for other leaf anatomical traits, indicating genuine expansion of bundle sheath cells rather than secondary effects from other structural changes. Discussion

Rice is the staple food for more than half of the population in the world. Rice production has reached plateau for several decades28. It is predicted that global population will reach to 9 billion by the year 2050. In order to meet the rice demand for food in future, it is imperative to increase rice area for production in spite of reduction of conventional rice fields due to urbanization and industrialization in different rice growing countries. The coastal rice fields in rice producing Asean countries are largely saline and rice cultivation is not possible. However, rice production and yield must increase within the context of climate change29. Coastal salt affected global rice areas of about 900, 000, 000 ha should be included for rice cultivation by applying scientific innovations and increase rice production5. One of those innovative technologies is to transfer genes/traits for salt tolerance from a wild Oryza species that grows well in coastal saline areas. Additionally, yield increase of modern rice varieties can be achieved by improving photosynthesis through C4 pathways even though cultivated rice is a C3 plant. In this study, we focused on transferring salt tolerance from a distantly related halophytic wild species, O. coarctata belonging to the tertiary gene pool of Oryza. We also identified C4 photosynesis characteristics in O. coarctata and transferred the C4 anatomical features into O. sativa background.

Extraordinary breeding effort required for MAAL development from O. coarctata

Developing monosomic alien addition lines (MAALs) from O. coarctata represents one of the most technically challenging achievements in rice genetics. Unlike other wild Oryza species, where alien addition has been developed with moderate effort, gene transfer from O. coarctata is hindered by extreme post-zygotic barriers and chromosomal incompatibilities26. There is no information yet regarding transfer of O. coarctata chromosome or traits into progenies even though some F1 hybrids were produced30,31. In this study, we produced a single F1 hybrid plant after pollination of 36,000 emasculated spikelets of the rice variety IR56 followed by special embryo rescue technique. Again, with backcrossing of over 36,000 spikelets, only one viable BC1F1 plant was produced through embryo rescue, resulting in a crossability rate of less than 0.05%. Such inefficiency underscores the profound reproductive isolation of O. coarctata from cultivated rice and highlights the extraordinary difficulty of exploiting this tertiary gene pool32.

By comparison, chromosome addition lines from other wild relatives have required far fewer resources. For instance, O. rhizomatis-derived MAALs were developed with ~ 14,000 pollinations yielding 28 BC1F1 plants7, O. punctata required fewer than 2000 crosses33, and O. latifolia introgressions were obtained with relatively moderate effort22. The O. officinalis, a CC genome species phylogenetically closer to O. coarctata, enabled the development of a complete MAAL set from a manageable number of interspecific crosses19,34.

Despite these obstacles, we demonstrate that introgression from O. coarctata is possible by combining high-frequency pollination, optimized early embryo rescue on quarter strength MS medium with Gelzan, and careful cytogenetic screening. The resulting MAALs and derived lines provide unprecedented access to alleles from O. coarctata including those underlying its exceptional salt tolerance3,11,14. This breakthrough established a new benchmark for utilizing highly incompatible wild germplasm and opened a pathway toward breeding climate-resilient rice cultivars4,9.

Selective recovery and chromosomal instability in early backcross progenies of O. coarctata

In our breeding program, two BC2F1 plants were successfully regenerated through embryo rescue following backcrossing of the sterile AKL F1 hybrid to the recurrent parent IR56. Cytogenetic analysis revealed striking chromosomal imbalances: one plant carried three additional chromosomes (2n + 3 = 27), whereas the other harbored as many as 18 extra chromosomes (2n + 18 = 42). Only the plant with 27 chromosomes survived well and restored partial fertility and subsequently contributed toward the development of both BC3F1 and BC2F2 progenies. The second plant, loaded with excessive alien chromosome additions, failed to survive beyond early regeneration. This somatic lethality can be attributed to genomic instability, mitotic disruptions, and deleterious gene dosage effects associated with large-scale alien chromosomal introgression24.

Such outcomes highlight the intrinsic difficulty of maintaining genome stability when introducing widely diverse chromosomes from the KKLL genome of O. coarctata into the AA genome of rice. Previous work has shown that wide hybrids with extensive alien chromosome additions often suffer from developmental abnormalities or lethality8,24. Hence, the recovery of intact O. coarctata chromosomes will require alternative strategies. One promising avenue is to exploit the surviving partially fertile BC2F1 plant to produce large BC3F1 populations, enabling segregation of diverse alien chromosomes that can then be tracked using O. coarctata-specific molecular markers11,14.

Beyond conventional backcrossing, chromosome engineering strategies-including centromere-mediated transfer and radiation-induced translocations, offer promising routes to stabilize and introgress alien chromosomes or large chromosome segments while minimizing disruption to the rice genome. Together, these approaches will be essential to construct a complete MAAL set from O. coarctata and to unlock the stress-resilience traits encoded within this halophytic genome3,4.

To assess potential linkage drag, agronomic traits performance was evaluated in advanced generation lines (BC3F₄), including plant height, tiller number, panicle number, and spikelet fertility, with IR56 as the control. The introgression lines showed comparable trait performance to IR56, indicating no detectable linkage drag. This is consistent with previous studies 35 reporting successful introgression of traits from wild rice genomes without yield penalties, likely due to rare homoeologous recombination events between the AA genome of O. sativa and the KKLL genome of O. coarctata (Table S6).

Discrepancy between morphological and chromosomal diversity in MAALs

Although MAALs were initially distinguished on the basis of morphological traits in the O. sativa × O. coarctata backcross populations, molecular and cytogenetic analyses revealed that these lines could be grouped into only four genetically distinct MAAL types, each carrying either chromosome 1, 2, 8, or a combination of chromosomes 1 and 8 from O. coarctata19,26. This discrepancy between morphological diversity of MAALs and chromosomal classification underscores the complexity of alien introgression.

Several mechanisms are likely contribute to this divergence. The chromosomes of O. sativa and O. coarctata are highly non-homologous as we do not observe chromosome pairing in the F1 hybrid plant. Nevertheless, we identified several value-added traits inherited from O. coarctata into O. sativa parent IR56. This might be due to cryptic rare homoeologous pairing between the chromosomes of AA and KKLL genome. An important insect resistance gene, BPH18 has been introgressed and cloned from EE genome wild species, O. australiensis although no chromosome pairing has been detected in the F1 hybrid35,36. The introgressed alien segments might have harboured pleiotropic genes influencing multiple traits simultaneously, thereby amplifying morphological variation from a single chromosomal addition8,24,33. Secondly, epistatic interactions between alien genes and the rice genome can cause variable phenotypic expression among the lines carrying the same alien chromosome3. Some MAALs may not represent complete alien chromosome additions but instead carry small segmental introgressions (~ 2.0–2.5 Mb), which can still induce noticeable phenotypic effects without being easily resolved by conventional cytogenetic approaches11,14. Additionally, gene dosage effects and environmental modulation of phenotypic traits might have further exaggerated diversity4,9.

These results emphasize the necessity of integrating morphological evaluation with high-resolution molecular markers and cytological tools to accurately characterize alien introgression lines. Importantly, the persistence of broad phenotypic variability despite limited chromosomal diversity highlights a complex interplay between O. coarctata genome segments and the cultivated rice background, warranting further dissection of the functional impact of specific introgressed loci.

Potential applications of the genome-wide polymorphic InDel markers

Molecular markers are essential tools for genetic analysis, evolution studies, and molecular breeding. At the initial stage, we used the simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers to confirm true hybridity. Most of SSR markers failed to amplify or showed very weak amplification from O. coarctata, and only a few markers showed polymorphism between IR56 and O. coarctata. This might be caused by sequence variability, especially at the primer annealing sites between rice and O. coarctata because the SSR markers were developed based on the sequences of O. sativa. Similar phenomena were observed when the AA (O. sativa) /BB (O. punctata) polymorphic markers were applied for far-distance genomes such as EE, FF, GG, HHJJ, and KKLL genomes37. Thus, development of genome-specific markers through direct sequence comparisons are highly recommended. Recently, the genome-wide polymorphic InDel marker sets were developed and released for efficient harnessing of wild rice species for AA-genome wild species38 , BB-genome species37 and CC-genome species39. In this study, we developed 132 polymorphic markers with even distribution (~ 2.9 Mb marker intervals) between rice and O. coarctata through direct sequence alignments. These markers will be useful for harnessing KKLL genome (O. coarctata) and gene transfer into rice cultivars.

Value-added traits in O. coarctata- derived breeding lines

The successful introgression of genetic material from O. coarctata into cultivated rice marks a major breakthrough in rice pre-breeding, given the species’ unique repertoire of agronomically valuable traits. In addition to its well-documented salt tolerance3,11,14 , O. coarctata has also been associated with submergence tolerance and blast resistance9,31. Its natural adaptation to saline and submerged coastal habitats has likely shaped this multi-stress resilience, making it an especially valuable donor for climate-resilient breeding. Notably, O. coarctata is the only wild Oryza species possesses C₄-like anatomical features, including Kranz anatomy, reflecting an evolutionary shift toward more efficient carbon fixation under high light and temperature conditions12. Our BC3F2 plants with bundle sheath cells and reduced number of mesophyll cell could be the most valuable trait inherited from O. coarctata. Incorporation of such economically important traits into O. sativa could improve photosynthetic efficiency and resource-use traits for the future climate scenarios25.

The development of introgression lines and monosomic alien addition lines (MAALs) carrying individual O. coarctata chromosomes provides a powerful platform to dissect and utilize these complex traits. These lines offer not only tools for fine-mapping and gene discovery but also establish pre-breeding materials for direct application in elite breeding pipelines. Importantly, the successful transfer of genetic material from a distantly related species with a KKLL genome, previously considered a formidable barrier due to sterility and incompatibility issues, underscores the strength of the strategies employed in this study-namely, large-scale pollination, embryo rescue, and cytogenetic screening. As climate change intensifies the frequency of salinity, flooding, and pathogen outbreaks, the introgressed population derived from O. coarctata represents an invaluable genetic resource for building climate-resilient rice cultivars. This study reports the first successful development of F1 hybrid from O. sativa (IR56) and O. coarctata cross, and subsequent production of advanced backcross progenies by harnessing the most important genes/traits for salt tolerance and characteristics for C4 photosynthesis aiming towards higher rice production especially in coastal salt affected soil.

Methods

Plant materials

The experimental materials consisted of the cultivated rice variety, IR56 (AA genome) and the halophytic wild species Oryza coarctata (Acc. no. IRGC 104,502) which carries the KKLL genome and is recognized for its tolerance to high salinity. Hybridization between IR56 and O. coarctata was carried out at the Novel Gene Resources laboratory of the PBGB Division, IRRI, Manila, Philippines. We applied massive pollination of O. coarctata pollen on a large number of emasculated spikelets of IR56 to produce F1 hybrid. Due to complete sterility of the single F1 hybrid plant, we multiplied it by clonal propagation and conducted repeated backcrossing with IR56 pollen to restore fertility and developed advance generation progenies. Embryo rescue techniques were employed throughout the crossing and backcrossing experiments to secure viable progenies. In addition to embryo rescue advanced backcross generation progenies with partial fertility were self-pollinated to establish segregating populations for further analysis.

Development of interspecific hybrids and AKL progenies

Hybridization was initiated by emasculating spikelets of IR56 panicles and manually pollinated those emasculated spikelets with fresh pollen from O. coarctata. To support fertilization and seed development, panicles were spray-treated with 75 ppm GA3 one time for five days. Pollinated spikelets were harvested at 7 days post-pollination, an earlier stage than conventional practice of 10 days to prevent embryo abortion.

Immature embryos were excised and cultured on a modified medium consisting of quarter strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) salts, with Gelzan (0.65% w/v) used as the gelling agent in place of agar. This modification was found to promote higher embryo germination efficiency. Successfully regenerated hybrids were acclimatized in Yoshida liquid culture solution40 and maintained in clay pots in the greenhouse conditions.

The sterile F1 hybrid plant was obtained from embryo rescue was maintained and used as female parents in subsequent backcrosses with IR56. In each backcross generation, immature embryos were rescued using the same embryo culture protocol to establish viable progenies. The partially fertile backcross progenies were advanced by either additional backcrossing or self-pollination. This approach facilitated the progressive recovery of advanced backcross lines, including progenies suitable for developing alien chromosome addition lines and introgression lines.

Measurement of ion content in leaf and root

Leaf and root tissues were collected 16 days after exposure to salt stress (180 mM NaCl) and oven-dried at 65 °C for 48 h. Dried samples (~ 10 mg) were transferred to 50 mL tubes and subjected to extraction using 10 mL of 0.1 N acetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 90 °C for 2 h. After digestion, samples were allowed to cool to room temperature, incubated overnight, and subsequently filtered through Whatman filter paper. Sodium (Na+) and potassium (K+) concentrations were determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Analyst 200, USA) operating in emission mode. Ion content values for both leaf and root tissues were calculated as the mean of five biological replicates per genotype.

Morphological and C₄ anatomical characteristics observation

Morphological traits were evaluated in the F1 hybrids, advanced backcross progenies, and the parental lines, IR56 (O. sativa, AA genome) and O. coarctata (KKLL genome). The recorded traits included plant height, leaf length (L), leaf width, panicle length, number of spikelets per panicle, spikelet fertility, grain length, grain width, and 1,000 grain weight. Plant height (cm) was measured from the base of the plant to the tip of the tallest panicle, while leaf length (cm) and leaf width (mm) were recorded from the third fully expanded leaf (L3). Panicle length (cm) was measured from the base to the tip of the panicle, and the number of spikelets per panicle was determined by manual counting. Spikelet fertility (%) was calculated as the proportion of filled grains relative to the total number of spikelets. Grain length, grain width, and 1000-grain weight (g) were assessed using standard grain evaluation procedures. Five plants from 15 BC3F4 lines were randomly selected from advanced DILs and were used for the above trait measurements, The mean values of the traits were recorded. These morphological and agronomic traits were systematically compared among the hybrids, backcross progenies, and parental lines to characterize alien trait introgressions from O. coarctata.

Since the C4 photosynthetic traits occur naturally in Oryza coarctata1, leaf anatomical analyses were performed to determine whether these characteristics were inherited in the hybrid and backcross progenies. The leaf anatomy of both parents (O. coarctata and IR56), as well as of the F1, BCF1, BC2F1, and 119 BC3F1 plants was examined. From each line, the middle Section (5–6 cm in length) of fully expanded leaves was sampled and fixed in FAA solution (50% ethanol, 5% [v/v] acetic acid, and 3.7% [v/v] formaldehyde). The samples were hand-sectioned and observed under fluorescent microscopes.

Leaf thickness was measured as the vertical height of the leaf section at the small vein. A 3D leaf cellular model was redrawn based on Chatterjee et al.27. Transverse leaf section images were captured under UV light using DAPI and RFP filters with Olympus BX61 and BX63 fluorescence microscopes equipped with a DP17 digital imaging system. Images were viewed and analyzed using CellSens software, and anatomical parameters were quantified using ImageJ.

Chromosome analysis

Young panicles were collected from the parental lines (O. sativa cv. IR56 and O. coarctata), F1 hybrids, and advanced backcross progenies during the morning hours (7:00–8:00 AM). Collected panicles were immediately fixed in freshly prepared Carnoy’s solution (ethanol:acetic acid, 3:1 v/v) with a trace amount of ferric chloride and stored for 24 h at room temperature. For cytological analysis, anthers were dissected from fixed spikelets, gently squashed on a glass slide with 1% acetocarmine solution, and examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX53) at 100 × magnification with oil immersion.

Well-spread pollen mother cells (PMCs) at meiotic diakinesis and metaphase I stages were observed to determine chromosome number and pairing behaviour. Cytological configurations, including univalents, bivalents, trivalents, and higher-order associations, were recorded. Images of meiotic cells were captured using a digital camera system attached to the microscope and analysed with Image-Pro Plus 7.0 software for precise chromosome counting and documentation.

Pollen fertility

Pollen fertility was assessed in the parental lines (IR56 and O. coarctata), the F1 hybrids, and the advanced backcross progenies using the iodine-potassium iodide (I2-KI) staining method. Fresh anthers were collected, gently crushed in 1% I2-KI solution, and observed under a light microscope (Olympus BX53). Fertile pollen grains were identified as completely stained, well-filled, and spherical in shape, whereas unstained, partially stained, or shriveled pollen grains were classified as sterile. Percentage pollen fertility was calculated as the proportion of fertile pollen grains over the total number of pollen grains examined.

InDel markers for molecular characterization of IR56 × O. coarctata progenies

Molecular characterization of the progenies derived from the cross between IR56 (O. sativa) and O. coarctata (F1, BC1F1, BC2F1, BC2F2, and BC3F1) was performed using species-specific InDel markers. One hundred thirty-two InDel markers which can discriminate the alleles between rice (AA) and O. coarctata (KKLL) were manually developed through direct sequence comparisons. BAC-end sequences (BES) of O. coarctata were obtained from the OMAP project41 and then, were compared with the reference genome of O. sativa (cv. Nipponbare) using the BLAST tool. Polymorphic loci across the 12 rice chromosomes with even distributions (~ 2.9 Mb marker intervals) were selected for marker development.

Genomic DNA was isolated from fresh leaf tissues using the CTAB extraction method with minor modifications. PCR amplification was carried out using the designed InDel markers under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 50 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were separated by electrophoresis in 3% agarose gels, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized under UV light.

The allele patterns of F1 and backcross progenies were compared with both parental controls to detect the presence of O. coarctata-specific bands. The marker data were used to generate graphical genotypes and identify alien chromosome introgressions across different generations.

Salinity tolerance screening

Salinity tolerance of the IR56 × O. coarctata progenies were evaluated at the seedling stage under controlled conditions in the Phytotron facility of IRRI, maintained at 29 °C/21 °C (day/night). Screening was conducted following the Standard Evaluation System (SES) for Rice42.

For each line, 12 seedlings were included, along with tolerant and susceptible checks. The sensitive controls were IR29 and IR56 (recurrent parent), while Pokkali and FL478 were used as salt tolerant checks. Salinity stress was imposed by exposing seedlings to different levels of electrical conductivity (EC) in hydroponic culture, beginning with moderate stress (EC 6 ds m−1) and advancing to higher levels (EC 12 ds m−1). The nutrient solution was renewed every three days to maintain consistent stress levels.

Individual plants within segregating lines were scored separately, and plants showing a tolerance score of 3 or better were rescued and transplanted into a screenhouse for recovery and seed multiplication. Advanced backcross lines, including BC2F2 and BC3F1 progenies, were subjected to successive rounds of screening to identify tolerant individuals for further evaluation and line advancement.
