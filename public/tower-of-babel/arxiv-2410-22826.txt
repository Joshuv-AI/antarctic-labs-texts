Distributionally Robust Linear Quadratic Gaussian Regulator with Stationary Distributions
Alain Schöbi, Nicolas Lanzetti, Florian Dörfler, Raffaello D’Andrea, Antonio Terpin

Abstract




Abstract
— We study the Linear Quadratic Gaussian regulation problem in the face of worst-case noise distributions when these are mutually independent, zero-mean, stationary, and within a radius (as measured by the Wasserstein distance) of some reference Gaussian noise distributions.
Compared with nonstationary ambiguity models, our stationary modeling choice reduces conservatism when the disturbances are stationary, as is often the case in practice.
We first show optimality of linear output-feedback policies via the existence of a Nash equilibrium in an equivalent zero-sum game between a control engineer and a fictitious adversary that compete to minimize and maximize the control cost.
Additionally, we show that Nash equilibria may fail to exist when the distributions are not restricted to have zero mean.
We then propose an iterated best-response algorithm to compute Nash equilibria and thereby the optimal feedback policies.
Our computational framework unifies two seemingly different viewpoints: game-theoretic iterated best response and Frank-Wolfe approaches.
Finally, we illustrate the robustness of the proposed controller to unknown noise distributions on an inverted pendulum with physical parameters, a canonical control benchmark.

II.  Theoretical Result

A.  Optimality of linear policies and Nash equilibrium

In the theorem below, we show that the
DR
control problem is solved by
linear policies
\boldsymbol{\pi}
of the form

\pi_{t}(y_{0},\dots,y_{t})=\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}K_{t,\tau}y_{\tau}

and
Gaussian distributions
\mathbb{P}
in the ambiguity set
\mathcal{W}
.
We denote the corresponding sets by
\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}
and
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}
.

Theorem 1

(Optimality of linear policies)
.

When solving the
DR
control problem (
4
), it suffices to restrict to the set of linear policies
\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}
and Gaussian distributions
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}
:

\eqref{eq:dr-control-problem}=\operatorname*{inf}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi}\operatorname*{sup}_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{J}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\pi},\mathbb{P}\right)=\operatorname*{min}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}}\operatorname*{max}_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}}\mathcal{J}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\pi},\mathbb{P}\right).

In particular,
the problem admits a Nash equilibrium
(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast},\mathbb{P}^{\ast})

with linear policy
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}

and Gaussian noise distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}
,
so that for all
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi
and
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{W}
,

\mathcal{J}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast},\mathbb{P}\right)\leq\mathcal{J}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast},\mathbb{P}^{\ast}\right)\leq\mathcal{J}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\pi},\mathbb{P}^{\ast}\right).

Consequently, any Nash equilibrium policy
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast}
solves (
4
).

Optimality of linear policies.

Theorem

1
states that
linear
policies remain optimal even in this
DR
setting.
Moreover, the optimal policy
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast}
is a best response to the worst-case distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\ast}
, which is Gaussian.
Consequently, the optimal controller retains the standard
Linear Quadratic Gaussian (LQG)
structure, i.e., a
Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)
driven by a
Kalman filter (KF)
, both of which can be computed efficiently.

Robustness.

Although both the reference and worst-case distributions are Gaussian, the optimal robust controller remains robust against
all
distributions in the ambiguity set
\mathcal{W}
, including the non-Gaussian ones (see
Fig.

2
).

Nash vs. Stackelberg games.

The existence of a Nash equilibrium implies that the zero-sum Stackelberg game is equivalent to a game where the players move simultaneously. In particular, there is no advantage in moving first.
Thus, the Nash equilibrium policy
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast}
is also a Stackelberg equilibrium policy of the sequential game where the engineer moves first, and thereby solves the
DR
control problem (
4
).

Extensions.

Although we focus on
Wasserstein ambiguity sets, our formulation
extends to the divergence-based ambiguity sets of
[

Before proving

Using this formulation, we parameterize linear policies

The expected cost (

where

The matrices

Since the cost depends only

and coinciding with it for Gaussian distributions

D.  On the zero-mean assumption

We show that the restriction to zero-mean distributions in

Assumption

1
is essential in our setting:
Nash equilibria may fail to exist in the nonzero-mean setting
(or, more generally, when allowing the means of the distributions in the ambiguity set to differ from the reference means).
Since the proof of
Theorem

1
is based on the existence of a Nash equilibrium, the same strategy does not establish optimality
of
affine
policies in the nonzero-mean case.
Importantly, this does not rule out the optimality of affine policies, but shows that establishing such a result would require different arguments.

Accordingly, we drop this restriction and consider the larger
ambiguity set
\mathcal{W}^{\odot}
, which contains distributions
with nonzero means; we denote by
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\odot}
its Gaussian
counterpart.
In this setting, let
\Pi_{\mathrm{aff}}
collect affine policies of the form

\pi_{t}(y_{0},\dots,y_{t})=\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}K_{t,\tau}y_{\tau}+q_{t}
.
The counterexample below shows that the nonzero-mean analogue
of
Theorem

1
may fail:
a Nash equilibrium in
\Pi_{\mathrm{aff}}\times\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\odot}
need
not
exist.

Counterexample.

Consider the linear system

x_{t+1}=x_{t}+u_{t}+v_{t},\quad y_{t}=x_{t}+w_{t},

with
horizon
T=2
,
initial state
x_{0}=\mathrm{x}=0

(here deterministic for simplicity of exposition),
and cost

x_{2}^{2}+u_{0}^{2}+u_{1}^{2}
.
Let the ambiguity set
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\odot}
be defined by

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{v}=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,

r_{v}=3
,
and

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{w}=\mathcal{N}(0,3^{2})
,

r_{w}=1
.
One verifies that the
maximin
problem attains the value

\operatorname*{sup}_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\odot}}\operatorname*{inf}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{aff}}}\mathcal{J}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\pi},\mathbb{P}\right)=28
,
for the Gaussian
\mathbb{P}^{\ast}
with means
{\mu}_{v}={\mu}_{w}=0
and standard deviations

\sigma_{v}=\sigma_{w}=4
.
The inner minimizer
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast}
is unique and given by

\pi_{0}^{\ast}(y_{0})=0

and

\pi_{1}^{\ast}(y_{0},y_{1})=-\frac{1}{4}y_{1}
(i.e., with zero offsets).
If an equilibrium existed, this pair would necessarily constitute one, by uniqueness of
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast}
.
However,
\mathbb{P}^{\ast}
is
not
a best response to
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\ast}
, since the feasible Gaussian with

{\mu}_{v}=3
,
{\mu}_{w}=-1
,

\sigma_{v}=1
,
\sigma_{w}=3

yields the strictly larger cost
33.25
.
Hence, no Nash equilibrium exists in
\Pi_{\mathrm{aff}}\times\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\odot}
.

Discussion.

This should be contrasted with the related nonstationary Nash equilibrium result of
[
12
]
, which allows nonzero-mean noise distributions, but only under the second-moment orthogonality constraint

\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\end{subarray}}\!\left[zz^{\prime\top}\right]=0
for any two distinct time-indexed noise terms.
Under mutual independence, this constraint becomes
\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\end{subarray}}\!\left[z\right]\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\end{subarray}}\!\left[z^{\prime}\right]^{\top}\!\!=\!0
, forcing all but possibly one noise marginal to have zero mean.
Thus, in the independent-noise setting considered here, which is standard in control,
[
12
]
provides only a limited nonzero-mean extension, and our counterexample shows that, without such a restriction, a Nash equilibrium may fail to exist.
Nevertheless, using
[
18
]
, one can show that a best response to affine policies remains Gaussian, suggesting a promising direction for future work.

IV.  Conclusion

Linear systems affected by stochastic disturbances are ubiquitous, yet accurately characterizing their noise distributions remains challenging in practice, making distributionally robust control essential.
Exploiting the stationarity of disturbances, we derive a controller that is less conservative than recent approaches.
Beyond its intuitive computational method, our results provide a foundation for several future research directions, including deployment on real-world systems such as
[
3
]
, investigation of the optimality of affine policies in the nonzero-mean setting, and online adaptation of the ambiguity radii as more data on the noise becomes available.
