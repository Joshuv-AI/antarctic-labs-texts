Testing Leptogenesis from Observable Gravitational Waves
Wei Liu, Yongcheng Wu

Abstract
Leptogenesis provides an elegant mechanism to explain the observed baryon asymmetry of the Universe (BAU), yet its experimental verification remains challenging due to requirements of either extremely heavy right-handed neutrinos or precisely fine-tuned mass splittings. We present a scenario where an additional scalar field, coupled to both the Higgs and right-handed neutrinos (RHNs), enhances the CP asymmetry through loop-level contributions, enabling successful leptogenesis at the electroweak scale. This same scalar sector triggers a strong first-order electroweak phase transition (EWPT), producing gravitational waves (GWs) within the reach of next-generation detectors. We demonstrate a robust correlation between the BAU and GW signal strength, providing a falsifiable prediction of leptogenesis that future GW searches can confirm or rule out.

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}

IV
Testing Leptogenesis from Observation of Gravitational Waves

The correlation shown in Fig.
3
demonstrates that
\eta_{B}
and the GW SNR move together, but does not by itself specify what a GW measurement would actually test. Future collider or displaced-vertex searches, together with neutrino oscillation data, could in principle pin down the RHN-sector parameters entering the decays
N_{2}\to\ell H
and
N_{2}\to N_{1}S
, namely
m_{N_{1,2}}
,
a_{21}
,
K_{1,2}
, and
m_{S}(T=0)
. However, the two scalar self-couplings
\lambda_{S}
and
\lambda_{SH}
that govern the EWPT dynamics are not directly fixed by these measurements; they require an independent probe of the scalar potential itself, e.g. via double/triple Higgs couplings, or via the EWPT dynamics they induce.

This is precisely where the shared origin identified above becomes a quantitative test rather than a qualitative analogy. For fixed RHN-sector parameters, the same couplings
(\lambda_{S},\lambda_{SH})
that set the strength and duration of the EWPT (and hence the GW amplitude, frequency, and SNR) also set the size and duration of the extra CP-violating sources
\varepsilon_{2}^{v,s}
(and hence
\eta_{B}
), through the common thermal history of Eq. (

\eta_{B,\rm obs}
selects a specific band in the
(\lambda_{S},\lambda_{SH})

(\lambda_{S},\lambda_{SH})

\eta_{B}
and the GW SNR, which we have checked to be robust against variations of the RHN-sector parameters in the Supplemental Material.

This correlation converts a GW measurement into a direct, two-sided statement about leptogenesis in our framework, for a given, independently measured RHN sector: (i) if a future detection yields a GW SNR and spectral shape whose inferred
(\lambda_{S},\lambda_{SH})
fall inside the band reproducing
\eta_{B,\rm obs}
, this constitutes highly non-trivial supporting evidence for the mechanism, since nothing in the theory otherwise forces the phase-transition strength and the baryon asymmetry to coincide; (ii) conversely, a null search at LISA/U-DECIGO in the relevant frequency band would exclude the region of
(\lambda_{S},\lambda_{SH})
required to reproduce
\eta_{B,\rm obs}
within this mechanism, thereby excluding scalar-assisted leptogenesis as the origin of the observed asymmetry for that RHN spectrum. In either case, the GW measurement—positive or null—carries genuine, falsifiable information about the viability of leptogenesis in our framework, unlike in generic scalar-extended models where the phase-transition sector and the baryogenesis sector are mutually independent and a GW measurement carries no information about the BAU mechanism. This is the sense in which we regard GW observations as a probe, rather than merely an observational avenue, for leptogenesis.

V
Conclusion

We have demonstrated that scalar-assisted leptogenesis provides a compelling solution to the challenges of conventional scenarios. By extending the scalar sector with particles that couple to both RHNs and the SM Higgs, we achieve successful low-scale leptogenesis with light RHNs and without fine-tuned mass degeneracy, while simultaneously inducing a strong first-order EWPT that generates observable GW signals. Importantly, our work establishes a direct multi-messenger connection: the baryon asymmetry and gravitational-wave signals originate from the same scalar-RHN dynamics, providing a novel testable pathway to probe leptogenesis through future GW observations. This represents a fundamental shift from conventional approaches where these observables are typically independent.

A key result is the intrinsic correlation between the baryon asymmetry
\eta_{B}
and the strength of the GW signal. Achieving detectable GWs requires a strong first-order EWPT with a low nucleation temperature
T_{n}
, driven by large scalar couplings. The same couplings control the temperature evolution of the scalar mass and trilinear interactions, which govern the kinematics and size of the CP asymmetry during leptogenesis. Consequently, the parameter space yielding sufficient BAU is tightly aligned with that predicting observable GWs, establishing a direct experimental link between leptogenesis and gravitational wave astronomy. We stress that neither the shape of the GW spectrum in isolation, nor the discovery of the singlet scalar or the RHNs at colliders, would by itself establish a connection to leptogenesis. What makes the connection genuine and falsifiable is the bidirectional logic detailed above: for a given, independently measurable RHN sector, the same couplings required to reproduce the observed baryon asymmetry also predict a specific GW signal, so that a future detection or non-detection directly supports or excludes scalar-assisted leptogenesis as the origin of the baryon asymmetry. This constitutes a qualitatively new type of multi-messenger connection between particle cosmology and gravitational-wave astronomy, in which the same underlying dynamics is simultaneously responsible for two independently measurable cosmological observables—a connection that is particularly timely given the imminent operation of LISA, Taiji, TianQin, and DECIGO-class missions.

Our framework offers promising avenues for future exploration. In particular, scenarios with supercooled EWPTs, where prolonged transitions can further amplify both the GW signal and the baryon asymmetry, deserve detailed investigation. Studies of RHN Yukawa coupling textures and their impact on the BAU-GW correlation will be pursued in future work. Ultimately, our results demonstrate that future GW detectors such as LISA and DECIGO
will provide crucial experimental verification of the leptogenesis mechanism,
establishing a novel probe of the baryon asymmetry origin within the coming decade.

Computation of the GW spectrum

The stochastic gravitational wave (GW) background generated during the electroweak phase transition (EWPT) originates from three primary sources: bubble wall collisions, sound waves in the plasma, and magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) turbulence. The resulting spectrum depends principally on the macroscopic thermodynamic parameters of the transition: the phase transition strength parameter
\alpha

\beta/H_{*}

T_{*}

[

\displaystyle v_{w}=\frac{\sqrt{1/3}+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+2\alpha/3}}{1+\alpha}.

(21)

\displaystyle\times\Delta(v_{w})S_{\rm coll}(f,v_{w}),

(22)

\displaystyle f_{\rm coll}

\displaystyle=1.65\times 10^{-5}\mathrm{Hz}\left(\frac{g_{*}}{100}\right)^{1/6}\left(\frac{T_{*}}{100\,\mathrm{GeV}}\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{H_{*}}\right)\left(\frac{f_{*}}{\beta}\right).

. The amplitude is also governed by the efficiency factor
\kappa_{\rm coll}
, representing the fraction of latent heat transformed into the kinetic energy of the bubble shell. An analytic fit for this efficiency is given as
[
52
]
:

\kappa_{\rm coll}=\frac{1}{1+0.715\alpha}\left(0.715\alpha+\frac{4}{27}\sqrt{\frac{3\alpha}{2}}\right).

(27)

It is crucial to note that the efficiency
\kappa_{\rm coll}
depends heavily on the expansion regime. In the runaway regime, the bubble walls accelerate continuously, allowing
\kappa_{\rm coll}\to 1
. However, for the thermal phase transitions considered in this work, the interaction between the bubble wall and the plasma typically generates sufficient friction to force the wall into a terminal velocity (the non-runaway regime)
[
13
,
14
]
. In this state, the vast majority of the vacuum energy is transferred to the plasma fluid motion (sound waves) rather than accelerating the wall itself. Consequently,
\kappa_{\rm coll}
becomes negligible (
\kappa_{\rm coll}\ll\kappa_{\rm sw}
), making the bubble collision contribution subdominant to the sound wave and turbulence signals
[
43
,
20
,
21
]
. Therefore, we neglect
\Omega_{\rm coll}h^{2}
in our calculation and focus on the dominant sound wave and turbulence sources discussed below.

Sound Wave Contribution:

The dominant source of gravitational waves in non-runaway phase transitions is typically the stochastic background produced by sound waves (bulk fluid motion) in the plasma. Based on numerical simulations and the sound shell model
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
, the resulting GW power spectrum is given by:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm sw}h^{2}

\displaystyle=2.59\times 10^{-6}\left(\frac{g_{*}}{100}\right)^{-1/3}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\rm sw}\alpha}{1+\alpha}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\beta}{H_{*}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\Delta(v_{w})\Upsilon(\tau_{\rm sw})S_{\rm sw}(f),

(28)

\displaystyle f_{\rm sw}

\displaystyle=8.9\times 10^{-6},\mathrm{Hz}\left(\frac{g_{*}}{100}\right)^{1/6}\left(\frac{T_{*}}{100,\mathrm{GeV}}\right)\frac{1}{\Delta(v_{w})}\left(\frac{\beta}{H_{*}}\right),

(29)

where we have defined the velocity factor
\Delta(v_{w})\equiv\max(v_{w},c_{s})/c
to account for the relevant length scale of the source, and
c_{s}\approx 1/\sqrt{3}
is the sound speed in the plasma. The spectral shape function
S_{\rm sw}(f)
follows a broken power law:

\displaystyle S_{\rm sw}(f)=\left(\frac{f}{f_{\rm sw}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{7}{4+3\left(f/f_{\rm sw}\right)^{2}}\right)^{7/2}.

(30)

A critical correction to the standard sound wave spectrum is the suppression factor
\Upsilon(\tau_{\rm sw})
, which accounts for the finite lifetime of the acoustic source. Sound waves do not persist indefinitely; they eventually develop non-linearities and form shocks, leading to the cessation of the acoustic phase and the onset of turbulence. The suppression is modeled as
[
36
,
41
]
:

\displaystyle\Upsilon(\tau_{\rm sw})=1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2H_{*}\tau_{\rm sw}}},

(31)

where
\tau_{\rm sw}
is the duration of the acoustic phase (the shock formation timescale). This timescale is estimated by the ratio of the mean bubble separation
R_{*}
to the root-mean-square fluid velocity
\bar{U}_{f}
:

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm sw}\approx\frac{R_{*}}{\bar{U}_{f}},\quad\text{with}\quad R_{*}=(8\pi)^{1/3}\frac{v_{w}}{\beta},\quad\bar{U}_{f}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}\frac{\kappa_{\rm sw}\alpha}{1+\alpha}}.

(32)

This factor ensures that if the shock formation time is shorter than the Hubble time (
H_{*}\tau_{\rm sw}<1
), the total GW power is reduced accordingly.

The amplitude of the signal also depends on the efficiency factor
\kappa_{\rm sw}
, which represents the fraction of vacuum energy converted into bulk fluid motion. We utilize the kinetic energy fraction fits provided by Espinosa et al.
[
37
]
, covering the subsonic (deflagration), sonic (Jouguet), and supersonic (detonation) regimes:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\rm sw}\simeq\begin{cases}&\frac{c_{s}^{11/5}\kappa_{A}\kappa_{B}}{(c_{s}^{11/5}-v_{w}^{11/5})\kappa_{B}+v_{w}c_{s}^{6/5}\kappa_{A}}\quad v_{w}\lesssim c_{s}(\text{Subsonic}),\\
&\kappa_{B}+(v_{w}-c_{s})\delta\kappa+\frac{(v_{w}-c_{s})^{3}}{(c_{J}-c_{s})^{3}}\left[\kappa_{C}-\kappa_{B}-(c_{J}-c_{s})\delta\kappa\right]\\
&c_{s}<v_{w}<c_{J}(\text{Sonic}),\\
&\frac{(c_{J}-1)^{3}c_{J}^{5/2}v_{w}^{-5/2}\kappa_{C}\kappa_{D}}{((c_{J}-1)^{3}-(v_{w}-1)^{3})c_{J}^{5/2}\kappa_{C}+(v_{w}-1)^{3}\kappa_{D}}\\
&v_{w}\gtrsim c_{J}\quad(\text{Supersonic}),\end{cases}

(33)

where the Chapman-Jouguet velocity is
c_{J}=(\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\alpha+\alpha^{2}}+\sqrt{1/3})/(1+\alpha)
and the crossover correction is
\delta\kappa\approx-0.9\log(\sqrt{\alpha}/(1+\sqrt{\alpha}))
. The boundary coefficients are given by:

\displaystyle\kappa_{A}

\displaystyle\simeq v_{w}^{6/5}\frac{6.9\alpha}{1.36-0.037\sqrt{\alpha}+\alpha},

(34)

\displaystyle\kappa_{B}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\alpha^{2/5}}{0.017+(0.997+\alpha)^{2/5}},

(35)

\displaystyle\kappa_{C}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{0.135+\sqrt{0.98+\alpha}},

(36)

\displaystyle\kappa_{D}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\alpha}{0.73+0.083\sqrt{\alpha}+\alpha}.

(37)

MHD Turbulence Contribution:

As the sound wave contribution described above proceeds, non-linearities in the fluid motion eventually become significant. The sound waves decay, generating shocks and subsequently leading to Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) turbulence in the plasma. This turbulence acts as a secondary source of gravitational waves, which can persist well after the acoustic source has effectively shut off. The resulting stochastic GW background is modeled analytically as
[
19
,
10
]
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm turb}h^{2}

\displaystyle=3.35\times 10^{-4}\left(\frac{g_{*}}{100}\right)^{-1/3}\left(\frac{\beta}{H_{*}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\rm turb}\alpha}{1+\alpha}\right)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\times\Delta(v_{w})S_{\rm turb}(f),

(38)

\displaystyle f_{\rm turb}

\displaystyle=2.7\times 10^{-5},\mathrm{Hz}\left(\frac{g_{*}}{100}\right)^{1/6}\left(\frac{T_{*}}{100,\mathrm{GeV}}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{\Delta(v_{w})}\left(\frac{\beta}{H_{*}}\right).

Consistent with the sound wave treatment, the characteristic velocity scale is set by
\Delta(v_{w})=\max(v_{w},c_{s})/c
. The spectral shape
S_{\rm turb}(f)
is determined by the Kolmogorov theory of turbulence, modified by a cutoff at the Hubble scale to respect causality. It is given by:

\displaystyle S_{\rm turb}(f)=\frac{\left(f/f_{\rm turb}\right)^{3}}{\left[1+\left(f/f_{\rm turb}\right)\right]^{11/3}\left(1+8\pi f/h_{*}\right)},

(40)

where the peak frequency
f_{\rm turb}
corresponds to the inverse of the mean bubble separation scale (
R_{*}
). The term
h_{*}
represents the redshifted Hubble rate at the time of generation, which sets the horizon scale:

h_{*}=1.65\times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{Hz}\left(\frac{T_{*}}{100\,\mathrm{GeV}}\right)\left(\frac{g_{*}}{100}\right)^{1/6}.

(41)

At high frequencies (
f\gg f_{\rm turb}
), the spectrum follows the characteristic Kolmogorov slope (
\Omega\sim f^{-5/3}
), while the term
(1+8\pi f/h_{*})
ensures the correct asymptotic behavior at scales approaching the horizon.

The amplitude of this signal is governed by the turbulence efficiency factor
\kappa_{\rm turb}
, which represents the fraction of total vacuum energy converted into vortical fluid motion. Recent numerical simulations of the EWPT suggest that the conversion from bulk fluid motion (sound waves) to turbulence is not fully efficient. It is generally estimated that a fraction
\epsilon\sim 5\%-10\%
of the bulk kinetic energy is transferred to the turbulent cascade
[
44
,
21
]
. Consequently, we adopt the standard approximation for our analysis:

\kappa_{\rm turb}\approx\epsilon\kappa_{\rm sw}\approx 0.1\kappa_{\rm sw}.

(42)

While often subdominant to the sound wave signal in peak amplitude, the turbulence contribution can dominate the high-frequency tail of the spectrum due to its slower spectral decay compared to sound waves.

v_{w}=0.6

Figure 5

v_{w}
affect the peak amplitude of the GW signal, with certain velocities leading to a suppression of the spectral strength (shifting the points downwards). However, the conclusion of our work remains robust against these variations. The strong correlation between the GW signal strength and the baryon asymmetry (BAU) generated via leptogenesis persists across different velocity benchmarks, demonstrating that this feature is a fundamental prediction of the model rather than an artifact of a specific parameter choice.
