Asymptotics for estimating a diverging number of parameters — with and without sparsity
Jana Gauss, Thomas Nagler

Abstract
We develop a general asymptotic theory for estimating equations whose dimension diverges with the sample size. For both unpenalized and sparse penalized problems, we establish population-level conditions for existence, consistency, uniqueness, and asymptotic normality; in the penalized setting, we also establish selection consistency under a generalized version of the mutual incoherence condition. Our results cover stepwise procedures with a diverging number of steps, independent but non-identically distributed observations, and dependent data. We allow penalties that are simultaneously nonconvex, non-coordinate-separable, and group-structured and may involve heterogeneous tuning parameters. Our population-level conditions imply a weak form of restricted strong convexity, and we provide an explicit example where the commonly used stronger form fails. The results are illustrated by several applications including Group SCAD-penalized estimation in generalized linear models, distributed inference under network dependence, and penalized stepwise estimation in causal inference.

Introduction

In modern applications, statisticians are facing increasingly complex and high-dimensional problems. Many data sets have a huge number of variables, calling for similarly many parameters
p
. In other scenarios, the number of variables is moderate, but adequately modeling the data requires highly complex, nonlinear models with many parameters.
The traditional fixed-
p
-large-
n
paradigm is inadequate in such situations.

This article adopts an asymptotic perspective, allowing both the sample size
n
and the number of parameters
p_{n}
to diverge. We consider general parametric problems where the estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
solves an estimating equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\hat{\theta}})=\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}},

(1)

with some function
\phi\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}\to\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
and independent, but not necessarily identically distributed
\bm{X}_{i}
.
A classical example is risk minimization, where
\phi
is the gradient of a loss function. However, the estimating equation framework is also suited for more complex methods, such as stepwise and multi-sample estimation procedures, where an optimization-based formulation is less convenient.
In fact, the lack of general asymptotic theory for stepwise procedures when
p_{n}\to\infty
is what originally motivated this work.

The main question we address is: under what conditions on the data-generating process, the function
\phi
, and the growth of
p_{n}
does an estimator solving (
1
) exist, and when is it consistent, unique, and asymptotically normal?
We also consider sparse problems where
p_{n}
may exceed
n
. Penalized inference within the general estimating equation framework can be formulated as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\hat{\theta}})\in\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{\hat{\theta}}),

(2)

where
\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}}
is the generalized gradient of a penalty function
p_{\bm{\lambda}}
, and
\bm{\lambda}
is a vector of tuning parameters.

Contributions

The main contribution of this work is a unified asymptotic theory for estimating equations with diverging dimension.
Rather than developing separate arguments for particular models or loss functions, we formulate verifiable population-level conditions on the estimating equation and its curvature.
The gaps in existing theory are most apparent when the estimating equations are not gradients of a single loss, the number of estimation steps diverges, observations come from heterogeneous or dependent populations, or penalties combine nonconvexity with group structure.
The resulting framework unifies several existing results and substantially extends their applicability in three directions.

First
, for unpenalized estimating equations, we provide general conditions for existence and consistency (
Theorem

1
), uniqueness (
Theorem

2
), and asymptotic normality (
Theorem

3
).
When specialized, these results closely align with the sharpest known conditions for linear and generalized linear models and substantially improve upon those for general maximum likelihood problems.
At the same time, the theory applies directly to estimating procedures that do not arise from risk minimization.

Second
, we develop consistency (
Theorem

4
), existence and selection consistency (
Theorem

5
), uniqueness (
Theorem

6
), and asymptotic normality (
Theorem

7
) for penalized estimating equations.
The penalty does not need to be convex or coordinate-separable and may be group-structured or involve multiple tuning parameters with varying strength.
This combination is not covered by the current general theory for sparse
M
-estimation: results allowing nonseparable or group-structured penalties generally require convexity, whereas results allowing nonconvex penalties typically assume coordinate separability.
The solution constructed in
Theorem

5
relies on a generalization of the mutual incoherence or irrepresentable condition, and suitable nonconvex penalties attain the oracle property.
Our population-level conditions imply a weak version of restricted strong convexity (RSC).
We show that the stronger form commonly used in the literature fails in a simple quadratic problem with non-rank-one sample Hessians, while our weaker condition remains easy to verify (
Section

5.2
).

Third
, the framework accommodates independent but non-identically distributed observations, and we extend the results to dependent data (
Theorem

8
).
This is particularly useful for stepwise and multi-sample procedures, whose estimating equations combine information from different stages or populations.
In the stepwise setting, the number of estimation steps is allowed to diverge with the sample size.

Our illustrative applications cover established results for
M
-estimation and also give results that are not covered by existing work, including stepwise estimation with a diverging number of steps, distributed inference under network dependence, and the oracle property of Group SCAD-penalized estimation in generalized linear models.

Relation to existing work

The study of unpenalized problems dates back at least to
Huber (1973)
, who focused on
M
-estimators in linear models. Following his seminal work,
Yohai and Maronna (1979)
,
Portnoy (1984)
,
Portnoy (1985)
,
Welsh (1989)
,
Mammen (1989)
established consistency and asymptotic normality under various conditions; see
Li et al. (2011)
for a comprehensive overview.
The sharpest known conditions are
p_{n}\ln p_{n}/n\to 0
for consistency
(
Portnoy, 1984
)
, and
(p_{n}\ln n)^{3/2}/n\to 0

(
Portnoy, 1985
)
or
p_{n}^{3/2}\ln n/n\to 0

(
Mammen, 1989
)
for asymptotic normality.

Fan and Peng (2004)
extend these results from generalized linear models to general maximum likelihood problems under more restrictive conditions, requiring
p_{n}^{4}/n\to 0
for consistency and
p_{n}^{5}/n\to 0
for asymptotic normality.

He and Shao (2000)
derived asymptotics for
M
-estimators with convex loss under a generic stochastic equicontinuity condition, though this approach provides limited insight into the settings where it applies.
Other work studies the exact distribution or predictive risk in linear models when
p_{n}/n
has a positive limit
(
El Karoui et al., 2013
,
Dobriban and Wager, 2018
,
Hastie et al., 2022
)
.

When
p_{n}
exceeds
n
, consistent estimation is still possible if the true parameter
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is sparse.
Various sparsity-inducing penalties have been proposed, including the Lasso
(
Tibshirani, 1996
)
, group-structured penalties such as the Group Lasso
(
Yuan and Lin, 2006
)
, and bias-reducing, nonconvex penalties such as
\ell_{q}
-penalties
(
Knight and Fu, 2000
)
, (Group) SCAD
(
Fan, 1997
,
Fan and Li, 2001
,
Wang et al., 2007
,
Guo et al., 2015
)
, and MCP
(
Zhang, 2010
)
.
State-of-the-art results address general
M
-estimation problems and broad classes of penalties
(
Negahban et al., 2012
,
Lee et al., 2015
,
Loh and Wainwright, 2015
,
Loh and Wainwright, 2017
,
Loh, 2017
)
; see also the monograph by
Wainwright (2019)
.
These results typically rely on an RSC condition on the realized loss and focus on deterministic bounds.
Frameworks that accommodate nonseparable or group-structured penalties generally assume convexity
(
Negahban et al., 2012
,
Lee et al., 2015
)
, while results for nonconvex penalties typically focus on coordinate-separable penalties
(
Loh and Wainwright, 2015
,
Loh and Wainwright, 2017
,
Loh, 2017
)
.
They therefore do not cover combinations such as Group SCAD, nor do their optimization-based formulations directly encompass general stepwise estimating equations.
Our asymptotic perspective is complementary: regularity conditions are formulated at the population level, making their applicability transparent, while distributional limits provide a tight characterization of estimation uncertainty.

2
Unpenalized Estimation

2.1
Setup and Notation

Suppose we observe independent random variables
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{n}\in\mathcal{X}

and want to estimate a parameter
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p_{n}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
with
p_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
We do not assume identical distributions to also cover multi-sample estimation problems.
The target value
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is the solution to the system of equations

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})]=\bm{0},

with continuous
\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})=\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
; for example, the gradient of a log-likelihood or loss function.
The
k
-th entry of
\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})
is denoted by
\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})_{k},k=1,\ldots,p_{n}
.
The estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is the solution of

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:estim1}\Phi_{n}(\bm{\hat{\theta}}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{\hat{\theta}})=\bm{0}.

(3)

Define the
p_{n}\times p_{n}
matrices

\displaystyle I(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Cov}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})],\quad J(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})],\quad\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})=\int_{0}^{1}J(\bm{\theta}^{*}+t(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}))dt.

The parameter
\bm{\theta}^{*}
, functions
\phi(\bm{\theta}),J(\bm{\theta}),\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta}),I(\bm{\theta})
, and the support and distribution of
\bm{X}_{i}
all depend on
n
, but we suppress this in the notation to avoid clutter.
We assume that
J(\bm{\theta})
is continuous in
\bm{\theta}
.

Throughout the paper,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm for vectors and the spectral norm
\|A\|=\sup_{\|\bm{x}\|=1}\|A\bm{x}\|
for matrices.

\lambda_{\max}(A)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of a matrix
A
and
\|A\|_{\infty}
denotes the largest absolute row sum.
Define
r_{n}=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(I(\bm{\theta}^{*}))/n}
and let
\Theta_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
be a sequence of sets such that for all
C<\infty
, there exists an
n_{C}
such that for all
n\geq n_{C}
, it holds that
\Theta_{n}\supset\{\bm{\theta}:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\leq r_{n}C\}
.
The matrix
I(\bm{\theta}^{*})
measures the variability of the estimating functions, while
J(\bm{\theta}^{*})
describes the local sensitivity of the population equation; under the identification conditions below,
r_{n}
is the corresponding natural estimation rate.

2.2
Consistency and Uniqueness

Our main assumption for consistency of the estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is the following:

(A1)

There exists a sequence of symmetric, matrix-valued functions
H_{n}(\bm{x})
such that:

(i)

For all
\bm{u}
such that
\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u}\in\Theta_{n}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

\bm{u}^{\top}[\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})-\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{*})]\leq\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}(\bm{x})\bm{u};

(ii)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{\max}(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})])\leq-c<0;

(iii)

For some sequence
B_{n}=o(n/\ln p_{n})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})\bm{u})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=o(n),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})^{2}\mathds{1}_{\|H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})\|\leq B_{n}}]\right\|

\displaystyle=o\left(\frac{n}{\ln p_{n}}\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\|H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})\|>B_{n})

\displaystyle=o(1).

Before discussing the assumptions in detail, we state our main results.

Theorem 1
.

Under assumption
(A1)
, the following holds with probability tending to 1:

(i)

The sets
\Theta_{n}
contain at least one solution of the estimating equation (
3
).

(ii)

Every solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}\in\Theta_{n}
satisfies
\lVert\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\rVert=O_{p}(r_{n})
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
(A1)
holds, and for any
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}+\bm{u}\in\{\bm{\theta}:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\leq a_{n}r_{n}\}
with
a_{n}\to\infty
arbitrarily slowly and all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\top}[\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}+\bm{u})-\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})]\leq\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}(\bm{x})\bm{u}.

(4)

Then the solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is unique on
\Theta_{n}
with probability tending to 1.

The natural convergence rate
r_{n}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(I(\bm{\theta}^{*}))/n}
is typically of order
\sqrt{p_{n}/n}
, for example when
\max_{i,k}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{k}^{2}]=O(1)
.
The trace formulation also accommodates diverging entries of
\phi
and slower rates, as in the multi-sample problems of
Section

5.3
.

To interpret condition
(A1)
, consider the core idea of the proof.
A sufficient condition for the estimating equation (
3
) to have a solution in a ball
\{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\leq r_{n}C\}
is

\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=r_{n}C}\bm{u}^{\top}\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})\leq 0

(
Fierro et al., 2004
, Theorem 2.3)
.
Condition
(A1)(i)
upper bounds the left-hand side via

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\top}\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})\leq\bm{u}^{\top}\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*})+\bm{u}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})\right)\bm{u}.

Standard arguments give
\|\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\|=O_{p}(r_{n})
, while
(A1)(iii)
and matrix concentration allow replacing the sample average by its negative definite expectation; see
(A1)(ii)
.
Thus, for
\|\bm{u}\|=r_{n}C
and sufficiently large
C
, the quadratic term dominates, yielding a solution in the ball with high probability.
Variations of this argument give consistency of other solutions and uniqueness.

The majorization approach controls the curvature around
\bm{\theta}^{*}
uniformly without covering arguments, allowing non-restrictive growth conditions on
p_{n}
; constructions of
H_{n}
are given in
Section

5
.
The function
H_{n}
may be viewed as an envelope for the symmetrized Jacobians

\left\{H_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\;\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})+\frac{1}{2}\;\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})^{\top}\colon\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}\right\}

in the positive semi-definite ordering
\preceq
of matrices,
but condition
(A1)(i)
is slightly weaker.
Condition
(A1)(ii)
only imposes concavity on average, not for every
\bm{x}
.
Lemma 4 in the supplement converts rate conditions such as
(A1)(iii)
into more interpretable moment conditions; for example, its second part holds if all
H_{n}(\bm{x})
are negative semi-definite and
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})]=O(1)
.
Choosing
\Theta_{n}=\{\bm{\theta}:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\leq r_{n}a_{n}\}
with
a_{n}\to\infty
arbitrarily slowly gives the weakest conditions, as only a shrinking neighborhood of
\bm{\theta}^{*}
matters; larger sets may require larger envelopes in
(A1)
(A1)(i)
and (
4
).

The assumptions implicitly restrict the growth of
p_{n}
.
For M-estimation in GLMs, where
\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})=\psi(Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta})\bm{X}_{i}
, they yield
p_{n}\ln p_{n}/n\to 0
under standard assumptions, matching
Portnoy (1984)
; see
Section

5.1.1
. By contrast,
Fan and Peng (2004)
required
p_{n}^{4}/n\to 0
for general nonlinear maximum likelihood problems.
Condition
(A1)
(A1)(iii)
need not even require
p_{n}/n\to 0
: for unrelated componentwise equations,
H_{n}
can be diagonal, and uniformly bounded entries would allow
B_{n}=O(1)
and
p_{n}\leq e^{o(n)}
.

2.3
Asymptotic Normality

We now turn to the asymptotic normality of the estimator. Because the dimension of the parameter space grows with the sample size, we state this in terms of convergence of finite-dimensional projections.
Let
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p_{n}}
be some matrix.

(A2)

For every
C\in(0,\infty)
and some sequence
D_{n}=o(\sqrt{n}/(r_{n}\,p_{n}))
, with
\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta})=\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})
or
\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta})=\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|,\|\bm{u}^{\prime}\|\leq r_{n}C}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}[\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})-\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u}^{\prime})]\|^{2}\right]}{\|\bm{u}-\bm{u}^{\prime}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=o\left(\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}\,p_{n}}\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|,\|\bm{u}^{\prime}\|\leq r_{n}C}\frac{\|A_{n}[\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})-\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u}^{\prime})]\|}{\|\bm{u}-\bm{u}^{\prime}\|}>D_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=o(1),

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|\leq r_{n}C}\|A_{n}[\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})-J(\bm{\theta}^{*})]\|

\displaystyle=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}}\right),

where the same choice of
\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta})
must be used in both the first and the second condition.

(A3)

It holds that
\textstyle\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\|^{4}\right]=o(n).

Assumption
(A2)
is a stochastic smoothness condition required to control fluctuations of the estimating equation.
Allowing for either
\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})
or
\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})]
leads to sharp conditions in both standard and multi-sample settings.
The moment condition
(A3)
typically requires
p_{n}^{2}/n\to 0
, e.g., if
\|A_{n}\|=O(1)
and
\max_{i,k}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{k}^{4}]=O(1)
.

Theorem 3
.

If conditions
(A1)
–
(A3)
hold for some matrix
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p_{n}}
with fixed
q
for which
\Sigma=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}I(\bm{\theta}^{*})A_{n}^{\top}
exists, it holds that

\sqrt{n}A_{n}J(\bm{\theta}^{*})(\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\Sigma).

In the finite-dimensional setting (
p_{n}=p<\infty
), choosing
A_{n}=J(\bm{\theta}^{*})^{-1}
yields the familiar statement

\sqrt{n}(\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},J(\bm{\theta}^{*})^{-1}I(\bm{\theta}^{*})J(\bm{\theta}^{*})^{-\top}).

The proof of
Theorem

3
uses mixed-entropy inequalities
(
Van der Vaart and Wellner, 2023
, Section 2.14)
building on Talagrand’s work on generic chaining
(
Talagrand, 2005
)
to obtain weak conditions.
For
M
-estimators in the linear regression model, we obtain asymptotic normality if
p_{n}^{2}\ln n/n\to 0
under standard assumptions, see
Corollary

1
in
Section

5.1.1
.

Portnoy (1985)
and
Mammen (1989)
obtain the slightly weaker condition
p_{n}^{3/2}\ln n/n\to 0
.
Both authors exploit the linear model structure through fourth-order expansions and a design-dependent standardization, effectively alleviating the need for the sample Hessian to converge.
Portnoy (1986)
showed that a general CLT cannot hold unless
p_{n}^{2}/n\to 0
, so our result appears to be close to optimal.

Fan and Peng (2004)
require the much stronger condition
p_{n}^{5}/n\to 0
for maximum likelihood estimation in general nonlinear models.

3
Penalized Estimation

3.1
Penalization of Estimating Equations

We now allow
p_{n}
to exceed
n
while assuming that the target parameter
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is sparse.
To motivate our formulation for general estimating equations, consider a penalized risk minimization problem.
Let
L
be some differentiable loss function and
p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})
be a function penalizing the magnitude of
\bm{\theta}
, e.g., the
\ell_{1}
-penalty
p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\lambda_{n}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}
.
The estimator is given by

\bm{\hat{\theta}}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})+p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})\right).

If both penalty and loss are differentiable, the first-order condition for
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\hat{\theta}})-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\hat{\theta}})=\bm{0}.

Sparsity inducing penalties are not differentiable at points with zero entries.
The vector
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is a solution to the minimization problem when the gradients cross
\bm{0}
at
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
, either continuously or with a jump.

To appropriately deal with non-differentiable or nonconvex penalties, we reformulate the estimation problem.
For a locally Lipschitz function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
,

Clarke (1990, Chapter 2)
uses the
generalized directional derivative
of
f
at
\bm{\theta}
in the direction
\bm{\Delta}
, denoted by
f^{\circ}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\Delta})
, to define the
generalized gradient
of
f
at
\bm{\theta}
, denoted by
\partial f(\bm{\theta})
:

\partial f(\bm{\theta})=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:f^{\circ}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\Delta})\geq\langle\bm{z},\bm{\Delta}\rangle\,\forall\bm{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\},\quad\text{where }f^{\circ}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\Delta})=\limsup_{\bm{\tilde{\theta}}\to\bm{\theta},\,t\downarrow 0}\frac{f(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}+t\bm{\Delta})-f(\bm{\tilde{\theta}})}{t}.

The generalized gradient reduces to the usual gradient if
f
is differentiable.
For convex functions,
\partial f(\bm{\theta})
coincides with the
subdifferential
, i.e., the set of all
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying
f(\bm{\theta}+\bm{\Delta})-f(\bm{\theta})\geq\langle\bm{z},\bm{\Delta}\rangle
.
With this notation, the estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
of a penalized risk minimization problem solves
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\hat{\theta}})\in\partial p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\hat{\theta}})
.
More generally, we define a penalized estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
as a solution to

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Def_{M}}\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})\in\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}).

(5)

We allow for a vector of tuning parameters
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
. This encompasses, for example, coordinate-separable penalties of the form
p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}p_{\lambda_{n,k}}(\theta_{k})
, which can be useful when the estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is composed of solutions to sub-problems of different dimensionality or sample size.
The target parameter
\bm{\theta}^{*}
still solves the unpenalized population equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})]=\bm{0}
.
In what follows, we assume without loss of generality that the true
\bm{\theta}^{*}
can be written as
\bm{\theta}^{*}=(\bm{\theta}^{*}_{(1)},\bm{\theta}^{*}_{(2)})
with
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{n}}
and
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{(2)}=\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}-s_{n}}
.
Similarly, write
\bm{v}_{(1)}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{s_{n}})
,
\bm{v}_{(2)}=(v_{s_{n}+1},\dots,v_{p_{n}})
for any vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
.
By
\partial f(\bm{\theta})_{(1)}
and
\partial f(\bm{\theta})_{(2)}
, we denote the sets of all subvectors
\bm{z}_{(1)}
and
\bm{z}_{(2)}
such that
\bm{z}\in\partial f(\bm{\theta})
.

A penalty that is not differentiable at
\bm{0}
can induce sparsity. To illustrate this, consider the Lasso penalty
p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\lambda_{n}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}
with scalar
\lambda_{n}
.
Its generalized gradient is a singleton at nonzero coordinates and the interval
[-\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n}]
at zero.
Hence, a nonzero solution satisfies
\Phi_{n}(\bm{\hat{\theta}})_{k}=\lambda_{n}\operatorname{sign}(\hat{\theta}_{k})
, which induces shrinkage, while
\hat{\theta}_{k}=0
only requires
\Phi_{n}(\bm{\hat{\theta}})_{k}\in[-\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n}]
.
This additional slack makes sparse solutions possible; SCAD
(
Fan, 1997
,
Fan and Li, 2001
)
and MCP
(
Zhang, 2010
)
have the same behavior near zero.

To cover non-coordinate-separable penalties such as the Group Lasso
(
Yuan and Lin, 2006
)
, let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{K_{n}}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
be potentially overlapping groups covering all coordinates, and denote the corresponding subvectors by
\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}
and
\partial f(\bm{\theta})_{G_{g}}
.
Let
I_{(2)}
contain the groups associated with
\bm{\theta}_{(2)}
, so that
\bigcup_{g\in I_{(2)}}G_{g}=\{s_{n}+1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
, and define
I_{(1)}
analogously.
The Group Lasso
p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{g=1}^{K_{n}}\lambda_{n,g}\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\|_{2}
replaces the interval at an inactive coordinate by an
\ell_{2}
-ball on an inactive group.
The supplement lists popular penalties covered by this framework and their generalized gradients.
To our knowledge, existing results do not jointly cover nonconvex and group-structured penalties, including combinations such as Group SCAD.

3.2
Conditions for the Penalty

Let
\tilde{r}_{n}
be the target rate defined in
Section

3.3
.
Retain the partition
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\theta}_{(1)},\bm{\theta}_{(2)})
, the groups
G_{1},\ldots,G_{K_{n}}
, and the index sets
I_{(1)}
and
I_{(2)}
introduced above.
Define
\Theta_{n}^{\prime}=\{(\bm{\theta}_{(1)},\bm{0})\colon\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\leq\tilde{r}_{n}a_{n}\}
, where
a_{n}\to\infty
arbitrarily slowly, and let
\Theta_{n}\supseteq\Theta_{n}^{\prime}
be specified in the theorems below.

(P1)

The penalty
p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}
is twice continuously differentiable with respect to
\bm{\theta}_{(1)}
at
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}
with
\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}}\|\overline{p}_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})\|=o(1)
, where
\overline{p}_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})\rvert_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}^{*}+t(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*})}dt
.

Note that this implies that
\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})_{(1)}
contains the single vector
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})
for
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}
.

(P2)

The generalized gradient satisfies, for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}^{\prime}
and
g\in I_{(2)}
,

\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})_{G_{g}}\supseteq B(\bm{0}_{|G_{g}|},\lambda_{n,g}).

(P3)

There is
\mu_{n}\geq 0
such that for any
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta_{n}
and
\bm{z}\in\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}),\bm{z}^{\prime}\in\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
,

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta},\bm{z}^{\prime}-\bm{z}\rangle\geq-\mu_{n}\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}\|^{2}.

(P4)

The Hessian matrix of
p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}
satisfies
\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}}\|\overline{p}_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})\|=o((\sqrt{n}\,\tilde{r}_{n})^{-1})
.

For the Lasso and SCAD, differentiability in
(P1)
requires the beta-min condition
\min_{1\leq k\leq s_{n}}|\theta^{*}_{k}|/\tilde{r}_{n}\to\infty
; for the Group Lasso, the active groups must likewise remain separated from zero.
The Hessian condition is mild and typically implied by
\max_{g}\lambda_{n,g}\to 0
.

Assumption
(P2)
induces sparsity.
It formalizes the sparsity mechanism described above
reduces to

\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})_{(2)}\supseteq[-\lambda_{n,s_{n}+1},\lambda_{n,s_{n}+1}]\times\cdots\times[-\lambda_{n,p_{n}},\lambda_{n,p_{n}}],

for coordinate-separable penalties. The general condition holds with equality for (Group) Lasso, (Group) SCAD, and MCP; the more general inclusion also covers
\ell_{q}
-penalties with
q<1
and asymmetric penalties.
Assumption
(P3)
controls nonconvexity and is used for uniqueness.
It holds with
\mu_{n}=0
for convex penalties, with
\mu_{n}=(a-1)^{-1}
and
a^{-1}
for (Group) SCAD and MCP, respectively, and is weaker than the
\mu
-amenability condition of
Loh and Wainwright (2017)
,
Loh (2017)
.
It fails for
\ell_{q}
-penalties with
q<1
, which cannot yield unique solutions unless
\bm{\theta}^{*}=\bm{0}
.
Finally,
(P4)
refines
(P1)
for asymptotic normality and is immediate for penalties such as Lasso and SCAD once their Hessian vanishes near the active coefficients.
Having isolated the required properties of the penalty, we now combine them with curvature and stochastic regularity conditions on the estimating equation.

3.3
Notation and Assumptions

To simplify some conditions, we assume from now on that
p_{n}\geq n^{a}
for some
a>0
. This only excludes uninteresting edge cases where
p_{n}
is effectively constant.
Define for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}\times p_{n}}

\displaystyle A_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\left(A_{ij}\right)_{i,j=1,\ldots,s_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{n}\times s_{n}},\quad A_{(2,1)}=\left(A_{ij}\right)_{i=s_{n}+1,\ldots,p_{n};j=1,\ldots,s_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p_{n}-s_{n})\times s_{n}},

\displaystyle A_{G_{g},(1)}

\displaystyle=\left(A_{ij}\right)_{i\in G_{g};j=1,\ldots,s_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|G_{g}|\times s_{n}},

e.g.,
J(\bm{\theta})_{G_{g},(1)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla^{\top}_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}\mathbb{E}[\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})_{G_{g}}]
.
To state the required assumptions, let

\displaystyle b_{n}^{*}=\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\|_{\infty},\quad\overline{b}_{n}=\sup_{\bm{v}\in\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}),\ \bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty},\quad\tilde{r}_{n}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(I(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{(1)})/n}+\sqrt{s_{n}}b_{n}^{*},

(6)

and let
\eta_{n}
be any sequence such that
\eta_{n}\geq 2\sigma_{n}\sqrt{\ln(p_{n})/n}
, where
\sigma_{n}=\max_{1\leq k\leq p_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{k}^{2}]}
.
Recall
\Theta_{n}^{\prime}=\{(\bm{\theta}_{(1)},\bm{0})\colon\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\leq\tilde{r}_{n}a_{n}\}
with
a_{n}\to\infty
arbitrarily slowly and
\bm{\theta}_{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{n}}
.
Define the cones

\Theta^{*}(\nu_{n})=\{\bm{\theta}\colon\|\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\nu_{n}}\|\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}\|_{2}\},

and suppose that
\Theta_{n}
are sets such that
\Theta_{n}^{\prime}\subseteq\Theta_{n}\subseteq\Theta^{*}(\nu_{n})
for some
\nu_{n}
.

The regularity conditions below have two roles: Assumptions
(A4)
and
(A5)
control curvature and score tails, while
(A6)
–
(A8)
ensure that a solution of the reduced problem also solves the full penalized equation.

(A4)

There exists a sequence of symmetric, matrix-valued functions
H_{n}(\bm{x})
such that:

(i)

For all
\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u}\in\Theta_{n}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

\bm{u}^{\top}[\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})-\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{*})]\leq\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}(\bm{x})\bm{u};

(ii)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{\max}(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})])\leq-c<0;

(iii)

For some sequence
\tilde{B}_{n}=o(n/(\nu_{n}\ln p_{n}))
, it holds that

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j,k\leq p_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})_{j,k}^{2}]

\displaystyle=o\left(\frac{n}{\nu^{2}_{n}\ln p_{n}}\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq j,k\leq p_{n}}|H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})_{j,k}|>\tilde{B}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=o(1).

(A5)

It holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\|\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\|_{\infty}>\sigma_{n}\sqrt{\frac{n}{4\ln p_{n}}}\right)=o(1).

(A6)

\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{(1)}
is invertible for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}
and there is
\alpha\in[0,1)
such that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:alpha}\max_{g\in I_{(2)}}\lambda_{n,g}^{-1}\|\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{G_{g},(1)}\;\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})^{-1}_{(1)}\;\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2}\leq\alpha\quad\text{ uniformly for all $\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}$}.

(7)

(A7)

For all
g\in I_{(2)}
,
\lambda_{n,g}
must fulfill, with
\alpha
as defined in
(A6)
and
\eta_{n}
as defined above,

\lambda_{n,g}\geq\frac{4}{1-\alpha}\>\eta_{n}\>\sqrt{|G_{g}|}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{n,g}\geq\frac{4}{1-\alpha}\>\eta_{n}\>J_{n,g},

where
J_{n,g}=\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n},\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\|\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{G_{g},(1)}\;\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})^{-1}_{(1)}\bm{x}\|_{2}
.

(A8)

With
\tilde{r}_{n}
,
\eta_{n}
as defined above and some sequence
\tilde{D}_{n}=o(n\eta_{n}/(\tilde{r}_{n}s_{n}+\tilde{r}_{n}\ln p_{n}))
and
\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta})=\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})
or
\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta})=\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta})]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq p_{n}}\sup_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta})_{k}-\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})_{k}|^{2}]}{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=o\left(\frac{n\eta_{n}^{2}}{\tilde{r}_{n}^{2}(s_{n}+\ln p_{n})}\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}}\frac{\|\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta})-\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|}>\tilde{D}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=o(1),

where the same choice of
\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta})
must be used in both conditions.

Assumption
(A4)
is a variant of
(A1)
ensuring restricted concavity with high probability; see the following section.
Assumption
(A5)
trades off the tails of the estimating equation against
p_{n}
: if
\sigma_{n}=O(1)
and all
\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}^{*})_{k}
are sub-Gaussian, it allows
p_{n}\sim e^{n^{a}}
for
a\in(0,1/2)
.
Assumption
(A6)
generalizes the
mutual incoherence
or
irrepresentable conditions

(
Wainwright, 2019
,
Zhao and Yu, 2006
,
Bühlmann and van de Geer, 2011
)
; see
Section

3.4.2
, while
(A7)
makes the inactive-group penalties large enough to induce sparsity.
Unlike sample-dependent formulations based on
n^{-1}\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\|_{\infty}

(
Negahban et al., 2012
,
Loh and Wainwright, 2015
)
, we use the population bound
\eta_{n}
.
Finally,
(A8)
controls convergence to the population equation and imposes growth restrictions on
p_{n}
analogous to
(A5)
.
Increasing
\eta_{n}
weakens this condition but requires larger
\lambda_{n,g}
in
(A7)
, which is less problematic for nonconvex penalties because these parameters typically do not enter the convergence rate; see the discussion after
Theorem

5
.

3.4
Estimation and selection consistency

3.4.1
Main results

We will provide three main results for penalized estimators. The first guarantees that any sparse solution to the penalized estimating equation is close to the true parameter vector.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
(A4)
and
(A5)
hold.
Then, any solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}\in\Theta_{n}\subseteq\Theta^{*}(\nu_{n})
satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\rVert=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\nu_{n}}(\eta_{n}+\overline{b}_{n})\right).

The optimal rate of convergence
\sqrt{s_{n}}\eta_{n}
is attained if
\nu_{n}=O(s_{n})
and
\overline{b}_{n}=O(\eta_{n})
which typically holds if
\|\bm{\lambda}_{n}\|_{\infty}=O(\eta_{n})
. In most classical applications, this rate simplifies to the usual convergence rate
\sqrt{s_{n}\ln p_{n}/n}
.
A similar rate was obtained by
Loh and Wainwright (2015)
under a
restricted strong convexity
(RSC) condition on the realized sample, whereas
Theorem

4
uses population-level conditions.
In most standard cases,
(A4)
implies with high probability the weaker RSC-type condition

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{u},\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*})-\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})\right\rangle\geq c\|\bm{u}\|^{2}-c_{1}\|\bm{u}\|_{1}^{2}\eta_{n},

for some constant
c_{1}\geq 0
.
Unlike
Theorem

4
, which is restricted to cones
\Theta^{*}(\nu_{n})
,
Loh and Wainwright (2015)
,
Negahban et al. (2012)
and
Wainwright (2019, Chapter 9)
allow larger sets of the form
\{\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}\leq k_{n}\}
by imposing the stronger condition

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{u},\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*})-\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+\bm{u})\right\rangle\geq c\|\bm{u}\|^{2}-c_{1}\|\bm{u}\|_{1}^{2}\eta_{n}^{2},

(8)

with high probability for all sufficiently small
\bm{u}
.
Under mild assumptions on the penalty and tuning parameter, (
8
) implies that
\bm{\hat{\theta}}\in\Theta^{*}(\nu_{n})
with
\nu_{n}=O(s_{n})
; see Lemma 5 in the supplement.
To the best of our knowledge, (
8
) has only been verified for variations of the linear model, where the rank-one structure of the sample-Hessian can be exploited, and it fails in the example of
Section

5.2
.
Restricting the statement to cones avoids this issue: any
\nu_{n}
-sparse solution belongs to
\Theta^{*}(\nu_{n})
, so
Theorem

4
applies.

Theorem

4
does not say anything about existence of solutions and selection consistency, which also explains why it does not require a lower bound on the tuning parameters.
The next theorem shows that there is an estimation and selection consistent solution to the penalized equation with high probability.
Specifically, we show that, with high probability, the reduced problem

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}((\bm{\theta}_{(1)},\bm{0}))_{(1)}\in\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}((\bm{\theta}_{(1)},\bm{0}))_{(1)}.

(9)

has a solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(1)}
close to
\bm{\theta}_{(1)}^{*}
, and that
(\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(1)},\bm{0})
is also a solution to the full problem (
5
).

Theorem 5
.

Suppose the reduced problem (
9
) satisfies
(A1)
on sets
\{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}\colon\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}^{\prime}\}
.
Suppose further that
(P1)
,
(P2)
and
(A5)
–
(A8)
hold.
Then, with probability tending to
1
, the sets
\Theta_{n}^{\prime}
contain a solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
of the penalized estimating equation (
5
) such that

\lVert\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\rVert=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(I(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{(1)})/n}+\sqrt{s_{n}}b_{n}^{*}\right),\quad\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(2)}=\bm{0}.

To simplify the discussion, suppose
\operatorname{tr}(I(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{(1)})=O(s_{n})
, which is the most common situation.
The rate of convergence depends on the number of non-zero coefficients
s_{n}
of
\bm{\theta}^{*}
and the bias
b_{n}^{*}
(defined in (
6
)) induced by the penalty.
For example, we have
b_{n}^{*}=\lambda_{n}
for the Lasso, and
b_{n}^{*}=0
for SCAD for
n
large enough if
\min_{1\leq k\leq s_{n}}|\theta^{*}_{k}|/\lambda_{n}\to\infty
.
For a Lasso-type penalty with different
\lambda_{n,k}
, i.e.,
p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}\lambda_{n,k}|\theta_{k}|
, we obtain
b_{n}^{*}=\max_{k=1,\ldots,s_{n}}\lambda_{n,k}
.
The rate of convergence does not explicitly involve the overall number of parameters
p_{n}
, although it may enter through the bias
b_{n}^{*}
(typically logarithmically). Similarly, the regularity conditions above usually depend on
p_{n}
only logarithmically.
This shows that estimation of
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is possible even if the total number of parameters
p_{n}
is much larger than the sample size
n
.
The non-asymptotic results of
Negahban et al. (2012)
and
Loh and Wainwright (2015)
imply a rate of
O_{p}(\sqrt{s_{n}\ln p_{n}/n})
. For the SCAD and similar penalties, this is still suboptimal as
Theorem

5
gives the rate
O_{p}(\sqrt{s_{n}/n})
.

In practice, we do not know which parameters
\theta_{k}
are 0, so it is unclear whether the solution from
Theorem

5
can be found.
Our final theorem gives conditions under which the solution is unique.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that the conditions of
Theorem

4
are satisfied and
(P3)
holds with
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}\allowbreak<c
, where
c
is defined in
(A4)
.
Suppose further that for all
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}+\bm{u}\in\Theta_{n}\cap\{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\leq a_{n}\sqrt{\nu_{n}}(\eta_{n}+\overline{b}_{n})\}
with
a_{n}\to\infty
arbitrarily slowly and all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\top}[\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}+\bm{u})-\phi(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})]\leq\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}(\bm{x})\bm{u}.

(10)

Then, with probability tending to 1, there is at most one solution in
\Theta_{n}
.

Theorem

6
implies the uniqueness of any solution in
\Theta_{n}
.
If the conditions of
Theorem

5
hold, the selection consistent estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is unique on
\Theta_{n}
with probability tending to 1.

3.4.2
Mutual incoherence & irrepresentable condition

Assumption
(A6)
generalizes familiar mutual incoherence and irrepresentable conditions to our broader framework.
It requires some
\alpha\in[0,1)
such that

\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}}\max_{g\in I_{(2)}}\lambda_{n,g}^{-1}\|\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{G_{g},(1)}\;\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})^{-1}_{(1)}\;\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2}\leq\alpha.

For SCAD and MCP, the condition holds with
\alpha=0
whenever
\min_{1\leq k\leq s_{n}}(|\theta_{k}^{*}|-\tilde{r}_{n})/\lambda_{n}\to\infty
; for Group SCAD, the analogous requirement is
\min_{g\in I_{(1)}}(\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}^{*}\|-\tilde{r}_{n})/\lambda_{n,g}\to\infty
.
For the Lasso and a scalar
\lambda_{n}
, the terms involving
\lambda_{n}
cancel and the condition can be simplified to

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}}\left\|\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{(2,1)}\;\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})^{-1}_{(1)}\;\operatorname{sign}(\bm{\theta}_{(1)})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha,

(11)

where
\operatorname{sign}(\bm{\theta})
denotes the vector of signs.
This condition is implied by the stronger assumption

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:alpha_{s}tronger}\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}}\left\|\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{(2,1)}\;\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})^{-1}_{(1)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha,

(12)

where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the maximum absolute row sum of a matrix.
Both conditions characterize the population equation and are independent of a scalar
\lambda_{n}
.
The normalized Jacobian block
\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{(2,1)}\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})^{-1}_{(1)}
measures how errors in the active coordinates affect the inactive equations.

(A6)
requires this effect to be small enough for the penalty to remove the resulting bias on
\bm{\theta}_{2}
, induced by the biased estimation of
\bm{\theta}_{1}
.
In the linear model,
J(\bm{\theta})_{(2,1)}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}_{(2)}\bm{X}_{(1)}^{\top}]
and
J(\bm{\theta})_{(1)}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}_{(1)}\bm{X}_{(1)}^{\top}]
, so the condition restricts the dependence between active and inactive covariates.
(
12
) is a population version of the mutual incoherence condition of
Wainwright (2019, Section 7.5.1)
, which requires approximate orthogonality.
(
11
) is the irrepresentable condition of
Zhao and Yu (2006)
and is necessary for Lasso selection consistency
(
Zhao and Yu, 2006
,
Zou, 2006
)
, but not for SCAD, MCP, and related nonconvex penalties
(
Loh and Wainwright, 2017
)
.
For the Group Lasso,
(A6)
reduces to the condition of
Bach (2008)
.

For heterogeneous tuning parameters,
(A6)
also describes how the entries of
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
should reflect the sensitivity of the equations.
For example, consider the weighted Lasso
p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}\lambda_{n,k}|\theta_{k}|
.
Assuming
J(\bm{\theta})_{(1)}=I_{s_{n}}
and replacing
\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{k,(1)}
by
J(\bm{\theta})_{k,(1)}
for simplicity, condition
(A6)
is implied by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{n,j}}\sum_{k=1}^{s_{n}}\lambda_{n,k}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{k}}\phi(\bm{\theta})_{j}\right]\right|\leq\alpha<1\quad\text{uniformly for all }j=s_{n}+1,\ldots,p_{n}.

Thus, a large effect of active-coordinate errors on equation
j
requires smaller active penalties or a larger
\lambda_{n,j}
.
Although the unknown support precludes tuning directly by activity, the weights may sometimes be scaled using the magnitudes of
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\phi(\bm{\theta})_{j}]
.

3.5
Asymptotic Normality and the Oracle Property

Finally, we establish asymptotic normality of the estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(1)}
.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
(P4)
holds and that for some matrix
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times s_{n}}
with
\|A_{n}\|=O(1)
and for which
\Sigma=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}I(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{(1)}A_{n}^{\top}
exists, the reduced problem (
9
) satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

3
with
r_{n}
replaced by
\tilde{r}_{n}
as defined in (
6
).
Then, the estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(1)}
in
Theorem

5
satisfies

\sqrt{n}A_{n}\left[J(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{(1)}(\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(1)}-\bm{\theta}^{*}_{(1)})-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\right]\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\Sigma).

Recall that
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
has the oracle property if it behaves like the hypothetical
oracle estimator
that knows
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{(2)}=\bm{0}
in advance. This is the case if
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(2)}=\bm{0}
and
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=o(1/\sqrt{n})
.
For SCAD and MCP with
\lambda_{n}\to 0
fast enough, one obtains
b_{n}^{*}=0
, so the rate of convergence is the same as if
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{(2)}=\bm{0}
is known in advance.
Since
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\bm{0}
, the SCAD and MCP-penalized estimators have the same efficiency as the oracle estimator.
As long as
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
is small enough that
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\bm{0}
asymptotically, it can be chosen large enough such that
(A8)
is fulfilled.

For the Lasso, we have
b_{n}^{*}=\lambda_{n}
and
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{(1)}}p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=O(\sqrt{s_{n}}\lambda_{n})
.
For the oracle property to hold, we would need
\lambda_{n}=o(1/\sqrt{s_{n}n})
.
This condition cannot be satisfied, because
(A6)
requires
\lambda_{n}\geq\eta_{n}\approx\sqrt{\ln p_{n}/n}.

This is in line with the results from
Zou (2006)
, who shows that in the linear model, the Lasso is only variable selection consistent at the cost of a slower rate of convergence.

4
Extension to dependent data

Our results were stated for independent data mainly to simplify the discussion of already
technical conditions and reduce the proofs to the core ideas. The arguments and results can
be extended to various types of dependent data, such as time series, spatial data, or graph
dependence.

To set the stage, suppose we observe a sequence of dependent random variables

\bm{Z}_{1},\dots,\bm{Z}_{N}
and we are interested in the solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}

solving the estimating equation

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi(\bm{Z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}.

Let
m_{N}\in\mathbb{N}
be a divisor of
N
, put
n_{N}=N/m_{N}
, and let

I_{1,N},\dots,I_{n_{N},N}
be a partition of
\{1,\dots,N\}
with

|I_{i,N}|=m_{N}
. For time series one may take

I_{i,N}=\{(i-1)m_{N}+1,\dots,im_{N}\}
. Define

\textstyle\bm{X}_{i}=(\bm{Z}_{j}:j\in I_{i,N}),\qquad\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{m_{N}}\sum_{j\in I_{i,N}}\varphi(\bm{Z}_{j};\bm{\theta}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n_{N}.

Then the estimating equation can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{n_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{N}}\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}.

If the blocks

\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{n_{N}}
are independent, we can apply the
results from the previous sections to the block-wise estimating equation.
While the block-wise independence assumption is strong, it serves as a useful starting point
for understanding how to extend our results to dependent data. To comply with the high-level
view of this paper, we will now state an abstract dependence condition that applies to a wide
range of dependence scenarios and slots nicely into our arguments for the independent case.
Blocking and coupling arguments of this type are standard in asymptotic theory for weakly dependent processes
(
Rio and others, 2017
, see, e.g.,)
.

For sub-
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{C}
, define the beta-mixing
coefficient by

\textstyle\beta(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\{A_{i}\},\{C_{j}\}}\sum_{i,j}\left|\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap C_{j})-\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\mathbb{P}(C_{j})\right|,

where the supremum is over all finite partitions of the sample space with

A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
and
C_{j}\in\mathcal{C}
.

(D)

There exist
m_{N}=o(N),L_{N}
and a partition
I_{1,N},\dots,I_{n_{N},N}
of
\{1,\dots,N\}
with
n_{N}\coloneqq N/m_{N}
and
|I_{i,N}|=m_{N}
, such that:

(i)

L_{N}/n_{N}\to 1
,

(ii)

L_{N}\max_{1\leq i\leq L_{N}}\beta\!\left(\sigma\{\bm{Z}_{j}:j\in I_{i,N}\},\sigma\{\bm{Z}_{j}:j\in\bigcup_{\ell\leq L_{N},\ell\neq i}I_{\ell,N}\}\right)\to 0
.

Intuitively, the condition demands that, if we drop blocks
I_{k,N}
for
k>L_{N}
, the dependence between the remaining blocks is weak. Part (i) requires that we only drop a negligible fraction of blocks. Part (ii) ensures that the dependence fades sufficiently quickly such that a standard coupling argument allows replacing the retained blocks by independent versions with high probability.

For the omitted blocks to have truly negligible effect, we require an additional condition that depends on the context. The argument is summarized in the following lemma, whose proof is given in the supplement.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that condition
(D)
holds, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a collection of functions.
If

\displaystyle\sum_{i=L_{N}+1}^{n_{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f(\bm{X}_{i})|>\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{n_{N}-L_{N}}\right)=o(1),

(13)

holds for a given
\varepsilon_{N}
, then there are independent random variables
\bm{X}_{1}^{*},\dots,\bm{X}_{n_{N}}^{*}
with
\bm{X}_{i}^{*}=_{d}\bm{X}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n_{N}
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{N}}f(\bm{X}_{i})-\frac{1}{n_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{N}}f(\bm{X}_{i}^{*})\right|=o_{p}(\varepsilon_{N}/n_{N}).

Adapting the proofs of our main results to the dependent case now amounts to invoking
Lemma

1
with the appropriate function class
\mathcal{F}
and choice of threshold
\varepsilon_{N}
before applying any concentration or limit theorem.
Conveniently, the assumptions of our main results already involve threshold sequences that control (
13
) for the relevant quantities.
The following theorem summarizes the conditions under which our main results can be extended to the dependent case.

Theorem 8
.

Consider the above setting and suppose that
(D)
holds. Let
n:=n_{N}
and
B_{n},\tilde{B}_{n},D_{n},r_{n},\tilde{r}_{n}
denote the corresponding quantities from
(A1)
–
(A8)

We require additional conditions of the form

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:assumpDep}\sum_{i=L_{N}+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{n}>E_{n}\right)=o(1).

(14)

•

The conclusions of Theorems
1
and
2
remain true provided

and (
14

The additional assumption (

and

, Markov’s inequality and the union bound imply (

provided

.
The blocking construction affects the covariance matrix appearing in the asymptotic normality statements:

I_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathds{C}\mathrm{ov}}(\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}^{*}))=\frac{1}{Nm_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j,k\in I_{i,N}}{\mathds{C}\mathrm{ov}}(\varphi(\bm{Z}_{j};\bm{\theta}^{*}),\varphi(\bm{Z}_{k};\bm{\theta}^{*}))

Although
I_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*})
depends on the block assignments

in
(D)

m_{N}I_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{*})
can be replaced asymptotically by the covariance matrix
\widetilde{I}_{N}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathds{C}\mathrm{ov}}(\varphi(\bm{Z}_{i};\bm{\theta}^{*}),\varphi(\bm{Z}_{j};\bm{\theta}^{*}))
of the original estimating equation.
A simple sufficient condition is that the covariance between retained blocks decays sufficiently quickly, e.g.,

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i<j\leq L_{N}}\|{\mathds{C}\mathrm{ov}}(A_{n}\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}^{*}),A_{n}\phi(\bm{X}_{j};\bm{\theta}^{*}))\|=o(1/n),

(15)

and that
(n-L_{N})^{2}/n=o(1)
, which ensures that the contribution of the dropped blocks is negligible.

Let us now discuss how to verify the abstract condition
(D)
in a few more concrete settings.

Example 1

(
\beta
-mixing time series)
.

Let
(\bm{Z}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a polynomially

-mixing time series, i.e.,
\beta_{\rm ts}(q)=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\beta\left(\sigma(\bm{Z}_{s}:s\leq t),\sigma(\bm{Z}_{s}:s\geq t+q)\right)\lesssim q^{-\kappa}
.
Take

m_{N}=\lfloor N^{\gamma}\rfloor
and
q_{N}=\lfloor N^{\delta}\rfloor
with

0<\delta<\gamma<1
and
\gamma+\kappa\delta>1
.
Define

L_{N}=\lfloor N/(m_{N}+q_{N})\rfloor

and
I_{k,N}=\{(k-1)(m_{N}+q_{N})+1,\ldots,(k-1)(m_{N}+q_{N})+m_{N}\}
for
k=1,\ldots,L_{N}
.
This takes blocks of size
m_{N}
and leaves gaps of size
q_{N}
in between.
The omitted observations are partitioned arbitrarily into sets

of cardinality
m_{N}
.
Then
L_{N}/n_{N}\sim m_{N}/(m_{N}+q_{N})\to 1
, and

\displaystyle L_{N}\max_{1\leq k\leq L_{N}}\beta\!\left(\sigma\{\bm{Z}_{i}:i\in I_{k,N}\},\sigma\{\bm{Z}_{i}:i\in\cup_{\ell\leq L_{N},\ell\neq k}I_{\ell,N}\}\right)\lesssim\frac{N}{m_{N}}q_{N}^{-\kappa}\lesssim N^{1-\gamma}N^{-\kappa\delta}\to 0.

Thus condition
(D)
holds.

Corollary

derives consistency and asymptotic normality of M-estimators in generalized linear models in this setting.

A similar construction works for a spatial grid, where retained blocks are formed from spatial hyper-cubes with gaps. A more interesting example arises when dependence is induced by a network.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with nodes
V=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and edges
E
, and define the graph distance as
d_{G}(i,j)=\min\{k:\text{there is a path from }i\text{ to }j\text{ using }k\text{ edges}\}
.
Suppose that the observed data
\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{N}
are generated from a process such that the dependence between
\bm{Z}_{i}
and
\bm{Z}_{j}
decays as the graph distance
d_{G}(i,j)
increases.
In a subcritical Erdős–Rényi graph, for instance, the largest connected component is of size
O(\log N)
, and different components are independent. In this case, we can construct blocks of size
m_{N}\gg\log N
from connected components, and the retained blocks are independent. Such graphs are not realistic models for many applications in, e.g., social or transport networks, where the largest connected component is often of order
N
, and some nodes (hubs) have very high degree. The following examples illustrate how to handle such cases.

Example 2

(Hub-screened network dependence)
.

Let
G_{N}=(V_{N},E_{N})
be a graph with
V_{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and graph
distance
d_{N}
. For
A,B\subset V_{N}
, write

d_{N}(A,B)=\min_{i\in A,j\in B}d_{N}(i,j),

and suppose the following bound for the graph-distance beta-mixing coefficient

\displaystyle\beta_{{\rm net},N}(q)=\textstyle\sup_{A,B\subset V_{N},d_{N}(A,B)\geq q}\beta\!\left(\sigma\{\bm{Z}_{i}:i\in A\},\sigma\{\bm{Z}_{j}:j\in B\}\right)\lesssim\beta(q)

with some
\beta(q)\to 0
for
q\to\infty
.
Suppose that there exist hub sets
H_{N}\subset V_{N}
and radii

q_{N}\to\infty
such that, with
U_{N}=\{i\in V_{N}:d_{N}(i,H_{N})\leq q_{N}\}
, and with
\mathcal{C}_{N}
denoting the connected components of

G_{N}[V_{N}\setminus H_{N}]
(the graph obtained by removing the hub nodes and their incident edges),
|U_{N}|=o(N)
and
\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{N}}|C\setminus U_{N}|=o(N)
.
Then, condition
(D)
holds.
The proof is given in the supplement.

5
Applications

Our general results are stated under rather abstract regularity conditions to keep them widely applicable.
In the following, we provide several examples of how these conditions simplify in specific applications.
While some of these examples can be analyzed using less sophisticated approaches tailored to the specific problem, this section illustrates the wide applicability of our framework.

p_{n}\sim\exp(n^{1/3-\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Similarly, polynomial moment bounds translate into polynomial growth conditions on
p_{n}
. For example, the choice
\rho(x)=x^{3}
requires sixth moments and allows
p_{n}\sim n^{2-\varepsilon}
.

If
\bm{\theta}
can be partitioned into
K_{n}
groups
G_{1},\ldots,G_{K_{n}}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
, we can apply a group-structured penalty such as the Group SCAD
p_{\lambda_{n}}=\sum_{g=1}^{K_{n}}p_{\lambda_{n}}(\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\|_{2})
, where
p_{\lambda_{n}}(\theta)
denotes the one-dimensional SCAD penalty.
This contains the SCAD penalty
p_{\lambda_{n}}=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}p_{\lambda_{n}}(|\theta_{k}|)
as a special case.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose the reduced problem (
9
) satisfies the conditions from
Corollary

1
with some
k
and some function
\rho
and (
20
) holds and let

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}\coloneq\max_{g\in I_{(2)}}\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n},\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\left\|\mathbb{E}\Big[\overline{\psi}(Y,\bm{X},\bm{\theta})\bm{X}_{G_{g}}\bm{X}_{(1)}^{\top}\Big]\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\overline{\psi}(Y,\bm{X},\bm{\theta})\bm{X}_{(1)}\bm{X}_{(1)}^{\top}\Big]^{-1}\bm{x}\right\|_{2}.

(23)

Suppose that
\sigma^{2}=\max_{1\leq k\leq p_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{k}^{2}]
is bounded away from zero and infinity,
(\min_{g\in I_{(1)}}\|\bm{\theta}^{*}_{G_{g}}\|_{2}-\sqrt{s_{n}/n})/\lambda_{n}\to\infty
,
\sqrt{n/s_{n}}\min_{g\in I_{(1)}}\|\bm{\theta}^{*}_{G_{g}}\|_{2}\to\infty
and
\lambda_{n}\geq 4\eta_{n}\max\left\{\sqrt{|G_{1}|},\ldots,\sqrt{|G_{K_{n}}|},\alpha_{n}\right\}
with some
\eta_{n}\geq\sqrt{4\sigma_{n}^{2}\ln p_{n}/n}
.
Then, if
s_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})
,
s_{n}^{3k}/n^{(2k-2)}=o(1)
, and

\frac{s_{n}(s_{n}+\ln p_{n})}{n^{2}\eta_{n}^{2}}=o(1),\quad p_{n}=o\left(\frac{\max\{\rho(n/(s_{n}\ln p_{n})),\ \rho(n^{3/2}\eta_{n}/(s_{n}^{2}+s_{n}\ln p_{n}))\}}{n}\right),

the Group SCAD-penalized equation has a solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
with
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(2)}=\bm{0}
with probability tending to 1,
\|\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|=O_{p}(\sqrt{s_{n}/n})
, and
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(1)}
is asymptotically normal (with
\|A_{n}\|=O(1)
) and as efficient as the oracle solution.
If
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{E}[\inf_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}}|\psi^{\prime}(Y,\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta})|\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}])\geq c
for some
c>0
and the Group SCAD penalty is used with
a>1+\frac{1}{c}
, this solution is unique with probability tending to 1.

Compared to the Lasso, we do not require the mutual incoherence condition (
21
), and
\alpha_{n}
in (
23
) may even diverge.
To ensure uniqueness, the non-convexity parameter
a
of SCAD must be chosen appropriately.

The four corollaries also apply to non-parametric regression problems, in which
\bm{X}_{i}
consists of appropriate basis functions.
For example,
Corollary

3
implies consistency of Lasso-assisted variable selection in high-dimensional nonparametric additive models
(
Huang et al., 2010
)
under appropriate conditions.

5.2
A problem where the classical RSC condition fails

We now give a simple example where the RSC condition (
8
) fails, while our weaker condition remains easy to verify.
Consider an allocation problem with linear payoffs and quadratic cost of the form
\max_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{\theta}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\theta}^{\top}C\bm{\theta}
, where
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
is a vector of expected payoffs and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}\times p_{n}}
is a symmetric, positive definite cost matrix.
Such problems arise, for example, in portfolio optimization, where
\bm{\mu}
contains expected asset returns and
C
reflects market risk and transaction or liquidity costs, or in marketing budget allocation, where
\bm{\mu}
contains expected returns of marketing channels and
C
captures diminishing returns and cross-channel effects such as cannibalization.

For the counterexample, we consider centered payoffs and set
C=I_{p_{n}}
, so the population solution is
\bm{\theta}^{*}=\bm{0}
.
Let
m_{n}=\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
,
M_{n}=\lfloor p_{n}/m_{n}\rfloor
, and let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{M_{n}}
be disjoint subsets of
\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
of size
m_{n}
.
For
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
, let
\bm{1}_{S}=(\mathds{1}\{j\in S\})_{j=1}^{p_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
denote its indicator vector.
Suppose that
\bm{Y}_{i}
has independent standard Gaussian entries and define the symmetric random cost matrices

X_{i}=I_{p_{n}}+\sum_{g=1}^{M_{n}}\xi_{i,g}\bm{1}_{S_{g}}\bm{1}_{S_{g}}^{\top}
,
where
\xi_{i,g}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

and
\xi_{i,g}
and
\bm{Y}_{i}
are mutually independent.
Thus,
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=I_{p_{n}}
, but the entries within each block
S_{g}\times S_{g}
are subject to a common random shock.
Consider the compact parameter set
\Theta_{n}=\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}\colon\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}\leq 1\}
and the penalized problem

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\theta}}\in\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{Y}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)\bm{\theta}-\lambda_{n}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}\right\}.

(24)

We consider the associated penalized estimating equation

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{Y}_{i}-X_{i}\bm{\theta})\in\lambda_{n}\partial\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}.

(25)

The random part of each sample Hessian has rank
M_{n}
, which diverges under the conditions below.

Corollary 5
.

Consider the setting above with
p_{n}\geq n
,
\eta_{n}=\sqrt{\ln p_{n}/n}
,
\ln p_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})
, and
\lambda_{n}=C_{\lambda}\eta_{n}
for a sufficiently large constant
C_{\lambda}
.
Let
1\leq\nu_{n}=O((n/\ln p_{n})^{1/2-\delta})
for some
\delta\in(0,1/2]
.
Then, with probability tending to one,
\bm{0}
is the unique solution to (
25
) on
\Theta^{*}(\nu_{n}).

On the other hand, the RSC condition (
8
) can only hold with vanishing probability.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\eta_{n}=\sqrt{\ln p_{n}/n}
,
p_{n}\geq n
, and
\ln p_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})
.
Then, for any
c,c_{1}>0
, the event

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{u},\Phi_{n}(\bm{0})-\Phi_{n}(\bm{u})\right\rangle\geq c\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}-c_{1}\|\bm{u}\|_{1}^{2}\eta_{n}^{2},\qquad\forall\|\bm{u}\|_{1}\leq 1,

has probability tending to zero.

Consequently, the theory of, e.g.,
Negahban et al. (2012)
,
Loh and Wainwright (2015)
,
Wainwright (2019)
does not apply.
The problematic directions have effective sparsity
m_{n}\asymp\sqrt{n}\gg\nu_{n}
and thus lie outside the cones covered by
Corollary

5
, showing that the rank-one structure exploited in proofs of (
8
) is consequential.

5.3
Multi-Sample Estimation

Consider a multi-sample estimation problem:
The data are given by
(k_{1},\bm{X}_{1}),\dots,(k_{n},\bm{X}_{n})
, where
k_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K_{n}\}
indicates to which of the
K_{n}
samples
\bm{X}_{i}
belongs.
Assume for simplicity that
p_{n}=K_{n}
and that each
\theta_{k}
is estimated using only the
k
-th sample.
Write
n_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{k_{i}=k\}
for the sample size of the
k
-th sample and
\phi^{\prime}_{k}(\bm{X};\theta_{k})=\partial\phi_{k}(\bm{X};\theta_{k})/\partial\theta_{k}
.
The estimation function, its Jacobian, and covariance matrix are

\displaystyle\phi(k_{i},\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\left(\mathds{1}\{k_{i}=k\}\,\frac{n}{n_{k}}\phi_{k}(\bm{X}_{i};\theta_{k})\right)_{k=1}^{K_{n}},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\phi(k_{i},\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathds{1}\{k_{i}=k\}\,\frac{n}{n_{k}}\phi^{\prime}_{k}(\bm{X}_{i};\theta_{k})\right)_{k=1}^{K_{n}},\quad I(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{n}{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{k}(\bm{X}_{i};\theta^{*}_{k})^{2}]\right)_{k=1}^{K_{n}}.

The standardization
n/n_{k}
is necessary to ensure that the eigenvalues of
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})]
are bounded away from
0
.
Consequently,
r_{n}=O((\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}n_{k}^{-1})^{1/2})
if
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{k}(\bm{X}_{i};\theta^{*}_{k})^{2}]
is uniformly bounded over
i
and
k
. This framework can easily be extended to multiple parameters
\bm{\theta}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{k}}
, potentially shared across subsamples. In the following examples, we stick to the single-parameter case for simplicity.

5.3.1
Example: Distributed Inference

An interesting application arises in distributed inference. Here,
iid
data are distributed over
K_{n}
different locations, and the goal is to estimate a parameter
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
from the distributed data. This is a common setup in federated learning, where data are distributed over different devices, and the goal is to estimate a common model.
Our general setup allows for differing sample sizes and population characteristics between locations. This may happen if, for example, the data is collected over hospitals who may share their estimate but not the data for privacy reasons. The distributed estimates
\hat{\theta}_{k}
can be reconciled into a global estimate through averaging
\hat{\theta}_{K_{n}+1}=K_{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\hat{\theta}_{k}
. To put this in our framework, we stack the individual estimating equations
\phi_{k}(\bm{X}_{i};\theta_{k})=\psi(\bm{X}_{i};\theta_{k})
as above, and append the reconciliation function
\phi_{K_{n}+1}(\bm{X}_{i};\theta)=K_{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\theta_{k}-\theta_{K_{n}+1}
.

Corollary 6
.

Let
\Theta_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
n_{1}=\dots=n_{K_{n}}=n/K_{n}
. Suppose that, for
\theta\in\Theta_{0}
,
\psi(\bm{x};\theta)
is uniformly bounded and
\psi^{\prime}(\bm{x};\theta)=\partial_{\theta}\psi(\bm{x};\theta)
is negative, uniformly bounded away from 0 and
-\infty
and Lipschitz in
\theta
.
Then, if
K_{n}^{3}/n\to 0
, with probability tending to one there is a unique solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
. Moreover, for all
k=1,\ldots,K_{n}
,
\sqrt{n/K_{n}}(\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k}^{*})\to N(0,\sigma_{k}^{2})
and
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_{K_{n}+1}-\theta^{*})\to N(0,\sigma^{2})
with

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{2}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\psi(\bm{X}_{i};\theta^{*}_{k})^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[\psi^{\prime}(\bm{X}_{i};\theta^{*}_{k})]^{2}},\quad\sigma^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{K_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\sigma_{k}^{2}.

While for fixed
K_{n}=K
, asymptotic normality of
\hat{\theta}_{K_{n}+1}
would follow easily from asymptotic normality of the independent
\hat{\theta}_{k}
, our results provide conditions on the rate of growth of
K_{n}
when
K_{n}\to\infty
.

If the samples have identical distributions, it holds that
\sigma_{1}=\cdots=\sigma_{K_{n}}
, so the averaged estimator is as efficient as one computed from the pooled sample.
For a fixed number of samples
K
and in the risk minimization setting, this is in line with the results in
Zhang et al. (2013)
, who laid the groundwork for further developments in this area, including a diverging dimension of the target parameter
(
Rosenblatt and Nadler, 2016
)
, a diverging number of samples
(
Huang and Huo, 2019
)
and penalized estimation
(
Battey et al., 2018
)
.
Unlike the references just mentioned, our framework allows for estimation problems beyond risk minimization/optimization and does not require the different samples to be identically distributed.

The independence assumption might be unrealistic, so we now assume dependence induced by a network as described in
Example

2
, and

\phi(\bm{k}_{i},\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\frac{1}{m_{N}}\sum_{j\in I_{i,N}}\left(\mathds{1}\{k_{j}=k\}\,\frac{N}{N_{k}}\psi_{k}(\bm{Z}_{j};\theta_{k})\right)_{k=1,\ldots,K_{n}},\quad\text{where }N_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\{k_{j}=k\}.

Note that now,
\nabla\phi(\bm{k}_{i},\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})
and
I(\bm{\theta})
are not necessarily diagonal matrices, as we allow
\bm{X}_{i}
to contain observations from different locations.
Again, we append the reconciliation function
\phi_{K_{n}+1}(\bm{X}_{i};\theta)=K_{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\theta_{k}-\theta_{K_{n}+1}
.
For simplicity, we assume that at each location
k
, the marginal distributions of the
\bm{Z}_{j}
with
k_{j}=k
are identical.

Corollary 7
.

Let
\Theta_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
N_{1}=\dots=N_{K_{n}}=N/K_{n}
. Suppose that, for
\theta\in\Theta_{0}
,
\psi(\bm{z};\theta)
is uniformly bounded and
\psi^{\prime}(\bm{z};\theta)=\partial_{\theta}\psi(\bm{z};\theta)
is negative, uniformly bounded away from 0 and
-\infty
and Lipschitz in
\theta
, and that the conditions of
Example

2
hold. Suppose further that (
15
) holds for the projection matrices associated with the asymptotic normality statements below.
Let
d_{n}\coloneqq m_{N}K_{n}^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}I(\bm{\theta}^{*})_{kk}
, assume the common growth conditions
K_{n}^{3}/n=o(1)
,
K_{n}N(n-L_{N})^{2}/(d_{n}n^{2})=o(1)
,
(n-L_{N})^{5}/n=o(1)
, and define

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\psi(\bm{Z}_{j};\theta^{*}_{k_{j}})\psi(\bm{Z}_{j^{\prime}};\theta^{*}_{k_{j^{\prime}}})]}{\mathbb{E}[\psi^{\prime}(\bm{Z}_{j};\theta_{k_{j}}^{*})]\mathbb{E}[\psi^{\prime}(\bm{Z}_{j^{\prime}};\theta_{k_{j^{\prime}}}^{*})]},

\displaystyle\sigma_{n,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{K_{n}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\{k_{j}=k_{j^{\prime}}=k\}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\psi(\bm{Z}_{j};\theta_{k}^{*})\psi(\bm{Z}_{j^{\prime}};\theta_{k}^{*})]}{\mathbb{E}[\psi^{\prime}(\bm{Z}_{j};\theta^{*}_{k})]^{2}}.

For the global estimator, suppose additionally that
N(n-L_{N})^{2}/(n^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2})=O(1)
and

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big((\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m_{N}})^{-1}\sum_{j\in I_{i,N}}\psi(\bm{Z}_{j};\theta_{k_{j}}^{*})\big)^{4}\Big]=o(n),

then
\sqrt{N}\,\sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{\theta}_{K_{n}+1}-\theta^{*})\to N(0,1)
.
For a given location
k=1,\ldots,K_{n}
, suppose instead that
K_{n}^{3}d_{n}/(n\sigma_{n,k}^{2})=o(1)
,
K_{n}N(n-L_{N})^{2}/(n^{2}\sigma_{n,k}^{2})=O(1)
,
d_{n}K_{n}^{3/2}/(\sqrt{N}\sigma_{n,k})=o(1)
and

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big((\sigma_{n,k}\sqrt{m_{N}})^{-1}\sum_{j\in I_{i,N}}\mathds{1}\{k_{j}=k\}\psi(\bm{Z}_{j};\theta_{k}^{*})\big)^{4}\Big]=o(n/K_{n}^{2}),

then
\sqrt{N/K_{n}}\,\sigma_{n,k}^{-1}(\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta^{*}_{k})\to N(0,1)
.

Compared to the
iid
setting in
Corollary

6
, the conditions are more complex since we impose very few assumptions on the dependence structure, which determines
n,L_{N},d_{n},\sigma_{n}
and
\sigma_{n,k}
.
Examining the best- and worst-case behavior of
d_{n}
,
\sigma_{n}
, and
\sigma_{n,k}
gives a clearer picture:
If the dependence within the
\bm{X}_{i}
is sufficiently weak, then
d_{n}=O(1)
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}\simeq 1,\sigma_{n,k}^{2}\simeq 1
, and we obtain asymptotic normality of
\hat{\theta}_{K_{n}+1}
and
\hat{\theta}_{k}
at
\sqrt{N}
and
\sqrt{N/K_{n}}
rate.
The fourth moment condition is plausible since
\mathbb{E}[(m_{N}^{-1/2}\sum_{j\in I_{i,N}}\psi(\bm{Z}_{j};\theta_{k_{j}}^{*}))^{4}]=O(1)
if the dependence is weak enough.
Among the additional conditions for asymptotic normality of
\hat{\theta}_{k}
, only
K_{n}N(n-L_{N})^{2}/(n^{2}\sigma_{n,k}^{2})=O(1)
remains non-trivial.
If the dependence within the
\bm{X}_{i}
is strong, we have
d_{n}=O(m_{N})
and
\sigma_{n}^{2},\sigma_{n,k}^{2}\simeq m_{N}
.
The slower rates (
\sqrt{n}
and
\sqrt{n/K_{n}}
) require fewer assumptions:
The fourth moment conditions become trivial, since the expectations are bounded, and all remaining conditions are implied by
K_{n}^{3}/n=o(1)
and
(n-L_{N})^{5}/n=o(1)
.

5.4
Stepwise Estimation

Another setting that shows the flexibility of our results is stepwise estimation. Assume the parameter vector can be grouped as
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\theta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{K_{n}})
. The parameters are estimated sequentially using the estimates
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{k-1}
from previous iterations:

\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{k}=\argmax_{\theta_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{k}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{k-1}),\quad\bm{\theta}_{k}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{k}(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\theta}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\theta}^{*}_{k-1})\right],

for some functions
f_{k}
.
Denote
\phi_{k}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{k-1})=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}f_{k}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{k-1})
.
Then, the sequential estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
can be expressed as the solution of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\hat{\theta}})=\bm{0}
and
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is the solution of
\mathbb{E}[\phi(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})]=\bm{0}
with

\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})=\left(\phi_{1}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}_{1}),\ \phi_{2}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}_{2},\bm{\theta}_{1}),\ldots,\ \phi_{K_{n}}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta}_{K_{n}},\bm{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{K_{n}-1})\right)^{\top}.

By treating the entire estimation path as a single parameter vector, this representation allows our general results to accommodate a diverging number of steps
K_{n}
.

5.4.1
Example: Causal Inference

As a concrete example, suppose we want to estimate the causal effect of some covariates
\bm{Z}
on an outcome
Y
in the presence of confounders
\bm{C}
from
iid
observational data. Part of the population has received a treatment, which we indicate by the binary treatment indicator
T
. Under the usual conditions for no unmeasured confounding, the
conditional average treatment effect (CATE)
can be defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{CATE}(\bm{z})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{YT}{\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid\bm{W})}-\frac{Y(1-T)}{\mathbb{P}(T=0\mid\bm{W})}\mid\bm{Z}=\bm{z}\right],

where
\bm{W}=(\bm{Z},\bm{C})
; see e.g.
Huber (2023)
.
We model the treatment probabilities and CATE by
\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid\bm{w})=\sigma(\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})
and
\mathrm{CATE}(\bm{z})=\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{2}
, where
\sigma
is an appropriate link function.
The parameters can be estimated by first estimating
\bm{\theta}_{1}
using maximum likelihood, and then estimating
\bm{\theta}_{2}
by the plug-in least squares estimator

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{2}=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{Y_{i}T_{i}}{\sigma(\bm{W}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{1})}-\frac{Y_{i}(1-T_{i})}{1-\sigma(\bm{W}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{1})}-\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{2}\right]^{2}.

This stepwise procedure can be reformulated as solving the estimating equation

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(Y_{i},T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i},\bm{W}_{i};\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\left[T_{i}\ln\sigma(\bm{W}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})+(1-T_{i})\ln[1-\sigma(\bm{W}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})]\right]\\
-\left[\frac{Y_{i}T_{i}}{\sigma(\bm{W}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})}-\frac{Y_{i}(1-T_{i})}{1-\sigma(\bm{W}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})}-\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{2}\right]\bm{Z}_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{0}.

Corollary 8
.

Suppose that, for some
\varepsilon>0
,

\varepsilon\leq\sigma(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})\leq 1-\varepsilon

almost surely, uniformly over
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}
, and that
\sigma
is twice continuously differentiable with uniformly bounded derivatives.
Suppose further that
|Y|\leq 1
,
\max_{k}\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y,t,\bm{Z},\bm{W};\bm{\theta}^{*})^{4}_{k}]=O(1)
and
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
satisfies the design conditions from (
17
).
Let
\overline{\sigma}=1-\sigma
and define

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(T,\bm{W})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}}\left[T(\ln\sigma)^{\prime\prime}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})+(1-T)(\ln\overline{\sigma})^{\prime\prime}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})\right],

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}(T,Y,\bm{W})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}}\left|\frac{TY\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})}{2\sigma(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})^{2}}\right|+\left|\frac{(1-T)Y\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})}{2\overline{\sigma}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})^{2}}\right|-1.

Suppose there is
c>0
such that
\max\{\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha_{1}(T,\bm{W})\bm{W}\bm{W}^{\top}\right]\right),\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha_{2}(T,Y,\bm{W})\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top}\right]\right)\}\leq-c
.

Then, if
p_{n}\ln p_{n}/n\to 0
and
\rho^{-1}(n)\ln p_{n}/n\to 0
(with
\rho
as defined in (
17
)), the estimating equation has a unique solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
on
\Theta_{n}
with
\|\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|=O_{p}(\sqrt{p_{n}/n})
.

If
p_{n}^{2}/n\to 0
and
p_{n}^{3/2\,k}/(n^{2k-2})\to 0
(with
k
as defined in (
17
)),
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is asymptotically normal.

The matrices inside the expectations of the eigenvalue condition are the blocks of a block-diagonal matrix
H_{n}
constructed in the proof. The eigenvalue condition is easiest to verify if both
\ln\sigma
and
\ln(1-\sigma)
are concave, as for logistic and probit links.
For these links, the bounded-away condition above is a uniform-overlap assumption on the realized linear predictors; for example, it holds when
\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1}
remains in a fixed compact set uniformly over
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{n}
.

If the number of covariates or confounders is large, we may want to add a sparsity penalty
p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})
.
For simplicity, suppose that the parameters are reordered such that
\bm{\theta}^{*}=(\bm{\theta}_{(1)}^{*},\bm{0})
.
The following corollary guarantees that the SCAD-penalized estimator
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is consistent, unique and asymptotically normal.

Corollary 9
.

Suppose that the regularity conditions from
Corollary

8
hold,
\bm{W}
satisfies the design conditions (
20
) (with
|\psi|
uniformly bounded),
\sqrt{n/s_{n}}\min_{1\leq k\leq s_{n}}|\theta_{k}^{*}|\to\infty
, and that the SCAD penalty is used with
a>1+\frac{1}{\min\{c,K^{2}\}}
, with
c
from
Corollary

8
and
K\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle|Y\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})/(2\sigma(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})^{2})|\leq K,\quad|Y\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})/(2\overline{\sigma}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{1})^{2})|\leq K,\quad\|\mathbb{E}[\bm{W}\bm{W}^{\top}]\|\leq K.

Suppose that
(\min_{1\leq k\leq s_{n}}|\theta_{k}^{*}|-\sqrt{s_{n}/n})/\lambda_{n}\to\infty
,
\sqrt{n/s_{n}}\min_{1\leq k\leq s_{n}}|\theta_{k}^{*}|\to\infty
, and

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\geq 8\sigma_{n}\sqrt{\frac{\ln p_{n}}{n}}\max\left\{1,\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{n}}\left\|\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})_{(2,1)}\;\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})^{-1}_{(1)}\right\|_{\infty}\right\}.

Suppose that
s_{n}
and
p_{n}
satisfy the conditions in (
22
).
Then, with probability tending to 1, the penalized equation has a unique solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
on
\Theta_{n}
with
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(2)}=\bm{0}
and
\|\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|=O_{p}(\sqrt{s_{n}/n})
. Additionally,
\bm{\hat{\theta}}_{(1)}
is asymptotically normal and as efficient as the oracle solution.

5.4.2
Example: Stochastic Optimization

Our results also apply when
K_{n}\to\infty
, as in iterative procedures such as gradient descent or boosting.
Suppose we want to learn
\theta_{\infty}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
solving
\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X};\theta_{\infty}^{*})]=0
.
Given an initial value
\theta_{0}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\theta_{k}^{*}=\theta_{k-1}^{*}-\alpha\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X};\theta_{k-1}^{*})]
.
Under appropriate conditions on the learning rate
\alpha
and smoothness of
f
, the sequence
\theta_{k}^{*}
converges geometrically to
\theta_{\infty}^{*}
.

Let
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{n}
be
iid
samples from the distribution of
\bm{X}
and let
\mathcal{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{K_{n}}
partition
\{1,\dots,n\}
into sets of size
n/K_{n}
.
Define
\hat{\theta}_{0}=\theta_{0}^{*}
and, for
1\leq k\leq K_{n}
,

\hat{\theta}_{k}=\hat{\theta}_{k-1}-\alpha\frac{K_{n}}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}f(\bm{X}_{i};\hat{\theta}_{k-1}),

and write the entire iteration path
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
as the solution of
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\hat{\theta}})=\bm{0}
with

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\theta})=\begin{pmatrix}K_{n}\mathds{1}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}[\theta^{*}_{0}-\theta_{1}-\alpha f(\bm{X}_{i};\theta^{*}_{0})]\\
\vdots\\
K_{n}\mathds{1}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{K_{n}}}[\theta_{K_{n}-1}-\theta_{K_{n}}-\alpha f(\bm{X}_{i};\theta_{K_{n}-1})]\end{pmatrix},

(26)

and similarly for the population version.
The following is one result under simple conditions.

Corollary 10
.

Let
f^{\prime}(\bm{x};\theta)=\partial_{\theta}f(\bm{x};\theta)
.
Suppose that
K_{n}^{3}/n\to 0
,
\|\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}=O(1)
,
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}}\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X};\theta)^{4}]\allowbreak=O(1)
,
f^{\prime}\in[\kappa,L]
and
|f^{\prime\prime}|\leq L
for some
\kappa,L\in(0,\infty)
, and that
0<\alpha\leq 1/L
.

Then
\|\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|=O_{p}\left(K_{n}/\sqrt{n}\right)
,
and
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is unique with probability
\to 1
, and for any
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times K_{n}}
with
\|A_{n}\|=O(1)
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n/K_{n}}A_{n}(\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}\Sigma_{n}A_{n}^{\top}\right),

where
\Sigma_{n}
is symmetric and, with the convention
\prod_{j=i}^{i-1}a_{j}=1
, it holds that for
i\leq j
,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{i}{\mathds{V}\mathrm{ar}}[f(\bm{X};\theta_{k-1}^{*})]\left[\prod_{m=k}^{i-1}(1-\alpha\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\bm{X};\theta_{m}^{*})])\right]^{2}\left[\prod_{m=i}^{j-1}(1-\alpha\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\bm{X};\theta_{m}^{*})])\right].

The corollary couples the sample and population iteration paths, showing that they are globally close and that finite-dimensional linear summaries converge to a Gaussian limit.
For example,
A_{n}=(0,\dots,0,1)
gives
\sqrt{n/K_{n}}
-convergence of the final iterate, while
A_{n}=(1/\sqrt{K_{n}},\dots,1/\sqrt{K_{n}})
gives
\sqrt{n}
-convergence of the averaged iterate.
Another choice is
A_{n}=(\bm{e}_{\lceil K_{n}/q\rceil},\bm{e}_{\lceil 2K_{n}/q\rceil},\dots,\bm{e}_{K_{n}})^{\top}
, where
\bm{e}_{k}
is the
k
th standard unit vector.
This discretizes the process
\hat{\theta}(t)=\hat{\theta}_{\lceil tK_{n}\rceil}
,
t\in[0,1]
.
For
n,K_{n}\to\infty
, one can verify that
\sqrt{n/K_{n}}A_{n}(\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,V)
with diagonal
V
, suggesting white-noise fluctuations around the population path.
The assumptions can be relaxed to allow, e.g., a slowly decaying learning rate or probabilistic bounds on
f^{\prime}
; other iterative algorithms can be handled similarly.

Together, these examples illustrate how the general theory accommodates various estimation procedures beyond standard settings, including dependent data and stepwise procedures with a diverging number of steps. Combined with the broad penalty conditions developed above, this provides a unified route to consistency and asymptotic normality in high-dimensional estimating equations.

Appendix A
Penalties covered by our framework

Example A.1

(Lasso)
.

The
\ell_{1}
penalty
(
Tibshirani, 1996
)

p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\lambda_{n}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}
is not differentiable at
0
and the generalized gradient is given by
\partial p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})_{k}=\lambda_{n}\operatorname{sign}(\theta_{k})
, where
\operatorname{sign}(0)
is allowed to be any number in
[-1,1]
.

Example A.2

(Elastic Net)
.

The elastic net penalty
(
Zou and Hastie, 2005
)
is given by
p_{\lambda_{n,1},\lambda_{n,2}}(\bm{\theta})=\lambda_{n,1}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}+\lambda_{n,2}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}
.
It holds that
\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})_{k}=\lambda_{n,1}\operatorname{sign}(\theta_{k})+2\lambda_{n,2}\theta_{k}
.
For
\lambda_{n,1}>0
, the elastic net can induce sparsity.

Example A.3

(
\ell_{q}
penalty)
.

The
\ell_{q}
penalty
(
Frank and Friedman, 1993
)

p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\lambda_{n}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{q}^{q}

with
q\in(0,1]
is not differentiable at 0 and can therefore induce sparsity.
The
\ell_{q}
penalty is also not Lipschitz around
0
for
q<1
, but the definition of the generalized gradient can be extended to functions that are not locally Lipschitz, see
Clarke (1990, Section 2.4)
.
This leads to generalized gradients of the form
\partial p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})_{k}=\{\lambda_{n}q\operatorname{sign}(\theta_{k})|\theta_{k}|^{q-1}\}
for
\theta_{k}\neq 0
and
\partial p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})_{k}=\mathbb{R}
for
\theta_{k}=0
.
The
\ell_{q}
penalty with
q<1
is special in the sense that
\bm{\theta}=\bm{0}
is always a solution to the penalized estimating equation.

Example A.4

(SCAD)
.

The SCAD penalty (
smoothly clipped absolute deviation
)
(
Fan, 1997
,
Fan and Li, 2001
)
was designed to produce a penalized estimator that is unbiased for large parameters.
For a single parameter, the SCAD penalty and its derivative are defined as

\displaystyle p_{\lambda_{n}}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\lambda_{n}|\theta|&\hskip-12.0pt\text{if }|\theta|\leq\lambda_{n},\\
\frac{2a\lambda_{n}|\theta|-\theta^{2}-\lambda_{n}^{2}}{2(a-1)}&\hskip-12.0pt\text{if }\lambda_{n}<|\theta|\leq a\lambda_{n},\\
\frac{(a+1)\lambda_{n}^{2}}{2}&\hskip-12.0pt\text{if }|\theta|>a\lambda_{n},\end{cases}\ p^{\prime}_{\lambda_{n}}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\lambda_{n}\operatorname{sign}(\theta)&\hskip-12.0pt\text{if }|\theta|\leq\lambda_{n},\\
\frac{\operatorname{sign}(\theta)(a\lambda_{n}-|\theta|)}{(a-1)}&\hskip-12.0pt\text{if }\lambda_{n}<|\theta|\leq a\lambda_{n},\\
0&\hskip-12.0pt\text{if }|\theta|>a\lambda_{n},\end{cases}

for some fixed
a>2
.
For multiple parameters, the SCAD penalty is used componentwise as
p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}p_{\lambda_{n}}(\theta_{k})
.
Around the origin, SCAD coincides with the Lasso penalty, so it can induce sparsity.
Since the derivative
p^{\prime}_{\lambda_{n}}(\theta)
is zero for all
|\theta|\geq a\lambda_{n}
, it leads to an unbiased estimating equation for large parameters.

Example A.5

(MCP)
.

A penalty that is unbiased for large coefficients (i.e.,
p^{\prime}_{\lambda_{n}}(|\theta|)=0
for
|\theta|\geq a\lambda_{n}
with some
a>0
) and induces sparsity (e.g.,
\lim_{\theta\downarrow 0}p^{\prime}_{\lambda_{n}}(\theta)=\lambda_{n}
) must be nonconvex.
The
minimax concave penalty
(MCP)
(
Zhang, 2010
)
is the “most convex” penalty among the penalties satisfying unbiasedness and sparsity, i.e., it minimizes the maximum concavity. For a single parameter, it is given by

\displaystyle p_{\lambda_{n}}(\theta)=\mathds{1}\{|\theta|\leq a\lambda_{n}\}\left(\lambda_{n}|\theta|-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2a}\right)+\mathds{1}\{|\theta|>a\lambda_{n}\}\frac{a\lambda_{n}^{2}}{2},

with derivative

p^{\prime}_{\lambda_{n}}(\theta)=\mathds{1}\{|\theta|\leq a\lambda_{n}\}\operatorname{sign}(\theta)\frac{(a\lambda_{n}-|\theta|)}{a}

for some fixed
a>0

(
Fan and Lv, 2010
)
. The penalty shares a similar behavior to SCAD in that it coincides with the Lasso around
\theta=0
and yields an unbiased estimating equations for
|\theta|
large.

Example A.6

(Fusion penalty)
.

The aim of a
fusion penalty
is to reduce the number of different coefficients, which is of particular interest for categorical data
(
Tibshirani et al., 2005
)
.
This is achieved by penalizing differences between coefficients, e.g.,
p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\lambda_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}-1}|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}|
.
This penalty fits within our framework via reparametrization, i.e., by defining
\beta_{1}=\theta_{1}
and
\beta_{k}=\theta_{k}-\theta_{k-1}
and adapting the estimation function
\phi
accordingly.
Then, zero-entries of
\bm{\beta}
correspond to parameters being “fused”.

Example A.7

(Group Lasso, non-overlapping groups)
.

The Group Lasso penalty
(
Yuan and Lin, 2006
)
is given by
p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{g=1}^{K_{n}}\lambda_{n,g}\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\|_{2}
with a partition
G_{1},\ldots,G_{K_{n}}
of
\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
.
In this case,
\bm{\theta}_{(2)}
contains all
\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}
with
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{G_{g}}=\bm{0}
.
The generalized gradient is given by

\displaystyle\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})_{G_{g}}=\mathds{1}\{\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\neq\bm{0}\}\,\lambda_{n,g}\frac{\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}}{\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\|_{2}}+\mathds{1}\{\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}=\bm{0}\}\,B(\bm{0}_{|G_{g}|},\lambda_{n,g})\quad\text{for all }g=1,\ldots,K_{n}.

Note that the Group Lasso contains the
\ell_{2}
norm penalty
p_{\lambda_{n}}(\bm{\theta})=\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}
as a special case with one single group
G_{1}=\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
.
In this case, the penalty only induces sparsity if
\bm{\theta}^{*}=\bm{0}
.

Example A.8

(Group Lasso, overlapping groups)
.

One may also use the Group Lasso with overlapping groups
G_{1},\ldots,G_{K_{n}}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
.
In this case,
\bm{\theta}_{(2)}
contains all
\bm{\theta}_{k}
belonging to at least one group
G_{g}
with
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{G_{g}}=\bm{0}
.
The generalized gradient is the sum of valid generalized gradients of Group Lasso with non-overlapping groups, i.e,

\bm{z}\in\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{\theta})\iff\bm{z}=\sum_{g=1}^{K_{n}}\bm{z}^{(g)},\text{ where }\bm{z}^{(g)}\in\partial f^{(g)}(\bm{\theta})\text{ for all }g=1,\ldots,K_{n},

where
f^{(g)}(\bm{\theta})\coloneq\lambda_{n,g}\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\|_{2}
.
This generalized gradient also satisfies Assumption
(P2)
.

Example A.9

(Group SCAD)
.

Another group penalty of the form
p_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}=\sum_{g=1}^{K_{n}}p_{\lambda_{n,k}}(\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\|_{2})
is the Group SCAD
(
Wang et al., 2007
,
Guo et al., 2015
)
, where
p_{\lambda_{n,k}}(\theta)
denotes the SCAD penalty defined above.
For
\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\|
small, Group SCAD coincides with Group Lasso, while for
\|\bm{\theta}_{G_{g}}\|>a\lambda_{n,g}
, the generalized gradient is
\bm{0}
, leading to unbiased estimates.

A similar extension of MCP to Group MCP
(
Breheny and Huang, 2009
)
is also possible.

Except for Group Lasso and Group SCAD, all presented penalties are coordinate-separable and can also be used with a vector of tuning parameters
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
, i.e., parameter specific
\lambda_{n,k}
.

B.1
Proof of

We first show that the sets

From an extension of the intermediate value theorem in

\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}\langle r_{n}C\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C\bm{u})\rangle\leq 0

\bm{\hat{\theta}}

We show that by choosing

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:th1proof}(r_{n}C)^{-1}\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C\bm{u})\rangle\leq-c<0

(27)

holds for some

becomes arbitrarily close to

We have

\displaystyle\quad(r_{n}C)^{-1}\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C\bm{u})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq(r_{n}C)^{-1}\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})\rangle+(r_{n}C)^{-1}\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}[\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C\bm{u})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})]\rangle.

The first term is of order

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Th1dep}\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}|\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})\rangle|=\|\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})\|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(I(\bm{\theta}^{*}))})=O_{p}(r_{n})

(28)

by
Lemma

7

Choosing

1

It holds that

\displaystyle(r_{n}C)^{-1}\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}[\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C\bm{u})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})]\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{n}[\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}\bm{u}]

(29)

\displaystyle=P[\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}\bm{u}]+(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)[\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}\bm{u}],

(A1)
(A1)(i)

(r_{n}C)^{-1}\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}[\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C\bm{u})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})]\rangle\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})]\right)+\|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)H_{n}\|.

(A1)(ii)

\lambda_{\max}(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})])\leq-c

9

Then, we can write
\bm{\hat{\theta}}=\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C_{n}\bm{\hat{u}}

It holds that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(r_{n}C_{n})^{-1}\langle\bm{\hat{u}},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C_{n}\bm{\hat{u}})\rangle\leq\sup_{\|\bm{u}\|=1}(r_{n}C_{n})^{-1}\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*}+r_{n}C_{n}\bm{u})\rangle

where the second inequality follows from the above arguments.
Hence,

B.2
Proof of
Theorem 2

The claim is trivial when no solution exists. Otherwise, let
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
be any solution to the estimating equation (
1

Theorem

\|\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\leq r_{n}C
for some
C\in(0,\infty)
and
n
large enough.
Suppose there is another solution
\bm{\hat{\theta}}+\bm{u}

By the strengthened assumption (
4

\displaystyle\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}}+\bm{u})\rangle=\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}}+\bm{u})\rangle-\langle\bm{u},\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{n}[\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=P[\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}\bm{u}]+(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)[\bm{u}^{\top}H_{n}\bm{u}]

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{u}\|^{2}(-c+o_{p}(1)),

uniformly on the set

B.3
Proof of
Theorem 3

We have

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})=\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})+\mathbb{P}_{n}[\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})+P[\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})]+(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)[\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})+\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\hat{\theta}})(\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})+(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)[\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})],

where
\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}J(\bm{\theta}^{*}+t(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}))dt
.
Rearranging and adding terms gives

-J(\bm{\theta}^{*})(\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})=\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})+[\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\hat{\theta}})-J(\bm{\theta}^{*})](\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})+(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)[\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})]

and

\displaystyle-\sqrt{n}A_{n}J(\bm{\theta}^{*})(\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})=

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}A_{n}\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})

\displaystyle+\sqrt{n}A_{n}[\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\hat{\theta}})-J(\bm{\theta}^{*})](\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})

(30)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{n}A_{n}(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)[\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})].

The second and the third term are negligible, since

\sqrt{n}A_{n}[\overline{J}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\hat{\theta}})-J(\bm{\theta}^{*})](\bm{\hat{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})=o_{p}\left(\sqrt{n}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}r_{n}}\,r_{n}\right)=o_{p}(1)

by assumption
(A2)
, and
Lemma

12
yields

\sqrt{n}A_{n}(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)[\phi(\bm{\hat{\theta}})-\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})]=o_{p}(1).

It remains to show a central limit theorem for

\sqrt{n}A_{n}\mathbb{P}_{n}\phi(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}A_{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{Y}_{i}.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\bm{Y}_{i}\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\lVert\bm{Y}_{i}\rVert>\varepsilon\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\bm{Y}_{i}\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\lVert\bm{Y}_{i}\rVert>\varepsilon\}\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\bm{Y}_{i}\rVert^{4}\right]/\varepsilon^{2},

and
\mathbb{E}[\lVert\bm{Y}_{i}\rVert^{4}]=n^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|A_{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\|^{4}]=o(n^{-1})
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, by
(A3)
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\bm{Y}_{i}\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\lVert\bm{Y}_{i}\rVert>\varepsilon\}\right]\to 0\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}_{i}]=\bm{0}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{Y}_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Cov}[A_{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})]=\frac{1}{n}A_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Cov}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})]A_{n}^{\top}=A_{n}I(\bm{\theta}^{*})A_{n}^{\top}\to\Sigma,

the conditions of the Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem
(

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{L_{N}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|f(\bm{X}_{i})-f(\bm{X}_{i}^{*})\right|+\frac{1}{n_{N}}\sum_{i=L_{N}+1}^{n_{N}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|f(\bm{X}_{i})-f(\bm{X}_{i}^{*})\right|\coloneqq T_{1}+T_{2}.

For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{1}\neq 0)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{L_{N}}\{\bm{X}_{i}^{*}\neq\bm{X}_{i}\}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L_{N}}\mathbb{P}(\bm{X}_{i}^{*}\neq\bm{X}_{i})\leq L_{N}\delta_{N}=o(1).

For the second term, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{2}>\varepsilon_{N}/n_{N})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=L_{N}+1}^{n_{N}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|f(\bm{X}_{i})-f(\bm{X}_{i}^{*})\right|>\varepsilon_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=L_{N}+1}^{n_{N}}\left\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f(\bm{X}_{i})|>\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{n_{N}-L_{N}}\right\}\cup\left\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f(\bm{X}_{i}^{*})|>\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{n_{N}-L_{N}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=L_{N}+1}^{n_{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f(\bm{X}_{i})|>\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{n_{N}-L_{N}}\right)=o(1).

Together, this gives that
T_{1}=0
with probability
\to 1
and
T_{2}=o_{p}(\varepsilon_{N}/n_{N})
, and hence the claim follows.
∎
