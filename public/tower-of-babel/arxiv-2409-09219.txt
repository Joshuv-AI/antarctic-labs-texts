Transition Threshold for Strictly Monotone Shear Flows in Sobolev Spaces
Rajendra Beekie, Siming He

Abstract
. We study the stability of spectrally stable, strictly monotone, and smooth shear flows in the 2D Navier-Stokes equations on 𝕋 × ℝ \mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R} with small viscosity ν \nu . We establish nonlinear stability in H s H^{s} for s ≥ 2 s\geq 2 with a threshold of size ϵ ​ ν 1 / 3 \epsilon\nu^{1/3} for time smaller than c ∗ ​ ν − 1 c_{*}\nu^{-1} with ϵ , c ∗ ≪ 1 \epsilon,c_{*}\ll 1 . Additionally, we demonstrate nonlinear inviscid damping and enhanced dissipation.



1.
Introduction

Consider the 2D incompressible Navier-Stokes equations

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla v-\nu\Delta v+\nabla p=0,\\
&\quad\nabla\cdot v=0,\\
&v(t=0)=v_{\rm in},\end{cases}

on the domain
(t,x,y)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
where
\nu>0
is the viscosity (or inverse Reynolds number),
p
the pressure, and
v_{\rm in}
the initial data. An important class of solutions of (
1.1
) are shear flows of the form
v(t,x,y)=(b(t,y),0)
where
b(t,y)
denotes a solution of the equation

(1.2)

\partial_{t}b(t,y)-\nu\partial_{y}^{2}b(t,y)=0,\quad b(0,y)=b(y).

We are interested in the stability of monotone shears, namely
\partial_{y}b(y)>0
, which is preserved for
b(t,y)
. See Assumption
1.1
and the following remarks for a more detailed discussion of the assumptions we have on
b(y)
. To study the stability of these shears, we consider solutions of (
1.1
) of the form

(1.3)

\displaystyle v(t,x,y)=(b(t,y),0)+u(t,x,y),

where the perturbation
u(t,x,y)=(u^{1}(t,x,y),u^{2}(t,x,y))
satisfies

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}u+b(t,y)\partial_{x}u+(\partial_{y}b(t,y)u^{2},0)-\nu\Delta u+\nabla p=0,\\
&\quad\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
&u(0,x,y)=u_{\rm in}(x,y),\end{cases}

and
u_{\rm in}(x,y)
is small in a sense to be made precise in Theorem
1.1
. It is convenient to work with the perturbation vorticity
\omega:=-\partial_{y}u^{1}+\partial_{x}u^{2}
which satisfies the equation

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}\omega+b(t,y)\partial_{x}\omega-b^{\prime\prime}(t,y)\partial_{x}\psi-\nu\Delta\omega+\nabla^{\perp}\psi\cdot\nabla\omega=0,\\
&\quad\Delta\psi=\omega,\\
&\omega(0,x,y)=\omega_{\rm in}(x,y),\end{cases}

where
\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{y},\partial_{x})
. To solve for the stream function
\psi
, we impose that
\psi
has zero mean over
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
. We can recover
u
from
\omega
via the Biot-Savart law
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega
.

1.1.
Statement and Discussion of Main Result

The main focus of this work is to precisely study the stability properties of (
1.5
) as
\nu\to 0
. Specifically, we are interested in the following stability threshold problem: Given a function space
X
, determine the minimal
\beta(X)
such that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\norm{\omega_{\rm in}}_{X}\ll\nu^{\beta}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\text{ asymptotic stability},

(1.7)

\displaystyle\norm{\omega_{\rm in}}_{X}\gtrsim\nu^{\beta}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\text{ instability}.

The motivation for the formulation of (
1.6
) comes from the problem of the “subcritical transition”
[
4
]

where in experiments, instabilities are observed at a lower Reynolds number than the linear theory predicts. In
[
18
]
, Kelvin suggested that the fluid may be increasingly sensitive to disturbances at higher Reynolds numbers, which is a qualitative version of (
1.6
). In the special case of Couette flow
b(t,y)=y
, there has been a significant amount of work done on the stability threshold problem. In
[
28
]
, it was shown that Couette flow is nonlinearly stable for all Reynolds numbers (with the allowable size of the perturbation depending on the Reynolds number). In
[
5
]
, stability independent of Reynolds number was proven for
X
being Gevrey-
s
with
s\in[1,2)
. At finite regularity, for
X=H^{N}
with
N>1
, the threshold
\beta=1/2
was established for Couette and shears close to Couette. The current state-of-the-art for Couette is summarized in the following table:

•

[
24
]
:
\norm{\omega_{\rm in}}_{H_{x}^{log}L_{y}^{2}}\leq\epsilon\nu^{1/2}\longrightarrow\text{ asymptotic stability;}

•

[
25
]
:
\norm{\omega_{\rm in}}_{H^{N}}\leq\epsilon\nu^{1/3},\,N\geq 40\longrightarrow\text{ asymptotic stability;}

•

[
21
]
:
\norm{\omega_{\rm in}}_{H_{x}^{1}L_{y}^{2}}\geq M\nu^{1/2-\delta}\longrightarrow\text{ instability, $M$ independent of
}\nu\text{ and any }\delta>0;

•

[
30
]
:
\norm{\omega_{\rm in}}_{H^{N}}\leq\epsilon\nu^{1/3},\,N\geq 2\longrightarrow\text{ asymptotic stability;}

•

[
20
]
:
\norm{\omega_{\rm in}}_{\mathcal{G}^{s}}\leq\epsilon\nu^{\beta}\longrightarrow\text{ asymptotic stability}
with
s\in\left[1,\frac{2-3\beta}{1-3\beta}\right],\ \beta\in[0,1/3]
;

•

[
10
]
:
\norm{\omega_{in}}_{\mathcal{G}^{s}}\leq\epsilon\longrightarrow\text{ instability}
with
s\in(2,\infty).

For general monotonic shear flows, there are significantly fewer results. The key issue is the presence of
\partial_{y}^{2}b(t,y)\partial_{x}\psi
in (
1.5
) which can lead to unstable eigenvalues
[
23
]
and at a technical level breaks the transport-diffusion structure that simplifies the analysis of Couette. We highlight the recent work
[
22
]
, which proved asymptotic stability in
H_{x}^{log}L_{y}^{2}
for perturbations of spectrally stable monotonic shear flows (see also
[
15
]
and
[
26
]
for proofs of nonlinear inviscid damping for spectrally stable monotonic shear flows). Our result relies on the linear analysis done in
[
17
]
(see also
[
8
]
for the case of a channel with non-slip boundary conditions). Following
[
17
]
, we state the main assumptions on
b(y)
.

Assumption 1.1
.

We assume that the shear flow
b(y)
satisfies the following conditions:

•

We normalize
b
so that
b(0,0)=0
.

•

For some
\sigma_{0}\in(0,1)
we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}b(y)\in[\sigma_{0},1/\sigma_{0}]\text{ for }y\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mathrm{support}\ \partial_{y}^{2}b\subseteq[-1/\sigma_{0},1/\sigma_{0}],\quad\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}e^{\sigma_{0}\langle\xi\rangle^{1/2}}|\widehat{b^{\prime\prime}}(\xi)|\leq 1/\sigma_{0};

•

The linearized operator
L_{k}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
defined for
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as

(1.9)

L_{k}g(y):=b(y)g(y)-b^{\prime\prime}(y)\varphi,\quad(\partial_{y}^{2}-k^{2})\varphi=g,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}

has purely continuous spectrum
\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 1.1
.

For all
t\geq 0
we have

(1.10)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}b(t,y)\in[\sigma_{0},1/\sigma_{0}]\text{ for }y\in\mathbb{R},\quad\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}e^{\sigma_{0}\langle\xi\rangle^{1/2}}|\widehat{b^{\prime\prime}}(t,\xi)|\leq 1/\sigma_{0}

as immediate consequences of
\partial_{y}b(t,y)
satisfying the heat equation.

Remark 1.2
.

The infinite regularity assumption is not necessary for Theorem
1.1
to hold as we will only be interested in finite regularity. Similarly, the assumption that
b^{\prime\prime}
is compactly supported
can be weakened to assuming that
b^{\prime\prime}
decays sufficiently fast as
|y|\to\infty
. The assumption that
L_{k}
has only continuous spectrum is essential. Finally, the assumption that
b^{\prime}(t,y)
has upper and lower bounds is extensively used throughout the linear and nonlinear analysis.

Before stating our main theorem, we introduce the projection operators

(1.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}f(t,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(t,x,y)dx,\quad\mathbb{P}_{\neq}f(t,x,y)=f(t,x,y)-\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}f(t,y).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
s\geq 2
, and suppose that the shear profile
b(t,y)
solves the equation

(1.12)

\partial_{t}b(t,y)-\nu\partial_{y}^{2}b(t,y)=0,\quad b(0,y)=b(y),

where the shear
b(y)
satisfies Assumption
1.1
. Then there exist
\epsilon_{0},\nu_{0},c_{*},\delta\in(0,1)
, which depend only on the initial shear profile
b(y)
, such that the following statements hold.
Assume that
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
,
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})
, and that the initial perturbation satisfies

(1.13)

\displaystyle\norm{\omega_{\rm in}}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}+\norm{\po u_{\rm in}^1}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\epsilon\nu^{1/3}.

Then the solution
(u,\omega)
of (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) exhibits the following three properties for
t\in[0,c_{*}\nu^{-1}]
:

(1)

Stability

(1.14)

\displaystyle\norm{\omega(t , x + tb(t,y), y )}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}+\norm{\po u^1 }_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\epsilon\nu^{1/3};

(2)

Enhanced Dissipation

(1.15)

\displaystyle\norm{{e^{\delta\nu^{1/3}|\pa_x|^{2/3}t }}\pn\omega(t , x + tb(t,y), y )}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\epsilon\nu^{1/3};

(3)

Inviscid Damping

(1.16)

\displaystyle\norm{ \pn u(t, x + tb(t,y), y)}_{L_{t}^{2}[0,c_{*}\nu^{-1}]H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\epsilon\nu^{1/3}.

Remark 1.3
.

A more detailed description of the perturbations is given in Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
.

Remark 1.4
.

The principal reason why Theorem
1.1
is not global in time is we only assume spectral stability for
b(t,y)
at

. For
t\gg\nu^{-1}
,
b(t,y)
may no longer be spectrally stable. This is also the reason why
[

]
needs to assume that
b(t,y)
is spectrally stable for

in order to obtain a global result.

Remark 1.5
.

The regularity in Theorem
1.1
matches the regularity obtained in
[
30
]
for Couette.

1.2.
Outline and Main Challenges

Recent literature identifies enhanced dissipation and inviscid damping as the two main mechanisms guaranteeing the stability of the shear flow.
These two phenomena can be easily pinpointed for a simplified linear model to (
1.5
), which is the passive scalar equation subject to the Couette flow
(y,0)
and small viscosity
\nu\leq 1
:

(1.17)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+y\partial_{x}f=\nu\Delta f,\quad f(t=0)=f_{\rm in},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}.

The above equation has been extensively studied since the work of Kelvin
[
18
]
and Orr
[
27
]
. If the ambient shear flow
b(t,y)=y
, then the linear part of the vorticity perturbation equation (
1.5
) reduces to that of (
1.17
). By taking the change of coordinate
z=x-ty,\,v=y
and set
F(t,z(x,y),v(y))=f(x,y)
, one ends up with the following equation

(1.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F(t,z,v)=\nu(\partial_{z}^{2}+(\partial_{v}-t\partial_{z})^{2})F(t,z,v).

Here, the Laplacian
\Delta
in the
(x,y)
-coordinate is transformed into a degenerate elliptic operator in the
(z,v)
-frame. By taking the Fourier transform
(z,v)\rightarrow(k,\eta)
, one ends up with

(1.19)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widehat{F}(t,k,\eta)=-\nu(|k|^{2}+|\eta-kt|^{2})\widehat{F}(t,k,\eta).

As long as the wave number
k\neq 0
, a direct integration in time yields that there exist universal constants
C\geq 1,\ \delta\in(0,1)
such that the following estimate holds

(1.20)

\displaystyle|\widehat{F}(t,k,\eta)|\leq C|\widehat{F}_{\rm in}(k,\eta)|\exp\{-\delta\nu^{1/3}t\},\quad k\neq 0,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

Hence, the solution decays with the rate
\mathcal{O}(\nu^{1/3})
, which is much larger than the classical heat dissipation rate,
\mathcal{O}(\nu)
as long as
\nu
is small. This accelerated dissipation phenomenon is called the
enhanced dissipation induced by shear flow advection
. Thanks to the heterogeneous nature of this phenomenon, we use the decomposition from (
1.11
).
It is worth highlighting that the enhanced dissipation phenomena and the inviscid damping phenomena only take effect on the “remainder” part of the solution
\mathbb{P}_{\neq}f.

There is extensive literature devoted to understanding the enhanced dissipation for passive scalar flows, and we refer interested readers to the classical work
[
2
]
and recent extensions
[
1
,
29
,
9
,
12
]
.

Thanks to the Biot-Savart law
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega
, the
L^{2}
norm of the velocity perturbation
\|u\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}
is equivalent to the
\dot{H}_{x,y}^{-1}
-norm of the solution
\omega
. For the passive scalar equation (
1.19
), the
\dot{H}^{-1}_{x,y}
-norm evolution of the solution has the following characterization

(1.21)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\mathbb{P}_{\neq}f\|_{\dot{H}_{x,y}^{-1}}^{2}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int\mathbb{P}_{\neq}f(-\Delta)^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}fdxdydt=\sum_{k\neq 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\widehat{F}(t,k,\eta)|^{2}}{|k|^{2}+|\eta-kt|^{2}}d\eta dt.

Now, thanks to the bound (
1.20
), we end up with a viscosity
\nu
independent estimate:

(1.22)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\mathbb{P}_{\neq}f\|_{\dot{H}_{x,y}^{-1}}^{2}dt\lesssim\sum_{k\neq 0}\frac{1}{|k|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|\widehat{F}_{\rm in}(k,\eta)|^{2}}{1+|t-\eta/k|^{2}}dtd\eta\leq\||\partial_{z}|^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}F_{\rm in}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since this
L^{2}_{t}H_{x,y}^{-1}
-estimate persists as
\nu\rightarrow 0
, it is called
inviscid damping
. By invoking higher regularity, one can derive sharp pointwise in time decay

(1.23)

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{\neq}f\|_{\dot{H}_{x,y}^{-1}}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{k\neq 0}\frac{1}{|k|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(1+|\eta|^{2})|\widehat{F}_{\rm in}(k,\eta)|^{2}}{(1+|\eta|^{2})(1+|t-\eta/k|^{2})}d\eta\lesssim\frac{\||\partial_{z}|^{-1}\partial_{v}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}F_{\rm in}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{1+t^{2}}.

Even though the inviscid damping decay is polynomial in time, it takes effect on a time scale that is much shorter than the enhanced dissipation time scale
\mathcal{O}(\nu^{-1/3})
. Hence, inviscid damping is crucial for stabilizing the motion of the fluid in the initial time layer. There is extensive literature on the inviscid damping of the Couette flow and other shear flows, and we refer the readers to the classical works
[
3
,
34
,
33
]
and extensions,
[
31
,
32
,
17
,
26
,
15
,
7
,
11
]
.

The goal of our work is to extend these simple observations on the passive scalar equation (
1.17
) to the general setting of (
1.5
). The main challenges that one faces are as follows:

•

The effect from the nonlocal term
b^{\prime\prime}\partial_{x}\psi
in (
1.5
). A key difficulty in extending the aforementioned estimates to the general strictly monotone shear flows arises from the linear nonlocal term
b^{\prime\prime}\partial_{x}\psi
. If the second derivative of the shear profile is small, i.e.,
\|b^{\prime\prime}\|\ll 1
, one can still carry out classical energy methods to recover the enhanced dissipation and inviscid damping estimates above. However, once
b^{\prime\prime}
becomes large, the linear nonlocal effect is no longer negligible, and the direct energy approach fails.

•

Elliptic estimates. To identify and employ the inviscid damping mechanism, detailed elliptic estimates of the stream function (or related quantities) are essential. We note that in the strictly monotone shear flow setting, the derivative of the shear profile
b^{\prime}
is only bounded and does not decay near spacial infinity. This will cause extra difficulties compared to the finite domain situations, e.g., channel
[
15
]
.

•

Commutator structures of the inviscid damping Fourier multipliers for the initial time layer. As we highlighted before, the inviscid damping effect plays a key role in stabilizing the dynamics of the fluid for
t\ll\nu^{-1/3}
. In the classical literature
[
34
,
6
]
, a norm induced by an inviscid damping multiplier (
\mathcal{W}_{I}
in (
3.1b
)) is utilized to derive the required estimate for the nonlinear system. However, our approach requires us to derive a nice commutator estimate of the multiplier for time
t\leq\nu^{-1/6}
.

To address these challenges, we utilize the following ideas.

•

The plan to address the nonlocal term is to invoke the delicate linear estimate derived in the paper
[
17
]
. To apply them in a nonlinear setting, we follow the idea in the paper
[
15
]
to decompose the solution
\omega
into the linear part and the auxiliary part. The linear part of the solution encodes the linear nonlocal effects in (
1.5
), and we can use the linear estimates in
[
17
]
to resolve the problems coming from the linear nonlocal term. For the auxiliary part, we rely on the Couette flow analysis in
[
30
]
.

•

To derive the elliptic estimates, we utilize a ‘local’ and ‘far-field’ decomposition of the Green’s functions associated with our elliptic operators, leveraging the fact that
b^{\prime\prime}(t,y)
converges to zero near spatial infinity. In the compact local region, we follow the approach in
[
15
]
, while in the far-field region, we treat the elliptic operator as a perturbation of a constant coefficient elliptic operator.

•

To address the last difficulty, we observe that the
\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{W}_{I}
has a nice structure, and one can handle nonzero mode interactions relatively easily. However, the interaction between the nonzero and zero mode is challenging to deal with. The problem mainly stems from the fact that one typically defines
\mathcal{W}_{I}(t,k=0,\eta)\equiv 1
. It turns out that for a short time
t\leq\nu^{-1/6}
, one can slightly adjust the zero-mode component of the multiplier (
3.1d
) and guarantee a nice commutator estimate (Lemma
C.4
).

Notation and Coordinate System

2.1.
Coordinate System

We consider the following change of variable on the time interval
[0,c_{*}\nu^{-1}]
:

(2.1)

\displaystyle z(t,x,y):=

\displaystyle x-t\ b(t,y),\quad v(t,y):=b(t,y).

We denote

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Omega(t,z(t,x,y),v(t,y))

\displaystyle:=\omega(t,x,y),

(2.3)

\displaystyle U(t,z(t,x,y),v(t,y))

\displaystyle:=u(t,x,y),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Psi(t,z(t,x,y),v(t,y))

\displaystyle:=\psi(t,x,y),

(2.5)

\displaystyle B(t,v(t,y))

\displaystyle:=\partial_{y}b(t,y),

(2.6)

\displaystyle B^{\prime}(t,v(t,y))

\displaystyle:=\partial_{yy}b(t,y).

Note that

(2.7)

\displaystyle B^{\prime}(t,v)=B(t,v)\partial_{v}B(t,v).

We will also use the notation

(2.8)

B_{0}(v):=B(0,v)=\partial_{y}b(b^{-1}(v)),\quad B_{0}^{\prime}(v):=B^{\prime}(0,v)=\partial_{y}^{2}b(b^{-1}(v)).

In what follows, we will drop the
(t,v)
dependence when writing
B,B_{0}
when there is no risk of confusion.
In the new coordinate system, we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\nabla_{L}:=(\partial_{z},\partial_{v}-t\partial_{z}),\quad\Delta_{L}:=\partial_{z}^{2}+(\partial_{v}-t\partial_{z})^{2},\\
&\partial_{z}F(t,z(t,x,y),v(t,y))=\partial_{x}f(t,x,y),\\
&\partial_{v}F(t,z(t,x,y),v(t,y))=\left(\frac{1}{v_{y}}\partial_{y}+t\partial_{x}\right)f(t,x,y),\\
&\nabla f(t,x,y)=\left(\partial_{z}F(t,z(t,x,y),v(t,y)),B(\partial_{v}-t\partial_{z})F(t,z(t,x,y),v(t,y))\right),\\
&\Delta f=\Delta_{t}F=(\partial_{z}^{2}+({B}(\partial_{v}-t\partial_{z}))^{2})F(t,z(t,x,y),v(t,y)).\end{cases}

In the new coordinate system, (
1.5
) can be written as

(2.10)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Omega-B^{\prime}\partial_{z}\Psi-\nu\tilde{\Delta}_{t}\Omega+B\nabla_{L}^{\perp}\Psi\cdot\nabla\Omega

\displaystyle=0,\quad\Delta_{t}\Psi=\Omega,

where

(2.11)

\tilde{\Delta}_{t}:=B^{2}(\partial_{v}-t\partial_{z})^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}.

We isolate the zero-mode of
\nabla_{L}^{\perp}\Psi
in the nonlinearity and write (
2.10
) as

(2.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Omega-B^{\prime}\partial_{z}\Delta_{t}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}\Omega-\nu\tilde{\Delta}_{t}\Omega-\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}U^{1}\partial_{z}\Omega+B\nabla_{L}^{\perp}\Delta_{t}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}\Omega\cdot\nabla_{L}\Omega=0,\quad-B\partial_{v}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}U^{1}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}\Omega.\quad

Following
[
15
]
, we introduce the operator

(2.13)

\Delta_{0}:=B_{0}^{2}(\partial_{v}-t\partial_{z})^{2}+B_{0}^{\prime}(\partial_{v}-t\partial_{z})+\partial_{z}^{2}

and decompose the solution
\Omega
as
\Omega=\Omega^{*}+F
where
F
(the “linear profile) will satisfy a nonhomogeneous linear equation with 0 initial data and
\Omega^{*}
(the “auxiliary profile”) will satisfy an equation that can be handled like the Couette case in
[
30
]
. More explicitly,
F
satisfies the equation

(2.14)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F-B_{0}^{\prime}\partial_{z}\Delta_{0}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}F-\nu\Delta_{0}F=B_{0}^{\prime}\partial_{z}\Delta_{0}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}\Omega^{*},\quad F(0,z,v)=0,

and
\Omega^{*}
satisfies the equation

(2.15)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}\Omega^{*}-\nu\tilde{\Delta}_{t}\Omega^{*}-\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}U^{1}\partial_{z}(F+\Omega^{*})+B\nabla_{L}^{\perp}\Delta_{t}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}(F+\Omega^{*})\cdot\nabla_{L}(F+\Omega^{*})\\
&\quad=(B^{\prime}-B_{0}^{\prime})\partial_{z}\Delta_{t}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}(F+\Omega^{*})+B_{0}^{\prime}(\Delta_{t}^{-1}-\Delta_{0}^{-1})\partial_{z}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}(F+\Omega^{*})+\nu(\tilde{\Delta}_{t}-\Delta_{0})F,\\
&-B\partial_{v}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}U^{1}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}\Omega^{*},\\
&\Omega^{*}(0,z,v)=\Omega(0,z,v).\end{cases}

The structure of (
2.15
) is complicated, so we briefly comment on the plan of attack. The first crucial observation is that the term
-B^{\prime}\partial_{z}\Delta_{0}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\neq}\Omega^{*}
is no longer in the auxiliary profile equation (
2.15
). Since
B^{\prime}
is not assumed to be small, its presence would have ruined any chances of doing direct energy estimates. Instead, we use the evolution of the “linear profile”
F
to balance this potentially harmful nonlocal term, which relies on the linear theory developed in
[
17
]
. The right-hand side of (
2.15
) can be made small by taking
\nu t
sufficiently small, which allows us to estimate it directly. Using the bootstrap assumptions on the “auxiliary profile”
\Omega^{*}
in combination with the linear theory for (
2.14
), we can get bounds for the linear profile
F
, which implies control on the profile of the full vorticity perturbation
\Omega=F+\Omega^{*}
. Finally, to control the velocity
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}U^{1}
, we directly use the velocity perturbation equation to prove
L^{2}
control on
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbbm{o}}U^{1}
(see Section
5.7
).

3.
Bootstrap and Key Linear Estimates

In order to introduce the functional setup to analyze the system (
2.14
), (
2.15
), we apply the spacial Fourier transform
f(t,z,v)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\widehat{f}(t,k,\eta)
. Next, we introduce the following multipliers:

a) Enhanced Dissipation Multiplier:

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{W}_{\nu}(t,k,\eta):=\pi-\arctan\left(\frac{\nu^{1/3}|k|^{2/3}}{{K}}\left(t-\frac{\eta}{k}\right)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{0<|k|\leq\nu^{-1/2}};

b) Inviscid Damping Multiplier:

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{W}_{I}(t,k,\eta):=\pi-\arctan\left(\frac{1}{{K}}\left(t-\frac{\eta}{k}\right)\right){\mathbbm{1}_{k\neq 0}};

c) Echo Multiplier:

(3.1c)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{W}_{E}(t,k,\eta):=\pi+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{1}{|\ell|^{2}}\left(\frac{\ell}{|\ell|}\arctan\left(\frac{1}{{K}}\left(\frac{\eta-\ell t}{1+|k-\ell|+|\ell|}\right)\right)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{{0\leq}|k|<\nu^{-1/2}}.

Here, the parameter
K\geq 1
is a constant that depends on the shear profile
B(t,v)
(
\|B^{\prime}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}H_{v}^{s}}+\|B\|_{L_{t,v}^{\infty}}+\|B^{-1}\|_{L_{t,v}^{\infty}}
) and will be chosen later in the proof. The
\mathcal{W}_{\nu}
is the multiplier that captures the enhanced dissipation, the
\mathcal{W}_{I}
multiplier captures the inviscid damping, and the
\mathcal{W}_{E}
multiplier controls the echo cascade. We note that the multipliers
\mathcal{W}_{\nu}\ \eqref{W_nu},\,\mathcal{W}_{I}
(
3.1b
), and
\mathcal{W}_{E}
(
3.1c
) are bounded and take values in
[\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}]
, and hence the norms they induce are equivalent to the
L^{2}
-based Sobolev norms. These multipliers have their origin in
[
6
]
,
[
30
]
. We remark that our multipliers are slightly different from those of
[
30
]
in the sense that the
\mathcal{W}_{\nu}
has frequency cutoff
0<|k|\leq\nu^{-1/2}
, and
\mathcal{W}_{I}
has support on all the nonzero modes. The echo multiplier
\mathcal{W}_{E}
is identical to the one in
[
30
]
. In order to obtain a nice commutator structure for the short time
t\in[0,\nu^{-1/6}]
, we further define,

(3.1d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{I}^{\circ}(t,0,\eta):=\pi+\arctan\left(\frac{1}{K}\frac{\eta-t/2}{1/2}\right),\quad t\leq\nu^{-1/6}.

We define

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(t,k,\eta):=\begin{cases}\mathbbm{1}_{k=0}\mathcal{W}_{I}^{\circ}(t,\eta)+\mathbbm{1}_{k\neq 0}\mathcal{W}_{I}(t,k,\eta),&\forall t\in[0,\nu^{-1/6}];\\
\mathcal{W}_{\nu}\mathcal{W}_{I}\mathcal{W}_{E}(t,k,\eta),&\forall t\in[\nu^{-1/6},\infty).\end{cases}

We slightly abuse the notation in the sense that at
t=\nu^{-1/6}
, the two definitions of
\mathfrak{M}
do not match. However, it will be clear from the context which multiplier we are considering.
To capture the
|k|
-dependent enhanced dissipation, we introduce the Fourier multiplier

(3.3)

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(t):=\mathbbm{1}_{k\neq 0}\exp\left\{\delta\nu^{1/3}(|k|^{2/3}+1)t\right\}+\mathbbm{1}_{k=0};\quad(\zeta(t,|\partial_{z}|)f(t,z,v))^{\wedge}=\zeta_{k}(t)\widehat{f}(t,k,\eta).

The
\zeta_{k}
weight has the following estimate:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\delta\nu^{1/3}(|k-\ell|^{2/3}+|\ell|^{2/3}+1)t\right\}\leq\begin{cases}\zeta_{\ell}(t)\zeta_{k-\ell}(t)\exp\{-\delta\nu^{1/3}t\},\,&(k-\ell)\ell\neq 0;\\
\zeta_{\ell}(t)\zeta_{k-\ell}(t),\,&(k-\ell)\ell=0.\end{cases}

Further, define that for
s\geq 2
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle A(t,k,\eta):=\zeta_{k}(t)\mathfrak{M}(t,k,\eta)\langle k,\eta\rangle^{s},\quad\widetilde{A}(t,k,\eta):=\mathfrak{M}(t,k,\eta)\langle k,\eta\rangle^{s}.

Here, the constant
0<\delta<1
will be chosen along the proof and will be independent of the
\nu
and the solution
\Omega^{*},F
. We observe that these multiplier functions (
3.1
) are monotonically decreasing in time. Hence, when we take time derivatives of the norm induced by these Fourier multipliers, we obtain multiple negative terms, which are referred to as the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya terms (
CK
-terms). These damping terms are defined as follows:

(3.6)

\displaystyle CK_{\iota}[H]:=\left\|A\sqrt{\frac{-\partial_{t}\mathcal{W}_{\iota}}{\mathcal{W}_{\iota}}}H\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\iota\in\{\nu,I,E\}.

Here
H
is an arbitrary
H^{s}
-function.
The properties of these multipliers are collected in the appendix. Next, we are ready to lay out the bootstrap argument.

Short time:
t\leq\nu^{-1/6}
.
We assume that

and

Lemma 4.1
.

\displaystyle\norm{B(t)}_{L_{v}^{\infty}}+\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}e^{\theta_{0}\langle\xi\rangle^{1/2}}|\widehat{\partial_{v}B}(t,\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq 1/\theta_{0},

(4.2b)

\displaystyle B(t,v)

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{0}.

A key technical difficulty is dealing with commuting various Fourier multipliers past multiplication by very smooth, but not necessarily decaying, coefficients. In the following, we record various lemmas that we will frequently use throughout the paper.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

,

, and
\mathfrak{C}

\displaystyle\norm{ \lb\p_z, \p_v \rb^s (\mathfrak{C} f) }_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{\mathfrak{C}}\norm{\lb\p_z, \p_v \rb^sf}_{L^{2}}.

Moreover, if

additionally satisfies (

)
then we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\norm{ \lb\p_z, \p_v \rb^s (\mathfrak{C} f) }_{L^{2}}\approx_{{}_{\mathfrak{C}}}\norm{\lb\p_z, \p_v \rb^sf}_{L^{2}}.

Lemma 4.3

Let

and

\displaystyle\norm{ \lb k, \p_v \rb^s [\mathfrak{C}, (-\Delta_L)^{-1/2} ] \pn f }_{L^{2}}\lesssim\norm{\p_v \mathfrak{C}}_{W^{\lceil s\rceil+3,1}}\norm{ \lb k, \p_v \rb^s (-\Delta_L)^{-1/2} \pn f }_{L^{2}},

4.2.
Elliptic Lemmas

Assume that
\varphi^{\prime},X^{\prime}:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are
C^{2}
functions satisfying

(4.8)

(\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi^{\prime}=X^{\prime},\quad\varphi^{\prime}\to 0,\quad|y|\to\infty.

Taking the Fourier transform in
x
, we can write (
4.8
) as

(4.9)

\displaystyle(\partial_{y}^{2}-k^{2})\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(y)=X_{k}^{\prime}(y)

where

(4.10)

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\varphi^{\prime}(x,y)e^{-ikx}\,dx,

(4.11)

\displaystyle X_{k}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}X^{\prime}(x,y)e^{-ikx}\,dx.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
we can solve for
\varphi_{k}^{\prime}
via the relationship

(4.12)

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(y)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-|k||y-z|}}{|k|}X_{k}^{\prime}(z)\,dz.

A key technical difficulty not present in the Couette case is that in the new variables from (
2.1
), the operator
\Delta_{0}
is no longer constant coefficient and does not have a convenient representation as a Fourier multiplier. The next lemma shows that the coefficients do not change the ellipticity properties from the linear case too drastically.

Lemma 4.6
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{C}(t,v)
satisfies (
4.2a
) and that
X_{k}\in C([0,T],H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. Let
\varphi_{k}\in C([0,T],H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
denote the solution to the equation

(4.13)

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\varphi_{k}=B_{0}^{2}(\partial_{v}-ikt)^{2}\varphi_{k}+B_{0}^{\prime}(\partial_{v}-ikt)\varphi_{k}-k^{2}\varphi_{k}=X_{k}(t,v).

Then for
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
M\in\{1,(k^{2}+(\xi-kt)^{2})^{-1/2}\}
and
s\geq 0
, we have that

(4.14)

\displaystyle\left\|\langle k,\xi\rangle^{s}M(t,k,\xi)\mathbb{P}_{\neq}(\Delta_{L}(\mathfrak{C}\varphi_{k}))^{\wedge}(t,\xi)\right\|_{L_{\xi}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\left\|\langle k,\xi\rangle^{s}M(t,k,\xi)\mathbb{P}_{\neq}\widehat{X_{k}}(t,\xi)\right\|_{L_{\xi}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

Proof.

We will closely follow the proof of Lemma 4.5 in
[
15
]
. The proof when
M(t,k,\xi)=(k^{2}+(\xi-kt)^{2})^{-1/2}
is harder so we will only provide the details in that case.
Define

(4.15)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t,x,y):=\varphi(t,x-tb(y),b(y)),\quad X^{\prime}(t,x,y):=X(t,x-tb(y),b(y)),

which implies

(4.16)

\displaystyle e^{-itkb(y)}\varphi_{k}(t,b(y))=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-|k||y-y^{\prime}|}}{|k|}e^{-itkb(y^{\prime})}X_{k}(t,b(y^{\prime}))dy^{\prime}.

Letting
(v,w)=(b(y),b(y^{\prime}))
, and defining

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}(b(y),b(y^{\prime})):=\frac{e^{-|k||y-y^{\prime}|}}{|k|},

we arrive at

(4.18)

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(t,v)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}X_{k}(t,w)e^{ikt(v-w)}\mathcal{G}_{k}(v,w)(1/B(w))dw.

Multiplying (
4.18
) by
\mathfrak{C}
and using the decomposition (
A.2
) for
\mathcal{G}_{k}(v,w)
from Lemma
A.1
gives

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(v)\varphi_{k}(t,v)=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}X_{k}(t,w)e^{ikt(v-w)}\mathcal{G}_{1,k}(v-w)\mathfrak{C}(w)\chi(w)(1/B(w))dw

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}X_{k}(t,w)e^{ikt(v-w)}(\mathfrak{C}(v)-\mathfrak{C}(w))\mathcal{G}_{1,k}(v-w)\chi(w)(1/B(w))dw

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}X_{k}(t,w)e^{ikt(v-w)}\mathcal{G}_{2,k}(v,w)\mathfrak{C}(v)(1/B(w))dw.

Taking the Fourier transform with respect to
v
yields

(4.20)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathfrak{C}\varphi}(t,k,\xi)

\displaystyle=-\widehat{\mathcal{G}_{1}}(\xi-kt)\widehat{\mathcal{C}X}(t,k,\xi)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{X}(t,k,\eta)H(\xi-kt,kt-\eta)\,d\eta

where
\mathcal{C}(w):=\mathfrak{C}(w)\chi(w)(1/B(w))
and

(4.21)

\displaystyle H(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\mathfrak{C}(v)-\mathfrak{C}(w))\mathcal{G}_{1}(v-w)\chi(w)(1/B(w))e^{-i\alpha v-i\beta w}dvdw

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{G}_{2}(v,w)\mathfrak{C}(v)(1/B(w))e^{-i\alpha v-i\beta w}dvdw.

By Lemma
A.1
and Lemma
4.3
, we have

(4.22)

\displaystyle|H(\alpha,\beta)|\lesssim\frac{1}{(k^{2}+\alpha^{2})\langle\alpha+\beta\rangle^{\lceil s\rceil+2}}.

Recalling that we are assuming
M(k,\xi,t)=(k^{2}+(\xi-kt)^{2})^{-1/2}
, (
4.20
