Vaginal Microbiota and Cytokines Among Pregnant and Breastfeeding Women in Clinical Trials of the Dapivirine Vaginal Ring and Oral Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate/Emtricitabine
Authors: Melinda A B Petrina, Tracy Q Dong, May Beamer, Katherine Bunge, Lee Fairlie, Felix Mhlanga, Jennifer E Balkus, Lisa M Noguchi, Jeanna Piper, Elizabeth R Brown, Sharon L Hillier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaginal dysbiosis is associated with HIV acquisition and preterm delivery. Our objective was to evaluate prevalent vaginal infections, and changes in microbiota and cytokines among pregnant and breastfeeding women from 4 African countries using dapivirine vaginal ring (DVR) or oral tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine (TDF/FTC) in 2 parallel nonoverlapping trials.MethodsMTN-042 (NCT03965923) was a randomized trial evaluating daily TDF/FTC or monthly DVR during pregnancy (n = 558), and MTN-043 (NCT04140266) was a randomized study evaluating TDF/FTC or DVR during breastfeeding (n = 197). Women were evaluated for vaginal infections at enrollment and follow-up using Nugent score and BD Max™ Vaginal Panel. Microbiota was evaluated by quantitative PCR for 12 bacterial targets; 5 cytokines and RANTES were evaluated by MAGPIX. Rates of change in vaginal microbiota and cytokines and prevalence of vaginal infections were evaluated using generalized linear mixed models and Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)-adjusted P values.ResultsVaginal infections, microbiota and cytokines did not differ between the 2 study arms at baseline. Lactobacillus crispatus was less frequently detected postpartum and during breastfeeding compared with pregnancy. Bacterial vaginosis (BV) and Candida were common during pregnancy and breastfeeding, but there was no significant difference in the rate of change in these infections by study arm. BV-associated microbiota and cytokines changed over pregnancy and postpartum, but the rate of change by study arm did not differ.ConclusionsVaginal infections, microbiota or cytokines/chemokines changes were similar in women using DVR or TDF/FTC. These data augment the robust safety profile for DVR to support its use during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

In 2023, 44% of new HIV-1 infections were among females [1]. In sub-Saharan Africa, females accounted for 62% of all new HIV infections [1], with biological, social, and structural factors contributing to increased vulnerability during pregnancy, postpartum [2], and breastfeeding [3]. Several products have demonstrated safety and efficacy for HIV prevention. Daily use of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine (TDF/FTC), an oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (OP) product, is an effective tool for HIV prevention [4]. Dapivirine vaginal ring (DVR) is also a safe, acceptable, and well-tolerated method for prevention of HIV [5, 6] and is registered for use in 10 African countries with more approvals pending (Anita Garg, Pop Council, personal communication). The DVR received a positive opinion by the European Medicine Agency in 2020 for HIV prevention [7] and approved an expansion of its label indication to include breastfeeding women in 2024. The DVR has been recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an additional HIV prevention option for women at substantial risk of infection [8] with expanded use for women at 16 years of age in 2025. Cabotegravir has also been proven to prevent HIV in women [9] and evidence suggests it is safe for use during pregnancy [10].

HIV prevention is important for pregnant and breastfeeding women, not only for their own health but to prevent HIV transmission to their infants. Out of the 120 000 children under the age of 5 who were infected with HIV, 44% of these infections occurred during breastfeeding [11]. African women are particularly vulnerable during pregnancy and breastfeeding when they are 3- to 4-fold more likely to acquire HIV [12]. For women living in high HIV prevalence areas where birth rates are high and breastfeeding is the norm, periods of pregnancy and breastfeeding represent a significant portion of their reproductive years.

Vaginal dysbiosis including bacterial vaginosis (BV) has been consistently associated with HIV acquisition [13–16]. The vaginal microbiota in BV is heterogeneous and no single species or vaginal community type has been detected in all women with BV [17]. A secondary analysis of samples from 150 nonpregnant women who acquired HIV compared with 436 women who remained HIV-negative reported 14 bacterial taxa associated with an increased risk of HIV acquisition whereas L. crispatus was the only bacterial taxa negatively correlated with HIV acquisition [18]. McClelland et al reported an increased risk of HIV-1 acquisition associated with 7 bacterial taxa including Sneathia [16] and Umbribacter vaginalis (Eggerthella-like species type 1) [16, 19]. Vaginal dysbiosis in pregnancy is a concern since non-L. crispatus dominant microbiomes enhance risk for HIV acquisition and preterm birth (PTB) [20, 21]. Trichomonas vaginalis (TV) has also been associated with preterm birth and genital inflammation [22].

Soluble immune mediators indicative of genital inflammation are associated with increased HIV acquisition risk [23–25]. Srinivasan et al reported that interleukin 1 alpha (IL-1α) interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin 17 (IL-17), induced protein 10 (IP-10), RANTES, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) were associated with increased HIV acquisition [18]. In a cross-sectional analysis of Kenyan women, there was an association between vaginal dysbiosis and higher concentrations of TNF-α and IL-1β, whereas L. crispatus was associated with lower TNF-α and IL-1β concentrations [26]. Intrauterine infection and inflammation are also an important contributor to PTB [27]. Elevated concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and TNF-α are linked with preterm labor [28].

Vaginal products for prevention of HIV should not induce local inflammation or disrupt the vaginal microbiome because both inflammation and vaginal dysbiosis are associated with enhanced HIV risk and preterm birth. During study protocol development, stakeholder meetings with community members, representatives of African ministries of health and local physicians and midwives were conducted [29]. During these meetings, community members expressed concerns that DVR use in pregnancy would increase infection risk, a theoretical risk assuming that having a foreign body such as the DVR in the vagina during pregnancy might create epithelial disruptions or alter the vaginal microbiome [30]. The objective of this secondary analysis of 2 randomized trials was to assess whether changes in the vaginal microenvironment occurred differentially during use of DVR compared with women using OP during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

METHODS

This analysis includes data from 755 HIV-uninfected women (558 pregnant and 197 breastfeeding) across 2 nonoverlapping clinical trial populations to assess the impact of DVR (25 mg) and oral TDF/FTC on vaginal microbiota and cytokines (Table 1). All women provided written informed consent. MTN-042 (NCT03965923) was a multisite, phase 3b, two-arm, randomized, open-label safety trial evaluating the safety and tolerability of daily oral TDF/FTC tablet or DVR self-inserted every 4 weeks in women having a singleton pregnancy aged 18–40 enrolled across 4 sites (Blantyre, Malawi; Johannesburg, South Africa; Kampala, Uganda; and Chitungwiza, Zimbabwe) from 2020 to 2024. Individuals with urinary tract infection, or reproductive tract infection were excluded, as were those with any known pregnancy complications during the current or past pregnancies. This study took a stepwise approach to enrollment starting at later gestational ages, then progressing to earlier gestational ages (Cohort 1: 36 0/7-37 6/7 weeks, Cohort 2: 30 0/7-35 6/7 weeks, and Cohort 3: 12 0/7-29 6/7 weeks). Participants used their assigned study product until their pregnancy outcome but no later than 41 6/7 weeks of gestation [29, 30].

MTN-043 (NCT04140266) was a multisite, phase 3b, randomized, open-label, mother–infant pair safety study evaluating 12 weeks of planned study product exposure to either oral TDF/FTC or DVR from 2020 to 2021. Healthy breastfeeding women aged ≥18 years and their healthy infants between 6 and 12 weeks of age were enrolled in this study across the same 4 sites. Eligible mothers exclusively breastfeeding 1 infant, and willing to continue exclusive breastfeeding during study participation. For mothers randomized to the DPV vaginal ring arm, the vaginal ring was self-inserted and worn continuously for ∼1 month (4 weeks) and replaced monthly for ∼3 months (12 weeks). Mothers in the TDF/FTC arm were instructed to take OP daily for ∼3 months (12 weeks) [31].

Vaginal samples were collected at enrollment prior to product use, at biweekly visits depending on cohort arm during pregnancy and at the 6-week postpartum visit for MTN-042. For MTN-043, samples were collected on the day of enrollment prior to product use and at the 3-month visit. Samples from all enrolled women at all visits in both clinical trials were included in this analysis and are listed in Supplementary Tables 1A-4B by cohort and arm.

Vaginal fluid was collected using at least 4 polyester-tipped swabs for the evaluation of vaginitis (BV, Candida, and TV) using Nugent criteria and BD MAX™ Vaginal Panel assay (BD MAX VP: Becton, Dickinson and Company, Sparks, Maryland, USA), microbiota using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and cytokines by MAGPIX (Luminex, Austin, Texas, USA). Supplementary Tables 1A (MTN-042) and S1B (MTN-043) describe the distribution of qPCR tests performed among women by study cohort and arm. Similar tables describe the distribution of Nugent score tests (Supplementary Tables 2A and 2B), of cytokines tests (Supplementary Tables 3A and 3B) and of MVP tests (Supplementary Tables 4A and 4B). Across the 4 sites, polyester-tipped swabs were always used but the same brand could not be guaranteed due to supply shortages during the COVID pandemic. Samples were stored at −70°C within 4 hours of collection and shipped to the network laboratory at Magee-Womens Research Institute (MWRI, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA) on dry ice for testing. At the clinical sites, a fresh vaginal swab was used to prepare a vaginal smear, which was air-dried for Gram stain and Nugent score evaluation by the network laboratory at MWRI [32].

One swab was used to perform an FDA-cleared nucleic acid amplification test to detect BV, Candida group, Candida krusei, Candida glabrata, and TV. Because the vaginal swabs were frozen before placing in the BD Molecular Swab Sample Buffer Tube (Becton, Dickson and Company), a validation study comparing the results from fresh versus frozen swabs was performed to demonstrate that placing the sample in the BD buffer after storage at −70°C did not alter the results (data not shown).

The vaginal swab for qPCR was eluted into 800 µL of sterile, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the bacterial DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA mini kit (Qiagen, Valencia, California, USA) and qPCR was performed using SYBR Green technology platform (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA) on the CFX Connect real-time detection system (Bio-Rad) [33]. Primer sets targeting species-specific 16S ribosomal RNA genes (Supplementary Table 5) were used to detect L. crispatus [34], L. jensenii [34], L. gasseri [34], L. iners [34], Gardnerella vaginalis [34], Fannyhessea vaginae [34], Limosilactobacillus vaginalis [35], Prevotella bivia [36], P. amnii [37], P. timonensis [37], Umbribacter vaginalis [37], and Sneathia [38]. L. crispatus, L. jensenii, and L. gasseri were selected because of their association with a Lactobacillus dominant microbiome. L. iners was chosen because of its high prevalence in women having vaginal dysbiosis. Limosilactobacillus vaginalis was included in these analyses because it may play an important role in the vaginal health of African women [39]. The remaining targets were chosen because they have been linked to genital inflammation [40, 41] or have been associated with increased risk of HIV acquisition [18, 40].

A fourth vaginal swab was used for measurement of cytokines and chemokines. Protein levels of cytokines and chemokines in cell-free supernatants were measured (pg/ml) in duplicate through multiplexing with the use of magnetic bead kits (EMD Millipore, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) according to kit directions and analyzed on a MAGPIX using the xPONENT® 4.2 software. The following 5 cytokines; interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and interleukin 10 (IL-10), and RANTES, were quantified in vaginal fluid by adding 400 µL of PBS (Mediatech, Inc., Herndon, VA) to each thawed vaginal swab. IL1-β and RANTES are associated with HIV acquisition. IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and IFN-γ were associated with communities with abundance of microorganisms associated with BV [42].

Statistical Methods

These were prespecified exploratory analyses. Frequency of vaginal infections and microbiota as well as mean log concentration in those positive for selected microbiota were compared descriptively between pregnancy cohorts (MTN-042), postpartum (MTN-042), and breastfeeding (MTN-043) women at baseline. For the vaginal microbiota qPCR tests, the lower limit of detection (LLD) was 100 copies per swab. We set assay results that were below the LLD to the midpoint between 0 and the LLD. For the cytokines/chemokine concentration, the threshold of censoring was 3.2 pg/ml. We set the censored concentration to the midpoint between 0 and censoring threshold. For each vaginal infection detectable by BD MAX VP and each vaginal microbiota species with <50% overall detection rate across all visits in MTN-042 or in MTN-043, a logistic generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) was fitted to estimate and compare the odds ratio (OR) of detection for every month of TDF/FTC and DVR use in pregnant women and breastfeeding women. For each vaginal microbiota species with at least 50% overall detection rate across all visits in MTN-042 and in MTN-043, cytokines/chemokine of interest, and Nugent scores, a linear mixed model (LMM) was fitted to estimate and compare the change in log concentration for every month of TDF/FTC or DVR use in pregnant women and breastfeeding women. In each of the GLMM and LMM, we used the binary indicator of positive detection or the continuous log concentration as the outcome variable, the randomization group indicator, time since enrollment in month, and the interaction between randomization group and time since enrollment in month as fixed effects, and person as a random effect. For models fitted to the data from pregnant women in MTN-042, we also included cohort-specific intercepts to adjust for cohort effects. For data collected from the pregnant women at 6 weeks postpartum, we used linear regression models to estimate the difference in mean log10 concentration of vaginal microbiota, cytokines and chemokines, and Nugent scores between the 2 arms, and used logistic regression models to estimate the difference in OR of vaginal microbiota and infection detections between the 2 arms. For all tests conducted above, statistical significance was determined using Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)-adjusted P values with a threshold of .05 to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

RESULTS

The study population included 755 women including 558 pregnant women enrolled in MTN-042 and 197 breastfeeding women enrolled in MTN-043 (Supplementary Table 6). The age of the pregnant women was a median of 26 years (DVR group) and 25 years (OP group). Twelve (2.2%) participants reported having an HIV-positive partner, 436 (78.1%) participants reported having an HIV-negative partner, and 110 (19.7%) participants did not know their partner's HIV status. Women in the MTN-043 study were similar to the women enrolled in MTN-042 with a median age of 27 years (DVR group) and 26 years (OP group). Overall, 97.5% of breastfeeding participants reported having a primary partner and 33% reported that they knew or suspected that their primary sexual partner had other partners.

All 558 pregnant participants were evaluated at enrollment, biweekly thereafter until delivery and 6 weeks postpartum. BV was common in this population whether Gram stain or molecular methods were used for diagnosis (Table 2). The frequency of BV at baseline by Nugent score was similar across all 3 pregnancy cohorts, ranging from 31.5% in late-stage pregnancy (cohort 1) to 23.3% in the second trimester of pregnancy (cohort 3). At the postpartum visit for all women in MTN-042 and at baseline for breastfeeding women in MTN-043, BV was detected in 51.1% and 46.7% of participants, respectively. Findings were similar when BV was assessed using the BD MAX VP assay (Table 2).

Candida group (C. albicans, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis, and C. dublinensis) was detected in 44.5% of women during pregnancy, but unlike BV, the frequency was substantially lower among the same women during their postpartum visit (10.3%). C. glabrata, although less common overall, also decreased postpartum compared with during pregnancy (Table 2). Breastfeeding women also had Candida detected at lower frequencies relative to pregnant women (5.8%). TV was detected in ≤ 5% of pregnant and breastfeeding women at enrollment.

Among pregnant participants, C. glabrata detection increased in the OP arm but decreased in the DVR arm (P = .023) during product use. The OP and DVR arms did not differ significantly for change in detection rate of BV, Candida, and TV over time during pregnancy (Table 3), nor was there a significant difference in detection rate for vaginal infections for the 2 randomized arms at 6 weeks postpartum (Supplementary Table 7). Among breastfeeding women, there was no statistically significant difference in the frequency of BV, Candida or TV by study arm after 3 months of product use (Table 3).

Vaginal microbiota detected by qPCR were evaluated in pregnant women enrolled in each of the 3 cohorts prior to product use and postpartum to assess the microbiota without product exposure. Breastfeeding women were also evaluated for these microbiota at enrollment (Table 4). L. crispatus was more prevalent in the second and early third trimester of pregnancy (45%-52%) versus the late third trimester (39%), postpartum (34%), and during breastfeeding (20%). The median concentration of L. crispatus and L. jensenii was 2 logs higher during pregnancy relative to postpartum (Table 4). Two Prevotella species, P. bivia and P. timonensis, were detected at higher frequencies during the postpartum period compared with during pregnancy. Other BV-associated taxa including G. vaginalis, F. vaginae, P. amnii, Sneathia, and U. vaginalis did not differ in frequency among visits during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

DISCUSSION

The present study provides more detailed microbiologic evidence that the DVR does not disrupt the vaginal microbiota, elicit inflammation, or lead to more vaginal infections when used in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy or during breastfeeding. The MTN-042 study demonstrated that OP and the DVR were well tolerated during pregnancy and had a good safety profile in women [29, 30] and their infants [43]. Untoward pregnancy outcomes, adverse events and pregnancy complications were uncommon, preterm birth was lower than the background rates within these communities and no women acquired HIV [29, 30]. Adherence to the study products was high in the MTN-042 study with 82% of rings at each visit showing evidence of some or high use and 98% of women having quantifiable plasma dapivirine concentrations at one or more visits. In the OP arm, 84% of participants had dried blood spot results indicating that they were ever exposed to oral TDF [30]. In the MTN-043 adherence to study product was also high with 87% of rings showing evidence of some or high use, and 99% of dried blood spots showing at least some use of OP (31). MTN-043 demonstrated that OP and the DVR were well tolerated and safe when used by breastfeeding women [31]. Few serious adverse event or grade ≥3 adverse event occurred among mothers or infants in either arm; those that did occur were deemed unrelated to product [31].

The data from this secondary analysis further support the safety of the DVR during pregnancy and breastfeeding for women at risk of HIV acquisition. This secondary analysis also provided an opportunity to evaluate vaginal infections and vaginal microbiota across the second and third gestations of pregnancy, postpartum and during breastfeeding. There have been few longitudinal studies that describe in detail the vaginal microbiome profile of African women during pregnancy and breastfeeding [44, 45]. In our study, the prevalence of BV was consistently higher based on MVP testing versus Nugent score among the 3 cohorts of pregnant women. However, BV frequency was higher during the postpartum and breastfeeding periods. Breastfeeding women experience profound hormonal shifts with a decrease in estrogen, a hormone that promotes glycogen deposition in the vaginal epithelium and supports proliferation of lactobacilli [46]. The hypoestrogenic environment causes lactobacilli to decrease dramatically, which likely accounts for the observed decrease in the concentrations of all lactobacilli during the postpartum period in our study. The shift in lactobacilli and the growth of anaerobic species during the postpartum period has been reported by other investigators [16, 47, 48]. A study of 178 US women which evaluated the vaginal ecosystem during pregnancy and postpartum reported that delivery was associated with a proinflammatory cytokine response and loss of vaginal Lactobacillus dominance [49]. Unlike BV, the prevalence of Candida is generally low in postpartum and breastfeeding women [50].

This analysis has several strengths and important limitations. The samples were derived from well-executed clinical trials with high participant retention (>95%), which ensured a nearly complete sample set for analysis with limited missing data [29–31]. This analysis was strengthened using both Nugent score and the BD MAX VP for detection of BV, and the use of BD MAX VP for detection of Candida species, which have not routinely been evaluated in studies of African women. Some limitations of this study were that only uncomplicated singleton pregnancies were included, although there is no evidence that vaginal microbiota differ substantially between singleton and nonsingleton pregnancies. All study sites were in urban/peri urban settings in Malawi, Uganda, South Africa and Zimbabwe and the vaginal microbiota may not be generalized to that of women living in rural areas or other regions of the African continent [29, 30]. Only selected microbiota and cytokines/chemokines were included in this analysis for logistical reasons, although each of these targets was selected based on their demonstrated relevance to HIV susceptibility or pregnancy outcomes. It is possible that not all changes in the vaginal microbiome or soluble inflammatory mediators were detected.

Based on routine monitoring for adverse events, the parent clinical trials of pregnant and breastfeeding women found no evidence of increased infections or vaginitis symptoms among women using the DVR [29–31]. The present study provides additional robust evidence, which demonstrates that the use of the DVR does not disrupt the vaginal microbiome and is not associated with an increased prevalence of vaginal infections or changes in soluble immune mediators compared with women randomized to daily oral TDF/FTC. The present study adds to the body of evidence suggesting that DVR for prevention of HIV is safe for use during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
