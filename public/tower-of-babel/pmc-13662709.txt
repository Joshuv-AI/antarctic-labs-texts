Preparation for bias conversations among Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian parent‐adolescent dyads: A qualitative content analysis of observational data
Authors: Frances M Lobo, Michelle Y Martin Romero, Paula Sanchez‐Hernandez, Kimberly Cang, Lisa Kiang, Stephanie Irby Coard, Gabriela Livas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreparation for bias conversations may be critical in raising youth awareness of racial‐ethnic discrimination, fostering a strong racial‐ethnic identity, and teaching them how to cope or respond to racialized stressors. However, the literature relies almost exclusively on survey approaches, limiting understanding of how these conversations actually unfold in real time. The present study used qualitative content analysis of observational data to describe the strategies 151 Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian parents (M age = 41.63, SD = 6.16) and youth aged 11–14 years (M age = 12.16, SD = 0.91) discussed to respond to four discrimination scenarios that varied in the location, the power asymmetry, and the nature of the interaction. Black/African American parents were primarily U.S.‐born whereas Latinx and Asian parents were predominantly first‐generation immigrants. We aimed to center participants' words to understand how they respond to discrimination in context, whether parents and youth differed, and how this may vary across racial‐ethnic groups. We identified strategies that could be categorized into ignoring the perpetrator, confronting, support‐seeking, reporting, activism, and protective compliance. Unique to our analysis was the ability to distinguish how strategies were embedded in the type of discrimination stressor, the context of race/ethnicity, and youth‐ versus parent‐suggested strategies. Though there was a broad range of strategies that were suggested, the findings illustrate that the context of the discrimination matters and that parents and youth also value different approaches.Keywords: Asian, Black, coping strategies, discrimination, Latinx, qualitative research

Racial‐ethnic discrimination is any personal or vicarious experience of unfair treatment related to membership in a minoritized racial‐ethnic group. Within the United States, adolescents of color experience racial‐ethnic discrimination based on stigmatized attributes (e.g., skin color, accent, language ability), cultural values (e.g., centrality of the family, or familismo), perceived affiliations (e.g., immigrant status), cultural practices (e.g., food), and stereotypes (e.g., model minority characterization, perceived criminality). Racial‐ethnic discrimination is a public health crisis: it is a pervasive, uncontrollable stressor that has an impact on both physical and mental health (Benner et al., 2018; Njoroge et al., 2021; Williams et al., 2003). In fact, systematic reviews reveal that the impact of racial‐ethnic discrimination goes skin deep, impacting how adolescents perceive threat in their environment and physiologically respond to it (Berger & Sarnyai, 2015). Given these detrimental effects, it is important to understand resilience processes that may support youth coping and help them thrive amidst this stressor.

Racial socialization is the process by which parents teach youth about the meaning and importance of race, their personal and group racial identity, the racial stratification of society, and how members of their racial‐ethnic group interact with each other and with other racial‐ethnic groups (Lesane‐Brown, 2006; Priest et al., 2014). As part of racial socialization, preparation for bias, whereby parents raise youth's awareness of racial‐ethnic discrimination and teach them how to cope with or respond to racialized stressors, may critically support youth coping efficacy with future discriminatory events and subsequent adjustment among Black, Latinx, and Asian American families (Ayón et al., 2020; Hughes et al., 2006; Juang et al., 2017). However, research on the effects of preparation for bias on youth mental health and academic outcomes remains equivocal (Umaña‐Taylor & Hill, 2020; Wang et al., 2020). A drawback to this literature is that it relies almost exclusively on parent‐ and youth‐reported frequency of parents' preparation for bias messages in a general way (e.g., how many times has your parent talked to you about the following?). As such, less is understood about how preparation for bias conversations unfold, the actual strategies parents and youth suggest for responding to discrimination, and how these vary by context, which can be gained from observational methods. Further, racial socialization has primarily been examined as a parent‐delivered process, ignoring adolescents' role in teaching their parents about race or racism (Patel, Stein, et al., 2023). This study leverages a qualitative content analysis of observations of parent‐adolescent preparation for bias conversations to offer a nuanced understanding of parent and youth strategies and how these may vary by different contexts and across racial‐ethnic backgrounds.

Preparation for bias conversations and noted barriers

In the face of a pervasive threat like racism, parents worry about their children experiencing discrimination (Anderson et al., 2021); thus, having “The Talk” and raising children's awareness of the racial realities of society, discussing how to navigate stressful racial encounters, and building youth coping repertoire is crucial (Coard et al., 2024). The Racial Encounter Coping Appraisal and Socialization Theory (RECAST; Anderson & Stevenson, 2019) argues that this racial socialization is an important moderator in the relation between parents' racial stress and their child's efficacy in coping with racialized stressors. Through racial socialization, youth are taught to appraise whether an event is discriminatory, and then to assess or calibrate whether their own self‐efficacy and coping resources can help them meet the demands of this racialized stressor. After these steps, youth make a decision about how to respond to the stressor (i.e., engage, seek help, etc.), building their own coping efficacy.

Similarly, in a systematic review of racial socialization in Black families, Lesane‐Brown (2006) found that parents provided messages that (1) instilled racial pride and taught them about their culture, (2) raised awareness of environments in which racial oppression could occur, or (3) de‐emphasized race with a focus on self‐development and co‐existence with other racial‐ethnic groups. Parents who were concerned about the inevitability of racism chose to raise their child's awareness of potential racism, identify racial barriers, and teach them specific cultural coping strategies (e.g., religious coping) for responding to racial antagonism (Lesane‐Brown, 2006).

Studies show that Latinx and Asian parents provide fewer preparation for bias messages than Black/African American parents (Priest et al., 2014). Research with Latinx immigrant families reveals that parents are often aware of the racialized stressors youth are facing, but some encourage youth to ignore discriminatory comments (i.e., adapt) while others support advocacy (i.e., reaching out to adults for support or standing up for oneself) (Ayón, 2016). Parents may also discuss immigration‐related issues that intersect with conversations about ethnic‐racial identity, such as nativity (i.e., where they and other family members were born) and documentation status (Ayón, 2016). Strategies for responding to discrimination may also be impacted by family fears around deportation, especially during periods of heightened xenophobia. With regard to Asian families, a recent systematic review found that mono‐racial Asian American and Pacific Islander parents preferred to focus on positive aspects of race (e.g., building cultural pride, supporting cultural pluralism), but refrained from discussing negative aspects (e.g., discrimination) (Nieri et al., 2024). However, due to the pandemic and rise of anti‐Asian hate, Asian parents may have been increasingly willing to discuss racism and prepare their children for a racialized society (Coard et al., 2024). This work suggests that there may be important differences in how preparation for bias conversations unfold by racial‐ethnic group.

In the broader literature, messages that center racial pride, cultural empowerment, and passing down culture predict a host of positive outcomes (e.g., self‐esteem, academic success, mental health, racial identity) (Ayón et al., 2020; Juang et al., 2017; Umaña‐Taylor & Hill, 2020). However, preparation for bias messages have not been consistently related to positive outcomes. While there is evidence that preparation for bias increases ethnic‐racial centrality and exploration (Hughes et al., 2006; Rivas‐Drake et al., 2009), promotive effects in relation to mental health and well‐being are mixed (Ayón et al., 2020; Juang et al., 2017; Umaña‐Taylor & Hill, 2020). For this reason, it may be important to understand how these conversations unfold among different racial‐ethnic groups and the barriers they experience.

Although there are multiple forms of discrimination (e.g., systemic/structural, internalized, vicarious; David et al., 2019; Martin Romero & Stein, 2023), preparation for bias conversations often focus upon prior personal experiences or potential youth encounters with personally‐mediated discrimination (i.e., experiencing bias in a personal encounter with the perpetrator). However, research today has not systematically considered how personally‐mediated discrimination can arise in many different contexts (e.g., online, at school, on the street, in a store) and involve a range of perpetrators varying in their level of familiarity with or authority over the youth (Bámaca et al., 2022). For instance, in relation to familiarity, the strategies that parents and youth may discuss for responding to a teacher that the child has to see every day and who is in charge of their grade may be very different from the strategies discussed when the perpetrator is a store clerk they meet once. Furthermore, encounters may evoke a range of concerns from parent and adolescent, especially if there are power asymmetries or other concerns at play (e.g., fear for physical safety in encounters with a bully or with a police officer) that may shape the strategies they choose.

For immigrant families, there may be other specific barriers to having effective preparation for bias conversations. Parents may not understand the racialized context of the United States, their positioning in its socially stratified racial hierarchy, nor the sociopolitical history of immigration, segregation, and discrimination for their own and other racial‐ethnic groups. Thus, parents and youth may encounter racialized stressors such as pan‐ethnic labeling and xenophobia (Lee, 2020), stereotyping (e.g., model minority myth, perceived criminality; Osborne, Smith‐Bynum, et al., 2023; Yoo et al., 2010), and racial‐ethnic discrimination (e.g., microaggressions vs. overt acts of racism) after immigrating and have to make meaning from these experiences. In the face of adversity, qualitative research has revealed that many parents refrained from preparation for bias altogether and found it difficult to discern whether an event was discriminatory (Gonzalez et al., 2022). Some parents may accept a narrative purporting that America is a land of opportunity (i.e., meritocracy) where racial and ethnic groups have an equal opportunity to rise socioeconomically; parents holding this perspective may minimize their discrimination experiences. For example, in focus groups, some immigrant parents used the word “bullying” to minimize the significance of racial bias or doubted that discrimination could be operating in accounts relayed by their child (Gonzalez et al., 2022).

Lastly, racial socialization has primarily been examined as a parent‐delivered process, but theorists suggest that socialization is a dynamic, bidirectional process (Hughes et al., 2006; Smith‐Bynum, 2023). Youth may shape preparation for bias through discussions of their own lived experiences (Patel, Stein, et al., 2023). Furthermore, through the school system, interaction with peers, and social media, adolescents may be exposed to different sources of discrimination as well as socialization about their racial‐ethnic identity. Thus, they may be able to teach their own parents about labeling events as discriminatory and responding to discrimination. Youth may also reflect more on societal inequities and be motivated to fight for systemic changes to address these injustices (i.e., anti‐racist action; Aldana et al., 2019). Thus, it is important to examine both parent and youth contributions to preparation for bias.

Coping and resistance frameworks for strategies for responding to discrimination

Coping is the process of enacting cognitive (e.g., reappraisal), behavioral (e.g., confrontation, support‐seeking), and emotion‐focused strategies (e.g., distraction) to manage the impact of internal or external stressors (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), and is theorized to play a critical role in minimizing the impact of racial‐ethnic discrimination on health outcomes (e.g., Clark et al., 1999). Over 400 coping strategies have been identified in the literature (Skinner et al., 2003). However, theory suggests that the effectiveness of coping strategies may vary depending upon the person, situational demands, such as the nature, severity, and duration of the stressor, and the time frame being examined (e.g., responding to a confrontation with a perpetrator of discrimination vs. coping with the effects across time) (Lazarus, 1993). Thus, coping must be studied in a context‐specific manner.

Resistance frameworks may help us better understand the function of coping strategies amidst discrimination. Ellefsen et al. (2022) argue that resistance is any action to oppose or resist racism and group such actions into these categories: ignoring, confronting, sharing experiences, reporting, and protesting. Ignoring is a strategy that downplays or disregards the significance of a racial incident, denying its power over someone, whereas confronting is using one's voice or agency to respond to a perpetrator when experiencing racism and standing up for oneself. Sharing racial experiences, or support‐seeking, involves seeking socioemotional support or aid with problem‐solving from family members, peers, or the community. Although discrimination can contribute to feeling excluded or rejected, support‐seeking may combat this by validating one's appraisal of unfair treatment, building solidarity with others who have had similar experiences, and enhancing feelings of connection with one's racial‐ethnic group. Reporting is the act of notifying authorities about incidents of racism, often to highlight the gravity of the incident. Meanwhile, protesting is a form of organization in response to a racial incident, drawing attention to the perpetration of harm. Research has identified similar communal sources of coping, such as gathering the support of others online in response to a discriminatory racial encounter (Martin Romero et al., 2022). This resistance framework allows us to interpret parent and youth strategies for responding to discrimination; it is important to understand the range of strategies that are discussed with children at this age range, and whether some are more salient during particular discrimination contexts.

The present study

Given that preparation for bias conversations often focus upon potential youth encounters with personally‐mediated discrimination, which can arise in many different contexts and involve a range of perpetrators (Bámaca et al., 2022), there is a need to understand how parents and youth adapt their coping to circumstance. The present study used a qualitative content analysis of observational data to understand how parents and youth respond to discrimination, while varying the location (i.e., school, neighborhood, store), the power asymmetry (e.g., peer vs. police officer or school counselor), and the nature of the interaction (e.g., asking for a recommendation letter vs. playing in the street). Given the different sociopolitical history and racial positioning of racial‐ethnic groups as well as the unique barriers experienced by immigrant families, we were interested in whether strategies would vary by racial‐ethnic group. We had two research questions: (1) What are the strategies that parents and youth suggest for responding to discrimination? and (2) How do these strategies vary by context and racial‐ethnic group?

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Families are part of a larger study testing the efficacy of parenting programs targeting youth psychopathology and self‐esteem. Families were recruited through school and community events, and through fliers posted in school newsletters and the social media accounts of community partners. Interested families were screened for eligibility via a brief phone conversation. Inclusion criteria included (1) they are a parent of a middle schooler (11–14 years old in middle school), (2) they are of Black/African American, East or Southeast Asian,1 or Latinx (Mexico and Central America) background, (3) the other parent is of the same background, (4) the child agrees to participate, (5) the child has at least a 3rd grade reading level, (6) the family has a stable internet connection, and (7) the family currently lives in the state of North Carolina.

Participants included 151 Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian parents (M age = 41.63, SD = 6.16, range = 29–56 years) and their adolescents aged 11–14 years (M age = 12.16, SD = 0.91; 22 boys, 31 girls, 1 nonbinary). For Black/African American families (n = 56), the majority of parents (53 mothers, 3 fathers) had been in the United States for multiple generations; only 5% were first‐generation immigrants (i.e., born in a foreign country and moved to the United States as a youth or adult). Four percent of youth reported they were first‐generation, 13% second‐generation (i.e., they were born in the United States but one or both parents were born in another country), and 84% third+ − generation (i.e., they and their parents were born in the United States). Parents primarily reported having a bachelor's degree or higher (64%); 32% reported an income less than $50,000, 25% between $50,000 and $75,000, and 39% above $75,000. For Latinx families (n = 45), the majority of parents (41 mothers, 4 fathers) reported they were first‐generation immigrants (91%) from Mexico (69%); 11% reported they were Honduran, 7% Guatemalan, 2% Nicaraguan, 2% Salvadoran, 2% “Hispanic,” 2% “White” and 4% did not report their ethnicity. Eighteen percent of youth reported they were first‐generation, 78% second‐generation, and 4% third+ − generation. On average, foreign‐born parents had moved to the United States at 23.29 years (range = 2–42) and had been in the United States for 17.33 years (range = 1.5–36 years). In relation to education attainment, 67% of parents reported having a high school diploma or GED, and 11% reported having a bachelor's degree or higher. Eighty‐two percent reported an income less than $50,000, 7% between $50,000 and $75,000, and 9% above $75,000. For Asian families (n = 50), the majority of parents (42 mothers, 8 fathers) noted they were first‐generation immigrants (88%) from China (70%); 8% reported they were Taiwanese, 6% Korean, 4% Japanese, 4% Vietnamese, 2% Laotian, 2% reported they were “Asian,” and 4% did not report their ethnicity. Thirty percent of youth reported they were first‐generation, 66% second‐generation, and 4% third+ − generation. On average, foreign‐born parents had moved to the United States at 29.07 years (range = 2–42) and had been in the United States for 17.76 years (range = 1–45 years). Parents primarily reported having a graduate or professional degree (78%). Six percent reported an income less than $50,000, 14% between $50,000 and $75,000, and 80% above $75,000.

Procedure

Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian parent‐youth dyads were invited to complete a 30‐min video‐recorded activity with a study team member online (i.e., Zoom, Microsoft Teams). Parents were compensated $30 and youth $20 for participating in the activity.

Racial socialization observational task

The Racial Socialization Observational Task (Smith‐Bynum et al., 2019) has been validated in both Black/African American and Latinx families (e.g., Osborne, Smith‐Bynum, et al., 2023; Osborne, Walsdorf, et al., 2023). Parents and youth were invited to discuss how they would respond to four discrimination scenarios as if they were happening to their family. These stories involved encounters with a school counselor, store clerk, peer, or a police officer that referenced stereotypes about their racial‐ethnic group (e.g., engages in criminal activity) (see Appendix A for text). We adapted the task by adding scenarios for Asian families that referenced stereotypes for this group (e.g., the model minority myth, perpetual foreigner stereotype). Additionally, we added a peer scenario since youth in this age range often describe racial incidents involving peers (Bámaca et al., 2022). After reading and hearing a recording of a scenario, dyads were given 5 min to discuss how they would respond to it. Given that many Latinx parents were only comfortable speaking in Spanish and we had Spanish‐speaking research assistants, Latinx families were given the choice of conducting their visit in English, Spanish, or both; 80% used Spanish, 18% English, and 2% both languages. Families in other racial‐ethnic groups were asked to speak in English; however, if it was easiest to communicate an idea in another language, they were encouraged to do so. Of our Asian families, 32% spoke some Mandarin and 6% some Korean. Task administrators were undergraduate and postbaccalaureate students trained by the first author. Eighty‐four percent of visits were conducted with an experimenter of the same racial‐ethnic background as the family.

Researcher positionality

We are an interdisciplinary research team made up of Black, Latinx, and Asian scholars with educational backgrounds in developmental and clinical psychology, human development and family sciences, public health, and medicine. We identify as cis‐gender women and vary in our generational status (i.e., first‐generation, 1.5‐generation, second‐generation). We have decades of experience studying the effects of cultural stressors on adolescents as well as investigating racial socialization and parent‐adolescent processes as protective factors from a cultural resiliency lens. Our work is motivated by a desire to identify intervention targets that promote youth coping efficacy and resilience in the face of racial‐ethnic discrimination. Our positionalities have shaped the entirety of the research process, from conceptualization to data collection, as well as analysis and interpretation of the findings.

With regard to our methodological experience, the first author has experience collecting and coding observational data from parent‐youth dyads. Most of us have participated in research teams that have engaged in qualitative work as coders and analysts. Much of the team draws from a postpositivist perspective, and the second author has extensive training in critical and constructivist qualitative inquiry. As a team, we have exposure to a range of qualitative approaches and analytic techniques (e.g., consensual qualitative research, content analysis, grounded theory, phenomenological qualitative research, and reflexive thematic analysis). In addition to our academic training, we bring extensive experience working with minoritized youth through clinical practice, community engagement, teaching, mentoring, and nonprofit work.

Data analytic method

First, videos from all 151 Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian dyads were transcribed (four different scenarios per dyad for 604 scenarios total) in their original language (i.e., English, Korean, Mandarin, or Spanish) by a team of trained bilingual undergraduate research assistants supervised by [Author #1; A1] and [A3]. These transcriptions were double‐checked for accuracy. Because we had a large team of bilingual Spanish speakers, Spanish transcripts were coded directly. For other non‐English transcripts, we completed two rounds of translation before coding; we used verbatim forward translation of parent‐youth conversations by a native speaker while also verifying accuracy via back translation. While translators were from the same racial‐ethnic background as the family, transcribers did not have to be (e.g., when transcripts were in English and did not need to be translated).

For coding, the research team utilized an inductive approach (i.e., data‐driven) to conduct a qualitative content analysis (QCA) of the transcripts of parent‐youth conversations. We began with a subset of 16 videos from Asian parent‐adolescent dyads because we had many bilingual research assistants interested in the experiences of Asian families. To familiarize themselves with the data, [A1], [A4], and 3 Asian coders watched the videos and read the transcripts. The coding team noted initial ideas for strategies observed and any rationale that parents and children provided for their strategies. [A4] then organized these initial strategies into codes and clustered these into categories, with supervision from [A1] and [A7]. Strategy codes were neither mutually exclusive nor exhaustive. A preliminary version of the codebook was developed at this stage.

Over the course of 16 months, teams of 2–3 coders met weekly to apply the initial codes for strategies to all transcripts and note the stated rationales for strategies. Coders were trained by reviewing the codebook with the first author, discussing examples, and having ongoing discussions during the coding process. Coders noted when identified strategies did not meet the initial codes; if five references were made to strategies not initially included in the coding scheme, they were upgraded to become a new inductive code. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until the coders reached consensus. To promote cultural sensitivity, at least one coder on the team had to have the same racial‐ethnic background as the family.

Once the first round of coding for the QCA was completed, [A1] and the coding team familiarized themselves with the breadth of coded data and utilized analytic memos to note patterns emerging among the codes. Using investigator triangulation, [A1] reorganized, named, and re‐defined the codes and the broader categories in consultation with [A2]. The final categories were reviewed by the whole author team and coding teams.

Trustworthiness of the data

Important measures were taken to improve the trustworthiness of these data. First, given the importance of cultural sensitivity within the data collection process, as far as possible, data were collected by experimenters who matched the racial‐ethnic background of the family. This was to increase comfort for families. Similarly, coding teams included at least one member who matched the family's racial‐ethnic background; this was to support the coding teams in interpreting nuanced or implied messaging on behalf of parents and youth with cultural sensitivity. Reflexivity and team‐based discussion were encouraged throughout the analytic process to limit the ability of coders' biases to impact interpretation. [A1] identified and refined categories of strategies in conversation with [A2] and those who had coded the data; [A1] chose not to collect or code the data initially since they did not share the same racial‐ethnic background as any of the families. To improve transferability of the categories and enhance rigor, we included a large sample size of families (N = 151) and employed data source triangulation by centering the voices of multiple racial‐ethnic groups.

RESULTS

Categories of strategies emerging in these data

Table 1 presents the strategies we identified within parent‐youth conversations for responding to discrimination across contexts, their definitions, and examples observed within these data. These strategies fell within six categories: ignoring the perpetrator, confrontation, support‐seeking, reporting, engaging in activism, and protective compliance. Ignoring the perpetrator included any references to leaving the situation without responding, moving on, or ignoring the perpetrator. Meanwhile, confrontation referred to any efforts to retaliate against the perpetrator, advocate for oneself, ask questions, educate the perpetrator about their racist actions, or engage in strategic resistance through careful confrontation to achieve one's goals. Support‐seeking referenced any actions to find someone else (i.e., parent, peer, other adult) to intervene in the scenario and advocate for the child or provide socioemotional support. Reporting involved seeking someone of higher authority (e.g., principal, store manager, police chief) to address the situation. Engaging in activism reflected any individual or community‐level action to draw attention to the racial incident, highlight its harms, and fight against social inequity, such as recording the incident or engaging in individual behavioral resistance or community‐engaged activism (e.g., protesting, petitioning, leaving a bad review). Finally, protective compliance referred to falling in line with the requests or comments of the perpetrator or being careful in one's communication so as not to escalate the situation (i.e., verbal deference). Given that these categories have also been found in prior work (Ellefsen et al., 2022; Martin Romero et al., 2022), we highlight differences in strategies by context and racial‐ethnic group.

Strategies by context

As our main focus of the QCA, we identified salient strategies across and within contexts by racial‐ethnic group. Table 2 indicates the most salient strategies across scenarios for parents and youth, respectively, in each racial‐ethnic group. Notably, ignoring the perpetrator was salient for youth across racial‐ethnic groups, which may reflect their developmental stage; middle‐school‐aged youth may not initially feel comfortable confronting perpetrators. Advocating for self was a salient strategy for more than a quarter of parents and youth across racial‐ethnic groups. Additionally, except for Latinx youth, parents and youth across racial‐ethnic groups suggested seeking structural support (i.e., from those with higher authority).

There were also important differences by racial‐ethnic group across scenarios. First, Black/African American parents and youth highlighted relying upon the parent as an advocate. Given the socially stratified racial hierarchy within the United States and the fact that the majority of the Black/African American families in this study had long histories of being in the United States (i.e., third generation or more), these parents may have long had preparation for bias conversations with their youth. This may be why Black/African American youth mentioned more salient strategies compared to Latinx and Asian youth, who were majority first‐ and second‐generation in the United States. Given the age of their children, parents may have been willing to intervene on behalf of their youth. Meanwhile, Asian parents referenced the importance of educating the perpetrator, a strategy that was not salient for any other racial‐ethnic groups across scenarios.

School counselor

In this scenario, the child has the goal of obtaining a recommendation letter but is denied this opportunity due to the prejudiced stereotypes of their school counselor (i.e., model minority myth for Asian youth, perceived inability to keep up for Black/African American and Latinx youth). Acknowledging the power asymmetry in the situation, a 13‐year‐old Black/African American girl noted, “I will report it to a higher up instead of trying to handle it on my level because as a student, there's nothing I can do to actually change it.” Indeed, parents and youth across racial‐ethnic groups voiced seeking structural support (e.g., getting help from the principal) or, unique to this scenario, reaching out to another adult (i.e., teacher, another counselor) to ask them to advocate on behalf of the child, provide a recommendation letter, or report the perpetrator and get them fired. For example, a Latinx mother of an 11‐year‐old boy noted, “It's always good to know everyone well in the school: counselors, your principals, and social workers, everything. Because one day, one of them could be the one who could help you in this.” In response to a suggestion from their child, an Asian mother of an 11‐year‐old boy said, “That's a smart way [to ask another adult to step in] and avoid direct conflict with Miss Nelson.” Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian parents also mentioned they would advocate for their child by talking to the counselor or emailing, calling, writing letters, notifying someone in higher authority, or finding the application or another recommender for their child.

Across racial‐ethnic groups, parents and youth also wanted youth to advocate for themselves, but this was not a prominent strategy across Asian youth. Parents emphasized the importance of not letting someone limit their child's opportunities. For example, a Latinx mother of an 11‐year‐old boy noted, “We have the right to attend that type of camp, we all have the opportunity… no one can deny it.” Conversations also reflected a desire to prove the perpetrator wrong. A Black/African American girl (aged 12) stated, “I feel like if I were Deja [the girl in the scenario], I would feel hurt because [someone is] telling me something that I can't do…I believe I can do it and if I have to, I'm going to prove you wrong and do what I have to do.” Parents and youth highlighted the importance of persisting, as evidenced by this quote from the mother of a 12‐year‐old Latinx boy: “And would you say to [the child in scenario] to continue doing what he has to do even if the lady says he cannot, that he knows that she can do it for him, and is capable of doing so, and that he doesn't have to stop just because of what one person told him.”

Racial‐ethnic group differences

Some Asian youth (30.00%) discussed ignoring the perpetrator due to the power asymmetry, or a fear of conflict or experiencing negative consequences. For example, an Asian girl aged 12 said, “I probably wouldn't do anything because… it'd probably be scary to say anything about it because they are the school counselor.” Asian youth also referenced ignoring if they felt that nothing beneficial would result from confrontation. For example, an Asian boy aged 12 stated, “I'm pretty sure I might ignore it as well if I thought that there was nothing that I could do about it.” These examples reveal that ignoring may be mentioned when there is a perceived lack of agency, often due to appraisals about the perpetrator (i.e., their mind cannot be changed, they have power over the situation).

Notably, some Black/African American youth (35.71%) wanted their parent to advocate for them. Additionally, 28.57% of Black/African American youth described being upset by being told that they would not be able to keep up and wanted socioemotional support from their parents. For example, a Black/African American boy (aged 11) stated, “I will be mad, but again it's a school counselor and she's older so there's actually nothing I can do. I will just be upset… I'll tell the teacher, my friends, family about what happened.”

Store clerk

The store clerk scenario involves an unfamiliar adult who shares their stereotypes about one's racial‐ethnic group (i.e., perpetual foreigner stereotype; perceived criminality). Across all racial‐ethnic groups, ignoring the perpetrator was salient for both parents and youth, possibly because the scenario does not have direct consequences for the child's opportunities, unlike the school counselor scenario. An Asian mother of a 12‐year‐old girl said, “I would probably just not care, I mean why would I care about random people talking trash?… I care about my heritage… if people are talking trash about it, it [doesn't] affect me.” Some parents and youth also mentioned ignoring the perpetrator out of concern for physical safety. A 12‐year‐old Asian boy noted, “I wouldn't say something back because there's a possibility he might use violence on me.” This sentiment of fear was similarly expressed across all racial‐ethnic groups.

Alternatively, because of the lack of power dynamic in a racial incident involving a store clerk, some families may actually feel comfortable standing up for themselves. Self‐advocacy was a salient strategy for 30%–45% of parents and youth in each racial‐ethnic group but was not prominent across Asian youth. Highlighting their intent to confront, an 11‐year‐old Latinx girl stated, “I'm going to say that the man was being mean, that he was saying bad things, and that you [the mother] know English, you also teach English.” Over one third of parents and youth across racial‐ethnic groups (except Latinx youth) also referenced seeking structural support to report the perpetrator's actions to the store manager, often with the intent of getting them fired or experiencing negative consequences for their actions. They also made references to calling the police, the governor, and the county to report any wrongdoing.

Behavioral resistance most often emerged during the store clerk scenario. Of note, 39.29% of Black/African American parents and 26.00% of Asian youth mentioned strategies such as writing a bad review, posting on social media, protesting, or boycotting the store. A Black/African American mother of a girl aged 12 noted: “I don't want to give my money to a store that doesn't think very highly of me or that doesn't treat me well.” Parents also mentioned recording the incident as proof and a means to gain power in the situation.

Racial‐ethnic group differences

For both parents and youth across racial‐ethnic groups, involving the parent for either advocacy or socioemotional support also came up. Ignoring the perpetrator was not a salient strategy for Black/African American parents, and both Black/African American parents and youth referenced drawing upon parents as an advocate; this may be because the store clerk mentioned a stereotype around perceived criminality. Noting that she had been followed in a store before because of the same assumption, a Black/African American mother of a 13‐year‐old boy noted they would advocate for their child saying, “I don't think you should approach an adult, because they may not handle it well. It will just blow it out of proportion or upset me more depending on his reaction to you coming to him.” Latinx and Asian parents did not mention advocating for their children; these parents were primarily first‐generation immigrants, and in response to a scenario that referenced language ability, a few parents expressed that their own English‐language ability was a barrier to confrontation. However, Asian youth mentioned wanting their parents to advocate for them in this scenario.

Parent‐youth differences

Over 30% of youth in each group did want to tell their parents what was said, and Asian parents referenced drawing upon parents for support, but Black/African American and Latinx parents mostly focused on other strategies. Unique to this scenario, 28.89% of Latinx parents and 26.00% of Asian parents suggested educating the perpetrator, whereas Black/African American parents did not. This may be due to the stereotype being discussed: It may be easier to educate someone around issues of diversity and acculturation than to respond to assumptions about criminality. A Latinx mother of a 12‐year‐old boy noted:

If you're in that type of situation, you also need to speak up and say that what they are saying is not right because they do not know the work immigrants are doing to support themselves financially here in this country and that is so much more work than someone who is (an American) citizen that can speak their own language.

However, youth across racial‐ethnic groups did not feel comfortable educating the perpetrator.

Peer

In the peer scenario, a peer makes fun of the child's name, calling their parents illiterate for giving them such a name. Once again, across racial‐ethnic groups, ignoring the perpetrator was a salient strategy for parents and youth; more than 50% of Latinx parents and youth and Asian youth mentioned it. An Asian mother of an 11‐year‐old boy pointed to the benefit of ignoring on one's emotions: “You don't have to feel sad… just choose [to] ignore. It could be a better way, right?” Parents and youth also mentioned that responding to this racial incident was not worth the child's time. For example, a Black/African American 13‐year‐old boy noted, “I'd just ignore them. I don't feel like wasting my time on that.” Ignoring may also protect youth from escalating a conflict with someone they may have to see regularly.

In relation to confrontation, parents and youth across racial‐ethnic groups did suggest self‐advocacy (except Asian parents) in order to stand up for oneself. As a Black/African American father of a 13‐year‐old girl emphasized, “You gotta confront it. Don't let it slide. Don't let anybody ever think that they have the right to disrespect you.” Relatedly, over one third of Latinx and Asian parents and youth suggested that youth seek structural support by addressing the situation with the teacher. Unique to this scenario was the emphasis on behalf of parents and youth across racial‐ethnic groups on educating the perpetrator (except Black/African American youth); in fact, 62% of Asian parents and 40% of Asian youth mentioned it. For parents and youth, this involved discussing where the child's name comes from, what it means, or how it is pronounced with the peer, or even having a class presentation on one's heritage. Parents and youth may see the opinions of children as being more malleable than adults.

Parent‐youth differences

In relation to confrontation, unique to this scenario, verbal retaliation (e.g., making fun of their peer's name) was a salient strategy for Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian youth. As one Asian girl (age 12) noted, “First off I would side‐eye them and… then I'd make fun of their names.”

Police

In the police scenario, a police officer questions why the child is standing in the street. Notably, across racial‐ethnic groups, there was a restricted range of strategies provided by parents and youth in response, but a marked emphasis on self‐advocacy to prove the child's innocence to the police officer. Parents and youth also mentioned protective compliance, describing a fear of negative consequences or concern for physical safety. For example, a Latinx boy aged 12 noted he would “speak with the police officer and do everything he tells me to do.” Similarly, a Black/African American mother of a girl aged 12 stated:

Answer his questions even if what he says is offensive, just answer his questions plainly and simply and comply; even if he asks you to do something ridiculous, just do it… you know you can always report it to the higher ups, but at that point in time, I wouldn't make a big deal out of it so you can stay alive.

Parents and youth also discussed speaking respectfully, calmly, or politely and acting with protective measures so that the child could avoid conflict (i.e., verbal deference).

Parent‐youth differences

A quarter of Latinx and Asian youth mentioned ignoring the perpetrator, but this was not salient for Black/African American youth and parents across racial‐ethnic groups.

Racial‐ethnic group differences

Black/African American parents described wanting to intervene on behalf of their child, but this was not raised by any other group. Within the context of police brutality, particularly against the Black/African American community, parents emphasized protecting their children when interacting with the police.

DISCUSSION

Given the mixed findings in the literature about the efficacy of parents' preparation for bias messages, it is important to understand how parents and youth approach different strategies for responding to discrimination. The present study utilized a qualitative content analysis of observational data to examine parent and youth contributions of strategies for responding to discrimination and how these may vary by context. In alignment with prior work on resistance in the face of racism (Ellefsen et al., 2022) and coping amidst hate (Martin Romero et al., 2022), we identified categories of strategies such as ignoring, confronting, support‐seeking, reporting, engaging in activism, and protective compliance. Unique to our analysis was the ability to distinguish how strategies were embedded in the type of discrimination stressor, the context of race/ethnicity, and youth‐ versus parent‐suggested strategies. Though there was a broad range of strategies that were suggested, the findings illustrate that the context of the discrimination matters, and that parents and youth also do value different approaches.

Coping as resistance

Theory suggests that in the face of racial antagonism, individuals may use general coping (e.g., support‐seeking, ignoring) and racism‐specific strategies (e.g., building cultural pride, protesting) (Clark et al., 1999; Lesane‐Brown, 2006). Ellefsen et al. (2022) argue that resistance includes any action to actively oppose or resist racism, including ignoring, confronting, sharing experiences, reporting, and protesting. When discussing how to respond to a discriminatory incident, we found these strategies as well as protective compliance, similar to other protective strategies found within prior coping with discrimination literature (Martin Romero et al., 2022).

Robinson and Ward (1991) argue that resistance can be defined broadly as the process by which individuals negotiate racism. In this framework, resistance for survival reflects individual‐focused actions aimed at responding to a situation quickly and surviving or enduring the consequences of racism. Akin to prior literature using interviews and focus groups (e.g., Patel, Martin Romero, et al., 2023; Romero et al., 2015), across scenarios, Latinx parents and youth and Black/African American and Asian youth suggested ignoring the perpetrator. Additionally, all groups referenced protective compliance as a way of falling in line with the directions of the police officer (e.g., answering their questions) and limiting any short‐term consequences. These behaviors may be protective when youth coping abilities are developing, especially in a situation where there is concern for physical safety or well‐being or a fear of escalation and negative consequences, reflecting this idea of survival. This is especially true if youth have difficulty managing their emotions or are likely to escalate conflict (Romero et al., 2015). However, further work is needed to understand how ignoring, protective compliance, and other survival strategies shape youth's coping efficacy and mental health amidst future discriminatory events.

Resistance for liberation is a refusal to accept mistruths and dehumanization embedded within racism; this could include strategies such as self‐advocacy, affirmation of one's racial identity and feelings, and building critical consciousness (i.e., awareness, motivation, and action aimed at reducing social inequity) or advocating for anti‐racism (Das et al., 2022). In the present study, ignoring also manifested as a liberation strategy when families did not feel that engaging was worth their time, as a means of denying any power the incident had over the child. Parents and youth also drew upon support‐seeking as a means of finding external (e.g., another adult) and structural support (i.e., someone with higher authority in the system) to draw attention to the harm of discrimination, to intervene for them, or to report the incident in hopes of getting the perpetrator fired or having them experience negative consequences for their actions. Similar to Das et al. (2022), other resistance as liberation strategies we identified included confrontation and engaging in activism (e.g., protesting, writing a bad review). Taken together, this work highlights the need for future research examining parents' and youth's rationales for the strategies they provide for responding to discrimination.

Unique preparation for bias patterns in Black/African American families

Across scenarios, Black/African American youth reported a slightly larger coping repertoire than Latinx and Asian youth. Black/African American parents had likely long provided socialization messages teaching their youth how to advocate for themselves in response to discrimination, building their coping repertoire by middle school (Hughes et al., 2006; Priest et al., 2014). Consistent with this notion, a systematic review found that Latinx and Asian parents provide fewer preparation for bias messages than Black/African American parents (Priest et al., 2014), especially at younger ages. Over a quarter of Black/African American parents and youth in this study mentioned self‐advocacy in response to all scenarios, highlighting the importance of standing up for oneself.

Moving beyond self‐advocacy, in this study, many Black/African American parents also encouraged behavioral resistance in response to the store clerk. This involved suggestions such as writing a bad review of the store, posting on social media, or protesting to draw others' attention to the perpetration of harm and the need for consequences to address this. Research has emphasized parents' socialization messages about race and ethnicity as a critical precursor for youth's development of critical consciousness and anti‐racist action (Mejia et al., 2024), but it was surprising that behavioral resistance was not a salient strategy for Black/African American youth given scholarship showing youth efforts to address social inequity (Aldana et al., 2019). Because youth in this sample were only ages 11–14, in line with the RECAST model (Anderson & Stevenson, 2019), they may have perceived barriers (e.g., needing a ride, difficulty overcoming the power asymmetry) preventing their participation in such actions and opted for other strategies in line with their self‐efficacy, coping, and resources. Further work is needed to understand the circumstances under which Black/African American youth feel empowered to engage in acts of behavioral resistance.

There was evidence that context matters for when Black/African American families choose to ignore the perpetrator. For example, this was not a salient strategy in response to the store clerk for parents and in response to the school counselor for both parents and youth. Rather, 50% of parents referenced drawing upon the parent as an advocate during the store clerk scenario, and 50% of parents and 35.71% of youth mentioned using the parent as an advocate during the school counselor scenario. Similarly, while Latinx and Asian youth also mentioned ignoring the perpetrator in response to the police officer, this was not a salient strategy for Black/African American parents or youth; in fact, 42.86% of Black/African American parents described wanting to intervene on behalf of their child. Given the context of systemic racism within the criminal justice system and police brutality, particularly against the Black/African American community, parents' intervention was referenced in situations involving unfamiliar adults that could potentially escalate into a threat to the physical safety or well‐being of these children. Prior work in Black/African American families indicates that communalistic coping is a culturally relevant form of coping emphasizing group efforts to respond to stressors (Gaylord‐Harden & Cunningham, 2009). Thus, parents may expect to intervene in incidents involving discrimination and, in fact, may have already done so for their children.

Unique preparation for bias patterns for Latinx and Asian families

A few unique patterns emerged for Latinx and Asian families too. Over one third of Latinx and Asian parents wanted to advocate for their children with the school counselor; they may have wanted to make sure that their child's opportunities were not being limited. However, the majority of Latinx and Asian youth did not suggest that their parents advocate for them with the school counselor or the store clerk, unlike Black/African American youth. In this sample, the majority of parents in these racial‐ethnic groups were first‐generation immigrants. Youth in immigrant families often help their parents navigate the larger culture in the United States due to differences in acculturation and English‐language proficiency, a process that has been associated with mixed family and well‐being outcomes for youth (Subramoney et al., 2025). Perhaps these youth are already engaging in cultural and language brokering and could not see benefits in their parents' intervention, especially with someone espousing anti‐immigrant views amidst rising anti‐Asian hate and xenophobia (e.g., Lee, 2020).

In the store clerk and peer scenarios, most Latinx and Asian parents did not mention advocating for their children; instead, over one quarter mentioned that youth should seek structural support (i.e., address the situation with the store manager or teacher, respectively) or educate the perpetrator. This could be because parents believed that youth should be able to communicate effectively around issues of diversity and cultural pride. Latinx and Asian parents do provide frequent messages to their children about their cultural heritage (Ayón et al., 2020; Juang et al., 2017). However, most Latinx and Asian youth did not mention educating the store clerk, and in fact, 34% of Asian youth wanted their parents to advocate for them. Thus, immigrant youth at this developmental stage may not feel confident educating adults about their beliefs, but seem willing to discuss discrimination with their peers, supporting work emphasizing the importance of peer ethnic‐racial socialization (Wang & Lin, 2023).

Ignoring the perpetrator was highly salient in response to the store clerk and peer for both Latinx and Asian parents and youth. This aligns with prior research highlighting strategies of adaptation rather than advocacy in Latinx families (e.g., Ayón et al., 2020). For example, Mexican‐origin parents were concerned that their children would escalate situations involving discrimination, urging them to refrain from using derogatory terms or fighting and to report incidents to teachers instead; youth expressed a reluctance to stand up to perpetrators for fear that this may lead to a physical altercation (Romero et al., 2015). Similarly, in a qualitative study with Indian American families, when parents discussed coping, they primarily encouraged their child to ignore or avoid instances of discrimination rather than confronting the perpetrator (Patel, Martin Romero, et al., 2023). A quarter of Latinx and Asian youth also mentioned ignoring the police officer, often due to fear of escalating the situation; however, the majority of Latinx and Asian parents did not provide this suggestion, wanting youth to prove their innocence.

A lack of self‐advocacy in Asian youth

Self‐advocacy was not a salient strategy for the majority of Asian youth in the school counselor or store clerk scenarios. Instead of direct confrontation, more salient strategies included ignoring the perpetrator, seeking support from the parent, another adult, or someone in higher authority, or using individual or community‐based activism. In line with RECAST (Anderson & Stevenson, 2019), youth at this age may not feel they have the resources to confront, opting for indirect methods of addressing the situation. The instinct for self‐advocacy may not have been as apparent due to an overall lack of parental messages around preparation for bias and responding to discrimination. A systematic review found that in mono‐racial Asian families, parents emphasized building cultural pride or highlighted the importance of cultural pluralism but did not discuss discrimination (Nieri et al., 2024). Asian youth at this developmental stage may need scaffolding around how to advocate for themselves, particularly during racial encounters involving power asymmetries or interactions with unfamiliar adults. Asian adolescents and emerging adults have reported feeling ill‐prepared to respond to discrimination (Patel, Martin Romero, et al., 2023; Young et al., 2021).

Limitations and future directions

Preparation for bias has primarily been examined as a parent‐delivered process using surveys (Wang et al., 2020). Observational methodology allows us to glimpse parents' and youth's contributions to preparation for bias, at a developmental stage when youth are experiencing racialized stressors and trying to make meaning from them (Mistry et al., 2016). This study drew from 151 observations of Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian parent‐youth dyads to explicate their context‐based strategies for responding to discrimination. It also offered insights into how parents' and youth's strategies may converge or diverge depending upon contextual factors (e.g., the power dynamic between the perpetrator and child, location), and highlighted important differences among racial‐ethnic groups.

However, the present study does have some notable limitations. First, since participants for this study were drawn from families recruited as part of a parenting intervention, this sample involved parents who were interested in learning how to communicate with their youth around difficult topics; thus, there may be a selection bias which could have shaped the degree to which parents suggested that they be a source of advocacy or socioemotional support for their children.

Second, given that the preponderance of the literature has emphasized preparation for bias conversations in Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian families (Priest et al., 2014), we chose to focus on these racial‐ethnic groups in this study and tailored the scenarios to the stereotypes for each group. Thus, results noted across racial‐ethnic groups should be interpreted cautiously. Our findings may not be generalizable to other racial‐ethnic groups who were not represented in this study. Further, because this study was conducted in an emerging immigrant destination, the Latinx and Asian parents in this sample were predominantly first‐generation immigrants; we did not have enough heterogeneity in immigration status within racial‐ethnic groups to examine differences by immigration status. Families living in established immigrant communities may have had access to communal resources (e.g., community organizations, community‐engaged activism) that were not strongly represented within this study's results.

Third, as part of the experimental design, families were responding to written scenarios for which the events depicted may have varied in the degree to which they touched upon the lived experiences of our families. For example, some scenarios for Asian families referenced specific ethnic groups rather than using a pan‐ethnic label, which may have impacted how representative families felt these scenarios were of their experiences. Thus, the observational task fomented simulated experiences of preparation for bias conversations in response to hypothetical scenarios rather than parents and youth discussing actual discrimination experiences, which may have been more externally valid. Scenarios were also presented in the same order (i.e., school counselor, store clerk, peer, and police officer); as such, prior conversations may have affected parent and youth comfort with discussing and responding to discrimination for later scenarios.

Finally, theory suggests that racial‐ethnic socialization is a bidirectional process. Though this study used observational methods to detail parent and youth contributions to preparation for bias conversations, research taking dynamic, dyadic approaches is needed to understand how parents and youth make meaning of racial incidents in the light of their own identity and context (Mistry et al., 2016). Such approaches could also illuminate the process of negotiation between parents and youth regarding how best to respond to racialized stressors. Unlike with focus group or interview data, we also could not probe to learn more about parents' and youth's strategies. Researchers should examine parents' and youth's motivations for the strategies they suggest for responding to discrimination and how these vary by circumstance, beliefs, and experiences (e.g., personal experiences with discrimination or immigration stress; Woo et al., 2020).

CONCLUSION

Across a large sample of Black/African American, Latinx, and Asian parent‐adolescent dyads, we identified the following categories of strategies described during observed preparation for bias conversations: ignoring the perpetrator, confronting, support‐seeking, reporting, engaging in activism, and protective compliance. However, there are many strategies that fall within these categories, indicating complexity in the approaches that parents and youth take for responding to discrimination. Further work is needed to understand how parents' and youth's motivations, beliefs, and experiences impact the strategies they suggest for responding to racial incidents, and how these may differ from strategies used to cope with the long‐term aftermath of a racial incident or other forms of discrimination (e.g., vicarious racism; Martin Romero & Stein, 2023). Findings also indicate that parents and youth do alter their suggested strategies for responding to discrimination depending upon the location (i.e., school, neighborhood, store), the power asymmetry (e.g., peer vs. police officer or school counselor), and the goal of the interaction (e.g., asking for a recommendation letter vs. playing in the street), supporting theory suggesting that coping with discrimination must be understood in context (Lazarus, 1993). Parents and youth choose which strategies to engage in after calibrating how the context intersects with the child's own self‐efficacy, coping, and resources (Anderson & Stevenson, 2019). Finally, there were important differences in coping strategies in Latinx and Asian families versus Black/African American families, which may reflect differences in the frequency of preparation for bias conversations at home and acculturation differences that necessitate that youth navigate cultural brokering processes for their families.

Footnotes

We specifically limited recruitment of our Asian families to East and Southeast Asian families and of our Latinx families to those from Mexico and Central America to limit the heterogeneity of the sample. These groups have more similarities in relation to cultural heritage, immigration history, phenotype, and discrimination experiences relative to South Asians and other groups in Latin America (e.g., Caribbean or South America), respectively. For readability, we reference these families in our sample as Asian or Latinx, respectively, in the rest of the manuscript.
