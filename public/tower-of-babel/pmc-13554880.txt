VA-ECMO Weaning in Low-Flow Low-Gradient Aortic Stenosis Post STEMI and ECPR
Authors: Erich Burton, David Sosa, Michael Williams, Erik Kraai, Trenton Wray, Nicole Collantes-Silva, Rudra Pratap Singh, Pamela Hsu

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow-flow, low-gradient (LF-LG) severe aortic stenosis (AS) in post–ST-elevation myocardial infarction cardiogenic shock supported with veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) presents complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Retrograde extracorporeal membrane oxygenation flow alters cardiac loading conditions and transvalvular flow, complicating accurate assessment of valvular disease.Case SummaryA 52-year-old woman with inferolateral ST-elevation myocardial infarction complicated by ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest, was placed on VA-ECMO support. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation weaning attempts failed due to severe bicuspid LF-LG AS. After multidisciplinary evaluation, she underwent transcatheter aortic valve implantation and was subsequently successfully weaned from VA-ECMO.DiscussionMechanical circulatory support alters preload, afterload, and transvalvular flow, making differentiation between true severe AS and low-flow states from cardiogenic shock or ventricular dysfunction difficult. No societal guidelines exist for grading LF-LG AS severity or guiding VA-ECMO weaning in this setting.Take-Home MessagesVA-ECMO complicates LF-LG AS assessment. Multimodal, multidisciplinary evaluation enables accurate diagnosis and appropriate intervention.

History of Presentation

A 52-year-old woman presented with inferolateral ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) complicated by witnessed ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest in our emergency department. Despite several rounds of chest compressions and defibrillation, there was no return of spontaneous circulation. Given the potentially reversible etiology and low likelihood of sustained return of spontaneous circulation, extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation and emergent bifemoral veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) were initiated. VA-ECMO flows commenced within 45 minutes of arrest period.Take-Home Messages•VA-ECMO complicates AS severity assessment, and a multimodal, multidisciplinary approach allows accurate diagnosis and guides accurate assessment and safe intervention.•Assessing readiness to be weaned from VA-ECMO in the setting of LF-LG AS is difficult and requires multimodal monitoring during weaning. Transcatheter aortic valve implantation may enable VA-ECMO liberation in patients with inability to wean due to severe AS.

Past Medical History

Past medical history was significant for tobacco use, type 2 diabetes, and hyperlipidemia.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis included valvular disease, ischemic cardiomyopathy, mechanical complication of STEMI, and acute STEMI with cardiogenic shock.

Investigations

Following initiation of VA-ECMO, transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated a calcified aortic valve (Figure 1), absence of aortic valve opening, and near cardiac standstill. Vitals were heart rate 97 beats/min, blood pressure 54/44 mm Hg, pulse pressure 10, respiratory rate 26 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 76%. Following intravenous fluids and vasopressor optimization, blood pressure improved to 82/65 mm Hg, pulse pressure 17, and oxygen saturation 86%. Coronary angiogram demonstrated a 100% right coronary artery occlusion which was successfully revascularized with a drug-eluting stent. Pulmonary artery catheter (PAC) showed normal filling pressures without pulmonary hypertension, cardiac output 6.6 L/min, cardiac index 3.9 L/min/m2, cardiac power output 1.4 W, and pulmonary artery pulsatility index 1. Placement of a percutaneous axial flow mechanical circulatory support device or intra-aortic balloon pump for left ventricular venting was deferred given normal filling pressures, pulse pressure >15 mm Hg, aortic valve opening, and absence of severe pulmonary edema.Figure 1Resuscitative Transesophageal Echocardiogram2-dimensional transesophageal echocardiogram midesophageal right ventricular inflow-outflow view (55°) obtained immediately following extracorporeal membrane oxygenation cannulation demonstrating severe aortic valve calcification.

On hospital day 2, the patient developed an upper gastrointestinal bleed precluding further transesophageal echocardiography. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 20%, severely reduced right ventricular function, severe aortic valve calcification, and 2-dimensional images could not rule out a bicuspid aortic valve (Figure 2). On hospital day 3, with sedation withheld, the patient was noted to be awake and following commands. In addition, the left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 35% to 40% and a weaning trial was performed. When extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) flow was reduced to 1 L/min, systemic and pulmonary artery hemodynamics did not change. As flows decreased to 0.5 L/min, the patient developed tachycardia, hypotension, increased pulmonary artery diastolic pressure, respiratory distress, hypoxemia, and ST-segment elevation on telemetry that resolved when flows were increased above 1 L/min. This process repeated each time flow was reduced below 1 L/min. Given the concern for low-flow low-gradient aortic stenosis (LF-LG AS), we performed a low-dose dobutamine stress echocardiogram the same day. Baseline parameters: left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) area 2.7 cm2, LVOT velocity time integral (VTI) 6.2 cm, stroke volume 16.74 mL, stroke volume index 9.3 mL/m2, aortic valve peak velocity 2.2 m/s, aortic valve area by continuity equation of 0.67 cm2 (Figures 3 and 4), and dimensionless index of 0.23. ECMO flows were decreased to 1 L/min. Dobutamine was titrated from 5 to a maximal dose of 10 μg/kg/min. Management

Because the patient could not be weaned from VA-ECMO due to LF-LG AS, a multidisciplinary team decision was made to perform a transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) on hospital day 5. The procedure was performed using a 23-mm Edwards Sapien Resilla valve, deployed to nominal pressure in an 80/20 position. Balloon aortic valvuloplasty for a bicuspid aortic valve was deferred because of anatomy of the valve and the risk of acute severe aortic regurgitation while on VA-ECMO. Surgical aortic valve replacement was considered high risk due to cardiogenic shock, recent STEMI, and VA-ECMO requirement.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Post deployment of the valve, transvalvular gradient decreased to 2 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure decreased to 11 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction was 34%. The following day, the patient completed a retrograde flow trial and was uneventfully decannulated. The patient discharged on hospital day 13 to a rehabilitation facility, neurologically intact, with a cerebral performance category score of 1.

Discussion

Our case highlights 2 clinical challenges: accurate assessment of AS severity during VA-ECMO support and determination of readiness for ECMO weaning in severe AS.

Grading AS severity relies on hemodynamic assumptions that are substantially altered during VA-ECMO. Standard echocardiographic indices including transvalvular gradients, Doppler-derived stroke volume, and valve area by the continuity equation are flow-dependent and assume relatively stable preload, afterload, and pulsatility.1, 2, 3 ECMO reduces native forward flow, and retrograde arterial circuit flow increases left ventricular afterload, reducing anterograde aortic ejection, leading to underestimation of stenosis severity.4 In a porcine model of cardiogenic shock with severe AS, escalating ECMO flows led to increased mean arterial pressure and lower left ventricular end-diastolic pressures. This is secondary to decreased preload achieved by increasing venous drainage, and the stenotic valve acting as a barrier, preventing retrograde transmission of VA-ECMO–generated afterload to the left ventricle.5 Although the left ventricle is protected from the increased afterload in AS, the combination of elevated aortic pressure from ECMO and fixed valvular obstruction reduces transvalvular flow. This leads to artificially low peak velocities, mean gradients, and underestimation of severity.

Although left ventricular–aortic pullback is the gold standard for assessing severity when echocardiographic findings are discordant, it remains limited by similar flow-dependent limitations.6,7 Computed tomography aortic valve calcium scoring provides an adjunctive method for severity assessment and may guide therapeutic decisions.8 Calcium score thresholds are better validated in calcific trileaflet aortic valves; however, in bicuspid valves, fibrotic remodeling predominates. The patient's studies including an aortic valve calcium score of 984, were consistent with severe bicuspid LF-LG AS, supporting TAVI candidacy.9

No societal guidelines exist for distinguishing true-severe from pseudo-severe AS in LF-LG on VA-ECMO. One challenge of stress echocardiogram is that in the absence of flow reserve, differentiating the contribution of the stenotic valve from the afterload created by ECMO flow becomes difficult. Weaning from VA-ECMO presents another challenge. As the principle of hemodynamic management in severe AS is “slow, full, and constricted,” higher flows may provide optimal support for unstable patients with severe AS. During weaning, however, afterload is suddenly decreased, often inducing reflex tachycardia, removing 2 of the 3 hemodynamic principles. This decreases coronary perfusion and increases myocardial oxygen demand, particularly in patients with concomitant coronary ischemia. This hemodynamic response also can be observed in patients with biventricular failure without AS who are not yet ready for weaning. Differentiating biventricular failure vs severe AS as the primary driver of VA-ECMO weaning failure remains challenging.5

Societal recommendations addressing the management of severe AS on VA-ECMO are lacking. In our case, we found it helpful to follow PAC data during weaning (Figure 8). Although thermodilution-derived cardiac output measurements are unreliable during normal flows on VA-ECMO, monitoring pulmonary artery diastolic pressure may help estimate left-sided filling pressures. Our patient met criteria for VA-ECMO weaning, including minimal vasopressor support, normotension, and strong pulsatility. When flow was reduced below 1 L/min we expected increased native cardiac output, normotension, and unchanged PAC numbers. Instead, the pulmonary artery diastolic pressure nearly doubled, right atrial pressure consistently 7 to 10 mm Hg, and hypotension, tachycardia, and hypoxemia would develop. Noting the isolated increase in pulmonary artery diastolic pressure and pulmonary edema while maintaining normal right atrial pressure, we considered that severe AS, rather than biventricular failure, was the primary contributor to weaning failure. This informed our decision to perform a TAVI before decannulation. If decannulation was attempted without addressing the stenosis, severe cardiogenic shock may have resulted. After TAVI, the need to keep high afterload and a slow heart rate was reduced, allowing for successful VA-ECMO weaning. This case illustrates the role of multimodality hemodynamic assessment to guide management and successful outcomes in patients with severe AS requiring VA-ECMO.

Conclusions

Our case highlights the complex interaction among cardiogenic shock, severe LF-LG AS, and VA-ECMO support. VA-ECMO alters loading conditions, complicating accurate grading of severity and obscures contributions of valvular stenosis vs biventricular dysfunction due to cardiogenic shock. A multimodal and multidisciplinary approach allows accurate diagnosis and guides safe intervention.
