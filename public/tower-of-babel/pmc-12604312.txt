KLC3 drives gastric cancer progression by stabilizing SLC2A5 to activate MAPK signaling and promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition
Authors: Zhen Ma, Bin Ma, Mali Chen, Puyi He, Xiangjie Li, Lin Xiang, Hao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) is associated with poor prognosis due to its intricate molecular mechanisms that drive multiple malignant functions. While KLC3 has been found to be abnormally expressed in various tumors, its specific mechanistic role in GC remains largely unknown.AimsThis study aimed to elucidate the functional role of KLC3 in GC and to investigate its involvement in regulating the MAPK signaling pathway via SLC2A5.MethodsThe expression of KLC3 was evaluated in GC tissues and cell lines. Functional assays (CCK8, colony formation, wound healing, Transwell) and an in vivo xenograft model were used to assess its role in proliferation, invasion, and migration. Transcriptome sequencing, co-immunoprecipitation, and rescue experiments were employed to uncover the underlying mechanisms.ResultsKLC3 was significantly upregulated in GC and associated with poor patient survival. Its knockdown suppressed GC cell growth, invasion, and migration in vitro and in vivo, attenuated EMT, and reduced SLC2A5 expression. Mechanistically, KLC3 interacts with SLC2A5, potentially regulating its membrane localization and stability, and thus activating the MAPK pathway. Crucially, overexpression of SLC2A5 rescued the inhibitory effects of KLC3 knockdown on the MAPK pathway and EMT.ConclusionThis study identifies KLC3 as a key oncoprotein that promotes GC progression by modulating the MAPK signaling pathway through a novel interaction with the fructose transporter SLC2A5. The KLC3-SLC2A5 axis may serve as a critical link between cellular motor machinery and metabolic reprogramming in cancer, presenting a promising therapeutic target for GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15084-x.Keywords: KLC3, SLC2A5, Gastric cancer, Migration, Proliferation, MAPK signaling

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a formidable global health challenge, representing a significant cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Despite considerable advances in diagnostic techniques and therapeutic modalities over the years–from surgery and chemotherapy to targeted therapies and immunotherapy–the overall prognosis for advanced GC remains dismal, with a 5-year survival rate that is persistently low [2, 3]. This underscores the critical and unmet need to deepen our understanding of the molecular drivers of GC pathogenesis and to identify novel therapeutic targets that can improve patient outcomes.

Gaining a profound understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying GC development is paramount for developing targeted therapeutic strategies and improving patient outcomes. Uncovering these mechanisms could pave the way for novel treatment approaches and provide valuable insights into the biological underpinnings of GC. The kinesin superfamily, an extensive group comprising 14 distinct proteins, plays a pivotal role in various cellular processes critical for tumorigenesis, such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and the transportation of tumor cells [4, 5]. Specifically, kinesin, a protein complex composed of two heavy chains (KHCs) and two light chains (KLCs), forms a heterotetramer with an approximate molecular weight of 380 kDa [6]. Notably, recent studies have implicated KLCs, primarily responsible for binding intracellular components, in tumorigenesis and chemotherapy resistance [7, 8].

Within the KLC family, four distinct isoforms exist: KLC1, KLC2, KLC3, and KLC4. KLC1 has been demonstrated to contribute to chemoresistance in lung cancer and to promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in breast cancer [7, 9]. Similarly, KLC2 has been shown to enhance radioresistance in lung cancer cells and is associated with a poor prognosis in non-small cell lung cancer patients [8, 10]. Extensive research has also focused on KLC4, which has been found to increase chemotherapy resistance in lung cancer and to enhance radioresistance in cervical cancer cells [11, 12].

However, the specific role of KLC3 in GC remains largely unexplored, necessitating further investigation to elucidate its potential contributions to GC pathogenesis and progression.This lack of knowledge regarding the involvement of KLC3 in tumorigenesis and cancer cell behavior constitutes a notable gap in our understanding of the broader contributions of this vital protein family to cancer biology. Elucidating the specific function of KLC3 in tumorigenesis and cancer cell behavior could yield profound insights into cancer biology and potentially reveal new therapeutic targets for cancer treatment.

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the expression patterns and prognostic significance of KLC3 in gastric cancer. Furthermore, we sought to explore the biological function of KLC3 in GC cells both in vitro and in vivo. By addressing these aims, we aimed to contribute to the growing body of knowledge on the kinesin superfamily’s role in cancer and potentially identify novel strategies for improving GC outcomes.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens and cell lines

During the period from October to November 2023, a total of 5 samples were collected from patients with gastric adenocarcinoma. Each sample included both the tumor tissue and its corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissue, which served as a control. These samples were primarily utilized for Western blot (WB) analysis to investigate the expression levels of KLC3 in GC. Additionally, to assess the prognostic relevance of KLC3 in a larger cohort, we retrospectively analyzed data from 88 patients with GC who underwent radical gastrectomy at the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University between January 2016 and December 2022. These patients’ tissue specimens were subjected to immunohistochemical(IHC) analysis. All patients were confirmed to have GC through pathological examination, and none of them had received targeted therapy, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy prior to surgery. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of The Second Hospital of Lanzhou University, ensuring adherence to ethical principles and guidelines.

For in vitro experiments, three human gastric cancer cell lines were employed: MKN-45, HGC-27, and AGS. These cell lines were acquired from the Cell Resource Center, Institute of Basic Medical Sciences (CAMS/PUMC), and were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The cells were cultured under standard conditions, including an appropriate temperature, humidity, and gas environment, to ensure their viability and growth for subsequent experiments.

Cell transfection

Lentiviral particles containing the desired constructs were procured from Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. For the purposes of this study, the experimental group was stratified into multiple subgroups, including wild-type (untreated) cells, a negative control (NC) group, and three groups (AGS#64, AGS#65, and AGS#66) transfected with lentiviruses encoding specific constructs. The multiplicity of infection (MOI) was carefully determined to optimize the transfection efficiency. Approximately 72 h post-infection, the cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope to assess the transfection efficiency, ensuring that a minimum of 90% of the cells exhibited successful transduction for inclusion in subsequent experiments.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol reagent (Shanghai Pufang Biotech Co., Ltd., China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using Bulge-Loop miRNA qPCR primer sets (Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd., China) to synthesize cDNA. qRT-PCR was then carried out on a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Roche (China) Co., Ltd.), employing SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara Bio Co., Ltd., China) for amplification. β-actin served as the housekeeping gene for normalization. The primer sequences used for qPCR were designed as follows:

β-actin, forward: 5’-GCGTGACATTAAGGAGAAGC-3’,reverse:5’-CCACGTCACACTTCATGATGG-3’.

KLC3, forward: 5’-CTCAACATCCTGGCGCTGGT-3’, reverse: 5’-TCCCGGTAACGCCCACGCTTC-3’.

VGLL1, forward: 5’-AACCTGCTCTGAAGTGAGCC-3’, reverse: 5’-ATGACACAGGTGACAGCTCTT-3’.

SLC2A5,forward:5’-GGTGGGAATATGTGCAGGTGT-3’, reverse: 5’-TGCTCCCGAGATGTCCTGAA-3’.

ADI1,forward: 5’-GGAGTTTGTTGTTCACTGGATGC-3’, reverse: 5’-GCGGATCTCATCGTCCAAGT-3’.

CGB8, forward: 5’-CTACTGCCCCACCATGACCC-3’, reverse: 5’-GGTAGTTGCACACCACCTGA-3’.

PRR22, forward: 5’-CAGCACCCCAAACCGTTCT-3’, reverse: 5’-TACAGGTTCAAGGAGCCCAT-3’.

Experimental tumorigenesis in nude mice

This study was conducted under the strict adherence to ethical guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital of Lanzhou University, ensuring compliance with all international standards for animal experimentation. Animal studies were designed and reported in consideration of principles for robust xenograft research [13]. Twelve female BALB/c Nude mice, sourced from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., were carefully selected and randomly allocated into four experimental groups, each consisting of three animals. This randomization process aimed to minimize potential biases and ensure the reliability of the findings.

The experimental design focused on investigating the tumorigenic potential of two distinct cell lines and their derivatives. Specifically, the first two groups of mice were subcutaneously injected with MKN45 and MKN45#66 cells, respectively.Similarly, the remaining two groups were injected with HGC27 and HGC27#66 cells, while AGS cells were excluded from the study due to their inability to induce tumor formation in nude mice, as preliminarily assessed.

Over the subsequent three weeks, the mice were closely monitored for signs of tumor development, including palpable masses at the injection sites. Regular observations were recorded, and the animals’ overall health status was assessed to ensure minimal distress. Any adverse effects or unexpected outcomes were promptly reported to the ethics committee and addressed accordingly.

Upon completion of the observation period, and guided by the ethics committee’s recommendations, humane euthanasia was performed on the mice. This process involved the administration of a triple overdose of pentobarbital sodium, a commonly used anesthetic agent for euthanasia in animal studies, ensuring a peaceful and painless death. Following euthanasia, the tumors were carefully excised from the mice, ensuring complete removal to prevent any tissue damage or contamination.

Subsequently, the excised tumors underwent rigorous processing for weight measurements. This step was crucial for quantifying the tumor burden in each mouse and comparing the tumorigenic potential of the different cell lines. The weights were recorded with precision, and the data was analyzed statistically to identify any significant differences between the groups.

IHC Analysis

The immunohistochemical staining protocol was meticulously adapted from a previously established study conducted within our laboratory [14], ensuring consistency and reliability. The primary antibody utilized specifically targeted KLC3 and was sourced from abcam (USA, catalog number ab180523), with a dilution ratio of 1:150. A total of twenty pairs of cancerous tissues and their adjacent non-cancerous counterparts were carefully selected for assessment, aiming to elucidate the differential expression levels between these two tissue types.

CCK-8 Proliferation and colony formation assays

To evaluate cell proliferation, both wild-type and transfected cells were seeded into 96-well plates and subjected to the CCK-8 assay. For three consecutive days, CCK-8 reagent was added to the wells, followed by a 2-hour incubation period. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was precisely measured using a microplate reader, providing quantitative insights into cell proliferation rates.

For the colony formation assay, wild-type and transfected cells were plated into 6-well plates at a uniform density of 1000 cells per well. Following a 14-day incubation period, the resulting colonies were fixed, stained, and the number of colony-forming units per well was meticulously counted, offering a visual and quantitative assessment of the cells’ ability to form colonies.

Wound healing migration and transwell migration assays

Cell migration and invasion abilities were assessed using wound healing and Transwell assays, which are well-established and standard techniques for evaluating these cancer cell behaviors.The wound healing migration assay commenced by seeding cells into 6-well plates until they achieved confluence. Subsequently, a standardized wound was introduced into the monolayer by gently scraping with a yellow pipette tip. The width of each wound was carefully calibrated under a microscope to ensure uniformity across samples. Cell migration into the wounded area was then monitored over time using an optical microscope, with experiments repeated three times to ensure reproducibility and statistical significance.

In the Transwell migration assay, 24-well plates equipped with 8 μm pore size membranes (Corning Inc., Lowell, MA, USA) were employed. The upper chamber was inoculated with serum-free media containing 2 × 10^4 cells, whereas the lower chamber was filled with complete media, creating a chemotactic gradient. After 24 h of incubation, non-migratory cells in the upper chamber were gently removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells adhering to the lower surface of the membrane were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with Giemsa, and counted in ten randomly selected fields of view under a microscope. This experiment was also conducted in triplicate to guarantee the reliability and robustness of the findings.

WB Analysis

Total protein extraction was carried out using a combination of RIPA lysis buffer and PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at a standardized ratio of 1:100 to ensure optimal protein yield. The extracted proteins were then subjected to SDS-PAGE for separation based on their molecular weights. Following electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane utilizing a Bio-Rad Laboratories transfer device (USA), ensuring efficient and uniform transfer.

The PVDF membrane was subsequently blocked to minimize non-specific binding and then incubated with a panel of primary antibodies specific to KLC3, E-cadherin, N-cadherin, vimentin, snail, Wnt1, Wnt3a, CCND1 (all sourced from Abcam, USA), as well as rabbit anti-human GAPDH, Tubulin (also from Abcam, USA), and SLC2A5 (Proteintech Group, Inc.). These antibodies allowed for the detection of key proteins involved in cellular processes such as migration, invasion, and proliferation.

After thorough incubation with the primary antibodies, the membrane was exposed using an ultrasensitive ECL chemiluminescence kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), facilitating visualization of the protein bands. To ensure accurate quantification, all results were normalized against internal reference proteins, specifically GAPDH and Tubulin, which served as loading controls.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the collected data was conducted using two widely recognized software packages: IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (San Diego, CA, USA). For quantitative data, Student’s t-test was employed to compare differences between two groups, while one-way ANOVA was utilized for comparisons involving more than two groups. For categorical data, chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were selected based on the appropriateness for the specific data set. A significance level of P < 0.05 was adopted throughout the analysis, indicating statistical significance.

KLC3 Promotes GC Cell Proliferation, Invasion, and Migration In Vitro and In Vivo

To elucidate the functional role of KLC3 in gastric cancer (GC) cells, we conducted a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments. Initially, we downregulated KLC3 expression in AGS, MKN-45, and HGC-27 GC cell lines using shKLC3 lentivirus. PCR analysis confirmed the successful knockdown of KLC3, with the #66 sequence exhibiting the most efficient knockdown (Fig. 2A). Western blotting further validated the knockdown efficiency, demonstrating over 80% reduction in KLC3 protein levels in all three cell lines (Fig. 2B), making them suitable for subsequent functional studies.To ensure the specificity of the observed phenotypes, we used three independent shRNA sequences targeting KLC3. Notably, the magnitude of the inhibitory effects on proliferation, migration, and invasion (Fig. 2C-F) correlated with the efficiency of KLC3 knockdown (Fig. 2A, B), with shRNA-#66 showing the most potent effect. This dose-dependent relationship strengthens the conclusion that these phenotypes are specifically caused by the loss of KLC3.

In vitro studies

Proliferation Assays: CCK8 and colony formation assays were performed to assess the impact of KLC3 reduction on GC cell proliferation. Both assays consistently showed that downregulation of KLC3 significantly inhibited the proliferation of AGS, MKN-45, and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 2C-D). These results indicate that KLC3 promotes GC cell growth.

Migration Assays: Wound healing experiments revealed that GC cells with reduced KLC3 expression exhibited a significantly decreased migration ability compared to control cells (Fig. 2E). This finding suggests that KLC3 is involved in enhancing GC cell migration.

Invasion Assays: Transwell migration assays were conducted to evaluate the effect of KLC3 on GC cell invasion. The results demonstrated that downregulation of KLC3 significantly inhibited the invasion ability of GC cells (Fig. 2F). This indicates that KLC3 also promotes GC cell invasion.

In vivo studies

To validate our in vitro findings, we established a nude mouse xenograft model using MKN45 and HGC27 cells. Since AGS cells are non-tumorigenic in this model, they were excluded. After 4 weeks, tumors in the KLC3 knockdown groups were significantly smaller than those in the wild-type groups for both MKN45 (Fig. 3A) and HGC27 (Fig. 3B) cells. This observation confirms that KLC3 promotes GC tumor growth in vivo.

In summary, our findings conclusively demonstrate that KLC3 promotes the proliferation and migration of gastric cancer (GC) cells, as evidenced by both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Immunohistochemical analysis of xenograft tumors derived from nude mice revealed a marked reduction in KLC3 expression levels specifically in tumors subjected to KLC3 knockdown. Importantly, compared to the control group, the expression of proliferation markers, notably PCNA, was significantly downregulated in the KLC3 knockdown group (Fig. 3C, D). These results highlight the pivotal role of KLC3 in driving the aggressive phenotype of GC cells.

KLC3 influences GC invasion and migration through EMT

Guided by our preceding findings, we delved into the underlying mechanism of KLC3’s stimulatory effect on GC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. As EMT is a pivotal process in GC cell invasion and migration, we investigated the expression of genes implicated in this transition. Notably, upon reduction of KLC3 expression, we observed an upregulation of E-cadherin (Fig. 4A), whereas the expression of N-cadherin, Snail, Vimentin, CCND1, wnt1, and wnt3a was downregulated (Fig. 4B). This pattern of gene expression alterations suggests that the decreased KLC3 levels weaken the invasive and migratory capabilities of GC cells, potentially via attenuation of EMT.

KLC3 targets SLC2A5 to positively regulate the MAPK pathway

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underpinning KLC3’s role in gastric cancer progression, we conducted an extensive analysis of the transcriptome. RNA-seq analysis of AGS and shAGS cells revealed alterations in 979 genes (Fig. 5A), with 813 genes downregulated and 166 genes upregulated in the KLC3 knockdown group compared to the wild-type group (Fig. 5C). Among the downregulated genes, SLC2A5 stood out as a candidate of interest, as confirmed by PCR validation (Fig. 5B) and Western blot analysis, which demonstrated a concurrent decrease in SLC2A5 protein levels with KLC3 reduction (Fig. 5F).

In terms of signaling pathways, differential gene expression analysis revealed a prominent enrichment in the MAPK pathway (6.67%), followed by the PI3K/AKT pathway (5.93%) (Fig. 5D, E). To validate these findings at the protein level, we assessed key components of the MAPK pathway. While the total levels of Erk1/2 and p38 remained unchanged, we observed a decrease in the phosphorylation of pErk1/2 and p-p38 (Fig. 5G, H). Collectively, our results indicate that KLC3 targets SLC2A5 to positively regulate MAPK pathway signaling, thereby contributing to the promotion of GC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration.

Functional rescue of KLC3 knockdown effects by SLC2A5 overexpression

To further substantiate the functional relationship between KLC3 and SLC2A5, we sought to determine whether the overexpression of SLC2A5 could rescue the phenotypic alterations observed in AGS cells following KLC3 downregulation. Initially, we validated the interaction between KLC3 and SLC2A5 using co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) assays, confirming their physical association (Fig. 6A). This interaction provided a basis for investigating the potential functional link between the two proteins.

B Restoration of Gene Expression by SLC2A5 Overexpression: Western blot analysis was performed to assess the effects of SLC2A5 overexpression on the downregulation of CCND1, Wnt3a, and Wnt1 caused by KLC3 depletion in AGS cells. Next, we performed functional rescue experiments by overexpressing SLC2A5 in AGS cells with KLC3 knockdown. Our results demonstrated that SLC2A5 overexpression significantly upregulated the expression of CCND1, Wnt3a, and Wnt1 compared to cells with KLC3 knockdown alone (Fig. 6B). This upregulation suggests that SLC2A5 can compensate for the loss of KLC3-mediated signaling to maintain the expression of these oncogenic factors.

Furthermore, we examined the impact of SLC2A5 overexpression on the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process and MAPK pathway activation. As shown in Fig. 6C and D, the overexpression of SLC2A5 alleviated the EMT phenotype induced by KLC3 knockdown, as evidenced by changes in EMT marker expression. Concurrently, the MAPK pathway was reactivated, with increased phosphorylation of pErk1/2 and p-p38 (Fig. 6D). These findings indicate that SLC2A5 overexpression is able to partially restore the oncogenic signaling disrupted by KLC3 knockdown.

Collectively, our results demonstrate that SLC2A5 plays a pivotal role in mediating the oncogenic effects of KLC3 in AGS cells. By interacting with KLC3 and maintaining the activation of downstream signaling pathways, SLC2A5 contributes to the maintenance of the malignant phenotype in gastric cancer cells. The functional rescue of KLC3 knockdown effects by SLC2A5 overexpression further underscores the importance of this interaction in gastric cancer progression.

Discuss

GC remains a lethal malignancy with limited therapeutic options and poor prognosis for advanced disease [1, 2]. Our study identifies kinesin light chain 3, a previously overlooked member of the molecular motor machinery, as a novel and potent oncoprotein in GC. We demonstrate that KLC3 is significantly upregulated in GC tissues and cell lines, where its expression correlates with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor patient survival. Through integrated in vitro and in vivo approaches, we established that KLC3 is essential for sustaining the proliferative, invasive, and migratory capacities of GC cells. Mechanistically, we unveil a previously unrecognized signaling axis wherein KLC3 interacts with and stabilizes the fructose transporter SLC2A5, thereby activating the MAPK pathway, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and ultimately driving tumor progression.

A model for KLC3-Mediated regulation of SLC2A5 and MAPK signaling

The core finding of our study is the functional and physical interaction between KLC3 and SLC2A5. While our co-immunoprecipitation data confirm this interaction, the concomitant decrease in SLC2A5 mRNA and protein upon KLC3 knockdown suggests a complex regulatory relationship. Based on the canonical role of kinesins in intracellular transport, we propose a model wherein the KLC3-containing kinesin complex is responsible for the microtubule-dependent trafficking and delivery of SLC2A5 to the plasma membrane [15]. In this scenario, KLC3 knockdown disrupts anterograde transport, leading to SLC2A5 mislocalization, cytoplasmic accumulation, and subsequent degradation. This model elegantly explains the observed reduction in SLC2A5 and provides a novel mechanism by which a component of the motor protein machinery directly regulates a metabolic transporter.

The critical downstream consequence of this regulation is the activation of the MAPK pathway. Our rescue experiments unequivocally place SLC2A5 as the key mediator, as its overexpression reversed the suppression of MAPK phosphorylation (p-ERK, p-p38) and the EMT phenotype caused by KLC3 depletion. We hypothesize that SLC2A5-mediated fructose uptake and metabolism (fructolysis) generates metabolic intermediates and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [16, 17], which are potent activators of the MAPK cascade through the oxidation of phosphatases or kinases [18]. Thus, our work delineates a clear pathway: KLC3 → SLC2A5 Membrane Localization/Stability → Fructose Metabolism → (ROS) → MAPK Activation → EMT → GC Progression.

SLC2A5: A metabolic hub linking environment to tumorigenesis

The role of SLC2A5 (GLUT5) extends beyond being a passive downstream target; it positions our findings at the nexus of environmental influences and intrinsic oncogenic signaling. As the primary high-affinity fructose transporter, SLC2A5 is a direct molecular link between modern high-fructose diets and GC progression, providing a plausible mechanism for diet-induced carcinogenesis.

Furthermore, this axis is likely modulated by the gastric microenvironment, particularly Helicobacter pylori infection. H. pylori is a potent driver of pro-migratory and EMT-like signaling [19, 20], and intriguingly, anti-H. pylori agents like Terminalia chebula extracts modulate inflammatory and stress pathways [21]. It is a compelling possibility that H. pylori virulence factors (e.g., CagA) upregulate or potentiate the KLC3-SLC2A5 axis, thereby mechanistically connecting this major environmental risk factor to metastatic progression. This would establish SLC2A5 as a central metabolic hub that integrates signals from diet and infection to drive tumor-promoting signaling within gastric epithelial cells.

Broader implications: KLC3 as a Non-Canonical regulator in oncology

Our findings resonate with an emerging paradigm in cancer biology: that so-called “non-canonical” molecules are pivotal regulators of classic hallmarks of cancer. KLC3 joins a growing list of unconventional pathway regulators, such as ion channels. For instance, the voltage-gated sodium channel β3 subunit modulates glioma cell motility via cytoskeletal dynamics independent of its ion-conducting function [22], and Nav channels can regulate MAPK signaling through non-classical mechanisms [23]. Our discovery that a kinesin light chain can regulate a core pathway like MAPK through metabolic reprogramming significantly expands the functional repertoire of motor proteins beyond cargo transport. This perspective not only strengthens the biological plausibility of our model but also opens new avenues for therapeutic intervention by targeting these non-classical oncoprotein modulators.

Translational perspectives and future directions

The KLC3-SLC2A5-MAPK axis presents several promising translational avenues. First, the strong association of KLC3/SLC2A5 with aggressive disease positions them as potential prognostic biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets. The development of SLC2A5 inhibitors to disrupt fructose uptake represents a viable strategy. The feasibility of targeting such unconventional molecules is supported by precedents in drug repurposing, such as the anti-tumor properties of local anesthetics [24–26], and the strategic use of targeted toxicity, as exemplified by arsenic trioxide in leukemia [18, 27–29].

Second, our findings invite exploration into the role of this axis within the tumor microenvironment (TME). It is plausible that KLC3-SLC2A5-driven fructose metabolism in cancer cells alters the secretion of oncogenic metabolites or signaling molecules packaged into extracellular vesicles (EVs), which could reprogram the entire TME to support tumor growth [30].

Finally, the transition to less invasive diagnostics is paramount. Assessing KLC3 or SLC2A5 in liquid biopsies—detecting their mRNA, protein, or EVs in circulation—presents an exciting frontier for monitoring treatment response and disease recurrence, as actively explored in other cancers [31, 32].

Limitations

We acknowledge the limitations of our study. The precise molecular details of the KLC3-SLC2A5 interaction and the specific metabolic intermediates or ROS sensors involved in MAPK activation remain to be fully elucidated. Furthermore, the clinical validation, particularly the Western blot analysis with five paired tissues, is based on a limited sample size. Future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts and metastatic in vivo models are warranted to fully confirm the clinical relevance and therapeutic potential of this axis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our work establishes KLC3 as a significant driver of GC progression through a novel mechanism involving SLC2A5 and MAPK signaling. We provide a model that not only explains how KLC3 promotes aggressive tumor phenotypes but also links cellular machinery to environmental factors like diet and infection. The KLC3-SLC2A5-MAPK axis represents a promising therapeutic target and a compelling example of the expanding universe of non-canonical regulation in cancer biology.
