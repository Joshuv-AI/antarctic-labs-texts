Abstract
A conforming partition of a rectilinear n n -gon P P (possibly with holes) is a partition of P P into rectangles without using Steiner points (i.e., all corners of all rectangles must lie on the boundary of P P ). The stabbing number of such a partition is the maximum number of rectangles intersected by an axis-aligned segment lying in the interior of P P . In this paper, we examine the problem of computing conforming partitions with low stabbing number. We show that computing a conforming partition with stabbing number at most 4 4 is 𝒩 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{N\!P} -hard, which strengthens a previously known hardness result [Durocher & Mehrabi, Theor. Comput. Sci. 689: 157-168 (2017)] and eliminates the possibility for fixed-parameter-tractable algorithms parameterized by the stabbing number unless 𝒫 = 𝒩 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N\!P} . In contrast, we give (i) an O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) -time algorithm to decide whether a conforming partition with stabbing number 2 2 exists, (ii) a fixed-parameter-tractable algorithm parameterized by both the stabbing number and treewidth of the pixel graph of the polygon, and (iii) a fixed-parameter-tractable algorithm parameterized by the stabbing number for polygons without holes in general position.

Partitioning an

We are interested in the

Preliminaries

Throughout the article, the polygons we consider are all simple (i.e., without self-intersections), rectilinear (i.e., the edges are axis-aligned) and may have holes (unless mentioned otherwise).
Although we do not make it a general assumption, we say that a polygon
P
is in

if no three vertices of
P
lie on one axis-aligned line (Figure

Moreover, we use the terminology of the Euclidean topology of the plane: a set of points in the plane may be

\mathbf{s}

Next, we recall the definitions leading to the notion of

Given a polygon

, a finite partition of

is

Alternatively, a rectangular partition is obtained by cutting
P
along disjoint axis-aligned open segments (i.e., segments excluding endpoints) lying in the interior of
P
that are of maximal length.
We say that such a set of segments is a
cut set
, and freely consider a rectangular partition as a cut set.
Given a polygon
P
, a
stabbing segment
of

is an axis-aligned line segment that lies in the interior of
P
.
Given a polygon

and a rectangular partition

of

is the maximum, over all stabbing segments
\mathbf{s}
of

\mathbf{R}

\mathbf{s}
.
A rectangular partition of

3
Intractability of Stabbing Number
4
or More

In this section, we prove the following
\mathcal{N\!P}
-completeness result.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all integers
k\geq 4
, the decision problems
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{STAB}
and
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
are
\mathcal{N\!P}
-complete.
Moreover,
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{STAB}
and
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
remain
\mathcal{N\!P}
-complete even for thin polygons (possibly with holes).
Furthermore,
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
remains
\mathcal{N\!P}
-complete even for rectilinear polygons (possibly with holes) in general position.

Before proving this result, we point out that Theorem
3.1
does not state anything about rectilinear polygons that are both thin and in general position.
Indeed, for a rectilinear polygon that is both thin and in general position,
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{STAB}
and
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
are solvable in polynomial time;
see Lemma
4.5
.
Further note that Theorem
3.1
does not state that
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{STAB}
is
\mathcal{N\!P}
-complete for polygons in general position as this remains an open problem.

Proof structure.

Thanks to Lemma 1 in
[
1
]
, we know that, to minimize the stabbing number of a rectangular partition, it suffices to restrict to anchored segments.
In other words, it suffices to restrict to the
O(n^{2})
vertices of the pixel graph for the Steiner points.
It is then straightforward to encode a polynomial-length certificate for
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{STAB}
and
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
, and verify its validity in polynomial time. This settles that
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{STAB}
and
k\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
are in
\mathcal{N\!P}
for any integer
k
.
We thus concentrate on proving
\mathcal{N\!P}
-hardness.

First, we prove that
4\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
is
\mathcal{N\!P}
-hard in thin polygons.
In a thin polygon, any optimal partition is conforming.
Consequently,
4\textnormal{-}\mathsf{STAB}
is also
\mathcal{N\!P}
-hard.
However, the gadgets take advantage of not being in general position.

Second, we provide an alternative version of this proof, this time for polygons in general position (but the gadgets take advantage of not being thin).

Third, using a similar approach and with a similar alternative version for polygons in general position, we prove that
5\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
, and thus
5\textnormal{-}\mathsf{STAB}
, is
\mathcal{N\!P}
-hard.

Finally, we show how to modify our constructions for

Combining gadgets.

We build the polygon

For each variable

We translate these split gadgets such that the right out-stab of one gadget (variable or split) exactly becomes the in-stab of the next gadget.
This leaves

We call these the

We assume that the variable rectangle of

We also assume, after stretching the drawing within the variable rectangle horizontally as needed, that the out-stabs of the literals exactly hit the boundaries of the variable rectangles at the place where the edge channels attach.
We include the edge channels in

As such, the three in-stabs of a clause gadget of clause

We prove that there exists a conforming partition

Without loss of generality, we assume

For the first implication, we are given the partition

We now prove that this assignment satisfies

Because the stabbing number of

Recall that, for split gadgets, the propagated value never increases.

For the converse implication, we are given an assignment satisfying

We partition each variable gadget such that the values propagated by its out-stabs equal the values of the corresponding literals in the assignment.

4
Tractability of Conforming Stabbing Number
2

In this section, we consider
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
, the problem of deciding whether a rectilinear
n
-gon
P
(possibly with holes) has conforming stabbing number at most
2
.
We present two different proofs for the tractability of
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
.
The first proof, the proof of Lemma
4.1
hereafter, is an easy reduction of
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
to
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{SAT}
and yields a quadratic running time decision algorithm.
The second proof, the proof of Theorem
4.1
which spans over the entire section, provides a decision algorithm with an improved running time of
O(n\log n)
.
Interestingly, in this second proof, we also prove that a special class of polygons, that is, the thin polygons in general position always have stabbing number at most
2
(see Lemma
4.5
).

First proof.

We start with a reduction from
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
to
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{SAT}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
P
be a rectilinear
n
-gon (possibly with holes).
Suppose that every stabbing segment intersects at most
\ell
reflex segments.
There exists an algorithm that decides
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
for
P
and provides a solution (if any) in
O(\ell n)
time.

Proof.

We reduce
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
to
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{SAT}
as follows.
We declare a Boolean variable
x(\mathbf{s})
for every reflex segment
\mathbf{s}
. A reflex segment
\mathbf{s}
is used in the solution if and only if
x(\mathbf{s})
is true.
To ensure that we have encoded a conforming partition, we require

(a)

x(\mathbf{h}_{p})\lor x(\mathbf{v}_{p})
for every reflex vertex
p
, as well as

(b)

\lnot x(\mathbf{h}_{p})\lor\lnot x(\mathbf{v}_{q})
for any two intersecting reflex segments
\mathbf{h}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{q}
(recall that reflex segments are open segments and thus do not intersect at their endpoints).

To ensure that the conforming stabbing number is at most
2
, we require that every stabbing segment intersects at most one chosen reflex segment.
In other words, we additionally require

(c)

\lnot x(\mathbf{s}_{1})\lor\lnot x(\mathbf{s}_{2})
for any two reflex segments
\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2}
intersected by a common stabbing segment.

Each of these restrictions only involve two variables, so this gives a
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{SAT}
instance that has
O(n)
variables.
For every reflex segment
\mathbf{s}
, variable
x(\mathbf{s})
belongs to at most two clauses of the first kind,
and at most
\ell
clauses each of the second and the third kind.
Thus, the number of clauses is
O(\ell n)
.
Since
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{SAT}
is decidable in linear time
[
2
]
, the lemma follows.
∎

In an arbitrary polygon there could be stabbing segments that intersect
\Theta(n)
reflex segments, so the running time of the
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{SAT}
approach is
O(n^{2})
in the worst case.
Our main contribution in this section is to give a faster algorithm, with
O(n\log n)
worst-case running time.

Second proof idea.

We call a reflex segment of
P

impossible
if no conforming partition of
P
with stabbing number
2
contains it, and
fixed
if any such conforming partition must contain it.
The idea of the second proof of Theorem
4.1
is an algorithm that determines via some rules that some segments are impossible and some are fixed, from which it deduces other segments to be impossible or fixed, and so on (Figure
12
).
We apply these rules recursively

•

until we find a reflex segment that is both fixed and impossible, meaning that
P\not\in 2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
, or

•

until
saturation
(i.e. until no more rule applies), in which case we show that
P\in 2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
.

The next lemma lists these rules.

Orthogonal ray-shooting.

Before we describe the
O(n\log n)
-time decision algorithm for
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
, note that we cannot afford to compute all intersections between reflex segments (or equivalently, the entire pixel graph), since there may be
\Omega(n^{2})
such intersections
(as in Figure
1
and Figure
18
for example).

Instead, it is possible to derive all required information via
orthogonal ray-shooting
queries, which we define first.
For such queries, consider a set
\mathbf{S}
of disjoint parallel line segments stored in some data structure that we call
orthogonal ray-shooting
data structure.
An orthogonal ray-shooting query receives as input an open ray that is perpendicular to the segments, and it reports the first segment in
\mathbf{S}
that is hit by the ray, or that there is no such segment.
(Here rays are considered open at their origin, i.e., we do not report a segment that only intersects the ray at its origin.)
Giyora and Kaplan
[
17
]
gave an implementation that performs such a query in
O(\log|\mathbf{S}|)
time and that also permits (within the same running time) to delete a segment of
\mathbf{S}
from the data structure.
In the algorithm described next, we use orthogonal ray-shooting data structure as a black box.

Algorithm.

We now describe the decision algorithm using a dynamic programming approach.
The idea is that the algorithm determines the status (i.e., fixed or impossible) for increasingly many segments, until it ends with an impossibility or a partition.
We express whether the status of a segment has been determined with a color scheme.
Initially all reflex segments of
P
are uncolored.
During the execution we color some of the sections in green or in blue while maintaining the following invariants:

1.

The green reflex segments are fixed.

2.

The blue reflex segments are impossible.

(Effectively, “green” means “the segment is fixed, and the algorithm has determined that the segments is fixed”, and similarly for “blue”.)
At any time during the execution, if the algorithm tries to color a blue segment in green (respectively a green segment in blue), then the execution is interrupted and the algorithm rejects.

Input.

The input is an axis-aligned polygon
P
possibly with holes, stored as a list of directed cycles of vertices (i.e., the coordinates and some room for additional pointers and markers), where each directed cycle corresponds to a connected component of the boundary of
P
with the convention that the interior of
P
lies on the left (relatively to the local direction of the cycle).
In the following, we assume that every coordinate is a rational number (not a floating-point number), and that computations involving coordinates are exact. In particular, the coordinates of an intersection of two segments are computed exactly, and it is reliable to test whether this intersection is equal to a point.

Preprocessing.

The challenge of the algorithm is to avoid computing all the intersections between pairs of reflex segments and all the intersections between a stabbing segment (up to equivalence) and a reflex segment.
To achieve
O(n\log n)
time, we preprocess the input polygon
P
to initialize and populate the following two pairs of orthogonal ray-shooting data structures (each pair having one data structure for horizontal segments and one for vertical segments).

\mathbf{R}^{{\textnormal{hor}}},\mathbf{R}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}
:

Contains the reflex segments of
P
which are either not colored (as is the case initially), or red (as we will remove a blue reflex segment upon coloring) to allow querying what is the first relevant reflex segment intersected by a stabbing segment.

\mathbf{S}^{{\textnormal{hor}}},\mathbf{S}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}
:

Contains the stabbing segments of
P
(more specifically, one representative per equivalent class of stabbing segment) which have not been processed (as we remove a stabbing segment after having colored all the intersected reflex segments) to allow querying what is the first relevant stabbing segment intersected by a reflex segment that we just colored red.

In the preprocessing, we also build a pair of lists
\mathbf{L}^{{\textnormal{hor}}},\mathbf{L}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}
of all horizontal and vertical reflex segments of
P
that will be unaffected by removals performed on
\mathbf{R}^{{\textnormal{hor}}},\mathbf{R}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}
.
In the following, we only detail how to compute the vertical reflex segments and vertical stabbing segments of
P
, and to populate
\mathbf{L}^{{\textnormal{ver}}},\mathbf{R}^{{\textnormal{ver}}},\mathbf{S}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}
. The horizontal ones and
\mathbf{L}^{{\textnormal{hor}}},\mathbf{R}^{{\textnormal{hor}}},\mathbf{S}^{{\textnormal{hor}}}
are computed similarly.

We compute the trapezoidal map of the horizontal edges of
P
.
The set of vertical edges of the trapezoidal map that are not edges of
P
is equal to the set of vertical reflex segments of
P

We use this fact to populate
\mathbf{L}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}
and
\mathbf{R}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}

Moreover, by construction, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the trapezes (which are in fact rectangles that are columns of adjacent pixels) and the equivalent class of vertical stabbing segments.
In order to populate
\mathbf{S}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}
with one representative per equivalent class of vertical stabbing segment, we choose a point in the interior of each trapezes and compute the endpoints of the vertical stabbing segment containing this point.

To finalize the preprocessing, for each reflex segment
\mathbf{s}
in
\mathbf{L}^{{\textnormal{hor}}},\mathbf{L}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}

as

Running time.

Lemma 4.3
.

The algorithm runs in
O(n\log n)
time.

Proof.

We first show with simple verifications that the preprocessing runs in
O(n\log n)
time.
Each step of the preprocessing takes
O(n\log n)
time.
Indeed, the two bottlenecks are the computation of the two trapezoidal maps in
O(n\log n)
time (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
), and the
O(n)
operations (either insertions or ray-shooting queries) on the orthogonal ray-shooting data structures in
O(\log n)

[
17
]
.

We now prove that the rest of the algorithm also runs in
O(n\log n)
time.
Observe that, once the preprocessing is over, we perform only two types of operations that are not in constant time: orthogonal ray-shooting queries and removals from orthogonal ray-shooting data structures.
As we perform no insertion in orthogonal ray-shooting data structures, the number of removals in upper bounded by the maximum size of the orthogonal ray-shooting data structures which is
O(n)
.
Besides, each of the
O(n)
reflex segments in

We now map each orthogonal ray-shooting query either to a removal from an orthogonal ray-shooting data structure or to a coloring of some reflex segment.
Since each removal and each coloring will have at most a constant number of queries mapped to it, this will prove that the number of such queries is also
O(n)
.

Every orthogonal ray-shooting query

In the rest of this section, we prove the correctness of the algorithm.

First observe that, by construction,
\mathbf{S}^{{\textnormal{hor}}}\cup\mathbf{S}^{{\textnormal{ver}}}
contains at least one stabbing segment for each equivalence class of stabbing segments in

A simple verification then shows that the algorithm implements the rules in Lemma

Therefore, Invariants
1.

2.

Moreover, if the algorithm accepts, then the rules in Lemma
4.2

Next, we verify the correctness of the algorithm in three cases, of which only the third case is not straightforward.
If the algorithm rejects (Figure
12

Thus,

If the algorithm accepts and if all reflex segments have been colored (Figure

with stabbing number at most

(by Invariants

).
In this case,

In the remaining case, the algorithm accepts and some reflex segments have not been colored (while the rules in Lemma

have been applied until saturation, as in Figure

Next, we show that a conforming partition of

.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
P
be a rectilinear
n
-gon (possibly with holes) that is thin and has no gates (which is the case if
P
is in general position).
Then,
P
has conforming stabbing number at most
2
.
Moreover, there exists an algorithm that computes a conforming partition of
P
with stabbing number at most
2
in
O(n)
time.

Proof.

We prove that
P
admits a conforming partition with stabbing number at most
2
.
First, we reduce our problem to
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{SAT}
by taking advantage of the fact that
P
is thin and has no gates.
Then, we prove that the
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{SAT}
formula we obtain is always satisfiable.

We start with some observations.
A conforming partition may be given by a set
\mathbf{R}
of reflex segments, but it may equivalently be given as a function
f
mapping each reflex vertex
p
to a subset of
\{\mathbf{h}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\}
, with the convention that the union of the image of the reflex vertices under
f
is the set
\mathbf{R}
.
By definition of being conforming, each reflex vertex is mapped to at least one reflex segment.
Moreover, if
\mathbf{R}
is minimal (i.e., no reflex segments can be removed while retaining a conforming partition; see Section
2
), then each reflex vertex is mapped by
f
to at most one reflex segment.
Thus, without loss of generality, we restrict our search to a conforming partition
f
of
P
with stabbing number at most
2
, where each reflex vertex is mapped by
f
to at most one reflex segment.
(Note that a given reflex segment
\mathbf{s}
may be the image of up to two reflex vertices, even if the conforming partition is minimal. For example, for a given reflex vertex
p
, it is possible that both
\mathbf{h}_{p}
and
\mathbf{v}_{p}
are in a minimal partition
\mathbf{R}
if
q
, the other endpoint of
\mathbf{h}_{p}
, is also a reflex vertex.
In this case,
f
would map, say,
p
to
\mathbf{v}_{p}
and
q
to
\mathbf{h}_{p}
.)

We now declare a Boolean variable
x(p)
for every reflex vertex
p
of
P
.
Given an assignment of all the variables, we build a function
f
from the reflex vertices of
P
to the reflex segments of
P
as follows.
If
x(p)
is true, then we define
f(p)
to be
\mathbf{h}_{p}
.
If
x(p)
is false, then we define
f(p)
to be
\mathbf{v}_{p}
.

We now list the clauses which ensure that the function
f
indeed encodes a conforming partition with stabbing number at most
2
.
By construction, we have ensured that for each reflex vertex
p
, at least one of
\mathbf{h}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}
is included in the partition
f
.
Moreover, since
P
is thin, no reflex segments intersect, and in particular, no reflex segments in the partition
f
intersect.
There remains to ensure that the stabbing number of

is at most
2
.
By Observation

, every stabbing segment intersects at most two reflex segments.
Thus, for each equivalence class of vertical stabbing segment that intersects exactly two reflex segments of the form
\mathbf{h}_{p},\mathbf{h}_{q}
, we require

(a)

\lnot x(p)\lor\lnot x(q)
(which is also equivalent to
x(p)\Rightarrow\lnot x(q)
and to
x(q)\Rightarrow\lnot x(p)
).

Similarly, for each equivalence class of horizontal stabbing segment that intersects exactly two reflex segments of the form
\mathbf{v}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{q}
, we require

(b)

x(p)\lor x(q)
(which is also equivalent to
\lnot x(p)\Rightarrow x(q)
and to
\lnot x(q)\Rightarrow x(p)
).

We finally prove that the conjunction
\phi
of these clauses is always satisfiable.
Recall that a
Horn clause
is a clause with at most one positive literal, and that
\mathsf{HORNSAT}
, the restriction of
\mathsf{SAT}
to conjunctions of Horn clauses, is in
\mathcal{P}
.
Our strategy is to show that

(i)

these clauses are
renameable
Horn clauses (i.e., they become Horn clauses after a change of variables), and that

(ii)

after renaming the appropriate variables, each clause contains a negative literal.

A conjunction of Horn clauses where each clause contains a negative literal is always satisfiable (by assigning every variable to false), hence the lemma.

To this end, we consider the undirected simple graph
H
, that we call the
constraint
graph of
P
, defined as follows (Figure

•

The vertices of
H
are the pixels of
P
that are the wedge-pixel of some reflex vertex
p
.
Each vertex of
H
is thus associated with a unique variable
x(p)
.

•

There is an edge between two pixels
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
whenever there exists a stabbing segment intersecting both
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
.
Each edge of
H
is thus associated with a unique clause of
\phi
.

We consider the constraint graph
H
to be drawn in the plane with straight line segments included in corresponding stabbing segments.
By Observation
2
, a vertex of
H
is incident to at most one vertical edge and at most one horizontal edge.
Therefore, the maximum degree of
H
is
2
, and
H
consists of a set of cycles and paths (which may be isolated vertices).
(In fact, one may show that
H
is either one cycle or a set of paths, but this is not useful for our purpose.)
Moreover, each cycle of
H
is even, as it is drawn as a rectilinear polygon (without holes).

We now rename every other variable
x(p)
along each path and each cycle of
H
as
x^{\prime}(p)=\lnot x(p)
.
This is possible because the cycles are all even.
By construction after this change of variables, the clauses are now Horn clauses, and each contains a negative literal.

To conclude the proof, we have declared a linear number of variables and built a linear number of clauses.
The renaming process that we have described is also linear, and so is the assignment computation.
Therefore, we have described an algorithm that computes a conforming partition of
P
with stabbing number at most
2
in linear time.
∎

We finally state and prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists an algorithm that, for any rectilinear
n
-gon
P
(possibly with holes), decides
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
and provides a solution (if any) in
O(n\log n)
time.

Proof.

The algorithm described above runs in
O(n\log n)
time by Lemma
4.3
and correctly decides
2\textnormal{-}\mathsf{CSTAB}
by Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.5
.
Besides, it is possible to compute a conforming partition with stabbing number at most
2
of
P
in
O(n)
extra time by Lemma
4.5
.
∎

6
Hole-Free Polygons in General Position

In this section, we give a second FPT algorithm, which makes a different assumption on the input polygon
P
.
We require

to have no holes and no gates (the latter holds in particular if
P
is in general position), but in exchange we no longer need to bound the treewidth.
The idea for this theorem is to distinguish two cases using the maximum number

of reflex segments intersected by a stabbing segment in
P
: if
m
is smaller than a well-chosen threshold
\ell

is at least

(Lemma

.

There exists an algorithm that, for any rectilinear

-gon

that has no holes and no gates (the latter holds if

is in general position), decides

.

Proof.

We show Theorem
6.1
by applying a dichotomy argument somewhat similar to bidimensionality
[

]
.
We choose the threshold

(the reason for this choice of

Let

be the maximum number of reflex segments that are intersected by a stabbing segment in
P

Case 1:

.
Recall that the

of a plane graph

is:

•

1

is outerplanar (i.e. all the vertices of

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we show that computing a conforming partition of a rectilinear polygon with stabbing number
k
is
\mathcal{N\!P}
-hard for all
k\geq 4
. Since the reduction uses only thin polygons, the hardness result follows even if we omit the conforming constraint. The polygons used in our reduction have holes. Therefore, determining the time complexity of computing an optimal (conforming) partition for polygons without holes remains open.

On the positive side, we provide an
O(n\log n)
-time algorithm to decide whether a polygon admits a conforming partition with stabbing number
2
.
Since the problem is
\mathcal{N\!P}
-hard already for conforming stabbing number
4
, only the case of conforming stabbing number
3
remains open.
For polygons (possibly with holes) with bounded treewidth and bounded conforming stabbing number, we give a quadratic-time algorithm to compute the minimum stabbing number.
An exciting direction would be to design fixed-parameter tractable algorithms for polygons without holes parameterized by the conforming stabbing number, which would complement the hardness result for polygons with holes. Interestingly, for polygons without holes that are in general position, we already gave such a fixed-parameter-tractable algorithm.
But we also proved that general position does not help the case of polygons with holes: computing a conforming partition with stabbing number at most
k
(for
k\geq 4
) remains
\mathcal{N\!P}
-hard for polygons in general position.

Extending all these results to higher dimensions would be interesting, even for the restricted class of orthogonal 3D-histograms where previous results focus on minimizing the number of partitions into rectangular boxes
[
3
,
16
]
.
