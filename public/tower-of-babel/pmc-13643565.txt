Pectus Excavatum
Authors: Krishnaswamy Chandrasekaran, Farouk Mookadam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Pectus excavatum (PE) is usually not considered causative in adults presenting with cardiopulmonary symptoms despite a thorough cardiopulmonary evaluation by cardiologists and pulmonologists for disabling cardiopulmonary symptoms.1,2 Kragten et al1 developed a score for evaluating symptoms, the symptomatic pectus excavatum in seniors (SPES) score, and the magnitude of pectus compression, the pectus evaluation index score (PEI). SPES score includes the clinical symptoms (dyspnea, palpitations, fatigue, chest pain, postural symptoms, and low exercise tolerance) along with diagnostic test results (electrocardiogram, Holter, echocardiogram, treadmill exercise test, and pulmonary function spirometry test), and the PEI score reveals the magnitude of compression of the pectus from “0” to “5”. Using these scores, they report that 63% (12 of 19) of their 41 patients with PE older than 50 years had disabling symptoms that began in their 4th or 5th decades and were labeled as “unexplained” despite their SPES score >5 and PEI score >3, indicating the magnitude of symptoms and severity of PE. The case reported by Alkhatib et al3 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports reveals that this is the common rule rather than the exception in many reports.1,2 This 62-year-old female patient with PE had intermittent sinus tachycardia and fatigue despite being on metoprolol succinate 200 mg daily. The investigators have demonstrated a low right atrial mean pressure at catheterization, indicating decreased right ventricular filling from PE compression of the right ventricle as the cause of her tachycardia. Corrective surgery relieved her symptoms and improved her exercise tolerance and functional capacity. Metoprolol was decreased to 100 mg at 3-month follow-up. Like this female patient, Winkens et al2 describe a 59-year-old male physician who experienced progressive, debilitating heart failure symptoms from PE for 10 years despite several cardiac and pulmonary evaluations where the compressive effect of chest deformity was overlooked and eventually got significant relief with corrective surgery.

In adults, PE deformity is generally considered to be cosmetic and associated cardiopulmonary symptoms are not attributed to the cardiac compression from the deformity and are attributed to competing conditions that come with age, such as hypertension, obesity, and general deconditioning etc, or to cardiac abnormalities associated with PE, such as mitral valve prolapse or referred to as “unexplained symptoms” if no cause could be found. This unfamiliarity of symptoms from PE stems from the presumption in part accrues from a lack of knowledge of the compressive effects of PE and the assumption that adult patients have had PE deformity for most of their life, without cardiopulmonary symptoms, and therefore the PE deformity is unlikely to be the culprit. In addition, a lack of understanding of maturation changes that take place in the thoracic cage as the child grows into adolescence and thence into adulthood. The chest wall compliance, which was able to accommodate the displaced or distorted cardiac and pulmonary system without producing any hemodynamic embarrassment to cause symptoms in children and adolescents, is lost around the age of 18 years, and this loss of compliance of the deformed chest cage in adults results in hemodynamic compromise and the emergence of symptoms in adults and seniors.4,5

In many adults with PE, a detailed history will reveal symptoms such as exertional shortness of breath, fatigue, palpitations, and chest pain, which may have been present for some time before being attributed to the pectus deformity. Often, these symptoms may be precipitated or exaggerated by changes in posture.1, 2, 3

In many of these patients with long-standing symptoms, subtle abnormalities noticed on cardiopulmonary evaluations using 2-dimensional echo and computed tomography scan of the chest are overlooked, only to be discovered later that they are responsible for the symptoms.1, 2, 3 Furthermore, because many of the tests are done in the supine or resting position as a routine, the use of physiologic testing is frequently necessary to show the causal relationship of the PE deformity to the cardiopulmonary symptoms. Pulmonary function tests, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, and positional as well as exercise echocardiography may display the symptoms in those with symptoms, and routine tests don’t reveal structural or functional abnormality.6

Advances in imaging modalities over the past 2 decades, especially in magnetic resonance imaging, gated computed tomography, and 3-dimensional echocardiography, provide anatomical and functional information of the heart and thoracic structures. Although these have been more frequently used in the pediatric group, application of these in adults with unexplained symptoms may improve the recognition of cardiac compression by the PE as the cause of their symptoms.7, 8, 9

Surgical correction of PE in symptomatic adult patients is generally not favored, as it is associated with increased postoperative complications compared to younger patients. However, modifying the techniques has resulted in acceptable outcomes with significant alleviation of symptoms.5 Therefore, symptomatic patients with PE, properly chosen after comprehensive evaluation, documenting the hemodynamic compromise from PE like the patient reported by Alkhatib et al3 in this issue of the journal as well as by Winkens et al2 are more likely to benefit from surgical correction that relieves compression by the pectus deformity.1, 2, 3

These reports demonstrate that detailed history with specific focus on positional characteristics of the symptoms and judicious use of multimodality functional and physiologic imaging will increase the likelihood of recognizing adult patients whose symptoms are attributable to PE and are therefore more likely to benefit from surgical correction. There is a significant paucity of data pertaining to PE in adults and how to approach them, as this entity is commonly seen by pediatricians. More data are needed in adults with PE, with a defined algorithm of preprocedural testing to best identify adult patients with PE whose symptoms are directly a result of the deformity and who are therefore most likely to have symptom amelioration post PE surgical repair.
