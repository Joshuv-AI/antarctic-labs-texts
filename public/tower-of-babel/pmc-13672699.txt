Perspectives and experiences of nurse academics teaching reflective practice to undergraduate Bachelor of Nursing students: a scoping review
Authors: Lisa Speedie, Gulzar Malik, Jacqueline Johnston, Christine East
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Reflective practice is essential in nursing education, as it promotes critical thinking and fosters professional growth. Although it is acknowledged in higher education and included in university attributes, the perspectives and experiences of nurse academics who teach it remain underexplored.Aim:This scoping review aimed to synthesise published primary studies exploring the perspectives and experiences of nurse academics teaching reflective practice to undergraduate Bachelor of Nursing students.Method:Databases searched included ProQuest Nursing, Medline, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library, with a focus on primary studies from 2010 onwards. Using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and PRISMA guidelines, 11 studies were reported and thematically analysed.Results:From synthesising the included studies, four key themes were identified: (1) Reflective practice and the role of academics, (2) Reflective practice and academic challenges, (3) Reflective practice learning approaches and (4) Reflective practice and academic vulnerability.Conclusion:The studies revealed a limited understanding of the experiences and expectations of academics teaching reflective practice, with a focus primarily on identifying barriers rather than exploring the personal experiences of those who teach it. Further research is necessary to understand the perspectives and experiences of academics who teach reflective practice to undergraduate Bachelor of Nursing students.Keywords: academics, critical reflection, experiences, perspectives, reflective practice, undergraduate nurses

Introduction

Reflective practice is widely recognised as a vital skill for supporting critical thinking, clinical reasoning and professional development for Bachelor of Nursing students (Del Vecchio et al., 2024; Hallaran et al., 2023; Smith, 2021). Reflection not only improves practical skills and knowledge but also highlights the importance of reflection competencies (Jack and Illingworth, 2019) and strengthens critical thinking, self-awareness, empathy and thoughtful action (Graham and O’Brien, 2020; Zeng et al., 2024). Reflective practice is embedded in the Australian Nursing and Midwifery Board of Australia (NMBA, 2016) Registered Nurse Standards for Practice, and linked to professional growth and sustaining clinical competence. Nonetheless, both students and academics often encounter challenges in understanding and implementing reflective processes (Gathu, 2022).

Teaching reflective practice to undergraduate nursing students enrolled in a Bachelor’s programme, however, is a complex task that requires an in-depth understanding of pedagogical strategies to promote effective reflective practice (Aryuwat et al., 2024; Gustavsson et al., 2019). The growing recognition of the significance of reflection in nursing practice highlights the need for effective teaching methods that support students in acquiring this skill (Artioli et al., 2021; Legare and Armstrong, 2017). When academics lack the necessary training to model and facilitate reflective techniques, the quality of reflective learning decreases for both students and staff (Del Vecchio et al., 2024; Graham and O’Brien, 2020).

To maintain competence and adapt to the changing healthcare environment, academics must be equipped to practice and facilitate reflection (Naicker and Rensburg, 2018). Del Vecchio et al. (2024) stated that training and mentoring are necessary to enable effective reflection and to provide sufficient time to create a safe environment for academics and students to engage in reflection (Clarke, 2021; Kurosh et al., 2020; Lubbe and Botha, 2020).

Significance of reflective practice in nursing education

Reflective practice facilitates the transition of nursing students from education to clinical practice and supports novice nurse educators in moving from clinical roles to academia (Legare and Armstrong, 2017). It helps students integrate theoretical knowledge with real-world experiences, fostering personal and professional growth (Schwind and Manankil-Rankin, 2020). Additionally, reflective practice contributes to the development of personal and aesthetic knowing, supporting holistic, person-centred relational practice (Horton et al., 2021; Schwind and Manankil-Rankin, 2020). Reflective practice also facilitates and supports novice nurse educators entering academia, promoting personal and professional development (Horton et al., 2021; Legare and Armstrong, 2017; Schwind and Manankil-Rankin, 2020).

To ensure competency and meet pedagogy requirements, academics must be equipped to practise and facilitate reflection, supported by adequate training, mentoring and time to create safe reflective spaces for staff and students (Clarke, 2021; Del Vecchio et al., 2024; Kurosh et al., 2020). Reflective practice extends beyond the evaluation of skills and the development of interpersonal understanding. It requires academics to model reflective thinking and encourage curiosity and critical questioning among students (Jacobs, 2016; Lubbe and Botha, 2020). Reflection is often understood as a process that occurs after an event, described by Schön (1983, 1987) as reflection-on-action. This concept has since been expanded to include reflection-in-action, which occurs during practice, and reflection-before-action, which involves anticipating and planning future practice (Schutz, 2026). Reflective practice typically involves analysing experiences to identify what worked well and what could be improved. Reflexivity, however, requires a deeper level of critical self-awareness (Sherwood, 2024). Reflexivity involves an ongoing internal dialogue through which academics examine their own agency, values and decision-making processes within their professional roles (Schutz, 2026). Reflexivity within individuals plays a role in generating knowledge, where personal experiences, beliefs and professional identities influence how knowledge is interpreted and constructed (Schutz, 2026; Sherwood, 2024). Through reflexivity, academics move beyond merely reviewing activities to critically examining how their beliefs and priorities shape their teaching practice and professional identity (Schutz, 2026).

Despite its acknowledged value, the literature on the perspectives and experiences of academics who teach reflective practice remains limited (Timizar-Le Pen et al., 2020). A lack of clarity or confidence in teaching reflective practice can hinder student development (Smadi et al., 2021). When academics are proficient in reflective practice, they not only enhance their professional growth but also contribute to curriculum development and improved student learning outcomes (Kurosh et al., 2020; Lubbe and Botha, 2020; Smadi et al., 2021). Recognising the importance of the academic experience is essential, particularly given that nursing curricula have embraced reflective practice as an effective tool for fostering self-awareness and developing critical thinking for over 20 years. However, responsibility has often rested with individual academics to apply reflective practice in ways that enhance and support student learning (Naicker and van Rensburg, 2018), a task that has posed several challenges and has often been poorly implemented or not implemented at all (Gustavsson et al., 2019).

Ossa Parra et al. (2015) argued that reflective skills can be taught but must be developed and practised over time (Chan and Lee, 2021; Gustavsson et al., 2019). A significant gap remains in the literature on developmental approaches to teaching reflective practice across higher education programmes, particularly in nursing (Smadi et al., 2021; Timizar-Le Pen et al., 2020). Academics need to understand their own beliefs and purpose when facilitating reflection; without this understanding, they limit both reflective learning and their students’ practices (Donohoe, 2019; Graham and O’Brien, 2020; Kurosh et al., 2020). However, without insights into the perspectives and experiences of those teaching reflection, the full impact of these dynamics on student learning remains unclear.

Aim

This scoping review aims to synthesise the literature from primary studies that explore the perspectives and experiences of academics teaching reflective practice to undergraduate Bachelor of Nursing students. By examining existing literature, this review seeks to provide a comprehensive overview of the current state of knowledge in this area. The findings may inform future pedagogical strategies and contribute to the ongoing development of effective reflective practice in nursing education.

Review question

What are the perspectives and experiences of academics teaching reflective practice to undergraduate Bachelor of Nursing students?

Methods

The scoping review was conducted following the framework originally identified by Arksey and O’Malley (2005). The review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology, with the development of the protocol, inclusion criteria and the PRISMA (Figure 1) Extension for Scoping Reviews checklist for reporting and was registered with the Centre for Open Science Forum. This process provides a rigorous framework for systematically mapping, exploring and synthesising the evidence gathered (Arksey and O’Malley, 2005; Moola et al., 2020).

Study selection process

The search was limited to publications from 2010 onwards, reflecting the introduction of reflective practice into undergraduate nurse education and the NMBA Registered Nurse Standards of practice from 2010. The inclusion criteria comprised primary research papers using qualitative, quantitative or mixed-methods designs, published in peer-reviewed journals.

Following the search, all identified citations were collated and uploaded into EndNote, and duplicates were removed. After removing duplicates, the search identified 321 potentially relevant studies [CINAHL (n = 154), Medline (n = 103), ProQuest (n = 27), Web of Science (n = 15) and Scopus (n = 12)]. All studies found through the manual search were identified in the databases. All 321 papers were imported into Covidence for screening. Titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by the lead researcher and two team members for suitability for the scoping review. At this stage, 281 studies were excluded. After initial screening, 38 full-text papers were reviewed by the lead and a second researcher for inclusion. Any conflicts were further reviewed and discussed within the team of three researchers to resolve. As a result, a further 27 studies were excluded, leaving 11 studies that met the inclusion criteria (see Figure 1).

Quality appraisal

All 11 eligible studies were assessed for methodological quality using the JBI Quality Appraisal tool (Moola et al., 2020). Any conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer, as recommended by Pollock et al. (2023). All 11 studies were retained because the average score across the selected studies exceeded 65%.

Data charting and extraction

For each paper, data were extracted, including the paper title, author(s), year, journal, country of publication, research purpose, research design, data collection methods, participants, key findings and quality appraisal score (see Table 4). The primary researcher extracted key data for each category and populated the table with information relevant to the review question. All researchers then reviewed the data to ensure consensus, and any conflicts were resolved through further discussion.

Summary of findings.

Data analysis

The included studies were analysed using thematic analysis informed by Braun and Clarke (2022) and guided by an inductive content approach. This method aligns with thematic analysis, as categories were developed by comparing similarities and differences within the data (Kyngäs et al., 2020). The primary researcher immersed themselves in the data and identified the characteristics of each study. This mirrors thematic analysis, in which patterns and themes are identified and refined to ensure that the research question is addressed (Braun and Clarke, 2022; Munn et al., 2018). Themes were identified, and all researchers discussed them to review and verify them, including searching, categorising, defining and reviewing them again (Clarke and Braun, 2016; Kyngäs et al., 2020).

Results

Study characteristics

Search outcomes

Study characteristics from the 11 included studies are as follows: countries of origin were the United Kingdom (n = 3), Sweden (n = 2), Canada (n = 2), France (n = 1) and one study each from South Africa, the United States and Australia. Study designs included qualitative (n = 8), mixed methods (n = 2) and one quantitative study. Data collection techniques varied across and within the studies. The most frequently used were questionnaires/surveys (n = 6), in-person interviews (n = 3), focus groups (n = 3), which were used alongside interviews on two occasions, case studies (n = 2) and databases (n = 3).

Participants in the 11 studies comprised academics teaching in undergraduate Bachelor of Nursing programmes and students enrolled in these programmes. Among the included studies, Timizar-Le Pen et al. (2020) surveyed 16 nursing schools, whereas Gustavsson et al. (2019) involved 525 students and 131 academics across 16 schools. Jack and Illingworth (2019) engaged 12 participants (three academics and nine pre-registration students), and Smith (2021) gathered perspectives from both students and academics within a single programme. The remaining studies focused solely on academics: Naicker and van Rensburg (2018) (n = 121), Graham and O’Brien (2020) (11 academics/facilitators), Koldestam et al. (2021) (12 Delphi panel academics), Lindberg (2018) (eight from one department), Mulli et al. (2022) (11 academics), Smadi et al. (2021) (11 academics) and Jacobs (2016), which involved a single academic response. Table 5 provides summary of findings.

Summary of themes

Four themes were developed from 11 included studies: (1) Reflective practice and the role of academics, (2) Reflective practice and academic challenges, (3) Reflective practice learning approaches and (4) Reflective practice and academic vulnerability.

Reflective practice and the role of academics

Academics were found to be vital to promoting reflective learning; they need to balance building trust with challenging students’ ideas. Several tensions were identified in how academics supported reflective learning, including navigating control versus uncertainty and managing closeness while maintaining appropriate distance (Gustavsson et al., 2019). Lindberg (2018) also recognised that academics were supervising students while developing their own reflective capacity. Although reflection was highly valued, many academics doubted their reflective skills and felt uncomfortable modelling it for students, which could undermine the authenticity of reflection in curricula (Jacobs, 2016; Mulli et al., 2022; Naicker and van Rensburg, 2018). Nonetheless, this modelling was seen as essential for demonstrating critical thinking, adaptability and openness (Gustavsson et al., 2019; Jack and Illingworth, 2019).

In three of the 11 studies, academics identified barriers, including poor simulation design, inadequate assessment options and insufficient preparation, that hindered reflective practice. Academics raised concerns that these hindrances stemmed from poor preparation, teaching methods and the selection of, or lack of knowledge about, the reflective tool and framework (Jacobs, 2016; Lindberg, 2018; Mulli et al., 2022). Both Timizar-Le Pen et al. (2020) and Smadi et al. (2021) reported feeling unprepared to design, implement and evaluate reflective assessments, resulting in an ad hoc approach that was often descriptive rather than reflexive, thereby lessening the effectiveness of the intended learning outcomes.

Reflective practice and academic challenges

Significant challenges were identified in integrating reflective practice into nursing curricula. Both Gustavsson et al. (2019) and Graham and O’Brien (2020) noted that academics are expected to plan, teach and evaluate reflective practice to meet curriculum outcomes. However, three papers reported that academics consistently lacked formal training in reflective methodologies (Lindberg, 2018; Naicker and van Rensburg, 2018; Smadi et al., 2021), leading to uncertainty about which frameworks and tools to use. Time was another significant barrier, with reflective practice often described as an ‘add-on’ in already crowded curricula (Naicker and van Rensburg, 2018; Timizar-Le Pen et al., 2020). Although some models, such as Koldestam et al.’s (2021) Model for Improvement in Learning Outcomes (MILO) framework, provided clear conceptual scaffolding for reflection, most academics reported inadequate support or mentorship opportunities to sustain reflective teaching practices (Graham and O’Brien, 2020; Smadi et al., 2021). Lindberg (2018) highlighted that a lack of self-trust in reflective practice exacerbated these challenges, leaving academics feeling exposed and under-supported. Three papers also reported that the lack of clear institutional strategies affected feedback processes, and without proper preparation or reflective skills, feedback was more likely to be methodological rather than developmental (Graham and O’Brien, 2020; Timizar-Le Pen et al., 2020; Koldestam et al., 2021).

Reflective practice learning approaches

The importance of clear pedagogical frameworks for strengthening academics’ confidence in teaching reflective practice was highlighted in five of the papers. Graham and O’Brien (2020) described how implementing a guided reflective model with dedicated time and group sessions required significant preparation and resource allocation for academics. Jack and Illingworth (2019) noted the challenges of promoting student-centred learning within such frameworks but emphasised that, for reflective practice to succeed, it was essential. Similarly, Mulli et al. (2022) and Timizar-Le Pen et al. (2020) noted the demands placed on simulation facilitators to identify and support reflection-in-action, necessitating strategies such as pre-briefing and prompts to manage learner anxiety and fear, which in turn affect reflective outcomes.

Variability in reflective teaching tools was evident across the literature. Timizar-Le Pen et al.’s (2020) study across 16 French nursing schools found considerable differences in how Situation Analysis (SA) was implemented and assessed, whereas the conceptual literature by Jacobs (2016) suggested that reflective activities are frequently shaped by individual academics’ teaching approaches rather than standardised pedagogical methods.

Reflective practice and academic vulnerability

The final theme identified was the experience of vulnerability in facilitating reflective practice. Lindberg (2018) and Gustavsson et al. (2019) explained that academics often struggle to balance closeness and distance in their relationships with students, highlighting the tension between maintaining authority and modelling openness. Reflection often requires demonstration to role model its worth and clinical purpose, but it also gives academics the chance to demonstrate authenticity and build trust (Gustavsson et al., 2019; Mulli et al., 2022). These qualities, in turn, influence both the outcome of teaching and the learning of reflective practice (Jacobs, 2016). Jack and Illingworth (2019) demonstrated how integrating poetry as a reflective tool requires educators to help students adopt unfamiliar, creative methods. Although these approaches call for more guidance and sensitivity from academics, they enable richer empathy and shifts in perspective, highlighting the need for academics to balance vulnerability with pedagogical innovation. Similarly, Smith (2021) found that guided reflective writing offered academics a structured way to scaffold reflection across different student levels, providing security for both students and academics while encouraging deeper critical thinking. These findings demonstrated that academics are expected to navigate vulnerability, adopt alternative methods and create safe spaces for reflection whilst still challenging the students.

Discussion

This scoping review drew on the perspectives and experiences of academics teaching reflective practice to undergraduate Bachelor of Nursing students. Findings from the included studies highlighted a consistent narrative: although reflective practice is recognised as vital in nursing education, effective implementation remains challenging. Academics frequently report feeling underprepared, under-supported and time-constrained, limiting their ability to integrate reflection meaningfully into teaching (Lindberg, 2018; Mulli et al., 2022; Naicker and van Rensburg, 2018). It is important to note at this time, some studies refer to ‘participants’ as both academics and students.’ For this review, only academics will be included in the discussion.

A central theme across the included studies is the gap between the theoretical value of reflection and its practical application. Many academics, particularly those transitioning from clinical practice into academia, are confident in their professional expertise but lack the pedagogical preparation to facilitate reflective learning (Gustavsson et al., 2019; Lubbe and Botha, 2020). These academics often feel overwhelmed by a lack of clarity about how to teach, assess and model reflection (Bagheri et al., 2019; Graham and O’Brien, 2020). Even when reflective practice is included in curricula, it is not always integrated into teaching culture, and academics are not adequately supported to deliver it effectively (Lubbe and Botha, 2020; Naicker and van Rensburg, 2018).

Several studies emphasise the importance of academics modelling reflection, engaging in their own reflective processes, addressing their vulnerabilities and actively building trust with students (Gustavsson et al., 2019; Mulli et al., 2022; Naicker and van Rensburg, 2018). However, many academics face structural and organisational barriers that hinder this modelling, including inadequate training, poorly designed simulations, limited time and a lack of feedback (Jacobs, 2016; Lindberg, 2018; Mulli et al., 2022). These barriers not only hinder students’ engagement but also diminish academics’ confidence in the value and authenticity of reflective practice.

The literature highlights tensions between authentic reflection and performative, assessment-driven practices, with reflection often reduced to a task rather than supported as a transformative learning process (Clarke, 2021; Edwards, 2017; Rolfe, 2014). Academics also report difficulties adapting reflective activities to diverse student needs, noting that although some students benefit from journaling or one-to-one dialogue, others engage more effectively through group or peer-led approaches (Agnew, 2022; Holdo, 2023). Academics further describe uncertainty about professional boundaries and the emotional labour involved in facilitating reflection, at times feeling overwhelmed by the responsibility of supporting students’ personal and professional growth (Gustavsson et al., 2019; Mulli et al., 2022; Naicker and van Rensburg, 2018). Without appropriate support, reflective practice risks becoming superficial or tokenistic rather than a meaningful pedagogical process (Andersen and Toubøl, 2024; Bagheri et al., 2019; Helberget et al., 2021).

Sherwood (2024) called for a review of reflective practice in higher education that reconnects with its philosophical foundations, promoting curiosity and critical questioning, whereas Agnew (2022) supports academics and students in their growth through reflection. This requires systemic investment in training, mentoring and structural time, as well as a cultural shift within institutions to value reflection as a scholarly and transformative activity (Lubbe and Botha, 2020). In summary, this review highlights the need to provide academics with the time, training and resources to teach reflective practice meaningfully. Educational organisations must prioritise a dual focus: equipping students with reflective capabilities while empowering academics to model, guide and evaluate reflection with integrity and authenticity.

Implications and Conclusion

This scoping review found limited research on nurse academics’ perspectives and experiences of teaching reflective practice to undergraduate nursing students. Despite its recognised value for developing students’ critical thinking, professional judgement, and theory-practice within undergraduate courses, the implementation of reflective practice remains inconsistent. Academics report insufficient training, institutional support, time, and confidence to apply reflective frameworks, often reducing reflection to a superficial or assessment-driven task rather than a transformative learning process.

Importantly, academics’ vulnerability, preparedness, and authenticity directly shape the depth and quality of the reflective learning they can facilitate. This indicates that improving student outcomes requires investment in academics themselves, not only in curricular frameworks. Educational institutions should therefore prioritise structured pedagogical support, dedicated training, mentoring, and adequate time, enabling academics to model and facilitate reflection with integrity and confidence.

Further research into academics’ lived experiences of managing these personal and institutional demands is needed to inform curriculum development and better prepare future nurses for practice. Without this investment, the transformative potential of reflective practice in nursing education remains unrealised.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this scoping review is the authors’ systematic search strategy across six major databases, supported by clearly defined PCC criteria to capture relevant studies. The review was informed by the JBI methodological framework and quality appraisal tools, which enhanced its rigour and transparency. Data extraction and thematic analysis were conducted collaboratively, enhancing consistency and reducing reviewer bias.

Limitations should be acknowledged. The number of eligible studies was small, and sample sizes in some were limited, which may have restricted the range of perspectives represented. Variability in study designs, contexts and approaches to reflective practice was also evident. Only English-language publications were included, which may have excluded relevant international research. Despite these limitations, this review offers valuable insights into the academic perspectives and experiences of teaching reflective practice in undergraduate Bachelor of Nursing programmes in Australia and internationally and highlights the need for further research to better understand how they manage the personal and institutional demands of teaching reflection. Future research may inform curriculum development, enhance academic growth and better prepare future nurses for the complexities of practice.
