Chewing the fat: the role of total cholesterol in MPN management
Authors: Andrew M Brunner
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Leiva et al1 explore the relationship between serum total cholesterol and patient outcomes in myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN). The authors evaluate patients with a diagnosis of myelofibrosis (MF), essential thrombocythemia, or polycythemia vera who had serum cholesterol measurements and were not receiving statin therapy to lower cholesterol levels; they found that total cholesterol was a strong predictor of worsened MPN outcomes, including major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).

Although various MPN subtypes have distinct clinical presentations, they also share a number of common features, including a varying risk of disease progression, thrombotic and cardiovascular risk, and overlapping molecular profiles. Clinically, following an MPN diagnosis, it is important to contextualize this risk for a given patient, as some will live an essentially normal life with limited impact from their diagnosis, whereas others require more active monitoring and treatment to prevent or manage complications—in particular those related to cardiovascular or thrombotic events. Most such risk assessments to date have depended either on disease-specific features such as the subtype of MPN, blood counts, and molecular profile,2,3 or depending on preexisting medical comorbidities to predict future complications.4

Paradoxically, in contrast to the general population where higher cholesterol is prevalent and an indication for cholesterol-lowering therapy, patients with MPNs, who have high rates of cardiovascular disease (CVD) overall,5 have been noted to frequently have lower serum cholesterol levels.6,7 Although several studies have identified this pattern of lower serum cholesterol in MPNs, it has remained unclear as to how much this impacts disease trajectories or how cholesterol levels should be managed in light of larger cardiovascular concerns.

In their multicenter retrospective analysis, Leiva et al identified patients with an MPN diagnosis who had available serum cholesterol measurements along with a transthoracic echocardiogram study performed after diagnosis, while excluding those who were on statin therapy. It should be noted as this was a retrospective study, patients who had a clinically-indicated transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) will likely be enriched for cardiovascular disease. The authors then looked at how serum total cholesterol was associated with outcomes including MACE, cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, and hematologic progression. They confirmed a number of associations with a low serum cholesterol, here defined as a total serum cholesterol <120 mg/dL, including enrichment for MF over other MPNs, larger spleen size, worsened blood parameters, and higher rates of heart failure and diabetes. These patients with low serum cholesterol levels also had higher rates of MACE, MPN disease progression, and mortality overall. Lower serum cholesterol levels correlated with the presence of high molecular risk mutations and other higher disease risk features, with the authors positing that it may reflect the inflammatory state of more advanced forms of disease.

Together, this suggests that routine testing of serum total cholesterol may be relevant to standard MPN management, with the caveat that this cohort was assessed in the absence of lipid-lowering therapy. The exact mechanism by which total cholesterol is a marker of worsened disease and cardiovascular outcomes is not fully defined; the authors hypothesize that cholesterol is a biomarker of overall systemic disease, and indeed may reflect patient nutrition, general organ function, or potentially be driven down by disease itself. We similarly do not know if there is a lipid subtype (high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, other lipoprotein content) that is most relevant to the findings described here. Interestingly, there was an association between low cholesterol levels and higher-risk mutational features, suggesting that serum total cholesterol testing, which is widely available, could be useful to identify higher-risk patients in settings where molecular diagnostics are less accessible.

Future steps include understanding if serum cholesterol can be used as a dynamic measure of disease; for instance, do cholesterol levels improve with disease control or JAK inhibition? Or is the low cholesterol a fixed problem related to the disease subtype or molecular features? Currently, patients are assessed for MPN disease burden dynamically using the total symptom score (TSS),8 and although this is a validated instrument, it is also subject to patient reporting and other confounding factors. We also do not know if improvement in TSS correlates with cholesterol levels; however, if total cholesterol can serve as a dynamic measure of disease, it could be useful for longitudinal monitoring, and could eventually even impact treatment goals in this space.

Although the role of total cholesterol in MPN prognosis will ideally need further replication in a prospective or other retrospective validation subsets, it is a simple, widely available metric to incorporate into the evaluation of patients with MPNs. The data presented by Leiva et al confirm prior reports identifying low cholesterol as a poor-risk feature, extend those associations to high-risk molecular features and increased risk of cardiovascular events, and suggest that at a minimum, finding a low total cholesterol level <120 mg/dL in a patient with an MPN diagnosis should trigger further assessment by their treating physician and serve as a warning of higher risk for that patient.
