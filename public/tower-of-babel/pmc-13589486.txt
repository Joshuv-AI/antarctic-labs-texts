CRISPRa-identified transcription factors reprogram glioblastoma into dendritic cell-like cells to elicit systemic antitumor immunity
Authors: Xiao Liu, Maorong Zhu, Cheng Zou, Zhengcong Cao, Yawen Wang, Guangzhao Yang, Xiaolin Liu, Yuxin Wu, Duo Yu, Dan Zheng, Kuo Zhang, Juan Li, Wangqian Zhang, Shuning Wang, Haozhe Qin, Qiang Hao, Yingqi Zhang, Anan Yin, Yalong He, Lei He, Xiaonan Luo, Wei Lin, Meng Li, Jintao Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlthough immunotherapy has transformed the treatment landscape for many types of cancer, its therapeutic efficacy in glioblastoma (GBM) is limited by insufficient antigen presentation and the immunogenic cell exclusion in the tumor microenvironment. Here, we develop a candidate-based CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) functional screen to identify regulators of conventional dendritic cell (cDC)-fate specification. We determine that the transcription factors Zfp366/Znf366, Pu.1, Irf8, and Batf3 (ZPIB) are sufficient to convert GBM cells into cDC-like cells. ZPIB-mediated reprogramming results in global transcriptional and epigenetic remodeling in glioma cells. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) profiling also reveals efficient and dynamic reprogramming of GBM cells to cDCs in vivo. Moreover, reprogrammed tumor cells remodel the microenvironment and elicit systemic tumor-eradicating and durable antitumor immunity in multiple mouse GBM models. Antitumor immunity elicited by ZPIB-DCs is synergistic with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Finally, we evaluate the clinical applicability of this approach by generating ZPIB-DCs from GBM patients within a humanized model. Our study represents a cellular reprogramming therapeutic strategy with broad implications for clinical immunotherapy.Keywords: lineage reprogramming, glioblastoma, cancer immunotherapy, tumor microenvironment, CRISPR screen

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most fatal type of primary central nervous system tumor, and despite aggressive therapies, the median survival time remains dismal, especially at recurrence.1,2 While immunotherapy has revolutionized treatment paradigms across various cancer types, its therapeutic efficacy remains limited in GBM.3 The main reasons include a low tumor mutation load, defective antigen-presenting function of dendritic cells (DCs), and limited immune cell infiltration.4,5

Cellular reprogramming refers to the concept of rewiring the transcriptional and epigenetic network of one cell state to that of a different cell type.6,7 Mouse and human somatic cells can be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells by inducing the expression of different combinations of transcription factors.8,9,10 Alternative somatic cell fates can also be instructed via direct lineage reprogramming by inducing the expression of transcription factors that specify the identity of specific cell types, such as myocytes, neurons, and hepatocytes.7,11,12 Hematopoietic progenitors and mature cells have also been generated using this strategy. In vivo cell fate reprogramming enables the conversion of endogenous somatic cells into a specified cell identity within an organism for therapeutic benefit directly at the disease location.13,14 This strategy has the potential to overcome the substantial challenge of ex vivo cell manufacturing for personalized cell therapies.

Cancer development is accompanied by modifications of the genome and epigenome. Compared with somatic cells, cancer cells are endowed with epigenetic plasticity that allows reversal or modification of their fate by cellular reprogramming.15,16 The extensive epigenetic remodeling elicited by cell fate reprogramming leads to disruption of the oncogenic transcriptional network, resulting in reduced tumorigenicity.17 Previous efforts to reprogram cancer cells have aimed to directly decrease the cancer cells’ oncogenic potential and induce cancer cell differentiation or conversion to an unrelated cell fate. For example, the exogenous expression of HNF1A, HNF4A, and FOXA3 promoted the conversion of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells to hepatocyte-like cells.18 In acute myeloid leukemia (AML) mouse models, perturbing LSD1 and WNT reprograms gene transcription and induces AML cell differentiation.19 Ronen et al.20 showed that the cellular plasticity of cancer cells undergoing epithelial-mesenchymal transition can be exploited to induce the transdifferentiation of breast cancer cells into post-mitotic and functional adipocytes, leading to the attenuation of primary tumor invasion and metastasis. However, this approach requires the successful conversion of most tumor cells, which remains challenging due to the low efficiency of both the reprogramming and delivery approaches. Thus, a reprogramming approach capable of simultaneously reducing the oncogenic potential and inducing immune activation in tumors holds therapeutic promise for enhancing cancer immunotherapy efficacy.

Here, we performed CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) screen for reprogramming GBM cells into professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and determined that the transcription factors Zfp366/Znf366, Pu.1, Irf8, and Batf3 (ZPIB) modulate the conversion of glioma cells into conventional dendritic cell (cDC)-like cells. ZPIB-induced DCs (ZPIB-DCs) presented a cDC phenotype and transcriptional program and possessed similar functions, including the ability to cross-present antigens to naive T cells. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis revealed the reprogrammed trajectory of GBM cells into cDC-like cells and the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment in vivo. Our findings also demonstrated that in situ induction of cDC reprogramming leads to robust, long-lasting, and systemic antitumor immunity independent of exogenous stimulation, providing a strategy for cancer immunotherapy.

Results

CRISPRa screens reveal the optimal transcription factor network for reprogramming glioma cells into cDC-like cells

Several transcription factors have been implicated in both DC development and subset specification.21 Given the pivotal role of DCs in driving immune responses, we asked whether a certain combination of transcription factors could induce DC differentiation and endow glioma cells with professional antigen presentation capacity. We constructed a lentiviral CRISPRa library. This library includes 5 single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) each of 26 transcription factors implicated in the differentiation, development, and activation of DCs. We then performed CRISPRa screens to identify key DC reprogramming regulators. The human and mouse cell lines, LN229 and GL261, were transduced with either the human sgRNA library or the mouse sgRNA library, respectively. The transduced cells were then sorted on the basis of the differential surface expression of immune- and antigen-specific markers (CD45/CD11c/MHCⅡ), and individual sgRNA read counts were then evaluated by deep sequencing (Figure 1A). Our screening strategy allowed us to identify the most critical regulatory factors for DC reprogramming in both human and mouse glioma cells (Figure 1B; Figures S1A and S1B).

Subsequently, intersection genes of the two parts were selected as candidate targets for further analysis. Among them, we found that sgRNAs for Pu.1 (Spi1), Zfp366/Znf366, Batf3, Irf8, and TCF4 were highly enriched in both screens (Figures 1C and 1D; Figures S1C and S1D). Interestingly, Pu.1, Irf8, and Batf3 (PIB) have been reported to reprogram fibroblasts or tumor cells into DC-like cells.22,23 Moreover, previous work has demonstrated that Zfp366/Znf366 (called Zfp366 in mouse and Znf366 in human cells) is a pivotal element of the regulatory network controlling cDC lineage specification and effector functions.24 Tcf4 is expressed specifically in plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) and is required for their development.25 Indeed, the upregulation of PIB was able to reprogram a subset of mouse and human glioma cells into DC-like cells. However, the expression levels of cDC and APC surface markers, as well as the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, remained detectable but suboptimal. The pools of four transcription factors (PIB + Tcf4 and PIB + Zfp366/Znf366) subsequently induced the expression of cDC and APC surface markers, as well as the secretion of cytokines. The addition of Zfp366/Znf366 significantly increased the efficiency of glioma cell reprogramming into cDCs, whereas the effect of adding Tcf4 was notably weaker (Figures S1E–S1H). Thus, we identified ZPIB (Zfp366/Znf366, Pu.1, Batf3, and Irf8) as the most effective transcription factor network required to induce comprehensive reprogramming of glioma cells and the acquisition of DC morphology.

To comprehensively compare the reprogramming efficiency and therapeutic efficacy of ZPIB with the previously reported PIB22 combination and the enhanced PIBD (PIB + ID2)26 variant, we performed systematic evaluations both in vitro and in vivo. Mouse and human glioma cells (GL261 and LN229) were transfected with plasmids encoding eGFP, PIB, PIBD, or ZPIB transcription factors. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that ZPIB transduction resulted in significantly higher proportions of CD45+MHC II+ ZPIB-DCs in both GL261 and LN229 cells compared with PIB and PIBD transductions (Figures S1I and S1J). Building on these in vitro findings, we evaluated therapeutic efficacy in orthotopic glioma models. The mice implanted with GL261 pre-transfected with ZPIB exhibited markedly reduced tumor volumes and prolonged survival compared with those receiving PIB- or PIBD-transfected cells (Figures S1K–S1M). Similarly, in human LN229 orthotopic models, ZPIB demonstrated superior tumor suppression and survival benefit over PIB and PIBD (Figures S1N–S1P). These results confirm that the inclusion of Zfp366/Znf366 in the transcription factor network significantly enhances both the reprogramming efficiency and antitumor efficacy compared with PIB or PIBD alone.

To validate whether the hit genes induced the DC phenotype, we examined the effects of the ectopic expression of ZPIB on the panhematopoietic marker CD45 and the APC marker MHCII in mouse glioma cell lines. The population of CD45+MHCII+ cells was named ZPIB-DCs and used to quantify the reprogramming efficiency. ZPIB-DCs were efficiently generated in GL261 glioma cells following 5 days of ectopic ZPIB expression (Figures 1E and 1F; Figure S2A). Reprogramming of glioma cells was accompanied by cell surface expression of the cDC-specific markers CD11c, CLEC9A, and XCR1 within CD45+MHC II+ cells, suggesting the induction of a cDC-like phenotype in mouse glioma cells (Figure 1G). Scanning electron microscopy revealed that ZPIB-DCs exhibited a stellate morphology with numerous dendrite-like protrusions extending from the cell body, characteristic of mature DCs, whereas control cells displayed a relatively smooth surface (Figure 1H). Similarly, to investigate human glioma cells, we used five human glioma cell lines and three primary GBM cell lines. Mirroring the reprogramming process in mouse cells, we observed the emergence of a population of reprogrammed CD45+HLA-DR+ cells ranging from 23.53% ± 5.03% to 39.3% ± 4.37% across all GBM cell lines and primary GBM cells transduced with ZPIB, whereas such a population was absent in the eGFP-transduced controls, suggesting that cDC reprogramming is universally applicable to mouse and human GBM cells (Figure S2B). We also detected increased surface expression of β-2-microglobulin (β2M) and MHC-I in human ZPIB-DCs, suggesting that ZPIB gradually increases tumor immunogenicity (Figures S2C and S2D). Similar morphological changes were observed in LN229 cells under scanning electron microscopy (Figure S2E). Consistent with the findings in the mouse system, reprogrammed CD45+HLA-DR+ cells expressed the cDC surface markers CLEC9A, XCR1, and CD11c (Figure S2F). We then evaluated the expression of the costimulatory molecules CD40, CD80, and CD86, as they represent a necessary second signal to ensure T cell activation. ZPIB induced the expression of costimulatory molecules in mouse and human glioma cells, whereas treatment of reprogrammed cells with a TLR3 agonist (poly(I:C)) further facilitated the expression of costimulatory molecules (Figure 1I). In human GBM cells, we detected the expression of costimulatory molecules starting on day 3, which increased gradually until day 9 (Figure S2G). ZPIB-DCs responded to TLR3/4 triggering, leading to increased surface expression of costimulatory molecules, implying that cDC reprogramming endows glioma cells with APC machinery to prime T cell responses (Figure S2H).

Next, we sought to address whether ZPIB-DCs acquired cDC function by evaluating the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, antigen uptake and processing, and T cell activation (Figure 1J). We initially focused on the capacity of ZPIB-DCs to secrete proinflammatory cytokines, which represent the third signal necessary for T cell activation. We observed the secretion by mouse ZPIB-DCs of IL-15, IL-12P70, IL-6, IL-18, TNF-α, and CXCL10, particularly following lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation (Figure 1K). Similarly, in the human system, ZPIB-DCs secreted IL-12p70, TNF-α, CXCL10, and IL-6 in response to poly(I:C) and LPS, indicating that reprogramming endows glioma cells with the capacity to secrete the cDC-related cytokines and chemokines required for immune cell recruitment and activation (Figure S2I). APCs routinely process and present tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) through phagocytosis of tumor cell-derived debris within the tumor microenvironment. We confirmed that ZPIB-DCs express key mediators of Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis (Fcgr2b, Pla2g4a, Gab2, Asap3, Pik3cd, Prkce, and Akt3) and phagocytosis of dead cells (Pld4, Pear1, Ncf4, and Folr1) (Figure S2J). Thus, we evaluated the capacity of ZPIB-DCs to capture exogenous antigens. First, we confirmed the ability of ZPIB-DCs to engulf Alexa Fluor 647-labeled ovalbumin (OVA), suggesting that ZPIB-DCs have phagocytic activity (Figure 1L). To understand whether ZPIB-DCs can similarly process extracellular antigens from the tumor milieu, we leveraged the DQ-OVA reagent, which can be used as a surrogate unprocessed tumor antigen. The incubation of DQ-OVA with GL261 cells and ZPIB-DCs revealed that ZPIB-DCs presented an increased capacity for processing exogenous antigens (Figure 1M). This process was inhibited by leupeptin and lactacystin, indicating its dependence on the protease and proteasome activity (Figure S2K). Moreover, human and mouse ZPIB-DCs engulfed fluorescently labeled dead cells, a hallmark of cross-presenting DCs that rely on Clec9a engagement (Figure 1N; Figures S2L and S2M). In DCs, efficient processing of antigens relies on the assembly and activity of the immunoproteasome.27 Accordingly, we observed an increase in the protein levels of the immunoproteasome subunits PSMB9 and PSMB10 in ZPIB-DCs, suggesting that increased antigen processing is mediated by the immunoproteasome (Figure S2N). To further examine whether ZPIB-DCs are capable of processing and presenting not only exogenous antigens but also self-derived TAAs, we utilized the OVA-expressing cell lines GL261-OVA and LN229-OVA. We evaluated the T cell priming ability of OT-I CD8+ T cells. Reprogrammed GL261 cells activated the proliferation of naive CD8+ T cells after a pulse with the OVA peptide SIINFEKL. Although eGFP-transduced cells failed to prime naive CD8+ T cells, further stimulation of ZPIB-DCs with poly(I:C) improved T cell priming (73.87% ± 3.7% ZPIB vs. 87.3% ± 5.95% poly(I:C)) (Figure 1O). Compared with co-culture with GL261 cells alone, ZPIB-DCs significantly increased the expression of the reactive markers CD69 and CD25, as well as the levels of the cytokines GZMB, IFN-γ, and TNF-α. This T cell stimulation activity could be further enhanced through the addition of R848 (Figure 1P). Finally, we sought to evaluate whether reprogrammed ZPIB-DCs can activate T cells to kill glioma cells. Compared with anti-CD3/CD28-activated CD8+ T cells, ZPIB-DC-activated CD8+ T cells exhibited significantly greater cytotoxicity against GL261 cells (Figure 1Q). Collectively, our results highlight that ZPIB-mediated reprogramming converts mouse and human glioma cells into ZPIB-DCs endowed with professional APC functions.

ZPIB-mediated reprogramming induces global transcriptional and epigenetic remodeling in glioma cells

To evaluate the kinetics of reprogramming at the transcriptional and epigenetic levels, human GBM cells and mouse glioma cells were transduced with lentivirus ZPIB. Reprogrammed (CD45+HLA-DR+) cells were profiled by RNA-seq and ATAC-seq (Figure 2A). ZPIB ectopic expression rapidly upregulated the expression of cDC-specific genes, including Irf8, CD74, Pu.1, Clec9a, Batf3, Zfp366, and Ptprc, highlighting the dynamic, stepwise unfolding of the cDC fate (Figure 2B). Furthermore, pathway, molecular function, and biological process analyses revealed enrichment of genes related to IFN-β/IFN-γ signaling and the immune response (Figure 2C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified key canonical pathways that were specifically activated or suppressed in ZPIB-DCs compared with parental cells. For example, ZPIB-reprogrammed glioma cells presented significantly suppressed cell cycle signaling pathways, which indicated that glioma cells lost their proliferative characteristics and entered a state of cell-cycle arrest (Figure 2D). We then tested whether the transcriptional patterns were reflected in reduced tumorigenicity in vitro. We purified partially and completely reprogrammed cells via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) on day 9 after lentiviral transduction and plated them in soft agar for 2 weeks. We did not observe the formation of colonies from either partially reprogrammed cells or completely reprogrammed cells (Figure S3A). To verify these findings in vivo, GL261, LN229, and primary human GBM cell-derived ZPIB-DCs or GFP-transduced cells were injected orthotopically into the brains of NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgem1cya (C-NKG) mice. In contrast to the mice transplanted with reprogrammed GBM cells, all of which formed tumors and died during the first 40 days, the mice implanted with ZPIB-DCs remained tumor free and survived for the duration of the experiment (Figures S3B–S3D). These data indicated that ZPIB reprogramming enforces cell-cycle arrest and reduces the tumorigenicity of glioma cells. A comparison of ZPIB-DCs and GL261 cells revealed terms associated with T cell activation, suggesting that antigen presentation is properly established in ZPIB-DCs (Figure 2D). We subsequently performed molecular analyses at the gene-set and individual-gene levels. We found a strong positive correlation between transcriptional changes during the transition of glioma cells to ZPIB-DCs (versus their treatment-naive states) and mature DCs (Figure 2E).

Next, we determined whether ZPIB-DCs were converted to cDCs from the chromatin landscape. Therefore, we analyzed chromatin accessibility across the genome between glioma cells and ZPIB-DCs. We observed that assays for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq) peaks were predominantly located in the promoter, intron, and distal intergenic regions, and dimensionality reduction analysis effectively distinguished these cell types (Figure S4). After aligning and removing duplicate and low-mapping-quality reads, we calculated the average ATAC-seq signals around transcription start sites (TSSs). The data indicated that ZPIB-DCs presented greater ATAC-seq signals around TSSs than nonreprogrammed cells did (Figure 2F). The peaks most significantly elevated in ZPIB-DCs were highly enriched in motifs recognized by Clec9a, Xcr1, Zfp366, H2-ab1, H2-dmb1, and H2-dmb2, which have been reported to engage in antigen recognition and presentation, migration, and lineage differentiation of cDCs (Figure 2G). However, these peaks were not enriched in nonreprogrammed cells, highlighting a fundamental difference in the chromatin landscape between ZPIB-DCs and nonreprogrammed cells. Motif discovery on chromatin regions that became accessible in reprogrammed cells revealed that they were enriched for the Pu.1, Batf3, and Irf8 motifs, confirming their dominant role in initiating cDC reprogramming. Motif analysis during reprogramming also revealed that the recruitment of Ctcf, Elf4, and Elf5 is an important regulator of chromatin architecture and cDC function after transdifferentiation (Figure 2H). Additionally, combinatory analysis using RNA-seq and ATAC-seq revealed that genes upregulated in ZPIB-DCs were associated with T cell activation, positive regulation of T cell proliferation, interferon (IFN)-mediated signaling pathway, antigen processing, and antigen presentation, reflecting the functional maturation of ZPIB-DCs (Figures 2I and 2J).

To verify whether the transcription factor network governing the ZPIB-DC transcriptome and chromatin remodeling is conserved between mice and humans, we transduced human GBM cells (LN229) with ZPIB. Consistent with the remodeling process in mouse glioma cells, differentially expressed genes were associated with the IFN-γ-mediated signaling pathway and the pathways related to phagocytosis and antigen processing and presentation (Figures S5A and S5B). Consistently, there was an overall greater number of cDC-related gene peaks with corresponding greater chromatin accessibility (Figures S5C and S5D). Pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that the differentially expressed motifs were associated with the pathways involved in immune response, such as pathways related to cytokine secretion, the cellular response to IFN-β, and T cell differentiation (Figures S5E and S5F). These findings underscore a fundamental difference in the chromatin landscape of ZPIB-DCs compared with nonreprogrammed cells.

ZPIB-DCs elicit systemic tumor-eradicating and durable antitumor immunity

To understand the potential therapeutic benefit of generating ZPIB-DCs in the context of tumor progression, we used a polycistronic construct encoding ZPIB cloned in a doxycycline-inducible lentiviral vector (Tet-On/ZPIB) (Figure S6A). Intracranial injection of reprogrammable, but uninduced, GL261 cells into immunodeficient Rag1−/− mice resulted in rapid disease progression and mortality. Notably, the induction of ZPIB-DCs from GL261 cells during glioma development significantly prolonged the median survival time in Rag1−/− mice, presumably by effectively inhibiting the malignant proliferation of the engrafted cells; however, it did not completely achieve tumor regression and eradication (Figures 3A and 3B; Figures S6B and S6C). This result demonstrates that forced glioma reprogramming in the absence of an immune compartment does not result in meaningful disease eradication. When immunocompetent syngeneic C57BL/6 mice were engrafted with the same reprogrammed GL261 cells, induction of ZPIB-DCs during disease progression led to complete elimination of glioma cells and long-term overall survival (Figure 3C; Figures S6D and S6E). We also observed elevated levels of CD45+ immune cells, CD8+ T cells, and MHCII+ APCs in tumor tissues, suggesting that local administration of reprogrammed glioma cells inside the tumor is sufficient to elicit expansion of antigen-specific T cells, leading to the control of tumor growth (Figure 3D; Figure S6F). To gain insight into the degree of ZPIB-DC induction required to elicit a therapeutic benefit, we titrated the percentage of reprogrammed GL261 cells injected intracranially into syngeneic animals. Thus, upon exposure to doxycycline-containing chow, we effectively elucidated the overall efficiency of in vivo reprogramming to defined ratios. While increased overall survival was strongly correlated with the increased reprogramming efficiency, in some animals, the engrafted glioma cells were completely eradicated after as little as 5% of the glioma cells underwent doxycycline induction. When the percentage of induced cells reached above 50%, all the mice achieved complete tumor eradication (Figure 3E).

We further probed the ability of local ZPIB-DC induction to elicit a systemic antitumor response by performing bilateral tumor challenge. Subcutaneous transplantation of inducible GL261 cells into the flanks of syngeneic C57BL/6 mice was performed concurrently with the transplantation of unmodified parental GL261 cells into the contralateral flank of the same animal. Additionally, a heterologous HCC tumor was transplanted into the upper back region of the same mice (Figure 3F). The systemic effect of local ZPIB-DC activity was assessed by monitoring the growth of the contralateral parental tumor. Strikingly, the induction of ZPIB-DCs in a single flank was sufficient to drive the regression of parental tumors in the contralateral flank of most animals but not the HCC tumors (Hepa1–6), indicating systemic and antigen-specific antitumor immunity (Figures 3G–3I). Reduced cell proliferation and apoptosis induction in the inducible GL261 group were confirmed by Ki-67 and TUNEL assays, respectively (Figures S6G and S6H). These data demonstrate that local ZPIB-DC-dependent immune priming elicits potent, systemic immunity capable of trafficking to and eradicating distant and residual lesions. To exclude the possibility that endogenous DCs are indirectly responsible for the observed antitumor effects of ZPIB-DC induction, we utilized Batf3-deficient mice, which lack endogenous cDC1. We observed reduced tumor growth, along with elevated numbers of CD45+ cells, CD8+ T cells, and MHC II+ cells in the tumor tissues (Figures 3J and 3K; Figure S6I). Additionally, there was an increase in survival rates and complete regression of tumor growth in doxycycline-induced mice, showing that reprogramming was sufficient to elicit antitumor immunity in the absence of the cDC1 compartment (Figure 3L).

To address whether immune memory was induced, we rechallenged surviving C57BL/6 or Batf3−/− animals that showed tumor regressions; while naive mice developed tumors, most of the surviving mice remained tumor free (83.33% of C57BL/6; 50% of BATF3KO). In the peripheral blood of the surviving mice, the proportions of central memory CD62L+CD44+CD8+ T cells (Tcm) and cytotoxic effector memory CD62L−CD44+CD8+ T cells (Tem) were markedly greater than those in the peripheral blood of age-matched naive mice, suggesting that durable immune memory was induced to prevent tumor recurrence (Figures 3M and 3N). Taken together, these findings highlight that in vivo reprogramming mediated by ZPIB is sufficient to elicit antitumor immunity and protects against tumor growth after rechallenging and that the effects are independent of endogenous cDC1.

scRNA-seq profiling reveals efficient reprogramming of glioma cells to cDCs and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment

To map the kinetics of tumor cell reprogramming and changes in the tumor microenvironment, inducible GL261 cells were implanted intracranially into syngeneic C57BL/6 mice, and scRNA-seq libraries were prepared from tumors harvested 7 days (early state) or 14 days (late state) after doxycycline induction (Figure 4A). The cells were clustered into seven major cell types—tumor/ZPIB-DCs, T cells, pericyte cells, NK cells, myeloid cells, endothelial cells, and B cells (Figures 4B and 4C). Subsequently, GL261-derived cells were categorized into eleven subclusters (Figure S7A). Non-reprogrammed GL261 cells predominantly comprise clusters 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9. Early-stage tumors primarily consisted of clusters 2, 4, 6, and 8, whereas late-stage tumors mainly included clusters 0, 2, 6, and 8. Among these, clusters 0, 4, and 10 were specific clusters that emerged only after reprogramming (Figure 4D). RNA velocity analysis was used to predict the future states of individual cells. The results revealed that clusters 0, 4, and 10 emerged from more primitive tumor cell types to constitute the largest population of doxycycline-induced tumors. Further trajectory analysis using diffusion maps and trajectory inference (Monocle 3) corroborated the pathway suggested by the RNA velocity analysis, which revealed that the cells from saline-treated GL261 tumors predominantly occupied the very early stage of the trajectory pseudotime; tumor cells harvested at day 7 were intermediary populations, and tumor cells harvested at day 14 were concentrated toward the late stage. Cluster 4 initially emerged from nonreprogrammed tumor cells following doxycycline induction during the early phase and subsequently evolved into cluster 0 in the later phase, whereas cluster 10 retained a small portion in both the early and late stages in the pseudotime trajectory (Figure 4E). Unsupervised clustering and differential gene expression analysis revealed early state or late state after doxycycline induction. Cluster 4 exhibited increased expression of genes involved in DC cytotoxicity (GZMB/GZMA), immune cell recruitment (CCL4), allergen recognition (FCER1G), and metabolic immune regulation (APOE), whereas cluster 0 exhibited high expression of genes involved in antigen cross-presentation (H2-K1/IGHA1), immune complex processing (IGHA1/IGKC/JCHAIN), and cell migration (TMSB4X). In agreement with these findings, reprogrammed cells presented increased expression of MHC class I (B2M & H2-K1) and MHC class II (CD74 & H2-Aa) molecules (Figure 4F; Figures S7B–S7D). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of state-specific genes revealed distinct functional roles of the clusters. Interferon production and the NF-κB pathway, which support the inflammatory response, were enriched in cells in the early phase of reprogramming and, thus, might help maintain elevated cytokine secretion. The activation of Toll-like receptors significantly promoted cytokine secretion and antigen processing and presentation in reprogrammed cells (Figure 4G). Moreover, pathways associated with cell differentiation, such as dendrite morphogenesis and cell fate commitment, were enriched in cells in the late phase of reprogramming (Figures S7E and S7F). Each cluster in our dataset was graded by a DC gene signature score. As expected, clusters 0, 4, and 10 presented higher DC signature scores, along with elevated expression of genes related to DC function and antigen presentation (Figures 4H and 4I). Overall, we demonstrated that scRNA-seq coupled with dimensionality reduction and trajectory inference techniques reveal the dynamics of ZPIB-DC reprogramming and identify critical pathways for establishing ZPIB-DC fate.

To further characterize the T cell responses elicited by in vivo cDC reprogramming, we segregated single cells expressing either CD8 or CD4 and performed cluster annotation. After the induction of doxycycline, there was a significant increase in T cell infiltration due to the elevation of multiple chemokines and inflammatory factors (Figure 4J). On day 7 post-doxycycline induction, we observed increased numbers of almost all T cell phenotypes within tumors undergoing reprogramming. Notably, effector CD8+ T cells (CD8_Teff), proliferative CD8+ T cells (CD8_prof), and Th17-polarized CD4+ T cells (CD4_T17) were among the most frequent T cell populations, indicating the induction of a robust immune response. On day 14 after doxycycline induction, the total number of infiltrating T cells was greater than that on day 7. CD8_Teff, effector memory CD8+ T cells (CD8_Tem), and exhausted CD8+ T cells (CD8_Tex) predominated the T cell infiltrate in tumors induced by ZPIB-DCs (Figure 4K). Notably, the observed increase in the percentage of CD8_Tex cells is likely due to continuous antigenic stimulation by ZPIB-DCs in the tumor microenvironment. To assess the potential crosstalk driving interactions between ZPIB-DCs and T cells, a CellChat analysis was performed, which revealed stronger interactions of ZPIB-DCs with CD8_prof, CD4_Treg, CD8_Tex, CD8_Teff, and CD8_Tem (Figure 4L; Figure S7G). We subsequently ran the CellChat pattern recognition module to reveal the key sequential signaling events during the interaction of ZPIB-DCs with T cells. At the outgoing end of signaling, we predicted that PARs and CCL signals are secreted by clusters 0 and 4, whereas IL-6, SEMA3, and CXCL are secreted by cluster 10 (Figures 4M and 4N; Figure S7H).

We further analyzed the phenotype of tumor-infiltrating myeloid lineage immune cells by scoring the expression of inflammatory gene signatures within these populations. Intriguingly, the induction of doxycycline led to increased myeloid cell activation and the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, which is consistent with the observed rapid orchestration of effective antitumor immunity (Figures S8A–S8C). Compared with vehicle-treated mice, doxycycline-treated mice presented greater expression of genes associated with M1-like macrophages (e.g., IL-6 and Socs3) but lower expression of genes associated with M2-like macrophages (e.g., SPP1, C1QC, and MRC1) (Figures S8D–S8F). We also calculated M1 and M2 gene signature-based scores at the single-cell level in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Overall, TAMs isolated from both early-stage and late-stage tumors from doxycycline-treated mice presented significantly higher M1 gene signature scores than those from saline-treated mice (Figure 4O). While the primary role of ZPIB-DCs is to enhance T cell-mediated antitumor immunity, we have also discovered their ability to secrete cytokines, such as CSF2 and IFN-γ, which promote M1 polarization of TAMs (Figure S8G). Consistent with this finding, depletion of either CD4+ or CD8+ T cells did not completely abrogate the therapeutic benefit of ZPIB-DC generation (Figure S8H). Together, these data are consistent with ZPIB-DC-dependent reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment, which promotes T cell infiltration and the activation of antitumor immunity.

LNP-ZPIB-mediated reprogramming elicits potent antitumor effects and reshapes the tumor microenvironment

Although intracranial injection of in-vitro-generated GBM-derived ZPIB-DCs into orthotopic GBM tumors delayed tumor growth and prolonged survival in mice, this approach poses significant safety concerns in clinical applications (Figures S9A–S9C). We next aimed to identify a platform to deliver ZPIB factors to tumors and elicit cDC reprogramming in situ. Lipid-based nanoparticles (LNPs) have emerged as revolutionary carriers owing to their favorable physicochemical characteristics, with specific applications and strengths that are particularly useful in immunotherapeutic agent delivery. We developed a transferrin-modified LNP delivery platform to encapsulate and deliver Zfp366/Znf366, Pu.1, Batf3, and Irf8 mRNAs to the transferrin receptor (TfR) on glioma cells. The resulting mRNA-encapsulated LNPs were designated LNP-ZPIB. The final solution of LNP-ZPIB had an average particle size of 141 nm and an encapsulation rate of >95% (Figures S10A and S10B). Subsequently, we examined the reprogramming efficiency of LNP-ZPIB in mouse glioma cell lines, where LNP-ZPIB successfully induced highly efficient cDC reprogramming (Figure S10C). We then profiled the reprogramming kinetics and detected rapid phenotypic changes on day 3 after LNP-ZPIB treatment (Figure S10D). LNP-ZPIB promoted the secretion of cytokines associated with T cell activation and immune cell recruitment (Figure S10E). The reprogramming also induced the expression of CD40, CD80, and CD86, confirming the acquisition of an immunogenic cDC-like phenotype (Figure S10F). Importantly, the presence of TAMs, the main immunosuppressive cells in GBM, did not impair ZPIB-mediated reprogramming efficiency, whereas the addition of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4, IL-10, IL-13, and TGF-β and the immunoregulatory factor GM-CSF had no significant effect on the reprogramming efficiency (Figures S10G and S10H).

To evaluate the efficacy of a gene therapy approach based on in situ ZPIB-DC reprogramming, we established GL261 tumors and a nestin-tva/Ink4a/Arf−/− spontaneous glioma model.28 In situ injection of Cy5.5-labeled LNP-ZPIB demonstrated that the drug was predominantly concentrated in the brain tumor region and was minimally present in other areas (Figure S11A). Consistent with scRNA-seq analyses, ZPIB-DCs not only increased the number of CD8_Teff cells but also promoted CD8_Tex cell accumulation. Consequently, we suggested that the incorporation of αPD-1 antibodies may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of LNP-ZPIB (Figure 5A). The results revealed that 3/8 of the mice receiving LNP-ZPIB monotherapy achieved a complete response (CR), and their median survival time was significantly prolonged. ZPIB-DC reprogramming synergized with αPD-1 treatment, leading to increased CR in mice injected with GL261 cells (Figure 5B; Figure S11B). Strikingly, 7/8 mice treated with LNP-ZPIB combined with αPD-1 achieved a CR, and they remained tumor free for 50 days, which also resulted in the expansion of CD45+, MHC Ⅱ+, and CD8+ T cells (Figure 5C). Similar results were obtained using a genetically engineered mouse glioma model (Nestin-tva/Ink4a/Arf−/− model), and the survival rate in the combination therapy group was 100% (Figures 5D and 5E; Figure S11C). To assess whether LNP-ZPIB-mediated antitumor immunity requires endogenous DCs, we established GL261 tumors in Batf3−/− mice. We observed reduced tumor growth, prolonged survival, and complete regression of tumor growth in LNP-ZPIB-treated animals, indicating that reprogramming mediated by LNP-ZPIB was sufficient to elicit antitumor immunity in the absence of endogenous DCs (Figures 5F and 5G). To address whether immune memory was induced, we rechallenged surviving C57BL/6 or Batf3−/− animals, which presented with tumor regression. While naive mice developed tumors, the majority of the surviving mice remained tumor free. Notably, the proportions of Tem and Tcm cells were significantly increased in tumor-free mice (Figure 5H; Figures S11D and S11E).

To further delineate how LNP-ZPIB reshaped the immune landscape in the tumor milieu, we performed high-dimensional single-cell immune profiling using time-of-flight mass cytometry (CyTOF) to analyze the microenvironments. Using CyTOF and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding analysis, we profiled 42 markers and identified 25 distinct immune cell clusters (Figures 5I and 5J; Figure S11F). Compared with saline treatment, the addition of LNP-ZPIB significantly increased the proportions of effector CD8+ T cells and effector memory CD8+ T cells. Moreover, we observed a dramatic decrease in the proportion of granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs; CD11b+Ly6G+) and a significant increase in the proportion of M1-like macrophages (F4/80+CD86+). As expected, the combination treatment of αPD-1 blockade and LNP-ZPIB further augmented the proportions of effector CD8+ T cells and resulted in a significant decrease in the proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells (Figures 5K–5M). These broad-based changes in adaptive immune populations were corroborated by flow cytometry, which revealed that, compared with vehicle treatment and monotherapy, the combination treatment of αPD-1 and LNP-ZPIB significantly augmented proportions of activated CD8+CD69+ T cells and elicited the secretion of the cytotoxic cytokines IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB, whereas the proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells was decreased in the combination group (Figure 5N). These results indicated that our LNP-ZPIB could effectively induce cDC reprogramming and exhibited superior antitumor efficacy in vivo.

LNP-ZPIB reprograms patient-derived GBM cells into cDCs to increase antitumor immunity

Finally, we explored the potential applicability of the generation of ZPIB-DCs to GBM patients (Figure 6A). Briefly, patient-derived GBM cells were cultured in media supplemented with 10% human serum to avoid exposure to xenogeneic antigens. The LNP-ZPIB reprogramming efficiency in patient-derived GBM cells ranged from 45% to 78% depending on the patient sample (Figure S12A). Bulk RNA-seq GSEA revealed enrichment in IFN-γ signaling, antigen presentation and processing, cytokine production, and T cell activation in the GBM03 and GBM07 specimens (Figure 6B). Indeed, pathway, molecular function, and biological process analyses also demonstrated robust upregulation of the gene modules associated with immune effector pathways, such as the STAT and NF-κB pathways, which regulate cDC differentiation, maturation, and inflammatory responses (Figures S12B and S12C). Global analysis of the differentially accessible regions revealed that incubation with LNP-ZPIB substantially changed the accessibility landscape of ZPIB-DCs compared with that of nonreprogrammed cells (Figure 6C). We detected increased chromatin accessibility in gene bodies as well as the upstream and downstream noncoding genomic regions flanking cDC-specific genes, including ITGAX, CLEC9A, HLA-DR, ETV6, PU.1, and ZNF366, potentially reflecting the dynamic evolution of cDC states after treatment with LNP-ZPIB (Figure S12D). Bioinformatic analysis of differentially accessible regions at promoters revealed differentially accessible transcription factor-binding motifs related to transcription factors associated with cDC lineage differentiation and maturation (Figure 6D). Next, we analyzed the ability of these primary ZPIB-DCs to stimulate autologous, unmanipulated T cells. Compared with T cells co-cultured with GBM cells alone, primary GBM-derived ZPIB-DCs co-cultured with bulk unmanipulated autologous T cells significantly increased T cell activation (Figure 6E). Importantly, T cell activation was inhibited in the presence of MHC-blocking antibodies, indicating that T cell activation is MHC dependent (Figure S12E). We sought to evaluate whether LNP-ZPIB-mediated reprogramming triggered the killing of GBM cells by CD8+ T cells isolated from the same patient. Compared with anti-CD3/CD28-activated CD8+ T cells, ZPIB-DC-activated CD8+ T cells exhibited increased cytotoxic capacity against GBM cells (Figure 6F). Thus, these data indicate that patient-derived ZPIB-DCs can stimulate autologous GBM-specific T cell activation and killing.

To investigate whether the increased effector functions promoted by ZPIB-DCs could contribute to enhanced antitumor immunity, we investigated T cell-based adoptive cell transfer (ACT) as a potentially effective approach. Human naive CD8+ T cells expanded in vitro were conditioned with anti-CD3/CD28 or ZPIB-DCs for 24 h before being transferred to C-NKG mice bearing human-established GBM tumor. Compared with anti-CD3/CD28-activated CD8+ T cells, ZPIB-DC-activated human CD8+ T cells more effectively controlled tumor growth (Figures 6G and 6H; Figures S13A–S13C). Interestingly, human CD8+ T cells activated by ZPIB-DCs exhibited antitumor efficacy in HepG2 tumors comparable to that of anti-CD3/CD28-activated CD8+ T cells that was significantly lower than their cytotoxicity against GBM03 and GBM07, which demonstrated their systemic and antigen-specific antitumor immunity.

Finally, peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-humanized C-NKG mice (Hu-PBL) were used to mimic the human immune response to LNP-ZPIB therapy in vivo (Figure 6I). Compared with that in the control group, the subcutaneous (Figures S14A, S14B, S14F, and S14G) and in situ tumor (Figures 6J and 6K; Figures S15A and S15B) volume was significantly reduced and survival time was significantly prolonged following intratumoral injections of LNP-ZPIB. Moreover, the combination of LNP-ZPIB and αPD-1 led to a profound synergistic antitumor effect, with almost complete tumor regression in all the mice. Reduced cell proliferation and apoptosis induction in the LNP-ZPIB group were confirmed by Ki-67 and TUNEL assays, respectively (Figures S14C, S14D, S14H, and S14I). Our data also indicate that the combination of LNP-ZPIB and αPD-1 led to the most favorable antitumor T cell immunity in the tumor microenvironment both in orthotopic model and subcutaneous model, characterized by the highest percentages of IFN-γ+, GZMB+, and TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells (Figures 6L and S15C; Figures S14E and S14J). Peripheral blood analysis in orthotopic model revealed that LNP-ZPIB treatment significantly increased the frequencies of polyfunctional CD8+ T cells compared with saline controls, demonstrating that LNP-ZPIB primed a systemic antitumor immune response that is further amplified by PD-1 checkpoint blockade (Figure 6M; Figure S15D). Furthermore, we noted no significant differences in the spleen weight or health of the vital organs in the mice among the four groups (Figure S16). These findings indicate that LNP-ZPIB not only demonstrates enhanced antitumor activity but also maintains safety without inducing immune system risks in huPBMC-C-NKG models.

To evaluate LNP-ZPIB biosafety, we conducted comprehensive acute and chronic toxicity assessments. In acute toxicity studies (3 and 7 days post-injection), body weight, hematological parameters, liver/kidney function markers, and serum cytokine levels showed no significant differences among the saline, LNP-eGFP, and LNP-ZPIB groups in tumor-bearing mice (Figures S17A–S17C). In chronic toxicity studies (8 weeks post-injection), body weight, hematological parameters, and liver/kidney function markers remained normal across the groups (Figures S17D and S17E). H&E staining of the major organs showed no tissue damage or inflammation (Figure S17F). Serum cytokine profiling revealed no evidence of cytokine storm (Figure S17G). Importantly, neurological assessment using Garcia and the modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS) scoring showed no deficits following intracranial administration (Figure S17H). To further evaluate neurological safety independent of tumor burden, we administered LNP-ZPIB to healthy C57BL/6 mice via intracranial injection. Neurological assessment using Garcia and mNSS scoring at 4 weeks showed no deficits (Figure S18A), and H&E staining of brain tissue revealed no treatment-related histopathological abnormalities (Figure S18B). Collectively, LNP-ZPIB demonstrates favorable safety without systemic toxicity or neurologic impairment, supporting its clinical translation potential.

Discussion

Here, we developed a candidate-based CRISPRa functional screen to identify regulators of cDC-fate specification. We identified ZPIB as the transcription factor network required to induce comprehensive reprogramming of glioma cells and the acquisition of DC morphology. Our results show that the delivery of ZPIB factors to tumors in situ drives a hybrid DC fate at the phenotypic, transcriptomic, and functional levels, results in tumor microenvironment remodeling, and leads to the generation of tumor-eradicating, systemic, and durable immunity. Finally, using primary patient samples, we demonstrated that ZPIB-DCs induce autologous, tumor-specific T cell activation and acquire potent antitumor activity in huPBMC-C-NKG models. These results represent significant advances in current immunotherapeutic approaches.

To date, most attempts to generate cancer vaccines have concentrated on identifying or predicting immunogenic neoantigens and delivering these neoantigens to patients using chemical or cellular adjuvants.29,30 Despite significant advancements of the tumor antigen selection, adjuvant efficacy, and vaccine delivery methods, these efforts have not yielded substantial antitumor activity in patients.31 Lineage reprogramming, or the direct induction of a functional cell lineage from an alternate lineage, has been a long-standing goal within the field of regenerative medicine, with clear implications for the clinical advancement of cellular therapeutics.13,32 Since the initial studies, forced expression of lineage-specific transcription factors has been used to generate a wide array of functionally diverse cells from unrelated lineages, including induced pluripotent stem cells, neurons, cardiomyocytes, and hepatocytes.33,34,35 Through CRISPR-based activation screening, we revealed that ZPIB-mediated direct reprogramming converts GBM cells into immunogenic cDC-like cells endowed with professional antigen presentation ability and decreased tumorigenicity. ZPIB-DCs resemble cDCs with increased expression of immunostimulatory genes, including cytokines, chemokines, and antigen presentation machinery. Within the tumor microenvironment, immunogenic cDCs cross-present tumor antigens to CD8+ T cells, a process that is critical for eliciting antitumor immunity. Importantly, the immunostimulatory activity of ZPIB-DCs did not rely on the presence of endogenous cross-presenting DCs, indicating that ZPIB-DCs are indeed sufficient to modulate various immune cells, which are crucial to the efficacy of immunotherapy.

The in vivo scRNA-seq results demonstrated that ZPIB-DC reprogramming is a gradual and asynchronous process that involves various intermediate populations, resulting in major global transcriptional and epigenetic remodeling and the acquirement of cDC functions, which could be sufficient to elicit antitumor immunity. The ability to recapitulate target cell identity with complete fidelity remains a challenge, mainly because of the partial retention of parental identity. Although further refinement of the cDC reprogramming strategy may strengthen ZPIB-DC identity, partial reprogramming helps retain the expression of tumor neoantigens and supports the priming of clonal expansion of T cells against the heterogeneous population of cancer cells. Thus, ZPIB-DCs are not limited to a defined, selected subset of antigens but have the potential to present a myriad of self-derived tumor antigens directly encoded in their genome, as well as exogenous antigens processed from the tumor microenvironment. They decrease the risk of tumor immune escape relative to the administration of a defined pool of antigenic peptides.36,37 Additionally, many tumor antigens are simply aberrantly expressed self-antigens, which can also be presented by ZPIB-DCs but are commonly overlooked by neoantigen prediction methodologies. ZPIB-mediated reprogramming completely reshaped the tumor immune microenvironment and potently promoted the activation of antigen- and tumor-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Notably, ZPIB-DCs exhibit a hybrid functional identity: they retain canonical cDC1 features essential for cross-presentation to CD8+ T cells, while acquiring cDC2-like capabilities, characterized by high MHC-II expression and potent CD4+ T cell priming. This dual functionality enables ZPIB-DCs to orchestrate a coordinated antitumor response, leveraging both cytotoxic effector function and CD4+ T cell help. In addition, ZPIB-DCs express proinflammatory cytokines and secrete chemokines (e.g., CXCL10, CCL2, and CCL5) involved in the chemoattraction of lymphocytes. A significant increase in the proportions of CD4_T17, CD8_Teff, CD8_prof, and CD8_Tem was detected in both the early and late stages of tumor reprogramming. Given the recently described crosstalk among cDCs, CD8+ T cells, and CD4+ T cells that is associated with positive outcomes, this interaction may also occur between ZPIB-DCs and T cells locally in the tumor microenvironment.38,39 Owing to continuous antigenic stimulation by ZPIB-DCs in the tumor microenvironment, T cells undergo a state of exhaustion. Previous studies have also demonstrated that persistent antigen stimulation gradually upregulates the expression of PD-1 in T cells.40 Therefore, the addition of α-PD1 further enhances the therapeutic efficacy of ZPIB reprogramming in vivo by effectively inhibiting T cell exhaustion. Notably, unlike cDCs, ZPIB-DCs exhibit more complex functions, including the capacity to modulate the activities of myeloid-derived macrophages, whereas TAMs are the predominant immunosuppressive cells in gliomas. ZPIB-DCs can secrete CSF2 and IFN-γ, which have been demonstrated to promote the antitumor polarization of TAMs. Depletion of either CD4+ cells or CD8+ T cells did not completely abrogate the therapeutic benefit of ZPIB-DC generation, further confirming that this benefit is not solely reliant on T cell function. These data indicate that ZPIB-DCs profoundly reshape the landscape of the tumor microenvironment, which is crucial for the treatment of immune-deserted tumors and those with poor immunogenicity.

While the survival benefit observed in these studies is encouraging, these results also underscore the need for further investigations to advance this approach into the clinic. To enable clinical translation, we tested a gene therapy concept by using a nonviral platform to deliver ZPIB to tumors and induce cDC reprogramming in situ. LNPs have been extensively studied for the delivery of RNA molecules and are already being used in the clinic.41,42 Over 60 therapeutics utilizing LNP delivery technology have received approval or are currently in clinical development, with 30 having advanced beyond phase 1 trials.43,44,45 These therapeutics address a variety of conditions, including cardiovascular diseases, liver fibrosis, and rare disorders such as propionic acidemia. Notably, a treatment aimed at preventing atherosclerosis through LNP-mediated base editing of PCSK9 has been demonstrated in clinical settings.46 Additionally, the clinical potential of LNP mRNA CRISPR gene editing to target transthyretin for the treatment of hereditary ATTR amyloidosis has been established.47,48 Our present study clearly demonstrates that LNP-ZPIB exhibits potent antitumor activity in both multiple glioma mouse models and a patient-derived humanized-NKG model. This strategy establishes immunological competence before tumor resection and supports the elimination of residual tumor cells.

Overall, we provide proof of principle that ZPIB reprogramming in situ represents a universal immunotherapeutic modality capable of orchestrating systemic and durable antitumor immunity, overcoming the primary factors, including the immunosuppressive microenvironment, tumor heterogeneity, and loss of antigen presentation, which contribute to the ineffectiveness of immunotherapies. This study paves the way for a class of immunotherapies on the basis of the specific function of cDCs generated in vivo through LNP-ZPIB-mediated reprogramming.

Limitations of the study

While our study demonstrates a promising reprogramming strategy, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our CRISPRa screen was a candidate-based functional screen focused on known DC-related transcription factors. This approach, while effective for identifying key regulators from a defined set, may have overlooked novel or uncharacterized factors essential for optimal cDC reprogramming or function. Second, the reprogramming efficiency, although significant, was incomplete. Residual, nonreprogrammed tumor cell populations or those achieving only a partial DC state could pose the risks of immune escape and eventual tumor recurrence. Third, while the LNP delivery platform showed promise in preclinical models, its translational efficacy, optimal dosing, and potential off-target effects in patients require further investigation. Finally, the long-term consequences of sustained in situ T cell activation and extensive microenvironment remodeling, including the potential for exhaustion or immune-related adverse events, need evaluation in clinical settings. Additionally, due to the limited sample size of the successfully established patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models and the retrospective nature of the primary sample collection, we were unable to perform a statistically robust analysis of sex- or gender-specific effects on reprogramming efficiency or therapeutic outcomes. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts are needed to evaluate whether biological sex or gender influences the efficacy of this cellular reprogramming strategy.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human study

All human tissue samples were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resection at Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and tissue use was conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No. KY20243619-1). All patient samples were anonymized in accordance with ethical guidelines, including the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

PDXs were generated from 3 patient samples with glioblastoma (WHO grade IV), and 2 of these models (GBM03 and GBM07) were successfully established and applied in this study (see Table S1 for details). The patients included 2 females (34 and 66 years old) and 1 male (62 years old) of Han Chinese ethnicity. All patients were treatment-naive at the time of surgical resection and had no history of prior chemotherapy or radiation therapy. The diagnosis of glioblastoma was confirmed by histopathological examination following surgical resection. Detailed patient demographics, including sex, age, tumor location, and molecular characteristics (TP53, EGFR, IDH1, TERT promoter, PTEN, and ATRX mutation status), are provided in Table S1.

Regarding the influence of sex and gender, due to the limited sample size of the successfully established PDX models (n = 2) and the retrospective nature of the primary sample collection, we were unable to perform a statistically robust analysis of sex- or gender-specific effects on reprogramming efficiency or therapeutic outcomes. We acknowledge this as a limitation of the current study, which is further discussed in the “limitations of the study” section.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No.KY20232400-F-1). Mice were housed in a dedicated specific-pathogen-free (SPF) facility under standard controlled conditions (maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2°C with 50%–60% humidity, and provided with ad libitum access to standard chow and water).Wild-type C57BL/6 mice and OT-1 transgenic mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center, Fourth Military Medical University. C-NKG mice and C57BL/6 Batf3em1/Cya mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences. Nestin-Tv-a; Ink4a/Arf−/− mice were obtained from Shaanxi Normal University. All mice used in this study were 6-week-old males, weighing approximately 20–25 g at the time of experimental initiation.

For orthotopic GBM model, GBM cells expressing luciferase were implanted intracranially using a stereotactic frame into mice. In brief, a burr hole was created with a dental drill measuring 0.7mm in diameter, 2.5 mm to the left of the sagittal suture, and 0.5 mm anterior to the bregma. The injection was made at a depth of 2.5mm. To analyze tumor growth, D-Luciferin potassium salt (Abcam, USA) was administered intraperitoneally to animals followed by anesthesia with isoflurane for imaging analysis. For Subcutaneous tumor model, tumor cells (5 × 106 cells) were resuspended in 50 μL of PBS, mixed thoroughly with 50 μL of matrix gel, and then subcutaneously injected into mice. Cells were pre-cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and verified as mycoplasma-free. Mice were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation (2–3% O2), and tumors were allowed to grow for 14 days post-injection. Tumor volume was monitored biweekly using calipers, calculated as (length × width2)/2. Body weight and tumor morphology were recorded.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human glioblastoma cell lines LN229, T98G, A172 and U87MG, human hepatoma cell line HepG2, mouse hepatoma cell line Hepa 1–6 were obtained from ATCC. Human glioblastoma cell line U251 was obtained from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures. Mouse glioblastoma cell line GL261 was obtained from BeNa Culture Collection. Human GBM03, GBM07 and GBM09 primary glioblastoma cells were obtained from patient samples at Xijing Hospital. Mouse OT-1 CD8+T cells were obtained from OT-1 mouse via MACS.

All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to use and were regularly monitored for cross-contamination. All cells were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

All tumor cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, USA, 11965092) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin (50 U ml−1), 1% streptomycin (50 μg mL−1). GBM03, GBM07 and GBM09 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, USA, 11965092) supplemented with 15% FBS, 1% penicillin (50 U ml−1), 1% streptomycin (50 μg mL−1). OT-1 CD8+T cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin, 1% streptomycin. All cells were screened to be mycoplasma free via routine testing with the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza) and the MycoStrip Mycoplasma Detection Kit (InvivoGen).

Method details

T cell isolation, activation and culture

Spleen was isolated from mice, mechanically disrupted, RBC-lysed and then incubated with a CD8 Microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec). After washing, CD8+ cells were isolated by applying a magnetic field and collected touched cells.

For peptide activation, the single-cell suspension of splenocytes was cultured with peptide (SIINFEKL for OT-I) and IL-2 overnight. For anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 activation, CD8+ T cells isolated as above were activated with 3 μg mL−1 plate-bound anti-CD3, 2 μg mL−1 soluble anti-CD28 and IL-2 overnight. Activated T cells were cultured in 50 U ml −1 IL-2.

Hu-PBL mice model

C-NKG mice (6–8 weeks) were obtained from Cyagen. Human peripheral blood was collected from the Transfusion Department of Xijing Hospital with informed consent. Peripheral blood was processed by red blood cell lysis to obtain PBMCs (peripheral blood mononuclear cells). Each mouse received an intravenous injection of 5 × 106 PBMCs to establish the model.

CRISPR activation screen

The lentiviral mouse CRISPR activation (mCRISPRa) library was designed to target 26 transcription factors critical for dendritic cell lineage differentiation, development and functional activation, with 5 sgRNAs per gene delivered via a dCas9-VPR fusion system. The library included 130 non-overlapping sgRNAs alongside 1,000 non-targeting control sgRNAs in each sub-pool. LN229 and GL261 glioma cells were transduced with the mCRISPRa lentivirus at an MOI of 0.3, followed by puromycin selection (3μg/mL for 48 h) to ensure stable Cas9 expression. Library complexity was validated by next-generation sequencing (NGS), demonstrating >99% sgRNA representation with <0.3% missing guides across both cell lines. The screening workflow employed a two-step PCR amplification: Genomic DNA was extracted using a column-based purification kit to retain high molecular weight DNA, followed by adapter ligation with TruSeq indexed adapters in the second PCR to enable multiplexed sequencing.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometric analysis was performed following standardized protocols. Data were analyzed using a consistent hierarchical gating strategy to ensure precise population identification. Initially, live cells were selected based on FSC/SSC properties and viability dye exclusion, followed by singlet gating to exclude doublets. In reprogramming assays, transduced cells were identified via eGFP expression (for lentiviral ZPIB delivery), and reprogrammed ZPIB-DCs were defined as the CD45+MHCII+ population within the eGFP+ gate. Subsequently, phenotypic markers (cDC1 markers: CLEC9A, XCR1, CD11c; Activation markers: CD40, CD80, CD86) were quantified within this CD45+MHCII+ ZPIB-DC population. For intracellular cytokine staining, T cells were fixed and permeabilized using a Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (cat. no. 554714, BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For tumor-infiltrating T cell phenotyping, CD8+ T cells were enriched via MACS separation from GBM tumors on day 14 post-implantation prior to analysis. For T cell activation assays, CD25 and CD69 expression levels were quantified by flow cytometry following 72-h co-culture with ZPIB-DCs under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2).

Immunofluorescence

The immunofluorescence staining and imaging protocol followed standardized procedures. In the case of mouse whole brains, 10 μm thick coronal cryosections were prepared. Subsequent steps included fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, blocking, primary/secondary antibody incubation (per manufacturer specifications), and thorough washing with PBST. Blocking was performed using 5% goat serum in PBS. Slides were finally treated with ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti) equipped with NIS-Elements software (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Tumor dissociation

Isolate tumor tissues from the mouse, wash three times with pre-cooled PBS, and cut into approximately 2 mm fragments using ophthalmic scissors. Process the tissues following the Mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) protocol using a gentleMACS™ Dissociator (program: 37C_m_TDK_2). Resuspend the digested tissues in pre-cooled PBS, filter through a 30 μm cell strainer to remove undigested tissue debris, centrifuge at 300×g for 5min, and resuspend the pellet in cell culture medium to obtain a single-cell suspension.

ScRNA sequencing

Male C57BL/6 mice (n = 9) were intracranially orthotopically inoculated with the GL261-ZPIB-TetON cell line. Following model establishment, mice were divided into three groups: Control group (n = 3); Early state group (n = 3) was administered doxycycline (2 g/L, dissolved in distilled water) from day 1–7 post-modeling; Late state group (n = 3) received doxycycline (2 g/L, dissolved in distilled water) from day 1–14 post-modeling. All mice were euthanized on day 14 post-modeling. Tumor tissues were collected, dissociated as previously described, and single-cell suspensions were resuspended in cell culture medium (1×106 cells/mL) and maintained on ice. Approximately 20 μL of each suspension was used for single-cell library construction.

Single-cell cDNA libraries were constructed using Chromium Single Cell 3′V3 Kit (10× Genomics) per manufacturer protocols, targeting 10,000 cells/library. Sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina), with reads aligned to a modified mm10 genome (GRCm38.p6) via Cell Ranger v7.2.0. Data analysis and quality control were conducted using Seurat (v4.1.0), with cells retained based on the following thresholds: mitochondrial RNA content <25%, UMI count >500, and number of detected genes >500. Normalized data identified 2,000 HVGs (variance-stabilized, >0.1 expression), followed by PCA on top 20 components. UMAP projection (3 neighbors, min. distance = 0.1) clustered cells, manually annotated via marker gene expression. Sub-clustering validated cell-type specificity, excluding multiplets using Doublet Finder.

RNA sequencing

GBM cells were transfected with lentivirus GFP or ZPIB, GFP+ cells were sorted by flow cytometry and lysed in TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and outsourced to BioMarker Technologies (Beijing) for subsequent RNA sequencing and library preparation.

ATAC sequencing

GBM cells were transfected with lentivirus GFP or ZPIB, GFP+ cells were sorted by flow cytometry and stored in 90% FBS+10% DMSO and outsourced to BioMarker Technologies (Beijing) for subsequent ATAC sequencing and library preparation.

LNP-ZPIB formulation and application

LNPs encapsulating mRNA and the near-infrared fluorescent dye Cy7 were prepared using a lipid film hydration method combined with microfluidic mixing. The lipid composition included Dlin-MC3-DMA (50%), cholesterol (38.5%), DSPC (10%), and DMG-PEG2000 (1.5%, molar ratio). Lipids were dissolved in ethanol (100% v/v) at a final phospholipid concentration of 8mM, with 200 μL of Cy7-ethanol solution (1 mg/mL) added for fluorescence labeling. The ethanol phase was filtered through a 0.2μm membrane.

The mRNAs were commercially synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). mRNA (4 × 500 μg) was diluted in 50 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.0) to 0.087 mg/mL. The aqueous mRNA solution and lipid ethanol phase were mixed via a microfluidic device at flow rates of 12 mL/min and 4 mL/min, respectively, generating LNPs with a 3:1 volume ratio. The mixture was diluted 30-fold with 10 mM PBS (pH 7.4) and concentrated via centrifugal ultrafiltration using 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) tubes at 5,000 × g for 10 min to remove ethanol. Post-concentration, DSPE-PEG-ANG (5 mg) was added for surface modification via post-insertion, yielding the final LNP@mRNA3&Cy7-ANG, namely LNP-ZPIB. In a representative batch of LNP-ZPIB, free mRNA concentration was 4.1 ng/μL and total mRNA concentration was 92.2 ng/μL (both values after 4-fold dilution). The calculated encapsulation efficiency was 95.5%, indicating that >95% of mRNA was successfully encapsulated within the lipid nanoparticles.

For therapeutic studies, LNP-ZPIB was administered via intratumoral injection. Specifically, mice bearing orthotopic tumors received a total dose of 10 μg LNP-ZPIB in a volume of 1 μL per injection. For subcutaneous tumor models, the dosage was adjusted to 20 μg LNP-ZPIB in a volume of 2 μL per injection. Injections were performed using a precision micro syringe equipped with a 33-gauge needle to ensure accurate delivery and minimize tissue trauma. Treatments were initiated at designated time points post-implantation according to the specific experimental design.

Single-cell mass cytometry (CyTOF)

Orthotopic GBM xenografts were established by intracranial injection of luciferase-labeled GL261 cells (5×104 cells in 2 μL PBS) into the striatum of C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old). Post-tumor establishment, mice received intratumoral injection of LNP-ZPIB (10 μg) on day 7, 13 and/or intraperitoneal injection of anti-PD1 mAb (200 μg) on day 7, 11 and 13. Tumors were harvested, dissociated into single-cell suspensions using the Mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec), and processed through a 70 μm filter. Cells were stained with a 35-plex antibody panel targeting immune cell surface markers (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD11b, Ly6G, Ly6C, MHC-II, PD-L1, TIM-3, LAG-3, TIGIT, etc.) conjugated to metal-tagged avidin-biotin-streptavidin (ABSS) system for CyTOF detection. Stained samples were barcoded using cisplatin (1 μM), pooled, fixed in 1.6% PFA, and analyzed on a Helios mass cytometer (Fluidigm). Data were normalized using MATLAB-based CyTOF kit, clustered via FlowSOM, and visualized by t-SNE. Cell subsets were annotated using reference datasets from ImmGen and CellMarker databases.

CD8+ T Cell Isolation

For mouse CD8+T cells, mouse spleens were dissociated by mechanical grinding in PBS, filtered through a 70 μm mesh to remove debris, and washed twice with PBS. For GBM patient CD8+ T cells, peripheral blood (20 mL) was collected from the patient forearm, lyse red blood cells, and wash twice with PBS. Cell pellets were resuspended in 90 μL buffer per 107 total cells, mixed with 10 μL Microbeads (For details, see Supplementary Materials) per 107 cells, and incubated at 4°C for 10 min. CD8+ cells were purified using LS columns (Miltenyi Biotec) followed the manufacturer protocol. Purity was confirmed by flow cytometry test. All patients signed an informed consent form before blood collection.

Phagocytic and antigen presentation functional assays

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates or confocal dishes. For OVA-Alexa Fluor 647 uptake assays, Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated ovalbumin (20 μg/mL) was added to the culture supernatant, and dynamic cellular internalization was monitored every 30 min by confocal microscopy. At 6 h post-treatment, flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa) was performed to quantify intracellular fluorescence. For DQ-OVA degradation analysis, DQ-OVA (20 μg/mL) was supplemented to the medium, and lysosomal protease activity was visualized as time-dependent fluorescence dequenching using confocal microscopy at 2 h. For dead cell phagocytosis assays, ZPIB-DCs were seeded into 12-well plates, while GL261-mCherry cells pre-treated with puromycin (3 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) for 6 h were co-cultured with ZPIB-DCs in fresh medium. Phagocytic interactions were dynamically tracked for 12 h using the JuLI™ Stage Live-Cell Imaging System, with images acquired every 17 min under 37°C and 5% CO2 conditions. All imaging parameters, including laser excitation/emission settings (Alexa Fluor 647: 633/668 nm; DQ-OVA: 495/515 nm; GL261-mCherry: 587/610 nm), were standardized across experiments.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

Cell monolayers were fixed with electron microscopy fixative for 2h, washed three times with PBS, and embedded in 1% agarose. Post-fixation was performed with 1% osmium tetroxide in the dark (2 h), followed by three PBS washes. Gradient ethanol dehydration (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%; 20 min per step) was applied, succeeded by two acetone rinses. Progressive infiltration with a graded acetone/Epon 812 series (1:1 to pure resin; 37°C–60°C) was conducted, followed by polymerization at 60°C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections were cut in 80nm, mounted on copper grids, double-stained with uranyl acetate (in the dark) and lead citrate (under CO2-free conditions), and examined under a transmission electron microscope.

Real-time cell analysis (RTCA)

RTCA for assessing cell proliferation uses the xCELLigence RTCA DP/MP system with E−16 gold-microelectrode plates. First, calibrate the instrument and prime sensor plates with complete growth medium (37°C, 5% CO2) for 15 min to stabilize impedance. 104 cells per well were seeded in 100 μL medium (avoid bubbles) and treated following experimental design. Load plates into the RTCA station, start recording Cell Index (CI) every 15 min under 37°C, 5% CO2, and incubate 60 h. Export CI data, normalize to t = 0 or blank wells, plot growth curves, and calculate population doubling time (PDT) via exponential fit (CI = CI0×eˆ(k×t)). Avoid air bubbles and non-uniform seeding to minimize background noise.

Lentiviral-mediated ZPIB overexpression in vitro

Lentiviral vectors encoding ZPIB (Zfp366/Znf366, Pu.1, Irf8, and Batf3) and control vectors were commercially synthesized and provided by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). For transduction, GBM cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 50–60% confluence and transduced with concentrated lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5–10. Transduced cells were selected with puromycin (3 μg/mL for GL261 and LN229, 2 μg/mL for T98, 1 μg/mL for patient-derived GBM cells) for 7 days starting from 48 h post-transduction. Successful ZPIB expression was validated by flow cytometry for eGFP fluorescence, and Western blot for protein expression.

Doxycycline-inducible systems

For inducible ZPIB expression, GBM cells were co-transduced with two lentiviral vectors: pLV-Tet3G (expressing the rtTA3G reverse transactivator; puromycin resistance) and pLV-TRE3G-ZPIB (containing the TRE3G promoter driving the multicistronic ZPIB cassette; G418 resistance). Cells were selected with puromycin (3 μg/mL) and G418 (600 μg/mL) for 10 days to generate stable double-transductants. ZPIB expression was induced by adding doxycycline (2 μg/mL for in vitro experiments; 2 mg/mL supplemented drinking water for in vivo studies) to the culture medium or diet, respectively.

Plasmid sequence availability

All lentiviral vectors used in this study, including the ZPIB overexpression construct (pLV-EF1α-ZPIB-IRES-eGFP) and the doxycycline-inducible system (pLV-Tet3G and pLV-TRE3G-ZPIB), were custom-synthesized and packaged by GeneChem (Shanghai, China).All viral constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing prior to delivery, and quality control documentation is available from GeneChem upon request.

Calculation of mRNA encapsulation efficiency in LNP-ZPIB

Based on the principle that fluorescent nucleic acid dyes cannot penetrate the lipid bilayer of LNPs and only bind to unencapsulated mRNA, the encapsulation efficiency (EE) of the mRNA-LNP formulation was determined. The concentration of free (unencapsulated) mRNA was measured by adding 2 μL of the LNP formulation to 198 μL of a working solution containing DEPC water and Qubit reagent. To measure the total mRNA (encapsulated + free), 2 μL of the formulation was first incubated with 2 μL of 10% Triton X-100 for 10 min to completely disrupt the LNPs, before being diluted with 196 μL of the Qubit working solution. Both samples were effectively diluted 4-fold prior to fluorescence measurement on a Qubit fluorometer. The free mRNA concentration was 4.1 ng/μL, and the total mRNA concentration was 92.2 ng/μL (both values are post-dilution). Using the standard formula, EE (%) = [(Total mRNA - Free mRNA)/Total mRNA] × 100%, the encapsulation efficiency was calculated to be approximately 95.5% (specifically, (92.2–4.1)/92.2 × 100% = 95.55%).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data conforming to normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD); non-normally distributed data are expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR).

Survival analysis: Overall survival (OS) curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the log rank test.

Group comparisons: Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with appropriate post-hoc tests.

Categorical variables: Differences in distribution between groups were analyzed using the chi-square test.

Significance threshold: All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001). p ≥ 0.05 was denoted as “ns” (not significant).
