Metabolomics and Mechanistic Approaches Reveal the Synergistic and Attenuating of Rubus chingii Hu on Tripterygium Glycoside Tablets in the Treatment of Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Cong Zhang, Zepeng Zhang, Fang Yan, Lei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTripterygium Glycoside Tablets (TGTs) are primary drugs for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) but pose a risk of nephrotoxicity. Rubus chingii Hu (RCH) may enhance kidney function. This study explores a combined RA treatment by examining RCH's chemical components and using a collagen‐induced arthritis (CIA) rat model to test TGTs with or without RCH. RCH boosted the therapeutic impact of TGTs in CIA rats, shown by decreased paw swelling, improved joint condition, and reduced serum TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β levels. The normalization of renal markers (BUN and Scr) and oxidative stress indicators (ROS, T‐NOS, and T‐SOD) may be associated with the Nrf2/NF‐κB pathway. Metabolomics indicated that RCH may counteract nephrotoxicity by influencing the metabolism of glycine, serine, threonine, tryptophan, histidine, taurine, and sulfaurine. In summary, RCH synergizes with TGTs to treat RA by inhibiting inflammation, affecting metabolic pathways, reducing oxidative stress, and minimizing kidney toxicity, offering a new treatment strategy for RA.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disorder marked by persistent synovial inflammation, which leads to joint deformity as well as the degradation of cartilage and bone destruction [1], with a global prevalence ranging from 0.5% to 1% [2]. Patients with RA may also have systemic multi‐system complications (such as, cardiovascular, pulmonary lesions, and osteoporosis) [3], and long‐term pain can seriously affect their quality of life. Traditional herbal medicines and their active constituents confer enhanced therapeutic benefits by modulating various interconnected therapeutic targets for RA, among which drugs developed for inflammatory targets have shown potential to treat RA [4].

Tripterygium Glycoside Tablets (TGTs) are widely utilized in the clinical management of RA [5], which is helpful to reduce inflammatory response and inhibit autoimmune response. Triptolide and Celastrol have anti‐inflammatory, anti‐oxidative, autophagy‐regulating, apoptosis, and other effects [6], which are both bioactive components and often toxic components in TGTs. Previous assessments of the anti‐RA effect and potential toxicity of TGTs have been divided, ignoring the link between efficacy and toxicity [7]. Therefore, it is attractive to realize the synergism and attenuation of TGTs through compatibility therapy for clinical safe drug use.

Fortunately, compatible medication is a tradition of Chinese medicine. RCH is abundant in bioactive compounds, including terpenoids, flavonoids, organic acids, alkaloids, and phenolic acids [8]. It exhibits substantial pharmacological effects, including anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti‐cytotoxic properties [9], and is widely used to treat kidney‐related diseases [10]. Clinical studies have indicated that the integration of traditional Chinese medicines (TCMs) with TGTs may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of TGTs while mitigating their associated toxicity [11, 12]. It is still unclear whether the combination of RCH and TGTs can play a similar synergistic and attenuated role. Metabolomics is an important method to explain complex pathological or physiological changes. It can elucidate the pathogenesis and mechanisms of action of therapeutic drugs by investigating specific molecular markers associated with physiological pathology [13].

Therefore, we developed a rat model of collagen‐induced arthritis (CIA) to assess RCH's therapeutic impact on TGTs and investigated its mechanism in reducing TGTs‐induced nephrotoxicity using metabonomics, aiding in the rational clinical treatment of RA.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

TGTs were purchased from Hunan Qianjin Xieli Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Zhuzhou, China). Bovine type II collagen was obtained from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Incomplete Freund's adjuvant (Sigma–Aldrich, USA); acetonitrile and methanol (Merk, Darmstadt, Germany), and formic acid (Aladdin, Nanjing, China) were all HPLC grade. Milli‐Q water purification system (Millipore, Milford, MA, USA). Universal tissue fixative (neutral) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China); BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China); Rat IL‐6, IL‐1, and TNF‐𝛼 ELISA detection kit (Fenxi Biotechnology, Shanghai, China); blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (Scr) kit (Neusoft Wittman Biotech, Nanjing, China). Total nitric oxide synthase (T‐NOS) and total superoxide dismutase (T‐SOD) kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and phosphorylated nuclear factor kappa B (p‐NF‐κB) primary antibodies (Wuhan Sanying Biotech, China), nuclear factor‐erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) primary antibody (Wuhan SevierBio, China), HRP‐labeled goat anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (Kirkegaard & Perry Laboratories, Maryland, USA).

Preparation of RCH Extract

The RCH samples were collected from Qiandao Lake area in Chun'an County, Zhejiang Province, in May 2025, and were identified as fresh fruits of Rubus chingii (R. chingii) Hu in Rubus of Rosaceae by Cao Yiming, a senior pharmacist. The certified specimens of plants are kept in the herbarium of the Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital of Kunshan. Took an appropriate amount of RCH samples and crushed, soaked in 10 volumes of 70% ethanol for 30 min, refluxed and extracted for 1 h, and the ethanol extract was collected after filtration. Then, the same operation was repeated again. The alcohol extracts obtained twice were combined, concentrated by rotary evaporator, freeze‐dried in vacuum, and stored in a normal temperature dryer.

Characterization of RCH Alcohol Extract

Measured the freeze‐dried powder, added 1 mL of a 1:1:1 (v/v) mixture of water, acetonitrile, and isopropanol, vortexed for 60s, performed low‐temperature ultrasound for 30 min, centrifuged at 12000 rpm at 4°C for 15 min, placed the supernatant at −20°C for 1 h, centrifuged again. The supernatant was vacuum‐dried, dissolved in 200 µl of 30% acetonitrile, and centrifuged at 14 000 g for 15 min at 4°C. It was then injected into the ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC‐Q Exactive HF‐X) system for detection. Waters UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 100 × 2.1 mm) was used, and the mobile phase was 0.01% formic acid‐aqueous solution (A)‐0.1% formic acid‐acetonitrile (B). The UHPLC system parameters were set to column temperature 40°C, sample volume 2 µL and flow rate 0.3 mL/min. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0.0‐1.0 min, mobile phase B is maintained at 0%; 1.0–12.0 min, mobile phase B linearly increases from 0% to 95%; 12.0–13.0 min, mobile phase B is maintained at 95%; 13.0–13.1 min, mobile phase B linearly decreases from 95% to 0%; 13.1–17.0 min, mobile phase B is maintained at 0%.

The full scanning mode of mass spectrometry was set to m/z 70–1050. The electrospray ionization (ESI) source conditions were: sheath gas at 40 arb and auxiliary gas at 10 arb; 3000 v/‐2800 v of ion spray voltage, 350°C of gas temperature, and 320°C of ion transport tube temperature.

Animal Conservation and Ethics

Male Sprague‐Dawley rats (200–250 g) were obtained from Hangzhou Qizhen Laboratory Animal Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (License: SCXK (Zhe) 2022‐0005). They were housed individually in ventilated cages under controlled conditions (22 ± 2°C, 50% ± 20% humidity, 12/12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. The experiment followed ethical guidelines approved by the Zhejiang Huitong Evaluation Technology Co.’s Animal Ethics Review Committee (No: HTDW‐202502012).

Construction and Treatment of CIA Rat Model

After a week of adaptive feeding, six rats were designated as the Control group and received daily intraperitoneal saline injections. The remaining rats were used to create a CIA model. On Day 0, they were intradermally injected with 0.3 mL of an emulsion containing equal parts bovine type II collagen and incomplete Freund's Adjuvant: 0.1 mL into the ankle and 0.2 mL at the tail base. A week later, 0.1 mL of the emulsion was injected subcutaneously into the tail. The model was developed over two weeks, with the arthritis index (AI) recorded daily post‐booster. An AI of 4 or higher indicated successful modeling [14].

The model rats were randomly divided into model group (Model), Tripterygium Glycoside Tablets group (TGTs) and Tripterygium Glycoside Tablets combined with R. chingii Hu group (R‐TGTs), with six rats in each group, and the drugs were administered on the 15th day after modeling. In the combination group, TGTs were given 30 min after RCH treatment. The experiment was intended to demonstrate both therapeutic efficacy and the reduction of toxicity simultaneously, thus the TGTs dose was selected to be 100mg·kg−1, which is approximately 10 times the clinical equivalent dose. The 400 mg·kg−1 dose of RCH extract was chosen based on the clinical yield of the decoction pieces (about 11%), which is approximately twice the clinical equivalent dose. Both TGTs and RCH lyophilized powder were prepared in 0.9% physiological saline. Both the Control and Model group rats received a daily gavage of 10 mL·kg−1 of 0.9% saline for 21 days.

Efficacy Evaluation and Sample Collection

Paw swelling and animal weight were periodically monitored after the initial immunization. In order to record the development of arthritis erythema or edema in the paw of rats and observe the effect of drugs, the width and height of hind feet were measured with vernier calipers to evaluate the swelling of rats' hind feet, and arthritis scores were also made. The five‐grade scoring method is used to evaluate the severity of arthritis [15]: 0 point for the joint condition is normal; 1 point for toes or joints with slight swelling and redness; 2 points for moderate swelling and redness of toes or ankles; 3 points for severe swelling of the ankle or all swelling below; 4 points for complete paw swelling or severe joint deformity.

After 4 h of treatment on the 21st day, blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta and separated by centrifugation (2000 × g, 15 min) to analyze biochemical markers and inflammatory cytokines. Finally, animals were euthanized using pentobarbital sodium, and their kidneys and hind paws were collected, washed with cold saline, and dried. Some kidney and joint tissues were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathology, while the rest of the kidney tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C [16].

Histopathological Observation

Bare joints and kidney tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 4 µm sections for HE staining. The sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin, differentiated with hydrochloric acid alcohol, blued in tap water, counterstained with eosin, dehydrated, cleared with xylene, and sealed with neutral resin. The histological changes were observed by light microscope. Histopathology score [17] was determined as: normal (0 point); mild inflammatory infiltration without obvious synovial hyperplasia (1 point); mild synovial hyperplasia without bone and cartilage damage (2 points); moderate synovial hyperplasia or synovial formation, mild bone or cartilage damage (3 points); severe synovial overgrowth and moderate to severe bone and cartilage damage (4 points).

Determination of Kidney Index, Biochemical Indicators, and Inflammatory Factor Levels

After weighing the kidney and body weight of rats, calculate the kidney index (KI) = wet weight of kidney (g)/ body mass (g) × 100%. Following the manufacturer's guidelines, ELISA kits measured TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 levels in rat serum, while an automatic biochemical analyzer assessed BUN and SCr levels. According to the requirements of the kit, the levels of T‐NOS and T‐SOD in kidney homogenate were detected by ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometry.

ROS Determination

Fresh kidney tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in OCT, and sliced to approximately 10 µm thick, re‐temperatured to dry water, then added with autofluorescence quencher and ROS dye solution, kept in a 37°C dark incubator for 60 min, shaken and washed twice on a decoloring shaker and slightly dried, then dripped with DAPI dye solution, shaken and washed three times on a decoloring shaker at room temperature in darkness, and sealed tablets with anti‐fluorescence quenching after slightly dried. Finally, observe and collect images under a laser confocal microscope [18] and calculate the fluorescence intensity.

Western Blotting

p‐NF‐κB and Nrf2 protein levels in kidney tissue were assessed using western blot. Kidney tissue homogenate was prepared with RIPA buffer, and protein concentration was measured using the BCA method. Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to a PVDF membrane, blocked with 5% skimmed milk, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Afterwards, the membrane was washed three times with PBST buffer and then incubated with HRP‐labeled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membrane was washed with PBST buffer for three times, and then dripped with evenly mixed ECL luminescent solution, and exposed in an exposure instrument. Finally, ImageJ software was used to perform density scanning analysis [19], and GAPDH was used as an internal reference to determine the expression amount of the target protein.

Untargeted Metabolomics Analysis

Sample Preparation

Thawed kidney samples slowly at 4°C, then mixed with a precooled methanol–acetonitrile–water solution (2:2:1, v/v/v). Vortexed for 1 min, ultrasonicated at low temperature for 30 min, kept at –20°C for 10 min, and centrifuged at 14000 rpm at 4°C for 20 min. Dried the supernatant under vacuum. Added 100 µL of acetonitrile‐water solution (1:1, v/v) to dissolve, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 4°C for 15 min. Used the supernatant for sample injection analysis. Prepared quality control (QC) samples by mixing 100 µL of supernatant from each sample. All samples were injected randomly and QC was performed at intervals to test the stability of the analytical platform [20].

Detection Conditions

Kidney samples were analyzed using the AB SCIEX TripleTOF6600 UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS system with an Agilent 1290 Infinity UPLC HILIC column. The analysis was conducted in both positive and negative modes. The UPLC system parameters were: mobile phase A (water with 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonia) and B (acetonitrile). The gradient elution was: 95% B at 0–0.5 min, decreasing to 65% B by 7 min, further to 40% B by 8 min, held at 40% B until 9 min, then increased back to 95% B by 9.1 min, and maintained until 12 min. The flow rate was 0.5 mL/min, sample volume was 2 µL, and column temperature was 25°C.

The mass spectrum parameters were configured with an ESI in both positive and negative ion modes, an ISFV of 5500 V, a DP of 60 V, and a collision energy of 35 ± 15 eV. The temperature was set to 600°C, with gas 1 and gas 2 at 60 psi and a curtain gas at 30 psi. The scanning range was MS 60∼1000 Da and MS/MS 25∼1000 Da.

Data Processing and Multivariate Analysis

Mass spectrum data were collected and imported into XCMS (https://xcmsonline.scripps.edu) software [21]. After peak detection, alignment, noise filtering, retention time correction, and normalization pretreatment, SIMCA‐P14.0 (Umetrics AB, Sweden) was used to generate principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal projections to latent structures‐discriminant analysis (OPLS‐D) models to evaluate the characteristics of RCH [22]. According to the variable importance projection (VIP) greater than 1, p<0.05 and |log2FC| >1, differential metabolites were identified [23]. Finally, the differential metabolites were analyzed for metabolic pathways and network diagrams using the KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/) database and MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).

Statistical Analysis

Data were processed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.5.1, expressed as standard deviation (SD). One‐way ANOVA compared group averages, with LSD and Dunnett T3 tests for multiple group analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 or p < 0.01.

RCH Combined With TGTs Enhances Efficacy and Reduces Toxicity in the Treatment of RA

TGTs have been clinically proven effective for treating RA [30], but it faces the risk of nephrotoxicity due to the narrow treatment window [31], and its clinical application is severely limited [32]. The existing toxicity studies are mostly limited to normal animals [33, 34], the lack of parallel research on efficacy and toxicity based on disease‐syndrome animals makes it difficult to fully reflect the true state of clinical application of TGTs. Therefore, this study established the CIA rat model to simulate the real application of TGTs. Considering that the medication period is short, it may be difficult to detect nephrotoxicity at conventional doses, so 10 times the clinical equivalent dose was chosen as the exposure dose, which is both higher than the clinical efficacy and easy to observe toxicity. Enhancing efficacy and minimizing toxicity through compatibility is a key advantage of traditional Chinese medicine in treating complex diseases [35]. Research indicates that RCH possesses anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties [36], and contributes to restore kidney function [37]. The results of this study confirmed the synergistic and attenuated effects of RCH on TGTs in the treatment of RA.

RCH Enhances the Therapeutic Effect of TGTs on Arthritis Symptoms in CIA Rats

Research indicates that the progression of RA is driven by excessive production cytokines of IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and IL‐6, which stimulate synovial, bone, and cartilage cells to produce collagenase and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) [38, 39], thus leading to cartilage destruction and bone damage [40]. Clinically, the serum levels of IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α in patients with RA are also high [41, 42]. Tripterygium preparations (TPs) can lessen RA severity and inflammatory cytokine expression in joints while inhibiting angiogenesis, thus alleviating RA symptoms [43, 44]. The powerful anti‐inflammatory effect of TGTs mainly relies on its active ingredients, such as celastrol, which can directly inhibit the formation and function of osteoclasts, because it can reduce the phosphorylation expression of NF‐kB and mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) [45], and reduce bone absorption and cartilage degradation [46].

The results found that arthritis symptoms in the Model group rats progressively deteriorated, manifested by paw swelling and limited activity, and the AI score remained at a high level, but all decreased after drug intervention (Figure 2A,B). Specifically, in the TGTs group, rats showed a significant decrease in AI scores (p<0.01) and reduced hind limb swelling compared to the Model group, but there was no significant difference. However, not only the swelling was eliminated obviously, but also the AI score decreased linearly in R‐TGTs group (p<0.01). As shown in Figure 2C, the Control group rats had healthy hind limbs with no erythema, while the Model group rats showed severe swelling, erythema, and joint deformities. In the There was no obvious erythema in TGTs group, but the joints were slightly edematous and deformed. After treatment with R‐TGTs, the hind limbs of the rats recovered well and were similar in appearance to the Control group.

The results indicated that RCH could enhance the anti‐inflammatory effect of TGTs.

RCH Alleviates TGTs‐Induced Kidney Injury

It cannot be ignored that TGTs have notable toxic side effects in RA treatment, with nephrotoxicity occurring in approximately 5.81% of cases [47], which may be related to the medication course or dose [48]. In this process, TGTs synthesized and secreted TNF‐α by activating macrophages, upregulated the expression of Fas, p53, and NF‐kB protein on the surface of renal cells, and down‐regulated the expression of Bcl‐2, thereby inducing apoptosis of renal cells to produce nephrotoxicity. TGTs also raised ROS levels in renal cells and heightened oxidative stress via the Nrf2 pathway, leading to cell apoptosis and organ damage [49]. At this time, if there is a candidate drug that can not only improve the original efficacy of TGTs, but also antagonize the toxic effects induced by TGTs, it will constitute an additional advantage. Previous studies have shown that RCH treatment can significantly reduce renal pathological damage, fibrosis and inflammation in adenine‐induced chronic kidney disease (CKD) model rats [50], possibly due to the kidney‐protective effects of flavonoids in RCH [51], which indirectly proves the feasibility of using alcohol extracts of RCH in this study. The pathological changes and increase in index of organs are direct reflections of drug toxicity [52].

TGTs could risk causing kidney damage when treating RA, and the KI is one of the indicators to measure kidney function [53]. Results indicated (Figure 4A) that TGTs alone increased KI, while R‐TGTs significantly lowered it (p < 0.05), showing no significant difference from the Model group. Regarding serum indicators (Figure 4B,C), the Model group's BUN and Scr levels were stable compared to the Control group. In CIA rats, TGTs alone significantly raised BUN and Scr levels (p < 0.05), whereas R‐TGTs significantly lowered them (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01).

HE staining of the kidney (Figure 4D) revealed intact renal tubules in the Control group, the epithelial cells arranged closely, and there was no inflammatory cell infiltration in the renal interstitium. In the Model group, the renal tissue structure was regular, the nucleus was evenly stained, the renal tubules and glomeruli were relatively intact, and no obvious inflammatory cell infiltration was found. In the TGTs group, renal interstitial edema worsened, inflammatory cell infiltration increased, renal tubules degenerated and expanded, and renal capsule cavity became larger. This shows that RCH intervention can alleviate the kidney injury induced by excessive TGTs.

It should be pointed out that ROS, an oxidative stress product, is a catalyst for the activation of inflammatory cells in kidney tissue, which can trigger lipid peroxidation reactions of various unsaturated fatty acids on the cell membrane and aggravate the inflammatory invasion [54, 55]. ROS is involved in various intracellular signaling pathways, including NF‐κB, which is crucial for apoptosis and inflammation. During inflammation, NF‐κB moves from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, regulating genes that produce inflammatory proteins like TNF and IL‐1, thus contributing significantly to the inflammatory response [56, 57]. Research indicates that the ROS/NF‐κB pathway is vital in the onset and progression of kidney diseases [58]. Activation of Nrf2 can upregulate the expression of antioxidant proteins such as heme oxygenase (HO‐1) and glutamate cysteine ligase (GCL) in kidney tissue, thereby clearing excess ROS [59]. Therefore, activating Nrf2 to induce its nuclear translocation can inhibit NF‐κB in the inflammatory response pathway, thereby inhibiting inflammatory mediators in kidney tissue, such as cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules, and matrix metalloproteinase‐9 (MMP‐9), cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) [60], and enhance antioxidant capacity to resist oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions [61], playing a role in kidney protection. This study found that RCH prevented the rise in kidney function markers (BUN and Scr) caused by TGTs and lowered oxidative stress levels (ROS, T‐NOS, and T‐SOD). At the same time, RCH intervention reduced oxidative stress by inhibiting inflammatory pathway protein (p‐NF‐κB) and restoring antioxidant protein (Nrf2), but its exact molecular mechanism requires further investigation.

Renal Metabolomics

Overall Changes of Metabolites

The TIC of QC kidney tissue samples in both ion modes show significant overlap (Figure S1), indicating sample homogeneity and instrument stability. PCA was conducted to visually differentiate the total metabolite spectrum among various populations. Metabolites in positive and negative ion modes were distinctly separated among the groups, and there was a clustering phenomenon in each group (Figure 7A,B). In terms of OPLS‐DA analysis (Figure 7C–F and Table 2), the kidney metabolites of the Model and Control groups are distinct yet somewhat similar, showing metabolic changes in CIA rats. Furthermore, the R‐TGTs and TGTs groups are clearly distinct from the Model group, indicating that drug intervention significantly alters the renal metabolite profiles in CIA rats. Most importantly, the R‐TGTs group was also significantly separated from the TGTs group, indicating that different administration modes produced different metabolic changes on the kidneys of CIA rats.

In addition, we identified 60 and 68 different metabolites between TGTs_versus_Model group and TGTs_versus_R‐TGTs group in positive and negative modes, respectively (Figure 11A,B). Following TGTs administration, 14 metabolites decreased and 72 increased compared to the Model group. After using R‐TGTs, the levels of metabolites were reversed, but only six metabolites did not recover significantly (Figure 11C). For example, CRH significantly down‐regulated the levels of kynurenic acid, cyanuric acid, argininosuccinic acid, guanidoacetic acid, indoxyl sulfate, fructose, maleic acid, D‐tryptophan, tryptophan, L‐pyroglutamic acid and isoleucine, and the levels of oleamide, embelin, ectoine, 3,4‐dihydroxy‐L‐phenylalanine (L‐DOPA), betaine and taurine were significantly increased, which indicated that the changing effects of TGTs on these renal metabolites could be interfered by CRH.

Subsequently, 86 reversed differential metabolites were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 to explore the related metabolic pathways. The results (Figure 11D,E) showed that the combined use of CRH and TGTs mainly affected phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and histidine metabolism.

Analysis results indicate that tryptophan metabolism is the pathway for RCH to interfere TGTs‐induced nephrotoxicity, which has been confirmed in nephrotoxicity induced by cisplatin [62]. The related metabolite D‐tryptophan is considered to be beneficial to the regulation of intestinal environmental balance [63], but excessive level of D‐tryptophan may cause adverse effects [64]. There is the same risk of excessive tryptophan. Young rats with 5.0% tryptophan in their diet observed obvious toxicity and growth retardation after 30 days, and kynurenic acid (KA), a metabolite of tryptophan‐nicotinamide pathway, increased in a dose‐dependent manner [65]. Tryptophan is also decomposed into indole under the action of intestinal bacteria, which is metabolized into indophenol by cytochrome P450 enzyme system in the liver, and combined with sulfuric acid under the action of sulfate transferase to produce indoxyl sulfate (IS). Normally, the kidney primarily excretes IS, but in cases of renal insufficiency, this process is impaired, which will lead to accumulation of IS in the body, reflecting the imbalance of intestinal‐renal axis. IS is a significant uremic toxin in CKD patients. As kidney function declines, IS accumulates, activating the NF‐κB pathway via ROS production. This leads to toxic effects on renal cells, including apoptosis of renal tubular epithelial cells and increased renal interstitial fibrosis, worsening kidney damage [66]. Isoleucine, similar to tryptophan, is an essential amino acid crucial for host defense, fatty acid metabolism, and glucose transport. The kidney is an important organ of amino acid metabolism, which is involved in regulating the nitrogen balance and acid‐base balance in the body. Its abnormal function will lead to the disorder of amino acid metabolism, which will affect the overall health. The level of amino acid reflects the scavenging ability of the kidney [67, 68]. In contrast, oleamide (cis‐9‐octadecylamine) can suppress lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐induced production of NO and PGE2, decreases iNOS and COX‐2 expression, blocks NF‐κB activation, and reduce kappaB kinase (Ikk) phosphorylation and ROS accumulation [69]. Betaine, a natural metabolite of kidney, can reduce inflammation and cell aging by inhibiting TANK‐binding kinase 1(TBK1), and offer multi‐organ protection in aging mice due to its antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties [70]. Taurine and its derivatives have antioxidant properties related to the kidney system. In the renal ischemia/reperfusion model, taurine pretreatment can lower serum creatinine and decrease renal epithelial cell apoptosis and necrosis, which may be mainly due to its local and systemic clearance of ROS, thus reducing oxidative damage [71]. Metabolomics is based on the global metabolic spectrum in complex biological matrix, which provides changes in the metabolic network of complex biological systems in response to many metabolites of pathological stimuli and drug therapy, and demonstrates strong potential for assessing the efficacy and toxicity of traditional Chinese medicine [72, 73]. This study investigated how RCH alcohol extract might reduce TGT‐induced nephrotoxicity using metabonomics. The results indicated that RCH reduced D‐tryptophan, kynurenic acid, isoleucine and indoxyl sulfate metabolic levels in kidney induced by TGTs, and increased the metabolic levels of oleamide, betaine, and taurine. These reversed metabolites are linked to RCH's anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and metabolic regulation, primarily involving glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, 2‐oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, and taurine and hypotaurine metabolism. Nevertheless, the exact way these metabolic pathways help RCH reduce TGTs‐induced kidney toxicity is unclear and requires more research.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a CIA rat model and discovered that RCH alcohol extract enhances the therapeutic effect of TGTs on CIA rats while mitigating TGTs‐induced kidney injury. RCH affects glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, histidine metabolism, taurine, and hypotaurine metabolism, and improves the therapeutic effect of RA and reduces the nephrotoxicity of TGTs through anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant stress. This combination provides a novel approach for safe and effective RA treatment.
