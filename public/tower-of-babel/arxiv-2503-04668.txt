Data-Driven Distributed Optimization via Aggregative Tracking and Deep-Learning
Riccardo Brumali, Guido Carnevale, Giuseppe Notarstefano

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a novel distributed data-driven optimization scheme. In particular, we focus on the so-called aggregative framework, namely, the scenario in which a set of agents aim to cooperatively minimize the sum of local costs, each depending on both local decision variables and an aggregation of all of them. We consider a data-driven setup in which each objective function is unknown and can be only sampled at a single point per iteration (thanks to, e.g., feedback from human users or physical sensors). We address this scenario through a distributed algorithm that combines three key components: (i) a learning part that leverages neural networks to learn the local cost functions descent direction, (ii) an optimization routine that steers the estimates according to the learned direction to minimize the global cost, and (iii) a tracking mechanism that locally reconstructs the unavailable global quantities. By using tools from system theory, i.e., timescale separation and averaging theory, we formally prove that, in strongly convex setups, the overall distributed strategy linearly converges in a neighborhood of the optimal solution whose radius depends on the given accuracy capabilities of the neural networks. Finally, we corroborate the theoretical results with numerical simulations.

Unknown environments as those mentioned above are particularly interesting in the context of the so-called

In this field, the goal is to minimize cost functions given by the sum of a known part, named engineering function and related to measurable quantities (e.g., time or energy), and an unknown part representing the user’s (dis)satisfaction with the current solution.
Since synthetic models based on human preferences often perform well only on average, failing to address the unique preferences of individual users, personalized optimization emphasizes data-driven strategies.
These approaches leverage feedback from specific users about the current solution to adapt and better meet their individual needs.
With growing human–robot collaboration, personalized optimization techniques can greatly enhance the control of cooperative robotic networks.

In this paper, we introduce DEep-Learning aggregative TrAcking (DELTA), a novel distributed data-driven optimization scheme for aggregative problems.
The peculiarity of DELTA is to tackle the typical convergence issues of standard
1
-point methods based on static gradient approximations by integrating a dynamic learning strategy based on neural networks that does not increase the number of cost evaluations per iteration.
More in detail, DELTA operates on a single timescale and integrates three core components: (i) a learning-oriented module, (ii) an optimization-oriented routine, and (iii) a tracking-oriented mechanism.
The learning-oriented component uses local neural networks to asymptotically estimate the correct costs (and their gradients) in a data-driven fashion.
Specifically, each neural network employs only a single cost evaluation per iteration taken in the neighborhood of the current local estimates.
The optimization-oriented component applies an approximated distributed gradient method to the aggregative problem.
Its inexactness arises from two factors: the fact that the cost functions are unknown and approximated by the networks and the need for global quantities that are locally unavailable.
The tracking-oriented component addresses the second challenge by locally reconstructing the global quantities, i.e., the aggregative variable and the derivative of the global cost with respect to that variable.
We analyze DELTA using tools from system theory based on timescale separation and averaging theory to formally prove that, in strongly convex settings, the algorithm linearly converges in a neighborhood of the optimal solution whose radius depends on the given accuracy capabilities of the neural networks.
A preliminary version of this work focusing on the time-varying settings is available in
[
13
]
.
However, in that version, the addressed scenario is significantly simpler.
Indeed, at each iteration, agents can (i) access multiple samples of their cost functions and (ii) completely train their neural networks until convergence between two consecutive optimization steps.
In contrast, here, at each iteration, we can access the cost functions only at a single point and the neural networks are trained only for a single step.
From a technical point of view, this translates in suitably integrating also the learning dynamics analysis in the optimization and consensus one.
Additionally, in
[
13
]
, the local cost functions are partially known, while in this paper, they are completely unknown.

II
Problem Formulation

We consider
N
agents that aim to cooperatively solve

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\>&\>\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}\left(x_{i},\sigma(x)\right),\end{split}

(1)

in which
x\coloneq\col(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the global decision vector, with each
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
and

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}

represents the
unknown

x_{i}
and the aggregative one given by

\displaystyle\sigma(x)\coloneq\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}(x_{i}),

(2)

where each

is the
i
-th contribution to
\sigma

As already mentioned above, the functions

are unknown.
More in detail, we focus on data-driven scenarios where each agent
i
can only receive a single feedback per iteration about the unknown local cost
f_{i}
.
We formalize this aspect as follows.

Assumption 1
.

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,

is unknown but agent

can receive a single feedback

per iteration where

\blacksquare

The choice of

will represent a design aspect of the overall distributed algorithm we aim to propose.
As one may expect and as we will see later, agent
i
will choose
(u_{x,i},u_{\sigma,i})
in the neighborhood of the current local estimates about (i) the
i
-th block of a problem solution and (ii) the aggregative variable.

Now, we formally characterize the functions appearing in problem (
1
).
To this end, we first introduce
f_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
to denote the overall cost function of problem (

Assumption 2

The symbol

.
The next assumption formalizes the class of graphs considered in this work.

Assumption 3
.

The graph
\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{A}
is doubly stochastic.
\blacksquare

Although we analyze DELTA in a static and deterministic scenario, we point out that its design is particularly well-suited for setups in which the cost functions vary over time and where the cost samples are possibly subjected to disturbances.
Indeed, the synergy between the learning, optimization, and tracking components of DELTA enables the algorithm to adapt to such changes without requiring a full restart of the neural network training, the optimization task, or the tracking process.
In the following we detail the three main components of DELTA.

III-A

Learning Update

We introduce in each agent
i
a local neural network to reconstruct
f_{i}
and the gradients
\nabla_{1}f_{i}
and
\nabla_{2}f_{i}
from data.
Let
\hat{f}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
be the estimate of
f_{i}
, where
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of parameters of the neural network.
In other words, for a generic parameter
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
, we consider

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i},s_{i},\theta_{i})\quad\text{proxy for}\quad f_{i}(x_{i},s_{i}).

Once each network structure is fixed (i.e., the number of layers, the number of neurons, and the activation functions are chosen), the function
\hat{f}_{i}
is analytically available.
Thus, by considering differentiable functions
\hat{f}_{i}
, we also introduce their gradients

\displaystyle\nabla_{1}\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i},s_{i},\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\quad\text{proxy for}\quad\nabla_{1}f_{i}(x_{i},s_{i})

(3a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i},s_{i},\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\quad\text{proxy for}\quad\nabla_{2}f_{i}(x_{i},s_{i}).

(3b)

We remark that these derivatives are typically available in most of the deep learning packages via automatic differentiation.
The next assumption characterizes the estimates
\nabla_{1}\hat{f}_{i}
and
\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{i}
.

Assumption 4
.

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the function
\hat{f}_{i}
is differentiable and its gradients
\nabla_{1}\hat{f}_{i}
and
\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{i}
are Lipschitz continuous with parameters
\hat{L}_{x},\hat{L}_{\sigma}>0
, respectively.
\blacksquare

Remark 1
.

The requirement in Assumption
4
is satisfied by choosing differentiable activation functions with Lipschitz‐continuous gradients, such as tanh, sigmoid, or softplus.

\blacksquare

To iteratively train each neural network using new sampled data, coherently with Assumption
1
, we exploit feedback
f_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x,i},\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}+\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i})+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma,i})

\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
and
\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
As we will formalize in the next,
\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x,i}
and
\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma,i}
are two dither signals that we add to force persistency-of-excitation-like properties in the learning process.
Formally, given
k\in\mathbb{N}
and a generic pair
(\mathrm{x}_{i},\mathrm{w}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider the learning problem

\displaystyle\min_{\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\tau=0}^{k}\ell_{i}(\mathrm{x}_{i}+\mathrm{d}^{\tau}_{x,i},\mathrm{w}_{i}+\mathrm{d}^{\tau}_{\sigma,i},\theta_{i}),

(4)

where each
\ell_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
is the loss function computed using a sample of the observed cost. A popular (but not unique) choice for
\ell_{i}
is the squared loss, namely

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(u_{x,i},u_{\sigma,i},\theta_{i})\!\coloneq\!\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert f_{i}(u_{x,i},u_{\sigma,i})\!-\!\hat{f}_{i}(u_{x,i},u_{\sigma,i},\theta_{i})\right\rVert^{2}\!.

(5)

The next assumption characterizes the dithers’ sequences
\{\mathrm{d}^{\tau}_{x,i},\mathrm{d}^{\tau}_{\sigma,i}\}_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}
and problem (
4
).

Assumption 5
.

There exist
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
,
\mu_{\ell}>0
,
\theta_{i}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
, and
\bar{r}_{i}>0

, the sequence
\{\mathrm{d}^{\tau}_{x,i},\mathrm{d}^{\tau}_{\sigma,i}\}_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}
is

-periodic and, for all

, problem (
4
) with

is
\mu_{\ell}
-strongly convex in
\mathcal{B}_{\bar{r}_{i}}(\theta_{i}^{\star})
and
\theta_{i}^{\star}
is its local minimum in
\mathcal{B}_{\bar{r}_{i}}(\theta_{i}^{\star})
.
Further, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
\nabla_{3}\ell_{i}(u_{x,i},u_{\sigma,i},\theta_{i})
is
L_{\ell}
-Lipschitz continuous for all
(u_{x,i},u_{\sigma,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some

.
\blacksquare

Remark 2
.

In the ideal case in which
(\mathrm{x}_{i},\mathrm{w}_{i})
is fixed and the

ensures (i) local strong convexity of each learning problem (

) into

and (ii) that

is the unique minimizer of its problem (

\mathcal{B}_{\bar{r}_{i}}(\theta_{i}^{\star})

\blacksquare

Once the local minimizers
\theta_{i}^{\star}
have been introduced, we characterize as follows their reconstruction capabilities.

Assumption 6
.

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, there exist Lipschitz-continuous functions
\mathrm{p}_{1,i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
,
\mathrm{p}_{2,i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

such that, for all
\mathrm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

, it holds

\displaystyle\nabla_{1}\hat{f}_{i}(\mathrm{x}_{i},\sigma(\mathrm{x}),\theta_{i}^{\star})=\nabla_{1}f_{i}(\mathrm{x}_{i},\sigma(\mathrm{x}))+\mathrm{p}_{1,i}(\mathrm{x})

(6a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{i}(\mathrm{x}_{i},\sigma(\mathrm{x}),\theta_{i}^{\star})=\nabla_{2}f_{i}(\mathrm{x}_{i},\sigma(\mathrm{x}))+\mathrm{p}_{2,i}(\mathrm{x})

(6b)

\displaystyle\nabla_{3}\ell_{i}(\mathrm{x}_{i}+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x,i},\sigma(\mathrm{x})+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma,i},\theta_{i}^{\star})=\mathrm{p}_{\ell,i}(\mathrm{x},k).

(6c)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{p}_{1,i}(\mathrm{x})\right\rVert

(7)

for all

,

, and

.
\blacksquare

Remark 3
.

Assumption

specifies each neural network’s ability to approximate the functions

and their gradients. This capability is captured by the parameter

, but it is not required by the algorithm.

\blacksquare

Once the capabilities of the neural network have been enforced, we can formalize the learning mechanism embedded in DELTA.
An immediate but computationally expensive approach would be to apply the gradient descent method to problem (
4
), iteratively updating an estimate
\theta_{i}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
as

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{k+1}\!=\!\theta_{i}^{k}\!-\!\gamma\sum_{\tau=0}^{k}\nabla_{3}\ell_{i}\big(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}\!+\!\mathrm{d}^{\tau}_{x,i},\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}\!+\!\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i})\!+\!\mathrm{d}^{\tau}_{\sigma,i},\theta_{i}^{k}\big).\!

(8)

However, the strategy in (
8
) would require storing the entire cost samples history and, more importantly, computing an increasing number
k
of gradients in the current estimate
\theta_{i}^{k}
.
To replace the computationally prohibitive method (
8
), we adopt a data-driven update based on a single sample per

, namely

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{k+1}=\theta_{i}^{k}-\gamma\nabla_{3}\ell_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x,i},\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}+\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i})+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma,i},\theta_{i}^{k}).

(9)

Indeed, with an eye to the structure formalized in (
5
), we underline that (
9
) can be implemented by only relying on a single feedback
f_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x,i},\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}+\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i})+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma,i})
in a neighborhood of
(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i},\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}+\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}))
, i.e., of the local estimate of agent
i
about the
i
-th solution block
x^{\star}_{i}
and
\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k})
, respectively.
Finally, we compactly represent the global cost addressed by the neural networks via
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\displaystyle\ell(x,s,\theta)\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i}(x_{i},s_{i},\theta_{i}),

(10)

where
s\!\coloneq\!\col(s_{1},\dots,s_{N}),\theta\!\coloneq\!\col(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N})
.
With the notation of Assumption
6
at hand, we also define
m\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}
,
\Theta\coloneq\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{B}_{\bar{r}_{i}}(\theta_{i}^{\star})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\theta^{\star}\coloneq\col(\theta^{\star}_{1},\dots,\theta^{\star}_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

III-B

Optimization Update

In a full information setup, problem (
1
) could be solved with a parallel implementation of the gradient method.
Namely, by denoting with
\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
the estimate of agent
i
at iteration
k
about the
i
-th block
x^{\star}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
of
x^{\star}
, each agent would run

\displaystyle\mathrm{x}^{{k+1}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}-\gamma\nabla_{1}f_{i}\left(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i},\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k})\right)-\gamma\frac{\nabla\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i})}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{2}f_{j}\left(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{j},\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k})\right),

(11)

where
\gamma>0
is the step size.
Nevertheless, update (
11
) requires the gradients of the unknown
f_{i}
.
We therefore modify it using the approximations (
3
), namely

\displaystyle\mathrm{x}^{{k+1}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}-\gamma\nabla_{1}\hat{f}_{i}\left(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i},\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k}),\theta_{i}^{k}\right)-\gamma\frac{\nabla\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i})}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{j}\left(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{j},\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k}),\theta_{j}^{k}\right).

(12)

Additionally, the unavailable global quantities
\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k})
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{j}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{j},\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k}),\theta_{j}^{k})/N
are needed in (
12
).
We thus interlace (
12
) with a tracking-oriented mechanism.

III-C

Tracking Update

Being
\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k})
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{j}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{j},\sigma(\mathrm{x}^{k}),\theta_{j}^{k})/N
unavailable, we introduce two auxiliary variables
\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i},\mathrm{z}^{k}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
to estimate them via
\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}+\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i})
and
\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i},\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}+\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}),\theta_{i}^{k})+\mathrm{z}^{k}_{i}
, respectively.
To this end, we update them according to the perturbed consensus dynamics

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}^{k+1}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}a_{ij}\left(\mathrm{w}^{k}_{j}+\phi_{j}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{j})\right)-\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i})

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{z}^{k+1}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}a_{ij}\left(\mathrm{z}^{k}_{j}+\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{j}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{j},\mathrm{w}^{k}_{j}+\phi_{j}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{j}),\theta_{j}^{k})\right)-\nabla_{2}\hat{f}_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i},\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}+\phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{i}),\theta_{i}^{k}),

(13b)

where we recall that the weights
a_{ij}
are the entries of the weighted adjacency matrix
\mathcal{A}
matching the graph
\mathcal{G}
.
We remark that the update (
13
) is fully distributed since it requires only local information and neighboring communication.

III-D

DELTA: Convergence Properties

To shorten the notation, consider
\theta^{k}\!\coloneq\!\col(\theta^{k}_{1},\dots,\theta^{k}_{N})
,
\mathrm{x}^{k}\!\coloneq\!\col(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{x}^{k}_{N})
,
\mathrm{w}^{k}\!\coloneq\!\col(\mathrm{w}^{k}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{w}^{k}_{N})
,
\mathrm{z}^{k}\!\coloneq\!\col(\mathrm{z}^{k}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{z}^{k}_{N})
,
\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k})\!\coloneq\!\col(\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{1}),\dots,\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k}_{N}))
, and for all
c\in\{1,2\}
we define

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{c}\left(x,w,\theta\right)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{c}\hat{f}_{1}\left(x_{1},w_{1},\theta_{1}\right)\\
\vdots\\
\nabla_{c}\hat{f}_{N}\left(x_{N},w_{N},\theta_{N}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(14)

Let the function
\Xi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\times\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\!\times\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\!\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+2Nd+m}
be

\displaystyle\!\!\Xi(\mathrm{x},\mathrm{w},\mathrm{z},\theta)\!

\displaystyle\coloneq\!\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{x}-x^{\star}\\
\mathrm{w}+\phi(\mathrm{x})-\mathbf{1}\sigma(\mathrm{x})\\
\mathrm{z}+(I-\frac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}}{N})\hat{G}_{2}(\mathrm{x},\mathrm{w}+\phi(\mathrm{x}),\theta)\\
\theta-\theta^{\star}\end{bmatrix}\!.

Finally, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
consider the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{i}\coloneq\mathcal{B}_{\bar{r}_{i}}(\theta_{i}^{\star})\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times\{0_{d}\}\times\{0_{d}\}.

The next theorem states the convergence properties of DELTA.

Theorem 1
.

Consider DELTA and let Assumptions
1
-
6
hold.
Further, assume
(\theta_{i}^{0},\mathrm{x}_{i}^{0},\mathrm{w}_{i}^{0},\mathrm{z}_{i}^{0})\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Then, there exist
\bar{\gamma},B,\kappa_{1}>0
and
\kappa_{2}\in(0,1)
such that, for all
\gamma\in(0,\bar{\gamma})
, it holds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{x}^{k}-x^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}^{k}\left\lVert\Xi(\mathrm{x}^{0},\mathrm{w}^{0},\mathrm{z}^{0},\theta^{0})\right\rVert+\epsilon B,

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

\blacksquare

The proof of Theorem
1
is provided in Section
IV
.
It is based on system theory tools aimed at finding a uniform ultimate bound for the trajectories of the dynamical system describing DELTA.
To this end, the proof relies on discrete-time averaging theory (see Appendix
A-A
) and timescale separation.

Besides the term
B
that depends on many problem parameters (e.g., the network connectivity, the Lipschitz continuity constants), we note that the bound in Theorem
1
directly depends on the accuracy capabilities of the neural networks (cf. Assumption
5
) through the parameter
\epsilon
.
In particular, in the case of perfect estimation (i.e., if
\epsilon=0
, see Assumption
5
) Theorem
1
ensures exact and linear convergence of DELTA toward the optimal solution
x^{\star}
.

IV
DELTA: Stability Analysis

In this section, we carry out the stability analysis of DELTA to prove Theorem
1
. Our analysis relies on five main steps.

1.

In Section
IV-A
, we reformulate DELTA and consider a “nominal” version in which the “finite accuracy” of the neural networks (see Assumption
6
) is compensated.
We interpret this nominal scheme as a time-varying two-time-scales system, i.e., as the interconnection of two subsystems identified respectively as
slow
and
fast
dynamics.
This allows us to study the stability properties of the interconnected system by analyzing two auxiliary dynamics called boundary-layer and reduced systems related respectively to the fast and slow dynamics;

2.

In Section
IV-B
, we show that the origin is an exponentially stable equilibrium of the boundary layer system;

3.

In Section
IV-C
, we carry out the reduced system analysis
in detail, we show exponential stability of the origin via
averaging theory
(see Theorem
2
in Appendix
A-A
);

4.

In Section
IV-E
, we exploit the results of steps 2) and 3) to conclude the analysis of the nominal system;

5.

Finally in Section
IV-E
, we return to the original system interpreting it as a perturbed version of the nominal one.
This interpretation allows us to use the stability properties of the nominal system (step 4)) to establish those of the original one, thereby providing the proof of Theorem
1
.

Assumptions
1
-
6
hold true throughout the entire section.

Remark 4
.

Although the stability analysis of DELTA is carried out in a static and deterministic environment, it paves the way for extensions to dynamic and uncertain environments subject to, e.g., variations of
f_{i}
over time and noisy cost samples.
\blacksquare

IV-A

DELTA as a time-varying two-time-scale system

By collecting all the local updates in Algorithm
1
, we can compactly rewrite DELTA as

\displaystyle\theta^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\theta^{k}-\gamma\nabla_{3}\ell(\mathrm{x}^{k}+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x},\mathrm{w}^{k}+\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k})+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma},\theta^{k})

(15a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{x}^{{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{x}^{k}-\gamma\left[\hat{G}_{1}(\mathrm{x}^{k},\mathrm{w}^{k}+\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k}),\theta^{k})+\nabla\phi\left(\mathrm{x}^{k}\right)\left(\hat{G}_{2}(\mathrm{x}^{k},\mathrm{w}^{k}+\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k}),\theta^{k})+\mathrm{z}^{k}\right)\right]

(15b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=A\mathrm{w}^{k}+\left(A-I_{Nd}\right)\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k})

(15c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{z}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=A\mathrm{z}^{k}+\left(A-I_{Nd}\right)\hat{G}_{2}(\mathrm{x}^{k},\mathrm{w}^{k}+\phi(\mathrm{x}^{k}),\theta^{k}),

(15d)

where
\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x}\coloneq\col(\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x,1},\dots,\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x,N})
,
\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma}\coloneq\col(\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma,1},\dots,\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma,N})
,
A\coloneq\mathcal{A}\otimes I_{d}
.
It is worth noting that the subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\coloneq\!\{\left(\theta,\mathrm{x},\mathrm{w},\mathrm{z}\right)\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{m}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\!\mid\!\mathbf{1}^{\top}\!\mathrm{w}\!=\!0,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\!\mathrm{z}\!=\!0\},

is invariant for system (
15
).
Thus, we introduce a change of coordinate to isolate the invariant part of the state and define the error coordinates relative to
\theta^{\star}
and
x^{\star}
. Formally, it is

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\theta\\
\mathrm{x}\\
\mathrm{w}\\
\mathrm{z}\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\theta}\\
\tilde{\mathrm{x}}\\
\bar{\mathrm{w}}\\
\mathrm{w}_{\perp}\\
\bar{\mathrm{z}}\\
\mathrm{z}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\theta-\theta^{\star}\\
\mathrm{x}-x^{\star}\\
\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}}{N}\mathrm{w}\\
R^{\top}\mathrm{w}\\
\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}}{N}\mathrm{z}\\
R^{\top}\mathrm{z}\end{bmatrix},

(16)

with
R\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd\times(N-1)d}
such that
R^{\top}\mathbf{1}=0
and
R^{\top}R=I_{Nd}
.
By using the definitions of
\bar{\mathrm{w}}^{k}
and
\bar{\mathrm{z}}^{k}
in (
16
) and since
\mathcal{A}
is doubly stochastic (cf. Assumption
3
), it holds

\displaystyle\bar{\mathrm{w}}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathrm{w}}^{k},\quad\bar{\mathrm{z}}^{k+1}=\bar{\mathrm{z}}^{k},

which implies
(\bar{\mathrm{w}}^{k},\bar{\mathrm{z}}^{k})=(\bar{\mathrm{w}}^{0},\bar{\mathrm{z}}^{0})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Further, the initialization
\mathrm{w}^{k}_{i}=\mathrm{z}^{k}_{i}=0
leads to
(\bar{\mathrm{w}}^{0},\bar{\mathrm{z}}^{0})=(0,0)
and, thus, it holds
(\bar{\mathrm{w}}^{k},\bar{\mathrm{z}}^{k})=(0,0)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Hence, we rewrite (
15
) in the new coordinates (
16
) and neglect the trivial dynamics of
\bar{\mathrm{w}}^{k}
and
\bar{\mathrm{z}}^{k}
, thus obtaining an equivalent restricted system

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}^{k+1}

\displaystyle\!=\tilde{\theta}^{k}-\gamma\nabla_{3}\tilde{\ell}\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k},\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k},\tilde{\theta}^{k},k\right)

(17a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{{k+1}}

\displaystyle\!=\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}-\gamma\left[\hat{G}_{1}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}+x^{\star},R\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}+\phi(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}+x^{\star}),\tilde{\theta}^{k}+\theta^{\star})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 9.0pt+\!\nabla\phi(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star})\hat{G}_{2}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\!,\!R\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!\phi(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}),\tilde{\theta}^{k}\!+\!\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle\hskip 9.0pt+\nabla\phi(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}+x^{\star})R\mathrm{z}_{\perp}^{k}\Big]

(17b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k+1}

\displaystyle\!=\!R^{\top}\!AR\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!M\phi(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star})

(17c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{z}_{\perp}^{k+1}

\displaystyle\!=\!R^{\top}\!AR\mathrm{z}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!M\hat{G}_{2}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star},\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!\phi(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}),\tilde{\theta}^{k}\!+\!\theta^{\star}).\!\!

(17d)

where
M\coloneq R^{\top}(A-I_{Nd})
and we introduce the compact notation
\tilde{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}},w_{\perp},\tilde{\theta},k)\coloneq\ell\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star}+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x},Rw_{\perp}+\phi(x)+\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma},\tilde{\theta}+\theta^{\star}\right).

Now, we introduce the “nominal” version of (
17
), i.e., an auxiliary system in which the finite accuracy of the neural networks (see Assumption
6
) is suitably compensated by adding some fictitious terms. Such a nominal system reads as

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}^{k+1}

\displaystyle\!=\tilde{\theta}^{k}\!-\!\gamma\!\left[\nabla_{3}\tilde{\ell}\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k},\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k},\tilde{\theta}^{k},k\right)-\mathrm{p}_{\ell}\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}+x^{\star},k\right)\right]\!\!

(18a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{{k+1}}\!

\displaystyle=\!\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!-\!\gamma\!\left[\hat{G}_{1}\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\!,R\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!\phi\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\right)\!,\tilde{\theta}^{k}\!+\!\theta^{\star}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 9.0pt+\!\nabla\phi\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!\!+\!x^{\star}\right)\!\hat{G}_{2}\!\left(\!\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!\!+\!x^{\star}\!,R\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!\phi\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!\!+\!x^{\star}\right)\!,\tilde{\theta}^{k}\!+\!\theta^{\star}\!\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.0pt+\!\nabla\phi\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\right)\!R\mathrm{z}_{\perp}^{k}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.0pt\!-\!\mathrm{p}_{1}\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\right)\!-\!\nabla\phi\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\right)\!\tfrac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}}{N}\mathrm{p}_{2}\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\right)\!\Big]\!\!

(18b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k+1}

\displaystyle\!=\!R^{\top}\!AR\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!M\phi\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\right)

(18c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{z}_{\perp}^{k+1}

\displaystyle\!=\!R^{\top}\!AR\mathrm{z}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!M\hat{G}_{2}\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\!,\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!\phi(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}),\tilde{\theta}^{k}\!+\!\theta^{\star}\right)\!\!,\!\!

(18d)

where
\mathrm{p}_{c}(x)\!\coloneq\!\col(\mathrm{p}_{c,1}(x),\dots,\mathrm{p}_{c,N}(x))
for all
c\!\in\!\{1,2\}
, while
\mathrm{p}_{\ell}\left(x,k\right)\!\coloneq\!\col(\mathrm{p}_{\ell,1}\!\left(x,k\right),\dots,\mathrm{p}_{\ell,N}\!\left(x,k\right))
.
Due to
\mathrm{p}_{1}
,
\mathrm{p}_{2}
, and
\mathrm{p}_{\ell}
,
(0,0,-R^{\top}\phi(x^{\star}),-R^{\top}\hat{G}_{2}(x^{\star},\mathbf{1}\sigma(x^{\star}),\theta^{\star}))
is an equilibrium of (
18
) (cf. Assumption
6
).
In detail, the equilibrium points of the subsystems (
18c
)-(
18d
) are parametrized in the states
(\theta,\mathrm{x})
of (
18a
)-(
18b
) through
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2(N-1)d}
defined as

\displaystyle h\left(\theta,x\right)\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}-R^{\top}\phi\left(x\right)\\
-R^{\top}\hat{G}_{2}\left(x,\mathbf{1}\sigma\left(x\right),\theta\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(19)

Further, the variations of (
18a
)-(
18b
) can be arbitrarily reduced via
\gamma
.
From these observations, we interpret system (
18
) as a time-varying two-time-scale system, where (
18a
)-(
18b
) is the so-called slow dynamics, while (
18c
)-(
18d
) is the so-called fast part.
According to this view, we now proceed by analyzing the boundary-layer and reduced system associated to (
18
).

IV-B

Boundary-Layer System Analysis

In this section, we analyze the boundary-layer system associated with (
18
), obtained by fixing an arbitrary
(\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\mathrm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
in (
18c
)-(
18d
), written in the error coordinates.

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathrm{w}}_{\perp}^{k}\\
\tilde{\mathrm{z}}_{\perp}^{k}\end{bmatrix}\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{w}_{\perp}^{k}\\
\mathrm{z}_{\perp}^{k}\end{bmatrix}-h\left(\tilde{\theta}+\theta^{\star},\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star}\right).

In light of the stochasticity property of
\mathcal{A}
(cf. Assumption
3
) and the definition of
h
(cf. (
19
)), we use the properties
R^{\top}\mathbf{1}=0
and
RR^{\top}=I_{Nd}-\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
to write this system as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{w}}_{\perp}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=R^{\top}AR\tilde{\mathrm{w}}_{\perp}^{k}

(20a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{z}}_{\perp}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=R^{\top}AR\tilde{\mathrm{z}}_{\perp}^{k}\!+\!M\Delta\hat{G}_{2}\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}\!+\!x^{\star},\tilde{\mathrm{w}}_{\perp}^{k},\tilde{\theta}\!+\!\theta^{\star}\right),

(20b)

where
\Delta\hat{G}_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{G}_{2}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star},\tilde{\mathrm{w}}_{\perp},\tilde{\theta}+\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle\coloneq\hat{G}_{2}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star},R\tilde{\mathrm{w}}_{\perp}+\mathbf{1}\sigma(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star}),\tilde{\theta}+\theta^{\star})-\hat{G}_{2}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star},\mathbf{1}\sigma(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star}),\tilde{\theta}+\theta^{\star}).

(21)

Let us introduce
\zeta\coloneq\col\left(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\right)\coloneq\col\left(\tilde{\mathrm{w}}_{\perp},\tilde{\mathrm{z}}_{\perp}\right)
,
\tilde{A}\coloneq\blkdiag(R^{\top}AR,R^{\top}AR)
, and
g_{\textsc{b}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2(N-1)d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{2(N-1)d}
defined as

\displaystyle g_{\textsc{b}}\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}},\zeta,\tilde{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}0\\
R^{\top}A\Delta\hat{G}_{2}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star},\zeta_{1},\tilde{\theta}+\theta^{\star})\end{bmatrix},

(22)

which allow us to compactly rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle\zeta^{{k+1}}=\tilde{A}\zeta^{k}+g_{\textsc{b}}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}},\zeta^{k},\tilde{\theta}).

(23)

With this notation at hand, we are ready to formalize the stability properties of the boundary-layer system (

).

Lemma 1
.

There exist a Lyapunov function
U:\mathbb{R}^{2(N-1)d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}>0
such that

\displaystyle b_{1}\!\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert^{2}\!\leq U\!\left(\zeta\right)\!\leq b_{2}\!\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert^{2}

(24a)

\displaystyle U\!\left(\!\tilde{A}\zeta\!+\!g_{\textsc{b}}\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}},\zeta,\tilde{\theta}\right)\right)\!-\!U\!\left(\zeta\right)\leq-b_{3}\!\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert^{2}

(24b)

\displaystyle\left\lvert U\!\left(\zeta\right)\!-\!U\!\left(\bar{\zeta}\right)\right\rvert\leq b_{4}\!\left\lVert\zeta-\bar{\zeta}\right\rVert\left(\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert\!+\!\left\lVert\bar{\zeta}\right\rVert\right),

(24c)

for any
\zeta,\bar{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2(N-1)d}
and
\left(\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\mathrm{x}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\blacksquare

The proof of Lemma

is reported in Appendix
B-B
.

IV-C

Reduced system analysis

In this section, we analyze the reduced system associated to (

for all

into (
18a
)-(
18b
), namely

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}^{k+1}\!

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}^{k}\!-\!\gamma(\nabla_{3}\tilde{\ell}(\!\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k},\mathbf{1}\sigma\!\left(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!+\!x^{\star}\right),\tilde{\theta}^{k},k)\!-\!\mathrm{p}_{\ell}\!\left(k\right))

(25a)

\displaystyle\!\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{{k+1}}\!

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\!-\!\gamma\hat{G}^{d}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}^{k},\tilde{\theta}^{k}),

(25b)

in which we introduce
\hat{G}^{d}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined as

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{d}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}},\tilde{\theta})

\displaystyle\coloneq\hat{G}_{1}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star},\mathbf{1}\sigma(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star}),\tilde{\theta}+\theta^{\star})-\mathrm{p}_{1}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star})

\displaystyle\hskip 13.0pt+\nabla\phi(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star})\tfrac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}}{N}\hat{G}_{2}(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star},\mathbf{1}\sigma(\tilde{\mathrm{x}}+x^{\star}),\tilde{\theta}+\theta^{\star})

(26)

Using the accuracy characterization (

) of

and the optimality of

for (

), we point out that the origin is an equilibrium of (

).
We then study its stability via the corresponding averaged system, obtained by averaging the time-varying vector field of (

) over an infinite time horizon (see Appendix

).
To this end, let
\tilde{\ell}_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
be

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{\mathcal{K}}(\tilde{x},\tilde{\theta})\coloneq\tfrac{1}{\mathcal{K}}\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}\ell(\tilde{x}\!+\!x^{\star}\!+\!\mathrm{d}^{k}_{x},\mathbf{1}\sigma(\tilde{x}\!+\!x^{\star})\!+\!\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma},\tilde{\theta}\!+\!\theta^{\star}).

The periodicity of

and
\mathrm{d}^{k}_{\sigma}
(cf. Assumption
6
) ensures the existence of the averaged system associated to (

VI
Conclusions

We proposed DELTA, a data-driven distributed algorithm for aggregative optimization in unknown scenarios.
DELTA combines an optimization-oriented part with a tracking mechanism and a learning one.
The latter takes on local neural networks elaborating cost samples around the current solution estimates to approximate their gradients.
We analyzed DELTA by using tools from system theory based on timescale separation and averaging theory.
In detail, we theoretically proved linear convergence in a neighborhood of the optimal solution whose size depends on the given accuracy capabilities of the neural networks.
Some numerical tests confirmed our findings.
