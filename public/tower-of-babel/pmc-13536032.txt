Risk of pneumonia in adults with chronic cough: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Man Young Park, Beom-Joon Lee, Kwan-Il Kim, Jun-Hwan Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic cough affects approximately 10% of adults and is increasingly recognised as a distinct clinical condition. This symptom has increasingly been associated with significant outcomes such as pneumonia. Nevertheless, few large long-term population-based studies have been conducted.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was performed using nationwide claims data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service in South Korea from 2013 to 2020. Adult patients who visited outpatient clinics for cough (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision: R05) were enrolled. Chronic cough was defined as ≥8 visits or ≥56 days. Patients with pneumonia within 2 years before and 6 months after the index date were excluded. The main outcome was incident pneumonia during the 3 year follow-up period. The association between chronic cough and incident pneumonia was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models, with HRs adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities and Charlson comorbidity index. Sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses by sex and age were also performed.ResultsAmong 385 417 participants (28 706 with chronic cough and 356 711 controls), the incidence of pneumonia was higher in the chronic cough group (18.87 vs 11.32 per 1000 person-years). Chronic cough was independently associated with an elevated risk of pneumonia (adjusted HR 1.13; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.19; p<0.001), and the association was robust across multiple sensitivity analyses with alternative exposures. The subgroup analysis showed a stronger association among men (HR 1.72) than among women (HR 1.39) and among adults aged 40–79 years, with the highest risk observed among adults aged 40–49 years (HR 1.37).ConclusionsChronic cough was independently associated with increased pneumonia risk, especially in middle-aged and male patients. Clinicians should be aware that chronic cough may signal increased vulnerability to lower respiratory tract infections (eg, pneumonia) and consider preventive strategies, including vaccination and close monitoring, for high-risk individuals.

Introduction

Chronic cough affects approximately 10% of the general population worldwide and is recognised as a significant public health concern because of its detrimental impact on quality of life and increased healthcare resource utilisation.1–3 A substantial subset of cases remains unexplained despite thorough investigation, and these patients often share demographic features of cough hypersensitivity.4 It is generally defined as a cough persisting for more than 8 weeks.5 6 While traditionally considered a symptom secondary to conditions such as asthma, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) or rhinitis, it is now increasingly being regarded as an independent clinical entity driven by cough reflex hypersensitivity.7–9 Recent studies have suggested that chronic cough may be associated with more serious health outcomes beyond symptom burden. A population-based cohort study by Landt et al demonstrated that chronic cough was significantly associated with an increased risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbations, pneumonia and all-cause mortality. Notably, the risk of pneumonia was elevated 2.2-fold (95% CI 1.7 to 2.7) regardless of airflow limitation or smoking status, indicating that chronic cough may serve as an independent risk factor for infectious pulmonary complications.

Pathophysiologically, impaired airway epithelial integrity and neuroplastic changes in afferent sensory pathways are the proposed mechanisms underlying chronic cough.6 8 9 Chronic cough has been significantly associated with conditions such as respiratory infections, bronchiectasis, asthma, COPD and GERD in a large population sample.10 In addition, silent aspiration is often observed in patients with chronic cough and has been associated with the development of pneumonia.11 12 Moreover, chronic mucus hypersecretion, which may also occur in patients with chronic cough, is associated with accelerated lung function decline and higher rates of hospitalisation.13

Most studies to date have been cross-sectional, used small sample sizes or had short follow-up periods, which limits their ability to delineate the causality between chronic cough and pneumonia.14–16 Moreover, the present clinical guidelines are mainly for the relief of symptoms and treatment of underlying disease,6 and preventive strategies, including for severe complications such as pneumonia, are limited.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the incidence of pneumonia among patients with chronic cough compared with a control group over a 3-year follow-up period using comprehensive nationwide claims data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) in Korea.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective population-based cohort study using data from the National Claims Database of the HIRA of Korea. The study period was from 1 January 2011 to 31 December 2020. The HIRA database records healthcare utilisation for the entire Korean population, providing demographic variables, diagnostic codes, procedures, prescriptions and others. All data were anonymised before being made available.

Study population and exclusion criteria

Adults aged 20 years or older who visited the outpatient clinics during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Chronic cough is usually defined as a cough that lasts for 8 weeks or more. However, the actual time of symptom onset was not recorded in the administrative claims data, and we applied an episode-based operational definition using outpatient visit records. The number of outpatient visits was included as a supplementary criterion alongside episode duration because in claims databases, a strictly temporal definition based solely on the interval between the first and last visits may not reliably distinguish sustained chronic symptoms from intermittent or recurrent episodes of acute cough. Visit count serves as a proxy for healthcare-seeking behaviour, which is indicative of persistent symptoms. Outpatient care with cough as the reason for the visit (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code: R05) was matched per episode (ie, visits <30 days apart) over time. An episode was considered new after an interval of at least 30 days between visits. Patients were divided into the chronic cough group if they had one or more episodes lasting equal to or more than 56 days or with eight or more outpatient visits. The control cohort included subjects who had at least one visit with cough during the observation period but did not meet the definition of chronic cough. The index date was defined as follows: for the chronic cough group, the index date was the date on which the chronic cough criteria were first met (ie, the date of the visit at which the episode reached ≥56 days in duration or ≥8 cumulative visits); for the control group, the index date was the date of the first outpatient visit with a cough diagnosis (ICD-10: R05) during the study period. Subjects were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) a history of pneumonia within 2 years prior to the baseline assessment or (2) a diagnosis of pneumonia within 6 months following the baseline assessment. Episode-based definition and classification of chronic cough

For every patient, we recorded the cough-related episodes, including the start date, end date, total number of visits and total number of days. Sequential visits for up to 30 days were considered during the same episode. Five alternative definitions of chronic cough were tested for the robustness of the findings in the sensitivity analyses: (1) main definition: episode duration ≥56 days or ≥8 visits; (2) strict definition: episode duration ≥56 days and ≥8 visits; (3) moderate definition: episode duration ≥56 days and ≥6 visits; (4) prolonged mild definition: episode duration ≥60 days and ≥5 visits; and (5) prolonged stronger definition: episode duration ≥70 days and ≥5 visits.

The covariates were age, sex and the most common underlying diseases that increase the risk of respiratory diseases. The Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) was developed from ICD-10 codes within the 2 years before the index date and included 17 comorbidity categories (such as malignancy, diabetes, chronic pulmonary disease and congestive heart failure). Scores were assigned per standard weights, and patients were categorised according to four CCI levels: 0, 1–2, 3–4 and ≥5.

Follow-up and outcome

The main outcome was pneumonia that developed after the index date. All patients were followed up from the index date for a maximum of 3 years until the first of the following events: diagnosis of pneumonia or termination of the study period (31 December 2020).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed based on sex (men and women) and age groups (20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, ≥80 years). To assess the effect modification, we introduced interaction terms, and to estimate the effect size, we used Cox proportional hazards models in each subset.

Statistical analysis

Means±SD or medians and IQRs were used to summarise continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were evaluated using the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while categorical variables were tested using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. The relationship between chronic cough and pneumonia was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression to obtain HRs and 95% CIs. Multivariate models accounted for factors such as age, sex and comorbidities, including asthma and GERD. In model 3, the CCI was included alongside individual comorbidities (COPD, GERD, chronic sinusitis and heart failure) because it captured the overall comorbidity burden as a summary measure, whereas individual comorbidities were included to adjust for their specific confounding effects on pneumonia risk. The CCI used in the present study did not include age as a factor. To assess the potential for collinearity between these variables, variance inflation factors were calculated and confirmed to be within acceptable limits (all variance inflation factors<5), indicating that multicollinearity did not substantially affect the model estimates. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by visual inspection of the Kaplan–Meier survival curves for the primary exposure variable (chronic cough vs control). The two survival curves did not cross or converge and maintained a consistent separation throughout the 3-year follow-up period with no evidence of convergence, indicating that the relative hazard between the groups remained approximately constant over time. The consistency of HR estimates across the five alternative exposure definitions in the sensitivity analyses provided additional support for the validity of the proportional hazards assumption. Analyses of HRs for alternative definitions of chronic cough and the robustness of the results by excluding early-onset pneumonia events (6 months after the index date of the chronic cough group) were also performed through sensitivity analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (V.4.2.2), and a p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study. As this study used anonymised and routinely collected claims data from a national health insurance database, it was not appropriate or feasible to involve patients or the public.

The chronic cough group comprised a larger proportion of patients aged 60–69 (25.5%) and 70–79 (22.9%) than the control group (17.2% and 11.0%, respectively), whereas the younger age group (20–39 years) was more frequent in the control group.

Respiratory disorders and associated comorbidities were more frequent in patients with chronic cough. Asthma (11.0% vs 4.4%), chronic sinusitis (6.6% vs 3.1%) and COPD (3.2% vs 1.3%) were significantly more common in the chronic cough group (all p<0.001). Similarly, GERD (29.9% vs 20.6%), diabetes mellitus (13.2% vs 8.2%), hypertension (21.8% vs 13.1%) and heart failure (2.4% vs 1.4%) were more common in this group. Lung cancer, though rare, was also more prevalent (0.7% vs 0.2%, p<0.001).

Patients with a CCI score of 0 were less common in the chronic cough group (70.0%) than the control group (81.6%). Patients with chronic cough had more comorbidities than the control group: 26.8% of the chronic cough group had CCI scores of 1–2 and 2.9% of them had CCI scores of 3–4 vs 16.9% and 1.4% of the control group, respectively (p<0.001).

In the unadjusted Cox regression model, chronic cough was associated with a 68% higher risk of pneumonia (HR 1.68; 95% CI 1.59 to 1.78; p<0.001). After adjusting for age, sex and baseline characteristics (model 2), the HR decreased but remained significant (adjusted HR 1.21; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.28; p<0.001). In the fully adjusted model (model 3), which included comorbidities and the CCI, the association persisted (HR 1.13; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.19; p<0.001), indicating an independent association between chronic cough and increased pneumonia risk. Consistent with these findings, the cumulative incidence of pneumonia over the 3-year follow-up period was higher for the chronic cough group than for the control group (online supplemental figure 1).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using five alternative operational definitions of chronic cough, all of which showed a consistently elevated risk of pneumonia in the chronic cough group when using fully adjusted models (model 3). As shown in figure 2, under the main definition (≥8 outpatient visits or an episode duration of ≥56 days), the HR for pneumonia was 1.13 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.19). When a strict definition of both ≥8 visits and ≥56 days was applied, the HR increased to 1.20 (95% CI 1.10 to 1.32). A moderate definition threshold (≥6 visits and ≥56 days) yielded an HR of 1.23 (95% CI 1.13 to 1.34), while definitions based on prolonged mild-stronger definition either ≥60 days and ≥5 visits or ≥70 days and ≥5 visits showed HRs of 1.23 (95% CI 1.13 to 1.34) and 1.19 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.31), respectively. Across all definitions, the pneumonia incidence rate in the chronic cough group ranged from 20.45 to 20.94 per 1000 person-years, consistently exceeding that in the control group (11.32 per 1000 person-years), thereby reinforcing the robustness of the main findings.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses, stratified by age and sex, revealed that the association between chronic cough and pneumonia was more pronounced in certain populations (table 3). Statistically significant associations were observed in patients aged 40–79 years. The strongest association was observed for individuals in their 40s (HR 1.37; 95% CI 1.09 to 1.74; p=0.008), followed by those in their 50s (HR 1.23; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.43), 60s (HR 1.20; 95% CI 1.07 to 1.34) and 70s (HR 1.10; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.21). No significant associations were observed among individuals aged 20–39 or ≥80 years. The interaction term for age was not significant (p=0.123).

Discussion

In this nationwide retrospective cohort study, the incidence of pneumonia was significantly higher in the chronic cough group than in the control group (18.87 vs 11.32 per 1000 person-years). After comprehensive adjustment for age, sex, comorbidities and the CCI, chronic cough remained independently associated with an elevated risk of pneumonia (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.19, p<0.001). This association was robust across multiple sensitivity analyses and was more pronounced in males (HR 1.72 vs 1.39; interaction p<0.001) and in adults aged 40–79 years, with the strongest relative risk observed in the 40–49 age group (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.74, p=0.008).

This study identified an independent association between chronic cough and an increased risk of pneumonia, reinforcing the notion that chronic cough is not merely a benign symptom but may be linked to more serious clinical outcomes.11 17 In a large Copenhagen-based population cohort, Landt et al reported a 2.2 times higher risk of pneumonia compared with patients with chronic cough during a maximum follow-up period of 5.9 years.17 Although the present study showed a similar increase in risk, the effect size (HR=1.13) was smaller. Nonetheless, owing to the high prevalence of chronic cough in the general population, a relatively small increase in risk may represent a considerable public health burden. Potential explanations for this difference include but are not limited to identified operational definitions of chronic cough (episode-based in the current study vs duration-based in Landt et al), differences in study population (nationwide Korean insurance claims vs a random sample of the Danish population), follow-up period (3 vs 5.9 years, maximum) and adjustment for covariates (the present study possesses a larger set adjusted for including comorbidities and the CCI) Our study aimed to isolate the effects of chronic cough itself more precisely by adjusting for a more expansive list of possible confounders.

Similarly, systematic reviews have also identified some risk factors associated with chronic cough, such as asthma, current smoking and low educational and/or socioeconomic status, that interact with each other to negatively affect respiratory health.18 These results suggest that chronic cough is a phenotype with an interaction between biology and exposure.

Some mechanisms may explain the link between chronic cough and pneumonia risk. Airway inflammation is commonly observed with chronic cough, which threatens the integrity of the respiratory mucosa and impairs the respiratory tract barrier function against pathogens, thereby predisposing to respiratory infection.19 Furthermore, the neuroplastic upregulation of the cough reflex, now conceptualised as cough hypersensitivity syndrome, may perpetuate airway insults and impair local immune defence mechanisms.20 In addition, impaired mucociliary clearance, a well-recognised state among patients with chronic respiratory symptoms, can cause secretions and microbes to be collected from the airways, which may increase the risk of pneumonia.21 22

According to Bailey,21 ciliary beat frequency decreases with age and mucus property changes, further increasing the risk of respiratory diseases (ie, pneumonia and bronchiectasis). Similarly, Ho et al22 showed that nasal mucociliary clearance was impaired with increasing age, identifying an age-associated physiological predilection for respiratory infections.

We adjusted for multiple potential confounders, including sex, age and a range of comorbidities such as asthma, chronic sinusitis, COPD, diabetes mellitus, GERD, heart failure, hypertension and lung cancer. Even after these adjustments, chronic cough was independently associated with an increased risk of pneumonia. Mediating factors such as anxiety, depression and poor quality of life could also have had an impact on respiratory health, which limits the conclusions drawn from our findings. Nevertheless, the field of cough now has a new, relatively objective finding that suggests the importance of chronic cough as a potential independent clinical marker of susceptibility to lower respiratory tract infections.

The finding that the association between chronic cough and the risk of pneumonia was stronger in males is noteworthy. Sex differences in the incidence of pneumonia have been described, with a higher risk of pneumonia and mortality in men in some studies.23–25 Falagas et al23 recognised sex as an important epidemiological factor, with men being more vulnerable to lower respiratory tract infections, and suffering from greater symptom severity and mortality. Likewise, studies on community-acquired pneumonia have demonstrated that men generally have a higher incidence of pneumonia, and that sex differences are more apparent in older ages.26–28

In our study, the proportion of female participants was higher in both the chronic cough and control groups, with a significantly greater proportion observed in the chronic cough group (74.0 vs 68.5%, p<0.001). Our findings are consistent with a recent systematic review that reported a much higher prevalence of chronic cough in women compared with men, which is consistent with several studies.1 29

However, it is worth noting that the risk of pneumonia in men with chronic cough was more than twice as high as the risk in women with chronic cough, even though the absolute prevalence of chronic cough is higher in women than men overall. This suggests that although cough symptoms may be present at a higher frequency in women, men with chronic cough may have greater biological susceptibility to pneumonia, perhaps owing to higher smoking rates or other underlying sex-specific immunological factors. However, these mechanisms warrant further investigation.

In all analyses stratified by age, having a chronic cough was associated with pneumonia risk among persons aged 40–79 years, and the effect was strongest among persons aged in their 40s (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.74, p=0.008). This suggests that the cumulative effects of repeated airway insults from chronic cough may make middle-aged and older adults more vulnerable to infection. The lack of important associations among those aged 20–39 years and 80 years or older may result from differences in aetiology of cough or pneumonia in these age groups or reduced statistical power due to smaller event rates.

The particularly elevated risk observed in the 40s contrasts with previous literature, which often highlights increased pneumonia risk primarily in older age groups, suggesting potential midlife-specific vulnerabilities that warrant further investigation to elucidate the underlying biological and environmental factors. In those 80 years or older, chronic cough may have had a lesser effect on pneumonia risk due to the greater influence of other comorbidities. Meanwhile, clinical studies have indicated that elderly cases of aspiration pneumonia are associated with risk factors such as decreased swallowing ability, dehydration and dementia, but not with advanced age itself.30 These results indicate that the effect of chronic cough may be less important than impaired function and concomitant diseases in affecting the risk of pneumonia in the oldest age groups.

Although multiple operational definitions of chronic cough have been used, its consistent association with increased pneumonia risk makes the findings robust. This consistency indicates that the association is not just an artefact of a specific case definition, but rather represents a more mechanistic relationship between chronic cough and pneumonia risk.

The strengths of this study include the utilisation of a large, nationwide, population-based cohort with high representativeness arising from the HIRA database of South Korea. This design offered the necessary statistical power to identify events with even small estimates of effects and allowed for extensive subgroup analyses. Key advantages included long-term follow-up (median, 3 years) that enabled the identification of many pneumonia events and temporal relationships between chronic cough and pneumonia. Moreover, the novel adjustment for a wide variety of possible confounders, including age, sex, multiple comorbid conditions and the CCI, provided incremental validity to our findings by reducing the grip of residual confounding and allowing the determination of the independent effect of chronic cough.

A notable methodological strength was the use of episode-based operational definitions to approximate chronic cough in the absence of a dedicated ICD code (R05.3) during the study period. The consistency of the findings across the five alternative definitions in the sensitivity analyses demonstrates that the observed association is not attributable to definitional bias, reinforcing the validity of chronic cough as an independent risk factor for pneumonia.

This study was limited by its retrospective cohort design; therefore, no causal conclusion could be drawn regarding chronic cough leading to pneumonia. Additionally, the episode-based operational definition of chronic cough, which incorporates both visit frequency and episode duration, may have included some patients with recurrent episodes of acute cough or poorly controlled underlying respiratory disease, rather than true chronic cough. Although we performed sensitivity analyses using five alternative definitions with varying combinations of duration and visit thresholds, all of which yielded consistent results, we acknowledge that a purely temporal definition may better approximate the clinical concept of chronic cough. Future studies using datasets with direct recordings of symptom durations are warranted to validate these findings. Furthermore, the control group in this study comprised patients with at least one outpatient visit for cough who did not meet the chronic cough criteria, rather than a cough-free general population or an age-matched and sex-matched cohort. This active comparator design was chosen to reduce surveillance and detection bias, because individuals who never sought care for cough symptoms would have had different healthcare utilisation patterns and baseline characteristics. However, we acknowledge that this approach may result in a more conservative effect estimate, as the control group itself may include individuals with subacute or recurrent cough, and the substantial age difference between the groups (mean 58.9 vs 50.7 years) may not be entirely resolved by statistical adjustment alone. An age-matched and sex-matched design could have mitigated some of this imbalance, although such matching can also reduce generalisability and introduce biases. Although we adjusted for age as a continuous variable and used age-stratified subgroup analyses, the marked age imbalance between the chronic cough and control groups (53.8 vs 31.2%; aged ≥60 years) may not have been fully addressed by conventional covariate adjustment. Additional methods, such as propensity score matching or inverse probability of treatment weighting, could further mitigate the residual confounding caused by this imbalance. However, the consistent direction and significance of the association across multiple age-stratified subgroup analyses provide reassurance that the findings are not solely attributable to age-related confounding factors. Unmeasured confounding variables might have distorted the observed associations. Most importantly, claims data lack the granularity of cough severity, underlying aetiologies, other environmental exposures or individual health behaviours that drive both exposure and outcome. We adjusted for numerous comorbidities, but we were unable to broadly characterise patient baseline health status or disease severity due to the administrative nature of the dataset. In particular, although smoking status was included as a covariate in model 2, the HIRA claims database does not systematically capture a detailed smoking history (eg, pack-years, current vs former vs never status), which may have limited the precision of this adjustment. Smoking is a well-established risk factor for both chronic cough and pneumonia, and residual confounding by smoking intensity cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, other respiratory comorbidities known to be associated with chronic cough and pneumonia risk—such as bronchiectasis and interstitial lung disease—were not included as separate adjustment variables in our models because of the relatively low prevalence and potential underreporting of these conditions in the claims data. Future studies with access to detailed clinical data, including verified smoking status and comprehensive respiratory phenotyping, are required to address these limitations.

This study has important clinical implications, as it confirms that chronic cough is an independent risk factor for pneumonia. Chronic cough must not be simply viewed as an unpleasant symptom, but as a possible clinical sign of lower respiratory tract infections.1 31 32 Clinicians should consider routine monitoring and preventive strategies for chronic cough, an important public and patient health issue (eg, pneumococcal and influenza vaccinations, cough medication and underlying conditions). Patient education for the early recognition of symptoms and early intervention is also key.

Further research is required to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying chronic cough and pneumonia to better understand these two conditions. This information should focus on why the risk is higher for males, why patients in their 40s have the greatest relative risk, and the mechanisms underlying the differences in susceptibility to pneumonia with varying causes of chronic cough.29 In addition, prospective studies should evaluate the impact of clinical factors such as cough severity, timing and type (eg, dry vs productive cough) on pneumonia risk trajectories.21

We believe that clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of pneumonia prevention interventions, including pharmacological therapies, physiotherapy and lifestyle modifications in high-risk populations with chronic coughing, are important priorities for future research. Finally, the present findings underscore the need for a consensus-validated case definition of chronic cough applicable to administrative health databases to improve the comparability and reproducibility of epidemiological studies in this field.

Taken together, these findings emphasise that chronic cough should be viewed not only as a clinical nuisance, but also as a meaningful marker of respiratory vulnerability, requiring both clinical vigilance and research attention.

Frequent coughing by patients with chronic cough can increase the risk of pneumonia. This major study, using nationwide health data from South Korea, shows that chronic cough is associated with a 13% higher risk of pneumonia in adults with than in adults without chronic cough. The extra risk was marked in men and those aged 40–79 years.

Patients with chronic cough require closer monitoring, and uncontrollable diseases should be detected and treated. Interventions include preventive measures such as pneumococcal and influenza vaccinations. Recognising chronic cough as a clinical warning signal rather than merely a symptom can help improve respiratory health and prevent severe infections such as pneumonia.
