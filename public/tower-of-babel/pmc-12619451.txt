Effects of different arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi on tobacco seedling growth and their rhizosphere microecological mechanisms
Authors: Siyu Wen, Jinghan Sun, Wanli Zeng, Haiying Xiang, Mingfang Zhao, Dan Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNumerous studies have demonstrated significant variations in the plant growth-promoting effects among different species of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). However, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, particularly regarding how distinct AMF species regulate the rhizosphere microbiome.ResultsFive AMF species (Funneliformis mosseae, Diversispora versiformis, Clariodeoglous etunicatum, Rhizophagus intraradices, and Acaulospora delicate) were inoculated to investigate their effects on tobacco seedling growth and rhizosphere microecological regulation. The results showed that all AMF inoculations significantly increased shoot and root biomass, N/P/K uptake, morphological traits (height, stem diameter, leaf area), chlorophyll content (SPAD), and root architecture (length, surface area, volume, diameter) of tobacco seedlings. Among them, the treatment inoculated with R. intraradices showed the most outstanding growth-promoting effect in all growth indicators. Metagenomic analysis indicated that AMF inoculation significantly altered the diversity and community structure of rhizosphere substrate microorganisms. Among them, R. intraradices inoculation yielded the highest microbial diversity, with an associated network exhibiting enhanced complexity. KEGG functional annotation revealed metabolic pathways (IAA biosynthesis, iron-siderophore transport regulation, exopolysaccharide production, and nutrient cycling) consistently associated with tobacco growth promotion in all AMF inoculations. However, species-specific mechanisms were observed: F. mosseae promotes tobacco seedling growth by enhancing IAA synthesis through the recruitment of beneficial microorganisms such as Nostoc, Flavisolibacter, Frateuria, and Sphingomonas. D. versiformis enhanced carbon fixation via the hydroxypropionate-hydroxybutyrate cycle, driven by the proliferation of Glaciecola, Pedococcus, Phycicoccus, and Hephaestia. C. etunicatum facilitated phosphorus/iron accumulation through organic phosphorus mineralization, phosphate transport, and iron acquisition accompanied by the recruitment of, Hartmannibacter, Lysobacter, Moheibacter, and Pseudolabrys. R. intraradices improved nitrogen assimilation through augmented nitrogen transport and assimilatory nitrate reduction (ANRA), correlated with the recruitment of Azospirillum, Sphingobium, Mesorhizobium, Paracoccus, and Parafilimonas. A. delicate stimulated plant growth via polyphosphate degradation and exopolysaccharide biosynthesis, associated with the enrichment of Segetibacter, Ferruginibacter, Hyphomicrobium and Pseudomonas. Notably, this study revealed that functional divergence in rhizosphere microbiomes associated with the five tested fungal species was primarily reflected in the abundance rather than the composition of functional genes.ConclusionIn summary, AMF inoculation significantly enhanced tobacco seedling biomass and agronomic traits by improving mineral nutrient assimilation efficiency and restructuring the rhizosphere microbial community. Different AMF species exhibited distinct microecological regulation patterns. This study elucidated the growth-promoting mechanisms of AMF from a microbial interaction perspective, providing a theoretical basis for establishing a sustainable tobacco cultivation system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07600-9.Keywords: Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, Growth characteristics, Microecological mechanisms, Tobacco, Metagenomic sequencing

Introduction

Tobacco is a major economic crop in China, with a leading global output, and it is also an important source of income for many farmers [1]. However, the continuous overuse of chemical pesticides and fertilizers has led to soil deterioration and a reduction in crop quality and production [2, 3]. In recent years, plant-microbe symbiotic systems have gained widespread recognition as a green and sustainable agricultural strategy. These systems enhance nutrient uptake by plants while improving soil structure [4, 5], thereby promoting crop growth and increasing yields while reducing reliance on chemical inputs.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are ubiquitous symbiotic microorganisms within soil microbiomes, forming mutualistic associations with over 80% of terrestrial flora [6]. These mycorrhizal fungi improve the hydraulic conductivity and mineral nutrient acquisition of their host plants by expanding the root system’s hyphal network [7, 8], strengthening plant resistance to biotic and abiotic stressors [9], and influencing rhizosphere microbial consortia, which increases microbial diversity and improves the ecological function and health of the soil [10]. These synergistic interactions optimize the plant-soil microenvironment, thereby promoting plant development and increasing crop productivity [11].

However, there was a great deal of variation in the ability of several arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi to promote plant growth on host crops. For instance, Xiang et al. [12] reported that, except for the F. mosseae inoculation, all other AMF treatments markedly improved tomato seedling development, with R. showing the strongest growth promotion. According to Hagh et al. [13], R. intraradices had a more significant impact than F. mosseae; however, both species considerably enhanced maize grain production, yield components, and nitrogen uptake. By using pot trials, Hu et al. [14] showed that the growth-promoting effects of two AMF differed greatly, with only the inoculation with C. etunicatum producing a rise in cucumber biomass.

It is noteworthy that the mechanisms by which different AMF promote host plant growth remain incompletely understood. Previous studies have provided some initial understanding of the mechanisms by which various AMF species promote growth. For instance, in alfalfa inoculation experiments, Smith et al. [15] found that Glomus caledonium can extract phosphorus from soil at greater distances than Scutellospora calospora, which primarily absorbs phosphorus in the rhizosphere. This suggests that different species have different spatial strategies for acquiring phosphorus. Canepelle et al. [16] showed that specific AMF have host organ specificity: Glomus formosanum mainly stimulated root proliferation, Acaulospora scrobiculata favored bud development, and Scutellospora pellucida inoculation improved root and stem development in sorghum, increasing grain yield. Although the mechanisms behind the differential growth promotion effects of AMF have been partially elucidated by these studies, further study is required to fully understand these mechanisms and improve the usefulness of AMF in agricultural settings.

More and more recent studies suggest that AMF play a crucial role in regulating rhizosphere microbial consortia. Through the modification of root exudate patterns, these symbionts aid in the recruitment of particular functional microbial species, leading to a notable reorganization of the rhizosphere microbiome [17]. For instance, Cao et al. [18] showed that AMF colonization causes significant changes in the makeup of bacterial communities, particularly increasing the representation of taxa like Anaerobacter, Corallibacteraceae, and Glycomyces. Jin et al. [19] also found that AMF selectively cultivate phosphorus-solubilizing bacteria under organophosphorus stress conditions, hence expanding their competitive niche. Additionally, Xu et al. [20] showed in an inoculation experiment that the rhizosphere microbial diversity is greatly altered by AMF colonization, with a notable enrichment of functional taxa related to the nitrogen cycle and phosphorus metabolism. This implies that AMF influence the assembly dynamics of rhizosphere bacterial communities, thereby selectively recruiting functional microorganisms to regulate soil nutrient cycling. Li et al. [21] found that AMF not only enhance the complexity of the rhizosphere microbial ecological network but also promote soil functional optimization by reinforcing symbiotic and cooperative interactions within soil microbial consortia. The mechanistic pathways by which AMF regulate rhizosphere microecology are still poorly understood, and more research is necessary to comprehend the coupling mechanisms of AMF in regulating rhizosphere microecology and their promoting effects. However, current research has primarily focused on the overall effects of AM fungi, with limited studies investigating the specific mechanisms by which different AMF species regulate plant rhizosphere microbiomes. This critical knowledge gap hinders our understanding of strain-specific promotion mechanisms and their precise application in agriculture.

This study utilized the new tobacco cultivar “Honey Sweet Aroma” as experimental material. Five AMF (Funneliformis mosseae, Diversispora versiformis, Claroideoglomus etunicatum, Rhizophagus intraradices, and Acaulospora delicata) [22–25], combined with metagenomic sequencing technology, to systematically analyze the effects of different fungal treatments on the structure of the tobacco rhizosphere microbial community, functional gene expression, and plant growth. This study aims to address the research gap in understanding the differential regulation of rhizosphere microecology by distinct AM fungal species, elucidate strain-specific mechanisms through which they promote tobacco growth via rhizosphere microecological regulation, and provide theoretical and technical support for the precise utilization of AM fungal resources and the development of specialized microbial fertilizers for tobacco.

Materials and methods

Materials

Five AMF species (Funneliformis mosseae, Diversispora versiformis, Claroideoglomus etunicatum, Rhizophagus intraradices, and Acaulospora delicata) were employed as inoculants in this study. All tested fungal strains were purchased as original inoculants from the Brachycyclus Genus Collection (BGC) and subsequently propagated in our laboratory. The AMF inoculum, consisting of spores and mycelium, was incubated for 5.5 months in a growth medium composed of grass charcoal, peat, and vermiculite, during which corn (Zea mays) and red clover (Trifolium repens) were cultivated to induce spore maturation. This study employed a newly developed tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) cultivar belonging to the Honey Sweet Aroma strain. Seeds underwent surface sterilization via immersion in 10% hydrogen peroxide for 20 min, followed by an overnight rinse in sterile distilled water. Sterilized seeds were subsequently sown into pots containing sterilized substrate and incubated in a controlled-environment growth chamber. The tobacco seedling-specific substrate complied with the YC/T310-2024 standard, and examination via the sucrose centrifugation-wet sieving and decanting method [26] revealed the absence of any AMF propagules in the substrate. Its physicochemical properties were as follows: pH 5.4; alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen content of 576.11 mg/kg; available phosphorus concentration of 27.55 mg/kg; available potassium level of 1422.12 mg/kg; and organic matter content of 414.66 g/kg.

Experimental design

This study established six treatment groups: uninoculated control (CK), single inoculation with inoculum No. 1 (A1, Funneliformis mosseae), inoculum No. 2 (A2, Diversispora versiformis), inoculum No. 3 (A3, Clariodeoglous etunicatum), inoculum No. 4 (A4, Rhizophagus intraradices), and inoculum No. 5 (A5, Acaulospora delicate). All treatments inoculated with AMF received a uniform inoculum of 5,000 total spores. The uninoculated control group (CK) received an equivalent volume of sterilized inoculum mixture that had undergone two rounds of autoclaving at 121 °C (120 min per cycle). Each treatment had 50 replicates, corresponding to 50 pots, with one tobacco plant grown per pot. Square seedling cups were used as planting containers, measuring 7.0 cm × 7.0 cm at the top, 5.0 cm × 5.0 cm at the bottom, and 7.0 cm in height. For each treatment group, the substrate and inoculum were mixed in bulk: first, the total mass of specialized tobacco seedling substrate required for each group was thoroughly mixed with the corresponding AMF inoculum. The application rate of the inoculum was adjusted based on spore density to ensure a consistent total spore count per pot: A1, 2.96 g per pot (spore density 1690 spores/g); A2, 2.90 g per pot (1725 spores/g); A3, 2.97 g per pot (1681 spores/g); A4, 2.86 g per pot (1750 spores/g); A5, 3.02 g per pot (1657 spores/g). The CK group received a mixture of equal amounts of the aforementioned five sterilized inoculants (3.02 g per pot). After thorough mixing, the substrate and inoculum were weighed and partitioned into individual cups at 90 g per pot, along with the corresponding inoculum, ensuring uniform inoculation across all pots. The tobacco variety was a new honey-sweet-aromatic type. Prior to planting, seeds were soaked in a 10% hydrogen peroxide solution for 20 min, followed by overnight soaking in clean water. Seeds were sown with 2–3 seeds per pot. After germination, seedlings were thinned to one plant per pot and cultivated in an artificial climate chamber. The growth conditions were maintained at 18–25 °C with a light intensity of 300 µmol m−2 s−1 during daylight hours. Watering was adjusted according to soil moisture levels, with consistent timing and volume for all plants. The plants were irrigated with water and Hoagland nutrient solution in alternating sessions, ensuring thorough saturation of the growing medium in all pots with each application. The plants grew for a total of 67 days from sowing to harvest.

Measurement indices and methods

Plant biomass and nutrient content

After harvest, 24 tobacco seedlings were randomly selected for evaluating agronomic traits, including plant height, stem diameter, maximum leaf length, and maximum leaf width, in compliance with the State Tobacco Monopoly Bureau of China (YC/T142-2010). Leaf area was calculated as leaf length × leaf width × 0.6345. Chlorophyll content was assessed with a Soil Plant Analysis Development (SPAD) instrument.

To determine dry biomass, the aboveground portion and root system of each seedling were separated, rinsed with tap or distilled water, blotted dry with absorbent paper, and subsequently oven-dried at 105 ℃ for 30 min before being transferred to 70 ℃ until a constant weight was achieved. Analysis of total N, P, and K was performed using the Kjeldahl method [27], the vanadium-molybdenum yellow colorimetric procedure [28], and flame emission spectrophotometry [28], respectively. Plant nutrient content was calculated by multiplying each element’s concentration by its corresponding dry weight.

Determination of the mycorrhizal colonization rate

For harvest, four tobacco seedlings were randomly selected from each treatment. Fresh root samples from the whole root system were used to cut root segments that were roughly 1 cm long. The mycorrhizal infection rate of the roots was assessed using trypan blue staining [29]. Thirty root segments were randomly selected from each sample for section preparation and microscopic examination. Following the classification criteria for arbuscular mycorrhizal infection and arbuscle abundance [30], the graded parameters recorded during microscopy were input into the MYCOCALC.EXE software to calculate the root system’s mycorrhizal infection intensity.

Root scanning

For root measurements, four tobacco seedlings per treatment were randomly selected. Plants were carefully removed from their pots, followed by manual separation of the root system from shoot tissues. Detached roots underwent gentle rinsing under a slow stream of distilled water to eliminate soil particles, during which care was taken to collect any residual roots for complete structural preservation. A scanner (Expression 11000XL, Epson, Nagano, Japan) was used to scan the roots. The WinRHIZO software was used to calculate the root diameter, total root volume, total root length, and total root surface area.

Enzyme activity assay

Six rhizosphere substrate samples were randomly collected from each treatment. Rhizosphere substrate was isolated using a root-shaking method [31], which involved removing tobacco seedlings, shaking off loose soil, and brushing tightly adhered particles with a sterile soft-bristled brush. Enzyme activities were determined according to the protocols established by Guan [32]: urease activity was measured using sodium phenolate-sodium hypochlorite colorimetry, acid phosphatase activity was assessed via disodium phenyl phosphate colorimetry, and sucrase activity was quantified with 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid colorimetry.

Metagenomic sequencing

Six rhizosphere substrate samples were randomly selected from each treatment group. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the TGuide S96 Magnetic Bead Soil Genomic DNA Extraction Kit following the manufacturer’ s protocol. DNA yield and purity were quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a Nanodrop One Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively, while fragment integrity was verified via 0.6% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Following quality control, libraries were constructed through DNA fragmentation, end repair, adapter ligation, magnetic bead purification and size selection. Library fragment distribution was analyzed using the Qsep-400 system, and concentrations were measured with a Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer. Qualified libraries met the following criteria: concentration ≧ 1 ng/µL, a normally distributed peak profile without spurious signals, a primary peak ranging from 430 to 530 bp, and an average fragment size between 420 and 580 bp. Eligible libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end 150 bp reads to generate raw sequencing data.

Low-quality reads were filtered from raw data using Fastp. Metagenomic assembly was performed using MEGAHIT [33], retaining contigs ≧ 300 bp. Gene prediction was conducted with MetaGeneMark [34], followed by clustering with MMseq2 at 95% sequence identity and 90% coverage to generate a non-redundant gene catalog. Taxonomic assignment and functional annotation were completed by aligning sequences against the NCBI NR and KEGG databases using DIAMOND [35]. All metagenomic sequencing procedures were performed by Beijing Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd.

Data processing and analyses

R [36] was used for the analysis of microbiome data. First, the vegan [37] package was utilized to compute the Chao1, ACE, Simpson, and Shannon indices for assessing α-diversity. Subsequently, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix. Adonis analysis was then employed to quantify the differences in β-diversity among the various treatments. With regard to community composition, a circular association network of species abundance at the phylum/genus level was created using the circlize [38] package, and LEfSe analysis (LDA score >4.0) was carried out using the microeco [39] package to find branches that have differential evolution. The microbial symbiosis network was constructed using igraph [40] (with thresholds of r ≧ 0.7 and p < 0.05) and visualized using the Fruchterman - Reingold layout algorithm in Gephi software (version 9.2). We calculated the within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi) to assess the roles of individual nodes and identify keystone taxa critical for ecosystem stability. Using the Zi-Pi threshold (Fig. S3), 21 (CK), 2 (A1), 5 (A2), 34 (A3), 36 (A4), and 15 (A5) keystone genera were identified (see Supplementary Table S2 for details). These keystone genera were then correlated with differentially abundant functions (Fig. S4). Genera showing no significant correlation with these functions were excluded, resulting in 87 keystone genera retained across all treatments. These 87 genera were subsequently subjected to significance testing (see Supplementary Table S3 for details), and 60 genera showing significant differences between treatments were selected for further analysis. Additionally, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using the corrplot package [41] to investigate the correlation of differential and keystone species with plant growth-promoting functional genes. The ggplot2 [42] package was used to create all of the visuals. Statistical comparisons of treatment mean for each parameter were conducted via the analysis of variance method in DPS v7.5 software, followed by Tukey’ s test to detect significant pairwise differences, with a threshold of P < 0.05.

Results

Effects of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi on tobacco seedling growth, nutrient acquisition, and root colonization

All mycorrhizal inoculation treatments substantially enhanced both aboveground and root dry weights of tobacco seedlings relative to CK (Figs. 1d and 2e). Among these, the A4 treatment yielded the highest biomass accumulation, with aboveground and root dry weights exceeding those of CK by 135% and 150%, respectively - values significantly greater than all other treatments. Notably, while the A5 treatment increased aboveground dry weight by 62.08% compared to CK, the A1, A2, and A4 treatments achieved even more pronounced enhancements (> 84.39%). Regarding root dry weight, A5 induced a 14.20% increase over CK, whereas A1, A2, and A4 produced increases exceeding 54%. These findings demonstrate that the applied mycorrhizal fungi effectively promoted growth of both shoot and root systems in tobacco seedlings, with strain A4 exhibiting superior growth-promoting efficacy among the tested strains.

As shown in Fig. 2f, no mycorrhizal structures were observed in the root systems of the control tobacco seedlings that were not inoculated with AMF. Except for treatment A5, every treatment involving the inoculation of AMF considerably raised the rate of mycorrhizal colonization in comparison to the CK. Effects of AMF on tobacco seedling growth and root architecture

Microbial inoculation substantially enhanced tobacco plants’ agronomic performance (Table 1; Fig. 1). All inoculation treatments significantly improved seedling height, stem diameter, maximum leaf area, and leaf number as compared to the CK. Treatment A4 yielded the most pronounced improvements, with plant height, stem thickness, maximum leaf area, and leaf count increased by 144.44%, 22.15%, 59.75%, and 16.28%, respectively, relative to CK; these values also were significantly greater than the values in all other treatments. This indicates that inoculation with AMF can significantly improve the agronomic traits of tobacco seedlings, but the effects vary among different AMF species.

All inoculation treatments significantly enhanced seedling root parameters, including total root length, total surface area, total root volume, and average root diameter, compared to CK (Figs. 2 and 3). Among these treatments, A4 induced the most substantial increases, with improvements of 103.64% in total root length, 95.20% in total surface area, 149.09% in total root volume, and 24.16% in average root diameter relative to CK. These increases were significantly higher than those observed in all other treatments.

Soil enzyme activity

Different treatments (A1-A5) significantly influenced soil urease, sucrase, and acid phosphatase activities, with all treatment groups exhibiting higher enzyme activities than the CK (Fig. 4). Among these, urease activity peaked in treatment A4, showing a significant increase of 73.73% compared to CK. Treatments A1 and A3 also exhibited significant increases of 52.66% and 51.68%, respectively. Sucrase activity similarly reached its highest level in treatment A4, demonstrating a significant enhancement of 104.11% over CK, while treatments A1 and A3 increased by 81.99% and 91.66%, respectively. Acid phosphatase activity peaked under treatment A3, showing a significant increase of 116.5% compared to CK, while treatments A2 and A4 also exhibited significant increases of 95.83% and 91.11%, respectively. These results indicate that inoculation with AMF significantly enhances soil enzyme activity, though the promotional effects vary among different treatments. Overall, treatment A4 showed the most pronounced enhancement of urease and sucrase activities, while treatment A3 exhibited particularly outstanding effects on acid phosphatase activity.

Beta diversity index of rhizosphere microorganisms in tobacco seedlings

To visualize between-treatment variations in microbial community composition, PCoA was performed based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices. The first two principal components (PCo1 and PCo2) explained 50.57% and 15.85% of the total variance in community structure, respectively, accumulatively accounting for 66.42% (Fig. 5a). Community composition in tobacco seedling rhizosphere substrate

At the phylum level, microbial community composition in tobacco seedling rhizosphere substrate exhibited broad similarity across treatments despite variations in relative abundance (Fig. 5b). Dominant phyla included Proteobacteria (37.5–42.57%), Acidobacteriota (16.4–25.76%), Actinobacteriota (8.18–13.91%), Gemmatimonadota (0.96–1.70%), Verrucomicrobiota (1.04–2.28%), Chloroflexi (4.83–7.72%), and Bacteroidota (3.48–7.65%). The abundance of Bacteroidota was higher in the AMF-inoculated treatment than in the CK (3.84%). Within Chloroflexi, the abundances in treatments A1 (7.72%), A3 (6.58%), and A4 (6.81%) were significantly higher than those in A2 (5.73%), A5 (4.83%), and CK (5.87%). At the genus level(Fig. 5c), dominant taxa comprised Sphingomonas (1.52–3.21%), Paraburkholderia (0.11–7.69%), Hyphomicrobium (0.46–1.36%), Ktedonosporobacter (1.12–1.36%), and Ktedonobacter (0.88–1.38%). Among these, Sphingomonas, Hyphomicrobium, and Sphingopyxi showed significantly higher relative abundances in all AMF-inoculated treatments compared to CK. Additionally, Hephaestia, Mesorhizobium, and Parafilimonas abundances exhibited elevated abundances in A1-A4 treatments relative to A5 and CK.

Differential microbial taxa in tobacco seedlings rhizosphere substrate

The rhizosphere substrate of tobacco seedlings was screened for microbial communities that differed significantly between treatments using LEfSe analysis. Branching evolutionary diagrams revealed 70 microbial taxa exhibiting significant abundance differences across the six treatment groups. Notably, 14, 9, 7, 18, 11, and 11 taxa were specifically enriched in CK, A1, A2, A3, A4, and A5 treatments, respectively (Fig. 6). The dominant microbial groups enriched without inoculation included Trinickia, Paraburkholderia, Pandoraea, Acidobacteriota, and Actinobacteria; the main taxa enriched by inoculation with A1 were Chloroflexi, Flavisolibacter, Frateuria, and Sphingomonas; the main taxon enriched by inoculation with A2 was Hephaestia; the main microbial groups enriched by inoculation with A3 were Pseudolabrys, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Lysobacter, and Moheibacter; the main taxa enriched by inoculation with A4 were Mesorhizobium, Paracoccus, Parafilimonas, Rhodanobacter, and Sphingopyxis, while inoculum A5 mostly enriched Ferruginibacter, Hyphomicrobium, and Pseudomonas.

Network analysis of rhizosphere microbial communities in tobacco seedlings

Network analysis indicates that the number of edges reflects the degree of microbial interactions (Fig. 7). Compared with the CK (1960), all inoculated treatments (A1-A5) exhibited substantially increased edge counts (4178, 4050, 3012, 4322, and 3104, respectively), with the A4 treatment recording the highest value (Table 3). This finding demonstrates that inoculation enhances the density of the microbial interaction network, with Treatment A4 specifically generating the most complex configuration of interactions. Modularity analysis revealed significantly lower values across all treatments (0.90–0.95) than in the CK (0.97), indicating reduced separation between functional modules and greater cross-group interactions. Overall, inoculation markedly increased the complexity of the rhizosphere microbial network, which supporting enhanced stress response capabilities and nutrient cycling efficiency.

Comparison of functional gene differences under different treatments and analysis of the correlation between different species and functions

Functional annotation of metagenomic data against the KEGG database revealed that inoculation significantly enriched numerous functional genes compared to the CK (Fig. 8). These included genes involved in the nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) cycling; carbon cycling; IAA synthesis; iron-siderophore transport regulation; exopolysaccharide synthesis; iron acquisition; and antioxidant activity. Notably, individual treatments exhibited distinct enhancement profiles: Treatment A1 showed the highest increases in IAA synthesis (7.14%), and P regulation (10.94%). Treatment A2 had the highest level of hydroxypropionate-hydroxybutylate pathway (11.13%). Treatment A3 displayed the most significant increases in organic P mineralization (72.42%), P transportation (16.62%), organic N metabolism (22.32%), K solubilization (18.21%), 3-Hydroxypropionate bi-cycle (44.54%), iron acquisition (26.36%), and antioxidants (32.13%). The greatest enhancements in N transport (50.31%) and polyphosphate synthesis (67.39%) were observed in Treatment A4. Finally, Treatment A5 showed the highest levels of denitrification (10.68%), polyphosphate degradation (6.61%), and exopolysaccharide production (14.95%).

In the A1 treatment, a significant positive correlation (P < 0.01) was observed between IAA synthesis and Nostoc, Flavisolibacter, Frateuria, and Sphingomonas (Fig. 9). Within the A2 treatment, Glaciecola, Pedococcus, Phycicoccus and Hephaestia abundance showed a strong positive relationship with hydroxypropionate-hydroxybutylate levels (P < 0.01). For the A3 treatment, organic P mineralization, organic N metabolism, K dissolution, iron acquisition, and antioxidant activity were all positively correlated (P < 0.01) with the genera Hartmannibacter, Lysobacter, Moheibacter, and Pseudolabrys. In the A4 treatment, Azospirillum, Sphingobium, Mesorhizobium, Paracoccus, and Parafilimonas exhibited a significant positive correlation (P < 0.01) with both polyphosphate production and N transport. Finally, in the A5 treatment, Segetibacter, Ferruginibacter, Hyphomicrobium, and Pseudomonas displayed a substantial positive correlation with denitrification and polyphosphate degradation (P < 0.01), while Segetibacter, Ferruginibacter and Hyphomicrobium also showed a significant positive association with exopolysaccharide synthesis (P < 0.05).

Discussion

All inoculation treatments significantly enhanced mycorrhizal colonization rates and plant biomass (shoot and root dry weights) in this experiment, with the notable exception of the A. delicata treatment. Rhizophagus intraradices had the greatest capacity for colonization and growth promotion; its shoot and root dry weight rose by 120.45% and 79.32%, respectively, in comparison to the control. This finding corroborates the systematic investigation by Xiang et al. [12], who demonstrated that Rhizophagus intraradices exerted the most pronounced growth-promoting effects on tomato seedlings both when applied singly and in combinatorial regimens. This further validates the reliability of the test results. Mycorrhizal colonization rate is a key index for evaluating the symbiotic efficiency of plants with AMF [43], the level of which directly affects plant-microorganism interaction effects. Multiple studies have confirmed that higher mycorrhizal colonization rates not only reflect increased plant dependency on mycorrhizae [44, 45], but also exhibit a significant positive correlation with the growth-promoting effects of AMF [46]. In this study, Rhizophagus intraradices demonstrated the highest colonization capacity and plant growth promotion efficacy. This finding aligns with Qin et al. [47], who reported a strongly positive correlation between AMF colonization intensity and maize biomass under low-to-medium phosphorus conditions. Studies have demonstrated that Rhizophagus spp. exhibit significantly higher colonization efficiency in host root systems compared to other AMF taxa [48, 49]. Zhou et al. [50] further confirmed that typical species of this genus (e.g., R. intraradices) produce substantially higher extraradical hyphal biomass than other fungal groups. This high colonization efficiency enables Rhizophagus fungi to rapidly occupy rhizosphere niches by establishing dense hyphal networks, thereby gaining a competitive advantage in nutrient acquisition and ultimately enhancing host plant growth in summary, Rhizophagus intraradices demonstrates superior performance in improving mycorrhizal colonization rates and promoting tobacco seedling growth, attributed to its high colonization capacity and competitive hyphal network. These mechanistic insights provide a theoretical foundation for the application of AMF in crop nutrient management.

As the primary regulator of the rhizosphere microecosystem, AMF can indirectly influence the organization of the rhizosphere microbial community by controlling the hyphal metabolites and root exudates of host plants [51]. AMF inoculation markedly altered the structure of the rhizosphere microbial community of tobacco seedlings: in α-diversity analysis, the R. intraradices treatment group’s Simpson and Shannon indices were significantly higher than those of other treatments, suggesting that the microbial community diversity of the R. intraradicese treatment group was significantly increased; PCoA analysis based on β-diversity revealed a significant difference in the rhizosphere microbial community structure between the inoculated treatment group and the non-inoculated treatment group; analysis of the microbial symbiotic network also revealed that R. Intraradices considerably expanded the network’ s edge count and created a more intricate network of microbial interactions. Prior research has demonstrated that microbial taxonomic variety plays a critical role in boosting functional diversity by fostering interspecies interactions and increasing the microbiome’s complexity [52]. Moreover, rhizosphere microbial diversity has been shown in various studies to be a critical factor in plant productivity [53]. For example, wheat field research by Ren et al. [54] revealed that high-diversity soils can boost crop yields by 15%. Additionally, more synergistic interactions between various microbial groups are implied by a complex microbial network topology, which supports richer functional diversity [55]. This increased complexity could promote the synergistic connections between microorganisms, giving the community superior functional qualities [56]. This study found that the R. intraradices treatment group had the most diverse rhizosphere microbial community and the most intricate network of microbial interactions, along with the strongest growth-promoting effects. This suggests that the growth-promoting effects of R. intraradices on host plants are not only dependent on the direct nutrient absorption mechanism mediated by mycelium but also achieved through indirect regulation of the rhizosphere microbial network.

Currently, research on the processes by which various AMF types control the microbiome in the plant rhizosphere is still comparatively lacking. According to metagenomic analysis, all AMF-inoculated groups showed strong enrichment of functional genes linked to the cycling of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, as well as activation of growth-promoting metabolic pathways like IAA synthesis, iron-siderophore transport regulation, and exopolysaccharide synthesis, when compared to the uninoculated control group. In carbon metabolism, the 3-hydroxypropionic bi-cycle and the reductive citrate cycle can regulate carbon cycling processes by influencing microbial utilization of highly oxidized organic matter, thereby affecting the nutrient utilization efficiency of plant rhizosphere microorganisms [57]. Sucrase is involved in the direct metabolism of soil organic matter, catalyzing the hydrolysis of sucrose into glucose and fructose, thereby providing readily available carbon substrates for soil microorganisms [58]. Therefore, the increased sucrase activity observed in all treatments in this study provides supporting evidence for the enhancement of microbial carbon turnover and organic matter decomposition (Fig. 4b). In the nitrogen cycle, nitrogen assimilation transforms nitrogen into ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺) that plants can use; the DNRA process transforms readily lost nitrate nitrogen into more stable ammonium salts, thereby minimizing nitrogen loss, guaranteeing a steady supply of nitrogen, and establishing a base for rhizosphere microorganism growth [59, 60]. In the phosphorus cycle, insoluble organic phosphorus and inorganic phosphorus in soil can be transformed into forms of phosphorus that plants can absorb and use through organic phosphorus mineralization and inorganic phosphorus dissolution, satisfying the phosphorus needs of plants [61–63]. In addition to nutrient cycling, Iron-siderophore not only directly enhance plant iron nutrition levels by chelating soil iron elements, alleviating iron deficiency stress in plants, and ensuring the normal progression of various physiological and biochemical reactions related to iron within plants; they also inhibit the proliferation of pathogenic microorganisms through iron competition [64]. This study demonstrates that AMF inoculation enhances the nutrient retention and transformation capacity of the rhizosphere micro-ecosystem through enrichment of functional genes associated with carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium cycling, which may thereby offer multi-tiered nutritional support for plant growth. Furthermore, the activated growth-promoting metabolic pathways demonstrate synergistic interactions that collectively enhance optimal plant growth and development.

It is worth noting that this study found that the functional differences among rhizosphere microorganisms associated with the five tested fungal species in the abundance rather than the composition of functional genes (Fig. 8). In this study, the F. mosseae treatment group significantly activated the IAA synthesis pathway in rhizosphere microorganisms. At low concentrations, IAA promotes main root extension; at higher concentrations, it induces lateral and adventitious root formation; this process increases the absorption capacity of the root system by altering the shape of the roots to increase their surface area [65, 66]. For instance, Achromobacter 5B1 controls polar transport and IAA signal transduction to optimize its spatial colonization pattern in the root system, which in turn controls root growth and development [67]. Based on the study’s findings, it is hypothesized that F. mosseae treatment activates the IAA synthesis pathway, which may encourage root growth, improve their ability to absorb nutrients, and positively regulate plant growth. The D. versiformis treatment group significantly activated the functional genes related to the hydroxypropionate-hydroxybutylate (HP-HB) cycle in the rhizosphere microbiota. As the primary chemoautotrophic carbon fixation pathway in nutrient-poor environments [68, 69], the HP-HB cycle’s activation improves the efficiency of plant carbon source absorption and utilization by releasing primary metabolic products (like short-chain fatty acids and amino acids) into the rhizosphere and increasing microbial carbon fixation capacity [70]. This carbon flux stimulation potentially augments photosynthetic carbon assimilation rates, optimizes plant carbon allocation, and consequently elicits pronounced growth promotion effects. The C. etunicatum treatment group significantly activated key metabolic pathways mediated by rhizosphere microorganisms for organic phosphorus mineralization and phosphorus transport. This finding is consistent with the significant increase in acid phosphatase activity observed in the A3 treatment in our experiment (Fig. 4c). Soil phosphatase plays a crucial role in plant phosphorus nutrition by mediating the release of inorganic phosphorus from organically bound phosphorus [71]. Research indicates that the organic phosphorus mineralization pathway hydrolyzes soil organic phosphorus into plant-available inorganic phosphates through extracellular phosphatases secreted by microorganisms [63, 72]; simultaneously, AMF directly enhances plant phosphorus absorption by specifically secreting phosphatases via their hyphae [73]. This functional synergy significantly enhances the phosphorus uptake capacity of tobacco seedlings, providing critical nutrient support for their growth and development. The polyphosphate degradation and exopolysaccharide synthesis pathways were most active in the A. delicata treatment group. Polyphosphate hydrolysis facilitates the mobilization of inorganic phosphorus from microbial reserves into plant-available phosphate (Pi), thereby directly enhancing phosphorus bioavailability for the host plant [74]. Simultaneously, EPS serve as critical structural constituents of biofilms, promoting microbial adhesion and colonization, which in turn stabilize the rhizosphere microenvironment and indirectly support nutrient acquisition [75, 76]. Supporting this, Sun et al. [77] demonstrated that EPS produced by Pantoea alhagi enhance rhizosphere colonization through biofilm formation and by modulating root exudates to improve bacterial chemotaxis and biofilm development. Collectively, these results indicate that A. delicata cultivates a conducive rhizosphere microenvironment for tobacco seedling development by simultaneously augmenting microbial phosphorus solubilization and colonization efficiency. The R. intraradices treatment group significantly activated key nitrogen metabolic pathways in rhizosphere microorganisms, including nitrogen transport and anammox nitrite reduction (ANRA). The treatment group also exhibited the highest urease activity (Fig. 4a). Urease plays a crucial role by hydrolyzing urea to produce NH3/CO2, providing plants with a directly available nitrogen source [78]. According to research, mycorrhizal symbionts can increase the capacity for nitrogen transport by promoting the expression of host transporter proteins [79, 80]. For example, AMF increases the expression of genes that encode nitrate and ammonium transporters [81, 82]. This mechanism is especially crucial for tobacco’s nitrogen metabolism because nitrogen frequently acts as the main crop growth constraint, greatly affecting the morphological development of tobacco plants [83]. It can also encourage root development by controlling root morphology, which increases the area that roots can absorb nutrients from [84]. In this study, the nitrogen content in the R. intraradices treatment increased by 141.11% compared to the CK, and all root system indicators showed extremely significant improvements, indicating that R. intraradices treatment can optimize nitrogen absorption and transport, promote nitrogen accumulation, and synergistically drive root system development and maximize growth-promoting effects.

The findings of this investigation suggest that the heightened activity of the IAA biosynthesis pathway in the F. mosseae treatment is potentially linked to the proliferation of specific microbial taxa, including Nostoc, Flavisolibacter, Frateuria, and Sphingomonas. Notably, Nostoc emerged as a keystone species within this microbial network analysis. Prior research indicates that wild-type Nostoc OS-1 enhances plant growth by producing IAA via its ipdC gene, with gene knockout abolishing this function and gene complementation restoring it [85]. This evidence strongly implies that Nostoc in this context synthesizes IAA through its ipdC-dependent pathway, thereby contributing to the overall activation of the IAA biosynthetic pathway. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the genus Sphingomonas, a significant plant-promoting bacterium, increases crop development by secreting IAA [86]. According to a study by Khanet al. [87], the Sphingomonas strain LK11 has a considerable capability for IAA production, and inoculating tomato plants with it greatly enhanced their growth traits. In conclusion, F. mosseae inoculation appears to synergistically promote the proliferation of multiple rhizosphere microorganisms capable of IAA biosynthesis, which, through activation of the IAA synthesis pathway, exerts positive synergistic effects on plant development. The significant improvement in HP-HB cycle efficiency observed in the D. versiformis treatment group is primarily attributed to specific microbial groups such as Glaciecola, Pedococcus, Phycicoccus, and Hephaestia. Although no previous literature has reported a direct association between these groups and this cycle, our study revealed through correlation analysis that they exhibit a significant positive correlation with HP-HB cycle efficiency. C. etunicatum treatment markedly improves organic phosphorus mineralization and phosphorus transport, primarily through the modulation of microbial consortia including Hartmannibacter, Lysobacter, Moheibacter, and Pseudolabrys. Notably, Hartmannibacter emerges as a keystone species within this microbial assemblage. Prior research indicates that the strain E19T of Hartmannibacter diazotrophicus exhibits phosphate-solubilizing activity, thereby facilitating plant phosphorus bioavailability [88, 89]. Consequently, C. etunicatum inoculation may enhance plant phosphorus acquisition and utilization by fostering the development of functional microbial networks and elevating the prevalence of phosphorus cycling-associated functional genes. This research demonstrates that inoculation with R. intraradices markedly enhances biological nitrogen fixation and nitrogen mobilization within the rhizosphere, primarily through the stimulation of microbial consortia including Azospirillum, Sphingobium, Mesorhizobium, Paracoccus, and Parafilimonas. Notably, Azospirillum and Sphingobium are identified as keystone species. Azospirillum engages in ammonia assimilation via two pathways: one mediated by glutamate dehydrogenase (gdhA) under elevated ammonium concentrations, and another involving the cooperative activity of glutamine synthetase (glnA) and glutamate synthase (gltB/gltD) under ammonium-limited conditions [90]. Sphingobium yanoikuyae strain 41R9 has also been shown to enhance plant nitrogen uptake: isotope tracing with 15N indicates this strain significantly promotes nitrogen absorption and translocation in rapeseed roots; transcriptomic profiling suggests the mechanism involves direct upregulation of nitrogen transporter genes, thereby augmenting the host plant’ s nitrogen acquisition capacity [91]. Mesorhizobium functions as both a pivotal and a differential microbial species, capable of establishing symbiosis with host plants and inducing nodule formation. The nitrogenase enzyme expressed by rhizobial bacteroids within nodules catalyzes the reduction of atmospheric N2 into bioavailable ammonium; host plants supply photosynthetically derived carbon substrates to sustain bacterial energy metabolism, forming a classic mutualistic symbiosis [92]. In conclusion, R. intraradices inoculation significantly amplifies rhizosphere microbial activity related to nitrogen fixation and transformation, thereby enhancing nitrogen use efficiency in plants. The polyphosphate degradation and exopolysaccharide biosynthesis pathways exhibited the highest activity in the A. delicata treatment group. Polyphosphate degradation was markedly upregulated in this group, closely correlated with microbial taxa including Segetibacter, Ferruginibacter, Hyphomicrobium, and Pseudomonas. Notably, Pseudomonas is recognized for polyphosphate degradation via PPX enzymes under nutrient-limited conditions, resulting in orthophosphate release that enhances phosphorus bioavailability in the rhizosphere [93]. Overall, A. delicata may modulate polyphosphate turnover by shaping rhizospheric microbial consortia, particularly Pseudomonas.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that inoculation with different arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) effectively promotes the growth of tobacco seedlings, with R. intraradices exhibiting the most significant growth-promoting effect. This promotion is primarily achieved by reshaping the rhizosphere microbial community, enhancing microbial network complexity, and consequently improving plant nutrient uptake. Further analysis revealed that the activation of microbial metabolic pathways (including nutrient cycling, IAA synthesis, iron transporter activity, and exopolysaccharide synthesis) represents a common mechanism underlying the growth promotion by different AMF inoculants. Meanwhile, the specific functional microbial groups enriched under different AMF treatments are likely a key reason for the observed variations in their growth-promoting efficacy. In summary, AMF enhance plant growth by regulating the rhizosphere microecology, albeit with distinct regulatory patterns among different strains. It is important to note that the metagenomic sequencing results primarily reflect the potential changes in microbial function; the actual expression levels of relevant genes and the dynamics of downstream metabolites still require further integrated analysis combining transcriptomics and metabolomics data to unveil a more comprehensive regulatory mechanism.
