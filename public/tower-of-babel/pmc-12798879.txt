A Study of the Association of Cerebrospinal Fluid Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio with Severity and Mortality in Patients with Tuberculous Meningitis
Authors: Saboor Mateen, Zeba Siddiqi, Shivesh Singh, Abhishek Singh, Monis Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increasing severity of tuberculous meningitis (TBM) carries a poor prognosis. This study aimed to correlate cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR) with TBM severity and assess its ability to predict in-hospital mortality. A prospective observational study was conducted from February 2023 to February 2025, enrolling 148 confirmed cases of TBM. The severity of TBM was graded according to the British modified Medical Research Council criteria. The mean age was 38.0 ± 17.6 years, with 69 males and 79 females. Of these, 47 had grade 1, 67 had grade 2, and 34 had grade 3 severity of TBM. The CSF-NLR ratio showed no significant correlation (r = 0.113, P = 0.170) with TBM severity. Thirty-three patients died; among them, 27 had low CSF-NLR (≤0.82), while six had high CSF-NLR (>0.82). A CSF-NLR cut-off value of 0.82 had a diagnostic accuracy of 39.19% (sensitivity 81.82%, specificity 26.96%) for predicting mortality. The results negate the use of CSF-NLR as a diagnostic marker for grading TBM severity or predicting mortality.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the most prevalent infectious diseases in developing countries and may present in either pulmonary or extra-pulmonary forms. Among the latter, central nervous system (CNS) involvement, particularly tuberculous meningitis (TBM), is a major concern.[1] TBM accounts for approximately 5%–10% of extra-pulmonary TB cases and represents an inflammatory meningeal condition resulting from the hematogenous spread of Mycobacterium tuberculosis to the CNS.[23]

Diagnosis of TBM remains challenging due to its nonspecific clinical manifestations.[2] Once diagnosed, however, determining disease severity and clinical staging is crucial, as both are directly linked to patient outcomes and mortality.[45] Given the inflammatory nature of TBM, inflammatory markers play an important role in evaluating disease severity.[6] Among these, the neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has been identified as a useful biomarker of systemic inflammation.[1]

While the prognostic role of serum NLR in TBM has been established,[12] the utility of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-NLR remains largely unexplored. Wang Y et al.[5] demonstrated the diagnostic value of CSF-NLR in differentiating bacterial from viral meningitis, reporting a cutoff value of >0.68 with a sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 75% for bacterial meningitis, and noted that higher CSF-NLR levels were associated with Gram-negative bacterial infections. However, evidence regarding its role in TBM is limited.

The present study was therefore designed to evaluate the association between CSF-NLR and TBM severity and mortality.

Methods

An observational study was conducted between February 2023 and February 2025. Out of 200 clinically suspected meningitis cases, 148 patients who fulfilled the inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled. The final diagnosis was established based on a combination of radiological findings, clinical assessment, initiation of antitubercular therapy (ATT), and the Thwaites diagnostic score. The sample size was determined using a proportion model, as no prior comparable studies were available. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients aged 18–75 years of either gender, diagnosed with TBM according to the Marais criteria or confirmed by culture/polymerase chain reaction,[78] were included. The Indian Council of Medical Research guidelines were followed for diagnosis [Supplementary Figure 1], and GeneXpert testing was incorporated into the diagnostic protocol. Exclusion criteria included insufficient data, pregnancy, malignancy, a history of head trauma within the preceding 3 months, or meningitis of viral, pyogenic, or fungal etiology.

All enrolled patients were assessed descriptively at admission. Baseline demographics, presenting symptoms, comorbidities, blood investigations (complete blood counts, liver function tests, kidney function tests, serum NLR), CSF analysis (sugar, protein, total leukocyte count [TLC], adenosine deaminase [ADA], lactate, CSF-NLR), and hospital outcomes (discharge/mortality) were recorded. Radiological evaluation with magnetic resonance imaging of brain was performed for all patients. The severity of TBM was graded according to the British modified Medical Research Council (MRC) criteria, which utilize the Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) and the presence or absence of focal neurological deficits. TBM was categorized into three grades:

Grade I (Mild): GCS 15, no focal neurological deficit.

Grade II (Moderate): GCS 11–14, or GCS 15 with focal neurological deficit.

Grade III (Severe): GCS ≤10, with or without focal neurological deficit.

Patients received standard ATT according to the institutional protocol, which was based on the National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme guidelines [Supplementary Tables 1 and 2]. The primary study outcomes were mortality and the diagnostic utility of CSF-NLR.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as frequency and percentage (n, %), mean ± standard deviation, or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on the distribution, comparisons were performed using either one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Associations between NLR and TBM severity were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to determine CSF-NLR cut-off values for predicting mortality. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25.0 (IBM Corporations, Chicago, USA), and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of 200 suspected meningitis cases, 148 were diagnosed with TBM, while the remaining cases were diagnosed with fungal, viral, or bacterial meningitis. Females comprised 79 (53.38%) and males 69 (46.62%). The mean age of the study population was 38.0 ± 17.6 years.

Among comorbidities, hypertension (16.22%) and diabetes mellitus (14.86%) were the most common, followed by stroke or cerebrovascular accident (10.81%). Other less frequent conditions included coronary artery disease (4.05%), seizure disorder (3.38%), hepatitis B (2.70%), and a few rare disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, optic neuritis, and neurocysticercosis (each 0.68%). Patients presented with various symptoms, the most common being fever (81.08%), followed by altered sensorium, headache, and vomiting. The distribution of TBM severity according to MRC grading showed that 67 (45.27%) cases were classified as grade 2, 47 (31.76%) as grade 1, and 34 (22.97%) as grade 3.

In terms of the association of TBM severity with blood parameters, TLC, neutrophils, lymphocytes, serum NLR ratio, monocytes, creatinine, and urea levels showed a significant association, with values showing a significant increase with increasing grade of TBM. In CSF investigations, only ADA showed a significant increase with disease severity (P = 0.005), while sugar, protein, TLC, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, and CSF-NLR ratio showed no significant association [Table 1].

All 148 TBM cases (100%) received ATT. Of these, 114 (77.03%) were treated with first-line therapy, while 34 (22.97%) received second-line therapy. Hospital outcomes revealed that 115 (77.70%) patients were discharged, whereas 33 (22.30%) died. The median duration of hospital stay was 9 days. Serum NLR showed a significant positive correlation with TBM severity (r = 0.266, P = 0.001), whereas CSF-NLR showed no significant correlation (r = 0.113, P = 0.170) [Figure 1].

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the diagnostic performance of CSF-NLR (Area under the curve [AUC] 0.504; 95% Confidence interval [CI]: 0.396–0.612; diagnostic accuracy 39.19%) was not significant.

The study results showed that 81.82% of patients who died had a CSF-NLR ≤0.82. Among survivors, 26.96% had CSF-NLR >0.82. Overall, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and diagnostic accuracy of CSF-NLR for predicting mortality were 81.82%, 26.96%, 24.3%, 83.8%, and 39.19%, respectively [Figure 2].

Discussion

TBM severity was graded by modified MRC staging into grade 1, 2, and 3,[2] and it was chosen as an objective measure since higher clinical stages of TBM are often associated with complications such as hypoproteinemia and hydrocephalus, which confer a poorer prognosis.[8]

Our study found no significant association between CSF-NLR and TBM severity but showed a statistically significant association of serum NLR with TB severity. Likewise, previous studies negated the use of CSF-NLR in prognosticating TBM.[29]

However, similar to our study, Gu Z et al.[1] demonstrated that higher serum NLR significantly correlates with TBM severity and predicts poor prognosis.

Elevated serum NLR reflects systemic inflammation driven by neutrophilia and/or lymphopenia/monocytopenia with advancing TBM severity. This systemic inflammatory response can be explained by cytokine release (IL-6, tumor necrosis factor [TNF]) from endothelial, astroglial, and microglial cells, which further amplifies immune activation and promotes the spread of infection to the meninges.[10] Thus, serum NLR appears to be a robust marker of the underlying inflammatory burden and disease progression.

Unlike serum NLR, CSF-NLR failed to exhibit a significant correlation with TBM severity, which may be attributed to several factors. First, CSF cellular patterns vary with the disease phase: neutrophils predominate in the early stage, whereas lymphocytes gradually become dominant (30%–90%) as the disease progresses.[5] Second, differences in M. tuberculosis lineages (ancient vs. modern) may alter immune responses and cellular profiles. Third, genetic polymorphisms affecting immune pathways (e.g., Toll-like receptor [TLR]-9, Single Immunoglobulin Interleukin-1 Receptor-Related molecule [SIGIRR], Sialophorin [SPN], TLR-2) could influence host inflammatory responses and thereby CSF findings. Finally, CSF parameters are shaped by multiple additional factors, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and monocyte activity, which may overshadow the CSF-NLR.[9]

Overall, our findings negate the use of CSF-NLR as a biomarker for TBM severity or the short-term prognosis of patients. Though it carried a high sensitivity of 81%, it had very low specificity (26.96%), conferring only 39% diagnostic accuracy for predicting mortality in TBM. The present study must be interpreted in light of the limitations of sampling bias, owing to it being a single-center study. Further multi-centric studies on larger population sizes are recommended.
