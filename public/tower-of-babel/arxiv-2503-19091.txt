High Probability Complexity Bounds of Trust-Region Stochastic Sequential Quadratic Programming with Heavy-Tailed Noise
Yuchen Fang, Javad Lavaei, Sen Na

Abstract
In this paper, we consider nonlinear optimization problems with a stochastic objective and deterministic equality constraints. We propose a Trust-Region Stochastic Sequential Quadratic Programming (TR-SSQP) method and establish its high-probability iteration complexity bounds for identifying first- and second-order ϵ \epsilon -stationary points. In our algorithm, we assume that exact objective values, gradients, and Hessians are not directly accessible but can be estimated via zeroth-, first-, and second-order probabilistic oracles. Compared to existing complexity studies of SSQP methods that rely on a zeroth-order oracle with sub-exponential tail noise (i.e., light-tailed) and focus mostly on first-order stationarity, our analysis accommodates biased (also referred to as irreducible in the literature) and heavy-tailed noise in the zeroth-order oracle, and significantly extends the analysis to second-order stationarity. We show that under heavy-tailed noise conditions, our SSQP method achieves the same high-probability first-order iteration complexity bounds as in the light-tailed noise setting, while further exhibiting promising second-order iteration complexity bounds. Specifically, the method identifies a first-order ϵ \epsilon -stationary point in 𝒪 ⁡ ( ϵ − 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2}) iterations and a second-order ϵ \epsilon -stationary point in 𝒪 ⁡ ( ϵ − 3 ) \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3}) iterations with high probability, provided that ϵ \epsilon is lower bounded by a constant determined by the bias magnitude (i.e., the irreducible noise) in the estimation. We validate our theoretical findings and evaluate practical performance of our method on CUTEst benchmark test set.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider stochastic optimization problems with deterministic equality constraints of the following form:

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;f({\bm{x}}),\quad\;\;\;\text{s.t.}\quad c({\bm{x}})={\bm{0}},

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the stochastic objective and
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
are deterministic equality constraints. For Problem (
1
), we assume that the exact objective value
f({\bm{x}})
, together with its gradient
\nabla f({\bm{x}})
and Hessian
\nabla^{2}f({\bm{x}})
, cannot be evaluated accurately but can be estimated via stochastic probabilistic oracles. For the sake of generality, we do not specify the underlying true sampling distribution of
f
, since the oracles may have different sampling distributions, leading to intrinsically biased estimates. Constrained stochastic optimization problems of form (
1
) appear ubiquitously in scientific and engineering areas, such as optimal control
(
Betts, 2010
)
, portfolio optimization
(
Çakmak and Özekici, 2005
)
, multi-stage optimization
(
Shapiro et al., 2021
)
, supply chain network design
(
Santoso et al., 2005
)
, and statistical machine learning
(
Cuomo et al., 2022
)
.

Deterministic constrained optimization has been extensively studied for decades. While numerous methods have been developed under favorable problem settings, Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) has proven to be robust and effective, especially for highly nonlinear problems
(
Bertsekas, 1982
;
Boggs and Tolle, 1995
;
Nocedal and Wright, 2006
)
. Recent studies on optimization problems where the objective function and constraints are evaluated subject to bounded noise further highlight the advantages of SQP methods
(
Sun and Nocedal, 2023
;
Lou et al., 2024
;
Oztoprak et al., 2023
;
Sun and Nocedal, 2024
)
. Inspired by the success of SQP, a series of Stochastic SQP (SSQP) methods has been proposed to solve Problem (
1
), where the exact computations of objective quantities are replaced by the corresponding estimates obtained through sampling. The quality of the estimates is assumed to satisfy specific conditions that depend on different sampling frameworks.

The first widely used framework is the
fully stochastic
setup, where at each iteration a single sample or a fixed batch of samples is drawn to estimate the objective gradient, and a prespecified sequence is utilized to assist the stepsize selection. Gradient estimates are typically assumed to be unbiased, with variance either bounded or subject to a growth condition.
Under this setup,
Berahas et al. (2021)
introduced the first line-search SSQP method, while
Fang et al. (2024a)
introduced the first trust-region SSQP method. Building on these designs, subsequent extensions in algorithm design and analysis have been reported
(
Berahas et al., 2024
;
Berahas et al., 2023
;
Curtis et al., 2024b
;
Curtis et al., 2024a
;
Beiser et al., 2023
;
Bollapragada et al., 2023
;
Berahas et al., 2025
;
Curtis et al., 2025a
;
Curtis et al., 2025b
;
Curtis et al., 2026
;
Kuang et al., 2025
, see, e.g.,)
.
The non-asymptotic iteration complexity has also been investigated under this setup. Based on the algorithm in
Berahas et al. (2021)
,
Curtis et al. (2023)
established a worst-case iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-4})
for first-order
\epsilon
-stationarity with high probability, assuming that the trial step is solved exactly in each iteration and the input prespecified stepsize-related sequence is constant. Meanwhile,
Na and Mahoney (2025)
established the local convergence guarantee of the SSQP method with decaying stepsize, demonstrating the same
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-4})
iteration complexity for first-order
\epsilon
-stationarity while allowing the trial step to be solved inexactly in each iteration. In addition,
Lu et al. (2026)
measured the
\epsilon
-stationarity by expected optimality residual and deterministic feasibility residual (i.e., constraint violation), and established
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\epsilon^{-3})
sample complexity and
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\epsilon^{-4})
iteration complexity for first-order stationarity, where
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\cdot)
hides the logarithmic term.

In contrast to the fully stochastic setup, methods in the
random model
setup do not require unbiased gradient estimates, and the stepsizes are also selected adaptively. This framework leverages zeroth-, first-, and second-order probabilistic oracles to estimate objective values, gradients, and Hessians, respectively, ensuring that the estimates satisfy proper, adaptive accuracy conditions with a high but fixed probability. The random model setup has a relatively long history in the literature on unconstrained problems, starting from the STORM method and its subsequent designs
(
Chen et al., 2017
;
Ghanbari and Scheinberg, 2017
;
Blanchet et al., 2019
;
Jin et al., 2024
)
. Under this setup,
Na et al. (2022a)
;
Fang et al. (2024b)
recently introduced some of the early SSQP methods for constrained problems. The studies have been further generalized in
Na et al. (2023)
;
Berahas et al. (2022)
;
Fang et al. (2026b)
;
Qiu and Kungurtsev (2023)
to accommodate inequality constraints, adaptive sampling mechanisms, and robust designs. However, the above studies in the random model setup share two key limitations: (1) they assume the estimation noise vanishes with fixed probability, and (2) they lack a non-asymptotic, high-probability iteration complexity analysis.

Recently,
Berahas et al. (2025)
addressed the two limitations in constrained optimization by analyzing a line-search (also referred to as step-search) method that allows for an biased, irreducible noise in the estimation. The authors conducted a non-asymptotic complexity analysis and established an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
for first-order
\epsilon
-stationarity with high probability, provided that
\epsilon
exceeds a threshold determined by the irreducible noise level. However, their zeroth-order oracle assumes the noise follows a sub-exponential distribution, hence excluding all heavy-tailed noise. Moreover, their algorithm does not utilize second-order Hessian information, so that theoretical analysis offered in that work does not cover second-order convergence property. Identifying only first-order stationary points can be sometimes unsatisfactory in nonlinear optimization, as such points may correspond to saddle points or even local maxima, leading to highly sensitive solutions
(
Dauphin et al., 2014
;
Choromanska et al., 2015
;
Jain et al., 2017
)
. To the best of our knowledge, in the random model setup, high-probability iteration complexity for second-order stationarity has only been explored in the context of unconstrained optimization. In this setting,
Cao et al. (2023)
performed a non-asymptotic analysis for a trust-region method, demonstrating an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
for first-order
\epsilon
-stationarity and
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3})
for second-order
\epsilon
-stationarity. However, their zeroth-order probabilistic oracle still requires the noise to follow a sub-exponential distribution.

Assuming the noise in the zeroth-order oracle following a sub-exponential distribution leads to a significant gap between non-asymptotic and asymptotic analyses, as such an oracle condition is not required in asymptotic analysis
(
Na et al., 2022a
;
Na et al., 2023
;
Fang et al., 2024b
)
. The sub-exponential noise condition can be restrictive, as it implies the existence of infinite-order moments, thereby excluding noise from any heavy-tailed distribution where only a finite number of moments are bounded. More importantly, there is no clear justification for the discrepancy between the noise conditions in the zeroth-order oracle and those in the first- and second-order oracles; the latter oracles do allow for heavy-tailed noise (e.g., only a finite second moment). This discrepancy highlights the desire to relax light-tailed noise in the zeroth-order oracle to heavy-tailed noise when analyzing the complexity of the methods.

In this paper, we make progress toward that goal. We introduce a trust-region SSQP method for constrained optimization in the random model setup that accommodates
irreducible and heavy-tailed noise
, and establish its high-probability iteration complexity bounds for achieving both
first- and second-order
\epsilon
-stationarities
. Our key contributions are summarized as follows.

(a)

Compared to
Na et al. (2022a)
;
Na et al. (2023)
;
Fang et al. (2024b)
, we allow irreducible noise in the zeroth-, first-, and second-order oracles, while their framework requires the noise to diminish with fixed probability. In addition, we extend the second-order asymptotic convergence guarantee from
Fang et al. (2024b)
to establish the first second-order non-asymptotic guarantee for constrained problems.

(b)

Compared to the step-search SSQP method in
Berahas et al. (2025)
, we study a trust-region SSQP method and provide a complexity analysis for second-order stationarity. As a classical complement to the line-search scheme, the trust-region scheme computes the search direction and selects the stepsize jointly. One significant difference from the line-search scheme is that the trust-region SQP subproblems remain well-defined without any Hessian regularizations, which are often necessary in the line-search scheme to ensure the search direction to be a descent direction.

(c)

Compared to
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Berahas et al. (2025)
for unconstrained or constrained first-order complexity analysis, we refine the definition of the stopping time (Definition
4.6
) of the iteration by using the deterministic KKT residual for first-order stationarity, and the maximum of deterministic KKT residual and deterministic negative curvature of the reduced Hessian for second-order stationarity.
Cao et al. (2023)
adopted a stopping time definition that depends on the Lipschitz constant and the irreducible noise, while
Berahas et al. (2025)
defined the stopping time based on the local model reduction of the merit function. Although different stopping time definitions lead to the same complexity bounds, our definition seems more natural while necessitating additional efforts in the analysis. See the discussion after Definition
4.6
for details.

(d)

Compared to the complexity analysis in
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Berahas et al. (2025)
, we significantly relax the parametric sub-exponential distribution assumption on the zeroth-order oracle noise. Specifically, instead of requiring infinite-order moments, we assume the noise has a bounded
1+\delta
moment for any (small)
\delta>0
. The key technical tools we leverage are the
Burkholder-type inequality

(
Burkholder, 1973
;
Chen and Sung, 2020
)
and the
martingale Fuk-Nagaev inequality
(Lemma
F.2
)
(
Fuk, 1973
;
Nagaev, 1979
;
Fan et al., 2017
)
, which help us quantify the tail behavior of the accumulated oracle noise. For any
\delta>0
, our complexity results suggest that the method can find a (first-order or second-order) stationary point within finite iterations almost surely. This matches the result in
Berahas et al. (2025)
, where infinite-order moments are imposed. We highlight that our relaxation of the noise condition is achieved purely through sharper analysis and is independent of various line-search or trust-region designs, suggesting that the results in the aforementioned literature should also hold under heavy-tailed noise condition utilizing our analytical techniques.
Our technique has been used in the follow-up work
(
Scheinberg and Xie, 2025
)
, which unifies the complexity analysis of unconstrained line-search and trust-region methods for achieving first-order stationarity, under a zeroth-order oracle noise with at least bounded second moments (i.e.,
\delta\geq 1
).

Under irreducible and heavy-tailed oracle noise conditions, we establish
first- and second-order
high-probability iteration complexity bounds for our trust-region SSQP method.
In particular, we establish that the method achieves a first-order
\epsilon
-stationary point in
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
iterations and a second-order
\epsilon
-stationary point in
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3})
iterations with high probability, provided that
\epsilon
is chosen above a threshold determined by the irreducible noise levels. We summarize the results, along with the dependence of
\epsilon
on irreducible noises in Table
1
. These results align with existing arguments for irreducible and light-tailed noise. Specifically, our results corroborate the first-order high-probability complexity bound established by
Berahas et al. (2025)
for a step-search SSQP method in constrained stochastic optimization, as well as the first- and second-order high-probability complexity bounds derived by
Cao et al. (2023)
for a trust-region method in unconstrained stochastic optimization. We implement our method on problems from the CUTEst test set. The actual performance validates our theoretical analysis.

A Trust-Region SSQP Method

Let
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\lambda}})=f({\bm{x}})+{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}c({\bm{x}})
be the Lagrangian function of Problem (
1
), where
{\bm{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
represents the Lagrangian multipliers. For any
\epsilon>0
, we call
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{\lambda}}^{*})
a first-order
\epsilon
-stationary point of (
1
) if

\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{\lambda}}^{*})\|=\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{\bm{x}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{\lambda}}^{*})\\
\nabla_{{\bm{\lambda}}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{\lambda}}^{*})\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{*})+G({\bm{x}}^{*})^{T}{\bm{\lambda}}^{*}\\
c({\bm{x}}^{*})\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon.

(2)

We call
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{\lambda}}^{*})
a second-order
\epsilon
-stationary point if, in addition to (
2
),

\tau({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{\lambda}}^{*})\geq-\epsilon,

where
\tau({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{\lambda}}^{*})
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of the reduced Lagrangian Hessian
Z({\bm{x}}^{*})^{T}\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{\lambda}}^{*})Z({\bm{x}}^{*})
. Here, we define
\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\lambda}})=\nabla^{2}f({\bm{x}})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bm{\lambda}}^{i}\nabla^{2}c^{i}({\bm{x}})
as the Lagrangian Hessian with respect to the primal variable
{\bm{x}}
.
We denote the optimality residual by
\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\lambda}})\|
, the feasibility residual by
\|\nabla_{\bm{\lambda}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\lambda}})\|
(i.e.,
\|c({\bm{x}})\|
), and the KKT residual by
\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\lambda}})\|
. Given the
k
-th iterate
({\bm{x}}_{k},{\bm{\lambda}}_{k})
, we denote
g_{k}=\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k})
,
\nabla^{2}f_{k}=\nabla^{2}f({\bm{x}}_{k})
, and
{\bar{g}}_{k}
,
\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}
as their estimates, which are constructed via probabilistic oracles in Section
3
. Similarly, we denote
c_{k},G_{k},\{\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. We define the
estimated
Lagrangian gradient as
\bar{\nabla}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}=(\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{k},c_{k})
with
\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}={\bar{g}}_{k}+G_{k}^{T}{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}
, and the
estimated
Lagrangian Hessian (with respect to
{\bm{x}}
) as
\bar{\nabla}^{2}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}=\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}^{i}\nabla^{2}c^{i}_{k}
. Throughout the paper,
\bar{H}_{k}
is used to denote the approximation of the Lagrangian Hessian
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{k}
. When only first-order stationarity is desired,
\bar{H}_{k}
can be constructed using various methods, such as setting it to the identity matrix, employing the SR1 update, defining it as
\bar{H}_{k}\coloneqq\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{k}
, or taking the average of
\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{k}
. However, to ensure second-order stationarity, we specifically set
\bar{H}_{k}
to
\bar{H}_{k}\coloneqq\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{k}
, since we will leverage the curvature information of

to explore its eigen directions. Further details on this construction are in Sections
2.2
and
5
.

In the remainder of this section, we first introduce step computation, a key component of our algorithm, and then describe the algorithm.

2.1
Step computation

Given the iterate
{\bm{x}}_{k}
and the trust-region radius
\Delta_{k}
in the
k
-th iteration, we formulate the trust-region SSQP subproblem to solve for the trial step
\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}
by using a quadratic approximation of the objective and a linear approximation of the constraints in (
1
), with an additional trust-region constraint:

\displaystyle\min_{\Delta{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}\Delta{\bm{x}}^{T}{\bar{H}}_{k}\Delta{\bm{x}}+{\bar{g}}_{k}^{T}\Delta{\bm{x}},\quad\quad\text{s.t.}\quad c_{k}+G_{k}\Delta{\bm{x}}={\bm{0}},\;\;\;\|\Delta{\bm{x}}\|\leq\Delta_{k}.

(3)

Due to the presence of the trust-region constraint, we need to deal with a potential infeasibility issue:

\{\Delta{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:c_{k}+G_{k}\Delta{\bm{x}}={\bm{0}}\}\cap\{\Delta{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\Delta{\bm{x}}\|\leq\Delta_{k}\}=\emptyset.

To address this issue, we follow the existing literature
(
Vardi, 1985
;
Byrd et al., 1987
;
Omojokun, 1989
;
Fang et al., 2024a
;
Fang et al., 2024b
)
by relaxing the linear constraint in (
3
) via the trial step decomposition. In particular, we decompose the trial step
\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}
into two orthogonal components,
\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}=\bm{w}_{k}+\bm{t}_{k}
, which are computed separately. The normal step
\bm{w}_{k}\in\text{im}(G_{k}^{T})
accounts for feasibility, while the tangential step
\bm{t}_{k}\in\ker(G_{k})
accounts for optimality. To satisfy the trust-region constraint, we further decompose the trust-region radius to control the lengths of the normal and tangential steps, respectively. Depending on whether we aim to achieve first- or second-order convergence, the trial step
\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}
is computed using either a gradient step or an eigen step. The gradient step reduces the KKT residual to achieve first-order convergence, whereas the eigen step explores the negative curvature of the reduced Lagrangian Hessian to achieve second-order convergence. However, when searching for second-order stationary points, the Maratos effect
(
Conn et al., 2000
)
can cause iterates to stagnate at saddle points. To mitigate this special issue in constrained optimization, we compute a second-order correction (SOC) step and append it to the trial step whenever the Maratos effect is detected.

2.1.1
Gradient steps

When computing the gradient step, we decompose the trust-region radius as

\breve{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{\|c_{k}^{RS}\|}{\|\bar{\nabla}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{RS}\|}\cdot\Delta_{k}\quad\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\quad\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{\|\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{RS}\|}{\|\bar{\nabla}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{RS}\|}\cdot\Delta_{k}

to control the lengths of
\bm{w}_{k}
and
\bm{t}_{k}
, respectively, and have
\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}=\bm{w}_{k}+\bm{t}_{k}
. Here,
c^{RS}_{k}\coloneqq c_{k}/\|G_{k}\|
,
\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{RS}\coloneqq\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}/\|{\bar{H}}_{k}\|
,
\bar{\nabla}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{RS}\coloneqq(\bar{\nabla}_{{\bm{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{RS},c^{RS}_{k})
denote
rescaled
feasibility, optimality, and KKT residual vectors, respectively. Decomposing the radius based on the rescaled residuals preserves the scale invariance property, so that if the objective and/or constraints are scaled by a (positive) scalar, the radius decomposition and further step computation remain unchanged. Such a desirable property cannot be achieved if using original residuals
\|c_{k}\|
and
\|{\bar{\nabla}}_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{L}_{k}\|
in the above radius decomposition.

Suppose
G_{k}
has full row rank (as assumed in Assumption
4.1
), we define

{\bm{v}}_{k}\coloneqq-G_{k}^{T}[G_{k}G_{k}^{T}]^{-1}c_{k}.

When
G_{k}
is rank deficient, the normal step is defined either as the least-squares solution
{\bm{v}}_{k}=\arg\min_{{\bm{v}}\in\text{im}(G_{k}^{T})}\|c_{k}+G_{k}\bm{v}\|^{2}
or as the trust-region solution
{\bm{v}}_{k}=\arg\min_{{\bm{v}}\in\text{im}(G_{k}^{T})}\|c_{k}+G_{k}\bm{v}\|^{2}
s.t.
\|\bm{v}\|\leq w\|G_{k}^{T}c_{k}\|
for some
w>0
. However, in this rank-deficient case, the method may not aim to converge to a stationarity point but rather to minimize the infeasibility
\|c({\bm{x}})\|^{2}
. We point to
Berahas et al. (2024)
for further details, while restrict our attention here to the full-row-rank case.
Without the trust-region constraint,
c_{k}+G_{k}\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}={\bm{0}}
would imply
\bm{w}_{k}={\bm{v}}_{k}
since
G_{k}\bm{t}_{k}={\bm{0}}
. However, the trust-region constraint enforces us to set the normal step
\bm{w}_{k}
by shrinking
{\bm{v}}_{k}
as

\bm{w}_{k}={\bar{\gamma}}_{k}{\bm{v}}_{k}\quad\text{with}\quad{\bar{\gamma}}_{k}\coloneqq\min\{\breve{\Delta}_{k}/\|{\bm{v}}_{k}\|,\;1\}.

(4)

The tangential step
\bm{t}_{k}
is solved from

{\min_{\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;m(\bm{t})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\bm{t}^{T}{\bar{H}}_{k}\bm{t}+({\bar{g}}_{k}+{\bar{H}}_{k}\bm{w}_{k})^{T}\bm{t},\quad\text{s.t.}\quad G_{k}\bm{t}={\bm{0}},\quad\|\bm{t}\|\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}.}

(5)

Since any feasible solution
\bm{t}_{k}
of (
5
) can be expressed as
\bm{t}_{k}=Z_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k}
, where
Z_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d-m)}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\ker(G_{k})
and
{\bm{u}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-m}
.
We can rewrite (
5
) as

\min_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-m}}\quad\widetilde{m}({\bm{u}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}{\bm{u}}^{T}Z_{k}^{T}{\bar{H}}_{k}Z_{k}{\bm{u}}+({\bar{g}}_{k}+{\bar{H}}_{k}\bm{w}_{k})^{T}Z_{k}{\bm{u}},\quad\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|{\bm{u}}\|\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{k},

(6)

which is a trust-region problem for unconstrained optimization. For the trust-region constraint in (
6
) we use the observation that
\|\bm{t}\|^{2}={\bm{u}}^{T}Z_{k}^{T}Z_{k}{\bm{u}}=\|{\bm{u}}\|^{2}
.

We only require
\bm{t}_{k}
to achieve a fixed fraction
\kappa_{fcd}\in(0,1]
of the Cauchy reduction. In particular, let
\bm{t}_{k}^{C}=Z_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k}^{C}
denote the Cauchy point
(see
Nocedal and Wright, 2006
, (4.11) for the formula)
, then we require
(see
Nocedal and Wright, 2006

:

2.1.2
Eigen steps

We define
{\bar{\tau}}_{k}
to be the smallest eigenvalue of
Z_{k}^{T}{\bar{H}}_{k}Z_{k}
and
{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{+}\coloneqq|\min\{{\bar{\tau}}_{k},0\}|
. Parallel to the gradient step
\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}=\bm{w}_{k}+\bm{t}_{k}

\breve{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{\|c_{k}^{RS}\|}{\|(c_{k}^{RS},{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{RS+})\|}\cdot\Delta_{k}\quad\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\quad\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{RS+}}{\|(c_{k}^{RS},{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{RS+})\|}\cdot\Delta_{k},

where
{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{RS+}\coloneqq{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{+}/\|{\bar{H}}_{k}\|
is the rescaled negative curvature and
(c_{k}^{RS},{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{RS+})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
denotes the vertical concatenation of
c_{k}^{RS}
and
{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{RS+}
. The tangential step is still
\bm{t}_{k}=Z_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k}
with
{\bm{u}}_{k}
(inexactly) solved from (

({\bar{g}}_{k}+{\bar{H}}_{k}\bm{w}_{k})^{T}Z_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k}\leq 0,\quad\quad\|{\bm{u}}_{k}\|\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{k},\quad\quad{\bm{u}}_{k}^{T}Z_{k}^{T}{\bar{H}}_{k}Z_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k}\leq-\kappa_{fcd}\cdot\bar{\tau}_{k}^{+}\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}.

(8)

When the above conditions are satisfied,
\bm{t}_{k}=Z_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k}
achieves the curvature reduction:

{m(\bm{t}_{k})}-m({\bm{0}})=\widetilde{m}({\bm{u}}_{k})-\widetilde{m}({\bm{0}})\leq-\frac{\kappa_{fcd}}{2}\bar{\tau}_{k}^{+}\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}<0.

(9)

where we use

Conditions in (

(
Conn et al., 2000

and can be satisfied by computing
{\bm{u}}_{k}
in different approaches. For example, we can compute
\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{k}

corresponding to the eigenvalue
{\bar{\tau}}_{k}
, and then rescale it as
\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{k}^{RS}\coloneqq\pm\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{k}\cdot\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}/\|\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{k}\|

\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{k}^{RS}

2.1.3
Second-order correction steps

It is observed by
Byrd et al. (1987)
that when
{\bm{x}}_{k}
is close to a saddle point, the (gradient or eigen) step
\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}
may increase
f({\bm{x}})
and
\|c({\bm{x}})\|
simultaneously. Since no reduction is made by
\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}
, the current iterate is not updated. Even worse, this issue cannot be resolved by reducing the stepsize, implying that the iterate is trapped at the saddle point. This phenomenon, known as the
(second-order) Maratos effect
, arises from the inaccurate linear approximation of constraints.
To address this, the second-order correction (SOC) step
{\bm{d}}_{k}
is computed to better capture the curvature of the constraints:

{\bm{d}}_{k}=-G_{k}^{T}[G_{k}G_{k}^{T}]^{-1}\left\{c({\bm{x}}_{k}+\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k})-c_{k}-G_{k}\Delta{\bm{x}}_{k}\right\}.

(10)

We will not compute SOC steps in every iteration, but only when the Maratos effect is likely to occur, see Section
2.2
Case 2 below for details.

Remark 2.1
.

In this remark, we briefly discuss the per-iteration computational cost of trust-region methods. We first consider the gradient step for achieving first-order stationarity. Without the trust-region constraint, the subproblem in line-search methods is equivalent to solving a linear system of size
d+m
. For a standard linear system solver such as conjugate gradient (CG), the computational cost arises from matrix-vector products, and is
\mathcal{O}((d+m)^{2})
flops per CG iteration with at most
d+m
CG iterations in total (it often terminates earlier).
As a comparison, trust-region methods involve computing a normal step (
4
) and a tangential step (
5
), with the latter dominating the computational cost. In particular, the tangential step requires solving a trust-region-constrained subproblem (
5
), which can be handled by a projected CG solver (as used in Knitro).
As preparation for the projected CG method, we form
G_{k}G_{k}^{T}
(
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}d)
) and compute its Cholesky factorization (
\mathcal{O}(m^{3})
), leading to a
one-time
cost of
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}d+m^{3})
. Each projected CG iteration then involves matrix-vector products (
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
) and projections onto
\ker(G_{k})
via triangular solves (
\mathcal{O}(dm+m^{2})
), leading to
\mathcal{O}((d+m)^{2})
flops per projected CG iteration. Thus, the total cost of solving the tangential step is

\mathcal{O}\big(m^{2}d+m^{3}+(d+m)^{2}\times\text{\# of projected CG iterations}\big).

Note that the number of projected CG iterations is typically much smaller than the worst-case bound of
d-m
(which corresponds to solving the subproblem exactly). In our setting, we only require achieving a fraction
\kappa_{fcd}\in(0,1]
of the Cauchy reduction and never solve the tangential subproblem exactly.

We now turn to the eigen step for achieving second-order stationarity. We focus on trust-region methods, as line-search methods with second-order convergence are less explored in the literature. The eigen step has to additionally compute the smallest eigenvalue of the reduced Lagrangian Hessian, and the normal and tangential steps then follow the same computation as discussed above. The smallest eigenvalue (and corresponding eigenvector) can be derived using a matrix-free Lanczos procedure. Similar to projected CG method, it requires forming
G_{k}G_{k}^{T}
(
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}d)
), performing a Cholesky factorization (
\mathcal{O}(m^{3})
), and each Lanczos iteration costs
\mathcal{O}((d+m)^{2})
flops in the general dense setting. Again, the total number of Lanczos iterations depends on the spectral gap and the desired accuracy, and is often smaller than
d-m
in practice.

Probabilistic Oracles with Irreducible and Heavy-Tailed Noise

In this section, we introduce probabilistic oracles that accommodate irreducible and heavy-tailed noise. In each iteration, the zeroth-, first-, and second-order probabilistic oracles generate estimates of the objective value
\bar{f}({\bm{x}},\xi)
, the objective gradient
\bar{g}({\bm{x}},\xi)
, and the objective Hessian
\bar{\nabla}^{2}f({\bm{x}},\xi)
, respectively, where
\xi
denotes a random variable defined on some probability space. These estimates are required to satisfy certain adaptive accuracy conditions with a high but fixed probability. Our oracle conditions do not prespecify a specific method for generating estimates and allow for estimates to have irreducible noise (
\epsilon_{f},\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{h}
below), leading to biased estimates. This is a significant extension of
Bandeira et al. (2014)
;
Blanchet et al. (2019)
;
Fang et al. (2024b)
;
Na et al. (2022a)
;
Na et al. (2023)
, which required the estimation noise to diminish with a fixed probability.

Furthermore, we remove all parametric assumptions about the estimation noise. In particular,
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Berahas et al. (2025)
assumed that the noise in the zeroth-order oracle follows a sub-exponential distribution in each iteration (see (
21
) below). Such a condition essentially assumes the existence of infinite-order moments, thereby excluding all distributions with heavy tails and limiting the scope of problems their analysis can cover. Additionally, the light-tailed noise condition in the zeroth-order oracle contrasts with the noise conditions in the first- and second-order oracles, which indeed allow for heavy-tailed noise. To resolve this discrepancy, as shown in (
20
), we relax the noise condition to require only the existence of a
(1+\delta)
-order moment for any small
\delta>0
, thereby covering noise from a broad class of heavy-tailed distributions.

Before introducing oracle conditions, we first formalize the randomness of the method. Define
{\mathcal{F}}_{-1}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\cdots
as a filtration of
\sigma
-algebras, where
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}=\sigma(\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k})
,
\forall k\geq 0
contains all the randomness before performing the
k
-th iteration. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{k-0.5}=\sigma\big(\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}

\cup\{{\bar{g}}_{k},{\bar{\nabla}}^{2}f_{k}\}\big)

.

, we have

\max\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|e_{k}-\mathbb{E}\left[e_{k}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right]\right|^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[\left|e_{s_{k}}-\mathbb{E}\left[e_{s_{k}}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1/2}\right]\right|^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1/2}\right]\right\}\leq\Upsilon_{f}.

(20)

(iii.2) Sub-exponential tail condition.
For some constants
v,b>0
,
e_{k}
and
e_{s_{k}}
satisfy

\max\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(e_{k}-\mathbb{E}[e_{k}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}])}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(e_{s_{k}}-\mathbb{E}[e_{s_{k}}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1/2}])}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1/2}\right]\right\}\\
\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}v^{2}}{2}\right),\quad\forall\lambda\in[0,\frac{1}{b}].

(21)

When
(iii.1) heavy-tailed condition
is satisfied, we say
e_{k},e_{s_{k}}
are generated via the
probabilistic heavy-tailed zeroth-order oracle
, while when
(iii.2) sub-exponential condition
is satisfied, we say
e_{k},e_{s_{k}}
are generated via the
probabilistic sub-exponential zeroth-order oracle
.

Remark 3.4
.

We further discuss the probabilistic zeroth-order oracle in this remark.

\bullet
The conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
in (
18
) and (
19
) are standard in the literature
(
Berahas et al., 2025
;
Cao et al., 2023
)
, which, however, do not provide any tail information regarding the noise. Thus, existing literature often additionally imposed
(iii.2)
for the considered zeroth-order oracle.

\bullet
For the sub-exponential oracle, if
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}<\epsilon_{f}
, then condition (
18
) can be implied by (
21
), provided
v,b
are sufficiently small (i.e., the probability mass of
e_{k},e_{s_{k}}
is almost concentrated around their means). See Remark
4.25
for a rigorous discussion. However, (
21
) should only be used to capture the tail behavior of
e_{k},e_{s_{k}}
, and requiring infinitesimally small scalars of
v,b
is overly stringent for the oracle (though the tail behavior remains sub-exponential). Thus, one may not need to consider the interconnections among conditions (
18
)–(
21
).

\bullet

The heavy-tailed condition in
(iii.1)
significantly relaxes the sub-exponential condition in
(iii.2)
by reducing the requirement from the existence of infinite-order moments to a finite
1+\delta
moment. Leveraging the
Burkholder-type inequality

(
Burkholder, 1973
;
Chen and Sung, 2020
)
and the
martingale Fuk-Nagaev inequality

(
Fuk, 1973
;
Nagaev, 1979
;
Fan et al., 2017
)
, we establish high-probability complexity bounds for any
\delta>0
.
By Theorem
4.17
and its subsequent discussion, we know our high-probability complexity bounds directly imply that, for any
\delta>0
, the algorithm finds a (first- or second-order)
\epsilon
-stationary point in a finite number of iterations
almost surely
. If we suppress the irreducible noise by setting
\epsilon_{h}=\epsilon_{g}=\epsilon_{f}=0
, this result further implies a liminf-type almost-sure convergence; that is, for any run of the method, there exists a subsequence of iterates for which the (first- or second-order) stationarity residuals vanish (cf. Definition
4.6
).
We note that although liminf-type almost-sure convergence for second-order stationarity with heavy-tailed oracles is common in the literature
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
;
Fang et al., 2024b
)
, the above asymptotic result implied from non-asymptotic analysis has a gap compared to the convergence results directly from asymptotic analyses in
(
Chen et al., 2017
, Theorem 4.18)
and
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
, Theorem 4)
, where the authors established lim-type convergence to first-order stationarity
without imposing any moment conditions
on the zeroth-order oracle.
That being said, those methods do not incorporate irreducible noise in all orders of the oracles, and have a substantially different convergence analysis based on an ad-hoc random function
(e.g.,
Blanchet et al., 2019
, (20))
.
Overall, it remains an open question whether one can strengthen our high-probability complexity bounds by completely removing the moment conditions in
(ii)
and
(iii.1)
.

\bullet

In the zeroth-order oracle, we follow the existing literature and impose conditions on the absolute objective value estimation errors
e_{k}
and
e_{s_{k}}
separately. However, we note that imposing accuracy conditions on the error difference
e_{s_{k}}-e_{k}
is sufficient for the analysis. That is, we can redefine
e_{k}\coloneqq|({\bar{f}}_{s_{k}}-{\bar{f}}_{k})-(f_{s_{k}}-f_{k})|
and impose oracle conditions on the newly defined
e_{k}
.

Remark 3.5
.

We introduce some existing iteration complexity results under heavy-tailed noise with bounded
1+\delta
moment. In fact, the bounded
(1+\delta)
-moment condition has been extensively studied in the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) literature for unconstrained nonconvex optimization, although the moment condition in those works refers to the gradient noise.
To handle heavy-tailed gradient noise, existing SGD methods typically incorporate gradient clipping, gradient normalization, momentum, or combinations of these techniques.
For example,
Zhang et al. (2020)
analyzed SGD with gradient clipping and established an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-(3\delta+1)/\delta})
;
Cutkosky and Mehta (2021)
proved a high-probability convergence rate using a combination of gradient normalization and clipping;
Nguyen et al. (2023)
studied SGD with adaptive clipping;
Liu and Zhou (2025)
;
Sun et al. (2025)
demonstrated that gradient normalization alone suffices to achieve the optimal rate; and
Fang et al. (2026a)
extended the analysis to SGD with stochastic preconditioners.
These methods also yield liminf-type almost-sure convergence to first-order stationary points. Our setting similarly allows heavy-tailed gradient noise; indeed, we impose no moment conditions at all (only the high-probability condition (
17
)) on the gradient noise.

Despite these similarities, the aforementioned approaches differ from our method in two respects.
First, clipping and normalization in SGD operate on gradient estimates, while our analysis requires analogous control on objective value estimates.
Second, their methods rely crucially on unbiased gradient estimates, while our model includes irreducible noise in objective value (and gradient) estimates, preventing a direct adaptation of their techniques. Whether clipping- or normalization-type ideas can be effectively applied to objective value estimates remains an open question.

In the next subsection, we briefly discuss how to construct estimates to satisfy the oracle conditions when the estimation noise is heavy-tailed.

3.2
Construction of oracles with heavy-tailed noise

Heavy-tailed noise with a finite
1+\delta
moment is prevalent across numerous application domains, including deep learning
(
Mahoney and Martin, 2019
)
, reinforcement learning
(
Medina and Yang, 2016
)
, robust regression
(
Pensia et al., 2024
)
, and quantitative finance
(
Müller et al., 1998
)
. Although the variance becomes infinite when
\delta<1
, we show that probabilistic zeroth-, first-, and second-order oracles defined in Section
3.1
can still be constructed.
We denote the sample sets used to estimate
\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k},{\bar{g}}_{k},{\bar{f}}_{k},{\bar{f}}_{s_{k}}
by
\xi^{h}_{k}
,
\xi_{k}^{g}
,
\xi_{k}^{f}
,
\xi_{s_{k}}^{f}
, respectively, and denote their corresponding sample sizes by
|\cdot|
.
Samples are drawn independently from a distribution
{\mathcal{P}}
, and each realization is assumed to be unbiased with a finite
1+\delta
moment. Without loss of generality, we restrict attention to
\delta\in(0,1]
, since bounded higher-order moments
(\delta>1)
imply bounded lower-order moments.

We explore three estimation strategies: the sample average method, the Median-of-Means (MoM) method, and the finite-difference method (with access only to noisy objective value estimates).
We summarize the sample complexity for each method below, while defer detailed proofs to Appendix
B
.

\bullet

Sample average.
For objective value estimate
{\bar{f}}_{k}
, we draw
|\xi_{k}^{f}|
i.i.d. samples at
{\bm{x}}_{k}
with each single realization
F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{f})
satisfying

\mathbb{E}[F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{f})\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}]=f_{k}\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\mathbb{E}[|F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{f})-f_{k}|^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}]\leq\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f},

and set the estimate as
{\bar{f}}_{k}={\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{f})=\frac{1}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|}\sum_{\xi^{f}\in\xi_{k}^{f}}F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{f})
. Then, by Markov inequality and Burkholder-type inequality (Lemma
D.1
), the zeroth-order oracle conditions (
18
), (
19
), and (
20
) are satisfied as long as
|\xi_{k}^{f}|
is respectively greater than

\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{1}{p_{f}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right),\quad\quad\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right),\quad\quad\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{\Upsilon_{f}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

(22)

Noting that the last term is independent of the noise
\epsilon_{f},\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}
and the same requirements apply to
\bar{f}_{s_{k}}
, we obtain the zeroth-order sample complexity:

\min\{|\xi_{k}^{f}|,|\xi_{s_{k}}^{f}|\}\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{1}{p_{f}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{1}{\min\{\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2},\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}\}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(23)

To satisfy first- and second-order oracle conditions in (
16
) and (
17
), same derivations yield

|\xi_{k}^{g}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{d}{p_{g}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right),\;|\xi_{k}^{h}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{d^{2}}{p_{h}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{d}{\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(24)

\bullet

Median-of-Means (MoM).

The MoM estimator provides robustness to outliers and heavy-tailed noise. Samples are partitioned into equal-size groups; the empirical mean of each group is computed; and the (coordinate-wise) median of these means is used as the estimator. The MoM method guarantees exponential-type concentration bounds even in finite-moment settings. Essentially, by applying standard MoM results (e.g.,
(
Devroye et al., 2016
, Theorem 3.1)
,
(
Bubeck et al., 2013
, Lemma 2)
,
(
Lugosi and Mendelson, 2019
, Theorem 3)
), we can improve the dependence of the sample complexity on failure probabilities from
1/p_{f}^{1/\delta},(d/p_{g})^{1/\delta},(d^{2}/p_{h})^{1/\delta}
in (
23
) and (
24
) to
\log(1/p_{f})
,
\log(d/p_{g})
,
\log(d^{2}/p_{h})
. In particular, we have all the oracle conditions satisfied as long as

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\min\{|\xi_{k}^{f}|,|\xi_{s_{k}}^{f}|\}\geq\mathcal{O}\bigg(\log\left(\frac{1}{p_{f}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\min\{\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2},\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}\}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\bigg),

(25)

\displaystyle|\xi_{k}^{g}|\geq\mathcal{O}\bigg(\log\left(\frac{d}{p_{g}}\right)\left[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\bigg),\;\;|\xi_{k}^{h}|\geq\mathcal{O}\bigg(\log\left(\frac{d^{2}}{p_{h}}\right)\left[\frac{d}{\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\bigg).

(26)

\bullet

Finite difference.
We now consider the setting where only noisy objective evaluations are available. In this case, we estimate gradients and Hessians via finite differences. Given
{\bm{x}}_{k}
, for any direction
\bm{y}
, we assume that each sample realization
F({\bm{x}}_{k}+\bm{y},\xi^{f})
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[F({\bm{x}}_{k}+\bm{y},\xi^{f})\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1},\bm{y}]=f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\bm{y}),\quad\mathbb{E}[|F({\bm{x}}_{k}+\bm{y},\xi^{f})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\bm{y})|^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1},\bm{y}]\leq\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}.

We also assume that the true gradient
\nabla f
and Hessian
\nabla^{2}f
are Lipschitz continuous with constants
L_{\nabla f}
and
L_{\nabla^{2}f}
, respectively.

For the finite-difference case, we can estimate the objective values
f_{k},f_{s_{k}}
using either sample average or MoM method, and their sample complexities are given by (
23
) and (
25
). Furthermore, the
j
-th gradient entry and the
(i,j)
-th Hessian entry are estimated by

\displaystyle[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left({\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{g})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{g})\right),

\displaystyle[\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left({\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i},\xi_{k}^{h})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})+{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{h})\right),

where
\bm{e}_{i},\bm{e}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the
i
-th,
j
-th canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Again, each objective value estimate in the above finite differences can be obtained using either sample average or MoM method. We should mention that, for simplicity, we do not consider more advanced simultaneous perturbation or Gaussian smoothing techniques for finite differences
(
Spall, 1998
)
, which may need fewer, dimension-independent number of function evaluations (but also introduce additional randomness through randomized perturbations). In addition, we do not require the independence between
{\bar{g}}_{k}
and
\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}
, meaning that we can use the same set of samples for estimating
{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{g})
and
{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})
to reduce function evaluations. We write
\xi_{k}^{g},\xi_{k}^{h}
to simply distinguish their sample complexities here.

Let us set
\sigma=\frac{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}{\sqrt{d}L_{\nabla f}}
for gradient estimation and
\sigma=\frac{\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k}}{dL_{\nabla^{2}f}}
for Hessian estimation. If we use sample average to estimate
\bar{f}(\cdot,\xi_{k}^{g})
and
\bar{f}(\cdot,\xi_{k}^{h})
, then the first- and second-order oracle conditions in (
16
) and (
17
)) are satisfied as long as

|\xi_{k}^{g}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{d}{p_{g}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{L_{\nabla f}d}{(\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1})^{2}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right),|\xi_{k}^{h}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{d^{2}}{p_{h}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{L_{\nabla^{2}f}^{2}d^{3}}{(\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k})^{3}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(27)

The failure probabilities
(d/p_{g})^{1/\delta},(d^{2}/p_{h})^{1/\delta}
are replaced by
\log(d/p_{g})
,
\log(d^{2}/p_{h})
if the MoM method is used to estimate
\bar{f}(\cdot,\xi_{k}^{g})
and
\bar{f}(\cdot,\xi_{k}^{h})
in finite differences instead.

4
High-Probability Complexity Bounds

In this section, we analyze the first- and second-order high-probability complexity bounds for the present trust-region SSQP method. In Section
4.1
, we introduce fundamental lemmas. In Sections
4.2
and
4.3
, we establish the complexity bounds under probabilistic heavy-tailed and sub-exponential zeroth-order oracles, respectively. We first state the
assumption
.

Assumption 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open convex set containing the iterates and trial points
\{{\bm{x}}_{k},{\bm{x}}_{s_{k}}\}
. The objective
f({\bm{x}})
is twice continuously differentiable and bounded below by
f_{\inf}
over
\Omega
. The gradient
\nabla f({\bm{x}})
and Hessian
\nabla^{2}f({\bm{x}})
are both Lipschitz continuous over
\Omega
, with constants
L_{\nabla f}
and
L_{\nabla^{2}f}
, respectively. Analogously, the constraint
c({\bm{x}})
is twice continuously differentiable, its Jacobian
G({\bm{x}})
is Lipschitz continuous over
\Omega
with the constant
L_{G}
.
For
1\leq i\leq m
, the Hessian of the
i
-th constraint,
\nabla^{2}c^{i}({\bm{x}})
, is Lipschitz continuous over
\Omega
with the constant
L_{\nabla^{2}c}
. We also assume that there exist constants
\kappa_{c}
,
\kappa_{\nabla f}
,
\kappa_{1,G}
,
\kappa_{2,G}>0
such that

\|c_{k}\|\leq\kappa_{c},\quad\quad\|\nabla f_{k}\|\leq\kappa_{\nabla f},\quad\quad\kappa_{1,G}\cdot I\preceq G_{k}G_{k}^{T}\preceq\kappa_{2,G}\cdot I,\quad\quad\forall k\geq 0.

Assumption
4.1
is standard in the SQP literature
(
Byrd et al., 1987
;
Powell and Yuan, 1990
;
El-Alem, 1991
;
Conn et al., 2000
;
Berahas et al., 2021
;
Berahas et al., 2024
;
Curtis et al., 2024a
;
Fang et al., 2024a
;
Fang et al., 2024b
)
.
To analyze first-order complexity, it suffices to assume that
f({\bm{x}})
and
c({\bm{x}})
are continuously differentiable, without conditions on Hessians
\nabla^{2}f({\bm{x}})
and
\nabla^{2}c^{i}({\bm{x}})
.
Assumption
4.1
implies that
G_{k}
has full row rank,
\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}\leq\|G_{k}\|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{2,G}}
and
\|G_{k}^{T}[G_{k}G_{k}^{T}]^{-1}\|\leq 1/\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}
. Consequently, both the true Lagrangian multiplier
{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}=-[G_{k}G_{k}^{T}]^{-1}G_{k}\nabla f_{k}
and the estimated counterpart
{\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}_{k}=-[G_{k}G_{k}^{T}]^{-1}G_{k}{\bar{g}}_{k}
are well defined. Additionally,
\|\nabla^{2}f({\bm{x}})\|\leq L_{\nabla f}
and
\|\nabla^{2}c^{i}({\bm{x}})\|\leq L_{G}
for
1\leq i\leq m
over
\Omega
.

4.1
Fundamental lemmas

In this section, we analyze basic properties of the trust-region SSQP method. Lemmas in this section apply to both the heavy-tailed and the sub-exponential oracles.
We first show that on the event
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
(cf. (
16
) and (
17
)), the Hessian estimate
{\bar{H}}_{k}
constructed for second-order convergence has an upper bound.

Lemma 4.2
.

Under Assumption
4.1
with
\alpha=1
, there exists a positive constant
\kappa_{B}\geq 1
such that
\|{\bar{H}}_{k}\|\leq\kappa_{B}
on the event
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
.

Proof.

Recall that
{\bar{H}}_{k}=\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}_{k}^{i}\nabla^{2}c^{i}_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bar{H}}_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}-\nabla^{2}f_{k}\|+\|\nabla^{2}f_{k}\|+\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}({\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}_{k}^{i}-{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}^{i})\nabla^{2}c^{i}_{k}\|+\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}^{i}\nabla^{2}c^{i}_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}-\nabla^{2}f_{k}\|+\|\nabla^{2}f_{k}\|+\|{\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}_{k}-{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}\|\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\nabla^{2}c^{i}_{k}\|^{2}\big\}^{1/2}+\|{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}\|\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\nabla^{2}c^{i}_{k}\|\big\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}-\nabla^{2}f_{k}\|+L_{\nabla f}+\frac{\sqrt{m}L_{G}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}}\|{\bar{g}}_{k}-g_{k}\|+\frac{\sqrt{m}L_{G}\kappa_{\nabla f}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}},

where the last inequality follows from Assumption
4.1
and the definitions of
{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}
and
{\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}_{k}
. On the event
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
,
\|\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}-\nabla^{2}f_{k}\|\leq\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k}
and
\|{\bar{g}}_{k}-g_{k}\|\leq\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{2}
. Since
\Delta_{k}\leq\Delta_{\max}
, it follows that

\|{\bar{H}}_{k}\|\leq\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{\max}+L_{\nabla f}+\frac{\sqrt{m}L_{G}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}}(\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{\max}^{2}+\kappa_{\nabla f}).

By setting
\kappa_{B}=\max\{1,\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{\max}+L_{\nabla f}+\sqrt{m}L_{G}/\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}\cdot(\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{\max}^{2}+\kappa_{\nabla f})\}
, we complete the proof.
∎

In the next lemma, we demonstrate that for the second-order stationarity, the difference between the true Lagrangian Hessian
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
and its estimate
{\bar{H}}_{k}
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\Delta_{k})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{h}+\epsilon_{g})
on the event
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
. Furthermore, the difference between
\tau_{k}^{+}\coloneqq|\min\{\tau_{k},0\}|
and its estimate
{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{+}
is bounded by the same quantity. This lemma ensures that
{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{+}
is an accurate estimate of
\tau_{k}^{+}
provided that both the objective gradient and Hessian estimates are accurate.

Lemma 4.3
.

Under Assumption
4.1
with
\alpha=1
, we have
\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}-{\bar{H}}_{k}\|\leq\epsilon_{H}+\kappa_{H}\Delta_{k}
and
|\tau_{k}^{+}-{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{+}|\leq\epsilon_{H}+\kappa_{H}\Delta_{k}
on the event
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
, where
\kappa_{H}\coloneqq\kappa_{h}+\frac{\sqrt{m}\kappa_{g}L_{G}\Delta_{\max}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}}
and
\epsilon_{H}\coloneqq\epsilon_{h}+\frac{\sqrt{m}L_{G}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}}\epsilon_{g}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}-{\bar{H}}_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\|\nabla^{2}f_{k}-\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}({\bm{\lambda}}_{k}^{i}-{\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}_{k}^{i})\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla^{2}f_{k}-\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}\|+\|{\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}_{k}-{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}\|\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}\|^{2}\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla^{2}f_{k}-\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}\|+\frac{\sqrt{m}L_{G}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}}\|g_{k}-{\bar{g}}_{k}\|\quad\quad(\text{Assumption \ref{assump:4-1}})

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left(\kappa_{h}+\frac{\sqrt{m}\kappa_{g}L_{G}\Delta_{\max}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}}\right)}_{\kappa_{H}}\Delta_{k}+\underbrace{\left(\epsilon_{h}+\frac{\sqrt{m}L_{G}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1,G}}}\epsilon_{g}\right)}_{\epsilon_{H}},

where the last inequality is due to the event
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
and
\Delta_{k}\leq\Delta_{\max}
. Next, we show
|\tau_{k}-{\bar{\tau}}_{k}|\leq\kappa_{H}\Delta_{k}+\epsilon_{H}
. Let
\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{k}
be a normalized eigenvector corresponding to
{\bar{\tau}}_{k}
, then

\tau_{k}-{\bar{\tau}}_{k}\leq\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{k}^{T}\left[Z_{k}^{T}(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}-{\bar{H}}_{k})Z_{k}\right]\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{k}\leq\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}-{\bar{H}}_{k}\|\leq\kappa_{H}\Delta_{k}+\epsilon_{H}.

Let
\bm{\zeta}_{k}
be a normalized eigenvector corresponding to
\tau_{k}
, then

{\bar{\tau}}_{k}-\tau_{k}\leq\bm{\zeta}_{k}^{T}\left[Z_{k}^{T}({\bar{H}}_{k}-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{k})Z_{k}\right]\bm{\zeta}_{k}\leq\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}-{\bar{H}}_{k}\|\leq\kappa_{H}\Delta_{k}+\epsilon_{H}.

Combining the above two displays, we have
|\tau_{k}-{\bar{\tau}}_{k}|\leq\kappa_{H}\Delta_{k}+\epsilon_{H}
, which implies
|\tau_{k}^{+}-{\bar{\tau}}_{k}^{+}|\leq\kappa_{H}\Delta_{k}+\epsilon_{H}
. We complete the proof.
∎

Let us define
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bar{\mu}}_{k}}^{s_{k}}\coloneqq{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bar{\mu}}_{k}}({\bm{x}}_{s_{k}})
and

For our non-asymptotic analysis, we recall the definitions of first- and second-order stationarity points in Section
2
and define the stopping time based on
\epsilon
-stationary points.

Definition 4.6

(Stopping time)
.

For first-order stationarity (
\alpha=0
), the stopping time is defined as the index of the first iteration in which the true KKT residual enters the neighborhood of radius
\epsilon
:

T_{\epsilon}=\min\{k:\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\|\leq\epsilon\}.

For second-order stationarity (
\alpha=1
), the stopping time is defined as the index of the first iteration in which the true KKT residual and the true eigenvalue both enter the neighborhood of radius
\epsilon
:

T_{\epsilon}=\min\{k:\max(\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\|,\tau_{k}^{+})\leq\epsilon\}.

We define the stopping time directly using the deterministic KKT residual and the negative eigenvalue of the reduced Lagrangian Hessian. In comparison,
(
Berahas et al., 2025
, Definition 3.7)
defines the stopping time for first-order stationarity based on the reduction of the linear approximation of the merit function.
In their Remark 3.8, the authors show that this definition is equivalent to our Definition
4.6
, but with the threshold
\epsilon
depending on the lower bound of an
unknown
true merit parameter. Such a definition may not be direct, since even after the algorithm terminates under that definition, the true KKT residual is only controlled with an unknown upper bound.
In addition, the stopping time in
Cao et al. (2023)
depends on the Lipschitz constants and the irreducible noises.

Similar to
Berahas et al. (2025)
;
Cao et al. (2023)
, we require
\epsilon
to exceed a threshold determined by irreducible noises
\epsilon_{f}
,
\epsilon_{g}
, and
\epsilon_{h}
. Specifically, we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 4.7
.

For the stationarity level
\epsilon
, we assume

\displaystyle\text{If }\alpha=0:\quad\epsilon

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{\Upsilon_{3}}\sqrt{\frac{8\gamma^{4}{\bar{\mu}}_{T-1}\epsilon_{f}}{\kappa_{fcd}\eta^{3}{\bar{\mu}}_{0}(p-1/2)}}+\frac{\Upsilon_{4}}{\Upsilon_{3}}\epsilon_{g},

\displaystyle\text{If }\alpha=1:\quad\epsilon

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{\Upsilon_{5}}\sqrt[3]{\frac{2{\bar{\mu}}_{T-1}\gamma^{6}\max\{\Delta_{\max},1\}(4\epsilon_{f}+\epsilon_{g}^{3/2})}{\kappa_{fcd}\eta^{3}{\bar{\mu}}_{0}(p-1/2)}}+\frac{\Upsilon_{6}}{\Upsilon_{5}}\epsilon_{g}+\frac{\Upsilon_{7}}{\Upsilon_{5}}\epsilon_{h},

where
\Upsilon_{3},\Upsilon_{4},\Upsilon_{5},\Upsilon_{6},\Upsilon_{7}
are constants independent of irreducible noise levels and are defined in Lemmas
4.9
and
4.10
. Furthermore, the failure oracle probabilities

are small enough such that
1-p_{h}-p_{g}-2p_{f}\eqqcolon p\in(1/2,1)

Here,

represents the lower bound of probability that
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{\prime}
holds, where
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{\prime}
denotes the event that accurate
{\bar{f}}_{k},{\bar{f}}_{s_{k}}
are regenerated after computing the SOC step. For notational consistency, we assume
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{\prime}
holds when the SOC step is not performed. As demonstrated in Assumption
4.7
, for first-order stationarity, we need
\epsilon\geq\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{f}}+\epsilon_{g})
; for second-order stationarity, we need
\epsilon\geq\mathcal{O}(\sqrt[3]{\epsilon_{f}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}+\epsilon_{h})
. These orders matches the ones in
Berahas et al. (2025)
;
Cao et al. (2023)
.

Remark 4.8
.

The dependence of
\epsilon
on
\epsilon_{f}
,
\epsilon_{g}
, and
\epsilon_{h}
is standard in existing literature
(
Cao et al., 2023
;
Berahas et al., 2025
;
Sun and Nocedal, 2023
)
. In the presence of biased, irreducible noise in objective estimation, the iterates can only be guaranteed to converge to an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of a stationary point, where
\epsilon
is lower bounded by a quantity determined by the magnitude of the bias. This dependence arises for the following reasons.
First, the first- and second-order stationarity conditions are characterized by the KKT residual and the negative curvature, both of which rely on gradient and Hessian information. When their estimates contain biased irreducible noise, different true gradients and Hessians are not identifiable whenever their differences lie within
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{h}
, respectively.
Second, the algorithm employs a merit function to adaptively update the trust-region radius, which depends on the objective value that can only be estimated up to an irreducible noise
\epsilon_{f}
. This adaptive mechanism leads to the dependence of
\epsilon
on
\epsilon_{f}
. We note that for non-adaptive algorithms, where the stepsize or trust-region radius sequences are prespecified and thus do not use objective value information, the dependence of
\epsilon
on
\epsilon_{f}
can be suppressed.

Lemma 4.13

Under Assumptions

(c)

\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(1-\Lambda_{k})(1-\Theta_{k})\geq\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(1-\Lambda_{k})\Theta_{k}

Remark 4.14
.

We discuss the insights behind the above lemma.

\bullet
In Lemma
4.13
(a), we demonstrate that among the first
T
iterations, the number of iterations that are large and sufficient (i.e.,
\Lambda_{k}\Theta_{k}=1
) is no less that the number of iterations that are large and insufficient (i.e.,
\Lambda_{k}(1-\Theta_{k})=1
), subtracted by a constant
\log_{\gamma}(\Delta_{0}/\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime})
. This conclusion is intuitive: when an iteration is large, its trust-region radius is lower bounded by a constant
\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}
. Thus, to maintain this lower bound, among all large iterations, the number of sufficient iterations (in which the radius increases) must be no less than the number of insufficient iterations (in which the radius decreases), except for a constant
\log_{\gamma}(\Delta_{0}/\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime})
representing the smallest number of iterations to reduce the radius from
\Delta_{0}
to
\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}
.

\bullet
Lemma
4.13
(b) follows directly by rearranging the terms in (a).

\bullet
In Lemma
4.13
(c), we show that the number of iterations that are small and insufficient (i.e.,
(1-\Lambda_{k})(1-\Theta_{k})=1
) is no less than the number of iterations that are small and sufficient (i.e.,
(1-\Lambda_{k})\Theta_{k}=1
). In fact, when an iteration is small, its trust-region radius is upper bounded by
\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}
. Thus, to maintain the upper bound, among all small iterations, the number of iterations that are sufficient (in which the radius increases) must be no more than the number of iterations that are insufficient (in which the radius decreases).

\bullet
In Lemma
4.13
(d), we show that the number of iterations that are small and accurate (i.e.,
(1-\Lambda_{k})I_{k}=1
) is no greater than the number of iterations that are small and inaccurate (i.e.,
(1-\Lambda_{k})(1-I_{k})=1
). The intuition is also straightforward: when an iteration is small and accurate, Lemmas
4.9
and
4.10
guarantee that the step will be sufficient, leading to an increase in the trust-region radius. Consequently, to maintain the upper bound
\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}
, the number of small and accurate iterations (in which the radius increases) must not exceed the number of small and inaccurate iterations (only in this case the radius may decrease).

The next lemma follows from the conclusion that
P(I_{k}=1\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1/2})\geq p
(recall
p=1-p_{h}-p_{g}-2p_{f}
)
and the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality (cf. Lemma
F.1
in Appendix

Lemma 4.15
.

For all
T\geq 1
,
\widehat{p}\in[0,p)
with
p\in(1/2,1)
, and both
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1

P\left[\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}I_{k}<\widehat{p}\;T\right]\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{(\widehat{p}-p)^{2}}{2p^{2}}T\right\}.

Proof.

It follows from
P(I_{k}=1\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1/2})\geq p
that
\{\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}I_{k}-pT\}
is a submartingale. Moreover,

\left|\left(\sum_{k=0}^{T}I_{k}-p(T+1)\right)-\left(\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}I_{k}-pT\right)\right|=\left|I_{T}-p\right|\leq\max\{|1-p|,|0-p|\}=p.

The last equality follows from
p>1/2

F.1

In the following lemma, we demonstrate that prior to algorithm termination (i.e.,
T_{\epsilon}>T-1
), if the number of accurate iterations (i.e.,

Lemma 4.16
.

For all

and
\alpha=1

Proof.

It suffices to show that

and
\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}I_{k}\geq pT
imply

.
For
k=0,1,\cdots,T-1
, we define the following sets and use
|\cdot|
to denote their cardinality:

•

{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{L}}}=\{k:I_{k}\Lambda_{k}=1\}
as the set of iterations that are large and accurate;

•

\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}=\{k:(1-I_{k})\Lambda_{k}=1\}
as the set of iterations that are large and inaccurate;

•

{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}=\{k:I_{k}(1-\Lambda_{k})=1\}
as the set of iterations that are small and accurate;

•

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}=\{k:(1-I_{k})(1-\Lambda_{k})=1\}

This classification is valid, noticing that
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}|+|{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|+|{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|=T

\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Lambda_{k}=|\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|+|{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|
that

\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Lambda_{k}\Theta_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}(|\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|+|{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|)-\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}}\right).

(36)

Since
\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}I_{k}\geq pT
, by the fact that an iteration is either large or small, we have

|\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}|=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(1-I_{k})=T-\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}I_{k}\leq T-pT.

(37)

Moreover, it follows from Lemma

(d) that

|{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}|=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(1-\Lambda_{k})I_{k}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(1-\Lambda_{k})(1-I_{k})=|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}|.

(38)

Combining the above displays, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Lambda_{k}\Theta_{k}I_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Lambda_{k}\Theta_{k}-\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Lambda_{k}\Theta_{k}(1-I_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{nequ:22}}}}{{\geq}}\;\;\frac{1}{2}(|\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|+|{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|)-\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}}\right)-\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Lambda_{k}(1-I_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(|\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|+|{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|)-\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}}\right)-|\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|=\frac{1}{2}(T-|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}|-|{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}|)-\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}}\right)-|\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{L}}}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{nequ:23}}}}{{\geq}}\;\;\frac{1}{2}(T-|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}|-|{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}|)-\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}}\right)+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}|-T+pT

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}|-|{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}|)+\left(p-\frac{1}{2}\right)T-\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{nequ:24}}}}{{\geq}}\;\;\left(p-\frac{1}{2}\right)T-\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\widehat{\Delta}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(p-\frac{1}{2}\right)T-\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\widehat{\Delta}}\right)-1.

In the last inequality, we use the relation

and thus

We complete the proof.

Remark 4.20
.

In this remark, we demonstrate the techniques used in establishing the iteration complexity and discuss the sample complexity.

\bullet

The heavy-tailed nature of the noise introduces significant challenges in complexity analysis. To this end, we employ the Burkholder-type inequality (Lemma
D.1
)
(
Burkholder, 1973
;
Chen and Sung, 2020
)
and martingale Fuk–Nagaev inequality (Lemma
F.2
)
(
Fuk, 1973
;
Nagaev, 1979
;
Fan et al., 2017
)
to study the tail behavior of
e_{k}
and
e_{s_{k}}
. In contrast,
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Berahas et al. (2025)
utilized concentration techniques for sub-exponential variables, which we also apply in Section
4.3
. We mention that the techniques used in analyzing heavy-tailed noise are independent of the algorithm designs, implying that same analysis can be applied to trust-region and line-search methods
(
Cao et al., 2023
;
Berahas et al., 2025
)
and derive the same complexity bounds.

\bullet

Due to the heavy-tailed noise,
P\{T_{\epsilon}\leq T-1\}
no longer grows to 1 at an exponential rate as in
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Berahas et al. (2025)
, but is instead reduced to a polynomial rate of
{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-\delta})
for
\delta>0
(cf. (
41
)).
Despite this slower rate, we still obtain for any
\delta>0
,
P[T_{\epsilon}=\infty]=P[\cap_{T=0}^{\infty}\{T_{\epsilon}>T\}]=\lim\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty}P[T_{\epsilon}>T]=0
, where the second equality is due to the monotonicity of the events
\{T_{\epsilon}>T+1\}\subseteq\{T_{\epsilon}>T\}
. Thus, for any
\delta>0
, our algorithm reaches a (first- or second-order)
\epsilon
-stationary point in finite time
almost surely
. If we suppress the irreducible noise by setting
\epsilon_{f}=\epsilon_{g}=\epsilon_{h}=0
, this result further yields a liminf-type almost-sure convergence: for any realization of the algorithm, there exists a subsequence of iterates converging to a (first- or second-order) stationary point.
This liminf-type second-order convergence with heavy-tailed oracles matches existing literature
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
;
Fang et al., 2024b
)
; however, this asymptotic result implied from our non-asymptotic analysis still leaves a gap compared with the stronger lim-type first-order convergence established in
(
Chen et al., 2017
, Theorem 4.18)
and
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
, Theorem 4)
, which requires no moment conditions on the objective value estimates. Closing this gap remains an open question.

Remark 4.21
.

We analyze the total sample complexity of the method following the introduction of oracle constructions in Section
3.2
.
By Assumption
4.7
, achieving first-order stationarity requires
\epsilon\geq{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{f}}+\epsilon_{g})
, leading to the relations
\epsilon_{f}\approx{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{2})
and
\epsilon_{g}\approx{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)
. Similarly, for second-order stationarity where
\epsilon\geq{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt[3]{\epsilon_{f}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}+\epsilon_{h})
, we obtain
\epsilon_{f}\approx{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{3})
,
\epsilon_{g}\approx{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{2})
, and
\epsilon_{h}\approx{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)
.

Suppose the noise of all orders of oracles has bounded variance (i.e.,
\delta=1
). As shown in Section
3.2
, estimating the objective value, gradient, and Hessian in each iteration requires sample sizes of
{\mathcal{O}}(\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}^{-2})
,
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon_{g}^{-2})
, and
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon_{h}^{-2})
. Thus, when
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}={\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon_{f})
, achieving first-order
\epsilon
-stationarity requires
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4})
and
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
samples per iteration for estimating the objective value and gradient, respectively. Given
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
iteration complexity, the total sample complexity amounts to
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-6})
.
Analogously, for second-order
\epsilon
-stationarity,
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-6})
,
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4})
, and
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
samples are required per iteration for estimating the objective value, gradient, and Hessian, respectively. Combined with
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-3})
iteration complexity, the total sample complexity amounts to
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-9})
.

We mention that the total sample complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-6})
for first-order
\epsilon
-stationarity matches the result in
Jin et al. (2024)
, which analyzed a trust-region method for identifying first-order
\epsilon
-stationary points in unconstrained optimization. However, this result is worse than that in
Curtis et al. (2023)
, which established a total sample complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4})
for a line-search method. The discrepancy arises because both our method and
Jin et al. (2024)
require objective value estimation at each iteration, leading to a per-iteration sample complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4})
(cf. Remark
4.8
).
If we adopt a non-adaptive, objective-value-free design as in
Curtis et al. (2023)
, where only gradient estimates are required, the per-iteration sample complexity will reduce to
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
, yielding a total sample complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4})
. However, as demonstrated in
Berahas et al. (2025)
,
objective-value-free
methods tend to perform worse in practice than those using objective value estimates, especially in the presence of irreducible noise.

Reducing the above sample complexity of adaptive SSQP methods is still a challenging problem. Some helpful insights can be drawn from well-studied stochastic methods such as stochastic gradient descent (SGD). Numerous variance-reduction techniques have been proposed for SGD in nonconvex settings and have demonstrated significant improvements over vanilla SGD. For example, Stochastic Variance-Reduced Gradient (SVRG)
(
Johnson and Zhang, 2013
;
Reddi et al., 2016
)
achieves an improved sample complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N+N^{2/3}\epsilon^{-2})
for finite-sum optimization problems with
N
samples. Building on this idea,
Fang et al. (2026c)
generalize SVRG to stochastic trust-region methods, attaining the same sample complexity. In the online setting, STOchastic Recursive Momentum (STORM)
(
Cutkosky and Orabona, 2019
)
achieves a sample complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3})
. Similarly, Stochastic Path-Integrated Differential EstimatoR (SPIDER)
(
Fang et al., 2018
)
provides a unifying framework, achieving
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3})
in the online setting and
\mathcal{O}(N+N^{1/2}\epsilon^{-2})
in the finite-sum setting with
N
samples.
In constrained stochastic optimization,
Lu et al. (2026)
applies both truncated recursive momentum and truncated Polyak momentum for penalty methods, attaining
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3})
sample complexity.
Berahas et al. (2023)
integrates SVRG into SSQP for finite-sum problems, attaining
\mathcal{O}(N+N^{2/3}\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity and matching the result in
Reddi et al. (2016)
for unconstrained optimization. However, the SVRG technique requires periodically computing a full gradient as a “checkpoint”, which presents challenges for directly adapting it to the online setting.
We leave the development of variance-reduced trust-region SSQP methods for improving sample complexity to future research.

Algorithm setup

10^{4}

For TR-SSQP and TR-SSQP2, we construct estimates of objective Hessians, gradients, and values by generating samples
(\xi_{k}^{h},\xi_{k}^{g},\xi_{k}^{f},\xi_{s_{k}}^{f})

1.

Identity matrix (Id). It has been used in various existing SSQP literature, especially for line-search methods

Remark 5.1
.

We further discuss the impact of Hessian approximation quality on both the theory and practice of our method. For first-order stationarity, our theoretical iteration complexity results do not require any high-accuracy Hessian approximation assumptions; that is, the quality of the Hessian estimate does not affect the theoretical guarantees. This is reasonable, since the first-order convergence analysis relies on establishing sufficient decrease guarantees, which depend only on gradient information and do not involve second-order oracles. Nevertheless, as our experiments indicate, the Hessian approximation can have a substantial effect on practical performance, as reflected in the noticeably different behavior produced by different Hessian construction methods in Figure
1
.

We highlight the contrast between the estimated Hessian (EstH) and the averaged Hessian (AveH). In stochastic settings, using the most recent Hessian estimate yields an unbiased approximation but suffers from high variance, which can significantly impair performance, sometime even performing worse than simply using the identity matrix. In contrast, averaging Hessian estimates over iterations dramatically reduces variance due to the noise concentration effect, though at the expense of introducing bias. Notably, as the iterate approaches a stationary point, the bias of the averaged Hessian estimate diminishes, while the variance of the last-iterate Hessian estimate still remains at a constant level. This bias-variance trade-off explains why AveH typically produces more stable and reliable performance than EstH. This behavior is also consistent with deterministic SQP theory, where higher-quality Hessian approximations are better able to satisfy the Dennis-Moré condition
(
Dennis and Moré, 1974
)
, which underpin local superlinear convergence, even though such a condition is not required for global convergence.

Finally, we note that for achieving second-order stationarity, the quality of the Hessian approximation becomes essential and
explicitly appears in the theoretical lower bound on
T
, as discussed in Remark
4.24
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we derived high-probability complexity bounds for a trust-region Stochastic Sequential Quadratic Programming (SSQP) method in identifying first- and second-order
\epsilon
-stationary points in equality-constrained optimization problems. Our method extends the existing literature by allowing irreducible and heavy-tailed noise in objective function estimation. Under weaker oracle conditions, we established that our method achieves an iteration complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
for identifying a first-order
\epsilon
-stationary point and
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-3})
for identifying a second-order
\epsilon
-stationary point with high probability, provided that
\epsilon
is above a threshold determined by the irreducible noise. We validated our theoretical analysis through numerical experiments on problems from the CUTEst dataset.

. We consider three estimation strategies: the sample average method, the Median-of-Means (MoM) method, and the finite-difference method.

B.1
Sample average

For the zeroth-order oracle, we need
{\bar{f}}_{k}
to satisfy (
18
), (
19
), and (
20
). We analyze the three conditions separately.

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
18
).
By Markov inequality and Burkholder-type inequality (Lemma
D.1
with i.i.d. case), we have for any
t>0
,

P\left(e_{k}>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)=P\left(|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|^{1+\delta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]}{t^{1+\delta}}\\
\leq\frac{2^{1-\delta}\sum_{\xi^{f}\in\xi_{k}^{f}}\mathbb{E}[\big|F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{f})-f_{k}\big|^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}]}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|^{1+\delta}\cdot t^{1+\delta}}\leq\frac{2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|^{\delta}\cdot t^{1+\delta}}.

(B.1)

By setting
t=\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2}
and requiring the right-hand-side term to be bounded by
0.5p_{f}
, we obtain the sample complexity

|\xi_{k}^{f}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{1}{p_{f}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.2)

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
19
).
By Jensen’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{E}[e_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]^{1+\delta}=\mathbb{E}[|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]^{1+\delta}=\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg|\frac{1}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|}\sum_{\xi^{f}\in\xi_{k}^{f}}F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{f})-f_{k}\bigg|\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right]^{1+\delta}\\
\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg|\frac{1}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|}\sum_{\xi^{f}\in\xi_{k}^{f}}F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{f})-f_{k}\bigg|^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{sample_avg_eq1}}}{{\leq}}\frac{2^{1-\delta}}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|^{\delta}}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}.

Thus, setting
\frac{2^{1-\delta}}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|^{\delta}}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}\leq\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}^{1+\delta}
leads to

|\xi_{k}^{f}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.3)

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
20
).
We note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\big|e_{k}-\mathbb{E}[e_{k}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}]\big|^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[e_{k}^{1+\delta}+(\mathbb{E}[e_{k}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}])^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{1+\delta}\mathbb{E}[e_{k}^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{sample_avg_eq1}}}{{\leq}}\frac{4}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|^{\delta}}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}.

Thus, we only need
\frac{4}{|\xi_{k}^{f}|^{\delta}}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}\leq\Upsilon_{f}
to satisfy (
20
), implying that

|\xi_{k}^{f}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left((\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}/\Upsilon_{f})^{1/\delta}\right).

(B.4)

Combining (
B.2
), (
B.3
), (
B.4
), noting that (
B.4
) is independent of
(\epsilon_{f},\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f})
, and applying the same analysis for
{\bar{f}}_{s_{k}}
, we obtain the sample complexity for the zeroth-order oracle:

\min\{|\xi_{k}^{f}|,|\xi_{s_{k}}^{f}|\}\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{1}{p_{f}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{1}{\min\{\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2},\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}\}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.5)

This verifies the results in (
22
) and (
23
).

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
17
).
We note that for any
t>0
,

P(\|{\bar{g}}_{k}-g_{k}\|>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\leq P\left(\max_{j\in[d]}|[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}-[g_{k}]_{j}|>\frac{t}{\sqrt{d}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)\\
\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}P\left(|[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}-[g_{k}]_{j}|>\frac{t}{\sqrt{d}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{sample_avg_eq1}}}{{\leq}}\frac{d^{1.5+0.5\delta}2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{g}}{|\xi_{k}^{g}|^{\delta}\cdot t^{1+\delta}},

(B.6)

where we use
\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{g}
to denote the upper bound of the
1+\delta
moment of (entry-wise) estimate
\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{g})
: for any
j\in[d]
,

\mathbb{E}[|[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}-[g_{k}]_{j}|^{1+\delta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|{\bar{g}}_{k}-g_{k}\|^{1+\delta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]\leq\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{g}.

Thus, we let
t=\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}
and require the right-hand-side term to be bounded by
p_{g}
. This leads to the sample complexity to satisfy (
17
):

|\xi_{k}^{g}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{d}{p_{g}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.7)

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
16
).
We follow the analysis in (
B.6
) and just note that

\displaystyle P(

\displaystyle\|{\bar{\nabla}}^{2}f_{k}-\nabla^{2}f_{k}\|>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\leq P\left(\|{\bar{\nabla}}^{2}f_{k}-\nabla^{2}f_{k}\|_{F}>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(\max_{i,j\in[d]}|[{\bar{\nabla}}^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}-[\nabla^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}|>\frac{t}{d}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}P\left(|[{\bar{\nabla}}^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}-[\nabla^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}|>\frac{t}{d}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\eqref{sample_avg_eq1}}}}{{\leq}}\;\frac{d^{2}2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{h}}{|\xi_{k}^{h}|^{\delta}}\left[\frac{d}{t}\right]^{1+\delta}.

(B.8)

Thus, we let
t=\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k}
and require the right-hand-side term to be bounded by
p_{h}
. This leads to the sample complexity to satisfy (
16
):

|\xi_{k}^{h}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{d^{2}}{p_{h}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{d}{\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.9)

Finally, we combine (
B.7
) and (
B.9
), and can verify the result in (
24
).

B.2
Median of means

We follow the analysis in Appendix
B.1
. We first introduce some notations of the median-of-means estimator. Let us decompose the sample set
\xi_{k}^{f}
into
K
mutually exclusive batches
\xi_{k}^{f}=\xi_{k}^{f,1}\cup\cdots\cup\xi_{k}^{f,K}
, with each batch having batch size
|\xi_{k}^{f,j}|=b
,
\forall j\in[K]=\{1,\ldots,K\}
. The median-of-means estimator can be expressed as

{\bar{f}}_{k}\coloneqq\text{median}\left\{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{f,1}),\ldots,{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{f,K})\right\}\quad\text{with}\quad{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{f,j})=\frac{1}{|\xi_{k}^{f,j}|}\sum_{\xi^{f}\in\xi_{k}^{f,j}}F({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi^{f}).

With these notations, we check the sample complexities
|\xi_{k}^{f}|,|\xi_{s_{k}}^{f}|
to satisfy the three conditions (
18
), (
19
), and (
20
) for the zeroth-order oracle.

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
18
).
By the analysis (
B.1
), for any
j\in[K]
and
t>0
,

P(|{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{f,j})-f_{k}|>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\leq\frac{2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}t^{1+\delta}}\eqqcolon p(t,b).

Since
|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|>t
implies at least
K/2
of the means
|{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{f,j})-f_{k}|>t
, we have

\displaystyle\hskip-25.6073ptP(|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\leq P\left(\text{Binomial}(K,\min(1,p(t,b)))\geq K/2\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-25.6073pt=P\left\{\text{Binomial}(K,\min(1,p(t,b)))-\min(1,p(t,b))K\geq(0.5-\min(1,p(t,b)))K\right\}.

(B.10)

Suppose

0.5-\min(1,p(t,b))\geq 0.25\Longleftrightarrow p(t,b)\leq 0.25,

(B.11)

we then apply Hoeffding’s inequality
(
Hoeffding, 1963
)
and have

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228ptP(|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt\leq P\left\{\text{Binomial}(K,\min(1,p(t,b)))-\min(1,p(t,b))K\geq 0.25K\right\}\leq\exp(-K/8).

(B.12)

Let us specify
t=\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2}
and
\exp(-K/8)\leq 0.5p_{f}
; combine with (
B.11
); and note that
|\xi_{k}^{f}|=K\cdot b
. Then, we know (
18
) is satisfied as long as

|\xi_{k}^{f}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{p_{f}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.13)

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
19
).
We note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}P(|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})dt

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}+\int_{\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}}^{\infty}P(|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|>t\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})dt

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{sn:2}}}}{{\leq}}\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}+\int_{\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}}^{\infty}\sum_{j=K/2}^{K}\begin{pmatrix}K\\[-6.0pt]
j\end{pmatrix}\min(1,p(t,b))^{j}dt

\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}+\int_{\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}}^{\infty}2^{K}\min(1,p(t,b))^{K/2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}+\int_{\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}}^{\infty}(4p(t,b))^{K/2}dt

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}+\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\delta}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\frac{K}{2}-\frac{2+\delta}{1+\delta}}du.

(B.14)

Thus, by requiring
K\geq 4
and
\left[\frac{4\cdot 2^{1-\delta}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}}{b^{\delta}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}\leq 0.5\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}
, we know (
19
) is satisfied as long as

|\xi_{k}^{f}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.15)

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
20
).
Following the derivations in (
B.14
) and noting that

\mathbb{E}[|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|^{1+\delta}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(|{\bar{f}}_{k}-f_{k}|>t^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right)dt,

we immediately obtain that (
20
) is satisfied as long as

|\xi_{k}^{f}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left((\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{f}/\Upsilon_{f})^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

(B.16)

Combining (
B.13
), (
B.15
), (
B.16
), noting that (
B.16
) is independent of
(\epsilon_{f},\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f})
, and applying the same analysis for
{\bar{f}}_{s_{k}}
, we obtain the sample complexity for the zeroth-order oracle:

\min\{|\xi_{k}^{f}|,|\xi_{s_{k}}^{f}|\}\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{p_{f}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\min\{\epsilon_{f}+\kappa_{f}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+2},\widetilde{\epsilon}_{f}\}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.17)

This verifies the first result in (
25
).

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
17
) and (
16
).
For the gradient and Hessian estimates, we apply coordinate-wise MoM method. We use the gradient estimate as an example, while the Hessian estimate follows the same derivations. Combining the analysis (
B.6
) with the complexity (
B.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle P(\|{\bar{g}}_{k}-g_{k}\|\geq\epsilon_{g}

\displaystyle+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\leq P\left(\max_{j\in[d]}|[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}-[g_{k}]_{j}|\geq\frac{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}{\sqrt{d}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}P\left(|[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}-[g_{k}]_{j}|\geq\frac{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}{\sqrt{d}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)\leq p_{g},

where the last inequality holds as long as

|\xi_{k}^{g}|\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{d}{p_{g}}\right)\left[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

A similar complexity for
|\xi_{k}^{h}|
holds by combining the analysis (
B.8
) with the complexity (
B.13
). This verifies the second result in (
25
).

B.3
Finite difference

For objective value estimates
{\bar{f}}_{k}
and
{\bar{f}}_{s_{k}}
, the sample complexities are specified in (
B.5
) and (
B.17
) for the sample average method and the MoM method, respectively. We focus here only on the gradient and Hessian estimates, and use the sample average method to estimate function values as an example.
The analysis for the MoM method
follows
the same derivations but enjoys exponential tail bounds as in (
B.12
), which improves the dependence of the sample complexity on the failure probabilities from
(d/p_{g})^{1/\delta}
,
(d^{2}/p_{h})^{1/\delta}
to
\log(d/p_{g})
,
\log(d^{2}/p_{h})
.

We recall that the
j
-th gradient entry and the
(i,j)
-th Hessian entry are estimated by

\displaystyle[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left({\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{g})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{g})\right),

\displaystyle[\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left({\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i},\xi_{k}^{h})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})+{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{h})\right).

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
17
).
By the analysis (
B.6
), we know it suffices to have

P\left(|[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}-[g_{k}]_{j}|>\frac{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}{\sqrt{d}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)\leq\frac{p_{g}}{d}\quad\quad\text{ for any }j\in[d].

(B.18)

We note that

\displaystyle|[{\bar{g}}_{k}]_{j}-[g_{k}]_{j}|=\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{g})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{g})}{\sigma}-[g_{k}]_{j}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{g})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})}{\sigma}+\frac{f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})-f({\bm{x}}_{k})}{\sigma}+\frac{f({\bm{x}}_{k})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{g})}{\sigma}-[g_{k}]_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})-f({\bm{x}}_{k})}{\sigma}-[g_{k}]_{j}\right|+\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{g})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})}{\sigma}\right|+\left|\frac{f({\bm{x}}_{k})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{g})}{\sigma}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\nabla f}\sigma}{2}+\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{g})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})}{\sigma}\right|+\left|\frac{f({\bm{x}}_{k})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{g})}{\sigma}\right|,

where the last inequality is due to the Lipschitz continuity of the gradient:
|f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})-f({\bm{x}}_{k})-\sigma[g_{k}]_{j}|\leq L_{\nabla f}\sigma^{2}/2
. Comparing the above display with (
B.18
), we can specify
\sigma=\frac{\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1}}{L_{\nabla f}\sqrt{d}}
and know that (
B.18
) is further implied by

P\bigg(\max\{|{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{g})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})|,|{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{g})-f({\bm{x}}_{k})|\}>\frac{(\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1})^{2}}{4L_{\nabla f}d}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\bigg)\leq\frac{p_{g}}{d},

which, according to (
B.2
), leads to the complexity

|\xi_{k}^{g}|\geq{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left[\frac{d}{p_{g}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{L_{\nabla f}d}{(\epsilon_{g}+\kappa_{g}\Delta_{k}^{\alpha+1})^{2}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.19)

By (
B.13
), the MoM method improves the above factor from
(d/p_{g})^{1/\delta}
to
\log(d/p_{g})
.

\bullet

Sample complexity to satisfy (
16
).
By the analysis (
B.8
), we know it suffices to have

P\left(|[{\bar{\nabla}}^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}-[\nabla^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}|>\frac{\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k}}{d}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)\leq\frac{p_{h}}{d^{2}}\quad\quad\text{ for any }i,j\in[d].

(B.20)

We note that

\displaystyle|[\bar{\nabla}^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}-[\nabla^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i},\xi_{k}^{h})-{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})+{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{h})}{\sigma^{2}}-[\nabla^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})+f({\bm{x}}_{k})}{\sigma^{2}}-[\nabla^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})}{\sigma^{2}}\right|+\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i},\xi_{k}^{h})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i})}{\sigma^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})}{\sigma^{2}}\right|+\left|\frac{{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{h})-f({\bm{x}}_{k})}{\sigma^{2}}\right|.

(B.21)

For the first term on the right hand side, by the Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})-f_{k}-\sigma([g_{k}]_{i}+[g_{k}]_{j})-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j})^{T}\nabla^{2}f_{k}(\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j})\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}L_{\nabla^{2}f}\sigma^{3}}{3},

\displaystyle\bigg|f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i})-f_{k}-\sigma[g_{k}]_{i}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\bm{e}_{i}^{T}\nabla^{2}f_{k}\bm{e}_{i}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\nabla^{2}f}\sigma^{3}}{6},

\displaystyle\bigg|f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})-f_{k}-\sigma[g_{k}]_{j}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\nabla^{2}f_{k}\bm{e}_{j}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\nabla^{2}f}\sigma^{3}}{6}.

Combining the above three inequalities, we have

\left|f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})+f({\bm{x}}_{k})-\sigma^{2}[\nabla^{2}f_{k}]_{i,j}\right|\leq\frac{5L_{\nabla^{2}f}\sigma^{3}}{6}.

Plugging the above display into (
B.21
), and specifying
\sigma=(\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k})/(L_{\nabla^{2}f}d)
, we know (
B.20
) is further implied by

P\bigg(\max\{|{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})|,|{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i},\xi_{k}^{h})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{i})|,\\
|{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j},\xi_{k}^{h})-f({\bm{x}}_{k}+\sigma\bm{e}_{j})|,|{\bar{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k},\xi_{k}^{h})-f({\bm{x}}_{k})|\}>\frac{(\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k})^{3}}{24L_{\nabla^{2}f}^{2}d^{3}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\bigg)\leq\frac{p_{h}}{d^{2}},

which, according to (
B.2
), leads to the complexity

|\xi_{k}^{h}|\geq{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left[\frac{d^{2}}{p_{h}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left[\frac{L_{\nabla^{2}f}^{2}d^{3}}{(\epsilon_{h}+\kappa_{h}\Delta_{k})^{3}}\right]^{\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}}\right).

(B.22)

By (
B.13
), the MoM method improves the above factor from
(d^{2}/p_{h})^{1/\delta}
to
\log(d^{2}/p_{h})
. Combining the results (
B.19
) and (
B.22
), we have verified the result in (
27
).

C.1
Proof of Lemma
4.5

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\big|{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bar{\mu}}_{k}}^{s_{k}}-{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bar{\mu}}_{k}}^{k}-\text{Pred}_{k}\big|

Lemma F.1

(Azuma-Hoeffding inequality,
Hoeffding (1963)
;
Azuma (1967)
)
.

Suppose
X_{k},k=0,1,\cdots
is a submartingale and
|X_{k}-X_{k-1}|<p
for some constant
p>0
almost surely. Then for all
T,c>0
, we have

P(X_{T}-X_{0}\leq-c)\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{c^{2}}{2Tp^{2}}\right\}.

Lemma F.2

(Fuk–Nagaev inequality,
(
Fan et al., 2017
, Corollary 2.5)
)
.

Assume
X_{k},k=0,1,\cdots
is a sequence of random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=0
. Denote by
S_{T}=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}X_{k}
and
\widetilde{S}_{T}=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]
. Let
\delta\geq 1
and assume
\|\mathbb{E}[|X_{k}|^{1+\delta}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]\|_{\infty}<\infty
for all
k=0,1,\cdots,T-1
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the essential supremum of a random variable. Then for all
x>0
,

P\left(S_{T}\geq x\right)\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{x^{2}}{2V}\right\}+\frac{C}{x^{1+\delta}},

where

V=\frac{1}{4}(3+\delta)^{2}e^{1+\delta}\,\|\widetilde{S}_{T}\|_{\infty}\quad\text{and}\quad C=\left(1+\frac{2}{1+\delta}\right)^{1+\delta}\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[|X_{k}|^{1+\delta}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]\right\|_{\infty}.
