Adolescent coracoid morphology: a computed tomography morphometric analysis of growth across age groups
Authors: Thomas K. Moore, Stefan Bauer, Cameron Muirhead, William Blakeney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoracoid transfer procedures, particularly the Latarjet, are increasingly employed to manage recurrent anterior shoulder instability in adolescents, given arthroscopic Bankart failure rates exceeding 40% in this young, active population. Despite this growing use, the morphologic characteristics of the adolescent coracoid remain poorly described. The purpose of this study was to describe the morphology and dimensions of the coracoid through adolescence to give insights and guidance for the planning of coracoid transfer procedures.MethodsIn this basic science morphometric study, 146 computed tomography scans from 11- to 17-year-old patients were included. Three-dimensional reconstructions were analyzed to obtain coracoid morphometric data, including length, width, and height at key anatomical landmarks. Morphological classification was conducted per established systems. Age-stratified analysis was performed. Interobserver reliability was assessed.ResultsMean coracoid length from tip to base was 41.5 mm (standard deviation [SD] = 4.6, confidence interval [CI]: 40.8-42.3). Mean length from tip to midsection and tip to elbow was 16.1 mm (SD = 3.1, CI: 15.6-16.6) and 25.4 mm (SD = 3.9 mm, CI: 24.8-26.1), respectively. Most dimensions significantly increased in size incrementally each year. Length of the coracoid in both coronal and axial views did not significantly increase between the ages of 11 and 13. By age 14, mean coracoid length was >40 mm and increased significantly with every subsequent year.ConclusionAdolescent coracoid morphology evolves with age. By mid-adolescence (14 years for both males and females), the adolescent coracoid has similar dimensions to that of the adult, suggesting it may support the feasibility of adult dual-fixation techniques in coracoid transfer procedures. Detailed pre-operative imaging and patient-specific planning remains essential to guide decision-making.

Traumatic anterior shoulder dislocations are common among young people.4 Recurrence rates are high following a primary dislocation in both skeletally mature and immature patients, with up to 94% experiencing subsequent instability.1 Arthroscopic Bankart repair is associated with high failure rates in patients with glenoid bone loss. Even in the absence of bone loss, younger age has been shown to significantly increase recurrence rates compared with the Latarjet procedure.3,6,19,29 In recent years, coracoid transfer has been used in younger patients to manage recurrent anterior instability.8,13,16 Concerns remain regarding a technically difficult procedure in skeletally immature patients with associated complication risks.8,10 This leads to difficult surgical decision-making.

Multiple coracoid transfer techniques have been described. The most commonly used are the Latarjet and Bristow procedures. Recently, the Latarjet procedure has been shown to have low recurrence rates with no significant effect on growth in the skeletally immature patient.8,16 A 2022 study by Waltenspül et al29 reported excellent function, satisfaction, and only 6% recurrence in adolescents undergoing Latarjet, compared to 57% in those with arthroscopic Bankart repair. However, a commentary published in the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery criticized the broad application of their conclusion, highlighting the importance of coracoid morphology in the selection of appropriate candidates for the Latarjet procedure: “…smaller coracoid size may require a smaller-than-preferred screw diameter…,” as well as emphasizing the associated risks.10

The morphology of the coracoid is well documented in the adult population,9,15,18,20 but little literature describes its developmental changes through adolescence.22 With bone block procedures increasingly utilized for recurrent instability in this age group, knowledge of the characteristics, morphology, and trends of the adolescent coracoid is required. The purpose of this study was to describe the morphology and dimensions of the coracoid through adolescence to give insights and guidance for the planning of coracoid transfer procedures. The authors hypothesized that by mid-adolescence, the coracoid morphology and dimensions would be similar to adult values and a size sufficient to support a coracoid transfer procedure with a dual-fixation technique.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

Ethics approval was obtained through our local HREC (no. RGS0000005420) prior to commencement of the study. A search of the Western Australia radiology database for computed tomography (CT) scans that included the shoulder was conducted. Dedicated shoulder CTs were first searched. After inclusion of all appropriate studies, CT chests were searched. Patients were included if they were aged 11-17 year old, had a CT scan with complete visualization of the glenoid and coracoid, and slice thickness ≤1 mm. Patients were excluded if either glenoid or coracoid fractures/dysplasia/bone lesions were present; or if the entire glenoid or coracoid was not visible or if slice thickness was >1 mm. If both shoulders were well visualized on the study, both were included in the study.

Data collection

A list of includable studies was created, and three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions were created using syngo.via software (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). This list was divided among 2 independent and blinded observers, and morphometric measurements were carried out as described below. A standardized protocol of measurement techniques was established, and a training session was held with the investigators prior to commencement of collection of data. Oversight was provided by the senior author. Forty-one studies were measured by both observers and interobserver reliability testing was conducted.

Demographic data including age, laterality, and indication for the scan were recorded. Statistical analysis

A power analysis determined that a sample size of 119 shoulders was necessary to detect a 2.5% change in coracoid length (α = 0.05, β = 0.05, power = 0.95), using previously published adult coracoid length data7,21,26,28 with a mean of 43.65 mm and standard deviation (SD) of 3.3.

The mean (SD) and median (interquartile range) were used for descriptive statistics of continuous variables and numbers (percentage) for categorical variables. To further account for within-subject correlations in bilateral morphometric measures, the generalized estimating equation was applied for assessing associations between age, sex, and laterality with morphometric measures. The exchangeable working correlation matrix was applied for estimating the correlation matrix. In addition, the least square means and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to estimate the mean adjusted morphometric measures by age groups, sex, and laterality. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), two-way random, absolute agreement, single measures, (ICC 2,1) with 95% CIs, were calculated for all continuous measurements, and Cohen κ and percent agreement for categorical variables were calculated for the assessment of interobserver reliability. ICC values of <0.5, 0.5-0.75, 0.75-0.9, and >0.9 were considered poor, moderate, good, and excellent, respectively.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and R language 4.3.0 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), with a two-tailed alpha of 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

In total, 167 studies were converted into 3D reconstructions. Twenty-one studies were excluded due to glenoid/coracoid fracture/dysplasia/bone lesion, leaving 146 studies that could be included in the current study. Laterality was split with 72 right and 74 left. Eighty-nine were male and 57 female. Mean age was 14.9 (SD = 1.6). Seventy-two CTs were dedicated shoulder CTs, the remainder were obtained from chest CTs. Malignancy and lung disease were the most common indication for CT scan (Appendix). Reliability

Interobserver reliability testing resulted in excellent scores across the majority of measurements, including length from tip to base in both coronal and axial planes. ICCs were otherwise good, with the exception of coronal height at the elbow being moderate, ICC = 0.61 (Appendix). Categorical classifications were less reliable between observers with κ scores between 0.01 and 0.48.

Morphometric measures

Overall, the mean length from tip to base was 41.5 mm (SD = 4.6, CI: 40.8-42.3), and 41 mm (SD = 4.8, CI: 40.2-41.7) when measured from the coronal and axial views, respectively. The mean width at the tip, midsection, and elbow was 9.8 mm (SD = 2.1, CI: 9.5-10.1), 12.9 mm (SD = 1.7, CI: 12.6-13.2), and 13.7 mm (SD = 1.8, CI: 13.4-14), respectively. The mean height at the tip, midsection and elbow was 8.3 mm (SD = 1.6, CI: 8.1-8.6), 10.9 mm (SD = 1.6, CI: 10.6-11.2), and 15.1 mm (SD = 2.3 mm, CI: 14.8-15.5 mm), respectively. Age effects

Generalized estimating equation models demonstrated significant annual increases in nearly all dimensions from age 13 onward (all P < .01; Appendix). Length of the coracoid in both coronal and axial views did not significantly increase between the ages of 11 and 13 (mean of 36.7 mm and 36.2 mm at 11 year old to means of 39.7 mm and 38.8 mm at 13 year old, respectively). For every year increase in age from 13 to 17, the coracoid length significantly increased in both views (P values <.01). Coronal length from tip to elbow fluctuated between the ages of 11 and 14 before showing significant incremental growth (Fig. 2, Table II, Appendix). Axial width at the elbow never showed any significant incremental growth; however, axial width at the tip showed incremental growth commencing after 14 years of age (Fig. 3).Figure 2Coracoid length with age and sex comparison.Figure 3Bar chart demonstrating changes in morphology over time.

Discussion

The most important finding of this study is that by mid-adolescence (14 years for both males and females), most patients demonstrate coracoid morphology and dimensions similar to published adult values and likely sufficient to support a coracoid transfer procedure with dual fixation using an adult technique and screw size. The majority of parameters demonstrated a steady increase in size with age. These data may assist surgical planning, assessment of graft feasibility, and future implant design and biomechanical research.

The literature describing the adolescent coracoid is sparse, with the authors finding only one paper that could be compared to the current study.22 However, Posey et al focused on the relationship between patient height and coracoid length, finding height to be the strongest predictor of coracoid length. Although the overall mean age of participants in their study was over 18 years of age, older than our cohort, our study focused on the chronological age rather than height or weight in a younger cohort than Posey et al.

The adult coracoid has been well described in the literature. In a cadaveric study, Dolan et al7 found a mean coracoid length of 45.6 mm (SD = 4.2 mm), within one SD of ages 14 and over in our study. However, this study found generally wider and taller coracoids. Salzmann et al24 conducted a cadaveric study finding a mean coracoid length of 43.1 mm (SD = 2.2) and tip height of 8.2 mm (SD = 1), again within one SD of ages 14 and above in these dimensions. However, they found wider coracoids compared to our adolescent cohort. Rios et al23 conducted a large cadaveric study of 120 scapulae, finding a mean coracoid length of 45.2 mm (SD = 4.1), once again finding a generally wider coracoid compared to our study. Imma et al14 found a mean coracoid length of 37.9 mm (SD = 4.3 mm) among the Malaysian population in 2017, which is within one SD of even our 11-year-old cohort. Kim et al17 found a mean tip to elbow length of 19.2 mm (SD = 2 mm) among a Korean population in a 2024 study, again quite similar across all our ages, although this measurement is not standardized and may have had slight methodological differences. Terra et al28 published their findings of mean coracoid length of 42.6 mm (SD = 2.6 mm) in their cadaveric study. Our findings demonstrated that from 14 years of age, the mean coracoid length exceeded 40 mm. This suggests that by this age, the coracoid process approaches adult dimensions in length; however, the mature coracoid is generally described as being slightly wider and taller in vertical height. It is important to note that comparison between our findings and the available literature is limited by the slight variations in methodology between studies, variations in indications for the respective studies and the dominance of data pertaining to the adult coracoid and lack of pediatric data.

Currently, there are multiple described coracoid transfer procedures. Most commonly used are the Latarjet and Bristow. These exist as both arthroscopic and open procedures, and multiple modifications have been described to the original techniques described by Latarjet and Helfet, respectively. The Bristow requires 10-15 mm of a coracoid graft, and for the Latarjet, 20-25 mm are recommended.5,12,25 The findings of this study suggest that the majority of adolescents aged 14 and older possess coracoid grafts sufficient in length and width to accommodate standard Latarjet techniques. However, when undertaking the osteotomy for these procedures, it is essential to consider the soft tissue structures attached to the coracoid, such as the coracoacromial ligament (sometimes sacrificed), the essential coracoclavicular ligaments (always preserved), and the pectoralis minor attachment. The location of these structures in the adolescent has not been described in the present study and may play a role in selecting an appropriate candidate for coracoid transfer. This study did not assess physeal status and instead relied on chronological age, which may not reflect developmental stage. There is a reluctance to perform a Latarjet in the skeletally immature patient, although there is evidence that suggests it is safe in this cohort.8,13,16

Variations in morphology of the coracoid have been described using various categorical systems, including those proposed by Xin et al, Asal et al, and Hardy et al.2,11,30 In terms of proportional frequency, our distribution of morphology types differed from those reported in Xin et al and Asal et al's respective adult cohorts. All 3 systems demonstrated poor interobserver reliability, suggesting that these categorical descriptors may be prone to subjectivity and have limited reproducibility and may have limited reliability for clinical or research applications. The low interobserver scores seen in these systems prevent any conclusions being drawn from these results. The lower number of gourd shaped coracoids may be related to forces through the coracoid, which are yet to cause the bony remodeling that may lead to a gourd shape. The osteophytic tip described by Asal et al suggests a degenerative process that is likely yet to occur in the young coracoid, which may explain the lower numbers of these types seen in our cohort. The clinical relevance of these morphologies in adolescents remains unclear and may not inform surgical decision-making.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The CT scans analyzed were obtained for clinical purposes unrelated to this research; therefore, imaging was not acquired using a standardized research protocol. However, those with fracture/dysplasia/bone lesions were excluded. Variation in acquisition parameters, such as slice orientation, may have influenced measurement accuracy. Nevertheless, the use of 3D reconstructions allowed reliable measurement of all predefined morphometric parameters. Our study did not examine markers of skeletal maturity, height, or weight, variables that may be associated with coracoid size. Future work may explore the effect of these variables on coracoid morphology. Comparison with prior literature is limited by heterogeneity in measurement techniques across studies. Finally, the cross-sectional design does not account for individual longitudinal growth patterns.

Conclusion

Adolescent coracoid morphology evolves with age. By mid-adolescence (14 years for both males and females), most individuals have sufficient coracoid bone stock that may support the feasibility of adult dual fixation techniques in coracoid transfer procedures. Detailed pre-operative imaging and patient-specific planning remains essential to guide decision-making.
