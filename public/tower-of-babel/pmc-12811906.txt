Jobs that suit women: A mixed-methods study of Chinese college women’s perceived benevolent sexism, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors
Authors: Qing Wang, Qinyue Zhu, Jingxin Li, Rongrong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBenevolent sexism (BS) may implicitly influence women’s career trajectories. This study examined the relationship between Chinese college women’s BS attitudes and experiences, their career aspirations and exploration behaviors, and the underlying mechanisms during early career development.MethodsAn explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was employed. Phase 1 (N = 427) quantitatively assessed the relationships among BS, career aspirations, and exploration behaviors. Phase 2 (N = 26) utilized Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) to explore the culturally specific understanding of BS and its perceived career impact.ResultsQuantitative findings indicated that BS experience positively predicted both career aspirations and exploration behaviors, mediated by career decision-making self-efficacy. Qualitative analysis revealed that alignment with traditional gender norms (e.g., choosing “female” jobs) paradoxically yielded positive outcomes, including increased family support and enhanced career self-efficacy, despite BS awareness.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that Chinese college women internalize benevolent sexism in their career development. The impact of this internalization varies significantly based on its alignment with cultural norms and its integration into their emerging career identity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03844-0.

Background

Benevolent sexism (BS) hinders women’s career advancement and personal development, particularly given the growing number of Chinese college women in early career stages [1]. BS is a form of sexism marked by subjectively favorable attitudes and behaviors that nonetheless reinforce restrictive and stereotypical views of women by confining them to traditional gender roles [2]. It sustains women’s subordination and adversely affects their career trajectories [3, 4]. For instance, a male manager might decline to recommend his female colleague for a demanding new client, claiming it was “to protect her from stress and overworking”. Due to its benevolent appearance, BS is harder for women to recognize than hostile sexism, which subtly hinders their career development [2, 5]. Although a growing body of research in Western contexts has demonstrated the negative influence of BS on women’s career choices and development [6, 7], few empirical studies have examined its impact on Chinese women’s career development. Focusing specifically on Chinese college women at early career stages, this study investigates the effects of BS, including benevolent sexism attitude (BSA) and benevolent sexism experience (BSE), on their career aspirations and career-exploration behaviors within a collectivist cultural framework.

The impact of BS on women’s career development

The relationship between BS, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors

According to Career Aspiration Development Theory (CADT) [8], BSA and BSE may undermine women’s career aspirations by adversely shaping their self-concept through the internalization of traditional gender roles [9–12]. Empirical studies in Western settings indicate that women with higher BSA exhibit lower self-esteem and perceive themselves as less competent at work, which may lead to reduced career aspirations [13–15]. Furthermore, in workplaces characterized by BS, male managers may assign fewer challenging tasks to women employees based on the perception that they lack career motivation and are unworthy of developmental investment [16, 17]. Such BSE can reduce women’s autonomy and perceived opportunities for growth, further limiting their career aspirations [18].

BS may also negatively affect career-exploration behaviors, which include both self- and environmental exploration [19–22]. Women with BSA may restrict their career goals and interests to conform to traditional gender roles [9, 23]. For instance, they may adopt a dependency-oriented approach by relying on male assistance in the workplace and sacrifice their own career development to support their male partners’ career goals, thereby diverting themselves from personal career plans [24–26]. Meanwhile, BSE may reinforce beliefs in traditional roles, restrict opportunities for skill development through challenging tasks, and even cause career interruptions [17, 18, 27]. Consequently, women with BSE may develop negative self-views and experience psychological distress [28–30], which impede engagement in career-exploration behaviors [31–33].

Based on this evidence, the current study proposes Hypothesis 1: BSA and BSE negatively predict career aspiration and career-exploration behaviors.

The mediating role of career decision-making self-efficacy

Career decision-making self-efficacy plays a pivotal role in the career development process and may serve as the explanatory mechanism in the relationship between BS, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors. According to the Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) [34], both BSA and BSE may negatively impact women’s career decision-making self-efficacy. Previous studies indicate that BSA is associated with reduced workplace self-esteem and self-efficacy [11, 35–37]. Similarly, BSE can restrict women’s opportunities to develop skills through challenging tasks [14], fostering negative self-perceptions and low competence [28, 38, 39]. In turn, career decision-making self-efficacy influences individuals’ career development processes, including career aspirations and career-exploration behaviors [34, 40–45]. Thus, career decision-making self-efficacy may mediate the relationships between BSA, BSE, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors.

Although extensive research in Western cultures has examined the effects of BS, few empirical studies have investigated the mechanism through which BSA and BSE influence career aspirations and career-exploration behaviors, particularly the mediating role of career decision-making self-efficacy. The current study aims to explore this mechanism and proposes Hypothesis 2: career decision-making self-efficacy mediates the relationships between BSA, BSE, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors.

BS in China and its impact on Chinese college women

Research on BS in collectivist countries often relies on Western-based theories and empirical evidence, with few studies examining its effects in China through a culturally specific lens [7, 46, 47]. Thus, the mechanisms by which BS shapes Chinese college women’s career development in collectivist societies remain underexplored, as does its potentially different impact in collectivist compared to individualistic cultures.

In collectivist cultures such as China, the pronounced familial influence and low relational fluidity intensify the transmission of BS, particularly from parents and partners, during the early stages of women’s career development [48–50]. This phenomenon is deeply rooted in traditional gender roles, which prescribe women as primary caregivers responsible for a domestic harmony and men as the main breadwinners [51–53]. Consequently, families frequently encourage young women to pursue careers characterized by stability and lower intentioned advice, which often obscures its underlying biased nature and makes the sexism difficult to recognize. Given the current fierce competitiveness of the Chinese job market and young women’s dependence on external guidance during career exploration, they may be especially susceptible to internalizing these BS-related career beliefs. By focusing on Chinese college women, this study aims to examine the special impact and mechanisms of BS in this distinct context and explore how these dynamics may differ from those observed in individualistic cultures.

The impact of BS on Chinese college women

Some cross-sectional studies in China have primarily explore the impact of BS on Chinese women sample, though lacking a situated perspective and detailed discussion on cultural specificity. Notably, unlike the consistently negative effects observed in Western settings, empirical studies involving Chinese women have yielded mixed results regarding the impact of BS on career development. For instance, some study found that women’s BSE and their male partners’ BSA negatively predicted their job flourishing, self-esteem and career growth [54–56], whereas another reported no significant relationship between BS and career self-efficacy among Chinese college women [57]. Adding complexity to the understanding, a recent study demonstrated that BS positively predicted Chinese women’s self-efficacy, suggesting that they may justify traditional gender roles and accept BS when confronting ideological dissonance [58]. These inconsistent findings highlight the need for further investigation into how BSA and BSE influence Chinese college women and through what mechanisms. Cultural factors, such as the strong familial influence and low relational fluidity characteristic of collectivist societies, may help account for these observed variations, thus warranting further exploration.

In addition, although extensive research in Western contexts has explored the effects of BS on middle-aged women’s promotion barriers and leadership attainment [6, 7, 59], less attention has been paid to its influence on college women in early career stages. This period is critical for developing career self-concept, articulating aspirations, and engaging in career-exploration behaviors [60]. Yet, during this phase, women often face challenges such as occupational stereotypes, constrained vocational interests, multiple-role burdens, inequities in career exploration, and patronizing or sexist support [61–64] - all of which may be linked to BS. This study aims to explore the characteristics of BS and its impact on the early career development among Chinese college women within a collectivist cultural context.

The present study

The existing literature lacks a comprehensive examination of the mechanisms through which benevolent sexism attitude (BSA) and benevolent sexism experience (BSE) influence career aspirations and career-exploration behaviors, particularly among Chinese college women at early career stages and within collectivist cultural contexts. To address this gap, this study utilized a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design to investigate three key areas: (a) the relationships among Chinese college women’s BSA, BSE, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors within a collectivist cultural setting; (b) the potential mediating role of career decision-making self-efficacy via a quantitative investigation; and (c) the contextualized mechanisms underlying these relationships via qualitative inquiry. The detailed design and methods are described in the following section.

Methods

Research design: the explanatory sequential Mixed-method design

Choosing a research design that best captured the complex phenomenon of women’s BS and its relationship with career-related decisions required careful consideration. The present study was located within a transformative research paradigm [65], within which mixed methods are preferred to highlight issues under investigation and provide voice to these issues. As this study concerned not only the links between BS and women’s careers but also the intrinsic influencing and explanatory mechanisms situated in the Chinese context, combining qualitative and quantitative methods could address our research aims better than using one method alone [66]. This study used an explanatory sequential design and involved extensive data collection in both phases of the study [67]. We chose a survey method for Phase 1 of the study, enabling us to describe and explore the relationships between BSA, BSE, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors and the mediating role of career decision-making self-efficacy. In Phase 2, we used interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) [68] to explore how Chinese college women understand BS and explain how BS influenced their career decisions and career development. The IPA approach has been widely used in feminism and career studies in which individuals’ distinctive life experiences and the processes by which they interpreted their lived experiences are closely researched [69–73]. IPA emphasizes an in-depth exploration of individuals’ lived experiences throughout a dual interpretative process: participants’ comprehension of their own experiences and researchers’ interpretation of participants’ understanding [70]. The process can facilitate a more profound comprehension of Chinese college women’s experiences of BS. Compared with other qualitative methods (such as thematic analysis), IPA could help us gain access to the most intuitive and meaningful constructs of the participants’ experiences and drive the reflective hermeneutics of how the Chinese culture influenced their interpretations of BSA, BSE, and related career development. Through the sequential explanatory mixed-methods design, participants’ lived experiences could supplement and deepen the quantitative findings. Compared with previous research on BS, this design may provide more contextualized explanations of the impact of BS and offer additional practical insights.

Phase 1: quantitative study

Participants

In Phase 1, Chinese college women were recruited via an online platform in Mainland China comparable to MTurk. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the authors’ institution. Eligible participants were Chinese college women who were either within two years of graduation or had graduated within the past two years but had not yet secured employment. Participants provided electronic informed consent and completed self-report questionnaires, which took approximately 15–20 min. Those who failed the attention checks or completed the survey in fewer than 6 min were excluded (N = 260).

The final sample consisted of 427 Chinese college women from a leading comprehensive university in Shanghai, with a mean age of 22.02 years (SD = 1.78). Among them, 86.4% identified as heterosexual, 58.3% majored in arts, humanities, or social science, 64.1% were currently or previously in a romantic relationship, and 76.6% had internship experience.

Data collection

Benevolent sexism attitude (BSA)

The Chinese version of the BS subscale from the Ambivalent Sexism Inventory [2, 74] was used to assess BSA. The subscale includes nine items rated on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (disagree strongly) to 6 (agree strongly), e.g., “A good woman should be set on a pedestal by her man.” In this study, the scale had a relatively low Cronbach’s alpha of 0.69; however, its reliability was equivalent to that of Chen and He’s [74] version (α = 0.71).

Benevolent sexism experience (BSE)

The Experiences with Benevolent Sexism Scale (EBSS) [15] was revised to assess BSE through a back-translation process. The 25-item scale comprises three dimensions: heterosexual intimacy (e.g., “You have been put on a pedestal by your romantic partner.”), complementary gender differentiation (e.g., “You have been informed that as a woman, you have a more refined sense of culture and tastes than do men.”), and protective paternalism (e.g., “People questioned your ability to handle situations by yourself.”). The scale rating was modified referring to the Experiences with Ambivalent Sexism Scale for STEM majors [36] and used the following labels: 1 = the event has never happened to you; 2 = the event happens rarely; 3 = the event happens sometimes; 4 = the event happens often; 5 = the event happens frequently; and 6 = the event happens very frequently. Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.87 in this study.

Career aspirations

The 24-item Career Aspiration Scale-Revised (CAS-R) [10] was revised to measure career aspirations through a back-translation process. The scale comprises three dimensions: achievement aspiration (e.g., “I want to be among the very best in my field.”), leadership aspiration (e.g., “I hope to become a leader in my career field.”), and educational aspiration (e.g., “I plan to reach the highest level of education in my field.”). Eight items of each dimension were rated on a scale from 1 (not at all true of me) to 5 (very true of me). Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.93 in this study.

Career-exploration behaviors

The Chinese version of Career Exploration Survey (CES) was used to measure career-exploration behaviors [21, 75]. The 12-item scale comprises two dimensions: environmental exploration (e.g., “Sought information on specific areas of career interest.”) and self-exploration (e.g., “Reflected on how my past integrates with my future.”). The participants were instructed to rate the items from 1 (disagree strongly) to 5 (agree strongly) according to their actual exploration behaviors in the previous three months. Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.84 in this study.

Career decision-making self-efficacy

The Chinese version of Career Decision-Making Self-Efficacy Scale-Short Form (CDMSE-SF) was used to measure career decision-making self-efficacy [76, 77]. The 25-item scale comprises five dimensions rated from 1 (no confidence at all) to 5 (complete confidence): self-appraisal (e.g., “Accurately assess your abilities”), occupational information (e.g., “Use the Internet to find information about occupations that interest you”), goal selection (e.g., “Select one major from a list of potential majors you are considering”), planning (e.g., “Make a plan of your goals for the next five years”), and problem-solving (e.g., “Determine the steps to take if you are having academic trouble with an aspect of your chosen major”). Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.93 in this study.

Data analysis

SPSS 25.0 and Mplus 8.3 were used for data analysis. First, common method bias was examined, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and chi-square tests were used to investigate the differences in the demographic characteristics among the main variables. Second, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore the correlations among the variables. Multiple linear regression and path analysis were then conducted to explore the mechanism through which BSA and BSE influence career aspirations and career-exploration behaviors and the mediating role of career decision-making self-efficacy.

Results

Descriptive Analysis and Demographic Information

Harman’s one-factor test showed 21 items with an initial eigenvalue greater than one, and the maximum factor variance explained was 17.60% (< 40%), which indicated that there was no significant common method bias [78].

Additionally, there was a significant and positive correlation between the participants’ ages and BSE (r = .24, p < .05). The positive direct effect of BSA on career aspirations

Results from multiple linear regression indicated that BSA positively predicted career aspirations (β = 0.146, p <.01). However, as BSA was not significantly correlated with career-exploration behaviors or career decision-making self-efficacy, no mediation analyses were conducted with BSA as the independent variable.

The positive effect of BSE and the mediation effect of career decision-making Self-efficacy

Mediation tests were performed with BSE as the independent variable (see Tables 3 and 4). Career decision-making self-efficacy significantly mediated the relationship between BSE and career aspirations (BootLLCI = 0.036, BootULCI = 0.115), as well as between BSE and career-exploration behaviors (BootLLCI = 0.034, BootULCI = 0.103). As shown in Fig. 1, BSE significantly predicted career decision-making self-efficacy (β = 0.20, t = 3.96, p <.001), which in turn positively predicted both career aspirations (β = 0.42, t = 10.50, p <.001) and career-exploration behaviors (β = 0.36, t = 8.20, p <.001).

The unexpected positive effect of BS on Chinese college women’s career development

Despite using measures of BSA and BSE validated in Western contexts, the Phase 1 findings revealed a result contrary to the initial hypotheses: benevolent sexism did not hinder, but rather unexpectedly enhanced Chinese college women’s career decision-making self-efficacy, career aspirations, and exploration behaviors. This outcome suggested a paradoxical influence, where the seemingly warm and supportive façade of BS may lead to the internalization of gendered beliefs, obscuring its underlying limiting effects. Consequently, Phase 2 employed a qualitative approach to deeply explore the participants’ perceptions, lived experiences, and nuanced understanding of BS and its complex influence on their career development. The subsequent discussion integrates findings from both the quantitative and qualitative phases to offer a comprehensive interpretation.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in Chinese using digital communication platforms (e.g., WeChat, Zoom, or Tencent Meeting), with each session lasting approximately 25–45 min (M = 39.15 min). A 24-year-old female master’s student in the research group conducted the interviews under the supervisor’s guidance. The semi-structured interview was designed based on the following questions: How do Chinese college women perceive and understand BS? How does BS affect their career aspirations and career-exploration behaviors during early-stage career development? The detailed questions were designed to be flexible and open-ended, covered six key domains (see Supplementary Material 1 for a English-translated version): (a) attitudes and personal experiences of BS; (b) career decision-making self-efficacy, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors, including influencing factors; (c) understanding of BS and its potential determinants; (d) perceived impact of BS on career-related decisions, aspirations, and exploration behaviors; (e) BS-related career challenges; and (f) desired career support.

Data analysis

Interview data were analyzed using MAXQDA software, following the five-step of IPA procedure outlined by Smith, Flowers, and Larkin [80]. First, interviews were transcribed verbatim within 48 h of completion and verified for accuracy before analysis. Second, the first participant’s transcript was repeatedly and closely read to produce initial annotations, comments, and thematic summaries. Third, the themes that emerged were identified and connections among them were examined. Fourth, transcripts from subsequent participants were analyzed to identify recurring and new themes. Finally, themes were integrated and higher-order themes were synthesized using illustrative excerpts from participants. To enhance validity, experienced researchers within the team participated in theme analysis and refinement [82], and participants were invited to review transcriptions and interpretations [83].

Theme 1: ambiguous awareness of BS

Participants demonstrated awareness of benevolent sexism (BS) across various aspects of their lives, including protective career advice, financial pressures, the balance between career and intimate relationships, and traditional gender roles. Although most participants reported experiencing BS, their attitude toward it remained ambivalent.

Protective career advice

Participants consistently reported experiencing benevolent sexism (BS) through the protective career advice offered by their parents, relatives, and friends during job seeking and career decision-making. This advice frequently took the form of suggestions that women should pursue stable, secure, and comfortable roles, such as teaching (a profession most often mentioned by the participants). For example, one participant stated: “My parents thought that girls should not work overtime, and a less-demanding job would be a good choice (07).” The participants demonstrated emotional identification with the advice’s good intentions, as noted by another: “I understand and appreciate that their advice is intended to secure a better life and a matching job for me (19).” However, this acceptance was often tempered by an awareness that the advice concealed sexism, which they viewed as “out-of-date and rigid thoughts (01).” Consequently, some participants actively “preferred freedom to stable jobs (24)” and maintained that women should prioritize personal career interests over decisions based primarily on stability and a light workload.

Financial support from partners

Within the Chinese cultural context, there is a prevailing suggestion that women should not bear excessive financial burden and are not necessarily required “to earn a lot of money (17).” Some participants felt that financial support from a male partner could alleviate certain career-development pressures on women, with one sharing that “it would definitely be great if my partner could provide me with some sort of financial support (21).” These participants often viewed such financial assistance not as an implication of dependence, but rather as “a way of equivalent exchanging (14),” serving to compensate for the additional time women dedicate to housework and childcare. Conversely, other participants noted that partner-provided financial support sometimes resulted in feeling “like a deadbeat (04).” Despite these differing attitudes toward reliance, the majority of participants agreed that women should “have their own job (21)” and maintain “financially independent (01)” status, rather than depending entirely on their partners.

Marriage advantage of suitable careers

Within Chinese culture, stable marriages are highly valued, leading to the recommendation of traditionally “light” and stable jobs (such as teaching) for women due to their high marriage-market value. These roles are often perceived to allow women to dedicate more time to relationships and family life, thereby aligning with cultural expectations that women should make sacrifices for men. As one participant noted: “My relatives often suggested that I become a teacher because they believed it would be easier for a woman who was a teacher to find a partner (25).”

However, not all participants adhered to this norm; some clearly prioritized their careers over intimate relationships, insisting that personal career plans should not be dictated by partners: “If my husband asked me to resign from my job or consider him in my career-development plan, I would turn down his request (11).” Others sought a balanced approach, emphasizing mutual effort and women’s independence: “Everything that my partner and I do must be for the benefit of us as a whole, and I should not be compromised because I am a girl (05).”

Traditional gender roles

A further justification for recommending these “suitable” jobs to women is their alignment with traditional gender roles, which makes women appear “naturally” competent in these fields and thus more likely to secure employment. Participants reported common assertions such as: “Women have more advantages as teachers due to their femininity, such as being soft, conscientious, attentive, and gentle (06)” and “Only women are capable of certain tasks in taking care of children, such as breastfeeding and child rearing (07).”

Some participants criticized these beliefs as “unreasonable” and viewed them as reinforcing occupational segregation, asserting that women are equally capable in male-dominated fields: “I believe that women can accomplish these jobs fairly well, including those that require a high level of technical skills or leadership charisma (22).” However, when lacking clear career goals, women were more likely to rely on these traditional roles, which facilitates the internalization and perpetuation of these beliefs across generations. This led some participants to accept BS as an inescapable social norm despite recognizing its bias: “I feel like there is nothing I can do in this environment (01)”; “I did not agree with these ideas as a teenager, but after entering university, they gradually made more sense to me (16).”

In summary, the qualitative data revealed that participants were not entirely unaware of the biased nature of BS, challenging the impression of complete internalization suggested by some Phase 1 findings. While participants clearly recognized and experienced BS, their responses varied: some offered firm rejection, while others demonstrated reluctant acceptance and strategic utilization of the associated benefits despite acknowledging the inherent bias.

Theme 2: the paradoxically “positive” impact of BS on career

The positive effects of BS on career development observed in Phase 1 contrasted sharply with established findings from Western contexts. To explore this apparent paradox, the following subthemes qualitatively illustrate how BS shapes Chinese college women’s career decision-making self-efficacy, career aspirations, and career-exploration behaviors.

On career decision-making self-efficacy

Participants whose career intentions aligned with their families’ BS attitudes (BSA) reported feeling supported and viewed themselves as competent in roles considered “suitable for women,” which consequently enhanced their career decision-making self-efficacy. As one participant shared, “I felt I would fit better in a job in the education field based on my parents’ suggestions; thus, I would be more confident in making this choice (01).” In sharp contrast, those whose career intentions deviated from their families’ BSA experienced self-doubt and “a slight blow to my confidence” (24), which weakened both familial support and career decision-making self-efficacy: “I do not receive much praise or encouragement, which makes me feel that insisting on my talents and goals is pointless (12).”

On career aspirations

The majority of participants believed that benevolent sexism (BS) negatively influenced their career aspirations. Strong BS attitudes often led to the view that women did not need to invest heavily in their education or careers, potentially depriving them of self-actualization and nudging them toward socially favored occupations. Although a few participants maintained high career aspirations due to strong personal convictions, stating, “I am a stubborn person. If I want to do something, I will try my best (11),” many others acknowledged BS as an unchangeable reality, even while recognizing its limiting effects. As some expressed, this led to feelings of resignation or self-doubt: “Sometimes I feel that it might make no sense to insist on my choice (12)” and “Sometimes I feel I am too indecisive to be a leader; thus, I had better do my best in my current position (09).”

On career-exploration behaviors

When others offered well-intentioned career advice, even if it was fundamentally based on benevolent sexism (BS), it paradoxically helped participants clarify their career goals by facilitating learning about different fields, understanding various career pathways, and conducting self-assessments. As some participants noted: “I will learn more about career fields and find some career alternatives after listening to their advice (13)” and “I will evaluate my suitability for a job based on others’ advice (e.g., whether they think a job is suitable for my traits (01).”

The degree of the impact

Despite the influence of benevolent sexism (BS), some participants firmly believed that it “is not a dominant factor in their career development (08).” While BS-related advice could occasionally sway their decisions, participants generally reported prioritizing independent career planning. As they explained: “It is my parents’ opinion, and it will influence my judgment and plans, but I believe I will have to follow my inner thoughts on where I want to work eventually (08)” and “I just listened to the advice, but I will not agree with others. What they said will not affect me at all (02).”

The limited impact of BS may be attributed to the greater influence of other factors, including individual professional attributes such as professional background, person-vocation fit, and self-value. One participant noted that “What kind of work I undertake in the future will primarily be determined by my major (01).” Furthermore, professional attributes like salary and benefits were important, as were family factors. Due to China’s former one-child policy, many participants were only children who strongly desired to maintain close ties with their parents, ensuring mutual support and the ability to provide care in later life. As one participant explained: “Most of my friends and I are only children who want to be close to our parents so that we can look after them when they grow older (09).”

To summarize, the results from Phase 2 were partially consistent with the Phase 1 quantitative results that participants pursuing careers considered “suitable for women” reported benefiting from the well-intentioned aspects of BS-related advice. However, the qualitative interviews offered a more nuanced view: several participants emphasized that BS was not the sole determining factor in their career development, explicitly identifying other crucial, non-BS-related influences on their career choices, a finding not captured by the quantitative data.

Theme 3: gendered job challenges

Participants reported several common challenges, including limited job information, lack of internship experience, and low future work self-salience. These issues collectively pointed to underlying difficulties such as unclear career plans, ambiguous values, and limited perceived job competence [84]. Consequently, participants often made passive career choices, which were partially guided by benevolent sexism (BS). In addition to these developmental hurdles, the majority of participants specifically highlighted two critical gender-related challenges: fixed gender roles and pervasive workplace discrimination.

Fixed gender roles

Most participants agreed that women would face greater difficulties and were more disadvantaged in the job market and workplace than men, largely due to fixed gender roles in China. As one participant noted, “One of the most serious issues we face is the gender stereotype of girls, which automatically categorizes women as appropriate or unfit for a career depending on their gender (01).” Furthermore, the implementation of China’s three-child policy since 2021 has heightened corporate concerns regarding women’s potential delays and reduced work efficiency due to pregnancies, which further solidifies traditional gender roles. As one participant shared, “The introduction of the three-child policy has made it considerably difficult for women to find jobs. Many companies have become even more hesitant to hire women (09).”

Workplace gender discrimination

Participants frequently identified workplace gender discrimination as a common and persistent challenge. Women in traditionally male-dominated fields were more likely to have their abilities questioned and subsequently faced greater job pressure. As one participant noted: “Interviewers considered women to be inferior to males in experimental skills (08).” Moreover, even in female-dominated professions such as teaching, employers increasingly preferred men and consequently set higher standards for women: “Employers unequivocally stated that boys would be preferred if they had the same abilities as girls (24).” Given the intense career competition in China (known as “nei juan,” a term describing involution and unnecessary over-competence in certain areas), these unequal criteria compel women to exert extra effort to remain competitive, which significantly increases both job pressure and psychological stress. As one participant shared: “The competition is more intense. It is difficult to choose among the best; thus, you will have to put in more effort (06).”

In addition to the quantitative findings, this theme underscores that even when participants leverage or benefit from the well-intentioned elements of BS, they still encounter significant gender-related challenges within the broader job market and workplace that often impede their long-term career progression.

Theme 4: desired career supports

Participants identified several desired forms of career support, focusing on the cultivation of career values, increased career confidence, and the improvement of gender equity in the workplace.

Cultivation of career values

Support for cultivating career values at the university level was viewed as essential for helping participants clarify their needs and make better career decisions, thereby potentially offsetting the influence of benevolent sexism (BS). As one participant noted, “I wish someone would come out and assist me in determining my true psychological needs when I am in doubt… so that I can make better decisions for myself (07).” Participants also strongly emphasized the role of supportive parenting that encourages personal growth rather than reinforcing fixed gender roles: “Most patterns of upbringing aim for women to grow up to be compassionate, virtuous, and capable of all-around caring; however, very few girls are raised to be independent, strong, brave, and straightforward… I think that if girls are told when they are young that they can be bold and do anything, they will have fewer fears (08).”

Increase in career confidence

A boost in career confidence was reported as crucial for helping participants manage stress, anxiety, and uncertainty often associated with job searches. Participants noted that both interpersonal support and internship experience were especially effective in increasing this confidence: “I believe that the experience gained from the internship will be extremely beneficial in obtaining a full-time job because it helps me understand whether my abilities match the job’s requirements (17).” Crucially, internships allowed participants to identify jobs that truly suited them, providing an evidence-based alternative to relying solely on benevolent sexism (BS)-based advice about “jobs that suit women.”

Improvement in gender equality in the workplace

Most participants emphasized the critical importance of a gender bias-free job-hunting process. As one participant noted, “I have seen many girls with different concerns. However, if I were a boy, I would not have these worries, and I would not abandon or change career plans and choices because of them. Therefore, I believe it is critical for girls to have these concerns addressed (11).” Participants also explicitly expressed a desire to work in gender-equal environments: “I am seeking a women-friendly workplace where I will not have to deal with sexism (14).”

In response to both perceived gendered job challenges and their own experiences of benevolent sexism (BSE), participants proactively suggested several structural supports. Specifically, they proposed that universities, families, and internship providers could play vital roles in cultivating women’s career values and confidence to effectively counteract BS. Ultimately, the participants expressed a clear desire to pursue careers unfettered by both benevolent and explicit workplace sexism.

Summary of qualitative results

In summary, the IPA study in Phase 2 revealed the ambiguous and paradoxical influence of BS on the career development of Chinese college women. Consistent with the quantitative findings in Phase 1, participants reported enhanced self-efficacy when their career intentions aligned with BSA expressed by family members and partners. Furthermore, although participants demonstrated clear awareness of BS, many reported strategically leveraging its well-intentioned aspects to strengthen their career aspirations and exploration behaviors. Conversely, the qualitative phase introduced nuance: unlike Phase 1, a subset of participants experienced reduced self-efficacy when their goals conflicted with BS-based expectations. Notably, most participants emphasized that BS was not the sole determinant of their career development; factors such as personal interests, academic backgrounds, and the pursuit of work–family balance were also cited as significant. Finally, participants highlighted persistent gendered career challenges, including traditional gender roles and workplace discrimination, underscoring the need for greater support in cultivating career values, building confidence, and promoting gender equality in the workplace.

Discussion

The present study examined how benevolent sexism (BS) influences Chinese college women’s career aspirations and career-exploration behaviors, along with the underlying psychological mechanisms, using a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design.

In contrast to findings reported in Western contexts [64, 85], the quantitative results from Phase 1 showed that BS experience (BSE) positively predicted both career aspirations and exploration behaviors, mediated by career decision-making self-efficacy. This positive association aligns with the seemingly warm and supportive nature of BS [59]. Phase 2 qualitative findings further elucidated this paradoxical effect, revealing that participants whose career goals aligned with external, norm-congruent expectations for “jobs that suit women” reported greater confidence and engagement in career exploration. Furthermore, some participants strategically chose to leverage the well-intentioned aspects of BS to secure employment, despite being aware of its potential for harm. Taken together, these findings suggest that the influence of BS on women’s career development is shaped by cultural mechanisms specific to the Chinese collectivist context as well as by women’s personal career identity, which will be discussed in the following sections.

Cultural mechanisms of BS internalization

Familial influence and low relational fluidity

According to the Bio-ecological Model of Human Development [86], individuals are shaped by both micro-systems (e.g., families) and macro-systems (e.g., culture). The Phase 2 qualitative findings suggest that the strong familial influence and low relational fluidity inherent in a collectivist culture may account for the paradoxical positive effects of BS observed in Phase 1. These cultural factors can intensify the internalization of BS [48–50, 87], making Chinese college women more vulnerable to its subtle influence and consequently more receptive to its perceived short-term benefits.

The career development of Chinese college women is significantly shaped by familial influence and embedded within a specific socio-cultural context [88, 89]. Their parents, who often strongly identify with traditional gender roles and a gendered division of labor, may frequently recommend “appropriate” careers for women under the guise of acting in their children’s best interests [4, 90]. Such early familial exposure to BS may persist throughout adolescence and emerging adulthood, critical periods for career identity formation [91, 92]. Due to the low relational fluidity characteristic of collectivist cultures [93], Chinese college women demonstrate lower sensitivity to familial BS and greater adherence to socially prescribed gender ideologies compared to their counterparts in individualistic cultures [90, 94]. Cross-cultural studies further indicate that Chinese women more likely to endorse BS than American women [47, 80]. Consequently, Chinese college women often internalize BS and adopt career preferences aligned with parental expectations, particularly favoring “jobs that suit women” [26, 95]. When career choices align with parental views, women receive greater familial support, report higher career decision-making self-efficacy, and engage more actively in exploring gender-traditional careers, thereby reinforcing the cycle of BS internalization driven by these perceived benefits [44, 96, 97].

Therefore, within the Chinese context marked by strong familial influence and low relational fluidity, Chinese college women are more likely to internalize BS, conform to traditional gender roles, and appear to derive short-term benefits in career aspirations and exploration behaviors.

Norm-congruent and Norm-deviant Self-identification

The findings also revealed a context-dependent influence of BS on the career aspirations of Chinese college women, with norm-congruent and norm-deviant self-identification and career identity serving as key explanatory factors [98, 99].

Norm-congruent women, who adhere to traditional gender roles, tend to establish gender-typical career goals and actionable plans, which provide consistent motivation for career exploration [9]. They are more likely to receive social support, derive gender-based advantages, and report higher career decision-making self-efficacy in gender-role-congruent careers [15, 100, 101]. Moreover, those with higher self-efficacy typically engage more actively in career exploration and planning [40, 42]. Thus, women who endorse traditional roles may appear to benefit from BS in their career development. The norm-congruent characteristics of the sample likely explain the unexpected positive effects of benevolent sexism (BS) observed in this study. Given the specific context of Chinese college women, some participants adopted norm-congruent career goals driven by intense job-market competition and underdeveloped career identities typical of this early career stage. When experiencing uncertainty or lack of direction, these women tended to conform to social expectations or strategically leverage BS to secure initial employment. However, such conformity may prove detrimental in the long run, as prior research on women at later career stages has consistently demonstrated that BS undermines self-esteem and career growth, suggesting that the apparent short-term benefits may actually hinder long-term development [52, 53, 87].

In contrast, norm-deviant women may experience more complex effects. According to system justification theory, serves as an ideological tool that reinforces gender inequality and can adversely affect women who deviate from traditional norms [30, 102]. Empirical evidence suggests that norm-deviant women with less defined career identities may experience diminished self-efficacy, engagement, and performance due to BS [103]. As a result, they often experience higher levels of sexism in the career decision-making, particularly when interacting with individuals who uphold traditional gender beliefs [39].

However, norm-deviant women with strong career identities may be less susceptible to BS. Motivational intensity theory posits that a well-defined career identity fosters clear and stable self-perceptions of goals, interests, and talents [104], promoting greater goal-directed investment, enhanced motivation, and persistence in challenging tasks [105–107]. Phase 2 interviews indicated that participants with stronger career identities engaged in comprehensive, independent career decision-making despite exposure to BS. Thus, this study suggest that a strong career identity may buffer against the adverse influences of BS, enabling women to persist in self-chosen career paths.

Theoretical and practical implications

This study advances the theoretical understanding of benevolent sexism within the Chinese cultural context, particularly among Chinese college women at the early career stage, via a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design. The mixed-methods design not only clarified the relationships among the examined variables, but also provided context-rich and real-life explanations for the unexpected association between BS and career-related outcomes, which enhanced the rigor and ecological validity of the findings. Drawing on both quantitative and qualitative evidence, the findings suggest that cultural dynamics, such as familial influence and low relational fluidity, along with women’s self-identification as either norm-congruent or norm-deviant, and their career identity, help explain the unexpected positive associations between BS, career aspirations, and career exploration. These results indicate that, despite recognizing the harms of BS, Chinese college women may strategically leverage its seemingly supportive aspects to navigate labor-market constraints. This challenges assumptions inherent in ambivalent sexism theory, which was developed in Western contexts, and suggests that existing frameworks may not fully capture the experiences of women in China.

In addition to cultural factors, the study underscores the importance of career development stage and career identity in shaping responses to BS. Future research should incorporate additional context-specific factors and qualitative methodologies to develop more comprehensive models of BS in non-Western settings.

The study also highlights the limited attention given to BS in career education on Chinese campuses, pointing to a need for programs that directly address socially embedded gender beliefs. University career services should emphasize that men and women are equally competent professionally, support women in recognizing BS especially in family and intimate contexts and encourage proactive help-seeking behaviors. Career guidance should also help students prioritize personal interests, values, and aspirations, rather than gendered expectations of “suitable” work.

Beyond BS, the findings identify broader influences on Chinese women’s early career development, such as person-job fit, future work self-salience, career values, sense of calling, and perceptions of meaningful work [108, 109, 110]. Strengthening women’s career competence, fostering self-exploration, and cultivating coherent career values through experiential learning and mentoring could thus promote more informed, autonomous career development.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of the current study should be noted. First, the quantitative sample consisted primarily of women students from universities with a high proportion of female enrollment and limited job experience. Future studies should broaden recruitment to include participants with more diverse educational backgrounds, geographical regions, socioeconomic statuses, and career stages. Additionally, cross-cultural samples are needed to further clarify the cultural specificity of benevolent sexism (BS) and its impact.

Second, the quantitative phase relied solely on self-report measures and assessed career aspirations without examining their alignment with societal expectations. Future research should investigate whether individual difference variables, such as career identity, or the congruence between career choices and social norms, moderate the relationship between BS and women’s long-term career outcomes.

Third, the cross-sectional design of the quantitative phase limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to examine how BS attitude and BS experience change over time and influence career trajectories. Furthermore, intervention studies could be developed and evaluated to mitigate the negative effects of BS and to support Chinese college women in their career development.

Fourth, the potential for an experimenter effect in the qualitative phase should be acknowledged, as the interviewer shared a similar background (Chinese college woman at an early career stage) with the participants. To minimize interpersonal influence and reduce shared social identity bias, future studies should employ interviewers who are demographically unrelated to the target population.

Lastly, this study did not distinguish between intrapersonal and interpersonal forms of BS. Given that BS perpetuated by others (e.g., managers or partners) can influence women’s access to social support and career advancement at both interpersonal and intrapersonal levels [6], future research should explore the combined effects of personal and environmental BS on women’s career development.

Conclusion

The mix-methods study demonstrates that BS paradoxically enhances Chinese college women’ s career aspirations and exploration behaviors through the mediation of career decision-making self-efficacy, reflecting the internalization of BS and its perceived short-term benefits. Although Chinese college women were broadly aware of BS, its impact were contingent upon both the norm-congruent and norm-deviant self-identification and the clarity of their career identity.
