Treatment Patterns in Pediatric Hidradenitis Suppurativa: Time to Treatment Initiation, Initial Treatment, and Time to Switching
Authors: Shaliz Aflatooni, Andrea Ure, Kimberly Smart, Julian A. Cortes, Gabriela Palma, Nicole Johnsen, Yvonne Nong, April W. Armstrong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHidradenitis suppurativa (HS) causes significant morbidity in children. Treatment patterns across sex, race, and ethnicity in pediatric HS have not been thoroughly evaluated. We aim to characterize treatment patterns in pediatric HS, including (1) time to treatment initiation, (2) first prescribed HS treatment, and (3) time to switching following initial therapy across demographic factors.MethodsWe conducted a real‐world, retrospective cohort study of patients < 18 years with a HS diagnosis, using a large U.S. national electronic health record database (2005–2025). Treatments prescribed within 1 year after HS diagnosis were categorized as systemic antibiotics, topicals, hormonal therapies, retinoids, biologics, or procedures.ResultsThe cohort included 6659 children with HS (mean age 12.8 ± 2.8 years at diagnosis), 74.9% female, 37.7% White, and 56.5% non‐Hispanic/Latino. Overall, 3.2% of children received biologics. Following HS diagnosis, the mean time to treatment initiation was 15 days. The most common initial treatment was systemic antibiotics plus topical therapies. The mean time on the initial treatment regimen prior to switching was 386 days. Multivariable analyses demonstrated that girls were more likely than boys to receive any treatments, including biologics, except major procedures. Non‐White children initiated treatment sooner than White children (1 vs. 17 days, p < 0.0001). Regardless of demographic subgroup, most children remained on their initial HS treatment for a mean duration of approximately a year before switching therapies.ConclusionPediatric HS care is characterized by delayed treatment escalation, antibiotic‐dominant initial therapy, and variation in treatment initiation timing across demographic groups.

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, inflammatory skin condition that causes significant morbidity in pediatric patients [1, 2]. HS affects approximately 0.028% of children and adolescents in the United States, with higher prevalence observed in African American girls aged 15–17 years [3]. Moreover, pediatric HS is frequently underdiagnosed, with a median symptom duration of 2.5 years prior to formal HS diagnosis [4]. By the time of initial diagnostic evaluation, most children present with moderate‐to‐severe disease [4, 5]. Delayed diagnosis in children may lead to substantial scarring, uncontrolled disease progression, and psychosocial burden.

Despite the evolving understanding of HS, evidence suggests that HS disease prevalence and burden differ among sex, race, and ethnicity [6]. Among pediatric patients, prior research has demonstrated that Hispanic and Black children experience more severe disease and have higher rates of emergency department visits and hospitalizations compared to White and non‐Hispanic children [7, 8]. However, the timing between HS diagnosis and initiating the first‐line treatment in pediatric patients remains poorly understood, and treatment patterns by sex, race, and ethnicity have not been thoroughly explored. This data is critical because differences in treatment may contribute to differences in disease progression, healthcare utilization, and long‐term outcomes. Thus, identifying these gaps in care can inform targeted interventions to optimize management in pediatric HS. In this study, we aim to characterize treatment patterns in pediatric patients with HS across sex, race, and ethnicity, including differences in (1) time to treatment initiation, (2) type of initial therapy, and (3) time to switching from the initial treatment.

Methods

We conducted a real‐world, retrospective cohort study using the U.S. TriNetX network database from January 2005 to September 2025. Pediatric patients aged < 18 years old with a diagnosis of HS were identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐10‐CM code L73.2. The index date was defined as the date of the first HS diagnosis. Treatments prescribed within 1 year of diagnosis were classified into seven categories: systemic antibiotics (doxycycline, minocycline, tetracycline, clindamycin, rifampin, moxifloxacin, metronidazole, dapsone, ertapenem), systemic hormonal therapies (metformin, spironolactone, finasteride, oral contraceptives including ethinylestradiol, norgestrel, cyproterone, drospirenone, gestodene, and desogestrel), topical and systemic retinoids (isotretinoin, acitretin, tretinoin, adapalene, tazarotene), topical therapies (clindamycin, resorcinol, chlorhexidine, zinc pyrithione, benzoyl peroxide, triclosan), biologics (adalimumab, ustekinumab, infliximab, anakinra, secukinumab), minor procedures (intralesional corticosteroids, incision and drainage, deroofing), and major procedures (excision, CO2/laser therapy). Time to treatment initiation was defined as the interval between HS diagnosis and the earliest HS treatment prescription. The first prescribed treatment was defined as the initial therapy (either monotherapy or combination therapy) that was started following the diagnosis of HS. Time to switching was defined as the duration from the start of the initial treatment to the date of discontinuation of that treatment and replacement with a different treatment.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize treatment patterns and demographic characteristics. Treatment patterns were compared separately by sex, race, and ethnicity. Race was categorized as White and Non‐White (Black or African American, Asian, American Indian, Native Hawaiian, Other Race). For each comparison, subjects were propensity score–matched 1:1 on co‐morbid conditions (e.g., prediabetes, diabetes mellitus, ovarian dysfunction), age, and all demographic variables except the variable under study. Comparisons of treatment patterns across demographic groups were assessed using risk ratios (RR) with 99.3% confidence intervals (CI), reflecting Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (α = 0.05). Independent samples t‐tests compared initial treatment initiation times and durations across demographic groups, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

The study included 6659 children with HS (mean age 12.8 ± 2.8 years at diagnosis), 74.9% female, 37.7% White, and 56.5% non‐Hispanic/Latino. The mean BMI was 32.3 ± 9.1 kg/m [2] (Table 1). Overall, topical therapies were the most common HS treatment prescribed to children (n = 1732, 26.0%) followed by systemic antibiotics (n = 1569, 23.6%) (Table 2). Furthermore, only 3.2% of children with HS were prescribed biologics. In a subgroup analysis, including only patients with HS diagnosis on or after October 2018, 6.1% of patients received biologics, reflecting uptake of biologic prescriptions in pediatric HS following FDA approval.

Likelihood of Initiating Any Treatment

This study examined whether differences existed based on sex, race, and ethnicity in receiving systemic antibiotics, systemic hormonal therapies, topical therapies, minor and major procedures, biologics, and topical and systemic retinoids. Girls were more likely than boys to receive systemic antibiotics, topical therapies, minor procedures, biologics, and topical and systemic retinoids. Girls were also more likely to receive hormonal therapies, and this is due to the anti‐androgenic mechanism of action of oral contraceptives and spironolactone (Table 2, Figure 1).

Forest plot showing risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for treatment patterns by demographic group in pediatric patients with hidradenitis suppurativa.

No differences were observed between White and non‐White children for systemic antibiotics, topical and systemic retinoids, biologics, minor procedures, and major procedures (Table 2, Figure 1). However, White children were more likely than non‐White children to receive systemic hormonal therapies but less likely to receive topical therapies.

No significant differences were observed between non‐Hispanic/Latino and Hispanic/Latino children in the use of systemic hormonal therapies, topical and systemic retinoids, systemic antibiotics, topical therapies, biologics, minor procedures, and major procedures, systemic hormonal therapies, and topical and systemic retinoids (Table 2, Figure 1).

Time to Initial HS Treatment

In the entire cohort, the time to initial HS treatment was 14.8 days (95% CI: 13.57–16.03). Time to initiating the first prescribed treatment did not differ significantly between girls and boys and between non‐Hispanic/Latino and Hispanic/Latino children. Following HS diagnosis, non‐White children initiated their first prescribed treatment approximately 16 days earlier than White children (1.0 day [95% CI: 0.85–1.13] vs. 16.8 days [95% CI: 14.7–18.8], respectively p < 0.0001) (Table 3).

Type of Initial Treatment

The most common first prescribed treatment in this cohort of pediatric patients with HS was combination systemic antibiotics and topical therapies (n = 2620, 56.7%). Subgroup analyses demonstrated the most common first prescribed treatment was topical therapies for both girls (n = 1237, 24.8%) and boys (n = 410, 24.5%). No racial differences were observed in the first type of prescribed treatment after adjusting for clinical and demographic factors. Systemic antibiotics combined with topical therapies were most frequently prescribed among both non‐White and White children (non‐White: n = 913, 44.6%; White: n = 991, 52.5%). Among non‐Hispanic/Latino children, topical therapies were most common (n = 1159, 39.9%), whereas Hispanic/Latino children most often received a combination of systemic antibiotics and topical therapies (n = 635, 61.0%).

Duration of Initial Treatment

The mean time on initial treatment prior to switching therapy was 385.7 days (95% CI: 383.9–387.5). Girls remained on their initial treatment of topicals for a shorter duration than boys (364.2 days [95% CI: 352.1–376.3] vs. 460.6 days [95% CI: 434.7–486.4], p < 0.0001) before switching to systemic antibiotics. White children remained on their first prescribed treatment for a shorter duration than non‐White children (424.7 days [95% CI: 406.2–441.8] vs. 467.0 days [95% CI: 448.3–485.7], p = 0.0013). Non‐Hispanic/Latino children remained on their first prescribed treatment for a shorter duration than Hispanic/Latino children (405.0 days [95% CI: 390.0–420.0] vs. 493.0 days [95% CI: 464.2–521.8], p < 0.0001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study is among the initial efforts to characterize real‐world treatment patterns for pediatric patients with HS across demographic factors. While prior studies in children have demonstrated significant differences in disease severity and burden by sex, race, and ethnicity, it remains unclear how these variations in severity and burden influence treatment decisions in pediatric HS [3, 7, 9, 10]. This study highlights three key patterns in pediatric HS care: prolonged delays in treatment escalation, frequent reliance on antibiotic‐based first‐line therapies, and differences in the timing of treatment initiation across demographic groups.

Our findings demonstrate that, regardless of demographic subgroup, most children remained on their initial HS treatment for a mean duration of approximately a year before switching therapies. It is important to note that, in some cases, prolonged persistence on initial therapy may reflect adequate disease control and clinical stability. Given the progressive nature of HS in many patients and evidence that early, effective intervention may improve outcomes, this prolonged time to treatment modification highlights a potential gap in timely disease escalation [11]. We recognize that some therapies may take several months to show full clinical efficacy; however, in other cases, earlier treatment modification may have been warranted in the setting of inadequate response. Delays may reflect barriers to timely follow‐up, including limited access to pediatric dermatologists and variability in clinician knowledge or comfort with treatment escalation. Pediatric guidelines may benefit from clearer recommendations on thresholds for escalation where appropriate and earlier referral to pediatric dermatology when indicated. These findings may underscore the importance of regular follow‐up during the initial treatment phase, before patients achieve a stable and effective treatment regimen.

This study showed that the most common first prescribed treatment for pediatric HS was a combination of systemic antibiotics and topical therapies. This suggests that patients may present at least at a moderate stage to warrant systemic antibiotic use. While our study cannot directly assess HS severity, the prescription pattern from our study suggests the possibility of delayed recognition or underdiagnosis of earlier disease stages, as well as decreased utilization of healthcare resources. This observation supports a recent report of 481 pediatric patients with HS, in which more than half (60%) presented with Hurley Stage II or III disease at initial diagnosis, and 48% already had evidence of skin scarring [4]. Alternatively, differences in provider familiarity with managing HS could influence treatment decisions. These observations highlight the value of early recognition and timely initiation of appropriate therapy to prevent disease progression and long‐term complications. However, access to pediatric dermatologists can be limited, which may contribute to delays in diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

In addition, this study found that non‐White children initiated treatment significantly earlier compared to White children. Literature shows that HS often presents with greater disease severity among non‐White children, which may necessitate earlier treatment intervention [7]. Additionally, prior research has shown that non‐White children with HS are more likely to utilize emergency department services or require hospitalization than White children [7, 9, 10]. Increased emergency department utilization among non‐White children with HS may contribute to earlier clinician recognition and initiation of therapy. Provider awareness of differences in HS presentation among patients with skin of color may contribute to a lower threshold for treatment initiation in non‐White children. Finally, ongoing clinician education regarding HS presentation in patients with skin of color is critical to reduce diagnostic delays and initiate early intervention.

These findings should be interpreted in the context of pediatric dermatology care in the United States. Pediatric dermatologists are largely based in urban areas (96%), with relatively few practicing in rural settings, and nearly one‐third of children live more than an hour from a pediatric dermatologist [12, 13]. Limited access may delay diagnosis and contribute to more advanced disease at presentation. Even when pediatric dermatologists are available, workforce constraints can limit timely follow‐up and continuity of care, which may slow treatment reassessment and escalation. As a result, care may occur in more episodic or acute settings rather than through longitudinal outpatient management, potentially contributing to the treatment patterns observed in this cohort.

These results should be interpreted with consideration of the study design and limitations. The database utilized in this study did not capture HS severity, limiting assessment of its influence on treatment patterns. Lack of insurance data may affect visit frequency and access to costly therapies such as biologics. Lastly, the TriNetX database does not distinguish whether care is delivered in pediatric dermatology clinics versus general dermatology centers, which may influence observed prescribing patterns.

Conclusion

In this real‐world cohort of pediatric patients with HS, treatment patterns were characterized by prolonged time to treatment escalation, frequent use of antibiotic‐based initial therapy, and variation in the timing of treatment initiation across demographic groups. Together, these findings suggest opportunities to improve early disease recognition, reassessment, and timely modification of therapy in pediatric HS. Greater clarity around treatment escalation, regular follow‐up during the initial treatment phase, and improved access to pediatric dermatology care may help reduce delays in effective management. Future studies incorporating disease severity, longitudinal outcomes, and care setting characteristics are needed to further inform evidence‐based strategies for optimizing care in this population.
