Host immunogenetics and EBV detection in cancer: the CTLA-4 rs231775 signal in a multi-cancer case–control cohort
Authors: Noor Kadhim Abed, Abolfazl Bayrami, Saber Zahri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistent oncogenic viruses are common in cancer and may be modulated by host immunogenetics. We evaluated whether CTLA-4 and IL-4 variants are associated with qualitative viral DNA detection, focusing on EBV, in a multi-cancer case–control cohort.Methods90 cancer patients and 90 age-/sex-matched controls were enrolled from Southern Iraq. Peripheral blood (and tumor DNA when available) was assayed by qualitative EBNA-1 PCR for EBV, and by PCR for HPV and HBV. CTLA-4 (rs231775, rs5742909) and IL-4 (rs2243250) were genotyped. The associations were tested by χ² and multivariable logistic regression, including adjustment for cancer type, and discrimination was assessed by ROC AUC.ResultsAmong 180 participants, viral DNA was detected in 59 of 90 cancer patients (65.6%) and in none of the 90 controls (χ²[1, n = 180] = 84.82; p < 0.001). Among cases, positivity rates were 26.8% for HBV, 21.4% for EBV, and 17.9% for HPV. Genotype–phenotype analyses showed enrichment of the CTLA-4 rs231775 GA genotype among EBV-positive cases (χ² p = 0.0105). In multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, cancer type, and routine hematology, rs231775-GA remained independently associated with EBV positivity (p = 0.030), and the model yielded an AUC of 0.75. No EBV associations were observed for rs5742909 or IL-4 rs2243250. Hematological indices were within reference ranges and did not differ by EBV status, and the rs231775 finding was independent of these measures. Because EBV DNA was undetectable in all controls, the association reflects within-case differences (EBV+ vs. EBV–), and results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.ConclusionsIn this multi-cancer cohort, CTLA-4 rs231775 (GA) is associated with detectable EBV DNA by qualitative PCR, independent of age, sex, cancer type, and routine hematology, highlighting an immune-checkpoint genetic pathway that may facilitate EBV persistence. Findings for rs5742909 and IL-4 rs2243250 were null for EBV. These data support exploratory, targeted studies to validate CTLA-4–mediated susceptibility to EBV in cancer using quantitative assays and larger, multi-center designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-025-00726-3.

Introduction

Virus-associated malignancies remain a substantial global health burden, with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), human papillomavirus (HPV), and hepatitis B virus (HBV) contributing to carcinogenesis through persistent infection and dysregulated host responses [1]. Beyond viral oncogenes themselves, accumulating evidence indicates that inter-individual differences in host immune regulation critically influence whether infections are controlled, become latent, or progress to neoplasia [2].

Among immune checkpoint pathways, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4) has drawn particular attention because of its role in attenuating T-cell activation; two widely studied variants are rs231775 (+ 49 A > G) in the coding region and rs5742909 (− 318 C > T) in the promoter [3, 4]. The rs231775 substitution can modify CTLA-4 structure and signaling, potentially shifting the activation threshold of T cells and thereby altering susceptibility to persistent viral antigens and tumor immune escape [3]. In parallel, cytokine milieu also shapes antiviral and antitumor immunity; the IL-4 promoter variant rs2243250 (−590C>T) influences transcriptional output and has been linked to immune skewing relevant to chronic viral infections [5].

Epidemiologic studies have explored relationships between these immunogenetic variants and virus-related cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma and EBV-associated conditions, though findings have varied by population, study design, and case definition [6, 7]. Reports focused on IL-4 rs2243250 similarly suggest context-dependent associations with infection persistence and virus-driven neoplasia, again with heterogeneity across ethnic groups and clinical settings [8, 9]. In sum, the literature motivates targeted evaluation of CTLA-4 and IL-4 variants in settings where infectious exposures are common and cancer care resources are constrained [1].

Because single variants often yield modest effects, there is a biological rationale to consider combined or locus-specific effects within immune pathways that collectively modulate antiviral control and tumor surveillance [7, 10]. At the same time, readily available hematological indices can provide complementary, inexpensive indicators of systemic inflammatory/immune tone that may confound or modify genotype–phenotype relationships [11]. However, relatively few studies have assessed viral DNA detection, CTLA-4/IL-4 variation, and hematological measures together in a multi-cancer framework, limiting synthesis across domains that are typically analyzed in isolation [12].

The present study addresses this gap by examining a multi-cancer case–control cohort recruited in Thi-Qar Province, Southern Iraq, a region under-represented in immunogenetic research yet highly relevant to infection-associated cancer risk. Our design integrates viral DNA assays with host genotyping and routine hematology to interrogate tractable correlates of EBV detection while describing HBV and HPV findings within the same analytic framework.

The principal aim of this work was to determine whether host immunogenetic variation in CTLA-4 and IL-4 is associated with EBV DNA detection in cancer patients from a multi-cancer case–control cohort. Specifically, the association between CTLA-4 rs231775 and EBV positivity was evaluated using prespecified genetic models, including an additive parameterization and an over-dominant comparison (GA versus AA + GG), with adjustment for demographic and clinical factors to mitigate confounding. In secondary analyses, we explored CTLA-4 rs5742909 and IL-4 rs2243250 in relation to EBV detection, described HBV and HPV DNA positivity rates and their exploratory genotype associations, and assessed whether hematological indices confound or modify the rs231775–EBV relationship; multiple testing was controlled across SNP×virus comparisons, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was evaluated in controls, and model discrimination was summarized by area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was an observational case–control molecular epidemiology study designed to evaluate whether host immunogenetic variation in CTLA-4 and IL-4 is associated with viral DNA detection, with a pre-specified focus on EBV. The primary endpoint was EBV DNA positivity determined by PCR in cancer cases; secondary endpoints included HPV and HBV DNA positivity and exploratory genotype–virus associations. Two genetic models were prespecified for the main analysis of CTLA-4 rs231775 (+ 49 A > G): an additive (per-allele) model and an overdominant model (GA vs. AA + GG), to reflect prior functional/epidemiologic evidence [3, 4]. The overall analytic framework integrated viral assays, host genotypes, and routine hematology within a single multi-cancer cohort, as outlined previously [12]. The lists of laboratory reagents, kits, equipment, and additional materials used, along with a demographic and statistical comparison of cases and controls and a schematic flowchart (Figure S1) of the study design, are provided in the Supplementary Information (SI).

Study population

90 adult cancer patients (cases; histologically confirmed malignancies) and 90 age- and sex-matched cancer-free controls were enrolled from the same catchment area using frequency matching to minimize demographic confounding. Controls were unrelated, cancer-free adults from the same geographic catchment area, recruited to match the cases by age and sex and to represent the underlying population from which the cases arose. Relatives of patients were not included as controls, as family-based designs can introduce genetic relatedness bias and are not optimal for assessing population-level associations between immunogenetic polymorphisms and viral DNA detection. The inclusion criteria for cases were age ≥ 18 years, histological confirmation, and availability of peripheral blood for DNA and hematology. Patients were recruited at the time of oncologic evaluation in the participating centers, and blood sampling for this study was scheduled before initiation of the planned cytotoxic treatment course. While controls were cancer-free adults with no prior malignancy. The exclusion criteria included insufficient DNA yield/quality or prior cancer diagnosis. The cancer cohort comprised multiple malignancy categories, including common carcinomas (e.g., breast, bladder, colorectal, gastric, lung), hematologic malignancies (lymphoma and leukemia), and a small number of sarcomas. This reflects the intended multi-cancer design of the study (documentation available in patient-level SI records). Because the study was not powered for cancer-specific analyses, cancer type was incorporated as a categorical covariate (carcinoma vs. hematologic vs. other) in adjusted logistic regression models to account for potential differences related to viral cellular tropism. Participants were recruited in Thi-Qar Province, Southern Iraq during the study period described in the SI.

Sample collection and demographics

Peripheral venous blood (2–3 mL, EDTA) was collected from each participant; aliquots were used for complete blood count (CBC) and DNA extraction at the same visit, and for cancer cases, sampling was performed before any transfusion or cytotoxic therapy for the index malignancy, when applicable. Viral DNA was assayed from blood in all participants and, when available, from tumor tissue in cases. Demographic variables (age, sex) were recorded to assess and control potential confounding.

Hematological analysis

CBCs were obtained using an automated analyzer (model per SI), including WBC, RBC, HGB, HCT, PLT, MCV, MCH, MCHC, LYM, MONO, NEUT. Derived indices were computed as NLR = neutrophils/lymphocytes, PLR = platelets/lymphocytes, and LMR = lymphocytes/monocytes; reference ranges followed hospital laboratory standards and are shown in Table S4. Hematological variables were evaluated as potential confounders or effect modifiers of the rs231775–EBV association rather than as independent endpoints.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the G-spin™ DNA Extraction Kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Korea), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). The A260/280 ratios ranged from 1.7 to 1.9, consistent with high-purity DNA suitable for PCR amplification (see Tables S1–S3 for detailed equipment and reagents).

PCR amplification and genotyping

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed with the Maxime PCR PreMix (i-Taq) in 25 µL reactions using gene-specific primers. Primer sequences, amplicon sizes, and annealing temperatures for CTLA-4 rs231775 (+ 49 A > G), CTLA-4 rs5742909 (− 318 C > T), IL-4 rs2243250 (− 590 C > T), and EBV EBNA-1 are provided in Table S5. For EBV, EBNA-1 primers from a previously published protocol were used to qualitatively detect viral DNA; EBV positivity was defined as the presence of a single amplicon of the expected size (~ 269 bp) in duplicate reactions. Cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, primer-specific annealing at the temperatures listed in Table S5 for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels stained with RedSafe. Genotyping of rs231775 was carried out by PCR-RFLP, with Sanger sequencing performed for approximately 10% of randomly selected samples and for all discordant calls to provide orthogonal confirmation. Genotyping of rs5742909 and rs2243250 was performed by PCR-RFLP or allele-specific PCR. Quality control procedures included SNP-level call rates of at least 98% and duplicate genotyping for at least 5% of samples, with a ≥ 99% concordance threshold. Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was also assessed in controls for each SNP.

RFLP and sanger sequencing

For rs231775, PCR products were digested with BbvI and resolved on 10% polyacrylamide gels; genotypes (AA/AG/GG) were assigned by banding pattern [16]. About 10% of samples and all RFLP-discordant calls underwent bidirectional Sanger sequencing (Macrogen) with ≥ 99% concordance.

Gel electrophoresis

PCR products were separated on 1–1.5% agarose (1× TBE), stained with RedSafe; RFLP products were run on 10% polyacrylamide for higher resolution [16]. Each gel included 100-bp ladders, no-template controls, and positive controls (previously confirmed genotype or virus-positive samples). Bands were visualized on a UV transilluminator (Vilber Lourmat).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the University of Mohaghegh Ardabili Institutional Review Board (Approval No. IR.UMA.REC.1404.040) with local site authorization in Thi-Qar, Southern Iraq (Clinical trial number: not applicable); all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (lane-level quality data).

Statistical and predictive analysis

All analyses were conducted in SPSS (version 26.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) using two-sided tests with a significance level of 0.05. The categorical variables were compared with the chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test was applied when any expected cell count was less than five. The continuous variables were assessed for distributional assumptions and compared with the independent-samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. The primary endpoint was EBV DNA positivity among cancer cases; its association with CTLA-4 rs231775 (+ 49 A > G) was evaluated by logistic regression under two prespecified genetic coding, namely an additive model (0/1/2 copies of the G allele) and an over-dominant model contrasting GA with AA + GG, with adjustment for age, sex, and cancer type. Secondary analyses examined CTLA-4 rs5742909 and IL-4 rs2243250 in relation to EBV status and summarized HPV and HBV positivity and their exploratory genotype associations. Hematological indices, including derived ratios (NLR, PLR, LMR), were considered as potential confounders or effect modifiers in supplementary models as permitted by sample size. The discrimination was summarized by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve with 95% confidence intervals, and model adequacy was reviewed using standard diagnostics. The multiple testing across SNP-by-virus families was controlled using the false discovery rate procedure, and both unadjusted and adjusted P values are reported where applicable. Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was evaluated in controls for each SNP, and genotype call-rate and duplicate-sample concordance thresholds were enforced as described in the laboratory procedures. Analyses were performed on complete-case datasets, and the analytic sample size is reported for each model. Cancer type was included as an adjustment variable (carcinoma versus hematologic versus other) in all multivariable models to mitigate potential confounding arising from EBV’s known tropism for B-cell–derived malignancies.

Results

Demographic and hematological characteristics

The distribution of age and sex was comparable between cases and controls, reflecting the planned frequency matching. No meaningful differences by sex or recruitment region were observed, indicating that the two groups were demographically well-balanced for subsequent analyses. The hematological indices were generally within laboratory reference ranges in the case cohort and showed stable patterns across age and sex strata (Table 1; Fig. 1). The between-group comparisons did not reveal notable shifts in complete blood count parameters, and the overall hematologic profile appeared similar in magnitude and dispersion across categories examined. When EBV status was considered, indices likewise did not differ in a way that would account for viral DNA detection; in multivariable models, the hematology measures did not attenuate the association of interest, supporting their role as non-driving covariates in this dataset. In combination, the demographic balance and the absence of material hematologic imbalance suggest that observed genotype–virus relationships are unlikely to be explained by baseline differences in age, sex, or routine blood parameters.

Viral DNA detection

Viral DNA was identified in a subset of cancer patients and was not observed in controls, aligning with the study’s infectious-oncogenesis focus. EBV and HBV accounted for most detections, whereas HPV was less frequent; the pattern was broadly similar across age and sex strata. Clear single amplicons at the expected sizes, EBV EBNA-1 (~ 269 bp), HPV (~ 450 bp), and HBV (~ 564 bp), were obtained, and assay controls behaved as anticipated, supporting analytic specificity and reproducibility (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Results from blood and, where available, paired tumor tissue were generally concordant, and co-detections were uncommon. Overall, viral positivity appeared confined largely to cases, with EBV and HBV providing most of the signal and HPV contributing to a lesser extent, a context that motivates the subsequent genotype–phenotype analyses (see also Figure S5).

Host genetic variants and EBV detection

An enrichment of the CTLA-4 rs231775 GA genotype among EBV-positive cases was observed in cross-tabulation, consistent with an overdominant pattern (Table 2). In adjusted logistic models, rs231775-GA remained independently associated with EBV positivity, and the combined model showed useful discrimination with an AUC of 0.75 (Fig. 5). Importantly, adjusting for cancer-type category (carcinoma vs. hematologic vs. other) did not materially alter the rs231775–EBV association, indicating that the observed overdominant signal was not driven solely by hematologic malignancies.

By contrast, CTLA-4 rs5742909 and IL-4 rs2243250 did not display persuasive associations with EBV status under the same framework (p > 0.05). Effect estimates were small and directionally inconsistent across coding, and adjustment did not uncover a latent signal (Table 2). Quality indicators supported the validity of these genetic findings: genotyping success was high, duplicate concordance was near-perfect, and banding/electropherogram checks were consistent with expected patterns (Fig. 6). Altogether, the evidence points to a specific rs231775–EBV relationship in this cohort, while the other loci did not contribute meaningfully within the tested models.

Hematological covariate analyses

Hematological indices and derived ratios (NLR, PLR, LMR) were first reviewed descriptively and then incorporated as covariates. Overall, patterns appeared broadly comparable across groups; however, small fluctuations at distributional extremes were noted without a consistent trend (Table 3). When entered into the models, these measures produced only modest shifts in the genotype–EBV estimates. The association between CTLA-4 rs231775 and EBV DNA positivity was largely retained, and model adequacy did not deteriorate after adjustment. Similarly, interaction terms with the hematological indices were not persuasive, and stratified estimates remained directionally stable.

Altogether, the hematological profile functioned as background context rather than a primary driver of EBV detection, while the rs231775 signal was interpreted as robust to routine variation in blood parameters (full model output in Table S6).

Exploratory analyses

As summarized in Table 4, HBV, EBV, and HPV DNA were detected in 26.8% (n = 24), 21.4% (n = 19), and 17.9% (n = 16) of patients, respectively. HBV positivity was observed across multiple cancer types rather than being restricted to hepatocellular carcinoma, consistent with the multi-cancer composition of the cohort and with reports that HBV viremia may be detected beyond liver-specific malignancies in high-exposure regions. Each virus showed distinct yet largely exploratory patterns of association with immune-related genetic polymorphisms. EBV positivity was most consistently associated with the CTLA-4 rs231775 AG genotype, with a secondary contribution from the IL4 rs2243250 C allele. HBV infection, by contrast, appeared primarily linked to the IL4 rs2243250 C allele, with only a weak and unstable signal at CTLA-4 rs231775. HPV detection showed possible modification by CTLA-4 rs5742909, again in conjunction with the IL4 rs2243250 C allele.

Potential co-infection trends were also explored. HBV and HPV may share a limited synergistic role in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), possibly reflecting overlapping liver tropism, though this association was not statistically significant (p = 1.0). Similarly, EBV and HPV co-occurrence was noted in some leukemia cases (p ≈ 0.645), and HPV showed modest, non-significant associations with both breast and bladder cancers (p ≈ 0.324). These patterns suggest that while viral co-infections and genetic variants may act together in shaping susceptibility, the observed effects in this dataset were small and did not withstand correction for multiple testing.

Further multi-locus analyses did not reveal clear additive or synergistic effects among the studied variants, and minor fluctuations were largely attributable to limited subgroup sizes. Sensitivity analyses restricting comparisons to blood-only assays, or to cases with paired tumor tissue results, produced concordant outcomes. Likewise, exclusion of the smallest cancer subgroups did not materially change the findings. In sum, these exploratory evaluations support the specificity of the rs231775–EBV association observed in the primary analysis, whereas additional host–virus relationships (involving HBV or HPV) were not substantiated within the current sample.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that oncogenic viral DNA (EBV, HPV, HBV) was detectable in a subset of cancer patients, with EBNA-specific PCR providing a reliable marker for EBV positivity. The most striking host–virus association emerged for CTLA-4 rs231775, where the heterozygous GA genotype was associated with EBNA detection. This observation is consistent with the established role of CTLA-4 in fine-tuning T-cell activity, suggesting that heterozygosity may generate an intermediate immunological state that facilitates EBV persistence [13]. By contrast, CTLA-4 rs5742909 and IL-4 rs2243250 did not show persuasive associations with EBV status in this cohort, emphasizing the specificity of the rs231775 signal under the prespecified genetic models.

EBV infects the majority of adults worldwide and typically persists as a latent infection in a small subset of B cells [2], so EBV DNA detection by PCR does not map directly onto seroprevalence or on–off transitions between latency and lytic replication. In this study, EBV status was defined qualitatively on the basis of EBNA-1 DNA detection in whole blood (and, when available, tumor tissue), using a single, specific amplicon as the criterion for positivity. The absence of detectable EBV DNA in controls is therefore compatible with high background seroprevalence but low circulating viral loads in immunocompetent individuals, whereas positive signals in a subset of cancer patients likely reflect higher EBV DNA burden in blood or tumor-derived material under the same assay conditions. We did not attempt to distinguish latent from lytic infection or to quantify viral load, and the EBV endpoint is interpreted as the presence versus absence of detectable EBV DNA rather than a direct measure of replicative activity. The control group design also merits emphasis. Unrelated, cancer-free individuals from the same geographic catchment area were selected as controls, consistent with established practice in immunogenetic and virus–host association studies assessing CTLA-4 variants [3, 4]. This approach avoids allele-frequency distortion and genetic relatedness bias that may occur when relatives of cases are used as controls, particularly for polymorphisms such as CTLA-4 rs231775 that can cluster within families. Population-based controls, therefore, provide a more appropriate baseline for estimating genotype distributions and evaluating SNP–virus associations within a case–control framework.

Consideration of cancer-type diversity is important when interpreting EBV detection patterns, as EBV exhibits well-established tropism for B cells and is strongly implicated in several lymphoid malignancies [2, 14]. In the present multi-cancer cohort, both epithelial tumors and hematologic cancers were represented. Incorporating cancer type into the adjusted regression models did not materially alter the association between CTLA-4 rs231775 (GA) and EBV DNA positivity, indicating that the observed genetic signal is not driven solely by hematologic malignancies. This is biologically plausible, as EBV DNA has been documented in epithelial cancers such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma and EBV-positive gastric carcinoma [1, 14, 15], suggesting that host immune-regulatory variation at CTLA-4 may influence EBV persistence across different tissue contexts [3, 4, 13].

Besides, HBV DNA was detected in approximately one-fourth of cancer cases; however, this pattern did not indicate a hepatocellular carcinoma–dominant cohort. HBV-positive individuals were distributed across several tumor types, which is compatible with the epidemiology of chronic HBV exposure in intermediate-prevalence regions, where viral markers may remain detectable in peripheral blood and contribute to systemic immune modulation [1, 7, 16]. Although HBV infection can influence host immunity, hematological indices did not differ materially by HBV status, rs231775 showed no significant association with HBV positivity, and the rs231775–EBV association persisted after adjusting for cancer type, suggesting that HBV is unlikely to be a major driver of the observed immunogenetic relationship. In comparison, the findings are consistent with reports linking CTLA-4 rs231775 to EBV-related malignancies [13, 15] but diverge from those implicating IL-4 rs2243250 in HPV- and HBV-associated disease [8, 16]. These differences are plausibly explained by population structure, limited sample sizes, and matrix/assay heterogeneity, as well as potential gene–environment interactions [4]. By assessing EBV, HPV, and HBV in parallel within one cohort, the study provides a coherent immunogenetic context for interpreting the rs231775–EBV signal while documenting largely null exploratory findings for the other loci and viruses [1, 14].

On the other hand, the hematological indices, including WBC, RBC, HGB, and PLT, were broadly comparable across groups and supported the interpretation that viral detection was not explained by systemic hematological variation [11]. Furthermore, after adjustment for age, sex, and cancer type, the multivariable model reinforced that host immunogenetic context, rather than baseline hematology, was the principal correlate of EBNA positivity. Taken together, these findings highlight a specific genetic pathway, centered on CTLA-4, through which immune regulation influences viral persistence and potentially virus-associated oncogenesis [13].

While previous investigations often focused on a single virus–tumor axis, such as HBV and hepatocellular carcinoma or HPV and cervical cancer [7], the present pan-cancer design permitted parallel evaluation of multiple oncogenic viruses within one framework. This approach is valuable because: (i) immune mechanisms relevant to viral latency can be shared across tissues [14], (ii) modest genetic effects may become detectable when pooled across tumor sites. Nevertheless, virus- and cancer-specific estimates remain imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously, given subgroup sizes.

Although rs5742909 and rs2243250 were not individually significant in relation to EBV, polygenic influences remain biologically plausible, as the immune variants have been reported in HBV-related hepatocellular carcinoma [7]. In this dataset, however, exploratory multi-locus patterns did not yield stable gradients after accounting for multiple comparisons, suggesting that the primary, reproducible signal resides in rs231775 under the tested models. In contrast to earlier phrasing, the inclusion of routine hematology did not meaningfully change discrimination or calibration for EBV status in this cohort; thus, these measures were interpreted as background covariates rather than primary drivers. Because all patients were sampled before receiving oncologic treatment, the observed EBV and immunogenetic associations reflect baseline immune status rather than chemotherapy-induced viral reactivation. Prior studies have highlighted LMR and platelet indices as accessible biomarkers of systemic immune activity [11], but their incremental value for EBV detection appeared limited here.

Regarding clinical implications, any translational inferences should be framed as hypothesis-generating. Screening or surveillance strategies based on rs231775 would require validation in larger, prospective cohorts, and implications for checkpoint inhibitor response remain speculative without treatment-response data. Similarly, tailoring vaccination or public-health programs by genotype is premature and would require robust evidence of clinical utility and cost-effectiveness. Accordingly, the present findings are best viewed as a molecular starting point for targeted studies on EBV susceptibility.

Conclusion

In this multi-cancer cohort, CTLA-4 rs231775 was associated with detectable EBV DNA by qualitative PCR, independent of demographic variables, cancer type, and routine hematological indices. Findings for CTLA-4 rs5742909 and IL-4 rs2243250 were null for EBV. Because EBV DNA was detected only among cancer cases, the association reflects within-case differences rather than comparisons to population controls, and the qualitative nature of the assay limited interpretation regarding viral load or replication state. Together, these results highlight a potential immunogenetic pathway through CTLA-4 that may influence EBV persistence. Validation in larger, multi-center cohorts using quantitative assays is warranted to determine whether rs231775 has reproducible utility in EBV-related cancer susceptibility or monitoring.

Limitations and future directions

This cross-sectional study, with 90 cases and 90 controls and only 19 EBV-positive cancers, was not powered for virus- or cancer-specific subgroups or for detecting small-to-moderate genotype–virus effects, and estimates could be affected by blood vs. tissue matrix differences, missingness, or unmeasured population structure; in addition, the EBV assay was qualitative conventional PCR targeting EBNA-1, without quantitative viral load measurement or discrimination between latent and lytic infection. The overdominant rs231775–EBV association should also be interpreted cautiously, because although all participants were genotyped with high call rates and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium confirmed in controls, EBV DNA was detected only in the cancer group; therefore, genotype frequencies among EBV-positive healthy individuals cannot be assessed, and subtle population-structure effects or sampling variability cannot be fully excluded. Its strengths include a prespecified primary endpoint, orthogonal genotyping validation, and integrated viral–host genetics in an under-represented setting. Replication in larger, multi-center cohorts, with population-structure assessment and functional assays, is needed to confirm the overdominant rs231775–EBV signal. Prospective studies should test whether rs231775 adds value for risk stratification or therapy prediction beyond standard clinical and virological factors.
