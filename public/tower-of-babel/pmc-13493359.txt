Impact of scan duration and scatter correction on quantitative accuracy in Yttrium-90 PET/CT imaging using LAFOV and SAFOV systems: a phantom and clinical evaluation
Authors: Konstantinos G. Zeimpekis, Magdalena Kulgawczuk, Carola Bregenzer, Marc Schindewolf, Christos Sachpekidis, Hasan Sari, Kalyani Pandya, Ali Afshar-Oromieh, Axel Rominger, Kuangyu Shi, Robert Seifert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study compared quantitative accuracy and image quality of post-radioembolization selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT) 90Y PET/CT using a long axial field-of-view (LAFOV) and standard axial field-of-view (SAFOV) system, focusing on absolute (ABS) versus relative (RS) scatter correction. Additionally, variance modeling and logistic regression were performed to define injected-activity thresholds ensuring reliable quantification at different acquisition durations.MethodsPhantom experiments were conducted on Biograph Vision Quadra (LAFOV) and Vision 600 (SAFOV) PET/CT scanners using NEMA IQ phantoms filled with 90Y activity. Reconstructions employed OSEM + TOF + PSF with ABS and RS scatter correction at 50 min (both scanners) and 10- and 1-minute (only for LAFOV). Metrics included NEMA recovery coefficients (RC) and background variability (BV). 44 post-SIRT patients (median age 70 y) were analyzed for image-based quantification of injected activity using whole-liver VOIs, across multiple durations (20, 15, 10, 5, 1 min). Metrics included Bland–Altman analysis, equivalence testing (± 10%), and Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC). Variance–activity relationships and logistic regression quantified thresholds for 80%, 90% and 95% success probability.ResultsLAFOV achieved higher RCs and ~ 3× lower BV than SAFOV. ABS yielded significantly improved accuracy and stability versus RS, particularly at short scans. Patient data acquired on the LAFOV system showed ABS quantification equivalent to injected activity within ± 10% for ≥ 5 min (CCC ≥ 0.99), whereas RS required ≥ 10 min. Variance followed a power-law decay (γ ≈ 1.5 at 5 min), and logistic regression identified an activity threshold of ~ 1.6 GBq for 90% accuracy at 5 min. At ≥ 10 min, quantification became independent of activity.ConclusionCompared with a SAFOV 50-minute clinical routine acquisition, LAFOV PET enabled a similar BV performance at 7-times accelerated scanning duration. LAFOV PET/CT with absolute scatter correction enables accurate 90Y quantification and dosimetry with acquisition times as short as 10 min regardless of the administered 90Y activity, supporting efficient clinical post-SIRT imaging workflows.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-026-00907-y.

Introduction

Selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT) is a well-established therapy for the treatment of primary and metastatic liver tumors [1–3]. The treatment principle is the selective intra-arterial delivery of β-emitting ⁹⁰Y microspheres to achieve targeted internal irradiation of hypervascularized tumors. Those have a relatively short range and enable a targeted energy delivery to the cancer cells. Several studies including Dosisphere, Legacy, and Raser have shown that the efficacy of the treatment is determined by the radiation dose that is delivered to the tumors [4–6]. Therefore, accurate pretreatment planning using a dose distribution simulation with 99mTc labelled macroaggregated albumin (MAA) together with single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging is essential for personalized treatment activity determination [7–10]. In addition to the pre-treatment imaging, post-treatment imaging of 90Y microspheres distribution is increasingly being investigated. Traditionally, SPECT imaging of the 90Y Bremsstrahlung has been used to confirm adequate tumor targeting, rule out extrahepatic uptake and provide a basis for optimized re-treatment, if necessary. However, SPECT offers inferior resolution and produces blurred images compared with positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, which detects the positron emitted through internal pair production (albeit at low yield). Therefore, the European association of nuclear medicine (EANM) guideline on SIRT recommends PET imaging after treatment [11–16].

The very low internal-pair positron yield of 90Y (0.0032%) [17] makes conventional standard axial field-of-view (SAFOV) PET post-treatment imaging challenging. Acquisition times for SAFOV post-SIRT PET imaging vary across institutions and scanner generations. While routine clinical protocols often range between 15 and 30 min depending on system sensitivity, longer acquisitions (up to approximately 40–50 min) have historically been used in low-sensitivity systems or phantom studies to provide high-count reference datasets for quantitative evaluation [18, 19]. In contrast, long axial field-of-view (LAFOV) PET scanners have a greater sensitivity driven by the larger acceptance angle of annihilation photons. Previous studies have demonstrated that LAFOV-PET for post SIRT imaging enables the quantification of the lung shunt and may reduce acquisition time to approximately 5 min [20]. While multicenter studies have evaluated quantitative discrepancies in 90Y PET across institutions and scanner types [21], controlled head-to-head comparisons between SAFOV and LAFOV PET systems under harmonized reconstruction conditions remain limited. In particular, it remains unclear how scatter correction strategies interact with scanner sensitivity and acquisition duration in the ultra–low-count regime characteristic of 90Y PET imaging.

Direct comparisons of SAFOV and LAFOV PET for 90Y imaging are relevant, as errors in PET-based activity quantification directly affect absorbed dose estimation, which is needed to assess treatment efficacy and plan potential re-treatments. Moreover, while scatter correction is a well-established component of quantitative PET for diagnostic isotopes like fluorine-18 (18F) [22] and gallium-68 (68Ga) [23], its behavior in the extremely low-count regime of ⁹⁰Y PET remains less well characterized. Recent multicenter work, such as Carlier et al. [24], has evaluated quantitative discrepancies and compared absolute (ABS) and relative (RS) scatter correction methods across predominantly SAFOV PET systems. However, the interaction between scatter correction strategy, acquisition duration, and the substantially higher sensitivity of long axial field-of-view (LAFOV) PET systems has not yet been systematically evaluated under controlled conditions. Since low count rates and high random fractions amplify reconstruction-related biases in ⁹⁰Y PET, understanding how these factors interact in LAFOV imaging is important for reliable activity quantification and absorbed dose estimation.

To address these gaps, the present study directly compares 90Y PET imaging performance on a SAFOV and a LAFOV PET/CT scanner using phantom datasets, while clinical patient data acquired on LAFOV are used to evaluate quantitative accuracy and determine minimum injected 90Y activity thresholds for reliable imaging at different scan durations. We assess image quality by recovery coefficients (RC) based on NEMA Standard [25], background variability, and dosimetric accuracy across different scan durations and scatter correction approaches. Furthermore, we hypothesize that LAFOV combined with ABS will provide superior clinical quantitative performance and dosimetry accuracy, particularly in shortened acquisition protocols. While previous studies have demonstrated improved quantitative performance of model-based scatter correction approaches compared with tail-fitting methods, it remains unclear how these effects interact with scanner sensitivity and acquisition duration in the low-count regime of 90Y PET imaging.

Methods

Patient characteristics

Between March 2021 and December 2024, 43 patients (35 males/8 females, median age: 70 years, interquartile range (IQR): 14) underwent post-treatment 90Y PET/CT on a Biograph Vision Quadra LAFOV PET/CT scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA). Diagnoses included hepatocellular carcinoma (n = 32), cholangiocarcinoma (n = 5), colorectal liver metastases (n = 3) and hepatic metastases from neuroendocrine tumors (n = 3). Of the total cohort, 35 had a single lesion, and 8 had multiple. All patients underwent SIRT with glass microspheres (TheraSphere®, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) [27]. Median injected activity was 2.475 GBq, the 25th percentile was 1.459 GBq and the 75th percentile 3.319 GBq. Imaging was acquired 2.5 ± 0.5 h post-treatment. The cohort included patients with a median tumor volume of 137 ml (IQR: 403 ml). A single tumor site was treated in 36 cases, whereas the remaining 9 patients received treatment to at least two tumor sites. Patients provided general consent. Part of this cohort has been reported previously: 17 patients in our 2024 EANMMI post-SIRT LAFOV PET scanning optimization study [20] and 32 in our 2025 EJNMMI PET vs. SPECT post-SIRT optimization analysis [28]. Detailed patient information can be found in Table 1. All clinical PET scans were visually reviewed to verify that the activity distribution was predominantly confined to the liver and that no relevant extrahepatic uptake (e.g., lung or gastrointestinal activity) was present.

PET/CT scanners

Phantom imaging was conducted on two systems: the Biograph Vision Quadra PET/CT (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA) as a long axial field-of-view (LAFOV) PET, and the Biograph Vision 600 (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA) as a standard axial field-of-view (SAFOV) PET. Except for the difference in axial FOV (106 cm vs. 21.6 cm, respectively), the performance characteristics are similar for both systems, as described previously [29, 30]. The LAFOV PET/CT system has an axial field-of-view (AFOV) of 106 cm with a sensitivity of 176 cps/kBq [29], whereas the SAFOV PET/CT system has an AFOV of 21.6 cm with 16.4 cps/kBq [30], reflecting the substantially higher intrinsic sensitivity of the LAFOV scanner.

Reconstructions using a 440 × 440 matrix resulted in an in-plane voxel size of 1.65 × 1.65 mm, whereas reconstructions with a 220 × 220 matrix corresponded to an in-plane voxel size of 3.30 × 3.30 mm; the slice thickness was 1.65 mm [31].

Phantom preparation

The LAFOV phantom scans analyzed in this study were previously acquired and described in detail in a prior study [31]. Briefly, two NEMA IEC body phantoms (Data Spectrum Corp.) containing six fillable spheres (six spheres spanning 10–37 mm) were utilized [32]. The analysis in this study focused on a phantom in which the background also contained activity. The activity levels were intended to be comparable to those of typical clinical 90Y treatments, i.e., ~ 1.0 MBq/ml in lesions (i.e. spheres) and ~ 0.1 MBq/ml in the unaffected background, as described previously [31].

Phantom imaging

The phantom was centered on the scanner bed both axially and transaxially with the sphere insert located near the axial center of the scanner, with a scanning time of 50 min. PET raw data were re-reconstructed using previously published parameters: The PET reconstruction used time-of-flight (TOF) and point spread function (PSF) correction in an ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm (2 iterations with 5 subsets) in the vendor’s ultra-high sensitivity (UHS) mode ; image smoothing was done with a Gaussian kernel of 2 mm (FWHM) and 440 × 440 or 220 × 220 matrices [31]. Reconstructions were performed with both ABS and RS 3D methods. Images were reconstructed for 50-, 10-, and 5-minute scan durations for LAFOV and for 50 min scan duration for SAFOV. A 50-min acquisition was used for the SAFOV system to approximate maximum achievable count statistics and signal-to-noise ratio. This approach was chosen to evaluate both systems under near-optimal conditions rather than strictly matched acquisition durations.

Patient imaging

The clinical PET scans were acquired for 20 min (head-to-thigh coverage) as the routine clinical protocol in our department. Similar to the phantom reconstruction, the clinical reconstruction was performed using TOF, PSF and UHS mode, but with a matrix size of 220 × 220 to reflect standard clinical practice. For comparability, phantom datasets were also reconstructed using a 220 × 220 matrix in addition to the higher-resolution 440 × 440 matrix used for detailed recovery analysis, and the variability between these reconstructions is indicated in the corresponding figures. Both ABS and RS scatter corrections were applied to assess their influence.

Scatter correction methods (ABS and RS)

Two vendor-implemented scatter correction approaches were evaluated. Absolute scatter correction (ABS) employs a model-based scatter estimation framework based on single-scatter simulation as originally described by Watson [33] and forward modeling of photon interactions within the scanner geometry, with additional refinement of scatter estimates using residual-based corrections in long axial field-of-view systems [34]. In contrast, relative scatter correction (RS) relies on scaling the estimated scatter sinogram to the measured emission data using tail fitting of the sinogram regions outside the object, a commonly used approach in clinical PET reconstruction frameworks [35, 36]. While both approaches rely on scatter estimation from emission data, ABS performs an absolute modeling of scatter contributions, whereas RS scales the scatter estimate relative to measured background regions of the sinogram. Both methods were implemented within the vendor reconstruction framework and applied using identical reconstruction parameters to isolate the effect of the scatter correction strategy.

NEMA background variability & recovery coefficient

NEMA-based recovery coefficients were measured using the nominal sphere dimensions. RCs were calculated as the ratio between the measured activity concentration (MBq/mL) and the known true activity concentration (1.1 MBq/mL in the spheres), then expressed as a percentage. An additional performance metric, background variability (BV), was evaluated to assess fluctuations in signal intensity within background regions. BV was calculated in accordance with the NEMA NU2-2018 standard [25]. VOI definition and quantitative analysis was done in syngo.via (Siemens Healthineers, Chicago, IL, USA).

Image-based quantification and dosimetry analysis in patient data

For clinical activity-recovery analysis, a large spherical VOI encompassing the entire visible post-treatment activity distribution within the PET field of view was defined using CT guidance. This approach was chosen to minimize potential activity loss due to respiratory motion, spill-out effects, and incomplete inclusion of the visible activity distribution. Total recovered activity was calculated as the mean activity concentration (Bq/mL) within the VOI multiplied by VOI volume (mL).

This was compared to the expected injected activity. The expected injected activity was determined from the dose calibrator measurement of the therapeutic vial prior to administration then, corrected for residual activity remaining in the administration system after treatment. Residual activity was estimated using dose-rate measurements (µSv/h) of the waste materials compared to the dose-rate measurement of the original therapeutic vial, allowing calculation of the net injected activity delivered to the patient.

Dosimetry was performed using the organ-based approach of Simplicit90Y (Mirada Medical Ltd, Oxford, UK; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) [37], and the voxel-based approach of HERMES (Voxel Dosimetry 1.1 toolbox HERMIA GOLD Smart Workstation 2.17, Hermes Medical Solutions AB, Stockholm, Sweden) [38, 39], both commercial software described previously [40]. For organ-based dosimetry with Simplicit90Y, the PET-derived integrated activity within the liver VOI was used as the activity input to the compartment-based dose model. The organ-based dosimetry approach implemented in Simplicit90Y follows a compartment-based model assuming homogeneous activity distribution and local energy deposition within defined volumes. This approach provides robust dose estimates that are widely used in clinical workflows but does not capture spatial dose heterogeneity within the liver. In contrast, the voxel-based HERMES dosimetry module calculates voxel-level absorbed dose maps from quantitative PET images using the simplified Monte Carlo (sMC) method, in which electron energy is deposited locally while photon transport is simulated through Monte Carlo–based propagation. This framework enables spatially resolved dose estimation and allows investigation of how reconstruction parameters influence voxel-level dosimetry.

Statistical analysis

Agreement between ABS and RS in clinical datasets was assessed using Bland–Altman plots, stratified by reconstructed time (20, 15, 10, 5, 1 min). For each patient and reconstructed time, the mean of both methods expressed as percentage of injected activity (% of injected activity) and their difference (ABS - RS, expressed in percentage points, pp) were calculated. The bias (mean difference) and 95% limits of agreement (LoA = bias ± 1.96 × SD) were reported. Variance modeling & logistic regression

Because PET measurement uncertainty is governed by counting statistics, variability is expected to decrease with increasing injected activity and acquisition time [15]. This produces heteroscedastic error, where variance depends on activity level. The power-law model was therefore used as a descriptive framework to summarize this dependence across acquisition durations.

To characterize the heteroscedasticity of PET quantification error [41, 42], the variance of the percent deviation from injected activity was modeled as a function of activity for each reconstruction duration. For each acquisition time, data were divided into quantile-based bins (minimum three points per bin). Within each bin, systematic error and random variability were separated by subtracting the bin-wise mean signed percent error (i.e., bias) prior to computing the variance. This yields an estimate of precision (spread) that is not inflated by a non-zero mean error. The variance–activity relationship was modeled using a power-law:

Results

The presented results refer to the evaluation of the hot spheres based on the hot phantom data, reconstructed with a matrix of 440 × 440 and a scan time of 50 min using the ABS model, unless otherwisestated. The LAFOV phantom datasets have been used in previous studies [31, 40] and this is noted where relevant. However, in the present work, we performed a novel evaluation. Earlier phantom analyses focused on assessing the performance of the LAFOV scanner using ABS only, reporting image quality metrics such as RC, BV, and dosimetry across various reconstruction times down to 5 min.

Phantom scans: RC, BV and dosimetric evaluation in SAFOV and LAFOV PET

NEMA-based hot-sphere measurements (NU2-2018) showed lower RCs for SAFOV (74%, 67%, 64%, 58%, 58%, 40%) than LAFOV (81%, 76%, 69%, 58%, 55%, 44%) at 50 min, in line with prior data [31] (Suppl. Table 1). At 5 min, LAFOV RCs declined to 74%, 62%, 62%, 55%, 46%, and 35% (Fig. 1). Comparing scatter-correction methods, LAFOV ABS and RS provided similar RCs at 50 min (81%–44% vs. 80%–42%). SAFOV values were 74%–40% (ABS) vs. 72%–37% (RS). At 5 min on LAFOV, RCs were 74%–35% (ABS) vs. 71%–31% (RS) (Suppl. Table 1). Background variability (BV) was substantially higher on SAFOV PET (90%–82%) compared to LAFOV. As expected from the substantially higher sensitivity of the LAFOV system, BV was consistently lower than SAFOV across all reconstructions. Since this effect primarily reflects scanner sensitivity rather than reconstruction methodology, BV results are presented here mainly as contextual image-quality information. LAFOV ABS yielded BV of 32%–26% (Fig. 2), in agreement with [31], and consistently lower than RS across times and matrices, with the advantage most pronounced at 5 min. (Fig. 3). LAFOV BV was 2.7 times lower than SAFOV at 50 min with ABS and a 440 matrix, averaged across sphere sizes (2.6 times with 220 matrix). As expected from the higher system sensitivity of LAFOV PET, background variability was substantially lower than SAFOV; however, the clinically more relevant finding is the preservation of quantitative accuracy at shorter acquisition times.

For dosimetry (hot phantom), true doses were computed via the FLUKA method, as previously described [40]. Table 2 reports mean absorbed dose estimates for the phantom spheres. This analysis is presented as a secondary illustration of how reconstruction-dependent quantification differences propagate into absorbed dose estimation. With voxel-based methods in HERMES, SAFOV was systematically lower in dose (51.9–29.8 Gy) relative to LAFOV (56.7–35.9 Gy). Simplicit90Y™ delivered stable values across conditions, except for the smallest sphere (Fig. 4). The larger discrepancies observed for the smaller spheres between the Simplicit90Y and HERMES calculations are mainly attributable to partial volume effects and the different modeling assumptions of the two dosimetry approaches. While Simplicit90Y assumes homogeneous activity distribution within predefined compartments, the voxel-based HERMES calculation directly propagates voxel-level activity distributions, making it more sensitive to spatial resolution limitations and image noise in small volumes. For 50 min and ABS, the % difference (SAFOV vs. LAFOV) in HERMES ordered by decreasing sphere diameter: -8.5%, -12.1%, − 5.9%, -7.4%, -9.5%, -16.9%. ABS consistently produced more accurate dose estimates (Table 2) across scan times and sphere sizes; the ABS – RS spread on LAFOV was largest at 5 min (ABS reconstruction published previously [31]).

Measured activity in patients did not differ significantly from injected activity at ≥ 10 min using ABS (median deviation: −2.1%); RS consistently underestimated (− 9.3%) activity at all durations (Fig. 5). Equivalence analysis (TOST)

With ABS, quantification was equivalent to injected activity within ± 10% at 20, 15, 10, and 5 min (TOST p ≤ 10− 4), with median bias near zero at 15–20 min (0.1% to 0.4%), mild underestimation at 10–5 min (− 1.3% and − 4.9%), and failure at 1 min (− 20.7%). With RS, equivalence held at 10–20 min (median bias − 5.7% to − 6.1%; p ≤ 0.014) but not at 5 min (-7.1%, p = 0.335) or 1 min (− 16.3%, p = 0.99) (Fig. 7A).

Lin’s CCC

Concordance with injected activity was consistently high for ABS at ≥ 10 min (CCC ≈ 0.994–0.995), remained excellent at 5 min (0.991), and dropped at 1 min (0.880). RS showed slightly lower, but still strong, CCC values at ≥ 10 min (≈ 0.977–0.980), with similar decrement at 5 min (0.977) and larger loss at 1 min (0.915). Bootstrap 95% CIs showed non-overlapping separation favoring ABS at most reconstructed times ≥ 10 min (Fig. 7B).

Logistic regression

After IQR filtering, 224/235 measurements of the clinical data remained (44–46 per reconstructed time). Reliability depended on both scan time and activity. At 1 min, accurate quantification (within ± 10%) was not attainable within the observed range (≤ 6.2 GBq); < 10% success; A₉₀ > 6.2 GBq). At 5 min, predicted thresholds were A80 ≈ 0.86 GBq, A90 ≈ 1.63 GBq and A95 ≈ 2.93 GBq. For ≥ 10 min, ˃ 90% of measurements were within ± 10% across the activity range, leading to numerically unstable A90 estimates (outside observed domain), reflecting effective quantitative convergence (Suppl. Figure 1). As expected, the probability of accurate quantification increased monotonically with both activity and scan time.

Discussion

This study directly compared 90Y PET imaging on a LAFOV (Biograph Vision Quadra) versus a SAFOV (Biograph Vision 600) using NEMA standards regarding RC on a NEMA image quality phantom. In addition, scatter-correction strategies (absolute, ABS; relative, RS) were systematically evaluated using both SAFOV and LAFOV PET phantom data as well as LAFOV patient data across simulated scan durations (20, 15, 10, 5, and 1 min), allowing a direct assessment of quantitative performance and minimal activity thresholds for clinical imaging.

Overall, LAFOV delivered lower BV and more stable recovery performance than SAFOV, while ABS yielded more accurate and consistent quantification than RS across acquisition durations with sufficient count statistics (≥ 5 min).

At the extreme low-count regime represented by the 1 min acquisition, statistical noise dominates the reconstruction and may lead to apparent fluctuations in quantitative agreement. Across sphere sizes and matrices, LAFOV produced lower BV than SAFOV at matched 50 min acquisitions, consistent with its substantially higher sensitivity. The observed reduction in background variability for LAFOV relative to SAFOV is largely attributable to the higher system sensitivity of the extended axial field-of-view. This behavior is expected and has been reported previously for LAFOV systems. In contrast, the more informative findings of the present study relate to the impact of scatter correction strategy on quantitative accuracy and its interaction with acquisition duration. .

In routine PET reconstruction, scatter estimates derived from single-scatter simulation are commonly scaled using tail-fitting approaches. However, model-based absolute scatter estimation may offer improved robustness in extremely low-count imaging conditions such as ⁹⁰Y PET. The comparison between ABS and RS in this work was therefore motivated by the hypothesis that model-based scatter estimation may become increasingly advantageous as count statistics decrease. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies providing a direct comparison between scatter-correction methods in 90Y PET across systems and scan times, including high-sensitivity LAFOV data. All method-comparison and agreement metrics converged on the same conclusion: ABS is quantitatively more reliable than RS for 90Y PET. The qualitative superiority of ABS over RS observed in this study is consistent with previous reports in conventional SAFOV PET systems [24]. The novel aspect of our analysis lies in demonstrating how this effect interacts with the substantially higher sensitivity of LAFOV PET, enabling reliable quantification at significantly reduced acquisition times. Importantly, the qualitative superiority of ABS over RS was consistent across both SAFOV and LAFOV systems. However, the higher sensitivity of LAFOV PET modifies the practical acquisition-time regime, enabling substantially shorter scans while preserving the quantitative stability provided by ABS scatter correction. In patient datasets, the 20-, 15- and 10-min acquisitions with ABS offered the best agreement with injected activity, whereas deviations increased with shorter times and were largest at 1 min, where both methods underestimated activity by > 18%. Five clinical cases showed discrepancies exceeding ± 10% between injected and PET-derived activity. While the present study was not designed to determine the exact origin of these deviations, they illustrate the level of variability that may occur in clinical practice and highlight the potential value of post-therapy imaging for independent verification of delivered activity. s. Bland–Altman analysis showed that at clinically relevant durations (≥ 10 min) the bias (ABS – RS) was consistently positive and modest (+ 6 to + 8 pp) with relatively narrow LoA, indicating that RS underestimates whole-liver activity versus ABS once counts stabilize. At 5 min, the ABS advantage persisted with slightly wider LoA; at 1 min, bias and proportional effects became unstable, consistent with noise-dominated frames. TOST equivalence testing (± 10% to injected) showed equivalence for ABS at 20, 15, 10, and 5 min (near-zero median bias at 15–20 min; small negative bias at 10–5 min), but not at 1 min. RS met equivalence only at ≥ 10 min (with a persistent ~ − 6% bias) and failed at 5 and 1 min. Thus, ABS achieves clinically acceptable accuracy at a shorter duration than RS (5 min vs. 10 min) and remains closer to zero bias at longer scan times. Lin’s CCC analysis likewise favored ABS at ≥ 10 min (CCC ≈ 0.994–0.995), remained excellent at 5 min (0.991) and dropped at 1 min; RS was slightly lower at all durations and degraded more at 1 min. These results match expectations since ABS uses a forward model of scatter that is more robust to low counts, whereas RS (tail-fit) scales scatter to noisy sinogram tails and is fragile when true coincidences are sparse and down-scatter backgrounds are complex (as in 90Y).

Our findings align with prior reports in 68Ga [23] and 18F [22] demonstrating the advantages of model-based ABS scatter correction, and with recent 90Y work contrasting ABS versus RS tail-fit background scaling [24]. Extending these comparisons to LAFOV systems is a novel contribution of this study: ABS remained robust despite the LAFOV’s broader acceptance of scattered/true events, supporting its use on modern high-sensitivity systems. As discussed by Lindemann et al. [23] and Keereman et al. [22], tail-fitted or renormalized approaches are fragile when true coincidences are sparse and down-scatter backgrounds are complex-conditions intrinsic to 90Y imaging (pair-production yield ∼ 0.032% [17], substantial randoms / Bremsstrahlung, and low SNR). Our results therefore support the implementation of ABS in 90Y PET reconstruction workflows for quantitative post-radioembolization assessment.

Comparison of two dosimetry tools showed that Simplicit90Y was less sensitive to reconstruction settings, maintaining dose estimates within 5% across reconstructed times and correction methods. In contrast, HERMES exhibited greater variability, particularly for small spheres and under RS, emphasizing the need for harmonized reconstruction settings when using voxel-based dosimetry. While compartment-based dosimetry provides a robust clinical approximation of absorbed dose, voxel-based approaches allow spatially resolved dose mapping and are therefore more sensitive to image reconstruction characteristics.

The variance analysis provides complementary insight into the noise characteristics of 90Y PET quantification. The observed inverse power-law relationship between variance and injected activity reflects the dominance of Poisson noise in low-count acquisitions. The steep slope for 5-min scans (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:\approx\:1.5$$\end{document}) approximates the theoretical behavior for variance dominated by counting error, implying that image noise is still governed by count statistics at this duration. In contrast, the near-flat scaling observed for 10–20 min suggests a transition toward a noise-floor regime in which residual variability is dominated by systematic factors rather than counting statistics. These include reconstruction regularization, scatter modeling uncertainties, and cross-calibration between the PET system and the dose calibrator used to define the reference injected activity. Together with the logistic regression results, these findings show that precision improves predictably with activity up to about 5 min, after which additional scan time yields minimal variance reduction. This plateau supports the use of 10-min reconstructions as a practical minimum for clinical 90Y PET dosimetry, balancing quantitative reliability and acquisition efficiency.

The logistic regression further delineates the transition from noise-dominated to quantitatively reliable imaging as a function of acquisition time. At 1 min, pronounced stochastic variability and systematic underestimation prevent accurate recovery within ± 10%, even at the highest activities (˃ 6 GBq). At 5 min, the probability of accurate quantification rises sharply with activity; the estimated thresholds were A80 ≈ 0.86 GBq, A90≈1.63 GBq, and A95≈2.93 GBq. Importantly, these activity thresholds are not intended to guide therapeutic dosing but rather to inform the minimum acquisition duration required to obtain quantitatively reliable post-treatment PET imaging for a given administered activity. For ≥ 10 min, all reconstructions satisfied the ± 10%, across the activity range, and the logistic curves plateaued, indicating effective quantitative convergence. The concordance between variance modeling (steep γ at 5 min; flat at ≥ 10 min) and logistic thresholds (transition near ∼ 1.6 GBq at 5 min; full reliability at at ≥ 10 min) reinforces that 10 min represents a quantitative steady state, while 5 min marks the transition from noise-dominated to stable conditions.

For post-SIRT 90Y PET/CT on LAFOV systems, 10 min acquisitions with ABS provide activity-independent quantitative accuracy (CCC ≈ 0.995; equivalence to injected within ± 10%), supporting routine clinical workflows and reliable dosimetry. Five-minute scans may be feasible in selected cases (e.g., Ainj 1.6 GBq in this cohort) when scan time is prioritized, acknowledging a modest residual bias and increased variance. This threshold should be interpreted cautiously, as quantitative performance depends not only on the total administered activity but also on the spatial distribution of microspheres and resulting count density within the liver. One-minute scans, although attractive for throughput, remained noise-dominated in this dataset and were not suitable for quantitative endpoints. From a clinical perspective, the key implication of these findings is the ability to tailor acquisition duration according to scanner sensitivity and expected activity levels. In particular, the higher sensitivity of LAFOV PET systems enables substantially shorter acquisition times while maintaining quantitative accuracy, which may facilitate more efficient post-SIRT imaging workflows and improved patient throughput.

Limitations

First, injected activity was estimated from dose-rate measurements rather than direct dose calibrator readings (pre-injection vial at fixed distance vs. post-procedure waste bin), which may introduce discrepancies between labeled and actual injected activity if handling errors occur; indeed, five cases showed deviations > 10% between injected and imaged activity. Second, applying respiratory motion correction (e.g., optimized binning and gating windows) could further improve accuracy, as residual motion near the diaphragm can blur liver-dome lesions. An additional source of uncertainty in the reference delivered activity is residual retention within the administration system. After application, the vial, catheter, and tubing are transferred together to the waste container. While the vial activity can be measured in the dose calibrator, residual activity within the catheter system cannot be reliably quantified directly because of its geometry and length. Although final flushing is intended to transfer most of the remaining activity to the patient, some activity may still be retained in the catheter in individual cases, which may contribute to discrepancies between estimated delivered activity and PET-derived activity. Although lung shunt fractions in this cohort were generally minimal based on pre-treatment evaluation, small amounts of activity outside the liver (on the order of several tens of MBq) may not be fully captured using a whole-liver VOI approach and could introduce a minor uncertainty in the comparison with injected activity. Finally, VOI-based dosimetry in both phantom and patient studies relied on accurate PET/CT co-registration and nominal sphere diameters. Manual VOI delineation introduces potential inter-operator variability, and partial-volume effects may degrade quantification for small lesions. Despite these constraints, the findings provide practical guidance for optimizing acquisition protocols and quantitative post-therapy assessment in 90Y LAFOV PET/CT.

Conclusions

The LAFOV system demonstrated superior quantitative performance compared to SAFOV, primarily due to its higher sensitivity. Equivalent performance can be achieved with SAFOV only at substantially longer acquisition times. Across all reconstructions, absolute (ABS) scatter correction outperformed the relative (RS) approach, providing higher recovery coefficients, lower variability, and closer agreement with injected activity. In patient scans, ABS enabled reliable 90Y quantification within ± 10% of injected activity at acquisition times ≥ 5 min, whereas RS required ≥ 10 min. Variance and logistic modeling further indicated that quantitative precision stabilizes at ≥ 10 min, while at 5 min reliable quantification was achieved for activities above approximately 1.6 GBq. These findings support LAFOV PET/CT with ABS scatter correction as a robust approach for fast and quantitatively reliable post-SIRT imaging and dosimetry.
