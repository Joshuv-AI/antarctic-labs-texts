Criticality of the abelian sandpile in dimension 1
Adrien REZZOUK

Abstract
We study the critical behavior of the dissipative abelian sandpile model on ℤ \mathbb{Z} with dissipative sites at arbitrary positions ( x k ) (x_{k}) . This is equivalent to studying whether the expected stopping time of a trapped random walk on ℤ \mathbb{Z} is finite. Our main contribution is a precise description of this phase transition via three distinct regimes. Our analysis captures criticality through the asymptotic behavior of an explicit recursive sequence, revealing counterintuitive phenomena in which traps may be farther apart yet the stopping time becomes shorter.

Introduction

A walker wanders on
\mathbb{Z}
but deadly traps are dispersed at fixed sites
x_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. When he encounters a trap, he may escape with probability
2/3
; otherwise his journey ends. How long can he survive? This question is equivalent to determining whether the Abelian sandpile model — a toy model introduced by physicists Per Bak, Chao Tang, and Kurt Wiesenfeld in
[
1
]
and later generalized by Deepak Dhar in
[
2
]
— exhibits critical behavior.

More specifically, we consider the Abelian sandpile model on the graph
\mathbb{Z}
, where certain sites, called dissipative or trap vertices are located at positions
x_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Each such site is connected to an additional vertex — the sink — through a third edge, allowing grains of sand to be lost. These trap vertices thus have degree 3. Whenever the height at such a vertex exceeds 3, it topples by sending one grain to the left, one to the right, and one into the sink. The dynamics continues until all vertices are stable, which occurs when the system is confined to a finite volume, e.g.,
\llbracket-n,n\rrbracket
, and the boundary sites are connected to the sink.

By adding grains of sand at uniformly chosen sites, one defines a Markov chain on the set of height configurations
\eta
over the graph. In finite volume, this chain admits a unique invariant measure, denoted
\mu_{n}
for the volume
\llbracket-n,n\rrbracket
, which is the uniform distribution over the set of recurrent configurations. For a concise yet precise introduction to the Abelian sandpile model, see, for instance
[
3

; for a more comprehensive treatment covering additional aspects, see
[

.

The dissipative version of the Abelian sandpile model is analyzed in
[
5
]
, where the authors introduce a notion of criticality on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For a given configuration
\eta
and site
x
, let
Av(x,\eta)
denote the number of sites involved in the avalanche triggered by adding a grain at
x
. The model is said to be non-critical on
\mathbb{Z}
if

\forall x\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{n}}[Av(x,\eta)]<\infty.

The transition between critical and non-critical behavior in one dimension is further explored in
[
6
]
. There, the authors use the characterization of the criticality of the model in terms of the divergence of a stopping time associated with a trapped random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. Their analysis focuses on the specific case where the traps are symetrically distributed and the distances between traps, denoted
\lvert I_{k}\rvert=x_{k}-x_{k-1}
, satisfy the recursive relation
\lvert I_{k+1}\rvert=c\lvert I_{k}\rvert^{2}
for
k\geq 0
. They show that the model exhibits critical behavior if and only if
c\geq 1
by deriving bounds the expected stopping time.

In this work, we consider arbitrary sequences
(x_{k})
of trap positions and compute the expectation of the above stopping time of the random walk. We first reduce the problem of criticality to the analysis of the trapped random walk on the half-line
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, where the traps are located at positions
x_{k}>0
.
Our analysis reveals several phenomena that challenge intuition. First, while one might expect that spacing traps further apart should always increase escape times, we discover situations where
doubling all trap distances can turn an infinite expected stopping time into a finite one
.

More fundamentally, we identify three distinct regimes separated by two natural thresholds. The first threshold appears at the geometric sequence
\left(\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{k}
— the square of the golden ratio — below which all trap configurations yield non-critical behavior. Above this lies an intermediate regime where critical and non-critical systems coexist, with a decisive criterion being whether the ratio
x_{k+1}/x_{k}^{2}
consistently stays above or below 1 (which also establishes that recursive constructions such as
\lvert I_{k+1}\rvert=c\lvert I_{k}\rvert^{2}
, studied in
[
6
]
, capture this transition).
Finally, any sequence that eventually dominates all double-exponentials
a^{2^{k}}
with
a>1
necessarily produces critical behavior. To do so, we restrict the walk to a finite interval
[-x_{n},x_{n}]
and compute the expected stopping time of this truncated walk. We then analyze its behavior as
n\to\infty
in order to determine whether the expected stopping time diverges, which corresponds to the critical regime.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we state our main results, and give various examples to illustrate the possible cases. Then, we begin by formulating the problem in terms of the Green function of the graph in Section
3
. We then prove that the criticality of the Abelian sandpile model is equivalent to the divergence of the expected value
\mathbb{E}_{x}(T)
, where
T
denotes the stopping time of a random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
in which the walker, when located at a trap site
x_{k}
, jumps with equal probability to the left, to the right, or into the sink.

In Section
4
, we derive the main equation on
\mathbb{E}_{x}(T)
that will be solved in Section
5
. We also prove that the criticality of the model is preserved under modifications of finitely many traps, and we show how the study of the walk on
\mathbb{Z}
reduces to the study of the walks on the half-lines
\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

Finally, in Section
5
, we express the criticality in terms of the limit of an explicit sequence, establishing this three-regime structure and providing practical criteria to determine the critical nature of many trap configurations.

2.1
Overview of the main results

We consider the trapped random walk with an arbitrary set of traps
D
.

The criticality of the full system is completely determined by the criticality of the corresponding half-line processes. By this, we mean the trapped random walk on
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
(resp.
\mathbb{Z}_{<0}=\{\dots,-3,-2,-1\}
) with the same transition probabilities as the original walk, which is stopped upon reaching either the absorbing state
s
or the boundary point
0
.

More precisely, the full system is critical if and only if at least one of these two half-line processes is critical (see Theorem
4.4
for the mathematical formulation).

Remark 2.4
.

The choice of
0
as the reference point is not essential. By Theorem
2.1
, the criticality of the system does not depend on the starting point
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, the integer line
\mathbb{Z}
can be split into two halves at any point.

Consequently, we can focus without loss of generality on the half-line
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. We denote

D_{+}=D\cap\mathbb{Z}_{>0}=\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1},\qquad 0<x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots,

and study the random walk with traps at positions
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. We use the convention
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
. In the following, when we refer to the model or system as critical or non-critical, we mean the walk on the half-line. For the full walk on
\mathbb{Z}
(or the original abelian sandpile), criticality occurs as soon as at least one of the half-line walks is critical.

Remark 2.5
.

In the special case of a symmetric distribution of traps (i.e.,
x\in D
if and only if
-x\in D
), the criticality of the walk on
\mathbb{Z}
is equivalent to the criticality of the walk on
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
D_{+}=\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the ordered sequence of trap positions on
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

Define the sequences
(p_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
by the recursions

\left\{\begin{aligned} p_{1}&=x_{1},\\
p_{n}&=x_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_{n}-x_{i})p_{i},&\text{for }n\geq 2,\end{aligned}\right.\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} q_{1}&=x_{1}^{2},\\
q_{n}&=x_{n}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_{n}-x_{i})(q_{i}+1),&\text{for }n\geq 2.\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

Then,
\dfrac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}
is a positive increasing sequence, and the model is critical if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}=\infty.

By analyzing this sequence, we find the following cases where we know whether the model is critical or non-critical.

Theorem 2.7
.

If
x_{k}=\mathrm{O}(\rho^{k})
with
\rho<\dfrac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, then the system is non-critical.

The geometric sequence with ratio
\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
acts as a threshold: sequences growing more slowly always yield critical systems, whereas slightly faster ones may be non-critical.

Theorem 2.8
.

For any increasing sequence of integers
(y_{k})
such that

k\left(\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{k}=o(y_{k})\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,

there exists a trap sequence
(x_{k})
such that the model is critical and
x_{k}\leq y_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
.

Remark 2.9
.

For all
\rho>\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, define
y_{k}=\lfloor\rho^{k}\rfloor
. Then we can apply Theorem
2.8
to find a trap sequence
(x_{k})
such that the model is critical and
x_{k}\leq\rho^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
. This shows that Theorem
2.7
does not extend beyond the threshold

\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, since for larger
\rho
one can construct counterexamples.

Above
\left(\dfrac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{k}
, critical and non-critical coexist. When the sequence is regular enough, in the sense that
\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_{n}^{2}}
stays consistently on one side of 1 (either always below or always above), then we can determine whether the system is critical or not:

Theorem 2.10
.

If
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{x_{n+1}}{x_{n}^{2}}<1
, then the model is non-critical.

On the contrary:

Theorem 2.11
.

If
\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(x_{n}\prod_{r=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{1+x_{r}}\right)=\infty
, then the model is critical.

Remark 2.12
.

This condition is not necessary: Theorem
2.8
provides counterexamples.

If
x_{k}\leq\rho^{k}
, then the series
\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(x_{n}\prod_{r=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{1+x_{r}}\right)
converges, because
\displaystyle\prod_{r=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{1+x_{r}}\leq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}
.

Corollary 2.13
.

If
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{x_{n+1}}{x_{n}^{2}}>1
, then the model is critical.

Corollary 2.14
.

If, for every
a>1
, there exists
k\geq 1
such that
x_{k}>a^{2^{k}}
, then the system is critical.

Remark 2.15
.

The sequences
a^{2^{k}}
thus form the boundary between sequences that may yield a non-critical system and those that necessarily yield a critical system.

Summary: if
x_{k}\ll\left(\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{k}
, the system is non-critical.
If
x_{k}\gg a^{\,2^{k}}
, the system is critical.
In all other cases, both regimes coexist; a practical test consists in examining the ratio
\dfrac{x_{k+1}}{x_{k}^{2}}
.

Example 2.16
.

Each of the following sequences of traps is associated with a non-critical model:

•

x_{k}\sim a^{b^{k}}
, with
a>1
,
1<b<2
.

•

x_{k}\sim\lambda a^{2^{k}}
, with
a>1
,
\lambda>1
.

Example 2.17
.

Each of the following sequences of traps is associated with a critical model:

•

x_{k}\sim a^{b^{k}}
, with
a>1
and
b>2
.

•

x_{k}\sim\lambda a^{2^{k}}
, with
a>1
and
\lambda<1
.

•

x_{k}=a^{2^{k}}(1+\varepsilon_{k})
with
a>1
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\varepsilon_{k}|<\infty
.

Remark 2.18
.

These examples make it possible to recover results from
[
6
]
: the recurrence relation on
\lvert I_{k}\rvert

|I_{k+1}|=c\,|I_{k}|^{2},

yields the explicit form

\lvert I_{k}\rvert=\frac{1}{c}\,(c\lvert I_{0}\rvert)^{2^{k}}.

Since
\lvert I_{k}\rvert=x_{k}-x_{k-1}
, we obtain

x_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lvert I_{j}\rvert\ \sim\ \lvert I_{k}\rvert\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Setting
\lambda=\frac{1}{c}
, these examples show that the system is
critical
for
c\geq 1
and
non-critical
for
c<1
.

Remark 2.19
.

The method exposed here can be used to solve a more general case where the probability to fall in a trap is
p\in(0,1)
.
Let
\alpha=\frac{2p}{1-p}
, if
\limsup\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_{n}^{2}}<\alpha
, the model is non-critical and if
\liminf\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_{n}^{2}}>\alpha,
the model is critical.

2.2
Remarks on monotonicity

We consider two sequences of traps
(x_{k})
and
(y_{k})
, and denote by
T_{x}
(resp.
T_{y}
) the associated stopping times. We would like to compare
\mathbb{E}(T_{x})
and
\mathbb{E}(T_{y})
if we have some comparison between
(x_{k})
and
(y_{k})
.

Proposition 2.20
.

If
\{x_{k}\}\subset\{y_{k}\}
, then
T_{x}\geq T_{y}
.

Proof.

This is clear from Proposition
4.1
: the random walk with traps placed at
\{y_{k}\}
encounters more traps than the one with traps at
\{x_{k}\}
.
∎

We would have liked a simple characterization of criticality in terms of the asymptotic analysis of the sequence
x_{k}
, that is, ideally a property like
“if
x_{k}=o(y_{k})
and
\mathbb{E}(T_{y})<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}(T_{x})<\infty
”
.
This would follow from a monotonicity of the expectation of the form
“if
x_{k}\leq y_{k}
, then
\mathbb{E}(T_{x})\leq\mathbb{E}(T_{y})
”
.
However, this hypothesis, although intuitive, turns out to be false due to the previously exposed results:

Counterexample:
we have seen that if
x_{k}\sim\lambda a^{2^{k}}
, then if
\lambda>1
,
\mathbb{E}(T_{x})<\infty
, whereas if
\lambda\leq 1
,
\mathbb{E}(T_{x})=\infty
.
Thus, by taking for example
\lambda_{1}=1
and
\lambda_{2}=2
,
one passes from an infinite to a finite expectation even though the traps have been more widely spaced (by a homothety)
.

Our intuition is challenged because one might expect that spacing out the traps would increase the time to get from one trap to the next, thus increasing the stopping time.
However, adopting the point of vue of Proposition
4.1
, we may understand that sometimes spacing out the traps places them in strategic positions that can stop trajectories that would otherwise have been very long.

What is even more remarkable is that for all
\rho>\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
there exists a sequence of traps
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
such that
x_{k}\leq\rho^{k}
, for which the model remains
critical
(see remark
2.9
). Observe that
x_{k}\ll a^{b^{k}}
for all
a>1
,
b>1
; that is, this sequence grows much more slowly than any double-exponential sequence, and is thus negligible compared to the non-critical cases discussed above.

3
Definition of criticality and connection with the trapped random walk

Define
D=\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
the set of traps.

We can then define the graph’s Laplacian matrix and Green’s function:

Definition 3.1

(Laplacian of the graph)
.

The Laplacian matrix
\Delta
is defined for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
by :

\Delta_{x,y}=\begin{cases}\deg(x)&\text{si }x=y,\\
-1&\text{si }x\sim y,\\
0&\text{sinon},\end{cases}

(3)

where the degree is given by:

\deg(x)=2+\mathbf{1}_{x\in D}.

Definition 3.2

(Green’s function)
.

Let
V_{n}=\llbracket-n,n\rrbracket
denote the restricted graph. The Green’s function on this graph is defined as:

G_{n}(x,y)=\Delta_{n}^{-1}(x,y),

(4)

where
\Delta_{n}
is the discrete Laplacian matrix restricted to the vertices in
\llbracket-n,n\rrbracket
.

Remark 3.3
.

We have
G_{n}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}(n(x,y,\cdot))
(see e.g.
[
4
]
), where expectation is taken according to the invariant measure of the Markov chain, and where
n(x,y,\eta)
denotes the number of grains that the vertex
y
emits before stabilization when a grain of sand is added to
x
from the configuration
\eta
.

Definition 3.4

(Criticality)
.

We define
\displaystyle G(x,y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\uparrow G_{n}(x,y)=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}G_{n}(x,y)\in[0,\infty]
whose existence will be justified later.
Then, as defined in
[
5
]
, the model is said to be
non-critical
if for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}G(x,y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{n}(x,y)<\infty.

Otherwise, the model is said to be
critical
.

Remark 3.5
.

The model is
non-critical
if, and only if, for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, the average size of an avalanche triggered in
x
is finite, as specified in
[
5
]
.

Connection with the trapped random walk defined in Section
2
.

Recall the random walk
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
V_{n}\cup\{s\}
introduced in Section
2
, with stopping time
T_{n}
upon reaching the sink
s
. Its transition matrix
P
from (
1
) can be written as

P(x,y)=\delta_{x,y}-\frac{\Delta_{n}(x,y)}{\deg(x)},

where
\Delta_{n}
is the graph Laplacian on
V_{n}
and
\deg(x)
is the degree of
x\in V_{n}
.

Let
\mathbb{E}_{x}(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}(\cdot\mid X_{0}=x).
We can write

\Delta_{n}=\Lambda(I_{n}-P)
where
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix with
\Lambda(x,x)=\deg(x)
. Hence, if the series of the right-hand term converges:

G_{n}=(I_{n}-P)^{-1}\Lambda^{-1}=(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P^{k})\Lambda^{-1}.

(5)

We can define
Q_{n}(x,y)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(P^{k})_{x,y}\in[0,\infty]
.

Thus
Q_{n}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{x}(\text{number of times $y$ is reached in the random walk})
.
Then,

\sum_{y\in V_{n}}Q_{n}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{x}(T_{n}).

(6)

It is then clear from Markov’s property that
\mathbb{E}_{x}(T_{n})
is finite, so
\sup_{x\in V_{n}}\sum_{y\in V_{n}}Q_{n}(x,y)<\infty
which guarantees that the
P^{k}
series converges.

Finally:

\displaystyle G_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\deg(y)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(P^{k})_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{n}(x,y)}{\deg(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\deg(y)}\,\mathbb{E}_{x}\big(\text{number of reaches of }y\text{ during random walk on }V_{n}\big).

G_{n}(x,y)
is therefore increasing with
n
: we can couple the random walks on the different
V_{n}
by considering the global random walk on
\mathbb{Z}\cup\{s\}
so that the walk on
V_{n}
stops when the global random walk falls in
s
or exits
\llbracket-n,n\rrbracket
.

This justifies the existence of
G(x,y)
. We can now express the criticality of the model from the random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
:

\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{E}_{x}(T_{n})\leq\sum_{y\in V_{n}}G_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{y\in V_{n}}\frac{Q_{n}(x,y)}{\deg(y)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x}(T_{n}).

Let
T=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\uparrow T_{n}
the time to reach
s
for the global random walk on
\mathbb{Z}\cup\{s\}
. We thus obtain the following characterization:

Proposition 3.6
.

The model is non-critical if and only if for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\ \mathbb{E}_{x}(T)<\infty.

Remark 3.7
.

We will see in the next section that this is equivalent to the existence of some
x\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{x}(T)<\infty
, which will complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

In practice, we will look at
\mathbb{E}(T_{n})
and then make
n
tend towards
+\infty.

Theorem 4.4

Moreover, setting

Proof.

We decompose the sample path into excursions away from

\tau_{0}=0,\quad\tau_{k+1}=\inf\{n>\tau_{k}:X_{n}\in\{0,s\}\mid X_{\tau_{k}}=0\}.

Let

be the number of excursions before absorption.
(Caution:
\mathcal{N}
is not the random variable defined in Proposition

Each excursion has the structure:

or

each, or directly to

\mathbb{E}_{0}[T]<\infty\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\mathbb{E}_{1}[\tau]<\infty\;\text{ and }\;\mathbb{E}_{-1}[\tau]<\infty

∎

which leads to the approximate solution

. Indeed, defining

where the second equality follows from equation (

Q_{k+2}=3Q_{k+1}-Q_{k}.

This second-order linear recurrence has characteristic roots

and

,
R_{k}=Q_{n-k}-Q_{n-k+1}\approx C\phi^{k}
for

. Evaluating at

gives, for

R_{j}\approx\frac{C^{\prime}}{\phi^{j}}.,

We would then conclude by lemma
5.2
: if

,
\rho<\phi
, then
R_{k}x_{k+1}
is summable.

To treat the real case, we introduce parameters
0<\lambda<1
— a uniform lower bound for
\tfrac{k^{2}}{j(j+1)}
on the indices retained in (
17
) — and
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, the number of indices kept in the sum. We will let
\lambda\to 1
, and
\kappa\to\infty
to approach the idealized case given by equation (
18
).

There exists
k_{0}
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}

\frac{k^{2}}{(k+\kappa)(k+\kappa+1)}\geq\lambda.

Hence, by inequality (
17
), for
k_{0}\leq k\leq n
,

R_{k-1}\geq R_{k}+\lambda\sum_{j=k}^{\min(k+\kappa,n)}R_{j}.

Let
p\geq k_{0}
,
n>p
. We will bound from above
R_{p}
independently of
n
. For all
k\leq p
,

R_{k-1}\geq R_{k}+\lambda\sum_{j=k}^{\min(k+\kappa,p)}R_{j}.

Define
(\alpha_{k})
by
\alpha_{0}=1
and

\alpha_{k+1}=\alpha_{k}+\lambda\sum_{j=\max(0,k-\kappa)}^{k}\alpha_{j}.

Then, by induction for all
k\leq p-k_{0}
,

R_{p-k}\geq\alpha_{k}R_{p}.

The sequence
(\alpha_{k})
satisfies a homogeneous linear recurrence relation of order
\kappa+1
for
k\geq\kappa
with characteristic polynomial

P(X)=X^{\kappa+1}-X^{\kappa}-\lambda(X^{\kappa}+\cdots+1).

We may apply Lemma
5.3
:
P
has a unique positive root that we denote by

. Clearly,
\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}\geq 1+\lambda
and by the lemma,

\alpha_{k}\sim C\,\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{k}\qquad(k\to\infty)

for some
C>0
.

For all
n\geq p
,

R_{p}\leq\frac{R_{k_{0}}}{\alpha_{p-k_{0}}}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{p}}

(19)

for some
C^{\prime}>0
(
R_{k_{0}}\leq 1
by (
14
)).

By definition,
\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}>1
satisfies

\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{\kappa+1}=\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{\kappa}+\lambda\frac{\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{\kappa+1}-1}{\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}-1},

which is equivalent to

\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{2}-(2+\lambda)\,\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}+1=-\frac{\lambda}{\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{\kappa}}.

Recall that
\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}>1+\lambda
, therefore for
\lambda\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
,

\frac{\lambda}{\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{\kappa}}\leq\frac{\lambda}{(1+\lambda)^{\kappa}}\leq\Bigl(\tfrac{2}{3}\Bigr)^{\kappa-1}\;\longrightarrow\;0\quad(\kappa\to\infty).

Taking
\kappa\to\infty
gives

\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}=\frac{2+\lambda+\sqrt{(2+\lambda)^{2}-4(1+\frac{\lambda}{\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}^{\kappa}})}}{2}\to\phi_{\lambda}:=\frac{2+\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4\lambda}}{2}.

Letting
\lambda\to 1
, we recover
\phi_{\lambda}\to\phi
.

Thus, for any
\rho<\phi
, there exist
\kappa,\lambda,\rho^{\prime}
such that

\rho<\rho^{\prime}<\phi_{\kappa,\lambda}.

Hence, by inequality (
19
), for all
p\geq k_{0}
,

R_{p}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\rho^{\prime p}}.

Finally,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}R_{k}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}R_{k}x_{k+1}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}-1}x_{k+1}+C^{\prime}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}x_{k+1}\rho^{\prime-k}.

Thus, if
x_{k}=O(\rho^{k})
the series converges and by lemma
5.2
, the model is non-critical.
∎
