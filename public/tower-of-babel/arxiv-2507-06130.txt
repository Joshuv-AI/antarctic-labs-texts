Unit lattices of D 4 D_{4} -quartic number fields with signature ( 2 , 1 ) (2,1)
Sergio Ricardo Zapata Ceballos, Sara Chari, Erik Holmes, Fatemeh Jalalvand, Rahinatou Yuh Njah Nchiwo, Kelly O’Connor, Fabian Ramirez, Sameera Vemulapalli

Abstract
. There has been a recent surge of interest on distributions of shapes of unit lattices in number fields, due to both their applications to number theory and the lack of known results. In this work we focus on D 4 D_{4} -quartic fields with signature ( 2 , 1 ) (2,1) ; such fields have a rank 2 2 unit group. Viewing the unit lattice as a point of GL 2 ( ℤ ) \ 𝔥 \gl_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mfh , we prove that every lattice which arises this way must correspond to a transcendental point on the boundary of a certain fundamental domain of GL 2 ( ℤ ) \ 𝔥 \gl_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mfh . Moreover, we produce three explicit (algebraic) points of GL 2 ( ℤ ) \ 𝔥 \gl_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mfh which are limit points of the set of (points associated to) unit lattices of D 4 D_{4} -quartic fields with signature ( 2 , 1 ) (2,1) .

Introduction

Our interest in the study of distributions of shapes of unit lattices in number fields began with the MathOverflow post
[
R
]
, in which A. Reznikov poses a very fundamental set of questions (communicated to him by M. Gromov) on the shape of unit lattices in number fields.

Namely, we are interested in the following question. For a permutation group
G\subseteq S_{n}
and a pair
(r,s)
of positive integers with
r+2s=n
, let
{\mathscr{F}}(G,r,s)
be the set of (isomorphism classes) of degree
n
number fields with Galois group
G
and signature
(r,s)
(by standard abuse of notation in arithmetic statistics, we say a number field has Galois group
G
if its Galois closure has Galois group
G
). For any field
L\in{\mathscr{F}}(G,r,s)
, let
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r}
be the real embeddings of
L
and
\tau_{1},\overline{\tau_{1}},\dots,\tau_{s},\overline{\tau_{s}}
be the pairs of complex conjugate embeddings of
L
. Let
E_{L}
be the group of units of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
modulo torsion. There is a
logarithmic embedding

\Log\colon E_{L}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r+s}

u\mapsto(\log\lvert\sigma_{1}(u)\rvert,\ldots,\log\lvert\sigma_{r}(u)\rvert,2\log\lvert\tau_{1}(u)\rvert,\ldots,2\log\lvert\tau_{s}(u)\rvert).

Restricting the standard quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{r+s}
to
E_{L}
equips
E_{L}
with the structure of a lattice of rank
r+s-1
. We say
E_{L}
is the
unit lattice
of
L
.

We define the
unit shape
of
L
to be the equivalence class of this lattice under scaling, reflection, and rotation. Hence for each such number field
L
we obtain a point, which we call
p_{L}
, in the moduli space
\mathcal{S}_{r+s-1}=\GL_{r+s-1}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\GL_{r+s-1}(\mathbb{R})/\GO_{r+s-1}(\mathbb{R})
of unimodular rank
r+s-1
lattices up to homothety and reflection. We call
p_{L}
the
unit shape
of
L
. Define the set:

\Omega(G,r,s)\coloneqq\{p_{L}:L\in{\mathscr{F}}(G,r,s)\}.

A very natural question is: given a group
G
and a signature
(r,s)
, where do the unit shapes of
G
-extensions with signature
(r,s)
lie in
\mathcal{S}_{r+s-1}
? This is, a priori, a very difficult question – for example, proving that
\Omega(G,r,s)\neq\emptyset
implies that there exists a
G
-extension of signature
(r,s)
. In particular, understanding when
\Omega(G,r,s)\neq\emptyset
would provide an answer to the inverse Galois problem.

Previous Work.

Very little is known about Question

\leq 1

2

2
, the the first signature and degree to consider is that of totally real cubic fields. In this case, we identify
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
with the standard fundamental domain, which we call
{\mathscr{F}}

\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mfh

When

{\mathscr{F}}

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{i\sqrt{3}}{2}

{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
with the standard fundamental domain of

\Omega(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z},3,0)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{i\sqrt{3}}{2}.

Now consider the case

and
(r,s)=(3,0)
. It turns out that Question
1
is already very difficult, but is easier when one allows non-maximal orders. Let
{\mathscr{F}}^{\ord}(G,r,s)
denote the set of (isomorphism classes of) orders in degree

number fields with Galois group

(r,s)

In this case, we have:

•

in 1991 shows that the closure

•

•

finally, work of

To the best of our knowledge, almost nothing is known about

[

]

[

]

[

involve producing orders which are likely maximal, but a very difficult squarefree sieve is needed to

Main Results.

In this paper, we investigate Question

D_{4}

2

For the rest of this article, let

2

\gl_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mfh

\mfh

{\mathscr{F}}

1.4.

Main Question for
D_{4}
with signature
(2,1)
. —

What is
\Omega(D_{4},2,1)
?

As in the totally real
S_{3}
case discussed above, we remark that answering Question
1
for (possibly non-maximal) orders in
D_{4}
-quartic fields with signature
(2,1)
is significantly easier than answering the problem for maximal orders. In this article, we focus on the case of maximal orders and, while the answer to Question

remains mysterious, we show that
\Omega(D_{4},2,1)
consists entirely of transcendental elements on the boundary of
{\mathscr{F}}
(see Theorem
1
). Despite the fact that
\Omega(D_{4},2,1)
is entirely transcendental, we further prove that its set of limits points contains at least three explicit algebraic numbers (see Theorem

) and conjecture the following:

1.5.

Conjecture. —

The set of limit points of
{\Omega(D_{4},2,1)}
equals the boundary of
{\mathscr{F}}
.

Our conjecture is supported by computational evidence; see Figure

. It would be interesting to conjecture a distribution for
\{p_{L}\}_{L\in{\mathscr{F}}(D_{4},2,1)}
given some ordering of
{\mathscr{F}}(D_{4},2,1)
(three natural choices of ordering are the ordering by absolute discriminant, conductor, and product of ramified primes).

Our first major result is that the shape of
E_{L}
is constrained, and the point
p_{L}
associated to it
must
be transcendental.

1.6.

Theorem. —

Let
L
be a
D_{4}
-quartic field with signature
(2,1)
. Then
p_{L}\in{\mathscr{F}}
is a transcendental number. Moreover,
p_{L}
is contained in the boundary of
{\mathscr{F}}
, i.e. is contained in either

(1)

the left vertical line
x=0
and
1\leq y
,

(2)

the lower arc

for
0\leq x\leq 1/2
and

(3)

or the right vertical line

and
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\leq y
.

In particular,

is never equal to the cuspidal points
i
and
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{i\sqrt{3}}{2}
. One basic question one may ask about the topology of

is: what is its set of limit points?
1

1

Corollary. —

With the notation as in Theorem

Theorem. —

The set of limit points of

Infinitely many points of

2.1.
Vinogradov notation

Throughout the paper we use the following asyptotic notation, first introduced by Vinogradov. For a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and two functions
f,g\colon S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and some parameter
\epsilon

f(x)\ll_{\epsilon}g(x)
means that there exist positive real numbers
M_{\epsilon}
and
C_{\epsilon}
, dependent on
\epsilon

x\in S
with
x\geq M_{\epsilon}
we have
f(x)\leq C_{\epsilon}g(x)
. Similarly, the notation
f(x)\asymp_{\epsilon}g(x)
means that there exist positive real numbers
M_{\epsilon}
,
C_{\epsilon}
, and
D_{\epsilon}
, dependent on
\epsilon
, such that for all
x\in S
with
x\geq M_{\epsilon}
we have
C_{\epsilon}g(x)\leq f(x)\leq D_{\epsilon}g(x)
.

We also abuse the big-O and little-o notation in the following way. We write

if there exist positive real numbers

such that for all
x\in S
with
x\geq M

\lvert f(x)-g(x)\rvert\leq Ch(x)
. Similarly, we write
f(x)=g(x)+o(h(x))

, there exists a positive real number

such that for all
x\in S

x\geq M_{C}
, we have
\lvert f(x)-g(x)\rvert\leq Ch(x)
.

\Omega(D_{4},2,1)
contains
i\sqrt{3}
. This comes from analyzing a different construction of explicitly parametrized units due to Nakamula
[
N
]
, which we begin by outlining here; we encourage the reader to consult Section
3
of
[
N
]
for further details. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, we define

f_{n}(x)\coloneqq x^{4}-nx^{3}+3x^{2}-nx+1.

5.12.

Lemma (Lemma 2,
[
N
]
). —

Suppose
n>3
,
n^{2}-4
is not a square,
n^{2}-4\equiv 1\pmod{4}
,
25-4n^{2}
is not a square, and
25-4n^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{n^{2}-4})\neq\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{25-4n^{2}})
. Then
f_{n}
is irreducible and
L_{n}\coloneqq\mathbb{Q}[x]/f_{n}(x)
is a
D_{4}
-quartic field with signature
(2,1)
and
L_{n}
contains the real quadratic subfield
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{n^{2}-4})
.

5.13.

Lemma (Lemma 3,
[
N
]
). —

There are infinitely many
n
satisfying the assumptions of Lemma
5
.

Now, in situation of Lemma
5
, we may explicitly describe the units of
L_{n}
. Let
\rho\in L_{n}
denote the root of
f_{n}
.

5.14.

Theorem (Proposition 4,
[
N
]
). —

Under the assumptions of Lemma
5
, a basis of
E_{L_{n}}
is given by
\{\iota(\rho),\iota(\rho+\rho^{-1})\}
.

5.15.

Proposition. —

The set of limit points of
\Omega(D_{4},2,1)
contains
i\sqrt{3}
.

Proof.
We will construct a sequence of
D_{4}
-quartic fields
\{L_{n}\}
such that
p_{L_{n}}\rightarrow i\sqrt{3}
. Let
n
denote an integer satisfying the assumptions of Lemma
5
. Note that Lemma
5
shows that there are infinitely many such
n
.

Because
f_{n}
is a reciprocal polynomial, if
f_{n}(\alpha)=0
for some nonzero
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, then
f_{n}(\alpha^{-1})=0
. Let
\epsilon
denote a real root of
f_{n}
with
\lvert\epsilon\rvert\geq 1
, and let
\epsilon^{-1}
be its inverse. Let
\eta,\eta^{-1}
denote the complex roots of
f_{n}
. As usual, let
\rho\in L_{n}
denote the root of
f_{n}
.

We begin by computing
\Log(\iota(\rho))
and
\Log(\iota(\rho+\rho^{-1}))
. Order the real embeddings
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
of
L_{n}
so that
\sigma_{1}(\rho)=\epsilon
. Then:

\Log(\iota(\rho))=(\log\lvert\epsilon\rvert,-\log\lvert\epsilon\rvert,2\log\lvert\eta\rvert).

Because the sum of the coordinates of
\Log(\iota(\rho))
must be
0
, we must have
\log\lvert\eta\rvert=0
, so we may write

(41)

\Log(\iota(\rho))=(\log\lvert\epsilon\rvert,-\log\lvert\epsilon\rvert,0).

Similarly, we have:

(42)

\Log(\iota(\rho+\rho^{-1}))=\big(\log\lvert\epsilon+\epsilon^{-1}\rvert,\log\lvert\epsilon+\epsilon^{-1}\rvert,-2\log\lvert\epsilon+\epsilon^{-1}\rvert\big).

A computation shows that
\Log(\iota(\rho))\cdot\Log(\iota(\rho+\rho^{-1}))=0
, which implies that
E_{L}
is an orthogonal lattice, so
p_{L_{n}}
lies on the left boundary of
{\mathscr{F}}
. Therefore, if
\frac{\rvert\iota(\rho+\rho^{-1})\lvert}{\lvert\iota(\rho)\rvert}\geq 1
, then

(43)

p_{L_{n}}=i\frac{\lvert\iota(\rho+\rho^{-1})\rvert}{\lvert\iota(\rho)\rvert}.

Combining Equation
41
and Equation
42
, we obtain:

(44)

\frac{\lvert\iota(\rho+\rho^{-1})\rvert}{\lvert\iota(\rho)\rvert}=\Bigg\lvert\frac{\sqrt{6}\log\lvert\epsilon+\epsilon^{-1}\rvert}{\sqrt{2}\log\lvert\epsilon\rvert}\Bigg\rvert=\Bigg\lvert\frac{\sqrt{3}\log\lvert\epsilon+\epsilon^{-1}\rvert}{\log\lvert\epsilon\rvert}\Bigg\rvert.

An explicit computation of the discriminant of
f_{n}
shows that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lvert\Disc(f_{n})\rvert=\infty
. Now,

\Disc(f_{n})=(\epsilon-\epsilon^{-1})^{2}(\epsilon-\eta)^{2}(\epsilon-\eta^{-1})^{2}(\epsilon^{-1}-\eta)^{2}(\epsilon^{-1}-\eta^{-1})^{2}(\eta-\eta^{-1})^{2}.

In the computation in Equation
41
, we showed that
\lvert\eta\rvert=1
because
\log\lvert\eta\rvert=0
. So, because
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lvert\Disc(f_{n})\rvert=\infty
, we must have
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lvert\epsilon\rvert=\infty
. Hence,
\log\lvert\epsilon+\epsilon^{-1}\rvert=\log\lvert\epsilon\rvert+o(1)
. Therefore:

(45)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{3}\log\lvert\epsilon+\epsilon^{-1}\rvert}{\log\lvert\epsilon\rvert}=\sqrt{3}.

Substituting Equation
45
and Equation
44
into Equation
43
, we obtain that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}p_{L_{n}}=i\sqrt{3}.

∎
