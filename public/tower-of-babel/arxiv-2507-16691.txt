On Causal Inference for the Survivor Function Corresponding author: robert_strawderman@urmc.rochester.edu
Benjamin R. Baer

Abstract
In this expository paper, we consider the problem of causal inference and efficient estimation for the counterfactual survivor function. This problem has previously been considered in the literature in several papers, each relying on the imposition of conditions meant to identify the desired estimand from the observed data. These conditions, generally referred to as either implying or satisfying coarsening at random, are inconsistently imposed across this literature and, in all cases, fail to imply coarsening at random. We establish the first general characterization of coarsening at random, and also sequential coarsening at random, for this estimation problem. Other contributions include the first general characterization of the set of all influence functions for the counterfactual survival probability under sequential coarsening at random, and the corresponding nonparametric efficient influence function. These characterizations are general in that neither impose continuity assumptions on either the underlying failure or censoring time distributions. We further show how the latter compares to alternative forms recently derived in the literature, including establishing the pointwise equivalence of the influence functions for our nonparametric efficient estimator and that recently given in Westling et al., 2024 .



1
Introduction

Survival analysis is a fundamental tool for the analysis of time-to-event data in various fields, such as medicine, epidemiology, and social sciences
(
Fleming and Harrington, 1991
;
Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002
)
. It involves studying the occurrence of events, such as death, disease progression, or failure, and understanding the underlying mechanisms that influence their timing. As researchers seek to go beyond descriptive analysis and understand the causal effects of interventions or treatments on survival outcomes, there is a growing interest in making valid causal inferences
(
Hernán and Robins, 2023
)
.

Of interest in this paper is causal inference for the counterfactual failure survival function, a problem that has been previously studied by
Hubbard et al., 2000
;
Chen and Tsiatis, 2001
;
Anstrom and Tsiatis, 2001
;
Bai et al., 2013
;
Bai et al., 2017
;
Cai and van der Laan, 2020
;
Rytgaard et al., 2022
;
Westling et al., 2024
, among others. As part of our development, several inconsistencies in this literature pertaining to characterizations of “coarsening at random” (CAR) are carefully addressed and the first proper characterization of CAR and sequential CAR for causal survival analysis are rigorously developed. Under sequential CAR, we also characterize the class of all influence functions for the desired estimand as a class of augmented inverse probability weighted estimators, and determine the nonparametric efficient influence function. This efficiency theory is developed using approaches recently explained by
Hines et al., 2022
(see also
Kennedy, 2023
), leading to estimators that are asymptotically efficient and robust to nuisance parameter estimation. Importantly, we do not make any absolute continuity assumptions, working with essentially arbitrary probability distributions for the observed data, as well as the required latent distributions for failure and censoring. In related developments, we also refine results in earlier work where smaller classes of influence functions containing the efficient influence function have previously been proposed
(
Anstrom and Tsiatis, 2001
;
Bai et al., 2013
;
Bai et al., 2017
, e.g.,)
. Finally, we prove by direct calculation that
our nonparametric efficient influence function is pointwise equivalent to that recently derived in
Westling et al., 2024
.

In a companion paper, we carefully study the problem of causal inference for recurrent event processes when observation is terminated by death (i.e., failure, or a terminal event)
(
Baer et al., 2023
)
. Many of the key technical results in this paper can actually be derived as special cases of the results in
Baer et al., 2023
; we leverage these connections where appropriate to de-emphasize technical proofs here, instead focusing on enhancing understanding as to how previous developments are related to the current work.

Notation: Counterfactual and Observable Random Variables

Let
L
denote a vector consisting of baseline variables for an individual. Assuming there is a binary treatment variable taking on the values zero and one, let
T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1}
be the potential failure times had an individual not been or been treated, respectively. We can then regard the unobservable full data for an individual as
(L,T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1}).

In causal inference for the counterfactual survival function, there are multiple coarsening variables involved. We denote
the observed treatment (i.e., exposure) variable as
A,
where
A=1
when treatment is assigned to an individual and
A=0
otherwise. We also define the potential coarsening variables
C^{*}_{0},C^{*}_{1},
where
C^{*}_{a},a=0,1
denotes the times that observation ceases had the exposure been
a
(i.e., for reasons other than failure).
The observed data
\mathcal{O}
is then defined by limiting the availability of the full data through the following mapping,

\Phi(L,T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1};A,C^{*}_{0},C^{*}_{1})=\Bigl(L,A,\Delta=I\{T^{*}_{A}\leq C^{*}_{A}\},X=\min\{T^{*}_{A},C^{*}_{A}\}\Bigr),

where the coarsening variables determine which part of the full data is available. As is evident in
\mathcal{O}
, it is assumed
that the baseline variables
L
and the exposure
A
are observed, along with the failure indicator
\Delta
and the observed time at risk
X
. Their definition incorporates the usual longitudinal assumption in survival analysis and the consistency assumption in causal inference.
For later use, we also define the observed failure process
N_{T}(t)=I\{X\leq t,\Delta=1\}
and the observed censoring process
N_{C}(t)=I\{X\leq t,\Delta=0\}
. These are one-jump processes that jump when a failure or censored time is observed, respectively.

Define the joint distribution of the full and coarsening variables as
\mathbb{P}^{*},
and let
\mathbb{P}
as the distribution of the observed data induced by
\mathbb{P}^{*}
. Throughout, we use an asterisk over functions and random variables to denote reliance on the full or coarsened variables; a function or random variable without an asterisk only depends on the observed data. For
a=0,1
we define
H^{*}(u;a,l)=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}>u\mid L=l\}
to be the conditional survival function for failure for the untreated and treated populations; similarly, let
K^{*}(u;a,l)=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{C^{*}_{a}>u\mid L=l\}
be the conditional censoring distribution and

\pi(a;l)=\mathbb{P}\{A=a\mid L=l\}
be the propensity score. Throughout, we adopt the convention that
0/0=0
.

\eta^{*}_{a}(t)=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}>t\}

;
Bai et al., 2013

Zhao et al., 2015

Rytgaard et al., 2022
;
Rytgaard et al., 2023
and
Westling et al., 2024
.

3.1
A proper characterization of coarsening at random (CAR)

The following result gives the first proper characterization of CAR, and also sequential CAR, for the causal survival problem with
general failure and censoring distributions.

Theorem 1
.

The observed data mapping satisfies CAR if and only if for each
a=0,1
,

\displaystyle(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,

\displaystyle(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp C^{*}_{a}\mid A=a,L\text{ on }C^{*}_{a}<T^{*}_{a},

\displaystyle T_{1-a}^{*}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp I(T_{a}^{*}\leq C_{a}^{*})\mid A=a,L,T^{*}_{a}.

Additionally, the observed data mapping satisfies sequential CAR if and only if for each
a=0,1
,

\displaystyle(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,

\displaystyle T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp C^{*}_{a}\mid A=a,L\text{ on }C^{*}_{a}<T^{*}_{a}.

The conditions for CAR do not imply sequential CAR; the reverse statement
is also true. A proof of this result is provided in the appendix.

We now turn to several important papers in the area of
causal survival analysis and consider the conditions
imposed for the purposes of identifiability and
estimation. To our knowledge,
Hubbard et al., 2000

is the first to attempt to characterize CAR for the causal survival
problem, imposing the conditions

\displaystyle(T_{0}^{*},T_{1}^{*})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,

\displaystyle[[C^{*}_{a}=c\mid A=a,L,T^{*}_{a}]]=[[C^{*}_{a}=c\mid A=a,L]]\text{ if }c<T^{*}_{a},

where
[[\cdot]]
denotes the Lebesgue hazard. However,
this characterization is evidently a special case of sequential CAR as given in the theorem above, and does not imply CAR in general. Parenthetically, each of
Moore and van der Laan, 2009
,
Stitelman and van der Laan, 2010
, and
Cai and van der Laan, 2020
also assert CAR, but fail to provide
any explicit characterization of this condition.

Chen and Tsiatis, 2001
instead assume that

\displaystyle(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,

\displaystyle(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp C\mid A,L,

noting that the second condition further implies
that
T\bot C\mid A,L,
where
T=AT^{*}_{1}+(1-A)T^{*}_{0}.

These authors do not make reference to CAR, per se, and also
do not appear to acknowledge the possibility of a counterfactual censoring time. However, if one assumes that their potential censoring time
C=AC^{*}_{1}+(1-A)C^{*}_{0}
and/or
C=C^{*}_{0}=C_{1}^{*},
then it can
again be seen that their proposed conditions imply sequential CAR, but not CAR.

In later work, other conditions are imposed. For example,
in
Bai et al., 2013
, it is assumed that

\displaystyle T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,

\displaystyle C^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp T^{*}_{a}\mid A,L,

\displaystyle C^{*}_{0}=C_{1}^{*}

for each
a=0,1.
They further posit that these assumptions imply monotone CAR. Similarly,
Rytgaard et al., 2023

assume, for each
a=0,1
,

\displaystyle T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,

\displaystyle C^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp T^{*}_{a}\mid A=a,L,

and suggest that these conditions imply CAR. Finally,
in
(
Westling et al., 2024
)
, the conditions

\displaystyle T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,

\displaystyle C^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp T^{*}_{a}\mid A,L,

\displaystyle C^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,

for each
a=0,1
are imposed. These conditions are similar
to those in
Bai et al., 2013
, though no claim of
implying CAR or sequential CAR is made; we note that the
last condition serves a different purpose
and is not relevant to identification of the desired counterfactual
survival curves.
In each case, the stated conditions neither lead to CAR nor sequential
CAR as formulated in Theorem
1
.
For example, in each of these papers,
it can be seen that the second conditions are related to, but respectively stronger than, the corresponding condition for either CAR or sequential CAR in Theorem
1
. It can be also seen
that the conditions imposed in
Bai et al., 2013
and

Westling et al., 2024
imply the
corresponding condition in
Rytgaard et al., 2023
, but
additionally impose a cross-world independence assumption
in the case where
A\neq a

(
Andrews and Didelez, 2020
)
.
In contrast, the first condition in each of these papers is
weaker than the corresponding condition in Theorem
1
for both CAR and sequential CAR. To see this, observe
that the conditional independence condition

(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L
implies,
for each relevant
t,u,
that

\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t,T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u,\mid A,L\}=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t,T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u,\mid L\};

(1)

letting
u\rightarrow\infty
then shows

\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t\mid A,L\}=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t\mid L\}.

That is, assuming
(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L
immediately implies the marginal independence conditions
T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L
for
a=0,1
. However, because imposing
T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L

for
a=0,1
does not restrict the joint distribution of

(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1}),
such marginal independence is necessarily
weaker than the required condition under CAR (i.e.,
(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L
) unless one further imposes the condition

T^{*}_{0}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp T^{*}_{1}\mid A,L.
To see that this latter
condition, combined with the marginal independence
condition
T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,
implies
(T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,
we first note that
T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L
implies

\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t\mid A,L\}\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u\mid A,L\}=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t\mid L\}\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u\mid L\}.

However,

\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t,T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u\mid A,L\}=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t\mid A,L\}\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u\mid A,L\}

if and only if
T^{*}_{0}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp T^{*}_{1}\mid A,L.

Hence, in combination,
the conditions
T^{*}_{0}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp T^{*}_{1}\mid A,L

and
T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp A\mid L,a=0,1
imply

\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t,T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u\mid A,L\}=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t\mid L\}\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u\mid L\}.

Because the right-hand side is independent of
A,

the left-hand side must also be independent of
A
;
that is, we have

\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t,T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u\mid A,L\}=\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{a}\leq t,T^{*}_{1-a}\leq u\mid L\},

which is exactly (
1
).

In summary, to the authors’ knowledge,
1
provides the first general characterization of CAR and sequential CAR
in the causal survival analysis setting. As suggested
by the general lack of equivalence of conditions that have been imposed
in the literature, neither
CAR nor sequential CAR can be considered as necessary conditions for identifying
\eta^{*}_{a}
. Nevertheless, each remains conceptually important and
provide sufficient conditions that will ensure the observed data likelihood factors into an ignorable term involving the coarsening variables and a term involving the full data
(
Cator, 2004
, e.g.,)
.

Remark 1
.

As noted above, the conditions imposed in
Bai et al., 2013

neither imply CAR nor sequential CAR as formulated in
Theorem
1
.
Importantly, however, their conditions do imply monotone coarsening under a missing data model in which

T^{*}_{1}
is assumed to be observed when
A=1
and
\Delta=1
and is considered to be missing otherwise. This approach,
focused on estimating a functional of
\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{1}\leq t\},

ignores the presence of the counterfactual
T^{*}_{0}.
As
will be seen later, this missing data view, although not truly
causal, still leads to a valid but restricted class
of influence functions for estimating
\mathbb{P}^{*}\{T^{*}_{1}\leq t\}.

3.2
Observed Data Identification

With the definitions of CAR and sequential CAR from
1
in place, we turn to the question
of observed data identification. Unless otherwise
noted, all results that follow will be derived
by imposing the following assumptions. In particular,

\mathbb{P}^{*},

\mathbb{P}
and the relationship between them
are assumed to satisfy the following set
of assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

All random variables have finite variance.

Assumption 2
.

The map
\Phi
correctly specifies the relationship between the full and observed data.

Assumption 3
.

Sequential coarsening at random holds.

Assumption 4
.

There exists a cutoff
\tau<\infty
such that
X\leq\tau
almost surely and
t\leq\tau
.

Assumption 5
.

There exists
\epsilon>0
so that
\epsilon<\pi(a;l)K^{*}(\tau;a,l)
almost surely for all supported
a,l
.

1
in standard in efficiency theory
(
Bickel et al., 1993
)
.

2
captures the consistency assumption in causal inference that one of the counterfactuals is observed and the “longitudinal assumption” in survival analysis that the time at risk is the minimum of the potential failure and censoring times.

3
states that sequential coarsening at random holds and hence that the two conditions in

1
hold.

4
is a technical condition that truncates the the analysis to a closed interval of finite length; this greatly simplifies the
technical analysis
(
Gill, 1983
)
. Together with
5
,
4
also implies that the potential censoring distribution has support that exceeds the support of the potential failure distribution. We note that
4
and
5
can be forced to hold by redefining the potential failure time to be truncated at some sufficiently small
\tau>0;
a related truncation condition used to ensure positivity is
often imposed in work involving inverse probability censoring weighted estimators.

Under
3
, the term

\displaystyle\pi(a;l)K^{*}(\tau;a,l)=\mathbb{P}^{*}\bigl\{A=a,C^{*}_{a}>\tau\mid L=l,T^{*}_{0},T^{*}_{1}\bigr\}

appearing in
5
is simply the coarsening probability given the full data.
This expression reinforces the important point that the causal survival analysis problem has
more than one coarsening variable; see also
Westling et al., 2024
.

Finall, define the model
\mathcal{M}^{*}
as the set of full and coarsened variable distributions satisfying all of the assumptions, and define
\mathcal{M}
as the derived set of observed data

3.2.1
Key Identification Result

Define the usual at-risk process
Y(u)=I\{X\geq u\}
.
Define
H(t;a,l)=\prodi_{u\in(0,t]}\{1-\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{T}(u;a,l)\}
for

\displaystyle\Lambda_{T}(t;a,l)=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{E}\{N_{T}(u)\mid A=a,L=l\}}{\mathbb{E}\{Y(u)\mid A=a,L=l\}},

where
\prodi
is the product integral
(
Gill and Johansen, 1990
)
.
Similarly define
K(t;a,l)=\prodi_{u\in(0,t]}\{1-\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{C}(u;a,l)\}
for

\displaystyle\Lambda_{C}(t;a,l)=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{E}\{N_{C}(u)\mid A=a,L=l\}}{\mathbb{E}\{Y^{\dagger}(u)\mid A=a,L=l\}},

where
Y^{\dagger}(u)=I\{X>u,\Delta=1\text{ or }X\geq u,\Delta=0\}.

In these expressions,
\mathbb{E}
is taken under the observed data distribution

\mathbb{P}.
The following lemma shows that
K
identifies
K^{*}
and likewise that
H
identifies
H^{*}.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{*}
be any full and coarsened data distribution with corresponding observed data distribution
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}
.
Then
K^{*}(u;a,l)=K(u;a,l)
and
H^{*}(u;a,l)=H(u;a,l)
for all
u,a,l
.

This lemma, proved in the Appendix, only assumes sequential CAR and does not impose the usual assumption of full conditional independence between the potential censoring and failure times. Additionally, it relies on a carefully constructed at-risk process
Y^{\dagger}
for censoring that avoids the necessity of imposing absolute continuity assumptions on either failure or censoring. As such, and in contrast to much of the available literature,
we allow for the possibility that ties can occur between failure and censoring times.

Remark 2
.

The process
Y^{\dagger}(\cdot)
is a modified version of the usual at-risk process
Y(\cdot).

Although
Y^{\dagger}(\cdot)
may seem unfamiliar, it is in fact appropriate to use when events for which
\Delta=1
have priority over events for which
\Delta=0
; see
Gill, 1994
and
Strawderman and Baer, 2024
for
related discussion. For example, this is the case for the observed data since
\Delta_{A}=I\{T^{*}_{A}\leq C^{*}_{A}\}
is defined with a “
\leq
” rather than a “
<
”; that is, any tie between
T^{*}_{A}
and
C^{*}_{A}
results in
the observation of
T_{A},
a failure. We note that

Y^{\dagger}(u)=Y(u)-\{N_{T}(u)-N_{T}(u-)\}
for every
u>0;
these
are almost surely equal when there are no shared discontinuities
between the underlying failure and censoring distributions
(e.g., at least one distribution is continuous).

The full data estimand is identified in
\mathcal{M}
, as the following proposition shows. This makes it possible
to estimate the full data estimand using the observed data and inverse probability weighting; a proof
is given in the Appendix.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{*}
be any full and coarsened data distribution with corresponding observed data distribution
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}
. For each time
t>0
, the full data estimand component
\eta_{a}^{*}(t)=\eta_{a}(t)
, where
\eta_{a}(t)=\mathbb{E}\{\varphi_{\eta,a}(t;\mathcal{O};\mathbb{P})\}
for

\varphi_{\eta,a}(t;\mathcal{O};\mathbb{P})=\frac{I\{A=a\}}{\pi(A;L)}\frac{\Delta}{K(X-;A,L)}I\{X>t\}.

We note that
\eta_{a}(t)
denotes the observed data identification of
\eta^{*}_{a}(t)
.

3.3
The class of influence functions

Baer et al., 2023
derived the class of influence functions
for the counterfactual mean of a recurrent event process when observation is terminated by death
(i.e., failure, or a terminal event). In the absence of a recurrent event process, the
identification conditions under which this
class of influence functions is derived
correspond to the sequential
CAR conditions summarized in
1
. It follows
that the class of influence functions for
\eta^{*}_{a}(t),
assuming that the conditional coarsening distributions are known, can be obtained as a special case;
the relevant result is summarized below.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions 1-5,
the class of influence functions for
\eta_{a}(t)=\eta^{*}_{a}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{I\{A=a\}}{\pi(a;L)}\frac{\Delta}{K(X-;a,L)}I\{X>t\}-\eta_{a}(t)-\{I\{A=a\}-\pi(a;L)\}h_{1}(L)+\int_{(0,\infty)}h_{2}(u;A,L)\,\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u;A,L),

where
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are arbitrary index functions and

M_{C}(u;A,L)=N_{C}(u)-\int_{(0,u]}Y^{\dagger}(s)d\Lambda_{C}(s;A,L).

Remark 3
.

As shown in
Baer et al., 2023
,
the class of influence functions
obtained when nominally allowing for
the presence of
treatment-specific counterfactual
censoring variables
C^{*}_{0}\neq C^{*}_{1}

is exactly equivalent to that derived under the assumption that there is a single coarsening variable for censoring, that is,
C^{*}=C^{*}_{0}=C^{*}_{1}.

This is because the observed data
cannot distinguish between the coarsening variables

C^{*}
and
C^{*}_{A}=AC^{*}_{1}+(1-A)C^{*}_{0}
.

The class given in
3
is wider than the classes given in earlier work. For example, although not necessarily explicit,
Anstrom and Tsiatis, 2001
;
Bai et al., 2013
;
Bai et al., 2017
each assume absolute continuity of the potential failure and censoring time
distributions and, for arbitrary
h_{1}
and
h_{2},
report the class to have elements

\frac{I\{A=a\}}{\pi(a;L)}\frac{\Delta}{K(X-;a,L)}I(X>t)-\eta_{a}(t)-\frac{I\{A=a\}-\pi(a;L)}{\pi(a;L)}h_{1}(a,L)+\frac{I\{A=a\}}{\pi(a;L)}\int_{(0,\infty)}h_{2}(u;a,L)\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{M}_{C}(u;a,L)}{K(u;a,L)}

(2)

for
\tilde{M}_{C}(u;A,L)=N_{C}(u)-\int_{(0,u]}Y(u)d\tilde{\Lambda}_{C}(u;A,L),
and where

\tilde{\Lambda}_{C}(u;A,L)

is defined analogously to

\Lambda_{C}(u;A,L),

with
\mathbb{E}\{Y(u)\}

replacing
\mathbb{E}\{Y^{\dagger}(u)\}.

Under continuity of failure, censoring or both, the at-risk process

Y^{\dagger}(u)=Y(u)
and hence
M_{C}(u;A,L)=\tilde{M}_{C}(u;A,L).

As will be seen in the next section, both
the influence function (
2
)
and that given in Theorem
3
include the efficient influence function (i.e., when absolute continuity holds); however, the class (
2
) is considerably smaller. In particular, the augmentation term for censoring (i.e., the summand with
\tilde{M}_{C}
) vanishes when
A\neq a,
while this does not occur for the class summarized in
3
.

A detailed derivation of the class in
Eq.

2
is not explicitly provided in the papers cited above. In the appendix, and following the brief description in
Bai et al., 2013
, we show how their class can be derived under a monotone missing data model rather than a causal survival model that jointly considers all counterfactuals. In particular, fixing
a,
it will be seen that it is sufficient to consider
T_{a}
as being observed when
A=a
and

\Delta=1,
and labeled as missing otherwise. In view of such a derivation, it should not come as a surprise that the class of influence functions in
Eq.

2
is smaller; the focus on estimating
\eta^{*}_{a}(t)
for a given level of
a
requires less data than that required under the indicated joint causal model considered in this paper.

3.4
The form of the efficient influence function

To the authors’ knowledge, the efficient influence function for
\eta_{a}(t)
was first derived in
Hubbard et al., 2000
; see also
Bai et al., 2013
, who
make use of developments in
Tsiatis, 2006
. Both give the efficient influence function as a re-centered augmented inverse probability weighted estimator, and each derive these results assuming absolute continuity of the latent failure and censoring distributions.
Using results
in
Baer et al., 2023

3

\displaystyle\varphi_{\eta,a}(t,\mathcal{O};\mathbb{P})-\eta_{a}(t)-\frac{I\{A=a\}-\pi(a;L)}{\pi(a;L)}H(t;a,L)+\frac{I\{A=a\}}{\pi(a;L)}\int_{u\in(0,\infty)}\frac{H(t\vee u;a,L)}{H(u;a,L)}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u;a,L)}{K(u;a,L)}.

3
)
reduces to the efficient influence functions for
\eta_{a}(t)
in both
Hubbard et al., 2000
and
Bai et al., 2013
when the indicated
continuity conditions hold on the
latent failure and censoring time
distributions. However, this does not
necessarily prove that (
3
)
is the nonparametric efficient influence
function under general conditions on
these latent distributions.

Stitelman and van der Laan, 2010
;
Moore and van der Laan, 2009
;
Zhao et al., 2015
;
Díaz et al., 2019
;
Westling et al., 2024
;

The nonparametric efficient influence

4
Estimation in Practice

A variety of approaches for estimation of the
counterfactual survival function
\eta^{*}_{a}(t)=\eta_{a}(t)

have been proposed
to date. Earlier works, such as

Hubbard et al., 2000
;
Anstrom and Tsiatis, 2001
;
Bai et al., 2013
;
Bai et al., 2017
, do not make use of machine learning or other
fully nonparametric procedures to estimate unknown nuisance
parameters (i.e., the treatment propensity
\pi(a,L),

survivor function
S(\cdot;a,L)
or related functionals,
and/or the censoring survival function

K(\cdot;a,L)
). The efficient
influence functions reported in these works
coincide with (
3
) when all modeling
assumptions are correct, and typically lead to doubly robust
estimators otherwise. Several such estimators for
\eta_{a}(t)
are described and compared by
Denz et al., 2023
; these include estimators obtained under IPTW and AIPTW, G-computation, propensity score matching, empirical likelihoood, and
those derived from pseudovalues. In general, similar
bias performance across such methods were observed when
the assumptions were met for consistency, with better
performance observed for methods making use
of “outcome regression” models (e.g., AIPTW).

In more recent work, including
Cai and van der Laan, 2020
;
Rytgaard et al., 2023
;
Westling et al., 2024
;
Wolock et al., 2024
,
machine learning methods are leveraged
to estimate all required nonparametric nuisance parameters,
leading to nonparametric efficient
\sqrt{n}-
asymptotic inference
for the counterfactual survivor function
\eta^{*}_{a}(t)

and relevant contrasts, such as
\eta^{*}_{1}(t)-\eta^{*}_{0}(t),

when each required nuisance parameter is estimated
consistently at sufficiently fast rates (i.e.,
o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
).
Compared to others noted above, the methods in
Wolock et al., 2024
have the advantage of being structured to allow for the use of the many different learning algorithms that have been developed for
binary classification problems, and are also unique in
the causal survival literature by allowing for left truncation
in addition to right censoring. In particular, although
estimation in each case is ultimately
based on (
4
),

Wolock et al., 2024
demonstrates that their global stacking
procedure provides some improvement over the
superLearner implementation described in
Westling et al., 2024
.
In
Baer et al., 2023
, it
is shown that estimation based on (
3
),
implemented using superLearner, is in
practice essentially indistinguishable from
the estimation approach described in
Westling et al., 2024

as long as the sample size is sufficiently
large.

5
Discussion

The field of causal survival analysis continues to expand at a
rapid pace. The primary goal of the present work is to clarify the appropriate characterization of coarsening at random, and sequential CAR, in this setting. We have carefully compared these conditions to other identification assumptions that have previously been used in the literature, many of which have been erroneously suggested as implying CAR. We have also demonstrated how different classes
of influence functions have been derived, and established
how the different classes (and most efficient choices) are
related to each other.

An open question of some interest is whether there are settings of practical importance for which imposing conditions that violate both CAR and sequential CAR
lead to consequential differences in estimators. For example, it is well known that characterizing CAR in multivariate right-censored data is challenging
(
van der Laan, 1996
)
. In such cases, researchers often impose the sequential randomization assumption (SRA), which is stronger than CAR. This implies that the model space under SRA is strictly smaller than the one under CAR and thus cannot be nonparametric; as a result, estimators derived under SRA are not nonparametrically efficient
(
van der Laan and Robins, 2003
, Section 5)
.

A more nuanced characterization of CAR in the presence of time-varying covariates and right censoring would also be valuable—particularly in settings where time-varying covariates, such as recurrent adverse events, are collected longitudinally. One compelling application would be the identification of mediated effects in such longitudinal survival data structures.

Appendix A1
Two Fundamental Identities

In this section, we state and prove two
general identities, both of which have their
roots in the earliest work in coarsening theory.
In the first subsection below, we
review two important preceding
results; in the second subsection,
we state and prove two
important generalizations.

A1.1
Review

In
Robins and Rotnitzky, 1992
, four fundamental identities are given. Two of these (i.e., equations 3.10a and 3.10b) are the result of simple integral calculus; the remaining identities have led to important insights and developments in modern survival analysis. However, before stating these latter two identities, we need to introduce some notation. Define the censoring hazard
\lambda_{C}(t)=\lim_{h\to 0+}\mathbb{P}(X<t+h,\Delta=0\mid X\geq t)/h

and the survival function
K(t)=\exp\left\{-\int_{(0,t]}\lambda_{C}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right\}.
Then, as shown in

Robins and Rotnitzky, 1992
, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{I(X\geq t)}{K(t)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}\frac{\Delta}{K(X)}I(X\geq t)+\int_{(t,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{C}(u)-I(X\geq u)\lambda_{C}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u}{K(u)},

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta}{K(X)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}1-\int_{(0,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{C}(u)-I(X\geq u)\lambda_{C}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u}{K(u)},

(A5)

where “
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}
” denotes almost sure equality.
These identities hold when the time at-risk
X
is absolutely continuous (with respect to the Lebesgue measure). Under an analogous absolute continuity assumption,
Strawderman, 2000
highlighted that
Eq.

A5
actually holds algebraically; that is, the identities rely principally on the relationship between
\lambda_{C}(\cdot)
and
K(\cdot),
and not on any particular relationship between
K(\cdot),

X,
and/or
\Delta
.

A1.2
The identities

In this subsection, we state and prove general versions of (
A5
) that hold exactly (i.e., not just almost surely), while also relaxing the absolute continuity assumption that underpins the identities summarized
in (
A5
).

Let

and
\Delta\in\{0,1\}
be arbitrary numbers
(or random variables)
that need not be related in any way.
Let

and

where

Here,

is an arbitrary function that is not necessarily the survival function of some potential censoring time.
Although we denote domains of integration as extending to
\infty

\tau

\Lambda_{C}(t)=-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{\mathrm{d}K(u)}{K(u-)};

note this is the cumulative hazard for censoring when

Lemma 2

Under the general setup just defined, the following two identities hold:

\displaystyle\frac{I(X>t)}{K(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{K(X-)}I(X>t)+\int_{(t,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u)}{K(u)}

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta}{K(X-)}

\displaystyle=1-\int_{(0,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u)}{K(u)}.

(A8)

Proof of
2
.

We start by stating a few helpful expressions. First,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K(t)}=-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{\mathrm{d}K(u)}{K(u-)K(u)}=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{C}(u)}{K(u)},

where the first equality follows from Equation (2.9) in
Fleming and Harrington, 1991
and the second equality follows from the definition of
\Lambda_{C}
.
Second, note that

\displaystyle\int_{(t,\infty)}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)\,\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{C}(u)}{K(u)}

\displaystyle=\Delta\int_{(t,X)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{C}(u)}{K(u)}+(1-\Delta)\int_{(t,X]}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{C}(u)}{K(u)}

\displaystyle=\Delta\int_{(t,X)}\mathrm{d}K^{-1}(u)+(1-\Delta)\int_{(t,X]}\mathrm{d}K^{-1}(u)

\displaystyle=I(X>t)\left\{\frac{\Delta}{K(X-)}+\frac{1-\Delta}{K(X)}-\frac{1}{K(t)}\right\}.

Now, the identity in

A7

\displaystyle\int_{(t,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u)}{K(u)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\Delta)I(X>t)}{K(X)}-\int_{(t,\infty)}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)\,\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{C}(u)}{K(u)}

\displaystyle=I(X>t)\left\{\frac{1}{K(t)}-\frac{\Delta}{K(X-)}\right\},

and the identity in

∎

We now justify equalities (1) and (2). The first equality follows from

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq t,\Delta=0\mid A=a,L=l)=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}_{a}\leq t,C^{*}_{a}<T^{*}_{a}\mid A=a,L=l)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t]}\mathbb{P}^{*}(T^{*}_{a}>u\mid A=a,L=l)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}_{a}\leq u\mid A=a,L=l),

making use of the condition that
T^{*}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp C^{*}_{a}|A=a,L=l

on
T^{*}_{a}>C^{*}_{a}.
The second equality follows from

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}^{*}(T^{*}_{a}>u,C^{*}_{a}\geq u\mid A=a,L=l)}{\mathbb{P}^{*}(T^{*}_{a}>u\mid A=a,L=l)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}_{a}\geq u\mid T^{*}_{a}>u,A=a,L=l)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}_{a}<u\mid T^{*}_{a}>u,A=a,L=l)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}_{a}<u\mid A=a,L=l)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}_{a}\geq u\mid A=a,L=l),

the third equality again making use of the indicated independence.

Finally, the result follows by plugging into the product integral above. That
H^{*}=H
follows analogously.
∎

Proof of
2
.

Notice that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\varphi_{\eta,a}(t;\mathcal{O};\mathbb{P})\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(A;L)}\frac{\Delta}{K(X-;A,L)}I(X>t)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{*}\left\{\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}\frac{I(T^{*}_{a}\leq C^{*}_{a})}{K^{*}(T^{*}_{a}-;a,L)}I(T^{*}_{a}>t)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{*}\left\{\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}\frac{I(T^{*}_{a}>t)}{K^{*}(T^{*}_{a}-;a,L)}\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(T^{*}_{a}\leq C^{*}_{a}\middle|A=a,L,T^{*}_{a}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{*}\left[\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}\frac{I(T^{*}_{a}>t)}{K^{*}(T^{*}_{a}-;a,L)}\Bigl\{1-\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(C^{*}_{a}<T^{*}_{a}\middle|A=a,L,T^{*}_{a}\right)\Bigr\}\right].

In the above, the expression
\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(T^{*}_{a}\leq C^{*}_{a}\middle|A=a,L,T^{*}_{a}\right)
is rewritten so that
the CAR assumption, which only furnishes conditional independence “on
C^{*}_{a}<T^{*}_{a}
,” may be leveraged.
In particular, under this assumption, we can now write

\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(C^{*}_{a}<T^{*}_{a}\middle|A=a,L,T^{*}_{a}\right)=\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(C^{*}_{a}<u\middle|A=a,L\right)\bigl|_{u=T^{*}_{a}}=\biggl\{1-\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(C^{*}_{a}\geq u\middle|A=a,L\right)\biggr\}\bigl|_{u=T^{*}_{a}}=1-K^{*}(T^{*}_{a}-;a,L);

substitution into the last expression
given for

\mathbb{E}\{\varphi_{\eta,a}(t;\mathcal{O};\mathbb{P})\}

then leads to

\mathbb{E}\{\varphi_{\eta,a}(t;\mathcal{O};\mathbb{P})\}=\mathbb{E}^{*}\left\{\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}I(T^{*}_{a}>t)\right\}=\mathbb{E}^{*}\left\{\frac{I(T^{*}_{a}>t)}{\pi(a;L)}\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(A=a\middle|L\right)\right\}=\mathbb{E}^{*}\left\{I(T^{*}_{a}>t)\right\},

proving the desired identification result.
∎

A4.1
A Missing Data Derivation of
(
2
)

In this section, we derive the class of influence functions reported in
Anstrom and Tsiatis, 2001
;
Bai et al., 2013
;
Bai et al., 2017
under a particular missing data model, rather than the causal model considered in this paper.

Let the full data as
(L,T^{*}),
and denote the full data estimand as
\psi(t):=\mathbb{P}^{*}(T^{*}>t)
for some
t>0
. In addition, define
C^{*}
to be a potential censoring time. As will be shown below,
Anstrom and Tsiatis, 2001
;
Bai et al., 2013
;
Bai et al., 2017
essentially derive their class using results in
Tsiatis, 2006
by assuming that

T^{*}_{1}=T^{*}
and that
C^{*}_{1}=C^{*};
critically,

(T^{*}_{0},C^{*}_{0})
is implicitly considered to be missing (or, just
T^{*}_{0}
, if we additionally assume
C^{*}_{1}=C^{*}_{0}
).

We begin by defining
R\in\{0,1\}
to be a binary indicator variable, and assuming that the observed data arises from the mapping

\Phi(L,T^{*};R,C^{*})=(L,R,R\min\{T^{*},C^{*}\},RI(T^{*}\leq C^{*}))=(L,R,RX,R\Delta),

where
X=T^{*}\wedge C^{*}
and
\Delta=I(T^{*}\leq C^{*}).
Evidently,
R=1
implies that
(L,X,\Delta)
is observed,
whereas
R=0
leads only to the observation of
L.
Following
Tsiatis, 2006
, this observed data construction
amounts to using a coarsening variable
\mathcal{C}\in(0,\infty)
that is given by

\mathcal{C}=0I(R=0)+XI(R=1,\Delta=0)+\infty I(R=1,\Delta=1).

The variable
\mathcal{C}
cleary reflects the indicated coarsening pattern and its structure reveals that the coarsening reflected
in
\Phi
is monotone.

Note that the construction above does not explicitly assume the existence of a treatment variable. However, upon equating
R
with
A
, it
can be seen that the above construction amounts to considering the problem of estimating
\psi(t)=P\{T^{*}>t\}=P\{T^{*}_{1}>t\}
from the subset of observations where
A=1
only; when
A=0
, the information for estimating
\psi(t)
is considered to be missing.

In deriving the corresponding class of influence functions, we assume that
\mathbb{P}^{*}(R=1,T^{*}\leq C^{*}\mid L,T^{*})>0,
and
also that the following version of sequential CAR also holds:

\displaystyle R\perp\!\!\!\!\perp T^{*}\mid L,

\displaystyle C^{*}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp T^{*}\mid L,R=1\text{ on }C^{*}<T^{*}.

It follows that the coarsening probabilities simplify as
\mathbb{P}^{*}(R=1\mid T^{*},L)=\pi(1;L)
and
\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}>u\mid T^{*},L,R=1)=K(u;L)
and hence
\mathbb{P}^{*}(R=1,\Delta=1\mid T^{*},L)=\pi(1;L)K(X-;L)
when
R,\Delta=1
.

For simplicity, assume both
\pi(1;L)
and
K(t;L)
are known. By
Tsiatis, 2006
, the
class of observed-data influence functions under the indicated monotone coarsening mechanism is given by

\frac{I(R=1,\Delta=1)}{\pi(1;L)K(X-;L)}\{I(T^{*}>t)-\psi\}+\mathrm{AS},

(A10)

where
\mathrm{AS}
is the “augmentation space”,
since
I(T^{*}>t)-\psi
is the unique full data influence function.

Before characterizing the augmentation space, we define

\displaystyle\lambda_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{*}(\mathcal{C}=u\mid\mathcal{C}\geq u,L,T^{*}),

\displaystyle K_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{*}(\mathcal{C}>u\mid L,T^{*}).

When
u=0
, these simplify as

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{*}(R=0\mid T^{*},L)=1-\pi(1;L),

\displaystyle K_{0}

\displaystyle=\pi(1;L).

When
u>0
is finite,
these expressions simplify as

\displaystyle\lambda_{u}

\displaystyle=I(T^{*}>u)\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}>u\mid C^{*}\geq u,T^{*}>u,R=1,L);

\displaystyle K_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}>u\mid L,R=1)\pi(1;L).

By
Tsiatis, 2006
, a typical element of the augmentation space may be written as

\displaystyle\frac{I(\mathcal{C}=0)-\lambda_{0}I(\mathcal{C}\geq 0)}{K_{0}}\tilde{h}_{1}(L)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{I(\mathcal{C}=u)-I(\mathcal{C}\geq u)\lambda_{u}}{K_{u}}\tilde{h}_{2}(u;L)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{(1-R)-\{1-\pi(1;L)\}}{\pi(1;L)}\tilde{h}_{1}(L)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{R\,\mathrm{d}N_{C}(u)-RY^{\dagger}(u)\lambda_{u}}{\pi(1;L)\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}>u\mid R=1,L)}\tilde{h}_{2}(u;L)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{R-\pi(1;L)}{\pi(1;L)}\tilde{h}_{1}(L)+\frac{R}{\pi(1;L)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u;1,L)}{\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}>u\mid R=1,L)}\tilde{h}_{2}(u;L).

Substituting this into (
A10
) and treating
R
as being equivalent to
A,
we obtain

\frac{I(A=1)}{\pi(1;L)}\frac{\Delta}{K(X-;L)}\{I(T^{*}>t)-\psi(t)\}-\frac{I(A=1)-\pi(1;L)}{\pi(1;L)}\tilde{h}_{1}(L)+\frac{I(A=1)}{\pi(1;L)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u;1,L)}{\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}>u\mid A=1,L)}\tilde{h}_{2}(u;L).

Since
\tilde{h}_{1}(L)
is arbitrary, we can use the second identity in
2
to algebraically rewrite the above expression as

\frac{I(A=1)}{\pi(1;L)}\frac{\Delta}{K(X-;L)}I(T^{*}>t)-\psi(t)-\frac{I(A=1)-\pi(1;L)}{\pi(1;L)}h_{1}(L)+\frac{I(A=1)}{\pi(1;L)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u;1,L)}{\mathbb{P}^{*}(C^{*}>u\mid A=1,L)}h_{2}(u;L),

where
h_{1}(L)=\tilde{h}_{1}(L)+\psi(t)
and
h_{2}(u;L)=\tilde{h}_{2}(u;L)+\psi(t)
. It is not difficult to see that this last equation is simply
(
2
) with
a=1
, hence the same class as that given in
Bai et al., 2013
.

A4.2
Proof of

In this section, we prove that two forms of the efficient influence function for

P(T_{a}>t)
that appear in the literature do in fact agree. We stress that the result
applies in either continuous or discrete time or a mixture of the two.

For notational simplicity, we suppress the conditioning set
A,L
. We start by simplifying the uncentered efficient influence function representation

\displaystyle H(t)-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}\frac{H(t)}{H(u)}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{T}(u)-Y(u)\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{T}(u)}{K(u-)}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle H(t)-\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}H(t)\bigg\{\int_{(0,t]}\frac{\mathrm{d}I(\Delta=1,X\leq u)}{K(u-)H(u)}-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{I(X\geq u)\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{T}(u)}{K(u-)H(u)}\bigg\}.

The first integral simplifies as

\int_{(0,t]}\frac{\mathrm{d}I(\Delta=1,X\leq u)}{K(u-)H(u)}=\frac{\Delta I(X\leq t)}{K(X-)H(X)},

which can be seen by splitting the integration domain into three parts:
(0,X),\{X\},
and
(X,t]
.
We now expand the remaining integral using integration by parts. The sum

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y(u)}{K(u-)}\mathrm{d}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{H(u)}\right\}+\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle I(X>t)\left[\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{K(u-)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{H(u)}\right\}+\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+I(X\leq t)\left[\int_{(0,X]}\frac{1}{K(u-)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{H(u)}\right\}+\Delta\int_{(0,X)}\frac{1}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}+(1-\Delta)\int_{(0,X]}\frac{1}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle I(X>t)\left[\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{K(u-)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{H(u)}\right\}+\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+I(X\leq t)\left[\int_{(0,X]}\frac{1}{K(u-)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{H(u)}\right\}+\int_{(0,X]}\frac{1}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}-\Delta\int_{\{X\}}\frac{1}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}\right].

Now, the standard integration by parts formula
(
Fleming and Harrington, 1991
, Theorem A.1.2)
applies since
K
and
H
are both right-continuous yielding for any
v>0
that

\displaystyle\int_{(0,v]}\frac{1}{K(u-)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{H(u)}\right\}+\int_{(0,v]}\frac{1}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}=\frac{1}{K(v)H(v)}-1.

Applying this result for
v=X
and
v=t
continues the simplification and shows

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y(u)}{K(u-)}\mathrm{d}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{H(u)}\right\}+\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle I(X>t)\left[\frac{1}{K(t)H(t)}-1\right]+I(X\leq t)\left[\frac{1}{K(X)H(X)}-1-\Delta\int_{\{X\}}\frac{1}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K(X\wedge t)H(X\wedge t)}-1-\frac{\Delta I(X\leq t)}{H(X)}\left\{\frac{1}{K(X)}-\frac{1}{K(X-)}\right\}.

Now that both integrals are simplified, we plug them back into the efficient influence function and find that

\displaystyle H(t)-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}

\displaystyle\frac{H(t)}{H(u)}\frac{dN_{T}(u)-Y(u)\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{T}(u)}{K(u-)}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle H(t)-\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}H(t)\bigg[-\frac{1}{K(X\wedge t)H(X\wedge t)}+1+\frac{\Delta I(X\leq t)}{H(X)}\frac{1}{K(X)}+\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}\bigg].

Rearranging terms, we find that the influence function equals

\displaystyle\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}\frac{I(X>t)}{K(t)}-\frac{I(A=a)-\pi(a;L)}{\pi(a;L)}H(t)+\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}H(t)\bigg[\frac{(1-\Delta)I(X\leq t)}{K(X)H(X)}-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}\bigg].

(A11)

However, since

-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}=-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)}{H(u)K(u)}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{C}(u),

we can write

\frac{(1-\Delta)I(X\leq t)}{K(X)H(X)}-\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y^{\dagger}(u)}{H(u)}\mathrm{d}\left\{\frac{1}{K(u)}\right\}=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{H(u)K(u)}dM_{C}(u),

where
dM_{C}(u)=dN_{C}(u)-Y^{\dagger}(u)d\Lambda_{C}(u).

To complete the proof, we note that
2
,
specifically (
A7

\frac{I(X>t)}{K(t)}=\frac{\Delta}{K(X-)}I(X>t)+\int_{(t,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u)}{K(u)}

and hence that (

\displaystyle\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}\frac{\Delta}{K(X-)}I(X>t)-\frac{I(A=a)-\pi(a;L)}{\pi(a;L)}H(t)+\frac{I(A=a)}{\pi(a;L)}\bigg[\int_{(0,t]}\frac{H(t)}{H(u)K(u)}dM_{C}(u)+\int_{(t,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{C}(u)}{K(u)}\bigg].

Since

the term in the square brackets reduces to

\int_{(0,\infty)}\frac{H(t\vee u)}{H(u)}\frac{dM_{C}(u)}{K(u)},

proving the desired equivalence.
