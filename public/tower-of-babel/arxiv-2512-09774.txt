Mostow Rigidity Made Easier
Richard Evan Schwartz

Abstract




1
The Main Result

Mostow’s rigidity theorem [
M
] is one of the most famous
and spectacular results
about hyperbolic manifolds.
The traditional proofs (and also Gromov’s proof [
T
]) rely on a fair
amount of real analysis. The student who wants to
learn the result
all the way to the bottom
is in for an arduous journey.
In this article I give a
proof of Mostow rigidity that is self-contained modulo undergraduate
real analysis.
The proof should be accessible to first-year
graduate students interested in geometry and topology. My approach
has a lot in common with existing proofs, but it is analytically
lighter. The ideas here also overlap with [
S
].

Given metric spaces
(X_{1},d_{1})
and
(X_{2},d_{2})
, a map

H:X_{1}\to X_{2}
is
BL
(bi-Lipschitz) if
H
is a bijection
and if there is some

K\geq 1
such that

K^{-1}d_{1}(x,y)\leq d_{2}(H(x),H(y))\leq Kd_{1}(x,y),\hskip 30.0pt\forall x,y\in X_{1}.

(1)

When
K=1
, the map
H
is an
isometry
.
I prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
(Mostow)

If
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are
compact hyperbolic
3
-manifolds and

f:M_{1}\to M_{2}
is BL,
then there is an isometry

g:M_{1}\to M_{2}
.
So, diffeomorphic compact hyperbolic

3
-manifolds are isometric.

This is a somewhat limited version of Mostow’s original Theorem.
In §
6
, I discuss
how the same arguments prove the full-blown theorem.
This chapter proves theorem
1.1
modulo two auxiliary
results which I prove in §2 and §3-5 respectively.
I thank Sujung Jo, Dan Margalit, and ChatGPT for helpful conversations.

1.1
Hyperbolic Geometry Background

Hyperbolic Space:

Let
C
denote the complex numbers.

Hyperbolic
3
-space
, denoted
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
, is a metric
space modeled on the upper
half space in
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\times(0,\infty)
.
Technically,
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
is a complete Riemannian manifold
of constant negative sectional curvature. You don’t need
to know this to read the proof of Mostow Rigidity.

Here are some useful facts about the hyperbolic metric.

F1.
When
a\not=0
, the map

(z,t)\to(az+b,|a|t)
acts isometrically on
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}

F2.
The geodesics in
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
, which are the length minimizing paths, are
either vertical rays or semicircles that meet
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\times\{0\}

at right angles.

F3.
Letting
\ell
and
\ell_{\mbox{\boldmath{$E$}}}

denote hyperbolic and Euclidean arc-length respectively, we have

a\ell\leq\ell_{E}\leq b\ell
on
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\times[a,b]
.

F4.
The map

\phi(p)=(0,\|p\|)
carries
p\in\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
to the point on

\gamma=\{0\}\times(0,\infty)
nearest to
p
.
If
t\in(0,1)
, the distance from
(0,t)
to
\gamma
is less than
\ln(1/t)+1
.

Hyperbolic Manifolds:

All manifolds are assumed to be compact.
Let
I
be the group of isometries of
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
.
A
hyperbolic manifold
is any quotient of the form

M=\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is a subgroup of
I
.
We only
make this definition when
M
is actually a manifold.
What this means is that we have a universal covering map

\pi:\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}\to M
, and
and
\Gamma
is the
deck group
.
\Gamma
is usually
called a
co-compact torsion-free lattice
, but we will
call
\Gamma
a
nice lattice
. The metric on
M
is such that

\pi
is a local isometry.

Conformal Transformations:

Let
{\bf S\/}=\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\cup\infty
be the Riemann sphere.
A
generalized circle
is either a round circle
in
C
or else a set of the form
L\cup\infty

where
L
is a straight line in
C
.
A
conformal transformation
is a homeomorphism
of
{\bf S\/}
which maps generalized circles to
generalized circles. When such a map is orientation
preserving, it has the form

z\to\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\hskip 30.0pta,b,c,d\in\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}},\qquad ad-bc=1.

(2)

As a special case, a
homothety
is a map of the form

z\to az+b
where
a\in(0,\infty)
. Such maps preserve
the directions of lines in
C
.

Every isometry of
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
extends to give a conformal
transformation of
{\bf S\/}
and every conformal transformation
of
{\bf S\/}
arises this way.

Affine Transformations:

An affine map of
R
is a map
x\to ax+b
with
a\not=0
.
Interpreting
C
as
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}^{2}
, we say that a

real affine transformation
of
C
is a map of the form

A(v)=T(v)+w
where
T
is an invertible real linear transformation
and
w\in\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}
. Most real affine transformations are not conformal.
However, if there are round circles

C_{1},C_{2}
such that
A(C_{1})=C_{2}
then
A
is in fact conformal.

Equivariance and Lifting:

A BL map
H:\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}\to\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
is
equivariant
if there
is a pair of nice lattices
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
such that

H\Gamma_{1}H^{-1}=\Gamma_{2}.

(3)

Here is how this arises in Mostow Rigidity.
We have
M_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}/\Gamma_{j}
where

\Gamma_{j}
is a nice lattice.
Suppose
f:M_{1}\to M_{2}
is BL.
Then
f
has an equivariant
BL
lift

H:\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}\to\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
which
conjugates
\Gamma_{1}
to
\Gamma_{2}

and is such that

f\circ\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}\circ H
.
Note that if
H
is
K
-BL then so is

fHg
for any
f,g\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
.

We make similar definitions for a
homeomorphism
h:{\bf S\/}\to{\bf S\/}

(which we usually call a
homeo
) The
homeo
h
is
equivariant if
h\Gamma_{1}h^{-1}=\Gamma_{2}
.
We will often work with pairs
(H,h)
where

h
is a continuous extension of
H
to
{\bf S\/}
.
(We prove the existence of such extensions below.)
In this case, we call
the pair
(H,h)

equivariant
if both
maps are similtaneously equivariant with
respect to the same pair of nice lattices.

Tame Sequences:

We call a sequence
\{g_{n}\}
in
I

tame
if

\{g_{n}(p)\}
is bounded for each

p\in\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
. In this situation
we can pass to a subsequence and arrange that
there are
4
points

p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4}\in\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
, say the
vertices of a regular tetrahedron, so that
all
4
sequences
\{g_{n}(p_{j})\}
converge.
But then
\{g_{n}\}
converges in
I
. In short,
a tame sequence converges on a subsequence.

Here is a criterion for tameness.
Suppose
a,b,c\in{\bf S\/}
are
3
distinct points, and

\{g_{n}(a)\}
,

\{g_{n}(b)\}
,

\{g_{n}(c)\}
all converge to

3
distinct points of
{\bf S\/}
.
Then
\{g_{n}\}
is tame.
This derives from the
fact that (up to specifying whether the map preserves
or reverses orientation) an element of
I
is
determined by what it does on
3
distinct
points of
{\bf S\/}
. The takeaway here is that
we can tell that a sequence
is tame by looking at how it acts on the ideal boundary.

Below we will generalize these ideas for
sequences of
K
-BL maps.

1.2
A Lesson in Calculus

Suppose that
h:\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\to\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}
is a function
that is differentiable at some point
a\in\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}
.
After translating we normalize so that
a=0

and
h(a)=0
. Then what we are saying is
that there is some constant
A
such that

\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{h(x)}{x}=A.

(4)

If follows that, for any nonzero real number
u
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{h(u/n)}{u/n}=A.

(5)

Put another way

\lim_{n\to\infty}nh(u/n)=Au.

(6)

We introduce auxiliary functions

f_{n}(x)=nx,\hskip 30.0ptg_{n}(x)=x/n.

(7)

We define

h_{n}=f_{n}\circ h\circ g_{n}.

(8)

Then we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}h_{n}(u)=Au.

(9)

This last equation holds for all
u\in\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}
, even when
u=0
.
Hence
h_{n}\to h^{\prime}
where
h^{\prime}
is the
linear function
h^{\prime}(u)=Au
.
Given the way that limits work – and I invite
you to think about this – the convergence
is uniform over compact sets of
R
.
In short
h_{n}\to h^{\prime}
uniformly
on compact sets. When
A=0
the
map
h^{\prime}
is the zero map. Otherwise

h^{\prime}
is a linear map.
This is how we want to think about the derivative: If
you
zoom in
to a point of differentiability,
you get a linear map in the limit.

Before moving on, let us extend the lesson a bit.
Suppose that
h:\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\to\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}
is a map normalized
so that
h(0)=0
and the directional derivative
of
h
exists in the direction of a line
L

through the origin. Then we can
define
h_{n}
as above. If we know that
the limit
h^{\prime}
exists, then we can say that

h^{\prime}|_{L}
is a linear
map from
L
into
C
. The whole map

h^{\prime}
might be a mystery but the restriction
to
L
is not a mystery.
Our constructions below are all built around this idea.

1.3
Extensions of Equivariant BL Maps

In §2 we prove the following well-known result.

Theorem 1.2

A BL map
H
of
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
extends continuously to a homeo
h

of
{\bf S\/}
. If
H
is equivariant with respect to a pair

\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
of nice lattices then so is
h
.

We work with equivariant
pairs
(H,h)
that arise from lifting (§
1.1
) and
Theorem
1.2
.
We will show that
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
. If this is true,
then
h
gives us an equivariant isometry

h^{*}:\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}\to\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
which in turn defines
an isometry
g:M_{1}\to M_{2}
.
So, proving Mostow Rigidity boils down to
proving that
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
.
We will move
h
around by isometries
until it spills its secret.

We write
(H,h)\sim(H^{\prime},h^{\prime})
if there are

f,g\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
so that

H^{\prime}=fHg
and
h^{\prime}=fhg
.
A sequence

\{(H_{n},h_{n})\}
is
derived from

(H,h)

if we have
(H_{n},h_{n})\sim(H,h)
for all
n
.
Compare Equation
8
.
Generalizing the definition given in §
1.1
,
we call a sequence
\{\phi_{n}\}
of
K
-BL maps

tame
if

\{\phi_{n}(p)\}
is bounded for each
p\in\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
. We call

\{(H_{n},h_{n})\}
tame if
\{H_{n}\}
is tame.
In §2 we prove the following generalization of the
tameness criterion in §
1.1
.

Lemma 1.3

Suppose that
\{(H_{n},h_{n})\}
is a sequence derived from
(H,h)
and
there are
3
distinct points
a,b,c\in{\bf S\/}

such that
\{h_{n}(a)\},\{h_{n}(b)\},\{h_{n}(c)\}
converge
to
3
distinct points in
{\bf S\/}
.
Then
\{(H_{n},h_{n})\}
is tame.

Before we implement our calculus lesson we need one more result,
a subtle and powerful one.

Lemma 1.4

Let
\{(H_{n},h_{n})\}
be a tame sequence derived from
(H,h)
.
Then
(H,h)\sim(H^{\prime},h^{\prime})
where, on a subsequence,

H_{n}
converges to
H^{\prime}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}

and
h_{n}
converges to
h^{\prime}
uniformly on
{\bf S\/}
.

Proof:
Let
H_{n}=f_{n}Hg_{n}
and
h_{n}=f_{n}hg_{n}
.
Note that
\{f_{n}\}
and
\{g_{n}\}
might not be tame.
For any
g\in\Gamma_{1}
we have

f_{g}\in\Gamma_{2}
so that
H=f_{g}Hg
and

h=f_{g}hg
. Combining these equations with the
fact that
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}/\Gamma_{1}
is compact,
we can
also
write
H_{n}=f_{n}^{\prime}Hg_{n}^{\prime}
and

h_{n}=f_{n}^{\prime}hg_{n}^{\prime}
, where
\{g_{n}^{\prime}\}
is tame.
Here

f_{n}^{\prime}=f_{g_{n}^{\prime}}=H_{n}\circ(g_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}\circ H^{-1}.

Since
\{H_{n}\}
and
\{(g_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}\}
are tame so is

\{f_{n}^{\prime}\}
. As noted in §
1.1
, we have

f_{n}^{\prime}\to f^{\prime}\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
and

g_{n}^{\prime}\to g^{\prime}\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
on a subsequence.
So, we get the desired convergence by setting

h^{\prime}=f^{\prime}hg^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}=f^{\prime}Hg^{\prime}
.

1.4
Zooming In

Let
(H,h)
be an equivariant pair. Our goal is to
show that
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
.
We normalize so that

h(\infty)=\infty
and interpret
h
as a homeo of
C
.

Lemma 1.5

If
h
is a real affine transformation
then
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
.

Proof:
The map
h
is equivariant with respect to nice
lattices
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
.
For any
f\in\Gamma_{1}
there is some

g\in\Gamma_{2}
such that

gh=hf
. We can choose a line
L
and an
element
f\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
so that
C_{1}=f(L)
is a round circle.
But then
C_{2}=h(f(L))
is a (possibly non-circular) ellipse.
On the other hand
h(L)
is a line and

C_{2}=g(h(L))
is either a line or a round circle.
The only way the two descriptions match is if

C_{2}
is a round circle.
But then
h
maps the circle
C_{1}
to the circle
C_{2}
.
As we mentioned in
§
1.1
, this forces
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
.

\spadesuit

A line
L\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}
is
good
for
h
if
the restriction
h|_{L}
is an affine map.
This means that
h(L)
is a line and
h
scales lengths by a
constant factor on
L
.
We say that the
direction
D
is
good
for
h
if every line of direction
D

is good for
h
.
The affine maps here may depend on the line but, since
h
is a
homeo, the image under
h
of this parallel line family is another
parallel line family.

Lemma 1.6

If two directions are good for
h
then
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
.

Proof:
There are real affine transformations
A,B
so that
the horizontal and vertical directions for
\phi=AhB
are
good, and
\phi
is the identity on the union of the
two coordinate axes. But
\phi(x,y)
is on the same
horizontal line as
(0,y)
and on the same vertical line as

(x,0)
. Hence
\phi(x,y)=(x,y)
. In short,
\phi
is the identity.
Hence
h=A^{-1}B^{-1}
is real affine. Hence

h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
by Lemma
1.5
.

\spadesuit

Choose
z\in\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}
.
Specializing the constructions from §
1.3
, we
call
\{(H_{n},h_{n})\}
the
zoom sequence based on

(h,z)
if

H_{n}=f_{n}Hg_{n}
and

h_{n}=f_{n}hg_{n}
, where

•

f_{n}
is the homothety that
fixes
h(z)
and scales distances by
n
.

•

g_{n}
is the homothety that
fixes
z
and scales distances by
1/n
.

We write
h\to h^{\prime}
if
\{(H_{n},h_{n})\}
is tame, and

(H_{n},h_{n})
converges on a subsequence to
(H^{\prime},h^{\prime})
in the sense
of Lemma
1.4
. For ease of notation we omit mention of
H
,
even though
H
is lurking in the background at every step.

By Lemma
1.4
, we have

h\sim h^{\prime}
if

h\to h^{\prime}
. Since
h^{\prime}\sim h
, the map
h^{\prime}
is
also equivariant.
We write

h\Rightarrow h^{(n)}\qquad{\rm if\/}\qquad h\to h^{\prime}\to h^{\prime\prime}\to...\to h^{(n)}.

(10)

By several applications of Lemma
1.4
we have

h\sim h^{(n)}
if
h\Rightarrow h^{(n)}
. So, if

h\Rightarrow h^{(n)}
then
h^{(n)}
is equivariant. Also, if

h\Rightarrow h^{(n)}\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
then
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
.

The
directional derivative
of
h
at
z
in the direction
v
,
when it exists, is given by

D_{v}h(z)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{h(z+vt)-h(z)}{t}.

(11)

We call this a
rational
directional derivative if the real and
imaginary
parts of
v
are rational.
A point
z\in\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}
is
an
asterisk
for
h
if every rational directional derivative
exists at
z
and if
D_{1}h(z)\not=0
.
In §3-5 we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.7

h
has an asterisk.

Corollary 1.8

Let
D
be a direction. Then
h\Rightarrow h^{\prime\prime}

where
D
is good for
h^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof:
Let
z
be an asterisk for
h
. Let
\{h_{n}\}
be the zoom sequence
based on
(h,z)
. Let
\cal Q
denote the set of lines of
rational slope through
z
and let
\cal R
denote the set of all
lines through
z
.
Remembering the calculus
lesson, we see that
the restriction of
h_{n}
to each line of
\cal Q

converges to an affine map.
In particular
h_{n}(z+m)\to h(z)+mD_{1}h(z)
for
m=0,1,2
,
and these points are distinct.
So
\{h_{n}\}
is tame by Lemma
1.3
. Hence

h\to h^{\prime}
and the restriction of the homeo
h^{\prime}
to every line of
\cal Q
is
a nontrivial affine map. Hence every line of
\cal Q

is good for
h^{\prime}
.
Since
h^{\prime}
is a homeo, and
\cal Q
is dense in
\cal R
,
all lines of
\cal R
are good for
h^{\prime}
.

Choose
z^{\prime}\in\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}
so that the line
\overline{zz^{\prime}}
has direction
D
.
Let
\{h^{\prime}_{n}\}=\{f_{n}^{\prime},h^{\prime}g_{n}^{\prime}\}

be the zoom sequence based on
(h^{\prime},z^{\prime})
.
The restriction of

h^{\prime}_{n}
to
\overline{zz^{\prime}}
is independent of
n
.
So, by Lemma
1.3
,

\{h^{\prime}_{n}\}
is tame. Every line in

(g^{\prime}_{n})^{-1}({\cal R\/})
is good for
h_{n}^{\prime}
,
and as
n\to\infty
this set converges to the
set of lines in direction
D
. (The point
(g_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}(z)
common to
all these lines moves to
\infty
along the line
\overline{zz^{\prime}}
.)
Hence
D
is good for
h^{\prime\prime}
.

\spadesuit

If
D
is good for
h
and
h\Rightarrow h^{\prime\prime}

then
D
is also good for
h^{\prime\prime}
.
So, applying Corollary
1.8
twice, for different
directions, we get
h\Rightarrow h^{(4)}
where
two directions are good for
h^{(4)}
, and
h^{(4)}
is equivariant.
But then
h^{(4)}\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
by Lemma
1.6
.
By Lemma

1.4
again,
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
.

1.5
Variations on a Theme

Our proof of Mostow Rigidity is done, but
let us continue with the notation and terminology from the previous
section and discuss some alternate proofs.

More Analysis:

As is well known, the map
h
is
quasi-conformal
.
Theorem
1.7

is an idiosyncratic corollary of a well-known result:
quasi-conformal homeos are almost everywhere
nonsingularly differentiable.
See [
LV
]. (Our proof of
Theorem
1.7
avoids the hard analysis needed for this result.)
If we accept that
h
has a
point of ninsingular differentiability at
z
then we get a swifter
proof. We consider the zoom sequence
\{h_{n}\}
based
on
(h,z)
and we get
h\to h^{\prime}
where
h^{\prime}
is real affine
and equivariant – and hence in
I
.

Three Fair Directions:

Let us say that
h
is
fair
on a line
L
if

h(L)
is a straight line. We say that a direction

D
is
fair
for
h
if
h
is fair for every
line in the direction
D
.
Corollary
1.8
says
in particular that for any direction
D
we have

h\Rightarrow h^{\prime\prime}
, where
D
is fair for
h^{\prime\prime}
.
Applying Corollary
1.8
three times we
get
h\Rightarrow h^{(6)}
, where
3
different
directions are fair for
h^{(6)}
. It is a fun exercise
to show that this forces the equivariant
h^{(6)}
to be real affine
– and hence in
I
.

Zoom Ad Infinitum:

We could apply Corollary
1.8
an infinite
number of times and then take a limit
(guaranteed by a result much like Lemma
1.4
)
and conclude that
h\sim h^{*}
where
h^{*}
is
fair on every line. But then the equivariance
implies that
h
preserves generalized circles and hence is in
I
.

The Minimal Proof:

The first half of the proof of Corollary
1.8
shows that

h\to h^{\prime}
where all lines through a point
z\in\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}

are good for
h^{\prime}
. Since
h\sim h^{\prime}
we see that
there are a pair of points
a,b\in{\bf S\/}
such that
the restriction of
h
to each generalized circle through
a,b
is a
conformal map. Since
h
is equivariant, there is a second
pair
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
of points, both distinct from
a
and
b
, such
that the restriction of
h
to each generalized circle through
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
is
a conformal transformation. It is a fun exercise to show that
these conditions force
h\in\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
. This proof is a bit harder to
generalize to higher dimensions.

The Extension

2.1
Proofs modulo the Morse Lemma

In this chapter we prove
Theorem
1.2
and Lemma
1.3
.
In this first section we reduce these
results to a well-known geometric result
called the Morse Lemma. This is one
of the usual approaches. We work with the
K
-BL map

H:\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}\to\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}.

Given a geodesic
\gamma\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
let

N_{r}(\gamma)
denote the set of points
in
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
at most
r
from
\gamma
.
The set
N_{r}(\gamma)
is called the

r
-
tubular neighborhood
of
\gamma
.

Lemma 2.1
(Morse)

Let
\gamma_{1}
be a geodesic. Then
there is a unique geodesic

\gamma_{2}
such that
H(\gamma_{1})\subset N_{K^{\prime}}(\gamma_{2})
.
The constant
K^{\prime}
only depends on
K
.

Our proof gets
the constant
K^{\prime}=4K^{3}+2K+1
, but this is not optimal.
The choice of
K^{\prime}
, however,
has no inpact on the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
The Morse Lemma holds in great generality,
with good control on
K^{\prime}
. See [
GS
].

Proof of Theorem
1.2
:

The Morse Lemma induces a map
of the set of oriented geodesics. If
\gamma_{1}
is an oriented
geodesic
then
\gamma_{1}\to\gamma_{2}
where

\gamma_{2}
is the unique geodesic such that

H(\gamma_{1})\subset N_{K^{\prime}}(\gamma_{2})
.
The orientation of
\gamma_{1}
combines with
H
to
determine the orientation of
\gamma_{2}
.
If
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}\to\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
and

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
have a common endpoint,
then
H(\gamma_{1})
and
H(\gamma_{1}^{\prime})
are asymptotic
to each other in the corresponding direction.
Hence
\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
also have
a common endpoint. This property lets us define

h
as the unique map of the Riemann sphere

{\bf S\/}
with the
following property: if
\gamma_{1}\to\gamma_{2}
and

\gamma_{1}
connects

p
and
q
, then
\gamma_{2}

connects
h(p)
to
h(q)
.
If we apply the construction to
H^{-1}
we get
h^{-1}
.

It remains to show that
h
is a homeo.
By construction,
h
is a bijection.
If
h
is not a homeo,
there are
H
-corresponding sequences of
geodesics
\{\gamma_{1,n}\}\leftrightarrow\{\gamma_{2,n}\}

such that the endpoints of the geodesics in the one sequence
come together on
{\bf S\/}
and the endpoints in the other
sequence do not. But then the one sequence exits every
compact subset of
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
and the other sequence does not.
This contradicts the BL nature of
H
.
Given the way that the extension only depends on
H
we see
that
h
is equivariant if
H
is equivariant.

\spadesuit

Before proving Lemma
1.3
we
need one preliminary lemma. An

ideal triangle
is a union of

3
geodesics defined by
3
distinct
points on
{\bf S\/}
. Any two
ideal triangles are equivalent under the
action of
I
.

Lemma 2.2

Let
\Delta
be an ideal geodesic triangle in
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
.
Then for any
R
, the set of points in
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
within

R
of all three geodesics of
\Delta
is compact.

Proof:
Let
\gamma_{a,b}
be the geodesic in
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}

whose endpoints are
a,b\in{\bf S\/}
.
We can normalize so that
\Delta
has vertices

0,1,\infty
. Let
p=(z,t)\in\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
.
If
t\to 0
then the distance from
p
to
one of
\gamma_{0,\infty}
or
\gamma_{1,\infty}

tends to
\infty
. If
t\to\infty
or

|z|\to\infty
then the distance from

p
to
\gamma_{0,1}
tends to
\infty
.
So, if
p
is close to all these geodesics when

t\sim 1
and
|z|
is fairly small.

Proof of Lemma
1.3
:

We can normalize by a pair of elements of

I
so that

\{(H_{n},h_{n})\}
is such that

h_{n}(0)\to 0
and
h_{n}(1)\to 1
and
h_{n}(\infty)\to\infty
.
Further composing with convergent sequences in

I
we can assume that
h_{n}
fixes each of
0,1,\infty
.
Let
p\in\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
be arbitrary. By the Morse Lemma,

H_{n}(p)
is uniformly close to all

. But then, by Lemma

,

\{H_{n}(p)\}
remains within a bounded subset

2.2
The Morse Lemma Modulo the Tube Lemma

We keep the notation from the previous section.
Let
d
,
\ell
respectively denote hyperbolic
distance, hyperbolic arc length.
As in
F4
from §
1.1
, we
define

\gamma=\{0\}\times(0,\infty),\hskip 30.0pt\phi(p)=(0,\|p\|)\in\gamma.

(12)

Again,
\phi
is the map such that

\phi(p)
is the point of
\gamma
closest to
p
.

In the next section we prove the following result.

Lemma 2.3
(Tube)

If
r>1
then
\ell\circ\phi\leq e^{-r+1}\ell
on

\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}-N_{r}(\gamma)
.

Now we use the Tube Lemma to prove the Morse Lemma.
The proof comes in two steps. The first step is showing
that an arc of
H(\gamma_{1})
cannot wander too far from
the geodesic connecting its endpoints. If the arc does
wander far away,
we can use
\phi
to produce a much shorter
competing path which connects the endpoints.
This will give a contradiction.
After we have this result about finite arcs of
H(\gamma_{1})
, the
second step involves considering a sequence of increasingly
long arcs of
H(\gamma_{1})
and taking a suitable limit.
The key point is that the geodesics connecting the
endpoints of these arcs all have to line up.

Lemma 2.4

Let
C=4K^{3}+2K
.
Let
\alpha_{1}
be a geodesic segment in
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
. Then

H(\alpha_{1})\subset N_{C}(\gamma_{2})
for
the geodesic
\gamma_{2}
through the
endpoints of
H(\alpha_{1})
.

Proof:
We normalize so that
\gamma_{2}=\{0\}\times(0,\infty)
, as in the
Tube Lemma. The figure shows a schematic picture of our
constructions.

Let
\beta_{1}=H(\alpha_{1})
.
Suppose
\beta_{1}\not\subset N_{C}(\gamma_{2})
.
Then there are
p,q\in\partial N_{2K}(\gamma_{2})
and an
arc
\beta
of
\beta_{1}
joining
p
to
q
that remains outside

N_{2K}(\gamma_{2})
and exits
N_{C}(\gamma_{2})
. Note that

\ell(\beta)\geq 8K^{3}
.
The path
\beta^{\prime}=\phi(\beta)
connects
\phi(p)
to
\phi(q)
.
By the Tube Lemma

d(\phi(p),\phi(q))\leq\ell(\beta^{\prime})\leq\ell(\beta)e^{-2K+1}.

(13)

By
F4
from §
1.1
, we have

d(p,\phi(p))=d(q,\phi(q))=2K
. By
Equation
13
and the triangle inequality

d(p,q)\leq d(p,\phi(p))+d(\phi(p),\phi(q))+d(\phi(q),q)\leq e^{-2K+1}\ell(\beta)+4K.

(14)

Since
\beta
is the image of a geodesic segment
under a
K
-BL map,

\ell(\beta)\leq K\ell(H^{-1}(\beta))=Kd(H^{-1}(p),H^{-1}(q))\leq K^{2}\,d(p,q).

(15)

Combining Equations
14
and
15
, and using

\ell(\beta)\geq 8K^{3}
, we have

\bigg(e^{-2K+1}+\frac{1}{2K^{2}}\bigg)\ell(\beta)\geq e^{-2K+1}\ell(\beta)+4K\geq d(p,q)\geq\frac{\ell(\beta)}{K^{2}}.

(16)

Multiplying through by
2K^{2}/\ell(\beta)
and subtracting
1
, we get

2K^{2}e^{-2K+1}\geq 1.

This is false for all
K\geq 1
, a contradiction.

\spadesuit

Proof of the Morse Lemma:

Let
C=4K^{3}+2K
be as in Lemma
2.4
.
We fix an origin
O
of
\gamma_{1}
and let
\alpha_{1,n}\subset\gamma_{1}
be a
geodesic segment of length at least
2n
centered at
O
.
Let the endpoints be
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
.
Each ray of
\gamma_{1}
emanating from
O
exits
every compact subset of
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
, so we can choose our
segments so that

\{H(a_{n})\}
and
\{H(b_{n})\}
both converge to
points
A,B\in{\bf S\/}
. Given our description of
hyperbolic geodesics, and
the fact (from Lemma
2.4
) that
\gamma_{2,n}
comes
within
C
of
H(O)
for all
n
, we cannot have
A=B
.

We normalize so that
A=(-1,0)
and
B=(1,0)
.
Let
\Delta_{r}\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}
be the ball of radius
r
about
O
.
Let
\gamma_{2}
be the geodesic connecting
(-1,0)
and
(1,0)
.
The endpoints of
\gamma_{2,n}
converge
to the endpoints of
\gamma_{2}
. By compactness and
Lemma
2.4
,
we have the following for sufficiently large
n
:

H(\gamma_{1})\cap\Delta_{r}=H(\alpha_{1,n})\cap\Delta_{r}\subset N_{C}(\gamma_{2,n})\cap\Delta_{r}\subset N_{C+1}(\gamma_{2})\cap\Delta_{r}\subset N_{C+1}(\gamma_{2}).

Letting
r\to\infty
we see that
H(\gamma_{1})\subset N_{C+1}(\gamma_{2})
. Now we set
K^{\prime}=C+1
.

2.3
Proof of the Tube Lemma

We keep the notation from the previous section. Also, let

\ell_{\mbox{\boldmath{$E$}}}
denote Euclidean arc length. Let
R=e^{r}
.
We use the facts
F1
–
F4
from §
1.1
.
Looking at the formula for
\phi
we can see that

\ell_{\mbox{\boldmath{$E$}}}\circ\phi\leq\ell_{\mbox{\boldmath{$E$}}}
.
That is,
\phi
does not increase Euclidean
arc length.
Define

\gamma^{\prime}=\{1\}\times(0,\infty),\hskip 30.0ptU=\big(\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\times(0,e^{-r+1})\big)\cap\phi^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\times(1,\infty))

(17)

The set
U
is open and
\phi(U)\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\times(1,\infty)
.
By
F3
, we have
\ell\leq\ell_{\mbox{\boldmath{$E$}}}
on
\phi(U)
and

\ell_{\mbox{\boldmath{$E$}}}\leq e^{-r+1}\ell
on
U
. Hence, on
U
, we have

\ell\circ\phi\leq\ell_{\mbox{\boldmath{$E$}}}\circ\phi\leq\ell_{\mbox{\boldmath{$E$}}}\leq e^{-r+1}\ell.

Combining
F3
and
F4
we see that

(1,t)
is less than
r
units from
\gamma
when

t\in[e^{-r+1},\infty)
. Therefore

\gamma^{\prime}-N_{r}(\gamma)\subset U.

(18)

Let
\Gamma\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}
denote the stabilizer
subgroup of
\gamma
. Elements of
\Gamma
have
the form
(z,t)\to(az,|a|t)
with
a\in\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}-\{0\}
.
Every point of
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}-N_{r}(\gamma)

can be mapped into
\gamma^{\prime}-N_{r}(\gamma)
by an element of

\Gamma
. Hence
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}-N_{r}(\gamma)\subset\Gamma(U)
, the
orbit of
U
under
\Gamma
.
But elements of
\Gamma
commute with
\phi
. For this reason,
our inequality
\ell\circ\phi\leq e^{-r+1}\ell
on
U
holds on all
of
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}^{3}-N_{r}(\gamma)
.
This proves the Tube Lemma.

3
Existence of Asterisks

3.1
Reduction to a Technical Lemma

A set
S\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}
is
null
if, for every

\epsilon>0
, there is a countable collection
\{D_{j}\}
of disks
such that
S\subset\bigcup D_{j}
and

\sum{\rm area\/}(D_{j})<\epsilon
. Otherwise,
S
is
fat
.

Let
(H,h)
be an equivariant pair, normalized
so that
h(\infty)=\infty
. The purpose of this chapter
is to prove that
h
has an asterisk, a point where
all the rational directional derivatives exist and
are nonzero.

Lemma 3.1

The subset of
[0,1]^{2}
where
\partial h/\partial x
does not exist
is null. The subset of
[0,1]^{2}
where
\partial h/\partial x\not=0
is
fat.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
:

Tile
C
by unit squares. By symmetry and Lemma
3.1

the derivative
\partial h/\partial x
exists in each square outside
of a null set. The countable union of null sets is null, so

\partial h/\partial x
exists outside of a null set
S_{0}\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}
. By
rotational symmetry
D_{r}h
exists outside a null set for all
rational vectors. The countable union
of these null sets is null, and outside
\bigcup S_{r}
all the
rational directional derivatives of
h
exist.
A subset of a null set is null, so the
fat set where
\partial h/\partial x\not=0

intersects the set where all the rational directional
derivatives of
h
exist. This gives us an asterisk,
and in fact many.

The rest of the chapter is devoted to the proof of

The

\sigma
-algebra
is the smallest

[0,1]^{d}
that contains

complements, countable intersections,

\sigma

Let

Then the set of points in

a Borel set.

We identify the set of horizontal lines in

[0,1]
in the obvious way.

Let

If

Suppose

A Primer on Measure Theory

4.1
Measurability

This chapter proves some standard measure-theoretic results,
exactly what we need for the results in §
3.2
.
See [
F
] for much more.

We work in
[0,1]^{d}
. We only care about
d=1,2
.
A
dyadic cube
in
[0,1]^{d}
is a cube we
get by starting with
[0,1]^{d}
and recursively
subdividing cubes into
2^{d}
equal-sized
sub-cubes and selecting one of them.
Two dyadic cubes are either nested or else have disjoint interiors.
Say that a
carpet
is a countable union of dyadic cubes
having pairwise disjoint interiors.
When
S\subset[0,1]^{d}
we define the
outer measure

\mu(S)=\inf_{S\subset\bigcup Q_{n}}\sum{\rm volume\/}(Q_{j})

(23)

The infimum is taken over all carpets containing
S
.

A subset
S\subset[0,1]^{d}
is
open
iff the complement

S^{c}-[0,1]^{d}-S
is closed.
Every open subset of
[0,1]^{d}
is the countable union of dyadic
cubes. In particular, this is true for open balls.
Using this fact it is an easy exercise to show that

S
is null iff
