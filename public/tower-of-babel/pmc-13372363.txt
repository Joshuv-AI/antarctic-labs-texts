Evaluation of Continuous Z-Plasty Technique for Linear Scar Repair on the Face: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Zhiliang Zhang, Zhihong Fan, Xiaoyun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the continuous Z-plasty technique for repairing facial linear scars and to assess patient satisfaction.METHODS:A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients with facial linear scars who underwent continuous Z-plasty at the authors’ institution from January 2020 to December 2022. Inclusion criteria were patients with facial linear scars, excluding those with other complex facial injuries. Surgical details, preoperative and postoperative Vancouver Scar Scale scores, scar width, and patient satisfaction survey results were collected and analyzed.RESULTS:This study included 21 patients (9 males, 12 females) with a mean age of 36.7 years. The frontal area was the most common location. Hypertrophic scars were present in 4 cases, superficial atrophic in 8, and normal in 9. The median time since scar formation was 14 months. Vancouver Scar Scale scores decreased from a median of 8 preoperatively to 3 postoperatively, with scar widths reducing from 4.5 to 1.5 mm. Follow-up at a median of 18 months showed no recurrence or complications, with significant aesthetic improvement. Comparative photos before and after surgery clearly demonstrated an improvement in scar appearance.CONCLUSIONS:Continuous Z-plasty has proven to be both an effective and safe approach for improving the appearance of facial linear scars. It not only enhances aesthetic outcomes but also boosts patient satisfaction. Future research should focus on evaluating its long-term results and comparing its effectiveness with other treatment options.

INTRODUCTION

Scars resulting from trauma or surgery, particularly on the face, can cause significant psychosocial distress. The ideal scar should be fine and flat, closely matching the color and texture of the surrounding skin, without causing deformities in adjacent tissues due to scar contracture.1 Although various treatments exist, surgical revision remains a cornerstone for improving the appearance of linear scars. Z-plasty is a well-established technique for reorienting scars and redistributing tension. Continuous Z-plasty, a refinement involving multiple sequential Z-plasties along a scar, may offer advantages for elongated linear scars by further dispersing tension and breaking up scar linearity. However, systematic evaluations of its application specifically for facial linear scars are lacking.

This study seeks to assess the effectiveness and patient satisfaction of continuous Z-plasty through a retrospective analysis of cases involving facial linear scars treated at the authors’ hospital. The authors propose that continuous Z-plasty could significantly diminish the visibility of such scars while enhancing patients’ quality of life.

METHODS

Clinical Data

This study retrospectively analyzed patient data from January 2020 to December 2022 at the Department of Plastic and Cosmetic Surgery, Ningbo Hangzhou Bay Hospital, Ningbo, China. Patients were selected consecutively and assigned to receive continuous Z-plasty based on clinical judgment. The inclusion criteria were: (1) linear facial scars; (2) scars with concavity or hypertrophy exceeding 3 mm in width; (3) repair using continuous Z-plasty; and (4) voluntary participation with signed informed consent and agreement to the use of their photographs. Exclusion criteria were: (1) withdrawal, abandonment, or participation in other clinical studies; (2) scars not in the stable phase for at least 6 months; (3) patients with keloids; and (4) incomplete treatment or loss to follow-up.

Therapeutic Procedure

All patients presented with facial scars resulting from either trauma or surgery, with a healing duration of at least 6 months, ensuring that the scars were in a stable phase. Preoperative evaluations included detailed facial photography and precise measurements of scar length and width to record their characteristics and assess their effects on surrounding tissues. The procedure was carried out under local anesthesia, ensuring that all patients were in satisfactory general health. Continuous Z-plasty was used to address and enhance the esthetic appearance of linear scars.

Patients underwent preoperative marking using methylene blue at the boundaries of their facial scar tissue (Figure 1A). The scar’s long axis and short arms of oblique Z-flaps (length ≤8 mm, spacing 5 to 8 mm) intersecting at 45 to 60 degrees with the long axis were meticulously designed along the scar’s longitudinal axis (Figure 1B). Local infiltration anesthesia (1% lidocaine + 1:100,000 epinephrine) was administered to the surgical area until the skin exhibited pallor and swelling. A surgical blade was used to precisely incise along the marked line at the scar’s edge, excising scar tissue and subcutaneous fibrous tissue. The incision extended along the short arm design line until reaching normal adipose tissue. Tissue dissection on both sides of the incision created continuous rhomboid flaps (Figure 1C). These dissected flaps were transposed to the opposite side and meticulously sutured to secure their tips. The subsequent adjustment involved meticulous tissue trimming to achieve tension-free alignment of the skin edges (Figure 1D). Closure was accomplished utilizing interrupted sutures with 6-0 Prolene thread (Johnson & Johnson) (Figure 1E). Postoperatively, the incision site was dressed with gauze, lightly compressed, and bandaged. Erythromycin eye ointment was applied to the incision. All surgical procedures were performed by the same senior surgeon. Dressings were changed on the day following surgery, and sutures were removed within 5 to 7 days. One week after suture removal, silicone gel application was initiated for all patients to minimize the risk of scar hypertrophy. Patients were instructed to apply a thin layer twice daily for a minimum recommended duration of 3 months.

Ethics

All research procedures involving human participants comply with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and adhere to the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments or similar ethical standards. Informed consent has been obtained from the patients themselves or the legal guardians of children.

RESULTS

This study included 21 patients, comprising 7 males and 14 females, aged 8 to 44 years, with a mean age of 34 years. Among these, 12 patients had frontal scars (Figure 2), 5 had cheek scars (Figure 3), 3 had scars along the chin and mandibular margin (Figure 4), and 1 had a lip scar (Figure 5). All scars were linear, stable, and pale in color, with widths ranging from 3 to 8 mm and an average width of 4 mm.

Before the intervention, Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) scores ranged from 4 to 8, with an average score of 5.6. The scars presented as follows: 8 cases of superficial atrophic scars, 4 cases of hypertrophic scars elevated above the skin surface, and 9 cases of normal scars measuring 6 to 8 mm in width. All scars originated from traumatic injuries, with 12 cases initially managed by emergency wound closure and 9 cases healing spontaneously. Despite this, patients remained dissatisfied with the cosmetic appearance of their scars. All scars had reached a stable phase, with a minimum duration exceeding 6 months.

The VSS scores improved significantly from a median preoperative score of 8 to a postoperative score of 3. The median scar width reduced from 4.5 mm preoperatively to 1.5 mm postoperatively. Follow-up times averaged 18 months, with all patients exhibiting improved scar conditions without recurrence of infection or dehiscence. No significant scar hyperplasia, redness, swelling, or itching was observed, indicating effective treatment outcomes. All information about the patients is shown in Table 1.

CLINICAL CASE DATA OF ENROLLED PATIENTS

Following continuous Z-plasty scar repair in all 21 patients, primary wound closure was achieved without any complications, including infection or vascular compromise. Dressings were changed within 24 hours postoperatively, and sutures were removed within 5 to 7 days. Patients were monitored for follow-up periods ranging from 6 to 18 months, with an average follow-up duration of 10 months. During the initial 3 to 6 months postoperatively, the incision scars typically appeared red and firm but gradually transitioned to a paler and softer appearance over time.

One patient experienced a postoperative site infection 3 months after surgery, whereas the remaining patients showed no signs of scar widening. The incision scars displayed linear characteristics with a smoother texture, reflecting significant improvement compared with their preoperative condition. According to the satisfaction survey, 18 patients reported being satisfied with the results, whereas 3 patients described the outcomes as good.

DISCUSSION

Traditional wound closure principles, as established by Halsted, emphasize a layered approach to eliminate dead space, reduce tension, and achieve precise epidermal apposition.2 A significant advancement was introduced by Zitelli and Moy3 in 1989 with the buried vertical mattress suture, which combined key features of prior techniques. This innovation served as a foundation for the subsequent development of numerous modified tension-reducing suture methods.4–10 Mechanical tension at the wound edges is considered a pivotal factor in scar formation11,12 and persists throughout all phases of healing.13 The tensile strength of scar tissue is ∼20% of normal skin at 3 weeks postoperatively, increases to 70% to 80% by 3 months, and reaches full maturation within 6 to 12 months.14,15 This indicates that nascent scars are subjected to sustained traction from the surrounding skin for an extended period, predisposing them to collagen overproduction and scar widening.2,3,16 Consequently, maintaining effective tension reduction throughout the scar maturation period is crucial.

Clinically, tension reduction for facial scars primarily relies on three approaches: (1) intraoperative tension-relieving sutures, such as the Zhang super tension-relieving suture, which disperses tension away from the wound edges through wide subcutaneous suturing;11 (2) external tension reduction, exemplified by tension-reducing tapes, with studies confirming their significant role in mitigating scar widening—patients without such intervention face a 13.6-fold higher risk of hypertrophic scarring;17 and (3) minimizing wound edge traction, for instance, by using botulinum toxin injections to inhibit periscar muscle activity.18

These tension-reduction suture techniques depend on the strength of the sutures to counteract or minimize mechanical tension during scar formation. The success of these methods is influenced not only by the suture technique itself but also by the duration for which the sutures maintain tension resistance. In comparison, W-plasty and continuous Z-plasty provide an alternative approach to tension adjustment during wound healing. These techniques reduce tension by altering the incision shape and redistributing mechanical forces from a single direction into multiple directions, thereby partially mitigating tension. The core principle of these methods involves transforming a linear incision into interlocking crossed triangles. This modification converts a linear scar into a zigzag pattern, dispersing contracture across multiple directions, improving local skin mobility, and progressively relieving tension on the scar.19

Traditional W-plasty entails predesigning continuous W-shaped incision lines, with the “W” angles typically positioned at needle-hole scars or wider scar areas to excise more scar tissue. However, complete excision of scar tissue in the skin and subcutaneous layers leads to significant distortion in the wound due to changes in tension. Consequently, the initially designed W-flap often requires revision during surgery.19,20 Thus, the authors have developed a modified approach to W-flaps, termed in this article as “continuous Z-plasty” scar revision surgery. The continuous Z-plasty surgical procedure commences with marking the extent of scar tissue removal and the lateral arms of the Z-shaped flaps. Following anesthesia, scar tissue and surrounding normal tissue are excised along the marked lines to create the flaps. Despite the reshaped incision differing from the original scar, the design of multiple Z-shaped flaps allows for more adaptable wound closure. During subsequent alignment sutures, suture tension is adjusted based on the modified wound orientation, including the removal of redundant skin and subcutaneous tissue. The adjusted suture tension primarily concentrates at the apex of the triangular flap, whereas the two lateral arms remain in a tension-free alignment. Following healing, the apex of the triangular flap bears most of the wound tension; nevertheless, no scar expansion or hypertrophy is observed postoperatively. The precise mechanism underlying this phenomenon warrants further investigation19 (Figure 6).

In this suturing technique, the authors use a precise fixation approach at the apex while simultaneously adjusting the lateral arms to ensure complete scar relaxation and accommodate tension variations during wound relocation. This strategy minimizes tissue excision.

The lateral arms on both sides are sutured without tension, and for cases involving unequal lengths—commonly seen at the incision ends or along irregularly shaped scar lines—the authors often shape the shorter side with an arc. In addition, angle adjustments using triangular flaps are implemented to correct length discrepancies when necessary. To address tissue disparities, the authors also incorporate a combination of Z-plasty and V-Y plasty techniques.

In plastic surgery, layered closure has traditionally been regarded as an effective method for minimizing tension during wound healing. However, the authors have chosen to forgo this approach in favor of full-thickness closure, which they believe offers superior benefits for wound healing. This technique also reduces the risk of foreign body reactions associated with the prolonged presence of subcuticular sutures used in layered closures. In full-thickness closure, all subcuticular sutures are entirely removed after healing, ensuring the incision remains free of foreign materials. Follow-up evaluations of the authors’ patients revealed no instances of scar widening or proliferation following subcuticular closure.

In facial scar revisions using Z-plasty, 3 primary objectives are pursued: (1) the elongation or release of contractures; (2) the disruption of the linear scar structure; and (3) the strategic realignment of the scar along relaxed skin tension lines (RSTL).21 Linear facial scars are particularly susceptible to contractures, and the influence of muscle movement on scar formation cannot be overlooked. By transforming linear scars into zigzag patterns through continuous Z-plasty, the unidirectional tensile forces exerted by muscle activity are redistributed across multiple directions. This reduces mechanical stimulation of scar tissue and promotes improved healing outcomes (Figure 7). When determining the direction of Z-flap limbs, careful attention is given to their alignment with RSTL. Typically, these limbs are oriented parallel to the skin’s natural tension lines or angled at 45 to 60 degrees. They may also coincide with natural transition zones between facial esthetic units. This thoughtful approach ensures that postoperative scars blend seamlessly with the surrounding skin texture, achieving optimal esthetic and functional outcomes.

Limitations

The number of patient cases analyzed in this study is relatively small, and all patients seeking improvement for facial scars had suboptimal healing, making it difficult to conduct a statistical analysis of the outcomes between Z-plasty and direct closure techniques. Secondly, for wider facial scars (≥1 cm), this method cannot be directly applied; instead, the scar must be excised and narrowed before proceeding with continuous remodeling surgery to enhance appearance. Thirdly, the surgical outcomes for nonfacial linear scars need further validation. Lastly, although the continuous Z-plasty technique shows good results for facial scars, its applicability to scars in other body areas requires further investigation.

As this study used a retrospective design, the authors relied on existing data records and lacked a randomization process, which may not control variables as rigorously as a prospective study. In addition, the sample size was relatively small (21 cases), which could affect the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, as a retrospective study, the analysis is subject to potential selection and follow-up bias. Patients who were dissatisfied with their outcomes might have been less likely to return for scheduled follow-up visits or might have sought care elsewhere. This attrition could lead to an overestimation of patient satisfaction and treatment success rates in our cohort.​ Although the authors observed positive therapeutic outcomes, these findings may not fully represent the effects in a broader patient population. Given these limitations, future research should consider adopting a randomized controlled trial design and implementing standardized, proactive follow-up protocols​ to more accurately evaluate the efficacy of continuous Z-plasty in the treatment of facial linear scars.

CONCLUSIONS

Z-plasty stands as a cornerstone technique in reconstructive surgery, constituting an essential skillset for plastic surgeons. A profound comprehension of the principles underlying Z-plasty empowers plastic surgeons to navigate through a myriad of diverse and intricate clinical situations. Moreover, the integration of Z-plasty with other techniques paves the way for the development of novel surgical methodologies, with continuous Z-plasty emerging as a notable illustration. Despite certain limitations, the findings provide valuable reference points for the clinical application and broader adoption of this technique.
