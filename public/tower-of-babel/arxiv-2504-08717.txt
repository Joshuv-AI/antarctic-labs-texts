On certain Lagrangian subvarieties in minimal resolutions of Kleinian singularities
Mengwei Hu

Abstract
. Kleinian singularities are quotients of ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} by finite subgroups of SL 2 ​ ( ℂ ) \mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}) . They are in bijection with the simply-laced Dynkin diagrams via the McKay correspondence. Anti-Poisson involutions and their fixed point loci appear naturally in the classification of irreducible Harish-Chandra modules over Kleinian singularities. There are three goals of this paper. The first is to classify anti-Poisson involutions of Kleinian singularities up to conjugation by graded Poisson automorphisms. The second is to describe the scheme-theoretic fixed point loci of Kleinian singularities under anti-Poisson involutions. The third and main goal is to describe the scheme-theoretic preimages of the fixed point loci under minimal resolutions of Kleinian singularities. These preimages are singular Lagrangian subvarieties in the minimal resolutions whose irreducible components are ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1} ’s and 𝔸 1 \mathbb{A}^{1} ’s. Additionally, we discuss the connections between anti-Poisson involutions and Lie algebra involutions.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Kleinian singularities

Kleinian singularities are quotients of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
by finite subgroups
\Gamma\subset\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. They can be described as surfaces in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
with isolated singularities at
0
. The McKay correspondence states that the finite subgroups of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
are in bijection with the simply-laced Dynkin diagrams. Let
\pi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X:=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
denote the minimal resolution of a Kleinian singularity. The reduced exceptional fiber is of the form

\pi^{-1}(0)_{\red}=C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{n},\ C_{i}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{1}.

Each
C_{i}
has self-intersection
C_{i}\cdot C_{i}=-2
, and the curves intersect pairwise transversely with dual graph a Dynkin diagram of types
ADE
.

The algebra
\mathbb{C}[u,v]
is a graded Poisson algebra with respect to the usual bracket and grading. The algebra of regular functions on a Kleinian singularity
\mathbb{C}[X]=\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
is a graded Poisson subalgebra of
\mathbb{C}[u,v]
. Let
\theta
be an
anti-Poisson involution
on
X
, which means that
\theta
is a graded algebra involution on
\mathbb{C}[X]
such that
\theta(\{\cdot,\cdot\})=-\{\theta(\cdot),\theta(\cdot)\}
. We would like to study the
scheme-theoretic fixed point locus

X^{\theta}:=\Spec\mathbb{C}[X]/(\theta(f)-f|f\in\mathbb{C}[X])
and its scheme-theoretic preimage
\pi^{-1}(X^{\theta})
under the minimal resolution. There are three goals of this paper.

(a)

Classify anti-Poisson involutions on
X
up to conjugation by graded Poisson automorphisms.

(b)

Describe
X^{\theta}
as a subscheme, i.e., determine its irreducible components, how they intersect, and whether the scheme is reduced.

(c)

Describe the preimage
\pi^{-1}(X^{\theta})
under the minimal resolution as a subscheme.

1.2.
Motivations

We discuss the motivation to study anti-Poisson involutions of Kleinian singularities and their fixed point loci. They are related to the classification of irreducible Harish-Chandra
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-modules annihilated by certain unipotent ideals. We review some results on nilpotent orbits in
Section

1.2.1
, which will be needed for the discussion on Harish-Chandra modules in
Section

1.2.2
.

1.2.1.
Nilpotent orbits and symmetric pairs

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semisimple Lie algebra over
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{O}\subset\mathfrak{g}
be a nilpotent orbit. Then
\mathbb{O}
can be identified with a coadjoint orbit in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
using the Killing form, and therefore carries the Kostant-Kirillov symplectic
2
-form. Let
\overline{\mathbb{O}}
be the closure of
\mathbb{O}
in
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathbb{O}^{\prime}
be an orbit of codimension
2
in
\overline{\mathbb{O}}
. Take
e\in\mathbb{O}^{\prime}
and let
\{e,f,h\}
be an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple containing
e
. Consider the
Slodowy slice

(1.1)

S:=e+\ker(\ad f),

which is a transversal slice to the orbit through
e

[
37
, Section 7.4]
. Assume that
e
is a normal point in
\overline{\mathbb{O}}
. Then
S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}
is a rational Gorenstein (due to Panyushev
[
34
, Theorem 1]
) singularity of dimension
2
, hence isomorphic to a Kleinian singularity
X
.
Note that
\overline{\mathbb{O}}
is always normal in type
A

[
21
, Theorem]
, and we can pass to its normalization in the general situation.

Anti-Poisson involutions arise naturally from involutions of Lie algebras. Let
\sigma\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra involution. We can decompose
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
, where
\mathfrak{k}
is the
1
-eigenspace and
\mathfrak{p}
is the
(-1)
-eigenspace of
\sigma
. The pair
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is called a
symmetric pair
. Set
\theta:=-\sigma
; then
\theta
is an anti-Poisson involution of
\mathfrak{g}
.

If we further assume that the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple
\{e,f,h\}
is
normal
, i.e.,
\sigma(e)=-e,\ \sigma(f)=-f,\ \sigma(h)=h
, then
S
is
\theta
-stable. As long as
\mathbb{O}^{\prime}\cap\mathfrak{p}\neq\emptyset
, such a normal triple exists
[
6
, Theorems 9.4.1, 9.5.1]
. Assume further that
\sigma
preserves
\mathbb{O}
. This condition is satisfied for all regular and subregular nilpotent orbits in any
\mathfrak{g}
, as well as for all nilpotent orbits in type
A
. Then
\theta
restricts to an anti-Poisson involution on
S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}\simeq X
. We have
S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p}=(S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}})^{\theta}\simeq X^{\theta}
, where the fixed point locus shows up.

Fixed point loci of this form for symmetric pairs in the classical types were analyzed by Sekiguchi
[
36
, Theorem 7, Tables IV–VI]
and Ohta
[
32
, Table VIII]
(cf.
Remark

10.10
for type
A
and
Remark

10.11
for type
D
). Our method also treats type
E
. For a detailed discussion of the connection between Lie algebra involutions and anti-Poisson involutions, see
Section

10
.

1.2.2.
Harish-Chandra modules

Let
\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k},\sigma,\theta
be as in
Section

1.2.1
. Let
G
be the simply connected algebraic group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and
K\subset G
the connected algebraic subgroup with Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}
. Let
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
be the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, equipped with the PBW filtration. By a
Harish-Chandra
(shortly HC)
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-module, one means a finitely generated
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
-module
M
such that
\mathfrak{k}
acts locally finitely and the action of
\mathfrak{k}
integrates to that of
K
. A
good
filtration on an HC
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-module
M
is a
K
-stable filtration that is compatible with the filtration on
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
and such that
\gr M
is finitely generated over
\gr\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
. Pick a good filtration on
M
. We can define the
associated variety

\AV(M):=\Supp(\gr M)
as a set. Note that
\AV(M)
is independent of the choice of the good filtration (cf.
[
40
, Section 2]
). When
M
is irreducible, we have
\AV(M)\subset\mathcal{N}\cap\mathfrak{p}
, where
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathfrak{g}
is the nilpotent cone.

We focus on a special class of irreducible HC
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-modules, namely those annihilated by unipotent ideals.
Let
\mathbb{O}\subset\mathfrak{g}
be a nilpotent orbit. The algebra of regular functions
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{O}]
is a graded Poisson algebra. It makes
sense to speak about filtered quantizations of
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{O}]
, and they are parameterized by
H^{2}(Y^{\reg},\mathbb{C})
, where
Y
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial terminalization of
\Spec(\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{O}])

[
26
, Theorem 3.4]
. Consider the
canonical
(in the terminology of
[
24
, Section 1.3]
) quantization
\mathcal{A}_{0}
, which corresponds to the parameter
0\in H^{2}(Y^{\reg},\mathbb{C})
. The kernel of the quantum comoment map
\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbb{O}):=\ker(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathcal{A}_{0})
is the
unipotent ideal associated with
\mathbb{O}
in the terminology of
[
24
, Section 1.3]
. All such ideals are maximal
[
27
, Theorem 5.0.1]
. Let
M
be an irreducible HC
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-module annihilated by
\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbb{O})
. Then we have
\AV(M)\subset\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p}
. Let us further assume that

(1.2)

\codim_{\overline{\mathbb{O}}}\partial\mathbb{O}\geqslant 4.

A result by Vogan states that
\AV(M)
is irreducible and hence is the closure of a single
K
-orbit in
\mathbb{O}\cap\mathfrak{p}

[
40
, Theorem 4.6]
. Let
\mathbb{O}_{K}
denote the open
K
-orbit in
\AV(M)
. Losev and Yu prove that there is a bijection between the set of irreducible HC
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-modules annihilated by
\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbb{O})
with associated variety
\overline{\mathbb{O}}_{K}
and the set of irreducible
K
-equivariant twisted (by half-canonical twist) local systems on
\mathbb{O}_{K}

[
25
, Theorem 1.3.1]
. The bijection sends an HC module
M
to the twisted local system
(\gr M)|_{\mathbb{O}_{K}}
.

However, when
\codim_{\overline{\mathbb{O}}}\partial\mathbb{O}\geqslant 2
, the classification of irreducible HC
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-modules annihilated by
\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbb{O})
is unknown. One difficulty is that
\AV(M)
may not be irreducible. For example, consider the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{C}\langle e,h,f\rangle
with the inner involution
\sigma:=\Ad(\diag\{1,-1\})
. Then
\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{g}^{\sigma}=\mathbb{C}h
is a Cartan subalgebra. Let
\mathbb{O}
be the regular nilpotent orbit in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. The unipotent ideal
\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbb{O})
is the two-sided ideal generated by the Casimir element
C=(h+1)^{2}+4fe
. Let
M
be an irreducible
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-module such that
h
acts semisimply with even integral eigenvalues and the Casimir element
C
acts by
0
. Then
M
has neither a highest weight nor a lowest weight vector. Equip
M
with a good filtration. Then
\AV(M)
is a union of two lines. Another difficulty is that the condition
\codim_{\overline{\mathbb{O}}}(\partial\mathbb{O})\geqslant 4
is essential to show that non-isomorphic HC modules correspond to non-isomorphic twisted local systems in the proof of
[
25
, Theorem 1.3.1]
.

1.2.3.
Springer resolutions and Beilinson-Bernstein localization

The geometric data needed in the unsolved codimension
2
case are therefore much more complicated than a twisted local system on an open
K
-orbit in
\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p}
. Assume that
\overline{\mathbb{O}}
admits a symplectic resolution given by the Springer resolution
\pi\colon T^{*}(G/P)\to\overline{\mathbb{O}}
, where
P\subset G
is a parabolic subgroup. This holds for the regular nilpotent orbit
\mathbb{O}_{\reg}
in every type, and the Springer resolution is given by
T^{*}(G/B)\to\mathcal{N}=\overline{\mathbb{O}}_{\reg}
, where
B\subset G
is a Borel subgroup and
\mathcal{N}
denotes the nilpotent cone. It also holds for every nilpotent orbit in type
A
. For other orbits in other types, further analysis is required (cf.
[
12
, Sections 3.3–3.4]
). We instead consider
K
-equivariant
\mathcal{D}
-modules on the flag variety
G/P
, whose global sections are HC
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-modules by the Beilinson-Bernstein localization theorem
[
2
]
. It is expected that the
\mathcal{D}
-modules are more manageable and exhibit refined geometric properties since they live on the smooth variety
G/P
.
Recall that the associated varieties of HC
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-modules annihilated by
\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbb{O})
are contained in
\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p}
. Thus, their Beilinson-Bernstein localizations have singular supports contained in
\pi^{-1}(\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p})
.

To proceed further with the classification in the codimension
2
case, it is necessary to understand the structure of the variety
S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p}
as well as its preimage
\pi^{-1}(S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p})
under the Springer resolution, where
S
is the Slodowy slice to a point in the codimension
2
orbit
\mathbb{O}^{\prime}
, as defined in
eq.

1.1
. As we explained at the end of
Section

1.2.1
,
S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}
is isomorphic to a Kleinian singularity
X
, and
S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p}\simeq X^{\theta}
is the fixed point locus. The restriction of the Springer resolution
\pi\colon T^{*}(G/P)\to\overline{\mathbb{O}}
to
S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}\simeq X
gives rise to the minimal resolution of the Kleinian singularity
X
. We have
\pi^{-1}(S\cap\overline{\mathbb{O}}\cap\mathfrak{p})\simeq\pi^{-1}(X^{\theta})
, where the preimage of the fixed point locus shows up.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Reminder on Kleinian singularities

We recall some basic facts about Kleinian singularities, following
[
5
, §1,2]
and
[
38
, §5]
. Let
\Gamma\subset\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
denote a finite subgroup. Up to conjugacy, there are five classes of such groups. We list the conjugacy classes below, where
\epsilon_{n}
denotes a primitive
n
-th root of unity for
n=5,8,10
.

(
A_{n}
) The cyclic group of order
n+1
, i.e.,
\big\{\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&0\\
0&\epsilon^{-1}\end{pmatrix}|\epsilon^{n+1}=1,\ n\geqslant 1\big\}
.

(
D_{n}
) The binary dihedral group of order
4(n-2)
, i.e.,
\big\{\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&0\\
0&\epsilon^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&\epsilon\\
-\epsilon^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}|\epsilon^{2(n-2)}=1,\ n\geqslant 4\big\}
.

(
E_{6}
) The binary tetrahedral group of order
24
.

It is generated by
\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\ \dfrac{1}{1-i}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\
1&-i\end{pmatrix}

(
E_{7}
) The binary octahedral group of order
48
.

It is generated by
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{8}&0\\
0&\epsilon_{8}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\ \dfrac{1}{1-i}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\
1&-i\end{pmatrix}
,

(
E_{8}
) The binary icosahedral group of order
120
.

It is generated by
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{10}&0\\
0&\epsilon_{10}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\ \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{5}-\epsilon_{5}^{4}&\epsilon_{5}^{2}-\epsilon_{5}^{3}\\
\epsilon_{5}^{2}-\epsilon_{5}^{3}&-\epsilon_{5}+\epsilon_{5}^{4}.\end{pmatrix}
.

The
Kleinian singularity
attached to a finite subgroup
\Gamma
is the quotient singularity

X:=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma=\Spec\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}.

Klein showed that
X
can be viewed as a hypersurface in
\mathbb{C}^{3}

X\simeq\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}|F(x,y,z)=0\},

with an isolated singularity at
0
(
[
20
]
). Here
F
denotes the relation among the three fundamental invariants
x,y,z
in
\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
. We list the most commonly used forms of these relations below.

(
A_{n}
)
x_{1}y_{1}+z_{1}^{n+1}=0
with
n\geqslant 1
.

(
D_{n}
)
x_{1}^{n-1}+x_{1}y_{1}^{2}+z_{1}^{2}=0
with
n\geqslant 4
.

(
E_{6}
)
x_{1}^{4}+y_{1}^{3}+z_{1}^{2}=0
.

(
E_{7}
)
x_{1}^{3}y_{1}+y_{1}^{3}+z_{1}^{2}=0
.

(
E_{8}
)
x_{1}^{5}+y_{1}^{3}+z_{1}^{2}=0
.

We use the subscripts in
x_{1},y_{1},z_{1}
because
x,y,z
are reserved for different choices of generators later in the paper (cf.
Proposition

2.3
). One can observe that the above list of finite subgroups of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is in bijection with Dynkin diagrams of types
ADE
. There are two ways to attach a simply-laced Dynkin diagram to a Kleinian singularity
X
. McKay observed a representation-theoretic way, called the McKay correspondence, in 1979
[
28
]
. Let
\Irr(\Gamma):=\{V_{0},\cdots,V_{n}\}
denote the set of irreducible representations of
\Gamma
and assume that
V_{0}
is the trivial representation. Set

a_{ij}:=\dim\Hom_{\Gamma}(V_{i}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2},V_{j}),

where
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is the tautological representation of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Then we have

(2.1)

\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{ij}\dim V_{j}=\dim(V_{i}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2})=2\dim V_{i}.

Moreover, it is easy to check that
a_{ij}=a_{ji}
because
\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{*}
as
\Gamma
-representations. Consider a graph with vertices
0,\cdots,n
and the number of unoriented edges between
i
and
j
equal to
a_{ij}
. This is called the
McKay graph
of
\Gamma
, which is exactly the extended Dynkin diagram in the corresponding type with the trivial representation
V_{0}
corresponding to the extended vertex
0
.

The other identification is algebro-geometric. Recall that by a resolution of
X
, one means a smooth variety
\widetilde{X}
equipped with a projective birational morphism to
X
. Since we are in dimension
2
, there is a
minimal
resolution
\pi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
; the minimality condition means that any other resolution factors through
\widetilde{X}
. The minimal resolution
\pi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
was essentially studied by Du Val
[
10
]
. For a Kleinian singularity
X:=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
, the reduced fiber over the isolated singularity
0
under the minimal resolution
\pi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
is a connected union of projective lines

\pi^{-1}(0)_{\red}=C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{n},\ C_{i}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{1},

with pairwise transversal intersection whose dual graph is the Dynkin diagram labeling
\Gamma
. More precisely, if
C_{i}\cap C_{j}\neq\emptyset
, then it is a single point, and each
C_{i}
has self-intersection number
\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}2
. Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the Cartan matrix of the corresponding type
ADE
. Then the intersection matrix
(C_{i}\cdot C_{j})
equals
\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}\mathcal{C}
. The intersection pairing will be defined rigorously in
Section

2.2
.

Remark 2.1
.

The scheme-theoretic exceptional fiber
\pi^{-1}(0)
is not reduced except in type
A
. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a simple Lie algebra of types
ADE
, and
\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n}\}
be the set of simple roots. There is a unique maximal root
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\alpha_{i}
in the adjoint representation. Then
\pi^{-1}(0)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}C_{i}
as a divisor
[
1
, Theorem 4]
. For example, in type
A_{n}
, we have
\delta_{i}=1
for all
i
, so
\pi^{-1}(0)
is generically reduced. (Actually,
\pi^{-1}(0)
is reduced in type
A_{n}
, which can be seen from iterated blow-ups.) In type
D_{4}
, we have
\delta_{i}=1,i\neq 2
and
\delta_{2}=2
, so
\pi^{-1}(0)
is not reduced.

2.2.
Intersection pairing

We define an intersection pairing on
\widetilde{X}
rigorously in this section. The main purpose of the discussion is to pave the way for the proof of
Proposition

4.17
. Note that the variety
\widetilde{X}
is not projective, so it is not possible to define a pairing with good properties on the entire group of Cartier divisors on
\widetilde{X}
. Instead, we define the pairing on a smaller subset of divisors on
\widetilde{X}
(cf.
Proposition

2.2
). We consider a slightly more general situation, following the exposition in
[
8
, §2.1]
. Let
S
be a smooth surface, and
Z
be an effective Cartier divisor on
S
with support projective over
\mathbb{C}
. We further assume that each irreducible component in the support of
Z
is smooth. Though we will only care about the situation
S=\widetilde{X}
and
Z=\pi^{-1}(0)
, it is easier to write down the results in a more general setting.

Let
\Div(S)
be the group of Cartier divisors on
S
, which coincides with the group of Weil divisors on
S
because
S
is smooth. Let
\Div_{Z}(S)
denote the subgroup of
\Div(S)
that consists of divisors with support contained in the support of
Z
. We identify effective divisors on
S
with closed, not necessarily reduced, subschemes of
S
defined by the ideal sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{S}(-D)
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a coherent sheaf on the projective scheme
Z
. We can define the Euler characteristic of
\mathcal{F}
by
\hat{\chi}(\mathcal{F}):=\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{i}(Z,\mathcal{F})
(cf.
[
16
, Exercise III.5.1]
). Let
C
be an effective divisor in
\Div_{Z}(S)
. For any invertible sheaf
\mathcal{L}
on
C
, we set

(2.2)

\deg(\mathcal{L}):=\hat{\chi}(\mathcal{L})-\hat{\chi}(\mathcal{O}_{C}).

For any divisor
D
on
S
, we set

(2.3)

C\cdot D:=\deg\mathcal{O}_{S}(D)\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\mathcal{O}_{C}.

Let
P=\sum_{i}n_{i}P_{i}
be a Weil divisor on
C
. Define
\deg P:=\sum_{i}n_{i}
as the degree of the divisor
P
. If
C
is irreducible, then
C
is a smooth projective curve by our assumption. By the Riemann-Roch Theorem
[
16
, Theorem IV.1.3]
, we have

\deg(\mathcal{O}_{C}(P))=\deg P.

Let
f
be a rational function on
S
. We denote by
\ddiv(f)
its corresponding divisor. Any divisor which is equal to the divisor of a function is called a
principal divisor
.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
13
, Example 2.4.9]

The pairing

(2.4)

\Div_{Z}(S)\times\Div(S)\to\mathbb{Z},\ (C,D)\mapsto C\cdot D

satisfies the following properties:

(1)

If
C
and
D
are smooth curves that meet transversely, then
C\cdot D=\#(C\cap D)
.

(2)

If
C,D\in\Div_{Z}(S)
, then
C\cdot D=D\cdot C
.

(3)

C\cdot(D_{1}+D_{2})=C\cdot D_{1}+C\cdot D_{2}
.

(4)

If
D
is a principal divisor, then
C\cdot D=0
.

We apply
Proposition

2.2
to the situation
S=\widetilde{X},\ Z=\pi^{-1}(0)
. The properties in
Proposition

2.2
will be useful in the proof of
Proposition

4.17
.

2.3.
Poisson brackets on Kleinian singularities

Consider the algebra
\mathbb{C}[u,v]
, with grading given by the degree of the polynomial function in variables
u,v
. There is a standard Poisson bracket of degree
(\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}2)
on
\mathbb{C}[u,v]
.

(2.5)

\{f_{1},f_{2}\}=\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial u}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial v}-\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial v}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial u}.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and consider the Kleinian singularity
X:=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
. Since the finite subgroup
\Gamma
preserves the Poisson bracket
eq.

2.5
, the algebra of invariant functions
\mathbb{C}[X]=\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
is a graded Poisson subalgebra of
\mathbb{C}[u,v]
. We use
\deg f
to denote the degree of a homogeneous element
f\in\mathbb{C}[X]
and
\mathbb{C}[X]_{d}
to denote the degree-
d
component of
\mathbb{C}[X]
.

Next, we write down the fundamental invariants in
\mathbb{C}[X]=\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
, and calculate the Poisson brackets among the generators. We take the fundamental invariants of
\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
to be of the following form, adapted from
[
8
, §1.3]
and better aligned with the fundamental invariants obtained through Nakajima quiver varieties in
Section

4.2
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\SL_{2}(C)
be a finite subgroup. The subalgebra
\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
is generated by the following polynomials.

(A_{n},\ n\geqslant 1)

x=u^{n+1}
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ y=v^{n+1}
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ z=uv
,

with relation
xy=z^{n+1}
.

(D_{n},\ n\geqslant 4\ \even)

x=u^{2}v^{2}
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ y=-\frac{1}{4}(u^{2n-4}-2u^{n-2}v^{n-2}+v^{2n-4})

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ z=\frac{1}{4}uv(u^{2n-4}-v^{2n-4})
,

with relation
xy(y-x^{\frac{n-2}{2}})=z^{2}
.

(D_{n},\ n\geqslant 5\ \odd)

x=u^{2}v^{2}
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ y=\frac{1}{4}(u^{2n-4}-v^{2n-4})

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ z=-\frac{1}{4}uv(u^{2n-4}-2u^{n-2}v^{n-2}+v^{2n-4})
,

with relation
xy^{2}=z(z-x^{\frac{n-1}{2}})
.

(E_{6})

x=108\sqrt{3}(1+i)uv(u^{4}-v^{4})
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ y=-108(u^{8}+14u^{4}v^{4}+v^{8})
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ z=648(\sqrt{3}(u^{12}-33u^{8}v^{4}-33u^{4}v^{8}+v^{12})-12iu^{2}v^{2}(u^{4}-v^{4})^{2})
,

with relation
z^{2}+zx^{2}+y^{3}=0
.

(E_{7})

x=-2^{2}3(u^{8}+14u^{4}v^{4}+v^{8})
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ y=2^{4}3^{3}(u^{5}v-uv^{5})^{2}
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ z=2^{5}3^{3}uv(u^{8}-v^{8})(u^{8}-34u^{4}v^{4}-v^{8})
,

with relation
x^{3}y+y^{3}+z^{2}=0
.

(E_{8})

x=-3^{3}uv(u^{10}+11u^{5}v^{5}-v^{10})
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ y=-\frac{3^{4}}{2^{2}}(u^{20}+v^{20}-228(u^{15}v^{5}-u^{5}v^{15})+494u^{10}v^{10})
,

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ z=\frac{3^{6}}{2^{3}}(u^{30}+v^{30}+522(u^{25}v^{5}-u^{5}v^{25})-10005(u^{20}v^{10}+u^{10}v^{20}))
,

with relation
x^{5}+y^{3}+z^{2}=0
.

It is easy to see that the relations among the fundamental invariants

listed in

2.1
up to a change of coordinates. For example, in type

D_{n}

. In type

x_{1}=\frac{\epsilon_{8}x}{\sqrt{2}},\ y_{1}=y,\ z_{1}=z+\frac{x^{2}}{2}
. The Poisson brackets among the fundamental invariants can be calculated either by hand or by software such as Mathematica. We summarize the results in the following proposition.

The Poisson brackets among the fundamental invariants in

3.
Anti-Poisson involutions and their fixed point loci

We classify the conjugacy classes of anti-Poisson involutions of a Kleinian singularity and compute their fixed point loci in this section. We use
X=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
to denote a Kleinian singularity throughout. Recall from
Section

2.3
that
\mathbb{C}[X]=\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
is a graded Poisson algebra with grading given by the degree of the invariant polynomials in variables
u,v
. The fundamental invariants of
\mathbb{C}[X]
are listed in
Proposition

2.3
, and the Poisson brackets are given in
Proposition

2.4
.

3.1.
Anti-Poisson involutions

Definition 3.1
.

By an
anti-Poisson involution
on
X
, we mean a graded algebra involution
\theta\colon\mathbb{C}[X]\to\mathbb{C}[X]
such that

(3.1)

\theta(\{f_{1},f_{2}\})=-\{\theta(f_{1}),\theta(f_{2})\},\ \forall\ f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{C}[X].

Example 3.2
.

Consider the Kleinian singularity of type
A_{1}\colon X=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
with
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\simeq\{\pm I_{2}\}
. We have
\mathbb{C}[X]=\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}=\mathbb{C}[x=u^{2},y=v^{2},z=uv]\simeq\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]/(xy-z^{2})
. The brackets among the generators are

\{x,y\}=4z,\ \{x,z\}=2x,\ \{y,z\}=-2y.

The graded automorphism
x\mapsto y,\ y\mapsto x,\ z\mapsto z
is an anti-Poisson involution.

Similar to the anti-Poisson involution on Kleinian singularities (or any Poisson algebra), we can define the
anti-symplectic involution
on a smooth symplectic variety
(N,\omega)
, which is an involution
\eta\colon N\to N
such that
\eta^{*}\omega=-\omega
. The following proposition will be very useful in
Section

4.3
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(N,\omega)
be a smooth symplectic variety with an anti-symplectic involution
\eta
. Then the scheme-theoretic fixed point locus
N^{\eta}
is either empty or a smooth Lagrangian subvariety.

Proof.

The smoothness of the scheme-theoretic fixed point locus
N^{\eta}
is a corollary of Luna’s slice theorem (cf.
[
9
, Proposition 5.8]
). The fact that
N^{\eta}
is a Lagrangian subvariety is an easy exercise in linear algebra, which we leave to the reader.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

Let
X=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
be a Kleinian singularity with anti-Poisson involution
\theta
. The
scheme-theoretic fixed point locus
of
X
under
\theta
is the scheme

(3.2)

X^{\theta}=\Spec\mathbb{C}[X]/J,

where
J=(\theta(f)-f|f\in\mathbb{C}[X])
.

Remark 3.5
.

The scheme-theoretic fixed point locus
X^{\theta}
turns out to be reduced, as follows from a case-by-case calculation in
Corollaries

3.13
,
3.16
,
3.19
,
3.22
,
3.25
and
3.28
.

We have
0\in X^{\theta}
because
\theta
is a graded automorphism. Similar to
Proposition

3.3
, one can show that the fixed point locus
X^{\theta}
is either a singular Lagrangian subvariety (one-dimensional) or consists only of the singular point. The latter case only appears in the Type III anti-Poisson involutions for the Kleinian singularity of type
A_{n}
when
n
is odd (cf.
Corollary

3.13
).

If two anti-Poisson involutions
\theta_{i},\ i=1,2
, are conjugate to each other by a graded Poisson automorphism
\sigma
, then
\sigma
induces an isomorphism between the fixed point loci
X^{\theta_{1}}\xrightarrow{\sim}X^{\theta_{2}}
. We want to classify anti-Poisson involutions on
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
up to conjugation by graded Poisson automorphisms. It turns out that the conjugacy classes form a finite set; this is proved in
Corollary

3.8
.

3.2.
Normalizers and diagram automorphisms

In this section, we discuss the action of the normalizer
N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
on the Kleinian singularity
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
and its induced action on the McKay graph associated to the Kleinian singularity. Recall that
\mathbb{C}[X]=\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
is a graded subalgebra of
\mathbb{C}[u,v]
. The grading induces
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-actions on both
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
X=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
, and the quotient morphism
q\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\to X
is
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-equivariant. Let
X^{0}:=X\setminus\{0\}
denote the smooth locus of
X
and
q^{0}\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\to X^{0}
denote the restriction of the quotient morphism. Then
q^{0}
is an (analytic) universal covering map. Let us denote the group of graded automorphisms of the Kleinian singularity by
\Aut_{\gr}(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)
. Given an element
g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
, we obtain an element in
\Aut_{\gr}(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)
by

\displaystyle\theta_{g}\colon\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma},

\displaystyle f(-)

\displaystyle\mapsto g\cdot f:=f(g^{-1}-).

Clearly,
\Gamma
induces the identity automorphism of
\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

The map

(3.3)

N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)/\Gamma\xrightarrow{}\Aut_{\gr}(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma),\ \ g\mapsto\theta_{g},

is a group isomorphism.

Proof.

Note that
\Gamma
is a normal subgroup of
N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
. Hence
eq.

3.3
is a well-defined group homomorphism. We only need to prove that it is a bijection.

We first show that the map in
eq.

3.3
is injective. Let
g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
such that
g
acts trivially on
X=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
. Then
g
preserves the
\Gamma
-orbits of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Consider the linear subspaces

V_{\gamma}:=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{2}|gv=\gamma v\},\ \gamma\in\Gamma.

For each
v\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, there exists a
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
gv=\gamma v
. Therefore, we have

(3.4)

\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}V_{\gamma}=\mathbb{C}^{2}.

The left-hand side of
eq.

3.4
is a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, so one of them, say
V_{\gamma_{0}}
, must be the entire
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. It follows that
g=\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma
.

Next, we show that the map in
eq.

3.3
is surjective. Let
\theta
be a graded automorphism of
X
. Consider the restriction
\theta\colon X^{0}\to X^{0}
, and lift it to the universal covering space
\hat{\theta}\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, i.e., we have
\theta\circ q^{0}=q^{0}\circ\hat{\theta}
. The lift
\hat{\theta}
is still holomorphic (cf.
[
11
, Theorem 4.9]
). We claim that
\hat{\theta}
commutes with the
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
induced from that of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. It suffices to show that
\lambda\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}\lambda
, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, and we prove this pointwise. First, we have that

q(\lambda^{-1}\hat{\theta}(\lambda v))=\theta(q(v))=q(\hat{\theta}(v)),\ \forall\ v\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\},

because
\theta
and
q
are both
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-equivariant. Hence,
\lambda^{-1}\hat{\theta}(\lambda v)
and
\hat{\theta}(v)
lie in the same fiber
q^{-1}(q(\hat{\theta}(v)))
. Next, let
\lambda(t),\ 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
be a continuous path connecting
\lambda(0)=1
and
\lambda(1)=\lambda
in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Then consider the following continuous map

\displaystyle\phi\colon[0,1]

\displaystyle\rightarrow q^{-1}(q(\hat{\theta}(v)))

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\mapsto\lambda(t)^{-1}\hat{\theta}(\lambda(t)v).

The range is a discrete space, so
\phi
has to be constant. Thus, we obtain that
\lambda^{-1}\hat{\theta}(\lambda v)=\phi(1)=\phi(0)=\hat{\theta}(v)
. The final step is to extend the lifting
\hat{\theta}
to a holomorphic map from
\mathbb{C}^{2}
to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
by Hartogs’s Extension Theorem (cf.
[
17
, Theorem 2.3.2]
). The only possible choice is to send
0
to
0
because
\hat{\theta}\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
commutes with the
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action. Now we have a holomorphic automorphism
\hat{\theta}
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
that commutes with the
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action, which has to be linear and invertible by looking at its Taylor expansion. Thus,
\hat{\theta}
must come from an element
g\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
Note that
\hat{\theta}
maps
\Gamma
-orbits to
\Gamma
-orbits, hence so does
g
. By an argument similar to the one used for the injectivity of
eq.

3.3
, we obtain that
g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Let
g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
. By
eq.

2.5
, we obtain that

(3.5)

\{g\cdot f_{1},g\cdot f_{2}\}=\det(g)^{-1}g\cdot\{f_{1},f_{2}\}.

There are two kinds of graded automorphisms in
\Aut_{\gr}(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)
that are worth special attention. The first is graded Poisson automorphisms, which come from elements in
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
. The second is graded anti-Poisson automorphisms, which come from elements in
N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
with determinant
\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1
, which we denote by
N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{-}
.

Let us denote the set of anti-Poisson involutions on
X=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
by
\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)
and the conjugacy classes of anti-Poisson involutions by
\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)/\sim
. An element
g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
gives rise to an anti-Poisson involution if and only if
\det(g)=-1
and
g^{2}\in\Gamma
.
Proposition

3.6
tells us that there is a bijection between sets

(3.6)

\{g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{-}|g^{2}\in\Gamma\}/\Gamma\xrightarrow{\sim}\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma).

Considering the conjugation action of
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
on the left-hand side of
eq.

3.6
, i.e., the set of
\Gamma
-cosets, then we further obtain the following bijection between sets

(3.7)

\big(\{g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{-}|g^{2}\in\Gamma\}/\Gamma\big)\big/N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)\xrightarrow{\sim}\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)/\sim.

Corollary 3.8
.

There are finitely many anti-Poisson involutions on
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
up to conjugation by graded Poisson automorphisms.

Proof.

We prove the type
A
case and the non-type
A
cases separately. In type
A_{n},
with
n\geqslant 1
, we have

\Gamma=\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&0\\
0&\epsilon^{-1}\end{pmatrix}|\epsilon^{n+1}=1\Bigg\}

is the cyclic group. We can calculate that

N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)=\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&d\end{pmatrix}|ad\neq 0\Bigg\}\bigsqcup\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}0&c\\
b&0\end{pmatrix}|bc\neq 0\Bigg\}.

Following the bijection in
eq.

3.6
, we have

\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&-a^{-1}\\
\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&c\\
c^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\bigg|\ a^{2n+2}=1\Bigg\}\bigg/\Gamma\xrightarrow{\sim}\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma).

Note that

\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}0&c\\
c^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\bigg|\ c\neq 0\Bigg\}

forms a single orbit under the conjugation action of
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
. Taking

h=\begin{pmatrix}a^{\prime}&0\\
0&a^{\prime-1}\end{pmatrix}\in N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)

such that
a^{\prime 2}c=1
, we can calculate that

h\begin{pmatrix}0&c\\
c^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}h^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,
\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)/\sim
is a finite set.

For the non-type
A
cases, we first prove that
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
is a finite group. Note that
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
is an algebraic group. It suffices to show that the connected component
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{\circ}
is a singleton. This will follow from the following two observations.

(1)

The action of the connected group
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{\circ}
on the finite group
\Gamma
has to be trivial, so
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{\circ}
is contained in the centralizer
Z_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
.

(2)

The tautological representation
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is irreducible as a
\Gamma
-representation. According to Schur’s Lemma,
Z_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
consists of scalar matrices of determinant
1
.

Therefore,
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{\circ}
consists only of the identity matrix because it is connected. Next, observe that there is a bijection
N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{-}\xrightarrow{\sim}N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
by sending

g\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&i\end{pmatrix}\cdot g.

Thus
N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{-}
is also finite. It follows that the set of anti-Poisson automorphisms on
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
is finite. Hence
\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)
, the set of anti-Poisson involutions, is also finite. As a corollary, the set of conjugacy classes
\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)/\sim
is also finite.
∎

Moreover, each conjugacy class of anti-Poisson involutions has a representative of simple form that can be written explicitly in terms of the generators of
\mathbb{C}[X]=\mathbb{C}[u,v]^{\Gamma}
. We list all the conjugacy classes of anti-Poisson involutions on Kleinian singularities in
Propositions

3.10
,
3.11
,
3.14
,
3.17
,
3.20
,
3.23
and
3.26
. We also explain which element in
N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)^{-}
gives the corresponding anti-Poisson involution in the remark following each proposition.

We would also like to study how an anti-Poisson involution, or more generally, an element in the normalizer, acts on the McKay graph of
\Gamma
. Recall that
\Irr(\Gamma)=\{V_{0},\cdots,V_{n}\}
denotes the set of irreducible representations of
\Gamma
. Let
g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
and
V_{i}\in\Irr(\Gamma)
. We define
V_{i}^{g}
, the
twist
of the representation
V_{i}
under
g
, as follows. As a vector space
V_{i}^{g}=V_{i}
, but the action of
\Gamma
is different and is given by

(3.8)

\gamma\cdot v:=g\gamma g^{-1}(v),\ \gamma\in\Gamma,\ v\in V^{g}_{i}.

It is easy to see that
V^{g}_{i}
is also an irreducible
\Gamma
-representation. Moreover,
\forall g\in N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)
, we have

(3.9)

\mathbb{C}^{2}\to(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{g},\ \ v\mapsto gv

is a
\Gamma
-equivariant isomorphism. Therefore, if
V_{i}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\oplus_{j}V_{j}
as
\Gamma
-representations, then
V^{g}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\oplus_{j}V^{g}_{j}
as
\Gamma
-representations.

An automorphism of a graph is a permutation of the vertices that preserves adjacency. Hence, the twist
eq.

3.8
induces an automorphism of the McKay graph of
\Gamma
, an extended Dynkin diagram.
The twist of the trivial representation
V_{0}
is still the trivial representation, no matter what
g
is. Thus, we obtain an automorphism of the finite Dynkin diagram
\Delta_{\Gamma}
labeling
\Gamma
(removing the vertex
0
and its adjacent edges). Together with the identification given in
Proposition

3.6
, we obtain a group homomorphism

(3.10)

N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)/\Gamma\xrightarrow{\sim}\Aut_{\gr}(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)\to\Aut(\Delta_{\Gamma}).

Note that
\Aut(\Delta_{\Gamma})
is either trivial or
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, except in type
D_{4}
, where
\Aut(\Delta_{\Gamma})\simeq S_{3}
. Let

(3.11)

\Inv(\Delta_{\Gamma}):=\{\tau\in\Aut(\Delta_{\Gamma})|\tau^{2}=\id\}

denote the set of involutions in
\Delta_{\Gamma}
. Let
\Inv(\Delta_{\Gamma})/\sim
denote the set of conjugacy classes of
\Inv(\Delta_{\Gamma})
under the conjugation action of
\Aut(\Delta_{\Gamma})
.

Restricting
eq.

3.10
to the set of anti-Poisson involutions
\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)
induces a well-defined map between the sets of conjugacy classes

(3.12)

\phi_{\Gamma}\colon\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)/\sim\to\Inv(\Delta_{\Gamma})/\sim.

Remark 3.9

3.12

3.15

3.3.
Type
A_{n}

The Poisson algebra of interest is
\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]/(xy-z^{n+1})
. Recall from
Proposition

2.3
and
Proposition

2.4
that the degrees of the generators are
\deg x=\deg y=n+1,\ \deg z=2
, and the Poisson brackets among them are given by

\{x,y\}=(n+1)^{2}z^{n},\ \{x,z\}=(n+1)x,\ \{y,z\}=-(n+1)y.

Proposition 3.10
.

The Kleinian singularity of Type
A_{n}
, with odd
n>1
, has three conjugacy classes of anti-Poisson involutions.

I.

\theta(x)=y,\ \theta(y)=x,\ \theta(z)=z
.

II.

\theta(x)=x,\ \theta(y)=y,\ \theta(z)=-z
.

III.

\theta(x)=-x,\ \theta(y)=-y,\ \theta(z)=-z
.

When
n=1
, the Poisson automorphism
x\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(x+y)-z,\ y\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(x+y)+z,\ z\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(x-y)
conjugates the Type
II
anti-Poisson involution to Type
I
, so there are two conjugacy classes.

Proof.

When
n=1
, we have
\Gamma=\{\pm I_{2}\}
and
N_{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)=\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. An element
g\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
gives rise to an anti-Poisson involution if and only if
\det(g)=-1
and
g^{2}=\pm I_{2}
by
Proposition

3.6
and
Remark

3.7
. It follows that
g
is diagonalizable, and its eigenvalues lie in
\{1,-1,i,-i\}
with the requirement that the product of the two eigenvalues is
-1
. Then up to multiplication by
\Gamma
and conjugation by
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, the element
g
is either
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

or
\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&i\end{pmatrix}
, which correspond to Type
I
and Type
III
anti-Poisson involutions respectively. It is obvious that they are not conjugate to each other.

When
n>1
, it is easy to verify that the involutions
\textup{I}-\textup{III}
are anti-Poisson using the Poisson brackets given in
Proposition

2.4
. Conversely, we show that any anti-Poisson involution can be conjugated to one of Types
\textup{I}-\textup{III}
. First, notice that we have
\theta(z)=\pm z
because
\theta
is a graded involution, and we have
\mathbb{C}[X]_{2}=\mathbb{C}z
. Consider the operator defined by
\{z,\cdot\}
on
\mathbb{C}[X]
. It is diagonalizable with integral eigenvalues, therefore produces a new grading on
\mathbb{C}[X]
, which we denote by
\deg_{z}:=\{z,\cdot\}
. Under this new grading, we have
\deg_{z}(x)=-(n+1),\ \deg_{z}(y)=n+1,\ \deg_{z}(z)=0
. When
\theta(z)=z
, it follows from

(3.13)

\{z,\theta(f)\}=\{\theta(z),\theta(f)\}=-\theta(\{z,f\}),\ \forall f\in\mathbb{C}[X],

that
\theta
reverses the new grading
\deg_{z}
. Thus we must have

\theta(x)=ay,\ \theta(y)=bx,\ a,b\in\mathbb{C}.

Moreover, using that
\theta^{2}(x)=x,\ \theta^{2}(y)=y
, we get
ab=1
. It is easy to verify that
\theta:x\mapsto ay,\ y\mapsto a^{-1}x,\ z\mapsto z
is an anti-Poisson involution on
\mathbb{C}[X]
and its conjugate
\sigma\theta\sigma^{-1}
is the Type
I
anti-involution, where
\sigma
is the Poisson automorphism
x\mapsto a^{\prime}x,\ y\mapsto a^{\prime-1}y,\ z\mapsto z
such that
a^{\prime 2}=a
.

When
\theta(z)=-z
, similar to
eq.

3.13
, we can show that
\theta
preserves the new grading
\deg_{z}
. Thus we must have

(3.14)

\theta(x)=ax,\ \theta(y)=by,\ a,b\in\mathbb{C}.

Moreover, using that
\theta^{2}(x)=x,\ \theta^{2}(y)=y
, we get
a^{2}=b^{2}=1
. Plugging
eq.

3.14
into

\theta(\{x,y\})=-\{\theta(x),\theta(y)\},

we get
ab=1
. The two solutions
a=b=1
and
a=b=-1
correspond to Type
II
and Type
III
anti-Poisson involutions respectively.

Finally, we show that Types
\textup{I}-\textup{III}
anti-Poisson involutions are not conjugate to each other. Any graded Poisson automorphism has to send
z
to a scalar multiple of
z
because
\mathbb{C}[X]_{2}=\mathbb{C}z
, so it cannot conjugate an anti-Poisson involution sending
z
to
-z
(Types
\textup{II},\ \textup{III}
) to those sending
z
to
z
(Type
I
). Furthermore, the fixed point locus of Type
III
anti-Poisson involution is the singular point, while the fixed point locus of Type
II
anti-Poisson clearly contains more than one point. Hence, Types
II
and
III
anti-Poisson involutions are not conjugate to each other, either.
∎

.

The Kleinian singularity of Type

I.

\theta(x)=x,\ \theta(y)=y,\ \theta(z)=-z

\theta(x)=x,\ \theta(y)=x^{\frac{n-2}{2}}-y,\ \theta(z)=z

Proof.

When
n=4
, we have
\Gamma=\big\{\pm I_{2},\ \pm\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{pmatrix},\ \pm\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},\ \pm\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\big\}

\det(g)=-1
and

by
Proposition

3.6
and
Remark

3.7
. Then up to multiplication by
\Gamma
and conjugation by
N_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(\Gamma)

is either
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{8}&0\\
0&\epsilon_{8}^{3}\end{pmatrix}

\epsilon_{8}
is an eighth primitive root of unity. Using the formulas of the generators

given in

2.3
, we see that the two matrices correspond to the Type
I
and the Type
II
anti-Poisson involutions respectively. It is obvious that they are not conjugate to each other.

When
n>4

\textup{I},\textup{II}
are anti-Poisson using the Poisson brackets given in
Proposition

2.4
.
Conversely, let
\theta
be an anti-Poisson involution of
\mathbb{C}[X]
. Note that
\deg z
is not an integral linear combination of
\deg x
and
\deg y
. Thus we may assume

\theta(x)=ax,\quad\theta(y)=by+b^{\prime}x^{\frac{n-2}{2}},\quad\theta(z)=cz,\quad

as
\theta
is graded. We have
\theta^{2}(x)=x,\ \theta^{2}(y)=y,\ \theta^{2}(z)=z
as
\theta
is an involution, which implies that
a^{2}=b^{2}=c^{2}=1
. From

0=\theta(xy(y-x^{\frac{n-2}{2}})-z^{2})=axy^{2}+ab(2b^{\prime}-a^{\frac{n-2}{2}})yx^{\frac{n}{2}}+ab^{\prime}(b^{\prime}-a^{\frac{n-2}{2}})x^{n-1}-z^{2},

we get

up to scaling. By plugging

into the latter two equations, we get

. Using

\{\theta(x),\theta(y)\}=-\theta(\{x,y\})

. This corresponds to the Type

to

(Type

0=\theta(z^{2}+zx^{2}+y^{3})=c^{2}z^{2}+c(2c^{\prime}+a^{2})zx^{2}+b^{3}y^{3}+c^{\prime}(c^{\prime}+a^{2})x^{4},

we get

using the relation
c^{2}=1
and the fact that

is the only relation of degree

in
\mathbb{C}[X]
up to scaling. Together with

, we can deduce that

. Using

\{\theta(x),\theta(z)\}=-\theta(\{x,z\})

as
\theta
is anti-Poisson, we can further determine that

. Setting
a=-1,\ c=1
, we get
c^{\prime}=0
from
c(2c^{\prime}+a^{2})=1
, which corresponds to the Type I anti-Poisson involution. Setting
a=1,\ c=-1
, we get
c^{\prime}=-1
from
c(2c^{\prime}+a^{2})=1
, which corresponds to the Type II anti-Poisson involution.

To see that Types
\textup{I},\textup{II}
anti-Poisson involutions are non-conjugate, note that any graded automorphism has to send

to a scalar multiple of

because
\mathbb{C}[X]_{6}=\mathbb{C}x
. Thus, an anti-involution sending

to

) cannot be conjugate to that sending

to
x
(Type
II
). ∎

4.1.
Representations of quivers

By a quiver
Q=(I,\Omega)
we mean an oriented graph with the vertex set
I=\{0,1,\cdots,n\}
and the edge set
\Omega
. For an arrow
a\colon i\to j\in\Omega
, we denote by
t(a)=i\in I
its initial vertex and by
h(a)=j\in I
its terminal vertex. The quivers we care about are extended Dynkin quivers of types
ADE
. Let
V=(V_{i})_{i\in I}
be a collection of finite-dimensional vector spaces corresponding to the vertices. The dimension of
V
is a row vector

\dim V=(\dim V_{0},\dim V_{1},\cdots,\dim V_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}_{\geqslant 0}.

For two collections of vector spaces
V
and
W
with
\delta=\dim V,\ w=\dim W
, we can consider the
framed representations
of the quiver
Q
.

R(Q,\delta,w):=\bigoplus_{a\in\Omega}\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{t(a)},V_{h(a)})\oplus\bigoplus_{i\in I}\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(W_{i},V_{i}).

Next, we consider the doubled quiver
\bar{Q}=(I,\bar{\Omega})
, where
\bar{\Omega}=\Omega\sqcup\Omega^{*}
, and
\Omega\xrightarrow{\sim}\Omega^{*},\ a\mapsto a^{*}
, is the reversal of the orientation. The space of framed representations of the doubled quiver
\bar{Q}
is

(4.1)

M(\delta,w):=\bigoplus_{a\in\Omega}\big(\Hom(V_{t(a)},V_{h(a)})\oplus\Hom(V_{h(a)},V_{t(a)})\big)\oplus\bigoplus_{i\in I}\Hom(W_{i},V_{i})\oplus\bigoplus_{i\in I}\Hom(V_{i},W_{i}).

An element of
M(\delta,w)
can be considered as a quadruple
(B,B^{*},l,k)
with

(4.2)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=(B_{a})_{a\in\Omega}\in\bigoplus_{a\in\Omega}\Hom(V_{t(a)},V_{h(a)}),\ \ B^{*}=(B_{a^{*}})_{a\in\Omega}\in\bigoplus_{a\in\Omega}\Hom(V_{h(a)},V_{t(a)}),

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=(l_{i})_{i\in I}\in\bigoplus_{i\in I}\Hom(W_{i},V_{i}),\qquad k=(k_{i})_{i\in I}\in\bigoplus_{i\in I}\Hom(V_{i},W_{i}).

We have
M(\delta,w)\simeq T^{*}R(Q,\delta,w)
by identifying
\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{i},V_{j})^{*}
with
\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{j},V_{i})
via the trace form
(A,B):=\tr(AB)
. Then the cotangent bundle
M(\delta,w)
is naturally endowed with a symplectic form

(4.3)

\omega((B_{a},B_{a^{*}},l,k),(B^{\prime}_{a},B^{\prime}_{a^{*}},l^{\prime},k^{\prime}))=\sum_{i\in I}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\Omega\\
h(a)=i\end{subarray}}\tr(B_{a}B^{\prime}_{a^{*}})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\Omega\\
t(a)=i\end{subarray}}\tr(B_{a^{*}}B^{\prime}_{a})+\tr(l_{i}k^{\prime}_{i}-k_{i}l^{\prime}_{i})\bigg),

where
(B_{a},B_{a^{*}},l,k),(B^{\prime}_{a},B^{\prime}_{a^{*}},l^{\prime},k^{\prime})
are regarded as tangent vectors.

Set
\GL(\delta):=\prod_{i\in I}\GL(V_{i})
and
\mathfrak{gl}(\delta):=\prod_{i\in I}\mathfrak{gl}(V_{i})
. Given
g=(g_{i})_{i\in I}\in\GL(\delta)
, it acts on the symplectic vector space
M(\delta,w)
via

(4.4)

g\cdot B_{a}=g_{h(a)}B_{a}g_{t(a)}^{-1},\ g\cdot B_{a^{*}}=g_{t(a)}B_{a^{*}}g_{h(a)}^{-1},\ g\cdot l_{i}=g_{i}l_{i},\ g\cdot k_{i}=k_{i}g_{i}^{-1}.

The
\GL(\delta)
-action on
M(\delta,w)
preserves the symplectic form in
eq.

4.3
, and is Hamiltonian. We can consider the corresponding moment map
\mu\colon M(\delta,w)\to\mathfrak{gl}(\delta)
, where
\mu=(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
, with

(4.5)

\mu_{i}(B,B^{*},l,k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\Omega,\\
h(a)=i\end{subarray}}B_{a}B_{a^{*}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\Omega,\\
t(a)=i\end{subarray}}B_{a^{*}}B_{a}+l_{i}k_{i}.

For a reference to
eq.

4.5
, one may consult
[
31
, §3.i]
. The equation
\mu=0
is also called the “ADHM equation”, referring to Atiyah-Drinfeld-Hitchin-Manin. A
character
is a group homomorphism
\chi\colon\GL(\delta)\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
. It is given by

\chi(g)=\prod_{i\in I}\det(g_{i})^{\chi_{i}},\ \text{where}\ g=(g_{i})_{i\in I},\ \chi=(\chi_{i})_{i\in I}\in\mathbb{Z}^{I}

because the only nontrivial characters of
\GL_{m}(\mathbb{C})
are integral powers of the determinant map and
\GL(\delta)
is just a product of
\GL_{m}(\mathbb{C})
’s. Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}\in\mathfrak{gl}(\delta)^{\GL(\delta)}
. Then the fiber
\mu^{-1}(\lambda)
is
\GL(\delta)
-invariant. Consider the
\chi
-semi-invariants
of weight
m
,

(4.6)

\mathbb{C}[\mu^{-1}(\lambda)]^{\GL(\delta),m\chi}:=\{f\in\mathbb{C}[\mu^{-1}(\lambda)]|f(g^{-1}(B,B^{*},l,k))=\chi(g)^{m}f((B,B^{*},l,k))\}.

The direct sum of
\mathbb{C}[\mu^{-1}(\lambda)]^{\GL(\delta),m\chi}
over
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
is a graded algebra. A
Nakajima quiver variety
is the GIT quotient

(4.7)

\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}_{\chi}(\delta,w):=\mu^{-1}(\lambda)/\!/^{\chi}\GL(\delta)=\Proj\bigoplus_{m\geqslant 0}\mathbb{C}[\mu^{-1}(\lambda)]^{\GL(\delta),m\chi}.

When
\chi
equals the trivial character
(0,\cdots,0)
, we have

\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}_{0}(\delta,w)=\mu^{-1}(\lambda)/\!/\GL(\delta)=\Spec\mathbb{C}[\mu^{-1}(\lambda)]^{\GL(\delta)}.

Moreover, there is a natural projective morphism

(4.8)

\pi\colon\mathcal{M}_{\chi}^{\lambda}(\delta,w)\to\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\lambda}(\delta,w).

In this paper, we mainly focus on the zero fiber of the moment map. We denote
\mathcal{M}_{\chi}(\delta,w):=\mathcal{M}^{0}_{\chi}(\delta,w)
for simplicity. We use
[B,B^{*},l,k]
to denote the
\GL(\delta)
-orbit of a point
(B,B^{*},l,k)
.

Remark 4.1
.

If the framing vector
w
is zero, we recover the usual representation space of the doubled quiver. The
\GL(\delta)
-action and the moment map
\mu
are obtained by setting
l=k=0
. We follow our previous notation to denote this space as
M(\delta,0)
.

Next, we discuss the
\chi
-semistable points. If
f
is a semi-invariant, then the set of nonvanishing locus
\mu^{-1}(0)_{f}
is a
\GL(\delta)
-invariant open subset of
\mu^{-1}(0)
. We define the
\chi
-semistable locus

(4.9)

\mu^{-1}(0)^{\chi-ss}:=\{u\in\mu^{-1}(0)|\exists f\in\mathbb{C}[\mu^{-1}(0)]^{\GL(\delta),m\chi}\ \text{for}\ m>0\ \text{such that}\ f(u)\neq 0\}.

We have
\mu^{-1}(0)^{\chi-ss}=\bigcup_{f}\mu^{-1}(0)_{f}
, where
f
runs through all the nonzero semi-invariants. King first interprets
\chi
-semistability of representations in terms of subrepresentations in
[
18
]
. Nakajima gives the following description of the semistable locus for the character
\chi=(-1,\cdots,-1)
.

Proposition 4.2

(
[
31
]
, Lemma 3.8)
.

Let
\chi=(-1,\cdots,-1)
. Then a point
(B,B^{*},l,k)
is
\chi
-semistable if and only if
\im(l)
generates the entire collection of vector spaces
V=(V_{i})_{i\in I}
under the action of
(B,B^{*})
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\chi=(-1,\cdots,-1)
. Then we have

(1)

\GL(\delta)
acts on
\mu^{-1}(0)^{\chi-ss}
freely, and each
\GL(\delta)
-orbit is closed.

(2)

We have
\mathcal{M}_{\chi}(\delta,w)\simeq\mu^{-1}(0)^{\chi-ss}/\!/\GL(\delta)
, and it is smooth.

(3)

The symplectic form
eq.

4.3
induces a symplectic form on
\mathcal{M}_{\chi}(\delta,w)
.

4.3.
Lifting anti-Poisson involutions to the minimal resolutions

Recall that
\pi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
denotes the minimal resolution of a Kleinian singularity. We would like to describe the preimage
\pi^{-1}(X^{\theta})
by answering the following questions.

(i)

What are the irreducible components?

(ii)

How do the irreducible components intersect each other?

(iii)

Is
\pi^{-1}(X^{\theta})
(generically) reduced?

Our main approach is through lifting the anti-Poisson involution
\theta
to the minimal resolution
\widetilde{X}
. By a
lift
of
\theta
, we mean an anti-symplectic involution

10.
Connections between anti-Poisson involutions and Lie algebra involutions

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a simple Lie algebra of types
ADE
and let
\mathcal{N}
denote the nilpotent cone of
\mathfrak{g}
. Note that
\mathcal{N}
is also the closure of the regular nilpotent orbit
\overline{\mathbb{O}}_{\reg}
. Let
S
be the Slodowy slice to a subregular nilpotent element
e\in\mathbb{O}_{\sreg}
. Then
S\cap\mathcal{N}\simeq X
, where
X
is the Kleinian singularity of the same type as
\mathfrak{g}
(cf.
Theorem

10.4
below).

Recall from
Section

1.2.1
that, for a Lie algebra involution
\sigma\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
, we can decompose
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
, where
\mathfrak{k}
is the
1
-eigenspace and
\mathfrak{p}
is the
(-1)
-eigenspace of
\sigma
. Set
\theta=-\sigma
; then
\theta
is an anti-Poisson involution of
\mathfrak{g}
. Under the further assumption that
e\in\mathfrak{p}
, we can choose a normal
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple such that the Slodowy slice
S
is
\theta
-stable
[
6
, Theorems 9.4.1, 9.5.1]
. It is clear that
\theta
preserves the nilpotent cone
\mathcal{N}
, and hence restricts to an anti-Poisson involution of
S\cap\mathcal{N}\simeq X
.

This raises the natural question of which anti-Poisson involutions of
X
arise as restrictions of Lie algebra involutions.
Proposition

10.1
below addresses this, and is the main result of this section.

Proposition 10.1
.

All anti-Poisson involutions of a Kleinian singularity
X
arise from Lie algebra involutions. Moreover, they are in bijection with quasi-split real forms of
\mathfrak{g}
, except in type
A_{n}
with
n
odd.

10.1.
Symmetries of the resolution diagram

Let
\theta
be an anti-Poisson involution of

, and let
\tau
denote the automorphism of the Dynkin diagram
\Delta_{\Gamma}
induced by
\theta
via

3.10

For an automorphism of the exceptional fiber

The action induced by

on the dual graph

of the exceptional fiber

coincides with the diagram automorphism
\tau
. Moreover, the following map

\phi_{\Gamma}:\API(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma)/\sim\to\Inv(\Delta_{\Gamma})/\sim,\ \ [\theta]\mapsto[\tilde{\theta}|_{\pi^{-1}(0)}]=[\tau],

with

Following the guidelines of

4.10
, the lifts
\tilde{\theta}
are constructed as involutions of quiver varieties in

5
,
6
,
7
,
8
and
9
, where the automorphism
\tau
of the Dynkin diagram is used in the construction. A case-by-case analysis, together with

3.12
,
3.15
,
3.18
,
3.21
,

and

\tilde{\theta}
on the dual graph of

agrees with the action of

on

.
∎

Remark 10.3
.

(1)

10.2.
Springer resolution and Dynkin curves

In this section, we review the Lie-theoretic approach to realize the minimal resolution
\pi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
, and provide a description of the irreducible components of
\pi^{-1}(0)
in terms of Lie-theoretic data, following
[

, Chapter III, §5–6]

Let
G
be the simply connected algebraic group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and let

be a Borel subgroup. Denote by
\mathfrak{b}=\Lie(B)
its Lie algebra and let
\mathfrak{n}=[\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{b}]
be the nilradical of

Next, consider the Springer resolution

(10.2)

\Phi:\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}=\{(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime},x)\in G/B\times\mathcal{N}|x\in[\mathfrak{b}^{\prime},\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}]\}\longrightarrow\mathcal{N},\qquad(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime},x)\longmapsto x.

Theorem 10.4
.

[
4
, Theorem]

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a simple Lie algebra of type
ADE

\mathcal{N}

is a Kleinian singularity of the same type as
\mathfrak{g}
, with the unique singular point at

. Moreover, the restriction of the Springer resolution in
eq.

10.2
to
\Phi^{-1}(S\cap\mathcal{N})\to S\cap\mathcal{N}
gives the minimal resolution of the Kleinian singularity
\pi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
.

Next, fix a maximal torus
T
contained in
B
and let

be the set of simple roots determined by the pair
(T,B)
. For each
\alpha_{i}
, let
P_{i}
be the minimal parabolic subgroup of

with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{b}+\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha_{i}}
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha_{i}}
is the root space associated to the negative simple root

. The fibers of the natural projection

Proposition

10.5
below will be useful in the proof of

.

Proposition 10.5
.

[

Assume

\alpha_{i}
and a line of type
\alpha_{j}
consists of at most one point in
G/B
.

Theorem

10.4
tells us that the Springer fiber
\Phi^{-1}(e)
over a subregular nilpotent element

is also the exceptional fiber

of the Kleinian singularity of the corresponding type.
Theorem

10.6
below gives a description of the irreducible components of

Theorem 10.6
.

[

We have
\pi^{-1}(0)_{\red}\simeq\Phi^{-1}(e)_{\red}=\cup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}C_{i}

10.4.
Nilpotent orbits in real forms

We first determine which Lie algebra involutions satisfy
\mathbb{O}_{\sreg}\cap\mathfrak{p}\neq\emptyset
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
be a real form of
\mathfrak{g}
, i.e., a real Lie algebra such that
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathfrak{g}
. A real form is determined by an antilinear involution
u:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
. For each such
u
, there exists a compact real form
u_{c}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
that is compatible with
u
, i.e.,
u\circ u_{c}=u_{c}\circ u
. Then the composition
\sigma=u\circ u_{c}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie algebra involution. Moreover, this construction gives a bijection between real forms
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
of
\mathfrak{g}
up to isomorphism and the Lie algebra involutions of
\mathfrak{g}
up to conjugation. For details, see
[
33
, §5.1]
.

Proposition 10.8
.

Let
\sigma
be a Lie algebra involution, and let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
be the corresponding real form.

(1)

\mathbb{O}_{\reg}\cap\mathfrak{p}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\mathbb{O}_{\reg}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\neq\emptyset
.

(2)

\mathbb{O}_{\sreg}\cap\mathfrak{p}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\mathbb{O}_{\sreg}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the Kostant-Sekiguchi bijection (cf.
[
6
, Theorem 9.5.1]
).
∎

Remark 10.9
.

Rothschild proved that
\mathbb{O}_{\reg}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
is quasi-split
[
35
, Proposition 5.1]
. Combining this with
(1)
of
Proposition

10.8
, we obtain that
\mathbb{O}_{\reg}\cap\mathfrak{p}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
is quasi-split.

Note that the subregular nilpotent element
e
is the unique singular point of
S\cap\mathcal{N}\simeq X
and
(S\cap\mathcal{N})\setminus\{e\}
consists only of regular nilpotent elements. Moreover,

is fixed by

as we choose

, hence

. That is to say,

is not a single point precisely when the corresponding real form is quasi-split. We will not use this equivalence directly, but it gives a conceptual explanation for the appearance of quasi-split real forms in

and for the exclusion of Type III in type
A_{n}
when

is odd; cf.

3.13
.

The classification of nilpotent orbits in real forms of complex semisimple Lie algebras can be found in

(1)

Type
