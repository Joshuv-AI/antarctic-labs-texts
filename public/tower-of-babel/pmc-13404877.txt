Aligned core-sheath structured fibers prevent intimal hyperplasia via modulate macrophage polarization and smooth muscle cell phenotype in vascular regeneration
Authors: Yunhuan Li, Yutong Liu, Kuihua Zhang, Nan Jiang, Keqing Hou, Liyu Chen, Fan Lin, Zhiyong Yan, Changlin Zhai, Tonghe Zhu, Anlin Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall-diameter vascular grafts face limited success due to thrombosis, intimal hyperplasia, and insufficient remodeling. Here, we developed a tubular graft composed of aligned core-sheath structured PLCL fibers with sodium tanshinone IIA sulfonate (STS)-loaded polyethylene oxide (PEO) cores. The graft exhibited uniform structures, appropriate porosity, and matched mechanical properties. In vitro, aligned fibers enhanced smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation and promoted a contractile phenotype (α-SMA, SM-MHC). Simultaneously, STS delivery modulated macrophage polarization, suppressing iNOS (M1-like) and enhancing CD206 (M2-like) expression. Co-culture assays revealed reciprocal regulation between VSMCs and macrophages, where VSMCs aligned and matured under macrophage-mediated anti-inflammatory cues, while contractile VSMCs reinforced M2-like polarization. These interactions effectively reduced intimal hyperplasia by preventing excessive smooth muscle cell proliferation and promoting a stable, anti-inflammatory microenvironment. In vivo rat implantation confirmed patency, endothelialization, and organized extracellular matrix resembling native vessels. These findings highlight the combined effect of graft design, macrophage and smooth muscle cell modulation, and cell–cell crosstalk in preventing intimal hyperplasia and driving vascular regeneration, offering a versatile strategy for functional small-diameter vascular grafts.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04606-9.Keywords: Aligned fibers, Core-sheath structure, Macrophage polarization, Smooth muscle cell phenotype, Vascular regeneration

Introduction

Small-diameter vascular grafts (≤ 4 mm) remain clinically challenging due to thrombosis, intimal hyperplasia, and insufficient remodeling, which significantly reduce long-term patency [1–6]. Conventional synthetic grafts provide mechanical support but lack the biological cues needed to regulate vascular healing. Thus, strategies that integrate mechanical stability with biological regulation are urgently needed.

Vascular regeneration is strongly influenced by the behavior of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and macrophages [7]. Phenotypic switching of VSMCs from contractile (smooth muscle myosin heavy chain (SM-MHC) and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)) to synthetic states contributes to intimal hyperplasia, while macrophages govern the inflammatory environment: pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages exacerbate injury, whereas M2-like macrophages promote repair and angiogenesis [8–12]. Maintaining or restoring the contractile phenotype of VSMCs, such as through cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) interactions, has been shown to mitigate pathological remodeling [1, 2]. Recent studies have highlighted the reciprocal regulation between vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and macrophages, particularly in the context of vascular remodeling [13]. Macrophage polarization has been shown to influence VSMC differentiation, with pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages promoting a synthetic VSMC phenotype characterized by increased proliferation and extracellular matrix (ECM) production [14]. This synthetic phenotype, in turn, contributes to the secretion of inflammatory cytokines [15], thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response and promoting vascular damage. On the other hand, M2-like macrophages, which are involved in tissue repair, facilitate the transition of VSMCs to a contractile phenotype, supporting vascular stability and regeneration [16, 17]. The role of synthetic VSMCs in driving M1-like macrophage activation underscores the critical involvement of VSMCs in modulating the inflammatory microenvironment, thereby playing a pivotal role in vascular disease progression and repair [18–21]. However, few graft designs explicitly leverage this crosstalk to enhance graft performance.

Electrospinning enables fine control of graft microstructure and drug release [22, 23]. Poly(L-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone) (PLCL) offers elasticity and biodegradability suitable for vascular grafts, while coaxial electrospinning allows incorporation of bioactive molecules [24]. Polyethylene oxide (PEO), a hydrophilic polymer that improves surface properties of PLCL [3] Sodium tanshinone IIA sulfonate (STS), a water-soluble derivative of tanshinone IIA, exhibits anti-inflammatory, anti-thrombotic, and anti-proliferative effects, making it an ideal candidate for modulating vascular healing (Fig. 1B) [25–28].

(A) Coaxial electrospinning setup for fabricating core–shell fibers with PLCL/PEO (STS); (B) Control of fiber orientation via collector speed; (C) Macroscopic view of vascular graft with uniform structure; (D) Aligned core–shell fibers guide cell alignment and support vascular regeneration

In this study, we fabricated aligned PLCL grafts with STS-loaded PEO cores using coaxial electrospinning (Fig. 1A). We systematically investigated their effects on VSMC phenotype, macrophage polarization, and their co-culture interactions, followed by in vivo implantation in rat abdominal aortas. Our findings demonstrate that the grafts effectively promote the differentiation of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) towards a contractile phenotype, thereby inhibiting the secretion of inflammatory cytokines by M1-like macrophages. This plays a crucial role in maintaining vascular stability and functionality. Furthermore, the grafts not only provide mechanical support but also create a pro-regenerative, anti-inflammatory microenvironment, accelerating vascular tissue formation, maintaining patency, and preventing intimal hyperplasia by modulating the reciprocal interaction between smooth muscle cells and macrophages (Fig. 1C and D).

Materials and methods

Materials

Poly (L-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone) (PLCL, 50:50, Mw ≈ 330,000 Da) was provided by Jinan Daigang (Jinan, China), and polyethylene oxide (PEO, Mw ≈ 100,000 Da) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, China). Sodium tanshinone IIA sulfonate (STS) was provided by Dalian Meilun Biotechnology (Dalian, China). All chemicals were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology, Shanghai, China, and all reagents used for cell culture were purchased from Gibco Life Technologies (Shanghai, China), unless stated otherwise. All cells were purchased from iCell (SAIBAIKANG), Shanghai, China.

Graft fabrication and characterization

Electrospun grafts were prepared using PLCL (10% w/v) as the shell material and PEO (6% w/v) solutions containing STS (1, 5, 10 mg/mL) as the core. The resulting samples are abbreviated as Random/@1/@5/@10 and Aligned/@1/@5/@10, where the “@” denotes the amount of STS loaded. A coaxial electrospinning system was employed, with optimized voltage (12 kV), flow rate (1 mL/h), and collection distance to obtain uniform fibers in either random or aligned alignment. The resulting grafts were dried under vacuum and sterilized before use. Fiber morphology was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the internal structure of the coaxial electrospinning fiber was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and fiber diameters were measured using ImageJ software. Porosity was scanned by Micro-CT (SkyScan-1272, Bruker, Germany). Mechanical properties were evaluated using a universal tensile tester (UTM2503, Shenzhen Sansi Zongheng). Rectangular strips (1 cm×4 cm) were moistened, measured, and subjected to uniaxial tensile testing at 20 mm/min until fracture.

Cell culture and seeding

Human umbilical artery smooth muscle cells (HUASMCs) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Macrophage cells (RAW264.7) were cultured under the same conditions. Prior to experiments, grafts were cut into discs (10 mm) and pre-soaked in culture medium. Cells were seeded onto grafts at a density of 1 × 104 cells per sample.

HUASMCs proliferation and phenotype assays

HUASMCs proliferation on grafts was quantified at days 1, 4, and 7 using the CCK-8 assay. For cell morphology evaluation, cells were fixed and stained Rhodamine-B and DAPI by fluorescence microscopy. Protein expression was further validated by western blotting (phenotype evaluation, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and smooth muscle myosin heavy chain (SM-MHC)).

Macrophage polarization analysis

RAW264.7 cells were cultured on grafts and quantified at days 1, 4, and 7 using the CCK-8 assay. For cell morphology evaluation, cells were fixed and stained Rhodamine-B and DAPI by fluorescence microscopy. Protein expression was further validated by Western blotting (nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, M1-like marker) and CD206 (M2-like marker)).

Co-culture experiments

To investigate reciprocal regulation, HUASMCs and RAW264.7 cells were co-cultured on grafts with direct contact. In each well, 10,000 HUASMCs and 4,000 macrophages were seeded. HUASMC proliferation was quantified on days 1, 4, and 7 using the CCK-8 assay. Cell morphology, including orientation, was assessed by fluorescence microscopy following Rhodamine-B and DAPI staining. Macrophage polarization markers were measured in parallel. Additionally, CCK-8 assays were performed to assess cell viability. Protein expression was further validated by Western blotting (α-SMA, SM-MHC, iNOS, and CD206). This experimental setup was designed to evaluate the bidirectional influence between HUASMCs and macrophages in the presence of grafts, providing insights into their dynamic interaction within a tissue-engineering context.

In vivo implantation

The animal experiments in this study were conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and complied with the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986, and associated guidelines, EU Directive 2010/63/EU, as well as the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978). All animal procedures were approved by the Jiaxing University institutional ethics committee and performed according to national guidelines. Male Sprague-Dawley rats (200–250 g) were anesthetized, and grafts (2 mm inner diameter, 10 mm length) were implanted into the abdominal aorta by end-to-end anastomosis (More information concerning the implant methods and the numbers implanted in Table. S2). Postoperative patency was monitored using Doppler ultrasound at 1, 2, 4, and 12 weeks. At harvest (12 weeks), grafts were fixed, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, Sirius Red, and Safranine-O to assess tissue morphology, collagen deposition and Calcification situation. Immunofluorescence staining for matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), CD31, α-SMA, SM-MHC, CD68, iNOS, and CD206 was performed to evaluate endothelialization, smooth muscle layer formation, and macrophage polarization, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise noted. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test (p < 0.05 considered significant).

Results and discussion

Macroscopic and microscopic morphology

The fabricated grafts exhibited uniform tubular structures with diameters of 2 mm and wall thicknesses ranging from 350 ~ 450 μm (Fig. 2A). SEM images showed dense fiber networks with clear alignment in the aligned grafts, while the random grafts displayed a disordered fiber structure. The micro-CT reconstructions revealed continuous, interconnected porous networks across the graft walls (Fig. 2C and D), ensuring a suitable environment for cell migration and nutrient diffusion. The TEM images of the Shell-core structure (Fig. 2B) show the distinct difference between the core and shell of the graft, where the shell provides structural support and the core allows for bioactives resever and continuous release. The suture strength (Fig. 2F) shows that, compared to the random structure, the aligned structure exhibits superior tensile strength across all components. Quantitative analysis of pore size and porosity (Fig. 2E, F, G, H and Table. S1) indicated that random grafts exhibited a larger average pore size and higher overall porosity, which may facilitate early cell ingress. In contrast, aligned grafts showed a smaller average pore size; however, their more uniform inter-fiber spacing and the presence of continuous, directionally connected inter-fiber channels (i.e., directional porosity) can still facilitate cell migration/infiltration and mass transport, while simultaneously improving mechanical stability, which may be advantageous for long-term implantation.

(A) Optics and SEM images of random and aligned fiber vascular grafts; (B) TEM images of shell-core structure vascular grafts. (C) and (D) Micro-CT reconstruction and SEM images of random fiber vascular grafts and aligned fiber vascular grafts structure; (E) Quantitative analysis of pore size; (F) Quantitative analysis of porosity from micro-CT data; (G) and (F) Quantitative analysis of open and closed pore porosity; (I) Suture strength of random- and aligned -fiber vascular grafts

These findings suggest that the grafts combine suitable porosity for cell migration with sufficient mechanical strength for implantation. Importantly, the aligned grafts demonstrated more uniform and organized fiber alignment, which supports the mechanical integrity and long-term functionality of the vascular grafts.

Smooth muscle cell proliferation and phenotype on fiber membranes

Confocal images (Fig. 3A and B) revealed distinct cellular organization on different fiber structures. On random fiber membranes, HUASMCs displayed a disordered distribution and irregular morphology, regardless of STS loading. In contrast, HUASMCs on aligned fiber membranes aligned along the fiber axis, exhibiting an elongated, spindle-like morphology, which is advantageous for forming an organized smooth muscle layer. The arrows in Fig. 3B show the alignment of cells along the fibers, indicating the effective guidance of cell morphology by the aligned grafts. In previous studies by our research group, it was demonstrated that an appropriate amount of STS exhibits good cell compatibility. Here, the high drug-loaded groups also show a clear directional growth trend compared to the low drug-loaded groups [29]. In both the random and aligned groups, cells proliferated over time. Although the proliferation rate of the aligned group slowed somewhat at high drug concentrations, overall, the proliferation rate in the aligned group was slightly higher than that in the random group. Finally, the experimental results also suggest that the aligned fiber structure promotes cell proliferation [4, 7, 30, 31]. Western blot analysis (Fig. 3E and F) confirmed the influence of graft type and STS concentration on the smooth muscle cell phenotype. On random grafts, the expression of α-SMA decreased with higher STS loading, while on aligned grafts, α-SMA expression increased in a dose-dependent manner with higher STS concentrations. Similarly, SM-MHC expression was more consistently maintained in aligned groups, indicating the supportive role of fiber alignment in promoting contractile differentiation. Quantitative analysis further supported these findings (Fig. 3G and H). While we observed a significant increase in α-SMA and SM-MHC expression on aligned grafts, we acknowledge that the ratio of SM-MHC to α-SMA is relatively low, suggesting that the smooth muscle cells may still be in a synthetic phenotype. This is consistent with the fact that, during early stages, smooth muscle cells often exhibit a greater secretion phenotype to support rapid proliferation, which can transition to a more contractile phenotype as the cells mature. Under the “aligned@10” condition, SM-MHC-positive cells were mainly observed in the outer layer, with limited expression in the interior (Fig. S5). These results suggest that the aligned@10 environment may partially promote smooth muscle cell differentiation toward a contractile phenotype. Further studies are needed to validate this effect and to assess whether prolonged culture can enhance complete smooth muscle cell maturation.

NS: No significant difference

In conclusion, these results highlight the unique effectiveness of aligned@10 grafts in facilitating smooth muscle cell differentiation toward a contractile phenotype. The circumferential arrangement of the aligned fibers, which mimics the physiological structure of smooth muscle cells, plays a key role in promoting their proliferation. The combination of fiber orientation and STS treatment accelerates the maturation of neotissue, enhances smooth muscle regeneration, and holds considerable potential for advancing vascular tissue engineering applications. However, as noted, further studies with extended time points are necessary to fully confirm the transition to a stable contractile phenotype in smooth muscle cells.

Macrophage polarization and growth on fiber membranes

Figure 4A and B show confocal microscopy images of RAW264.7 cells on random and aligned fiber membranes. In the high drug-loaded groups, with cells on the aligned grafts aligning along the fiber direction, cells exhibited more organized distribution, demonstrating directed growth. In contrast, cells on the random grafts showed more disordered distribution. Macrophages polarize toward pro-inflammatory M1-like or pro-regenerative M2-like phenotypes [8]; iNOS is a canonical M1-like marker and CD206 identifies M2-like macrophages [9, 10]. CCK-8 assays showed time-dependent growth of RAW264.7 on both graft types, but high STS doses attenuated proliferation (Fig. 4C and D). On random membranes, random@5 and random@10 significantly reduced on day-7 absorbance compared with random or random@1. A similar attenuation was observed on aligned membranes at aligned@10, indicating a dose-dependent growth inhibition at high drug levels.

(A) and (B) Confocal images of RAW264.7 on random fibers and aligned fibers; (C) and (D) CCK-8 assay showing macrophage proliferation on random fibers and aligned fibers; (E) and (F) Western blot analysis of CD206 (M2-like) and iNOS (M1-like) expression on random fibers and aligned fibers; (G) and (H) Quantification of CD206 and iNOS expression. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

Phenotypic analysis revealed that the effects of fiber architecture combined with STS loading were distinct. On random membranes, CD206 was relatively higher in drug-free and low-dose groups, while iNOS expression increased at high STS (Fig. 4E). In contrast, on aligned membranes, STS loading enhanced CD206 expression and reduced iNOS expression, suggesting that fiber alignment, in combination with STS, strongly promotes M2-like macrophage polarization. These findings indicate that fiber alignment, in addition to STS delivery, significantly influences macrophage polarization, shifting it toward an anti-inflammatory, pro-regenerative M2-like state (Fig. 4F). Quantification confirmed these trends: CD206 was significantly elevated, and iNOS was reduced on aligned membranes with STS (Fig. 4G and H).

Overall, these results suggest that fiber orientation, in combination with STS delivery, is associated with a shift toward an M2-like phenotype (increased CD206 and decreased iNOS), while excessive STS may partially suppress macrophage proliferation [32–34]. Notably, macrophage polarization in this study was inferred primarily from representative markers (iNOS and CD206), reflecting a phenotype tendency rather than a comprehensive profiling of macrophage states. Nevertheless, this phenotype-associated immunomodulatory trend may be beneficial for subsequent vascular remodeling and neo tissue formation.

Macrophage-smooth muscle cell interactions in co-culture

In the co-culture system, both HUASMCs and RAW264.7 cells exhibited sustained proliferation over time (Fig. 5D and E). HUASMCs on random grafts were disorganized, while those on aligned grafts preferentially aligned along the fiber axis (Fig. 5B and C). This alignment effect was more pronounced at higher drug concentrations, as shown by marker analysis in Fig. 5F, G. Together, fiber architecture and STS loading influenced marker expression in the co-culture system. We speculate that HUASMC phenotypic modulation and macrophage polarization may be interrelated, although the evidence remains correlative and does not confirm causal relationships. Western blot analysis revealed significantly higher expression of α-SMA and SM-MHC in the aligned@10 group compared to all random graft groups (Fig. 5J and K), indicating a clear shift of smooth muscle cells toward a contractile phenotype. This was accompanied by a reduction in synthetic smooth muscle cells and a decrease in the secretion of pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages [14–16, 18–20].

(B) and (C) Confocal images of HUASMCs and RAW264.7 co-cultured on random fibers and aligned fibers with different STS loadings; (D) and (E) CCK-8 assay showing proliferation of co-cultured cells on random fibers and aligned fibers; (F) and (G) Western blot analysis of CD206, iNOS, α-SMA, and SM-MHC expression in co-cultures on random fibers and aligned fibers. Quantification of CD206 (H), iNOS (I), α-SMA (J) and SM-MHC (K) expression. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

To assess macrophage polarization, we measured CD206 and iNOS expression (Fig. 5H and I). On random grafts, higher STS loading increased iNOS expression, indicating a pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophage phenotype. In contrast, on aligned grafts, higher STS loading enhanced CD206 expression without suppressing iNOS, suggesting that the combination of aligned grafts and STS loading induced a dual effect on both HUASMCs and RAW264.7 cells. We speculate that Macrophage polarization, to some extent, promotes the transition of smooth muscle cells to the contractile phenotype, while the contractile phenotype of smooth muscle cells further encourages M2 polarization of macrophages, thus creating a favorable regenerative microenvironment [13, 15, 17, 35]. The dual impact on smooth muscle cells and macrophages appears to support vascular remodeling and tissue formation, yet the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

While the increased iNOS expression observed in the early stages of the in vitro experiments suggests a pro-inflammatory response, we also observed that this early immune response might contribute to immune modulation. The enhanced expression of CD206 in the drug-loaded groups further supports the hypothesis that STS, in combination with fiber alignment, influences macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory, M2-like phenotype [36–38]. Our in vitro experiments primarily validated the early-stage effects, where SMCs support macrophage immune modulation with a synthetic phenotype. In the later stage (12 weeks), we aim to verify the long-term effects, focusing on the transition to a contractile phenotype, with M1-like cells down and M2-like cells up. As for the individual effects of STS as a drug and its drug release profile, our research group has previously conducted extensive studies in this area, all of which have contributed to deriving the conclusions mentioned above [29, 39, 40].

In vivo evaluation of vascular graft patency

Patency is a key determinant of vascular grafts success [41]. Long-term graft function can be compromised by intimal hyperplasia, which is primarily caused by the abnormal interactions between smooth muscle cells and macrophages. Excessive smooth muscle cell proliferation, combined with the pro-inflammatory polarization of macrophages, leads to excessive thickening of the intima, thereby impairing graft function over time. Additionally, platelet adhesion can contribute to thrombosis, further exacerbating vascular occlusion and graft failure. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 6A, Doppler ultrasound was employed to evaluate blood flow dynamics following graft implantation. Based on the previous study, we selected the 10 mg STS/10 mL-loaded random and aligned grafts, which exhibited the best overall performance, to compare with the drug-free grafts (Fig. 6B). As shown in Fig. 6C, all grafts maintained patency at weeks 1, 2, 4, and 12, with normal blood flow signals observed. Notably, at week 12, while the random, random@10, and aligned grafts exhibited signs of vascular dilation (indicated by white arrows), suggesting the potential formation of aneurysms, the aligned@10 graft showed no such expansion, and the implanted vessel maintained its normal structure and excellent flow characteristics. This observation highlights the superior hemodynamic stability of aligned@10 grafts, which did not show signs of distortion or obstruction, suggesting better long-term patency and vascular integrity compared to the other grafts.

(A) Schematic diagram of blood flow patency experiment; (B) Intraoperative images of PLCL-(PEO/STS) graft implantation in the rat abdominal aorta; (C) Doppler ultrasound assessment of grafts patency at 1, 2, 4 and 12 weeks after implantation

Overall, these results suggest that the combined use of fiber aligned and STS delivery is associated with more stable flow profiles and a lower tendency toward graft dilation at 12 weeks. Nevertheless, the current in vivo study is limited by the rat abdominal aorta model, a 12-week follow-up period, and the lack of a benchmark comparison with clinically used grafts (e.g., ePTFE). Therefore, in our subsequent work, we will perform studies with longer follow-up, larger sample sizes, and direct comparisons with clinically relevant benchmark grafts to further validate long-term durability and translational relevance [42].

Vascular remodeling and patency analysis

Figure 7A presents H&E-stained cross-sectional images of the different groups. The autologous blood vessel group shows typical vascular morphology, while the Random and Random@10 groups exhibit irregularities in vessel structure, with significant material expansion and ineffective vascular tissue formation. In contrast, the Aligned and Aligned@10 groups maintain stable vessel shapes, and the vascular tissue is well-formed, similar to the Autologous blood vessel group (H&E staining images for each sample are showed in supplemented, Fig. S4). Figure 7B and C show the quantitative analysis of lumen area and vessel area, respectively. In terms of vascular remodeling, the Random and Random@10 groups exhibited significant material expansion, failing to effectively reconstruct the vessel structure, while the Aligned and Aligned@10 groups demonstrated strong vascular remodeling ability, comparable to the autologous blood vessel group. This suggests that the stability of the material shape contributes to the formation of vascular tissue. Figure 7D shows the thrombus area for each group. In this analysis, the Random and Random@10 groups had larger thrombus areas, indicating poor patency, while the Aligned@10 group showed no thrombus formation, maintaining good patency similar to the Autologous blood vessel group. Figure 7E and Table. S3 present the patency rate statistics. It can be observed that during the first week, all groups showed good patency. However, by week 12, the patency rates of the Random and Random@10 groups dropped to only 20% and 40%, respectively, while the Aligned@10 group maintained a patency rate of 75%, indicating that the vascular stability of the Aligned@10 group was superior to the other groups. Figure 7F shows neointima thickness. In the in vivo experiment, we observed that the Aligned and Aligned@10 groups maintained a neointima thickness similar to that of the autologous blood vessel group, preserving the normal state of the intima, while the Random and Random@10 groups exhibited significantly increased neointima thickness, indicating abnormal thickening. This difference suggests that the Aligned and Aligned@10 groups, through the regulation of smooth muscle cells and macrophages, reduced intimal hyperplasia, thereby improving endothelial layer coverage and enhancing the blood flow within the vascular lumen [13].

Histological evaluation of tissue remodeling

Histological staining was performed to assess graft-guided tissue remodeling (Fig. 8A). As shown in Fig. 8B, H&E staining revealed that although the Random and Random@10 groups exhibited abundant cell infiltration, they did not form a fully intact lumen structure. Notably, the aligned@10 group displayed the most similar cellular distribution to native blood vessels, with no signs of inflammatory infiltration, indicating effective integration with the host tissue and maintaining patency. This suggests that aligned@10 grafts not only promote smooth muscle cell proliferation but also preserve the structural integrity of the blood vessel, successfully mimicking the architecture of native blood vessels. Masson staining showed the expected distribution of muscle fibers (stained red) predominantly in the medial layer, with collagen (blue) located in the adventitia in autologous blood vessels. The aligned@10 grafts replicated this pattern, demonstrating proper organization of smooth muscle and collagen. In contrast, the other graft groups exhibited collagen-rich middle layers with reduced muscle fiber content, indicating a less effective reconstruction of the vascular wall and restoration of normal blood vessel structure. Sirius Red staining further confirmed that aligned@10 grafts supported dense, continuous collagen deposition that closely resembled the collagen structure of native blood vessels. This highlights the strong extracellular matrix support provided by the aligned@10 grafts, which is critical for maintaining the structural and functional integrity of the blood vessel. Safranin-O staining showed no evidence of calcification in any of the groups, indicating that the grafts did not induce pathological calcification, which is a common complication in vascular tissue engineering.

(A) Schematic diagram of histological staining; (B) Histological analysis of autologous blood vessels and random or aligned -fiber vascular. (bars: 1 mm and 100 μm; asterisk: vascular lumen)

Taken together, these results demonstrate that aligned fiber grafts with STS delivery provide the most favorable extracellular matrix composition and organization, closely resembling the structure of native blood vessels. This approach holds significant potential for functional vascular regeneration, ensuring both the structural and cellular integrity necessary for long-term success in vascular tissue engineering.

Macrophage polarization and inflammatory modulation

To evaluate the changes in macrophage phenotypes following graft implantation, immunofluorescence staining was performed (Fig. 9A). In this experiment, iNOS (green) and CD68 (red) were used to label M1-like macrophages (Fig. 9B), while CD206 (red) and DAPI (blue) were used to label M2-like macrophages (Fig. 9C). In autologous blood vessels, iNOS (green) and CD68⁺ (red) macrophages were expressed at low levels. This pattern suggests a relatively low level of M1-like macrophage markers (CD68 + iNOS) [43]. In contrast, the Random grafts, exhibited significant CD68 + iNOS co-localization (green), indicating elevated M1-like macrophage marker expression and a more pro-inflammatory polarization profile. Consistent with the results observed in co-culture experiments, where synthetic smooth muscle cells partially promoted M1-like macrophage activation. Additionally, while CD206 staining was relatively weak, suggesting limited expression of M2-like phenotype markers [44, 45]. Notably, grafts containing STS exhibited a distinct trend. Specifically, in the Aligned@10 group, the CD68 + iNOS signal (green and red) was significantly reduced, while CD206 + DAPI co-localization (red) was enhanced, indicating a stronger shift toward M2-like macrophage polarization. These findings suggest that the combined effects of fiber alignment and STS loading promote macrophage polarization toward an M2-like phenotype. We will further validate these findings in future studies, including cytokine profiling and mechanistic investigations, to better understand the underlying pathways driving this shift.

(A) Schematic of macrophage staining; (B) Fluorescence microscopy analysis of iNOS-labeled M1-like macrophages; (C) Fluorescence microscopy analysis of CD206-labeled M2-like macrophages. (bars: 100 μm and 20 μm; asterisk: Vascular lumen)

Taken together, the immunofluorescence results indicate that aligned fibers in combination with STS delivery are associated with reduced M1-like marker signals and enhanced M2-like marker expression. This shift in phenotype is consistent with a favorable remodeling response. However, in the early-stage in vitro experiments, both iNOS and CD206 levels were relatively high in the aligned fiber group, reflecting the early inflammatory response associated with immune modulation. In contrast, after twelve weeks of in vivo implantation, a significant reduction in iNOS expression and a higher expression of CD206 in the aligned fiber group were observed. These results align with our expectations: the early in vitro experiments promoted immune modulation, while the later in vivo results contributed to anti-inflammatory polarization, highlighting the dynamic and time-dependent nature of macrophage polarization and its potential role in tissue remodeling.

Graft-mediated extracellular matrix remodeling and functional vessel formation

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9), endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells (SMCs) are key players in vascular remodeling. Here, we comparatively analyzed these parameters among different graft groups (Fig. 10A). As shown in Fig. 10B, three months after implantation, MMP-2 (green) levels in the Random and Random@10 groups were higher than those in the Aligned group, and MMP-2/MMP-9 appeared more diffusely and irregularly distributed. Excessive MMP-2 has been reported to adversely affect vascular remodeling and may be associated with apoptosis. Moreover, elevated MMP-2 and MMP-9 can degrade extracellular matrix components, potentially influencing SMC migration and differentiation and ultimately compromising vessel wall stability. This trend was consistent with CD31 and SM-MHC/α-SMA staining (Fig. 10C and D), where the Random-related groups showed less continuous endothelial coverage and a more disorganized SMC distribution compared with autologous vessels.

(A) Analysis of vascular remodeling factors and experimental illustration; (B) MMP-2/9 staining in graft groups and autologous vessels; (C) CD31 staining in graft groups and autologous vessels; (D) SM-MHC and α-SMA staining in graft groups and autologous vessels. (bar = 100 μm. Asterisk: Vascular lumen)

In contrast, the Aligned and Aligned@10 groups exhibited a more regular, linear distribution of MMP-2 and MMP-9 at three months post-implantation (Fig. 10B). CD31 and SM-MHC/α-SMA staining (Fig. 10C, D and Fig. S5) suggested more continuous endothelial cell coverage and a more organized SMC layer in these groups. Notably, CD31-positive coverage in the Aligned@10 group appeared more prominent and morphologically closer to that of autologous vessels (Fig. 10C). Importantly, endothelialization in this study was evaluated primarily by CD31 staining; therefore, the present data indicate endothelial cell presence/coverage rather than functional endothelial integrity. Functional endothelial assessments (e.g., eNOS expression, barrier function, shear responsiveness, or anti-thrombogenic performance) were not performed and will be addressed in future work. Taken together, our results suggest that aligned fiber grafts with STS delivery are associated with a more stable matrix remodeling profile, improved endothelial coverage, and a more organized SMC layer, which may contribute to vascular structure formation and long-term stability [46–51].

Conclusions

In summary, we fabricated small-diameter vascular grafts using coaxial electrospinning to create aligned PLCL fibers loaded with Sodium Tanshinone IIA Sulfonate (STS). The aligned topography of these grafts provided contact guidance, promoting the elongation and alignment of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) along the fiber direction. In vivo implantation demonstrated that these grafts significantly improved vascular regeneration, as evidenced by the formation of a well-organized extracellular matrix and a confluent endothelial layer. Furthermore, the released STS effectively modulated the host response, leading to a reduction in intimal hyperplasia and maintaining high patency rates. These results indicate that the combination of structural guidance and STS delivery is effective in promoting functional blood vessel formation.

In future studies, we aim to explore the precise mechanisms underlying cell-cell communication and validate the safety and efficacy of this strategy in more clinically relevant settings.
