Treatment burden and health‐related quality of life in young adults with type 1 diabetes
Authors: Anka Van Gastel, Matthijs Graner‐Baars, Anne M. Stiggelbout, Viet‐Thi Tran, Erik J. Giltay, Sean F. Dinneen, Jessica Mesman, Joris Swaak, Henk‐Jan Aanstoot, Victor M. Montori, Eelco J. P. De Koning, Marleen Kunneman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aims to assess the treatment burden and health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) in young adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D).MethodsOnline survey on experienced treatment burden (Treatment Burden Questionnaire, TBQ, 0–150, higher scores indicate higher burden) and HRQoL (Type 1 Diabetes and Life questionnaire for ages 18–25 and 26–45, T1DAL, 0–100, higher scores indicate better HRQoL) among Dutch young adults with T1D (aged 18–30).ResultsIn total, 294 young adults with T1D completed the survey. Mean TBQ score was 59.5 (SD = 24.9). Regular medical care reminding participants of their health problems scored highest. Mean T1DAL scores were 55.9 (SD = 12.9) for ages 18–25, and 62.1 (SD = 13.2) for ages 26–30. Lowest scores were in the subcategory ‘Emotional experiences and daily activities’ (18–25: median = 41.7, IQR = 24.9; 26–30: median = 47.8, IQR = 22.5). TBQ was strongly negatively correlated with T1DAL (r = −0.69, 95% CI [−0.74, −0.62], p < 0.001). Characteristics associated with higher treatment burden and lower HRQoL included female gender, lower time in range and dissatisfaction with diabetes management approach.ConclusionYoung adults with T1D report considerable treatment burden and moderate HRQoL, suggesting a high impact of diabetes care on their lives. Treatment burden is particularly affected by regular medical care activities, whereas diabetes affects the daily functioning and emotional experiences domains of HRQoL. Further research is needed to understand how young adults make care fit and to improve care for these patients, resulting in minimally disruptive care plans. While cautiously interpreted, this may be especially relevant for women and those with adverse clinical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is one of the most common chronic conditions among young adults (18–30 years). 1 Managing T1D requires persistent daily efforts to achieve and maintain optimal glycaemic control, to reduce the risk of short‐term complications, such as diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and severe hypoglycaemia, and long‐term complications such as retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy. 1 These tasks coincide with a life stage marked by major transitions, including moving out of the parental home, forming intimate relationships, pursuing higher education and/or entering the workforce. During this period, young adults also develop their identity and grow toward independence. 2 The dual demands of young adulthood and living with T1D are reflected in lower reported health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) compared to healthy peers, across multiple domains including physical, emotional and school or work functioning. Compared to young adults with other chronic conditions, those with T1D report comparable HRQoL, with slightly higher scores in some domains (e.g. physical and social functioning). Nevertheless, fatigue remains common, with approximately one in four young adults with T1D reporting fatigue. 3 They also tend to have poorer medical and psychosocial outcomes than people with diabetes in other age groups. 4 This includes higher HbA1c levels, indicating less optimal glycaemic outcomes, and the highest rate of DKA‐related hospitalizations, with one in every 20 young adults reporting more than one episode of (DKA) in the past 3 months. 4 , 5 Young adults with T1D are also at increased risk of diabetes‐specific distress, with up to two‐thirds of young adults reporting being distressed. 3 , 6 These medical and psychosocial adverse outcomes likely reflect, at least in part, the complexity of managing diabetes within the dynamic context of young adulthood. Treatment burden—the workload of managing one's health and the impact of this workload on daily life—is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of outcomes in diabetes care and may be one of the contributing factor in the context of young adulthood. 7 A high treatment burden can lead to feelings of being overwhelmed and, over time, to treatment fatigue—reducing a person's capacity to meet treatment demands and reinforcing a negative cycle of suboptimal outcomes. 8 , 9

Despite growing recognition of the importance of treatment burden in diabetes care, little is known about how young adults with T1D experience this burden or how it affects their quality of life. Gaining a deeper understanding of this experience may help care teams to design care plans that better align with young adults' daily lives. Improved alignment may help reduce treatment burden and fatigue, ultimately improving both health and quality of life outcomes. 8 , 9 , 10

This study assesses the treatment burden and health‐related quality of life in young adults with T1D. Specifically, we aim to examine (1) how treatment burden and quality of life are experienced in daily life; (2) which factors are associated with variations in their nature and intensity; (3) how these experiences differ across subgroups defined by sociodemographic characteristics, diabetes‐specific attributes and satisfaction, and clinical outcomes; and (4) their association using correlation analysis and visualization.

METHODS

Study design and population

This cross‐sectional survey study included young adults aged 18–30 years (an age range consistent with literature on emerging adulthood 11 , 12 , 13 ), who had a self‐reported diagnosis of T1D and were able to read Dutch. We recruited participants between November 2023 and April 2024 through open invitations by email‐lists, flyers and calls on social media channels of multiple Dutch diabetes (patient) organizations and hospitals (see Acknowledgements). All participants provided written informed consent and completed the survey through Castor, an online data management platform. 14 The Medical Research Ethics Committee of Leiden University Medical Center exempted our study from a full ethics review, according to the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (Wet Medisch‐Wetenschappelijk Onderzoek met Mensen; nWMO‐D4‐2023‐015).

Study measures

We collected participants' self‐reported sociodemographic, biomedical and treatment‐related characteristics (Table 1). Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire specifically in relation to their diabetes. Satisfaction with the diabetes management approach (i.e. insulin delivery and glucose monitoring devices) was included as a separate construct, reflecting participants' perceived fit, usability and acceptability of their current management strategy. We used the validated Treatment Burden Questionnaire (TBQ) to assess participant‐reported burden of diabetes treatment. 7 The TBQ consists of 15 items. Fourteen items are scored on a ‘not a problem’ (0) to ‘big problem’ (10) scale, while one item (item 8; see Table 2) is scored on a ‘never’ (0) to ‘always’ (10) scale. The total score of the TBQ ranges from 0 to 150, with a higher score implying a higher treatment burden. 8 We used the validated Type 1 Diabetes and Life questionnaire (T1DAL) for the age bands Young Adults (age 18–25) and Adult‐1 (26–45) to measure health‐related quality of life (HRQoL). 15 Both questionnaires consist of 27 items scored on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from not true (1) to very true (5). The developers of the T1DAL based these age bands on developmental stages and the scale is validated per age band. Subscales scores and total scores are converted to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating a better diabetes‐specific HRQoL.

Statistical analysis

We excluded participants from the analysis if they had not completed the TBQ, or if they were apparent duplicate participants based on unique combination of their characteristics (included in Table 1). For the TBQ and T1DAL, we checked the normality of data using Shapiro–Wilk test and reported means or medians accordingly. We calculated the TBQ score by summing scores on the individual items and considered ‘Non‐applicable’ responses as 0 (indicating no problem). 7 For the T1DAL, we calculated the total score and subdomain scores when 90% of items were answered, following the guidelines from the developers. 15 We tested differences between subdomains using the Friedman test and Kendall's W.

We used descriptive statistics to summarize the sociodemographic and diabetes‐specific characteristics and dichotomized HbA1c (<48 mmol/mol vs. ≥48 mmol/mol), 16 , 17 satisfaction with diabetes management approach (satisfied/not satisfied) and ketoacidosis (no/yes). An overview of original and recoded variables is provided in Supporting Information A. We coded the responses to the open‐ended question on other health conditions according to the Health Research Classification System (HRCS) health categories using Excel. 18 We used frequencies to get a better understanding of experienced treatment burden at the item level and HRQoL at the domain level.

We handled missing data using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) generating five imputed datasets using predictive mean matching with a fixed random seed. We performed standardized multivariable linear regression analyses for both outcomes. Regression coefficients were pooled across imputed datasets using Rubin's rules, combining within‐ and between‐imputation variance. We report results as coefficients with 95% confidence intervals and p‐values. We used two forest plots to summarize the multivariable adjusted associations for TBQ and T1DAL (Figures 1 and 2). To assess multicollinearity among predictors within each model we used the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF). Treatment burden and HRQoL represent related patient‐reported constructs with overlapping aspects of living with diabetes. To avoid multicollinearity and unstable estimates, we did not include them in the same regression model. We therefore assessed their association assessed using Pearson's correlation and scatterplot visualization. We used the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29 for the first part of our analyses. The univariate and multivariable analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2, using the ‘stats’ (version 4.5.2), ‘tidyverse’ (version 2.0.0) and ‘forestplot‘(version 3.1.3) packages.

RESULTS

Participants

In total, 496 young adults with T1D started the survey, of whom 305 (61%) completed the full TBQ. We excluded 11 participants due to apparent duplicate submission, thus ending up with a sample of 294 (59%) young adults with T1D for analysis. Missing data were present in two variables (HbA1c and time in range), with N = 9, 3.1% and N = 8, 2.7% missing, respectively. The mean age of participants was 24.8 (SD = 3.5) years. Most participants were female (N = 247, 84%), educated at tertiary level (N = 183, 62%) and employed (N = 199, 68%). The average time since diagnosis was 12.2 years (SD = 7.1) (Table 1). The largest proportion of participants used a hybrid closed‐loop system (N = 145, 49%). Additionally, 22% reported HbA1c levels below 48 mmol/mol (<6.5%). Close to half (42%) reported having more than one hypoglycaemic event in the last week, and 13% experienced ketoacidosis in the last year. Of our sample, 30% reported living with other health conditions in addition to diabetes, mostly related to other metabolic and endocrine conditions, oral and gastrointestinal conditions, and mental health (range 1–5 conditions in addition to diabetes) (Table 1; Supporting Information B).

Treatment burden

The mean treatment burden score of participants was 59.6 out of 150 (SD = 25.1, range 8–145). Table 2 summarizes TBQ items scores (see Supporting Information C for number of ‘not applicable’ responses per item). Participants scored highest on the item ‘the need for medical healthcare on a regular basis reminds me of my health problems’ (item 8; median 6.0 [IQR = 5.0]), adding most to the experienced treatment burden. The items about necessary precautions when taking medication and relationships with healthcare providers added the least to the experienced treatment burden (items 1D and 2D, median = 2.0 [IQR = 5.0]).

In the adjusted model, gender and time in range were independently associated with treatment burden (Figure 1). Female participants reported higher treatment burden than male participants, with a mean difference of 13.54 TBQ points (95% CI: 5.56; 21.53), whereas higher time in range was associated with lower treatment burden, corresponding to a decrease of 6.32 TBQ points per SD increase in time in range (95% CI: −9.63; −3.00). Multicollinearity among the predictors was low, with all adjusted GVIF values below 1.5.

Health‐related quality of life

The mean T1DAL‐score was 55.9 (SD = 13.1, range 9.3–92.0) for the 18–25 age group, and 61.8 (SD = 13.6, range 25–91.7) for the 26–30 age group. Differences in T1DAL scores were observed between age groups (Table 3). Within both age groups, we found significant variation across sub‐domains, corresponding to a moderate effect (18–25: p = <0.001, Kendall's W = 0.34; 26–30: p = <0.001 Kendall's W = 0.37). The lowest scores were consistently found in the domain ‘Emotional behaviours and daily activities’ (18–25: median = 41.7 [IQR = 24.9]; 26–30: median = 47.8 [IQR = 22.5]; Table 3). Participants reported the highest scores in the domains of healthcare experiences (18–25: median = 75.0, IQR = 18.8) and family relationships (26–30: median = 83.3 [IQR = 25.0]).

In the adjusted model, satisfaction with the diabetes management approach and time in range over the past 24 h were independently associated with HRQoL (Figure 2). Those who were not satisfied with their diabetes management approach reported lower HRQoL compared to those who were satisfied, with a mean difference of −5.57 HRQoL points (95% CI: −10.81; −0.33). Higher time in range was associated with higher HRQoL, corresponding to an increase of 3.30 HRQoL points per SD increase in time in range (95% CI: 1.54; 5.06). Treatment burden was negatively correlated with T1DAL (r = −0.69, 95% CI [−0.74, −0.62], p < 0.001), indicating a strong association between treatment burden and HRQoL. DISCUSSION

This study explored treatment burden and health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) among young adults living with type 1 diabetes (T1D). Our findings show that young adults experience a considerable level of treatment burden. More than half of participants reported burden levels that, in other populations, have been associated with an unsustainable workload of care. 8 While this cutoff was originally defined in elderly patients with comorbidity and is not yet validated for young adults with T1D, it suggests that a substantial proportion of this group may be at risk of becoming overwhelmed by the demands of their care. Reported HRQoL was moderate, consistent with the validation study of the T1DAL measure, 15 and strongly associated with reported treatment burden. Treatment burden was higher for female participants and for those who had a lower time in range. HRQoL was lower for young adults who were dissatisfied with their diabetes management approach and had a lower time in range.

This study has several strengths. We used validated instruments (TBQ and T1DAL) to assess treatment burden and HRQoL, and recruited participants through diverse channels, including social media. This enabled us to reach a geographically dispersed group of young adults across the Netherlands and to recruit participants beyond outside outpatient diabetes clinics. Using multivariable analyses adjusted for all relevant covariates strengthened the robustness of our findings and reduced the risk of confounding when examining relationships between participant and clinical characteristics and outcomes. There are also limitations. Our cross‐sectional design precludes any conclusions about causality. Further, our study included a high number of women and higher‐educated participants. Young adults' self‐selection to participate in this study may have led to selection bias, with individuals who are more health‐literate or more engaged with their diabetes care to be more likely to participate. Previous research showed that voluntary samples tend to report better mental and subjective health, which would suggest that the high treatment burden and low HRQoL in our study may even be an underestimation of the broader population of young adults with T1D. 19 Consequently, findings should be interpreted with caution regarding their applicability to more diverse populations of young adults with T1D. While we aimed to explore variation across subgroups, some groups may have been too small to detect subtle differences. All variables were self‐reported, including diabetes‐related outcomes, without clinical verification, which may have introduced bias such as recall bias. In addition, our method did not allow a formal verification of a diagnosis of T1D, and some questionnaire items (e.g. within the TBQ) were marked as ‘not applicable’, which may reflect variation in interpretation or applicability of treatment tasks. Participants reported their most recent HbA1c value without indicating when this measurement had taken place, and HbA1c was analysed using a cut‐off of 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) based on established guidelines (e.g. ADA and NICE), 16 , 17 which may limit comparability with studies applying alternative clinically used thresholds, such as 53 mmol/mol (7.0%). Time in range referred to the previous 24 h and may therefore reflect short‐term fluctuations only. Finally, the survey design did not allow participants to indicate combinations of work situations.

Our findings align with a growing body of evidence showing that young adulthood is a life stage associated with elevated diabetes‐specific distress and suboptimal clinical outcomes. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 15 , 20 , 21 Treatment burden levels and HRQoL in our sample are comparable to those reported in other diabetes populations, although differences in study populations and measures should be considered. 7 , 15 , 21 , 22 Compared to people living with other chronic conditions, self monitoring seems especially burdensome for young adults with T1D. 7 , 22 Indeed, participants reported relatively high burden from being frequently reminded of their health problems through ongoing healthcare demands. The alignment with the lower HRQoL observed in daily activities and emotional behaviours suggests that constant illness‐related cues may be relevant for both functional and emotional well‐being, although we could not directly link these outcomes in our multivariable analyses.

Overall, our findings show that different aspects of living with T1D are reflected across both treatment burden and HRQoL, highlighting complementary but distinct patterns in how diabetes technology, glycaemic outcomes, and psychosocial factors relate to each outcome in young adults.

Our findings reveal distinct patterns in how aspects of living with T1D relate to treatment burden and HRQoL in young adults. Our study suggests that satisfaction with one's diabetes management approach is more strongly related to HRQoL than the specific type of device used. This aligns with previous research indicating that while diabetes technologies can improve clinical and quality of life outcomes, they may also introduce new burdens. 4 , 23 , 24 In a follow‐up to the current study, we used photovoice methods to explore how diabetes and diabetes care affect young adults' daily lives. Participants highlighted that support—often provided through devices and technology—can both alleviate and amplify the impact of care. 13 Potential gender differences in treatment burden outcomes that we observed may reflect a combination of biological and sociocultural factors—including hormonal variation, caregiving responsibilities and differences in psychological stress. 25 , 26 Importantly, our data suggest that higher treatment burden is not associated with better clinical outcomes. No clear associations were observed between treatment burden and most clinical outcomes, except for time in range over the past 24 h, which appeared to be lower in those with reporting higher treatment burden. On the one hand, lower time in range may make diabetes management feel more demanding. Time spent above or below range can cause fatigue, and performing diabetes management tasks (e.g. monitoring, insulin adjustments) while glucose levels are out of range may be mentally tiring and undermine feelings of achievement. 27 On the other hand, experiencing a high treatment burden may reflect that care plans are not fully aligned with young adults' personal priorities, or that interventions are experienced as complex or disruptive, potentially making it harder to keep glucose levels in range. The perceived burden of these tasks likely depends on how sensible and meaningful this work is to the patient, and how well it fits into a patient's life. When frequent care demands repeatedly remind young adults of their diabetes and interfere with their daily life, it may contribute to disengagement from treatment and difficulty in achieving glycaemic targets, which in turn may negatively affect both health and quality of life. 28 The proportion of young adults in our study reporting potentially unsustainable treatment burden was higher than previously found in other populations. For instance, Tran et al. found that 38% of elderly patients with chronic conditions and 20% of people with diabetes reported such unsustainable burden levels. 8 This mismatch between care demands and a person's capacity to manage them may lead to diabetes distress, fatigue and disengagement from care—key elements of diabetes burnout. 29 , 30 In our study, mental health conditions were among the most commonly reported additional conditions, possibly reflecting the consequences of an unsustainable burden. Consistent with this, a German study found that depression and stress were relatively common among 18‐ to 40‐year‐olds with T1D, highlighting the high prevalence of mental health challenges in this age group. 31

This study deepens our understanding of the ‘work’ young adults with T1D do to manage their condition and how this work affects their quality of life. 32 For high‐quality care, this burden should be made explicit and be considered, especially when co‐designing care plans with young adults. Clinicians could, for example, ask patients about the strategies and work they do to manage diabetes, how care fits into their daily lives, and where it creates friction or emotional strain. To support these potentially complex conversations, we are collaborating with young adults to co‐design a tool that facilitates reflection and discussion about the efforts involved in managing diabetes and their impact on daily life. Future studies should aim to establish clinically meaningful differences for treatment burden and health‐related quality of life measures in young adults with T1D to improve the interpretability of effect estimates. In addition, future research should explore strategies to reduce treatment burden, examine whether burden contributes causally to poorer outcomes, and test whether alleviating it improves both clinical and quality of life outcomes. More diverse samples will be essential to identify subgroup‐specific needs and tailor appropriate supports.

CONCLUSION

Young adults with T1D experience substantial treatment burden which is particularly high for daily care tasks. Their HRQoL is moderate, with the emotional and daily functioning domains being the lowest scoring domains. Higher burden and lower HRQoL were associated with female gender, lower time in range in the past 24 h and dissatisfaction with the diabetes management approach, although these findings should be interpreted with caution. It is important for clinicians to recognize the considerable and continuous efforts young adults invest in managing their diabetes and the impact this has on their well‐being. Further research should investigate how individual and clinical factors shape treatment burden and evaluate tailored interventions to alleviate it. Supporting shared decision making about diabetes treatments and technologies may help design care plans that better align with young adults' unique situations and priorities while minimizing disruption to their daily lives.
