Astrocyte activation in the ventrolateral medulla modulates breathing and arousal states
Authors: Luiz Marcelo Oliveira, Nicole Castro Miranda, Hyun-Kyoung Lim, Liza Severs, Ana Carolina Takakura, Thiago Santos Moreira, Li Li, Franck Kalume, Jan-Marino Ramirez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Astrocytes play vital roles in regulating brain states across organisms. Specifically, they regulate breathing behaviors and associated brain states, including facilitating transitions between breathing phases by sensing changes in O₂ and CO₂ levels, regulating the sleep-wake cycle, and impacting arousal and wakefulness. Here, we test the hypothesis that astrocytes in the ventral respiratory column (VRC) are important for arousal and sigh generation in alert mice. Our results reveal that a subset of Aldh1l1 cells in the VRC are activated prior to sigh generation and are recruited by hypoxia. Chemogenetic or optogenetic activation of Aldh1l1 astrocytes in the VRC increased the probability of evoking arousals with sighs. We also demonstrated that activating Aldh1l1 astrocytes increased calcium transients in catecholaminergic neurons in the VRC immediately before arousal with sighs. We conclude that medullary astrocytes can modulate sigh generation and arousal transitions, and are important for the ventilatory and arousal response to hypoxia.

Introduction

Breathing is vital for sustaining life, ensuring the continuous exchange of oxygen (O₂) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) in the body1,2. This process requires precise, uninterrupted regulation to meet the metabolic demands of the organism1,2. Such control relies on a brainstem network of specialized neurons and astrocytes responsible for orchestrating the responsiveness, as well as the rhythm and pattern of respiration3–6. A critical structure within this network is the ventral respiratory column (VRC), which contains key regions such as the pre-Bötzinger Complex (preBötC)7,8. This region serves as the primary rhythm generator for breathing to maintain a steady inspiratory cycle1,9.

Astrocytes play an essential role in preserving network stability by regulating extracellular neurotransmitter concentrations, maintaining ionic balance, and influencing synaptic transmission10–13. Astrocyte function extends beyond merely supporting neurons; astrocytes are actively involved in managing transitions between different breathing phases and responding to subtle fluctuations in O₂ and CO₂ levels, which is critical for sustaining consistent homeostasis6,10,14,15. Furthermore, astrocytes are key players in regulating the sleep-wake cycle, directly influencing states of arousal and alertness13,16,17. By releasing gliotransmitters, modulating neuronal activity, and maintaining the integrity of the blood-brain barrier, glia helps to preserve cognition, and other behavioral functions during wakefulness and sleep13,14,18,19.

Recent studies have revealed that astrocytes play a role in generating sighs through the release of adenosine triphosphate (ATP)10,13,20, which involves the activation of P2Y1 receptors in the preBötC as shown in vitro20. Sighs constitute a unique respiratory pattern that serves both physiological and emotional purposes21–23. It is thought to act as a reset mechanism for the respiratory system, providing a deep breath that helps to maintain lung function by preventing alveolar atelectasis21,24,25. Emotionally, sighing often reflects shifts in arousal states, marking transitions such as moving from stress to relaxation or signaling moments of anticipation or relief25.

Here, we find that chemogenetic or optogenetic activation of VRC astrocytes alters breathing and consistently triggers arousal followed by sighs, particularly during NREMS and REMS. These results identify astrocytes as key regulators of state-dependent breathing and couple astrocyte activity to the coordination of arousal and sighing.

Results

In a cohort of 7 freely behaving mice (Fig. S1), the sigh frequency increased while animals were in non-rapid eye movement sleep (NREMS; 0.98 ± 0.02 sighs/min) or rapid eye movement (REMS; 1.36 ± 0.15 sighs/min) compared with wakefulness state (0.55 ± 0.03 sighs/min; p = 0.014; one-way ANOVA; F(2,20) = 19.45). Throughout the analyses, arousal was identified by a shift in the electroencephalogram (EEG) from high-amplitude, delta-frequency activity characteristic of NREMS or the synchronized pattern of REMS to a low-amplitude, mixed-frequency EEG signal, accompanied by a concomitant increase in electromyogram (EMG) activity. Behavioral correlates of these electrographic events seen on video included body movement or postural adjustments. Consistent with these recordings, arousal were frequently accompanied by a sigh, with a high probability of co-occurrence (0.87; Fig. S1g).

Optogenetic activation of Aldh1l1-transduced astrocytes leads to an increased EMG neck tone, a change in respiratory pattern, and the induction of sighs in both awake and sleeping animals. Fig. 1b depicts representative EEG/EMG recordings together with the normalized (to resting breathing) plethysmography data and optogenetic stimulation. We observed co-occurrence of arousal with sigh generation, each followed by the characteristic post-sigh apnea (Fig. 1c). In contrast, in cre(-/-) AAV-PHP.eB-CAG-DIO-ChR2(H134R)-eYFP-injected mice, no sighs or arousal were evoked in any wake-sleep state. In cre(-/-) animals, latency to evoking an arousal versus a sigh was similar to the latency to generating spontaneous sighs (57.8 s). We also found a higher probability of evoking an arousal accompanied by a sigh across all vigilance states (Awake: 61.4 ± 3.6; NREMS: 55.8 ± 1.6; REMS: 44.1 ± 3.1 vs. cre(-/-): 0.2 ± 0.2; p < 0.001; one-way repeated-measures ANOVA; F(3,25) = 20.334). Across all sleep-wake states there was a high correlation of eliciting sighs and arousals together (Fig. 1e–g). The average time for eliciting a sigh and arousal was 5.54 ± 0.23 s while the animals were awake and 4.93 ± 0.19 s while animals were in NREMS. However, as shown in Fig. 1g, the transition from REMS to arousal occurred later compared to the other states (7.66 ± 0.46 s; p = 0.045; one-way ANOVA; F(2,46) = 15.24).

Photostimulation of astrocytes in the VRC did not affect the eupneic fR, but VT of eupneic activity was increased in wakefulness (6.60 ± 0.27 vs. before: 4.48 ± 0.15 µl/g; p < 0.001; one-way repeated-measures ANOVA; F(2,20) = 37.86), NREMS (6.94 ± 0.29 vs. before: 4.48 ± 0.10 µl/g; p < 0.001; one-way repeated-measures ANOVA; F(2,20) = 34.31) and REMS (6.12 ± 0.23 vs. before: 4.90 ± 0.14 µl/g; p < 0.001; one-way repeated-measures ANOVA; F(2,20) = 16.07) (Fig. 1i–n and Supplementary Tables 1, 2). Optogenetic activation of astrocytes also increased irregularity in breathing frequency in all wake-sleep states (Fig. 1h). In this analysis (Fig. 1h–n), we excluded sigh events, so the data represents only eupneic breathing during astrocyte optogenetic activation.

We next tested whether chemogenetic activation of VRC astrocytes would produce effects similar to those observed with optogenetic stimulation. Cre(-/-) mice received bilateral injections of either AAV-GFAP-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry (N = 8) or a control virus (AAV-GFAP-mCherry, N = 5) into the VRC, followed by implantation of EEG/EMG electrodes. Twenty-one days later, ventilation and EEG/EMG parameters were recorded as described in the “Methods” section. We confirmed by in situ hybridization that 27.9% of the transfected cells (mCherry) also expressed Aldh1l1 and GFAP (Fig. S3). Each animal was monitored for approximately 8 h following intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of either vehicle or clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, 1 mg/kg). As shown in Fig. 2, CNO administration increased fR during wakefulness (1 h after CNO: 243.9 ± 19.1 vs. vehicle: 195.3 ± 7.5 breaths/min; p = 0.023; Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA; F(9,79) = 11.356), and the frequency of sighs up to 3 h after CNO administration (1 h after CNO: 1.56 ± 0.09 vs. vehicle: 0.87 ± 0.13 sighs/min; p < 0.001; Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA; F(9,79) = 12.829). The same pattern was observed in sleeping animals; however, the increase in fR was statistically detected only after 2 h of CNO administration. In addition, CNO-treated animals spent more time awake and exhibited reduced sleep duration. Astrocytes in the VRC are activated before the onset of sighs and their depletion delays the generation of arousal followed by sighs in hypoxia

Following these experiments, we observed a depletion of approximately 77% of astrocytes in Aldh1l1Cre mice (34.1 ± 4.5 vs. cre(-/-): 153.1 ± 8.3 cells; p < 0.001; t-test; t(1,13) = 8.841; Fig. 3c, d). Despite this depletion, when analyzing the functional data over ~5 h, we still observed sigh and arousal episodes in the astrocyte-depleted group (Fig. 3f). Astrocytes in the preBötC are known to detect changes in oxygen levels20,26. To test this, we exposed all animals to hypoxia (10% O2) for 1 h and evaluated the time needed to induce arousal/sighs. VRC astrocyte depletion affected the acute phase of the hypoxic response which occurs shortly after oxygen levels decrease and consists of the arousal and an increased ventilation (as shown in Fig. 3k). The group with astrocyte depletion in the VRC had a delayed arousal response to hypoxia (65.25 ± 5.09 vs. cre(-/-): 21.77 ± 0.87 s; p = 0.002; t-test; t(1,13) = 28.0, Fig. 3l). After 5 min of hypoxia, we did not observe significant changes in fR and VT between the two groups (Fig. 3n–q). The frequency of sighs in the Aldh1l1-depleted group was not statistically altered compared to the cre(-/-) group (Aldh1l1Cre: 1.11 ± 0.06 vs. cre(-/-): 1.24 ± 0.06 sighs/min; p = 0.133; t-test; t(1,13) = 1.612, Fig. 3p).

In this approach, GCaMP fluorescence serves as an indirect indicator of cellular activity by detecting calcium transients. We transduced 259.4 ± 25.8 Aldh1l1 cells in the VRC, and confirmed that 37.7% of those cells also expressed GFAP (Fig. S4f). As shown in Fig. 4, during eupnea, astrocytic calcium activity exhibited continuous fluctuations without large variations in signal intensity. In another approach, Aldh1l1Cre mice received AAV-CAG-FLEX-jGCaMP8m-WPRE injections into the VRC. Twenty-one days later, the animals were sedated with urethane i.p., and the VMS was exposed while recording diaphragm (Dia) EMG, as demonstrated in Fig. 4f. Calcium imaging data acquired in a 2-photon microscope, revealed that some preBötC astrocytes in the VRC (22% of recorded cells, 16 out of 72 cells) were active in close time-proximity to sigh generation (Fig. 4g, h). This group of cells was analyzed together, and we found a high correlation between each ROI and the delay of the ROI signal relative to sigh generation (r2 = 0.847, Fig. 4k). We also measured the correlation between non-phase-locked astrocytes activity and sigh generation (57 out of 72 cells) and found a low, non-significant correlation. However, these astrocytes exhibited occasional high ROI activity randomly, and the probability of a close time-proximity to astrocyte activating during eupnea burst was 0.103.

We also measured the activity of these cells under hypoxia for 30 s. As shown in Fig. 4g, calcium imaging revealed that Aldh1l1-positive cells displayed increased activity during hypoxia, and the peak activity measured in the ROIs correlated highly with the peak of the Dia EMG signal, differing from normoxia data (Fig. 4l–o). Taken together, these data suggest that this population of cells exhibits peak activity during sigh generation and may play an important role in central chemoreception in response to low oxygen levels.

Interaction of astrocytes and C1 neurons may be important for arousal and sigh generation

It was elegantly reported by Burke and colleagues that C1-adrenergic neurons are crucial for arousal and sigh generation27. Based on this, we hypothesized that the interaction between astrocytes and C1 neurons could play a role in eliciting arousal and sighs in mice. To test this hypothesis, we next performed experiments using ThFlpO/- mice that received bilateral injections of a mixture of two viral constructs: AAV-GFAP-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry, targeting GFAP-positive astrocytes, and a FlpO-dependent GCaMP construct (AAV-Ef1a-fDIO-GCaMP6f), targeting C1/A1 neurons in the VRC (Fig. 5a, b). The anatomical location of the fiber optics, and the transduction rate (number of mCherry/GFAP positive cells) are reported in Fig. S5. As shown in Fig. 5c, chemogenetic activation of astrocytes by i.p. CNO administration increased the excitability of C1/A1 neurons in the VRC (Normalized ∆ at 1 h: 6.68 ± 1.33 vs. before: 0.07 ± 0.18, p < 0.001; Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA; F(7,63) = 11.311). This elevated calcium activity was maintained throughout the 6-h experimental interval. Astrocyte-driven sigh generation and arousal in the VRC require P2Y1 signaling

To determine whether astrocyte-driven sigh generation and arousal depend on purinergic and potassium signaling within the VRC, as well as on the integrity of C1/A1 neurons, we combined optogenetic activation of Aldh1l1 astrocytes with local pharmacological and neurotoxic manipulations. In anesthetized mice, photostimulation of VRC astrocytes reliably evoked sighs in the presence of vehicle (Probability: 0.58 ± 0.05). This response was preserved following selective blockade of Kir4.1 channels alone (0.50 ± 0.04), but was completely abolished after combined administration of the Kir4.1 blocker VU0134992 and the P2Y1 receptor antagonist MRS2279 into the VRC (Fig. S8d, f–g). Importantly, spontaneous sighs remained present following drug delivery, indicating that the antagonist cocktail selectively disrupted astrocyte-driven sigh generation without suppressing baseline respiratory activity and sighs.

The area transduced by the AAV-PHP.eB-CAG-DIO-ChR2(H134R)-eYFP targeting Aldh1l1 cells bilaterally was consistent with other experimental groups (15,820 ± 498 µm²) and the depletion produced by 6-OHDA was 88.74% (Fig. S9). In freely behaving Aldh1l1Cre mice (Fig. 9c), optogenetic stimulation of Aldh1l1 astrocytes failed to alter fR during either wakefulness or sleep (Fig. 9g–l). Aldh1l1 cells in the VRC are activated during hypoxia

To investigate whether astrocytes could be activated by hypoxic challenges, we continued using Aldh1l1Cre mice. Adult mice were acclimated to the plethysmography chamber for 2 h, after which they were exposed to either 1 h of hypoxia (10% O₂) or room air. Thirty minutes after the end of the hypoxia trial, animals were deeply anesthetized and perfused. Hypoxia led to the activation of Aldh1l1 cells in the VRC as measured by an increase in astrocyte Fos expression (Hypoxia: 63.5 ± 5.6 vs. Normoxia: 4.2 ± 1.2 Fos+/GFAP+/Aldh1l1+ cells; p = 002; one-way ANOVA; F(1,9) = 8.346). We also report the presence of cellular activation in the Nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS), Locus coeruleus (LC), Parabrachial complex (PBN), and Kölliker-fuse (KF) during hypoxia (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11).

Discussion

Here we explored the role of astrocytes in hypoxia and the generation of arousal followed by a sigh in freely behaving mice, with a specific focus on the astrocyte populations in the VRC and RTN. Our study builds on prior research showing that astrocytes play a crucial role in regulating breathing, arousal, and sigh generation5,6,10,20,28,29. These studies showed that astrocytes influence rhythmogenic neurons in the preBötC via purinergic signaling30,31. Sighing, a distinct type of deep breathing that is generated by excitatory neurons in the preBötC involves various sigh-specific cellular mechanisms20,24,32–35. In freely behaving mice, we measured respiratory patterns along with EEG and EMG recordings and found that astrocyte activation in the preBötC increased the VT and induced arousal followed by sighs, especially during NREMS and REMS. Arousal was also reflected by an increase in neck muscle tone. In this study, we show that arousal were consistently followed by a sigh, as has previously been described36,37.

By contrast, astrocyte stimulation in the RTN, evoked neither sighs nor arousal, though it increased fR and VT of eupnea. The effect on eupneic breathing is consistent with the established role of the RTN as a key central chemosensitive region that provides tonic excitatory drive to the respiratory network and stabilizes baseline ventilation. The RTN plays a critical role in detecting changes in CO₂ levels in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid, which translates into pH changes38–41. Astrocytes in the RTN have been shown to play a crucial role in central chemoreception by detecting decreases in pH and releasing ATP, which then stimulate nearby RTN neurons, and further act as a vasoconstrictor in the region14,26,38,40. This signaling amplifies the respiratory response, enhancing the drive to breathe more rapidly and expel excess CO₂38,42. Neuronal activation of the RTN speeds up the fR and also provokes sleep-wake cycle transitions or arousals43–45. Thus, astrocytes boost the sensitivity of RTN neurons to CO₂ and fine-tune the chemoreceptive process to maintain blood pH and overall respiratory homeostasis46. However, our data suggest that astrocyte activation in the RTN does not play a critical role in the induction of arousal and the generation of sighs. The differential effects observed for RTN and preBötC are consistent with the concept that astrocytes in different brain regions play differential roles in the central control of breathing, central chemosensation, arousal and sleep/wake transitions. This is also consistent with the notion that astrocytes do not function only as simple support cells for neurons, helping with metabolic and structural support13,19,47, but rather that astrocytes are active participants in regulating rhythmic behaviors, chemoreception, arousal and in the sleep/wake cycle16,26.

We observed a concerted activation of arousal and sighs when stimulating the VRC, specifically the preBötC. Thus, we hypothesized that astrocytes located in the VRC interact with catecholaminergic C1 neurons. It has previously been demonstrated that optogenetic stimulation of C1 neurons induces arousal with sigh events in rats27. Here we show that after depleting C1 neurons with 6-OHDA, sighs and arousals were still evoked with optogenetic stimulation of astrocytes in the preBötC. However, the time to evoke these responses was delayed compared to vehicle-injected animals. Based on these findings we propose that C1 neurons are part of the arousal circuitry27,48, but they are not necessary for arousal and sigh generation. However, astrocytes and C1 neurons together likely enhance the generation of arousal and sighs.

Our pharmacological experiments further identify purinergic signaling through astrocytes as essential components of this pathway. Blockade of Kir4.1 channels or P2Y1 receptors alone did not prevent astrocyte-induced arousal with sighs; however, combined inhibition completely abolished both arousal and sigh generation. Kir4.1 channels play a central role in extracellular potassium buffering, whereas P2Y1 receptors mediate ATP-dependent astrocyte-neuron communication. The requirement for both mechanisms suggests that astrocytes influence respiratory circuits through coordinated regulation of ionic homeostasis and purinergic signaling, thereby modulating neuronal excitability at the network level.

Following a significant depletion of astrocytes (around 77%) in the VRC, spontaneous sighs and arousal episodes persisted at levels comparable to those observed in non-lesioned controls. Interestingly, we also report in our study the presence of Aldh1l1-expressing cells in the VRC exhibiting activity in close time-proximity with sighs. Even though we found a significant number of these cells, a portion of astrocytes in the preBötC are not directly rhythmogenic or related to sighs or eupnea. However, our experiments confirm their role in sigh modulation, as previously demonstrated20. We also found that this population of astrocytes in the VRC increased their activity under hypoxic conditions. This finding supports the idea that astrocytes contribute to the brain’s detection of low oxygen levels and likely play a role in generating corrective respiratory behaviors like sighing6,10,20.

Here, we show that astrocyte depletion in the VRC does not abolish the hypoxic ventilatory response to hypoxia (10% O₂). This was expected given the important role of the carotid bodies (CB) in mediating the hypoxic response and the generation of sighs as previously demonstrated21,49,50. In our experiments, the CB and the known pathways from the CB to the preBötC were left intact. Thus, it may seem surprising that both arousal and sighs were significantly delayed after partial depletion of astrocytes in the preBötC. This finding suggests that hypoxia-activated CB inputs require the participation of the central astrocytes in the preBötC to evoke the coordinated and rapid activation of sighs and arousal. The delay caused by the partial depletion of these astrocytes could be physiologically significant given that a rapid arousal response is critical under hypoxic conditions. This supports the notion that disturbances in sighing and arousal could contribute to the failure to arouse in sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS)51. This delay is also consistent with the finding that SIDS victims show gliosis in the preBötC region52. However, while astrogliosis has repeatedly been demonstrated in SIDS, changes in the number of astrocytes in this condition are complex53. Moreover, the role of astrocytes in SIDS has primarily been studied using GFAP staining. Thus, studies using Aldh1l1 marker will be necessary to further support this potentially important association. Nevertheless, irrespective of their role in SIDS, our study supports the notion that central medullary astrocytes play an important role in complementing oxygen sensing by the CB and in modulating adaptive responses to low oxygen environments6,14,30. This is also indicated by the finding that astrocytes in the VRC are activated in response to hypoxia6. Here we showed hypoxia-induced Fos expression in astrocytes and neurons in several areas of the brainstem. Thus, our data illustrate a broader role for astrocytes and neurons in coordinating the hypoxic response across multiple brain regions.

Overall, these results indicate that astrocyte activation within the VRC can contribute to respiratory modulation, arousal, and sigh generation. Moreover, astrocytes are part of a broader network involving other neuronal populations including C1 area and the preBötC. This interaction between astrocytes and neurons in the VRC and surrounding regions appears to play a crucial role in maintaining respiratory homeostasis, controlling the rhythmicity responsible for adapting to environmental changes.

Methods

Animals

Experiments were performed on 129 adult C57BL6/J male and female mice weighing between 25 and 30 g and aged P90–100. All experimental and surgical procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Seattle Children’s Research Institute (SCRI) Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol #00033). Aldh1l1Cre mice (Hemizygous) were obtained from Jackson Laboratories [FVB-Tg (Aldh1l1Cre)JD1884Htz/J, Jax stock No. 023748]; ThFlpO mice (Hemizygous) were obtained from Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centers [6N-Thtm1Awar/Mmmh, stock No. 050618-MU]. All the animals were bred at the Seattle Children’s Research Institute animal facility. We also bred Aldh1l1Cre with ThFlpO mice generating Aldh1l1Cre::ThFlpO heterozygous animals. All animals had free access to water and food and were housed in a temperature-controlled (22 ± 1 °C) facility with a 12:12 h light/dark cycle and 50% humidity.

Adenovirus Injections

Aldh1l1Cre animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (2.0–2.5% balanced with 100% oxygen) and fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus (Stoelting model 620), followed by local administration of lidocaine (7 mg/kg), and a small craniotomy using a micro drill (0.7 mm). After cranial opening, unilateral or bilateral injections of AAV-PHP.eB-CAG-DIO-ChR2(H134R)-eYFP (AAV-PHP.eB, 2.1 × 1013 GC/ml, Cat #127090-PHPeB, Gift from Viviana Gradinaru54), AAV-GFAP-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry (AAV5, 2.7 × 1013 GC/ml, Cat #50478-AAV5), AAV-CAG-FLEX-jGCaMP8m-WPRE (AAV1, 2.2 × 1013 GC/ml, Cat #162381-AAV1), AAV-Ef1a-fDIO-GCaMP6f (AAV1, 2 × 1013 GC/ml, Cat #128315-AAV1), AAV-Ef1a-Coff/Fon-sRGECO (AAV8, 1 × 1013 GC/ml, Cat #137128-AAV8), AAV_hsyn_NES-his-CaMPARI2-WPRE-SV40 (AAV1, 1 × 1013 GC/ml, Cat #101060-AAV1), all acquired from Addgene; or bilateral injections of AAV-CAG-DIO-taCaspase3-TEVp-WPRE (AAV9, 7 × 1012 GC/ml, Cat #NTA-2012-ZP273), acquired from Creative Biolabs; as control virus, we performed injections of AAV-PHP.eB-CAG-eYFP (AAV-PHP.eB, 1 × 1013 GC/ml, Cat #104055-PHPeB), AAV-CAG-eYFP (AAV5, 7 × 1012 GC/ml, Cat #104055-AAV5), AAV-GFAP104-mCherry (AAV5, 1 × 1013 GC/ml, Cat #58909-AAV5) all acquired from Addgene, were administered (30–50 nL/injection) using a glass micropipette attached to the Nanoject equipment (Thomas scientific). Injections targeted the VRC (focusing on preBötC region) with the following coordinates: 1.7 mm caudal to the lambda, 1.2 mm lateral to the midline, and 4.5 mm ventral to the dura mater; or retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN): 1.6 mm caudal to the lambda, 1.3 mm lateral to the midline, and 4.7 mm ventral to the dura mater55.

Fifteen days after viral injections, mice were implanted unilaterally with one 125 μm (RWC, Cat #807-00022-00) or 200 μm fiber optics cannulaes (RWC, Cat #R-FOC-L200C-22NA) targeting the preBötC or RTN, using a 15-degree rostral to caudal sagittal angle. We used the following coordinates, (a) preBötC: 1.1 mm caudal to the lambda, 1.2 mm lateral to the midline, and 4.8 mm ventral to the dura mater; or (b) RTN: 1.0 mm caudal to the lambda, 1.3 mm lateral to the midline, and 5.0 mm ventral to the dura mater.

Neurotoxin injections

Under the same anesthesia protocol, separate groups of animals received bilateral injections of either 6-hydroxydopamine hydrochloride (6-OHDA; 10 µg/µL, 100 nL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), or vehicle (1 µg ascorbic acid in 1 µL of 0.9% saline) into the VRC. 6-OHDA is selectively taken up by catecholaminergic neurons via monoamine transporters, where it undergoes auto-oxidation, generating reactive oxygen species that induce oxidative stress and neuronal degeneration. No overt behavioral effects were observed following 6-OHDA administration.

Electrode implantation and post-surgical recovery

For freely behaving experiments, all animals also received implantation of plate screws in the parietal bone region, and received electroencephalogram (EEG) and electromyogram (EMG) electrodes for EEG recordings and EMG recording of the neck muscle. Additionally, a reference electrode was placed in the occipital bone region to minimize wave noise, and a ground electrode was placed subcutaneously in the anterior chest region.

After all surgical procedures, fiber optics and electrodes were fixed using dental cement (Embrace wetbond, Pulpdent Corporation, Cat #231215) and mice were placed in individual cages and treated with Ketofen (7 mg/kg/day for 2 days), monitored daily, and maintained 5–6 days for acclimatation and functional experiments. The AAVs, fiber optics or electrodes did not produce any readily observable behavioral effects.

Ventilation and EEG/EMG recordings

Twenty-one days after AAVs injections, mice were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane (5%, <30 s), carefully tethered to the fiber optic cable connected to a blue (447 nm) laser and DPSS driver, and connected to the EEG/EMG cables (at 7 a.m.) and allowed at minimum 2 h to acclimate to the chamber environment. Recordings began after acclimatization (at 9 a.m.). Three consecutive days prior to experimental procedures, animals were acclimatized for 1 h/day.

EEG/EMG

Continuous recordings were obtained via a slip ring (Air Precision, Le Pressis Robinson) that enabled the mice to move freely. The signals were amplified by 10,000× (CyberAmp 380, Axon Instruments, California, USA), with EEG signals filtered between 1–70 Hz and EMG signals between 10 and 300 Hz. The data were sampled at 1 kHz per channel and recorded using a Cambridge digitizer (micro1401, CED, Cambridge, UK) and Spike 2 software (version 7.07, CED). EEG and EMG data was classified as wakefulness, rapid eye movement sleep (REMS) or non-rapid eye movement sleep (NREMS) based on the following criteria: Wakefulness was identified by sustained muscle activity in the EMG with EEG patterns showing low-amplitude, high-frequency waves; NREMS was marked by a decline in muscle tone and exhibited medium-amplitude, low-frequency waves in EEG readings; and REMS was distinguished by muscle atonia and EEG patterns displaying low-amplitude, high-frequency activity. In addition to the EEG/EMG recordings, we also confirmed the sleep-wake states by video recording the animals throughout the experiments.

Baseline EEG/EMG recordings were acquired 1 h prior to any photostimulation and classified as baseline EMG when there was resting muscle activity level when the animal is not moving, and baseline EMG defined by the normal ongoing activity pattern for a given state as described below. All EEG/EMG recordings were segmented into short and consecutive 4-s windows, referred to as epochs. Each epoch was automatically scored according to characteristic patterns in the EEG and EMG signals as follows: wakefulness was identified by low-amplitude (50–150 µV), mixed-frequency EEG activity combined with high muscle tone in the EMG. NREM sleep was scored by high-amplitude, delta (0.5–4 Hz)-frequency dominated EEG activity, and sustained decrease in EMG activity below 40% of baseline wake EMG. REM sleep is characterized by low-amplitude, theta (5–8 Hz)-rich EEG signals together with muscle atonia in the EMG. All sleep-wake scoring was performed in a semi-automated manner and subsequently confirmed by an experimenter blinded to experimental conditions. Agreement between video-based behavioral assessment and EEG/EMG scoring exceeded 95% ensuring reliable and reproducible classification.

In this study, an “arousal” event was defined as a rapid transition from NREM or REM sleep to wakefulness, characterized by (1) a sudden increase in EMG activity exceeding 50% of baseline, (2) EEG desynchronization into mixed-frequency activity accompanied by a reduction in signal amplitude, and (3) observable movement or changes in posture in the video recordings. Once arousals were identified using these combined physiological and behavioral criteria, the timing and probability of events were quantified relative to the onset of the transition.

Ventilation

Pulmonary ventilation (VE, µl/g/min) measurements were conducted as outlined in the literature55–57. In summary, respiratory rate (fR, respiratory frequency) and tidal volume (VT, µl/g) were assessed using whole-body plethysmography. This method involved a sealed and constantly ventilated chamber with 1–2 L/min of air (21% oxygen balanced with nitrogen), maintaining stable temperature and humidity (21 ± 0.5 °C and ±10%, respectively). Pressure transducers recorded small changes in pressure inside the chamber to obtain various respiratory cycle parameters. We also calculated the regularity score as the ratio of the standard deviation (SD) to the mean of fR55. Calibration of volume was performed for each ventilation measurement by injecting a calibrated air volume (1 mL) into the chamber. Flow controllers were adjusted for hypoxia (8% O2 balanced with N2) challenges. The pressure signals were amplified, filtered, and recorded for offline analysis using PowerLab software (PowerLab 16/30, ML880/P; ADInstruments, New Zealand)55.

A sigh was identified when the inspiratory peak increased by twice the amplitude of a eupneic breath and followed by an apnea (Fig. S1). EEG and EMG recordings were conducted within the plethysmography chamber for 6 continuous hours, starting at 9:00 a.m. Offline analysis of the sleep-wake cycle and ventilation were performed in a blinded manner using LabChart8 software.

Optogenetic stimulations

Once the animals showed calm behavior and a continuous stable respiratory rate, we recorded ventilation and EEG/EMG for 30 min. After this baseline period (no stimulation), optogenetic stimulations were performed by the experimenter randomly using a 7–8 mW laser power. The stimulations were performed for 5–10 s every 30 min. We attempted multiple stimulations per day of experiments to obtain events where animals were awake or sleeping in REMS and NREMS. We were only able to distinguish those moments in our post-recording analyses. Subtle non-specific effects were observed in cre(-/-) mice when laser powers are greater than 8 mW9. Therefore, as controls for our optogenetic photostimulation experiments, we used cre(-/-) animals submitted to the same viral injections, optic fiber and EEG/EMG electrodes implants, and optogenetic stimulations as described above, and did not observe changes in any measured breathing parameters (N = 5, Fig. 1).

Fiber photometry

Fiber photometry recordings were acquired after optical fiber was connected to the implanted fiber via a ferrule sleeve. GCaMP6s or sRGECO fluorescence was excited using three sinusoidal LED sources: a 531 Hz signal (M470F3, Thorlabs) bandpass-filtered at 470 ± 20 nm, a 531 Hz signal (M470F3, Thorlabs) bandpass-filtered at 590 ± 20 nm and a 211 Hz signal (M405FP1, Thorlabs) bandpass-filtered at 405 ± 10 nm (filter cube: Doric FMC4; LED driver: DC4104). The 470 and 590 nm excitation evoked Ca²⁺-dependent fluorescence, while the 405 nm excitation provided a Ca²⁺-independent isosbestic control signal. Before each recording, both excitation channels were applied for 180 s to minimize baseline drift. Laser intensity at the fiber tip was adjusted to ~50 μW daily. Emitted fluorescence was isolated with a 525 ± 25 nm bandpass filter, detected using a femtowatt silicon photoreceiver (2151, Newport), and recorded by a real-time processor (TDT RZ5P). The envelopes of the 531 Hz and 211 Hz signals were extracted in real time using the Synapse software (TDT) at a sampling rate of 1017.25 Hz. For photostimulation, a 565 nm LED was applied at ~2.5 μW intensity at the fiber tip, while signals were simultaneously recorded from VRC.

Data were analyzed and isosbestic signal was subtracted from the Ca²⁺-dependent signal, and baseline drift from photobleaching was corrected by fitting a double exponential curve to the raw trace. The data was normalized by subtracting the median value and dividing by the absolute median of the isosbestic signal. The resulting trace was z-scored relative to the mean and standard deviation across the entire recording. Mean z-scores were compared across corresponding time periods based on stimulus duration or experimentally defined neural response intervals.

Electrophysiology

Twenty-one days following AAV-CAG-FLEX-jGCaMP8m-WPRE injections into the VRC of Aldh1l1Cre mice, respiratory parameters in all animals were recorded electrophysiologically and two-photon calcium imaging recordings were taken (Fig. 6A). Initially, general anesthesia was induced using urethane (1.5 g/kg, i.p., Sigma Aldrich, USA). To access the VMS, a tube was place in the trachea (24 G) allowing the animals to spontaneously breathe 100% O2 for the remainder of the surgery and part of the experimental protocol. Core temperature was maintained through a water heating system (PolyScience, USA) built into the surgical table. The neck muscles were removed and a basioccipital craniotomy was performed. Pial vessels in the ventral marginal layer were located at 1.0 mm lateral from the basilar artery and 0.4 mm rostral to the most rostral portion of the hypoglossal nerve. A cover glass (3 mm round and 0.13 mm thickness, Warner Instruments, Germany) was placed covering the VMS.

Bipolar electrodes were placed to record diaphragm muscle (DiaEMG) activity with a signal amplification of 10,000×. Anesthesia depth was continuously assessed by testing the absence of withdrawal response; a supplemental dose of 0.01 mL of urethane was given to maintain adequate anesthetic depth. All analog data (EMG activity) were recorded via a Micro1401 digitizer (Cambridge Electronics Design, UK) and processed with Spike2 software (version 6). Integrated electromyography activity (ᶘEMG) was obtained through rectification, data was further processed using a band pass filtered (200–700 Hz, 40 Hz transition gap) and smoothing (σ = 0.002 s). DiaEMG was determined by averaging ᶘEMG over 30 s, then normalized by assigning a baseline value recorded at room air. Raw data and differences (∆) between baseline (normoxia) and hypoxia (10% O2) were reported.

In a separate group of animals previously injected with AAV-PHP.eB-CAG-DIO-ChR2(H134R)-eYFP, we performed microinjections into the VRC of VU0134992 (a Kir4.1 blocker, 20 µM) alone or in addition to MRS2279 (a P2Y1-antagonist, 20 µM) diluted in vehicle (DMSO 3% in saline, NaCl 0.9%) with beads microespheres (2%). The drugs were applied sequentially on the VMS surface after washout with sterile saline with at least 40 min interval between drug deliveries. The first 10 min, we performed sequential optostimulations directly in the VRC with a fiber optic placed closely to the ventral surface, with 1 min interval between light pulses (5 s each); the remaining 30 min after drug applications, we measured the frequency of sighs naturally generated. In total, the recording were acquired for ~2 h. The dilutions used in our experiments were selected based on previous studies.

Two additional experimental groups also received a bilateral guide cannula (27G/C.C 2.0; Model 62071, RWD Life Science Inc.) implanted in the skull with dental cement, and microinjections into the VRC of a cocktail of VU0134992 and MRS2279 was preloaded into a bilateral internal cannula (Model 62271, RWD Life Science Inc.) connected to a 1µl-Hamilton Syringe.

Two-photon astrocytes calcium imaging

Imaging was performed on a Bruker Investigator microscope with Prairie-View imaging software, coupled to a Spectra-Physics InSight X3 tunable laser (680–1300 nm). Laser excitation wavelength for GCaMP was set to 920 nm, and the emitted signals were collected through 525/70 nm. A 10× (0.30 numerical aperture, NA) air objective (FN26.5, Olympus; UPLFLN10X2) was first used to navigate through the medulla. We zoomed into the GCaMP expressing spots in VMS and acquire imaging data at 0.226 µm/pixel resolution. Sequential stacks of images over 2 min was acquired for each trial was acquired from the VMS at a depth of 100–300 mm.

Electrophysiology and two-photon cross-correlation analyses

Each round of recordings consisted of ROIs generated at 600 time points over 4 min (~0.4-s intervals). The average Dia amplitude was calculated (~0.6 mV), and we defined a sigh as an inspiratory peak that exceeded twice the amplitude of an eupneic breath. We plotted each ROI vs. the Dia EMG amplitude for each recorded cell and correlated the unitary ROI with the time difference between the peak of each cell activity and the sigh generation. During hypoxia, we measured the multiunitary ROI of the region and correlated it with the time difference between population peak activity and peak Dia EMG. We also plotted the ROI versus Dia EMG for eupneic activity, but in contrast to sighs, no correlation was found.

CaMPARI photoconvertion

CaMPARI photoconversion was performed in freely behaving mice expressing the calcium integrator CaMPARI under the control of the synapsin promoter. Animals received unilateral injections of a AAV-PHP.eB-CAG-DIO-ChR2(H134R)-eYFP and AAV_hsyn_NES-his-CaMPARI2-WPRE-SV40 into the ventral respiratory column (VRC), followed by implantation of two optic fiber positioned above the injection site. After 3 weeks of viral expression, mice were subjected to photoconversion experiments. Photoconversion was achieved by simultaneous illumination with 405 and 470 nm light delivered through the implanted optic fibers while animals were awake and freely moving. Light stimulation was applied randomly for 2 s each epoch over 30 min. The 405 nm light induces irreversible conversion of CaMPARI from its green to red fluorescent form selectively in neurons exhibiting elevated intracellular calcium during the illumination period. At the end of the experimental session, animals were deeply anesthetized as describe below. Neurons were classified as non-converted (green), partially converted (yellow), or fully converted (red) based on fluorescence intensity ratios, allowing identification of neuronal populations active during the photoconversion window.

Histology

At the end of the experiments, all animals were deeply anesthetized with 4% isoflurane in 96% oxygen. They were then perfused through the ascending aorta with 20 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4), followed by 20 ml of 4% phosphate-buffered paraformaldehyde (0.1 M; pH 7.4; Electron Microscopy Sciences, Fort Washington, PA). The brains were removed and stored in the perfusion fixative at 4 °C for 4 h, then transferred to 20% sucrose for 8 h. Coronal sections (25 μm) were cut from the brains using a cryostat and stored in a cryoprotectant solution at −20 °C (20% glycerol plus 30% ethylene glycol in 50 ml phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) prior to histological processing. All histochemical procedures were performed using free-floating sections55.

Immunohistochemistry: ChAT was detected using a polyclonal goat anti-ChAT antibody (AB144P; Millipore; 1:100), eYFP was detected using a polyclonal chicken anti-GFP (1020; Ave Labs; 1:500), TH was detected using a polyclonal rabbit anti-TH (AB152; Millipore; 1:1000), GFAP was detected using a polyclonal rabbit anti-GFAP (Z0334; DAKO; 1:1000), c-Fos was detected using a monoclonal rabbit anti-c-Fos (MA5-15055; Invitrogen; 1:10,000), all diluted in PB containing 2% normal donkey serum (017-000-121, Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories) and 0.3% Triton X-100, and sections were incubated in it for 24 h. The sections were then rinsed in PBS and incubated for 2 h with secondary antibodies: Alexa 488 goat anti-chicken (A11039; Invitrogen; 1:500), Alexa 594 donkey anti-rabbit (A-21207; Invitrogen; 1:500), Alexa 647 donkey anti-rabbit (A31573; Invitrogen; 1:500) and Alexa 647 donkey anti-goat (A21447; Invitrogen; 1:500). Control experiments confirmed that no labeling occurred when primary antibodies were omitted. Some sections were also stained with DAPI for 1 min, rinsed with PBS, mounted on slides in rostrocaudal sequential order, dried, and covered with Fluoromount (00-4958-02; Thermo Fisher). Coverslips were affixed with nail polish.

Sections were also examined to confirm that fiber optics were placed in the intended location. Mice with incorrectly positioned fiber optics were excluded from the analysis. According to the Paxinos and Franklin mouse atlas58, for targeting the preBötC, the unilateral tips were located just dorsal to the nucleus ambiguus near Bregma level −6.84 mm, about 1 mm from the midline, and about 400 μm above the marginal layer (Fig. S2). For the RTN, the fiber tips were near the facial nucleus at Bregma level −6.64 mm, about 1 mm from the midline, and about 250 μm above the marginal layer (Fig. S7).

In situ hybridization: We utilized the RNAscope™ Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics). Initially, brain slices were incubated in 0.1 M PBS for 5 min, followed by a 10-min incubation with RNAscope Hydrogen Peroxide (#322381) at room temperature (RT). This was succeeded by three washes with PBS, each lasting 2 min. The tissue was then treated with Protease IV (#322381) for 30 min at RT, followed by another 2-min incubation in 0.1 M PBS. Next, the tissue was incubated with Aldh1l1 and Fos probes for 2 h at 40 °C, followed by a 2-min wash with PBS. For hybridization, the tissue underwent sequential incubations with RNAscope Multiplex Detection Reagents (#323110) consisting of FL v2 Amp 1, for 30 min at 40 °C, with a 5-min PBS wash after each step. The tissue was then incubated with RNAscope Multiplex FL for 30 min at 40 °C, followed by a 5-min PBS wash. To develop the HRP-C1 signal and HRP-C2 signal, the tissue was incubated with RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 HRP-C1 or HRP-C2 for 15 min at 40 °C, followed by a 5-min wash with PBS. Finally, the tissue was treated with TSA Plus fluorescein (FP1168; amplification reagent, 1:250) for 30 min at 40 °C, followed by a 5-min PBS wash. The tissue was then incubated with RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 HRP blocker for 15 min at 40 °C, followed by a 2-min wash with PBS. All slides were mounted with Fluoromount (00-4958-02; Thermo Fisher), and coverslips were secured using nail polish.

Cell counting, imaging, and data analysis

A VS120-S6-W Virtual Slide Scanner (Olympus) was used to scan all the sections. Red signals (mCherry) were reassigned to magenta as an artificial pseudocolor for clarity. To avoid bias, photomicrography and counting were performed by a single researcher who was blinded. ImageJ (version 1.41; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) was used for cell counting or area measurements, and Canvas software (ACD Systems, Victoria, Canada, version 9.0) was used for line drawings. Photomicrographies in green or red were artificially converted to cyan or magenta, respectively. A one-in-two series of 25-μm brain sections was used per mouse, meaning that each section analyzed was 50 μm apart. We reported the analyzes as mean ± SEM. Section alignment for the RTN, preBötC, Nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS), Locus coeruleus (LC) and Kölliker-fuse/Parabrachial complex (KF/PB) was based on a reference section, following previous studies from our laboratory and the Paxinos and Franklin (2012) mouse atlas58.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using Sigma Stat version 11.0 software (Jandel Corporation, Point Richmond, CA). Statistical tests included t-tests with follow-up Mann-Whitney U tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunn’s comparison test, or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test, as appropriate. Data are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.
