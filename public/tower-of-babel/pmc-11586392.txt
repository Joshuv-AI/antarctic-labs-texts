Descriptive analysis of incidence, demographic characteristics, and survival outcomes of soft-tissue sarcoma of the extremities in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Wazzan Aljuhani, Abdullah Alanazi, Eyad Alshehri, Mohammed K Alageel, Emad Masuadi, Mostafa Zolaly, Abdulrahman Bobsait
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoft-tissue sarcomas are uncommon, aggressive, and histologically heterogeneous, malignant tumors. Soft-tissue tumor types have different age and anatomical site distributions. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and incidence of soft-tissue sarcomas of the extremities to facilitate early diagnosis and intervention in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. This retrospective study included all patients diagnosed with primary soft-tissue sarcomas of the extremities and pelvis between January 2006 and December 2015. The data were obtained from the Saudi Cancer Registry. Means and confidence intervals were reported for numerical variables, whereas the frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. The chi-squared test and analysis of variance were used to test the associations between categorical numerical variables. Cox regression analysis was used to determine the hazard ratios for each sarcoma type. Of the 659 patients, 145 (22%) were aged 19–30 years, 365 (55.4%) were men, and 411 (62.4%) had lower limb sarcoma. Most of the tumors (54.3%) were localized. The incidence of sarcoma ranged from 2 to 2.9 cases per one million (average, 2.4 cases per one million). Moreover, liposarcoma was the most commonly diagnosed (n = 135; 20.5%), followed by undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (n = 87; 13.2%). The incidence of soft-tissue sarcomas in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia were lower than those in Western countries. However, the distribution of soft-tissue sarcoma subtypes was similar to those in other countries. Background

Soft-tissue sarcomas are uncommon, aggressive, and histologically heterogeneous malignant tumors of mesenchymal origin that emerge from soft tissues such as fibrous tissues, deep skin tissues, nerves, fat, and blood vessels1. Soft-tissue sarcomas have more than 50 distinct subtypes and can occur in any part of the body, but they most commonly occur in the lower (45%) and upper limbs (15%)2. Different types of soft-tissue sarcomas are associated with different ages and have varying anatomical site distributions. For instance, leiomyosarcoma is most commonly detected in the retroperitoneum and abdominal cavity3. On the other hand, liposarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas are most common in the legs. In contrast, epithelioid sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and clear cell sarcoma most frequently occur in the upper limbs. Regarding the age distributions, rhabdomyosarcoma is detected more frequently in children and young adults; synovial sarcoma is more common in young adults; and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (previously called malignant fibrous histiocytoma) and liposarcoma are more common in older adults1. Soft-tissue sarcomas may be associated with multiple factors, including certain genetic disorders such as Li-Fraumeni syndrome, neurofibromatosis, exposure to carcinogens, infections such as HIV, and radiation impact. However, only a few patients have definite causes and risk factors4.

There is a direct association between the size and outcomes of soft-tissue sarcomas. Larger tumors imply a worse prognosis, and the clinical stage is the most important predictor of prognosis5. Superficially located tumors (dermis and subcutaneous tissue) have a relatively better prognosis than deep tumors (intermuscular/intramuscular and retroperitoneal) with similar histological types. This difference is probably due to the early detection of superficial tumors6,7. Surgical resection is considered the cornerstone of treatment for soft-tissue sarcomas, as it preserves function to the greatest extent possible through limb-sparing surgery and reconstruction techniques. Amputation is necessary in exceptional cases. However, treatment strategies for patients with poor prognosis and anatomical individuality are challenging8.

The annual incidence of soft-tissue sarcomas is 4.5/100,000 in Europe9. Approximately 70–80% of patients are diagnosed at a locally advanced stage of the disease; the median age at diagnosis is 58 years10. However, lower incidence and median age of diagnosis have been reported for the upper than for the lower extremities9. Approximately 50% of soft-tissue sarcomas in the upper extremities occur in the shoulder or upper-arm region; 30–40% occur in the elbow or forearm; and 10–20%, occur in the wrist or hand11,12. Soft-tissue sarcomas are considered rare. However, approximately 13,130 new cases (0.7% of all new cancer cases) and 5,350 deaths (0.9% of all cancer deaths) were reported in 2020, with a 5-year survival rate of 64.7%. Men have a slightly higher risk of soft-tissue sarcoma than women13.

In a study conducted in the United States of America that compared the improvements in the overall survival rate, the 5-year overall survival for soft-tissue sarcomas in the extremities improved progressively during the study period from 28% in 1991–1996 to 40% in 1997–2003 and 62% in 2004–201014. However, there is a paucity of data on the epidemiology, treatment patterns, clinical management, and clinical outcomes of soft-tissue sarcoma in the Middle East. Memon et el. reported comparable proportions of male and female adult patients treated for soft-tissue sarcoma. The patients were also predominantly middle-aged. The most frequent anatomical location of soft-tissue sarcoma is the limbs15. Due to the significant lack of research, the present study aimed to assess the prevalence and incidence of soft-tissue sarcomas in the extremities the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA). Such studies would facilitate the early diagnosis and treatment of soft-tissue sarcomas in the region.

Methods

This retrospective study included all patients diagnosed with primary soft-tissue sarcomas of the extremities and musculoskeletal sarcomas of the pelvis between January 2006 and December 2015. Patients with carcinoma, skin cancer, bone sarcoma, or metastatic sarcoma were excluded. Patients diagnosed with carcinoma, skin cancer, bone sarcoma, and metastatic sarcoma were excluded.

Data were obtained from the Saudi Cancer Registry (SCR). The SCR collects data from all hospitals in the country, including private, military, and health ministry hospitals, through five regional hospitals. Data analyses and periodic reporting were conducted at the main office in Riyadh. The information provided by the SCR included patient characteristics and demographics such as age, sex, place of residence, tumor site, laterality, grade, extension of the tumor, year of diagnosis, tumor type, and survival status. Diagnoses were coded according to the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (Third Edition [ICD-O-3]).

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 23). Means and confident intervals were reported for numerical variables, whereas frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. The chi-squared test was used to test the association between categorical variables. Analysis of variance was used to test the associations between numerical variables. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to generate survival curves for each sarcoma type. Cox regression analysis was used to determine hazard ratios for each sarcoma type.

The incidence was calculated as the number of cases per million people, and the population data for the KSA for each year were obtained from the General Authority for Statistics. Ethical approval was obtained from King Abdullah International Medical Research Center, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (approval number: RC20\229\R). Patient confidentiality and anonymity were ensured. The data were only used by the research team. Informed consent was not required due to the retrospective nature of the study. Further, the study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to assess the demographic characteristics of soft-tissue sarcomas in the KSA. Our findings provide valuable insights into the epidemiology of soft-tissue sarcomas in the country and can serve as a basis for comparisons with other regions globally, specifically the Middle East.

The average incidence of soft-tissue sarcomas in the KSA was 2.4 cases per one million (range, 2–2.9 cases per one million). These incidence were notably lower than those reported in the United States (6.3 cases per 100,000 persons per year)13and Europe (4.0 cases per 100,000 persons per year)16. However, the incidence in our study were comparable to those reported for other Middle Eastern countries, which may be due to shared genetic or environmental factors. For example, an Iranian study based on a national cancer registry that included 2,593 patients reported an incidence of 6.3 cases per one million for soft-tissue sarcomas17. Differences in healthcare systems, population demographics, and reporting standards may contribute to variations in incidence across different countries and regions.

The age distribution in our study revealed that soft-tissue sarcomas was more prevalent among younger age groups, with 22% of the cases occurring in the 19–30 years age group. This contrasts with the age distribution reported for Western countries, where soft-tissue sarcomas is more common in older populations18. This difference in age distribution can be attributed to the overall younger population in the KSA relative to those in Western countries. Our study also found a male predominance (55.4%), which is consistent with the reports of other studies conducted in the Middle East and globally13,16,17,19,20. In contrast, a European study showed that the incidence of soft-tissue sarcomas was higher in women due to the inclusion of uterine and breast soft-tissue sarcomas9. The reason for the male predominance remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Most cases occurred in the central region, followed by those in the Western and eastern regions (32%, 25%, and 21%, respectively). This is mostly attributed to the population and hospital distribution, as the central region is the most populated region in the KSA, followed by the Western and eastern regions. The most common soft-tissue sarcoma subtypes were liposarcoma (20.5%), malignant fibrous histiocytoma (13.2%), synovial sarcoma (11.1%), fibrosarcoma (9.9%), and leiomyosarcoma (8.2%). These findings are consistent with those of other studies conducted in Western countries18,19and the Middle East21, indicating that the distribution of soft-tissue sarcoma subtypes in the KSA is similar to that in other regions.

Regarding survival rates, patients with fibrosarcoma and liposarcoma had the longest mean survival durations (7.75 and 7.74 years, respectively), whereas those with sarcoma NOS and rhabdomyosarcoma had the shortest mean survival durations (2.68 and 2.96 years, respectively). These findings are consistent with the results of previous studies conducted in other countries16,22,23, confirming the aggressive nature of certain sarcoma subtypes and the need for improved treatment approaches for these patients.

This study has several limitations. First, our analysis relied on retrospective data from the SCR, which may be subject to bias, inaccuracies, and potential data limitations. Second, the 10-year study duration may not be sufficient to capture the long-term trends of the incidence and survival of soft-tissue sarcomas. Furthermore, the lower incidence may be attributed to underreporting of SCR by hospitals, lower population, geogenetic factors, and risk factors that need to be assessed locally. Moreover, the grading of sarcomas was unknown for most cases, which may have affected the interpretation of the results. Future research should address these limitations by using prospective study designs, larger sample sizes, and longer follow-ups.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive overview of the epidemiology of soft-tissue sarcomas in the KSA. The incidence of soft-tissue sarcomas in KSA were lower than those in Western countries, and the distribution of soft-tissue sarcomas subtypes was similar to that in other countries. The survival rates identified in our study highlight the need for continued research and targeted interventions to improve outcomes for patients with soft-tissue sarcoma, particularly for those with more aggressive subtypes such as sarcoma NOS and rhabdomyosarcoma.
