Abstract
We study a signature-driven numerical scheme to solve multi-dimensional linear-quadratic (LQ) stochastic control problems. Using that linear signature functionals are dense in the natural class of admissible controls, we show that our approach turns the original LQ problem into a deterministic convex quadratic polynomial optimisation. To underpin a numerical approach based on truncated signatures, we prove that the problem’s value function can be approximated by finite-dimensional polynomial approximations when the truncation levels are chosen sufficiently high. Remarkably, our numerical experiments show very decent accuracy already for small truncation levels. Key tools for our analysis are (i) the algebraic representation of controlled stochastic differential equations and the associated cost function as linear functionals of the path signatures of the driving noise, (ii) the convergence of the truncated linear functionals, and (iii) the density of signature controls.

Introduction

Stochastic optimal control is classically approached by characterising the associated value function. In sufficiently regular Markovian settings, the dynamic programming principle leads to a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) partial differential equation (PDE)
[
10
,
36
]
, alternatively, the Pontryagin maximum principle yields characterisations in terms of backward stochastic differential equations
[
41
,
12
]
. These routes are powerful but quickly become challenging in high dimensions, path-dependent settings, or with lack of regularity. Although there are results for optimal linear-quadratic (LQ) control problems driven by fractional Brownian motion
[
30
,
21
,
22
]
, these often require fractional integrals to obtain the optimal controls.

Over the last decade, machine learning methods have brought renewed optimism for high-dimensional control, largely by targeting the HJB equation (or related PDE objects) through function approximation
[
40
]
. While these developments have produced impressive results in certain regimes, they still inherit the conceptual bottleneck of the HJB route: one approximates the value function first, and only then recovers the optimal control (or its approximation). This motivates complementary approaches that parametrize controls directly in a rich, high-dimensional feature space and solve the resulting optimisation problem without passing through a PDE.

In this paper, we pursue such an alternative by using signatures of the driving noise to parametrize admissible controls. The signature of a path, introduced by Chen
[
16
]
and developed systematically in rough path theory
[
32
]
, is a collection of iterated integrals which provides a faithful and algebraically structured representation of the path
[
27
,
11
]
. In particular, linear functionals of (time-augmented) signatures form an algebra
[
32
]
and enjoy strong approximation properties on suitable path spaces, making them a natural candidate for constructing expressive families of non-anticipative functionals. In stochastic analysis, iterated stochastic integrals underpin stochastic Taylor expansions
[
37
,
38
,
31
]
, and signature theory consolidates these expansions into a unified algebraic framework in which truncation and polynomial structure can be handled systematically.

The idea of restricting to controls that are linear functionals of signatures (or expected signatures) has recently been explored in concrete stochastic optimisation tasks such as optimal execution
[
29
,
13
]
. A key theoretical step was taken in
[
7
]
, which proves convergence of value functions when the objective functions are bounded in a way that permits dominated convergence arguments. While this yields a clean theory and accommodates rougher drivers, the boundedness requirement rules out the canonical LQ setting, despite LQ problems being a standard benchmark class. Nevertheless, the LQ structure suggests that a signature approach should be feasible if one can represent both the controlled state and the cost in a tractable signature-based form. Thus, our goal in this paper is to develop a rigorous signature-driven numerical scheme for multi-dimensional LQ stochastic control.

Our starting point is that linear signature functionals are dense in the natural class of admissible controls for the LQ problem driven by Brownian noise, based on the recent density results in
[
15
]
. This allows us to restrict attention to signature controls without losing optimality in the limit. The second ingredient is an algebraic representation viewpoint, inspired by ideas sketched in
[
29
]
: by working with time-augmented signatures and their tensor algebra, we represent the controlled state process (at least for Brownian drivers) via an infinite-dimensional linear functional acting on the signature of the driving noise, and we express the quadratic cost functional in the same algebraic language. The last ingredient is the celebrated Fawcett formula which established a closed-form expression for the expected signature of Brownian motion
[
23
,
34
,
35
]
. As a result, once the control is parameterised as a linear functional of the (truncated) signature, the original stochastic control problem reduces to a deterministic convex quadratic polynomial optimisation in finitely many parameters.

To support a practical numerical method, we truncate (i) the tensor representation of the state at some level
L
, and (ii) the signature features used to parametrize controls up to level
M
. A central theoretical question is whether such finite-dimensional truncations yield value convergence as
L,M\to\infty
. Here we leverage recent growth and convergence estimates for tensor/signature expansions (in particular the growth estimates developed in
[
1
]
) to prove a rigorous consistency result: for high truncation levels, the value function of the truncated polynomial optimisation approximates the true LQ value function. Importantly, this provides a convergence statement at the level of value functions, not merely almost-sure approximation of candidate controls.

Our numerical experiments confirm that the method we propose converges to the known LQ solution and, strikingly, achieves good accuracy for small truncation levels. Finally, the methodology itself is not tied to Brownian drivers: it extends naturally to other signals such as fractional Brownian motion, where PDE methods are less directly applicable. Establishing a fully rigorous approximation theorem in such settings would require appropriate signature growth estimates for the chosen driver, which lies beyond the scope of the present paper. Nevertheless, our experiments indicate that the signature-driven optimisation remains effective in these non-Markovian regimes.
In summary, we make the following contributions:

(i)

We develop a signature-driven numerical scheme for multi-dimensional LQ stochastic control by parametrizing controls as linear functionals of (time-augmented) path signatures.

(ii)

Using density of linear signature functionals in the admissible control class, we justify the signature-control as an asymptotically non-restrictive approximation.

(iii)

We show that, under signature truncation, the LQ problem reduces to a deterministic convex quadratic polynomial optimisation problem.

(iv)

Using tensor/signature convergence and growth estimates, we prove consistency of the finite-dimensional truncations and obtain value-function approximation results as truncation levels increase.

(v)

We provide numerical experiments demonstrating convergence to the known LQ solution with good accuracy at low truncation levels, and we discuss extensions beyond Brownian drivers (e.g. fractional Brownian motion).

3
Problem formulation

In this section, our aim is to express, algebraically, and as a (potentially infinite) linear combination of coefficients involving the driving noise signatures: (i) the controlled multidimensional state process and (ii) the linear-quadratic cost functions. Afterwards, we propose a truncation method to approximate the controlled system and the associated cost with a finite sum. We will show that optimising for these finite-dimensional approximations is a good substitute to the actual infinite-dimensional problem.

3.3
Linear-quadratic cost functional and its algebraic properties

Next, we are going to express the linear-quadratic cost function as a pairing with the driving noise signature. Additionally, we are also going to describe the algebraic and convexity properties of the cost function.

3.3.1
Tensor representation of the cost functional

For linear state dynamics

as in (
13

\displaystyle J(u)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\Big\{\big(X_{t}^{u}\big)^{\top}\,A(t)\,X_{t}^{u}\,+u_{t}^{\top}\,B(t)\,u_{t}

(39)

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt+2\,C(t)^{\top}\,X_{t}^{u}+2\,D(t)^{\top}\,u_{t}\,\Big\}\mathrm{d}t+\big(X_{T}^{u}\big)^{\top}\,E\,X_{T}^{u}+2\,G^{\top}\,X_{T}^{u}\Bigg],

(40)

for continuous matrix valued mappings
A,B,C,D
on
[0,T]
and matrices
E,G
such that
A(t),E\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are symmetric, positive semi-definite,
B(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}

As we seek to minimize these costs, it is natural to focus on admissible controls

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{u:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{K}\,\bigg|\,u\text{ is ${\mathbb{F}}$-progressively measurable}

(41)

\displaystyle\hskip 150.00023pt\text{and }\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|u_{s}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]<\infty\bigg\}\,.

(42)

\displaystyle A(t):=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}A_{m}\,,\quad B(t):=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}B_{m}\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+2\,\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}D_{m}^{(k)}\,\frac{t^{m}}{m!}\,u^{(k)}_{t}\Bigg\}\,\mathrm{d}t

(55)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sum_{n,n^{\prime}=1}^{N}E^{(n,n^{\prime})}\,X^{u,(n)}_{T}\,X^{u,(n^{\prime})}_{T}+2\,\sum_{n=1}^{N}G^{(n)}\,X^{u,(n)}_{T}\Bigg]

(56)

\displaystyle=\langle\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle\,,

(57)

where
\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u})
is given by (

). As

is a linear combination of finitely-many tensors in

\displaystyle\langle\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle=\lim_{{\mathrm{L}}\to\infty}\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle\,.

(58)

Next, we seek to investigate the algebraic properties of the cost function

.

Lemma 7
.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}:\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}\to\big(T^{\textup{ext}}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{N}

(59)

(60)

1.

,

\mathtt{u}
.

For every

,

,
{\mathrm{L}}_{1},{\mathrm{L}}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, and linear map

, the expression

\left\langle P\Big(\big(L_{1}(\mathtt{u})^{(n)}\big)^{\leq{\mathrm{L}}_{1}}\shuffle\big(L_{2}(\mathtt{u})^{(n^{\prime})}\big)^{{\mathrm{L}}_{2}}\Big),G\right\rangle

To prove statement

(61)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(\underbrace{P_{2}\circ\dots\circ P_{2}}_{\text{${\mathrm{L}}$ times}})(L(\mathtt{u})))\,.

(62)

However, we have

\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathrm{L}}}^{N}(L(\mathtt{u}))=\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathrm{L}}}^{N}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{{\mathrm{L}}-1}(\underbrace{P_{2}\circ\dots\circ P_{2}}_{\text{$m$ times}})(P_{0})+(\underbrace{P_{2}\circ\dots\circ P_{2}}_{\text{$m$ times}}\circ\,P_{1})(\mathtt{u})\right)\,.

(63)

As

is a linear map acting on

\left\langle P\Big(\big(L_{1}(\mathtt{u})^{(n)}\big)^{\leq{\mathrm{L}}_{1}}\shuffle\big(L_{2}(\mathtt{u})^{(n^{\prime})}\big)^{{\mathrm{L}}_{2}}\Big),G\right\rangle

\displaystyle\left\langle\big(L_{1}(\mathtt{u})^{(n)}\big)^{\leq{\mathrm{L}}_{1}}\shuffle\big(L_{2}(\mathtt{u})^{(n^{\prime})}\big)^{{\mathrm{L}}_{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle

(64)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}|+|{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}|=|{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}|}\left\langle\big(L_{1}(\mathtt{u})^{(n)}\big)^{\leq{\mathrm{L}}_{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}\right\rangle\,\left\langle\big(L_{2}(\mathtt{u})^{(n^{\prime})}\big)^{\leq{\mathrm{L}}_{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}\right\rangle\,\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}\shuffle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}\rangle\,

(65)

for any word
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}
. From the previous statement, it follows that
\left\langle\big(L_{1}(\mathtt{u})^{(n)}\big)^{\leq{\mathrm{L}}_{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\big(L_{2}(\mathtt{u})^{(n^{\prime})}\big)^{\leq{\mathrm{L}}_{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}\right\rangle

\mathtt{u}
.

In light of our tensor equation (

) encoding the system dynamics, it is straightforward to see that the mapping

satisfies the conditions of Lemma

. As we can decompose

, the expression

so the actual cost functional is also a quadratic polynomial of the coefficients of

.

Corollary 8
.

Let

, the functions

3.3.2
Strict convexity of the cost functional

The linear-quadratic cost function
J(\cdot)
in (
40
) is strictly convex in the space of controls
{\mathcal{A}}
; automatically, it is also convex in the subspace
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{Sig}}
. As we are working with the tensor representation of the control, we will also need the cost function
\langle\mathtt{J}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
to be strictly convex in the space
\big(T((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}
. To show this, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

The empty word
\ell={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is the only
\ell\in T((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})
such that
\langle\ell,{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{t}\rangle=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.

Proof.

Let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=\left\{\ell\in T((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\,:\,\langle\ell,{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{t}\rangle=0\quad\forall t\in[0,T]\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-almost surely}\right\}\,.

(66)

Consequently,
\mathcal{N}
forms a sub-vector space of
T((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})
. Consider some
\ell\in\mathcal{N}
. For a word
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}
, let us denote

\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}}:=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{w}}}\in V_{m}}\ell^{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{w}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{w}}}\,.

(67)

Moreover, let us denote
W^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{a}}(j+1))}=W^{(j)}
for
j=1,\dots,D
. By Ito’s formula for pairings with Brownian signatures (see Theorem 3.3 in
[
1
]
for the extended pairing)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\ell,{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{t}\rangle

(68)

\displaystyle=\ell^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\boldsymbol{\phi}}}+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{1}}},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{u}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}u+\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{2}},\cdots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{\mathbf{D}}}\}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{u}\rangle\,\circ\mathrm{d}W_{u}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}})}

(69)

\displaystyle=\ell^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\boldsymbol{\phi}}}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{1}}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{2}},\cdots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{\mathbf{D}}}\}}\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{u}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}u+\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{2}},\cdots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{\mathbf{D}}}\}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{u}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}W_{u}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}})}\,.

(70)

Thus, if
\ell\in\mathcal{N}
, then
\ell^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\boldsymbol{\phi}}}=0
,
\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{1}}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{2}},\cdots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{\mathbf{D}}}\}}\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}}\in\mathcal{N}
, and
\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}}\in\mathcal{N}
for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{2}},\cdots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{\mathbf{D}}}\}
. This implies that
\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}}\in\mathcal{N}
for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{j}}\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{2}},\cdots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{\mathbf{D}}}\}
, which in turn implies that
\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}}\in\mathcal{N}
for any word
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}
. Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}
be the longest word such that
\ell^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}\neq 0
. Then, we would have
\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}}\in\mathcal{N}
; however,
\ell\,|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}}=\ell^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\boldsymbol{\phi}}
, implying
\ell^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}=0
, a contradiction. Thus,
\mathcal{N}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\}
.

Using Lemma
9
, we can show that the tensor representation of the cost functional inherits the strict convexity property from the original cost functional.

Lemma 10
.

The cost functions
\langle\mathtt{J}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
and
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
are strictly convex in
\left(T((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\right)^{K}
.

Proof.

The convexity of
\langle\mathtt{J}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
follows from the convexity of the actual cost function and Lemma
9
, so we only need to show the convexity of
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
. Let us consider any
\mathtt{u},\tilde{\mathtt{u}}\in\left(T((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\right)^{K}
such that
\mathtt{u}\neq\tilde{\mathtt{u}}
. Define
u:=\left\langle\mathtt{u},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\right\rangle
and
\tilde{u}:=\left\langle\tilde{\mathtt{u}},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\right\rangle
. From Lemma
9
, we have
u^{(k)}\neq\tilde{u}^{(k)}
. Denote by
X
and
\tilde{X}
the controlled systems associated with
u
and
\tilde{u}
respectively and take
\mathtt{X}
,
\tilde{\mathtt{X}}
as their tensor respective representation, i.e.
X=\langle\mathtt{X},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\rangle
and
\tilde{X}=\langle\tilde{\mathtt{X}},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\rangle
. Let us take
s\in[0,1]
and define

\hat{\mathtt{u}}:=s\,\mathtt{u}+(1-s)\,\tilde{\mathtt{u}},\quad\hat{u}:=s\,u+(1-s)\,\tilde{u}\,.

Then
\hat{u}^{(k)}=\left\langle\hat{\mathtt{u}}^{(k)},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\right\rangle
for all
k=1,\cdots,K
. Denote by
\hat{X}
the controlled system associated with
\hat{u}
and
\hat{\mathtt{X}}
as its tensor representation, i.e.
\hat{X}=\langle\hat{\mathtt{X}},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\rangle
. It follows that

\hat{X}=s\,X+(1-s)\,\tilde{X}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\mathtt{X}}=s\,\mathtt{X}+(1-s)\,\tilde{\mathtt{X}}\,.

By taking a truncation up to level
{\mathrm{L}}
, we have

\hat{\mathtt{X}}^{{\mathrm{L}}}=s\,\mathtt{X}^{{\mathrm{L}}}+(1-s)\,\tilde{\mathtt{X}}^{{\mathrm{L}}}\,.

From the linear-quadratic form of the performance criterion, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\hat{\mathtt{u}}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle\leq s\,\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle+(1-s)\,\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\tilde{\mathtt{u}}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle\,.

(71)

Moreover, as
B(t)
is continuous and strictly positive definite, the inequality above is strict if
s\in(0,1)
. Thus,
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
is strictly convex.

3.4
Convergence of the truncated problem and density results

In this section, we will prove that minimising the cost functional over
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{Sig}}
is the same as minimising it over the larger space
{\mathcal{A}}
. Moreover, we are going to propose a truncation method to approximate the infinite-dimensional optimisation problem by a finite-dimensional one while preserving convexity and consistency. The following theorem sums up the main findings in this section.

Theorem 11
.

For sufficiently large truncation levels
{\mathrm{L}},M
, the minimization of the truncated cost functional
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
over
\mathtt{u}\in\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}
is a proxy for the original linear quadratic optimization problem in the sense that

\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{{\mathrm{L}}\to\infty}\inf_{\mathtt{u}\in\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}}\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle=\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}}J(u)\,,

(72)

and

\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{{\mathrm{L}}\to\infty}\langle\mathtt{u}^{{\mathrm{L}},M},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\rangle=u^{*}\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\otimes\mathrm{d}t)

(73)

where
\mathtt{u}^{{\mathrm{L}},M}
denotes the minimizer of the truncated problem and
u^{*}
is the optimal control in the linear quadratic optimization problem.

To prove Theorem
11
, first we need the following result from
[
15
]
on the
L^{2}
-universality of linear functionals acting on Brownian signatures.

Theorem 12
.

For every
u\in{\mathcal{A}}
, there exists
(\mathtt{u}_{m}^{(k)})_{m=1}^{\infty}\subset T((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})
,
k=1,...,K
, such that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\max_{k=1,\dots,K}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\big|u_{t}^{(k)}-\langle\mathtt{u}_{m}^{(k)},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{t}\rangle\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=0\,.

(74)

Proof.

This follows directly from (
[
15
]
, Corollary 4.3.(i)). In fact, the density result still holds in the space of
L^{p}
-integrable processes for
p>1
.

Employing Theorem
12
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 13
.

We have that
\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}}J(u)=\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{Sig}}}J(u)\,.

Proof.

Let us choose any
u\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Then we can choose
(\mathtt{u}_{m}^{(k)})_{m=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{Sig}}
satisfying the statement of Theorem
12
and define
u_{m}
by
u_{m,t}^{(k)}:=\langle\mathtt{u}_{m}^{(k)},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{t}\rangle
. Due to the linear drift and volatility of
X
, we obtain

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}|X_{s}^{u}-X_{s}^{u_{m}}|^{2}\right]=0\,,

(75)

and thus from the linear-quadratic nature of the cost functional, we have
J(u_{m})\to J(u)
. Thus, the set of real numbers
\{J(u)\,|\,u\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{Sig}}\}
is dense in
\{J(u)\,|\,u\in{\mathcal{A}}\}
, and the equality follows.

Now, we are ready to prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem
11
.

Let us fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
. From Proposition
6
, Lemma
8
and
10
, if one restricts oneself to the space
\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}
, the coefficients of the (strictly convex) quadratic polynomial
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\cdot),{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}\rangle
converge for
{\mathrm{L}}\to\infty
to those of the strictly convex quadratic polynomial
\langle\mathtt{J}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
. The same is then true also for the respective infima, i.e.

\lim_{{\mathrm{L}}\to\infty}\inf_{\mathtt{u}\in\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}}\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle=\inf_{\mathtt{u}\in\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}}\langle\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle\,.

By taking
M\to\infty
, applying Proposition
6
and Corollary
13
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}

\displaystyle\lim_{{\mathrm{L}}\to\infty}\inf_{\mathtt{u}\in\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}}\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle=\lim_{M\to\infty}\inf_{\mathtt{u}\in\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}}\langle\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle

(76)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathtt{u}\in\big(T((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}}\langle\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle=\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{Sig}}}J(u)=\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}}J(u)\,.

(77)

In fact, when
L\to\infty
, it follows that even the minimiser
\mathtt{u}^{{\mathrm{L}},M}
of
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\cdot),{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}\rangle
over
\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}
converges to
\mathtt{u}^{M}
, the minimiser of
\langle\mathtt{J}(\cdot),{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}\rangle
over this domain.

Let us argue why
u^{M}:=\langle\mathtt{u}^{M},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\rangle
converges weakly to
u^{*}
, the minimiser of
J
over
{\mathcal{A}}
. From the above, we deduce that
J(u^{M})\to\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}}J(u)
. In particular,
(u^{M})_{M=1,2,\dots}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\otimes dt)
. Thus, by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there exists a weak accumulation point
\tilde{u}^{*}
and, by Mazur’s theorem, we can find
\tilde{u}^{M}\in\mathrm{conv}(u^{M},u^{M+1},\dots)
,
M=1,2,\dots
, converging to
\tilde{u}^{*}
in the strong
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\otimes dt)
-sense. By standard stability results of linear SDEs, the cost functional is continuous with respect to the latter convergence and so

\displaystyle J(\tilde{u}^{*})=\lim_{M}J(\tilde{u}^{M})\leq\lim_{M}J(u^{M})=\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}}J(u),

(78)

where the estimate is due to the convexity of
J
. It follows that
\tilde{u}^{*}
minimises
J
over
{\mathcal{A}}
. By uniqueness of this minimiser,
u^{M}
thus converges weakly in
L^{2}
to the minimiser of
J
over
{\mathcal{A}}
.

3.5
Beyond Brownian noise

Observe that the extended tensors
\mathtt{X}^{\mathtt{u}}
(associated with the controlled state) and
\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u})
(associated with the cost function) do not depend on the driving noise
W
. As such, heuristically, we may replace the driving noise in controlled linear systems (
13
) with a general process
Y
, such as a fractional Brownian motion, with signature
{\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}}
and hope that a similar result to Theorem
11
would still hold, i.e.

\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{{\mathrm{L}}\to\infty}\inf_{\mathtt{u}\in\big(T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})\big)^{K}}\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}}_{T}]\rangle\overset{?}{=}\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}}J(u)\,.

(79)

Here, there are two main challenges to establish this broader result:

1.

It is now unclear whether the process
\langle\mathtt{X}^{\mathtt{u}},{\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}}\rangle
is even well-defined; we need the truncated pairing
\langle\big(\mathtt{X}^{\mathtt{u}}\big)^{\leq{\mathrm{L}}},{\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}}\rangle
to converge to the process
X^{u}
in some sense, and the latter process also needs to have a proper meaning. But our result (Theorem
11
) to this effect makes essential use of the Brownian motion and its signature.

2.

There is currently limited
L^{p}
-universality result for linear functionals of
classical
signatures beyond Brownian motion. Such a result does exist for
robust
signatures
\lambda({\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}})
(see
[

); however, we are not aware of a way to find a tensor
\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u})
such that
\langle\mathtt{J}(\mathtt{u}),\lambda({\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}})\rangle=J(u)
for

L^{p}
-universality result by
[

]
for linear functionals of a class of rough paths which includes Gaussian and Markovian rough paths.

4.1
Brownian noise

In the Brownian case, we set
X_{0}=10
,
A(t)=C(t)=D(t)=G=0
, and the remaining model parameters of the state dynamics and cost functional to be one. By Fawcett’s formula (see
[
23
,
34
]
), we utilise the following closed-form equation for the expected Brownian signature,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{T^{n}}{n!}\,\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{2}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{2}}\right)^{\otimes n}\,.

(80)

Using the above formula, we are able to calculate all coefficients of the quadratic polynomial explicitly for the truncated problem. For
\mathtt{u}\in T^{M}((\mathbb{R}^{\bar{D}})^{*})
, let us write

\displaystyle\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathtt{u}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle=\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}\in V_{m_{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}\in V_{m_{2}}\\
m_{1}+m_{2}\leq M\end{subarray}}c^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}}\,\mathtt{u}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}}\,\mathtt{u}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}}\right)+\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}\in V_{m}\\
m\leq M\end{subarray}}c^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}\,\mathtt{u}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}}\right)+c_{0}.

(81)

Suppose that we have a black-box function that evaluates
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
. It is easy to check that

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\boldsymbol{\phi}}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle\,,\quad c^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}}=\frac{\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle+\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(-{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle}{2}-c_{0}\,,

(82)

\displaystyle c^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle-\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(-{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle}{2}\,,

(83)

\displaystyle c^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle-c^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}}-c^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}}-c_{0}}{2}\,\quad\forall{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{1}\neq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{v}}_{2}\,.

(84)

After obtaining the polynomial coefficients, we apply first-order condition to minimise
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}]\rangle
, thus obtaining the coefficients of the minimizer
\mathtt{u}^{{\mathrm{L}},M}
. Afterwards, we simulate
u^{{\mathrm{L}},M}=\langle\mathtt{u}^{{\mathrm{L}},M},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}\rangle
a total of
20,000
times by partitioning the time horizon into
1,000
equally-spaced intervals. As benchmark, we compute the actual optimal control (obtained by solving the HJB equation) and simulate the performance using the same randomness as in the signature approach. The performance of the strategies is reported in the first and second panel of Figure
1
.

First and second panel: Estimation of
J(u)
with 95% confidence interval (in this case
J(u^{*})=455

On the first and third panel of Figure
1
we observe that the level of state truncation (
{\mathrm{L}}
) strongly influences the optimization results. If one were to choose a low
{\mathrm{L}}
, solving the truncated problem would produce a highly suboptimal cost, no matter how complex the control model (more complexity = higher truncation
M
for the control). In fact, for a given
{\mathrm{L}}
, the cost value appears to decrease initially and then increase after around
M={\mathrm{L}}-1
. This is due to the fact that in Equation (
17
), the coefficients of
\mathtt{u}
at a level
m
appear only in the coefficients of
\mathtt{X}^{\mathtt{u}}
at levels
m+1
or higher. As the coefficients of
\mathtt{u}
at higher levels will not affect the coefficients of
\mathtt{X}^{\mathtt{u}}
at lower levels, the procedure can be considered as an overfit, i.e. optimising a complex control model with less accurate target function. This is also reflected in our main result (Theorem
12
) that we have an ordered limit where
{\mathrm{L}}
should be significantly larger than
M
.

On the second and fourth panel of Figure
1
we observe that for each level of control truncation (
M
), the cost value decreases as we increase the accuracy of the cost functional (more accuracy corresponds to higher level of state truncation
{\mathrm{L}}
). The cost values seem to plateau to quantities above the actual optimal cost, but said quantities seem to converge to the optimal cost as the complexity of the controls (
M
) increases.

4.2
Fractional Brownian motion (
H=1/4
)

In this case, we set
X_{0}=b_{0}=\sigma_{0}=C(t)=D(t)=E=G=0
, and the remaining model parameters of the state dynamics and cost functional to be one. The optimal control thus seeks to minimize the expected average squared control effort and distance from the origin of a geometric fractional Brownian motion whose drift is additively controlled.

First, we implement Monte-Carlo simulation (
10,000
sample path with
1,000
time partitions) to approximate the expected fractional Brownian signature
\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}^{H}_{t}]
. Next, we apply the first-order condition to minimise
\langle\mathtt{J}^{\mathrm{L}}(\cdot),\hat{\mathbb{E}}[{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}_{T}^{H}]\rangle
as in the Brownian case to obtain the (approximate) minimizer
\mathtt{u}^{{\mathrm{L}},M}
. Afterwards, we simulate
u^{{\mathrm{L}},M}=\langle\mathtt{u}^{{\mathrm{L}},M},{\widehat{\mathbb{W}}}^{H}\rangle
a total of
10,000
times by partitioning the time horizon into
1,000
equally-spaced intervals. As benchmark, we compute the optimal control from Corollary 3.4 in
[
21
]
and simulate the performance using the same randomness as in the signature approach. Note that this benchmark is also an approximate to the actual optimal control as we have to approximate several fractional integrals. The performance of the strategies is reported in Figure
2
.
