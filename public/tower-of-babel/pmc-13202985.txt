Delayed diagnosis and successful surgical management of bilateral choanal atresia in a 10-year-old male: a case report

Background

Choanal atresia is a rare congenital defect in which the posterior choanae are completely blocked. Unlike unilateral choanal atresia, bilateral choanal atresia is life-threatening. It was first reported in 1910, with approximately 90% of cases being osseous or osteo-membranous, and 10% being purely membranous [1]. A modern study reviewing CT and histologic specimens from 63 patients found that 29% of cases involved pure bony atresia, while 71% had a combination of membranous and bony atresia, with no cases of pure membranous atresia [2].

Unilateral choanal atresia is typically identified later in childhood, presenting with unilateral nasal obstruction or persistent nasal discharge, and rarely causes immediate respiratory distress. In contrast, bilateral choanal atresia is usually diagnosed at birth as a neonatal emergency because infants with this condition experience immediate respiratory distress. However, exceptionally rare cases of bilateral choanal atresia remain undiagnosed until adulthood [1, 3, 4].

When both choanae are blocked, the condition becomes life-threatening, with symptoms including dyspnea (difficulty breathing), intermittent cyanosis (bluish skin, especially during crying or when the mouth is closed), and respiratory failure. Choanal atresia is commonly associated with congenital syndromes such as CHARGE, Treacher Collins, Crouzon, and Pfeiffer syndromes. However, the specific cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying choanal atresia remain unclear [5, 6].

Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment are critical. The diagnosis is confirmed through a thorough clinical evaluation, patient history, and diagnostic tests such as CT scans and nasoendoscopy, which help determine the location, extent, and nature of the atretic plate (whether bony, membranous, or a combination) [7].

Surgical treatment options include transnasal perforation, transpalatal resection, transnasal endoscopically assisted perforation, and transnasal endoscopic choanoplasty. The latter is widely regarded as the preferred approach by otolaryngologists and involves the endoscopic removal of atretic tissue and the posterior nasal septum, creating a single open choana (unichoana) [8].

Case presentation

A 10-year-old male from southern Ethiopia was referred to our outpatient clinic for evaluation and management of chronic nasal obstruction, nasal discharge, snoring, and mouth breathing, which had been present since birth. There were no significant perinatal complications, However, the parents reported episodes of cyanosis during early infancy, which resolved with crying, as well as intermittent breastfeeding difficulties.

The child had been treated for suspected obstructive sleep apnea and adenoid hypertrophy with repeated courses of intranasal steroids at a nearby health center since the age of 1 year, but there was no significant improvement in symptoms. Consequently, the patient was referred for further evaluation due to persistent symptoms and concerns regarding an underlying anatomical cause.

On physical examination, the child appeared well-nourished and in no acute distress. No abnormalities were noted in the eyes, ears, or facial structure. The oral cavity revealed a patent airway, and there were no signs of respiratory distress. Nasal examination showed chronic nasal obstruction with markedly reduced airflow on both sides, and the child exhibited a characteristic mouth-breathing pattern.

Nasoendoscopy revealed bilateral atretic choanae, consistent with choanal atresia (Fig. 1). There were no signs of nasal polyps, tumors, or other masses obstructing the airway. CT scan confirmed the presence of both membranous and bony atretic plates obstructing the posterior nasal passages (Fig. 2). Echocardiogram results were normal, ruling out any significant cardiac anomalies or congenital heart defects.

Endonasal endoscopic choanoplasty was performed under general anesthesia. The procedure involved drilling the vomer bone to open the choanal passages. A nasal stent with endotracheal tube was placed to maintain patency of the newly formed choanae and prevent re-obstruction during the postoperative healing period. Postoperatively, the child recovered well with no signs of infection or complications. The nasal stent was removed after three weeks, and follow-up endoscopic examination revealed patent choanae on both sides after 3 months (Fig. 3). The child’s nasal obstruction, mouth breathing, and snoring significantly improved. After 9 months the choana was patent and no stenosis was identified.

Discussion

Mouth breathing is typically obstructed in newborns due to the close proximity of the epiglottis to the soft palate, preventing the tongue from creating an effective seal for mouth breathing. As a result, newborns are obligate nasal breathers during the first month of life. Between 4 and 6 weeks of age, they begin to learn mouth breathing. Bilateral choanal atresia, however, can result in severe asphyxia immediately after birth, as infants who are obligate nasal breathers cannot breathe through their mouths. This can lead to suffocation. Infants with this condition may also experience feeding difficulties and weight loss due to an inability to suckle.

Our patient presented with nasal secretions, feeding difficulties, snoring, and mouth breathing during infancy but did not experience weight loss or facial abnormalities, which is atypical for this condition [9, 10].

The pathogenesis of choanal atresia is thought to involve the persistence of the buccopharyngeal membrane, excessive proliferation of epithelial cells in the nasal cavities during gestation, abnormal migration of neural crest cells, or excessive growth of the vertical and horizontal processes of the palatine bones [11].

CT imaging is crucial for diagnosis, as it distinguishes between membranous and bony atresia. Diagnosis should be confirmed after suctioning and decongestion of the nasal cavity to avoid confusion with mucus in the unprepared nose [5].

Early recognition and surgical intervention are critical in managing choanal atresia. In our case, transnasal endoscopic choanoplasty was successfully performed, offering an effective solution to the patient’s nasal obstruction and improving his quality of life [8].

The endonasal approach to choanal atresia repair is a minimally invasive technique that marks a significant advancement in surgical management. It causes less tissue trauma, offers superior visualization of the surgical field, and reduces the risk of bleeding. A major benefit is the ability to assess the nasal cavity effectively during and after the procedure, which is why it is widely favored. On the other hand, the transpalatal approach provides better exposure of anatomical landmarks and reduces the risk of surgeon disorientation, as well as intracranial complications. However, it may negatively affect the growth of the hard palate, maxillary alveolar ridge, and midline facial structures. Additionally, it poses a higher risk of bleeding and potential damage to the soft palate, as documented in existing studies [12].

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of including choanal atresia in the differential diagnosis for children with chronic nasal obstruction, snoring, and feeding difficulties. Early recognition, aided by nasoendoscopy—a valuable, non-invasive tool for visualizing nasal passage obstructions—can lead to timely diagnosis and effective treatment planning. Surgical intervention through endonasal choanoplasty offers a minimally invasive solution with significant improvements in breathing, feeding, and overall quality of life. Ongoing follow-up is crucial to ensure the long-term success of the procedure, maintain choanal patency, and promptly address any complications.
