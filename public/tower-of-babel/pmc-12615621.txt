Integrated spherical phase change modules in concrete roofs enhance thermal performance in hot climates
Authors: Abdin Bedada Huluka, S Muthulingam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRising energy demand for building cooling exacerbates the environmental challenges associated with energy consumption. Incorporating phase change materials (PCMs) into building envelopes, particularly sun‑exposed roofs, can substantially reduce energy use. This study examines the thermal‑storage efficiency of metallic, spherical PCM modules embedded within a reinforced concrete roof, designed for hot‑climate conditions. The roof is divided into four distinct thermal zones: Zone‑1 (conventional concrete), Zone‑2 (empty spherical modules), and Zones 3–4 (modules filled with organic PCMs, organic mixture, 35 °C (OM35) and organic mixture, 37 °C (OM37)). Important thermal performance metrics, such as temperature distribution, heat flux, thermal load, time lag, decrement factor, key response index, and carbon emissions savings, are evaluated. Integrating spherical PCM modules led to significant improvements. These include an average reduction in indoor surface temperature of 10.2 °C, a decrease in cooling load of upto 69%, and a reduced decrement factor. In addition, OM35 showed a higher key response index and enhanced thermal performance than OM37. The findings demonstrate the practical viability of spherical PCM‑integrated roofs as a passive‑cooling strategy for buildings in hot climates.Keywords: Cooling load, Energy savings, Phase change materials, PCM-integrated roof, Spherical PCM module, Zone approachSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Rising temperatures and economic advancements, particularly in warmer regions, have significantly increased energy demand for building space cooling applications. By 2020, cooling systems accounted for 16% of building electricity consumption globally, amounting to 1885 TWh1. In hot climates, cooling can exceed 60% of total electricity use2. In India, buildings consume over 40% of the nation’s electricity, with a large share allocated to air conditioning for thermal comfort3. Energy demand is projected to double by 2050, with cooling contributing a major share to this growth4. Amid accelerating energy demand and climate stressors, passive design offers the most durable path to affordable comfort in dwellings. Envelope‑led strategies–material thermal properties, window‑to‑wall ratio, glazing, orientation, and natural ventilation–consistently moderate indoor conditions and lower energy demand without reliance on mechanical systems5. Vernacular houses already embed climate understanding, yet material limitations warrant reassessment. Among thermal energy storage technologies, phase change materials (PCMs) leverage latent heat during phase transitions to stabilize indoor temperatures, reducing reliance on mechanical cooling6. Experimental studies report 14–90% energy savings, underscoring their effectiveness for passive cooling across diverse climates7.

PCM encapsulation critically affects efficiency and constructability. Encapsulation techniques are classified into three primary scales: nano, micro, and macro, each with specific advantages and limitations. Microencapsulation involves enclosing PCMs in microscale shells, enabling their direct incorporation into materials such as gypsum boards and cement-based composites8. Nanoencapsulation represents a more advanced approach, producing nano-sized PCM particles to enhance thermal energy storage and improve structural stability9. Conversely, macroencapsulation involves more significant volumes of PCMs in robust enclosures, making it suitable for applications requiring substantial thermal capacity, such as building envelopes and large-scale storage systems10. While synthesizing micro- and nano-encapsulated PCMs requires sophisticated methods to achieve optimal performance, production cost and scalability challenges remain critical11. Compared to micro- and nano-systems, macroencapsulation offers the advantage of higher PCM loading capacity and greater resistance to mechanical stresses, making it particularly effective in scenarios demanding significant thermal mass and structural durability12.

This study advances strategies to reduce heat transfer through reinforced-concrete roofs, which are directly exposed to solar radiation and therefore a major source of indoor heat gain. The work specifically investigates roof systems embedded with spherical, macroencapsulated PCM modules, combined with an insulation layer and reflective tiles. The findings are presented incrementally, with this part focusing on a zone-based analysis. A zone-based evaluation is carried out, focusing on temperature distribution, heat-flux variations, decrement factor, time lag, and related thermal performance indices to determine the system’s ability to mitigate indoor cooling demand. The novelty of this research lies in its integrated approach to PCM application. Earlier investigations (Ref.27,28), constrained by simplified unit-cell approximations, did not fully capture the coupled energy interactions between PCMs and their surrounding environment. In contrast, the present study models the roof–PCM–environment as a single, interacting assembly, thereby accounting for the dynamics of capsule geometry, macroencapsulation, and environment. This advances upon the existing literature, which has largely emphasised isolated strategies such as insulation or reflective surfaces (e.g. Ref.29,30), but rarely explored their synergistic behaviour when combined with PCM. The outcomes hold direct practical relevance. The study delivers clear performance metrics that can guide the adoption of PCM-based passive cooling in both retrofitted and newly built structures. By aligning with established roofing practices and emphasizing constructability, it ensures that these solutions are not only effective in reducing cooling demand but also practical to implement at scale.

Materials and methods

PCMs selection and characterization

Diurnal and seasonal variations in ambient outdoor temperature strongly affect PCM efficiency. Because of its distinctive climate, Rupnagar, India, was chosen as the study’s focus location. Situated at 30.9661°N and 76.5231°E, with an elevation of 260 m, Rupnagar experiences a subtropical humid environment characterized by dry winters. Analysis of 30 years of meteorological data reveals that June to May are the warmest months, while the coldest seasons are December and the beginning of January31. The region experiences extreme climates in summer ranging from an average temperature of 47 °C to a low of 0 °C during winter (Ref. Fig. 1). Months of summer are characterized by large daily temperature fluctuations, with average of 43 °C during the day and 27 °C at night. Structures are subjected to severe discomfort due to this thermal variance, which makes it the perfect environment for researching PCMs thermal behaviour.

Climatic condition of study area31.

The main factor influencing PCM efficiency is their thermophysical characteristics, which include their density, melting point, specific heat, latent heat, and thermal conductivity. A prior study32 used a multi-criteria decision-making framework to assess the suitability of 26 PCMs for India’s climatic conditions. For ambient air temperatures in the 30–40 °C range, the analysis identified organic mixture, 35 °C (OM35) and organic mixture, 37 °C (OM37), and n-eicosane as the most suitable options. Guided by these findings and the local diurnal temperature profile of the study area, the present work selected OM35 and OM37 for evaluation. However, a thorough examination was required because of discrepancies in the reported properties of PCMs across the body of existing research, which resulted from the absence of standardized characterization techniques. Accordingly, detailed characterizations of the selected PCMs were conducted to determine their thermophysical properties accurately.

The thermophysical properties of OM35 and OM37 were thoroughly characterized using advanced techniques. A transient plane source analyzer (Model: TPS 500) was used to test thermal conductivity. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy was employed to investigate the chemical composition, addressing the frequency range of 500–4000 cm⁻1 at a resolution of 4 cm⁻1 utilizing a Bruker Tensor-27 spectrometer equipped with a KBr disc. Crystallization behavior was analyzed through X-ray diffraction utilizing a Cu Kα radiation source-equipped Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer (1.54 Å) spanning a 2-theta range of 5° to 80° when operated at 45 kV and 40 mA. Heat flow properties were determined via differential scanning calorimetry (Model: STA 449 F1 Jupiter), ensuring high precision (± 0.01 °C) and accuracy (± 0.05 °C), with a temperature ramp between 25 and 60 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min. These methodologies provided a comprehensive understanding of the materials’ thermophysical behaviors, with detailed findings documented in27,28.

PCM module

PCM modules for reinforced concrete must meet these essential requirements: (i) outer shell must be both leak-proof and compatible with stored PCM; (ii) chemically inactive with concrete; (iii) exhibits better heat transfer characteristics; (iv) promotes smooth cyclic phase transitions; and (v) strong and stiff enough to withstand loads. The outer shell of the PCM module is made out of Al 1200, conforming to ASTM-B-209. The thermal and mechanical properties of Al 1200 are given in Table 2. Al 1200 has 2700 kg/m3 density, 70 MPa proof strength, 105 MPa ultimate strength, 69 GPa elastic modulus, and 12% elongation at break.

Preventing PCM leakage into the concrete matrix during cyclic phase change is critical. Otherwise, the compressive strength of concrete may be affected by the volume changes of PCM that occur during cyclic phase change. For instance, due to such volume change, concrete compressive strength was reported to decrease by 32%33. The shell material should not corrode due to the presence of PCM for compatibility. Organic PCMs, such as OM35 and OM37, are typically non-corrosive, making them compatible with aluminum (e.g.34). In addition, it should be noted that aluminium is compatible with concrete. While some studies indicate that aluminium may experience corrosion in an alkaline concrete environment, it is important to note that it can still be compatible under certain favorable conditions35. Moreover, aluminum PCM modules are commonly used in building envelopes (e.g.36–40).

Metals outperform plastics in thermal conductivity, making them highly effective for heat transfer applications. Al 1200, for instance, has a specific heat capacity of 900 J/kg-K and a thermal conductivity of 230 W/m–K, which significantly enhances the thermal performance of PCMs. Additionally, the module’s shape significantly impacts how PCMs behave during phase transitions. According to research, rectangular designs with sharp edges may cause unstable supercooling, while spherical and cylindrical shapes are suggested because they can provide a 70% increase in energy efficiency41,42. In this study, spherical modules are developed using a forging process. The Al 2100 plates are forged into two equal semi-spherical bowls and connected using a tungsten inert gas welding process. A 10 mm diameter hole is drilled into one of the semi-spherical parts to allow for the filling of PCM. The absence of sharp edges in the spherical geometry is expected to facilitate a smooth cyclic phase transition of PCMs in the developed module.

Previous methods involved encapsulating PCMs in either solid or liquid states, tailored to specific geometric and application requirements (e.g.34,36). In this study, the PCM was uniformly heated on a hot plate to 60 °C to ensure complete liquefaction. The liquid PCM was then filled into the module through an inlet, occupying 75% of the module’s diameter (Ref. Fig. 2), which corresponded to an encapsulation thickness of 75 mm. Each module was filled with 354 g of OM35 or 358 g of OM37, as determined by established guidelines in the literature (e.g.43). The module’s opening was tightly sealed post-filling to avoid the possibility of leakage, and the PCM was stored under controlled environmental conditions.

Roof design typology

This section delves into the structural design of roofs enhanced with PCM modules. The integration involves embedding spherical PCM modules in the form of spheres inside the concrete matrix lengthwise to widthwise. To ensure the roof’s flexural integrity under gravitational loading, the modules are installed in the tension zones of the concrete, where the influence of tensile strength is insignificant. Substituting portions of concrete with PCM, which is significantly lighter, leads to a considerable reduction in self-weight. Because of this reduction, the roof can support longer spans under comparable loading conditions or support heavier loads over identical spans. Additionally, lighter roofs decrease the material requirements for supporting elements such as beams and columns, resulting in cost savings. Furthermore, reduced self-weight lowers seismic forces, simplifying the design of earthquake-resistant components. Integrating PCM modules achieves a dual objective: reducing the roof’s weight by approximately 40% at replacement points and transforming it into a passive cooling system without sacrificing flexural strength. Experimental results indicate that the ultimate flexural capacity of PCM-integrated roofs is equivalent to that of traditional concrete roofs44.

Following IS 456:2000, EN 1992-1-1:2004, and ACI 318:2019, the PCM module-integrated roof design considers the reduction in the effective concrete area caused by the modules, which decreases both shear and flexural capacity. Additionally, under the impact of punching shear, the position of the crucial failure plane changes, requiring adjustments to shear design equations. A roof measuring 1.325 × 1.325 × 0.15 m was designed in this study, considering both its weight and a superimposed load of 2 kN/m2. The materials used included M20 concrete and Fe550 steel, reinforced with square meshes of 10 mm diameter bars at 130 mm spacing. Experiment details

To construct a PCM-integrated roof, the bottom reinforcement mesh is initially positioned on the formwork. The top reinforcement mesh is applied after PCM modules are positioned above the bottom concrete layer in longitudinal and transverse alignments. As shown in Fig. 4a–d, green concrete was used for casting. Positioned on a steel plate, the wooden mold with dimensions of 1.325 × 1.325 × 0.15 m holds the bottom reinforcement, which is carefully placed on the cover concrete. The concrete is mixed and poured in layers using M20-grade proportions (1:1.8:3.1:0.5), with compaction done at each step. The first step is to pour green concrete until it reaches the bottom reinforcement mesh. Over the concrete layer, PCM modules are positioned in both directions. The roof is separated into four isolated zones, separated by 25 mm extruded polystyrene sheets to prevent thermal interaction between them. The top reinforcement mesh is then added, and new concrete is poured over the PCM modules. The last step of the procedure is to pour green concrete into the wooden mold, guaranteeing adequate compaction.

Casting procedure: (a) wooden mould with concrete; (b) PCM modules integration; and (c) finished roof.

Zone details of the PCM integrated roof and instrumentation are illustrated in Fig. 5a and b, respectively. The roof is divided into four zones. Zone–1 is made of conventional concrete only, while Zone–2 contains empty modules without PCM. In Zone–3, OM35 PCM modules are used, and in Zone–4, OM37 PCM modules are used. The zone approach is based on several key assumptions: heat flow between zones is negligible; complete thermal contact between different layers only occurs at the zone level; and each zone functions as an independent system, resulting in negligible thermal interactions between them. Using extruded polystyrene–with a thermal conductivity of 0.02 W/mK–that is 100 mm high and 25 mm thick to isolate zones at the level of the PCM modules thermally can effectively reduce heat transfer between these zones. J-type thermocouples are employed to measure temperature profiles at multiple levels of the roof within each zone. These sensors are positioned on the internal and external surfaces of every zone and on the upper and lower sides of designated PCM modules. The thermocouples were calibrated in the laboratory using ice and boiling water, achieving an accuracy of ± 0.1 °C.

Schematic of the roof: (a) roof plan; and (b) sectional view.

Temperature measurements are recorded automatically at one-minute intervals using a 32-channel datalogger. The outdoor experimental arrangement and the instruments utilized are shown in Fig. 6a and b, respectively. Note that the roof is supported on the brick wall. In addition, extended polystyrene sheets are attached to the edges of the roof along with glass wool fillings to minimize heat transfer through the edges. Designed to promote one-dimensional heat transfer, this arrangement significantly improves the efficiency of the zone methodology. The system is continuously exposed to outdoor environmental conditions on a daily basis.

Temperature variations in all zones.

On every day of the study, in Zones 3 & 4 is lower than during the day but higher during the night. The observed behavior stems from PCMs storing thermal energy as latent heat during the day and releasing it at night. This process allows PCM modules to balance external thermal flux, providing essential thermal regulation to the roof and ensuring occupant comfort. Additionally, PCMs undergo daily phase transitions, melting during the daytime and solidifying at night across all observed days. The PCMs release their stored latent heat at night and are ready to be charged again the next day. Thus, it is evident that the PCMs in Zones 3 & 4 are working effectively. Further, heat transfer mechanisms play an important role in facilitating phase transitions. During the initial phases, PCM melts, heat transfer primarily takes place through conduction. Thermal buoyancy causes the liquid PCM near module surface rise to top of the module. With further, increase in temperature, heat transfer ensues via conduction and convection. During this stage, residual heat ascends until it reaches the upper spherical region of the module47,48. Cyclic melting and solidification are critical when choosing PCMs for building applications. Here the chosen PCMs were determined to fulfil this requirement.

During the day, PCMs in Zones 3 & 4 experience a solid–liquid phase transition, leading to a notable decrease in indoor surface temperature. Figure 8 shows the recorded decrease in indoor temperature in Zones 3 & 4 throughout the period. The reductions in temperature are derived by analyzing the differences between and . The temperature in Zone–3 drops by 9.1 °C at its lowest, 10.7 °C at its highest, and averages 10.0 °C. In Zone–4, the corresponding drops are 9.2, 11.1, and 10.2 °C, respectively. This is attributed to PCMs storing thermal energy as latent heat during their solid-to-liquid phase change, lowering temperature. Further, compared to OM35 in Zone–3, OM37 in Zone–4 provides a marginal improvement in temperature reduction. The higher latent heat storage capacity of OM37 than OM35 is responsible for the increased temperature reduction, as it allows for more thermal energy storage. These observations indicate integrating PCM modules into roof can significantly reduce the indoor temperature. It is worth pointing out that PCM phase change behaviour and latent heat storage capacity are closely related. During the melting process, the PCM goes through a transitional phase where it lies between the solid and liquid states. With increasing temperature, this mushy phase transitions to a liquid. The process of phase change involves states like solid-mushy, liquid-mushy, or a combination of these phases49.

Interior temperature reductions in all zones.

At night, PCMs undergo a phase change from liquid to solid, allowing them to release stored heat into the indoor environment. This behavior contributes to a rise in indoor surface temperature, as indicated in Fig. 7. Zone–3 shows temperature increases ranging from 0.8 to 3.5 °C, with values frequently around 3.1 °C. Similarly, Zone–4 experiences a temperature rise starting at 0.1 °C, peaking at 3.5 °C, and typically close to 2.9 °C. A key practical implication of these observations is that reduced indoor temperatures, relative to the thermal comfort level, result in decreased space heating demands. It is undesirable for interior temperatures to rise above the thermal comfort threshold due to latent heat release. Under these circumstances, measures to transfer heat to the external environment are necessary. For instance, under favorable ambient conditions, nighttime ventilation can effectively release heat to the surroundings and promote solidification of PCM50. Nevertheless, the findings indicate PCM module integrated roof can release stored heat, increasing indoor temperature.

Temperature reductions are also noticed in Zone–2, where empty modules are placed (Ref. Fig. 8). The observed temperature changes include a highest reduction of 8.7 °C, a lowest of 6.2 °C, and an average reduction of 8.2 °C. One possible explanation is that an empty module creates an air cavity in the roof. Typically, the presence of an air cavity can provide insulation, resulting in a decrease in indoor temperature51. It can also be noticed that in Zone–3 closely aligns with the trends of , albeit with a slight time lag. Nevertheless, maximum values in Zones 3 & 4 are lower than maximum values, which implies that integrating PCMs in the roof could effectively reduce levels below during the day. Further, the zone approach allows for a comparison of temperature variation between a conventional roof (Zone–1) and roofs with integrated PCM modules (Zones 3 & 4) and empty modules (Zone–2). In Zones 3 and 4, the temperature experiences a significant decrease of upto 6.8 °C, while in Zone–2, it drops by a maximum of 3.9 °C. Thus, compared to conventional reinforced concrete roof, PCM module integrated roof was able to reduce the temperature by upto 6.8 °C during the three days of testing.

Temperature time lag

Due to the transient heat transfer in each zone, the temperature profile between the outdoor and indoor surfaces varies in amplitude and wavelength. Magnitude correlates with temperature, whereas frequency is temporal. While solar radiation and surface air circulation affect temperature on outdoor surfaces, thermophysical properties cause fluctuations on the inner surfaces. Thus, the heat wave magnitude is higher outdoors than indoors. In this regard, the time lag is the duration it takes a heat wave to go from an outdoor to an indoor surface. The trend supports two interpretations. First, PCM-integrated capsules improve performance: OM35 yields a large step change over empty balls, and OM37 adds a smaller but consistent gain over OM35. This suggests that latent-heat utilization is effective in both PCMs, with OM37 marginally better under the tested conditions–plausibly due to closer alignment of its phase-change temperature and enthalpy characteristics with the roof’s diurnal temperature cycle. Second, because savings scale with the fuel’s emission intensity, the same thermal improvement produces greater absolute carbon abatement in coal/lignite contexts than in gas-dominated ones.

Conclusions

In this study, the latent heat storage behaviour of PCM modules integrated reinforced concrete roof is experimentally studied under hot climatic conditions. Following the macroencapsulation technique, the modules are directly integrated into the concrete matrix. A zone approach is introduced for conducting experiments. This approach thermally divides the roof into four zones. Zone-1 has traditional concrete, Zone-2 has empty spherical modules, and Zones 3 and 4 include OM35 and OM37 organic PCMs. Temperature, heat flow, thermal load, time lag, decrement factor, and key response index are examined. The major conclusions drawn from the study are given below:

i.During the day in Zones 3 and 4, indoor surface temperatures reduce by upto 11.1 °C, with a low of 9.2 °C and an average of 10.2 °C. PCMs store thermal energy as latent heat during their solid-to-liquid phase change, which lowers the temperature.

ii.Compared to Zone–1, the thermal loads in Zones 2, 3, and 4 are significantly lower, with reductions of 59, 68, and 69%, respectively. This has the potential to enhance the thermal comfort of the inhabitants significantly.

iii.Zone–4 has the shortest average time lag among the PCM integrated zones and the lowest decrement factor among all zones. Zone–3 shows the highest key response index, suggesting that OM35 significantly contributes to better thermal performance.

Deployment of roofs embedding spherical, macroencapsulated PCMs is recommended for hot climates. The zone‑based framework should be substantiated through closed‑form analysis, numerical modelling, and focused laboratory/field testing. To broaden applicability, pair instrumented full‑scale trials with three-dimensional transient simulations; assess cascaded PCM layers and hybrid active–passive configurations; and quantify thermal performance and durability gains from nano‑engineered PCMs. Finally, apply AI/ML‑based multiobjective optimisation to co‑design materials, encapsulation/integration strategy, and stability measures under techno‑economic–environmental constraints, enabling robust, net‑zero‑aligned buildings.
