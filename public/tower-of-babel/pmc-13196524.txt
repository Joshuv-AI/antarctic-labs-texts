Effects of fine-to-coarse limestone ratio on performance, egg quality, bone traits, and renal CYP27B1 protein abundance in peak and aged Lohmann Brown laying hens
Authors: Heloísa Pagnussatt, Carlos Henrique de Oliveira, Jean Kaique Valentim, Ricardo Vianna Nunes, Fernando de Castro Tavernari, Giovanna Lima Vieira, Beatriz Alvarez de Honório, Arele Arlindo Calderano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo trials were conducted to evaluate performance, egg quality, blood and bone traits, and renal 1α-hydroxylase (CYP27B1) protein abundance in laying hens fed different fine-to-coarse limestone (FL:CL) ratios during distinct production phases. A total of 180 Lohmann Brown hens at 26 weeks of age (Trial 1) and 96 hens at 66 weeks of age (Trial 2) were assigned to dietary FL:CL ratios of 60:40, 50:50, and 30:70 in Trial 1 and 50:50 and 30:70 in Trial 2. In both trials, dietary FL:CL ratios did not affect performance, egg quality, or blood parameters. In Trial 1, the 30:70 FL:CL ratio reduced relative tibia weight and tibia breaking strength (p < 0.05), whereas hens fed the 50:50 ratio exhibited approximately 20 % lower renal CYP27B1 protein abundance (p < 0.05). In Trial 2, the 30:70 ratio increased feed intake (p < 0.05) and reduced tibial Ca content (p < 0.05), while other variables remained unaffected. Overall, altering FL:CL ratios had no effect on eggshell quality and had limited effects on productive performance, but higher proportions of coarse limestone negatively affected specific bone traits. In addition, the intermediate 50:50 FL:CL ratio was associated with lower renal CYP27B1 protein abundance, which is consistent with a reduced demand for vitamin D3 activation and may indicate a more stable Ca homeostasis.Keywords: Eggshell, Limestone granulometry, Limestone particle size, Vitamin D

Introduction

In laying hens, eggshell formation occurs daily in the uterus and represents one of the most rapid biomineralization processes known. To deposit approximately 6 g of shell as calcium carbonate (CaCO3), the hen exports about 2.4 g of calcium (Ca) per day, which corresponds to nearly 10 % of her total body calcium (Nys et al., 2022). Although dietary sources supply approximately two-thirds of the Ca requirement, Ca intake occurs mainly during the day, whereas eggshell formation takes place predominantly at night, resulting in a temporal mismatch that leads to bone Ca mobilization (Nys, 2017; Nys et al., 2022).

To support optimal egg production, strategies involving different limestone particle sizes have been shown to be effective in improving eggshell thickness and strength (Hervo et al., 2022, 2023), internal egg quality (Khosht et al., 2020), and bone mineral content and strength (Molnár et al., 2017). These beneficial effects are attributed to the slower and more gradual release of soluble Ca from coarse limestone (CL) compared with fine limestone (FL), which reduces the desynchronization between Ca supply and demand (Bueno et al., 2016; Hervo et al., 2022). These changes may also impact physiological mechanisms involved in Ca regulation, including the activity of renal 1α-hydroxylase (CYP27B1), which is modulated by Ca homeostasis. However, there is no consensus regarding the optimal fine-to-coarse limestone (FL:CL) ratio, and findings across studies remain inconsistent.

In the avian kidney, the enzyme CYP27B1 catalyzes the activation of 25-hydroxycholecalciferol, to form 1α,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (Nys et al., 2022). This active form of vitamin D₃ plays a key role in Ca homeostasis by enhancing intestinal Ca absorption (Pizauro Junior et al., 2017). However, to our knowledge, no previous studies have evaluated how dietary FL:CL ratios influence renal CYP27B1 protein abundance in laying hens. This represents an important knowledge gap, as changes in Ca supply dynamics may alter the endocrine regulation of Ca metabolism. The use of FL:CL ratios reflects common feeding practices and allows the evaluation of particle size distribution effects while maintaining constant dietary Ca levels, thereby enabling the assessment of how Ca release kinetics influence physiological regulation.

Additionally, as hens age, Ca absorption efficiency declines, increasing their dependence on bone-derived Ca for adequate eggshell formation. In this context, increasing the proportion of CL particles may represent a nutritional strategy to improve the temporal availability of Ca, due to their prolonged retention in the gizzard and more gradual solubilization. This sustained Ca release may help reduce the reliance on bone Ca mobilization and support eggshell formation, particularly in older hens.

It was hypothesized that increasing the dietary proportion of CL would promote more stable Ca homeostasis in laying hens, thereby enhancing eggshell and bone quality, with more pronounced effects in older hens due to their reduced Ca absorptive efficiency. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate productive performance, internal and external egg quality, blood parameters, bone characteristics, and renal CYP27B1 protein abundance in laying hens during the early production phase (26 to 41 weeks of age) and the late production phase (66 to 81 weeks of age).

Materials and methods

Two trials were conducted at the Department of Animal Science of the Federal University of Viçosa (UFV), located in Viçosa, Minas Gerais, Brazil. All procedures complied with the guidelines established by the Brazilian National Council for the Control of Animal Experimentation (CONCEA) and were approved by the Ethics Committee on the Use of Production Animals of UFV (CEUAP-UFV), under protocol number 133/2023. The trials assessed different phases of the production cycle. Whenever applicable, experimental procedures and analytical methods were performed according to previously described protocols and established methodologies in the literature.

Trials 1 and 2 were conducted in a completely randomized design. Treatments were randomly assigned to experimental units, and cage positions were uniformly distributed within the facility to minimize potential location effects. Both trials lasted 102 days and were conducted simultaneously using independent groups of hens. The hens allocated to Trial 2 were reared under standard commercial conditions until 66 weeks of age, following the management guidelines of Lohmann (2020) and the nutritional recommendations of Rostagno et al. (2024), prior to the beginning of the experimental period. In both trials, birds were selected based on average body weight (BW) and laying rate to ensure initial uniformity among experimental groups.

In Trial 1, a total of 180 Lohmann Brown laying hens at 26 weeks of age, with an average BW of 1.81 kg and an egg production rate of 98.37 %, were housed in 34 × 47 cm metal cages and allocated to three treatments, with ten replicates per treatment and six hens per experimental unit. In Trial 2, 96 Lohmann Brown laying hens at 66 weeks of age, with an average BW of 1.86 kg and an egg production rate of 89.66 %, were assigned to two treatments, with eight replicates per treatment and six hens per experimental unit. In both trials, each experimental unit consisted of three cages, with two birds per cage. All cages were equipped with trough feeders and nipple drinkers, allowing ad libitum access to feed and water.

The experimental diets were formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of laying hens according to Rostagno et al. (2024) for the phases from 26 to 33 and 34 to 41 weeks of age in Trial 1 (Table 1) and from 66 to 81 weeks of age in Trial 2 (Table 2). Within each trial, all diets were formulated to be isoenergetic and isonitrogenous, differing only in the FL:CL ratios. The dietary treatments were based on different inclusion ratios of two limestone particle sizes, both derived from the same limestone source, with identical chemical composition, and processed to obtain different particle sizes, with FL having a geometric mean diameter of 0.200 mm and a geometric standard deviation of 2.58 mm, and CL having a geometric mean diameter of 3.166 mm and a geometric standard deviation of 1.59 mm. Accordingly, the treatments consisted of three FL:CL ratios (60:40, 50:50, and 30:70) in Trial 1 (Table 1) and two FL:CL ratios (50:50 and 30:70) in Trial 2 (Table 2).

At the beginning and end of the experimental period, laying hens were weighed to determine BW gain (BWG, kg/bird). Egg production was recorded daily to calculate the egg production rate (EPR, %) by dividing the total number of eggs produced by the total number of hens. The experimental period was divided into 28-day cycles, during which feed offered and the feed refusal were weighed to determine feed intake (FI, g/bird/day) and subsequently calculate the feed conversion ratio (FCR, kg/kg). In addition, the proportion of noncommercial eggs (%) was calculated by dividing the number of broken and defective eggs by the total number of eggs collected.

At the end of each 28-day cycle, eggs were collected over five consecutive days within the experimental period to assess egg quality variables. Eggs collected during the first two days were used to assess eggshell breaking strength (EBS, kgf) using a texture analyzer (GR Electrical Manufacturing Company, Manhattan, KS, USA). Eggs collected during the remaining three days were used to determine average egg weight (EW, g), egg mass (EM, g/bird/day), and to perform internal and external quality analyses.

Egg quality measurements were conducted using objective instrumental methods, minimizing potential observer bias. For internal quality assessment, eggs were weighed, cracked onto a specialized glass table, and immediately evaluated for albumen and yolk quality. Yolk quality was assessed using the yolk index (YI), calculated as the ratio of yolk height (YH, mm), measured with a digital micrometer, to yolk diameter (YD, mm), measured with a digital caliper. Additionally, yolk color was evaluated using a DSM colorimetric fan. Subsequently, the yolk and shell were weighed, and albumen weight was calculated by subtracting their combined weight from the total egg weight. The proportions of the egg components were also calculated (%).

For external quality assessment, eggshell weight (ESW, g) was recorded after the shells were washed to remove residual albumen and air-dried for 48 h. Eggshell thickness (EST, mm) was measured using a digital micrometer. Measurements were taken at three equidistant points along the equatorial region of the shell, and the arithmetic mean of these values was used to represent EST. At the end of the experimental period, one hen per experimental unit (n = number of replicates per treatment) was selected based on the BW of its respective unit for blood collection via brachial vein puncture. The samples were used to analyze serum levels of Ca (mg/dL), phosphorus (P, mg/dL), and total alkaline phosphatase (ALP, U/L) using an automated biochemical analyzer (Cobas c 311; Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Basel, Switzerland) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the same hen was euthanized for gizzard and tibia sampling. The gizzard weight (g) was measured. The breaking strength of the right tibia (kgf) was determined using a texture analyzer, whereas the left tibia was used to determine Ca, P, and ash contents. The tibia was manually broken using a press, defatted in Soxhlet extractors (Soxhlet, 1879) and ground in a ball mill for analysis. For ash determination, 2 g of air-dried sample were incinerated in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 4 h. After complete combustion, the crucibles were placed in a desiccator for 30 min, weighed, and the results recorded (AOAC International, 2016). To determine the percentages of Ca and P, a nitric-perchloric digestion was performed (Sarruge and Haag, 1974), in which 0.5 g of the ground sample was treated with 10 mL of a nitric acid and perchloric acid solution in a 4:1 ratio, under a fume hood. The digestion was carried out on a heating plate, starting at 80 °C and gradually increasing the temperature up to 200 °C, until a clear and crystalline extract was obtained. After cooling, the volume was adjusted to 25 mL with deionized water. The concentrations of Ca and P were quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES – Nolte, 2003; Perkin Elmer Optima 8300 DV instrument), previously calibrated with a multielement solution in the same matrix as the samples. In addition, relative tibia weight (RTW, %) was calculated as the ratio of tibia weight (g) to BW, and the Seedor index (SI) was calculated as bone mass (mg) divided by bone length (mm), according to Seedor et al. (1991).

Kidney samples were also collected to evaluate the CYP27B1 protein abundance. Tissue homogenates were prepared by washing kidney fragments in 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and homogenizing them in a ratio of 0.3 g of tissue to 1.5 mL of PBS. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,500 x g for 10 minutes, and the resulting supernatant was stored at −20 °C until further analysis. CYP27B1 levels were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Chicken 25-hydroxyvitamin d-1 alpha hydroxylase ELISA Kit; MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA), based on the double-antibody sandwich technique. The assay is designed for the specific detection of chicken CYP27B1, using antibodies validated for this species, as indicated by the manufacturer. The assay involved incubation of samples in 96-well plates pre-coated with anti-CYP27B1 monoclonal antibodies, followed by sequential addition of a biotinylated polyclonal antibody, enzyme–avidin–peroxidase conjugate, and chromogenic substrate (TMB). Optical density was read at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (MultiskanTM Go, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA). Sample concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve prepared with known concentrations of CYP27B1, following the manufacturer's instructions. All samples were analyzed in duplicate, and mean values were used for subsequent analysis. To minimize analytical variability, all samples were processed within a single assay. Results were expressed as U/mg according to the ELISA kit output, without normalization to total protein content.

Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using the GLM procedure of SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with FL:CL ratio as the fixed effect in a completely randomized design. No random effects were included in the model. The experimental unit consisted of three cages (six hens). Performance variables were analyzed using the experimental unit as the statistical unit. Egg quality measurements were based on the average of eggs collected per experimental unit. Blood and bone variables, as well as renal CYP27B1 protein abundance were determined using one hen per experimental unit. The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Hartley tests, respectively. Data were subjected to analysis of variance at a 5 % significance level, and when significant effects were detected, treatment means were compared using Tukey’s test.

Results

In Trial 1, varying the FL:CL ratios had no significant effect on performance, egg quality, or blood parameters across all evaluated variables (p > 0.05) (Table 3). Bone quality, gizzard weight, SI and tibial Ca, P, and ash contents were also not influenced by FL:CL ratios (p > 0.05) (Table 3). However, the highest proportion of CL (30:70) resulted in a reduced RTW (p = 0.050) and lower tibia breaking strength (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Furthermore, hens fed the 50:50 FL:CL ratio exhibited approximately 20 % lower renal CYP27B1 protein abundance compared to those fed 60:40 and 30:70 ratios (p = 0.018).

Discussion

Given that approximately two-thirds of the Ca deposited in the eggshell is absorbed directly from the diet, while the remaining one-third is mobilized from bone due to the gap between daytime feeding and nighttime shell calcification (Nys et al., 2022), studies on limestone particle size are particularly important for laying hens. Notably, a meta-analysis encompassing 251 observations across 40 publications reported no significant effects of limestone particle size, or its interaction with hen age, on egg production or egg weight (Hervo et al., 2022). The authors attributed these findings to the fact that, in all experiments included in the meta-analysis, hens received adequate energy and nutrients to meet their requirements. Our findings are consistent with these results, as no differences were observed in the productive performance of laying hens during either production phase. Similarly, other studies have reported that physical form of limestone does not affect laying performance (Świątkiewicz et al., 2015; Hervo et al., 2023). Guo and Kim (2012) previously suggested that dietary Ca level and genetic strain could influence egg production. In the present study, the experimental diets were formulated to fully meet the hens’ nutritional requirements. In addition, Ca levels did not differ among diets, and the same genetic line was used. Thus, under these conditions, limestone particle size does not appear to affect laying performance.

Although FI was not influenced during the early phase (22–41 weeks of age), a higher proportion of CL in the diet increased intake of older hens (66–81 weeks of age). Previous studies have suggested that larger limestone particle sizes or mixtures of Ca sources may increase FI compared with finely ground limestone, as finely ground limestone provides limited mechanical stimulation in the gizzard, potentially impairing gastrointestinal function (Guo and Kim, 2012; Saki et al., 2019). In addition, the increase in FI observed with higher CL inclusion may be associated with the lower immediate availability of Ca for intestinal absorption. Coarse limestone particles are retained longer in the gizzard and release Ca more gradually, which may limit Ca availability during periods of high demand and delay its intestinal absorption. This effect is particularly relevant in older hens, in which the efficiency of Ca absorption is reduced. According to Gloux et al. (2020), older laying hens exhibit reduced expression of TRPV2 and TRPM7 channels in the jejunum, as well as decreased vitamin D receptor expression, which limits Ca absorption efficiency and leads to compensatory increases in FI. Wistedt et al. (2019) reported that at 70 weeks, hens showed reduced carbonic anhydrase activity in the duodenum, resulting in a lower Ca absorption capacity and, consequently, a greater feed intake requirement to meet the demands for eggshell calcification and bone maintenance. Under these conditions, hens may increase FI as a compensatory mechanism to meet Ca requirements. This response is consistent with the lower tibial Ca content observed in Trial 2, supporting the hypothesis of reduced effective Ca availability under higher CL inclusion. It should be noted that gizzard retention time and Ca solubility were not directly measured in the present study, and therefore the proposed mechanisms remain inferential. However, despite the increase in FI, no improvements in eggshell traits were observed. This may be explained by the dietary Ca levels used in the present study, which were sufficient to meet the hens’ requirements for eggshell formation. Under these conditions, Ca supply was not limiting, and therefore increased FI would not be expected to further enhance eggshell quality.

A recent meta-analysis using regression models reported that increasing dietary limestone particle size improves egg specific gravity, EST, and EBS (Hervo et al., 2022). The physiological mechanisms by which CL particles may influence eggshell traits are multifactorial. Coarse particles are retained longer in the gizzard, resulting in a slower release of Ca, particularly during the nighttime, when eggshell calcification occurs and Ca intake has ceased (Hervo et al., 2022). This prolonged Ca availability reduces bone mobilization and helps maintain adequate plasma Ca concentration during peak eggshell formation. Furthermore, CL is less likely to form insoluble Ca–phytate complexes in the gastrointestinal tract (Hervo et al., 2023). Consequently, increasing the proportion of CL, with up to 75 % coarse particles, has been associated with increased eggshell proportion, weight, thickness, and strength (Khosht et al., 2020; Hervo et al., 2023). However, despite these expected mechanisms, unexpected results were observed in the present study, as eggshell parameters were not influenced by limestone particle size. Rostagno et al. (2024) recommend dietary Ca levels of 41.0 g/kg for laying hens with an estimated BW of 1.8 kg and FI of 114.8 g/hen/day, and 40.3 g/kg for hens with a BW of 1.85 kg and FI of 112.4 g/hen/day. According to the Lohmann management guide (Lohmann, 2020), Ca levels of 35.7 g/kg are recommended for hens aged 19 to 50 weeks with an estimated FI of 115 g/hen/day, and 40.9 g/kg for hens older than 70 weeks with an estimated FI of 110 g/hen/day. In the present study, hens during the early laying phase had an average BW of approximately 1.81 kg and FI of 115.2 g/hen/day, receiving a dietary Ca level of 43.5 g/kg. During the late phase, hens had a BW of 1.86 kg and FI of 109.2 g/hen/day, with a dietary Ca level of 43.0 g/kg. These Ca levels exceeded current recommendations, which may explain the absence of effects of limestone particle size on eggshell traits, as the FL proportion in the diets was sufficient to meet the Ca requirements for adequate shell formation. This represents a limitation of the present study, as the results apply to conditions in which dietary Ca meets or exceeds requirements and may not be directly extrapolated to situations of marginal Ca supply, where responses to limestone particle size could be more pronounced. Previous studies, in agreement with our findings, have shown that the physical form of limestone does not affect eggshell quality, even in aged laying hens (Świątkiewicz et al., 2015; Lima et al., 2024). Collectively, these results indicate that eggshell quality can be maintained provided the minimum Ca intake and an appropriate balance between Ca solubility and gastrointestinal retention time are ensured. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted within the context of adequate or slightly excessive dietary Ca supply, the use of a modern commercial strain (Lohmann Brown), and controlled experimental conditions, which may limit their extrapolation to other production systems.

Internal egg quality has been shown to be less sensitive to dietary Ca levels and limestone particle size (Pizzolante et al., 2009; Cordeiro et al., 2017), which is consistent with our findings, as replacing FL with CL did not affect yolk or albumen quality. According to Wilson and Duff (1990), internal egg quality is more closely related to the physiological state of the hen and egg storage duration than to the mineral composition of the diet. Furthermore, Van de Velde et al. (1984) reported that, throughout the laying cycle, medullary bone cell activity regulates Ca mobilization for eggshell formation without directly influencing internal egg components. In this context, the results of the present study indicate that, despite changes in dietary FL:CL ratios, the Ca supply was sufficient to maintain egg internal quality during the evaluated period.

Blood parameters were also not affected by the dietary treatments in the present study, suggesting efficient maintenance of mineral homeostasis and stable osteoblastic activity across different limestone particle sizes. These findings are consistent with those reported by Świątkiewicz et al. (2015), who observed that limestone particle sizes ranging from 0.2 to 0.6 mm to 1.0–1.4 mm did not influence serum Ca, P, or ALP concentrations in laying hens aged 25 to 70 weeks.

CL was expected to improve bone quality, as larger particles are retained longer in the gizzard and provide Ca more gradually, thereby reducing the desynchronization between Ca supply and physiological demand (Bueno et al., 2016; Hervo et al., 2022). This delayed Ca release was hypothesized to enhance Ca absorption in the small intestine and, consequently, reduce the need for bone Ca mobilization (Wistedt et al., 2019). However, such effects were not observed in the present study. Contrary to the initial hypothesis, the highest proportion of CL (30:70) was associated with negative effects on specific bone traits, including reduced tibia breaking strength and RTW in Trial 1, and lower tibial Ca content in Trial 2, although other bone parameters were not significantly affected. These findings suggest that higher proportions of CL may negatively affect different aspects of bone quality depending on the production phase. Overall, general indicators of bone status, such as Seedor index and tibial ash content, were maintained. These findings also suggest a potential trade-off between maintaining adequate Ca supply for eggshell formation and preserving tibial Ca reserves under higher proportions of CL. While the gradual release of Ca from coarse particles may support eggshell calcification, it may not optimally sustain bone mineralization, particularly under conditions of high dietary Ca supply.

Increasing the proportion of CL may alter the temporal pattern of Ca availability in a manner that is not optimal for bone remodeling, particularly when Ca supply is already sufficient or in excess. Under such conditions, the physiological system regulating Ca homeostasis may be less responsive to changes in Ca release kinetics, potentially leading to suboptimal utilization of Ca for bone mineralization. Consistent with these findings, Pelicia et al. (2011) reported an interaction between dietary Ca levels and limestone particle size on tibial Ca content in 35-week-old laying hens, showing that increases in both dietary Ca concentration and CL proportion resulted in reduced tibial Ca deposition. Jardim Filho et al. (2005) also reported inconsistent effects of limestone granulometry on bone mineralization. When limestone source 1 was used, hens fed coarser particles exhibited higher tibial Ca content at 53 and 61 weeks of age. In contrast, when limestone source 2 was used, coarser particles reduced tibial Ca content in hens at the same ages. Although both limestone sources had similar geometric mean diameters, these differences were attributed to variations in limestone origin, which resulted in distinct Ca solubility and availability. These findings suggest that differences in Ca solubility or microstructural characteristics of limestone may influence Ca availability, meaning that coarser particles do not necessarily result in improved bone mineral deposition.

The assessment of renal CYP27B1 protein abundance in response to different limestone particle size ratios represents a novel contribution of the present study. Plasma Ca concentrations are tightly regulated within narrow physiological limits by feedback mechanisms involving parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcitonin (CT), and vitamin D₃, which coordinate Ca fluxes among the small intestine, bone, and kidneys (Pizauro Junior et al., 2017). In the avian kidney, the enzyme CYP27B1 catalyzes the conversion of the inactive vitamin D₃ form (25-hydroxycholecalciferol) to the hormonally active form (1α,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol) (Nys et al., 2022). The active form of vitamin D₃ plays a pivotal role in Ca homeostasis by enhancing intestinal Ca absorption through both genomic and non-genomic mechanisms (Pizauro Junior et al., 2017). A decline in plasma Ca is detected by the calcium-sensing receptor, which stimulates PTH synthesis. PTH, in turn, activates renal CYP27B1, increasing the production of active vitamin D₃ and thereby enhancing intestinal Ca absorption. Once plasma Ca concentration are restored, PTH expression is downregulated through negative feedback, maintaining homeostasis (Pizauro Junior et al., 2017). In the present study, hens fed the 50:50 FL:CL ratio exhibited lower renal CYP27B1 protein abundance compared to those fed 60:40 or 30:70 ratios, indicating reduced activation of vitamin D₃ metabolism. This finding suggests that the intermediate ratio may provide a more balanced and sustained Ca supply, reducing fluctuations in Ca availability and, consequently, the physiological need to upregulate vitamin D₃ activation. This response is consistent with the absence of negative effects on bone traits observed at the 50:50 ratio, in contrast to the 30:70 treatment, which showed reduced tibia breaking strength and tibial Ca content. Together, these findings support the interpretation that the 50:50 ratio is consistent with a more stable Ca homeostasis, reducing the physiological demand for vitamin D₃ activation while maintaining bone integrity. This suggests that the 50:50 FL:CL ratio may provide a more physiologically balanced Ca release profile under high dietary Ca conditions, resulting in reduced stimulation of the PTH–vitamin D regulatory axis. This pattern also indicates that both higher proportions of FL or CL may lead to less synchronized Ca availability, either due to rapid Ca release (fine particles) or delayed Ca release (coarse particles), thereby increasing the physiological demand for CYP27B1-mediated regulation compared to the intermediate ratio. This U-shaped response suggests the involvement of a biological regulatory mechanism controlling Ca homeostasis, in which both insufficient and delayed Ca availability may stimulate compensatory activation of the PTH–vitamin D axis.

In conclusion, under the conditions of the present study, in which dietary Ca levels exceeded current recommendations, the FL:CL ratios did not affect laying performance, blood parameters, or internal and external egg quality. The absence of significant effects on eggshell traits is likely associated with the adequate Ca supply provided, which may have masked potential effects of limestone particle size. Therefore, under conditions in which Ca supply is not limiting, increased FI does not necessarily translate into improvements in eggshell quality. However, a higher proportion of coarse limestone (30:70 FL:CL) reduced tibia breaking strength and RTW during the early production phase and decreased tibial Ca content during the late phase, indicating subtle but unfavorable effects on selected bone traits. In addition, the intermediate 50:50 FL:CL ratio was associated with lower renal CYP27B1 protein abundance, which is consistent with a more stable Ca homeostasis and a reduced demand for vitamin D activation, although this interpretation is based on indirect evidence. Overall, under conditions in which dietary Ca is adequate or exceeds current recommendations, altering FL:CL ratios does not improve eggshell quality and may negatively affect specific bone parameters under higher proportions of CL. Future studies should evaluate FL:CL ratios under marginal Ca supply and include direct measurements of Ca solubility and gizzard retention time to better define optimal granulometry strategies.
