How well can off-the-shelf LLMs elucidate molecular structures from mass spectra using chain-of-thought reasoning?
Authors: Yufeng Wang, Lu Wei, Lin Liu, Hao Xu, Haibin Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mass spectrometry (MS) is a powerful analytical technique for identifying small molecules, yet determining complete molecular structures directly from tandem mass spectra (MS/MS) remains a long-standing challenge due to complex fragmentation patterns and the vast diversity of chemical space. Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) has shown promise for reasoning-intensive scientific tasks, but their capability for chemical interpretation is still unclear. We present a zero-shot chain-of-thought (CoT) benchmark for evaluating how well off-the-shelf general-purpose LLMs can infer molecular structure from text-serialized MS/MS peak lists and a known molecular formula, without fine-tuning, retrieval, external chemistry tools, or constrained search. Rather than proposing a new spectrum-trained model, our goal is diagnostic: to isolate what the evaluated off-the-shelf LLMs can and cannot do in a direct MS/MS-to-SMILES generation setting when only textualized spectral evidence and prompting are available. This distinction is important because our benchmark targets a deliberately constrained regime: direct structure generation from text-formatted spectral evidence alone, rather than multimodal reasoning with spectral images, candidate reranking over structures generated by specialized chemistry systems, or knowledge and search-augmented LLM workflows. We formalize expert chemists’ reasoning steps (such as double bond equivalent (DBE) analysis, neutral loss identification, and fragment assembly) into structured prompts and assess multiple state-of-the-art LLMs (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o-mini, and Llama-3 series) using the MassSpecGym dataset. Our evaluation across metrics of SMILES validity, formula consistency, and structural similarity reveals that while LLMs can produce syntactically valid and partially plausible structures, they fail to achieve chemical accuracy or link reasoning to correct molecular predictions. These findings highlight both the interpretive potential and the current limitations of LLM-based reasoning for molecular elucidation, providing a foundation for future work that combines domain knowledge and reinforcement learning to achieve chemically grounded AI reasoning. Scientific contribution We introduce a zero-shot chain-of-thought benchmark for direct MS/MS-to-SMILES prediction with off-the-shelf general-purpose LLMs, providing a controlled test of text-only, no-tools molecular structure elucidation. In contrast to prior approaches based on spectrum-trained models, multimodal reasoning, or tool-augmented candidate pipelines, our study isolates the capabilities and limitations of prompting alone. We show that fluent chain-of-thought reasoning does not translate into chemically grounded structure recovery, establishing a reproducible baseline and a clear target for future method development.

Introduction

Mass spectrometry (MS) is a fundamental analytical technique for small-molecule identification, widely used in natural product discovery, metabolomics, and drug development [1]. Its high sensitivity, fast throughput, and ability to resolve complex mixtures make it suitable for analyzing the chemical composition of biological and environmental samples [2]. By providing accurate mass measurements and fragmentation patterns through tandem MS (MS/MS), this technique enables determination of molecular formulas and substructures, supporting both the identification of known compounds and the partial characterization of unknowns [3]. Despite these advantages, determining complete molecular structures from MS data alone remains a long-standing challenge due to the ambiguity of fragmentation spectra and the vast diversity of chemical space [4]. Consequently, expert chemists rely on domain knowledge, careful interpretation of fragment ions, and iterative hypothesis refinement to infer plausible molecular structures.

Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) methods, especially deep generative and discriminative models, have been increasingly explored for molecular structure elucidation from mass spectrometry data [5–12]. Early systems such as SIRIUS formulate identification as a fragmentation-tree optimization problem, using Bayesian scoring over putative fragment trees to infer molecular formulas and rank candidate structures from spectral libraries [5]. MSNovelist combines spectrum-derived molecular fingerprints with an encoder-decoder network that generates SMILES (Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System) strings de novo from MS/MS data, enabling open-set structure prediction beyond database lookup [6, 13, 14]. MassGenie trains a large Transformer (around 400 M parameters) directly on collections of simulated spectra and SMILES strings to learn mappings from fragmentation patterns to structures [7]. End-to-end encoder-decoder models such as Spec2Mol [8, 15] similarly process spectra with a neural encoder and decode candidate SMILES sequences. These approaches perform well when spectra contain rich fragmentation information, but their performance often degrades on experimental MS/MS data due to weak generalization to unseen chemotypes and the lack of explicit domain constraints (e.g., atom valency or molecular formulas). In addition, sequence-based models are sensitive to peak order, can produce inconsistent results under minor spectral perturbations, and are generally difficult to interpret because they output structures without exposing intermediate chemical reasoning or substructure relationships. To overcome some of these limitations, more recent work has integrated domain-specific priors and structured chemical representations into spectrum-to-structure models [9–11, 16–18]. MIST [9] proposes a spectrum-to-fingerprint Transformer that leverages peak annotations, neutral losses, and a progressive fingerprint decoding process, improving interpretability and downstream reconstruction. DiffMS [10] extends this idea with a formula-constrained graph diffusion model that generates molecular graphs directly from spectra while respecting atom-composition constraints; the diffusion mechanism also supports permutation invariance in both molecular and spectral representations. MADGEN [11] introduces a two-stage approach that first retrieves a scaffold via contrastive learning and then generates full structures around it, narrowing the search space and improving robustness for sparse spectra. While these methods emphasize structural constraints and inductive biases, they remain primarily model-centric: they focus on learning accurate mappings from spectra to structures but still provide limited visibility into the reasoning process by which spectral evidence is translated into molecular hypotheses.

In parallel, LLMs have demonstrated strong general-purpose reasoning capabilities. CoT prompting, where a model is instructed to generate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer, has been shown to substantially improve performance on tasks that require multi-step logic, arithmetic, and symbolic manipulation [19]. Beyond pure prompting, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful tool for sharpening these reasoning abilities. InstructGPT and related reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) systems fine-tune LLMs with human preference feedback, yielding models that follow instructions more faithfully, provide more truthful explanations, and better align their step-by-step reasoning with user intent [20–24]. ReAct-style agents interleave natural-language reasoning with external actions, generating thought-action trajectories that query tools or environments and update plans based on observations [25], and more recent work uses RL to improve multi-step reasoning by exposing models to progressively more challenging reasoning tasks [26, 27]. Together, these CoT and RL advances move beyond static pattern matching toward models that can iteratively plan, reflect, and adapt, suggesting a promising yet largely unexplored direction for applying LLM-style reasoning to physically grounded scientific domains such as mass spectral structure elucidation.

Interpreting MS/MS spectra to determine molecular structures is inherently a multi-step analytical process: chemists start from the precursor formula and DBE, identify key peaks and neutral losses, group fragments into substructures, and finally assemble a globally consistent molecular graph [28, 29]. These characteristics make the MS-to-molecule problem naturally suitable for CoT-style reasoning. However, it is not yet clear to what extent general-purpose LLMs can perform such structured analysis or whether their explanations meaningfully reflect underlying chemical constraints [30]. A key scope clarification is necessary. In this work, we do not evaluate multimodal LLMs operating over raw spectral images, tool-augmented agents that call external chemistry software, retrieval-based systems, or fine-tuned spectrum-specialized models. Instead, we study a narrower but important diagnostic setting: off-the-shelf general-purpose LLMs, prompted in a strictly zero-shot manner, given text-formatted MS/MS peak lists and a molecular formula, and asked to directly generate a SMILES string without external tools or task-specific adaptation. We intentionally isolate this setting to test whether fluent CoT reasoning alone is sufficient for chemically grounded mass spectral structure elucidation.

Our contributions are as follows: We introduce a zero-shot CoT benchmark for direct MS/MS-to-SMILES prediction using off-the-shelf general-purpose LLMs, where the model receives text-serialized peak lists and a molecular formula and must generate a structure without any model updates or external chemistry tools.We provide a diagnostic evaluation protocol that measures not only structural correctness and similarity, but also the consistency between the model’s intermediate CoT reasoning and its final predicted structure.We show that, in this constrained no-tools setting, the four evaluated off-the-shelf LLMs can produce fluent and expert-like chemical explanations while failing to recover chemically correct structures, revealing a substantial gap between linguistic plausibility and mechanistic grounding.

Related work

Classical and learning-based molecular structure elucidation. Molecular structure elucidation has a long history in computational chemistry, including computer-assisted structure elucidation (CASE) systems and spectrum-aware pipelines that combine expert rules, database matching, and specialized predictive models. More recent machine learning methods have approached spectrum-to-structure prediction directly, including mass-spectrometry-specific generative and retrieval-oriented systems. These methods are typically designed around spectral inductive bias, chemistry-aware constraints, or task-specific training, which distinguishes them from the off-the-shelf prompted LLM setting studied here.

LLMs and benchmark-style evaluation for structure elucidation. Recent work has begun to examine structure elucidation as a challenging reasoning problem for LLMs. Most closely related is MolPuzzle [31], which formulates molecular structure elucidation as a multimodal puzzle-solving benchmark involving molecular formulas together with IR, mass, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{1}$$\end{document}1H-NMR, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{13}$$\end{document}13C-NMR evidence. MolPuzzle decomposes the task into molecule understanding, spectrum interpretation, and molecule construction, and shows that even strong LLMs remain far below human performance, with the best exact-match rate reported as only 1.4%. These results are highly relevant to our study because they likewise suggest that structure elucidation remains difficult for general-purpose LLMs, especially in the stages requiring deep spectral interpretation and consistent molecule construction. However, our setting is different: instead of a multimodal puzzle benchmark with multiple spectroscopy modalities and staged sub-tasks, we focus specifically on direct MS/MS-to-SMILES generation from text-serialized peak lists and a known formula, and we explicitly analyze the consistency between CoT traces and final structural predictions.

LLMs for spectroscopy-based chemical reasoning benchmarks. Recent work has also shown that LLM performance in chemistry can be substantially stronger on certain structured spectroscopy reasoning tasks than on full direct structure reconstruction. For example, NMR-Challenge for LLMs [32] evaluates multiple LLMs on text-formatted NMR structure-determination problems derived from educational and experimental settings, and reports that newer reasoning-focused models can outperform undergraduate students on that benchmark. Importantly, this study emphasizes reasoning over raw-spectrum perception by converting spectral information into a textual representation, and shows that model performance depends strongly on the problem format and available formula information. We view these findings as complementary rather than contradictory to ours: they suggest that LLMs can succeed on some spectroscopy-centered reasoning tasks, while our results show that direct zero-shot MS/MS-to-SMILES generation without external chemistry scaffolding remains substantially more difficult.

LLMs as components in tool-augmented elucidation workflows. Another important line of work uses LLMs not as standalone inverse solvers, but as reasoning modules embedded inside broader chemistry pipelines. For example, Priessner et al. [33] integrate reasoning-capable LLMs into a candidate-based NMR structure elucidation workflow in which specialized systems first generate molecular candidates, spectral-analysis modules quantitatively compare those candidates to experimental data, and the LLM then performs evidence-aware analysis and reranking. In that setting, the LLM improves top-ranked identification substantially, demonstrating that language models can add value when placed in the role of interpretable evaluator over candidate sets. This is complementary to our benchmark rather than contradictory to it: in contrast to their workflow, our models receive no pre-generated candidate structures, no dedicated spectral matching module, and no chemistry-specific evaluation tool during inference.

Knowledge- and search-augmented LLM reasoning for elucidation. A third line of work improves LLM performance by adding explicit domain scaffolding during inference. The recent K-MSE framework [34] augments a base LLM with a molecular substructure knowledge base, a molecule-spectrum scoring model, and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)-style reasoning and self-refinement. On the MolPuzzle benchmark, this knowledge-enhanced search procedure substantially improves over the underlying base models. Importantly, this paper attributes the weakness of plain LLMs to limited chemistry-specific knowledge and poor self-evaluation, which aligns closely with our observation that free-form CoT can appear chemically plausible while remaining weakly grounded in the spectrum. K-MSE therefore does not invalidate our negative result; instead, it suggests a concrete path forward: meaningful gains may require retrieval, explicit search, and chemistry-specific reward signals rather than prompting alone.

Position of this work. Relative to these prior studies, our paper addresses a deliberately narrower diagnostic question: How well do off-the-shelf general-purpose LLMs perform in a zero-shot, text-only, no-tools setting when asked to directly infer a molecular structure from MS/MS evidence and a known formula? This setting differs from multimodal structure-elucidation benchmarks, candidate-reranking systems, and knowledge/search-augmented reasoning frameworks. Our contribution is therefore not a claim that LLMs have never been studied for structure elucidation, but rather a targeted evaluation showing that, in the absence of chemistry-specific scaffolding, fluent CoT reasoning does not yield chemically reliable direct MS/MS-to-SMILES prediction.

Methods

Dataset: MassSpecGym

All experiments in this study use the MassSpecGym benchmark [35], a large-scale, well-established resource published at NeurIPS 2024 (Track on Datasets and Benchmarks). MassSpecGym is the largest publicly available collection of quality-controlled, expert-validated MS/MS spectra paired with confirmed molecular structures, comprising 231,104 spectra representing approximately 29,000 unique molecules. Critically, the benchmark annotations were subjected to a rigorous, multi-stage quality-control pipeline: (1) a SIRIUS-based formula-decomposition filter removing spectra where less than 50% of total intensity could be explained by chemically valid fragment sub-formulae; (2) cross-validation against the commercial NIST20 spectral library, removing spectra with no shared peaks or cosine similarity below 0.2; and (3) pairwise intra-dataset outlier removal [35]. A manual audit confirmed that discarded entries were “at least suspicious, with many clearly being wrongly annotated.” The resulting dataset draws from the established public repositories GNPS, MoNA, and MassBank, as well as in-house authentic-standard measurements. Each data instance consists of: (i) a tandem MS/MS spectrum given as a list of (m/z, relative-intensity) peak pairs acquired in positive-ion mode on Orbitrap-type instruments at a range of collision energies; (ii) the molecular formula of the precursor compound, provided as an oracle input (i.e., assumed known); (iii) the canonical SMILES string of the ground-truth structure, used as the evaluation target; and (iv) acquisition metadata including instrument type, adduct, and collision energy. The molecular formula is not predicted by the models; it is given as part of the input, reflecting a common real-world scenario in metabolomics where the formula is determined from the high-resolution precursor mass before structural elucidation.

Problem formulation as a reasoning and generation task

In this study, we evaluate a deliberately constrained benchmark setting. Each model is prompted in a zero-shot manner and receives as input a text-serialized representation of an MS/MS spectrum together with the known molecular formula and optional acquisition metadata. The model is then asked to produce a chain-of-thought trace and a final SMILES prediction. No model parameters are updated, and no external chemistry engines, retrieval systems, calculators, spectral libraries, or constrained decoding modules are used. The benchmark therefore isolates the capabilities of off-the-shelf LLMs under prompting alone. The reason is that SMILES is a non-unique linear notation: the same molecule can be written in many syntactically distinct but semantically equivalent ways. For example, the amino acid tyrosine can be represented as NC(Cc1ccc(O)cc1)C(=O)O, OC(=O)[C@@H](N)Cc1ccc(O)cc1, or N[C@@H](Cc1ccc(O)cc1)C(=O)O, among dozens of other valid SMILES strings. The Levenshtein distance between the first and second representation is 12 (several insertions for stereochemistry markers and reordering of the carboxyl group), yet the two strings encode identical molecular structures with Tanimoto = 1.0=1.0. Conversely, a small string edit (e.g., changing one ring attachment point) can produce a chemically very different molecule with Levenshtein = 1=1 but Tanimoto ≪ 1≪1. String-edit distances on SMILES therefore do not reliably reflect chemical similarity.

Instead, we pair formula consistency (a hard, chemistry-driven criterion that checks whether the model obeys the known elemental composition) with one graph-based structural similarity measure (Tanimoto, v) and one graph-based dissimilarity measure (MCES, vi), both computed from the molecular graph rather than the raw SMILES string and therefore independent of superficial string-order differences across equivalent representations. This combination provides both a strict correctness check and a graded notion of structural proximity, which is more informative for diagnosing the specific failure modes of LLM-based structure generation than string-edit metrics alone.

Several clarifications are warranted regarding the MCES score. First, the MCES algorithm operates on the complete molecular graph, not only the largest connected component: it finds the globally largest subset of chemical bonds (edges) that can be matched between the predicted graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G(\hat{s})$$\end{document}G(s^) and the ground-truth graph G(s) simultaneously across the entire molecule. Second, the normalized dissimilarity score above explicitly accounts for graph-size differences via the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max (|E(G(s))|, |E(G(\hat{s}))|)$$\end{document}max(|E(G(s))|,|E(G(s^))|) denominator, so larger molecules are not artificially penalized for having more bonds. Third, a small peripheral edit (e.g., changing one substituent from-CH3 to -CH2OH) may contribute only modestly to the MCES score when a large shared scaffold dominates the common edge set. This is an acknowledged limitation of MCES-based similarity; however, our evaluation pairs MCES with Tanimoto similarity, which is computed from Morgan circular fingerprints and is sensitive to local substructure differences regardless of global scaffold size. The two metrics therefore complement each other: MCES captures global graph-connectivity agreement, while Tanimoto captures local substructure sharing, and together they provide a more complete picture of structural accuracy than either alone. Together, Tanimoto and MCES distinguish models that merely produce syntactically valid SMILES from those that capture deeper structural constraints.

Throughout this paper, “SMILES validity” refers strictly to RDKit parsability (i.e., Chem.MolFromSmiles returns a non-None Mol object); this is a syntactic criterion and does not guarantee full chemical correctness or physical plausibility.

CoT reasoning quality is additionally assessed through format adherence and step-wise chemical plausibility checks ("Qualitative behavior of CoT reasoning" section). We intentionally avoid using a second LLM as a fidelity judge for CoT traces, as this would introduce an additional model-dependent source of uncertainty; instead we use rule-based checks: DBE correctness, formula correctness, and contradiction analysis between the CoT and the final SMILES.

CoT prompting

To guide structure prediction from spectra, we use a CoT prompting strategy embedded in the LLM input. The reasoning chain follows five steps:Formula and DBE analysis to constrain feasible scaffolds;Key peak identification (molecular ion and base peak) to anchor molecular weight and stable fragments;Neutral loss analysis from prominent ions to infer functional groups;Fragment-ion relationships and substructure assembly to connect candidate substructures consistently with F and DBE; andStructure elucidation and SMILES hypothesis that synthesizes the evidence into candidate structures.The template provides explicit headings and brief instructions for each stage (e.g., DBE: C − H/2 + N/2 + 1+1”), promoting a consistent, chemically meaningful reasoning order from global constraints to specific fragments. This design mirrors expert practice, improves transparency of the model’s analysis, and supports qualitative error analysis. All reasoning is generated by the model itself under prompting alone; no external calculators, chemistry engines, retrieval systems, or agentic tool calls are available during inference. Experiments and results

In this section, we describe our evaluation setup and present a systematic analysis of how CoT prompting affects LLM behavior on the MS-to-SMILES task. We analyze global performance, size-stratified behavior by molecular weight, the consistency of the generated CoT reasoning, and output reproducibility.

Experimental setup

Models and prompting. We benchmark four representative LLMs: Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, and Llama-3-8B-Instruct. All models are used in a strict zero-shot setting and receive the same structured CoT input template described in "CoT prompting" Section. The prompt encodes the spectrum, precursor formula, and instrument metadata, and explicitly asks the model to reason within a <think> block before listing the final SMILES candidates in an <answer> block. No model training, fine-tuning, or task-specific adaptation is performed; performance therefore reflects the native reasoning and generation capabilities of the four evaluated off-the-shelf LLMs under a consistent, domain-aware prompting scheme.

Decoding settings. All experiments were run with temperature 0, top-p =1.0=1.0, and a maximum output length of 4096 tokens. This configuration is intended to be near-deterministic. However, because inference is performed through hosted model APIs, minor provider-side nondeterminism may still lead to output variation across repeated calls, even under the same decoding parameters. We additionally evaluate repeated-run variability in "Output variability and reproducibility" Section.

First, all models are capable of following the high-level CoT instructions, but with varying reliability. Claude-3.5-Sonnet achieves nearly perfect adherence to the prescribed format, with Think and Answer rates above 99.9% and a SMILES validity of 93.7%. GPT-4o-mini also respects the <think>/<answer> structure in most cases and yields valid SMILES in roughly three-quarters of instances. In contrast, Llama-3-8B frequently truncates or misformats its output (Answer Rate 2.21%), which directly translates into a very low validity rate (1.44%). Scaling to Llama-3-70B substantially improves format adherence and validity (51.14% Answer Rate; 38.12% Validity), indicating that larger models are more robust under long, structured prompts.

Second, structural similarity remains modest even for the best-performing models. Tanimoto scores hover around 0.09–0.12 and MCES dissimilarity values (0.14–0.17; lower means more similar) confirm only partial graph-level overlap with the ground truth. Claude-3.5-Sonnet and GPT-4o-mini achieve marginally lower MCES dissimilarity (0.15 Top-1) than the Llama models (0.17 Top-1), indicating slightly better graph-level agreement on average; the Llama models’ higher dissimilarity reflects more structurally divergent predictions when valid SMILES are produced. The differences are small in absolute terms, and no model comes close to reproducing the full scaffold or substitution pattern of the ground truth.

Third, chemical consistency metrics are uniformly poor. DBE accuracy remains in the 3.9–4.2% range, and formula consistency is effectively zero for all models (except for a marginal 0.02% for GPT-4o-mini and Llama-3-70B). Most strikingly, Top-1 and Top-10 exact-match accuracy are identically zero across the board: none of the models ever predicts the correct canonical SMILES, even when allowed ten attempts. This shows that, although CoT prompting leads to outputs that look chemically sophisticated at the text level (e.g., DBE formulas, named neutral losses), the final structures are neither formula-faithful nor spectrally grounded.

Taken together, these results indicate that the four evaluated off-the-shelf LLMs can follow a structured, chemically flavored reasoning template and generate syntactically valid SMILES for some fraction of spectra, but they fail to translate that reasoning into accurate or chemically self-consistent molecular structures. In the next subsection, we ask whether this failure is uniform across the chemical space, or whether certain molecule classes are more amenable to CoT-guided prediction.

Size-stratified performance by molecular weight

To examine how molecular complexity influences LLM behavior, we stratify the MassSpecGym benchmark into five precursor-weight bins: [0, 200), [200, 400), [400, 600), [600, 800), and [800,∞ )[800,∞) Da (Dalton, the standard molecular-mass unit [37]). For each bin, we compute Top-1/Top-10 Tanimoto and MCES as well as Top-1/Top-10 exact-match accuracy across all four evaluated models (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3-8B, and Llama-3-70B). Consistently, Top-1 and Top-10 exact-match accuracy remain zero in every bin and for every model, confirming that LLMs never reconstruct the correct SMILES in any molecular-weight regime; the absence of exact matches in global results is therefore not attributable to a few difficult molecules but reflects a pervasive inability to invert mass spectra into structures. More informative differences only appear when examining graded similarity metrics such as Tanimoto and MCES. Here, a consistent pattern emerges: structural similarity peaks in the 200–400 Da range. Claude-3.5-Sonnet achieves Top-1/10 Tanimoto scores of 0.092/0.103 and MCES of 0.178/0.166, GPT-4o-mini shows similar values (0.092/0.121 Tanimoto; 0.165/0.076 MCES), and the Llama models slightly exceed these numbers (e.g., Llama-3-8B at 0.097/0.105 Tanimoto and 0.198/0.183 MCES; Llama-3-70B at 0.100/0.123 Tanimoto and 0.192/0.156 MCES). These scores match or slightly surpass each model’s global averages, suggesting that mid-sized molecules represent a shared “comfort zone” where LLMs can assemble partially correct fragments—likely reflecting dataset prevalence and broader alignment with more common small-molecule patterns.

Beyond 400 Da, performance declines steadily for all models. For example, Llama-3-8B’s Top-1 Tanimoto drops from ∼∼0.10 in the 200–400 Da bin to ∼∼0.08 (400–600 Da) and ∼∼0.05 (600–800 Da), with MCES showing an even sharper decline; Llama-3-70B, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, and GPT-4o-mini exhibit comparable trajectories. This deterioration reflects the combinatorial explosion of chemically plausible structures at higher molecular weights: without any spectral encoder, chemical constraints, or modeling of fragmentation physics, the evaluated LLMs effectively sample from a vast, weakly constrained scaffold space. Larger molecules also produce denser, more ambiguous fragment spectra, requiring mechanistic knowledge that the evaluated models do not encode. Interestingly, very small molecules (<200<200 Da) also underperform compared to the 200–400 Da range. Although their structures are simpler, their spectra often contain fewer informative fragments, and many molecules share similar low-mass signatures; this intrinsic ambiguity is reflected in the modest Top-1 Tanimoto scores (0.087–0.092) and MCES values (0.14–0.16) across models in this bin. Sample distribution further amplifies these effects: the 200–400 Da bin dominates the dataset (16–18k spectra for GPT-4o-mini and the Llama models), whereas the 800+ Da bin contains far fewer samples (hundreds per model; only 30 for Claude), so global averages are heavily influenced by the mid-mass region and partially obscure the severity of performance degradation at the extremes. Yet even in the most favorable regime, similarity scores remain low and exact matches never occur, highlighting that CoT alone cannot bridge the gap between textual reasoning and the mechanistic interpretation required for MS-based structure elucidation.

The contradiction rate further highlights this disconnect between linguistic reasoning and chemical validity. Conflicts between the CoT and the final SMILES occur in 72.8% of cases for Claude-3.5-Sonnet, 92.2% for GPT-4o-mini, 92.0% for Llama-3-8B, and 82.3% for Llama-3-70B. Notably, larger models such as Llama-3-70B produce substantially longer CoT explanations on average (435.5 words), yet this increased verbosity does not improve DBE or formula correctness; instead, it often amplifies the amount of erroneous or contradictory reasoning produced. Together, these trends demonstrate that CoT explanations lack mechanistic grounding and fail to guide the model toward chemically valid outcomes.

Overall, the CoT analysis mirrors the structural-similarity findings: LLMs can fluently emulate the language of expert mass spectral interpretation without internalizing the mechanistic principles required for accurate structure elucidation. In this setting, CoT prompting acts more as a stylistic scaffold than as a functional reasoning mechanism. Although the explanations appear detailed and expert-like, they rarely contain logically coherent or chemically correct deductions, and they frequently contradict the generated structures. This gap highlights the central challenge of applying text-only LLMs to scientific reasoning tasks where correctness depends on underlying physical and chemical constraints rather than linguistic plausibility.

Discussion

Relationship to recent positive results on LLM-assisted structure elucidation

At first glance, our negative findings may appear to conflict with recent papers reporting encouraging results for LLM-assisted chemical reasoning. We believe these results are in fact consistent once the task setting is carefully specified. MolPuzzle [31] shows that general-purpose LLMs struggle on full structure-elucidation puzzles, particularly in spectrum interpretation and molecule construction, which supports our finding that chemically faithful multi-step reasoning remains difficult for the evaluated off-the-shelf models. By contrast, more positive recent results arise in settings where the LLM is not acting as an unassisted direct inverse solver. Priessner et al. [33] place the LLM inside a candidate-based workflow, where specialized chemistry modules first generate and score plausible structures and the language model serves as an evidence-aware reranker. K-MSE [34] improves over base LLMs by adding a substructure knowledge base, a dedicated molecule-spectrum scoring signal, and tree-search-based refinement. Taken together, these studies suggest that LLMs may currently be more effective as components of hybrid structure-elucidation systems than as standalone zero-shot direct generators from text-formatted spectral input. Our benchmark isolates precisely this latter setting and shows that CoT prompting alone is not enough.

Recent evidence also suggests that LLMs can perform well on some chemistry reasoning benchmarks. For instance, the NMR-Challenge benchmark [32] reports that reasoning-focused models can exceed undergraduate performance on text-based NMR structure-determination tasks. However, that setting differs materially from ours: it targets NMR reasoning in a benchmark designed around structured peak interpretation, whereas our benchmark evaluates direct MS/MS-to-SMILES generation from text-serialized tandem-mass-spectrometry evidence. Taken together, these results suggest that LLM capabilities in chemical reasoning are highly sensitive to modality, representation, and task formulation.

What this negative result does and does not imply

Our results should be interpreted with care. They do not imply that LLMs are broadly useless for molecular structure elucidation, nor do they rule out success from hybrid systems that incorporate chemistry-specific tools, candidate generators, retrieval over spectral databases, constrained search, or task-specific fine-tuning. Rather, our results show that free-form zero-shot CoT prompting alone is insufficient in the direct MS/MS-to-SMILES setting considered here. In other words, when off-the-shelf general-purpose LLMs are given only text-formatted spectral evidence and a molecular formula, without external scaffolding, fluent reasoning traces do not translate into chemically grounded structure recovery. We therefore view this benchmark as both a warning against unassisted deployment of off-the-shelf LLMs for this task and an explicit call to the research community to develop better LLM-based solutions, including task-specific fine-tuning, reinforcement learning with chemistry-grounded reward signals, spectral encoder integration, and constrained decoding, that can close the substantial capability gap documented here.

Main findings

The results presented in "Experiments and Results" Section collectively reveal a consistent and robust picture of how the four evaluated off-the-shelf LLMs behave when tasked with MS-to-SMILES structure elucidation under CoT prompting. Despite their strong fluency in natural language and apparent ability to mimic expert-style reasoning patterns, all four models (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3-8B, and Llama-3-70B) exhibit fundamental limitations in chemical fidelity, spectral grounding, and structural correctness. CoT prompting successfully enforces a structured output format (with Think/Answer adherence exceeding 97% for Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o-mini, and Llama-3-70B), yet this surface-level compliance does not translate into chemically valid reasoning: DBE accuracy remains between 3.9% and 4.2%, formula correctness is effectively zero, and none of the models ever produce the correct SMILES under Top-1 or Top-10 evaluation. CoT therefore acts primarily as a stylistic scaffold that shapes how models present their reasoning, not as a mechanism for grounding their predictions in the underlying chemistry of mass spectrometry. When we compare LLMs to specialized MS-based structure generation systems, the limitations become even clearer. As shown in Table 5, spectrum-trained architectures, including SMILES/SELFIES Transformers, Spec2Mol, MIST+NeuralDecipher, and especially DiffMS outperform LLMs by a substantial margin. DiffMS achieves 2.30% Top-1 and 4.25% Top-10 accuracy, with Tanimoto similarity reaching 0.39, three to four times higher than the best LLM scores (0.09–0.12). Even older baselines without diffusion, such as Spec2Mol or MIST+NeuralDecipher, match or exceed LLM performance despite being far smaller and domain-specific. This gap highlights a key distinction: while DiffMS and related models ingest real spectral features, enforce chemical constraints, and incorporate fragmentation physics, the LLMs in our study treat mass spectra as formatted text and rely solely on broad chemical patterns learned from language pretraining. Their predictions therefore reflect linguistic familiarity rather than mechanistic spectral interpretation.

The size-stratified analyses reinforce this interpretation. All four models perform best within the 200–400 Da range, likely reflecting dataset prevalence and possibly better alignment with more common drug-like fragments seen during pretraining. Yet even in this range, Top-1 Tanimoto remains roughly 0.10, far below the performance of spectrum-conditioned models. Beyond this range, performance decays sharply: small molecules (<200<200 Da) lack sufficient spectral fragment information to constrain the inverse problem (Fig. 6a), while large molecules (>400>400 Da) become increasingly difficult due to the combinatorial explosion of plausible structural isomers (Fig. 6c). This U-shaped behavior across molecular weight demonstrates that the four evaluated models do not fail uniformly, but rather exhibit a narrow window of partial plausibility shaped more by dataset and pretraining biases than by true spectral reasoning.

Finally, our CoT analysis reveals that the internal reasoning traces are verbose (200–435 words) yet unreliable: DBE computations almost never match ground truth, formula counts are nearly always incorrect, and contradiction rates exceed 70% across all models. This mirrors the structural results: LLMs can fluently emulate expert analytical language, including references to neutral losses, ring patterns, or DBE formulas, but these textual elements are not mechanistically linked to the final molecular predictions. The superficially detailed explanations thus mask a lack of chemical grounding and often contain inconsistencies within the reasoning trace itself or between the CoT and the generated SMILES. CoT-guided reasoning, in this setting, enhances interpretability only at the surface level while offering little improvement in correctness.

Taken together, these findings reveal that the four evaluated off-the-shelf LLMs lack (1) grounding in fragmentation physics, (2) mechanisms to enforce formula or valency constraints, and (3) the ability to integrate local reasoning steps into globally coherent molecular graphs. These limitations persist across model families, sizes, and molecular-weight regimes, and become particularly stark when contrasted with spectra-trained models like DiffMS. Progress in MS-to-structure prediction will therefore require integrating LLMs with domain-grounded components such as spectral encoders, chemically constrained decoding, hybrid symbolic–differentiable reasoning modules, and multimodal pretraining that couples textual reasoning with molecular graphs and spectra. While zero-shot CoT prompting enables these models to produce fluent, expert-like narratives, it does not confer the mechanistic understanding required for reliable mass spectral structure elucidation. Substantial architectural and multimodal advances will be necessary to bridge this gap.

Limitations and conclusion

This work establishes a systematic benchmark for evaluating LLMs on MS-to-SMILES prediction under CoT prompting. While all models reliably follow the structured <think> and <answer> format and often generate syntactically valid SMILES, our results reveal clear limitations in chemical fidelity and spectral grounding.

Taken together, our findings reveal that the limitations of the four evaluated zero-shot LLMs arise from a combination of weak chemical priors, unreliable reasoning, and structural ambiguity inherent to MS/MS data. Because none of the models receive task-specific training, their behavior reflects only the generic chemical knowledge acquired during pretraining, which manifests in uniformly poor chemical metrics: DBE accuracy remains below 4.2%, formula correctness is effectively zero, and exact-match accuracy is zero across all models and molecular-weight bins. Although CoT prompting increases reasoning transparency, it does not improve reasoning accuracy, DBE computations are correct in only 3.9–4.2% of cases, formula statements are almost always wrong, and contradiction rates exceed 72%, indicating that the seeming “expert-like" explanations are largely disconnected from the generated structures. Even when examining molecular-weight effects, where all models perform best on mid-sized molecules (200–400 Da), the improvements are marginal: Tanimoto similarity still hovers around 0.10 and MCES dissimilarity remains high (near 0.15–0.17), with performance degrading sharply for larger molecules due to combinatorial explosion and for very small molecules due to sparse fragment information. Altogether, these patterns show that the evaluated zero-shot LLMs, even when scaffolded with CoT, lack the mechanistic grounding required to perform reliable spectral-structure inversion.

In summary, this work provides a diagnostic evaluation of direct MS/MS-to-SMILES prediction by off-the-shelf general-purpose LLMs under zero-shot CoT prompting. Across the four evaluated models, we find that the generated responses are often fluent and chemically plausible in style, yet remain weakly grounded in the underlying spectral evidence. Repeated-run analysis confirms that output diversity does not rescue correctness: exact-match accuracy remains zero, formula consistency remains near zero, and structural similarity remains low across all five runs. Each query requires one generated CoT trace and one final candidate list; we do not report a detailed latency benchmark because the focus of this work is diagnostic correctness rather than deployment efficiency, and the negative correctness results make runtime comparisons secondary. Overall, our results show that, for the evaluated off-the-shelf models, fluent chain-of-thought reasoning over text-serialized MS/MS evidence does not translate into chemically reliable direct structure prediction. We therefore view this benchmark as both a warning against unassisted deployment of off-the-shelf LLMs for this task and an explicit call to the research community to develop better LLM-based solutions, including task-specific fine-tuning, reinforcement learning with chemistry-grounded reward signals, spectral encoder integration, and constrained decoding, that can close the substantial capability gap documented here.
