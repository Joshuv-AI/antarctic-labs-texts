Finite-Sample Inference for Sparsely Permuted Linear Regression
Hirofumi Ota, Masaaki Imaizumi

Abstract
. We study a linear observation model with an unknown permutation called permuted/shuffled linear regression , where responses and covariates are mismatched and the permutation forms a discrete, factorial-size parameter. The permutation is a key component of the data-generating process, yet its statistical investigation remains challenging due to its discrete nature. We develop a general statistical inference framework on the permutation and regression coefficients. First, we introduce a localization step that reduces the permutation space to a small candidate set building on recent advances in the repro samples method, whose miscoverage decays polynomially with the number of Monte Carlo samples. Then, based on this localized set, we provide statistical inference procedures: a conditional Monte Carlo test of permutation structures with valid finite-sample Type-I error control. We also develop coefficient inference that remains valid under alignment uncertainty of permutations. For computational purposes, we develop a linear assignment problem computable in polynomial time and demonstrate that, with high probability, the solution is equivalent to that of the conventional least squares with large computational cost. Extensions to partially permuted designs and ridge regularization are further discussed. Extensive simulations and an application to air-quality data corroborate finite-sample validity, strong power to detect mismatches, and practical scalability.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

Modern data integration and privacy mechanisms often disrupt the one-to-one correspondence between covariates and responses.
In this context, the
shuffled/permuted linear regression model

(
2
;
24
)
serves as a fundamental regression framework that explicitly accounts for possible mismatches through an unknown permutation acting on the design or response indices.
This model provides a canonical baseline for studying statistical inference under alignment uncertainty and has become a central object in the recent literature on regression with unobserved correspondences.

Such structures arise in settings involving anonymized or aggregated data
(
21
)
, probabilistic record linkage
(
7
;
5
;
34
)
, and privacy-preserving or decentralized data collection protocols
(
31
;
6
)
.
In these situations, the pairings between predictor and response units may be only partially known or implicitly inferred, thereby motivating the study of regression models under alignment uncertainty.
We focus on the regime in which only a small fraction of the sample pairs are mismatched, often referred to as
sparsely permuted
data
(
27
)
.
Even a small number of mismatches can distort parameter estimates and invalidate uncertainty quantification if ignored.

The appearance of sparsely permuted data motivates two core inferential questions:
whether any permutation is present in the data, and how to construct confidence sets for the regression coefficients that remain valid under alignment uncertainty
.
Addressing these problems presents methodological challenges, because the permutation is discrete and combinatorial, and the search space grows factorially with the sample size, which undermines local asymptotics, smooth likelihood expansions, and tractable selective conditioning. We therefore adopt a nonasymptotic, finite-sample approach based on the
repro samples method

(
35
;
36
;
32
)
and the principle of
locally simultaneous inference

(
37
)
. At a high level, we localize the permutation space to a data-dependent candidate set and perform inference uniformly over that set; details are presented in Section
2
-
3
.

1.2.
Problem statement

We consider the linear regression problem with permuted data, where the observed response vector
\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is paired with an
n\times p
deterministic design matrix
\bm{X}
with full column rank
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{X})=p
. The model can be described as

\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}=\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\sigma_{0}\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}},

(1)

where
\bm{\beta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an unknown coefficient vector,
\Pi_{0}
is an unknown
n\times n
permutation matrix, and
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
is a realization from
\bm{U}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{n})
, which is the noise vector associated with the observation
\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}
. The scale parameter
\sigma_{0}>0
is also unknown. Under the frequentist framework, the statistical model in (
1
) can also be described as the following random-sample version of the data-generating process

\bm{Y}=\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\sigma_{0}\bm{U},\qquad\bm{U}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{n}).

(2)

We further assume that the number of mismatches is at most
k
. Such situation is referred to as sparsely permuted data first appeared in
27
, indicating that the permutation affects only a small fraction of all response-predictor pairs.
To quantify the number of mismatches,
we define the Hamming distance between an identity matrix
I_{n}
and a
n\times n
permutation matrix as

d(\Pi,I_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}\{\Pi_{ii}=0\},

(3)

where
\Pi_{ii}
denotes the
i
-th diagonal element of
\Pi
, and focus on the
k
-sparse permutation matrix that satisfies
d(\Pi,I_{n})\leqslant k
. Furthermore, we denote the set of
k
-sparse permutation matrices as

\mathcal{P}_{n,k}=\{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}:d(\Pi,I_{n})\leqslant k\}.

(4)

Based on the canonical setting, this paper addresses two goals of the statistical inference:

(G1)

Testing for mismatches.

Construct a finite-sample valid test of the composite hypothesis
H_{0}:d(\Pi_{0},I_{n})\leqslant k_{0}
(equivalently,
\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}
) versus
H_{1}:d(\Pi_{0},I_{n})>k_{0}
(equivalently,
\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}
). The special case
k_{0}=0
tests whether any mismatch is present.

(G2)

Confidence sets for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
under unknown
\Pi_{0}
.

Build a confidence set for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
that accounts for the uncertainty in estimating
\Pi_{0}
and attains nominal coverage in finite samples.

1.3.
Overview of contributions

We develop a methodology for statistical inference toward the above goals, which consists of two stages: generating candidate sets and conducting inference based on them.
In the first step, we generate a candidate set of plausible permutations based on the repro samples method
(
35
;
36
)
. We generate artificial noise
L
times, and for each noise draw, we optimize an objective function that mimics the least-squares objective under the corresponding noise realization, and then collect the resulting optimizers into a data-dependent candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\subset\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
. This localized domain is then used to conduct coefficient inference by taking union of projection-based
F
-statistics over
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
, which yields locally simultaneous confidence regions for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
.
In the second step, we develop a test for the hypothesis about the permutation structure:
H_{0}:\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}
. We construct a discrete statistic based on
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
and calibrate it via conditional Monte Carlo uniformly over the localized null set
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}:=\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\cap\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}
. These steps achieve the two statistical inference goals (G1) and (G2).

We summarize the contributions of our study.

•

Our developed approach is theoretically guaranteed to be valid. For the generation of the candidate set in the first step with the localization, we prove a polynomial-rate bound on the miscoverage probability
\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{0}\notin\mathcal{C}^{(L)})
in terms of the number of repro samples
L
(Theorem
4.1
). Also, we show the coverage of the confidence regions for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
at the nominal level up to the localization error that vanishes at a polynomial rate in
L
(Theorem
4.4
). For testing, the developed statistical test controls type-I error at any sample size (Theorem
4.3
) localized on the event
\{\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\}
. Marginally, the size inflation is bounded by the miscoverage probability of
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
.

•

We develop a computationally effective scheme to implement the method. In particular, we develop a score-weighted linear assignment problem (LAP) as a surrogate to the original least square problem for generating the candidate set. This surrogate problem is solved by the Hungarian algorithm with the computational complexity
O(n^{3})
, which avoids the combinatorial explosion of the computational complexity of the original problem. We also prove the high-probability equivalence of the surrogate problem to the original problem (Theorem
4.5
).

•

Our methodology is comprehensively validated through both simulation and real-data analysis in an applicati on to Beijing air-quality data. We also apply our approach to the partially permuted designs and the permuted ridge regression model.
Further, we propose selection methods for several necessary hyperparameters, and their proper functioning is verified experimentally.

1.4.
Related works

1.4.1.
Shuffled regression

Several recent methods use Hungarian/LAP solvers as computational surrogates for shuffled regression, e.g., spectral matching via covariance eigenbasis alignment
(
17
)
or LAP subproblems within alternating schemes
(
16
)
.
Relatedly, local search over
k
-sparse permutations performs greedy swaps with monotone loss descent but offers no guarantee of reaching a global optimum in noisy settings
(
20
)
.
In contrast, Theorem
4.5
provides a finite-sample certification: under explicit conditions, our score-weighted LAP attains the same minimizer as the repro-sample objective, and thus agrees with its global minimizer with high probability.

Research related to shuffled regression includes the estimation of shape-constrained models when the correspondence between covariates and responses is unknown.
For one-dimensional covariate,
25
obtain minimax rates of an estimation error for uncoupled isotonic regression via minimum Wasserstein deconvolution.
In multivariate settings, there are more studies.
For coordinate-wise isotonic regression with axis-wise permutations,
22
derive minimax and adaptation results and propose a polynomial-time partition estimator.
29
establish identification via cyclical monotonicity and develop optimal-transport/likelihood-based estimators with certain error bounds. Permutation uncertainty also appears in structured arrays:
19
give information-theoretic limits and near-optimal polynomial-time recovery for monotone matrices, and
14
provide statistically optimal and computationally efficient estimators for smooth tensors.

A complementary line of work develops statistical inference for shuffled linear regression.
28
propose a pseudo-likelihood-based asymptotic inference for the coefficient vector and provide EM-type algorithm for the optimization.
3
study sparsely permuted data from a robust Bayesian perspective, treating mispaired entries as outliers within a generalized fractional posterior, and establish posterior consistency alongside practical sampling strategies.

1.4.2.
Repro samples method

Our work adopts the repro samples method
(
35
;
36
)
, which provides non-asymptotic, simulation-based inference by quantifying uncertainty through artificial samples.
In high-dimensional linear models, repro samples have been used to construct model confidence sets and valid inference for coefficients
(
32
)
, with extensions to high-dimensional logistic models
(
11
)
.
We transfer these ideas from support uncertainty (scaling primarily with
p
) to the qualitatively different regime of permutation uncertainty (scaling factorially with
n
): we build a data-dependent candidate set of permutations
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
that contains the truth with high probability, then conduct inference uniformly over
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
in the spirit of locally simultaneous inference.
This yields (i) a finite-sample valid test for the presence of any mismatch and (ii) union-type confidence sets for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
that account for alignment uncertainty.

2.
Candidate set of permutations via repro samples

We develop the candidate set of permutation matrices, which is the key ingredient of our statistical inference framework.
This section includes (i) a localization approach based on the repro sample method, and (ii) an score-weighted linear assignment problem (LAP) to handle our setup.

2.1.
Localization principle

We present the localization approach to make a candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\subset\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
, which focuses on a small subset containing the permutation
\Pi_{0}
with high probability and avoids the exhaustive search over all permutations in
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
.
This approach leverages the fact that the permutation estimated under artificially generated noise, known as a repro sample, is close to the true permutation identified under the realized noise.

To illustrate the idea, we first consider an ideal situation in which the unobserved realization of noise
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
in (
1
) is fully available. The following Proposition
2.1
claims that we can identify the true permutation
\Pi_{0}
with
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
under
k
-sparse setup.

We make the following baseline assumption on the design matrix
\bm{X}
.

Assumption A

(Design matrix)
.

The rows of
\bm{X}
are generated i.i.d. from a distribution on

\mathbb{R}^{p}
with a Lebesgue density.

Throughout the paper, we treat
\bm{X}
as a fixed design matrix and
conduct inference conditionally on its realized value.
The assumption above is imposed solely to guarantee that, with
probability one, the realized design matrix satisfies the required
full-rank condition.

Proposition 2.1

(Unique recovery in the oracle setting)
.

In addition to Assumption
A
, suppose
n-2k\geqslant p
and
\bm{\beta}_{0}\neq\bm{0}
hold. Then, the true permutation
\Pi_{0}
uniquely satisfies

\Pi_{0}=\argmin_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}\|(I_{n}-M_{(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}})})\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}^{2},

(5)

where
M_{(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}})}
is the orthogonal projector onto the range of
(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}})
.

When
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
is available, Proposition
2.1
states that
\Pi_{0}
is uniquely recovered. Also, we note that the
k
-sparse setup enables this recovery result. The complete proof is deferred to Appendix
E.1
.

Next, we construct a candidate set in the case where
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
is not available.
By utilizing the insight from the oracle result as Proposition
2.1
, we generate artificial samples of the noise that mimic the objective in (
5
). Specifically, we generate
L
i.i.d. vectors
\bm{u}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\bm{u}_{L}^{*}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{n})

and, for each
\ell=1,\ldots,L
, we define the least square problem and its optimizer

\widehat{\Pi}_{\ell}=\argmin_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}\|(I_{n}-M_{(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{u}_{\ell}^{*})})\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

This is an analogue of the oracle problem (
5
) by replacing the realization
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
by its artificial counterpart
\bm{u}^{*}_{\ell}
. We expect
\widehat{\Pi}_{\ell}
is close to
\Pi_{0}
when
\bm{u}_{\ell}^{*}
is sufficiently aligned with
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
.

Finally, we collect
\widehat{\Pi}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\Pi}_{L}
to form the candidate set

\mathcal{C}^{(L)}=\left\{\widehat{\Pi}_{\ell}:\ell=1,\ldots,L\right\}.

(7)

2.2.
Score-weighted linear assignment problem

We develop a methodology to approximately solve the least square objective in (
6
).
Since the objective in (
6
) minimizes a nonlinear projection residual over permutation matrices, the problem remains computationally demanding due to its combinatorial nature even when we restrict the search to
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
(see
23
;
24
).
To obtain a tractable surrogate, we replace (
6
) by a score-weighted linear assignment problem (LAP), which has the form
\min_{\Pi}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\Omega_{ij}\Pi_{ij}
with a cost matrix
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and can be solved exactly in
O(n^{3})
time by the Hungarian algorithm
(
12
)
.

To develop the LAP problem as a surrogate, for a fixed repro sample
\bm{u}^{*}
, we reform the original objective (
6
) into a (scaled) LAP cost plus a projection-mismatch term. Concretely, we expand (
6
) as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|(I_{n}-M_{(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{u}^{*})})\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(8)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-m_{j})^{2}\Pi_{ij}}_{=:T_{1}(\Pi)}+\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i,j}Y_{i}m_{j}\Pi_{ij}-{\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}}^{\top}M_{(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{u}^{*})}\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\right)}_{=:T_{2}(\Pi)}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}^{2}\right)}_{=:T_{3}}.

The first term
T_{1}(\Pi)
is a standard LAP objective with costs
(Y_{i}-m_{j})^{2}
where
m_{j}
denotes the
j
-th element of
M_{(\bm{X},\bm{u}^{*})}\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}
, and the third term
T_{3}
is ignorable since it does not depend on
\Pi
.
About the second term
T_{2}(\Pi)
, since
I_{n}
is one of the minimizers of the term, i.e.,
T_{2}(I_{n})=\min_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}T_{2}(\Pi)
, we can approximate the minimization of
T_{2}(\Pi)
by augmenting a cost which induces an identity to be a minimizer.
In particular, with some coefficients
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
, we consider a LAP with a sparsity-inducing cost
\lambda_{1}\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}\{j\neq i\}-\lambda_{2}\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}\{j=i\}(Y_{i}-m_{i})^{2}
, where the first term penalizes each off-diagonal reassignment (an
\ell_{0}
-type cost on the number of moved indices), and the second term adds a credit to the diagonal residual.

Consequently, we consider an estimator by solving the following score-weighted LAP as a surrogate for the least square problem (
6
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Pi}=\argmin_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Omega_{ij}(\bm{u}^{*})\Pi_{ij},

(9)

where
\Omega(\bm{u}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a cost matrix with
\bm{u}^{*}
defined as

\displaystyle\Omega_{ij}(\bm{u}^{*})=(Y_{i}-m_{j})^{2}+\lambda_{1}\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}\{j\neq i\}-\lambda_{2}\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}\{j=i\}(Y_{i}-m_{i})^{2}.

(10)

Theorem
4.5
formalizes this reduction: for suitable
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, the score-weighted LAP minimizer coincides with the argmin of (
6
) with high probability.
We will further discuss the selection of the parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
in Section
6
.
For the sake of completeness, we present the original Hungarian algorithm in Algorithm
1
in Appendix
C
.

2.3.
Unique recovery of the parameters

We extend the results of the recovery of permutation matrices
\Pi_{0}
in Proposition
2.1
to include coefficients
(\bm{\beta}_{0},\sigma_{0})
. This is crucial for performing parameter inference.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
n-2k\geqslant p
holds, then, given the noise realization
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
, the triple
(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0},\sigma_{0})\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the unique minimizer of

\min_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k},\ \bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\ \sigma\geqslant 0}\left\|\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta}-\sigma\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(11)

We compare the identifiability condition
n-2k\geqslant p
in Proposition
2.2
to that of a previous study without the
k
-sparsity.
In particular, for the recovery problem without any constraints,
(
30
)
derived the identifiability condition
n\geqslant 2p
.
Compared with this condition, our derived condition shows that
k
-sparsity can reduce the required sample size compared to the unrestricted case when
k
is small.
See Theorem
F.1
in Appendix
F
for details.

3.
Finite-sample statistical inference

3.1.
Inference on the permutation matrix

This section construct a statistic for the
k_{0}
-sparsity test with the hypotheses

H_{0}:\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}},

(12)

where
k_{0}\leqslant k
, by introducing a conditional Monte Carlo method for valid finite-sample statistical inference of the permutation matrix
\Pi_{0}
. The special case with
k_{0}=0
reduces to testing whether any mismatch is present (i.e.,
\Pi_{0}=I_{n}
). In the context of shuffled regression, the presence of a permutation is of direct interest
(
28
)
.

3.1.1.
Applying localization

First, we localize the null domain using the candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
constructed in Section
2
.
The key property established there is that
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
contains the true permutation
\Pi_{0}
with high probability (see Theorem
4.1
).
Localization restricts attention to the data-dependent but typically small set
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}:=\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\cap\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}
, on which we calibrate the rejection threshold uniformly over
\Pi\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}
.
This approach reduces the computational burden of the conditional Monte Carlo calibration under

H_{0}:\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}
, which requires controlling null distribution whose size is combinatorial in the sample size
n
.

More formally, we compute a least squares estimator over the candidate set,

\widehat{\Pi}(\bm{Y})\in\argmin_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}^{(L)}}\left\|(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{Y}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(13)

and use the test statistic

D(\bm{Y}):=d\left(\widehat{\Pi}(\bm{Y}),I_{n}\right),\qquad D^{\mathrm{obs}}:=D(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}).

(14)

Intuitively, under
H_{0}
, the best-fitting permutation in
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
should typically have at most
k_{0}
mismatches, whereas under
H_{1}
, it must typically use more than
k_{0}
mismatches to explain the data.

3.1.2.
Handling nuisance

Next, we remove an effect of the nuisance parameter
(\bm{\beta},\sigma)
to enable inference focused on
\Pi
, which is our primary interest.
To this aim, we consider a statistical model
\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}=\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta}+\sigma\bm{U}
with
\bm{\theta}=(\Pi,\bm{\beta},\sigma)\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\bm{U}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{n})
, then study a conditional distribution of
\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}
, which is necessary for the conditional Monte Carlo approximation.

We derive the conditional distribution of
\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}
using a formula of sufficient statistics.
By the reformulation
\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{M}_{\Pi\bm{X}}\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}+(I_{n}-\bm{M}_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{M}_{\Pi\bm{X}}\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}+\sigma(I_{n}-\bm{M}_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{U}
with a fixed
\Pi
,
we define a pair of sufficient statistics for
(\bm{\beta},\sigma)
as

S_{1,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}):=M_{\Pi\bm{X}}\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}},\qquad S_{2,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}):=\left\|(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}\right\|_{2}.

(15)

Then,
we have the representation of the conditional distribution as

\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}\mid\left(S_{1,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}),S_{2,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}})\right)\overset{d}{=}\ S_{1,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}})+S_{2,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}})\frac{(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{U}}{\|(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{U}\|_{2}}.

(16)

For a fixed
\Pi
, the pair
(S_{1,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}),S_{2,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}))
is sufficient for
(\bm{\beta},\sigma)
, and the direction
\frac{(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{U}}{\|(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{U}\|_{2}}
is ancillary, being independent of
(\bm{\beta},\sigma)
. Consequently, the conditional distribution of
\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}\mid(S_{1,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}),S_{2,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}))
is free of
(\bm{\beta},\sigma)
. This yields the conditional Monte Carlo approximation of the distribution of
D(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}})
in (
14
).

3.1.3.
Critical value

We derive a critical value localized on
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}
by the conditional Monte Carlo approximation.
Given
\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}
, we set
s_{1,\Pi}^{\mathrm{obs}}=S_{1,\Pi}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})
and
s_{2,\Pi}^{\mathrm{obs}}=S_{2,\Pi}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})
.
Let
\widehat{c}_{1-\alpha}(\Pi)
denote
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the distribution of
D(\bm{Y})\mid(S_{1,\Pi}(\bm{Y}),S_{2,\Pi}(\bm{Y}))=(s_{1,\Pi}^{\mathrm{obs}},s_{2,\Pi}^{\mathrm{obs}})
, and define the composite critical value localized on
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}
:

\widehat{c}_{1-\alpha}^{(k_{0})}:=\max_{\Pi\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}}\widehat{c}_{1-\alpha}(\Pi),\mbox{~~and~~}\widehat{B}^{(k_{0})}_{1-\alpha}:=\{0,1,\ldots,\widehat{c}_{1-\alpha}^{(k_{0})}\}.

(17)

We reject
H_{0}
when
D^{\mathrm{obs}}\notin\widehat{B}^{(k_{0})}_{1-\alpha}
holds, i.e., when
D^{\mathrm{obs}}>\widehat{c}_{1-\alpha}^{(k_{0})}
holds. To approximate the critical value, we generate i.i.d.
\bm{u}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\bm{u}_{M}^{*}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{n})
and define

\bm{Y}_{m,\Pi}^{*}:=s_{1,\Pi}^{\mathrm{obs}}+s_{2,\Pi}^{\mathrm{obs}}\frac{(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{u}_{m}^{*}}{\|(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{u}_{m}^{*}\|_{2}},\qquad m=1,\ldots,M.

For each
m=1,\dots,M
, we compute a test statistic
D_{m,\Pi}^{*}:=D(\bm{Y}_{m,\Pi}^{*})
as (
14
) and use the
(1-\alpha)
-empirical quantile of the conditional Monte Carlo samples
\{D_{m,\Pi}^{*}\}_{m=1}^{M}
.

Theorem
4.3
in Section
4
shows that, the resulting test controls the type-I error at level
\alpha
in finite samples conditionally on
s_{1,\Pi}^{\mathrm{obs}}
and
s_{2,\Pi}^{\mathrm{obs}}
. We also obtain the marginal type-I error control, where the only additional error comes from the miscoverage probability of the candidate set.

Remark 3.1
.

An alternative test statistic is possible. For example, the statistic
|\min_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}\|(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}-\|(I_{n}-M_{\bm{X}})\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}|

is continuous and supported on
[0,\infty)
, which avoids computing the minimizer explicitly but complicates conditional Monte Carlo approximation.

3.2.
Inference on the coefficient vector

We now construct a finite-sample valid confidence set for the coefficient vector
\bm{\beta}_{0}
, accounting for uncertainty from estimating the permutation. Since the parameter of interest is
\bm{\beta}
, we treat
\Pi
and
\sigma
as nuisance. We first consider a pivotal map that depends only on
(\Pi,\bm{\beta})
, and then quantify uncertainty about
\Pi
via the candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
. The resulting region is the union of coefficient confidence sets obtained by fixing each
\Pi\in\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
.

We define a statistics for the purpose. With given
(\Pi,\bm{\beta})
, we define it as

\displaystyle T(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}},(\Pi,\bm{\beta}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{M}_{\Pi\bm{X}}\bm{U}/p}{\bm{U}^{\top}(I_{n}-\bm{M}_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{U}/(n-p)}=\frac{(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta})^{\top}\bm{M}_{\Pi\bm{X}}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta})/p}{(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta})^{\top}(I_{n}-\bm{M}_{\Pi\bm{X}})(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta})/(n-p)}.

Under the case
(\Pi,\bm{\beta})=(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0})
with the true parameters, this statistic has an
F_{p,n-p}
distribution.

Then, we define the level-
\alpha
confidence set as

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{coef}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})=\bigcup_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}^{(L)}}\left\{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi,\bm{\beta}))\leqslant F_{p,n-p}^{-1}(\alpha)\right\},

(18)

where
F_{p,n-p}^{-1}
is the quantile function of the
F_{p,n-p}
distribution. We show in Theorem
4.4
how the coverage probability of
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{coef}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})
is controlled by the nominal level
\alpha
and by the miscoverage probability
\delta
of the candidate set.

4.
Theory

4.1.
High-probability recovery of
k
-sparse permutation matrices

We study the candidate set (
7
) and show that it contains the true permutation
\Pi_{0}
with high-probability.
Let
\mathbb{P}_{\left(\mathcal{U}^{L},\bm{U}\right)}
denote probability with respect to the joint law of
(\mathcal{U}^{L},\bm{U})
, where
\mathcal{U}^{L}=(\bm{U}_{1},\dots,\bm{U}_{L})
.

To state the main results, we define the numerical constant

C_{\min}(\Pi_{0})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}\\
\Pi\neq\Pi_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\|(I_{n}-\Pi M_{\bm{X}}\Pi^{\top})\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{n\max(d(\Pi_{0},I_{n})-d(\Pi,I_{n}),1)},

(19)

where
M_{\bm{X}}=\bm{X}(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}
. The constant
C_{\min}(\Pi_{0})
is the degree of separation between the true structure
\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}
and its projections onto shuffled designs.

We give a theoretical analysis on the miscoverage probability of the candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
.

Theorem 4.1

(Coverage probability of the candidate set)
.

Suppose
C_{\min}(\Pi_{0})>0
and
n>p+1
hold. Then, there exists a positive real sequence
(\delta_{L})_{L\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying

\mathbb{P}_{\left(\mathcal{U}^{L},\bm{U}\right)}\left(\Pi_{0}\notin\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\right)\leqslant\delta_{L},

(20)

for all
L\geqslant 2
, such that we have the following as
L\to\infty
:

\delta_{L}=O\left(\frac{\left(\log L\right)^{(n-p)/2}}{L^{(n-p-1)/(2(n-1))}}\right).

(21)

Theorem
4.1
theoretically validates the localization principle that underlies our framework.
The candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
acts as an empirical analogue of a confidence region for
\Pi_{0}
: as
L
grows, the probability that
\Pi_{0}
is excluded from
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
decays polynomially.
Thus, Theorem
4.1
shows that
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
provides a localized representation of the combinatorial parameter space of
\Pi
, turning an intractable search into a manageable and possibly small random subset that contains the truth with high probability.

Theorem
4.1
is the permutation analogue of the model candidate set results
(
32
;
11
)
by the repro-sample approach: whereas those works treat support uncertainty (scaling with
p
), Theorem
4.1
addresses permutation uncertainty (scaling with
n
), providing the first finite-sample localization guarantee on the discrete setup.

Remark 4.2

(Comparison with selective inference)
.

Theorem
4.1
offers a principled alternative to classical selective inference, where exact conditioning on the selection event is analytically and computationally infeasible for permutations due to their nonpolyhedral, combinatorial nature.
Our candidate-set approach replaces exact conditioning with a localization step: it identifies a small data-dependent subset that contains
\Pi_{0}
with high probability and then performs inference uniformly over this subset.

4.2.
A finite-sample valid test for the permutation matrix

We now formalize the validity of the
k_{0}
-sparsity test introduced in Section
3.1
.
Fix
k_{0}\leqslant k
and define the statistic
D(\bm{Y})
in (
14
) and the localized null set
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}
.

Then, we validate the critical value
\widehat{c}_{1-\alpha}^{(k_{0})}
and the confidence set
\widehat{B}_{1-\alpha}^{(k_{0})}
in (
17
).

Theorem 4.3

(Type-I error control for the sparsity test)
.

Suppose
C_{\min}(\Pi_{0})>0
and
n>p+1
hold.
Then, for the true parameter
\theta_{0}=(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0},\sigma_{0})
with
\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{0}}
, we have the finite-sample bound

\mathbb{P}_{\left(\mathcal{U}^{L},\bm{U}\right)}\left(D(\bm{Y}_{\theta_{0}})\notin\widehat{B}^{(k_{0})}_{1-\alpha}\right)\leqslant\alpha+\delta_{L},

(22)

where
\delta_{L}
is specified in Theorem
4.1
.

There are two terms in the right-hand side of (
22
).
The second term
\delta_{L}
is the error from the localization, which is revealed in Theorem
4.1
.
The first term
\alpha
is the type-I error without the localization.
To derive the term, we define the composite critical value with fixed
\Pi
:

c_{1-\alpha}^{(k_{0})}:=\max_{\Pi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}}c_{1-\alpha}(\Pi),\mbox{~~and~~}B^{(k_{0})}_{1-\alpha}:=\{0,1,\ldots,c_{1-\alpha}^{(k_{0})}\},

(23)

where
c_{1-\alpha}(\Pi)
denotes the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the conditional distribution of
D(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}})
given
(S_{1,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}),S_{2,\Pi}(\bm{Y}_{\bm{\theta}}))
, then we show
\mathbb{P}(D(\bm{Y}_{\theta_{0}})\notin B^{(k_{0})}_{1-\alpha}\mid S_{1,\Pi_{0}}(\bm{Y}_{\theta_{0}}),\ S_{2,\Pi_{0}}(\bm{Y}_{\theta_{0}}))\leqslant\alpha
(See Proposition
E.1
).
The key ingredients are the conditional representation (
16
) and the fact that
c_{1-\alpha}^{(k_{0})}
is chosen uniformly over the localized null
\widehat{\Pi}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}

In practice, we approximate the conditional quantiles by conditional Monte Carlo as described in Section
3.1
.

4.3.
A finite-sample valid confidence set for the coefficient vector

We next state the finite-sample coverage guarantee for the confidence region
constructed from the candidate permutation set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
.

Theorem 4.4

(Coverage probability of the confidence set)
.

Suppose
C_{\min}(\Pi_{0})>0
and
n>p+1
hold. Then, it holds that

\mathbb{P}_{\left(\mathcal{U}^{L},\bm{U}\right)}\left(\bm{\beta}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{coef}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})\right)\geqslant\alpha-\delta_{L},

(24)

where
\delta_{L}
is specified in Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem
4.4
establishes that the coverage of
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{coef}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})
for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
up to two small, vanishing errors: one from the localization for the candidate set and another from Monte Carlo approximation.
As
L
grows, the coverage converges to the nominal level
\alpha
.

Theorem
4.4
represents a
locally simultaneous
form of selective inference, which is conducted uniformly over a localized, data-dependent neighborhood of plausible permutations
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n,k_{0}}
that contains the true alignment with high probability (as shown in Theorem
4.1
), instead of conditioning on a single realized permutation. Within this neighborhood, classical reference laws such as the
F
distribution remain valid, providing finite-sample coverage without the need to describe the full combinatorial selection event. At the same time, it highlights a notion of robustness to alignment uncertainty. In particular, if the true data-generating alignment is not uniquely identifiable but lies within this candidate set (as in record linkage, partial mismatching, or anonymization), then inference on
\beta_{0}
still remains valid.

4.4.
Algorithmic guarantees

In this section, we show that the estimator defined as the solution to the LAP (
9
) proposed in Section
2.2

coincides with the solution to the original least square problem (
6
) with high probability.
To clarify the dependence of the solutions on
\bm{U}
and
\bm{U}^{*}
, we denote the solution to the LAP (
9
) as
\widetilde{\Pi}(\bm{U},\bm{U}^{*})
, and the solution to the original problem (
6
) as
\widehat{\Pi}(\bm{U},\bm{U}^{*})
.

Theorem 4.5

(High-probability equivalence)
.

For
\xi\in(0,1)
, let
B_{Y}(\xi),B_{\rm diag}(\xi),\eta_{\rm op}(\xi),\underline{\Delta}_{\xi}
be the positive constants defined later in Section
E.5
.
Choose
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geqslant 0
such that

\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}B_{\rm diag}(\xi)+\eta_{\rm op}(\xi)<\frac{n\underline{\Delta}_{\xi}}{2k}

(25)

with the condition
\lambda_{1}\geqslant B_{Y}(\xi)/k
.
Then, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U},\bm{U}^{*}}\left(\widehat{\Pi}(\bm{U},\bm{U}^{*})=\widetilde{\Pi}(\bm{U},\bm{U}^{*})\right)\geqslant 1-4\xi.

(26)

About the constraint (
25
),
B_{\rm diag}(\xi)
is the upper bound of the largest diagonal residual and
\eta_{\rm op}(\xi)

controls the projection-mismatch error (see (
61
) and (
62
)),
while
\underline{\Delta}_{\xi}
is an explicit lower bound on the gap of the objective function
(See Proposition
E.4
).

Theorem
4.5
establishes a nonasymptotic argmin equivalence between a single linear assignment problem and the least-squares objective. Specifically, under an explicit finite-sample margin condition (quantified by
\underline{\Delta}_{\xi}
) and a transparent tuning window (
25
), the solution of the score-weighted LAP is identical to the global minimizer of the least square problem with probability at least
1-4\xi
.
The result provides an exact, rather than approximate, optimization correspondence under explicitly verifiable finite-sample conditions.
To our knowledge, this is the first formal proof of finite-sample equivalence between an
O(n^{3})
-time assignment solver (Hungarian algorithm) and the global minimizer of a nonlinear least-squares problem defined over the combinatorially large permutation space.

5.
Extensions

5.1.
Partially permuted designs

In some applications the covariates can be decomposed as
(\bm{X},\bm{Z})
, where only
\bm{X}
is
misaligned with the response. We consider the partially permuted
Gaussian linear regression model

\bm{Y}=\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1,0}+\bm{Z}\bm{\beta}_{2,0}+\sigma_{0}\bm{U},\qquad\bm{U}\sim N(0,I_{n}),

(27)

where
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{1}}
and
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{2}}
are fixed.

For any
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
, define the expanded design matrix

W(\Pi):=\big[\Pi\bm{X},\ \bm{Z}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p_{1}+p_{2})},\qquad M_{W(\Pi)}:=W(\Pi)\left(W(\Pi)^{\top}W(\Pi)\right)^{-1}W(\Pi)^{\top}.

(28)

Then the objective (
6
) becomes

F_{\rm partial}(\Pi):=\left\|(I_{n}-M_{W(\Pi)})\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(29)

Permutation candidates are generated exactly as in Section
2.1

by augmenting
W(\Pi)
with the random direction
\bm{u}_{\ell}^{*}
:

\widehat{\Pi}_{\ell}^{\rm partial}\in\arg\min_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}\left\|(I_{n}-M_{(W(\Pi),\bm{u}_{\ell}^{*})})\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\right\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\ell=1,\dots,L,

and we define the candidate set

\mathcal{C}_{\rm partial}^{(L)}:=\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}:\ \exists\ell\in[L]\ \text{s.t.}\ \widehat{\Pi}_{\ell}^{\rm partial}=\Pi\right\}.

(30)

Theorem
F.3
shows that, under a
separation condition
C_{\min}^{\rm partial}(\Pi_{0})>0
, the miscoverage probability

\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{0}\notin\mathcal{C}_{\rm partial}^{(L)})
is bounded by

\delta_{L}^{\rm partial}=O\left(\frac{(\log L)^{(n-(p_{1}+p_{2}))/2}}{L^{((n-(p_{1}+p_{2}))-1)/(2(n-1))}}\right).

Given
\Pi\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm partial}^{(L)}
, a joint confidence set for
(\bm{\beta}_{1},\bm{\beta}_{2})

can be built using the standard
F_{p_{1}+p_{2},n-(p_{1}+p_{2})}
-distribution based on
W(\Pi)
:

T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi,\bm{\beta}_{1},\bm{\beta}_{2})):=\frac{n-(p_{1}+p_{2})}{p_{1}+p_{2}}\frac{\left(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-W(\Pi)\binom{\bm{\beta}_{1}}{\bm{\beta}_{2}}\right)^{\top}M_{W(\Pi)}\left(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-W(\Pi)\binom{\bm{\beta}_{1}}{\bm{\beta}_{2}}\right)}{\left(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-W(\Pi)\binom{\bm{\beta}_{1}}{\bm{\beta}_{2}}\right)^{\top}(I_{n}-M_{W(\Pi)})\left(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-W(\Pi)\binom{\bm{\beta}_{1}}{\bm{\beta}_{2}}\right)},

(31)

and resulting
\alpha
-level confidence set is given by

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{joint}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}):=\bigcup_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{partial}}^{(L)}}\left\{(\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}+p_{2}}:\ T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi,\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}))\leqslant F_{p_{1}+p_{2},n-(p_{1}+p_{2})}^{-1}(\alpha)\right\}.

(32)

If the main interest is
\bm{\beta}_{1}
with
\bm{\beta}_{2}
treated as a nuisance parameter, we use the
partialled–out
F
-distribution. Let
M_{\bm{Z}}
be the orthogonal projector onto
\range(\bm{Z})
and
set
P_{\bm{Z}}:=I_{n}-M_{\bm{Z}}
. For any
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
,
define

T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi,\bm{\beta}_{1})\mid\bm{Z}):=\frac{n-(p_{1}+p_{2})}{p_{1}}\frac{(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1})^{\top}(M_{W(\Pi)}-M_{\bm{Z}})(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1})}{(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1})^{\top}(I_{n}-M_{W(\Pi)})(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1})}.

(33)

The resulting
\alpha
-level confidence set is given by

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\rm partial}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}):=\bigcup_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm partial}^{(L)}}\left\{\bm{\beta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}:\ T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi,\bm{\beta}_{1})\mid\bm{Z})\leqslant F_{p_{1},n-(p_{1}+p_{2})}^{-1}(\alpha)\right\},

and its coverage guarantee is shown in Theorem
F.6
.

5.2.
Permuted ridge regression

Our methodology can also be extended to a permuted regression model with a ridge penalty.
Consider the data generating process analogous to (
2
),

\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}=\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\sigma_{0}\bm{U},\qquad\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}.

For a fixed
\lambda>0
, define the ridge objective

F_{\lambda}(\Pi):=\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\|\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-\Pi\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\right\},\qquad\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}.

It is convenient to rewrite the ridge objective in an augmented least-squares form
(e.g.,
10
). Define

\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{aug}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\\
\bm{0}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+p},\qquad\bm{X}^{\mathrm{aug}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}I_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p)\times p},\qquad\widetilde{\Pi}:=\begin{pmatrix}\Pi&0\\
0&I_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{P}_{n+p}.

Then
F_{\lambda}(\Pi)=\min_{\bm{\beta}}\|\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{aug}}-\widetilde{\Pi}\bm{X}^{\mathrm{aug}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Let
\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}
denote the block-diagonal embedding of
\Pi_{0}
, namely

\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}:=\begin{pmatrix}\Pi_{0}&0\\
0&I_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{P}_{n+p},

and define the augmented noise
\widetilde{\bm{U}}:=(\bm{U},\bm{0}_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
.
Under the model we have
\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{aug}}=\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}\bm{X}^{\mathrm{aug}}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\sigma_{0}\widetilde{\bm{U}}
.
For
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
write
W_{\lambda}(\Pi):=\widetilde{\Pi}\bm{X}^{\mathrm{aug}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p)\times p}
.

For i.i.d.
\bm{U}^{*}_{1},\dots,\bm{U}^{*}_{L}\sim N(0,I_{n})
let

\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}_{\ell}:=(\bm{U}^{*}_{\ell},\bm{0}_{p})
and define

\widehat{\Pi}_{\lambda,\ell}\in\arg\min_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}\left\|(I_{n+p}-M_{(W_{\lambda}(\Pi),\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}_{\ell})})\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{aug}}\right\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}^{(L)}:=\{\widehat{\Pi}_{\lambda,1},\dots,\widehat{\Pi}_{\lambda,L}\}.

(34)

Remark 5.1
.

In the augmented representation, the last
p
pseudo-observations are noiseless, so the projected augmented noise is no longer exactly spherically symmetric when
\lambda
is very small. To keep the theory parallel to the linear regression case, we impose the mild non-degeneracy condition

c_{\lambda}:=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\|\bm{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}\geqslant\underline{c}\in(0,1),

(35)

under which the argument used in Lemma
D.4
carries over up to constants (see Lemma
F.8
in Appendix
F.3
).

6.
Selection of tuning parameters

We propose a selection method of the two regularization parameters
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
for the score-weighted LAP in Section
2.2
.

Specifically, we select the parameters as satisfying the constraint (
25
) in Theorem
4.5
.
Throughout this section, we fix
\xi=0.05
.

The constraint (
25
) is stated in terms of the explicit lower bound

n\underline{\Delta}_{\xi}
.
At the proof level, the comparison is with the (random) separation gap

\Delta_{F}
, which will be defined in (
66
), and Proposition
E.4
shows that

\Delta_{F}\geqslant n\underline{\Delta}_{\xi}
with probability at least
1-\xi
.
Motivated by this connection, our implementation chooses
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
via simple
plug-in estimates of
B_{\rm diag}(\xi)
,
\eta_{\rm op}(\xi)
, and
\Delta_{F}
.

(1)

Compute the empirical analogs
\widehat{\bm{m}}=M_{(\bm{X},\bm{u}^{*})}\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}
and set
\widehat{B}_{\rm diag}:=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\left(Y_{i}^{\mathrm{obs}}-\widehat{m}_{i}\right)^{2}
.

(2)

Evaluate the closed-form expression of
\eta_{\rm op}(\xi)
in (
62
) by
replacing the unknown norms by the observable quantity
\|\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}^{2}
,
yielding
\widehat{\eta}_{\rm op}
.

(3)

Estimate the separation gap
\Delta_{F}
in (
66
) as follows.
First, compute a baseline
k
-sparse permutation
\Pi_{\rm base}
as a solution of the score-weighted LAP with
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=(0,0)
. Next, generate a small collection of
neighbouring permutations by applying random swaps to two rows of
\Pi_{\rm base}
, and compute

F(\Pi)-F(\Pi_{\rm base})
for these candidates. We set
\widehat{\Delta}_{F}
to be the minimum of
the positive differences.

(4)

Define an available budget
b:=0.9(\frac{\widehat{\Delta}_{F}}{2k}-\widehat{\eta}_{\rm op})_{+}
,
where the constant
0.9
is a fixed safety factor to hedge against estimation error.
Finally, split the budget equally between
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
as
\lambda_{1}=\frac{b}{2}
and
\lambda_{2}=\frac{b}{2\max\{\widehat{B}_{\rm diag},10^{-12}\}}
.

Through our experiments in Section
7
, we adopt these tuning parameters as a default rule.

7.
Numerical results

7.1.
Simulation analysis

In this section, we assess the finite-sample performance of the proposed method.
We focus on three aspects:
(i) the size and coverage probability of the candidate set (Section
2.1
);
(ii) the size and power of the conditional test for the existence of a permutation (Section
3.1
); and
(iii) the coverage probability and the volume of the confidence set for the coefficient vector (Section
3.2
).

All experiments are based on the shuffled linear regression model

\bm{Y}=\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\sigma_{0}\bm{U},\qquad\bm{U}\sim\mathrm{N}(\bm{0},I_{n}),

where
\bm{\beta}_{0}=(0.5,-1,2)^{\top}
and the design matrix
\bm{X}\sim\mathrm{N}(\bm{0},I_{p})
with

.
For each configuration,
\Pi_{0}
and
\bm{X}
are fixed across Monte Carlo replications.

We vary the sample size
n\in\{100,200,400\}
, the number of mismatches

,
the noise level
\sigma_{0}\in\{0.01,0.05,0.10,0.20,0.30,0.40,0.50\}
,
and the number of repro samples

.
Each setting is replicated
200
times.
The repro step employs the score-weighted LAP (Algorithm
1
)
with tuning parameters
(\xi,\rho,\alpha_{\mathrm{diag}})=(0.05,0.9,0.5)
specified in Section
6
.

7.1.1.
Performance of the candidate set

We assess the empirical performance of the candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
.
For each configuration, we compute the average size of
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
(the number of unique permutations) across Monte Carlo replications, the empirical probability that the true permutation
\Pi_{0}
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}

Conclusion

This paper develops a finite-sample inference framework for linear regression with sparsely permuted data. Building upon the repro sample method and a localization step, the method converts an intractable search over
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
into inference that is uniform over a small, data-dependent candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
.

Our theory shows: (i) the miscoverage probability of the candidate set
\mathbb{P}\{\Pi_{0}\notin\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\}
decays polynomially in
L
as the number of repro samples grows (Theorem
4.1
); (ii) a conditional Monte Carlo test for the
k_{0}
-sparsity with finite-sample type-I error control (Theorem
4.3
); (iii) confidence regions for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
obtained by taking union over the candidate set
\mathcal{C}^{(L)}
with finite-sample coverage (Theorem
4.4
); and (iv) a computational guarantee that the proposed score-weighted LAP attains the same global minimizer as the least square problem with high probability (Theorem
4.5
) with suitable tuning parameters. Extensions to partially permuted designs and ridge regularization are also discussed.

Empirically, a few hundred repro samples deliver near-nominal behavior: the test controls the type-I error, the union region is robust to mild misalignment, and computation scales as
O(n^{3})
with tuning that ensures the argmin equivalence.

Future research includes two main directions. First, extending the calibration beyond Gaussian errors to sub-Gaussian or heavy-tailed settings would enhance robustness. Second, exploring high-dimensional extensions with structured regularization and multi-response
26
or block-permutation designs
1
would broaden applicability. Overall, the results demonstrate that locally simultaneous, repro samples-based inference offers a general and computationally feasible foundation for exact finite-sample validity in irregular, combinatorial statistical models including permutations.

A.1.
Repro samples method

In this subsection, we give a brief review on the repro samples framework to obtain a performance-guaranteed confidence set on general parameters. The
repro samples method

35
;
36
is a simulation-inspired general inference method, which is particularly effective for making inference on irregular inference problems
33
. The general setup is as follows: suppose the sample data
\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{Y}
are generated from a model with the true parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\Theta
. We also assume that we know how to simulate
\bm{Y}
from the statistical model indexed by
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
and it has the form of generative algorithmic model:

\bm{Y}=G(\bm{\theta},\bm{U}),

(36)

where
\bm{U}=(U_{1},\cdots,U_{r})\in\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
is a random vector whose distribution is free of
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
and
G:\Theta\times\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}
is a known mapping. Let

\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}=G(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}})

(37)

be the realized version of
\bm{Y}
and also
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{rel}}
be the unobserved realization of
\bm{U}
.

The general framework of repro samples method to construct a performance-guaranteed confidence set is as follows. We generate
\bm{u}^{*}\sim\bm{U}
given
\bm{\theta}^{*}
based on the generative algorithmic model (
36
), and call
\bm{Y}^{*}=G(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{u}^{*})
as the repro samples. The key idea to construct a valid confidence set is to quantify the uncertainty of
\bm{U}
through
\bm{u}^{*}
. Let
B_{\alpha}\subset\mathcal{U}
be a level-
\alpha
Borel set for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, such that
\mathbb{P}(\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha})\geqslant\alpha
. Then, we define

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})=\left\{\bm{\theta}:\exists\bm{u}^{*}\in B_{\alpha}\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}=G(\bm{\theta},\bm{u}^{*})\right\}.

It is shown that the
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})
is a level-
\alpha
confidence set for
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y}))\geqslant\alpha,\ \hbox{for }\alpha\in(0,1).

In the simplest case above, we only need to quantify the uncertainty of
\bm{U}
via a level-
\alpha
Borel set
B_{\alpha}
. More generally, we can consider the case where a nuclear mapping
T:\mathcal{U}\times\Theta\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
is available. Let
B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
be a level-
\alpha
Borel set such that
\mathbb{P}(T(\bm{U},\bm{\theta})\in B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\geqslant\alpha
. Then, the following

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})=\left\{\bm{\theta}:\exists\bm{u}^{*}\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}=G(\bm{\theta},\bm{u}^{*}),T(\bm{u}^{*},\bm{\theta})\in B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})\right\}

(38)

is also a valid level-
\alpha
confidence set, i.e., it holds that,

\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{Y}))\geqslant\mathbb{P}(T(\bm{U},\bm{\theta})\in B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\geqslant\alpha,\ \hbox{for }\alpha\in(0,1).

(39)

Appendix B
Connection to selective/post-selection inference

The classical selective inference provides valid post–model‐selection inference by
conditioning on the event that a data–driven selection procedure chooses a particular model. For example,
8
;
15
;
18
proceed statistical inference by conditioning on the realised event and deriving the exact conditional distribution of the test statistic under this restriction. The
conditional distribution is characterized as a truncated multivariate normal whose
support is a polyhedron, via the Gaussian polyhedral lemma
15
.
While the resulting procedures control coverage and type-I error conditional on the selected event, three practical difficulties are common: (i) the selection event is described by high-dimensional linear
inequalities, so explicit formulas are rare and simulation is costly; (ii) conditional guarantees do not automatically imply unconditional
guarantees so that aggressive selection can produce undercoverage when the conditioning is removed; (iii) conditioning discards information that would otherwise contribute to
statistical power, especially in small samples.
Another notable methodology for the selective inference is locally simultaneous inference
37
,
which addresses these issues by replacing conditioning with simultaneous control over a data-dependent neighbourhood. Compared to the standard solution such as the Bonferroni-type method taking the unions across all candidate parameters, the locally simultaneous inference provides a valid uncertainty quantification over the data-depoendent localized parameters and therefore is less conservative.

While the conditional inference fixes the selected model and conditions on that event, the locally simultaneous inference keeps the selection random and enforces validity uniformly over the random set. Furthermore, the conditional methods theoretically and practically require a precise characterization of the conditional law which is often achieved by the Gaussian polyhedral lemma, while locally simultaneous methods use standard parametric (or possibly nonparametric) reference distributions and avoid any geometric description of the selection event.

From these perspectives, we adopt the locally simultaneous inference for shuffled/permuted linear regression models. Our approach first provides the valid localization scheme based on the repro samples method: Theorem
4.1
establishes the true permutation is included in the candidate set of permutations with probability. Within this candidate set, we test the null hypothesis for the underlying permutation structure and construct a union confidence set for the regression coefficient. Our results demonstrate that locally simultaneous inference extends naturally to problems with discrete and combinatorial parameters.
The methodology is conceptually simple, requires only classical distribution
theory, and exhibits strong finite-sample performance, thereby providing a
practical alternative to conditional selective inference.

Appendix C
Details of Hungarian Algorithm

Given a cost matrix
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the linear assignment problem (LAP) is given by

\widehat{\Pi}\ \in\ \argmin_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\langle\Omega,\Pi\rangle=\argmin_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Omega_{ij}\Pi_{ij}.

(40)

In Algorithm
1
, we apply this LAP with the score-weighted cost matrix

\Omega=\Omega(\bm{u}^{*})
.

A convenient viewpoint is to consider the linear-programming relaxation

\min_{\Pi\geqslant 0}\left\{\langle\Omega,\Pi\rangle:\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Pi_{ij}=1\ \forall i,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{ij}=1\ \forall j\right\},

whose extreme points are exactly the permutation matrices. Hence the relaxation attains an optimizer in

\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Also, the dual problem is given by

\max_{\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}:a_{i}+b_{j}\leqslant\Omega_{ij}\ \forall i,j\right\}.

The Hungarian algorithm
12
solves (
40
) by maintaining
(i) a dual-feasible pair
(\bm{a},\bm{b})
with
a_{i}+b_{j}\leqslant\Omega_{ij}
for all
i,j
and
(ii) a partial matching
\mathcal{M}\subseteq[n]\times[n]
using only the
tight
(equality) edges

E(\bm{a},\bm{b}):=\left\{(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]:a_{i}+b_{j}=\Omega_{ij}\right\}.

For any set of rows
S\subseteq[n]
, define its tight-neighborhood

N(S)\ :=\ \left\{j\in[n]:\ \exists i\in S\ \text{such that}\ (i,j)\in E(\bm{a},\bm{b})\right\}.

At each iteration the algorithm either augments the matching along tight edges
or updates the dual variables to create at least one new tight edge, while preserving dual feasibility.
When a perfect matching on
E(\bm{a},\bm{b})
is obtained, complementary slackness implies primal optimality.

\|\bm{U}\|_{2}^{2}\sim\chi_{n}^{2}
and

depends only on directions,
conditioning on

Applying the

Lemma D.4
.

Fix
\gamma_{1}\in(0,1)
. Then, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}\phi\left((I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\bm{U}^{*},(I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}})\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\geqslant\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{2}{\pi}|\mathcal{P}_{n,k}|\left(\arccos(\gamma_{1})\right)^{n-p-1}.

Proof.

Fix
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
and set
P_{\Pi}:=I_{n}-M_{\Pi\bm{X}}
.
Let
r:=\mathrm{rank}(P_{\Pi})=n-p
. Define

Z:=P_{\Pi}\bm{U}^{*},\qquad w:=P_{\Pi}\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}\in\range(P_{\Pi}).

If
w=\bm{0}
, then by convention
\phi(Z,w)=0
(and, in any case, the event

\{\phi(Z,w)\geqslant\gamma_{1}^{2}\}
is empty for
\gamma_{1}>0
), so the desired bound holds trivially.
Hence, assume that
w\neq\bm{0}
and set
e:=w/\|w\|_{2}
, which is a unit vector in
\range(P_{\Pi})
.

Since
P_{\Pi}
is an orthogonal projector of rank
r
, there exists an orthogonal matrix

Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

Q^{\top}P_{\Pi}Q=\begin{pmatrix}I_{r}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Write
\bm{U}^{*}=Q\begin{pmatrix}G\\
H\end{pmatrix}
, where
(G,H)\sim N(0,I_{n})
with

G\sim N(0,I_{r})
and
H\sim N(0,I_{n-r})
independent. Then, we have

Z=P_{\Pi}\bm{U}^{*}=Q\begin{pmatrix}G\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, conditional on
\{G\neq 0\}
, the direction
G/\|G\|_{2}
is uniform on the unit
sphere
\mathbb{S}^{r-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
and is independent of
\|G\|_{2}
.
Because
Q
is an isometry and
\range(P_{\Pi})=Q(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\{0\})
, it follows that
V:=\frac{Z}{\|Z\|_{2}}
is uniform on the unit sphere in
\range(P_{\Pi})
.

Observe that

\phi(Z,w)=\frac{(Z^{\top}w)^{2}}{\|Z\|_{2}^{2}\|w\|_{2}^{2}}=\left(\frac{Z^{\top}e}{\|Z\|_{2}}\right)^{2}=(V^{\top}e)^{2}.

Hence, we have

\left\{\phi(Z,w)\geqslant\gamma_{1}^{2}\right\}=\left\{|V^{\top}e|\geqslant\gamma_{1}\right\}.

Let
\theta:=\arccos(V^{\top}e)\in[0,\pi]
be the angle between
V
and
e
, and set

\alpha:=\arccos(\gamma_{1})\in(0,\pi/2]
. Then
|V^{\top}e|\geqslant\gamma_{1}
is equivalent to

\theta\in[0,\alpha]\ \cup\ [\pi-\alpha,\pi],

which is the union of two spherical caps of half-angle
\alpha
centered at
\pm e
.

Because
V
is uniform on
\mathbb{S}^{r-1}
, the angle
\theta
has density proportional to

\sin^{r-2}(\theta)
, i.e.

\mathbb{P}(\theta\in dt)=c_{r}(\sin t)^{r-2}dt,\qquad c_{r}^{-1}=\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin t)^{r-2}dt.

Therefore, with
\alpha:=\arccos(\gamma_{1})\in(0,\pi/2]
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(|V^{\top}e|\geqslant\gamma_{1}\right)=2\frac{\int_{0}^{\alpha}(\sin t)^{r-2}dt}{\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin t)^{r-2}dt}.

Using
\sin t\leqslant t
for
t\geqslant 0
and
\sin t\geqslant\frac{2}{\pi}t
for
t\in[0,\pi/2]
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(|V^{\top}e|\geqslant\gamma_{1}\right)\leqslant 2\frac{\int_{0}^{\alpha}t^{r-2}dt}{2\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}t\right)^{r-2}dt}=\frac{2}{\pi}\alpha^{r-1}=\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\arccos(\gamma_{1})\right)^{r-1}.

Since
\{\phi(Z,w)\geqslant\gamma_{1}^{2}\}=\{|V^{\top}e|\geqslant\gamma_{1}\}
, this proves that

\mathbb{P}\left(\phi\left(P_{\Pi}\bm{U}^{*},P_{\Pi}\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\geqslant\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\arccos(\gamma_{1})\right)^{r-1}

for fixed
\Pi
.
Finally, applying a union bound over
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
completes the proof.

Lemma D.6
.

For any
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, define

v_{0}:=(I_{n}-M_{\bm{X}})\bm{U}^{*},\qquad v_{\Pi}:=(I_{n}-M_{\bm{X}})\Pi^{\top}\bm{U}^{*}.

Then, it follows that

\left\|\Pi M_{(\bm{X},\bm{U}^{*})}-M_{(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{U}^{*})}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|M_{v_{0}}-M_{v_{\Pi}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

(46)

In particular, whenever

and

are nonzero, we have

(47)

Proof.

By orthogonal invariance of projectors, we have

M_{(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{U}^{*})}=\Pi M_{(\bm{X},\Pi^{\top}\bm{U}^{*})}\Pi^{\top}.

Hence, it holds that

\Pi^{\top}\left(\Pi M_{(\bm{X},\bm{U}^{*})}-M_{(\Pi\bm{X},\bm{U}^{*})}\right)\Pi=M_{(\bm{X},\bm{U}^{*})}-M_{(\bm{X},\Pi^{\top}\bm{U}^{*})}.

\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the subspaces
\mathrm{span}(\bm{X})
and
\mathrm{span}\left((I_{n}-M_{\bm{X}})z\right)
are orthogonal, and hence we have

Applying this with
\bm{z}=\bm{U}^{*}
and

gives

M_{(\bm{X},\bm{U}^{*})}-M_{(\bm{X},\Pi^{\top}\bm{U}^{*})}=\left\{M_{\bm{X}}+M_{v_{0}}\right\}-\left\{M_{\bm{X}}+M_{v_{\Pi}}\right\}=M_{v_{0}}-M_{v_{\Pi}}.

Conjugation by the orthogonal matrix
\Pi
preserves the operator norm, yielding (
46
).

For (
47
), write
\widehat{u}:=u/\|u\|_{2}
for
u\neq 0
. Then, the following holds:

\|M_{v_{0}}-M_{v_{\Pi}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|\widehat{v}_{0}\widehat{v}_{0}^{\top}-\widehat{v}_{\Pi}\widehat{v}_{\Pi}^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sqrt{1-(\widehat{v}_{0}^{\top}\widehat{v}_{\Pi})^{2}}\leqslant\|\widehat{v}_{0}-\widehat{v}_{\Pi}\|_{2}\leqslant\frac{2\|v_{0}-v_{\Pi}\|_{2}}{\min\{\|v_{0}\|_{2},\|v_{\Pi}\|_{2}\}}.

Finally, due to

or

D.1
(applied with
\gamma_{2}=\gamma

\mathbb{P}(\tau=\infty)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{L}A_{\ell}^{c}\right)\leqslant\left(1-\frac{\gamma^{n-1}}{n-1}\right)^{L}.

for
\ell=1,\dots,L
.
Conditionally on
\bm{U}

\bm{U}_{1}^{*},\dots,\bm{U}_{L}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{t-1}A_{j}^{c},\ A_{t}\cap B_{t}\mid\bm{U}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{L}(1-q(\bm{U}))^{t-1}r(\bm{U})

F.1.
A counterexample when
n-2k<p

Theorem F.1
.

Let
p<n
and
n-2k\leqslant p-1
. Further, assume
k\geqslant 2
so that a nontrivial transposition is permitted.
Then, there exist a full-column-rank matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, a pair of distinct permutations
\Pi_{0},\Pi_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
,
and coefficient vectors
\bm{\beta}_{0}\neq\bm{\beta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that

\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}=\Pi_{1}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1}

holds. Consequently, the minimizers of (
11
) are not unique in general, even with
\sigma_{0}=0
.

Proof.

Fix two distinct indices
a\neq b
in
[n]
. Set
\Pi_{0}:=I_{n}
and let
\Pi_{1}
be the transposition that swaps
a
and
b
.
Then
d(\Pi_{1},I_{n})=2\leqslant k
because
k\geqslant 2
, hence we have
\Pi_{0},\Pi_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
.

Set

t:=\max\{n-2k,0\}.

By the assumption
n-2k\leqslant p-1
we have
t\leqslant p-1
. Choose any subset
T\subset[n]
with
|T|=t
and set
S:=[n]\setminus T
.

We construct a full-column-rank design
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
together with
\bm{\beta}_{0}\neq\bm{\beta}_{1}

and
\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}=\Pi_{1}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1}
.

First, consider the case
p=1
. Put
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
with
X_{a}=1
,
X_{b}=-1
, and
X_{i}=0
for
i\notin\{a,b\}
.
Then, we have
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{X})=1
(full column rank). Take
\beta_{0}=1
and
\beta_{1}=-1
.
We have
(\bm{X}\beta_{0})_{a}=1
and
(\bm{X}\beta_{0})_{b}=-1
, while
\bm{X}\beta_{1}
equals
-1
at
a
and
1
at
b
,
so applying the transposition
\Pi_{1}
swaps these two values and yields

\Pi_{1}\bm{X}\beta_{1}=\bm{X}\beta_{0}=\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\beta_{0},

with
\beta_{0}\neq\beta_{1}
.

Next, consider the case
p\geqslant 2
.
Let
v:=e_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
w:=e_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, so
w\in v^{\perp}
and
\|v\|_{2}^{2}=1
. Define two row vectors

r_{a}:=w+v,\qquad r_{b}:=w-v,

so that we have
r_{a}v=1
and
r_{b}v=-1
.

We define the rows of
\bm{X}
as follows.

•

Put
X_{a}:=r_{a}
and
X_{b}:=r_{b}
.

•

For the
t
indices in
T
, assign distinct standard basis vectors among
\{e_{2},e_{3},\dots,e_{t+1}\}
:
for example, after ordering
T=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{t}\}
, set
X_{i_{j}}:=e_{j+1}
.
This gives
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{X}_{T})=t
and
\bm{X}_{T}v=0
.

•

For the remaining indices in
S\setminus\{a,b\}
, assign the missing basis vectors among
\{e_{2},\dots,e_{p}\}

so that the set of rows contains
\{e_{2},\dots,e_{p}\}
at least once; if there are extra rows, assign any vectors in
v^{\perp}
. This is feasible because
n\geqslant p+1
(since
p<n
), hence

|S\setminus\{a,b\}|=n-t-2\geqslant p-t-1
.

By construction, all rows except
a,b
lie in
v^{\perp}
, hence we have
X_{i}v=0
for all
i\notin\{a,b\}
.
Moreover, the row set contains
\{r_{a},e_{2},\dots,e_{p}\}
, which is linearly independent, so
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{X})=p

(full column rank) holds.

Now choose
\bm{\beta}_{0}:=-\tfrac{1}{2}v+\tfrac{1}{2}w
and
\bm{\beta}_{1}:=\bm{\beta}_{0}+v=\tfrac{1}{2}v+\tfrac{1}{2}w
.
Then, we have

r_{a}\bm{\beta}_{0}=(v+w)\cdot\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}v+\tfrac{1}{2}w\right)=0,\qquad r_{b}\bm{\beta}_{0}=(w-v)\cdot\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}v+\tfrac{1}{2}w\right)=1,

and

r_{a}\bm{\beta}_{1}=(v+w)\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{2}v+\tfrac{1}{2}w\right)=1,\qquad r_{b}\bm{\beta}_{1}=(w-v)\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{2}v+\tfrac{1}{2}w\right)=0.

For every
i\notin\{a,b\}
, because of
X_{i}\in v^{\perp}
we have
X_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{0})=X_{i}v=0
, hence it holds that
X_{i}\bm{\beta}_{1}=X_{i}\bm{\beta}_{0}
.
Therefore,
\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1}
equals
\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}
at all rows except
a,b
, where the two values are swapped.
Applying the transposition
\Pi_{1}
thus yields

\Pi_{1}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1}=\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}=\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0},

with
\bm{\beta}_{0}\neq\bm{\beta}_{1}
.

In either case, we construct the design matrix
\bm{X}
with full column-rank, the permutations
\Pi_{0}\neq\Pi_{1}
in
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
, and the coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{0}\neq\bm{\beta}_{1}

satisfying
\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{0}=\Pi_{1}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1}
. This yilelds the non-uniqueness of (
11
) even when
\sigma_{0}=0
.
∎

(\bm{\beta}_{1,0},\bm{\beta}_{2,0})\notin\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{joint}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})\ \Longrightarrow\ T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1,0},\bm{\beta}_{2,0}))>F^{-1}_{p,n-p}(\alpha).

Hence, by Theorem
F.3
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left((\bm{\beta}_{1,0},\bm{\beta}_{2,0})\notin\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{joint}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta_{L}^{\mathrm{partial}}+\mathbb{P}\left(T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1,0},\bm{\beta}_{2,0}))>F^{-1}_{p,n-p}(\alpha)\right).

For the second term, under (
27
) we have

\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}-\Pi_{0}\bm{X}\bm{\beta}_{1,0}-\bm{Z}\bm{\beta}_{2,0}=\sigma_{0}\bm{U},\qquad\bm{U}\sim N(0,I_{n}).

Define
M_{0}:=M_{(\Pi_{0}\bm{X},\bm{Z})}
and
P_{0}:=I_{n}-M_{0}
. Under the rank condition

\mathrm{rank}(\Pi_{0}\bm{X},\bm{Z})=p
,
M_{0}\bm{U}
and
P_{0}\bm{U}
are independent Gaussian vectors with

\|M_{0}\bm{U}\|_{2}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{p},\qquad\|P_{0}\bm{U}\|_{2}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{n-p},

and they are independent. Therefore, we have

T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1,0},\bm{\beta}_{2,0}))=\frac{\|M_{0}\bm{U}\|_{2}^{2}/p}{\|P_{0}\bm{U}\|_{2}^{2}/(n-p)}\sim F_{p,n-p},

which implies
\mathbb{P}\left(T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1,0},\bm{\beta}_{2,0}))\leqslant F^{-1}_{p,n-p}(\alpha)\right)=\alpha
. Combining the bounds yields

\mathbb{P}\left((\bm{\beta}_{1,0},\bm{\beta}_{2,0})\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{joint}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})\right)\geqslant\alpha-\delta_{L}^{\mathrm{partial}}.

∎

Theorem F.6

(Marginal confidence set for
\bm{\beta}_{1}
)
.

Fix any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Consider the partially permuted design (
27
)
defined in (
88
). It holds that, for any
L\geqslant 2
,

\mathbb{P}_{(\mathcal{U}^{L},\bm{U})}\left(\bm{\beta}_{1}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{partial}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})\right)\geqslant\alpha-\delta_{L}^{\mathrm{partial}},

where
\delta_{L}^{\mathrm{partial}}
is defined in (
80
).

Proof.

By definition, we have

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{partial}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}}):=\bigcup_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{partial}}^{(L)}}\left\{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}:\ T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi,\bm{\beta})\mid\bm{Z})\leqslant F_{p_{1},n-p}^{-1}(\alpha)\right\}.

(88)

Hence, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\beta}_{1}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{partial}}(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}})\right)\geqslant\mathbb{P}\left(T\left(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1})\mid\bm{Z}\right)\leqslant F_{p_{1},n-p}^{-1}(\alpha)\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\Pi_{0}\notin\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{partial}}^{(L)}\right).

Under the true parameters
(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1})
, the statistic

T(\bm{Y}^{\mathrm{obs}},(\Pi_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1})\mid\bm{Z})
has an
F_{p_{1},n-p}
distribution, so the first probability equals

Theorem

bounds the second probability by

F.3.
Theory on permuted ridge regression

Recall the augmented design matrix
\bm{X}_{\lambda}:=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}I_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p)\times p}
and, for any
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
, the embedded permutation
\widetilde{\Pi}:=\mathrm{diag}(\Pi,I_{p})
and the corresponding augmented design

\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi):=\widetilde{\Pi}\bm{X}_{\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}\Pi\bm{X}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}I_{p}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
P_{\Pi,\lambda}:=I_{n+p}-M_{\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi)}
be the residual projector. Note that
\mathrm{rank}(P_{\Pi,\lambda})=n
.
For the augmented Gaussian perturbation
\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}:=(\bm{U}^{*},\bm{0}_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
with
\bm{U}^{*}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{n})
,
we have
\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,D)
where
D:=\mathrm{diag}(I_{n},0_{p})
.

Lemma F.7
.

For any
\lambda>0
, define

c_{\lambda}:=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\|\bm{X}\|_{\rm op}^{2}}\in(0,1).

Fix
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
and define
Z_{\Pi}:=P_{\Pi,\lambda}\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}
.
Let
\Sigma_{\Pi}
denote the covariance of
Z_{\Pi}
restricted to
\mathrm{range}(P_{\Pi,\lambda})
.
Then, it holds that

c_{\lambda}I_{n}\preceq\Sigma_{\Pi}\preceq I_{n}.

Proof.

Set
P:=P_{\Pi,\lambda}
and write
v=(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
For any
v\in\mathrm{range}(P)
we have
Pv=v
and hence it holds that

\mathrm{Var}(v^{\top}Z_{\Pi})=v^{\top}PDPv=v^{\top}Dv=\|a\|_{2}^{2}.

It therefore suffices to lower and upper bound
\|a\|_{2}^{2}
over unit vectors
v\in\mathrm{range}(P)
.

Let
v\in\mathrm{range}(P)
with
\|v\|_{2}=1
.
Since
\mathrm{range}(P)=\mathrm{col}(\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi))^{\perp}
, we have
\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi)^{\top}v=0
, i.e.,

\bm{X}^{\top}\Pi^{\top}a+\sqrt{\lambda}b=0
and thus
b=-(1/\sqrt{\lambda})\bm{X}^{\top}\Pi^{\top}a
.
Consequently, we have

1=\|v\|_{2}^{2}=\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|b\|_{2}^{2}=\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\bm{X}^{\top}\Pi^{\top}a\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\|a\|_{2}^{2}\left(1+\frac{\|\bm{X}\|_{\rm op}^{2}}{\lambda}\right),

which yields
\|a\|_{2}^{2}\geqslant\lambda/(\lambda+\|\bm{X}\|_{\rm op}^{2})=c_{\lambda}
.
The upper bound
\|a\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant 1
is immediate from
\|v\|_{2}=1
.
Because of
\mathrm{Var}(v^{\top}Z_{\Pi})=\|a\|_{2}^{2}
for all unit
v\in\mathrm{range}(P)
, the claim follows.
∎

Lemma F.8
.

Fix
\Pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
and let
\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}=\mathrm{diag}(\Pi_{0},I_{p})
.
For
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
define
P_{\Pi,\lambda}:=I_{n+p}-M_{\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi)}
and consider
\phi\left(P_{\Pi,\lambda}\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*},P_{\Pi,\lambda}\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}\bm{X}_{\lambda}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)
.

Then for any
\gamma_{1}\in(0,1)
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}\phi\left(P_{\Pi,\lambda}\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*},P_{\Pi,\lambda}\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}\bm{X}_{\lambda}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\geqslant\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)\leqslant 2|\mathcal{P}_{n,k}|c_{\lambda}^{-n/2}\left(\arccos(\gamma_{1})\right)^{n-1}.

Proof.

Fix
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
and abbreviate
P:=P_{\Pi,\lambda}
.
If
P\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}\bm{X}_{\lambda}\bm{\beta}_{0}=0
, then the corresponding
\phi(\cdot,\cdot)
is zero and can be ignored.
Otherwise, set
w:=P\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}\bm{X}_{\lambda}\bm{\beta}_{0}\neq 0
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p)\times n}
be any matrix with orthonormal columns spanning
\mathrm{range}(P)
.
Then, we have
Q^{\top}PQ=I_{n}
,
Q^{\top}w\neq 0
, and
\bar{Z}:=Q^{\top}P\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\Sigma)
with

\Sigma:=Q^{\top}DQ
.
Moreover, since
Q
is an isometry on
\mathrm{range}(P)
, we have

\phi(P\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*},w)=\phi(\bar{Z},\bar{w}),\qquad\bar{w}:=Q^{\top}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

By Lemma
F.7
,
\Sigma
satisfies
c_{\lambda}I_{n}\preceq\Sigma\preceq I_{n}
and hence

\Sigma^{-1}
has eigenvalues in
[1,1/c_{\lambda}]
.

Define
V:=\bar{Z}/\|\bar{Z}\|_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\bar{e}:=\bar{w}/\|\bar{w}\|_{2}
.
Then, it holds that
\phi(\bar{Z},\bar{w})=(V^{\top}\bar{e})^{2}
, so we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\phi(P\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*},w)\geqslant\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(|V^{\top}\bar{e}|\geqslant\gamma_{1}\right).

For
\bar{Z}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\Sigma)
, the direction
V
admits the density

f(v)=\frac{(v^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}v)^{-n/2}}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}u)^{-n/2}d\mu(u)}\qquad(v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1})

with respect to the uniform surface measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
Since
(v^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}v)^{-n/2}\in[c_{\lambda}^{n/2},1]
for all
v
, the normalizing constant is at least
c_{\lambda}^{n/2}
,
and hence
f(v)\leqslant c_{\lambda}^{-n/2}
for all
v
. Therefore, for any measurable
A\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(V\in A)\leqslant c_{\lambda}^{-n/2}\mu(A)
.

Taking
A=\{v:|v^{\top}\bar{e}|\geqslant\gamma_{1}\}
and using the bound

\mu(A)\leqslant 2(\arccos(\gamma_{1}))^{n-1}
yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\phi(P\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*},w)\geqslant\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)\leqslant 2c_{\lambda}^{-n/2}\left(\arccos(\gamma_{1})\right)^{n-1}.

A union bound over
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
completes the proof.
∎

We next state the recovery guarantee for the ridge-augmented candidate set.
Define

C_{\min}^{\mathrm{ridge}}(\Pi_{0}):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k},\\
\Pi\neq\Pi_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\|(I_{n+p}-M_{\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi)})\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi_{0})\bm{\beta}_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{n\max(d(\Pi_{0},I_{n})-d(\Pi,I_{n}),1)}.

(89)

Theorem F.9
.

Assume
C_{\min}^{\mathrm{ridge}}(\Pi_{0})>0
holds and fix
\underline{c}\in(0,1)
.
Suppose
\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{\min}:=\|\bm{X}\|_{\rm op}^{2}\underline{c}/(1-\underline{c})
holds, so that
c_{\lambda}\geqslant\underline{c}
.
Then the conclusion of Theorem
4.1
continues to hold for the confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}^{(L)}
,
with
C_{\min}(\Pi_{0})
replaced by
C_{\min}^{\mathrm{ridge}}(\Pi_{0})
and with the last term of
\Delta(\gamma)

(see (
E.2
)) similarly replaced by

2^{(n+1)/2}|\mathcal{P}_{n,k}|\underline{c}^{-n/2}\left(\gamma\log(e/\gamma)\right)^{(n-1)/2}.

Proof.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,1/4]
.
Recall the augmented perturbation
\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}:=(\bm{U}^{*},\bm{0}_{p})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,D)
with

D=\mathrm{diag}(I_{n},0_{p})\preceq I_{n+p}
, and the augmented designs

\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi)=\widetilde{\Pi}\bm{X}_{\lambda}
with residual projectors

P_{\Pi,\lambda}:=I_{n+p}-M_{\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi)}
(so
\mathrm{rank}(P_{\Pi,\lambda})=n
).

We follow the proof of Lemma
D.5
with
\bm{W}(\Pi)
replaced by
\bm{W}_{\lambda}(\Pi)
,

\bm{U}^{*}
replaced by
\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}
, and with the separation constant

C_{\min}(\Pi_{0})
replaced by
C_{\min}^{\rm ridge}(\Pi_{0})
.
Indeed, for any orthogonal projector
P
of rank
n
and any
x\geqslant 1
, write

\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}=D^{1/2}G
with
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n+p})
. Then, we have

\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*\top}P\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}=G^{\top}\left(D^{1/2}PD^{1/2}\right)G,\qquad 0\preceq D^{1/2}PD^{1/2}\preceq P,

so all eigenvalues of
D^{1/2}PD^{1/2}
lie in
[0,1]
. Hence

\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*\top}P\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}
is stochastically dominated by
\chi_{n}^{2}
, and therefore we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*\top}P\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}\geqslant nx\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\chi_{n}^{2}\geqslant nx)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2}\Psi(x)\right),

which is exactly the tail bound used in Lemma
D.5
.
Likewise, for any fixed
v
,
v^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*}
is Gaussian with variance
v^{\top}Dv\leqslant\|v\|_{2}^{2}
,
so the corresponding Gaussian tail bounds are also unchanged.

The only place in Lemma
D.5
where spherical symmetry of projected perturbations is used
is in controlling the following event

\max_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}\phi\left(P_{\Pi,\lambda}\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{*},P_{\Pi,\lambda}\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}\bm{X}_{\lambda}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right).

Here Lemma
D.4
is replaced by Lemma
F.8
, which yields the modified last
term
2^{(n+1)/2}|\mathcal{P}_{n,k}|\underline{c}^{-n/2}(\gamma\log(e/\gamma))^{(n-1)/2}
.

Combining these bounds with the union bound over
L
i.i.d. draws

\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{1}^{*},\dots,\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{L}^{*}
yields the desired claim.
∎
