Evaluation of occupational safety and influencing factors among nurses working in internal medicine clinics
Authors: Vildan Kocatepe, Dilek Yildirim, Ayşegül Türkmenoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare workers, particularly nurses in internal medicine clinics, are exposed to specific occupational hazards such as infection risk, long working hours, and musculoskeletal problems. This study aims to evaluate the occupational safety of nurses working in internal medicine clinics and the factors influencing it.MethodsThe descriptive and analytical cross-sectional study was conducted with 300 nurses working in internal medicine clinics at a hospital in İzmir. The data were collected using a Participant Information Form and the Occupational Safety Scale (OSS) for Healthcare Personnel Working in Hospitals. The prediction of the OSS for Healthcare Workers in Hospitals Total Score among nurses, as well as the factors of Verbal Violence, Working Environment Equipment and Design, Quality of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), and Equipment Used, were evaluated using multiple linear regression analysis.ResultsThe nurses’ mean age was 29.6 ± 4.7 years. A total of 55.3% (n = 166) of the nurses reported either that they had not read the regulation on ensuring patient and worker safety in healthcare institutions or that they were unaware of the regulation itself (6%, n = 18). The variables of Working Environment Equipment and Design, Quality of PPE, and Equipment Used were found to be statistically significant, explaining 29.8% of the variance in the OSS for Healthcare Workers in Hospitals total scores (p < 0.05).ConclusionNurses perceived occupational health and safety as moderately adequate. Among the examined variables, Working Environment Equipment and Design, Quality of PPE, and Equipment Used were found to significantly influence nurses’ occupational health and safety. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve clinical workspace design, ensure high-quality PPE, and optimize equipment use, thereby enhancing nurses’ safety and well-being.

Background

Nurses are among the key healthcare professionals who play a vital role in maintaining and improving public health. In particular, nurses working in internal medicine clinics are exposed to a wide range of occupational hazards due to the complexity and intensity of patient care in these units. They frequently care for patients with chronic and infectious diseases, handle hazardous medications, and perform physically demanding tasks such as patient lifting and transfer. Moreover, factors such as insufficient staffing, long working hours, and emotional strain further increase their risk of occupational injuries, fatigue, and burnout. Therefore, ensuring a safe and supportive work environment for these nurses is crucial, not only for protecting their physical and psychological health but also for maintaining the quality and continuity of patient care. These occupational risks have been widely recognized by international authorities, including the World Health Organization and the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, all emphasizing the need for comprehensive safety policies and preventive strategies to protect healthcare workers [1–3].

It is well known that occupational risks for healthcare workers vary widely. Studies conducted by Tullar et al., and Joseph & Joseph have identified nurses, doctors, laboratory technicians, and medical waste handlers as the healthcare professionals at the highest risk. These occupational groups face not only physical and chemical exposures but also psychosocial risks such as high stress levels, long working hours, and emotional exhaustion [4, 5]. Recent studies have highlighted that nurses are particularly vulnerable to these psychosocial risks due to increased workload, insufficient staffing, and the emotional intensity of patient care. Labrague (2021) reported that pandemic-related stress and extended working hours significantly affected nurses’ sleep quality and mental well-being [6]. Similarly, Häussl et al. (2021) emphasized that prolonged exposure to psychological and physical stressors during hospital work contributes to burnout and emotional exhaustion among nurses [7]. In addition, Młynarska et al. (2022) found that long working hours and cumulative fatigue were closely associated with sleep disorders and decreased occupational satisfaction in nursing professionals [8].

While individual factors such as age, gender, and lifestyle may also influence healthcare workers’ health [9], this study focuses primarily on institutional factors, including perception of occupational safety, availability of personal protective equipment, and adequacy of work-related resources. Therefore, when developing policies to reduce health risks for workers, it is important to consider both individual and environmental factors together.

Healthcare workers are at high risk for occupational hazards [10]. Nurses working in internal medicine clinics are exposed to various risk factors, such as exposure to infectious diseases (e.g., pathogens carrying infection risks like Hepatitis B, C, HIV during patient care), heavy lifting and patient handling (due to inadequate ergonomic conditions, leading to musculoskeletal issues), injuries from sharp objects (such as needle sticks during insulin injections and other invasive procedures), exposure to hazardous drugs (such as chemotherapy drugs and other toxic substances), insufficient staffing, burnout due to long working hours and shifts, and emotional exhaustion from long-term work with chronic patients. However, research on occupational health risks specific to nurses in Turkey is limited. Studies indicate that while nurses face significant occupational hazards, such as exposure to infectious agents, physical strain, and psychological stress, comprehensive national studies addressing these issues are scarce. For instance, a study by Kaya (2023) found that nurses’ perceptions of occupational safety and their awareness of related risks are often inadequate, highlighting the need for more focused research in this area [11].

The aim of this study is to evaluate the occupational safety and influencing factors among nurses working in internal medicine clinics. This study is one of the first to conduct multiple regression analysis using the Occupational Safety Scale (OSS) in internal medicine wards in Turkey. Assessing the physical, chemical, biological, and psychosocial risks that nurses encounter in their professional lives is crucial for identifying strategies to prevent these risks. The findings obtained will provide significant contributions both to the protection of nurses’ occupational health and to enhancing the continuity and quality of healthcare services. The research questions of this study are as follows: (1) What is the level of occupational safety perceived by nurses working in internal medicine clinics? (2) Which factors, such as working environment design, quality of PPE, equipment used, and exposure to verbal violence, are associated with nurses’ perceptions of occupational safety?

Methods

Research design

This is a descriptive and analytical cross-sectional study.

Population and sample

The population of the study consisted of all nurses working in the internal medicine clinics of a hospital in İzmir. In the study, a multiple regression analysis was conducted with 300 nurses and 4 independent variables. A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power software. For a medium effect size (𝑓 2 = 0.15) and a significance level of 0.05, the sample size provided sufficient statistical power to detect a significant relationship. The calculated power was approximately 99%, indicating a very high likelihood of detecting a medium-sized effect with this sample. The study was completed with 300 nurses who voluntarily agreed to participate, had been working in the designated hospital for at least one month, and answered all questions completely. Nurses with communication problems, psychiatric health issues, or who were on leave during the data collection period were excluded from the study.

Data collection

The researchers collected the data using a questionnaire through face-to-face interview technique between 15/11/2024 and 15/01/2025. It took approximately 15 min to fill out the questionnaire. The data were collected using an Participant Information Form and the Occupational Safety Scale (OSS) for Health Care Personnel Working in Hospital. To ensure participant anonymity and minimize social desirability bias, no identifying information was recorded on the data collection forms, and participants were informed that their responses would remain completely confidential. Informed consent forms and data collection forms were collected separately; participants’ names were written only on the consent forms, not on the data collection forms. Participants were encouraged to answer honestly and independently.

Participant information form

The Participant Information Form was developed during the creation of the Occupational Safety Scale (OSS) for Healthcare Personnel Working in Hospitals by Öztürk and Babacan. The form consists of 16 questions, including sociodemographic data such as the nurse’s age, gender, education level, and other characteristics related to occupational safety [12].

Occupational safety scale (OSS) for healthcare personnel working in hospitals

The scale was developed by Öztürk and Babacan (2012) and has undergone validity and reliability testing [12]. It consists of 45 items and includes 7 sub-dimensions: occupational diseases and complaints, health screening and record systems, accidents and intoxications, managerial support and approaches, material, equipment, and tool control, protective measures and regulations, and physical environment suitability [12].

It is a Likert-type scale with six levels: 1 = Strongly Disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Slightly Disagree, 4 = Slightly Agree, 5 = Agree, 6 = Strongly Agree. The scale consists of 45 items, with total scores ranging from 45 to 270. A score closer to 270 indicates that occupational health and safety in the hospital are adequate, while a score closer to 45 indicates insufficient occupational health and safety. The Cronbach alpha coefficient for nurses has been reported as 0.95, indicating high reliability [12]. In the present study, the Cronbach alpha value of the scale was found to be 0.946. The Cronbach’s α values for the subscales are as follows: occupational diseases and complaints α = 0.854, health screening and record systems α = 0.786, accidents and intoxications α = 0.932, managerial support and approaches α = 0.943, material, equipment, and tool control α = 0.924, protective measures and regulations α = 0.909, and physical environment suitability α = 0.729.

Data analysis

The data collected for this study were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarized with frequencies and percentages. There were no missing data in the study. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using both graphical methods (Q-Q plots and histograms) and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Considering the large sample size (n = 300), graphical evaluation was emphasized, as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test may be overly sensitive in large samples. Additionally, the Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to confirm the results. The total score of the Occupational Safety Scale for Healthcare Workers in Hospitals was predicted using multiple linear regression analysis, with nurses’ levels of Verbal Violence, Working Environment Equipment and Design, Quality of Personal Protective Equipment, and Equipment included as independent variables. The decision to include or exclude variables from the model was based on the results of the multicollinearity test. Multicollinearity among the variables (Verbal Violence, Working Environment Equipment and Design, Quality of Personal Protective Equipment, and Equipment Used) was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance values. Variables with a tolerance value greater than 0.1 and a VIF less than 10 were included in the regression analysis [13]. No multicollinearity was found among the variables. As there was no indication of multicollinearity, all the variables—Verbal Violence, Working Environment Equipment and Design, Quality of Personal Protective Equipment, and Equipment Used—were included in the regression model. Prior to performing regression analyses, all assumptions were checked. Normality of residuals, linearity between predictors and the outcome, and homoscedasticity were assessed and found to be satisfactory, indicating that the regression model was appropriate for the data. A significance level of 0.05 was adopted [14].

Ethics

Ethical approval for conducting the research was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Istanbul Aydin University with the decision number 2023/03, dated 30.03.2023. Permission to conduct the study was also obtained from the institution where the research was carried out. Additionally, detailed information about the study was provided to the nurses who formed the sample, and both verbal and written consent were obtained from them. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

In our study, a total of 61.3% of the nurses reported either that they had not read the regulation on ensuring patient and worker safety in healthcare institutions (55.3%) or that they were unaware of the regulation itself (6%). Of those who had not read the regulation, 55.3% stated that they had not done so due to a lack of awareness. Similarly, in a study conducted by Akkaya and Atay, 61.5% of the nurses reported that they were unaware of the regulation concerning occupational safety [15]. This finding indicates a low level of awareness among healthcare workers regarding this issue. It suggests that institutions should increase their informational and educational activities regarding regulations for their employees. Additionally, the fact that employees are unaware of such critical regulations could pose a risk to both patient and employee safety.

In this study, it was observed that the average scores for the Occupational Safety of Healthcare Personnel in Hospitals were above the theoretical midpoint of the scale. This could be due to the increased attention given to nurses’ occupational risks in recent years in the country. It can be assumed that the increased awareness among nurses regarding occupational risks may be related to the heightened focus on preventive measures following the pandemic; however, this was not directly assessed in the present study. Some studies in the field of occupational health and safety, similar to the findings in this study, have shown that workers feel adequately protected regarding occupational safety [16, 17]. However, other studies have reported that workers do not consider occupational safety to be sufficient, and that nurses’ awareness of the issue remains low [18, 19]. The relatively low scores in the subscales of Occupational Diseases and Problems, Health Screening and Record Systems, and Physical Environment Suitability may be related to limited occupational disease monitoring, irregular health screenings, and inadequate physical conditions in hospitals. Heavy workloads, insufficient institutional support, and infrastructural limitations may also contribute to these lower perceptions of safety among healthcare personnel [2, 3, 20]. The lowest score was obtained in the ‘Physical Environment Suitability’ subscale (20.8), indicating that infrastructural and ergonomic deficiencies in hospital settings remain a major concern and may negatively affect nurses’ perception of occupational safety and overall well-being. The findings of previous research are consistent with these results [19, 21]. However, due to the differences in ergonomic conditions, record-keeping/reporting systems between hospitals, the novelty of these developments, and the slow pace of cultural changes, there are still deficiencies. Ineffective measures and insufficient oversight lead to the inability to prevent occupational diseases and weaken the perception of occupational health and safety.

In this study, nearly half of the nurses (50.7%) reported experiencing an occupational disease, with sleep disorders being the most frequently mentioned (35%). Although sleep problems are not formally classified as occupational diseases, they are widely recognized as work-related health issues among nurses due to shift work, night duties, and workload stress. Previous studies have shown that irregular working hours and high occupational stress are major contributors to sleep disturbances in healthcare professionals [22, 23]. Therefore, the high prevalence of sleep disorders observed in this study may reflect work-related fatigue and stress rather than a formally diagnosed occupational disease. In this study, 42.3% of nurses reported experiencing work-related accidents or injuries, which represents a relatively high rate compared with findings from similar studies. This may be explained by factors such as heavy workloads, rotating shift systems, inadequate staffing levels, and insufficient institutional safety protocols. Previous studies have shown that fatigue, long working hours, and lack of training on occupational safety significantly increase the risk of workplace accidents among nurses [24, 25]. Therefore, the high rate observed in this study likely reflects both organizational and environmental factors rather than individual negligence. These findings align with the results of other studies in the literature. For instance, Młynarska et al. (2022) found that 73.33% of shift-working nurses experienced insomnia. In a study conducted with 1232 healthcare workers in Switzerland, nearly a quarter of the participants reported severe or very severe musculoskeletal disorders, and almost one-seventh reported severe sleep disorders [26]. These prevalence rates were higher among nurses compared to doctors and other healthcare workers. The same study identified general stress, work-related stress, physical exertion at work, and particularly painful or tiring postures as strong risk factors for musculoskeletal disorders. In addition, the high rates of musculoskeletal injuries (20%, n = 60), soft tissue trauma (18.7%, n = 56), and verbal violence (18.7%, n = 56) indicate that nurses are frequently exposed to both physical and psychological occupational risks. These findings highlight the multifaceted nature of occupational hazards in nursing and underline the need for comprehensive prevention strategies addressing both physical strain and workplace violence. In contrast, only general and work-related stress were significantly associated with sleep disorders [27]. A systematic review based on 23 retrospective and prospective observational studies (cross-sectional and cohort) conducted among healthcare professionals revealed that the 1-year prevalence rates of work-related musculoskeletal issues among healthcare professionals ranged significantly from 28% to 96% [28]. These findings further emphasize the high prevalence of occupational diseases and injuries among healthcare workers, particularly in nursing, highlighting the need for improved preventive measures and support systems in healthcare settings.

In hospitals, ensuring the continuity of services and providing effective care to patients is closely linked to the implementation of occupational safety measures. Given that hospitals, especially high-risk areas such as intensive care units, emergency departments, and chemotherapy units that handle toxic medications, face significant risks of occupational diseases and accidents for their employees, it is crucial to prioritize safety precautions. Moreover, physical infrastructure deficiencies in hospitals, as well as inadequate implementation of preventive measures and safety rules, are common issues that need attention. These factors can negatively influence employees’ perceptions of their workplace safety.

In our study, it was observed that 84.3% (n = 253) of the nurses work in inpatient units with shift schedules and experience high levels of work-related stress. Furthermore, the lowest satisfaction among nurses was reported regarding working hours, shifts, and task distribution (38.3%, n = 115), highlighting the critical role of workload and scheduling patterns in influencing overall job satisfaction. This stress is linked to a higher prevalence of sleep disorders and musculoskeletal issues among the nurses. These findings emphasize the importance of addressing the specific risks faced by healthcare workers in these high-pressure and physically demanding environments. It also highlights the need for hospitals to take comprehensive actions, such as improving workplace infrastructure, implementing effective safety protocols, and providing mental health and ergonomic support, to safeguard the health and well-being of healthcare professionals.

Among the factors examined, Working Environment Equipment and Design (β = 0.145, p = 0.032), PPE (β = 0.240, p = 0.008), and Equipment Used (β = 0.274, p = 0.004) were found to significantly affect nurses’ occupational health and safety. However, verbal violence was not a significant predictor (p >0.05). These results highlight the importance of institutional support, safe equipment design, and the effective use of PPE in promoting occupational safety among nurses. The quality and design of equipment in the work environment are crucial for nurses to perform their duties effectively. Research has shown that inadequate infrastructure and equipment in healthcare settings can negatively impact nurses’ job performance [29, 30]. The quality of PPE directly influences nurses’ job satisfaction and psychological well-being. A study found that the physical and psychological challenges experienced by nurses while using PPE negatively affected their job satisfaction [7, 31]. Additionally, incidents of violence in the workplace are a significant factor that threatens nurses’ occupational health and safety. Nurses experience psychological stress and anxiety due to the violence they face from patients and their families [32]. Among the factors that impact nurses’ occupational health and safety, the working environment equipment, the quality of PPE, the equipment used, and exposure to violence play a crucial role. Although exposure to violence is an important occupational concern for healthcare workers, in this study, verbal violence did not have a statistically significant impact on the overall occupational safety perception of nurses. Instead, institutional factors, including the design of the working environment, the quality of personal protective equipment, and the adequacy of the equipment used, were identified as significant predictors of occupational safety. This finding suggests that nurses’ sense of safety is more strongly influenced by the organizational environment and material conditions than by isolated experiences of verbal violence. Improving these factors is expected to positively influence nurses’ job performance and psychological health. Given the lingering effects of the pandemic, it is likely that nurses place great importance on physical measures such as the design of their working environment, the quality of PPE, and the equipment they use to ensure their safety. This focus on safety measures is crucial in helping them feel protected and supported in their demanding roles.

Limitations of the study

The inclusion of nurses working in all internal medicine clinics in this study allowed for a comprehensive assessment. However, clinic-specific factors, such as the number of patients receiving chemotherapy daily, the number of emergency room visits, and the exposure of nurses to cytotoxic agents, may have reflected on their perceptions of occupational safety and preventive measures. Additionally, nurses’ recent experience with the pandemic and the training they received in infection control measures and PPE use could have influenced their perceptions of occupational health and safety. Because participation was voluntary, the potential for selection bias should be considered as a limitation of the study.

Furthermore, the study did not fully address selection bias or social desirability bias, particularly given the face-to-face data collection method. Finally, the study was conducted in a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to nurses working in internal units of other hospitals or regions.

Future studies could adopt multicenter and longitudinal designs to enhance generalizability and allow for causal inferences. Methodological considerations should also be incorporated to minimize potential biases, including selection and social desirability biases, providing a more robust understanding of nurses’ occupational health and safety perceptions.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the occupational safety and influencing factors among nurses working in internal medicine clinics. The findings revealed that a considerable proportion of nurses had either not read or were unaware of the regulation on ensuring patient and worker safety in healthcare institutions, indicating a lack of awareness regarding occupational safety regulations. Additionally, nurses frequently reported experiencing sleep disorders, musculoskeletal problems, and work-related injuries, underscoring the need for stronger preventive and support mechanisms in hospital settings.

The study identified that factors such as the quality of the working environment and equipment design, the quality of personal protective equipment (PPE), and exposure to violence significantly influence nurses’ occupational health and safety. These results emphasize the importance of improving workplace infrastructure, ensuring the availability of high-quality PPE, and implementing effective violence prevention strategies.

From a practical standpoint, it is recommended that healthcare institutions introduce mandatory courses and regular in-service training programs on occupational safety regulations, ergonomic practices, infection control, and stress management. Furthermore, hospitals should regularly assess and improve the physical conditions of clinical environments, strengthen reporting and monitoring systems, and establish clear policies to prevent workplace violence.

In alignment with the study’s objectives, these findings highlight the need to enhance nurses’ awareness and protection regarding occupational safety. Future research should consider multicenter and longitudinal designs to explore causal relationships and develop comprehensive, evidence-based strategies to improve occupational health and safety among healthcare professionals.
