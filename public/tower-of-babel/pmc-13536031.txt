Manic switching is seldom observed during rTMS therapy for unipolar depression: Implications for antidepressant mechanisms
Authors: Yuki Matsuda, Shun Takahashi
Published: 2026

Recent Japanese real‐world studies may provide an informative context for considering mood switching during repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) for depression. In both studies, treatment under the Japanese public health insurance system was delivered using the NeuroStar TMS Therapy System, with the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex target defined as 5.5 cm anterior to the site used to determine the motor threshold. Stimulation was delivered at a frequency of 10 Hz and an intensity of 120% of the standard motor threshold. Each session comprised 75 trains, each lasting 4 s stimulation, yielding a total of 3000 pulses. Treatment was administered once daily, generally 5 days per week, for up to 30 sessions. 1 , 2 In a registry‐based report from the Kansai TMS network, Imazu et al. 1 described 102 consecutive patients treated with rTMS within the Japanese public health insurance system and reported no serious adverse events (0/102; 0%); manic or hypomanic events were not separately reported. In our subsequent nationwide observational report, Matsuda et al. 2 retrospectively collected clinical data from 21 institutes between June 2019 and December 2023; 497 patients with treatment‐resistant depression were candidates for analysis, dropout due to adverse effects was 4.2%, and no convulsive seizures or manic changes were observed (0/497; 0%). In light of these observations, treatment‐emergent mania or hypomania appears to be seldom observed during standard rTMS for appropriately selected patients with unipolar depression. This observation is relevant not only to the safety profile of rTMS but also to the possibility that different antidepressant interventions differ in switch liability.

This impression is broadly consistent with the wider literature (Table S1). A review of rTMS‐associated mania and hypomania suggests that such events are rare overall, and that many published cases occurred in clinical contexts that differ from present‐day standard rTMS for unipolar depression, including bipolar‐spectrum illness, concurrent antidepressant treatment, sleep deprivation, or heterogeneous stimulation paradigms. 3 Quantitatively, a review identified 10 of 53 randomized controlled trials of rTMS for depression that specifically addressed treatment‐emergent mania or hypomania. Across these trials, such events were reported in 3 of 356 participants receiving active rTMS (0.84%) and 1 of 137 receiving sham stimulation (0.73%), with no statistically significant difference. Among participants with unipolar depression receiving active rTMS, 1 of 291 experienced a switch (0.34%). 4 A subsequent systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials in unipolar depression identified seven trials reporting mood shifts from depression to hypomania. Across these trials, such shifts occurred in 4.2% of participants receiving active rTMS (4/94) and 0% receiving sham rTMS (0/95); the difference was not statistically significant (odds ratio, 3.85; 95% confidence interval, 0.60–24.64; p = 0.15). 5 Notably, two of the four participants who experienced a mood shift were younger than 18 years of age, and the overall rate of hypomanic switching across their review was 0.002% with active rTMS and 0% with sham rTMS. 5 Thus, the most defensible conclusion is not that rTMS can never precipitate mania, but that the risk under contemporary standard protocols for appropriately selected patients with unipolar depression appears very low.

This observation is clinically important because it contrasts, at least in part, with the literature on antidepressant‐associated switching. In a meta‐analytic review of patients diagnosed with major depressive disorder, mania or hypomania was reported more often with antidepressant exposure than without it (aggregate rates, 5.97% vs. 1.24%); a separate random‐effects meta‐analysis of 22 MDD studies yielded a pooled risk ratio of 3.76 (95% confidence interval, 2.77–5.09; p < 0.0001), suggesting that antidepressant exposure may carry switch liability in some vulnerable patients. 6 Although some cases likely reflected latent bipolarity or diagnostic heterogeneity, the contrast remains noteworthy: in routine modern rTMS practice and in the reviewed rTMS literature, treatment‐emergent mania appears difficult to detect, whereas switching has been described more clearly in parts of the antidepressant literature.

A plausible explanation is that standard rTMS exerts focal, non‐convulsive modulation of cortical networks rather than the broader systemic effects produced by antidepressant drugs. In other words, rTMS may influence dysfunctional mood‐regulation circuits with greater spatial and physiological constraint. This hypothesis also helps position rTMS relative to convulsive neuromodulation. By contrast, conventional antidepressant drugs act systemically and modulate monoaminergic signaling and downstream neuroplastic processes, 7 whereas electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) applies relatively nonfocal electrical stimulation, induces a generalized seizure, and produces widespread electrophysiological, neurochemical, and neuroendocrine effects. 8 Alterations in catecholaminergic activity, neuroplastic signaling, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal‐axis function, and circadian regulation have been proposed as contributors to mood switching, although no single mechanism has been established. 7 ECT has long been recognized to be associated with treatment‐emergent hypomania or mania despite its well‐established antidepressant efficacy. 9 , 10 In a historical retrospective chart study of patients with psychotic unipolar depression, hypomanic switches were recorded in 8 of 79 patients who received ECT (10.1%) and 16 of 445 patients who did not receive ECT (3.6%; p < 0.01). 9 A subsequent systematic review identified 17 patients with ECT‐emergent hypomania or mania across 12 case reports or case series—9 who had no known history of mania or hypomania and had been diagnosed with unipolar depression, and 8 with bipolar disorder. Because these were case‐based data, no denominator was available from which incidence could be estimated. 10 By contrast, contemporary randomized trial evidence indicates that TMS is generally well tolerated and does not show a meaningful signal of frequent mood switching. 5 This gradient is hypothesis‐generating: as antidepressant interventions move from broad pharmacological effects or generalized seizure induction toward more focal and non‐convulsive circuit modulation, switch liability may diminish. However, this mechanistic interpretation remains hypothetical and has not been established in direct comparative studies.

At the same time, this interpretation should remain cautious. These numerical estimates were derived from studies with substantial differences in patient populations, treatment and observation periods, definitions of mood switching, concomitant treatments, and ascertainment methods and therefore should not be interpreted as direct head‐to‐head comparisons. The low observed rate of treatment‐emergent mania with rTMS should not be misread as proof of zero risk. The existing literature is limited by the rarity of events, heterogeneity of ascertainment, and the persistent challenge of excluding unrecognized bipolarity with complete certainty. In addition, low switch liability in contemporary rTMS practice may reflect not only the characteristics of the intervention itself but also careful patient selection and exclusion of patients at elevated bipolar risk.

Nevertheless, the repeated clinical observation that treatment‐emergent mania is seldom observed during standard rTMS for unipolar depression appears meaningful. It suggests that the antidepressant effects of rTMS may often be achieved without engaging some of the broader biological processes that, in vulnerable individuals, contribute to mood destabilization under antidepressants or convulsive treatments. Accordingly, the rarity of treatment‐emergent mania may represent a clinically meaningful safety feature of standard rTMS in properly selected patients with unipolar depression. At a minimum, this observation supports the view that not all antidepressant interventions share the same switch liability. More broadly, it raises an important mechanistic question for future work: whether focal, non‐convulsive neuromodulation can relieve depressive symptoms while minimizing the risk of pathological mood elevation.
