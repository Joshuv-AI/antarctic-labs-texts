Salivary Zinc Levels in Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Anshul Sharma, Anjali Arya, Chetan Kumar Sharma, Ankit Gupta, Richa Sharma, Madhvika Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to evaluate and compare salivary zinc levels in individuals diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis and healthy controls.Materials and Methods:A total of 20 participants were included in the study after obtaining informed consent. The participants were divided into two equal groups. Group 1 consisted of 10 healthy individuals who served as controls, while Group 2 included 10 individuals diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis. Saliva samples were collected from all participants, and the zinc levels were measured and analyzed to determine any differences between the two groups.Results:The average salivary zinc level in the rheumatoid arthritis group (Group 2) was 83.52 ± 5.9 μg/dL. In comparison, the control group (Group 1) showed a lower mean level of 69.56 ± 11.2 μg/dL. This indicates a noticeable difference in zinc concentrations between the two groups.Conclusion:Based on the findings, individuals with rheumatoid arthritis exhibited higher salivary zinc levels compared to healthy controls. This suggests that salivary zinc concentration may be altered in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and could potentially serve as a marker for disease activity or metabolic changes associated with the condition.

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is the most common inflammatory joint disease worldwide. It is a chronic, complex, and heterogeneous autoimmune disorder characterized by ongoing inflammation of synovial (diarthrodial) joints. This inflammation often leads to symmetric polyarthritis and thickening of the synovial membrane, eventually causing progressive joint damage, cartilage and bone destruction, and visible deformities.[12]

RA is not limited to joint involvement; it also affects multiple systems throughout the body, resulting in extra-articular manifestations. Comorbid conditions are frequently observed, contributing to significant physical disability, reduced quality of life, and a mortality rate that is nearly twice as high as that of the general population. Predicting the onset of RA remains a challenge in personalized medicine and depends on understanding gene–environment interactions, cytokine profiles, and the presence of specific autoantibodies.[345]

Zinc is a vital trace element involved in the functioning of approximately 300 enzymes and hormones in the human body.[67] These enzymes play critical roles in the metabolism of proteins, carbohydrates, fats, and nucleic acids. Zinc is also an essential structural component of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), with four zinc atoms incorporated into the enzyme’s structure. It stimulates ALP production in osteoblasts and plays a key role in bone formation and mineralization.[8]

MATERIAL AND METHODS

In this study, there were 20 subjects. The study procedure was explained to all the subjects, and the participants were asked to give consent. All the subjects had been informed about the procedure, and hence all the subjects had been included in the study. The subjects had been divided into two groups of 10 each. Group 1 consisted of 10 subjects who were controls. Group 2 consisted of 10 subjects who had rheumatoid arthritis. The mean salivary levels were estimated in the patients of both groups. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software.

DISCUSSION

Genetic factors play a major role in the development of rheumatoid arthritis (RA). The disease arises from a complex interaction between an individual’s genetic makeup and environmental influences. A large twin study conducted in the United Kingdom, involving 91 monozygotic (MZ) and 112 dizygotic (DZ) twin pairs, reported a concordance rate of 15% among MZ twins and 5% among DZ twins, highlighting a genetic component in RA susceptibility.[9]

Heritability estimates suggest that seropositive RA is 40–65% heritable, while seronegative RA has a heritability of approximately 20%. Specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles—namely HLA-DRB104*, HLA-DRB101*, and HLA-DRB110*—are strongly associated with increased RA risk. These alleles contain a conserved amino acid sequence, referred to as the “shared epitope” (SE), in the third hypervariable region of the DRB1 chain, which is believed to contribute to disease development.[10]

In the present study, salivary zinc levels were compared between two groups: Group 1 (10 healthy controls) and Group 2 (10 individuals with RA). The mean salivary zinc level in the RA group was higher (83.52 ± 5.9 μg/dL) than in the control group (69.56 ± 11.2 μg/dL).

Mierzecki et al.[11] assessed zinc concentrations in serum, erythrocytes, and hair among 74 RA patients and 30 healthy controls using atomic absorption spectroscopy. They found that the zinc content in hair was significantly lower in RA patients. Additionally, serum zinc levels showed a negative correlation with disease duration, whereas erythrocyte zinc levels were positively correlated with prednisone dosage. The study suggested that hair zinc levels may serve as a useful biomarker of zinc status in RA.

Zoli et al.[12] evaluated serum zinc and copper levels in 57 women with active RA and found significantly lower zinc and higher copper levels compared to controls and patients with osteoarthritis or psoriatic arthritis. No significant difference in trace element levels was observed based on rheumatoid factor status. Furthermore, serum zinc negatively correlated with inflammation markers (ESR and acute-phase proteins), while copper showed a positive correlation. Elevated cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α were inversely related to zinc and directly related to copper levels.

Grennan et al.[13] investigated serum copper, zinc, and caeruloplasmin in 49 RA patients, 33 osteoarthritis patients, and healthy controls. RA patients exhibited significantly increased copper and caeruloplasmin levels and decreased zinc levels compared to controls. While copper elevation was primarily due to caeruloplasmin-bound copper, non-caeruloplasmin copper was also elevated in RA patients. These changes were independent of age, medication, or radiographic severity, and correlated with markers of systemic inflammation such as ESR and alpha-1 globulin.

CONCLUSION

From the results of this study, it can be concluded that the mean salivary zinc levels in patients with rheumatoid arthritis were greater than those in controls.
