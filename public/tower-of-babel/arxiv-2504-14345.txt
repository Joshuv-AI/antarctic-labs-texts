Integrating LLM-Generated Views into Mean-Variance Optimization Using the Black-Litterman Model
Youngbin Lee, Yejin Kim, Suin Kim, Yongjae Lee

Abstract
Portfolio optimization faces challenges due to the sensitivity in traditional mean-variance models. The Black-Litterman model mitigates this by integrating investor views, but defining these views remains difficult. This study explores the integration of large language models (LLMs) generated views into portfolio optimization using the Black-Litterman framework. Our method leverages LLMs to estimate expected stock returns from historical prices and company metadata, incorporating uncertainty through the variance in predictions. We conduct a backtest of the LLM-optimized portfolios from June 2024 to February 2025, rebalancing biweekly using the previous two weeks of price data. As baselines, we compare against the S&P 500, an equal-weighted portfolio, and a traditional mean-variance optimized portfolio constructed using the same set of stocks. Empirical results suggest that different LLMs exhibit varying levels of predictive optimism and confidence stability, which impact portfolio performance. The source code and data are available at https://github.com/youngandbin/LLM-MVO-BLM .

Introduction

Portfolio optimization is a fundamental problem in finance, traditionally approached using mean-variance optimization
Markowitz 1952
.
However, a well-known limitation of mean-variance optimization is its sensitivity to input parameters, such as expected returns and covariance estimates.
The Black-Litterman asset allocation model
Black & Litterman 1992
was introduced to mitigate these problems by incorporating investor views into expected returns through a Bayesian framework.
Although the Black-Litterman model remains widely used in asset management industry, including Goldman Sachs, a key challenge lies in how to define and quantify these views in a systematic and reliable manner.
Recent advances in LLMs have shown promise in processing large amounts of financial data and generating insights that could potentially be used to formulate investor views
Zhao et al. 2024
and optimized portfolios
Hwang et al. 2025
.
This raises the question: can we leverage LLMs to generate structured and data-driven views that enhance portfolio construction while maintaining the robustness of the Black-Litterman framework?

Traditional investor views are based on subjective judgment, expert analysis, or historical trends

;
Kara et al. 2019
.
Some approaches use quantitative forecasting models
Feng et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2022
, but these require extensive feature engineering and assumptions.
A major limitation is the lack of an automated, scalable method to generate investor views that dynamically adapt to market conditions.

We propose a novel approach that integrates LLM-generated views into the mean-variance optimization using the Black-Litterman model.
Our approach leverages LLMs to predict expected stock returns based on historical performance and company metadata, systematically incorporating uncertainty quantification as a variance of LLM predictions into the model.
This method allows for a data-driven, automated, and scalable way to generate views, reducing human bias in portfolio construction.

Data and Methodology

2.1
Data Description

We use historical stock price data from the top 50 S&P 500 constituents by market capitalization, as of March 26, 2025, sourced from Yahoo Finance to evaluate our proposed methodology. The dataset spans from
June 2024 to February 2025
. To ensure adaptability to changing market conditions, the portfolio is rebalanced every two weeks. At the beginning of each two-week period, the LLM is provided with the past two weeks of stock price data to generate average return predictions for the following two weeks, and a new portfolio is constructed accordingly. This process is repeated iteratively, ensuring that each rebalancing is based on the most recent available data. As a result, for the evaluation of portfolio performance, we assess the outcomes of trading over an 8-month period, using portfolios constructed with views generated by the LLM.
In addition, we incorporate company metadata (detailed in Figure
1
) to improve the contextual understanding of the LLM and improve return predictions.

2.2
Selection of Large Language Models

To incorporate predictive views, we employ four models with the most recent versions available, each with a knowledge cutoff prior to the evaluation period of our dataset. This ensures that the models do not have direct exposure to future stock performance, preserving the validity of the evaluation. We used instruction-tuned models to ensure consistency in the generated return predictions.

•

•

•

Qwen-2-7B

GPT-4o-mini

GPT-4o

We used vLLM

for optimized inference. Each model was prompted to predict the average daily return for the next two weeks based on the past two weeks of stock returns and company metadata. To quantify confidence in the predictions, each model was queried 10 times per stock, and the variance of the responses was used as a measure of confidence.

2.4.1
Overview of the Black-Litterman Model

The Black-Litterman model (BLM) is a portfolio optimization framework that combines market equilibrium returns derived from the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)
Sharpe 1964
with investor views to generate more stable and intuitive asset allocations. Unlike traditional mean-variance optimization, which is highly sensitive to input parameters, the Black-Litterman model integrates external views by adjusting equilibrium returns in a Bayesian manner
Kim et al. 2024
.

In the standard Black-Litterman framework, the equilibrium return vector
\bm{\pi}
is derived from market capitalization weights and serves as a prior estimate of expected returns for each stocks. Views on specific assets, denoted by a view vector
q
and the corresponding confidence matrix
\bm{\Omega}
, are incorporated to adjust
\bm{\pi}
and compute the posterior expected returns
\bm{\mu}
, which, along with the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
of the stocks, are then used as input for portfolio optimization to get optimal weights
\mathbf{w}^{*}
for stocks
Idzorek 2007
, as detailed in Appendix
A.2
.

That is, we solve for the posterior expected returns
\bm{\mu}
from the Equation
1
:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\bm{\mu}+\bm{\varepsilon},\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{V})

(1)

\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\pi}\\
\mathbf{q}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{P}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\tau\mathbf{\Sigma}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{\Omega}\end{bmatrix}

(2)

where
\tau
is a hyperparameter for confidence in the equilibrium model, which is set to 0.025 for this study following
Walters et al. 2013
, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is the covariance matrix of stocks.

In this study, we extend the Black-Litterman model by leveraging LLM-generated predictive views instead of human-defined views. These views, extracted from multiple queries to LLMs, represent the expected average daily return for each asset and are incorporated into the model alongside their confidence levels.

2.4.2
Incorporating LLM-Generated Views

When calculating the posterior expected returns
\bm{\mu}
, we can determine the equilibrium return vector
\bm{\pi}
and the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
of the stocks from historical return data. To integrate LLM-generated predictive views into the Black-Litterman model, we now define the view expectation vector
q
, the picking matrix
\mathbf{P}
, and the confidence matrix
\bm{\Omega}
based on the LLM output. This process is described in Figure
2
.

View Vector (
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
)

indicates
k
views. For each stocks, the LLM is queried 30 times with an identical prompt, instructing it to predict the stock’s average daily return for the next period. The expected return for each stock is then defined as the mean of the 10 responses. Here,
k
represents the number of views (predictions) and the vector
\mathbf{q}
contains the expected return for each view.

Picking matrix (
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
)

specifies which of the
n
stocks correspond to each of the
k
views. Since we generate an independent expected return for every stock, we use an identity matrix. This structure ensures that each LLM-generated view directly corresponds to a single asset, rather than a linear combination of multiple stocks.

Confidence Matrix (
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
)

represents uncertainty of
k
views. To quantify the reliability of LLM-generated views, we compute the variance of the 10 responses for each stock. This variance serves as an uncertainty measure and is used to construct the diagonal elements of the confidence matrix
\bm{\Omega}
. A higher variance indicates greater uncertainty in the LLM’s predictions, resulting in a lower weight assigned to that view in the optimization process.

By incorporating these elements into the Black-Litterman framework, we adjust the market equilibrium returns to reflect the predictive insights provided by LLMs. The resulting posterior expected returns are then used in the portfolio optimization process.

3.2
Analysis of Views Distributions

Figure 4:
LLM-generated Views Over Time at Rebalancing Intervals

To further investigate how LLMs formulated asset views, we analyzed the cross-sectional distribution of the predicted daily returns that were actually used in our portfolio construction. Each LLM generated 10 return predictions (views) per stock, and we previously used the average of these 10 predictions as the final view for each stock. In this analysis, we collected those averaged views across all 50 stocks at each rebalancing point and visualized their distribution. Figure
4
displays time-series boxplots constructed from these 50 averaged views, with the y-axis clipped to a range of -2% to 2% for clearer visual comparison.

Compared to other models, BLM-Llama produced a larger number of extreme outliers in its views, particularly on the negative side. These outliers often led to portfolios heavily concentrated in a few selected stocks—a pattern observable in Figure
5
. The model consistently assigned highly negative expected returns to less promising stocks, which likely acted as a strong filtering mechanism. This decisive separation appears to have contributed positively to its superior portfolio performance.

In contrast, BLM-Gemma exhibited greater variability and a downward bias in its return forecasts. Its medians often trended below zero, and the overall spread was wider, reflecting unstable or pessimistic assessments over time.

BLM-Qwen showed relatively neutral and cautious behavior. Its median predictions were typically centered near zero with narrower dispersion than Gemma or GPT. This conservative view generation may explain the moderate but stable portfolio results of the model.

Overall, these results indicate that not only the level of expected returns, but also the sharpness, consistency, and confidence embedded in asset-level views, are critical to building effective portfolios. Models like BLM-Llama that offer both clear ranking and return magnitude differentiation are better positioned to produce superior outcomes.

4
Conclusion and Future Work

This study demonstrated the potential of integrating LLM-generated views into the Black-Litterman framework for portfolio optimization, emphasizing the ability of LLMs to provide a dynamic and automated approach for generating investor views. Our findings highlight the varying predictive strengths of different LLMs, which significantly influence portfolio performance.

Future research can build upon this work by leveraging the natural language understanding capabilities of LLMs to incorporate financial textual information—such as news articles or earnings reports—into the portfolio optimization process using the Black-Litterman model. Additionally, incorporating a validation period with systematic hyperparameter tuning may further enhance the effectiveness of LLM-enhanced Black-Litterman models.

Appendix A
Appendix

A.1
Prompt Design Details

To clarify the temporal context for the return prediction task, we include an explicit reference date in the system prompt. This date represents the point in time at which the LLM is expected to provide its analysis—positioned between the past time series data and the future return period. By anchoring the prediction task to a specific date, we help the model better interpret the directionality of the input data and generate temporally coherent forecasts.

To ensure consistent and parseable responses from the LLMs, we adopt a structured output format using the JSON schema supported by the chat completions API endpoint. This approach standardizes the outputs across all LLMs and ensures that each model provides consistently structured responses across repeated queries, reducing the likelihood of formatting errors and facilitating reliable downstream processing.

A.2
Portfolio Optimization

In this study, we implement two distinct portfolio optimization approaches: one based on the views generated by LLMs and the other using traditional mean-variance optimization as a baseline. The objective is to compare the performance of LLM-based portfolios against a benchmark portfolio optimized using conventional techniques.

1. LLM-Based Portfolio Optimization:

The first approach utilizes the posterior returns derived from the views generated by the LLMs and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
. The portfolio optimization problem is formulated as a mean-variance optimization problem, where the goal is to maximize the expected return of the portfolio while minimizing its risk.

The optimization problem is expressed as:

\min_{\mathbf{w}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{T}\Sigma\mathbf{w}-\lambda\cdot\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{r}\right)

where:

•

\mathbf{w}
is the vector of portfolio weights,

•

\mathbf{r}
is the vector of expected returns (posterior returns) from the LLM views,

•

\Sigma
is the covariance matrix of returns,

•

\lambda
is the risk aversion parameter, which determines the trade-off between return and risk.

In this formulation,
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{r}
represents the portfolio return, and
\mathbf{w}^{T}\Sigma\mathbf{w}
represents the portfolio risk (variance). The objective is to maximize portfolio return while minimizing the risk, adjusted by the risk aversion parameter
\lambda
, which we set as 0.1. The optimization is subject to the constraints:

•

The sum of the portfolio weights equals 1 (
\sum w_{i}=1
),

•

No shorting is allowed, meaning each weight is non-negative (
w_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
).

The optimal portfolio weights are obtained by minimizing the objective function.

2. Baseline Optimized Portfolio:

For comparison, a baseline portfolio is optimized using the traditional mean-variance framework, which is based on historical returns and the covariance matrix of asset returns. The mean-variance optimization problem aims to maximize the expected return while minimizing the portfolio’s risk.

The optimization problem is formulated as:

\min_{\mathbf{w}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{T}\Sigma\mathbf{w}-\lambda\cdot\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{r}\right)

where:

•

\mathbf{w}^{T}\Sigma\mathbf{w}
represents the portfolio risk (variance),

•

\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{r}
represents the portfolio return,

The portfolio risk (variance) and portfolio return are defined similarly in both optimization problems, with
\mathbf{w}^{T}\Sigma\mathbf{w}
representing risk and
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{r}
representing return. The optimization problem is subject to the same constraints:

•

The sum of the portfolio weights equals 1 (
\sum w_{i}=1
),

•

No shorting is allowed, meaning each weight is non-negative (
w_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
).

The result of this optimization provides the portfolio weights for the baseline portfolio.

Both the LLM-based portfolio and the baseline mean-variance optimized portfolio are evaluated over the same period to compare their performance. This allows us to assess how well the LLM-based views perform relative to a traditional, data-driven optimization method.
