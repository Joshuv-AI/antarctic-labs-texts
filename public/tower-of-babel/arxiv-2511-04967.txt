Hybrid action Reinforcement Learning for quantum architecture search
Jiayang Niu, Yan Wang, Ke Deng, Azadeh Alavi, Mark Sanderson, Yongli Ren

Abstract
Designing expressive yet trainable quantum circuit architectures remains a major challenge for variational quantum algorithms, where manual or heuristic designs often lead to suboptimal performance. We propose HyRLQAS ( H ybrid- A ction R einforcement L earning for Q uantum A rchitecture S earch), a unified framework that couples discrete gate placement and continuous parameter generation within a hybrid action space. Unlike existing approaches that treat structure and parameter optimization separately, HyRLQAS jointly learns circuit topology and initialization while dynamically refining previously placed gates through a reinforcement learning process. Trained in a variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) environment, the agent constructs circuits that minimize molecular ground-state energy. Experiments show that HyRLQAS achieves consistently lower energy errors and shorter circuits than both discrete-only and continuous-only baselines. Furthermore, the hybrid action space not only leads to better circuit structures but also provides favorable parameter initializations, resulting in post-optimization energy distributions with consistently lower minima. These results suggest that hybrid-action reinforcement learning provides a principled pathway toward automated, hardware-efficient quantum circuit design.

Introduction

In the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era (
1
), current quantum hardware remains constrained by limited qubit counts and significant noise. Consequently,
hybrid quantum–classical algorithms
have emerged as the dominant paradigm for achieving near-term quantum advantage. These methods rely on iterative feedback loops between a quantum processor and a classical optimizer, and their effectiveness largely depends on the design of the underlying
Parameterized Quantum Circuit
(PQC), also known as an
ansatz
. Representative examples include the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) (
27
), the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) (
8
), and various variational quantum machine learning models (
31
;
23
).

Among them, the VQE framework provides a prototypical example for illustrating how PQCs are used in practice: it estimates the ground-state energy of a molecular Hamiltonian by optimizing the parameters of a quantum circuit to minimize the expected energy

E(\bm{\theta})=\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|H|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle,

(1)

where
H
denotes the molecular Hamiltonian and
|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle
is the variational quantum state. Hence, the performance of VQE—and more generally of hybrid quantum–classical algorithms—critically depends on the structure and expressivity of the chosen PQC.

While several PQC (ansatz) families have been proposed—such as hardware-efficient, chemically inspired, and problem-specific templates—most are still designed manually or follow rigid heuristic patterns. However, such hand-crafted circuits often struggle to generalize across problem domains or adapt to hardware noise and connectivity constraints, thereby limiting scalability, expressivity, and robustness on realistic NISQ devices (
22
;
4
;
13
). These challenges underscore the need for
automated Quantum Architecture Search
(QAS), which seeks to discover PQC structures optimized for specific tasks and hardware characteristics. In this context, VQE is commonly employed as a downstream evaluation task, as its optimized energy
E(\bm{\theta})
directly reflects the effectiveness of the discovered quantum architectures.

Recent progress in Quantum Architecture Search (QAS) has explored a variety of paradigms to overcome the limitations of manually designed ansatz structures.
Among these,
evolutionary and genetic algorithms
search for effective circuit configurations through iterative mutation and selection processes (
37
;
29
;
2
). In parallel,
sampling-based strategies
(
34
;
10
), such as Monte Carlo sampling and supernet weight sharing (
39
), have been developed to reduce search complexity and enable more efficient benchmarking.
Although both paradigms have shown promising results, evolutionary methods often suffer from poor scalability as the number of parameterized gates increases (
40
;
28
), while sampling-based approaches still struggle to achieve chemically accurate results under realistic noise, limiting their practical applicability on NISQ devices (
21
).

In contrast to search-based approaches, reinforcement learning (RL) (
22
;
36
) offers a dynamic framework for QAS. In RL-based QAS (
41
), an agent sequentially places quantum gates, while an external optimizer (e.g., COBYLA, SPSA (
32
), Adam (
15
)) adjusts parameters, and the final energy serves as the reward for policy updates. Nonetheless, existing frameworks (
25
;
26
;
6
) exhibit three major issues: (i) they focus solely on learning gate placement, ignoring how gate parameters interact with circuit structure, thus failing to capture the intrinsic dependency between parameter initialization and gate topology; (ii) parameter optimization is entirely delegated to an external optimizer that restarts from scratch in every episode, preventing experience reuse and leading to redundant computation; and (iii) external optimizers themselves suffer from inherent limitations—as circuit depth increases, they often encounter barren plateaus (
22
;
18
), where gradient information vanishes and convergence becomes highly sensitive to initialization (see Fig.
1
).

To overcome these issues, we propose the
H
ybrid-
A
ction
R
einforcement
L
earning for
Q
uantum
A
rchitecture
S
earch (
HyRLQAS
) framework. HyRLQAS expands the agent’s action space to jointly learn
where
to place gates and
how
to initialize their parameters during training. For the first issue, our agent explicitly learns both gate placement and parameter initialization. Before conducting the main experiments, we initially attempted to let the agent directly learn optimal parameter values without relying on any external optimizer; however, this approach did not converge successfully (see Section
4.3
), indicating the necessity of using learned parameters as effective warm-starts rather than final optima. For the second issue, by enabling the agent to learn parameter initialization distributions, HyRLQAS reuses optimization knowledge that was previously discarded in existing works. Prior frameworks rely solely on external optimizers to fine-tune circuit parameters but never allow the agent to capture the underlying distribution of successful initializations—an inefficiency that HyRLQAS eliminates by incorporating this learning process directly into training. For the third issue, employing the learned initialization parameters significantly increases the likelihood of achieving lower post-optimization energies. In practice, circuits initialized by the learned policy consistently converge to better energy minima compared to those with random initialization, yielding more stable and reliable outcomes.

Building upon this foundation,
HyRLQAS
formulates quantum architecture search as a unified
hybrid-action
reinforcement learning problem, where discrete gate selection and continuous parameter generation are jointly optimized under a single policy. Unlike prior approaches that treat gate placement and parameter tuning as two independent processes, HyRLQAS embeds parameter generation directly into the RL loop, enabling the agent to iteratively refine initialization strategies as training progresses. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

We propose
HyRLQAS
, a hybrid-action reinforcement learning framework that unifies discrete gate placement and continuous parameter generation within a single policy, enabling the agent to jointly learn circuit structures and parameter initialization strategies during training.

•

We introduce a parameter-initialization learning mechanism in which the parameters learned by the agent act as informed warm-starts for external optimizers. This design leverages optimization experience accumulated during training and mitigates the inefficiency of restarting from random initializations in existing QAS frameworks.

•

We validate the effectiveness of HyRLQAS through extensive experiments on multiple molecular Hamiltonians, showing that its performance gains stem from both the learned initialization parameters and the hybrid learning–induced improvements in circuit structure.

Related Work

Reinforcement learning (
22
;
36
) has been increasingly explored for quantum architecture search (QAS), where task-specific reward signals are derived from variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) such as VQE, state preparation, or combinatorial optimization (
14
). In the context of VQE, (
25
) were the first to introduce curriculum learning into RL-based ansatz search, enabling agents to progressively construct circuits. Building on this line, (
26
) proposed CRLQAS, which augments curriculum learning with structured state encodings, constraints on illegal actions, and a new external optimizer to improve sample efficiency and robustness under noise. Beyond VQE, reinforcement learning (
6
;
11
) has also been applied to QAS with alternative metrics such as fidelity or task-specific costs, enabling broader applications in classification and optimization. More recently, scalability concerns have been addressed by (
16
), who proposed TensorRL-QAS that integrates tensor-network representations to compress the state space and extend applicability to larger qubit systems.

In parallel, another line of research improves scalability by structuring the search space with reusable subcircuits (gadgets or blocks). Examples include block-based ansatz construction for combinatorial optimization (
35
), gadget reinforcement learning with composite gates (
17
), and systematic discovery of Clifford gadgets to accelerate RL agents (
24
). Beyond circuit architecture search, RL has also been employed for circuit optimization. For instance, Quarl
20
leverages graph neural networks and a structured action space to guide optimization decisions, significantly outperforming classical optimizers on benchmark circuits. In addition, non-RL frameworks have been developed to reduce computational cost, such as the hardware-tested QAS of
5
and the training-free QAS of
10
, which rely on proxy-based evaluations instead of circuit training.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on efficiency-enhancing strategies for QAS, such as leveraging structured subcircuits or compressing state representations, yet most still treat gate choices and parameter settings as separate processes. By contrast, the RL literature has systematically explored hybrid action spaces, where discrete actions and continuous parameters are modeled jointly. Early work by (
9
) introduced deep reinforcement learning in parameterized action spaces, followed by
7
, who proposed a hybrid actor-critic architecture to decompose structured actions into sub-actors for more efficient training. More recently, (
19
) advanced this line by introducing Hybrid Action Representation (HyAR), which learns compact latent embeddings to capture the joint structure of discrete-continuous actions. This progression establishes a methodological foundation for hybrid action reinforcement learning, offering a natural perspective for quantum circuit search where gate selection and parameter optimization are inherently coupled.

, HyRLQAS comprises four interconnected modules. The

converts the current circuit into a tensor state directly consumable by the agent. The

3.1
Environment and Reward Definition

In this paper, the environment encapsulates the entire workflow of constructing and evaluating a parameterized quantum circuit. At each step
t
, the agent incrementally expands the circuit by selecting and placing a quantum gate. If the chosen gate is parameterized (
a_{t}^{disc}
corresponds to a parameterized gate), the agent additionally provides an initial estimate of its variational parameter
\theta_{t}
; otherwise, only the gate placement decision
a_{t}^{disc}
is required. Moreover, after placing a new gate, we refine the parameters of the previously inserted parameterized gates by applying a set of adjustment values
\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t}={\Delta\theta_{t}^{(1)},\Delta\theta_{t}^{(2)},\ldots,\Delta\theta_{t}^{(k)}}
to incorporate the effect of the new placement and its possible parameter initialization, where
k
denotes the number of parameterized gates that have been placed before step
t
.

Once a candidate circuit has been specified up to the current step, an external classical optimizer is employed to refine all variational parameters in order to minimize the target energy. The optimized parameters are then used to prepare the quantum state
|\psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle
, and the energy feedback is computed as the expectation value of the molecular Hamiltonian
H
:

E_{t}=\langle\psi(\vec{\theta})|H|\psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle.

(2)

(See Appendix
B
for details on Hamiltonian representation and expectation value computation.)

The value
E_{t}
is recorded at the end of step
t
and serves as the primary performance indicator of the constructed circuit, reflecting how close the current state is to the ground-state energy of the target molecule. To guide the agent, we design a reward function
R
based on energy feedback:

R=\begin{cases}+5,&\text{if }E_{t}<\xi,\\
-5,&\text{if }t\geq L\ \text{and}\ E_{t}\geq\xi,\\
\max\left(\dfrac{E_{t-1}-E_{t}}{E_{\text{start}}-E_{\min}},-1\right),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3)

Here,
t
denotes the current step within an episode. The threshold
\xi
is dynamically adjusted through feedback-driven curriculum learning. The hyperparameter
L
defines the upper bound of the episode length in terms of steps. The actual termination step is determined by a random halting mechanism, meaning that if the agent has not reached the energy threshold
\xi
before step
L
, the episode terminates.
E_{\min}
is a proxy lower bound that cannot be attained in practice but provides a stable normalization reference, and
E_{\text{start}}
is the initial energy at the beginning of training. The first two cases in Eq
3
correspond to terminal rewards at episode completion, while the third case, unlike previous designs
25
;
26
, provides stepwise feedback proportional to the relative energy improvement at the end of each step. Detailed descriptions of the feedback-driven curriculum learning procedure, the random halting mechanism, and the rationale behind our modification of the reward function are provided in Appendix
C.1

C.2
and
C.3
.

The Tensor-based Binary Circuit Encoding module transforms the current circuit configuration—comprising the sequence of placed gates and their associated parameters—into a tensor representation that serves as the agent’s state at each step (
26
) (see Appendix

for details).

3.2
hybrid action space

The action determines how the quantum circuit is further extended, and in our formulation it follows a hybrid action space (
7
) composed of three components:

(1) Discrete action

a_{t}^{\text{disc}}
— selecting and placing a specific type of quantum gate within the circuit;

(2) Continuous initialization action

\theta_{t}
— estimating the initial parameter of the newly introduced parameterized gate; and

(3) Continuous refinement action

\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t}=\{\Delta\theta_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
— refining the parameters of previously placed parameterized gates to incorporate the effect of the new gate placement.

In our design, the hybrid action space tightly couples discrete gate placement with continuous parameterization.
The discrete component includes three types of single-qubit rotation gates (
R_{X}
,
R_{Y}
,
R_{Z}
) and two-qubit CNOT gates, yielding a discrete action space of size
2\times{N\choose 2}+3N
for
N
qubits.
Given the current tensor-based circuit state, the policy network produces two types of outputs in parallel:
(i) a vector of logits over discrete gate candidates, and
(ii) the parameters of Gaussian distributions (mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
) associated with continuous actions.
Each discrete gate candidate corresponds to one Gaussian distribution; however, only those representing parameterized rotation gates are activated, while CNOT-related entries are masked by assigning their logits a large negative value.
In addition, the discrete logits are dynamically masked to exclude illegal operations such as redundant rotations or inverted CNOT pairs (see Appendix
C.5
).
This
mask-and-sample
procedure ensures that physically invalid and semantically redundant gate placements are removed from the candidate pool before sampling.

At each step
t
, the hybrid action
a_{t}=(a_{t}^{\text{disc}},\theta_{t},\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t})
is sampled as follows.
First, a discrete action
a_{t}^{\text{disc}}
is drawn from the masked categorical distribution over valid gate candidates.
If the selected gate is parameterized, its initial rotation angle
\theta_{t}
is sampled from the corresponding Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2})
.
The refinement head then outputs a set of Gaussian parameters for all previously placed rotation gates,
\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t}=\{\Delta\theta_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
, of which only valid rotation entries are retained while CNOT positions are masked out.

For refinement, the network conditions its prediction on three information sources: the current circuit state, an embedding of the newly selected discrete gate, and a latent vector summarizing the history of gate–parameter assignments.
This design allows parameter updates to depend jointly on the new gate decision and the accumulated parameter context, establishing a strong coupling between gate placement and parameter generation while supporting incremental refinement of earlier gates.

The overall hybrid formulation is optimized using the REINFORCE algorithm (
3
).
Compared with value-based methods such as DQN or DDQN, which assume a discrete and fixed-dimensional action space, policy-gradient approaches are more suitable for our hybrid setting that combines discrete sampling and continuous parameter generation. Among them, REINFORCE represents the simplest yet most general formulation; if this baseline policy-gradient method already yields stable training and consistent improvement, more advanced variants such as PPO or A2C can be expected to perform similarly or better.
Further architectural details, sampling strategies, and parameter-sharing schemes for the Gaussian heads are provided in Appendix
D
.

4.1
Main Experimental

The primary objective of our experiments is to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed HyRLQAS algorithm on the Quantum Architecture Search (QAS) task, and to demonstrate its superiority over existing approaches. Specifically, we benchmark HyRLQAS on the ground-state energy estimation of three molecular systems: hydrogen (H
2
), lithium hydride (LiH), and water (H
2
O). The target is to accurately approximate their ground-state energies within the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) framework. All molecules are represented in the minimal STO-3G basis, with Hamiltonians mapped to qubits using Jordan–Wigner or Parity transformations. Following prior work (
26
), we exploit problem symmetries to taper off redundant qubits, resulting in the following benchmark systems: 4-qubit H
2
(H
2
–4), 4-qubit LiH (LiH–4), 6-qubit LiH (LiH–6), and 8-qubit H
2
O (H
2
O–8). The main experiments are conducted in a noiseless setting, which reduces the confounding effects of hardware noise and allows us to more clearly evaluate the intrinsic performance of HyRLQAS.

We compare HyRLQAS against two representative reinforcement learning–based QAS baselines:

26

25

\bullet
(ii) BenchRL (DQN-rank), a recent benchmarking framework that evaluates multiple RL algorithms for QAS, among which DQN-rank is reported as the most stable and competitive across molecules and thus chosen for comparison.

4.3
Ablation Study A: Effect of each component in the HyRLQAS architecture

This ablation study aims to quantify the contribution of each architectural component within the proposed HyRLQAS framework, namely the
hybrid action space
, the
refine head
, and the
external optimizer
.
The hybrid space enables simultaneous learning of discrete gate placement and continuous parameter initialization; the refine head is designed to re-adjust existing gate parameters whenever a new gate is added, ensuring consistency in the evolved circuit; The external optimizer (e.g., COBYLA) is applied after the agent’s design phase to globally optimize all circuit parameters, refining the overall performance of the generated quantum circuit.

To assess their impact, three reduced variants of HyRLQAS were constructed by disabling one component at a time:

•

w/o Hybrid space (discrete-only):
The model learns only discrete gate placement, without the continuous parameter head. This variant isolates the contribution of continuous parameter learning.

•

w/o Refine Head:
The refine head—which, after placing a new gate and its initial parameter at each step, outputs incremental adjustments
\Delta
to
all previously placed parametric gates
—is removed. Without it, earlier parameters remain unchanged; the model only emits the initial parameter for the newly placed gate.

•

w/o External Optimizer:
The agent-generated parameters are evaluated directly without any external fine-tuning, demonstrating that global optimization (e.g., COBYLA) remains necessary for achieving optimal circuit performance even when hybrid-action learning is employed.

Each model variant was trained under the same configuration and evaluated using the retained policy on both LiH-4 and LiH-6 molecules. The best-performing circuit from each configuration was further optimized (if applicable), and the corresponding
energy estimation error
,
parameter count
,
circuit depth

gate count

As shown in Table

Removing the hybrid space, which disables continuous parameter learning, substantially increases the error. Interestingly, removing only the refine section—responsible for adjusting previous parameters after each new gate insertion—leads to an even larger degradation, underscoring the necessity of maintaining parameter consistency as the circuit evolves. Although the preceding results reveal the contribution of each component, they do not specify whether the performance gain from expanding the RL action space arises mainly from improved circuit topology exploration or from enhanced parameter initialization. To decouple these effects, we perform a second ablation study detailed below.

4.4
Ablation Study B: Effect of Circuit Design and Parameter Initialization

Building on Ablation A, we further disentangle where the performance gains of
HyRLQAS
come from when reinforcement learning operates in a hybrid action space.
Intuitively, improvements may arise from (i) learning better
circuit structures
(discrete actions) and/or (ii) learning better
parameter initializations
(continuous actions) that warm-start the classical optimizer.
We therefore design three controlled settings.
In all cases, we evaluate the policy using COBYLA and report the
best energy estimation error
on
LiH-4
and
LiH-6
.

•

B1 — Full HyRLQAS (warm-up init).

The trained HyRLQAS policy jointly learns gate placement (discrete) and parameter initialization (continuous). During evaluation, the policy is frozen; each policy-generated circuit is optimized by COBYLA starting from the agent-learned (
warm-up
) initialization.

•

B2 — HyRLQAS (zero init).

We reuse the same trained policy to generate circuits but
reset all learned continuous parameters to zero
before COBYLA.
This isolates the value of learned initialization while keeping circuit structures identical to B1.

•

B3 — w/o Hybrid Space (discrete-only).

We train a variant that removes the continuous branch, i.e., it learns only discrete gate placement.
At evaluation, COBYLA starts from zero initialization.
This isolates the value of learned circuit design alone.

Since lower energy estimation error indicates better convergence to the ground state, we report the
relative error reduction (%)
over the discrete-only baseline (B3):

\text{Error Reduction}(\%)=\frac{\mathcal{E}(\text{B3})-\mathcal{E}(\text{B}i)}{\mathcal{E}(\text{B3})}\times 100\%,

where
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
denotes the best energy estimation error. A higher percentage thus reflects greater improvement over the baseline.

The results in Table 3 demonstrate that the performance gains of HyRLQAS arise not only from the learned parameter initialization but also from the improved circuit structures enabled by hybrid-action reinforcement learning. 5
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed
HyRLQAS
, a hybrid-action reinforcement learning framework that simultaneously learns how to place single-qubit gates and initialize parameters for rotation gates in quantum circuits. This approach leads to noticeable performance improvements over traditional discrete-only search methods. Through controlled ablation experiments, we further disentangled the performance gains and found that jointly learning discrete gate placement and continuous parameter initialization not only provides better initialization for rotation gates but also enables the agent to learn more effective gate placement strategies. These findings highlight the benefit of hybrid-action learning in bridging circuit topology design and parameter-space optimization, offering a unified way to enhance variational quantum circuit performance. Nevertheless, the current framework still relies on external classical optimizers for fine-tuning circuit parameters and has not yet been validated under realistic noisy hardware conditions. Future work will focus on developing fully end-to-end optimization schemes and extending the evaluation to noise-aware and hardware-efficient quantum environments.

Appendix B
Energy Evaluation

In this work, the energy at step
t
is evaluated as the expectation value of the molecular Hamiltonian with respect to the quantum state prepared by the parameterized circuit, as defined in the main body Eq.
2
:

B.1
Hamiltonian Representation

The molecular Hamiltonian
H
is first expressed in the second-quantized form using fermionic creation and annihilation operators. To simulate it on qubits, we map the fermionic operators to qubit operators through a fermion-to-qubit encoding. Two widely used schemes in literature are:

•

Jordan–Wigner (JW) encoding
(
12
;
25
): maps each fermionic mode to a single qubit. Fermionic creation/annihilation operators are represented as strings of Pauli
Z
operators followed by a local Pauli
X
or
Y
, which ensures the correct anti-commutation relations.

•

Parity encoding
(
30
;
39
): encodes fermionic operators into qubits using cumulative parity information of occupation numbers, such that the
k
-th qubit stores the parity of the first
k
modes. Compared to JW, this mapping often shortens the Pauli strings for operators acting on higher-index orbitals, and is particularly advantageous when combined with qubit tapering techniques that exploit system symmetries.

After such a transformation, the Hamiltonian takes the form:

H=\sum_{i}h_{i}P_{i},\quad P_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n},

(4)

where each
P_{i}
is an
n
-qubit Pauli string (a tensor product of single-qubit Pauli matrices
I,X,Y,Z
) and
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are coefficients determined by the molecular system and basis set.

B.2
Expectation Value Computation

At each step, the parameterized quantum circuit prepares a variational state
\ket{\psi(\vec{\theta})}
. Expanding Eq.
2
with the Pauli decomposition of
H
, the energy can be written as:

E_{t}=\sum_{i}h_{i}\langle\psi(\vec{\theta})|P_{i}|\psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle.

(5)

In simulation, each expectation value
\langle P_{i}\rangle
can be computed exactly from the state vector. On quantum hardware, these expectation values are estimated statistically: the circuit is executed multiple times, measurement outcomes in the relevant basis are collected, and the empirical averages are used to approximate
\langle P_{i}\rangle
. The weighted sum over all Pauli terms then gives the total energy estimate.

Appendix C
Reinforcement Learning Environment Design

C.1
Random Halting of the RL Environment

Following
26
, the maximum number of steps per episode,
n_{\text{step}}
, defines the upper bound on the circuit construction process. Under the random halting (RH) scheme, the episode length
L
is drawn from a negative binomial distribution parameterized by
n_{\text{step}}
and a failure probability
p
. This stochastic cap on episode length allows variability across episodes. Furthermore, if the agent constructs a circuit whose estimated energy falls below a given threshold, the episode is terminated early, resulting in
L<n_{\text{step}}

3
. The main motivation for RH is to encourage the agent to adapt to variable-length episodes and to improve exploration efficiency by enabling the discovery of more compact circuits in early successful episodes.

C.2
Feedback-driven Curriculum Learning

Following
25
, we adopt a moving-threshold curriculum that adjusts a tolerance around an unattainable lower bound of the energy. Let
E_{\min}
denote a proxy lower bound on the ground-state energy, which cannot be attained in practice. The threshold used in the main body is

\xi\;:=\;E_{\min}+\tau,

(6)

where
\tau>0
is a dynamic tolerance. Hence the reward condition
E_{t}<\xi
is equivalent to
E_{t}-E_{\min}<\tau
.

Initialization.

We initialize
\tau\leftarrow\xi_{1}
(a hyperparameter), yielding
\xi=E_{\min}+\xi_{1}
.
We also maintain the
lowest energy
observed so far as a global quantity, initialized by the starting energy:

E_{\text{low}}\leftarrow E_{\text{start}}\quad\text{and updated at the end of every step by}\quad E_{\text{low}}\leftarrow\min\{E_{\text{low}},\,E_{t}\}.

(7)

Greedy shift (periodic recalibration).

Every
G
episodes (
G
is a hyperparameter), the tolerance is recalibrated using the current gap to the lower bound plus a small safety margin:

\tau\;\leftarrow\;\bigl|E_{\text{low}}-E_{\min}\bigr|+\delta,\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\xi\;\leftarrow\;E_{\min}+\tau,

(8)

where
\delta>0
provides slack. This periodic update typically
tightens
the threshold by pushing it closer to the best energy found so far; however, if amortization has previously over-tightened the threshold, this recalibration can
relax
it to a more achievable level.

Amortization (gradual tightening).

When the agent attains a specified number of successful episodes (success count exceeding a threshold), the tolerance is gradually tightened:

\tau\;\leftarrow\;\tau-\frac{\delta}{\kappa},\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\xi\;\leftarrow\;E_{\min}+\tau,

(9)

where
\kappa
controls the number/size of incremental reductions. Counters are reset whenever a greedy shift occurs. This step raises task difficulty smoothly, encouraging finer improvements beyond the current
E_{\text{low}}
.

Summary.

In practice,
E_{\text{low}}
is tracked globally and updated after every step, while threshold updates (both greedy shifts and amortization) occur only at the end of each episode. Greedy shifts provide periodic downward
recalibration
toward the current lowest energy, with a safety margin that can also
relax
the threshold if it was over-tightened. Amortization then
gradually tightens
the threshold between greedy shifts to promote steady refinement. Together, these mechanisms balance exploration depth and training stability without requiring prior knowledge of the true ground-state energy.

C.3
Modification of the Reward Normalization Term

In prior designs
(
25
;
26
)
, the reward increment at step
t
was computed as

R_{t}=\max\!\left(\frac{E_{t-1}-E_{t}}{E_{t-1}-E_{\min}},\,-1\right),

(10)

where
E_{t}
denotes the energy value at step
t
, and
E_{\min}
is a proxy lower bound that cannot be attained in practice but provides a stable normalization reference.
However, this formulation introduces an
asymmetry problem
when the energy fluctuates during optimization.
Since the denominator
(E_{t-1}-E_{\min})
depends on the previous step’s absolute energy level, an identical energy improvement
(E_{t-1}-E_{t})
may yield different normalized rewards depending on the magnitude of
E_{t-1}
.
Consequently, equivalent energy drops can produce inconsistent feedback when the optimization trajectory oscillates near a local minimum.

To eliminate this inconsistency, we fix the normalization scale using the initial energy at the beginning of the episode:

R_{t}=\max\!\left(\frac{E_{t-1}-E_{t}}{E_{\text{start}}-E_{\min}},\,-1\right),

(11)

where
E_{\text{start}}
is the energy value at the start of the episode.
This modification ensures that the denominator remains constant across steps, providing a stable reward range and making the relative reward solely determined by the instantaneous improvement
(E_{t-1}-E_{t})
.

Extended Case Study: Oscillation-Induced Asymmetry.

To further validate the asymmetry problem, consider two trajectories that end at the same energy
E_{T}=-0.5
but follow different paths.
Let
E_{\min}=-1.0
and
E_{\text{start}}=0.0
.
In
Case A
, the agent directly reaches
E_{T}
in one step (
0.0\!\to\!-0.5
); in
Case B
, it oscillates before converging (
0.0\!\to\!-0.4\!\to\!-0.3\!\to\!-0.5
).

Using the original normalization

R_{t}^{\text{old}}=\frac{E_{t-1}-E_{t}}{E_{t-1}-E_{\min}},

(12)

we obtain

R_{\text{total}}^{\text{old}}(\text{A})=0.5,\quad R_{\text{total}}^{\text{old}}(\text{B})=0.4-0.1667+0.2857=0.519.

Although both trajectories yield the same final energy, the oscillating path accumulates a
larger total reward
(0.519 vs. 0.5), which contradicts the intuitive expectation that equivalent end states should produce equal returns.
This bias arises because the denominator
(E_{t-1}-E_{\min})
decreases when
E_{t-1}
approaches
E_{\min}
, artificially amplifying rewards near the lower bound.

In contrast, the proposed fixed-scale normalization

R_{t}^{\text{new}}=\frac{E_{t-1}-E_{t}}{E_{\text{start}}-E_{\min}}

(13)

keeps the denominator constant, yielding
R_{\text{total}}^{\text{new}}(\text{A})=R_{\text{total}}^{\text{new}}(\text{B})=0.5
. This ensures that the cumulative reward depends solely on the net energy reduction, eliminating oscillation-induced asymmetry and providing consistent policy feedback.

Extended Case Study: Oscillation-Induced Asymmetry.

The above example illustrates that even when two trajectories converge to the same energy
E_{T}
, the oscillating path may accumulate a larger total reward due to the varying denominator
(E_{t-1}-E_{\min})
in the original normalization.
It is worth noting that this case study omits the discount factor for clarity; however, the issue persists even when discounting is introduced. In practice, when the discount factor
\gamma
is close to 1, the inflated rewards from intermediate oscillations can still lead to higher cumulative returns for oscillatory behaviors.Ideally, the cumulative reward of
Case B
should be equal to or smaller than that of
Case A
in the undiscounted setting, ensuring that transient energy rebounds do not yield excessive reinforcement signals.This property is naturally satisfied by the proposed fixed-scale normalization, which maintains consistent reward accumulation aligned with the true energy descent.

C.4
Tensor-based Binary Circuit Encoding

Figure 5:
Illustration of the Tensor-based Binary Circuit Encoding for a 4-qubit quantum circuit. The purple and orange tensors store the position and type information of CNOT gates and rotation gates, respectively, where the
Gate indicate
axis spans four dimensions for CNOTs and three dimensions for rotation gates. The blue tensors further encode the parameters of the rotation gates. The
Moments index
corresponds to the layer index in the quantum circuit, allowing a structured representation of gate placement and parameterization across different circuit layers.

Following (
26
), we encode the RL state primarily as a binary 3D tensor of size
N\times(N+3)\times n_{\text{step}}
, where the last axis indexes action slots, referred to as
moments
. A moment corresponds to a circuit layer in which multiple gates acting on disjoint qubits can be executed in parallel. At moment
\ell
, the leading
N\times N
block is a binary adjacency map for CNOT placement: entry
(i,j)=1
indicates a CNOT with control qubit
i
and target qubit
j
scheduled in moment
\ell
(zero otherwise). The next
N\times 3
block is a binary one–hot indicator for single–qubit rotations: for qubit
q
, the three columns correspond to
\mathrm{R}_{X}
,
\mathrm{R}_{Y}
, and
\mathrm{R}_{Z}
gates, with a one marking the chosen rotation.

To support parameter–aware policies, we augment the binary tensor with an auxiliary parameter tensor that stores the current rotation angles. Formally, this tensor is a parallel
N\times 3\times n_{\text{step}}
array aligned with the binary indicator block, where each entry records the angle of a placed rotation gate (zero if none is present). While not part of the binary encoding itself, this auxiliary tensor is essential for coupling with the hybrid action space described in the main body: the agent must observe previously assigned parameters in order to (i) initialize the angle of a newly introduced rotation gate, and (ii) refine the angles of already placed gates. Without this information, continuous actions would be ill-defined, as the agent would only perceive gate placements but not their parameterization.

Circuit layering follows a “local stacking” rule: when placing a rotation on qubit
q
, its moment index is set to one plus the maximum moment already used by
q
; when placing a CNOT on
(i,j)
, the moment index is one plus the maximum moment used by either qubit. This ensures consistency between structure and parameter alignment, while still allowing gates acting on disjoint qubits to share the same moment. Consequently, the effective circuit depth (occupied moments) can be smaller than the total number of gate placements. The fixed cap
n_{\text{step}}
yields a uniform tensor size across episodes, with the binary tensor encoding structural choices and the auxiliary parameter tensor providing the continuous context needed for policy updates and incremental refinement.

C.5
Illegal Actions

To prevent redundant or degenerate operations during circuit construction, the environment continuously maintains an
illegal action list
throughout the entire building process. The list is initialized as empty and dynamically updated after each action executed by the agent. At every step, it is regenerated based on the current circuit configuration—specifically, the last gate applied on each qubit wire. Two main types of illegal actions are identified:

(1) Consecutive identical rotation gates.

Applying two identical single-qubit rotation gates (e.g.,
R_{X}
,
R_{Y}
, or
R_{Z}
) consecutively on the same qubit collapses to a single rotation with a combined angle:

R_{\alpha}(\theta_{1})R_{\alpha}(\theta_{2})=R_{\alpha}(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}),\quad\alpha\in\{X,Y,Z\}.

This operation introduces no new transformation and only increases circuit depth.
Hence, if the last operation on a qubit is
R_{\alpha}
, the same rotation gate type on that qubit is added to the illegal action list for the next step.

(2) Inverted CNOT pairs.

For two-qubit operations, a
\mathrm{CNOT}(q_{c},q_{t})
gate immediately followed by its inverse
\mathrm{CNOT}(q_{t},q_{c})
cancels out the entanglement effect and provides no additional expressive power. Accordingly, when a
\mathrm{CNOT}(q_{c},q_{t})
is placed, the reversed pair
\mathrm{CNOT}(q_{t},q_{c})
is marked as illegal until another valid gate is applied on either qubit.

Implementation.

At each decision step, the agent receives the updated illegal action list from the environment. Before sampling, the policy network masks out all actions in this list from the discrete action set
\mathcal{A}_{\text{disc}}
, preventing the agent from selecting any invalid operation.
Once a valid action is executed, the illegal action list is immediately updated based on the new gate placement. This dynamic and persistent filtering mechanism ensures that the agent avoids redundant gate sequences, maintains meaningful circuit growth, and improves sample efficiency during training.

Appendix D
Hybrid Action Space Implementation Details

D.1
Network and Discrete Action Space

Given the circuit state
s_{t}
(Appendix
5
), the policy network encodes
s_{t}
through a shared backbone and outputs three heads:

[\bm{\ell}_{t},\,\bm{\mu}_{t},\,\bm{\sigma}_{t}]=f_{\theta}(s_{t}),

where
\bm{\ell}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}_{\text{disc}}|}
are logits over discrete gate candidates, and
(\bm{\mu}_{t},\bm{\sigma}_{t})
parameterize Gaussian distributions for the corresponding continuous variables.
Two binary masks are applied:
(i) a
parameter mask

m^{\text{param}}
that activates only parameterized rotation gates and suppresses CNOT entries;
(ii) an
illegal action mask

m^{\text{IA}}_{t}
derived from the environment’s illegal action list (see Appendix
C.5
).
The effective logits are
\tilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t}=\bm{\ell}_{t}+\log(m^{\text{IA}}_{t})
, where invalid entries receive large negative values.
The discrete action is then sampled as

a_{t}^{\text{disc}}\sim\text{Categorical}(\text{softmax}(\tilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t})),

with
|\mathcal{A}_{\text{disc}}|=2{N\choose 2}+3N
for an
N
-qubit system covering both
\{R_{X},R_{Y},R_{Z}\}
and all ordered CNOT pairs.

D.2
First Continuous Variable: Parameter Initialization

Let
|\mathcal{A}_{\text{disc}}|=2{N\choose 2}+3N
denote the number of discrete candidates.
The policy outputs
\bm{\mu}_{t},\bm{\sigma}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}_{\text{disc}}|}
, where the
i
-th entry parameterizes the Gaussian for candidate
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{disc}}
.
A binary
parameter mask

m^{\text{param}}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{A}_{\text{disc}}|}
activates only parameterized rotation gates (RX/RY/RZ) and deactivates CNOT entries:

m^{\text{param}}_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&a_{i}\ \text{is a rotation gate},\\
0,&a_{i}\ \text{is a CNOT}.\end{cases}

Given the sampled discrete action
a_{t}^{\text{disc}}
, initialization is defined
only if

m^{\text{param}}[a_{t}^{\text{disc}}]=1
:

\theta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\,\bm{\mu}_{t}[a_{t}^{\text{disc}}],\;\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{2}[a_{t}^{\text{disc}}]\,\bigr),

and is skipped otherwise (CNOT).

D.3
Second Continuous Variable: Parameter Refinement

To enable adaptive adjustment of prior parameters, the refinement head produces two vectors

\bm{\mu}_{\Delta},\,\bm{\sigma}_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{step}}},

where each entry corresponds to a gate placement index in the current episode (i.e., one per construction step).
At step
t
, the refinement module takes as input three components:

\mu_{\Delta},\sigma_{\Delta}=f_{\theta}^{\text{refine}}(s_{t},e(a_{t}^{\text{disc}}),h_{t}^{\text{param}}),

where
s_{t}
is the current circuit state,
e(a_{t}^{\text{disc}})
is the embedding of the newly selected discrete gate, and
h_{t}^{\text{param}}
summarizes the historical sequence of gate–parameter assignments.

A
delta mask

\delta_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{\text{step}}}
is maintained throughout circuit construction.
It records the gate type placed at each prior step, with

\delta_{t}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if step $i$ placed a rotation gate},\\
0,&\text{if step $i$ placed a CNOT gate}.\end{cases}

The mask is initialized as an all-zero vector and updated after every placement.
This dynamic record allows the agent to distinguish which prior gates are eligible for refinement.

The sampled refinement vector

\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\bm{\mu}_{\Delta},\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\sigma}_{\Delta}^{2})\bigr)

is then element-wise filtered by
\delta_{t}
, yielding

\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t}\leftarrow\delta_{t}\odot\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t},

so that only previously placed rotation gates receive nonzero adjustments.
Each nonzero entry
\Delta\theta_{t}^{(i)}
is applied to update the corresponding gate’s parameter
\theta_{i}\!\leftarrow\!\theta_{i}+\Delta\theta_{t}^{(i)}
.
This mechanism ensures that refinement operates sequentially and consistently with the temporal order of circuit construction.

D.4
State Encoding and Masking

The state
s_{t}
follows the tensor representation in Appendix
5
, consisting of binary placement maps and auxiliary parameter tensors.
Flattening this tensor provides a unified vector input for the policy.
During training, both the parameter mask
m^{\text{param}}
and the time-dependent illegal-action mask
m^{\text{IA}}_{t}
jointly ensure that (i) non-parameterized gates do not trigger continuous sampling and (ii) redundant or self-canceling operations are excluded from the candidate pool, maintaining valid and efficient circuit construction.

D.5
Policy Gradient Training

We optimize the hybrid policy using the REINFORCE algorithm
(
38
;
33
)
, which naturally accommodates the joint discrete–continuous action structure.
Let the full action at step
t
be

a_{t}=(a_{t}^{\text{disc}},\,\theta_{t},\,\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t}),

(14)

where
a_{t}^{\text{disc}}
is the selected gate type and location,
\theta_{t}
denotes the sampled initialization parameter for the newly inserted rotation gate, and
\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t}
represents the refinement vector applied to previously placed gates.

The hybrid policy is factorized as

\pi_{\phi}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})=\pi_{\phi}(a_{t}^{\text{disc}}\mid s_{t})\;\pi_{\phi}(\theta_{t}\mid s_{t},a_{t}^{\text{disc}})\;\pi_{\phi}(\Delta\bm{\theta}_{t}\mid s_{t},a_{t},\theta_{t}),

(15)

where
\phi
denotes all learnable parameters of the policy network.

The policy gradient objective is given by

\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\phi}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\,(R_{t}-b_{t})\right],

(16)

where
R_{t}
is the return-to-go from step
t
, and
b_{t}
is a baseline term (we use mean-normalization rather than a learned value function).

To encourage exploration and prevent premature convergence, an entropy regularization term is added:

\nabla_{\phi}J_{\text{total}}(\phi)=\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi)\;+\;\beta\,\nabla_{\phi}\mathbb{E}\!\left[H\!\bigl(\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\bigr)\right],

(17)

where
\beta
controls the entropy strength.

D.6
Reward Discounting

At each step
t
, the agent receives an immediate reward
r_{t}
. The return-to-go is computed as

R_{t}=\sum_{k=t}^{T}\gamma^{\,(k-t)}r_{k},

(18)

where
T
denotes the maximum number of placement steps in one episode, and
\gamma
is the per-step discount factor. Rather than fixing
\gamma
directly, we set

\gamma=\bigl(\gamma_{\text{final}}\bigr)^{1/T},

(19)

so that over a full trajectory of length
T
the cumulative discount satisfies
\gamma^{T}=\gamma_{\text{final}}
. This schedule ensures that rewards are consistently discounted across different episode lengths while preserving a target final discount factor
\gamma_{\text{final}}
.

D.7
Motivation

This hybrid design tightly couples gate placement and parameter initialization, preventing decoupled optimization that often leads to suboptimal circuits. The refinement component further enables retroactive adjustment of earlier gates, improving stability. Because of the mixed discrete–continuous structure, value-based methods (e.g., DQN) are unsuitable, whereas a pure policy-gradient method such as REINFORCE provides a natural and effective solution.
