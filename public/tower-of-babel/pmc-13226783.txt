Knee dislocation associated with patellar tendon rupture: case report and literature review
Authors: Jorge Isla, Rafael Calvo, Héctor Cifuentes, Consuelo Carrasco, Waldo González, Manuel Terraza, Hernán Salas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKnee dislocations and multiligament knee injuries represent a significant clinical and surgical challenge, particularly when associated with patellar tendon rupture, a rare combination with a high functional impact that may initially go unrecognized.Management of these injuries requires meticulous planning to restore anatomy, joint stability, and extensor mechanism function in order to optimize long-term functional outcomes.We report the case of a patient who sustained a knee dislocation associated with patellar tendon rupture, which was surgically managed using a two-stage approach.Current evidence in the literature is limited to case reports and small case series; therefore, no specific classification system or standardized management guidelines are currently available.Level of evidence: V (Case report).Keywords: Multiligament knee injury, Patellar tendon rupture, Knee dislocation, Joint instability, Multiligament reconstruction

Introduction

Multiligament knee injuries are defined as injuries involving two or more of the major stabilizing ligaments of the knee [1]. These injuries are uncommon, with an incidence of less than 10 per 10,000 persons per year, and are more frequently observed in men in their third and fourth decades of life [1]. They are closely associated with knee dislocations, approximately 50% of which reduce spontaneously before presentation to the emergency department, while the remaining cases present with obvious deformity [2].

These severe injuries account for less than 0.02% of all orthopaedic injuries [3]. Their association with patellar tendon rupture is even rarer, with a reported incidence ranging from 0.57% to 5% [4], [5].

Multiligament knee injuries are typically caused by high-energy trauma [6], except in specific populations such as obese patients, in whom low-energy mechanisms, including simple falls, may result in knee dislocation and ligamentous disruption [6], [7].

Only a limited number of cases describing concomitant patellar tendon rupture and multiligament knee injury have been reported in the literature [4]. Therefore, we present this case to highlight diagnostic challenges and surgical management considerations.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old male patient with no relevant medical history, except for obesity (body mass index 36), presented after a low-speed bicycle accident with twisting injury of the right knee caused by entrapment of the foot in the pedal. Initial evaluation at a local emergency department included plain radiographs, which showed no abnormalities, and the injury was diagnosed and treated as an acute patellar dislocation.

Three days later, due to persistent pain, functional impairment, and significant joint effusion, the patient presented to our institution. Physical examination revealed marked swelling, ecchymosis, and extensor mechanism dysfunction of the right knee, with preserved distal neurovascular status.

Radiographs (Fig. 1) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 2) were obtained. MRI demonstrated complete rupture of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL), distal medial collateral ligament (MCL) injury with pes anserinus interposition consistent with a Stener-type lesion, proximal patellar tendon rupture, partial rupture of the popliteus tendon and popliteofibular ligament, and a multidirectional tear of the lateral meniscus. Based on the mechanism of injury and imaging findings, a spontaneously reduced knee dislocation was suspected.

Initial radiographs anteroposterior and lateral view.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed bicruciate complete injury, medial collateral ligament associated with Stenner injury, and multidirectional injury external meniscus, furthermore proximal patellar tendon injury.

The patient was initially managed with a hinged knee brace, analgesics, anti-inflammatory medication, and physiotherapy, and a two-stage surgical strategy was planned.

Surgical management

The first surgical stage was performed eight days after injury and addressed the patellar tendon rupture and distal MCL injury. The MCL was reinserted and reinforced using high-strength tape suture (SuturTape) fixed to the tibia with a 5.5-mm Corkscrew® FT anchor placed 6 cm distal to the joint line. Intraoperatively, distal avulsion and interposition of the gracilis tendon were identified and repaired using an additional 5.5-mm Corkscrew® anchor.

Patellar tendon repair was performed after decortication of the inferior pole of the patella, followed by reinsertion using two 5.5-mm Corkscrew® anchors and augmentation with high-strength sutures in a Krackow configuration, restoring extensor mechanism continuity. Postoperatively, the patient was kept non-weight bearing with a ROM brace locked in extension, with gradual progression of knee flexion up to 90°.

The second surgical stage was performed four months later, once soft tissue conditions had improved and knee ROM reached 110°. Two allografts were used (Achilles tendon and posterior tibial tendon). After diagnostic arthroscopy and lateral meniscus repair using all-inside and inside-out techniques, single-bundle PCL reconstruction was performed using an Achilles tendon allograft, fixed with a titanium interference screw on the tibial side and a PEEK screw on the femoral side with the knee at 90° of flexion. ACL reconstruction was then performed using a posterior tibial tendon allograft, fixed with femoral cortical suspension and a tibial PEEK interference screw near full extension.

Intraoperative assessment confirmed satisfactory knee stability, and postoperative radiographs demonstrated adequate graft positioning (Fig. 3). Partial weight bearing with a PCL brace was initiated postoperatively, with avoidance of hamstring strengthening exercises during early rehabilitation.

Radiographs anteroposterior and lateral views, showed the postoperative results of the second surgery.

Outcome

At two-year follow-up (Fig. 4), the patient demonstrated a stable knee, full extension, flexion up to 120°, no pain or stiffness, and had returned to recreational physical activity. The patient presented good functional scores, with a SF-12 Physical Score of 53.8, SF-12 Mental Score of 48.8, and an Oxford Knee Score of 46.

Discussion

Functional outcomes following patellar tendon rupture are superior when early diagnosis and treatment are achieved; however, evidence guiding management when associated with multiligament knee injury remains limited [5], [8].

In complex cases, staged surgical management should be considered, taking into account soft tissue conditions and prioritizing restoration of the extensor mechanism. Delayed patellar tendon repair may be technically demanding due to proximal patellar migration, potentially requiring extensive releases or augmentation techniques; therefore, early repair within two weeks is recommended [9], [10].

Primary repair with suture anchors has demonstrated biomechanical properties comparable or superior to traditional transosseous techniques, with improved resistance to cyclic loading and reduced gap formation [11]. Augmentation with high-strength sutures or biological grafts may further enhance repair strength, particularly in cases of poor tissue quality [12].

Postoperative rehabilitation remains controversial, with no standardized protocols available. Most authors recommend initial immobilization to protect the patellar tendon repair, followed by gradual rehabilitation to restore joint motion and muscle strength [13], [14].

Conclusion

Patellar tendon rupture associated with multiligament knee injury is a rare but severe condition associated with poorer functional outcomes. A high index of suspicion and preoperative MRI are essential for accurate diagnosis. Early surgical management focused on extensor mechanism restoration, followed by delayed ligament reconstruction, appears to provide reliable functional outcomes. Further studies are needed to determine optimal rehabilitation strategies and to incorporate this injury pattern into existing multiligament knee injury classification systems.
