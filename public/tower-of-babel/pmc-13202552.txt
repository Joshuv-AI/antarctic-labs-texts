METTL3 Regulates the Senescence of Stem Cells From the Apical Papilla by Mediating the m6A Modification of E2F3
Authors: Cheng Sun, Yuxin Fang, Zehan Li, Wuli Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsWe aimed to investigate whether N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) methylation modification mediated by METTL3 regulates the senescence of SCAPs, and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsGain- and loss-of-function assays were performed using METTL3 overexpression and knockdown models to evaluate their effects on SCAP senescence and proliferation. To identify downstream targets of METTL3, Me-RIP (methylated RNA immunoprecipitation) high-throughput sequencing was conducted. The functional role of the METTL3-m⁶A-E2F3 axis in SCAP cell cycle progression was further validated via rescue experiments.ResultsSCAP senescence was accompanied by reduced m⁶A modification levels and downregulated METTL3 expression. Functional assays demonstrated that METTL3 overexpression attenuated SCAP senescence and enhanced proliferation, whereas METTL3 knockdown exerted the opposite effects. Me-RIP sequencing identified E2F3 as a potential downstream target gene of METTL3 in regulating SCAP senescence and proliferation. Rescue experiments confirmed that the METTL3-m⁶A-E2F3 axis modulated SCAP cell cycle progression, establishing a mechanistic link between METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification and SCAP fate.ConclusionThe METTL3-E2F3 axis serves as a critical anti-senescence mechanism in SCAPs under in vitro culture conditions.Clinical RelevanceThese findings lay the groundwork for the development of novel epigenetic therapies aimed at improving the therapeutic efficacy of SCAPs in regenerative endodontics.Keywords: N6-methyladenylate, Senescence, SCAPs, E2F3

Introduction

Apical periodontitis of young permanent teeth is a prevalent inflammatory disease in endodontics field. Current therapeutic approaches predominantly employ pulp revascularization, which can facilitate continued root development, radicular dentin deposition, and apical foramen closure.1 This process is largely attributed to the proliferation and directional differentiation of stem cells from apical papilla (SCAPs).2,3 Since in 2006, SCAPs have garnered significant interest in tissue regeneration and clinical research. Beyond sharing common characteristics with other mesenchymal stem cells, SCAPs offer an acquisition advantage due to their ease of isolation from human wisdom teeth or orthodontically extracted teeth. Previous studies have demonstrated that SCAPs possess a robust capacity for differentiation into dental and bone-like tissues.4 Furthermore, there are reports of their application in treating clinical conditions such as nerve injury and in immunotherapy.5,6 Among these potential applications, SCAPs are regarded as a principal source of stem cells for achieving regenerative endodontic therapy, owing to their anatomical location.

The extraction of SCAPs from dental papilla tissue has reached a high level of refinement. However, the quantity required for fundamental research and routine clinical applications remains substantial.7 Consequently, the in vitro expansion of these cell populations is essential. Under standard in vitro culture conditions, cells eventually enter a state of senescence due to their limited replicative capacity.8 Cellular senescence is an intrinsic physiological process that occurs over time, leading to a gradual decline in the proliferative capacity, differentiation potential, and self-renewal ability of stem cells.9,10 This decline adversely impacts the efficacy of clinical treatments. Such limitations hinder the application of SCAPs in regenerative medicine. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying cellular senescence during the in vitro cultivation of SCAPs is a critical issue that must be urgently addressed in SCAP-based stem cell therapy research.

Epigenetic inheritance represents a dynamic and reversible mechanism that modulates gene expression without inducing alterations in the DNA sequence. Recent investigations have elucidated the epigenetic modifications associated with the process of senescence.11 In contemporary biological research, N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) has emerged as a subject of significant interest. This modification involves the methylation of the nitrogen atom at the 6th position of the adenine base in RNA, a process mediated by methyltransferase enzymes.12,13 As a pivotal component of the m⁶A methyltransferase complex, the METTL3 protein has been extensively implicated in various cellular senescence processes. It performs diverse functions and collaboratively regulates the dynamic and reversible process of m⁶A methylation.14,15 The m⁶A modifications of target genes, mediated by METTL3, are recognized by downstream "reader" proteins, which can induce alterations in the stability or translation of the modified target genes and other cellular processes, ultimately influencing cellular biological characteristics.16 Notably, METTL3-mediated m6A modification has been shown to regulate the cell cycle progression and committed differentiation of dental pulp stem cells, a closely related dental MSC population.17,18 Furthermore, the regulatory roles of m6A modifications in oral tissue homeostasis and disease are increasingly recognized.19,20 However, the potential role and mechanism of METTL3-mediated m6A modification in SCAP senescence remain unexplored.

In this study, we observed a reduction in m⁶A activity and METTL3 expression associated with the senescence of SCAPs. Besides, we demonstrated that METTL3 can retard SCAP senescence during in vitro culture expansion via m⁶A modification, thereby enhancing SCAP proliferation. Subsequent findings indicate that E2F3 serves as a downstream target gene regulated by METTL3, influencing SCAP senescence and proliferation through m⁶A modification. These findings might contribute to the development of SCAP-based therapeutic strategies for dental pulp repair.

Material and methods

Cell culture

This study received ethical approval (no. T2022023) from the Ethical Committee of the Stomatological School at Anhui Medical University. SCAPs were obtained from donors aged 12-16 years requiring orthodontic treatment, with informed consent secured at Anhui Provincial Stomatological Hospital. Briefly, the tooth surface was rinsed three times with PBS. The dental apical papilla was meticulously dissected using aseptic forceps and then incised with sterile scissors. It was subsequently immersed in a solution containing 4 mg/mL trypsin (Gibco) and 3 mg/mL collagenase type I (Sigma) for 1 hour at room temperature.

Lentivirus infection

Recombinant lentiviruses targeting METTL3 (20332-2), along with a blank control (CON238), were procured from GenePharma Company. Subsequently, SCAPs were infected with lentiviruses at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5-10 in the presence of 10 µg/mL polybrene. Post-infection, the cells were cultured in a standard medium. The expression level of METTL3 was initially assessed using an inverted fluorescence microscope and further quantified by qRT-PCR.

Small interfering RNA transfection

To achieve knockdown of METTL3 and E2F3, small interfering RNA (siRNA) sourced from RiboBio (Guangzhou) was utilized. The cells were transfected with either the specific siRNA or a negative control siRNA at a working concentration of 50 nM, using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen). Following a transfection period of 72 hours, the medium was replaced with fresh medium.

Flow cytometry

To evaluate the purity of isolated SCAPs, a flow cytometry assay was employed to detect specific cell surface markers. SCAPs were harvested through trypsin digestion (Beyotime) and subsequently resuspended in 0.01 mol/L PBS twice. The cells were then incubated with primary antibodies targeting CD105, CD73, CD29, CD34, and CD45 at 4°C in the dark for 1 hour. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and analysed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

For cell cycle analysis, transfected cells were similarly obtained via trypsin digestion (Beyotime) and washed with PBS. The distribution of cell cycle phases (G0/G1, S, and G2/M) was determined using a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). This experimental procedure was conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated utilizing the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and subsequently reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) employing the PrimeScript RT Master Mix kit (Vazyme). Quantification of relative mRNA expression levels was performed using the SYBR Green quantitative PCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The primer sequences corresponding to the genes analysed in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. The relative expression of mRNA was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH employed as the internal normalization reference.

Dot blot and Western blot analysis

Total RNA isolated from SCAPs underwent denaturation at 95°C for 3 minutes. The samples were then transferred onto Hybond N+ membranes (Merck KGaA), followed by crosslinking with UV light for 1 minutes. The membrane was subsequently stained with 0.02% methylene blue to assess and normalize total RNA loading. After blocking with 5% milk for 1 hour at room temperature, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with a specific m⁶A antibody (Cell Signalling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Following membrane washing, incubation with the corresponding anti-rabbit IgG was performed for 1 hours at room temperature. Dot blotting results were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified with ImageJ software, with methylene blue staining serving as the loading control.

The primary antibodies utilized for Western blot in this study included anti-METTL3, anti-E2F3, anti-P16, anti-P21, anti-P53 (Proteintech), and anti-GAPDH (Abcam). The specific Western blotting procedures followed were consistent with those described in our previous work.21

Colony formation assay

SCAPs were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 600 cells per well. After one week, the cells were fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 30 minutes. The colonies were subsequently stained with a 0.2% crystal violet solution (Beyotime) for 15 minutes at room temperature and examined microscopically.

Cell counting Kit-8

SCAPs were seeded at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in 96-well culture plates. Following 24 hours of serum deprivation, each well was treated with 10 μL of the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent and 90 μL of α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM), and incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. Optical density (OD) values were recorded at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek). Data collection occurred on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 of culture.

MeRIP-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from SCAPs subjected to various treatments. After RNA fragmentation, immunoprecipitation was performed using the GenSeq m⁶A MeRIP kit (Genseq) by incubating A/G immunomagnetic beads with an anti-m⁶A antibody, with a non-specific IgG antibody serving as a negative control. The input RNA sample was used for normalization. The enrichment of m⁶A-modified mRNA was quantified through qPCR analysis.

Senescence associated β galactosidase staining

SCAPs were introduced into 6-well plates and subsequently fixed with a fixative for 15 minutes at room temperature. The cells were then rinsed with PBS and subjected to senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining solution (Cell Signalling Technology), followed by overnight incubation at 37°C in the absence of CO2. Images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica) in bright field mode. The number of cells exhibiting blue staining was quantified by counting at least three randomly selected fields of view per sample, and the data were expressed as the percentage of positively stained cells.

5-ethynyl-2-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

SCAPs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well and cultured under standard conditions for 24 hours. The cells were then treated with 50 mM EdU (Ribobio) for 2 hours at 37°C, followed by fixation with 4% formaldehyde for 30 minutes at room temperature. The cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 and incubated with 1 × Apollo solution for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, SCAPs were incubated with 100 µL of 1 × Hoechst-33342 solution for 30 min. Fluorescence microscopy (Leica) was used to capture images from three randomly selected fields of view per group. Cell proliferation rates were quantified and assessed by ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

SCAPs were initially fixed in a 4% formaldehyde solution for 30 min and subsequently washed with PBS. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime) for 10 minutes, followed by blocking with goat serum at 37°C for 2 hours. Thereafter, the cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting METTL3, P53, and E2F3. On the following day, the cells were exposed to the appropriate fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibody (BIGB-BIO, Beijing, China) for 1 hour at 37°C in the absence of light. Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI for 15 minutes. Visualization of the samples was carried out using a Leica inverted fluorescence microscope.

Karyotype analysis

Karyotype analysis of SCAPs, which included determining chromosome numbers and obtaining G-banded karyotypes, was performed utilizing a kit supplied by HaoGe Biotechnology Corporation Limited.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, where each data point corresponds to an independent observation. Statistical significance was assessed via Student's t-test and Chi-square test. All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) derived from three biologically independent replicates. A threshold of P < .05 was adopted to define statistical significance.

Results

METTL3 and m⁶A are negatively correlated with SCAPs senescence

We extracted dental papilla tissue from the mandibular third molar of young patients (Supplementary Figure 1A). The representative morphology of primary and third-passage cells is depicted in Supplementary Figure 1B. Flow cytometric analysis verified that the isolated SCAPs expressed characteristic mesenchymal stem cell markers, including CD105, CD29, and CD73, while lacking hematopoietic (CD34) and leukocytic (CD45) lineage markers. Furthermore, results from ALP staining, Alizarin Red S staining, Oil Red O staining, and Alcian blue staining demonstrate that the isolated cells possess the defining characteristics of SCAPs, namely multipotent differentiation potential (Supplementary Figure 1C-D).

We developed a model of natural senescence in SCAPs by isolating cells at passages 5, 10, 15, and 20. The senescence progression across these cell generations was confirmed through classical β-galactosidase staining assays (Figure 1A and B). Chromosome karyotyping analysis revealed no chromosomal abnormalities in the senescent SCAPs (Figure 1C). To explore potential alterations in m⁶A modification levels associated with senescence, we conducted m⁶A dot blot assays on SCAPs from the 5rd, 10th, 15th, and 20th generations. The results indicated a gradual decline in m⁶A levels concomitant with cellular senescence (Figure 1D). Additionally, qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that the expression levels of senescence markers P16, P21, and P53 increased progressively with SCAP senescence. Notably, the expression pattern of the m⁶A methyltransferase METTL3 exhibited an inverse trend (Figure 1E-G). These findings suggest that METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification may play a regulatory role in the senescence process of SCAPs.

METTL3 mediated m⁶A modification delays the senescence process of SCAPs

We generated SCAPs with METTL3 overexpression by employing lentiviral vector infection and assessed the efficiency of overexpression using fluorescence microscopy and qRT-PCR. For loss-of-function studies, we achieved METTL3 knockdown through the application of siRNAs and confirmed the knockdown efficiency (Supplementary Figure 2). Importantly, to investigate the potential impact of METTL3 on delaying the senescence process in SCAPs, we utilized naturally senescent cells from the 20th passage in the METTL3 overexpression experiments, while cells from the 5th passage were used in the knockdown experiments.

The m⁶A dot blot analysis revealed an upregulation of total m⁶A levels following the overexpression of METTL3, whereas the knockdown of METTL3 resulted in a decrease in m⁶A modification levels (Figure 2A and 3A). Furthermore, both mRNA and protein levels of P16, P21, and P53, were significantly diminished upon METTL3 overexpression (Figure 2B-D), with METTL3 knockdown exhibiting the opposite effect (Figure 3B-D). Additionally, as illustrated in Figure 2E, the number of SA-β-gal-positive SCAPs was markedly reduced following METTL3 overexpression. Conversely, a significant increase in SA-β-gal-positive SCAPs was observed in the METTL3 knockdown group compared to the si-NC group (Figure 3E-F). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that the expression of METTL3 (indicated by green fluorescence) was weaker, while P53 expression was stronger in the si-METTL3 group compared to the si-NC group (Figure 3G). These findings collectively suggest that METTL3 plays a role in mitigating replicative senescence in SCAPs during in vitro culture.

METTL3 mediated m⁶A modification enhances SCAPs proliferation ability

Research has demonstrated that the proliferative capacity of cells markedly declines following senescence.22,23 Consequently, we investigated whether METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification influences the proliferative potential of SCAPs. Overexpression of METTL3 led to increased cell clump formation compared to the OE-NC group (Figure 2G-H). Conversely, METTL3 knockdown inhibited colony formation (Figure 3H-I). Flow cytometry analysis revealed the proliferation index (PI = G2M + S phase) was elevated in SCAPs overexpressing METTL3 relative to the OE-NC group, whereas a reduced PI was observed in SCAPs with METTL3 knockdown compared to the si-NC group. CCK-8 assay results indicating a similar trend in SCAP proliferation capacity (Figure 2I-K and Figure 3J-L). The number of EdU-positive cells significantly rose with METTL3 overexpression compared to the OE-NC group (Figure 2L). However, the METTL3 knockdown group exhibited the opposite trend (Figure 3M). Furthermore, regardless of whether METTL3 expression in SCAPs was upregulated or downregulated, karyotype analysis revealed no abnormalities in chromosome number or structure (Figure 2M and Figure 3N).

METTL3 regulates the m⁶A modification of cell senescence- and cell cycle progression-related genes

Considering that METTL3 plays a regulatory role in the senescence process of SCAPs, we aimed to explore the impact of METTL3 overexpression on gene expression associated with this process. Initially, we analysed the m⁶A distribution patterns in OE-NC and OE-METTL3 SCAPs at the 20th generation. Our observations indicated that m⁶A was enriched within the same consensus motifs in both OE-NC and OE-METTL3 samples. The m⁶A peaks in both groups were predominantly located within the coding sequence (CDS). The overexpression of METTL3 did not alter the overall m⁶A distribution patterns in transcripts when compared to the control samples (Figure 4A and B). Moreover, bioinformatic analysis identified 1,479 and 1,564 m⁶A methylated mRNAs in the OE-NC and OE-METTL3 groups, respectively, with 644 m⁶A methylated mRNAs common to both groups (Figure 4C).

To investigate the impact of METTL3 overexpression on gene expression, we conducted RNA sequencing on OE-NC and OE-METTL3 samples at the 20th generation. Analysis of the RNA-seq data revealed that 7741 genes exhibited differential expression in the METTL3 overexpression samples compared to the NC samples, with 3887 genes (50.21%) being down-regulated and 3854 genes (49.79%) being up-regulated. Among the down-regulated genes, 559 (14.38%) were methylated by m⁶A, whereas 856 (22.21%) of the up-regulated genes were m⁶A-methylated (Figure 4D-E). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the m⁶A-tagged up-regulated genes were significantly enriched in the TGF-β signalling pathway, cell cycle, and Hippo signalling pathway (Figure 4F). Collectively, these findings suggest that altered m⁶A modification due to METTL3 overexpression affects the expression of genes associated with cell senescence and cell cycle progression.

Through the analysis of m⁶A sequencing data, we identified that the transcripts of E2F3, a gene integral to cell senescence and the cell cycle, exhibited m⁶A modifications and were upregulated in the OE-METTL3 group. Notably, utilizing a sequence-based m⁶A modification site predictor, we determined that the E2F3 gene harbours five high-confidence potential m⁶A modification sites (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp; Figure 4G). The representative Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) image demonstrated a significant increase in m⁶A abundance in E2F3 mRNAs following METTL3 overexpression (Figure 4H). To further investigate whether E2F3 is a target of METTL3, we performed gene-specific m⁶A-qPCR assays in SCAPs. As depicted in Figure 6I, the m⁶A abundance of E2F3 was markedly elevated upon METTL3 overexpression.

E2F3 regulates the senescence process and proliferation potential of SCAPs

Subsequently, we investigated the potential influence of METTL3 on the expression of E2F3 in SCAPs. Utilizing qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis, we observed a significant upregulation of E2F3 gene and protein levels in the METTL3 overexpression group compared to the control group. Conversely, E2F3 expression levels were reduced following METTL3 knockdown (Figure 5A-C). To confirm the necessity of E2F3 for m⁶A modification mediated by METTL3 in regulating the senescence and proliferation processes of SCAPs, we constructed and transfected E2F3-specific siRNA to assess its impact on the biological characteristics of SCAPs (Supplementary Figure 3).

(A-C) qRT-PCR and Western blot results showed that the expression level of E2F3 was positively regulated by METTL3. (D-F) Knockdown of E2F3 significantly increased the expression levels of p16, p21 and p53 in SCAPs. (G, H) The results of β-galactosidase staining and immunofluorescence showed that the number of senescent SCAPs increased after E2F3 knockdown. (I) The number of cell clones decreased significantly after transfection with si-E2F3. (J-M) Knockdown of E2F3 decreased the proliferation of SCAPs. These results suggest that E2F3 is essential to maintain the proliferation potential of SCAPs. *P < .05, **P < .01.

The qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that silencing E2F3 markedly elevated the expression levels of P53, P21, and P16 (Figure 5D-F). Furthermore, β-galactosidase staining revealed a significant increase in the number of senescence-positive cells in the si-E2F3 group compared to the si-NC group. Immunofluorescence staining corroborated these findings by showing enhanced expression of P53 in SCAPs following E2F3 knockdown (Figure 5G-H). Collectively, these findings suggest that E2F3 knockdown accelerates the senescence process in SCAPs. Additionally, cell cloning assays indicated a reduction in the clonogenic capacity of SCAPs upon E2F3 silencing (Figure 5I). Complementary assays, including CCK-8, EdU incorporation, and flow cytometry, further demonstrated a significant decrease in the proliferative capacity of SCAPs with E2F3 knockdown (Figure 5J-M). These results collectively underscore the critical role of E2F3 in sustaining the proliferative potential of SCAPs.

Knocking down E2F3 weakens the delaying effect of METTL3 on the senescence process of SCAPs

To further substantiate the role of E2F3 as a downstream target of m⁶A modification mediated by METTL3 in SCAPs, we conducted a rescue experiment. qRT-PCR and western blot analyses demonstrated that silencing E2F3 significantly counteracted the downregulation of P53, P21, and P16 expression levels induced by METTL3 overexpression in SCAPs (Figure 6A-C). Additionally, E2F3 knockdown led to an increase in the number of senescent SCAPs in the METTL3 overexpression group, suggesting that E2F3 functions as a downstream target gene of METTL3 in delaying SCAP senescence (Figure 6D-E). Furthermore, cloning experiments indicated that E2F3 knockdown attenuated the enhancing effect of METTL3 on the cloning capacity of SCAPs (Figure 7A and B). Flow cytometry and EdU incorporation assays corroborated that E2F3 knockdown reversed the METTL3-induced enhancement of SCAP proliferation (Figure 7C-F). Collectively, these findings imply that E2F3 is a downstream target gene involved in METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification, playing a crucial role in regulating the senescence and proliferative potential of SCAPs (Figure 7G).

Discussion

SCAPs are widely acknowledged as crucial progenitor cells in tissue engineering, attributable to their remarkable multi-lineage differentiation potential and robust proliferative capacity. Recent advancements in tissue regeneration research utilizing dental stem cells have introduced novel strategies for the clinical management of irreversible pulpitis and related conditions.24,25 Nevertheless, the initial yield of cells directly isolated from donor tissue is typically limited, necessitating in vitro expansion to achieve the substantial cell quantities required for tissue engineering applications. This expansion process is often complicated by the onset of cellular senescence during serial passaging, which adversely affects the biological properties of the stem cells.26 Consequently, there is a pressing need to identify effective targets to mitigate cell senescence, thereby enhancing the clinical applicability of stem cell therapies. m⁶A is a prevalent epigenetic modification found in mammalian RNA. The enzyme METTL3 is extensively studied as an m⁶A "writer" protein. Research has demonstrated that m⁶A modifications mediated by METTL3 play a critical role in modulating the self-renewal, differentiation, and lineage determination of dental-derived stem cells.19,27,28 Additionally, studies have confirmed that METTL3-mediated m⁶A modifications are involved in regulating the proliferative capacity of dental pulp stem cells.18 Consequently, we focus on METTL3 and m⁶A modifications as key entry points to investigate strategies for delaying the senescence of SCAPs.

In this study, we initially monitored the alterations in the expression levels of METTL3 and total m⁶A in SCAPs under normal senescence conditions. The findings indicated a negative correlation between the expression levels of METTL3 and total m⁶A and the degree of SCAP senescence. Thus, we hypothesize that METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification may contribute to delaying SCAP senescence. Subsequently, through gain- and loss-of-function experiments, we demonstrated that the overexpression of METTL3 in senescent SCAPs results in a reduction in the expression of senescence markers P16, P21, and P53, while preserving cellular proliferative capacity. Furthermore, the knockdown of METTL3 in relatively young cells accelerates the senescence process of SCAPs and impairs their proliferative ability. These processes are accompanied by alterations in m⁶A levels. These findings pave the way for developing potential strategies to improve the therapeutic efficacy of SCAPs in regenerative dentistry.

The m⁶A modification pattern is evolutionarily conserved, exhibiting enrichment in 3′ untranslated regions (3′UTRs) and proximal to stop codons across various cell types.29 In the transcriptome of SCAPs, our data reveal a comparable distribution profile and motif enrichment for m⁶A. This conservation underscores the critical role of m⁶A in diverse cellular systems. Previous research has demonstrated that METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification functions by regulating the expression of downstream genes.30 Through KEGG analysis, we identified E2F3 among the genes exhibiting upregulated m⁶A modification and expression levels following METTL3 overexpression. E2F3 is a key regulator of the cell cycle and proliferation, playing an important role in the cellular senescence of neural progenitor cells.31 We confirmed through methylated RNA immunoprecipitation, qRT-PCR, and Western blot experiments that METTL3 regulates the expression of E2F3 via m⁶A modification. Subsequent functional assays demonstrated that E2F3 knockdown in SCAPs promoted cellular senescence and inhibited cell proliferation. Finally, we validated through rescue experiments that E2F3 is an essential downstream target gene for METTL3 in regulating the proliferation process of SCAPs. While E2F3 emerged as a key downstream target through our integrated analysis, it is likely that other m6A-modified genes identified in our sequencing data contribute to the METTL3-mediated regulation of SCAP biology, warranting further exploration.

Following the m⁶A modification of the gene by METTL3, "m⁶A reader" proteins are able to identify the specific modification site, subsequently facilitating either the translation or degradation of mRNA. For instance, the insulin-like growth factor-2 binding protein (IGF2BP) family enhances mRNA translation in a manner dependent on m⁶A, thereby stabilizing the mRNA.32,33 Conversely, YTHDC1 and YTHDC2 primarily recognize m⁶A-modified mRNA within the nucleus, promoting its degradation.34,35 A limitation of this study is that the specific " reader" protein responsible for recognizing the m6A modification on E2F3 remains to be identified. Proteins such as IGF2BPs are potential candidates given their role in mRNA stabilization, but this requires experimental validation in future studies.

By elucidating the role of METTL3-mediated m6A modification in stabilizing E2F3 expression to delay cellular senescence, our research provides a novel strategy to enhance the biological performance of SCAPs. Specifically, pharmacological activation or genetic modulation of the METTL3-E2F3 axis may improve ex-vivo stem cell expansion protocols, allowing for the generation of sufficient cell numbers for tissue engineering while maintaining their regenerative potential. The targeted delivery of METTL3-overexpressing SCAPs or agents that stabilize E2F3 during pulp regeneration procedures may act synergistically with current strategies, such as bleeding induction and scaffold-based therapies, to improve tissue repair outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals a previously unrecognized function of METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification in maintaining the stemness of SCAPs through E2F3-dependent cell cycle regulation. These findings lay the groundwork for the development of novel epigenetic therapies aimed at improving the therapeutic efficacy of SCAPs in regenerative endodontics.
