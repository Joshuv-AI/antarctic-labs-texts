Analysis of biogas injera baking stove with modified circular ring burner and clay baking pan
Authors: Taha Abdella Geda, Demiss Alemu Ambie, Kamil Dino Adem, Rashmi P Shetty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional biogas stoves used for injera baking face major drawbacks, including uneven heat distribution, high fuel use, and inconsistent product quality that undermines cultural authenticity. This study designed and experimentally tested a novel biogas stove with a circular concentric burner paired with a thin clay–composite baking pan (Ø44 cm × 15 mm). Thermal tests showed the optimized pan reached its working temperature of 230 °C in just 20 min—cutting heating time by half compared to conventional pans, which required 40 min to reach 234 °C. A standardized batch process was established: each injera baked in 3 min, followed by a 2-minute idle recovery, ensuring stable temperature control within ± 5 °C. Using this cycle, a 5-injera batch was completed in 43 min, versus 63 min with conventional systems. Quality assessments confirmed authentic injera with consistent porosity (15–17 pores/in2), high flexibility (able to fold 180° without cracks), and dependable non-stick performance. Overall, the improved design delivers quicker operation, even heat distribution, and superior injera quality, offering a sustainable and culturally appropriate cooking solution for Ethiopian households.Keywords: Biogas, Circular burner, Energy-efficient, Injera baking stove, Thermal uniformitySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Ethiopia faces a pressing energy access challenge, especially in rural areas where more than 80% of households depend on traditional biomass fuels such as firewood, charcoal, and animal dung for daily cooking. This dependence is particularly evident in the preparation of injera, the national staple. Traditional griddle stoves used for this purpose have extremely low thermal efficiencies (5–10%) and require around 929 g of wood to produce just 1 kg of injera1. Beyond inefficiency, these stoves contribute to severe environmental damage—driving deforestation at an estimated 92,000 hectares per year—and expose women and children to hazardous indoor air pollutants1,2.

In response, Ethiopia’s National Clean Cooking Roadmap (2023–2035) outlines an ambitious plan to achieve universal clean cooking access by 2035, backed by USD 3 billion in financing to scale decentralized waste-to-energy solutions3. This framework underscores the strategic importance of biogas, given Ethiopia’s vast organic resource base, including manure from over 100 million tropical livestock units (TLU) and abundant agricultural residues4. With a calorific value of 20–25 MJ/m³, biogas represents a renewable energy source that can simultaneously provide clean cooking fuel and organic fertilizer5,6, while supporting the nation’s Climate Resilient Green Economy agenda7. However, realizing consistent household-level biogas access requires overcoming technical, infrastructural, and economic barriers.

High digester costs and water scarcity remain major obstacles to household-scale systems8,9. These can be mitigated by promoting modular, low-cost digesters made from fiberglass or prefabricated components7,10, and by integrating water-recycling systems or designs that minimize water use. Infrastructure gaps also pose challenges, particularly the lack of standardized appliances suited for energy-intensive and culturally specific tasks such as injera baking11. Addressing this requires co-developing efficient biogas stoves explicitly designed for Ethiopian cooking traditions.

Economic barriers compound the problem: inefficient stoves increase fuel demand, forcing households to install larger, costlier digesters, often impractical where livestock holdings or feedstock availability are limited4,10. A dual strategy is needed—first, deploying efficient appliances that reduce fuel demand and enable the use of smaller, affordable digesters12; and second, optimizing feedstock use through co-digestion of manure with crop residues or through community-scale digesters that stabilize supply across seasonal variations13,14. Together, these measures—cost-reduced digesters, culturally adapted appliances, and optimized feedstock management—can ensure reliable, year-round biogas supply, including for injera production. Such an integrated approach is essential for achieving the Roadmap’s 2035 target of universal clean cooking access.

Research into biogas stoves for injera baking spans more than three decades, with iterative efforts to resolve these challenges. Early designs, such as concentric-ring burners15, achieved only modest efficiency improvements (16%) with high fuel consumption (193 L/injera)16. Later developments, including Alemayehu’s triple-ring stove in 2012, emphasized scaling, while insulation approaches using clay–vermiculite composites showed reductions in heat loss17.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) was introduced by Mulugeta et al. (2017) to optimize burner port geometry, though field validation was absent18. More recent designs, such as Chala’s conical burner (2019) and Nega et al.’s prototype (2021), demonstrated efficiency gains of up to 25%, but faced limitations in scalability and cultural validation19,20.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remains: the absence of a scalable, high-performance biogas stove engineered specifically for the unique thermal requirements of Ethiopian mitads and validated under real cooking conditions against both technical and cultural benchmarks. To address this, the present study was designed to prototype and experimentally validate a dedicated biogas stove tailored to Ethiopia’s socio-technical context. Importantly, the intention is not to claim immediate large-scale scalability but rather to demonstrate feasibility through a controlled proof-of-concept. For this reason, a limited household sample was deliberately selected to allow detailed field observations within available resources. Within this framework, the study pursues three objectives: (1) to develop a concentric-flame burner that ensures uniform heat distribution across standardized 45-cm mitads; (2) to quantify the impact of pan size on baking performance; and (3) to assess cultural compatibility by evaluating the stove’s ability to produce authentic injera. The core innovation lies in combining concentric burner geometry with a thin-profile, reduced-mass clay pan—a design hypothesized to resolve the long-standing mismatch between biogas combustion dynamics and the demanding heat profile of injera, thereby achieving unprecedented thermal uniformity and efficiency.

Materials and methods

The biogas injera baking stove is fabricated from corrosion-resistant AISI 316 L austenitic stainless steel (1.5 mm thickness) to withstand acidic biogas condensates and is paired with a clay baking pan (Fig. 1). The structure incorporates a cylindrical base stand (1 mm thick steel) that serves as the primary air inlet, supporting a circular concentric burner assembly. This burner consists of concentric tiers spaced 70 mm apart, each fitted with 2.5 mm flame ports at 5 mm inter-port spacing and constructed from AISI 316 L steel to ensure durability. Biogas is delivered through ½-inch diameter tubes (200 mm length), feeding a concentric hollow-pipe manifold designed for even fuel distribution.

Schematic of the optimized biogas injera stove.

Above the burner, a gypsum-insulated pan seat (50 mm thickness), clad in protective steel, minimizes heat loss and provides stable thermal support. The cooking surface is a traditional fired clay mitad (440 mm diameter, 15 mm thickness), selected for its high thermal mass, chemical inertness to combustion byproducts, and cultural relevance. Its thermal properties were characterized using reported values: specific heat capacity of 0.83 kJ/kg·K and thermal conductivity of 0.5 W/m·K21. The circular burner ports are strategically positioned to align with the pan’s 440 mm perimeter, ensuring uniform heat distribution across the baking surface.

Critical thermal zones—including the combustion chamber walls and pan seat—are insulated with 50 mm gypsum layers covered by steel cladding to further reduce heat loss. A fixed 30 mm distance between the burner ports and the pan ensures efficient heat transfer, helping maintain consistent injera quality. For safety and energy efficiency, the system is enclosed during operation with an aluminium cover (440 mm diameter) fitted with a wooden handle.

To ensure reproducibility, the study provides complete technical documentation, including full CAD specifications and detailed descriptions in the supplementary material. The development of the biogas-powered injera baking stove followed a structured methodology encompassing design, fabrication, and multi-parameter performance validation. The design phase emphasized energy efficiency and temperature uniformity across the mitad surface. To support these goals, the combustion chamber was elevated to a height of 25 cm and fitted with gypsum-insulated walls to minimize thermal losses. Importantly, to preserve cultural authenticity and ensure practical applicability, the advanced prototypes were integrated with traditional clay mitads during final assembly and testing. For fuel supply, biogas was sourced from a standard anaerobic digester using animal manure as the primary substrate, reflecting typical production conditions in rural Ethiopian households.

Experimental setup and instrumentation

The design was validated through prototype construction and testing under controlled yet authentic baking conditions. Surface temperature distribution was first examined using a thermal imaging camera to verify uniform heat across the baking pan. Subsequent performance trials employed standardized protocols with a consistent batter of teff flour and water, fermented for 72 h and poured onto the mitad at 21 °C. Key baking metrics included the time from batter pour to the safed stage (identified by edge detachment) and expert evaluation of injera quality based on browning uniformity and pore structure.

Performance was assessed through multiple complementary measures. Temperature profiles were quantified using thermal imaging, while user acceptance was evaluated through a stratified multi-phase framework. Fifteen skilled operators were recruited according to strict criteria: (1) at least 10 years of experience with traditional mitad stoves, (2) current users of household biogas systems, and (3) balanced age representation (25–55 years). Each participant completed three blinded prototype trials, baking five injera per cycle with identical batter.

Product quality was analyzed both quantitatively—pore density (pores/in²) and flexibility (fold angle before cracking)22—and qualitatively through validated criteria including texture uniformity, sourness, non-stick performance, and visual appearance. Benchmarking was conducted against injera prepared on each operator’s own traditional mitad. To ensure contextual authenticity, all trials utilized naturally fermented batter, traditional stove references, and were carried out in five rural households in the Oromia Region.

The energy input to the stove is calculated from the total volume of biogas consumed during the baking process and its known lower heating value (LHV). This requires an accurate measurement of the biogas flow rate, which is achieved indirectly using a U-tube water manometer. This device measures gas pressure by connecting one end of a U-shaped tube to the biogas supply line while the other end remains open to the atmosphere. The pressure of the gas forces the water column on its side down, causing the water on the atmospheric side to rise. The difference in height between these two water columns, measured in millimeters of water column (mmWC), provides a direct and reliable reading of the gas pressure within the line.

To convert this pressure measurement into a flow rate, a calibrated restriction, such as an orifice plate, is installed in the pipe. This plate creates a deliberate pressure drop as the gas flows through it. The manometer is then connected to points before and after this restriction to measure this pressure difference. The resulting height difference (Δh) read from the manometer is directly proportional to the square of the volumetric flow rate. For a specifically calibrated system, this relationship allows for the direct conversion of the manometer reading into a flow rate value, typically in liters per minute.

By recording this derived flow rate at regular intervals throughout the injera baking cycle, an average flow rate can be determined. The total volume of biogas consumed is then simply the product of this average flow rate and the total baking time. Finally, the total energy input to the stove is calculated by multiplying this total biogas volume by the calorific value of the biogas, providing a complete measurement of the energy derived from the biogas during the process.

Baking time and heating time

The characterization of baking and idle times was conducted through a direct, stopwatch-based method during the stove’s cyclic operation. The process began with the initial heating phase, where timing commenced immediately upon ignition of the biogas, and the pan’s temperature was recorded at set intervals until it first reached the target working temperature of approximately 230 °C. Once this stable operating state was achieved, the cyclic baking process was measured. For each individual cycle, the baking time was recorded by starting a stopwatch at the precise moment the batter was poured into the pan and stopping it the instant the finished product was removed. Following this, the idle time was measured by starting the stopwatch again immediately after the pan was emptied. This timer was stopped only when the pan’s temperature, having dipped after cooling, had fully recovered to the target working temperature, signaling it was ready for the next batter pour. This meticulous method of using a watch for the initial heating and a stopwatch for every subsequent action—pour, removal, and temperature recovery—provided the precise, hands-on data for the baking and idle time metrics presented in the study.

Result and discussion

Thermal efficiency and pan optimization

Based on the experimental data, the optimized biogas stove with a 15 mm thick, 440 mm diameter pan demonstrates significant time savings across all operational phases compared to the conventional 25 mm thick, 520 mm pan .The most dramatic improvement occurs during the initial heating phase, where the optimized pan reaches the target baking temperature of ~ 230 °C in just 20 min – a 50% reduction compared to the 40 min required by the conventional pan (Fig. 2). This acceleration aligns with Fourier’s Law, as the reduced thickness significantly lowers thermal resistance and inertia, enabling faster heat transfer.

Pan heating temperature vs. Pan thickness highlighting optimal baking time and temperature.

Once operational temperature is achieved, both pans exhibit a similar baking cycle pattern, alternating between high-temperature baking phases (~ 230 °C) and low-temperature idle/cooling/reloading phases (~ 120 °C), as documented in (Table 4). Each individual injera requires approximately 3 min of baking time at high temperature, followed by 2 min of idle time for cooling, batter pouring, and reloading.

However, the substantial reduction in preheating time with the optimized pan translates directly into major gains in batch processing efficiency. For a standardized batch of 5 injera, the optimized system completes the entire process in 43 min total time. In contrast, the conventional pan requires 63 min total time for the same 5-injera batch.

This efficiency gain stems almost entirely from the optimized pan geometry (thinner walls, smaller diameter) minimizing thermal inertia and enabling rapid preheating, while the monolithic reduced-clay construction ensures structural integrity at operating temperatures. The baking and idle times per injera remain consistent, but the drastically reduced time-to-first-bake unlocks superior overall productivity.

Early biogas stove designs for injera baking faced significant limitations. Alemayehu’s 2012 communal stove, suffered from prolonged heating times and cold-edge defects exacerbated by its large 530 mm diameter pan. Subsequent research, such as Chala’s study, attempted optimizations using a smaller 400 mm pan to improve thermal response. While reduced baking time to 4 min per injera and reheating intervals to 4 min, the 25-minute initial heating time remained suboptimal, and the smaller size introduced spatial constraints for batter spreading.

The current study introduces a comprehensively optimized system. The key advancement lies in its 440 mm diameter pan—a strategic balance that mitigates the cold-edge issues of Alemayehu’s oversized pan while avoiding the spatial limitations of Chala’s undersized design20. Combined with a reduced thickness of 15 mm (versus the conventional 25 mm), this geometry drastically lowers thermal resistance and inertia, aligning with Fourier’s Law.

Once operational, the optimized stove demonstrates superior cyclic performance. Baking time per injera is reduced to 3 min (from Chala’s 4 min), and the reheating/cooling/ interval is similarly reduced to 2 min (from 4 min). Thermal imaging confirms exceptional heat uniformity across the entire 44 cm pan surface, maintaining temperature variations within ± 5 °C.

A performance comparison between the optimized and conventional biogas pans is summarized in (Table 5). The optimized pan demonstrated significant efficiency improvements, reducing the heat-up time in half (from 40 to 20 min). Both systems operated at a similar working temperature (230 vs. 234 °C), confirming that the performance gains were achieved without sacrificing operational capability.

Injera quality

The optimized biogas injera stove consistently produced high-quality injera characterized by uniform thickness (2.0 ± 0.2 mm), optimal porosity (15–17 pores/in²)27, and exceptional flexibility, as demonstrated by 180° folding tests without structural failure (Fig. 3). These parameters align with established culinary standards across Ethiopia and the Horn of Africa28,29. Heat distribution

Experimental validation of the optimized 44 cm diameter baking pan demonstrated exceptional thermal uniformity, with spatial temperature variations of < 5 °C across its surface (Fig. 4), a transformative improvement over the > 55 °C gradients observed in Alemayehu’s (2012) 53 cm pan17. This near-isothermal performance—achieved through synergistic geometric optimization (reduced diameter minimizing radial heat dissipation) and material selection (15 mm monolithic clay enhancing transverse conduction)—eliminated cold-edge defects while enabling rapid thermal stabilization.

Conclusion

This study successfully designed, manufactured, and experimentally validated a novel biogas-fired injera baking stove that resolves critical heat distribution challenges inherent in conventional systems. By integrating an optimized circular burner with strategically positioned concentric ports and a thin-profile clay baking pan, the stove achieved exceptional thermal uniformity (± 5 °C variation) across the cooking surface. This design eliminated risks of flame lift and backflash while enabling rapid baking cycles of 3 min per injera with 2-minute idle intervals between batches.

Field validation confirmed the system’s capability to produce culturally authentic injera, exhibiting uniform peripheral “eyes” (15–17 pores/in²), ideal flexibility, and non-stick release—matching market benchmarks in texture, taste, and appearance. Critically, the stove’s high efficiency directly mitigates the primary economic barrier to biogas adoption by drastically reducing biogas demand per cooking task, which enables the use of smaller, more affordable digesters and brings systems within economic reach of more households. However, the stove’s benefit is contingent on a reliable biogas supply. Therefore, achieving the roadmap’s goals require an integrated approach that simultaneously addresses upstream barriers. The viability of the fuel supply itself can be enhanced by deploying cost-reduced digester designs supported by financial mechanisms, optimizing feedstock through co-digestion of manure with crop residues to maximize yield and mitigate seasonal fluctuations, and alleviating water scarcity through water-efficient digester designs and greywater recycling. This holistic strategy—pairing culturally adapted, efficient appliances with optimized resource management and cost-effective digester systems—is fundamental to securing a viable biogas ecosystem for Ethiopian households.

Looking forward, this research establishes a platform for innovation. Future work should prioritize adaptive, AI-driven combustion control systems to dynamically optimize the biogas-air ratio, further reducing fuel consumption. These advancements—spanning smart algorithms, advanced materials, and circular economy integration—will transform the stove from a passive appliance into an intelligent node within a sustainable energy-food ecosystem. This integrated vision serves as a vital catalyst for scalable, equitable, and culturally resonant clean cooking solutions in Ethiopia and beyond.
