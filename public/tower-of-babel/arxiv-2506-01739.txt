On the quadratic 8-edge case of the Brown–Erdős–Sós problem
Oleg Pikhurko, Shumin Sun

Abstract
Let f ( r ) ​ ( n , s , k ) f^{(r)}(n;s,k) be the maximum number of edges in an n n -vertex r r -uniform hypergraph containing no k k edges on at most s s vertices. Brown, Erdős and Sós conjectured in 1973 that the limit lim n → ∞ n − 2 ​ f ( 3 ) ​ ( n , k + 2 , k ) \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-2}f^{(3)}(n;k+2,k) exists for all k k . Recently, Delcourt and Postle settled the conjecture and their approach was generalised by Shangguan to every uniformity r ≥ 4 r\geq 4 : the limit lim n → ∞ n − 2 ​ f ( r ) ​ ( n , r ​ k − 2 ​ k + 2 , k ) \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-2}f^{(r)}(n;rk-2k+2,k) exists for all r ≥ 3 r\geq 3 and k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 . The value of the limit is currently known for k ∈ { 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 } k\in\{2,3,4,5,6,7\} due to various results authored by Glock, Joos, Kim, Kühn, Lichev, Pikhurko, Rödl and Sun. In this paper we look at the case k = 8 k=8 , determining the value of the limit for each r ≥ 4 r\geq 4 and presenting a lower bound for k = 3 k=3 that we conjecture to be sharp.

Introduction

For an integer
r\geq 2
, an
r
-uniform hypergraph
(in short,
r
-graph
)
H
consists of a vertex set
V(H)
and an edge set
E(H)\subseteq\binom{V(H)}{r}
, that is, every edge is an
r
-element subset of
V(H)
. Given a family
\mathcal{F}
of
r
-graphs, the
Turán number
of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted by
\mathrm{ex}(n;\mathcal{F})
, is defined as the maximum number of edges in an
n
-vertex
r
-graph containing no element of
\mathcal{F}
as a subgraph.

In this note, we focus on the family
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}(s,k)
, consisting of all
r
-graphs with
k
edges and at most
s
vertices. Brown, Erdős and Sós
[
2
]
initiated the systematic investigation of the function

f^{(r)}(n;s,k):=\mathrm{ex}(n;\mathcal{F}^{(r)}(s,k)).

They showed that

\Omega(n^{(rk-s)/(k-1)})=f^{(r)}(n;s,k)=O(n^{\lceil(rk-s)/(k-1)\rceil}).

If
t:=(rk-s)/(k-1)
is an interger, i.e.
s=rk-tk+t
, then
f^{(r)}(n;rk-tk+t,k)=\Theta(n^{t})
. In the sequel, we are mainly interested in the case when
t=2
; thus
s=rk-2k+2
and the magnitude of the function is
\Theta(n^{2})
. A natural question here is whether the limit

\pi(r,k):=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-2}f^{(r)}(n;rk-2k+2,k)

exists, which was originally conjectured by Brown, Erdős and Sós for
r=3
.

In their initial paper
[
2
]
, Brown, Erdős and Sós confirmed the conjecture for
k=2
by observing that
\pi(3,2)=1/6
.
Many years later, Glock
[
9
]
solved the case
k=3
and showed that
\pi(3,3)=1/5
. In a recent work, Glock, Joos, Kim, Kühn, Lichev and Pikhurko
[
6
]
proved the case
k=4
by showing that
\pi(3,4)=7/36
. Building on their work, Delcourt and Postle
[
3
]
finally resolved the Brown–Erdős–Sós conjecture, namely,
\pi(3,k)
exists for any
k\geq 2
, without determining its value.

For more general case
\pi(r,k)
with uniformity
r\geq 4
, the existence of limit was shortly confirmed by Shangguan
[
12
]
, following the approach of Delcourt and Postle (without explicit value). The natural remaining question is to determine the limiting values. Recently, a range of results regarding this direction has been established. Apart from values for
r=3
mentioned above, the celebrated work of Rödl
[
11
]
on the existence of approximate Steiner systems (under the condition
t=2
) implies that
\pi(r,2)=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r}
for every
r\geq 4
. Moreover, Glock, Joos, Kim, Kühn, Lichev and Pikhurko
[
6
]
proved that for every
r\geq 4
,

\pi(r,3)=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(r,4)=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r}.

Very recently, Glock, Kim, Lichev, Pikhurko and Sun
[
8
]
obtained the limiting value for
k\in\{5,7\}
, which is same as
k=3
.

\pi(r,5)=\pi(r,7)=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r-1}\quad\text{for every $r\geq 3$.}

They also resolved the case
k=6
, however the value behaves differently when
r=3
and
r\geq 4
as follows:

\pi(3,6)=\frac{61}{330}\quad\text{and\quad$\pi(r,6)=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r}$ for every $r\geq 4$.}

Meanwhile, Letzter and Sgueglia
[
10
]
provided the exact value

\pi(r,k)=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r}

(1)

for even integer
k
and
r\geq r_{0}(k)
sufficiently large. In their paper, they asked for the smallest
r
such that (
1
) holds.

In this note, we determine the limit for
k=8
and
r\geq 4
as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
r\geq 4
, we have
\pi(r,8)=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r}.

Moreover, we provide a lower bound for
r=3
, which implies that
r=4
is the smallest uniformity such that
\pi(r,8)=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

\pi(3,8)\geq\frac{3}{16}
.

We conjecture that this lower bound is sharp.

Conjecture 1.3
.

\pi(3,8)=\frac{3}{16}
.

A connection to generalised Ramsey numbers.

In a recent work, Bennett, Cushman and Dudek
[
1
]
found a connection between the Brown–Erdős–Sós function for
4
-graphs and generalised Ramsey numbers, that were introduced by Erdős and Shelah
[
5
]
and were first systematically studied by Erdős and Gyárfás
[
4
]
.

For integers
p,q
such that
p\geq 3
and
2\leq q\leq\tbinom{p}{2}
, a
(p,q)
-colouring
of
K_{n}

is a colouring of the edges of
K_{n}
such that every clique of size
p
receices at least
q
colours. The
generalised Ramsey number

\mathrm{GR}(n,p,q)
is the minimum number of colours such that
K_{n}
has a
(p,q)
-colouring.

Erdős and Gyárfás
[
4
]
proved among other results that for arbitrary
p\geq 3
and
q_{\rm{quad}}:=\tbinom{p}{2}-\lfloor p/2\rfloor+2
, it holds that

\mathrm{GR}(n,p,q_{\rm{quad}})=\Omega(n^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{GR}(n,p,q_{\rm{quad}}-1)=o(n^{2}).

Thus
q_{\rm{quad}}
is the threshold for quadratic growth.

Bennett, Cushman and Dudek
[
1
]
showed the following connection between generalised Ramsey numbers and the Brown–Erdős–Sós function.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
1
]
*Theorem 3)
.

For all even
p\geq 6
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{GR}(n,p,q_{\rm{quad}})}{n^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}-\pi(4,\frac{p}{2}-1).

In particular, the limit on the left exists by
[
12
]
.

From Theorem
1.1
, together with Theorem
1.4
, we directly obtain the following asymptotic value of
\mathrm{GR}(n,18,146)
.

Theorem 1.5
.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{GR}(n,18,146)}{n^{2}}=\frac{5}{12}.\qed

and
w(uv)=w_{F_{1}}(uv)=1\leq 1
.

If
w_{F_{1}}(uv)=1/3
(which implies
2\in C_{F_{1}}(uv)
), then by Lemma
4.2
, the number of
2
-clusters
F\in\mathcal{M}_{2}\setminus F_{1}
with
2\in C_{F}(uv)
is at most
2
. Thus
s\leq 3
and
w(uv)\leq 3\cdot w_{F_{1}}(uv)\leq 1
.
∎

Now, it is sufficient to verify (
12
) for every
F\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
.

Claim 4.11
.

Let
r\geq 5
. For all
F\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
, we have
w(F)\geq\binom{r}{2}|F|
.

Proof.

Suppose that
F
is obtained by merging
m
1-clusters
T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
with
|T_{i}|=e_{i}
. Then by Lemma
4.7
, we have

|F|=\sum_{i\in[m]}e_{i},\ |P_{1}(F)|=\sum_{i\in[m]}\bigg(e_{i}\binom{r}{2}-e_{i}+1\bigg)\ {\ \text{and}\ }|P_{\,\overline{1}2}(F)|\geq 1-m+\sum_{i\in[m]}(e_{i}-1)(r-2)^{2}.

Recall that

w(F):=\sum_{xy\in\binom{V(F)}{2}}w_{F}(xy)=|P_{1}(F)|+\frac{1}{3}|P_{\,\overline{1}2}(F)|.

By routine calculations, we have from the above that

2w(F)-r(r-1)|F|\geq\frac{2}{3}(r^{2}-4r+1)\cdot(|F|-m)-\frac{2}{3}m+\frac{2}{3}

(13)

To get the right hand side of (
13
) is at least
0
, it suffices to verify the following:

|F|\geq m+\frac{m-1}{r^{2}-4r+1}.

(14)

Note that
|F|\geq m+1
, since there must exist an
i
-tree in the sequence with
i\geq 2
according to the merging rule. Hence given that
r\geq 5
, if
m\leq 7
the we derive that

|F|\geq m+1\geq m+\frac{m-1}{6}\geq m+\frac{m-1}{r^{2}-4r+1}.

On the other hand, if
m\geq 8
, then
|F|\geq 9
. By Lemma
4.7
, we conclude that
F\in\mathcal{S}_{m-1}
and
m\leq 3\binom{r}{2}+1
(as any other family has
m<8
). Thus,

|F|=2(m-1)+1\geq m+\frac{m-1}{r^{2}-4r+1}.

This finishes the proof of the upper bound for
r\geq 5
.
∎

4.2.2
The upper bound of Theorem
1.1
for
r=4

For a
2
-cluster
F
and
uv\in\binom{V(G)}{2}
, we define the following functions
h_{i}^{F}
:

h_{1}^{F}(uv):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{ if }1\in C_{F}(uv),\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.
,
h_{2}^{F}(uv):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1/3&\text{ if }2\in C_{F}(uv),\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.

h_{3}^{F}(uv):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1/2&\text{ if }2,4\in C_{F}(uv),\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.
,
h_{4}^{F}(uv):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1/2&\text{ if }3,4,5\in C_{F}(uv)\text{ and }uv\notin P_{1}(G),\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.

h_{5}^{F}(uv):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{ if }3,5,6\in C_{F}(uv)\text{ and }uv\notin P_{1}(G),\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.

We assign the weight

w_{F}(uv):=\max_{1\leq i\leq 5}\ h_{i}^{F}(uv).

Claim 4.12
.

For every
uv\in\binom{V(G)}{2}
, it holds that
w(uv)\leq 1
.

Proof.

Given
uv\in\binom{V(G)}{2}
, let
F_{1},\dots,F_{s}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
be all
2
-clusters assigning positive weight to
uv
ordered so that
w_{F_{1}}(uv)\geq w_{F_{2}}(uv)\geq\cdots\geq w_{F_{s}}(uv)
. In the following proof, let
h_{i}
mean
h_{i}^{F_{1}}
, the function coming from the 2-cluster
F_{1}
(that assigns the maximum weight to
uv
).

Case 1.
w_{F_{1}}(uv)=1.

There exists some
i\in\{1,5\}
such that
w_{F_{1}}(uv)=h_{i}(uv)=1
.

If
w_{F_{1}}(uv)=h_{1}(uv)=1
, then
1\in C_{F_{1}}(uv)
, which means that
uv\in P_{1}(G)
. Thus, given an arbitrary
2
-cluster
F\in\mathcal{M}_{2}\setminus F_{1}
, it holds that
h^{F}_{4}(uv)=h^{F}_{5}(uv)=0
. Note that
2\notin C_{F}(uv)
, since otherwise
F_{1}
would merge with
F
by our merging rule for
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Thus
h^{F}_{2}(uv)=h^{F}_{3}(uv)=0
. Analogously, by the merging rule for
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, we have
1\notin C_{F}(uv)
and
h^{F}_{1}(uv)=0
. Hence,
w_{F}(uv)=0
and
w(uv)=w_{F_{1}}(uv)=1
.

If
w_{F_{1}}(uv)=h_{5}(uv)=1
, then
3,5,6\in C_{F_{1}}(uv)
and
uv\notin P_{1}(G)
. For every other
2
-cluster
F\in\mathcal{M}_{2}\setminus F_{1}
, by Lemma
4.2
, we know
2,3\notin C_{F}(uv)
and thus
h^{F}_{2}(uv)=h^{F}_{3}(uv)=h^{F}_{4}(uv)=h^{F}_{5}(uv)=0
. Since
uv\notin P_{1}(G)
,
h^{F}_{1}(uv)=0
as well. In total, we have
w_{F}(uv)=0
and
w(uv)=w_{F_{1}}(uv)=1
.

Case 2.
w_{F_{1}}(uv)=1/2.

In this case, we know
w_{F_{1}}(uv)\in\{h_{3}(uv),h_{4}(uv)\}
. This means
4\in C_{F_{1}}(uv)
. For every other
2
-cluster
F\in\mathcal{M}_{2}\setminus F_{1}
, by Lemma
4.2
, we derive
4\notin C_{F}(uv)
, and thus
w_{F}(uv)\leq h^{F}_{2}(uv)=1/3
. Again by Lemma
4.2
, there is at most one
2
-cluster of
\mathcal{M}_{2}\setminus F_{1}
, say
F_{2}
, such that
2\in C_{F_{2}}(uv)
and
w_{F_{2}}(uv)=h^{F_{2}}_{2}(uv)=1/3
. Therefore,
w(uv)\leq w_{F_{1}}(uv)+w_{F_{2}}(uv)\leq 1/2+1/3\leq 1
.

Case 3.
w_{F_{1}}(uv)=1/3.

In this case, we know
w_{F_{1}}(uv)=h_{2}(uv)
which implies
2\in C_{F_{1}}(uv)
. By Lemma
4.2
, there are at most two
2
-clusters in
\mathcal{M}_{2}\setminus F_{1}
that
2
-claim the pair
uv
. Each of these clusters (if exists) contributes at most
1/3
while all other clusters do not contribute anything to the weight of
uv
. Thus
w(uv)\leq w_{F_{1}}(uv)+2/3\leq 1
.
∎

Claim 4.13
.

Let
r=4
. For all
F\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
, we have
w(F)\geq 6|F|
.

Proof.

Let
h_{i}
refer to
h_{i}^{F}
.
Assume that
F
is obtained by merging
m
1-clusters
T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
with
|T_{i}|=e_{i}
. Let us first focus on
1
-claimed pairs
P_{1}(F)
and
\overline{1}2
-claimed pairs
P_{\,\overline{1}2}(F)
. Each pair of
P_{1}(F)
will contribute the weight
1
(by the definition of
h_{1}
), and each pair of
P_{\,\overline{1}2}(F)
contribute at least weight
1/3
(by the definition of
h_{2}
). We start with figuring out those
2
-clusters
F
such that the weights contributed by
P_{1}(F)\cup P_{\,\overline{1}2}(F)
is sufficient, i.e.
w(F)\geq|P_{1}(F)|+|P_{\,\overline{1}2}(F)|/3\geq 6|F|
.

By Lemma
4.7
, we have

|F|=\sum_{i\in[m]}e_{i},\ |P_{1}(F)|=\sum_{i\in[m]}(5e_{i}+1)\ {\ \text{and}\ }|P_{\,\overline{1}2}(F)|\geq 1-m+4\sum_{i\in[m]}(e_{i}-1).

Then,

w(F):=\sum_{xy\in\binom{V(F)}{2}}w_{F}(xy)\geq|P_{1}(F)|+\frac{1}{3}|P_{\,\overline{1}2}(F)|\geq\frac{19}{3}|F|-\frac{2}{3}m+\frac{1}{3}.

(15)

Thus if
|F|\geq 2m-1
, then (
15
) is at least
6|F|
.

Now it is sufficient to prove the case when
|F|<2m-1
. We split the remaining discussion depending on the value of
m
.

Case 1.

m\leq 2.

This case is impossible, as we would have

|F|\geq m+1\geq 2m-1.

Case 2.

m=3.

We know
4=m+1\leq|F|<2m-1=5
. Thus
|F|=4
and
F
has a composition
(2,1,1)
where a diamond
D\subseteq F

\overline{1}2
-claims two pairs
x_{1}y_{1},x_{2}y_{2}
through which two
1
-trees merge to
D
. These pairs are distinct as otherwise the two 1-trees would be in the same 1-cluster.
Also, note that
2,4\in C_{F}(x_{1}y_{1})\cap C_{F}(x_{2}y_{2})
. Thus
w_{F}(x_{i}y_{i})\geq h_{3}(x_{i}y_{i})=1/2
, and

w(F)\geq\sum_{xy\in P_{1}(F)}w_{F}(xy)+w_{F}(x_{1}y_{1})+w_{F}(x_{2}y_{2})\geq 23+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}=24=6\times 4.

Case 3.

m=4.

In this case, we have
5=m+1\leq|F|<2m-1=7
. We split the proof into two cases depending on the size of
F
.

First, suppose that
|F|=5
. Then
F
possesses a composition
(2,1,1,1)
. Consider an arbitrary
1
-tree
T
and the diamond
D
(
\overline{1}2
-claiming a pair in
T
) in the sequence. It is easy to see that
|P_{\,\overline{12}3}(F)|\geq|P_{\,\overline{12}3}(D\cup T)|=8
. By
8
-freeness, at most 2 pairs of

lie in
P_{1}(G)
. Therefore, the remaining pairs

satisfies that

3,4,5\in C_{F}(uv)
and
w_{F}(uv)\geq h_{4}(uv)=1/2
. Hence, we get

w(F)\geq\sum_{xy\in P_{1}(F)}w_{F}(xy)+\sum_{xy\in P_{\,\overline{12}3}(D\cup T)\setminus P_{1}(G)}w_{F}(xy)\geq 29+(8-2)\times\frac{1}{2}=32>6\times 5,

as desired.

Thus it remains to consider the case
|F|=6
. Then the non-increasing composition of
F
is
(2,2,1,1)
or
(3,1,1,1)
.

Let us show that there is at least one flexible
1
-tree
T_{0}
in

. Indeed, for
(2,2,1,1)
, re-order the sequence starting from a

-tree

, then the two diamonds
(2|1)
-merge with

Let

1
-tree in

be a diamond
2
-claiming

. At most one pair of

-freeness. Moreover, since
F
has a flexible edge (namely, the one in

), each pair

of
P_{\,\overline{12}3}(D\cup T)\setminus P_{1}(G)
satisfies
5,6\in C_{F}(uv)

Case 4.

m=5.

In this case, we have
6=m+1\leq|F|<2m-1=9
. By
8
-freeness,
|F|\neq 8
.

First suppose that
|F|=6
. Its non-increasing composition must be

-tree

with

2
-claiming a pair in
