Optimal conditions for detecting optical dichroism at the nanoscale by electron energy-loss spectroscopy
Marek Zálešák, Martin Ošmera, Martin Hrtoň, Andrea Konečná

Abstract
The emergence of optical circular dichroism in chiral nanoscale and molecular systems provides not only a way for analyzing the sample chirality itself but also additional degrees of freedom in manipulating light. Such manipulation can be reached even at the nanoscale level; however, probing and understanding the properties of optical fields well below the diffraction limit requires an adequate technique. Electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) with orbital angular momentum (OAM)-based electron state sorting has been suggested as a suitable candidate, but to date, no conclusive experiments have been performed. We, therefore, theoretically explore the emergence of dichroism in EELS for a canonical single-twist helix nanostructure and present a detailed analysis of the optimal parameters to obtain a robust signal. Our work offers novel insights into the interpretation and volatility of the OAM-resolved EELS signal, which can inspire and guide future experimental efforts.

To evaluate the dichroic signal in EELS, we first focus on the relative dichroism, defined as the relative difference between the EEL probability for a transition
\ell_{\mathrm{i}}\rightarrow\ell_{\mathrm{f}}

4

\displaystyle\mathcal{RD}_{\ell_{\mathrm{i}}}^{\ell_{\mathrm{f}}}(\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathcal{F}_{\Delta\ell}\left(t_{n}\right)}-{\mathcal{F}_{-\Delta\ell}\left(t_{n}\right)}}{{\mathcal{F}_{\Delta\ell}\left(t_{n}\right)}+{\mathcal{F}_{-\Delta\ell}\left(t_{n}\right)}}.

(5)

\Delta\ell

within

We present a theoretical framework that allows for quantitative evaluation of the optical dichroic signal in electron energy-loss spectroscopy. Using an example of a plasmonic nanohelix, we demonstrate that both the sign and magnitude of the dichroic signal are governed not only by geometrical parameters of the sample and properties of its optical modes, but also by the exchanged OAM (
\Delta\ell
) and electron energy. We find analytical conditions to maximize the relative dichroism, analogous to phase-matching conditions for maximizing EELS with conventional electron beams
8
;
62
;
56
, and demonstrate the importance of theoretical analysis by showing the complex dependence of the dichroic signal over the parameter space.

We also focus on the influence of geometrical misalignments that may affect experiments. We show that, within a reasonable level of precision, the dichroic signal should be robust with respect to shifts and tilts between the sample and beam axes. However, it is still important to check the dependence of the signal on the tilt, as a non-zero, yet small and volatile, extrinsic dichroic signal can emerge for a tilted achiral sample. We further suggest that it is preferable to focus on low OAM transfers and small
\ell_{\mathrm{i}}
, ensuring the best spatial overlap with the projected sample potential, which typically varies on the nanoscale level when dealing with optical excitations. In such a scenario, one can achieve higher magnitudes of absolute dichroism, effectively overcoming issues with low signal-to-noise ratios. We note that the presented magnitudes could be further enhanced by optimizing the incident electron beam profile for an optimal overlap with the projected sample potential.

Our work sets the ground for near-future measurements of optical dichroism in setups that allow post-selection of electron states based on OAM. Such experiments could help to understand the OAM transfer and behavior of chiral optical fields at the nanoscale.

.3
Details on boundary element method and numerical simulations in MNPBEM

.3.1
Formulation of boundary element method and electron energy-loss probability in the quasi-static regime and in SI units

To establish expressions for the electron energy-loss probability in SI units, we followed the derivation from Ref.
13
, making appropriate changes to Maxwell’s equations and definitions of electrostatic potentials and Green’s functions. Starting with Gauss’s law of electrostatics relating electric displacement
{\bf D}=\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon{\bf E}
to external charge density
\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\bf D}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon{\bf E})=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon\bm{\nabla}\phi\right)=\rho_{\mathrm{ext}},

(S26)

where
\varepsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity,
\varepsilon
is the relative permittivity, and the electrostatic potential
\phi
has to satisfy Poisson’s equation

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon}-\frac{\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi}{\varepsilon},

(S27)

where the second source term containing the permittivity gradient
\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon
is related to inhomogeneities in dielectric response. The solution to the above equation can be conveniently expressed using the free-space Green’s function

\displaystyle W_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|},

(S28)

satisfying

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}W_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})=-\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}).

(S29)

Denoting
\bm{\nabla}^{\prime}
the differential operator that acts on primed coordinates, the electrostatic potential reads

\displaystyle\phi({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,\,W_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r}^{\prime})}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon}+\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,\,W_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})\frac{\bm{\nabla}^{\prime}\varepsilon({\bf r}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{\nabla}^{\prime}\phi({\bf r}^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r}^{\prime})}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|}+\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\frac{\bm{\nabla}^{\prime}\varepsilon({\bf r}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{\nabla}^{\prime}\phi({\bf r}^{\prime})}{4\pi\varepsilon|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|}.

(S30)

Assuming the dielectric permittivity
\varepsilon
is homogeneous everywhere except at abrupt interfaces between objects, the second source term can be expressed using the surface delta function
\delta_{\mathrm{s}}
that defines an interface between two media with dielectric permittivities
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi}{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon\bm{\nabla}\phi}{\varepsilon_{0}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{{\bf D}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{{\bf D}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\cdot{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}\right)\ =\left({\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot{\bf D}\right)\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}\delta_{\mathrm{s}},

where the surface normal
{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}
points from medium 1 to medium 2. Recognizing the expression in front of the surface delta function as an induced boundary charge density

\displaystyle\sigma({\bf s})=\left({\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot{\bf D}\right)\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}},

(S31)

the electrostatic potential can be split into two contributions: one that represents the direct response to the external charge density

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r}^{\prime})}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|},

(S32)

and one that accounts for the presence of other objects through the boundary charge induced at interfaces

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{bnd}}({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}.

(S33)

To find the boundary charge, we need to evaluate the normal component of the electric displacement in Eq. (
S31
). Note that there is ambiguity in choosing which side of the interface should be used for this evaluation—both are acceptable, the main difference resides in the treatment of the singularity lurking in Eq. (
S33
) when
{\bf r}\rightarrow{\bf s}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\frac{1}{|{\bf s}\pm t{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|}=-\frac{{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot({\bf s}-{\bf s}^{\prime})}{|{\bf s}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|^{3}}\mp 2\pi\delta({\bf s}-{\bf s}^{\prime})=F({\bf s},{\bf s}^{\prime})\mp 2\pi\delta({\bf s}-{\bf s}^{\prime}).

(S34)

The choice of the sign in front of the delta function depends on whether we approach the interface from the side of medium 1 (bottom sign) or medium 2 (top sign). Choosing the latter, the normal component of the electric displacement becomes

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot{\bf D}({\bf s})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}\left[-{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf s})-{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma({\bf s}^{\prime})}{4\pi}\bm{\nabla}\frac{1}{|{\bf s}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|}\right]

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}\left[-{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf s})-\frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\,\,F({\bf s},{\bf s}^{\prime})\sigma({\bf s}^{\prime})+\frac{\sigma({\bf s})}{2}\right]=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}\sigma({\bf s}).

(S35)

When inserted into Eq. (
S31
), we obtain a self-consistent equation for the boundary charge density

\displaystyle 2\pi\frac{\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}{\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}\sigma({\bf s})=\Lambda(\omega)\sigma({\bf s})=4\pi\,{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf s})+\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\,\,F({\bf s},{\bf s}^{\prime})\sigma({\bf s}^{\prime})

(S36)

which, in the absence of an external stimulus, reduces to an eigenvalue equation with eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle 2\pi\lambda_{n}\sigma_{n}({\bf s})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\,\,F({\bf s},{\bf s}^{\prime})\sigma_{n}({\bf s}^{\prime}),

(S37)

where the eigenvectors
\sigma_{n}({\bf s})
form a complete basis set with the following orthogonality property

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\frac{\sigma_{m}({\bf s})\sigma_{n}^{\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime})}{|{\bf s}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|}

\displaystyle=\delta_{mn}.

(S38)

This allows us, ultimately, to expand the inhomogeneous term in Eq. (
S36
) in this basis set and express the boundary charge as a linear combination of the eigenvectors (or in other words, the eigenmodes of the studied nanoparticle)

\displaystyle\sigma({\bf s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,\mu}\frac{f_{n\mu}}{\Lambda-2\pi\lambda_{n}}\sigma_{n}({\bf s}),

(S39)

\displaystyle f_{n\mu}

\displaystyle=4\pi\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\frac{{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi_{\mathrm{ext},\mu}({\bf s})}{|{\bf s}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|}\sigma_{n}^{\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime}),

(S40)

with the summation over the index
\mu
allowing for the general case when the external charge density extends over multiple media (e.g. an electron beam passing through a particle).

Evaluation of EEL spectra in this work relies heavily on the introduction of a screened potential
W_{\mathrm{SI}}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)
and its expansion in terms of potentials
w_{n}({\bf r})
associated with individual eigenmodes of the helix nanoparticle

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{SI}}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)=\sum_{n}h_{n}(\omega)\,w_{n}({\bf r})w_{n}^{\ast}({\bf r}^{\prime})=\sum_{n}h_{n}(\omega)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{n}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime})}{4\pi|{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|}.

(S41)

The presence of the subscript SI indicates that the above screened potential is defined in SI units and is, therefore, not exactly the same as the one appearing in the main text.

To find the expression for the spectral function
h_{n}(\omega)
that determines the weight of the
n
-th eigenmode in the above expansion, one needs to evaluate the coefficients
f_{n\mu}
in Eq. (
S40
) when the external potential is set to

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{ext},\mu}({\bf s},{\bf r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{\mu}|{\bf s}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|}.

(S42)

Careful treatment of the singularity that appears in the external potential when
{\bf r}^{\prime}\rightarrow{\bf s}
yields

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi_{\mathrm{ext},\mu}({\bf s},{\bf r}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{\mu}}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\frac{1}{|{\bf s}\mp t{\bf n}_{\mathrm{s}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|}=\frac{F({\bf s},{\bf r}^{\prime})\pm 2\pi\delta({\bf s}-{\bf r}^{\prime})}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{\mu}}.

(S43)

Note that, unlike in Eq. (
S34
), we are approaching with the source and not with the observation point, which leads to a reversal in the signs.

For the sake of simplicity, let us now limit ourselves to the case where both the source and the observation point lie in medium 2 so that only the coefficients
f_{n2}
are non-zero and the upper sign in Eq. (
S34
) is considered. The screened potential then becomes

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{SI}}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\sum_{n}\frac{f_{n2}}{\Lambda(\omega)-2\pi\lambda_{n}}\sigma_{n}({\bf s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\Lambda(\omega)-2\pi\lambda_{n}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{n}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime\prime}\frac{F({\bf s}^{\prime},{\bf r}^{\prime})+2\pi\delta({\bf s}^{\prime}-{\bf r}^{\prime})}{|{\bf s}^{\prime}-{\bf s}^{\prime\prime}|}\sigma_{n}^{\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime\prime}).

(S44)

Next, we evaluate the potential generated by an eigenmode
\sigma_{m}
at a position
{\bf r}
using the above expression for the screened potential

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf r}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\,\,W_{\mathrm{SI}}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\,\sigma_{m}({\bf r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\Lambda(\omega)-2\pi\lambda_{n}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{n}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime\prime}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime\prime})}{|{\bf s}^{\prime}-{\bf s}^{\prime\prime}|}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf r}^{\prime}\left[F({\bf s}^{\prime},{\bf r}^{\prime})+2\pi\delta({\bf s}^{\prime}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\right]\sigma_{m}({\bf r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\Lambda(\omega)-2\pi\lambda_{n}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{n}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime\prime}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime\prime})}{|{\bf s}^{\prime}-{\bf s}^{\prime\prime}|}\left[2\pi\lambda_{n}\sigma_{m}({\bf s}^{\prime})+2\pi\sigma_{m}({\bf s}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}\sum_{n}\frac{\lambda_{n}+1}{\Lambda(\omega)-2\pi\lambda_{n}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{n}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime\prime}\frac{\sigma_{m}({\bf s}^{\prime})\sigma_{n}^{\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime\prime})}{|{\bf s}^{\prime}-{\bf s}^{\prime\prime}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}\sum_{n}\frac{\lambda_{n}+1}{\Lambda(\omega)-2\pi\lambda_{n}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{n}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}\delta_{mn}=\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}\frac{\lambda_{m}+1}{\Lambda(\omega)-2\pi\lambda_{m}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{m}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|},

(S45)

where we subsequently exploited Eq. (
S37
) (eigenvalue equation) and the orthogonality property given by Eq. (
S38
). If we now replace the screened potential with its expansion from Eq. (
S41
) and evaluate the above integral again, we arrive at

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,\,W_{\mathrm{SI}}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\,\sigma_{m}({\bf r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}h_{n}(\omega)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{n}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf r}^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\frac{\sigma_{m}({\bf r}^{\prime})\sigma_{n}^{\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime})}{4\pi|{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}h_{n}(\omega)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{n}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}\frac{\delta_{mn}}{4\pi}=\frac{h_{m}(\omega)}{4\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\frac{\sigma_{m}({\bf s})}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf s}|}.

(S46)

The spectral function
h_{n}(\omega)
is then readily obtained by comparing the term in front of the final integral with its counterpart in Eq. (
S45
)

\displaystyle h_{n}(\omega)=\frac{8\pi^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}\frac{\lambda_{n}+1}{\Lambda(\omega)-2\pi\lambda_{n}}=\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}\frac{(\lambda_{n}+1)(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})}{(\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{1})-(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})\lambda_{n}}=-\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{2}}+\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{1}(1+\lambda_{n})+\varepsilon_{2}(1-\lambda_{n})}.

(S47)

Interestingly, the only frequency-dependent terms entering the spectral function are dielectric functions, eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
are dictated by geometry, and they are frequency-independent (as expected in non-retarded approximation).

The final step towards the sought expression for electron energy-loss probability is to find its link to the screened potential. According to Poynting’s theorem, the rate at which an electron beam loses energy due to an interaction with a field induced by the beam’s surroundings reads

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E({\bf r},t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\bf j}({\bf r},t)\cdot{\bf E}_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)={\bf j}({\bf r},t)\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)=\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}\,{\bf j})-\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\bf j})=\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}\,{\bf j})+\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\partial t},

(S48)

where we used the fact that the charge and current densities are tied through the continuity equation
\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\bf j}+\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\partial t}=0
. The global dissipation rate at any given time is then obtained by integration over the entire space

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\frac{\mathrm{d}E({\bf r},t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\oint_{\partial V}\mathrm{d}{\bf A}\cdot\big[\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)\,{\bf j}({\bf r},t)\big]+\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\,\,\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},t)}{\partial t}.

(S49)

Utilizing the divergence theorem, the volume integration of the first term in Eq. (
S48
) results in integration over the space boundary. Assuming this boundary is located sufficiently far away from any objects, the induced potential will vanish there and only the second term will contribute to the overall loss rate. The total energy loss is then obtained by integrating the above dissipation rate over time

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\frac{\mathrm{d}E(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\,\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\Bigg\{\Big[\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},t)\,\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!-\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\,\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},t)\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)}{\partial t}\Bigg\}=-\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\,\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},t)\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)}{\partial t},

(S50)

where we employed integration by parts and the fact that both at the beginning and at the end, the electrons forming the beam are far away from the probed objects, and the induced potential is essentially zero. Noting that the electrostatic potential is a real quantity and its Fourier components have to satisfy the condition
\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},-\omega)=\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}^{\ast}({\bf r},\omega)
, we can replace
\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)
in the above equation by its Fourier spectrum in the form

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},t)=\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\,\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},\omega)\,\,{\rm e}^{-i\omega t}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\,2\,{\rm Re}\Big\{\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},\omega)\,\,{\rm e}^{-i\omega t}\Big\},

(S51)

and the energy loss becomes

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle=-\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\,\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},t)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\,2{\rm Im}\Big\{\omega\,\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},\omega)\,\,{\rm e}^{-i\omega t}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\,4\pi\omega\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\,\,{\rm Im}\Big\{-\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},\omega)\,\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\,\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},t)\,{\rm e}^{-i\omega t}\Big\}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\,4\pi\omega\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\,\,{\rm Im}\Big\{-\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\ast}({\bf r},\omega)\,\phi_{\mathrm{ind}}({\bf r},\omega)\Big\}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}(\hbar\omega)\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{e}\,\frac{4\pi e}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}\int_{V}\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,\,{\rm Im}\Big\{-\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\ast}({\bf r},\omega)\,W_{\mathrm{SI}}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\,\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\Big\}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}(\hbar\omega)\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{e}\,\Gamma(\omega),

(S52)

where we expressed the induced potential using the screened potential introduced earlier. The particular choice of the physical constants in front of the electron energy-loss probability function
\Gamma(\omega)
ensures that its physical dimension is
\mathrm{eV}^{-1}
and the energy loss is in Joules.

To clearly identify all the changes brought by the transfer from atomic to SI units, it is convenient to focus on a specific case that can also be easily tested numerically by an established tool such as MNPBEM. To that end, we chose to evaluate the loss probability function
\Gamma(\omega)
for a single electron propagating along a non-penetrating trajectory parallel to the
z
-axis with a speed
v
at a lateral distance
{\bf r}_{\mathrm{b}}
from the origin. The charge density then takes the form

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\,\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r},t)\,\,{\rm e}^{i\omega t}=\frac{-e}{2\pi}\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\,\delta({\bf R}-{\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}})\,\delta(z-vt)\,\,{\rm e}^{i\omega t}=-\frac{e}{2\pi v}\delta({\bf R}-{\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}})\,{\rm e}^{i\omega z/v},

(S53)

and the energy loss reads

\displaystyle\Gamma(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\pi\hbar^{2}v^{2}}\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}{z}\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}{z}^{\prime}\,\,{\rm Im}\Big\{-W_{\mathrm{SI}}({\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}},z,{\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}},z^{\prime},\omega)\,{\rm e}^{-i\omega(z-z^{\prime})/v}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\pi\hbar^{2}v^{2}}\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}{z}\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}{z}^{\prime}\,\,{\rm Im}\bigg\{-\sum\limits_{n}h_{n}(\omega)\,w_{n}({\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}},z,\omega)\,{\rm e}^{-i\omega z/v}\,w_{n}^{\ast}({\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}},z^{\prime},\omega)\,{\rm e}^{i\omega z^{\prime}/v}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\pi\hbar^{2}v^{2}}\,\sum\limits_{n}{\rm Im}\big\{-h_{n}(\omega)\big\}\,\left|\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}{z}\,\,w_{n}({\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}},z,\omega)\,{\rm e}^{-i\omega z/v}\right|^{2}.

(S54)

Apparently, volume integrals in Eq. (
S52
) are now reduced to line integrals along the
z
-coordinate that can be worked out analytically. Introducing projected potential
w_{n}({\bf R},\omega)

\displaystyle w_{n}({\bf R},\omega)=\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}z\,\,w_{n}({\bf R},z)\,{\rm e}^{-i\omega z/v}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\,\,\sigma_{n}({\bf s})\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega s_{z}/v}K_{0}\left(\frac{\omega\lvert{\bf R}-{\bf s}_{\perp}\rvert}{v}\right),

(S55)

where
{\bf s}_{\perp}=(s_{x},s_{y})
are lateral coordinates of the surface element
\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}
, and further assuming that the electron probes a single nanoparticle with permittivity
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon(\omega)
embedded in vacuum (
\varepsilon_{2}=1
), the electron energy-loss probability function turns out to be

\displaystyle\Gamma(\omega)=\frac{e^{3}}{\pi\hbar^{2}v^{2}}\,\sum\limits_{n}\,{\rm Im}\big\{-h_{n}(\omega)\big\}\,|w_{n}({\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}},\omega)|^{2},

(S56)

with the spectral function

\displaystyle h_{n}(\omega)=\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon(\omega)(1+\lambda_{n})+(1-\lambda_{n})}.

(S57)

In our calculations, we assume silver as the material of the helix. To describe the optical properties of silver, we use the Drude model, where the dielectric function is defined as

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_{\infty}-\frac{\omega_{\rm p}^{2}}{\omega^{2}+{\rm i}\omega\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}},

(S58)

where
\varepsilon_{\infty}=4
is the background permittivity,
\omega_{\rm p}=9.17
eV is the plasma frequency, and
\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}=0.021
eV characterizes the damping.

Note that the spectral function
g_{n}(\omega)
appearing in the expansion of the screened potential
W({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)
in the main text is not the same as
h_{n}(\omega)
. To establish the link between the two and ensure that the energy-loss probability function is consistently expressed in
\mathrm{eV}^{-1}
throughout the paper, we evaluated Eq. (
S6
) for the specific case of a tightly focused electron beam. In that scenario, the incident wave can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{\perp,i}}({\bf R})\right\rvert^{2}=\delta({\bf R}-{\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}}),

(S59)

where
{\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}}
is the electron beam position. We consider an infinite detector and final states in the form of plane waves

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{\perp,f}}({\bf R})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\bf Q}\cdot{\bf R}}.

(S60)

After insertion into Eq. (
S6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{NR}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}(\omega)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf Q}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf R}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf R}^{\prime}\,\psi^{\ast}_{\mathrm{\perp,i}}({\bf R})\psi_{\mathrm{\perp,i}}({\bf R}^{\prime})\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\bf Q}\cdot({\bf R}-{\bf R}^{\prime})}\,w_{n}({\bf R},\omega)w_{n}^{\ast}({\bf R}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}(\omega)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf R}\,\left\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{\perp,i}}({\bf R})\right\rvert^{2}\,\left\lvert w_{n}({\bf R},\omega)\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{e^{2}}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}(\omega)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf R}\,\delta({\bf R}-{\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}})\,\left\lvert w_{n}({\bf R},\omega)\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}(\omega)\,\left\lvert w_{n}({\bf R}_{\mathrm{b}},\omega)\right\rvert^{2}.

(S61)

Clearly, the above energy-loss probability function can match the one given by Eq. (
S56
) only when

\displaystyle g_{n}(\omega)=\frac{e}{\hbar}{\rm Im}\big\{-h_{n}(\omega)\big\}=\frac{4\pi e}{\varepsilon_{0}\hbar}\,{\rm Im}\bigg\{\frac{-2}{\varepsilon(\omega)(1+\lambda_{n})+(1-\lambda_{n})}\bigg\}.

(S62)

.3.2
Normalization of helix eigenmodes

For the helix nanoparticles presented in this paper, one can either calculate the eigenmodes
\sigma_{n}({\bf s})
numerically, for example, using the eigensolver implemented in the MNPBEM toolbox, or set up analytical Ansatz eigenmodes with an ideal cosine dependence. We should note that in both cases, the eigenmodes need to be normalized first to satisfy the orthogonality condition given by Eq. (
S38
). Denoting
\sigma_{n}^{\prime}({\bf s})
the original eigenmode, the norm
\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{n}^{\prime}({\bf s})/\sigma_{n}({\bf s})
then reads

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}=\sqrt{\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{\prime}({\bf s})\,\sigma_{n}^{\prime\ast}({\bf s}^{\prime})}{|{\bf s}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|}}.

(S63)

Since the analytical evaluation of the norm proved to be tricky, we mapped the Ansatz eigenmode distribution onto a discretized helix mesh generated in MNPBEM and calculated the norm using the same procedure as for the numerical eigenmodes. This procedure exploits the fact that the surface charge density can be approximately treated as a slowly varying function that is constant within a single discretization element
\Delta S_{i}
and the integration subsequently turns into a summation over the discrete elements

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}=\sqrt{\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\Delta S_{i}\,\sigma_{n}^{\prime}({\bf s}_{i})\,G({\bf s}_{i},{\bf s}_{j})\,\sigma_{n}^{\prime\ast}({\bf s}_{j})},

(S64)

where elements of the Green’s function matrix

\displaystyle G({\bf s}_{i},{\bf s}_{j})=\int_{\Delta S_{j}}\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}{\bf s}^{\prime}\frac{1}{|{\bf s}_{i}-{\bf s}^{\prime}|}

(S65)

are conveniently computed by the MNPBEM solver and can be easily accessed.

.3.3
Validity of the non-retarded analytical approximation and effects of retardation on mode energy

So far in this work, we used the analytically calculated screened potential in all simulations except Fig.
1
. However, we should also discuss the accuracy of this approximate approach. In this section, we present the comparison of the potentials calculated in the MNPBEM for a thick silver single-twist helix with the charge distribution calculated by solving Maxwell’s equations and an infinitesimally thin silver single-twist helix with a charge distribution described by cosine dependence. In Fig.

, we present the absolute value and phase of the functions
w_{n}
for the first free modes, and we compare them with the MNPBEM simulation. The accuracy of the approximation is outstanding. The first mode exhibits very good accuracy in the absolute value (a2) and phase (a4), and is even better in the area denoted by circles, which represents the region where 95% of Laguerre-Gaussian beam intensity lies. The calculations for the second and third modes are also very accurate. The large values [around 80% in sub-figures (b2) and (c2)] are caused by dividing by a very small number. Outside these areas, the analytical result is similar to the one from MNPBEM. The phase of the second and third mode potential exhibits a point where the phase has a singularity (b3) and (c3). When we subtract the phase of potential calculated by MNPBEM from the analytical one, we see the singularity here as well. This singularity in phase does not cause any problems in the calculations, because the absolute value of
w_{n}
is, in this case, zero. In the sub-figures (b4) and (c4), one can see that the difference in phases is close to zero outside the singular points.
