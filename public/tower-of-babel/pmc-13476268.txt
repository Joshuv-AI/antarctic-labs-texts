Dual-source mesoporous nanosilicas from horsetail plant and chemical precursors show structural and textural evidence for vitamin C loading and release
Authors: Zahra Shabestari-Khiabani, Sara Fazli-Shokouhi, Soodabeh Davaran, Azin Yavari, Sahar Porrang, Maasoumeh Khatamian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focuses on the synthesis and characterization of a novel silica material from horsetail-derived bio-silica, modified via mesoporous structure generation (mesoporous bio-silica) and its subsequent application as a carrier for Ascorbic Acid (Vitamin C, VC) delivery. The synthesized material was systematically characterized using low-angle X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms of Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET). Raw bio-silica demonstrated an amorphous structure, confirmed by broad XRD peaks. Subsequent templating yielded a mesoporous silica structure exhibiting characteristic MCM-41 diffraction patterns. VC loading significantly altered the textural properties, notably reducing BET surface area and pore volume, which is consistent with pore blocking by VC. FTIR analysis confirmed the successful incorporation of VC through the appearance of characteristic C–H (2850, 2925 cm−1) and C–C (965 cm−1) stretching vibrations, while BET/BJH analysis provided quantitative evidence of pore blockage. The VC release from mesoporous silicas showed a slow release profile, reaching a plateau at approximately 13 h. Release efficiency was comparable or adjacent to that of TEOS-based systems in our assays. Compared with TEOS-derived silica, the horsetail-derived silica required fewer synthesis steps and avoided organic solvents, suggesting lower production complexity. These findings indicate that plant-derived bio-silica may serve as an alternative source for silica-based drug carriers.Keywords: Mesoporous silica, Horsetail, Porous structure, Vitamin C, Delivery systemsSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Silicon, a metalloid with atomic number 14, is the second most abundant element in the Earth’s crust1. It exists in various oxidation states, with silicon dioxide being the most common compound2. Silicon dioxide, primarily found in quartz and sand, occurs both in crystalline and amorphous forms3. Silicon in soil mainly comes from minerals and plant processes4. It has been suggested that silicon can enhance plant growth, disease resistance, and drought tolerance, although some authors have reported that silica nanoparticles negatively affect plant growth by altering the pH of the growth medium5,6. Despite the abundance of silicon in soil, its availability to plants is limited by its insolubility7,8. Silicon’s solubility is crucial for plant absorption, as it is taken up in its soluble form, H4SiO4. This form polymerizes into SiO2 near plant transpiration ducts and returns to the soil after plant decay. The decomposition process also contributes to the formation of humus (humic acid), enhancing soil properties. Microbial activities further recycle SiO2, highlighting the importance of plants and microbes in the silicon cycle9. Silicon in plants is primarily stored as phytoliths, composed of amorphous hydrated silica. Various plant families and some conifers accumulate silica, with certain plants having high silica content in their ash. The average silica content of rice husk and straw is 82–93%10,11. Wheat straw, oil palm ash, and sugarcane bagasse have 90.56%, 46%, and 73% SiO2, respectively12. Silica polymerization involves polyamines, carbohydrates, and proteins, leading to the formation of amorphous silica13. Biosilicification, the process of silica accumulation in plants, is notable in species such as horsetail, one of the oldest vascular plants14,15. This compound can be relatively easily sourced from plant matte, and there are numerous scientific articles describing the preparation, properties, and applications of biogenic silica originating mainly from straw and husks sourced from rice16–18 and other crops, including wheat, sugarcane, oil palm, or barley19. Silica is one of the important materials used in health and high-value industries. The synthesis procedure influences the cost, application, and properties of the resulting materials. Thus, silica technology is one of the impactful branches of knowledge. The byproducts of synthesizing mesosilica from bio-silica are lower than those of MCM-41 synthesized from chemical precursors20.

Equisetum spp., or horsetail, is a unique plant known for its high silica content, which gives it strength21. The extensive medicinal use of horsetail over decades is largely attributed to its high silica content. As a rich source of silica and silicic acids, it is known to boost the body’s resistance to infections, enhance the elasticity and resilience of the skin and connective tissue, and offer medicinal benefits including the treatment of diabetes and osteoporosis. Beyond its medicinal value, horsetail has historical applications in cleaning and polishing, further underscoring the utility of its silica-rich composition17,22. The mentioned facts suggest that bio-silica extracted from horsetail plants has no side effects on the human body. Therefore, this material can be used as a drug delivery carrier, with vitamin C (VC) employed as a model drug to test its carrier behavior. The importance of using horsetail in green chemistry is highlighted due to its eco-friendly, non-toxic, inexpensive, stable, and recyclable nature17. Equisetum is the sole genus in the Equisetaceae family, with species like E. arvense and E. myriochaetum23.

The revolution in nanotechnology took off in the mid-1950s, marking a period of swift advancements evident in the creation of diverse nanomaterials ranging from 1 to 100 nm. These materials are crucial due to their distinct attributes, such as straightforward synthesis, scalability, and their morphology, along with physicochemical properties that vary with size and shape24. Nanomaterials possess two key characteristics: (i) a substantial surface area that enables the immobilization of different guest species and the targeting of host molecules, and (ii) a high surface-to-volume ratio that facilitates the encapsulation of various entities, including small drug molecules, genes, and proteins25. Nanoparticles are widely used across various fields due to their adaptability. Silica nanoparticles, in particular, are notable for their stability, low toxicity, and versatile applications, including drug delivery and environmental remediation. Their unique structural features make them highly effective in these roles26. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) and other mesoporous structures, having high surface areas, are beneficial for adsorption and catalysis. These materials were developed using templating techniques27,28. Since the discovery of the M41S family of mesoporous molecular sieves in 199229, significant research has focused on the synthesis and applications of MCM-41. These materials are synthesized through the sol–gel method and self-assembly30. Efforts have been made to modify pore size and structure through multiple synthesis methods and by introducing functional groups29.

Integrating drugs into porous structures has been considered in recent years, as it allows for safeguarding the drug, regulating its release, enhancing its solubility, and shielding it from detrimental external factors31,32. To attain these benefits from porous delivery systems, it is vital to ensure that the drug is effectively incorporated into the carrier’s internal pores31. In this regard, the capability of mesoporous silica as a carrier for VC was investigated here. VC is an essential dietary nutrient for humans, as we cannot synthesize it internally. It supports collagen synthesis, antioxidant defense, immune function, and iron absorption, while its deficiency causes scurvy. Beyond preventing scurvy, its antioxidant activity may help reduce the risk of oxidative stress-related diseases such as certain cancers and cardiovascular disease33. Thus, VC was chosen as a model compound owing to its pronounced sensitivity to oxidative and thermal stress, which presents well-recognized formulation difficulties. Its inherent instability and limited bioavailability are representative of the common obstacles associated with hydrophilic bioactives. Incorporation into porous silica nanoparticles directly targets these limitations by: (i) shielding the molecule from ambient moisture and oxygen through physical confinement, and (ii) suppressing thermal degradation by reducing molecular mobility within the confined nanopore environment34. Bio-silica was extracted from horsetail (Equisetum spp.) and employed to synthesize mesoporous bio-silica structures. The performance of the bio-derived mesoporous silica in VC loading and controlled release was compared with that of conventionally synthesized MCM-41.

Materials and methods

Materials

Stems and leaves of horsetail plant (Equisetum arvense L.) collected from Oskou, East Azerbaijan Province, Iran, were bought from Tabriz Grand Bazaar. N-cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, > 99%), vitamin C (99%), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 98%), and 25% ammonia solution (NH3)were purchased from Merck Company (Germany). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 97%) was obtained from Loba Chemie (India). Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%) were supplied by Dr. Mojallali Company (Iran). All solutions were prepared using deionized water.

Extraction of Silica from plant (bio-silica)

Twenty grams of horsetail plant were washed with drinking water and dried for 24 h. The dried plant was washed again and oven-dried at 110 °C for 5 h. The obtained material was refluxed with 400 mL of 4 M HCl in a round-bottom flask at boiling water temperature to remove metallic impurities for 2 h. The black-colored horsetail was separated and washed with deionized water using a centrifuge (2500 rpm, 10 times) until a neutral pH was achieved. The resulting material was dried in an oven at 50 °C for 2 h in an air atmosphere, followed by the calcination process to completely remove the organic components. The calcination was carried out at 500 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 1 °C min−1 at air atmosphere35. The steps for synthesizing the bio-silica are shown in Fig. 1.

The steps of synthesizing bio-silica from horsetail plant.

Synthesis of mesoporous bio-silica from bio-silica

One gram of the extracted bio-silica was dispersed in 7.04 mL of 1 M NaOH solution to obtain a 2.36 M silicate solution. The solution was stirred at 80 °C for 1 h. The concentration was subsequently raised to 4.72 M by reducing the volume to one-half through continuous heating at 80 °C, resulting in the conversion of the sol to a gel. Separately, 8 g of CTAB was dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water and stirred at 40 °C for 1 h. The pH of CTAB solution was then adjusted to 6.5 by dropwise addition of 0.01 M sulfuric acid. The prepared silica solution was gradually added to the CTAB solution under stirring for 1 h. The stirring was continued for an additional 1 h.

The selection of the pH values was based on the fundamental mechanism of mesoporous silica nanoparticle (MSN) synthesis, which consists of two main steps: hydrolysis and condensation in the sol–gel process. Hydrolysis is generally faster under acidic conditions. As the pH increases from strongly acidic values toward neutral conditions, silica species (Si–OH) begin to partially deprotonate, leading to the formation of Si–O− groups. The charge density of silicate species increases up to approximately pH 7.5, which enhances their electrostatic interaction with cationic surfactants such as CTAB. By initially adjusting the pH to 6.5, we aimed to promote controlled nucleation while maintaining strong electrostatic silica–CTAB interactions. At this pH, partial deprotonation facilitates micelle–silicate assembly without triggering excessively rapid condensation, allowing better control over particle formation.

When the pH exceeds 10, silica species become highly deprotonated (Si–O⁻), and condensation becomes strongly base-catalyzed. Under these conditions, siloxane bond formation (Si–O–Si) accelerates, network formation increases, and particle growth becomes more pronounced. Therefore, the pH was subsequently increased to 11.3 to accelerate base-catalyzed condensation and stabilize the mesoporous silica framework. This two-step pH adjustment allowed us to decouple nucleation and growth stages, enabling better structural control and stabilization of the final MSN architecture. The resulting mixture was transferred into an autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 90 °C for 24 h. The obtained solid was washed, dried, and calcined at 400 °C for 5 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min−136–38.

Synthesis of MCM-41 from chemical precursors

In a beaker, 2.4 g of CTAB was mixed with 120 mL of distilled water and heated at 50 °C until dissolved. To this, 10.8 mL of a 25% ammonia solution (0.14 mol) was added dropwise, and the pH was adjusted to 11–12 with 0.01 M NaOH solution. Subsequently, 10 g of TEOS (0.05 mol) was gradually added over 15 min with continuous stirring to form a homogeneous gel. After an hour of stirring, the gel was rinsed with 100 mL of distilled water and then dried in an oven at 90 °C for 12 h. Finally, the gel powder underwent calcination at 550 °C for 5 h in air atmosphere.

Vitamin C loading in MCM-41 and mesoporous bio-silica

VC (300 mg) was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water. Then, 300 mg of mesoporous silica was added to the solution, and the mixture was stirred at 2000 rpm for 24 h at room temperature in a dark cabinet. The mass ratio of the VC to mesoporous silica was 1:1. The solid was then separated from the mixture and washed three times with deionized water. Finally, the vitamin-loaded silica was dried under vacuum at room temperature39. Release of vitamin C from MCM-41, mesoporous bio-silica, and bio-silica

To examine VC release, a simulated body fluid (SBF) solution with pH = 7.4 was prepared following the procedure described by Xu et al.40. The solution was prepared by dissolving NaCl (0.7996 g), NaHCO₃ (0.0350 g), KCl (0.0224 g), K₂HPO₄·3H₂O (0.0228 g), MgCl₂·6H₂O (0.0305 g), CaCl₂ (0.0278 g), Na₂SO₄ (0.0278 g), and C₄H₁₁NO₃ (0.6057 g) in 100 mL of distilled water, in the order listed. The pH was then adjusted to 7.4 using approximately 4 mL of 1 N HCl. Three vials were filled with 2 mL of this solution for each type of silica. Approximately 10 mg of VC-loaded silicas were added to the vials, which were then placed in a shaking incubator at 37 °C and 80 rpm. Samples of the release medium were collected at specific intervals and replaced with fresh solution. The concentration of released VC in the samples was determined by a dual-beam UV–vis spectrophotometer at 265 nm using the SBF solution as the reference blank39.

Characterization techniques

The crystalline structure and phase identification of the synthesized materials were analyzed by low-angle X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Philips PW1730 diffractometer. The crystallinity of the VC and VC-loaded mesoporous biosilica was examined by X-ray diffraction on a TD-3700-Tongda diffractometer, employing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) over a 2θ range of 4–70°.The surface morphology and particle size were examined using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) with a Tescan MIRA3 FEG-SEM (Central Laboratory, University of Tabriz). To identify the functional groups and chemical bonds, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 using a Bruker Model Tensor 27 spectrometer (Faculty of Chemistry, University of Tabriz). Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were obtained at 77 K using a Micromeritics surface area analyzer (Mahamax Laboratory, Tehran) to determine the specific surface area and pore size distribution based on the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) methods, respectively. The concentration of VC during loading and release studies was determined using a Shimadzu 1700 UV–vis spectrophotometer at the characteristic wavelengths.

Results and discussion

Bio-silica was extracted from horsetail plant and subjected to hydrothermal templating in the presence of CTAB. The synthesis of mesoporous silica includes two main steps, i.e. hydrolysis and condensation. As established, hydrolysis is faster under acidic conditions, so the procedure was started by adjusting the pH to 6.5 to provide the required conditions for the hydrolysis process. At the same time, partial deprotonation of silanol groups at this pH leads to the formation of a Si–O− groups, which interact with cationic CTAB via strong electrostatic attraction. By increasing the pH to about 11 in the next step, the condensation process accelerates through a base-catalyzed mechanism and Si–O–Si groups’ formation increases. In conclusion, the mentioned two-step pH adjustment leads to better structural control and stabilization of mesoporous silica network. The prepared mesoporous bio-silica was then examined in the loading of VC. Different instrumental techniques were employed to study the structural and textural properties of mesoporous silica before and after VC loading. The results were compared with those of MCM-41 synthesized using chemicals.

Loading of vitamin C by MCM-41 and mesoporous bio-silica

The loading efficiency of VC on bio-silica, mesoporous bio-silica, and MCM-41 was determined using UV–vis spectroscopy at 265 nm. The results demonstrated that the bio-silica extracted from the horsetail plant had the highest loading capacity (45.9%), followed by MCM-41 (26.4%), and mesoporous bio-silica (14.7%). This trend directly correlates with the specific surface area of each material as discussed in the next section.

Structural evidences for the loading of vitamin C

To study the structural changes after VC loading, different characterization techniques were used on fresh and VC-loaded samples. Figure 2 presents the low-angle XRD patterns of prepared materials prior to VC loading. The low-angle XRD pattern of silica derived from horsetail Fig. 2 shows no peaks in the 2θ range of 0–10°. This indicates that the silica is amorphous. The absence of peaks is attributed to the lack of long-range crystalline order, resulting in destructive interference of diffracted X-rays. Figure 2 shows the low angle XRD pattern of mesoporous bio-silica. The presence of peaks at 2θ = 2° and 4°, matching those of reference patterns, is consistent with the mesoporous nature of the plant-derived silica. For more evidence, the mesoporous nature of bio-silica is investigated with other techniques such as BET. The peaks at 2θ = 2° and 4° are hallmark of highly ordered hexagonal array of cylindrical mesopores, indicating a periodic nanostructure. The first-order reflection, observed at 2θ ≈ 2° and indexed as (100), represents the distance between adjacent rows of pores (center-to-center). The low-intensity peak near 4°, indexed as (200), corresponds to the second order of the (100) plane and typically appears only as a weak shoulder on the (110) peak.

The FTIR spectra of the extracted silica and synthesized mesoporous materials are shown in Fig. 6. In the FTIR spectrum of bio-silica obtained from the horsetail plant Fig. 6a, the bands at 3437 cm−1 and 1442 cm-1 are related to the stretching and bending vibrations of Si–OH groups and adsorbed water. Strong bands at 1085, 794, and 459 cm−1, corresponding to the asymmetric Si–O–Si stretching, symmetric stretching, and bending vibrations of silica, respectively, confirm the presence of silica. The band at 3452 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching of Si–OH groups41–43. The FTIR spectrum of mesoporous silica resembles that of extracted silica before mesopores formation. Similarly, the FTIR spectrum of synthesized mesoporous MCM-41 (Fig. 6(c)) demonstrates various bands associated with different vibrations, including those of Si–O–Si, water molecules, silanol groups, and aliphatic C–H.

The FTIR spectra of pure vitamin C and VC-loaded samples are presented in Fig. 7. The FTIR spectrum of VC indicates bands related to the vibrations of various functional groups, including stretching vibration of the C = C double bond and the C–O vibration at 1674 and 1087 cm−1, respectively. Another notable band appears at 1271 cm−1, attributed to the in-plane bending vibration of the O–H groups44. Unfortunately, as shown in Fig. 7, these bands are masked by broad, intense bands of silica in the same regions, making the FTIR spectra of VC-loaded samples look very similar to those of pure ones. However, precise examination of the FTIR spectra reveals the appearance of low-intensity bands at 2850 and 2925 cm−1, corresponding to C–H stretching. Moreover, a new band is detectable at 965 cm−1 in the FTIR spectrum of VC-loaded bio-silica, which can be assigned to the C–C stretching vibration of vitamin C. The assignment of this band is difficult in the case of mesoporous bio-silica and MCM-41, as the unloaded samples exhibit a band at 950 cm−1 that overlaps with the aforementioned band. Nevertheless, this band shifts to higher wavenumbers in the VC-loaded samples.

The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of the prepared materials are depicted in Fig. 8a. Although the isotherm type is different for extracted bio-silica and mesoporous bio-silica, a hysteresis loop appears for both samples. The hysteresis loop reflects capillary condensation, which is typically associated with mesoporous materials; however, mesoporosity should be confirmed by pore size distribution analysis (BJH). The hysteresis loop is of type H3 for both bio-silica and mesoporous bio-silica, suggesting that the particles have flexible and expandable slit-like pores. The adsorption–desorption isotherm of bio-silica also shows a pore-filling step at low P/P0 values (P/P0 < 0.1), confirming the existence of micropores in this sample. These micropores are absent in the isotherm of mesoporous bio-silica. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm of MCM-41 shows an H4 hysteresis loop.

(a) Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms and (b) pore size distribution analysis of bio-silica, mesoporous bio-silica, and MCM-41.

The pore size distribution of prepared materials calculated by the BJH (Barrett-Joyner-Halenda) method is illustrated in Fig. 8b. The BJH plot of plant-derived silica shows a broad pore size distribution with a main peak centered at 2.1 nm and an additional peak near approximately 12.1 nm. This plot indicates that the bio-silica is predominantly mesoporous. The BJH diagram of mesoporous bio-silica shows a main peak at around 2.8 nm, confirming the material’s mesoporous nature. The diagram also displays distinct peaks at 10.6 nm, 18.8 nm, and 45.5 nm, providing further evidence of mesoporosity. The BJH plot of MCM-41 shows that the maximum peak is centered at 4.6 nm. It is worth noting that the main peak observed in BJH plots is different from the mean pore diameter reported in Table 1. The "mean pore diameter" is not a direct measurement; it’s a calculated value derived from the pore volume (V) and the surface area (A) of the pores, often using a cylindrical pore model.

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore size distribution of mesoporous bio-silica and MCM-41 after VC loading are shown in Fig. 9. Although the hysteresis loop is still present in the adsorption–desorption isotherms of both samples after VC loading, the adsorbed volume of N2 shows a significant decrease due to the blocking of pores by the loaded vitamin. The pores in the 2–50 nm range are either almost eliminated or show significantly reduced intensity in the BJH diagram of MCM-41 after VC loading. The BJH plot of VC-loaded mesoporous bio-silica indicates a heterogeneous porous structure characterized by multiple peaks in the mesopore region. These observations confirm the incorporation of vitamin C into the silica pores.

(a) Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms and (b) pore size distribution analysis of vitamin C-loaded mesoporous bio-silica, and MCM-41.

The significant reduction in BET surface area and pore volume of the samples after VC loading suggests that VC is incorporated into the samples’ pores. It is worth noting that the mean pore diameter of both mesoporous bio-silica and MCM-41 increased after VC loading. The results provided by different analyses confirmed the successful loading of VC onto the prepared mesoporous bio-silica. It can also be deduced from SEM images and BET analysis that the VC mainly accumulated in the pores of MCM-41, while it is present on the external surface and within the pores of mesoporous bio-silica.

Vitamin C release studies

Release of vitamin C from VC-loaded samples was investigated over a 33-h period, and the results are illustrated in Fig. 10. Comparison of the release profiles of the three samples reveals that the bio-silica extracted from horsetail has the most efficient release characteristics, and about 18% of the loaded VC is released by the end of this period.

VC release profile of VC-loaded silica samples.

The release efficiency is similar for MCM-41 and mesoporous bio-silica. The lower release efficiencies observed for the porous samples can be attributed to the accumulation of VC within their pores and its possible stronger interaction with the silica structure. It is also clear that the bio-silica exhibits fast release behavior, releasing approximately 16% of loaded VC within the first hour. In contrast, MCM-41 and mesoporous bio-silica show a slow-release profile.

The TGA diagrams confirm the results obtained from UV–visible spectra. The mesoporous bio-silica diagram in Fig. 11 shows a weight loss at 100 °C, which is related to the adsorbed water molecules present during the synthesis of mesoporous bio-silica and the loading of VC into it. Another weight change occurs between 100 and 483 °C, corresponding to the degradation of loaded VC. The weight change in this temperature range is 8.78%. Moreover, the TGA diagram of the mesoporous bio-silica after release test confirms the presence of VC even after the release test the loaded VC for 33 h. Conclusion

In this study, the capability of bio-silica extracted from the horsetail plant, mesoporous plant-derived silica nanoparticles, and MCM-41 silica nanoparticles for loading of VC was compared using low-angle XRD, FESEM, FTIR, and N2 adsorption–desorption techniques. Low-angle XRD results revealed that mesoporous plant-derived and MCM-41 silica nanoparticles exhibit a regular mesoporous structure, whereas horsetail-extracted silica is amorphous. VC loading caused a significant reduction in the intensity of low-angle XRD peaks, confirming successful loading. Morphological examination revealed that the bio-silica has adherent rectangular shapes, while mesoporosity transformed them into non-uniform spheres, similar to MCM-41. The changes in morphology, along with a decrease in BET surface area after VC loading, revealed the adsorption of Vitamin C both on the surface and within the pores of mesoporous bio-silica, while the accumulation in pores is the predominant process for VC loading by MCM-41. The results indicate that bio-silica extracted from horsetail exhibited the highest VC loading capacity, followed by conventional MCM-41, and then mesoporous bio-silica derived from horsetail. The release efficiency was low for all samples. Although the release profiles reach a plateau after 13 h, the predominant fraction of loaded VC remained unreleased over the entire 33-h experimental period.

The findings indicate that plant-based silica can serve as an alternative to costly precursors like tetraethyl orthosilicate for synthesizing silica compounds. The proposed method provides a route to produce mesoporous materials from plant sources. More investigations including in vitro studies are needed to confirm the biocompatibility of the plant-derived silica materials.
