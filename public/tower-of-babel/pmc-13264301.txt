Chemotherapy-Induced Toxicities in Pediatric Cancer Patients in Tanzania [Letter]
Authors: Stanley Mwita
Published: 2026

Dear editor

I read with great interest the article by Katabalo et al1 on the burden of chemotherapy-induced toxicities (CITs) among pediatric cancer patients in Tanzania.1 CTIs present significant challenges in pediatric oncology, impacting patient outcomes, extending hospital stays, and diminishing overall quality of life.2 The authors are to be commended for conducting the study using a validated version of the Pediatric Patient-Reported Outcomes Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (Ped-PRO-CTCAE) tool. Their finding that 100% of children experienced at least one CIT, with a mean of five per child, powerfully highlights the urgent need to prioritize supportive care in the region’s pediatric oncology programs.

However, I wish to draw attention to a critical point that the study did not fully address: the association between the absence of supportive care medications and the potential underreporting of severe toxicities due to study design. The authors report that 32.5% of participants received no supportive care medications, while simultaneously observing that only mild (grade 1) toxicities predominated, and no grade 4 or 5 events were documented. This discrepancy is attributable to selection bias, acknowledged as a study limitation: only patients actively attending the oncology clinic were eligible for recruitment, meaning that children who were critically ill or unable to return for follow-up were excluded from the sample.

I recommend the pediatric oncology community in Tanzania to undertake a prospective cohort study that enrolls children at the initiation of chemotherapy and follows them throughout all treatment cycles would provide a more comprehensive assessment of toxicity incidence, severity, timing, and outcomes. Unlike cross-sectional or facility-based assessments that may miss events occurring between clinic visits, prospective monitoring allows real-time documentation of adverse events and better attribution of toxicities to specific treatment regimens. Similar prospective study has been conducted in Bharati Hospital, India by Parande et al.3

Further, a health systems assessment of supportive medicine availability in Tanzanian cancer centers is a priority. Such an assessment should evaluate the availability, affordability, accessibility, and utilization of essential supportive-care medicines. Findings from a previous study conducted in East Africa revealed that access to childhood cancer medicines across East Africa was marked by gaps in availability that have implications for effective treatment delivery for a range of childhood cancers.4

Findings from these investigations would have important implications for clinical practice and health policy in Tanzania. Prospective toxicity surveillance would enable clinicians to identify patients at elevated risk of severe toxicities, optimize monitoring schedules, and implement timely preventive or therapeutic interventions. Concurrently, evidence on supportive medicine availability would assist policymakers, hospital administrators, and procurement agencies in addressing supply-chain gaps and ensuring consistent access to essential supportive-care medicines.
