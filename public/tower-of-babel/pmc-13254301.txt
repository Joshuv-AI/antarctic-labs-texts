Octahedral-rigidity-engineered linear dielectrics for harsh-temperature energy storage capacitors
Authors: Qi Wang, Qiuyu Zheng, Xuetong Zhao, Jing Guo, Shenglin Kang, Jianglin Wang, Yuchen Li, Xinyuan Hou, Hongwen Liu, Li Cheng, Lijun Yang, Ruijin Liao, Long Cheng, Fei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Next-generation dielectric energy storage technologies, spanning renewable energy systems, electrified transportation, and advanced propulsion platforms, necessitate stable operation under extreme thermal conditions. However, the inherent trade-off between high capacitive performance and thermal stability in existing dielectric materials imposes a critical bottleneck on their practical deployment. Here we engineer an octahedrally rigid framework by 1:2 B-site ordering (Mg/Nb) within an ABO₃ perovskite structure, synergistically coupled with Sr/Bi A-site chemistry to lock structure rigidity and tailor polarizability, culminating in a high-symmetry dual-cubic phase matrix for harsh-temperature capacitive energy storage. Finite-temperature ab initio molecular dynamics simulations combined with density functional theory analysis demonstrate the retention of cubic symmetry with minimal lattice expansion up to 500 °C, consistent with the temperature-stable permittivity and bandgap required for ultra-wide-temperature capacitive energy storage. Further experiments confirm the outstanding energy storage of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics, achieving an energy density of 2.2 J cm–3 and an efficiency of 84% at 270 °C under 800 kV cm–1, alongside a remarkable enhancement in energy density from 3.0 J cm–3 (96.5% efficiency) to 4.9 J cm–3 at 1150 kV cm–1 enabled by the cold sintering process. The symmetry-driven design, rooted in a cubic matrix, provides critical insight into achieving capacitors with both high energy density and thermal stability under harsh operating conditions.

Introduction

Compared with electrochemical energy storage systems, electrostatic energy storage using ceramic-based dielectrics plays an indispensable role in modern electrical and electronic technologies owing to its ultrahigh power density (rapid charge–discharge capability) and excellent stability1–3. However, the use of dielectric capacitors is presently limited by their low energy storage density, especially in applications requiring lightweight design and compact packaging. Beyond this, the next generation of dielectric capacitors is not merely aimed at incremental improvements in energy density, but increasingly driven by the demand for reliable performances in engines, drilling devices, and aerospace systems, eliminating the need for additional cooling solutions at temperatures exceeding 200 °C4. Consequently, there has been a surge of attention toward developing “dual high” dielectric capacitors with high energy density and thermal stability.

Excellent capacitive energy storage materials are primarily classified into three categories: relaxor ferroelectric, antiferroelectric, and linear dielectrics, each exhibiting unique advantages that enhance recoverable energy storage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W}_{{{{\rm{rec}}}}}={\int }_{Pr}^{{P}_{\max }}E{{{\rm{d}}}}P$$\end{document}Wrec=∫PrPmaxEdP, where E represents the applied electric field, P denotes polarization, and Pr and Pmax correspond to the remnant polarization and the maximum polarization, respectively)5,6. To date, a broad range of strategies has been implemented to refine relaxor ferroelectric and antiferroelectric ceramics, driving notable progress in energy storage technologies. In the low-field regime, Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3-based and weak-relaxor ferroelectric systems show nearly hysteresis-free polarization and energy efficiencies above 95%7,8, while NaNbO3-based ceramics deliver >4.1 J cm–3 at ~190 kV cm–1 and remain effective down to ~100 kV cm–19,10. Under high-field conditions, representative Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3-, BaTiO3-, and NaNbO3-based ceramics have exceeded 10 J cm–3 via high-entropy design strategies11–13. Noteworthy advancements in high-temperature energy storage have been demonstrated in relaxor ferroelectric and antiferroelectric ceramics, with the Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3-Bi0.5K0.5TiO3-Sr(Sc0.5Nb0.5)O3 ternary system exceeding 8 J cm–3 at 160 °C14, while NaNbO3-based ceramics have achieved up to 7 J cm–3 with efficiency of 73% at 200 °C5. However, sustaining high-energy-storage performance beyond 250 °C is impeded by the intricate phase structures of these dielectrics, whereas commercial high-temperature dielectric capacitors predominantly rely on linear dielectrics15. In contrast, linear dielectrics based on cubic or orthorhombic phases primarily exhibit ionic or electronic displacement polarization, thereby preventing the formation of domain structures inherent to relaxor ferroelectrics and antiferroelectrics16. Free from the thermal activation of dipoles, domain switching losses, and phase transition disruptions, the low-defect linear dielectrics exhibit exceptional temperature insensitivity, consistently low dielectric loss, and superior breakdown strength at elevated temperatures. Benefiting from high-symmetry structures, the SrTiO3-modified CaZrO3 multilayer ceramic capacitor (MLCC) was reported to exhibit nearly unchanged capacitance over the temperature range from −55 to 200 °C, with extremely low dielectric loss (<0.016%), meeting the X9G specification (capacitance variation is within 0 ± 30 ppm/°C over the temperature range of −55 to 200 °C)17. Of particular significance is that the CaZrO3-based X9G MLCC exhibits a linear P − E hysteresis loop even at 170 °C, while large hysteresis can be easily observed in relaxor ferroelectric at the same temperature due to the thermal activation of dipoles18. Additionally, the incorporation of 1.5 wt% amorphous glass into Ca(Zr1-xTix)O3 ceramics yields a linear dielectric, enabling thin-film capacitors fabricated via tape casting to achieve an energy density exceeding 4 J cm–3 under 1500 kV cm–1 at 250 °C, regardless of large polarization hysteresis4. Although traditional CaTiO3 and SrTiO3 linear dielectrics are theoretically capable of high breakdown strength19,20, their practical utility under harsh temperature conditions is constrained by premature breakdown and excessive polarization loss. Currently, numerous modified SrTiO3-based bulk ceramics have been reported to achieve both high polarization and efficiency, with a record energy storage density of 5.9 J cm–3 at room temperature21. However, domain formation increases polarization to ~40 μC cm–2 while compromising linear dielectric behavior, reclassifying them as quasilinear relaxor ferroelectrics21. True bulk linear dielectrics rarely exceed 3 J cm–3 in energy storage density at room temperature. Therefore, the next generation of thermally stable energy storage capacitors should be inspired by the structure symmetry of linear dielectrics, leveraging high-symmetry designs to achieve ultra-low loss and stable dielectric behavior at elevated temperatures.

For the construction of perovskite structures, the designation of B-site cations is critical for forming the TiO6 octahedra, necessitating the maintenance of both structural stability and high symmetry. According to the extended Glazer model, the anti-phase tilting (a–a–c0, a0a0c– tilt system) or in-phase tilting (a0a0c+ tilt system) of the octahedra enables a complex perovskite structure, where dual cations (B1/B2) co-occupy the B-site of the ABO3 lattice, forming a characteristic 1:2 ordered arrangement22,23. The 1:2 ordering is commonly found in perovskite with considerable differences in ionic radii or charge states (e.g., Mg1/3/Nb2/3, Mg1/3/Ta2/3, Zn1/3Nb2/3)24. This configuration reinforces octahedral rigidity through an intrinsic ionic “locking effect” and short-range repulsion, thereby significantly reducing thermal expansion and structural distortions at elevated temperatures. Early investigations into perovskite-based microwave dielectric ceramics have established that (Mg2+/Nb5+) systems predominantly stabilize in a cubic phase, yielding superior temperature coefficients and high-quality factors25,26. The radius and electronegativity of A-site cations play a pivotal role in governing oxygen octahedral distortion and the overall dielectric behaviors of perovskite materials. Sr2+ (1.44 Å, CN = 12), owing to its large ionic radius, more effectively drives the tolerance factor (t) of Sr-based perovskites toward 1, aligning with an ideal perovskite structure and thereby ensuring structural stability16,27. Furthermore, the absence of 6s2 lone-pair electrons in Sr²⁺ suppresses second-order Jahn-Teller (SOJT) distortions, ensuring a symmetric lattice and stabilizing Sr-based perovskites like SrTiO3 against A-site-induced transitions to lower-symmetry phases28. Conversely, Bi3+ (1.17 Å, CN = 12) features a stereochemically active 6s2 lone pair29, facilitating off-center displacements that disrupt lattice symmetry while simultaneously contributing to a tunable local polarization field and a remarkably high polarizability of 6.12 Å3,30. By judiciously combining Sr2+ and Bi3+, a delicate balance can be achieved between structural rigidity and local polarization, offering a tunable approach to optimizing dielectric properties while maintaining phase stability at elevated temperatures.

Here, finite-temperature ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations combined with subsequent density functional theory-based (DFT) electronic-structure calculations were performed to investigate the structural evolution, dielectric properties, and bandgap (Eg) variation of the Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 system under elevated temperatures. The results reveal that this perovskite dielectric maintains a robust cubic structure across elevated temperatures, undergoing only minimal thermal expansion. The dielectric constant remains within 60–88 over a broad temperature range (25–500 °C), while the Eg shows only a moderate reduction from 2.5 eV to 2.07 eV. The high-symmetry structure ensures stable capacitance and intrinsic breakdown strength at elevated temperatures, confirming the theoretical feasibility of our design. In situ XRD measurements confirm that the cubic phase persists at high temperatures, aligning well with DFT predictions. Crucially, a series of electrical measurements and structural characterizations collectively verify the linear dielectric behaviors of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3. Furthermore, the cold sintering process (CSP) was employed to enhance densification, yielding an exceptional breakdown strength of 1150 kV cm–1 and boosting the energy storage density to 4.9 J cm–3 with the efficiency of 87.1%. Notably, even under an ultrahigh field of 800 kV cm–1 and a harsh temperature of 270 °C, the ceramic maintains an energy density of 2.2 J cm–3 with an efficiency exceeding 84%.

Results and discussion

Computational insights into electrical performance

The stability of the perovskite structure is foundational, wherein a prescribed (Mg1/3Nb2/3)4+ is implemented on the B-site to establish a 1:2 ordered configuration for stabilizing the BO6 oxygen octahedra, while the structural robustness influenced by (Sr1-1.5xBix)²⁺ ion pair variations at the A-site is quantified through the tolerance factor (t)29. For the B-site composition constrained, the dynamic variation of the A-site dual cations becomes more tractable, yielding the variation of the tolerance factor (Fig. 1a). The ionic configuration of (Sr1-1.5xBix)2+, inevitably destabilizes the perovskite structure as Bi incorporation increases, as evidenced by a gradual decline in the tolerance factor. Considering the trade-off between structural stability and polarizability, Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 with t of 0.917 was chosen as the representative composition, and thus a 2 × 2 × 3 supercell was constructed based on its atomic configuration (Sr9Bi2Mg4Nb8O36) (Fig. 1b). Finite-temperature ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed to capture thermally driven structural evolution, followed by density functional theory-based calculations on equilibrated configurations to evaluate the electronic structure and dielectric response. It should be noted that the present temperature-dependent dielectric and band-gap trends primarily reflect thermal lattice expansion effects captured by AIMD, while explicit electronic temperature effects (e.g., electron–phonon coupling) are not included in the static calculations. All computations were carried out using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)31,32, with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional33 and the projector augmented wave (PAW) method34. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 450 eV was applied, with energy and force convergence criteria of 10–6 eV and 0.02 eV·Å−1, respectively. Across six representative temperatures (25–500 °C), ab initio molecular dynamics simulations reveal only a slight and monotonic lattice expansion in Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3. The averaged c-axis lattice constant, obtained by dividing the supercell length by three, increases smoothly from 4.0715 Å at room temperature to 4.0962 Å at 500 °C (Fig. 1c). Dielectric properties were evaluated based on the thermally equilibrated structures obtained from ab initio molecular dynamics simulations. Given the tensorial nature of the dielectric response, the orientation dependence of the dielectric permittivity was analyzed along different crystallographic directions. The total static dielectric constant comprises electronic and lattice-mediated ionic contributions, which were decomposed within density functional perturbation theory according to εtotal = ε∞ + εion, where ε∞ and εion denote the respective contributions from electronic and ionic polarizations to the dielectric tensor35,36. Figure 1d summarizes the electronic contribution to the permittivity of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 at 25 °C along the coordinate axes, while Fig. 1e visualizes the anisotropic dielectric ellipsoid arising from ionic contributions, clearly revealing the dominant role of ionic polarization (Fig. S2). Likewise, Fig. 1f and g present the electronic and ionic components of the permittivity at 500 °C, respectively. It is evident that the permittivity remains stable without significant fluctuations, even at elevated temperatures. In general, the average permittivity (εr = ∑i=x,y,z εifi) can be evaluated by statistically weighting directional contributions, and applying the volumetric averaging assumption under an isotropic approximation with equal weighting factors (fx,a = fy,b = fz,c = 1/3)37 (Fig. 1h). In addition, for thermal-stable capacitive energy storage, electrical insulation performance at elevated temperatures is a critical factor. Despite the inevitable presence of various weaknesses (e.g., chemical defects, porosity, and uneven grain distribution) in bulk samples, theoretical evaluation of the Eg remains a valuable approach for understanding the intrinsic breakdown strength. Notably, as shown in Fig. 1i and Fig. S3, even over a temperature span of 475 °C, the permittivity exhibits a minimal variation of only ±20%, accompanied by only a moderate decrease in Eg from 2.5 to 2.07 eV. From a theoretical perspective, these results validate that the A/B dual-cation system design effectively suppresses symmetry deviations at elevated temperatures, thereby ensuring the stability of both high permittivity and breakdown strength.

Structural mechanism of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics

The XRD analysis of a series of Sr1-1.5xBixMg1/3Nb2/3O3 bulk ceramics synthesized via solid-state reaction reveals that the modulation of (Sr1-1.5xBix)²⁺ drives a structural transition from a medium-symmetry hexagonal phase (P-3m1) to a high-symmetry cubic phase (Fd-3m) (Fig. S4), ultimately yielding a coexistence of two distinct cubic phases (Fig. 2a). In addition, the cold-sintered samples exhibit pronounced multiphase coexistence (Fig. S5, 6), which is closely associated with the dissolution–reprecipitation mechanism. Relative density evaluation pinpointed cold sintering at 270 °C combined with annealing at 1170 °C as the optimal processing route (Fig. S7). XRD Rietveld refinement further indicates that the progressive incorporation of Bi³⁺ induces a gradual transformation from cubic phase-1 to cubic phase-2 (Table S1), highlighting its dual role in promoting both structural evolution and local polarization. As previously discussed, the high symmetry of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics stems from the B-site ion configuration, making it crucial to characterize the distribution of Mg and Nb ions. The ordered structure features a trigonal supercell with a 1:2 {···Mg-Nb-Nb···} repeat sequence along the <111> direction of the parent cubic cell (Fig. 2b1), with the BI and BII columns exclusively occupied by Mg and Nb cations, respectively. Based on the high-resolution TEM image along the <110> zone axis (Fig. 2b2), the inverse Fourier filtered image reveals a well-ordered periodic structure (Fig. 2b3), indicating high local symmetry. Additionally, the bright spots from strongly scattering Sr/Bi columns and the darker spots from weaker Mg/Nb columns form a periodic contrast (Fig. 2b4), yet this alone is insufficient to confirm the 1:2 ordering induced by Mg/Nb. We thus identified the presence of 1/2(111)-type reflections by rotating the zone axis to <110 > , <111 > , and <012> directions, with the <111> direction distinctly exhibiting a characteristic 1:2 ordered superlattice reflection pattern (Fig. 2b5-b7)25,38. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns along the <001> zone axis confirm the (Pm-3m) symmetry corresponding to cubic phase-1 (Fig. 2c1-c2), while the (Fd- 3m) symmetry was also identified in the region marked in Fig. 2c3-c4. High-resolution TEM and high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) Z-contrast STEM was utilized to characterize the atomic-scale microstructure, thereby offering insights into the A/B-site occupancy as well as the perovskite structure (Fig. 2d). The periodic atomic arrangement in the HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 2d1) confirms the perovskite framework, prompting further analysis of the selected region for B-site column intensities (Fig. 2d2) and corresponding displacement mappings (Fig. 2d3-d4). The B-site column intensity profile suggests the presence of short-range B-site ordering. Traces of nano-domains can be discerned by analyzing the projected displacements of B-site cations (Mg/Nb, exhibiting weaker intensity contrast) relative to the lattice centers of their four neighboring A-site cations (Sr/Bi, with stronger intensity contrast)39,40. Surprisingly, the displacement mapping of B-site cations reveals subtle yet co-directional shifts, indicative of a highly symmetric and domain-free structure. The in-situ XRD analysis in Fig. 2e tracks the phase evolution of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 samples from 25 to 1000 °C, complemented by simultaneous thermal analysis via TG-DSC (Fig. S9). Strikingly, no transition from the identified cubic phases to any non-cubic counterparts was detected. Only a progressive shift of diffraction peaks toward lower angles was observed. This low-angle shift is not limited to the representative peak at 45.2 ° but occurs consistently across the entire measured range, signifying lattice thermal expansion and affirming the structural stability. The analysis of B-O bonds is crucial for assessing octahedral rigidity, with Mg-O and Nb-O bond evolution derived from XRD Rietveld refinements (Fig. S10). Identical thermal vibration parameters for Nb and Mg at the B-site were justified using Nb-O bonds to represent the B-site environment (Table S2). In-situ Raman spectroscopy was performed on SBMN (Fig. 2f and Fig. S11) to further verify its structural symmetry and thermal stability. The low-frequency region (50–150 cm⁻¹), associated with A-site vibrations and lattice dynamics, evolves smoothly with temperature42. The mid-frequency bands (200–500 cm⁻¹), related to octahedral bending modes, show no mode splitting or activation43. Notably, the high-frequency BO6 stretching band (780–850 cm⁻¹) persists up to 600 °C, indicating a robust octahedral framework43. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was further performed to verify the chemical-state stability and defect suppression in Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 (Fig. S12). The Nb 3 d spectra display well-defined doublets characteristic of Nb5+, with no signatures of reduced Nb species, reflecting a chemically robust NbO6 framework44. O 1 s fitting reveals a dominant lattice-oxygen component with only a weak defect-related contribution, indicative of a low oxygen-vacancy activity45. Meanwhile, Sr 3 d, Bi 4 f, and Mg 1 s signals agree well with the expected Sr2+, Bi3+, and Mg2+ states46, supporting stable cation chemistry and minimized defect-assisted conduction.

To uncover how structural thermal stability influences the intrinsic permittivity behavior, dielectric properties were evaluated over a temperature range from -150 to 450 °C (Fig. 2g, Figs. S13, 14), unveiling not only a moderate permittivity akin to that of CaTiO3 ceramics but also an extraordinary thermal insensitivity. Specifically, Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 exemplifies a remarkably stable permittivity of 50 (±2%) across the entire temperature range, whereas Sr0.4Bi0.4Mg1/3Nb2/3O3, benefiting from enhanced local polarization, exhibits an elevated relative permittivity of up to 100 while still maintaining a minimal fluctuation of ±7.6% (Fig. S13). Beyond permittivity, its exceptionally low dielectric loss of 0.0007 (1 kHz) at room temperature, combined with its outstanding thermal stability, is equally impressive. The remarkable thermal stability and ultra-low dielectric loss are likely due to the inherent high symmetry of the cubic phase, which suppresses octahedral distortions at elevated temperatures and thereby mitigates defect aggregation within the crystal. In addition, the annealing-assisted cold-sintered Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics exhibit both comparable dielectric stability and enhanced permittivity (~72), primarily attributed to the formation of a weakly polar tetragonal phase (Sr2Bi2Mg0.5Nb2.5O12, ~21.3%) arising from reactions of cold-sintered intermediate phases (Fig. S15). The formation of secondary phases can be traced to the “dissolution–precipitation” mechanism intrinsic to CSP47,48, despite the chemical cleanliness of formic acid. Analysis of the frequency-dependent dielectric response demonstrates that the dielectric behavior is predominantly governed by ionic or electronic polarization, as evidenced by the steadfastly invariant permittivity across the entire frequency range (Fig. S14). The same phenomenon was further corroborated by frequency-dependent conductivity and dielectric loss measurements (Fig. S16). In general, Maxwell-Wagner interfacial polarization is mainly activated at low frequencies (typically <104 Hz), whereas dipolar orientational relaxation may extend toward higher frequencies (up to ~106 Hz) depending on relaxation dynamics16. Therefore, the absence of any detectable dielectric dispersion or relaxation signatures over the entire frequency window suggests that the dielectric response is dominated by intrinsic ionic and electronic displacements, consistent with the linear dielectric nature of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3.

Linear dielectric properties of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3

Dielectric materials can be classified based on whether there is a domain structure, where the absence of ferroelectric domains is a defining feature of linear dielectrics. Employing the ‘square-loop’ voltage writing mode (inset of Fig. 3a) as a representative approach49, both AC bias (Fig. 3a, f) and DC bias (Fig. 3b, c, g, h) were applied to probe domain evolution in Sr1-1.5xBixMg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics, where micron/submicron-scale domains would manifest as distinct contrast variations. However, regardless of the applied voltage modes, domains-free features were observed, a phenomenon consistently present across all compositions (Fig. S17–18). To rule out the possibility that an insufficient voltage prevents the hypothetical domains from overcoming the domain wall energy barrier and moving, the applied voltage was progressively increased to 120 V, which is adequate to trigger the hypothetical domains, given that prior studies have demonstrated 90 °/180° switching of nano/microscale domains under voltages as low as 50 V50,51. A prominent signal (blue line) detected in Fig. 3a–c hints at the possible presence of domains. Accordingly, a cross-sectional linear scan across the 5 × 5 μm² region (Fig. 3d) was conducted to analyze the phase difference, particularly focusing on the suspected domain-like signal. However, along the entire scanning path, phase signals remain below 60 °, except for a single abrupt shift exceeding 90 °, which arises from topographical variations induced by surface roughness (Fig. 3e). The angles of domain walls are predominantly governed by the crystal system and the number of available polarization states, manifesting as characteristic angles such as 90 ° and 180 ° in the tetragonal phase, 71 °, 109 °, and 180 ° in the rhombohedral phase, and 60 °, 90 ° in the orthorhombic phase52–54. In conjunction with the Piezoresponse Force Microscopy (PFM) results, we preliminarily infer that the Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 and Sr0.40Bi0.40Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics (Fig. 3f–h) are devoid of ferroelectric domains, or at the very least, lack large-angle domain walls and micron-scale domain structures. For nano-scale domains, their diminutive size poses significant challenges for detection via PFM, making high-resolution TEM the most effective approach for directly visualizing their morphology (Fig. 3i–k). At a 500 nm scale, no moiré fringes were observed within the triangular region, further confirming the absence of nano domains. Generally, dielectrics in the cubic-dominated system, such as those in a high-temperature paraelectric state, inherently lack domains due to their high symmetry. Exemplary cases include BaTiO3, PbTiO3, and SrTiO3, which adopt a cubic phase at elevated temperatures, precluding the formation of spontaneous polarization. Revisiting the XRD results, Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectric ceramics exhibit coexistence of cubic phases across the entire temperature range, which naturally accounts for the absence of nano- and micron-scale domains. The high symmetry of the mixed cubic phase can be rationalized by the co-directional displacement of B-site cations (Fig. 3l). In symmetry-lowered polar or antipolar perovskites, displacement vectors typically exhibit multi-variant textures such as antiparallel alternation, periodic modulations, or domain-variant switching, reflecting symmetry breaking and the development of long-range ordered distortion modes55. In contrast, the displacement field in Fig. 3l shows a narrow magnitude distribution without evident alternation or domain-like rotation (Fig. S19), indicating that any local off-centering does not evolve into long-range polar order and is therefore consistent with a preserved average cubic high-symmetry structure39,40. The polarization and current responses under high electric fields further corroborate the identification of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics as a linear dielectric. Under a bias field ranging from 100 to 500 kV cm–1, the P–E hysteresis loops of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 remained highly linear, a characteristic also evident in other compositions, as shown in Fig. 3m and Fig. S20. Moreover, the current density-electric field (J–E) curve under the same conditions remains an exceptionally flat profile, devoid of any current peaks, further affirming its linear dielectric characteristics. More importantly, the near-zero remnant polarization under bipolar bias fields, coupled with its high breakdown endurance, underscores its immense potential for dielectric energy storage applications.

Energy storage performances of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics

The Eb of dielectric ceramics exhibits a significant increase with rising Bi content, surging from 595 kV cm–1 (x = 0.1) to 860 kV cm–1 (x = 0.2). However, exceeding x = 0.2 results in a noticeable decline, indicating that excessive Bi content compromises the uniformity of the internal electric field. The trade-off between Eb and Pmax is evident, as excessive Bi incorporation, despite reducing Eb, facilitates a steady increase in polarization—e.g., from 3 μC cm–2 (x = 0.1) to 7.6 μC cm–2 (x = 0.4) under 400 kV cm–1 (Fig. S20). Thermal stability of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics

For dielectric energy storage under harsh temperatures, an excellent dielectric breakdown strength functions as a fundamental prerequisite, yet this remains a significant challenge for most polycrystalline ceramics. Phase-field simulations visualize the internal electric field distribution in Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics when subjected to an identical electric field of 860 kV cm–1 across the temperature range of 25–300 °C (Fig. 5a), using experimentally determined thermal parameters (Fig. 5b and Fig. S27). At 25 °C (Figs. 5a3 and 5a4), the internal electric field remains uniformly distributed without conspicuous localized field intensification, with grain boundaries absorbing higher field concentrations. In contrast, rising temperatures accelerate breakdown, evident from the reduced breakdown time. Referring to breakdown paths of identical depth (Fig. 5a4, a6, a8), the breakdown progression occurs within 5.6 μs at 300 °C, whereas it extends to 9.6 μs at 100 °C. Elevated temperatures inevitably accelerate carrier dynamics within the dielectric, a well-established phenomenon. In the case of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 linear dielectrics, the highly symmetric cubic-dominated phase not only minimizes defect accumulation but also ensures a more uniform electric field distribution, thereby enhancing dielectric field stability. On the other hand, linear dielectrics like Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3, lacking polar nanoregions (PNRs), sacrifice high polarization strength but inherently resist electric breakdown triggered by domain switching at elevated temperatures. This phenomenon is reflected in the conductivity-time curve depicted in Fig. 5b, where one can observe that the conductivity remains as low as 4×10–15 S m–1 at 25 °C before increasing to 1.22×10–11 S m–1 at 300 °C. Notably, at 300 °C, the Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 sample exhibits the best insulation performance among all compositions (Fig. S28). Simultaneously, the temperature coefficient of capacitance (TCC) is another critical parameter for evaluating high-temperature capacitive energy storage performance, which can be quantified through temperature-dependent permittivity60. It is worth noting that the electric field of 800 kV cm–1 at high temperature closely approaches the room-temperature Weibull breakdown field of 860 kV cm–1. This behavior is inherently associated with its linear dielectric characteristics, fundamentally driven by the construction of a high-symmetry hybrid cubic phase. Without localized polarization clusters or intrinsic spontaneous polarization, linear dielectrics exhibit rapid depolarization upon the removal of the external electric field. From the standpoint of dielectric loss, Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectric exhibits remarkably low tanδ of 0.002 throughout the broad temperature range of −150 to 300 °C, offering a compelling rationale for the minimal Pr observed at high electric fields and elevated temperatures. Furthermore, The P–E loops were measured under a thermal field with an applied electric field of 700 kV cm⁻¹ to systematically investigate its thermal endurance (Fig. S29). Evidently, across the wide temperature range of 25 to 275 °C (step: 25 °C), the P–E loops remain distinctly slender, signifying that increasing temperature fails to induce excessive remnant polarization, thereby sustaining superior energy storage performance. As shown in Fig. 5f, the Wrec decreases slightly from 2.06 J cm–3 at 25 °C to 1.93 J cm–3 at 275 °C, alongside an ~11% fluctuation in η. In addition, Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 maintains a high energy-storage efficiency exceeding 80% even after prolonged cycling up to 106 cycles (Fig. S31). This superior energy storage performance validates the strategic role of the high-symmetry hybrid cubic phase in enhancing dielectric energy storage capabilities at elevated temperatures.

In summary, we demonstrate a symmetry-engineered design that stabilizes the cubic phase and enhances the thermal stability of Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics for capacitive energy storage under harsh conditions. The Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 dielectrics maintain a stable permittivity (57–65) and a high bandgap (exceeding 3.139 eV) over 25–500 °C, confirming the phase robustness predicted by DFT calculations. With cold sintering optimization, an exceptional breakdown strength of 1150 kV cm–1 enables a high energy density of 4.9 J cm–3 and efficiency of 87.1%, while retaining 2.2 J cm–3 at 270 °C under 800 kV cm–1 with efficiency of 84%. The symmetry-driven approach minimizes defect accumulation and ensures uniform field distribution, providing an effective paradigm for high-temperature linear dielectrics. This strategy provides a promising route for next-generation capacitors, addressing the trade-off between energy density and thermal stability in extreme environments.

Methods

Ceramic fabrication

A series of Sr1-1.5xBixMg1/3Nb2/3O3 ceramics (x = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4) were synthesized via the conventional solid-state reaction using high-purity Bi2O3 (99%), TiO2 (98%), SrCO3 (99%), MgO (99.9%), and Nb2O5 (99.8%) (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) as raw materials. The precursor powders were weighed according to the designed stoichiometry and ball-milled at 300 rpm for 12 h. The slurry was thoroughly dried and then calcined at 850 °C for 5 h to initiate phase formation. Subsequently, the calcined powders underwent a second ball-milling process in anhydrous ethanol for 8 h to further refine the particle size. After drying, the powders were granulated with a 3 wt% aqueous solution of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as a binder. The granulated powders were then compacted into pellets (12 mm in diameter) via cold isostatic pressing at 200 MPa. Finally, the green bodies were sintered at 1190 °C–1280 °C for 2 h to achieve densification.

Cold sintering process

The precursor powder for cold-sintered Sr0.7Bi0.2Mg1/3Nb2/3O3 ceramics was derived from the calcined powder prepared at 850 °C in the solid-state synthesis. The precursor powder was mixed with glacial formic acid in a 2:1 mass ratio and finely milled. The resulting slurry was pressed into 12 mm pellets under 300 MPa, followed by cold sintering at 270 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the cold-sintered samples (CSP-270) were transferred to a muffle furnace and annealed at 1170 °C to enhance ion solubility and relieve residual stress, ultimately yielding high-performance linear dielectrics.

Dielectric and electrical characterization

The temperature- and frequency-dependent dielectric properties (−150 to 450 °C, 1 kHz to 1 MHz) were analyzed using a broadband dielectric spectrometer (Novocontrol Concept 80, Germany). Temperature-dependent conductivity was measured under an applied voltage of 800 V using a high-resistance meter (TH2690, Tonghui, China) combined with a thermal stage (Huace 650 T, China) at 25 °C, 100 °C, 200 °C, and 300 °C. The breakdown strength (Eb) was measured with a high-voltage test system (PK CPE2020-AI-20 kV, PolyK Technologies, USA) at a ramp rate of 500 V s–1. current–electric field (J–E) loops and polarization–electric field (P–E) loops were acquired under various electric fields, frequencies, temperatures, and cycle numbers using the same ferroelectric test system. The ferroelectric measurements were conducted on samples with a thickness of 50–70 μm, using gold electrodes with an area of 0.785 mm² (1 mm in diameter). The overdamped (1000 Ω) charge/discharge tests were conducted according to Tongguo (TG) technology (CFD003, China).

Structural and thermophysical characterization

The ultraviolet and visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra (200–800 nm) were measured using a UV-9000 spectrometer (METASH, China) with BaSO₄ as reference. Phase structures at both room temperature and elevated temperatures were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Empyrean, PANalytical B.V., Netherlands) using Cu Kα radiation with a scanning rate of 5 °/min. Temperature-dependent XRD patterns were collected from 25 °C to 1000 °C at 100 °C intervals, with a 5-minute isothermal hold prior to each scan. The Rietveld refinements of XRD were analyzed using the program GSAS II. In-situ Raman spectroscopy was performed using a Renishaw inVia Qontor system (UK) with a 633 nm excitation laser over the temperature range of 25–600 °C. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out on a Thermo Fisher Scientific instrument (USA). Thermal diffusivity and specific heat capacity were determined via laser flash analysis (LFA467HT, Netzsch, Germany). Thermogravimetric (TG) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analyses were performed using a simultaneous thermal analyzer (TGA/DSC1/1600LF, METTLER TOLEDO, Switzerland). Domain morphology and surface features were imaged by PFM (Cypher ES, Asylum Research) under 10 V AC and 30–120 V DC biases. Microstructure and elemental distribution were examined using SEM (Carl Zeiss) equipped with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). TEM (FEI Talos F200X) was used to observe morphology and acquire SAED patterns, and operated in STEM mode to obtain atomic-scale HAADF images. The precise atomic positions in STEM images were elucidated through 2D Gaussian fitting.

First-principles simulations

All first-principles calculations were carried out within the framework of density functional theory (DFT) using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The interactions between valence electrons and ionic cores were described by the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method, and the exchange–correlation functional was treated within the generalized gradient approximation in the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) form. To ensure sufficient convergence of the stress tensor and total energy, a uniform plane-wave cutoff energy of 450 eV was employed throughout all calculations. To overcome the intrinsic limitations of static approximations in capturing thermal expansion and anharmonic lattice effects, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed under the isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble to rigorously evaluate lattice dynamics at finite temperatures. This approach allows the simulation cell parameters and atomic positions to relax simultaneously, thereby realistically capturing temperature-driven structural evolution. The simulations were conducted over a temperature range from 298 K to 773 K. At each temperature point, the system was evolved until thermodynamic equilibrium was achieved, as evidenced by steady-state oscillations of the total energy and lattice constants around their equilibrium values. Statistically sufficient configurations were then extracted from the equilibrated trajectories for subsequent structural analyses. Based on the thermalized structures obtained from AIMD, further static calculations were performed to evaluate the electronic density of states (DOS), band gap, and dielectric response properties. In these self-consistent calculations, the total energy convergence criterion was tightened to 10−6 eV, and the residual force threshold was set to 0.02 eV Å−1. A Gaussian smearing of 0.05 eV was applied to facilitate electronic convergence.
