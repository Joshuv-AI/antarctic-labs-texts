Background

Prostate cancer is the second leading cause of death in the USA after heart disease. Metastasis often involves the axial skeleton (84%), pelvic lymph nodes (10.6%), liver (10.2%), and thorax (9.1%), with the remainder occurring at multiple sites (18.4%) [1–3]. Bone metastasis of the skull is a known complication of metastatic prostate cancer; however, it is uncommon for patients to develop leptomeningeal involvement, which is mostly found in the dura mater [4]. A total of 1–6% of prostate cancer leptomeningeal metastasis are found in post mortem examinations [5]. Neurologic complications of metastasis include localized back pain (90–95%), weakness (75–80%), autonomic dysfunction (57%), and sensory changes (50%) when present in the spinal cord. Brain metastasis complications are limited to headaches (34%), motor deficits (26%), altered mental status (23%), and seizures (8%), and are at times asymptomatic [6]. The diagnosis of prostate cancer is based on elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels and tissue biopsy. Metastatic prostate cancer presenting with aphasia is rare (3%) and not commonly seen [7]. We report a 60-year-old male patient who developed aphasia with nonspecific magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings concerning for diffuse irregular dural/pachymeningeal thickening and enhancement, suspicious for prostate cancer metastasis.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old Caucasian male was diagnosed with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer with extensive sclerotic bone metastases and soft tissue metastases in the left mesorectum. The diagnosis of prostate cancer is based on elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels and tissue biopsy. Patient underwent multiple courses of hormone and chemotherapy including enzalutamide and rucaparib on clinical trial A031902 (started 09/19/22). The patient was on treatment when prior and then developed expressive aphasia. Past medical history includes right ear fluctuating hearing loss with atypical Meniere’s, status post-intratympanic steroid injection two times and chronic subdural hematoma. Family history is significant for bipolar disorder type 1 in his brother and sister, Crohn’s disease in his brother, dementia in his mother; depression in his brother and mother, myocardial infarction in his father, hyperlipidemia in his father and mother, rheumatoid arthritis in his mother, and urolithiasis in his mother. Physical exam is significant for mild bradykinesia and rigidity but pronounced bilateral lower extremity weakness, which was mild and diffuse, and slightly more pronounced on the left. Patient reports double vision with lateral gaze and vertical gaze extremes, as well as decreased sensation on the chin and lower lip. He also has sensorineural hearing loss in the right ear. Computed tomography (CT) and MRI evaluations were unremarkable for evidence of acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke and CT angiogram was unremarkable for vessel occlusion (Fig. 1a and b). MRI showed nonspecific, diffuse irregular dural/pachymeningeal thickening enhancement (Fig. 2a and b). Radiology and oncology reviewed MRI imaging and suggested leptomeningeal disease involvement is likely, although was unable to be confirmed by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology due to thrombocytopenia (Fig. 3).

In the 18 months prior to his presentation, his disease progressed despite treatment including leuprolide, apalutamide, enzalutamide with rucaparib on a clinical trial, docetaxel, palliative radiotherapy to the spine and femur, and radium-223. Abiraterone was started. Laboratory work-up was unremarkable and did not explain the patient’s speech disturbance, which included difficulty initiating a sentence, delayed speech with stuttering quality, and difficulty with articulation. Upon evaluation by neurology, electroencephalogram (EEG) was negative for an epileptiform correlate. The workup included a lumbar puncture to rule out infectious etiology. Laboratory values of the patient’s prostate-specific antigen were increasing despite multiple prior lines of treatment.

The patient’s neurological status stabilized, and he was discharged home with a plan for continued therapy with abiraterone and prednisone. Due to advanced malignancy, the patient enrolled in hospice and died 3 weeks after hospital discharge. Autopsy was not performed.

Discussion and conclusions

Leptomeningeal metastasis occurs in approximately 5% of patients with prostate cancer and typically indicates a very poor prognosis [8]. The most common brain metastases are from organs such as the lungs, kidneys, breasts, melanomas, and colorectal neoplasms [9, 10]. Despite the high prevalence of prostate cancer, metastasis to the leptomeninges is uncommon and is usually seen in patients with other osseous and soft tissue metastases. In a study of 16,280 patients with prostate carcinoma, only 131 patients had craniospinal metastasis, which was then further divided into intraparenchymal metastasis (103 or 79%), dural metastasis (28 or 21%), and leptomeningeal disease (5, or 4%) [7]. Of these 131 patients, the most common symptom or finding was headaches (36%), delirium (51%), memory deficits (17%), asymptomatic (11%), and cranial nerve VII or other lower cranial nerve neuropathies (9%). Other symptoms and findings, which ranged from 3% to 7% of cases, included diplopia, hemiparesis, visual field cuts, seizures, ataxia, and finally aphasia. Aphasia was present in 3 of the 131 patients, accounting for the least common finding in patients with craniospinal metastasis from prostate carcinoma [9]. Evaluations including CSF cytology and histopathology are required for confirmation of diagnosis, which was limited in our patient due to thrombocytopenia and patient’s desire for no autopsy. Using imaging findings and clinical course, our suspicion was that leptomeningeal metastasis was a possible cause.

Our patient underwent extensive therapy that included leuprolide, apalutamide, enzalutamide with rucaparib on a clinical trial, docetaxel, palliative radiotherapy to the spine and femur, and radium-223. These therapeutic options demonstrate the growing advances in medical oncology, which have increased life expectancy. As patients live longer, metastatic disease may progress in unorthodox locations and patients may display unique clinical presentations. A single-institution retrospective observational study assessed the incidence of brain metastases before and after the docetaxel therapy regimen, finding that metastases to the brain occurred in 0.8% of patients in the pre-docetaxel time versus 2.8% in post-docetaxel therapy availability [10]. With the now robust and multiple therapeutic options for prostate cancer, the incidence of leptomeningeal metastases is expected to rise, leading to the importance of further studies and evaluations discussing prostate cancer and brain metastasis.

Due to the scarcity of leptomeningeal metastases in prostate cancer, diagnostic approaches involving brain imaging are not always indicated. Even with multiple available diagnostic approaches, which include integrated positron emission tomography (patch), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanners, and prostate-specific membrane antigen with 18 F labeled and 68 GA labeled agents, central nervous system metastases in prostate cancer remains a challenging diagnosis [11]. In an 11-year analysis of imaging characteristics of leptomeningeal metastases from prostate cancer, the MRI appearance was highly variable and often led to a poor prognosis. Of the 21 patients studied, the most common sites of parenchymal brain metastases were the frontal lobes, which were involved in 86% of patients. The enhancement pattern varied from purely solid to mixed cystic and solid to ringlike [12]. In these 21 patients, only 7 had pathologically proven metastasis. In our patient, the nonspecific findings of diffuse irregular dural/pachymeningeal thickening and enhancement did not provide a clear diagnosis and were not seen in the aforementioned study of the frontal lobe as the most involved region. Similar limitations of inability to obtain histopathology were present to confirm diagnosis.
