Should Weight be Given Less Weight in Public Health?
Authors: Cal Paterson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity is a complex chronic illness of adiposity, yet body weight and obesity are routinely conflated in public health wording and policy design. This paper examines a recent recommendation from the Australian College of Nursing (ACN) to combat obesity via the routine collection and discussion of weight and height data of all school-aged children in the country. The obesity-specific ethical framework developed by ten Have and colleagues (2013) has been used to critique the ACN proposal, and to propose a shift away from weighing people, particularly young people, in health promotion activities for obesity.

Introduction

Obesity remains a worldwide epidemic of non-communicable disease, with complex aetiology (Vandenbroeck et al., 2007), and multitudes of public health policies, programmes and initiatives seeking its prevention or reduction. Obesity is a disease of adiposity, rather than body mass, yet weight and obesity are conflated in public health wording and policy design (e.g. National Library of Medicine, 2016; World Health Organisation, 2025), and the Body Mass Index (BMI) continues to be advocated for by the World Health Organisation as the definitive measure of obesity and its risk (World Health Organisation, 2011). However, for both ethical and evidentiary reasons the clinical community is moving away from solely relying on BMI as a measure of obesity (Mallik et al., 2023; Suthahar et al., 2025; The Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology, 2025).

In September 2024 the Australian College of Nursing (2024, p. 3) (ACN) released a position statement, with the aim of improving the prevention and treatment of obesity and ‘tackling stigma’, while proposing ‘the normalisation of collection and discussion of height and weight data of all children, with longitudinal mapping on WHO-recommended growth charts’ (p. 3). This proposal, with its emphasis on individual weight and BMI as a means to curb obesity, provides a case example through which the ethics of weighing in public health can be examined. The proposal makes frequent reference to the evidence base for its recommendations but also contains the acknowledgement that ‘limited economic evidence has been put forward to evaluate these strategies’ (p. 2).

Health promotion policy in the context of body weight should be formulated using not only evidence, but ethical argument (Carter et al., 2011). To evaluate the ethics of the ACN proposal, and of weighing people more generally, eight obesity-specific ethical questions proposed by ten Have and colleagues (2013) have been adapted and applied below, allowing us to consider: does body weight deserve its current status as the definitive public health measure for obesity?

How Does Weighing Affect Health?

Obesity policymakers must balance their programme’s capacity to improve health against its potential adverse health effects. The accessibility and ubiquity of scales does not override consideration of their impact on the individuals being weighed. Weighing school-aged children can be done without physical risk, but carries the risk of psychological illness, particularly eating disorder (Hahn et al., 2021) and depression (Ogden and Evans, 1996). The ACN proposal makes no provision for the mitigation of these risks in the collection and discussion of weight and height data.

How Does Weighing Affect Psychosocial Well-being?

Shame, stigma and social isolation are routinely experienced by those with abnormal weight. These experiences of stigmatisation can in turn become a social determinant of obesity (Vartanian and Smyth, 2013). There is thus growing interest among professional and patient obesity advocacy and peak bodies in reducing weight-related stigma (Emmer et al., 2020; Kendrick et al., 2023). The ACN proposal acknowledges the problem of stigma but does not address how a programme focussed on body weight might mitigate or perpetuate stigma.

How Does Weighing Affect Equality?

The ACN proposal calls for the nurse-led collection of BMI for all children, regardless of individual circumstances, and the finite nature of nursing resources. Socioeconomic status and obesity are linked (Autret and Bekelman, 2024), and the principle of equity would behove a focus on lower socioeconomic areas, or groups known to be at greater risk of obesity. Obesity risk at a given body mass is higher in Asian populations (WHO Expert Consultation, 2004), and the health risks associated with higher weight have been observed to be proportionally greater in migrant populations (Murphy et al., 2017). There is thus both an ethical and an economic rationale for a more targeted rather than plenary approach to weight collection.

How Does Weighing Affect Informed Choice?

Problems are created when obesity-related programmes provide inaccurate or incomplete information about obesity and health behaviours. The ACN position paper, like many obesity policy documents, refers to obesity and weight interchangeably, perpetuating the misconception that obesity is a disorder related to body shape or size, rather than composition. Weighing patients is neither the only nor necessarily most accurate metric for screening of population obesity risk (Alruwaili et al., 2024), and risks oversimplifying obesity as an illness that is easily modifiable through individual health behaviours (ten Have et al., 2013). There are risks associated with weighing, as noted above, but there is no mention of consent in the proposal. The proposal refers to weighing in the context of nurse-led care, and there is thus scope for participants to be given choices about suitable methods of data collection around risk of chronic illness. There is a growing range of options for clinical assessment of obesity risk in children that do not involve anthropometry, such as behavioural or familial indicators (Alruwaili et al., 2024).

How Does Weighing Affect Social and Cultural Values?

Programmes that focus solely on altering health behaviours risk undermining cultural and social norms, practices, and ceremonies involving food (ten Have et al., 2013). Childhood and adolescence are periods characterised by a high degree of focus on social and peer acceptance. Adolescence is a developmental epoch filled with rites of passage, such as birthday parties, graduations, coming-of-age ceremonies and cultural festivals, and these milestones are routinely celebrated with food. The ACN proposal stipulates anthropometry and nurse-led behavioural intervention above other considerations for the wellbeing of the children it seeks to target.

How Does Weighing Affect Privacy?

The schoolyard is a context where privacy is extremely difficult to preserve. The track record of anthropometry in schools preserving student privacy is demonstrably poor (Wilson et al., 2023). The ACN proposal to ‘normalise’ weight collection is at odds with the privacy needs of young people at risk of obesity; while physically benign, weighing is nevertheless a medical procedure, and therefore subject to the concomitant level of medical confidentiality. If weighing is necessary in any public health programme, privacy should be preserved as it would be for any other medical procedure. The need to maintain individual privacy in the context of obesity prevention also has important intersections with shame and stigma (Puhl and Heuer, 2010).

How Does Weighing Affect the Attribution of Responsibilities?

Where should the responsibility for obesity rest: with the individual, with society, or a proportion of each? Regulatory interventions such as sugar taxes have lower implementation costs than programme-based interventions (Ananthapavan et al., 2020) and allocate the responsibility for obesity at its proper origin, but weight collection programmes such as the ACN proposal locate the problem at the level of the individual. Students being weighed who are then told they are healthy may mistakenly assume that the problem of obesity is no longer their concern.

How Does Weighing Affect Liberty?

Many people living with obesity hold sincere concerns about loss of personal identity and sense of self if they lose weight (Farrell et al., 2024). The ACN proposal emphasises the weighing of all children, and rests on the assumptions that those being weighed (i) are unaware of their weight, (ii) wish to act upon their weight data (iii) are able to act upon it and (iv) are at risk of obesity due to the lack of these things. Public health programmes for obesity should be developed with the recognition that obese individuals of any age are grappling with a complex, chronic problem they themselves did not cause, and have only limited individual control over.

Conclusion

Ethical considerations play an important and useful role in the formulation of public health policy for obesity, a complex illness with a limited evidentiary basis for action at the population level (Carter et al., 2011). When an obesity-informed ethical framework is applied to the practice of weighing, its limitations as a basis for health promotion initiatives such as that promoted by the ACN become apparent. It may be time to look for alternatives to weight as a measure of obesity risk, and to the use of weighing as a population-level intervention strategy. Alternatives must be sought that offer a more equitable, less stigmatising, and safer methodology, and which place the locus of responsibility outside, rather than upon the overweight person.
