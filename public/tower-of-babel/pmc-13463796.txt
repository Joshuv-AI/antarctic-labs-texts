Workflow and dosimetric results for direct-to-unit single-fraction adaptive lung SBRT delivered on the ONE-STOP prospective pilot clinical trial
Authors: Farnoush Forghani, Thomas R Mazur, Joshua P Schiff, Daniel Hong, Alden D'Souza, Ryan Mueller, Jessica Hamilton, Jennifer Harris, Tami Jamruk, Jessica Hilliard, Robbie Beckert, Amit Bhatt, Gregory R Vlacich, Clifford G Robinson, Pamela P Samson, Eric Laugeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDirect-to-unit (DTU) workflows coupled with online adaptive radiation therapy can streamline stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) by eliminating conventional simulation and prolonged treatment planning, thereby reducing treatment burden while maintaining high dosimetric quality.AimTo evaluate the feasibility and dosimetric robustness of a CBCT-guided DTU workflow for single-fraction lung SBRT.MethodsPatients with early-stage non–small cell lung cancer or oligometastatic lung disease were enrolled in this prospective pilot clinical trial. Patients received single-fraction SBRT (30–34 Gy) using a DTU workflow on a ring-gantry and CBCT-guided adaptive platform. Dosimetric quality was assessed by comparing target volumes and dosimetric indices between DTU plans generated on treatment CBCT and backup plans derived from 4DCT imaging.ResultsEight of ten patients (80%) were successfully treated using the DTU workflow, meeting the predefined feasibility endpoint. One patient was screen failed due to respiratory motion exceeding trial criteria. All DTU plans adhered to protocol requirements, meeting P1 organ-at-risk constraints and target coverage objectives when achievable, with plan quality comparable to simulation-based workflows. Treatment sessions were completed within 1.5 h. Introduction

Online adaptive radiation therapy (ART) has expanded rapidly in clinical practice through commercial platforms including magnetic resonance-guided radiotherapy (MRgRT) systems and cone-beam CT (CBCT) based platforms [1], [2], [3] that enable daily contouring and re-optimization. Clinical implementation of online adaptation has largely focused on mitigating dosimetric uncertainties from inter-fractional organ-at-risk (OAR) positional variation. By updating treatment plans to reflect daily anatomy, adaptation facilitates dose escalation to targets, reduces planning target volume (PTV) margins, and lowers OAR toxicity across anatomical sites [4], [5], [6], [7].

In parallel with expanded application of ART, direct-to-unit (DTU) or simulation-free treatment approaches are becoming increasingly popular for streamlining clinical workflows. These approaches forego conventional CT simulation and instead leverage prior imaging – typically diagnostic CT or PET/CT – to specify target volumes and support dose calculation. Until recently, DTU workflows have mostly been restricted to palliative treatments utilizing simple field geometries without inverse planning [8], [9]. These limitations stem from geometric and dosimetric uncertainties related to differences between diagnostic and treatment positioning, lack of immobilization, and the absence of electron density calibration on diagnostic scanners. Recent studies – including FAST-METS – have demonstrated that DTU workflows can support palliative IMRT and improve both treatment efficiency and patient satisfaction (10). However, using diagnostic imaging alone to support high-precision stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) remains largely unexplored clinically, with only limited and resource-intensive applications reported [11], [12], [13].

Stereotactic treatment for early-stage, medically inoperable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a well-established standard of care. While multi-fraction regimens are common, randomized data (e.g., RTOG 0915) demonstrate the safety and efficacy of single-fraction ablation (treated to 30–34 Gy) when strict localization and dosimetric constraints are met (14). Similarly, multi-center randomized data from the SAFFRON II trial in patients with pulmonary oligometastases demonstrated no significant differences in severe toxicity, local control, overall survival, or disease-free survival between single- and multi-fraction SBRT (15). Same-day consultation and single-fraction lung SBRT have been demonstrated with MRI-guidance, whereby intra-fractional imaging supported lesion tracking and gating (16). We postulate that a similar DTU lung SBRT workflow can be implemented on a CBCT-guided adaptive platform. Single-fraction DTU lung SBRT can substantially reduce treatment burden compared with conventional workflows, which require dedicated simulation and prolonged treatment planning and quality assurance. In contrast, DTU condenses this timeline into a single visit, reducing “time toxicity” and costs, particularly for patients in remote locations or those facing physical and social barriers to care (17).

Our institution recently completed accrual to a prospective pilot clinical trial – One Fraction Simulation-Free Treatment with CT-Guided Stereotactic Radiotherapy for Patients with Oligometastatic and Primary Lung Tumors (ONE-STOP) – evaluating the feasibility of a CBCT-guided DTU workflow for treating small and peripheral lung tumors via single-fraction SBRT. In this manuscript, we describe the workflow implemented for this protocol, utilizing a ring-gantry CBCT-based adaptive platform and summarize dosimetric quality for the patients treated on this study. A companion manuscript will describe clinical outcomes for patients treated on this study.

Methods and materials

Study overview

This prospective pilot clinical trial (NCT06236516) evaluated the feasibility of a DTU workflow for single-fraction lung SBRT. This study was performed with IRB approval (protocol no. 202401015) and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The primary outcome was feasibility, defined as successful treatment of ≥70% of enrolled patients using the ONE-STOP workflow, which if achieved would merit further exploration of DTU treatment. The study aimed to accrue ten evaluable subjects. Inclusion criteria required subjects to be ≥ 18 years old with histologically or radiographically diagnosed AJCC Stage I–IIA NSCLC or lung oligometastatic disease from any primary histology. Oligometastatic disease was defined as 1–5 metastatic sites or oligo-progressive lung disease in the setting of >5 total metastatic sites. Tumors were required to be ≤5 cm in maximum dimension, ≥2 cm from the proximal bronchial tree, and exhibit <1 cm superior-to-inferior motion on 4DCT. The motion threshold for screen failure was selected based on an institutional motion management threshold for treating patients without respiratory gating. Limiting respiratory motion served to 1) ensure that internal target volume (ITV) could be reliably captured with a slow CBCT acquisition and 2) preclude the need for respiratory gating (requiring breath-hold on the specific treatment platform) which would add undue complexity for the pilot study. No screening was performed based directly on lobular location. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, breastfeeding, or prior radiotherapy within the projected treatment field. All protocol treatments were delivered on a ring-gantry linear accelerator (Varian Ethos) equipped with a high-speed and large field-of-view CBCT imaging panel (Varian HyperSight) (18).

Pre-treatment preparation

For each subject, a diagnostic CT or CT from a PET/CT scan was retrieved to serve as a template for pre-planning, provided that the lesion be fully contained within the scan field-of-view. This diagnostic image – typically acquired without immobilization and on a curved non-indexed table-top – was imported into the Ethos treatment management system. On this static anatomy, a placeholder for an ITV was estimated to generate a base plan for downstream online adaptation. This preliminary ITV serves as a placeholder for defining an optimization template and preliminary plan prior to DTU. The final ITV is derived based on a slow CBCT acquisition obtained on the treatment unit as described below. A planning target volume (PTV) was generated as a derived 5 mm isotropic expansion of the ITV.

Optimization goals were configured using rule-based structures to maximize PTV coverage while strictly adhering to Priority 1 (P1) constraints for serial organs-at-risk (OARs), including stomach, brachial plexus, esophagus, spinal cord, chest wall, skin, trachea, bronchus, great vessels and heart. Protocol-specified planning goals and constraints utilized for this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Treatment geometry generally consisted of multiple partial arcs, selected based on lesion laterality to minimize entrance dose through the contralateral lung. Plans incorporated multiple fields to allow interrupting treatment delivery for intra-fraction verification CBCTs.

On the day of scheduled DTU treatment, 4DCT imaging was acquired to assess tumor motion amplitude in an imaging-only session. If superior-to-inferior motion exceeded 1 cm, patients were categorized as a screen failure and triaged to a conventional simulation-based workflow using an ITV defined on an average CT and treated on a dedicated SBRT unit (Varian Edge with HD MLC). Patients with motion ≤1 cm proceeded directly to DTU treatment, with 4DCT data retained for retrospective analysis. For all subjects, retrospective plans were generated on average CT datasets and compared with DTU plans prepared on CBCT imaging.

Direct-to-unit workflow

At the treatment machine, therapists positioned the patient in the immobilization device prepared at the imaging-only session. To accommodate the anticipated duration of the adaptive session while considering beam geometry, the ipsilateral arm was positioned above the head when tolerated, while the contralateral arm was typically lowered for comfort. Lasers were aligned to a stable anatomical landmark – most commonly the xiphoid process – which served as the reference for relative isocenter specification on the diagnostic pre-plan. When a surface rendering could be extracted from the diagnostic scan, setup was further guided by a surface-guided radiation therapy (SGRT) platform (Varian Identify).

After driving the treatment couch to the expected isocenter, a 60-s CBCT acquisition was performed using a pre-defined “Slow Thorax” protocol. This acquisition mode samples multiple respiratory cycles to visualize a blurry envelope representing the full extent of lesion motion. Image reconstruction was performed using the iCBCT Acuros algorithm, which applies a calibration curve to convert Hounsfield units to mass density, allowing direct dose calculation on the CBCT dataset. Numerous previous studies have validated dose calculation on Hypersight-based CBCT acquisitions [19], [20], [21]. In-house commissioning was performed to confirm the adequacy of an institutional calibration protocol between HU and mass density for clinical dose calculations on Hypersight-based CBCTs. Images were reviewed by the covering physicist and physician to confirm 1) adequate patient positioning relative to the isocenter, 2) sufficient field-of-view for anatomy specification and dose calculation, and 3) acceptable image quality for target and OAR visualization.

Following image acceptance, the ITV from the pre-plan was rigidly aligned to the target visualized on CBCT and manually edited by the covering physician to encompass the apparent motion envelope from the “Slow Thorax” acquisition. OAR contours were initialized automatically by onboard deep learning models and deformable image registration. All OARs within a defined expansion of the ITV (labeled the “Contour Ring”, typically an anisotropic expansion of ∼2 cm axially and 1 cm cranial-caudal) were manually reviewed and refined for accuracy by an advanced practice radiation therapist, physicist and physician.

Once contours were approved, plan optimization was performed to generate the DTU plan directly on the daily CBCT geometry. Unlike conventional adaptive radiotherapy, in which a re-optimized plan is compared to a scheduled plan, the re-optimized plan was invariably selected for treatment due to expected changes between diagnostic imaging and the CBCT-of-the-day. Plan approval criteria, in order of priority, included 1) strict adherence to all P1 OAR constraints, 2) PTV V100% ≥95%, 3) centralization of hot spots within the ITV and away from OARs, and 4) verification that monitor units (MUs) were consistent with clinical expectations (typically an MU ratio of 3–4 relative to prescription dose). A secondary dose calculation using an independent engine (Varian Mobius3D) verified plan fidelity prior to delivery, using a 3%/2 mm gamma criterion with a 10% dose threshold.

Following approval, patient position was updated via verification imaging, and intra-fractional motion was assessed using surface monitoring. After shifting the patient into the final treatment position, a new reference surface was captured. Mid-treatment CBCTs were acquired between fields to assess any intra-fractional motion not evident on surface monitoring. Upon completion of delivery, a final CBCT was acquired to document any residual shifts.

Dosimetry assessment

To quantify the dosimetric quality of the DTU workflow, we compared PTV volumes and dosimetric indices between DTU plans generated on the Ethos platform and backup plans derived from 4DCT acquisitions obtained for motion assessment. Evaluated metrics included PTV V100% and D95% (target coverage), ITV D99% (near-minimum dose to lesion), conformity index (CI), and R50. Total procedural timing was recorded from patient entry into the treatment vault until acquisition of the post-treatment CBCT.

To further assess robustness of the DTU approach with respect to ITV specification and CBCT dose calculation fidelity, cross-platform recalculations were performed. DTU and backup datasets were rigidly registered using the ITV. DTU plans were transferred to an average CT from the backup 4DCT, and backup plans were transferred to the DTU CBCT. In both scenarios, dose was recalculated and the aforementioned metrics extracted relative to the ITV as drawn on the respective calculation dataset.

Plan quality metrics were compared between DTU and backup plans using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for pairwise comparisons (p < 0.05 for significance). Uncertainty in median differences was quantified using bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples). Non-inferiority of the DTU plans relative to the backup plans was assessed using one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with pre-specified margins, including 5 percentage points for PTV V100%, 1 percentage point for ITV D99% (reflecting a stringent coverage requirement for ITV), 2 percentage points for PTV D95%, 0.10 for conformity index, and 0.30 for R50 (representing approximately 10% of the expected R50 value for typical lung SBRT cases). Non-inferiority was concluded when the one-sided p-value was less than 0.05. Post-hoc power analyses were performed using the non-central t-distribution approximation to estimate the minimum detectable effect size at 80% power and the sample size required to detect the observed effects. Cross-plan dosimetric robustness was assessed by recalculating each plan on the comparison anatomy: DTU plans were recalculated on backup CT datasets, and backup plans were recalculated on DTU CBCTs. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests compared to native and recalculated metrics for each plan type independently. A pre-specified sensitivity analysis was performed excluding one patient (Subject 8) for whom the ITV differed appreciably between CBCT and average CT scans likely due to differences in patient setup. All analyses were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA).

Results

Eight of ten accrued patients were successfully treated using the DTU workflow. Two patients did not receive DTU and were triaged to the conventional workflow: one subject exceeded the 1 cm tumor motion threshold on backup 4DCT (screen failure), and online plan optimization produced unacceptable dosimetry for the second subject. No statistically significant differences in target coverage were observed for PTV V100% (p = 0.55 with −2.2% median difference and 95%CI [−10.1, 3.6]), ITV D99% (p = 0.46 with +1.9% median difference and 95% CI [−4.3, 5.2]), or PTV D95% (p = 0.67 with +0.6% median difference and 95% CI [−3.1, 5.2]). Non-inferiority of the DTU plans was not statistically demonstrated for any target coverage metric, with p = 0.16, 0.13, and 0.13 for V100%, D99%, and D95%, respectively. However, the median differences for ITV D99% (+1.9%) and PTV D95% (+0.6%) favored the DTU plan, and the inability to establish non-inferiority is attributable to the limited sample size rather than evidence of inferior coverage. R50 was significantly higher for DTU plans (p = 0.008 with +0.74 median difference and 95% CI [0.42, 1.41]), with all eight DTU plans demonstrating greater values than backup plans. CI trended higher for DTU plans but was not significantly different (p = 0.078 with +0.14 median difference and 95% CI: [0.00, 0.23]). Non-inferiority was not demonstrated for either R50 (p = 1.00) or CI (p = 0.73). Differences in R50 and CI are likely attributable to backup plans being prepared with non-coplanar arcs per institutional practice on a treatment unit that supports couch rotations. DTU PTV volumes were significantly smaller than backup volumes (p = 0.039 with −1.3 cm3 median difference and 95% CI: [−3.4, −0.6]).

In cross-plan analyses, in which native plans were re-calculated on the comparison datasets, both DTU plans recalculated on backup CT (p = 0.039 with +6.4% median difference and 95% CI [1.0, 19.4]) and backup plans recalculated on DTU CBCT (p = 0.008 with +9.6% median difference and 95% CI [4.2, 17.8]) showed a significant decrease in PTV D95%. ITV D99% remained stable for both plan types when recalculated on comparison datasets (p = 0.22 and p = 0.15 for DTU and backup plans, respectively), and CI showed no significant change for either (p = 0.81 and p = 0.64). Collectively, these findings indicate that PTV-based coverage metrics are sensitive to differences in anatomy and ITV definition between datasets in both directions, while the near-minimum dose to the ITV itself is robust across both imaging modalities and plan types. In sensitivity analysis excluding Subject 8, for whom the ITV differed appreciably between CBCT and average CT scans for reasons likely attributable to patient setup, the median PTV V100% difference shifted from −2.2% to +1.5%, confirming this patient as a primary driver of any apparent coverage differences. The bootstrap 95% CI for ITV D99% narrowed to [0.8, 5.2], with the lower bound exceeding zero, with non-inferiority remaining not significant (p = 0.078). Significant differences in R50 were robust to this exclusion (p = 0.016).

Post-hoc power analyses indicated that the minimum detectable paired effect size at 80% power for this sample was d = 1.16 (where d represents the mean difference normalized to its standard deviation). Observed effect sizes for target coverage metrics were small (|d| = 0.16–0.29), and achieving 80% power for these comparisons would require 95–200 patients. The R50 comparison (d = 1.30) achieved 88% power, consistent with the significant finding, while the CI comparison (d = 0.85) achieved 55% power with only 13 patients needed to reach 80% power. These findings underscore the pilot nature of this study, with just 10 subjects accrued for demonstrating feasibility of the proposed techniques.

The median (range) total treatment time, from patient entry into the vault to post-treatment CBCT acquisition, was 72.35 (62.7–94.8) minutes. The median (range) number of CBCTs acquired per subject throughout the entire procedure was 6 [5], [6], [7], [8]. Verification imaging confirmed high positional accuracy, with a median (range) shift magnitude of 2.2 (0.2–4.7) mm on verification CBCTs. Mid-treatment CBCTs identified positional deviations >2 mm in five scans across four subjects, requiring couch corrections before resuming delivery.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully treated 8 of 10 patients accrued to the ONE-STOP protocol using the DTU workflow, with one untreated patient a screen failure due to motion. Dosimetrically, all delivered DTU plans strictly adhered to protocol, meeting all P1 OAR constraints and target coverage goals when P1 OAR objectives were achievable. Plan quality metrics were comparable to conventional workflows. All treatments were completed within 1.5 h, the standard appointment duration for online-adapted SBRT treatments at our institution.

Our results are largely consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating the feasibility and dosimetric adequacy of DTU workflows across platforms and anatomical sites. Palacios et al. reported 100% feasibility in 10 patients treated with same-day consultation and single-fraction lung SABR on an MR-linac, with simulation, planning and delivery requiring approximately 5 h (16). For non-lung sites, the FAST-METS study demonstrated simulation-free radiotherapy for palliative and non-stereotactic regimens on the Ethos platform in 100 treatments with a mean department time of 84 min and high patient satisfaction (22). Liu et al. reported 90% feasibility for DTU spine SBRT on an MR-linac in 10 patients with a median fraction time of 87 min and less than 5% difference in PTV coverage between MR-based and CT-based plans (12). Similarly, de Leon et al. demonstrated simulation-free prostate SABR on an MR-linac in 15 patients with only minor dosimetric differences relative to conventional planning (13), and McGrath et al. showed in silico that direct-to-treatment spine SBRT on the Ethos platform produced no significant dosimetric differences from standard workflows even when using a library patient or phantom as the reference plan (23). Across these studies, feasibility rates are consistently high, dosimetric results are accurate and appropriate, and treatment times are typically under 90 min. Our results extend this evidence to single-fraction lung SBRT on a CBCT-guided platform.

Analysis of the two patients triaged to the conventional workflow highlights critical logistical and anatomical considerations for future DTU implementation. For one subject, the DTU workflow was attempted but ultimately aborted. In this case, the interval between diagnostic scan and scheduled treatment was 1.5 months. During this period, the patient's condition worsened, precluding standard arms-up geometry. Instead, the patient was positioned with a 10-degree wedge under the back with arms at their sides. Plan optimization was attempted twice on the treatment couch. In both instances, P1 constraints remained unmet, and plan hot spots and modulation grossly exceeded expectations based on institutional experience. The session was aborted, and the patient was treated via a backup plan. Unlike our standard practice of normalizing adaptive plans to meet OAR constraints, a normalization condition was applied to ensure PTV V34Gy ≥95%. The combination of atypical setup and coverage-based normalization resulted in the unacceptable plans created on the treatment unit. Based on this experience, unless normalization can be toggled or adjusted on the treatment platform, we likely would forego any coverage-based normalization due to risk of making a plan unacceptably hot.

The second patient exhibited superior-inferior lesion motion exceeding 1 cm on 4DCT and was therefore a screen failure. For patients treated via DTU, we relied on the “Slow Thorax” acquisition – sampling multiple breathing cycles – so that the ITV could be estimated from the lesion blur. In principle, this workflow could be applied to arbitrary motion amplitudes under breath-hold treatment. We avoided breath-hold in this study, because single-fraction SBRT doses of 30–34 Gy produce beam-on times exceeding 10 min, requiring tens of breath-holds and careful patient assessment. Generalizing DTU treatment in the lung will require that we provide options for target delineation and motion management for tumors that move appreciably with respiration (e.g. motion amplitude >1 cm or larger than the lesion diameter).

Future directions for treating lesions with appreciable motion may include additional acquisitions to better characterize target motion. For instance, other units support 4D-CBCT, where projections are oversampled and binned by respiratory surrogate; individual phases or derived datasets (e.g., maximum intensity projections) could refine ITV delineation. Simpler alternatives include fluoroscopy, reviewed on perpendicular views to create an ITV via anisotropic expansion of a static GTV. Neither 4D-CBCT nor fluoroscopy is currently available on the Ethos platform, though research is ongoing to generate 4D reconstructions from under-sampled projections similar to the Slow Thorax protocol.

From a logistical perspective, these two cases triaged to conventional workflow highlight important operational considerations associated with a DTU workflow. Because treatment planning occurs on the treatment unit, cases where DTU treatment fails can compromise throughput and efficiency on the treatment machine (e.g. by requiring unexpected appointment times or requiring a rescheduled session with sessions taking up to 90 min). Therefore, careful patient selection, including confidence in expected motion extent and a patient's ability to tolerate the treatment duration, will be important to minimize workflow disruptions and maximize efficiency. Future implementations may also benefit from minimizing the interval between diagnostic imaging and treatment to reduce the risk of changes in patient condition or positioning capability that might jeopardize treatment.

Among patients treated via DTU, dosimetry was comparable between DTU and backup plans as confirmed through statistical tests. To assess the robustness of the DTU plans and CBCT dose calculation, we performed cross-platform re-calculations as described in the Results. Interpreting these data is challenging because re-calculations intertwine variables including differences in underlying CT numbers and ITV contour definitions. Nonetheless, if the ITV near-minimum dose remains at least the prescription dose (or consistent with the DTU plan) upon re-calculation, this provides evidence for the robustness of the treatment. In only one case did the ITV near-minimum dose appreciably decrease on re-calculation. Specifically, for Subject 8, ITV D99% and PTV V100% for the DTU plan calculated on the backup CT were 83.6% and 68.8% respectively of the values on the treated DTU plan. In this instance, the ITV volume was approximately 1.3 times greater on the backup CT than on the DTU CBCT and target motion was ∼13 mm. Notably, at the time of the backup 4DCT acquisition, this patient's breathing was irregular, necessitating discretionary application of abdominal compression – not applied for the DTU treatment – and verbal coaching (both of which were not present for actual treatment). Therefore, the dosimetric differences in this circumstance are likely attributable to gross variations in setup and respiration rather than dose calculation accuracy. Review of surface monitoring tracking and verification CBCTs from this subject's treatment show no evidence of motion greater than what was specified by ITV.

This study has several important limitations. First, the patient cohort was small with stringent selection criteria based on size, location and motion extent which limits the generalizability of the workflow. Given the small sample number, the study has insufficient power to comprehensively compare dosimetric results between DTU and backup plans. Second, the treatment times for the DTU workflow were long, requiring up to approximately 90 min, and may not be feasible for institutions with high patient volume that cannot accommodate a treatment appointment of this duration. Third, the workflow required multiple CBCT acquisitions per patient given the lack of alternative imaging techniques, and ITV can only be inferred through a blurry envelope visualized on a slow CBCT acquisition which can require some discretionary judgement.

Results and lessons learned from ONE-STOP inspire confidence in expanding this workflow to a broader patient population that can benefit from the condensed treatment course duration. A natural extension of this workflow is to other palliative treatments, such as spine SBRT. A challenge in this expansion is that target definition is often based on clinical guidelines (e.g. clinical target volumes based on vertebral anatomy) rather than readily visualizable gross disease, although developing automation tools for such targets is feasible (23). Furthermore, these techniques should be applicable to targets in other anatomical sites that move appreciably, provided imaging can resolve motion with higher precision. For sites such as the liver, successful implementation will likely require co-registered multi-modality imaging on the treatment unit. The most recent iteration of the Ethos platform supports registration between CBCT acquisitions and diagnostic imaging, suggesting that with proper motion management, treatments like single-fraction liver SBRT could be feasible. Indeed, DTU treatment for celiac plexus ablation has already been described in the literature (24).

Conclusions

In this prospective pilot clinical trial we demonstrated the feasibility of a DTU workflow for single-fraction lung SBRT, successfully treating 80% of accrued patients. The workflow drastically reduced the treatment burden, condensing a multi-week process into a single clinical visit while maintaining high dosimetric quality comparable to conventional simulation-based planning. Detailed analysis confirmed that plan quality on the adaptive platform is robust, with deviations driven primarily by anatomical setup variation rather than calculation fidelity. These findings support the continued expansion of high-precision DTU workflows to other anatomical sites and indications, promising a reduction in time toxicity for vulnerable patient populations.
