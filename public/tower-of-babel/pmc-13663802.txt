Risk of atrial fibrillation according to electrocardiographic risk factors: a nationwide cohort study of 1.5 million patients
Authors: Casper Binding, Christoffer Polcwiartek, Kristian Kragholm, Sam Riahi, Mikkel Porsborg Andersen, Helle Collatz Christensen, Mohamad Hassan El-Chouli, Signe Juul Riddersholm, Lise Da Riis-Vestergaard, Peter Søgaard, Gregory Y H Lip, Christian Torp-Pedersen, Daniel Friedman, Anders Nissen Bonde, Claus Graff, Frieder Braunschweig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAtrial fibrillation (AF) is associated with an increased risk of stroke, yet no evidence exists on the collective effect of electrocardiogram (ECG) risk factors in early detection of AF. We aimed to evaluate the risk of new-onset AF and stroke according to specific ECG risk factors to identify patients at high risk of developing AF.Methods and resultsWe included patients who had an ECG performed and grouped them according to how many of the following ECG risk factors they presented with: Elevated heart rate at rest, prolonged PR-interval, biphasic P-waves, abnormal P-terminal force in V1, and prolonged P-wave duration. Among 1 434 491 included patients, 632 628 had none of the five ECG risk factors and 802 163 had at least one ECG risk factor on their ECG. The 5-year cumulative incidence of AF and stroke was lowest among patients with no ECG changes and exceeded 8% and 7%, respectively among patients with all five ECG risk factors [AF: 8.3%, 95% confidence interval (CI): 5.7–11.0%, stroke: 7.3%, 95% CI: 4.7–9.9%]. Patients with all five ECG risk factors had a two-fold increased risk of AF [hazard ratio (HR): 2.57, 95% CI: 1.85–3.57] and of stroke (HR: 2.11, 95% CI: 1.47–3.01) compared to patients with none.ConclusionThe selected ECG risk factors were commonly observed. The 5-year incidence of AF and stroke was low among patients with no ECG risk factors, yet increased in a dose–response-like manner according to the number of ECG risk factors. Scoring patients according to simple ECG risk factors effectively identified patients at high risk of AF and stroke.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Stroke, Prevention

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is increasing in incidence and in prevalence on a global scale due to demographic shifts in age and improved survival among diagnosed patients.1 Notoriously, AF is also associated with an increased risk of acute cardiovascular diseases. Upwards five-fold increased risk of stroke was found in the Framingham study when comparing patients with AF to patients without, and more recent findings showed a similar increase in risk of heart failure among patients with AF.2,3 Nonetheless, a substantial proportion of patients with AF are asymptomatic, and among those who do experience symptoms, the symptoms accompanying the disease can be unspecific ranging from fatigue, malaise, and light-headedness to palpitations and dyspnoea.4–6 Asymptomatic patients with AF are common, and these patients are at high risk of severe cardiovascular events due to delays in diagnosis.

To establish risk markers of AF from electrocardiograms (ECGs), some previous studies have used artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches to identify patients at risk of developing AF.7 Yet the everyday use of such tools on ECGs obtained in the clinic is often not straightforward and could be difficult to implement as it would require validated AI algorithms to be available on a large scale.

More simple evaluations of specific recognizable ECG characteristics and their associated risks of new-onset AF have been made using large ECG databases. Previously, five distinctive ECG characteristics, mainly attributed to the P-wave, have been individually linked to an increase in risk of new-onset AF. These are prolonged P-wave duration, prolonged PR-interval, biphasic P-waves, abnormal P-terminal force in V1, and elevated heart rate at rest.8–12 Yet no evidence on the collective effect of these risk factors exists, and no research has been made to clarify how these risk factors can be used to identify patients at high risk of developing AF.

The purpose of this study was therefore to evaluate how the five ECG risk factors could be used to improve early detection of high-risk patients.

Methods

Study design and population

This study used national registers and the Danish nationwide ECG cohort13 to design, investigate, and explore the research question in this prospective cohort study. The STROBE guidelines for reporting of cohort studies were applied to this study in order to clarify the study design and to facilitate interpretation of research results.

Patients between the ages of 40 and 90 years and no prior AF were included the first time they underwent ECG measurements in a hospital or pre-hospital. A blanking period of 30 days was introduced to avoid including patients with intermittent AF diagnosed at the time of recording. Patients with events during blanking were not included, and follow-up started 30 days after ECG recording. To avoid including patients who were critically ill at the time of ECG recording, we excluded patients who within 60 days prior of inclusion were diagnosed with either chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, severe liver disease, chronic kidney disease, or cancer. Patients who underwent surgery or who suffered from acute myocardial infarction or pulmonary embolism during blanking were also excluded. Patients with either atrial arrythmia, missing P-wave characteristics due to non-sinus beats, or any paced rhythm on their baseline ECG were excluded. As were patients treated with antiarrhythmic agents and patients with invalid registry files.

The baseline ECGs from the enrolled patients were evaluated to identify the following ECG risk factors previously linked to risk of AF8–12: heart rate at rest >80 b.p.m., prolonged PR-interval (>196 ms for women and >204 ms for men), biphasic P-waves (positive and negative amplitude within the same P-wave), P-terminal force in V1 ≥ 6000 μV·ms, and P-wave duration ≥120 ms. Patients received a score according to how many of the five mentioned risk factors were present in the index ECG and then divided into the following six groups: patients with none, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 risk factors on their baseline ECG.

The study period was set between 1 January 2000 and 1 January 2022, and patients were followed for 5 years forth or until the event of interest, death, or emigration to evaluate the risk of AF and stroke.

Data sources

The main data source for this study was the Danish nationwide ECG cohort, which is a cohort consisting of more than 12 million ECGs from more than 2 million patients. The ECGs in the cohort were obtained at pre-hospital and hospital (in- and outpatient) settings and are from all hospitals in Denmark. To extract uniform and generalizable measurements, all ECGs were reanalysed with the Marquette 12-SL algorithm, which provided global and lead-specific measurements from all segments of the ECGs.

The information from the ECGs was crosslinked to several nationwide registers using unique personal identification numbers. We obtained information regarding socioeconomics and health data in the following nationwide registers: the Danish Civil Registration System, the Danish Income Statistics Register, the Danish National Patient Register, and the Danish National Prescription Register. We linked the different registers on an individual level to extract data regarding birth, vital status, citizenship, income, cohabitation status, prescribed medications and associated Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes (ATC-codes), hospital admissions, and diagnoses in the International Classification of Diseases 10 (ICD-10) standard.

Comorbidities and concomitant medication

ICD-10 diagnosis codes were used to define comorbidities from hospital contacts within 10 years of index and ATC codes were used to determine concomitant medication from prescriptions filed within 6 months of index.

Diabetes was defined by the type of glucose-lowering drug and age. Patients under the age of 18 years receiving insulin treatment and patients under the age of 30 years receiving monotherapy with insulin were considered to have type I diabetes. Patients discharged with an ICD-10 code for diabetes type-II and in concurrent treatment with glucose-lowering medications were considered to have diabetes type-II, except women younger than 40 years of age in monotherapy with metformin as they were considered to be suffering from polycystic ovarian syndrome.

Left ventricle hypertrophy was defined from baseline ECGs using the Sokolow–Lyon criteria and the Cornell criteria. If either criterion was met, the patient was regarded as having left ventricular hypertrophy.

All ICD-10 codes and ATC codes used for programming and data analysis can be found in Supplementary material online, Table S1.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this project were AF and stroke.

Atrial fibrillation was assessed through the nationwide registers as a hospital contact concerning the ICD-10 diagnosis codes for AF and through ECGs recorded during follow-up.

Stroke was defined from hospitalization with ICD-10 codes of ischaemic stroke, systemic arterial thromboembolism, transient ischaemic attack, or unclassified stroke among patients with a hospital stay which lasted more than 12 h (inpatients). Among patients with unclassified stroke, the proportion of haemorrhagic strokes has previously been shown to account for <10%, and validation of the ischaemic stroke diagnosis found a positive predictive value of 97%.14

All codes used to define the outcomes can be found in Supplementary material online, Table S1.

Statistics

Baseline comorbidities and concomitant medications were handled as dichotomous variables and handled as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were handled as interquartile ranges and medians. Income was handled as an ordinal variable by dividing the patients into four equally sized groups based on annual earnings. The patients were divided into 5-year age groups based on their ages at inclusion.

The Aalen–Johansen estimator was used to calculate cumulative incidences over the 5-year follow-up period. To evaluate relative risks, cox models were used to calculate hazard ratios adjusted for potential confounding variables. Models evaluating AF were adjusted for sex, age, income, hypertension, heart failure, diabetes type 2, left ventricular hypertrophy, hyperthyroidism, prior acute myocardial infarction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, valvular disease, and treatment with beta-blockers. Models investigating stroke were adjusted for sex, age, income, diabetes type 2, left ventricular hypertrophy, hyperthyroidism, prior stroke, treatment with statins, and treatment with oral anticoagulants. In all analyses, death served as a competing risk for the specific outcomes. To depict the causal relationship between variables used in the statistical models, we created direct acyclic graphs (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1).

Supplementary analyses were conducted to evaluate the risk of outcome during 2 years of follow-up to identify short-term effects and in a cohort without blanking to evaluate the risk in a broader context. Furthermore, we evaluated the risk of AF and stroke among patients with one ECG risk factor compared to patients with no ECG risk factors and also evaluated the risk of AF and stroke among patients with two ECG risk factors compared to patients with no ECG risk factors to identify single or combinations of risk factors associated with high risk of event.

To evaluate whether the observed trends in risk persisted in patients with varying stroke risk factors, we evaluated the risk of AF and stroke according to the ECG risk factors in subgroups according to CHA2DS2-VA score.

Collinearities between exposure variables were assessed through calculation of variance inflation factors to evaluate potential increases in variance because of collinearity. No evidence of collinearity was found.

Ethics

This study was approved by the institute responsible for data in accordance with the Danish Data Protection Act and General Data Protection Regulation. This study was created with the sole purpose of statistic and scientific research and was not obligated by law to acquire ethical approval. No further data sharing is possible due to current legislation and ethical considerations.15

Results

Study population

A total of 1 434 491 patients had a clinical ECG performed between 1 January 2000 and 1 January 2022, and met study criteria. Among these patients, 632 628 (44.1%) had none of the five ECG risk factors, 559 254 (39.0%) had one risk factor, 190 469 (13.3%) had two risk factors, 44 816 (3.1%) had three risk factors, 6783 (0.5%) had four risk factors, and 541 (0.04%) displayed all five ECG risk factors (Figure 1).

Patients with five ECG risk factors were more likely to be male, older, living alone, and to have a low annual income compared to patients with fewer ECG risk factors (Table 1). Furthermore, patients with all five of the ECG risk factors on their ECG were more likely to be suffering from concurrent diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, and left ventricle hypertrophy. Correspondingly, patients with five ECG risk factors had a higher proportion of patients receiving concomitant medications including statins, acetylsalicylic acid, and renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, than patients with fewer ECG risk factors. Patients with four ECG risk factors comprised most patients on concomitant treatment with beta-blockers.

Risk of AF

The lowest cumulative 5-year incidence of AF was found among patients with none of the ECG risk factors (1.6%, 95% CI: 1.6–1.7%) and increased with the number of ECG risk factors (Figure 2). Among patients with all five of the ECG risk factors, the 5-year cumulative incidence exceeded 8% (8.3%, 95% CI: 5.7–11.0%).

After adjusting for potential confounders, the relative risks of AF increased with the number of ECG risk factors in a dose–response-like relationship, resulting in a two times higher risk among patients with four of the ECG risk factors [hazard ratio (HR): 2.17, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.95–2.41] and more than 2.5-fold increased risk of AF among patients with all five of the ECG risk factors on their baseline ECG (HR: 2.57, 95% CI: 1.85–3.57) compared to patients with none of the ECG risk factors (Figure 3).

When analysing the risk of AF in a 2-year follow-up period, we obtained similar results to those observed in the main analysis (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2). The absolute and relative risks of AF in the cohort if blanking was omitted were likewise comparable to those found in the main analysis (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3).

When analysing the risk of AF and stroke in subgroups according to CHA2DS2-VA score, we observed a similar dose–response-like relationship between the number of ECG risk factors and risk of event in all subgroups of CHA2DS2-VA score with estimated overlapping cumulative incidences between CHA2DS2-VA groups (see Supplementary material online, Figures S4 and S5).

Among patients with only one of the risk factors present on their baseline ECG, P-wave duration ≥ 120 ms, prolonged PR-interval, heart rate at rest >80 b.p.m., and P-terminal force in V1 ≥ 6000 μV·ms, and biphasic P-waves were all associated with an increased risk of AF compared to patients without any of the ECG risk factors (see Supplementary material online, Figure S6). Among patients with two of the risk factors on their baseline ECG, any combination of the ECG risk factors was associated with an increased risk of AF compared to patients without any of the ECG risk factors (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7).

Risk of stroke

The 5-year cumulative incidence of stroke was 7.3% (95% CI: 4.7–9.9%) among patients with all five of the ECG risk factors (Figure 4). This corresponded to a two-fold increased risk of stroke among patients with five ECG risk factors compared to patients with no ECG risk factors after adjusting for potential confounders (HR: 2.11, 95% CI: 1.47–3.01) (Figure 3). Displaying four of the ECG risk factors on the baseline ECG was associated with 50% increased risk of stroke compared to patients displaying none of the risk factors (HR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.32–1.70), and the cumulative 2-year incidence of stroke was 4.5% (95% CI: 4.0–5.1%) among the same patients.

Cumulative incidence of stroke according to number of electrocardiographic risk factors. 5-year cumulative incidences of stroke according to number of ECG risk factors. Aalen–Johansen estimator was used to calculate cumulative incidences. Number of patients at risk for each year is displayed at the bottom of the figure.

When comparing the risk of stroke in a 2-year follow-up period and in a 5-year follow-up period without blanking, the results observed were similar to those obtained in the main analysis (see Supplementary material online, Figures S2 and S3).

The risk of stroke according to ECG risk factors did not change in subgroups of CHA2DS2-VA and the cumulative incidence of stroke among patients with 5 ECG risk factors and CHA2DS2-VA = 1 was estimated to be higher than among patients with CHA2DS2-VA ≥ 2, although the results were statistically uncertain (see Supplementary material online, Figures S4 and S5).

Among patients with one of the risk factors on their ECG, displaying any of the risk factors was associated with an increased risk of stroke compared to patients with none, yet the estimated relative risk increase was not statistically significant among patients with prolonged P-wave duration (see supplementary material online, Figure S6). Among patients with two ECG risk factors, any combination of risk factors was associated with an increase in relative risk of stroke compared to patients with no ECG changes (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7).

Discussion

This study sought to identify patients at high-risk of AF by using five specific ECG risk factors. The following key findings were made: (i) 55.9% of the patients (801 863) with no prior AF diagnosis displayed at least one of the five ECG changes on their ECG. (ii) Among patients without any of the ECG risk factors, the cumulative 5-year incidence of AF and stroke was low. (iii) The risk of AF and stroke increased with the number of ECG risk factors in a dose–response-like manner. (iv) Patients with all five risk factors had an absolute 5-year risk of AF of 8.3% and an absolute 5-year risk of stroke of 7.3%.

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to evaluate the collective effect of readily ECG changes and their predictive value in a large nationwide cohort. Nonetheless, identifying ECG changes with connection to AF is not new, and multiple ECG changes and their association with AF have been identified and studied in different settings.16,17

However, many of the recent studies investigating such ECG changes and risk of AF have primarily been conducted using complex machine learning scenarios.18–20 Not only does such approaches increase the risk of chance findings and of findings not related to electrophysiological mechanisms but it also challenges the clinical implementation since the subclinical patterns may not be recognizable and would require validated AI analysing tools. This study explored five distinct ECG risk factors, that were clinically identifiable, and their associated risk of subsequent AF and found a dose–response-like relationship between the number of ECG characteristics and the risk of new-onset AF.

Risk factors on ECG and development of AF

The pathogenesis of AF is complex and not fully understood yet revolves around the formation of an atrial substrate from which AF can initiate.21 The development of such substrates involves both electrical and structural remodelling, atrial enlargement, and reparative fibrosis, which may manifest as ECG changes.22

The five ECG changes used in this study have previously one-by-one been associated with an increased risk of AF in smaller cohorts using data from the Copenhagen general practitioners’ laboratory (CGPL) database.8–12

Heart rate at rest is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular mortality and was previously studied in 281 451 primary care patients in which the strongest association with development of AF was found among patients with elevated heart rates at rest.10 Similar J-shaped associations between heart rate at rest and AFs have been found in subsequent studies including a large meta-analysis.23 Heart rate at rest is thought to be influenced by various factors such as physical activity, stroke volume, and genetic dispositions, and could be accompanied by pathophysiological changes that inflict structural and functional adaptations.24,25

PR-interval is highly influenced by the autonomous nervous system tone and the association between PR-interval and AF was studied among 288 181 patients from the CGPL cohort and an increased risk of AF was observed among patients with prolonged PR-interval with minor sex differences in cut-off points.9 A large meta-analysis and results from the Framingham Heart Study found prolonged PR-interval defined as first-degree atrio ventricular (AV) block to be associated with increased risk of AF compared to patients without.26,27

Atrial conduction velocity directly influences the P-wave duration in an inverse relationship and is logically also related to the risk of AF as it facilitates the perpetuated electrical activity observed in AF. P-wave duration was examined among primary care patients subjected to ECG measurements among whom prolonged P-wave durations significantly increased the risk of AF development.8 The highest risk of AF among patients was found in patients with P-wave durations above the 95th percentile like previous findings suggested.28,29 The findings were further evaluated in other cohorts and among patients with loop-recorders and found to be positively correlated with the development of AF and interestingly also with new-onset subclinical AF.30,31

Interatrial block, defined in this project as biphasic P-waves, was evaluated in more than 100 000 ECGs from primary care, and a strong association was found between interatrial block and the risk of AF especially if biphasic P-waves were present in multiple leads.11 The prevalence of partial interatrial block has been regarded as high,32 and the linkage between biphasic P-waves and new-onset AF has also been found in a subsequent meta-analysis.33

Conduction abnormalities in the atriums can also be assessed through the P-terminal force in lead V1. Investigations of the PTFV1 and the risk of AF found that increasing PTFV1 was associated with increasing risk of AF12 and that values above 6000 μV·ms were associated with increased risk of AF and death in a general population.34

Screening for AF

A substantial proportion of patients with AF are asymptomatic, though it is very difficult to ascertain how many asymptomatic patients exist because they might not be diagnosed. A previous study including patients with established AF estimated the proportion of asymptomatic patients to be upwards of 40%.4 In clinical settings the detection of AF will often firstly include ECG measurement, and if AF is not detected, rely on Holter monitoring. One drawback in this context is that the Holter monitoring is of short duration, often limited to a maximum of 72 h, and therefore paroxysmal AF may not be detected. In these cases, diagnosing AF requires monitoring through implantable loop recorders, which is not only costly but also involves interventional insertion of the recording device.

The question of whether or not large-scale screening for AF should be conducted has been debated widely in recent years. While international guidelines and collaborations advocate for implementation of tailored screening programmes,35,36 others argue that the evidence of benefits and harms is insufficient as of now and that screening is not currently feasible.37,38

This study found the 2-year cumulative incidence of AF and stroke among patients with all five ECG risk factors to be 5.7% and 4.1%, respectively, indicating that the ECG could help identify patients at risk of being diagnosed with AF within a short time period. The LOOP Study found screening of AF to be efficient yet not affecting stroke in a general population, indicating that screening should be focused on specific groups of patients if it is to show a clinical effect.39 Although extensive screening might not be favourable and holds ethical considerations as well, a multicentre randomized controlled trial (RCT) enrolling 75–76-year-olds with no prior AF found screening for AF to reduce the occurrence of stroke, bleeding, and all-cause death.40 Thus, early detection of AF could prove important in some patients, yet the issue then seems to be identifying these individuals.

In this study the number of ECG risk factors present on a baseline ECG increased the risk of AF among patients without prior AF in a dose–response-like manner suggesting that simple ECG measurements could contribute to identifying the patients who could benefit from screening. These findings persisted in subgroups of CHA2DS2-VA score. However, this study included patients subjected to ECG measurements in clinical settings and thus did not mimic a screening scenario but reflected the everyday encounter of patients. These findings could provide information on how to use ECGs to identify patients at risk of AF among those already seen by clinicians yet needs to be evaluated in the context of other risk factors for AF and stroke if scoring algorithms are to be efficient.

Further studies examining the risk of stroke according to ECG risk factors in patients with asymptomatic AF and studies evaluating the risk of stroke and AF according to ECG risk factors together with other risk factors of AF could help identify how and which persons should be monitored or screened for AF. The association between these ECG risk factors and AF should also be investigated in other populations to validate our findings. Furthermore, studies assessing whether initiation of anticoagulation therapy mitigates the risk of stroke could reveal potential benefits of ECG-based AF-screening.

Strengths/limitations

This study included patients on the basis of a real-world setting in which the patients were subjected to ECG measurements because there was a clinical indication. This reflected how patients were referred and encountered in the everyday healthcare system. Furthermore, this study used nationwide data from Denmark, in which all citizens are covered by the health care system, and which ensured no loss to follow-up was encountered.

Patients were included from the Danish National ECG cohort, which is a nationwide cohort of patients subjected to ECG measurements and linked to other nationwide registers containing a large collection of clinical and socioeconomical information. In the registers the diagnosis code used to define AF has been well validated previously with a positive predictive value of 95%.41

Despite our efforts to reduce bias and adjust for potential confounders, the risk of residual confounding might have persevered due to unmeasured variables not available in the registers such as weight, smoking status, exact arterial blood pressure, diet composition, and physical training. Moreover, the subgroup of patients with all five ECG risk factors consisted of very few patients, which caused statistical uncertainty and limits clinical interpretation, yet the absence of evidence observed in this subgroup was not evidence of absence.42

Conclusions

Among 1 434 491 patients with no prior AF, the five ECG risk factors were commonly observed. Among patients with none of the ECG risk factors, the cumulative 5-year incidence was low. The risk of AF and stroke increased with the number of ECG risk factors in a dose–response-like manner and resulted in an 8.3% 5-year cumulative incidence of AF among patients with all five risk factors and a 7.3% 5-year risk of stroke among the same patients. Scoring patients according to simple ECG risk factors effectively identified patients at low risk and at high risk of AF and stroke and could help tailor future screening for AF.
