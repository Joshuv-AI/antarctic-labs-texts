The ALMA Survey of Gas Evolution of PROtoplanetary Disks (AGE-PRO): X. Dust Substructures, Disk Geometries, and Dust-disk Radii
Miguel Vioque, Nicolás T. Kurtovic, Leon Trapman, Anibal Sierra, Laura M. Pérez, Ke Zhang, Pietro Curone, Giovanni P. Rosotti, John Carpenter, Benoît Tabone, Paola Pinilla, Dingshan Deng, Ilaria Pascucci, James Miley, Carolina Agurto-Gangas, Lucas A. Cieza, Rossella Anania, Dary A. Ruiz-Rodriguez, Camilo González-Ruilova, Estephani E. TorresVillanueva, Aleksandra Kuznetsova

Abstract
We perform visibility fitting to the dust continuum Band 6 1.3 mm data of the 30 protoplanetary disks in the AGE-PRO ALMA Large Program. We obtain disk geometries, dust-disk radii, and azimuthally symmetric radial profiles of the intensity of the dust continuum emission. We examine the presence of continuum substructures in the AGE-PRO sample by using these radial profiles and their residuals. We detect substructures in 15 out of 30 disks. We report five disks with large ( > > 15 au) inner dust cavities. The Ophiuchus Class I disks show dust-disk substructures in ∼ \sim 80% of the resolved sources. This evidences the early formation of substructures in protoplanetary disks. A spiral is identified in IRS 63, hinting to gravitational instability in this massive disk. We compare our dust-disk brightness radial profiles with gas-disk brightness radial profiles and discuss colocal substructures in both tracers. In addition, we discuss the evolution of dust-disk radii and substructures across Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper Scorpius. We find that disks in Lupus and Upper Scorpius with large inner dust cavities have typical gas-disk masses, suggesting an abundance of dust cavities in these regions. The prevalence of pressure dust traps at later ages is supported by a potential trend with time with more disks with large inner dust cavities (or transition disks ) in Upper Scorpius and the absence of evolution of dust-disk sizes with time in the AGE-PRO sample. We propose this is caused by an evolutionary sequence with a high fraction of protoplanetary disks with inner protoplanets carving dust cavities.



I
Introduction

The millimeter continuum emission of protoplanetary disks allow us to study the millimeter sized dust component of the disk midplanes, and its evolution over time (see
Andrews 2020
;
Bae et al. 2023
;
Manara et al. 2023
;
Miotello et al. 2023
, and references therein).
Tripathi et al. 2017
and
Andrews et al. 2018a
found that the millimeter continuum luminosity scales with the disk area, indicating a large contribution to the luminosity from optically thick emission. Nevertheless, millimeter continuum luminosities have often been used to estimate a total dust-disk mass, provided some assumptions on average disk temperature, dust opacity, and optically thin emission (e.g.
Andrews et al. 2013
;
Pascucci et al. 2016
;
Tychoniec et al. 2020
). These masses have in turn been used extensively to probe the evolution of the mass reservoir in protoplanetary disks as they disperse, and material is accreted, ejected, or locked into planets (e.g.
Manara et al. 2016
;
Tychoniec et al. 2018
;
van Terwisga et al. 2022
;
Maucó et al. 2023
). If multiple millimeter wavelengths are considered, continuum thermal emission can also be used to constrain grain growth and drift (e.g.
Testi et al. 2014
;
Sierra et al. 2021
).

From the millimeter continuum emission of protoplanetary disks, dust-disk radii can also be measured. However, it has proved challenging to correctly define an outer radius in protoplanetary disks, and often radii enclosing a certain percentage of the total flux are used (see
Miotello et al. 2023
, and references therein). Dust-disk radii have also been used extensively to describe disk populations (e.g.
Rosotti et al. 2019b
;
Hendler et al. 2020
;
Sanchis et al. 2021
) and to characterize internal and environmental processes of protoplanetary disk evolution.

Another focus of dust continuum emission studies is on dust substructures, and their relation to planet formation mechanisms (see
Bae et al. 2023
, and references therein). Dust-disk substructures are diverse and seem ubiquitous whenever disks are observed with enough resolution (
Long et al. 2018
;
Bae et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
). Hence, these studies have been particularly enhanced by Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) high-resolution observations (
Andrews et al. 2018b
;
Andrews 2020
). Disk substructures have been studied to great detail in several objects (e.g.
ALMA Partnership et al. 2015
;
Keppler et al. 2019
;
Macías et al. 2021
;
Guerra-Alvarado et al. 2024
), but have also been used as a tracer of evolution in populations of protoplanetary disks (e.g.
Eisner et al. 2018
;
Long et al. 2019
;
Ohashi et al. 2023
;
Yamaguchi et al. 2024
). Axisymmetric structures (rings, gaps) appear to be more common than asymmetric ones (spirals, crescents). It is known that dust substructures play a major role in planet formation and disk evolution mechanisms, but their variety and complexity indicate they are sensitive to several phenomena and have different origins, and are often challenging to reconcile with theoretical predictions (
Bae et al. 2023
).

As part of the ALMA Large Program AGE-PRO (the ALMA Survey of Gas Evolution of PROtoplanetary Disks,
Zhang et al. 2025
, 2021.1.00128.L) we dedicate this paper to the analysis of the Band 6 1.3 mm dust continuum emission of the AGE-PRO sample of 30 protoplanetary disks, studying evolution across the three considered regions: 10 disks in Ophiuchus, 10 disks in Lupus, and 10 disks in Upper Scorpius, all in a narrow stellar mass range (0.3-0.8
M_{\odot}
).

We follow the same methodology homogeneously for all disks, which can be summarized in three steps. First (Sect.
II
), we obtain disk inclinations and position angles (PA). In a second step (Sect.
III
), we fit the visibilities of the dust continuum emission using as the input the geometries found in the first step. The resulting models provide us with azimuthally symmetric radial intensity profiles of the continuum emission. We use these radial profiles to identify dust substructures (Sect.
III.1
) and to obtain curves of growth from which to extract dust-disk radii (Sect.
III.2
). In the last step (Sect.
IV
), we analyze the residuals of the best model for each source to identify nonazimuthally symmetric structure. AGE-PRO observations have an angular image resolution of
\sim 0.15-0.30
”, but via visibility fitting, we achieve smaller resolution elements (Table
2
). The average continuum sensitivity of the survey is 0.025 mJy/beam rms. More information on the data can be found in the AGE-PRO papers of the three regions:
Ruiz-Rodriguez et al. 2025
,
Deng et al. 2025
, and
Agurto-Gangas et al. 2025
.

We analyze and discuss our results in Sect.
V
. We start by placing the AGE-PRO sample of disks into context with respect to the broader population of known protoplanetary disks. We then describe trends of the dust-disk radii with age and between star forming regions (Sect.
V.2
). We analyze the evolution and type of dust substructures at different ages and in different regions in Sects.
V.3
and
V.4
. We conclude the analysis with a comparison of the radial intensity profiles of the dust continuum emission reported in this work with the gas-disk radial profiles derived in
Trapman et al. 2025a
. We summarize our conclusions in Sect.
VI
.

II
Geometries of AGE-PRO Disks

We dedicate this section to characterize the disk geometries (i.e. inclination and PA) of the AGE-PRO sample from the continuum visibilities (Sect.
II.1
). For those disks with marginally resolved continuum visibilities, we try to obtain disk geometries from the
12
CO moment zero maps (Sect.
II.2
).

II.1
Gaussian Visibility Fitting

In this section, we obtain disk geometries and phase offsets by fitting the interferometric continuum visibilities using
Galario
(
Tazzari et al. 2018
), a Python library that compares observed visibilities with model visibilities in the visibility plane (abbreviated as uv-plane). The Band 6 (
Kerr et al. 2014
) visibilities are extracted from the self-calibrated, phase-centered, and line-free AGE-PRO measurement sets using a modified version of the
export uvtable
function in
Tazzari 2017
. In our version, the uv-distances are normalized using the wavelength of each channel and spectral window, instead of the average wavelength of the whole data set.

Using
Galario
, we fit with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC;
emcee
;
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
) the observed continuum visibilities of each disk with an azimuthally symmetric Gaussian function:

I=f_{0}\,e^{-0.5(R/\sigma)^{2}}.

Some sources required small deviations from these ensembles for better convergence. We note that for 2MASS J16202863-2442087 (Upper Scorpius 7) we only use observations of quiescent continuum emission (see
Miley et al. 2025

A number of AGE-PRO sources have companions within the field of view, which were masked during the data calibration (
Agurto-Gangas et al. 2025
;
Deng et al. 2025
;
Ruiz-Rodriguez et al. 2025

Miley et al. 2024

There are eight disks that are marginally resolved, leading to higher uncertainties in the Gaussian fit of their dust visibilities. These sources are Ophiuchus 5, Lupus 4, 5, 8, and 9, and Upper Scorpius 2, 4, and 5 (see Table
1
for naming convention). We consider these sources as poorly resolved in continuum visibility space. A different approach to try to constrain the geometries of these sources from the gas is presented in Sect.
II.2
.

The results for the geometry and phase offset of each disk from the
Galario
plus MCMC fit are summarized in Table
1
. Uncertainties of each parameter indicate the 16% and 84% percentiles of the posterior distribution. For AGE-PRO internal consistency, in the case of 2MASS J16120668-3010270 (Upper Scorpius 1), we adopt the geometries identified in
Sierra et al. 2024a
using a similar procedure to the one of this work.

II.2
Sources with Geometries from the Gas-disk

For the eight sources that are marginally resolved in their continuum visibilities, we consider a different approach to constrain their disk geometries. Gas emission is often more extended than the dust, thereby providing an additional and often more reliable constraint on the disk geometry in these cases where the dust-disks are only marginally resolved. Using the method described in
Trapman et al. 2025a
, we fit two-dimensional Gaussians to the
12
CO moment zero maps of these sources (except for Ophiuchus 5, which shows too much
12
CO cloud contribution for this methodology to work). The details of the results obtained with these fits to the
12
CO moment zero maps are presented in Appendix
A
.

In summary, of the seven Lupus and Upper Scorpius marginally resolved sources in the continuum visibilities (Sect.
II.1
), we consider in this work the
12
CO-based geometries for Lupus 4, 5, and Upper Scorpius 5. The other sources show nonconclusive fits to the
12
CO moment zero maps. Even though it is well resolved in continuum emission, for Upper Scorpius 3, we also use the
12
CO-based geometries as they provide much better residuals (see Sect.
IV
). Table
3
in Appendix
A
details the sources for which
12
CO-based geometries were adopted, and shows the comparison with the dust-based geometries obtained for them in Sect.
II.1
.

The final set of geometries (PA and inclinations) and phase offsets for the AGE-PRO sample of protoplanetary disks is presented in Table
1
. All phase offsets come from the
Galario
fit of Sect.
II.1
. The geometries derived in Sect.
II.1
and
II.2

III

III
Radial Profiles and Dust-disk Radii

Frankenstein
is a Python library that fits nonparametrically the radial brightness profile of a disk using the observed visibilities and assuming azimuthal symmetry (
Jennings et al. 2020
). We use
Frankenstein
because it allows for direct nonparametric fitting of the visibilities, enabling the recovery of subbeam features (compared to the CLEAN image) while exploiting the full sensitivity of the data.

The inputs of
Frankenstein
are the geometries and phase centers identified in Sect.
II
(Table
1
). We run a grid of 17 different
Frankenstein
fits for each source (using the
LogNormal
method), exploring the full range of geometry and phase center uncertainties, and the full range of
Frankenstein
hyperparameters recommended in
Jennings et al. 2020
.

In some cases (Lupus 1, 3, 7, and Upper Scorpius 10),
the real component of the
Frankenstein
fit to the observed visibilities did not converge to zero brightness at the higher deprojected uv-distances. This could cause emission to be inferred on scales that are not well sampled in the uv-plane of our observations, specifically unresolved emission at short separation from the phase center. In these cases, we added a point source of the necessary flux to force the
Frankenstein
model to end at zero brightness. This point source was then removed to calculate the residuals of the model (Sect.
IV
). Not surprisingly,
Frankenstein
fails to produce a fit to the visibilities for the eight marginally resolved sources (Sect.
II.1
).

The intrinsic angular resolution of the
Frankenstein
fit to each source is computed following the methodology described in
Sierra et al. 2024b
and implemented in
Carvalho et al. 2024
. The visibilities of a delta ring are sampled with the same uv-coverage of each observation, and then, they are fitted with
Frankenstein
using the same hyperparameters as for the original data. We locate the delta ring at R
max
/2, with R
max
being the maximum radius given to
Frankenstein
for fitting the original data. The obtained
Frankenstein
solution is similar to a Gaussian function (interpreted as a point-spread function), with an FWHM that depends on the uv-coverage. We take this FWHM as the
Frankenstein
resolution element. In AGE-PRO data, typical values for the
Frankenstein
resolution element are of
\sim 0.09-0.15
”, while the typical CLEAN beam size is of
\sim 0.2-0.35
”. Hence, on average, the
Frankenstein
resolution elements we obtain for the AGE-PRO disks are 1.5 to 2.5 times smaller than the CLEAN beam (in agreement with
Jennings et al. 2022a
;
Jennings et al. 2022b
). The
Frankenstein
resolution elements of the visibility fitting for each source are reported in Table
2
.

We find mean resolution elements of 13, 22, and 22 au for the sources at Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper Scorpius, respectively. This is due to the fact that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of Ophiuchus observations is much higher than for Lupus and Upper Scorpius observations (which are similar among themselves), such that longer baselines have sufficient SNR to significantly influence the visibility fit. Hence, for the
Frankenstein
-based substructure identification of this work, we have similar resolution for Lupus and Upper Scorpius, and around a factor of 2 better resolution for Ophiuchus.

III.1
Dust-disk Radial Profiles

For each source, we visually inspected the fit to the visibilities of each
Frankenstein
solution and selected the best one using two criteria: minimal chi-squared between model and data, and convergence to zero brightness at the visibilities with higher deprojected uv-distances. If multiple fits provide similarly good solutions, we opted for the more flexible
Frankenstein
hyperparameters (lower
\alpha
and
wsmooth
, see
Jennings et al. 2020
). The best
Frankenstein
brightness radial profile for each well-resolved AGE-PRO protoplanetary disk is presented in Fig.
1
for all three regions.

For 15 sources, substructures are apparent from the best
Frankenstein
fit. Seven sources show no substructure at the resolution of AGE-PRO (visibility fitting resolution element of
\sim 0.09-0.15
”). The other eight sources are barely resolved in the continuum visibilities, and no radial profiles could be obtained for them (Sect.
III.1
). Individual profiles are presented in Figs.
2
,
3
, and
4
for Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper Scorpius sources, respectively. Temperature brightness radial profiles are computed from the radial intensity profiles using the full blackbody equation (the Rayleigh-Jeans approximation is not assumed), and a central frequency of 230 GHz.

We group the radial intensity profiles of the dust continuum emission into three categories:
inner dust cavity
group, for the disks with an inner dust cavity and a ring;
irregular substructure
group, for the disks with a nonsmooth irregularly structured dust radial profile but with no resolved inner dust cavity; and
not-structured
group, if the dust radial profile is merely an unstructured decline. There are 5 disks with inner dust cavities (Ophiuchus 9, and Upper Scorpius 1, 6, 7, 8) and 10 irregularly structured disks (Ophiuchus 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 10, Lupus 2, 10, and Upper Scorpius 9, 10). The inner dust cavities are reminiscent of the so-called
transition disks
(
Strom et al. 1989
;
van der Marel 2023
). In this work, we refrain from calling them as such as we do not explore the correspondence of our sources with the original spectral energy distribution (SED)-based classification of transition disks. Table
2
summarizes our results on the presence of dust substructures in the AGE-PRO sample of protoplanetary disks.

Ophiuchus 2 and 6 have top flat brightness profile distributions that could resemble an inner dust cavity (Fig.
2
). However, the brightness contrast and size of these structures are too small to clearly discern them as cavities. We hence place Ophiuchus 2 and 6 in the irregular structure category.

There are seven not-structured disks at AGE-PRO resolution (Ophiuchus 3, 4, Lupus 1, 3, 6, 7, and Upper Scorpius 3). Of these later sources, we put down Ophiuchus 4 (Elias 2-32) and Lupus 6 (2MASS J16085324-3914401) as ‘tentatively substructured’ as they show variations in their visibilities that might be indicative of substructure that
Frankenstein
was unable to model.

In Fig.
5
we show how the type of substructure correlates with the gas-disk masses obtained in
Trapman et al. 2025b
. To limit the effect of resolution in the interpretation of this figure, we only show those sources for which our resolution is good enough that if substructures are present, we should have detected them in most cases (see Sect.
V.1
for more information). We see that disks with large inner dust cavities have gas-disk masses compatible with the median gas-disk masses of their regions (
Trapman et al. 2025b
). However, the structured disks without inner dust cavities (the
irregular substructure
group) have gas-disk masses 1 to 2 orders of magnitude above the median value of their regions. This is further discussed in Sect.
V.3
.

We can evaluate the radial intensity profiles we present in this work by comparing with other observations. Among the AGE-PRO sample, there are seven sources with publicly available observations, which have angular resolutions higher than that of AGE-PRO. We compare these observations with our radial intensity profiles of the AGE-PRO dust continuum emission in Appendix
B
. We find good agreement between the visibility modeled radial intensity profiles of this work (using the moderate resolution AGE-PRO observations) and the radial profiles obtained from observations at higher angular resolution. In some cases, there are substructures detected at high resolution that are not detected in the CLEAN images of AGE-PRO, but which are retrieved by the visibility modeled profiles of this work at the right locations. This comparison shows that we can trust the brightness radial profiles obtained in Sect.
III.1
from visibility modeling for those sources in which there is no high angular resolution available.

To summarize, we detect substructures in most of the well-resolved disks. Of the nine well-resolved Ophiuchus sources, seven show substructure (plus a tentative detection in Ophiuchus 4). Of the six well-resolved Lupus sources, two show substructure (plus a tentative detection in Lupus 6). Of the seven well-resolved Upper Scorpius sources, six show substructure. We provide a detailed discussion of the radial profile of each disk in Appendix
C
, and further discussion on the type and evolution of substructures in Sect.
V.3
.

III.2
Dust-disk Radii

The 17
Frankenstein
brightness radial profiles for each source (Sect.
III
) provide 17 different curves of growth, defined as the cumulative contribution to the source brightness as a function of the angular distance from the center. While there is no obvious way to properly define disk radii, in this work, we calculate the angular radii containing 68%, 90%, and 95% of the emission (R
68%
, R
90%
, and R
95%
, respectively). R
68%
has often been identified as a better proxy for protoplanetary dust-disk size (
Miotello et al. 2023
), although AGE-PRO sensitivity should be small enough to provide robust R
90%
and R
95%
values in most cases. The R
68%
, R
90%
, and R
95%
radii for all sources appear tabulated in Table
2
. Central values are the median from all 17
Frankenstein
fits, and uncertainties are the maximum deviations from the median. Angular radii are converted to physical radii using the distance to each source (from Gaia DR3
Bailer-Jones et al. 2021
geometric priors). AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sources do not have Gaia DR3 parallaxes, so we took the average distance to the cloud (
139\pm 20
pc,
Grasser et al. 2021
).

Eight sources are marginally resolved in the continuum visibilities (Sect.
II.1
), and none of the
Frankenstein
fits for them converges adequately. For these sources, we take as upper limits for the R
68%
and R
90%
radii 2 times and 3 times the
\sigma
of Sect.
II.1
Gaussian fit, respectively (Eq.
1
, Table
2
). No R
95%
radii are reported for these marginally resolved disks.

We compare the R

and R

dust-disk radii derived in this work with the dust-disk radii derived in

;
Sanchis et al. 2021
for the Lupus sources, and in

and R

V.1
Sample selection

We first put the AGE-PRO sample into perspective with respect to the three different star forming regions. In Fig.
8
we compare the R
68%
dust-disk radii of the
Hendler et al. 2020
compilations for Lupus and Upper Scorpius with the AGE-PRO samples of those regions. For deriving the median dust-disk sizes, we only consider the spectral type range of the AGE-PRO sample (K6–M3,
Zhang et al. 2025
) to minimize the effect of underlying trends with stellar mass. For Upper Scorpius, the median R
68%
of the
Hendler et al. 2020
sample and the median R
68%
of the AGE-PRO sample are consistent (
32.9^{+8.3}_{-14}
vs
22^{+16}_{-14}
au, respectively). For Lupus, we find a somewhat smaller R
68%
median value for the AGE-PRO sample (
13.8^{+11}_{-7.8}
au vs.
26.5^{+38}_{-8.1}
au in
Hendler et al. 2020
), although they are consistent within error bars. However, the Lupus analysis in
Hendler et al. 2020
was based on low-sensitivity ALMA data (
Ansdell et al. 2016
), hence biasing the size measurements toward the brighter and thus larger disks of the Lupus population. This is confirmed by
Guerra-Alvarado et al. 2025
, who in a more sensitive Lupus survey find a median R
68%
value for Lupus of
13.5
au, in line with the AGE-PRO results (Fig.
8
). We hence conclude that AGE-PRO is representative in dust-disk radii of both Lupus and Upper Scorpius regions.

It is harder to make a fair comparison of the AGE-PRO sample with the whole Ophiuchus region. Existing Ophiuchus surveys are shallow and AGE-PRO only selected Class Is from this region (
Zhang et al. 2025
), which also contains a rich population of Class II sources (
Cieza et al. 2019
). Our AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sample has a median R
68%
of
30
au with a standard deviation of
20
au. Comparing with
Dasgupta et al. 2025
, it seems our sample is typical of the region in dust-disk size, although it leans toward large sizes.

Ruiz-Rodriguez et al. 2025
,
Deng et al. 2025
, and
Agurto-Gangas et al. 2025
papers have shown that the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper Scorpius samples are also not biased in millimeter or
12
CO flux with respect to the entire K6–M3 population of their respective regions. We can hence conclude that, while small, the AGE-PRO sample of protoplanetary disks is not biased by its observational properties and is reasonably representative of the full K6–M3 disk population in the three considered regions.

Regarding the representativeness of the disk-substructures found in this work,
Bae et al. 2023
showed that if significant high resolution and sensitivity are used, substructures are detected in almost all disks. In particular, from a compilation of 479 young stars, if
\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}>10
, 95% of the sources have substructures (with
\theta_{D}
being the angular diameter containing 90% of the emission, and
\theta_{res}
the angular resolution of the observation). This percentage goes down to 50% if
3<\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}<10
, and to 2% if
\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}<3
. We compare these percentages with the AGE-PRO sample, taking as
\theta_{res}
the
Frankenstein
resolution elements derived in Sect.
III
. Of the AGE-PRO disks, Ophiuchus 1, 7, 10, Lupus 2, 10, and Upper Scorpius 1 have
\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}>10
. Of these six sources, we find substructures in all of them. Ophiuchus 2, 3, 6, 8, 9, Lupus 1, and Upper Scorpius 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 have
3<\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}<10
. Of these 11 sources, 9 have substructures (82%), with the exceptions of Ophiuchus 3 (ISO-Oph 129) and Lupus 1 (Sz 65). The remaining 13 AGE-PRO sources have
\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}<3
. Of the latter, none show substructures (although we report Ophiuchus 4 and Lupus 6 as tentative detections in Sect.
III.1
). Comparing to
Bae et al. 2023
results, our higher detection fraction in the intermediate resolution regime (
3<\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}<10
, 82% vs. 50%) could be due to the much higher than average sensitivity of the AGE-PRO program, or to the differences between detecting substructures from the visibility fitting instead of from the image plane. Hence, the fraction of substructures we have identified in this work considering the resolution we obtained agrees well with
Bae et al. 2023
statistics on a larger population.

Rosotti et al. 2019b

Zormpas et al. 2022

We note that this is different from claiming an absence of dust-disk radii evolution with time. The AGE-PRO sample is only representative of the surviving population in Upper Scorpius. Lupus has a Class II disk fraction of
\sim 50\%
while the older Upper Scorpius region has a disk fraction of
\sim 15\%

>100

Carpenter et al. 2025

This conclusion connects with the results of other AGE-PRO papers.
Kurtovic et al. 2025
independently suggests that the dust content of the AGE-PRO disks in Upper Scorpius has mostly survived until their current age due to the presence of dust traps. Another support to this idea comes from the absence of evolution in the AGE-PRO sample of the

Trapman et al. 2025a
), which cannot be explained with smooth disks (
Anania et al. 2025

V.3
The Evolution of Dust Substructures

There are different possibilities for the origin of dust substructures, and their evolution mechanisms are not well understood (
Andrews 2020
;
Bae et al. 2023
;
Benisty et al. 2023
;
Drkażkowska et al. 2023
and references therein). It seems clear that substructures become easier to trace over time, as dust trapping acts, and we can achieve higher contrasts as the emission turns optically thinner (e.g.
Ohashi et al. 2023
). However, disk substructures are theorized to be ubiquitous and to appear early (
van der Marel et al. 2019
;
Sheehan et al. 2020
;
Delussu et al. 2024
).

The main trend of dust substructures with age that can be appreciated in AGE-PRO is the much higher fraction of disks with large inner dust cavities (
>15
au) in Upper Scorpius compared to Ophiuchus and Lupus (Fig.
1
). Considering only those sources for which we have enough resolution (
\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}>3
) to detect substructures in the majority of cases (Sect.
V.1
); four out of six disks in Upper Scorpius show inner dust cavities, for one out of eight in Ophiuchus (and this one, Ophiuchus 9, is among the most evolved sources of the Ophiuchus sample,
Ruiz-Rodriguez et al. 2025
), and zero out of three in Lupus. In contrast, six of the eight Ophiuchus sources show irregular structure, for two in both Lupus and Upper Scorpius. This suggests an evolution of disk populations from having a high fraction of disks with irregular substructures toward having a high fraction of disks with large inner dust cavities and a ring.

In the AGE-PRO Lupus sample, 7 out of the 10 sources have
\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}<3
, which might have caused the fact that we are not effectively resolving substructures in Lupus. However, we note that cavities are often detected in Lupus (
Guerra-Alvarado et al. 2025
) and the similar age region of Taurus (
Long et al. 2018
;
Shi et al. 2024
), indicating that small unresolved cavities might be present in the AGE-PRO Lupus sample. Hence, we claim that the transition toward disk populations with a high fraction of disks with large inner dust cavities happens at the
\sim 1-2
Myr timescale (Lupus age, Fig.
10
).

Another piece of evidence favoring the evolution with time toward a larger fraction of disks with large inner dust cavities is presented in Fig.
5
. Fig.
5
shows that disks with large inner dust cavities have a gas-disk mass compatible with the median gas-disk mass of their regions (
Trapman et al. 2025b
). This means that, at the age of
\sim 1-2
Myr, most disks have regulated the evolution of their gas-disk mass in a similar way that disks with large inner dust cavities do (
van der Marel et al. 2015
;
van der Marel et al. 2016a
), suggesting they also have developed similar dust traps. A different argumentation could be that disks with irregular structure but no large dust cavity have abnormally high gas-disk masses for their regions. These high gas-disk masses could be explained via late infall material and interaction with the environment (
Kuffmeier et al. 2023
;
Pineda et al. 2023
;
Winter et al. 2024a
;
Winter et al. 2024b
). This interaction could also be responsible for the irregular structure, as large scale structures at the outer edges of disks can be replicated easily within a relatively short period of infall (
Kuznetsova et al. 2022
).

Large inner dust cavities are unlikely to have been created via photoevaporation alone (see
Picogna et al. 2023
;
van der Marel 2023
;
Sierra et al. 2024b
, and references therein), with planetary companions clearing a gap as the most promising explanation (e.g. PDS 70,
Keppler et al. 2018
;
Haffert et al. 2019
).
Gárate et al. 2023
and
Huang et al. 2024
have shown that not very massive planets are needed to produce these inner cavities. Indeed, there are two AGE-PRO sources with inner dust cavities for which disk-to-star accretion rate measurements are available (
Zhang et al. 2025
), and these cannot be reconciled with photoevaporating disks (Fig. 6 of
Ercolano & Pascucci 2017
).

We hence propose an evolutionary sequence in which protoplanets at inner orbits have time to open cavities in
\sim 1-2
Myr. This would result in disk populations with a higher fraction of large inner dust cavities at later ages. In turn, this fixes the outer dust-disk radii in a fraction of the population for which R
68%
does not evolve over time and causes the flattening of the R
dust
-L
mm
relationship in Upper Scorpius (Sect.
V.2
). Disks that do not develop these dust traps keep reducing their dust-disk radii and are mostly dispersed or undetected at the Upper Scorpius age. This evolutionary scenario would imply a large fraction of protoplanetary disks with inner planets capable of creating large dust cavities. This fraction is at least 40% of the disk-hosting Upper Scorpius population, based on our limited sample size statistics. This large fraction can be reconciled with the exoplanet demographics observed around main-sequence stars (e.g.
Kiefer et al. 2024
). These planets must have formed fast (within
\sim 2
Myr), which is compatible with theory (e.g.
Lau et al. 2024
). As a final piece of argumentation, we already have a tentative detection for a massive inner protoplanet in the dust cavity of one of the AGE-PRO disks (Upper Scorpius 1,
Sierra et al. 2024a
).

Similar evolutionary sequences have been previously suggested from observational evidence (e.g.
Garufi et al. 2018
;
Garufi et al. 2020
;
Cieza et al. 2021
;
van der Marel & Mulders 2021
;
Parker et al. 2022
;
Orcajo et al. 2025
). This AGE-PRO work provides the first evidence for an evolution of protoplanetary disk populations toward having higher fractions of
transition disks
based on a representative sample of sources in three different star forming regions.

V.4
Substructures in Class I Ophiuchus Sources

We have detected a very high fraction of substructures in our Ophiuchus sample (
\sim 80\%
of the resolved objects). The AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sources were selected by their Class I SED (
Zhang et al. 2025
). Analyzing the R
dust
–L
mm
correlation, in Sect.
V.2
we have shown that the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sample is likely placed toward the end of the Class I phase. This agrees with the age estimations presented by the eDisk program (
Ohashi et al. 2023
). AGE-PRO Ophiuchus 7 (IRS 63) is the oldest source in eDisk (
Flores et al. 2023
), but it is among the youngest sources of AGE-PRO (
Ruiz-Rodriguez et al. 2025
), being the only source in common between both surveys.

It is thus interesting to put the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sample of Class Is in perspective with other studies of dust substructures in protostellar sources. The eDisk program has studied 12 Class 0 and 7 Class I protostars. They have found that the Band 6 1.3 mm continuum emission barely show any sharp high-contrast features, despite the high angular resolution of the program (
\sim 0.04
”). This is true even in those instances where the visibilities were fitted to try to recover substructures (e.g.
Santamaría-Miranda et al. 2024
). In total, only three eDisk sources have substructures reported, including IRS 63 (
Segura-Cox et al. 2020
; Sect.
III.1
, Fig.
2
). A more direct comparison with AGE-PRO is the work of
Michel et al. 2023
, which employed a visibility modeling analysis similar to the one of Sect.
III
, with similar resolution observations.
Michel et al. 2023
analyzed nine Class Is in Ophiuchus, and proposed substructures for four of them, with another four being ambiguous. Hence, there seems to be an increment in the fraction of substructured disks from Class 0/early Class Is (eDisk), to Class Is (
Michel et al. 2023
; see also
Hsieh et al. 2024
;
Hsieh et al. 2025
), to late Class Is (AGE-PRO).
Cieza et al. 2021
also observed a much higher incidence of substructures within Ophiuchus toward the sources that have a more Class II SED shape.

One possible explanation for this transition is that substructures appear during the Class I phase, and they are only ubiquitous at the late Class I stage AGE-PRO is tracing. Another explanation is that early Class I objects are too optically thick at 1.3 mm to identify the existing substructures, but quick dust evolution or disk-settling (
Nazari et al. 2025
) permits substructures to be more easily detected at the late Class I stage. While substructures might evolve during the Class 0 and Class I stages, the optical thickness explanation is likely majorly responsible for the observed trend.
Delussu et al. 2024
suggest that substructures appear early (within 0.4 Myr), and
Maureira et al. 2024
has already found a tentative substructure detection in a Class 0 disk, while substructure detections around young Class I disks are becoming more frequent (e.g.
Nakatani et al. 2020
;
Sheehan et al. 2020
;
Michel et al. 2023
;
Yamato et al. 2023
;
Hsieh et al. 2024
;
Shoshi et al. 2024
).

We conclude that the very high fraction of substructures detected in the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sample compared to previous Class I studies is due to its late Class I stage. AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sources had enough time for their disks to become optically thinner, which caused more dust substructures to be recovered. We note that the very high fraction of substructures in the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sample implies that dust-disk substructures are already ubiquitous at
\sim

0.5-1
Myr.

V.5
Radial Profiles: Dust-disk versus Gas-disk

In this section, we compare the dust-disk radial profiles obtained in Sect.
III.1
with the gas-disk radial profiles derived in
Trapman et al. 2025a
from the AGE-PRO
12
CO moment zero maps (using Nuker and Sersic profiles).
Trapman et al. 2025a
methodology could not be applied to the Ophiuchus sources due to cloud contamination (
Ruiz-Rodriguez et al. 2025
). We note that, given the significant differences between both approaches, the comparison between
Frankenstein
profiles and Nuker profiles presented in this section is only for guidance.

Fig.
11
shows the dust-disk and gas-disk brightness radial profiles for the well-resolved Lupus and Upper Scorpius sources in the continuum. In all cases, the gas-disk extends farther than the dust-disk (see also
Trapman et al. 2025a
). We see tentative gas cavities on Upper Scorpius 3, 6, and 9 (we note none of them appear resolved in the moment 0 maps,
Trapman et al. 2025a
). Of these, Upper Scorpius 6 (2MASS J16163345-2521505) has a dust cavity with a dust-ring located at a similar distance to where the gas-disk peaks, at around 30-40 au. It is the only AGE-PRO disk with a large inner dust cavity whose
12
CO moment zero map is better modeled with a gas cavity. There are well-studied
transition disks
with gas in their dust cavities and those with gas-poor dust cavities (e.g. PDS 70, GM Aur, HD 100546, see
Wölfer et al. 2023
). The presence of gas in the cavity has been associated with multiple Neptunes, with only gas giants being able to carve a gas cavity (
Huang et al. 2024
). We hence pose Upper Scorpius 6 as a disk with a gas-cleared inner dust cavity. Upper Scorpius 3 is one of the most compact AGE-PRO gas-disks, so the gas cavity might be an artifact from
Trapman et al. 2025a
methodology. We note that for Upper Scorpius 3 the dust-disk radii and gas-disk radii are similar. As for Lupus 9 (Appendix
C
), a companion truncating the gas-disk is a possible explanation. Upper Scorpius 9 shows a small gas cavity extending to 10 au. This might suggest Upper Scorpius 9 has an unresolved dust cavity at the 10 au scale (supporting the discussion of Sects.
V.2
and
V.3
).

The other disks with inner dust cavities Upper Scorpius 1, Upper Scorpius 7, and Upper Scorpius 8 show very extended gas-disks, extending to similar sizes
\sim 145-165
au, independently of the position of the dust-ring (e.g., at 78 au for Upper Scorpius 1 vs. at 17 au for Upper Scorpius 8). More information on the relation between gas and dust structure in Upper Scorpius 1 can be found in
Sierra et al. 2024a
, where hints of a protoplanet in the dust cavity are presented. The colossal extent of Lupus 10 (V1094 Sco) is worth mentioning, extending to
\sim 833
au in
12
CO gas.

VI
Conclusions

We performed visibility fitting to the Band 6 1.3 mm continuum data of the 30 AGE-PRO ALMA Large Program protoplanetary disks (
Zhang et al. 2025
). AGE-PRO contains 10 disks in Ophiuchus (
0.5
-
1
Myr), 10 disks in Lupus (
1
-
3
Myr), and 10 disks in Upper Scorpius (
2
-
6
Myr), all in a narrow stellar mass range (0.3-0.8 M
⊙
). We derived disk geometries (inclinations and position angles, Table
1
) for the AGE-PRO protoplanetary disks. For all sources, we homogeneously obtained dust-disk radii (R
68%
, R
90%
, and R
95%
, Table
2
), and azimuthally symmetric radial profiles of the intensity of the dust continuum emission (Figs.
2
,
3
,
4
). We showed that the AGE-PRO sample is representative of the considered regions (Sect.
V
). The mean resolution elements achieved in the visibility fitting are
\sim
13, 22, and 22 au for Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper Scorpius sources, respectively (Sect.
III
).

We examined the presence of continuum substructures by using the radial profiles and their residuals (Fig.
7
). Then, we analyzed the evolution of dust-disk radii and substructures across Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper Scorpius. The main conclusions we have obtained are as follows:

•

We identify substructures in 50% of the AGE-PRO disks (seven in Ophiuchus, two in Lupus, and six in Upper Scorpius). Our methodology detects substructure in almost all sources (
\sim
90\%
) observed at high enough resolution for their disk size (
\theta_{D}/\theta_{res}>3
). We report five disks with large (
>
15 au) inner dust cavities and a ring (or
transition disks
), four of them in the Upper Scorpius sample. This suggests a trend toward disk populations with higher fractions of disks with large inner dust cavities at later ages.

•

We detect no R
dust
–L
mm
significant correlation for the AGE-PRO Upper Scorpius sample. This can be reconciled with previous literature results considering the narrow mass range and the small size of the AGE-PRO sample. For Ophiuchus and Lupus, we recover L
mm
\sim R^{2}
(Fig.
9
). We see no evolution of dust-disk radii with time in the AGE-PRO sample (Fig.
10
). We suggest that a high fraction of disks in Upper Scorpius with dust trapping substructures is the best explanation for these two results.

•

Upper Scorpius disks with large inner dust cavities (or
transition disks
) have gas-disk masses compatible with the median gas-disk mass of the Lupus and Upper Scorpius regions (Fig.
5
). In contrast, Lupus and Upper Scorpius substructured disks with no large dust cavities have gas-disk masses 1 to 2 orders of magnitude higher than the average values in their regions. This suggests that at the age of
\sim
1
-
2
Myr a significant fraction of disks have developed dust traps which are similarly effective to large inner dust cavities to remove gas mass. A different interpretation is to explain the large gas-disk masses via late infall events and interaction with the environment.

•

A spiral is identified in IRS 63 (Fig.
7
), hinting to gravitational instability in this massive disk.

•

The AGE-PRO Ophiuchus Class I disks show dust-disk substructures in
\sim
80% of the well-resolved sources (Fig.
2
). This evidences the early formation of substructures in protoplanetary disks, which appear to be ubiquitous at
0.5
-
1
Myr. Using the R
dust
–L
mm
relationship, we deduce the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sample is at the end of the Class I phase, reconciling our results with other Class I studies that detect a lower incidence of substructures at earlier ages, and suggesting an evolutionary trend or optical thickness effects (Sect.
V.4
).

•

We present a comparison of the radial intensity profiles of the dust continuum emission reported in this work with the gas-disk radial profiles derived in
Trapman et al. 2025a
from the AGE-PRO
12
CO moment zero maps. We discuss colocal substructures in both tracers.

Considering the above results, we propose an evolutionary sequence in which protoplanets at inner orbits have time to open dust cavities in
\sim
1
-
2
Myr. This results in disk populations with a higher fraction of large inner dust cavities at later ages. In turn, this fixes the outer dust-disk radii in a fraction of the population for which the dust-radius does not evolve over time and causes the absence of significant R
dust
-L
mm
correlation in the AGE-PRO Upper Scorpius sample. This evolutionary scenario also explains why disks with large inner dust cavities have gas-masses typical of their region, the dust modeling results of
Kurtovic et al. 2025
, and the absence of evolution of the
R_{\rm CO,\ 90\%}/R_{\rm dust,\ 90\%}
ratio in the AGE-PRO sample from Lupus to Upper Scorpius (
Trapman et al. 2025a
). This evolutionary scenario would imply a large fraction of protoplanetary disks with inner planets capable of creating large dust cavities, of at least 40% of the disk-hosting Upper Scorpius population, based on our limited sample size statistics.

This AGE-PRO work constitutes the first analysis of the evolution of dust-disk radii and dust substructures with a representative sample of protoplanetary disks in three different star forming regions. A detailed discussion for each individual object is presented in Appendix
C
.
