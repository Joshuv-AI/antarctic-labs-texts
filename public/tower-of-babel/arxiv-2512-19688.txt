Pietro Capuozzo, Jack Holden, Andy O’Bannon, James Ratcliffe, Ronnie Rodgers, Benjamin Suzzoni

Abstract
In type IIB supergravity (SUGRA), in the extremal background of a number N c N_{c} of D3-branes we consider a number N f N_{f} of probe D7-branes extended along all four D3-brane directions, ( x 0 , x 1 , x 2 , x 3 ) (x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}) . Using ( x 2 , x 3 ) (x_{2},x_{3}) to define complex coordinates ( z , z ¯ ) (z,\bar{z}) and defining the D7-branes’ worldvolume scalars as ( y , y ¯ ) (y,\bar{y}) , we prove that any holomorphic function y ⁡ ( z ) y(z) or antiholomorphic function y ⁡ ( z ¯ ) y(\bar{z}) is a BPS solution of the D7-branes’ equations of motion preserving 𝒩 = ( 4 , 0 ) \mathcal{N}=(4,0) or ( 0 , 4 ) (0,4) supersymmetry (SUSY) along ( x 0 , x 1 ) (x_{0},x_{1}) , respectively. In the near-horizon geometry, five-dimensional Anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetime times a five-sphere, the AdS/Conformal Field Theory correspondence states that type IIB SUGRA is holographically dual to four-dimensional 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 supersymmetric S ​ U ​ ( N c ) SU(N_{c}) Yang-Mills theory at large N c N_{c} and large ’t Hooft coupling, and the N f N_{f} D7-branes are dual to N f N_{f} 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 hypermultiplets in the fundamental representation, i.e. flavour fields. Our D7-brane solutions are dual to a position-dependent hypermultiplet mass m m that is holomorphic, m ⁡ ( z ) m(z) , or antiholomorphic, m ⁡ ( z ¯ ) m(\bar{z}) . We provide field theory proofs, valid for any N c N_{c} , N f N_{f} , and ’t Hooft coupling, that such m m preserve 𝒩 = ( 4 , 0 ) \mathcal{N}=(4,0) or ( 0 , 4 ) (0,4) SUSY along ( x 0 , x 1 ) (x_{0},x_{1}) . We also perform holographic calculations with probe D7-branes to show that the theory with such m m has zero energy and zero expectation value of the mass operator, and that a zero of m m is dual to a superconformal defect described by chiral fermions along ( x 0 , x 1 ) (x_{0},x_{1}) .

Introduction and summary

Many of the most important open questions in physics involve strongly coupled degrees of freedom without translational and/or rotational symmetry. Examples range from condensed matter physics, such as the Kondo lattice or Hubbard model, to particle physics, such as lattice Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) or high density QCD, whose ground state may break these symmetries (colour superconductor LOFF phase, quarkyonic crystals, etc.). For some such systems, reliable approximation methods exist, such as Monte Carlo importance sampling. However, for many such systems no reliable approximation method currently exists, including in particular systems at non-zero density, where the sign problem renders Monte Carlo importance sampling practically useless. Moreover,
exact
solutions for such systems are exceedingly rare. In this paper we present exact solutions for one such system by using a special symmetry, namely supersymmetry (SUSY).

A textbook theorem of complex analysis states that a non-constant holomorphic function
y(z)
can have only
isolated
zeroes
1
. Said differently, in the complex
z

y(z)
can vanish only on a surface of codimension two, also known as a point, and cannot vanish along a surface of codimension one, also known as a curve. In general, we expect any zero to produce a defect of the kind above, namely a
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(8,0)
superconformal defect. For example, the solution
y(z)=\frac{1}{k}\sin(kz)
, with wave number
k
, describes a “lattice” of such superconformal defects, namely one defect at each zero,
z=n\pi/k
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Another textbook theorem of complex analysis states that a holomorphic function
y(z)
can have only isolated poles
1
. In terms of D-branes, a pole corresponds to the D-branes forming an infinitely-long “spike” in a direction orthogonal to its original worldvolume
9
. Obviously, a pole, such as
y(z)\propto z^{-D}
with integer degree
D

m(z)\propto z^{-D}
, and hence represents a hypermultiplet mass that diverges at
z=0
, decreases linearly as
|z|
increases, and
\to 0
as
|z|\to\infty

d=2
defect off of which the quarks and squarks could scatter, i.e. a “scattering centre.” Solutions with periodically-spaced poles, such as
y(z)=1/(k\sin(kz))
, could describe a fixed lattice of such scattering centres.

In the complex plane the most general holomorphic function without an essential singularity at infinity is a rational function,
y(z)=\frac{(z-a_{1})(z-a_{2})\ldots}{(z-b_{1})(z-b_{2})\ldots}
with complex-valued constants
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)
and
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)

y(z)
thus allows infinite possibilities for holographic descriptions of strongly coupled

z
-plane (through the choice of
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)
and
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
). We hope that this class of exact solutions may be useful for studying problems involving strongly-coupled degrees of freedom without translational and/or rotational symmetry, as mentioned above.

2.3
Subtleties about poles

Strictly speaking, only holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
solve the equation of motion eq. (
18
), while meromorphic or antimeromorphic
y
do not. Put more simply, poles in
y
do not solve the equation of motion. To see why, consider for example a simple pole at the origin,
y(z)=1/z
, for which
\bar{\partial}y\neq 0
at the pole, instead being a delta function:

\bar{\partial}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)=2\pi\,\delta^{(2)}(z,\bar{z}).

(19)

As a result, each term on the left-hand side of eq. (
18
) no longer vanishes, so the equation of motion is violated locally, precisely at the pole. Meromorphic or antimeromorphic
y
still solve the equation of motion
away
from any poles, however. Analogous statements apply to higher-degree poles, such as
y\propto 1/z^{D}
with integer
D>1
, because they also have
\bar{\partial}y\neq 0
: taking
D-1
derivatives
\partial
of eq. (
19
) gives

\bar{\partial}\left(\frac{1}{z^{D}}\right)\propto(-1)^{D-1}\partial^{D-1}\delta^{(2)}(z,\bar{z}),

(20)

which again does not solve the equation of motion locally, at the pole.

Refs.
9
;
16
;
15
;
3
;
2
encountered similar issues about poles, and suggested two methods to modify the equation of motion such that poles become solutions.

The first method to make poles in
y
solve the equation of motion is to add source terms to the equation, such as delta function sources on the right-hand side of eq. (
18
) that can cancel any delta functions produced by poles on the left-hand side. A pole describes a “spike” of D7-brane that extends to spatial infinity, and delta function sources in general represent other string theory objects, such as strings or D-branes, ending on or dissolved into the D7-branes at the tip of the spike, at spatial infinity. In our case such objects are presumably other D7-branes: anything else would source worldvolume gauge field flux on our D7-branes, but in our solutions the gauge fields vanish.

The second method to make a pole in
y
solve the equation of motion is to
excise
all points in the
z
plane where poles are located. In general we must then introduce boundary conditions around those excised points. In other words, we replace the pole with boundary conditions. A detailed discussion of this method appears for example in sec.
4
of ref.
2
.

In what follows we will use the second method to make poles in
y
solve the equations of motion, that is, we will excise points in the
z
plane where any poles are located. Furthermore, we will assume that we can impose boundary conditions around around any excised points so as to preserve the BPS bound of sec.
2.4
and to preserve the SUSY we find in sec.
3

as holomorphic or antiholomorphic, even when

appears to have a pole—because in fact we will implicitly be excising any such poles from the

2.4
D7-brane BPS bound

In this section we will prove a BPS bound on the D7-branes’ action and energy, and prove that holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
saturate this BPS bound. In a sector with fixed BPS charge, such
y
thus extremise the action and solve the equation of motion. The BPS bound will thus explain why these solutions exist, and what they describe.

Our proof will closely follow that of ref.
23
for BPS bounds on actions and energies of various types of probe branes in
d=10
and
d=11
Minkowski spacetimes, in terms of central charges of SUSY algebras. To follow the proof of ref.
23
we must make a crucial distinction between two different SUSY algebras: the target space SUSY algebra (also known as the background or spacetime SUSY algebra) and the worldvolume SUSY algebra. We will briefly review some key properties of these SUSY algebras that we will need for our proof, leaving the full details to the references.

In string and M-theory, the
target space
SUSY algebra’s central charges correspond to stable branes
17
;
43
;
44
;
45
;
10
. Indeed, branes are solitons of string and M-theories, and the central charges provide quantum numbers that protect such solitons from decay. We can define the number of
p
-branes,

, via an asymptotic integral of Ramond-Ramond and/or Neveu-Schwarz
(d-p-2)
-forms in the directions transverse to the
p
-branes. In the target space SUSY algebra the
p
-branes’ central charge
density
then appears as a
p
-form along the
p
-brane’s spatial directions, with coefficient
\propto N_{p}
. Integrating that
p
-form over the
p
-branes’ spatial directions then gives the central charge itself,
Z
. The central charge
Z
is non-zero only if the
p
-branes wrap a non-trivial compact cycle or fill an infinite direction in the target space, since otherwise they would collapse due to their tension (unless something provides a stabilising counter-force, such as worldvolume gauge field flux). The central charge
Z
thus essentially counts the number of
p
-branes wrapping the given cycle/direction, which is topological in the sense that any continuous deformation of the cycle/direction cannot change the number of wrapped
p
-branes. The target space SUSY algebra then gives a BPS condition equating
Z
to the
p
-branes’ total energy. If the
p
-branes are static, then their action is minus their energy, integrated over time. A bound on static
p
-branes’ energy in terms of

Z

On the worldvolume of

19

p

p

p

The target space and worldvolume SUSY algebra central charges must be consistent with one another, since they can both describe the same objects from different perspectives. We should therefore be able to express a BPS bound on brane energy or action in terms of either the target space or worldvolume SUSY algebra. Ref.
23

2.5
8ND D7-brane perspective

Eq. (
37
) is invariant under the exchange of the 4ND and 8ND D7-branes, suggesting that if we start with 8ND D7-branes along
(y,\bar{y})
and write an ansatz
z(y,\bar{y})
and
\bar{z}(y,\bar{y})
, then we will find that holomorphic or antiholomorphic
z
are BPS solutions describing exactly marginal bound states with 4ND D7-branes. Indeed, that is straightforward to show by suitably adapting the proof above, although we will not present the details.

Such BPS solutions for 8ND D7-branes are not necessarily identical to our BPS solutions for 4ND D7-branes. In particular, a holomorphic solution for the 4ND D7-branes,
y(z)
, provides a holomorphic solution for the 8ND D7-branes,
z(y)
, only when
y(z)
has a holomorphic inverse,
y^{-1}=z(y)
. An inverse exists only in domains where
y
is one-to-one (i.e. injective), which is equivalent to a non-vanishing first derivative,
\partial y\neq 0
, which in turn is equivalent to a non-zero Jacobian,
|\partial y|^{2}\neq 0
. The inverse, when defined, is automatically holomorphic:
y\circ y^{-1}=1
implies
\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{y}}\left(y\circ y^{-1}\right)=0
, so that the chain rule gives
\partial y\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{y}}y^{-1}\right)+\bar{\partial}y\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{y}}y^{-1}\right)=0
, and then imposing that
y
is holomorphic gives
\partial y\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{y}}y^{-1}\right)=0
, and then imposing that
y
is injective gives
\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{y}}y^{-1}=0
. Analogous statements of course apply for antiholomorphic
y
and
y^{-1}
.

An illustrative example is a power law solution,
y(z)=a\,z^{D}
with complex constant
a\neq 0
and integer
D=0,1,2,3,\ldots
. All such functions are holomorphic in
z
, and hence are BPS solutions for the 4ND D7-branes. However, such functions are not always one-to-one, and hence do not always have a holomorphic inverse that provides a BPS solution for the 8ND D7-branes. The case
D=0
gives the constant function
y(z)=a
, which maps all points in the
z
-plane to one point in the
y
-plane, which is clearly not one-to-one. Correspondingly,
\partial y=0
for all
z
. As a result,
y(z)=a
has no inverse, and hence provides no BPS solution for the 8ND D7-branes. In contrast,
D=1
gives a linear function,
y(z)=az
, which is one-to-one for all
z
, and correspondingly
\partial y=a\neq 0
for all
z
. As a result,
y(z)=az
has a holomorphic inverse,
z(y)=y/a
, which does provide a BPS solution for the 8ND D7-branes. Indeed, a linear function is not only holomorphic but entire, and in fact is the only entire function with an entire inverse. In contrast to both of those cases,
D>1
gives
y(z)=az^{D}
, which is one-to-one everywhere except the origin,
z=0
, which is a branch point. Correspondingly,
\partial y(z)=a\,D\,z^{D-1}
vanishes at the origin. As a result,
y(z)=az^{D}
has a holomorphic inverse,
z(y)=(y/a)^{1/D}
, only away from
z=0
, and so provides a BPS solution for the 8ND D7-branes only in that domain of the
z
-plane. A non-trivial source could potentially make the branch cut in
z(y)\propto y^{1/D}
a solution.

When a BPS solution for the 4ND D7-branes has an inverse, the corresponding solution for the 8ND D7-branes can provide a useful alternative perspective. For example, what does
N_{8}=N_{f}\,D
mean? Why are the numbers of 4ND and 8ND D7-branes different? Why do the 8ND D7-branes describe
D
copies of the
y
-plane wrapping the
z
-plane? To answer these questions suppose for simplicity that
N_{f}=1
and hence
N_{8}=N_{f}D=D
. From the 8ND D7-brane perspective, the
D
copies of the
y
-plane are simply
D
different 8ND D7-branes. These have a
U(N_{8})
worldvolume gauge group, with the worldvolume scalar
z
is valued in the adjoint of
U(N_{8})
’s complexified Lie algebra, which can describe
N_{f}<N_{8}
units of 4ND D7-brane charge. An example is
z(y)=\left(y/a\right)^{1/D}
, which has
D
sheets, where each sheet is a single 8ND D7-brane and the solution describes their bound state. Further examples of such non-Abelian solutions appear for instance in ref.
37
, which showed that the degree
D
of a pole determines the number of branes in a transverse direction.

In general, however, the 4ND D7-brane perspective is more intuitive than that of the 8ND D7-branes, largely because the former describe flavour fields propagating in all
d=4
directions of the D3-brane worldvolume theory while the latter describe flavour fields propagating on a
d=2
defect. A BPS solution for the 4ND D7-branes,
y
, describes a spatially-dependent mass
m=y/(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})
, which is relatively intuitive. In contrast, a BPS solution for the 8ND D7-branes,
z
, describes the position of the defect changing as we move in the
y
-plane, which is less intuitive. How do we interpret such a solution? At the moment we cannot answer this question completely, but we can provide two useful observations. First, in the D3-branes’ worldvolume theory
y
and
\bar{y}
are adjoint scalars, so a solution like
z(y)
or
z(\bar{y})
describes a coupling to an adjoint scalar that, in effect, changes the defect’s position. How can a defect’s position change? That brings up our second observation: like any non-topological defect, this defect’s operator spectrum includes the displacement operator, which describes local changes in the defect’s position. A solution
z(y)
or
z(\bar{y})
must somehow describe a coupling between the adjoint scalar
y
or
\bar{y}
and the displacement operator such that SUSY is preserved,
SO(6)_{R}
breaks to
SO(4)
, and so on. We leave a complete analysis of the BPS solutions of 8ND D7-branes for future research.

. We therefore only need to find how many of
\varepsilon
’s
16
components survive the projection conditions in eq. (
59
), again recalling that the BPS bound forces us to choose the upper sign in eq. (
59a
). The two matrices on the left-hand side of eq. (
59
) are linearly independent. As a result, only one-quarter of
\varepsilon
’s
16
components survive the D7-branes’ projection condition in eq. (
59
), that is,
4
of
\varepsilon
’s components survive, corresponding to
4
real supercharges.

In summary, then, we have shown that to preserve SUSY
y
must saturate the BPS bound
\mathcal{L}\geq 1\pm\mathcal{Z}
, and hence must be either holomorphic or antiholomorphic in
z
, in which case
y
preserves
4
real supercharges i.e. one-quarter of the D3-brane background’s SUSY.

3.2
All preserved supercharges have the same
d=2
chirality

A generic holomorophic or antiholomorphic function
y
will break translational symmetry in the
z
-plane, and since supercharges anti-commute into generators of translations we expect such
y
to break all SUSY in the
z
-plane. We thus expect any preserved supercharges to be those that anti-commute to the generators of translations in the
(x_{0},x_{1})
plane. In this section we answer a key question about the
4
real supercharges preserved by holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
: is their
d=2
chirality
\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
,
(2,2)
, or
(0,4)
?

The chirality operator in the directions
(x_{0},x_{1})
is
\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{01}
. We will define left-handed spinors by
\left(\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{01}\right)\varepsilon=+\varepsilon
and right-handed spinors by
\left(\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{01}\right)\varepsilon=-\varepsilon
. To determine the chirality of our preserved supercharges we will use two projection conditions. The first is the D3-branes’
\kappa
symmetry projection condition from eq. (
44
),
i\sigma_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{0123}\varepsilon=\varepsilon
. The second is the D7-branes’
\kappa
symmetry projection condition in eq. (
59
), and specifically the projection condition in eq. (
59b
), whose sign is sensitive to whether
y
is holomorphic or antiholomorphic:
\left(i\sigma_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{23}\right)\varepsilon=\pm\varepsilon
, with the upper or lower sign for holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
, respectively. Using these projection conditions we find

\displaystyle(\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{01})\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\pm(\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{01})(i\sigma_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{23})\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\pm(i\sigma_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{0123})\varepsilon

(66)

\displaystyle=\pm\varepsilon,

hence holomorphic
y
preserve
4
left-handed supercharges, or
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
SUSY, while antiholomorphic
y
preserve
4
right-handed supercharges, or
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(0,4)
SUSY.

As a simple check, for a constant solution
y=y_{0}\neq 0
our arguments apply unchanged up to eq. (
57
). If
y
is constant then
\mathcal{Z}=0
. Plugging that into eqs. (
57
) and (
58
) leads only to the projection condition in eq. (
59a
), where again the BPS bound forces us to choose the upper sign,
-i\sigma_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{89}\,\varepsilon=+\varepsilon
, and we do not impose the projection condition in eq. (
59b
). As a result, both chiralities in eq. (
66
) survive, so the constant
y
solution preserves
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
SUSY, which is equivalent to
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY, as expected.

As mentioned below eq. (
37
), back in the original 4ND D3/D7 intersection of tab.
3
, at points where the D7-branes touch the D3-branes, meaning
y\to 0
and
\rho\to 0
so that
r^{2}\equiv\rho^{2}+2|y|^{2}\to 0
, we obtain 8ND D8-branes. These 8ND D8-branes preserve either
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(8,0)
or
(0,8)
SUSY, depending on their orientiation. How does the SUSY enhancement from
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
occur? If
|\partial y|^{2}-|\bar{\partial}y|^{2}\neq 0
as
r\to 0
then
f(r)\propto r^{2}\to 0
and
\mathcal{Z}=f(r)^{-2}(|\partial y|^{2}-|\bar{\partial}y|^{2})\to\infty
, in which case
\mathcal{L}=1\pm\mathcal{Z}\to\pm\infty
. In the first line of eq. (
57
) we can then drop the term
\propto\sigma_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{89}
relative to the term
\propto\mathcal{Z}\left(\sigma_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{23}\right)
, so that we no longer need to impose the projection condition involving
\sigma_{2}\otimes\Gamma_{89}
in eq. (
59a
). Dropping that projection condition doubles the number of preserved supercharges but does not change the chirality analysis in eq. (
66
), thus leading to the doubling of SUSY from
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
to
\mathcal{N}=(8,0)
or
(0,8)
, respectively. We emphasise that such SUSY enhancement emerges
only
when
r\to 0
.

4
Near-horizon limit and holographic duality

For the general
f(r)
in eq. (
4

solve the equation of motion in eq. (

), in sec.
2.4

In this section we restrict to

such that the metric is that of

, and we study the holographic duals of holomorphic or antiholomorphic

. In sec.

preserves
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)

(0,4)

’s zeroes and poles. In sec.
4.2
we move to the AdS side of the correspondence, and use holographic renormalisation for the probe D7-branes

;
6
;
47
;
32
;
34
;
29

the probe D7-branes’ contribution to the renormalised energy vanishes and the VEV of the operator dual to

, which we denote
\mathcal{O}
, vanishes, i.e.
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle=0
. We also illustrate how the probe 4ND D7-branes’ worldvolume geometry encodes holomorphic or antiholomorphic

, with emphasis on the D7-branes’ behaviour near a zero of

where we obtain the 8ND D7-branes and the SUSY enhances to

\mathcal{N}=(8,0)
or
(0,8)
.

4.1
SYM side: (anti-)holomorphic mass and SUSY

In this subsection we will work exclusively in the SYM theory, and prove that a holomorphic or antiholomorphic hypermultiplet mass
m
preserves
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
SUSY along
(x_{0},x_{1})
, respectively. We will present two different field theory proofs. Our first proof, in sec.
4.1.1
, uses methods similar to those of refs.
22
;
20
;
14
;
13
, who showed that to preserve SUSY we can introduce classical background values of additional gauge or SUGRA multiplets. We will introduce a background
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet such that if the multiplet’s scalar acquires a VEV then the hypermultiplets acquire a mass. We will show that a holomorphic or antiholomorphic VEV preserves
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
SUSY along
(x_{0},x_{1})
, respectively. Our second proof, in sec.
4.1.2
uses no background fields, and instead involves showing explicitly that the SYM theory’s classical action is invariant under
d=2

(4,0)
or
(0,4)
SUSY along
(x_{0},x_{1})
in the presence of holomorphic or antiholomorphic
m
. In sec.
4.1.3
we also show that holomorphic or antiholomorphic
m
describe holonomies of a non-dynamical, flat, background
U(1)_{R}
gauge field around zeroes and poles of
m
.

In this subsection
\gamma^{M}
with
M=0,1,2,3
denote Dirac matrices of
d=4
Minkowski spacetime. Using a mostly-plus
d=4
Minkowski inverse metric
\eta^{MN}
, our convention for the Clifford algebra is

\{\gamma^{M},\gamma^{N}\}=2\,\eta^{MN}.

(67)

We take
\gamma^{0}
to be anti-Hermitian and the
\gamma^{M}
with
M=1,2,3
to be Hermitian. The
d=4
chirality matrix,
\gamma_{5}\equiv-i\gamma^{0123}
, is then Hermitian, where multiple indices denote a normalised antisymmetric product, as in sec.
3
. The
d=4
chirality projectors
P_{\pm}
are then

P_{\pm}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{1}\pm\gamma_{5}\right).

(68)

A
d=4
Majorana spinor
\zeta
obeys the Majorana condition
\zeta^{*}=B\zeta
, where the matrix
B
satisfies
(\gamma^{M})^{*}=B\gamma^{M}B^{-1}
, and consequently
(\gamma_{5})^{*}=-B\gamma_{5}B^{-1}
. Since we use the complex coordinates
(z,\bar{z})
defined in eq. (
1
), we also define

\gamma^{z}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\gamma^{2}+i\gamma^{3}\right),

(69a)

\gamma^{\bar{z}}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\gamma^{2}-i\gamma^{3}\right).

(69b)

Our SYM theory has
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY, with R-symmetry
U(2)_{R}\simeq SU(2)_{R}\times U(1)_{R}
. The SUSY parameters are an
SU(2)_{R}
doublet, which we denote
\xi_{I}
with
I=1,2
. These satisfy the condition

\xi_{I}^{*}=i\epsilon_{IJ}B\gamma_{5}\xi_{J},

(70)

with
\epsilon_{IJ}
the
d=2
Levi-Civita symbol with
\epsilon_{12}=+1
. One way to derive
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY is via dimensional reduction from
d=6

\mathcal{N}=1
SUSY, where eq. (
70
) follows from the symplectic Majorana condition on the
d=6
SUSY parameter. Each
\xi_{I}
has
4
complex components, for a total of
8
real components, but eq. (
70
) reduces that to
4
real components. Counting
I=1,2
then gives the
8
real supercharges of
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY.

4.1.1
Background
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet

In this proof we start with
massless
hypermultiplets, and introduce a background
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet in the Cartan of the
U(N_{f})
flavour symmetry. The field content of the
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet is a field strength, which we set to zero, an
SU(2)_{R}
doublet of gauginos,
\Lambda_{I}
with
I=1,2
, and a complex scalar,
\Phi
. We will give
\Phi
a non-zero VEV,
\langle\Phi\rangle\neq 0
, which in turn will give the hypermultiplets a non-zero mass,
m\propto\langle\Phi\rangle
. We let
\Phi
depend on
(z,\bar{z})
so the hypermultiplet mass
m(z,\bar{z})\propto\langle\Phi(z,\bar{z})\rangle
depends on
(z,\bar{z})
as well.

Under a
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY transformation, the
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM vector multiplet, the hypermultiplets, and the background
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet all have non-trivial variations. However, when
\Phi
depends on position the action is invariant under the SUSY variations of the
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM vector multiplet and the hypermultiplets, but not necessarily under the variations of the background
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet. In particular, to preserve SUSY we must find the conditions under which
\Lambda_{I}
has vanishing SUSY variations
46
. When the background
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet has zero field strength, when the background spacetime is Minkowski, and when
\Phi=|\Phi|e^{i\psi}
depends on position,
\Lambda_{I}
’s SUSY variations are

\displaystyle\delta\Lambda_{I}

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{2}\gamma^{M}\partial_{M}\left(|\Phi|e^{-i\psi\gamma_{5}}\right)\xi_{I}

(71a)

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{2}\,\gamma^{M}\,\partial_{M}\Phi\,P_{-}\xi_{I}+i\sqrt{2}\,\gamma^{M}\partial_{M}\bar{\Phi}P_{+}\xi_{I}.

(71b)

One way to derive eq. (
71
) is via dimensional reduction of the SUSY variations of the gauginos in a
d=6

\mathcal{N}=1
vector multiplet. Plugging
\Phi(z,\bar{z})
into eq. (
71
) and demanding that
\delta\Lambda_{I}=0
then gives

(\gamma^{z}\,\partial\Phi+\gamma^{\bar{z}}\,\bar{\partial}\Phi)\,P_{-}\xi_{I}=0,

(72a)

(\gamma^{\bar{z}}\,\bar{\partial}\bar{\Phi}+\gamma^{z}\,\partial\bar{\Phi})\,P_{+}\xi_{I}=0.

(72b)

For generic
\Phi(z,\bar{z})
the only solution of eq. (
72
) is to demand
P_{-}\xi_{I}=0
and
P_{+}\xi_{I}=0
, meaning all SUSY is broken.

However, suppose we demand that
\Phi(z,\bar{z})
is holomorphic in
z
, so that
\bar{\partial}\Phi=0
and
\partial\bar{\Phi}=0
. In that case eq. (
72
) reduces to

\displaystyle\gamma^{z}P_{-}\xi_{I}

\displaystyle=0,

(73a)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\bar{z}}P_{+}\xi_{I}

\displaystyle=0.

(73b)

Actually, if eq. (
73a
) is satisfied then eq. (
73b
) is satisfied too, because eq. (
70
) gives

\gamma^{\bar{z}}P_{+}\xi_{I}=-i\,\epsilon_{IJ}\left(\gamma^{z}P_{-}\xi_{J}\right)^{*}.

(74)

For holomorphic
\Phi(z)
we thus find that
\delta\Lambda_{I}=0
reduces to the single projection condition in eq. (
73a
). Half of the components of
\xi_{I}
survive that projection condition, meaning
4
real supercharges survive. We thus find that holomorphic
\Phi(z)
preserves
4
real supercharges.

Analogously, suppose that
\Phi(z,\bar{z})
is antiholomorphic in
z
, so that
\partial\Phi=0
and
\bar{\partial}\bar{\Phi}=0
. In that case eq. (
72
) reduces to

\displaystyle\gamma^{\bar{z}}P_{-}\xi_{I}

\displaystyle=0,

(75a)

\displaystyle\gamma^{z}P_{+}\xi_{I}

\displaystyle=0,

(75b)

where again eq. (
70
) guarantees that if eq. (
75a
) is satisfied then eq. (
75b
) is satisfied too. As a result,
\delta\Lambda_{I}=0
again reduces to a single projection condition, namely that in eq. (
75a
). Half of the components of
\xi_{I}
survive that projection condition, meaning
4
real supercharges. We thus find that antiholomorphic
\Phi(\bar{z})
preserves
4
real supercharges.

A generic holomorphic or antiholomorphic
\Phi
breaks translations in the
z
-plane, so we expect the
4
preserved supercharges to be those that anti-commute to generators of translations in the
(x_{0},x_{1})
directions. What is the
d=2
chirality of the preserved supercharges:
\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
,
(2,2)
, or
(0,4)
? In sec.
3.2
we showed that a holomorphic or antiholomorphic D7-brane solution
y
preserves
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
\mathcal{N}=(0,4)
SUSY along
(x_{0},x_{1})
, respectively. We can straightforwardly prove the analogous statement in the SYM theory, as follows. Multiplying eqs. (
73b
) and (
75b
) from the left by
\gamma^{2}
and rearranging gives us

i\gamma^{23}P_{+}\xi_{I}=\pm P_{+}\xi_{I},

(76)

with the plus or minus sign for holomorphic or antiholomorphic
\Phi
, respectively. Note the similarity between eq. (
76
) and eq. (
59b
) for the SUSY preserved by a holomorphic or antiholomorphic D7-brane solution

. Multiplying eq. (
76
) from the left by
\gamma_{5}

\gamma_{01}P_{+}\xi_{I}=\pm P_{+}\xi_{I},

(77)

again with the plus or minus sign for holomorphic or antiholomorphic
\Phi
, respectively. Since
\gamma_{01}
is the

chirality operator for the directions
(x_{0},x_{1})
, eq. (
77

\Phi
preserves left-handed supercharges, meaning

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
SUSY, while an antiholomorphic
\Phi
preserves right-handed supercharges, meaning

\mathcal{N}=(0,4)
SUSY, just as occurred in our SUGRA analysis of SUSY in sec.

.

We have thus proven that holomorphic or antiholomorphic
\Phi
preserves
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
SUSY in
(x_{0},x_{1})
, respectively. Since the hypermultiplet mass

, the same results apply for holomorphic or antiholomorphic
m
, namely these preserve
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
SUSY in
(x_{0},x_{1})
, respectively.

This method of proof is extremely general, relying only on the existence of a background
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet and its coupling to the hypermultiplet, such that

. The proof is therefore valid for any values of
N_{c}
,
\lambda
, and
N_{f}
. The proof also clearly agrees with the SUGRA proof in sec.
3
where the two proofs overlap, namely in the near-horizon limit of the SUGRA proof where we can invoke holographic duality with SYM in the ’t Hooft large
N_{c}
limit, the strong coupling limit
\lambda\to\infty
, and the probe limit
N_{f}\ll N_{c}
.

Of course holography also provides results that are difficult to derive directly in the SYM theory, even with SUSY. A prime example is from sec.
2.4
, where we argued that a zero of
m
should exhibit SUSY enhancement from
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
to
(8,0)
or
(0,8)
, respectively. Proving that statement directly in the SYM theory is non-trivial, requiring a renormalisation group (RG) flow that integrates out everything except the massless degrees of freedom. Proving such SUSY enhancement may be within reach given the relatively high amount of SUSY present, but doing so is beyond the scope of this paper.

Being very general, the method of proof in this subsection can be straightforwardly adapted to practically any system in any
d
that admits a background vector multiplet, although the interpretation will depend on how the vector multiplet scalar
\Phi
couples to other fields. Ref.
11
provides further discussion of more general cases. In the next subsection we present our second proof, which is less general, relying crucially on the form of the action of massive
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
hypermultiplets.

4.1.2
SUSY variations of massive hypermultiplets

In our second proof we will start with a
free

d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
hypermultiplet with
constant
mass
m
, whose action is invariant under SUSY variations of
8
real supercharges, and show that a holomorphic or antiholomorphic
m
preserves
4
real supercharges all of the same chirality in
(x_{0},x_{1})
. We will then argue that coupling one or more such hypermultiplets to
d=4

\mathcal{N}=4
SYM does not change our arguments, i.e. holomorphic or antiholomorphic
m
still preserves
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
SUSY along
(x_{0},x_{1})
, respectively.

The matter content of an on-shell
\mathcal{N}=2
hypermultiplet consists of a Dirac spinor
\chi
and a pair of complex scalars
q
and
\tilde{q}
. The theory has R-symmetry
U(2)_{R}\simeq U(1)_{R}\times SU(2)_{R}
. The
U(1)_{R}
acts as an axial symmetry on the Dirac fermions
\chi
and leaves invariant the two scalars
q
and
\tilde{q}
. Under
SU(2)_{R}
the Dirac fermion is invariant while the two scalars form a doublet,
(q,\tilde{q}^{\dagger})
, which we denote
q_{I}
where
I=1,2
with
q_{1}\equiv q
and
q_{2}\equiv\tilde{q}^{\dagger}
. The classical action for a free
\mathcal{N}=2
hypermultiplet with complex mass
m=|m|e^{i\psi}
is then

S_{\mathcal{N}=2}=\int d^{4}x\left(-\partial^{M}q_{I}^{\dagger}\partial_{M}q_{I}-|m|^{2}q_{I}^{\dagger}q_{I}+i\bar{\chi}\not{\partial}\chi-i|m|\bar{\chi}e^{i\psi\gamma_{5}}\chi\right),

(78)

where
\not{\partial}\equiv\gamma^{M}\partial_{M}
and
\bar{\chi}\equiv\chi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}
, and repeated R-symmetry doublet indices are summed.

When
m
is constant, the free hypermultiplet action in eq. (
78
) is invariant under the
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY transformations:

\displaystyle\delta q_{I}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,i\,\bar{\xi}_{I}\chi,

(79a)

\displaystyle\delta\chi

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{2}\,\gamma^{M}\xi_{I}\,\partial_{M}q_{I}-\sqrt{2}\,|m|e^{-i\psi\gamma_{5}}\xi_{I}q_{I},

(79b)

where
\xi_{I}
are again the SUSY parameters, which obey eq. (
70
). One way to derive eq. (
79
) is via dimensional reduction of the SUSY variations of a
d=6

\mathcal{N}=1
hypermultiplet.

When
m
depends on position, the action in eq. (
78
) is no longer invariant under the entire
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY transformation in eq. (
79
) due to spacetime derivatives acting on
|m|
and
\psi
. Specifically, when
m
depends on position eq. (
78
)’s SUSY variation is

\displaystyle\delta S_{\mathcal{N}=2}=\sqrt{2}\,i\int d^{4}x\biggl[

\displaystyle\left(\bar{\chi}\gamma^{M}P_{-}\xi_{I}\,q_{I}+q_{I}^{\dagger}\,\bar{\xi}_{I}P_{-}\gamma^{M}\chi\right)\partial_{M}m

(80)

\displaystyle+\left(\bar{\chi}\gamma^{M}P_{+}\xi_{I}\,q_{I}+q_{I}^{\dagger}\,\bar{\xi}_{I}P_{+}\gamma^{M}\chi\right)\partial_{M}\overline{m}\biggr],

where
\overline{m}=|m|e^{-i\psi}
. In eq. (
80
) the factors of
P_{\pm}
arise because
|m|e^{i\psi\gamma_{5}}=mP_{+}+\overline{m}P_{-}
. If we let
m
depend on
(z,\bar{z})
but not on
(x_{0},x_{1})
, then eq. (
80
) becomes

\displaystyle\delta S_{\mathcal{N}=2}=\sqrt{2}\,i\int d^{4}

\displaystyle x\biggl[\left(\bar{\chi}\gamma^{\bar{z}}P_{-}\xi_{I}\,q_{I}+q_{I}^{\dagger}\,\bar{\xi}_{I}P_{-}\gamma^{\bar{z}}\chi\right)\bar{\partial}m

(81)

\displaystyle+\left(\bar{\chi}\gamma^{z}P_{+}\xi_{I}\,q_{I}+q_{I}^{\dagger}\,\bar{\xi}_{I}P_{+}\gamma^{z}\chi\right)\partial\overline{m}

\displaystyle+\left(\bar{\chi}\gamma^{z}P_{-}\xi_{I}\,q_{I}+q_{I}^{\dagger}\,\bar{\xi}_{I}P_{-}\gamma^{z}\chi\right)\partial m

4.1.3
Background
U(1)_{R}
holonomies

Typically, for position-dependent sources to preserve SUSY requires holonomies of flat background R-symmetry gauge fields
22
;
20
;
14
;
13
. We will end this subsection by showing that holomorphic
m
indeed describe a non-trivial background gauge field of the
U(1)_{R}
, which acts on
\chi
as an axial
U(1)
. An analogous argument applies for antiholomorphic
m
.

In the hypermultiplet action eq. (
78
) we can remove the mass term’s factor of
e^{i\psi\gamma^{5}}
by a field redefinition,
\chi\equiv e^{-i\psi\gamma^{5}/2}\chi^{\prime}
with new Dirac fermion
\chi^{\prime}
. If
\psi
depends on position then the derivative in
\chi
’s kinetic term acts on
\psi
, generating a new term in the action:

i\bar{\chi}\gamma^{M}\partial_{M}\chi-|m|\bar{\chi}e^{i\psi\gamma^{5}}\chi=i\bar{\chi}^{\prime}\gamma^{M}\partial_{M}\chi^{\prime}-|m|\bar{\chi}^{\prime}\chi^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{M}\psi)\bar{\chi}^{\prime}\gamma^{M}\gamma^{5}\chi^{\prime}.

(83)

Since
\frac{1}{2}\bar{\chi}^{\prime}\gamma^{M}\gamma^{5}\chi^{\prime}
is the axial current for the Dirac fermion
\chi^{\prime}
, the right-most term in eq. (
83
) corresponds to a coupling of
\chi^{\prime}
to a background axial vector field,
\mathcal{A}_{M}\equiv\partial_{M}\psi
, or in form notation
\mathcal{A}\equiv d\psi
. Using

\psi=\frac{1}{2i}\log\left(\frac{m}{\overline{m}}\right),

(84)

we can write
\mathcal{A}
as

\mathcal{A}=\frac{1}{2i}\left(\frac{\partial m}{m}dz-\frac{\bar{\partial}\overline{m}}{\overline{m}}d\bar{z}\right).

(85)

Since
\mathcal{A}=d\psi
is locally exact, and hence has vanishing field strength,
\mathcal{A}
can at most have non-trivial holonomies around zeroes or poles of
m
. Indeed, the integral of
\mathcal{A}
along a closed contour
\mathcal{C}
is the same as that in Cauchy’s argument principle, so if we define
n
as the number of zeroes of
m(z)
enclosed by
\mathcal{C}
, weighted by their multiplicity, and
p
as the number of poles of
m(z)
enclosed by
\mathcal{C}
, weighted by their order, then

\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{A}=2\pi(n-p).

(86)

In short, holomorphic
m
automatically describes the background
U(1)_{R}
/axial gauge field
\mathcal{A}
in eq. (
85
) whose only non-trivial content is the integer holonomy in the
z
-plane in eq. (
86
). Analogous statements apply for antiholomorphic
m
: in
\mathcal{A}
’s definition eq. (
85
) simply replace
\partial m/m\to-\partial\overline{m}/\overline{m}
and
\bar{\partial}\overline{m}/\overline{m}\to-\bar{\partial}m/m
and in the holonomy equation eq. (
86
) multiply the right-hand side by
-1
.

4.2
Gravity side: worldvolume geometry of (anti-)holomorphic solutions

In this subsection we work exclusively with probe D7-branes in

4.2.1
Review: holographic dual of constant mass

In
AdS_{5}\times S^{5}
and when
y=0
the
N_{f}
4ND D7-branes are extended along
AdS_{5}\times S^{3}
, where the
S^{3}
is equatorial on the
S^{5}
. These D7-branes are holographically dual to a number
N_{f}\ll N_{c}
of
massless
hypermultiplets in the fundamental representation of
SU(N_{c})

31
. The fact that the D7-branes preserve the isometries of

is dual to the fact that massless hypermultiplets in the probe limit preserve
d=4
conformal symmetry, or more precisely

\mathcal{N}=2
superconformal symmetry. The fact that the D7-branes break the

isometry of the

is dual to the fact that massless hypermultiplets break the

, where
SO(4)\simeq SU(2)_{R}\times SU(2)
, thus producing the

\mathcal{N}=2
R-symmetry
SU(2)_{R}\times SO(2)_{R}
. The
SO(2)_{R}\simeq U(1)_{R}
acts as an axial symmetry on the hypermultiplet fermion, i.e. the “quark”
\chi
, but does not affect the hypermultiplet scalars, i.e. the “squarks”

.

The D7-branes’ complex-valued worldvolume scalar

is holographically dual to the hypermultiplets’ complex-valued mass operator,
\mathcal{O}
. We will write

and denote
|y|
’s dual operator as
\mathcal{O}_{m}
and
\psi
’s dual operator as
\mathcal{O}_{\psi}
. The D7-branes’
U(N_{f})
worldvolume gauge fields are dual to the
U(N_{f})
flavour symmetry currents. Explicit expressions for these operators in terms of SYM fields appear for example in refs.
39
;
29
.

In AdS/CFT we compute SYM correlators from variations of the on-shell action in AdS. The fact that SYM correlators generically have UV divergences that we renormalise using local counterterms is dual to the fact that generically the on-shell action in AdS has divergences at large
r
that we remove using local counterterms on a large-
r
cutoff surface—a procedure called “holographic renormalisation”
18
;
6
;
47
. The counterterms we need to renormalise our probe D7-branes’ on-shell action appear in refs.
32
;
34
;
29
.

To our knowledge, the only non-trivial holomorphic solutions studied to date are the constant solutions,
y(z)=y_{0}
with complex constant
y_{0}

31
;
36
. These solutions are holographically dual to probe flavours with constant mass
m=\sqrt{2}\,y_{0}/(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})
. A non-zero constant mass preserves
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY, including translational and rotational symmetry in the SYM directions
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, but breaks conformal symmetry explicitly. A non-zero constant mass also breaks axial symmetry explicitly, here meaning that
SO(4)\times SO(2)_{R}
breaks down to
SO(4)
.

How do the D7-branes along
AdS_{5}\times S^{3}
encode the properties of massive hypermultiplets? When
m\propto y_{0}\neq 0
, at the
AdS_{5}
boundary the D7-branes still wrap an equatorial
S^{3}\subset S^{5}
, but now as the D7-branes extend into
AdS_{5}
the
S^{3}
“slips” on the
S^{5}
(being a trivial cycle) and eventually shrinks to zero size at a pole of the
S^{5}
. In our coordinates the
S^{3}
collapses to zero size at
\rho=0
, which via eq. (
11
) corresponds to a finite value of
r=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+2|y|^{2}}
, which we denote
r_{0}\equiv\sqrt{2}|y_{0}|
. The D7-branes in
AdS_{5}
thus appear to “end” at
r_{0}
. Since
r
is holographically dual to the SYM theory’s energy scale,
r_{0}
is the holographic dual of the flavour fields’ mass gap: the fact that the D7-branes are present for
r>r_{0}
and absent for
r<r_{0}
is holographically dual to the fact that the flavour fields are present at energies above their mass gap and absent at energies below their mass gap, respectively. When
m\propto y_{0}\neq 0
the D7-branes’ worldvolume therefore only
asymptotically
approaches
AdS_{5}\times S^{3}
near the
AdS_{5}
boundary and is deformed as we move into the
AdS_{5}
bulk. The holographically dual statement is that the SYM theory with massive hypermultiplets is
asymptotically
superconformal in the UV but has a non-trivial RG flow such that in the IR the massive hypermultiplets decouple.

In the SYM theory with constant
m
, the
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY requires the total energy to vanish, including the probe flavour contribution, and also requires
\langle\mathcal{O}_{m}\rangle=0
and
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\rangle=0
because the mass operator is the SUSY variation of another operator

;
21
. SUSY does
not
require
\mathcal{O}_{m}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\psi}
’s higher-point correlators to vanish, however. Holographic renormalisation reproduces these properties
32
;
34
;
33
;
29
: for the constant solution
y=y_{0}
the probe D7-branes’ holographically renormalised on-shell action vanishes, dual to the fact that the probe flavour contribution to the energy vanishes, and the first variation of the on-shell action with respect to
y
’s value at the
AdS_{5}
boundary vanishes, dual to the fact that
\langle\mathcal{O}_{m}\rangle=0
and
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\rangle=0
. Higher-order variations generically do not vanish, however. For example
\mathcal{O}_{m}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\psi}
’s two-point functions are non-zero, and when
m\neq 0
they exhibit poles corresponding to massive mesons

.

4.2.2
Holographic dual of (anti-)holomorphic mass

We have shown than any holomorphic function
y(z)
is a BPS solution of the 4ND D7-branes’ equation of motion. Such a solution is dual to a position-dependent mass,

m(z)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}y(z).

(87)

Similar statements apply for antiholomorphic
y(\bar{z})
.

In the appendix we perform holographic renormalisation for a generic holomorphic solution
y(z)
. In particular, we compute the probe flavour contribution to the energy, and we compute
\langle\mathcal{O}_{m}\rangle
and
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\rangle
. We find that the probe flavour contribution to the energy is zero. That is no surprise, since probe branes dual to SUSY-preserving defects typically have vanishing energy
32
;
33
. Indeed, we could have guessed that the on-shell action and hence the energy would vanish: eq. (
37a
) shows that our on-shell action is simply the sum of the trivial 4ND and 8ND D7-branes’ actions, each of which vanishes independently after holographic renormalisation, due to SUSY. In the appendix we also show that our BPS solutions give

and
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\rangle=0
. In other words, not just the constant solution

but in fact

gives

and

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)

\mathcal{N}=(0,4)

in eq. (

in eq. (

Finally, as mentioned in sec.
1
, we can think of zeroes of
m
as conformal defects and poles of
m
as scattering centres. A key question about such objects is: what results from their fusion? For example, suppose we have two zeroes or two poles of
m

1
, in the complex plane the most general holomorphic function without an essential singularity at infinity is a rational function,
y(z)=\frac{(z-a_{1})(z-a_{2})\ldots}{(z-b_{1})(z-b_{2})\ldots}
with complex constants
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)
and
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
. Fusing two zeroes means making two of the
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)
coincide, which converts two zeroes of degree one into a single zero of degree two. Similarly, fusing two poles means making two of the
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
coincide, which converts two poles of degree one into a single pole of degree two. Fusing a zero with a pole means making one of the
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)
coincide with one of the
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
in which case they cancel between the numerator and denominator of
y(z)
. In short, the “fusion algebra” of zeroes and poles in
y
is described simply by taking a signed sum of their degrees.

5
Summary and outlook

In the background geometry of eq. (
4
), produced by a large number
N_{c}
of D3-branes along
(x_{0},x_{1},z,\bar{z})
, we introduced a probe number
N_{f}\ll N_{c}
of 4ND D7-branes, and in sec.
2.2
we proved that a worldvolume scalar
y
holomorphic or antiholomorphic in
z
solves the D7-branes’ equations of motion. In sec.
2.4
we showed that such solutions saturate a BPS bound and represent an exactly marginal bound state of 4ND and 8ND D7-branes, and in particular we argued that zeroes or poles of holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
describe 8ND D7-branes. Correspondingly, in sec.
3
we proved that holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
preserve
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
SUSY along
(x_{0},x_{1})
, respectively, while zeroes of
y
should exhibit SUSY enhancement to
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(8,0)
or
(0,8)
, respectively. In sec.
4
we restricted to the geometry’s near-horizon
AdS_{5}\times S^{5}
region and invoked holographic duality, wherein type IIB SUGRA in
AdS_{5}\times S^{5}
is dual to
d=4

SU(N_{c})

\mathcal{N}=4
SYM at large
N_{c}
and large
\lambda
while the probe
N_{f}\ll N_{c}
4ND D7-branes are dual to probe hypermultiplets in the fundemental representation of
SU(N_{c})
. Our holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
are dual to a holomorphic or antiholomorphic hypermultiplet mass
m
, and in sec.
4.1
we presented two logically distinct proofs, entirely within SYM theory (with no use of SUGRA or holography), that such
m
preserve
d=2

\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
or
(0,4)
SUSY, respectively, and that such
m
also describe holonomies of a background
U(1)_{R}
gauge field. Finally, in sec.
4.2
we performed a holographic calculation to show that holomorphic or antiholomorphic
m
produces zero VEV of the mass operator,
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle=0
, and that zeroes of
y
describe D7-brane worldvolumes that interpolate between the 4ND D7-branes’ worldvolume,
AdS_{5}\times S^{3}
, and the 8ND D7-branes’ worldvolume,
AdS_{3}\times S^{5}
, which obviously involves a topology change from a trivial cycle
S^{3}\subset S^{5}
to the non-trivial cycle
S^{5}
.

As suggested in sec.
1
, via holography our solutions should be useful for studying many questions about the effects of translational and rotational symmetry breaking, as well as about RG flows from
d=4
fields (the hypermultiplets) to
d=2
fields (
d=2
SUSY-preserving chiral fermions), about conformal defects or “quantum wires”, about fusion of such conformal defects, and more. For example, a solution like
y(z)=\frac{1}{k}\sin(kz)
is holographically dual to mass with zeroes periodically spaced by
1/k
, and hence describes a one-dimensional lattice of conformal defects/quantum wires, while a solution like
y(z)=1/(k\sin(kz))
describes a one-dimensional lattice of infinitely massive scattering centres. The holographically dual probe D7-brane solutions should allow for calculations of meson spectra, entanglement entropy, and more in the presence of such
m
.

Looking farther afield, other intersections of D-branes should admit solutions with similar properties, namely BPS and SUSY-preserving solutions that depend holomorphically or antiholomorphically on spatial coordinates of the SYM theory on the worldvolume on one stack of D-branes. Many such intersections should also have holographic duals. Can we find, and possibly classify, all such intersections and their holomorphic or antiholomorphic solutions? The companion paper ref.
11
will address many such questions. However, many more general questions also deserve further research. For example, in our probe D-brane solutions the only non-trivial worldvolume fields were scalars–what about solutions with non-trivial worldvolume gauge fields or fermions? Can we go beyond the probe limit, and find fully back-reacted holomorphic or antiholomorphic solutions? We hope that our solutions provide the first steps towards addressing these and many other important questions about systems with broken translational and rotational symmetry.

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(z,\bar{z})+\psi_{L}(z,\bar{z})u^{2}\log(u/L)+\psi_{2}(z,\bar{z})u^{2}+\mathcal{O}(u^{3}\log u),

(A.7b)

where
\theta_{1}
,
\theta_{3}
,
\psi_{0}
, and
\psi_{2}
must be fixed by boundary conditions, while
\theta_{L}
and
\psi_{L}
are determined in terms of
\theta_{1}
and
\psi_{0}
by the equations of motion:

\displaystyle\theta_{L}

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}\,\partial\psi_{0}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}-\partial\bar{\partial}\,\theta_{1},

(A.8a)

\displaystyle\psi_{L}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\theta_{1}}\left(\partial\theta_{1}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}+\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}\,\partial\psi_{0}+\theta_{1}\,\partial\bar{\partial}\,\psi_{0}\right).

(A.8b)

Plugging a holomorphic or antiholomorphic solution
y
into the coordinate transformations of eq. (
A.1
) or (
A.2
) gives worldvolume scalars
\theta(z,\bar{z},u)
and
\psi(z,\bar{z},u)
that depend on all of
(z,\bar{z},u)
. In particular, for a holomorphic
y(z)
the FG expansion of eq. (
A.7
) is

\displaystyle\theta(z,\bar{z},u)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,|y(z)|}{L^{2}}\,u+\frac{\sqrt{2}\,|y(z)|^{3}}{3L^{6}}\,u^{3}+\mathcal{O}(u^{5}),

(A.9a)

\displaystyle\psi(z,\bar{z},u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\log\left(\frac{y(z)}{\bar{y}(\bar{z})}\right),

(A.9b)

which clearly depend on all of
(z,\bar{z},u)
. However, these FG expansions do not have
\log(u/L)
terms, so that
\theta_{L}=0
and
\psi_{L}=0
. To be explicit: from eq. (
A.9
) we identify

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}|y(z)|}{L^{2}},

(A.10a)

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\log\left(\frac{y(z)}{\bar{y}(\bar{z})}\right),

(A.10b)

and plugging these into eq. (
A.8
) gives
\theta_{L}=0
and
\psi_{L}=0
. Analogous statements apply for antiholomorphic solutions.

In the AdS/CFT correspondence we identify the hypermultiplet mass
m
from the boundary values of the D7-branes’ worldvolume scalar via

m=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\sqrt{2}\,y,

(A.11)

which in the original 4ND D3/D7 intersection of tab.
1
is simply the string tension,
1/(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})
, times the asymptotic separation between the D3- and D7-branes. Plugging in a holomorphic solution
y(z)
obviously gives

m(z)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,y(z),

(A.12)

as stated in eq. (
3
). An analogous statement applies for antiholomorphic
y
. To find
m
in terms of
\theta(z,\bar{z},u)
and
\psi(z,\bar{z},u)
, we plug the change of coordinates in eq. (
A.1
) or (
A.2
) into eq. (
A.11
), which gives

m=\frac{L^{2}}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\lim_{u\to 0}\frac{\theta(z,\bar{z},u)}{u}e^{i\psi(z,\bar{z},u)}.

(A.13)

Plugging the generic FG expansions of eq. (
A.7
) into eq. (
A.13
) gives

m=\frac{L^{2}}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\theta_{1}\,e^{i\psi_{0}}.

(A.14)

For holomorphic solution
y(z)
, plugging the FG expansions of eq. (
A.9
) into eq. (
A.13
), or equivalently plugging eq. (
A.10
) into eq. (
A.13
), reproduces eq. (
A.12
), as expected.

We subsequently compute correlation functions of the mass operator,
\mathcal{O}
, by taking variational derivatives of the on-shell D7-brane action with respect to
m
. We thus plug the generic FG expansions in eq. (
A.7
) into the action eq. (
A.6
) and perform the integrations. Of course, the integral over
u
diverges near the
AdS_{5}
boundary
u\to 0
. We regulate this divergence by integrating only over
u\geq\epsilon
, for some short distance cutoff
\epsilon
. We denote the resulting regulated action
S_{\textrm{reg}}
. Explicitly, the divergent terms in
S_{\textrm{reg}}
, obtained by expanding the integrand for small
u
and then performing the integral over
u\geq\epsilon
, are

S_{\textrm{reg}}=\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\left[-\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{4}}+\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}+\left(\partial\theta_{1}\,\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}-\theta_{1}\,\partial\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}+2\theta_{1}^{2}\,\partial\psi_{0}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}\right)\log(\epsilon/L)\right]+O(\epsilon^{0}).

(A.15)

Following the holographic renormalisation recipe, we add counterterms at
u=\epsilon
to remove these divergences, render the variational problem well-posed, and produce finite, unambiguous results for observables. The counterterms we need appear in ref.
29
: if we define

\Theta\equiv\theta\,e^{i\psi},

(A.16)

and
\gamma_{\mu\nu}
as the induced metric on the intersection of the D7-branes with the cutoff surface
u=\epsilon
, then the counterterms we need are

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4L^{4}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{|\gamma|},

(A.17a)

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2L^{4}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{|\gamma|}\,|\Theta|^{2},

(A.17b)

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{12L^{4}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{|\gamma|}\,|\Theta|^{4},

(A.17c)

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{|\gamma|}\,\gamma^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\Theta\,\partial_{\nu}\Theta^{*}\log|\Theta|,

(A.17d)

\displaystyle S_{5}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4L^{2}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{|\gamma|}\,\gamma^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\Theta\,\partial_{\nu}\Theta^{*}.

(A.17e)

Plugging the generic FG expansions in eq. (
A.7
) into eq. (
A.17
) and dropping terms that vanish as
\epsilon\to 0
then gives

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{4}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x,

(A.18a)

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\left[\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}+\theta_{1}\theta_{3}+\left(\theta_{1}^{2}\,\partial\psi_{0}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}-\theta_{1}\,\partial\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}\right)\log(\epsilon/L)\right],

(A.18b)

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{12}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\theta_{1}^{4},

(A.18c)

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\left(\partial\theta_{1}\,\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}+\theta_{1}^{2}\,\partial\psi_{0}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}\right)\log(\epsilon\theta_{1}),

(A.18d)

\displaystyle S_{5}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\left(\partial\theta_{1}\,\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}+\theta_{1}^{2}\,\partial\psi_{0}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}\right).

(A.18e)

Comparing eq. (
A.18
) to eq. (
A.15
) shows that the counterterms will cancel all
\epsilon\to 0
divergences of the on-shell action. The resulting renormalised action,
S_{\textrm{ren}}
, defined as

S_{\textrm{ren}}\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(S_{\textrm{reg}}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}S_{i}\right),

(A.19)

will thus be finite and unambiguous.

A.1 Renormalised on-shell action

To obtain the value of
S_{\mathrm{ren}}
we need the
O(\epsilon^{0})
term in
S_{\textrm{reg}}
in eq. (
A.15
), which requires performing the
u
integral in eq. (
A.6
). For generic
\theta(z,\bar{z},u)
and
\psi(z,\bar{z},u)
performing that integral explicitly is difficult. However, for holomorphic or antiholomorphic solutions
y
the integrand simplifies, allowing us to perform the integral explicitly. For holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
, and dropping terms that vanish as
\epsilon\to 0
, we find

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{reg}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathcal{N}_{D7}}{L^{8}}\int d^{4}x\,\int_{\epsilon}^{L^{2}/\sqrt{2}\,|y|}\frac{du}{u^{5}}\left(L^{4}-2u^{2}|y|^{2}\right)\left[L^{4}+u^{4}(|\partial y|^{2}+|\bar{\partial}y|^{2})\right]

(A.20a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\left[-\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{4}}+\frac{|y|^{2}}{L^{4}\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{|\partial y|^{2}+|\bar{\partial}y|^{2}}{2L^{4}}\left(1+\log\left(\frac{2\epsilon^{2}|y|^{2}}{L^{4}}\right)\right)-\frac{|y|^{4}}{L^{8}}\right].

(A.20b)

The upper limit on the
u
integral comes from the coordinate transformation in eq. (
A.1
) or (
A.2
), which implies that
u
is bounded from above by
L^{2}/\sqrt{2}\,|y|
. Substituting the FG coefficients for a holomorphic or antiholomorphic solution
y
from eq. (
A.10
) into the counterterms eq. (
A.18
) gives

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{4}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x,

(A.21a)

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\left(\frac{|y|^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}L^{4}}+\frac{2|y|^{4}}{3L^{8}}\right),

(A.21b)

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{3L^{8}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,|y|^{4},

(A.21c)

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2L^{4}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\left(|\partial y|^{2}+|\bar{\partial}y|^{2}\right)\log\left(\frac{2\epsilon^{2}|y|^{2}}{L^{4}}\right),

(A.21d)

\displaystyle S_{5}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2L^{4}}\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\,\int d^{4}x\,\left(|\partial y|^{2}+|\bar{\partial}y|^{2}\right).

(A.21e)

Plugging eq. (
A.20
) for
S_{\textrm{reg}}
and eq. (
A.21
) for the counterterms of holomorphic or antiholomorphic
y
into eq. (
A.19
) for the renormalised action then gives

S_{\textrm{ren}}=0.

(A.22)

Such a result is not entirely surprising: as mentioned below eq. (
87
), for our holomorphic or antiholomorphic solutions
y
the on-shell D7-brane action is a sum of 4ND and 8ND D7-brane actions, each of which vanishes independently after holographic renormalisation, due to SUSY. Moreover, as explained above eq. (
23
), because
S\equiv-\int dx_{0}\,E
the result in eq. (
A.22
) implies that the total energy
E
vanishes as well.

A.2 Renormalised one-point functions

We denote the operator dual to
\theta
as
\mathcal{O}_{m}
and the operator dual to
\psi
as
\mathcal{O}_{\psi}
. The one-point functions
\langle\mathcal{O}_{m}\rangle
and
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\rangle
are given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{L^{2}}\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{ren}}}{\delta\theta_{1}},

(A.23a)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{ren}}}{\delta\psi_{0}}.

(A.23b)

To compute the variation of the renormalised action,
\delta S_{\textrm{ren}}
, we need the variations of the regulated action,
\delta S_{\textrm{reg}}
, and of the counterterms. To compute
\delta S_{\textrm{reg}}
, we define a Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
from the regulated D7-brane action
S_{\textrm{reg}}
from eq. (
A.6
) via

S_{\textrm{reg}}=-\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int_{\epsilon}du\int d^{4}x\,\mathcal{L},

(A.24)

or in other words
\mathcal{L}
is the integrand in eq. (
A.6
). The variations
\theta\to\theta+\delta\theta
and
\psi\to\psi+\delta\psi
then produce a variation of the regulated action,

\delta S_{\textrm{reg}}=\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\theta}}\delta\theta+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\psi}}\delta\psi\right]_{u=\epsilon}.

(A.25)

If we plug the generic FG expansions for
\theta(z,\bar{z},u)
and
\psi(z,\bar{z},u)
of eq. (
A.7
) into eq. (
A.25
) and integrate by parts to remove and
z
and
\bar{z}
derivatives from the variations, and drop terms that vanish as
\epsilon\to 0
, then we find

\displaystyle\delta S_{\textrm{reg}}=\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\biggl[

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{1}\,\delta\theta_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}}+4(\theta_{L}\,\delta\theta_{1}+\theta_{1}^{2}\psi_{L}\,\delta\psi_{0})\log(\epsilon/L)+\theta_{1}\,\delta\theta_{3}

(A.26)

\displaystyle+\left(3\theta_{3}-2\theta_{1}^{3}+\theta_{L}\right)\delta\theta_{1}+\theta_{1}^{2}\left(2\psi_{2}+\psi_{L}\right)\delta\psi_{0}\biggr].

We next need the variations of the counterterms from eq. (
A.18
),

\displaystyle\delta S_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

(A.27a)

\displaystyle\delta S_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\left[-\frac{\theta_{1}\,\delta\theta_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}}-2\left(\theta_{L}\,\delta\theta_{1}+\theta_{1}^{2}\psi_{L}\,\delta\psi_{0}\right)\log(\epsilon/L)-\theta_{1}\,\delta\theta_{3}-\theta_{3}\,\delta\theta_{1}\right],

(A.27b)

\displaystyle\delta S_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{3}\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\theta_{1}^{3}\,\delta\theta_{1},

(A.27c)

\displaystyle\delta S_{4}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\biggl[-2(\theta_{L}\,\delta\theta_{1}+\theta_{1}^{2}\psi_{L}\,\delta\psi_{0})\log(\epsilon\theta_{1})

\displaystyle\phantom{=\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,h^{3}c^{2}\biggl[}+\frac{\partial\theta_{1}\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}-\theta_{1}^{2}\partial\psi_{0}\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}}{\theta_{1}}\delta\theta_{1}+\left(\partial\theta_{1}\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}+\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}\partial\psi_{0}\right)\theta_{1}\,\delta\psi_{0}\biggr],

(A.27d)

\displaystyle\delta S_{5}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\left(\theta_{L}\,\delta\theta_{1}+\theta_{1}^{2}\,\psi_{L}\,\delta\psi_{0}\right).

(A.27e)

Following eq. (
A.19
), we add these to
\delta S_{\textrm{reg}}
to obtain
\delta S_{\textrm{ren}}
:

\displaystyle\delta S_{\mathrm{ren}}=2\,\mathcal{N}_{D7}\int d^{4}x\,\biggl[

\displaystyle\biggl(\theta_{3}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{3}}{6}-\theta_{L}\log(\theta_{1}L)+\frac{\partial\theta_{1}\,\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}-\theta_{1}^{2}\,\partial\psi_{0}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}}{2\theta_{1}}\biggr)\delta\theta_{1}

(A.28)

\displaystyle+\theta_{1}^{2}\biggl(\psi_{2}-\psi_{L}\log(\theta_{1}L)+\frac{\partial\theta_{1}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}+\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}\,\partial\psi_{0}}{2\theta_{1}}\biggr)\delta\psi_{0}\biggr].

As required by the variational principle,
\delta S_{\mathrm{ren}}
is finite in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, and is independent of the variation of the normalisable coefficients
\delta\theta_{3}
and
\delta\psi_{2}
.

Finally, plugging
\delta S_{\mathrm{ren}}
from eq. (
A.28
) into eq. (
A.23
) gives the one-point functions,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{3}}\biggl[\theta_{3}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{3}}{6}-\theta_{L}\log(\theta_{1}L)+\frac{\partial\theta_{1}\,\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}-\theta_{1}^{2}\,\partial\psi_{0}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}}{2\theta_{1}}\biggr],

(A.29a)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda N_{f}N_{c}}{8\pi^{4}}\,\theta_{1}^{2}\biggl(\psi_{2}-\psi_{L}\log(\theta_{1}L)+\frac{\partial\theta_{1}\,\bar{\partial}\psi_{0}+\bar{\partial}\theta_{1}\,\partial\psi_{0}}{2\theta_{1}}\biggr).

(A.29b)

For a holomorphic or antiholomorphic solution
y
, plugging the FG expansions of eq. (
A.9
) into eq. (
A.29
), we find that all terms cancel, so that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(A.30a)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=0.

(A.30b)

As mentioned in secs.
1
and
4.2.2
, we suspect that the result in eq. (
A.30
) follows from SUSY, although we have not proven so.
