Endovascular management of a ruptured pancreaticoduodenal artery pseudoaneurysm using a stent-graft: a case report and literature review
Authors: Anas A. Abukhalaf, Alaa R Al-Ihribat, Walid K.M Sabarna, Baraa M. AbuMandil, Rajai ALhusseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Pancreaticoduodenal artery (PDA) pseudoaneurysms are rare vascular complications, accounting for approximately 2% of all visceral artery aneurysms. They are often associated with chronic pancreatitis and have a high risk of rupture, which can be life-threatening.Case presentation:A 45-year-old male with a history of chronic alcoholic pancreatitis presented with recurrent epigastric pain radiating to the back, associated with anorexia and significant weight loss. He had undergone multiple prior endoscopies and biopsies without a definitive diagnosis. During endoscopy at our institution, a pulsatile submucosal mass in the duodenum was identified, prompting CT angiography, which revealed a pseudoaneurysm of the superior posterior PDA with an arteriovenous fistula. Shortly afterward, the patient developed massive gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding and hemodynamic instability. Emergency angiography was attempted; however, coil embolization failed due to intra-procedural rupture. A covered stent (CeloNova 4.0 mm × 23 mm) was deployed, successfully excluding the pseudoaneurysm and stabilizing the patient.Discussion:PDA pseudoaneurysms are diagnostically challenging and often missed on standard imaging and endoscopy. Our case demonstrates the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients with chronic pancreatitis and unexplained GI findings. Endovascular therapy is the preferred treatment, with coil embolization as the standard approach; however, in cases of rupture, arteriovenous fistula, or failed embolization, covered stent grafting is a life-saving alternative.Conclusion:This case highlights a rare presentation of a ruptured PDA pseudoaneurysm with an AV fistula, initially missed despite multiple prior evaluations, successfully managed with a covered stent following failed embolization. Prompt recognition and flexible endovascular techniques are critical in preventing mortality in such complex vascular emergencies.

Introduction

Pancreaticoduodenal artery (PDA) pseudoaneurysms are extremely rare vascular lesions, accounting for approximately 2% of all visceral artery aneurysms[1]. They are most commonly associated with pancreatitis, where local inflammation and enzymatic activity contribute to arterial wall erosion and pseudoaneurysm formation[2]. Case presentation:

A 45-year-old male patient with a background of chronic alcoholic pancreatitis was referred to our hospital due to ongoing abdominal pain and suspected pancreatic pathology. The patient had a long-standing history of recurrent abdominal symptoms, for which he underwent multiple endoscopic evaluations and biopsies at different centers without a definitive diagnosis or therapeutic benefit.

Five days prior to admission, he developed colicky epigastric pain radiating to the back, worsened by food intake and associated with fatigue, anorexia, and significant weight loss. Upon presentation to our medical ward, he was stable and scheduled for endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP).

During the scheduled endoscopy, a pulsatile submucosal mass-like lesion was noted in the bulbar and post-bulbar region of the duodenum. Due to its vascular appearance, no biopsy was taken. The procedure was halted, and computed tomography (CT) angiography was promptly performed.

Imaging revealed a lobulated, thick-walled cystic lesion in the pancreatic tail (7 × 4.5 cm) and another component near the spleen (3.8 × 2.5 cm), consistent with pancreatic pseudocysts. Additionally, an atrophic pancreas with ductal calcifications and a 1-cm pancreatic duct dilation was noted, indicative of chronic pancreatitis (Fig. 1). Importantly, a pseudoaneurysm of the superior posterior PDA with suspected arteriovenous (AV) fistula formation was identified (Fig. 2), and the splenic vein was not visualized, raising concerns of thrombosis or prior ligation. Shortly after the imaging, the patient developed hematemesis and melena, became hemodynamically unstable with hypotension, and was transferred to the ICU. He received fluid resuscitation, Sandostatin, proton pump inhibitor infusions, and a total of 6 units of packed red blood cells (PRBCs).

Emergency celiac angiography was undertaken; however, initial embolization attempts failed due to rupture of the pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 3A). Consequently, a covered stent (Celosia 4.0 mm × 23 mm) was deployed at the origin of the superior posterior PDA (Fig. 3B&C), successfully excluding the lesion and controlling the hemorrhage. Post-intervention, the patient remained vitally stable. Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT imaging of the abdomen demonstrated multiple intrahepatic bile duct dilatations, splenomegaly, and gastroesophageal varices, consistent with portal hypertension. No active gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding or solid hepatic lesions were detected. He was discharged on Lantus, Metformin, ASA, Clopidogrel, and PPI, with follow-up arranged for EUS and ERCP.

Discussion

Visceral artery aneurysms are rare, occurring in only 0.1–0.2% of the population, with PDA pseudoaneurysms accounting for approximately 2% of these cases [5]. These aneurysms are often associated with chronic pancreatitis, where repetitive inflammation and enzymatic degradation erode the arterial wall, leading to weakness and pseudoaneurysm formation[2]. The pathophysiology is complex, involving both local inflammatory processes and hemodynamic stressors, especially in patients with coexistent portal hypertension or celiac axis stenosis[6]. Once formed, pseudoaneurysms carry a high rupture risk, estimated to be as high as 50%, independent of size, making early detection and treatment essential[7].

Our patient illustrates a critical diagnostic challenge: multiple endoscopies and biopsies had been performed previously, yet no vascular abnormality was identified. PDA pseudoaneurysms often present with non-specific abdominal pain, anemia, or recurrent pancreatitis-like symptoms rather than overt bleeding until rupture occurs[8]. Even when bleeding is present, upper endoscopy frequently shows only nonspecific findings such as mucosal edema or submucosal masses, which are easily misinterpreted as neoplastic or inflammatory lesions[9].

In our case, it was only when a pulsatile submucosal mass was visualized during endoscopy that the possibility of a vascular lesion was raised, prompting urgent CT angiography. Literature consistently shows that delayed diagnosis is common, often due to reliance on endoscopy or non-angiographic imaging, leading to missed opportunities for early intervention[10]. This emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and incorporating angiographic imaging early in patients with unexplained duodenal lesions or recurrent GI bleeding.

Endovascular intervention has largely replaced open surgery as the first-line therapy for visceral artery pseudoaneurysms, with success rates exceeding 90% and lower morbidity[11]. Coil embolization remains the most widely used technique and is effective for pseudoaneurysms without critical branch perfusion requirements[12]. Liquid embolics (e.g., glue, Onyx) are alternatives, particularly in small-caliber vessels or complex collateral networks[13].

However, in cases complicated by rupture or the presence of an AV fistula, as in our patient, simple embolization may be insufficient or even hazardous. We initially attempted embolization, but rupture occurred during the procedure, necessitating the rapid deployment of a covered stent graft. Covered stents allow the exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm while maintaining arterial patency, which is crucial in vessels supplying the pancreas and duodenum[14]. This approach, though technically demanding, can be lifesaving and has been reported only in a handful of PDA cases worldwide[5].

The main limitations of covered stents in visceral applications are infection risk, thrombosis, and in-stent restenosis, particularly in pancreatitis-related pseudoaneurysms where a contaminated field is common[8]. Patients treated with covered stents are often placed on antiplatelet therapy to maintain stent patency[11]; however, the optimal regimen remains individualized depending on bleeding risk, stent location, and clinical context. In our case, dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated due to the high risk of stent thrombosis, and the patient experienced no recurrent bleeding; he was subsequently discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy with planned follow-up using EUS and periodic contrast-enhanced CT angiography to monitor stent patency and detect any recurrence.

Several PDA pseudoaneurysm cases have been reported in the literature, but our case differs in several key aspects. Samarasinghe et al described a PDA aneurysm successfully treated with coil embolization, but without rupture or the presence of an AV fistula[1]. Kling et al reported a spontaneous rupture managed by embolization alone, with no requirement for stent placement or vessel preservation[2]. Ida et al documented the use of a stent graft in a PDA pseudoaneurysm, but in a non-ruptured setting and without AV fistula involvement[9]. In contrast, our patient had a ruptured PDA pseudoaneurysm associated with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, multiple prior negative diagnostic evaluations, and an AV fistula, with initial embolization failure necessitating rapid conversion to a covered stent graft. These unique features make our case exceptionally rare and highlight important diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Conclusion:

Ruptured PDA pseudoaneurysms remain a rare but life-threatening complication of pancreatitis, frequently misdiagnosed due to their non-specific clinical and endoscopic presentation. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for vascular lesions in patients with recurrent, unexplained GI findings and chronic pancreatitis. Although coil embolization is the mainstay of treatment, covered stent grafting is a valuable alternative when embolization fails or when vessel preservation is essential. Our experience demonstrates that timely recognition and flexible endovascular strategies can be lifesaving in complex visceral artery pseudoaneurysms.
