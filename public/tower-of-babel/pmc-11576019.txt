Does type II diabetes mellitus increase the morbidity of patients with diverticulitis?
Authors: Marei H. Alshandeer, Walid M. Abd El Maksoud, Khaled S. Abbas, Fahad S. Al Amri, Maha A. Alghamdi, Hassan A. Alzahrani, Abdullah Dalboh, Mohammed A. Bawahab, Aisha J. Asiri, Yahia Assiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Diverticular disease is a common condition that has numerous complications. Understanding the impact of diabetes mellitus (DM) on these complications, especially diverticulitis, is crucial for optimizing patient care. This study aimed to determine the relationship between type II DM and the complications of colonic diverticulitis. A retrospective cohort study was conducted on 158 patients complaining of diverticulitis at Asir Central Hospital, Abha, Saudi Arabia, between January 2013 and December 2023. Data on gender, age, and chronic diseases, especially DM, were collected. Data retrieved regarding diverticulitis included the involved segment, complications, Hinchey classification, and management. We classified the patients into groups A for nondiabetics and B for diabetics. We analyzed the data using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, t tests, and analysis of variance. Diabetic patients were significantly older than their nondiabetic counterparts. Diabetic patients showed a significantly higher complication rate (62.5%) and a higher degree of Hinchey classification compared to nondiabetic patients (43.7%). Furthermore, in comparison to individuals without diabetes, they were hospitalized for a considerably extended period (8.06 ± 7.38 days vs 5.26 ± 5.90 days, respectively). In addition, surgical intervention was observed to be considerably more common in patients with diabetes (46.9%) than in those without diabetes (16.5%). The study showed that DM adversely affected patients with diverticulitis. A greater incidence of complications and a higher category of Hinchey classification were associated with DM compared to nondiabetics. Additionally, diabetics underwent more surgical interventions and had longer hospital stays. Diabetics with diverticulitis require particular care to prevent severe complications.

1. Introduction

Diverticular disease is a common condition in which the colonic mucosa herniates through the muscular layer of the wall.[1] Diverticular disease’s exact cause remains unclear, but risk factors such as lack of physical activity, smoking, and low dietary fiber intake contribute to its development.[2] Furthermore, the risk of diverticular disease includes colonic wall abnormalities, intestinal motility disorders, genetics, and sedentary lifestyle.[3]

Diverticular disease of the colon is one of the most premier causes of hospitalization and outpatient visits in Western population. There has been a rise in the prevalence of this condition among individuals below the age of 40, contrary to previous beliefs that it predominantly affected the elderly.[4]

Diverticular disease of the colon is not uncommon in Middle Eastern population. Previous studies among some Middle Eastern populations revealed that there has been an increase in the prevalence of diverticulosis, particularly among older males.[5,6] A study conducted in Saudi Arabia revealed that diverticulosis was identified in 7.4% of patients who had colonoscopy for various causes from 2007 to 2011.[6] Adoption of a western lifestyle may explain the increasing prevalence of colonic diverticulosis.[5]

While the majority of individuals with diverticulosis are asymptomatic, approximately 10% to 25% will eventually suffer an acute episode of diverticulitis.[7] The typical consequences associated with diverticulitis include hemorrhage, peridiverticular abscess, perforation, stricture, and fistula formation. While some complications can be managed conservatively, others may require surgical intervention and carry a significant risk of morbidity and mortality.[8,9]

The prognosis for complications arising from diverticulitis depends on both the severity of the complication and the management plan followed. The mortality rate in simple diverticulitis is low with appropriate conservative treatment. In general, early diagnosis and proper care substantially enhance outcomes.[10] Immunocompromised patients face an elevated risk of severe repercussions and a poorer prognosis due to impaired localization capabilities and a disrupted healing process. These consequences may be more serious and necessitate immediate medical care surgical intervention.[11]

Evidence in medical literature indicates that inadequate glycemic control and hyperglycemia are linked to compromised wound healing and heightened infection risk, resulting in an increased likelihood of postoperative complications in colorectal surgery.[12] Moreover, hyperglycemia impairs the inflammatory response, resulting in the failure of local vasodilation, bacterial opsonization, neutrophil adherence, chemotaxis, and phagocytosis. These consequences lead to diminished peripheral blood flow and angiogenesis, ultimately hindering wound healing.[13] Such immunological and physiological alterations adversely impact healing of anastomoses and elevate the incidence of septic complications, resulting in inferior surgical results. Presently, data from multicenter and cohort studies indicate that diabetic patients undergoing colorectal surgery have a markedly elevated risk of surgical site infection[14,15] and anastomotic leakage[16] in the postoperative period.

The association between diabetes and diverticular disease is unclear, with studies showing conflicting results.[17,18] Researchers have asserted a direct correlation between type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) and diverticular disease. This means that people with type 2 DM are more likely to get diverticulosis with their subsequent complications.[19] In contrast, a study with a substantial sample size and rigorous control of potential confounding variables revealed no evidence for a correlation between the 2 conditions.[18] Another study had mixed results regarding nonoperative and surgical management of diverticular disease among diabetic and nondiabetic patients.[20]

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine the relationship between type II DM and the complications of colonic diverticulitis.

2. Methodology

2.1. Study design

This was a retrospective case–control observational study that included all patients who were diagnosed with colonic diverticulitis in Asir Central Hospital during the period of 10 years (from January 2013–December 2023).

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The files of the patients were revised, and patients with the diagnosis of diverticulitis or complications of diverticulitis were included in the study. We excluded patients with no clear diagnosis, type I DM, or missing data from the study. Additionally, patients with diverticulosis not diverticulitis, those presenting with hemorrhage, and individuals diagnosed with colon cancer or inflammatory bowel disease upon final histological evaluation, who were erroneously identified as having diverticulitis, were also removed.

2.3. Data collection

The following data were retrieved from the files: demographic data, diabetic status, clinical picture, hospital stay, and management. We performed a search of these patients’ computed tomography (CT) scans to confirm the diagnosis, reporting findings suggestive of diverticulitis.

Patients included were divided into 2 groups: group (A) for patients who did not have DM and group (B) for patients with DM, based on their medical records and investigations on admission. Comparisons between the 2 groups were performed regarding multiple variables, including the presence or absence of complications and the severity of complications, using modified Hinchey classification by Sher et al.[21]

2.4. Sample size determination

All the patients with the diagnosis of diverticulitis or complications of diverticulitis in Asir Central Hospital during the period of 10 years (from January 2013–December 2023) were included in the study.

2.5. Outcome measures

Determining the impact of type II DM on the complications of diverticulitis by comparing their data to a nondiabetic control group using the modified Hinchey classification.

2.6. Statistical analysis

We fed data into the computer and analyzed them using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences software package version 24.0. Qualitative data were described using numbers and percents. Quantitative data were described using range (minimum and maximum), mean, standard deviation, and median. The significance of the obtained results was judged at the 5% level.

For categorized parameters, the chi-square test was used, while for numerical data to compare between 2 groups, the Student t test was used. The level of significance was 0.05.

2.7. Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at King Khalid University (HAPO-06-B-001), with the number ECM#2023-2106.

The implementation of several procedures obscured the patients’ identities. There was a complete absence of any records containing names, addresses, or any other sort of identifying information.

3. Results

The study included 158 patients who were diagnosed with diverticulitis (117 males and 41 females) with a mean age of 49.77 ± 14.80 years (range 24–106 years). A total of 32 patients (20.3%) were diabetics, of whom 23 (71.9%) had uncontrolled DM. Most of the patients, 124 (78.5%), had diverticulitis limited to the sigmoid colon. Of the patients, approximately 47.5% presented with complicated diverticulitis, with 52% classified as Hinchey II. 129 patients (81.6%) were experiencing their first episode of diverticulitis. 4. Discussion

The study’s main findings are: the rationale for this research is to comprehensively understand the impact of type II DM on diverticulitis outcomes. Key findings revealed that diabetic patients were significantly older than their nondiabetic counterparts. Diabetic patients showed a significantly higher complication rate and a higher degree of Hinchey classification compared to nondiabetic patients. Additionally, they experienced a significantly longer duration of hospitalization. Furthermore, surgical intervention was significantly more prevalent among diabetic patients.

4.1. Interpretation of the main study findings

In 2014, Saudi Arabia became the world’s 19th-largest economy, experiencing rapid growth.[22] Household income surged by 75% from 2003 to 2013, driven by increased public-sector employment and wages.[21] Modernization efforts brought prosperity and societal change; as about 83% of the population lives in cities, urbanization exceeds that of some Western European countries.[23,24] These shifts led to dietary changes, including increased consumption of foods high in added sugars, salt, and unhealthy fats, as well as decreased physical activity and rising sedentary behaviors, contributing to the country’s rising morbidities.[25] Saudi Arabia reported an overall prevalence of DM of 23.7%.[26,27]

In our study, about two-thirds of the patients who were diagnosed with diverticulitis were under the age of 60, which appears to be a lower age than it is worldwide, keeping in mind that we did not include patients with diverticular disease who did not have diverticulitis as well as patients who had other presentations such as lower gastrointestinal bleeding. Bamanie et al[28] conducted a study in Saudi Arabia and reported a median age of 53 years among Saudi patients with acute diverticulitis. The occurrence of diverticular disease was first associated with older individuals, particularly those aged 70 years and older.[1] Furthermore, the incidence of colon diverticulitis has increased, particularly among younger populations. These individuals have been shown to undergo a more severe illness progression, requiring more frequent hospitalizations and a higher demand for surgical intervention.[29]

About 35.9% of our patients had 50 years or less. Several studies have documented a rising prevalence of acute diverticulitis in hospitalized persons under the age of 50, with rates ranging from 18% to 34%,[30,31] which aligns with these findings. On the other hand, in their prospective analysis, Lahat et al[32] found that patients under the age of 45 accounted for only 12% of the studied patients. They also stated that young Latinos have a higher propensity to acquire acute diverticulitis. We think that the different ethnic groups may influence the age of the patients who are susceptible to developing diverticulitis. Furthermore, lifestyle may contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of diverticulitis.[33] With the westernization noticed in Saudi Arabia in the last few years, the food habits are shifting towards non-fiber meals, especially among the young population.[34] It was reported that nondietary fiber and red meat consumption are associated with diverticulitis risk.[35]

Regarding the gender of our studied group, men were more affected than women (74.1% vs 25.9%). A similar higher prevalence of males compared to females in the incidence of diverticulitis was reported in several studies.[1,28,31] In our study, the female patients in group B had significantly more than those in group A. We were unable to find a clear explanation for this discovery. We may suggest that the incidence of obese patients among females in Saudi Arabia is quite high. According to Al-Shammari et al,[36] 41.9% of Saudi females had a moderate level of obesity, with a body mass index (BMI) ranging from 30 to 40 kg/m2. Furthermore, a BMI exceeding 40 kg/m2 classified 5.1% of Saudi females as morbidly obese. Researchers reported a significant association between colonic diverticulosis and obesity.[37] Nevertheless, further studies may be required to figure this out.

In the current study, the left side of the colon was more affected in both diabetic and nondiabetic patients. In agreement with our study, AlLehbi et al[5] reported, in their study in Saudi Arabia, that in DM patients, the most predominant site of diverticulosis is in the sigmoid region, followed by left-sided/sigmoid. Similar to our community, in western societies, left-sided diverticulitis was shown to be the most prevalent.[38] Conversely, data from Korea suggests that there is a clear correlation between diabetes and diverticulosis in the entire colon and right colon, but not in the left-sided.[39] Again, ethnicity and dietary habits while adopting the western lifestyle may be the cause of that difference. This explanation is supported by the results of the meta-analysis conducted by Hajibandeh et al[40] who revealed that the incidence of left-sided disease is increasing among the Asian population due to the adaptation of a more Western diet and lifestyle.

The study revealed that patients in group B had a significantly greater incidence of complications compared to those in group A (62.5% vs 43.7%, respectively, P = .046). Additionally, they were associated with a significantly higher Hinchey classification category, indicating a higher level of morbidity. A meta-analysis by Lin et al[19] showed that patients with DM had approximately 1.201 times higher colonic diverticular disease morbidity in prospective studies. Furthermore, Cologne et al[20] reported that DM patients were more likely to present to the hospital with statistically higher modified Hinchey scores. They found significantly more DM patients presented with complicated diverticular disease, specifically abscess, fistula, and the presence of peritonitis, compared with nondiabetic patients. Omar et al[41] concluded in their study that diabetes is associated with advanced severe diverticulitis, which presents with peritonitis.

Our study revealed that DM had an adverse impact on the management of diverticulitis. Patients in group B had a significantly greater incidence of surgical intervention, laparotomy, and Hartmann procedure compared to those in group A. As a result, they experienced a significantly longer length of hospitalization. In their meta-analysis, Lin et al[19] discovered that individuals with DM had a significantly increased risk of colectomy, almost 2.3 times greater than those without DM. The researchers determined that DM was a plausible risk factor for illness and the need for surgery, specifically colectomy, in patients with diverticulitis. In contrast, Cologne et al[20] discovered that diabetic patients with diverticulitis have a nonoperative success rate that is comparable to that of nondiabetic patients. Their findings link surgical treatment in diabetes patients to a greater occurrence of infectious complications and acute kidney injury. However, it does not seem to raise the risk of death during the operation.

We believe that the pathophysiology of the disease could explain more complications and severer morbidity among diabetic patients. The hyperglycemic milieu, which promotes immunological dysfunction, is responsible for the higher incidence of infections in diabetes patients. In addition, DM has been linked to diminished T-cell responses, impaired neutrophil function, and disorders of humoral immunity. As a result, DM increases the vulnerability to infections.[42] About 71.9% of the diabetic patients in our study had uncontrolled DM on admission. This can explain the high incidence of complications among the diabetic patients in our study.

There is a vicious circle between infection and DM. DM can lead to infection, and infections can worsen the status of DM.[42] Accordingly, special care should be given to diabetic patients with diverticulitis to prevent the consequences of this vicious circle on DM and on diverticulitis.

4.2. Implications of this study

This study underscores the necessity of focusing on diabetic diverticulitis patients. We should update the data on diverticulitis patients due to changes in westernization and lifestyle. It also proposes studying how ethnicity affects diverticulitis outcomes.

4.2.1. Limitations of the study

As a retrospective study, it is susceptible to retrospective data collection flaws such as incomplete or missing data and selection bias. Because the study was conducted in a single center, its conclusions may not apply to larger groups, especially that ethnicity and lifestyle may influence the incidence and outcomes of diverticulitis. However, this study underscores this important point.

5. Conclusions

The study revealed that DM had a substantial adverse effect on people with diverticulitis. Diabetes mellitus is associated with a significantly higher rate of complications and a higher level of Hinchey classification compared to people without diabetes. Moreover, diabetic individuals exhibited a considerably higher frequency of surgical intervention and a prolonged duration of hospitalization. To prevent these negative consequences, diabetic individuals with diverticulitis require special attention.
