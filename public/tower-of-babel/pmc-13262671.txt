Abnormal B-Cell Exosome Proapoptotic and Antiapoptotic Cargo in Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Gautier Breville, Ayman Rezk, Samuel Weissman, Diego A. Espinoza, Simon Thebault, Luana D. Yamashita, Yeseul Kim, Mihir Kakara, Liljana Nedelkoska, Nicole Doyon-Reale, Celine Delucinge-Vivier, Leah Zuroff, Hanane Touil, Paul Stemmer, Joyce A. Benjamins, Robert P. Lisak, Amit Bar-Or
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesCompartmentalized CNS inflammation involving B cells is implicated in gray matter injury and disease progression in multiple sclerosis (MS). Products secreted by B cells of patients with MS can kill oligodendrocytes and neurons, a cytotoxicity conferred by their exosome-enriched extracellular vesicle (Ex En) fraction.MethodsTo explore the potential molecular mediators of this cytotoxicity, we profiled proteomic and transcriptomic cargo of Ex En isolated from B cells of patients with treatment-naive MS and matched healthy controls.ResultsMS B-cell–derived Ex En appeared enriched in cell-death–associated proteins (including fibrinogen, complement C9, APP, and SPARC) and deficient in cell-survival–associated proteins (such as galectin-3). Abnormal enrichment for cell-death proteins was supported by gene set enrichment analysis. Protein pathway analysis revealed densely connected prodeath modules in the MS B-cell–derived Ex En, contrasting with homeostatic signatures in controls. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that Ex En of MS B cells appeared to carry reduced levels of miRNAs (miR-182, miR-212, and miR-1270) known to inhibit apoptosis.DiscussionOur findings indicate that B-cell–derived Ex En of patients with MS, previously shown to impair neuronal and glial survival, harbor an abnormal cytotoxic molecular profile that may contribute to CNS-compartmentalized injury and progressive MS biology.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease of the CNS that results in inflammatory demyelination and long-term progressive loss of oligodendrocytes and neurons.1 B cells are recognized for important roles in MS pathophysiology that extend beyond their immunoglobulin-producing capacity.2,3 In this context, B-cell–rich aggregates of immune cells in the meninges and subependyma represent key features of CNS-compartmentalized inflammation in MS.4-6 Their presence adjacent to the parenchyma has been closely associated with areas of cortical and deep gray (e.g., thalamic) injury, whose extent is correlated with progressive MS severity.4,7,8 This gray matter pathology is characterized by loss of oligodendrocytes and an apparent “surface-in” gradient of microglial activation and neuronal loss that is greatest in the most superficial layers and diminishes in deeper layers.2,6,9-11 It has therefore been postulated that 1 or more toxic factors may be released by the B-cell–rich immune cell aggregates in the meninges and subependyma, which may then reach the superficial gray matter, contributing to the observed surface-in gradient of injury.

Previous evidence indicates that B cells of patients with MS, but not B cells of healthy controls, can release products capable of inducing oligodendrocyte and neuronal cell death.12-15 The cytotoxic potential of MS patient B-cell–secreted products was shown to be harbored by the exosome-enriched extracellular vesicle (Ex En) fraction of the B-cell–secreted products,13 although the toxic mediators have remained undefined. To test the hypothesis that MS patient B-cell–derived Ex En carry abnormal cytotoxic cargo, we isolated Ex En fractions from B cells of untreated patients with MS and healthy controls and subjected them to differential proteomic and transcriptomic analyses. We observed that Ex En released by MS patient B cells are characterized by an imbalance favoring proapoptotic pathways. These findings point to a novel molecular mechanism by which B cells may contribute to progressive MS disease biology.

Methods

Study Participants

Participants with MS (diagnosed based on McDonald 2017 criteria16) were recruited early in their clinical course to prioritize obtaining samples that were not exposed to disease-modifying therapies (i.e., DMT-naïve patient, and with no systemic steroid exposure for at least 30 days). A total of 10 patients with MS were recruited at the University of Pennsylvania (UPenn) Comprehensive MS Center (n = 10; 9F/1M). Mean disease duration (from MS symptom onset) was 9.4 months, with mean duration from last clinical event of 4.7 months and from last observation of Gd+ lesion on MRI of 4.0 months. All were positive for CSF-restricted oligoclonal bands. Participants had an average of 1.6 total relapses before sampling and an average MS typical lesion number of 14.4 in the brain (periventricular, juxtacortical, and infratentorial) and 2.2 in the spinal cord and a mean EDSS of 2.3. Age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls were recruited in parallel (Table 1).

Isolation of Human Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells and Sorting of Human B Cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated according to standard proceeding operations developed at the Center for Neuroinflammation and Neurotherapeutics. Whole blood was diluted 1:1 with sterile PBS/2 mM EDTA (both ThermoFisher Scientific) and was transferred into 50 mL SepMate tubes (StemCell Technologies) containing Ficoll Paque Plus density gradient media (Cytiva). Tubes were spun at 1,200×g for 10 minutes with brakes on. The cellular monolayer was collected, washed twice with PBS before proceeding to the isolation of B cells using CD19 microbeads according to manufacturer's recommendations (Miltenyi Biotec). Sorted B cells typically showed purity exceeding 95%.

B-Cell Culture

X-VIVO 10 media (Lonza) was filtered to remove particles with size greater than 300 kDa using Jumbosep Centrifugal Devices (Cytiva)—herein referred to as “filtered X-VIVO 10.” Sorted B cells were resuspended in filtered X-VIVO 10 (Lonza) containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at a final concentration of 3 × 106 cells/mL. 3 × 105 B cells were transferred into each well of a 96-well, U-shaped plate and cultured without exogenous stimulus for 72 hours in a humidified incubator (5% CO2 and 37°C). After 72 hours, cell culture supernatant was harvested and spun at 1,500× rpm for 10 minutes to remove contaminant cells. The supernatant was aliquoted into DNAse-free/RNAse-free microcentrifuge tubes (Eppendorf) and stored at −80°C until downstream analysis. Cell culture supernatant aliquots were shipped on dry ice to either System Biosciences (SBI) or Wayne State University for transcriptomic and proteomic analysis, respectively.

Exosome-Enriched Extracellular Vesicle Isolation and Protein Mass Spectrometry

Ex En were isolated by ultracentrifugation from B-cell culture supernatants obtained from 9 patients with MS and sex-matched and age-matched healthy controls. Aliquots of supernatants, 3 mL per sample, were centrifuged at 10,000gmax for 30 minutes. The resulting supernatants were diluted with 3 volumes of Dulbecco modified Eagle medium (DMEM) and centrifuged at 62,500gmax for 2 hours. The pellets were washed with DMEM and again centrifuged at 62,500gmax for 2 hours. Particle sizing of suspended pellets prepared by this method showed a major peak around 90–100 nm on a Spectradyne nCS1 analyzer. Sample preparation and mass spectrometry were performed by the Proteomics Core at Wayne State University. Proteins in Ex En were reduced using 5 mm dithiothreitol (DTT) and then alkylated using 15 mM iodoacetamide (IAA). After quenching unreacted IAA with a second addition of 5 mM DTT, proteins were digested with trypsin, labeled with TMT multiplexing reagents, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer. An abundance value for each peptide was determined by the area under the curve for the reporter ion peak. Signal strength values for all peptides from the same protein were combined to calculate the protein-level abundance. Normalization was based on total signal strength for each sample.

RNA Sequencing

Ex En were purified from B-cell culture supernatants collected from 10 patients with MS and sex-matched and age-matched healthy controls using an ExoQuick Exosome Isolation kit (SBI) followed by RNA extraction, analysis, and library preparation. Ex En were lysed, and crude RNA extracted using a spin column-based RNA purification kit (SBI). RNA quantity and integrity were evaluated on an Agilent Bioanalyzer using the RNA Pico workflow, and the small RNA fraction was inspected as part of this QC step. For small RNA sequencing, as little as 1 ng of total RNA was used as an input for library construction with the Qiagen small RNA library preparation kit. Libraries were size selected by gel extraction to enrich for the expected insert range, then quantified and amplified by qPCR; libraries exceeding 1 nM were advanced to sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq platform (single-end, 75 bp) targeting ∼10–15 million reads per sample. Libraries were pooled, and fragments in the 140–300 bp range were selected and quantified before a NextSeq 500/550 High Output run (SR75; High Output v2 kit). Primary processing and differential abundance analyses were performed using the SBI Maverix Exosome RNASeq Analysis platform, which provides QC reporting, normalization, transcript identification/quantification, and pairwise differential expression outputs.

Data Analysis and Statistics

Proteomic Data

Differential protein expression analysis was conducted using a multitest approach to maximize robustness while accounting for the paired study design. Three complementary statistical methods were used: t test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and permutation test. Each test is sensitive to different effects regarding data distribution and variance structure. An empirical Brown method was subsequently applied to combine p values from the 4 tests, yielding a unified statistical measure that maintains type I error control and accounts for correlation structure between tests. This approach mitigates the limitations of individual tests and provides more reliable significance estimates in the context of proteomic data.17,18 Fold changes were calculated as the ratio of mean protein abundance values in patients with MS to those in healthy controls (mean[MS]/mean[HC]) and expressed as log2 transformations. Proteins were considered overrepresented or underrepresented (n = 61) based on dual criteria: unadjusted combined p value ≤0.05 (empirical Brown method on 2-sided concordant p values) and |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.30 (equivalent to ≥30% abundance difference).

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)19,20 implemented through the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.0). Before analysis, proteins detected in >95% of samples were retained to ensure robust statistical inference, and missing values were imputed using minimum observed protein intensity to maintain dataset completeness. Background gene sets included curated collections from the molecular signatures database (MSigDB)19: Gene Ontology Biological Processes gene sets (GO:BP),21,22 KEGG pathways,23,24 and Reactome pathways.25 These collections provide comprehensive coverage of known biological processes, metabolic pathways, and disease-associated molecular signatures relevant to neuroinflammatory conditions. Enrichment analysis used a preranked approach using log2 fold changes as the ranking metric. Pathways were considered significantly enriched at FDR <0.05 after Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Leading-edge analysis was performed to identify core enrichment genes driving pathway significance, providing mechanistic insights into the most influential proteins within each enriched pathway. Visualization of enrichment results used dot plots and enrichment maps to display pathway significance, normalized enrichment scores, and gene set overlap relationships. Network-based visualization of pathway interactions was generated using the enrichplot package to reveal functional modules and pathway crosstalk patterns characteristic of MS-associated B-cell exosome cargo.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were systematically constructed from comparative proteomic analysis of B-cell–derived Ex En from patients (n = 9) and age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls (n = 9). Overrepresented or underrepresented proteins meeting dual significance criteria (combined p value ≤0.05, Brown empirical method; |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.30) served as seed nodes for network construction, ensuring inclusion of proteins with both statistical robustness and biological relevance. High-confidence protein-protein interactions were sourced from the STRING database (version 11.5),26 which integrates experimental evidence, coexpression data, neighborhood information, gene fusion events, co-occurrence patterns, and computational predictions across multiple species. Network density optimization used differential confidence thresholds: combined score ≥0.7 for healthy control networks and ≥0.8 for multiple sclerosis networks to enhance visual discrimination between conditions while preserving biologically meaningful interactions. Graph construction and topological analysis used the igraph package (version 1.4.0). Network visualization used the ggraph package (version 2.0.5) implementing Fruchterman-Reingold force–directed algorithms. Statistical significance was encoded through node border coloration using a 4-tier gradient based on combined p values. Edge transparency and thickness reflected STRING interaction confidence scores, providing visual representation of interaction reliability.

Transcriptomic Data

Differential expression was assessed in a paired framework using edgeR27 with a quasilikelihood F-test (parameters specified below). Gene set enrichment analyses were performed using GSEA software (Broad Institute)20 identifying pathways from MSigDB.19 To further characterize the biological significance of the identified microRNAs, predicted target genes were obtained from validated databases (miRDB,28 miRTarBase,29,30 and TargetScan31,32). Gene identifiers were standardized to Entrez IDs using org.Hs.eg.db.32 Functional enrichment analysis was then performed with clusterProfiler, applying hypergeometric testing to compare the frequency of GO21,22 terms in the predicted microRNA target gene sets against the background human genome. p values were adjusted for multiple testing with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the FDR.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Pennsylvania (UPenn). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the UPenn IRB. All patients with MS and matched healthy controls provided written informed consent for participation in the research study. Patients with MS were consented under IRB protocol 816805 and healthy controls under IRB protocol 828283.

Data Availability

Raw and processed FASTQ transcriptomic and proteomic files will be deposited and available for download on publication.

Results

Proteomic Profiling of Normal Human B-Cell–Derived Exosome-Enriched Extracellular Vesicles

Previous work describing the cargo of EVs released by immune cells has largely focused on tumor cells21,34-38 with studies of primary immune cells largely limited to T cells and myeloid cells.39-44 We therefore decided to survey the proteome first, followed by the transcriptome of Ex En released by normal circulating human B cells (Figure 1A). Protein mass spectrometry of Ex En isolated from culture supernatants of healthy donor B cells (n = 9) identified 2,151 proteins. GO enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler revealed 1,944 significantly enriched terms (FDR <0.05), spanning “biological processes” (n = 1,393), “cellular components” (n = 325), and “molecular functions” (n = 226). The top-10 ranked GO terms (Figure 1B, eTable 1) were dominated by cellular component annotations consistent with the established composition of exosomes,45-47 including “secretory granule lumen” (GO:0034774, 167 proteins, FDR = 5.88 × 10−77), “cytoplasmic vesicle lumen” (GO:0060205, 167 proteins, FDR = 9.54 × 10−76), “cytoplasmic translation” (GO:0002181, 109 proteins, FDR = 1.73 × 10−60), “cell substrate junction” (GO:0030055, 197 proteins, FDR = 2.88 × 10−78), “focal adhesion” (GO:0005925, 194 proteins, FDR = 4.60 × 10−78), and “cadherin binding” (GO:0045296, 170 proteins, FDR = 1.10 × 10−72). In addition, as expected given the B-cell source of the Ex En, top ranked “immunobiology” terms (Figure 1C, eTable 2) were enriched for pathways associated with B-cell biology including “MHC class II protein complex binding” (GO:0023026, 21 proteins, FDR = 1.10 × 10−13), “B-cell receptor signaling pathway” (GO:0050853, 35 proteins, FDR = 1.43 × 10−12), “humoral immune response” (GO:0050853, 75 proteins, FDR = 2.89 × 10−10), “immunological synapse” (GO:0001772, 20 proteins, FDR = 8.67 × 10−8), and “B-cell activation” (GO:0042113, 62 proteins, FDR = 9.63 × 10−7). We further observed that 3 of the top-10 pathways sorted by fold enrichment (eTable 3) comprised complexes that have previously been shown to play functional roles in exosome-mediated intercellular signaling, by both B cells and other cells.36 First was the “MHC class II receptor activity” pathway (GO:0032395), with the presence of all elements of both the classical and nonclassical HLA class II α/β chains, previously shown to be released within B-cell Ex En and mediate antigen-specific CD4 T-cell activation.48 Also of note was the highly enriched “aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase multienzyme complex” (GO:0017101), including its constituent lysyl-tRNA synthetase (KRS) because previous work has shown that exosome-associated KRS from cancer cells can mediate macrophage activation.49,50 Finally, the “proteasome regulatory” and “accessory” complexes were also highly enriched in the B-cell Ex En, and proteasome complexes carried within EVs have been found to remain enzymatically active and able to promote degradation of proteotoxic substrates in recipient cells.e1 Taken together, these findings indicate that human B-cell–derived Ex En harbor a proteomic cargo not only enriched for the expected vesicular machinery and basic B-cell machinery reflecting their successful isolation, but also for protein complexes with the potential to mediate intercellular communication and modulate the biology of recipient cells.

B Cells of Patients With MS Release Exosome-Enriched Extracellular Vesicles That Are Abnormally Enriched With Cell-Death–Related Proteins

We next compared protein mass spectrometry profiles of Ex En isolated from the culture supernatants of healthy B cells and from age-matched and sex-matched patients with MS. We identified 61 overrepresented or underrepresented proteins among Ex En of MS patient B cells compared with control B cells by dual fold-change and significance criteria (Figure 2A, eTable 4). Notably, B-cell–derived Ex En from patients with MS appeared enriched in several proteins individually linked to cell-death signaling, and to pathways that hinder cellular renewal and survival. Among the most abnormally increased, were several molecules previously implicated in the CNS pathology of MS and/or other CNS inflammatory and degenerative pathologies: these included the multiple fibrinogen (FG) subunits alpha (FGA; fold change [FC] = 2.05, p = 0.01), beta (FGB; FC = 2.29, p = 0.01), and gamma (FGG; FC = 2.06, p = 0.02), which have been shown to be toxic to CNS elements and can also impede oligodendrocyte progenitor cell differentiation and myelin repaire2-e6; complement component 9 (C9; FC = 1.82, p = 0.03), a constituent of the membrane attack complex capable of inducing cell lysis and apoptosise7,e8; and amyloid-β precursor protein (APP; FC = 1.51, p = 0.02), which promotes mitochondrial oxidative stress and intrinsic apoptotic signaling through the caspase-3 pathway14,e9 and is upregulated in MS lesions.e10 Of interest, activation of caspase-3 has been implicated in inflammatory cell death within progressive MS pathology.e11 Also appearing elevated in Ex En of MS B cells were levels of SPARC (secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine; FC = 1.44, p = 0.05), a modulator of extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and potentiator of ER stress-induced apoptosis that was recently reported as one of the most upregulated ECM proteins in MS tissue compared with control tissue.e12 At the same time, proteins with established antiapoptotic functions appeared underrepresented in B-cell–derived Ex En from patients with MS, including Ras-related protein Rap-2c (RAP2C; FC = 0.80, p = 0.05)e13 as well as galectin-3 (LGALS3; FC = 0.76, p = 0.01),e14,e15 which is also known to support normal glial crosstalk and oligodendrocyte functions.e16,e17

GSEA Reveals Cell-Death Pathway Enrichment in Exosome-Enriched Extracellular Vesicles of MS Patient B Cells

Given the suggestion of an abnormal profile of injury-promoting proteins in the MS B-cell–secreted Ex En, together with our prior demonstration that the Ex En of MS B cell mediate in vitro death of neural cells,13 we next focused on characterizing cell-death–associated pathways within the proteomic data set. To this end, we performed GSEA on the total 2,151 proteins identified in B-cell Ex En from patients with MS and healthy controls (Figure 2B, eTable 5). Using a multidatabase approach encompassing KEGG, Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP), Reactome, and MSigDB C2 databases, we successfully mapped 2,119 proteins (98.5%) to Entrez gene identifiers for pathway analysis. Comprehensive screening identified 110 significantly enriched pathways (FDR <0.05) across all databases. Among them, all identified cell-death–associated pathways (n = 46) exhibited positive enrichment scores (NES >0) indicating coordinated upregulation of cell-death–associated proteins in the B-cell Ex En of patients with MS compared with healthy controls. We selected 10 core pathways that represent the major mechanisms through which B-cell Ex En may contribute to death of target cells (Figure 2B). The most significantly enriched pathway was “Diseases of Programmed Cell Death” (NES = 2.479, FDR = 5.51 × 10−7), encompassing core apoptotic machinery, suggesting activation of chromatin-mediated apoptotic signaling. Two complementary pathways emerged as prominently enriched: “Oxidative Stress-Induced Senescence” (NES = 2.261, FDR = 4.14 × 10−5) and “DNA Damage/Telomere Stress-Induced Senescence” (NES = 2.212, FDR = 2.29 × 10−5). Concurrently, “Inhibition of DNA Recombination at Telomere” (NES = 2.194, FDR = 2.59 × 10−5) was also significantly enriched in the MS B-cell Ex En, potentially pointing to dual mechanisms of senescence induction through both oxidative stress and telomere dysfunction. “Defective Pyroptosis” (involved in inflammasome-mediated cell death) emerged as the third most enriched pathway (NES = 2.238, FDR = 5.08 × 10−7), with significant enrichment also noted for “Complement and Coagulation Cascades” (NES = 2.029, FDR = 1.40 × 10−2), “Positive Regulation of TNF Production” (NES = 1.975, FDR = 1.30 × 10−2), and “Toll-Like Receptor 4 Cascade” (NES = 1.949, FDR = 1.01 × 10−2). The enrichment of “Integrin Cell Surface Interactions” (NES = 1.956, FDR = 8.09 × 10−3) might be associated with anoikis-related biology, wherein loss of appropriate cell-matrix interactions triggers apoptotic cell death. Finally, “Amyloid Fiber Formation” (NES = 1.965, FDR = 1.92 × 10−2) enrichment indicates activation of protein misfolding and aggregation pathways, potentially contributing to proteotoxic stress in target cells capable of triggering the caspase-3-associated apoptosis pathway. The positive enrichment scores across all these pathways indicate that MS patient B-cell Ex En carry an abnormally enhanced molecular arsenal with the potential to induce or participate in multiple forms of both intracellular and extracellular cell-death pathways, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, anoikis, oxidative stress, and senescence, as well as inflammasome activation and complement-mediated lysis.

Protein-Protein Interaction Analysis Reveals a Coordinated Pro–Cell-Death Network Architecture in B-cell–Derived Exosome-Enriched Extracellular Vesicles of Patients With MS

To contextualize the differential expression of B-cell–derived Ex En proteins within broader protein interaction networks, we constructed protein-protein interaction (PPI) maps using high-confidence STRING-derived interactions (Figure 2, C and D). Seed nodes were defined by proteins appearing overrepresented or underrepresented in patients with MS compared with HCs (unadjusted p value <0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.30). The MS-derived PPI network was densely connected (1,999 edges) and centered around multiple hubs associated with cell-death pathways, including the aforementioned fibrinogen subunits (FGA, FGB, FGG), complement component C9, APP, and SPARC, as well as CFL1 and CD40. These central nodes, each identified as upregulated in MS Ex En, formed densely connected clusters through high-confidence first-order interactions as defined by STRING. The observed network architecture highlights the central positioning of pro–cell-death proteins within the MS-derived Ex En proteome. By contrast, the HC-derived PPI network was less dense (1,309 edges) and organized around distinct nodes such as LGALS3 and RAP2C, both proteins that we found to be downregulated in MS B-cell–derived Ex En and known to contribute to glial support and antiapoptotic signaling. Clusters in the HC network further encompassed regulators of transcription, mitochondrial integrity, and metabolic homeostasis, suggesting a molecular configuration more consistent with cellular maintenance and possibly protection. Together, these observations suggest that MS B cell–derived Ex En exhibit a network organization characterized by the relative enrichment of proteins associated with cell death and a relative loss of proteins with putative protective functions, an imbalance that is thus reflected at both the individual protein and network levels.

MS B-Cell–Derived Exosome-Enriched Extracellular Vesicles Exhibit a Deficiency in Antiapoptotic miR-182, miR-212, and miR-1270

Previous work showed that exosomes contain functional mRNAs and microRNAs that can be delivered to recipient cells and translated, establishing a novel mechanism of genetic exchange between cells.e18 More recently, Kalluri and LeBleu36 highlighted the diverse roles of exosomes in cancer, immunity, and drug resistance, underscoring the importance of exosomal RNA cargo in shaping the biology of target cells. Given these insights, we next undertook a transcriptional analysis of B-cell–derived Ex En to determine whether mRNAs and noncoding RNAs could in parallel contribute to the reported cytotoxic property of MS patient B-cell–derived Ex En. Bulk RNA sequencing identified 512 transcripts that appeared overrepresented or underrepresented in B-cell Ex En of patients with MS compared with healthy controls by dual fold change and significance criteria (Figure 3A). MS B-cell Ex En appeared to harbor lower levels of the antiapoptotic micro-RNAs miR182 (FC = 0.27, p = 0.01) which is known to downregulate CASP9; miR212 (FC = 0.30, p = 0.04) known to inhibit apoptosis in tumor cells; and miR1270 (FC = 0.26, unadjusted p = 0.02) known to inhibit apoptosis and promote cell proliferatione19 (Figure 3A, eTable 6). GSEA for the target genes of miR-182, miR-212, and miR-1270 identified multiple biological processes significantly enriched with apoptosis-related terms (Figure 3B). The most statistically significant pathways included the “intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway” (GO:0097193; FDR = 2.02*10−6 which regulates apoptosis through intracellular signaling and the “extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway” (GO:0097191; FDR = 5.76*10−5) which is mediated by death receptor signaling. Together, these findings suggest that loss of miR-182, miR-212, and miR-1270 may result in proapoptotic signaling across both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways. Additional enriched terms, such as the “intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway in response to DNA damage” (GO:0008630; FDR = 0.0018), the “intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway by p53 class mediator” (GO:0072332; FDR = 2.62*10−9), the “neuron apoptotic process” (GO:0051402; FDR = 0.002), the “extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway via death domain receptors” (GO:0008625; FDR = 0.0365) and “ferroptosis” (GO:0097707; FDR = 0.0007) together suggest that the implicated miR targets are associated with processes governing programmed cell death under a range of specific stress conditions.e20

Discussion

Although the immunobiology of MS relapses is now relatively well understood and highly effective treatments are available that limit or abrogate injury due to acute relapses,e20 the mechanisms that underlie ongoing CNS injury due to progressive MS biology remain poorly understood and represent a key unmet therapeutic need. CNS compartmentalization and persistence of cells of the B-cell lineage are a hallmark of MS pathology, and their presence in the meninges and subependyma has been associated with the ‘surface-in pattern’ of gray matter injury, considered to be a major pathologic substrate of progressive MS.6,10,e21 The proximity of these B cells to regions of progressive MS injury, yet the lack of actual physical contact between them and the CNS parenchyma, suggests that any contribution of B cells to this injury process would be mediated by diffusible factors. Since immunoglobulin deposition is not a particular feature of the MS gray matter pathology, relevant secreted factors would be expected to reflect 1 or more non–antibody-dependent functions of B cells. Earlier work demonstrated that secreted products of B cells isolated from patients with MS (but not from healthy controls) were toxic to oligodendrocytes and neurons in vitro.13,15 This cytotoxicity, which could not be attributed to individual B-cell–secreted molecules (including immunoglobulins, cytokines or chemokines, or molecules known to confer toxicity such as Fas/FasL or granzyme),13 was conferred in the >300 kd fraction of the MS B-cell supernatants and was subsequently shown to be harbored within the exosomes.14 Here, we compared Ex En released by B cells of patients with MS and healthy controls at the proteomic and transcriptomic levels (Figure 1A) and observed that MS B-cell Ex En carry an abnormal cargo profile, characterized by increased levels of death-promoting molecules, yet lower levels of molecules that may limit cell death and/or support normal cell function. These abnormalities of MS patient B-cell–derived extracellular vesicles represent a novel mechanism by which CNS-compartmentalized B cells may contribute through released factors to propagation of non–relapsing-progressive MS biology. Our findings also suggest an intriguing novel role of normal human B-cell–derived extracellular vesicles that could contribute to tissue homeostasis.

A key observation was the identification of multiple fibrinogen subunits among the top proteins overrepresented in the Ex En of MS patient B cells. Fibrinogen (and its cleaved product fibrin) has known cytotoxic potential and has now been strongly implicated in CNS inflammation and degeneration in MS.e4,e22-e24 Fibrinogen deposition has been observed not only in perivascular white matter lesions of MSe25 but also in the MS gray matter, where (in the absence of immune cell infiltrates and with an apparently intact blood-brain barrier), the extent of fibrinogen deposition strongly correlated with neuronal loss.e26 In animal models, fibrin can induce proinflammatory microglial activation and oxidative stress,e22 both of which are also pathologic hallmarks of MS gray matter injury. In chronic active/inactive MS lesions, fibrin(ogen) colocalized with astrocytic and axonal processes, and fibrin(ogen) deposition in MS lesions correlated with the extent of microglial activation and degree of neuronal loss.e2 The presence of fibrinogen may also impair MS lesion repair through the upregulation of BMP-responsive genes in oligodendrocyte precursor cells preventing them from maturing into oligodendrocytes.e6 It is intriguing to speculate that the abnormal enrichment of fibrinogen in B-cell–derived Ex En of patients with MS may contribute to progressive neuronal and oligodendrocyte injury and potentially also limited repair as part of progressive MS biology. An important caveat is that relatively little is known about exosome interactions with the glia limitans, which separates the CSF from the brain parenchyma. Transition of exosomes across CNS barriers has been best studied in the context of cancer and stroke where proposed mechanisms for exosome transport include micropinocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolin-mediated endocytosis, and adsorptive-mediated endocytosis, all of which may be more active in the context of inflammation.44,e27-e29 In noninflammatory states (where exosomes may not efficiently traverse the glia limitans), a more plausible mechanism could involve fusion of the exosomes with the barrier and release of the exosome contents. Elements of the exosome cargo could then traverse the barrier—potentially including the 3 polypeptide fragments FGA, FGB, and FGG that comprise fibrinogen (all of which we found increased in MS B-cell Ex En EVs).

B-cell−derived Ex En appeared to also carry increased levels of SPARC (secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine) in MS participants compared with healthy controls. SPARC, which was recently identified as one of the most upregulated ECM proteins in MS tissue,e12 is a glycoprotein that can induce autophagy and apoptosis.e30-e32 Although studies of autophagy in the context of MS have variably pointed to potentially detrimental and beneficial roles,e33,e34 SPARC-induced autophagy is more likely to be detrimental. SPARC not only induces the formation of membrane-bound autophagic vacuoles but also induces the lysosomal enzyme cathepsin B (which, in turn, triggers mitochondrial activation of the proapoptotic caspase-3) and interferes with the activity of antiapoptotic Bcl-2.e30-e32 SPARC may thus serve a dual role of promoting autophagy and apoptosis by disrupting the delicate balance between proapoptotic and antiapoptotic signals. Although increased deposition of APP is not a pathologic hallmark of MS, the possibility that its levels are abnormally increased in EVs released by B cells in proximity to gray matter injury is of interest given the well-known capacity of APP to mediate apoptosis and neurodegeneration, including through the induction of mitochondrial oxidative stress and microglial activation,14,e9,e35 themselves recognized features of MS pathology.e36 Perhaps of interest, in the context of Alzheimer disease research, β-amyloid peptides derived from APP could be identified within exosomes released by neural cells, which has been postulated as a potential mechanism for propagation of pathogenic signals.e37 One could further speculate that increased levels of C9, a component of the membrane attack complex (MAC), could further contribute to B-cell Ex En cytotoxicity. C9 serves as a key component of the MAC alongside C5b, C6, C7, and C8 and mediates the critical terminal step in the complement activation pathway, resulting in cell lysis.e7,e8,e38 Increased levels of early complement factors and markers of complement activation in MS CSF have been recently associated with MS severity and progression.e39,e40 Although additional work including in larger, independent cohorts, and complemented by mechanistic studies, will be necessary to validate the implication of any one of the identified molecules, taken together, the enrichment we observe of cell-death–associated proteins in MS B-cell–derived Ex En supports a potential contribution to MS-associated “inflammatory degeneration” (Figure 2B). Future studies with larger cohorts may also enable evaluation of relationships between implicated protein levels and different clinical courses, fluid biomarkers (e.g., GFAP and NfL), and imaging parameters, including those that may more specifically reflect chronic compartmentalized progressive disease biology, such as the subset of chronic active lesions captured by MRI as paramagnetic rim lesions.

Our results also suggest that B-cell–derived Ex En of patients with MS exhibit an abnormal RNA cargo, characterized by reduced levels of several miRs that protect cells from death. These include miR-182 which is known to inhibit caspace-9,e41 miR-212 that has been shown to suppress neuronal apoptosis and ferroptosis,e42,e43 and miR-1270 known to promote several normal cell functions including survival.e44 Gene ontology enrichment analysis focusing on targets of these 3 dysregulated miRs provides a broader view and illustrates their implication in protecting cells from multiple cell-death pathways, including the intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway (in response to DNA damage, oxidative stress, and endoplasmic reticulum stress) and the extrinsic apoptotic pathway (death receptor-mediated processes) as well as ferroptosis which is recognized as a distinct form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidatione45 that has been implicated in progressive MS injury.e46 Together, our findings suggest an abnormal protein and RNA cargo profile of MS B-cell–derived Ex En, that seems to be characterized by an overabundance of death-promoting molecules and a relative deficiency in regulatory elements that would otherwise support cellular homeostasis and survival. This perturbed EV cargo profile, with its potential to shift the homeostatic balance of recipient cells toward cell-death pathways, may provide a mechanistic explanation for the observed cytotoxic effect of MS B-cell–secreted factors on oligodendrocytes and neurons.

Our study adds to the growing body of literature implicating immune-cell derived Ex En as important mediators of intercellular communication in both health and disease states.36,44,e47,e48 In the context of health, our observation that normal B-cell–derived EVs strike a balance between death-promoting vs survival-promoting molecules, raises the fascinating prospect that a new function of circulating immune cells that can survey tissues (in this case B cells, but perhaps relevant to other surveying immune cells) is to provide local cues that may influence cell survival, perhaps as part of normal cellular turnover. Our observation that, in patients with MS, blood-derived B cells may release cargo that is locally toxic with potential implications to CNS injury, raises an interesting question as to whether these circulating B cells may be toxic to other tissues. One might speculate that the CNS of patients with MS is particularly vulnerable to potentially toxic factors (i.e., a “multihit” model) as compared with other organs which could confer a degree of target-organ specificity for the effect. It is also possible that an important determinant of when potential involvement of cargo released by such cells in normal tissue turnover becomes toxic depends on whether the cells are merely surveying the tissue (i.e., present transiently), or become compartmentalized and persist locally, in which case the ongoing persistent release may establish a higher local concentration over an extended period, which as noted, may be the case with B-cell compartmentalization in the MS CNS.

Previous work on immune-cell derived EVs in MS has largely considered their role in relapsing disease biology. An example of recent work demonstrated that human regulatory T-cell (Treg)–derived EVs carry miR-142-3p that is capable of suppressing conventional CD4 T-cell activation and that levels of this miR were significantly reduced in Treg-derived EVs of patients with MS which resulted in impaired Treg suppressive capacity, and was associated with greater relapsing disease activity.e49 Here, in addition to harkening back to classic literature reporting that CSF of patients with MS can be toxic to brain cells (with capacities to induce apoptosis, toxic NO production, oxidative stress, disturbed glucose metabolism, and decreased expression of neuroprotective genes),e50-e54 our study points to the potential importance of interactions between infiltrating immune cells (including B cells) and CNS cells in MS.e55,e56 We suggest a novel potential mechanism, wherein MS B-cell–derived EVs could influence disease neurobiology by impacting the health of CNS intrinsic cells, which would have particular relevance to non–relapsing-progressive MS biology, through what might be considered “inflammatory degeneration.”

It has become clear that the biological processes underlying non–relapsing-progressive MS are already present very early in the MS clinical course, as evidenced by the subclinical presence of brain MRI hallmarks of progressive injury at the time of the initial clinical presentation in both adults and children,e57-e59 and now also reflected in the new MS diagnostic criteria.e60 Indeed, we opted to assess B-cell Ex En EVs obtained from patients with MS soon following their diagnosis and before initiation of immune therapy, which enabled us to avoid potential confounding effects of treatment, and also limited the risk that changes in immune-cell biology may reflect consequences of (rather than contributors) to chronic disease processes. Given the major unmet need represented by progressive disease biology, new mechanistic insights raise the exciting prospect of EVs as therapeutic targets, including for CNS compartmentalized diseases.e61-e63 Since subclinical progressive disease injury manifests decades before it is appreciated clinically, also of considerable interest for future work will be whether EVs could be used to monitor subclinical progressive disease biology. It remains to be seen whether EVs of circulating B cells could be practically pursued for this purpose, although recent work demonstrated that circulating EVs (with source cells identified as neurons, oligodendrocytes, T cells, and B cells) carried specific microRNA signatures that predict clinical relapse activity, cognitive decline, and brain atrophy and to some extent response to disease-modifying treatments.e64

In conclusion, our observations provide new evidence that MS B-cell–derived Ex En, previously shown to be cytotoxic to oligodendrocytes and neurons, carry an abnormal cargo characterized by overrepresentation of proapoptotic molecules and underrepresentation of antiapoptotic molecules. These findings point to a potential novel mechanism of inflammatory degeneration, by which B cells known to become compartmentalized in the MS CNS, might contribute to propagation of progressive MS biology. Our work adds to the motivation to therapeutically target CNS-compartmentalized B cells in MS with approaches that may include shuttling approaches that can transport existing antibodies (e.g., anti-CD20 and anti-CD19) into the CNS compartment, CAR-T cells that are expected to access the CNS, as well as CNS penetrant BTK inhibitors. Future work will be needed to validate the implication of particular molecules which may provide opportunities for more refined interventions. Finally, our findings also indicate that the Ex En of normal B cells strike a certain balance of proapoptotic and antiapoptotic cargo, raising the intriguing possibility that a normal function of B cells, perhaps as they survey tissues, is to contribute through EV release to death/survival decisions of other cells in the local environment.
