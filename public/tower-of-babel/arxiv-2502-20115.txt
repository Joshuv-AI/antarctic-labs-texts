Multi-View Causal Discovery without Non-Gaussianity: Identifiability and Algorithms
Ambroise Heurtebise, Omar Chehab, Pierre Ablin, Alexandre Gramfort, Aapo Hyvärinen, [.8em] Inria, CEA, University of Paris-Saclay, France

Abstract
Causal discovery is a difficult problem that typically relies on strong assumptions on the data-generating model, such as non-Gaussianity. In practice, many modern applications provide multiple related views of the same system, which has rarely been considered for causal discovery. Here, we leverage this multi-view structure to achieve causal discovery with weak assumptions. We propose a multi-view linear Structural Equation Model (SEM) that extends the well-known framework of non-Gaussian disturbances by alternatively leveraging correlation over views. We prove the identifiability of the model for acyclic SEMs. Subsequently, we propose several multi-view causal discovery algorithms, inspired by single-view algorithms (DirectLiNGAM, PairwiseLiNGAM, and ICA-LiNGAM). The new methods are validated through simulations and applications on neuroimaging data, where they enable the estimation of causal graphs between brain regions.



1
Introduction

Causal discovery is a fundamental problem in scientific data analysis as well as several technological applications
[
28
]
. The basic problem is that we are given a number of observed variables, and we need to infer which variables cause which, and with what “connection strengths”. In the typical case, we only observe the data passively without any possibility of performing interventions, and this purely observational quality of the data makes the problem difficult.

Often, the problem is formalized as a structural equation model (SEM)
[
4
]
, also called a functional causal model
[
24
]
. Then the question is how to estimate the SEM parameters using statistical theory. In fact, before even considering estimation methods, we need to know if it is at all possible to solve the problem. This is the question of
identifiability
of the model: can the parameters that describe the causal relations and directions be (uniquely) estimated?

While deep learning research has recently proposed completely nonlinear methods for causal discovery
[
21
,
42
,
23
,
43
]
, it is probably true that real-world applications overwhelmingly use
linear
methods. This is in no small part because nonlinear models have their own limitations when deployed: they require much more data and more computation to train, the identifiability results need more assumptions, and such methods are likely to get stuck in local minima. Furthermore, linear methods are easier to interpret. This is why we focus on linear SEMs in what follows.

A well-known fact is that if all variables are Gaussian and the model is linear, identifiability of the SEM is very problematic. Some of the earliest work showed that the directions can sometimes be recovered by an analysis of the
conditional independencies
between variables
[
38
]
; however, this is only possible in some special cases and notably not possible when observing only two variables.
Recent literature has, therefore, focused on different departures from the linear-Gaussian framework to achieve identifiability. A major advance was to consider a linear model together with
non-Gaussianity

[
34
]
, which leads to full identifiability of the model under weak conditions such as acyclicity; however, such non-Gaussianity may not be strong enough in many data sets.
A related framework was proposed by assuming that the cause undergoes a
nonlinear transform
, while the disturbance is still additive
[
15
]
; however the nonlinearity may not be strong enough in many applications, and such nonlinearity slightly contradicts the linear additivity of the disturbance.
Further related frameworks were proposed by
[
27
,
45
,
19
]
;
continuous optimization approaches have also been considered by
Zheng et al. [46]
,
Zhu et al. [47]
.

An alternative recent framework is given by the
multi-view
setting, where data is collected from different sources so that the data points are paired. The correlations between the views can then be leveraged for estimation and identifiability. However, current approaches using multi-view structure are scarce and limited;
either they
ignore dependencies across views
[
36
]

or make strong assumptions about these dependencies
[
6
]
.
We further note that methods in the
multi-domain
setting are fundamentally different from the multi-view case since multi-domain data is not paired: the distinction is detailed in Section
7
below.

Here, we consider the linear
multi-view SEM, providing a general theory and algorithms that leverage the multiple views.
Our main contributions are:

1.

We generalize three
of the most well-known
algorithms
for single-view causal discovery to the multi-view setting: PairwiseLiNGAM, DirectLiNGAM and ICA-LiNGAM. The generalizations of PairwiseLiNGAM and DirectLiNGAM result in completely new and fast
causal discovery using second-order statistics only, while our adaptation of ICA-LiNGAM improves on
Chen et al. [6]
.

2.

We show that our algorithms are
consistent
under weak conditions: we can replace the non-Gaussianity assumption on the disturbances by conditions on correlations between the views and “diversity” of such Second-Order Statistics (SOS). Still, if the disturbances are non-Gaussian, we generalize
Shimizu [36]
,
Chen et al. [6]
.

3.

The proofs above directly lead to rigorous
identifiability
results on the model, including cases where all the disturbances are Gaussian, or, more generally, identifiability results based on SOS only. Importantly, only weak conditions on SOS are necessary for identifiability, greatly generalizing previous work.

4.

We make our algorithms usable by practitioners by providing them as
open-source
, and demonstrate their usefulness by benchmarking them on real neuroimaging data.

2
Background

Causal ordering

In the following, we consider a random vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
whose entries are nodes of a directed acyclic graph (DAG).
The DAG is represented by an adjacency matrix
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, where non-zero values indicate the edges of the graph. The entries of
{\bm{x}}
can be reordered such that each “causes” the next. This is formalized by the adjacency matrix admitting a special decomposition
{\bm{B}}={\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}{\bm{P}}
, where
{\bm{T}}
is a strictly lower triangular matrix and
{\bm{P}}
is a permutation matrix referred to as the
causal ordering
. In general, the causal ordering is not unique.

LiNGAM: A single-view model for causal discovery

We start by defining a linear structural equation model (SEM), or a functional causal model.
The data follows

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}}

(1)

and the entries
e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}
of the random vector
{\bm{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are independent noise terms, or
disturbances
.
Causal discovery consists in inferring the parameters
{\bm{B}}
of the model, from observations of
{\bm{x}}
.
Yet, the identifiability of the
model and therefore the uniqueness of
{\bm{B}}
is not straightforward.
In fact, it is well-known that with Gaussian disturbances, the model is unidentifiable in general
[
32
,
9
]
.
A major advance by
Shimizu et al. [34]
was assuming
non-Gaussian
disturbances, leading to their Linear Non-Gaussian Acyclic Model (LiNGAM).

Identifiability of LiNGAM

Shimizu et al. [34]
showed that the LiNGAM model is identifiable in terms of the matrix
{\bm{B}}
. A rigorous re-statement of this result is given in Appendix
A.2
.
A question that has received less attention is under what conditions the model is identifiable in terms of the causal ordering
{\bm{P}}
. In fact, it is not in general: there may exist many permutation matrices
{\bm{P}}
and strictly lower triangular matrices
{\bm{T}}
such that the generated data has the same distribution and the generating permutation cannot be identified.
For instance, in the degenerate case
{\bm{B}}={\bm{T}}={\bm{0}}
, any permutation matrix
{\bm{P}}
gives the same data distribution. As is well-known, a DAG in general defines only a
partial
ordering in the sense that for some pairs of variables, we cannot necessarily say which is “earlier” and which is “later”. Thus, if we want to make the causal ordering unique —
i.e.
to obtain a
total
ordering — we need further assumptions, as considered in later sections.

Estimation of LiNGAM

Since the LiNGAM model was proposed, two important categories of algorithms for estimating this model have been developed.
First, algorithms based on
recursively
computing the
residuals
of pairwise regressions include DirectLiNGAM
[
35
]
and PairwiseLiNGAM
[
17
]
. These compare pairs of variables from
{\bm{x}}
and deduce the causal direction between them based on regressing the variables in the two directions. By aggregating this information, they finally determine a causal ordering
{\bm{P}}
. Once an ordering is found, the matrix
{\bm{T}}
can easily be obtained with conventional methods such as least-squares (LS) regression, which then gives
{\bm{B}}={\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}{\bm{P}}
.

:

Visual representation of our multi-view LiMVAM model in Eq.
3
. Each view of the data is described by a causal graph, nodes represent variables, and disturbances are not illustrated.
We see that the causal ordering given by the DAGs is shared over views
1\ldots m
, but causal weights can differ.

The second category is the ICA-based algorithm that was proposed in the original LiNGAM paper
[
34
]
. It is based on the observation that the LiNGAM model can be rewritten as a latent variable model, in particular an ICA model
[
16
]
as

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{e}}\;.

(2)

Again, the entries in
{\bm{e}}
are independent and non-Gaussian, and the “mixing” matrix
{\bm{A}}
expresses how the data is generated from the latent variable
{\bm{e}}
. Many methods developed for ICA can be used to estimate the matrix
{\bm{A}}
. However, it is important to consider the special structure of the mixing, since
{\bm{A}}=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}})^{-1}
, where
{\bm{B}}
is a DAG
[
34
]
.
This method first recovers the adjacency matrix
{\bm{B}}
, and then estimates a causal ordering
{\bm{P}}
from
{\bm{B}}
.

3
A Multi-View Model for Causal Discovery

LiMVAM: A multi-view model for causal discovery

We now extend the single-view
model in Eq.
1
to the multi-view setting, leading to a Linear Multi-View Acyclic Model (LiMVAM). Suppose we observe
m
different views of a
p
-dimensional vector. We write this as

{\bm{x}}^{i}={\bm{B}}^{i}{\bm{x}}^{i}+{\bm{e}}^{i}

(3)

for views
i\in[\![1,m]\!]
. The
{\bm{B}}^{i}
are view-specific adjacency matrices of DAGs, and
{\bm{e}}^{i}
are view-specific disturbance vectors (with entries of non-zero variance).

Similarly to Eq.
2
, the LiMVAM model can equivalently be written as a latent-variable model

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}^{i}={\bm{A}}^{i}{\bm{e}}^{i}

(4)

where the mixing matrix has a special structure,
{\bm{A}}^{i}=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{i})^{-1}
, which we will leverage later in Section
5
.

Model assumptions

To exploit the multi-view structure, we follow
Shimizu [36]
,
Chen et al. [6]
and assume that all adjacency matrices
{\bm{B}}^{i}

share the same causal ordering
.
Formally, they admit a decomposition
{\bm{B}}^{i}={\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{i}{\bm{P}}
where
{\bm{T}}^{i}
is strictly lower triangular and
{\bm{P}}
is a permutation matrix that is shared among the views
i
.
Note that this is not merely a modeling convenience; it reflects many realistic scenarios such as brain responses of different subjects to a common stimulus, where it is expected that the causal structure
{\bm{P}}
is shared (directions of effect are similar), while variability arises in the strength of the responses
{\bm{T}}^{i}
.
Moreover, as is commonly assumed in causal discovery, we require the components of each
{\bm{e}}^{i}
to be mutually independent, for any given
i
. (In contrast, correlations between views
i
and
i^{\prime}
will be seen to be essential for identifiability.)
Contrary to most existing approaches, we do not impose any restriction on the distribution of the disturbances, such as non-Gaussianity, which greatly broadens the applicability of the model. Figure
1
(left panel) illustrates the proposed model.

We emphasize that the multi-view framework considered here is fundamentally different from the multi-domain or multi-environment settings, as it assumes that observations across different views are paired; a detailed discussion is deferred to Section
7
.

The next two sections develop efficient methods for estimating the adjacency matrices
{\bm{B}}^{i}
, together with identifiability theory.
We begin in Section
4
with methods that estimate the causal ordering using recursive residuals and using only second-order statistics: these lead to our PairwiseLiMVAM and DirectLiMVAM algorithms, which extend the single-view algorithms PairwiseLiNGAM
[
17
]
and DirectLiNGAM
[
35
]
. In Section
5
, we propose a modified version of the ICA-based multi-view model from
Chen et al. [6]
.

4
Estimation by SOS and recursive residuals

Here, we show how to estimate the model parameters using Second-Order Statistics (SOS) only, and how the SOS alone lead to identifiability. To do so, we will rely on a principle
that we call
recursive residuals
. While it has been used by
Shimizu et al. [35]
,
Hyvärinen and Smith [17]
,
Shimizu [36]
, we provide here a unified treatment.

4.1
Causal ordering using recursive residuals

Methods based on what we call recursive residuals are built on the following principle: 1) we can find a root variable (
i.e.
one with no parents) by analyzing the causal directions between all the pairs of variables, and 2) when all other variables are regressed on a root variable, the resulting residuals preserve the causal ordering of the original
variables
[
35
, Lemma 2 and Corollary 1]
. In the multi-view case, the same principles apply since all views are assumed to share a common ordering. A formal proof is provided in Appendix
D.3.1
and
D.3.2
.

Algorithms based on recursive residuals therefore follow the same recursive scheme to recover the causal ordering: (i) perform pairwise regressions to obtain residuals, (ii) identify a root variable according to a specific criterion of causal direction computed on the residuals, and (iii) remove the selected root and repeat the procedure on the residuals until all variables are ordered. In practice, we will use Ordinary LS to compute the residuals in step (i). The main difference between algorithms lies in step (ii),
i.e.
in how the root variable is selected.

Since a root variable has no parents, we can find one by testing the causal direction for every pair of variables and taking as a root a variable that is never inferred to be an effect.
(A practical way to select the root variable from such tests with finite samples is described in Appendix
E.4
.)
Thus, root selection reduces to designing a criterion of causal direction capable of reliably distinguishing the causal direction between two variables. In the following, we introduce two new such criteria in the multi-view setting, after first discussing
a condition to ensure that these criteria are consistent.

4.2
Correlation assumption (bivariate case)

Example of two views (
i
and
i^{\prime}

{\bm{x}}_{1}

{\bm{x}}_{2}
.

Now, we consider the case of two variables, which is the foundation of the recursive residuals procedure.
For simplicity of notation, we can consider these two random variables aggregated across views:
{\bm{x}}_{1}=(x_{1}^{1},\dots,x_{1}^{m})^{\top}
and
{\bm{x}}_{2}=(x_{2}^{1},\dots,x_{2}^{m})^{\top}
, with
either
{\bm{x}}_{1}
causing
{\bm{x}}_{2}
, or the converse.
Covariance matrices are denoted by
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{x}}_{1}}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{1}{\bm{x}}_{1}^{\top}]
, and likewise for
{\bm{x}}_{2}
,
{\bm{e}}_{1}
and
{\bm{e}}_{2}
.

We introduce a fundamental assumption
that makes the causal direction recoverable.
Essentially, the views need to be correlated and correlations need to be diverse.

Assumption 1

(Correlation and diversity across views, bivariate case)

Consider without loss of generality that
{\bm{x}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{2}
(in the sense that
{\bm{B}}_{21}^{i}\neq 0
for at least one
i
).
There exist two distinct views
i\neq i^{\prime}
such that a)
{\bm{x}}_{1}
and
{\bm{x}}_{2}
are correlated in at least one of those views,

\mathrm{corr}(x_{1}^{i},x_{2}^{i})\neq 0\text{ or }\mathrm{corr}(x_{1}^{i^{\prime}},x_{2}^{i^{\prime}})\neq 0
,
and b) their correlations fulfill the “diversity” condition

|\mathrm{corr}(x_{1}^{i},x_{1}^{i^{\prime}})|\neq|\mathrm{corr}(e_{2}^{i},e_{2}^{i^{\prime}})|
.

Figure
2
illustrates this bivariate case.
Discussion on the intuitive meaning of such an assumption is deferred to Section
4.6
.
In practice, this assumption becomes increasingly mild as the number of views grows.
More general conditions are discussed in Appendix
B.6
.

4.3
Likelihood-based criterion: PairwiseLiMVAM

We first derive a new criterion that extends the PairwiseLiNGAM criterion of
Hyvärinen and Smith [17]
to the multi-view setting.
Assuming Gaussian disturbances, we compute the expected log-likelihoods of two models:
{\bm{x}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{2}
and
{\bm{x}}_{2}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{1}
, where an arrow denotes a directed edge between two adjacent variables in the causal graph.
This leads to the following likelihood ratio (LR) criterion:

Proposition 1

(LR criterion and its consistency)

The log-likelihood ratio of the two models is given by:

\mathrm{LR}_{12}=-\log\det{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{x}}_{1}}-\log\det{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{e}}_{2}}+\log\det{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{x}}_{2}}+\log\det{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{e}}_{1}}\kern 5.0pt.

(5)

Under Assumption
1
, this criterion is positive when
{\bm{x}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{2}
, and negative in the opposite direction.

We prove this in Appendix
B
. Note that this consistency result applies
no matter whether the disturbances are Gaussian or non-Gaussian
. Importantly, this makes the causal direction identifiable under very general circumstances.
In practice, we replace the covariance matrices
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{x}}_{1}}
,
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{e}}_{1}}
,
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{x}}_{2}}
, and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{e}}_{2}}
with consistent estimators (see Appendix
E.1
).

4.4
Cross-covariance-based criterion: DirectLiMVAM

Our next criterion is a new multi-view extension of the DirectLiNGAM criterion
[
35
]
. In a single-view setting,
Shimizu et al. [35]
relied on the fact that
x_{1}
and
e_{2}
are
independent
if the causal direction is
x_{1}\rightarrow x_{2}
, and measured the independence using a kernel-based estimator of mutual information called KGV
[
3
]
. A multi-view extension was actually proposed by
Shimizu [36]
, where the correct direction was chosen by comparing the
sums over views
of these scores for the two directions.
However, such summation over views does not fully take advantage of the multi-view structure. Thus, the disturbances still needed to be non-Gaussian.

Here, we efficiently exploit the multi-view framework while only looking at SOS. We choose to evaluate the
cross-covariance
between the
entire
view-aggregated vectors
{\bm{x}}_{1}
and
{\bm{e}}_{2}
for the direction
{\bm{x}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{2}
, and between
{\bm{x}}_{2}
and
{\bm{e}}_{1}
for the direction
{\bm{x}}_{2}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{1}
.
The correct direction is chosen by comparing the Frobenius norms of the cross-covariances between regressors and residuals.
Thus, we obtain the following “FC” criterion that is consistent:

Proposition 2

(FC criterion and its consistency)

Consider the following criterion:

\mathrm{FC}_{12}=\left\|\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{x}}_{2}{\bm{e}}_{1}^{\top}\big]\right\|_{F}-\left\|\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{x}}_{1}{\bm{e}}_{2}^{\top}\big]\right\|_{F}\kern 5.0pt.

(6)

Under Assumption
1
, this criterion is positive when
{\bm{x}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{2}
, and negative in the opposite direction.

We prove this in Appendix
C
.

4.5
Estimating the causal coefficients

Applying the recursive scheme explained in Section
4.1
,
and using the criteria from Eq.
5
or Eq.
6
, we obtain a causal ordering encoded by
{\bm{P}}
, in the general multivariate case.
Importantly, since the ordering is now known, the causal matrices
{\bm{B}}^{i}
can be uniquely recovered with little effort.
In fact, the model becomes

{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{T}}^{i}{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}+{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}\;,\qquad i\in[\![1,m]\!]

(7)

where
{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{P}}{\bm{x}}^{i}
are the reordered observations and
{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}={\bm{P}}{\bm{e}}^{i}
are the reordered disturbances. By construction, each variable
x^{\prime i}_{j}
only depends on its predecessors
x^{\prime i}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime i}_{j-1}
.
For each
j\in[\![2,p]\!]
, we estimate the
j
-th row of all matrices
{\bm{T}}^{i}
jointly using one-step Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS)
[
44
]
, which is more efficient than Ordinary LS. A detailed description of one-step FGLS is provided in Appendix
E.5
.
Finally, the adjacency matrices are recovered as
{\bm{B}}^{i}={\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{i}{\bm{P}}
.

The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1

and illustrated in Figure
1
(middle and right panels); it notably requires no hyperparameter tuning.
Its worst-case
computational complexity
is in
O(m^{3}\cdot p^{3}\cdot n)
, where
n
is the number of observations, which we show in Appendix
E.6
. In practice, these algorithms are highly parallelizable as they rely on basic algebraic operations (
e.g.
multiplying pairs of matrices), and fast as they do not require iterative optimization of an objective function.

.

(c)

4.6
General identifiability using second-order statistics

To theoretically justify when our algorithm can find
unique
parameters
{\bm{B}}^{i}
and
{\bm{P}}
, we next present a general identifiability theory. It uses the following generalization of Assumption
1
from two variables to many variables.

Assumption 2

(Correlation and diversity across views)

For any two variables
{\bm{x}}_{j}
and
{\bm{x}}_{j^{\prime}}

such that
{\bm{x}}_{j}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{j^{\prime}}
(in the sense that
{\bm{B}}_{j^{\prime}j}^{i}\neq 0
for at least one
i
), there exist two distinct views
i\neq i^{\prime}
such that a) those variables are correlated in at least one of those views,

\mathrm{corr}(x_{j}^{i},x_{j^{\prime}}^{i})\neq 0\text{ or }\mathrm{corr}(x_{j}^{i^{\prime}},x_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}})\neq 0,
and b) their correlations fulfill the “diversity” condition:

|\mathrm{corr}(e_{j}^{i},e_{j}^{i^{\prime}})|\neq|\mathrm{corr}(e_{j^{\prime}}^{i},e_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}})|\kern 5.0pt.

(9)

Even milder sufficient conditions are discussed in Appendix
D.1
but they are harder to interpret.
To better understand this assumption, note first that it requires some
correlation across views
: it rules out the trivial case where all cross-view correlations are zero. Moreover, it demands some
diversity
in these correlations. Consider the extreme situation where, for standardized variables,
x^{i}_{j}=\alpha x^{i^{\prime}}_{j}
and
x^{i}_{j^{\prime}}=\alpha x^{i^{\prime}}_{j^{\prime}}
(and analogously for the residuals), for some scalar
\alpha
. In this case, all correlations equal
1
, so Eq.
9
is violated. Appendix
D.2
provides more realistic examples of violations, while Appendix
H.1.4
introduces a simple score to assess whether Assumption
2
holds in practice.

We next introduce an additional assumption that ensures identifiability of the total causal ordering.

Assumption 3

(Dense connectivity of the graph when pooled across views)

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the union graph of the
m
views, obtained by taking the union of their edge sets. Assume that the ordering induced by
\mathcal{G}
is
total
, i.e. there exists a directed path between any two variables.

With these assumptions, we prove in Appendix
D.3
the following identifiability result.

Theorem 3

(Identifiability of the ordering and adjacency matrices
{\bm{B}}^{i}
)

Assume the data follows the model in Eq.
3
, and that Assumption
2
holds.
Then, the causal (partial) ordering and causal coefficients
{\bm{B}}^{i}
are identifiable. If we further make Assumption
3
, then
{\bm{P}}
is identifiable as well.

The identifiability of the
partial
ordering does not necessarily mean that the permutation matrix
{\bm{P}}
can be recovered uniquely. If the ordering is only partial (for example, in a three-nodes graph:
{\bm{x}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{2}
and
{\bm{x}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{3}
, but there is no relation between
{\bm{x}}_{2}
and
{\bm{x}}_{3}
, so both orderings
1\rightarrow 2\rightarrow 3
and
1\rightarrow 3\rightarrow 2
are valid), then
{\bm{P}}
is only identifiable up to a class of permutations that induce the same partial ordering. The above theorem shows a case where
{\bm{P}}
can be (uniquely) identified.

5
Estimation by multi-view ICA assuming shared disturbances

Next, we provide an alternative approach to the LiMVAM model definition and estimation. We utilize possible non-Gaussianity of the disturbances to obtain identifiability conditions that are different from the previous section. However, we still do not impose non-Gaussianity as a necessary condition.

Model definition

The approach here is to propose a particular model for the disturbances by supposing that they can be additively composed into two terms: one term is view-specific, while the other is shared across views and therefore makes the views dependent. Specifically:

Assumption 4

(Shared disturbances)

The disturbances can be decomposed as

\displaystyle{\bm{e}}^{i}={\bm{D}}^{i}{\bm{s}}+{\bm{n}}^{i}

(10)

where the

{\bm{D}}^{i}
are diagonal matrices with positive entries on the diagonal, the
{\bm{s}}
has mutually independent entries and second-order moment
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{s}}{\bm{s}}^{\top}]={\bm{I}}_{p}
, and the view-specific noises are Gaussian
{\bm{n}}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}}^{i})
and depend on the view
i
via the second-order moments
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{i}
that are diagonal matrices. Lastly, the vectors
{\bm{s}}
and
{\bm{n}}^{i},i=1,\ldots,m
, are assumed to be mutually independent.

This assumption and Eq.
10
are related to
Chen et al. [6]
who were originally inspired by the multi-view
Shared ICA of
Richard et al. [31]
. Specifically,

Chen et al. [6]
considered the special case where the matrices
{\bm{D}}^{i}={\bm{I}}_{p}
, so that there are the same disturbances
{\bm{s}}
over views.
We argue here that their model was too restrictive and unrealistic as it imposes an arbitrary scaling for the data variables. Consider the root variable of the DAG, denoting it by
x_{i}
. Its disturbance is
s_{i}+n_{i}
which has variance of at least one since the variance of
s_{i}
is defined as one. Thus, the root variable has variance of at least one which is absurd: the model should be invariant to the scaling of the variables. Likewise, suppose we
create a new data set by dividing just one view, say
{\bm{x}}^{1}
by, say
10
, giving new data
{\bm{x}}^{\prime 1}
.
This new data does not follow the model by
Chen et al. [6]
anymore since
{\bm{s}}
should be rescaled to
{\bm{s}}/10
for just this view; now the
{\bm{s}}/10
here is different from the
{\bm{s}}
in other views. Such problems motivated us to develop a model where the scaling terms
{\bm{D}}^{i}
are new parameters, and thus to the definition in Eq.
10
.

Identifiability

Now we proceed to show the identifiability of the model based on Assumption
4
.
The starting point is that our model can be rewritten as a multi-view ICA model as in Eq.
4
, just like in the case of LiNGAM.
Thus, the methods developed for multi-view ICA can be used to analyze its identifiability.
In the case of Gaussian disturbances, we use the following assumption.

Assumption 5

(Diversity of the views with Gaussian disturbances)

If
j
and
j^{\prime}
,
j\neq j^{\prime}
, are the indices of two Gaussian common disturbances in
{\bm{s}}
, then the number of views is at least three, and there exists a view
i
such that
\frac{{\bm{\Sigma}}^{i}_{jj}}{({\bm{D}}^{i}_{jj})^{2}}\neq\frac{{\bm{\Sigma}}^{i}_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime}}}{({\bm{D}}^{i}_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime}})^{2}}
.

This assumption, inspired by
Richard et al. [31]
, is trivially fulfilled if all disturbances are non-Gaussian. For Gaussian disturbances, it essentially states that the views should be diverse in terms of disturbances’ SOS. This follows from the theory of Shared ICA which does not require the common disturbances
{\bm{s}}
to be non-Gaussian if there is enough diversity
[
31
,
2
]
.

We can now state our main identifiability result for this variant of the model.

Theorem 4

(Identifiability with shared disturbances)

Assume the LiMVAM model in Eq.
3
together with Assumption
4
. Under Assumption
5
,
the model is identifiable in the sense that the parameters
{\bm{B}}^{i}
,
{\bm{D}}^{i}
, and
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{i}
are identifiable. If we further make Assumption
3
,

{\bm{P}}
is identifiable as well.

The proof of the theorem is given in Appendix
F
and we provide an extension when the causal ordering
{\bm{P}}^{i}
is view-dependent in Appendix
F.3
.
We thus see that for non-Gaussian disturbances, identifiability is achieved with weak conditions; in fact, no special conditions on the covariances are needed. Still, the theory of Shared ICA leads to identifiability even for Gaussian disturbances, but with stronger conditions than obtained in Section
4
. Moreover, the Shared ICA framework naturally provides an algorithm.

Estimation

The model
can be estimated by a combination of the ICA-based estimation procedure by
Shimizu et al. [34]
, combined with the Shared ICA methods by
Richard et al. [31]
. We call it ICA-LiMVAM and explain it in detail in Algorithm
4
. Surprisingly, the resulting algorithm gives estimates of
{\bm{B}}_{i}
that are identical to
Chen et al. [6]
, even though our model is more general. In contrast, our algorithm estimates different noise variances and scalings
{\bm{D}}_{i}
.
The worst-case
computational complexity
is in
O(tnmp^{3}+mp^{5})
, for
t
iterations of the Shared ICA (ML) algorithm,
n
samples,
m
views and
p
components. We detail this in Appendix
G.2
.

7
Discussion and related work

Multi-view vs. multi-domain distinction

We wish to emphasize that the multi-view setting considered here is very different from
multi-domain
methods. As an intuitive example, multi-view would correspond to a person seeing the same object from different angles, while multi-domain would correspond to a person seeing different objects. In multi-view data, the data points in different views are
paired
over domains, while in multi-domain methods, no such correspondence between data points exists. In statistical terms, the fundamental difference is that in the multi-view setting, the views are statistically dependent (correlated), while in the multi-domain setting, the domains are independently generated, typically with a distribution shift over domains. In fact, several methods study causal discovery in the multi-domain case
[
10
,
1
,
22
]
;
multi-environment
methods are a special case of multi-domain methods, where distributional shifts arise from interventions
[
26
,
25
]
; these are discussed in detail in Appendix
I
.

Related work

A multi-view causal model based on LiNGAM was considered by
Shimizu [36]
, but their model was very different. The fundamental difference is that we assume the disturbances have some shared information across views and can be Gaussian, whereas
Shimizu [36]
estimate necessarily non-Gaussian disturbances, while ignoring dependencies across views.
Thus, they only showed how to use the multiple views to improve the estimation while still assuming non-Gaussianity.

Chen et al. [6]
considered multi-view SEM using a special case of our shared disturbances model, which is in its turn a special case of our general LiMVAM model. Algorithmically, there is little difference between their work and our ICA-LiMVAM. However, their identifiability proofs are not available nor did they consider the identifiability of the causal ordering, while we do both.
Moreover, as we already argued in Section
5
, their model formulation has the serious flaw that it forces an arbitrary scaling of the data variables. Crucially, we propose new algorithms, PairwiseLiMVAM and DirectLiMVAM, that use only SOS; they are shown to be empirically superior to the ICA-LiMVAM in most cases.
Finally,
Zhu et al. [48]
studied a special case of our model that is bivariate and where the views correspond to time lags, from a purely empirical viewpoint.

8
Conclusion

This work provides a full treatment of multi-view causal discovery that was under-explored in the literature. We start by defining a multi-view model called LiMVAM. We derive a comprehensive identifiability theory of the causal ordering and weights, using second-order statistics (SOS,
i.e.
covariance structure) only. Beyond these theoretical guarantees, we also improve an existing algorithm (ICA-LiMVAM) and introduce two novel ones — PairwiseLiMVAM and DirectLiMVAM — for estimating the parameters of the multi-view model; again, SOS alone are enough. We rigorously prove the consistency of our algorithms and empirically show that they are both
fast
and result in
highly efficient estimation
. On synthetic data, they outperform comparable algorithms; results on brain imaging are promising.

.
It follows that
{\bm{D}}^{\prime}{\bm{Q}}{\bm{D}}^{-1}={\bm{I}}
.
Since
{\bm{D}}
and
{\bm{D}}^{\prime}
have positive diagonal entries, it follows that the entries of
{\bm{Q}}
cannot equal
-1
. So,
{\bm{Q}}={\bm{I}}
, and thus
{\bm{D}}^{\prime}={\bm{D}}
.
This concludes the proof of the Lemma.

A.3.2
Proof of Lemma
6

From the equality
{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}{\bm{P}}={\bm{P}}^{\prime\top}{\bm{T}}^{\prime}{\bm{P}}^{\prime}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}=\tilde{{\bm{P}}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\prime}\tilde{{\bm{P}}}

(27)

where
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}={\bm{P}}^{\prime}{\bm{P}}^{\top}
is the permutation matrix represented by function
\phi
, which is defined such that:
\forall k\in[\![1,p]\!]
,
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}_{k,\phi(k)}=1
and
\forall l\neq\phi(k)
,
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}_{k,l}=0
.
Using the notation
\phi
instead of
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}
, Eq.
27
can be rewritten as,
\forall k,l\in[\![1,p]\!]
,

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}_{k,l}={\bm{T}}^{\prime}_{\phi(k),\phi(l)}\kern 5.0pt.

(28)

We proceed by a proof by contradiction: assume that
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}\neq{\bm{I}}
.
As a consequence, there exists an index
k
such that
\phi(k)\neq k
.
Let us fix such an index as
k
.
We can assume without loss of generality that

\displaystyle\phi(k)>k

(29)

otherwise we just have to invert signs and switch rows and columns in the following.
By assumption, the strictly lower triangular part of
{\bm{T}}
only has non-zero elements, so we have

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}_{\phi(k),k}\neq 0\kern 5.0pt.

(30)

Yet, from Eq.
28
, we know that
{\bm{T}}_{\phi(k),k}={\bm{T}}^{\prime}_{\phi(\phi(k)),\phi(k)}
and all the non-zero elements of
{\bm{T}}^{\prime}
are in its strictly lower triangular part, which implies

\displaystyle\phi(\phi(k))>\phi(k)\kern 5.0pt.

(31)

This logic can be applied repeatedly as in the preceding lemma’s proof, and we see that

\displaystyle\phi(k)<\phi^{(2)}(k)<\phi^{(3)}(k)<\dots\kern 5.0pt.

(32)

Now, this leads to an infinite strictly increasing sequence, which is contradictory since the index cannot grow greater than
p
.
So,
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}={\bm{I}}
, which means that
{\bm{P}}^{\prime}{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}={\bm{P}}
, and thus, using the orthogonality of
{\bm{P}}
,

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}^{\prime}={\bm{P}}\kern 5.0pt.

(33)

It also follows that
{\bm{T}}^{\prime}={\bm{T}}
, which concludes the proof of Lemma
6
.

Appendix B
Likelihood-based criterion

B.1
Basic setting

The fundamental question here is to choose the causal direction for two variables, having many views
i
. We denote the two variables by
x
and
y
to avoid too many indices. It could be that the causal direction is
x\rightarrow y
:

y^{i}=b_{i}x^{i}+e^{i}\qquad\text{for all }i

(34)

or
y\rightarrow x
:

x^{i}=c_{i}y^{i}+d^{i}\qquad\text{for all }i\kern 5.0pt.

(35)

Recall that superscripts are for view in the case of the random variables; however, for the regression coefficients we use subscripts because we need to take squares of such quantities. For the direction
x\rightarrow y
, collect the regressor and residuals in the vectors
{\bm{x}}=(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})^{\top}
and
{\bm{e}}=(e^{1},\dots,e^{m})^{\top}
, respectively; and likewise for
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{d}}
in the opposite direction.
Second-order moment matrices are denoted by
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}]
,
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{y}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{y}}{\bm{y}}^{\top}]
, and likewise for
{\bm{d}}
and
{\bm{e}}
.
Note that the independence between
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{e}}
and between
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{d}}
, and the fact that
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
are standardized, imply that

b_{i}=c_{i}=\mathrm{cov}(x_{i},y_{i})\qquad\text{for all }i\kern 5.0pt.

(36)

B.2
Derivation of the expected log-likelihood in one direction

We start by formulating the likelihood of the model in the case where disturbances are assumed to be Gaussian.

Consider the log-likelihood of
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
for the direction
x\rightarrow y
.
Under the model, the prior (marginal) distribution of
{\bm{x}}
is
\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})
, and the prior distribution of
{\bm{e}}={\bm{y}}-\bm{b}\odot{\bm{x}}
is
\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e})
— where
\odot
denotes the elementwise product.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\log p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}};\bm{b},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e},x\rightarrow y)

\displaystyle=\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}};\bm{b},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e},x\rightarrow y)+\log p({\bm{x}};\bm{b},x\rightarrow y)

(37)

\displaystyle=-({\bm{y}}-\bm{b}\odot{\bm{x}})^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}^{-1}({\bm{y}}-\bm{b}\odot{\bm{x}})-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}|-{\bm{x}}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}^{-1}{\bm{x}}-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|

(38)

\displaystyle=-\tr\big( \mSigma_e^{-1} (\vy- \bm{b} \odot\vx) (\vy- \bm{b} \odot\vx)^\top\big)-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}|

\displaystyle-\tr\big( \mSigma_x^{-1} \vx\vx^\top\big)-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|

(39)

where
\tr
is the trace operator, and where we neglect the part of the normalization constant that does not depend on any parameters.

The expected log-likelihood is thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}};\bm{b},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e},x\rightarrow y)]

\displaystyle=-\tr\big( \mSigma_e^{-1} \E[ (\vy- \bm{b} \odot\vx) (\vy- \bm{b} \odot\vx)^\top] \big)-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}|

\displaystyle-\tr\big( \mSigma_x^{-1} \E[\vx\vx^\top] \big)-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|

(40)

\displaystyle=-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}|-2m\kern 5.0pt.

(41)

Denote by
\mathcal{L}
this expected log-likelihood. We have (up to constants):

\mathcal{L}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e};x\rightarrow y)=-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}|\kern 5.0pt.

(42)

B.3
Derivation of the likelihood-based criterion

We now derive a criterion that can be used to find the causal direction.

The expected log-likelihood of the model for the direction
y\rightarrow x
can be obtained by switching the roles of
x
and
y
in Eq.
42
. Thus, we have

\mathcal{L}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{y},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d};y\rightarrow x)=-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{y}|-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d}|

(43)

where we recall that
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{y}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{y}}{\bm{y}}^{\top}]
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{d}}{\bm{d}}^{\top}]
.

The correct direction can then be found by comparing the expected log-likelihoods of both directions. In particular, we calculate the difference between the two log-likelihoods, which can be reformulated as a likelihood ratio (LR):

\mathrm{LR}:=\mathcal{L}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e};x\rightarrow y)-\mathcal{L}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{y},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d};y\rightarrow x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}};\bm{b},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e},x\rightarrow y)}{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}};{\bm{c}},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{y},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d},y\rightarrow x)}\right]\kern 5.0pt.

(44)

Using Eq.
42
and Eq.
43
, the criterion
\mathrm{LR}
becomes

\mathrm{LR}=-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}|+\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{y}|+\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d}|\kern 5.0pt.

(45)

Intuitively, the criterion in Eq.
45
will be large if the
x^{i}
are highly correlated (more than the
y^{i}
) over the views, since then the first determinant will be small. This should make intuitive sense since it means the cause is highly structured.
Likewise, if the residuals for
x\rightarrow y
are highly structured, that direction is more likely. In other words, the direction that exhibits more structure wins.

Surprisingly, in this formulation, there is actually no particular model for the dependencies of the disturbances: the likelihood should simply prefer dependent disturbances. It is important to note that the criterion makes sense even if the distribution is non-Gaussian, as will be proven below. We thus obtain a method that works for data of any distribution, but using only second-order statistics.

B.4
Non-negativity of the likelihood-based criterion

In this section, we prove that the criterion in Eq.
45
is non-negative for the direction
x\rightarrow y
. Importantly, no assumption of Gaussianity needs to be made here.

Consider the direction
x\rightarrow y
, and let us write
{\bm{B}}:=\mathrm{diag}(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})
.
Using the matrix
{\bm{B}}
rather than the vector
\bm{b}
makes notations clearer in the proofs; please note that this notation is different from the main paper. In the following, we use interchangeably
\bm{b}
and
{\bm{B}}
.
The covariance matrix of
{\bm{y}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}}
is

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{y}={\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}\kern 5.0pt.

(46)

Given that
b_{i}=c_{i}
for all
i
, we have
{\bm{d}}={\bm{x}}-{\bm{B}}{\bm{y}}={\bm{x}}-{\bm{B}}({\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}})=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2}){\bm{x}}-{\bm{B}}{\bm{e}}
. Thus, the covariance matrix of
{\bm{d}}
is

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d}=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2}){\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2})+{\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}{\bm{B}}\kern 5.0pt.

(47)

Using these expressions, the criterion in Eq.
45
becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{LR}=-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}|+\log\left|{\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}\right|+\log\left|({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2}){\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2})+{\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}{\bm{B}}\right|\kern 5.0pt.

(48)

Collect the variances of the residuals
{\bm{e}}
in the diagonal matrix

{\bm{L}}:=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(e^{1})},\dots,\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(e^{m})}\right)

(49)

and define the correlation matrix of the residuals as
\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}
, so that

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}={\bm{L}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{L}}\kern 5.0pt.

(50)

Now, the objective becomes

\mathrm{LR}=-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|-\log|\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}|-\log|{\bm{L}}^{2}|+\log\left|{\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{L}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{L}}\right|\\
+\log\left|({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2}){\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2})+{\bm{B}}{\bm{L}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{L}}{\bm{B}}\right|\kern 5.0pt.

(51)

Importantly, the fact that
x
and
y
are standardized means that
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{y}
have unit diagonal, so Eq.
46
implies

{\bm{L}}^{2}+{\bm{B}}^{2}={\bm{I}}\kern 5.0pt

(52)

and it follows that the diagonal entries of
{\bm{L}}
and
{\bm{B}}
are in
[-1,1]
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{LR}

\displaystyle=-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|-\log|\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}|-\log|{\bm{L}}^{2}|+\log\left|{\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{L}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{L}}\right|+\log\left|{\bm{L}}^{2}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{L}}^{2}+{\bm{L}}{\bm{B}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{B}}{\bm{L}}\right|

(53)

\displaystyle=-\log|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}|-\log|\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}|+\log\left|{\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{L}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{L}}\right|+\log\left|{\bm{L}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{L}}+{\bm{B}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{B}}\right|

(54)

where
{\bm{L}}
and
{\bm{B}}
commuted in the first equality because they are diagonal.

The following lemma (proven in Appendix
B.5
) shows that this criterion is non-negative, and even positive under some assumptions.

Lemma 8

Let
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}
and
\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}
be
m\times m
real symmetric positive definite matrices with unit diagonal entries. Let
{\bm{B}}=\mathrm{diag}(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})
and
{\bm{L}}=\mathrm{diag}(l_{1},\dots,l_{m})
be diagonal matrices with entries in
[-1,1]
, satisfying
{\bm{B}}^{2}+{\bm{L}}^{2}={\bm{I}}
(i.e.
b_{i}^{2}+l_{i}^{2}=1
for all
i
).
Define the function

\displaystyle J({\bm{B}},{\bm{L}}):=-\log\det{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}-\log\det\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}+\log\det\left({\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{L}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{L}}\right)+\log\det\left({\bm{L}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{L}}+{\bm{B}}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}{\bm{B}}\right).

(55)

Under the above assumptions, we have
J({\bm{B}},{\bm{L}})\geq 0
. Moreover,
J({\bm{B}},{\bm{L}})=0
if and only if

{\bm{L}}\,\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}\,{\bm{B}}\;=\;{\bm{B}}\,{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}\,{\bm{L}}\kern 5.0pt.

(56)

B.5
Proof of a useful lemma

Let us prove Lemma
8
.

Step 1: define
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{C}}
—

Define

{\bm{A}}:={\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{L}}\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}{\bm{L}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{C}}:={\bm{L}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{L}}+{\bm{B}}\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}{\bm{B}}\kern 5.0pt.

(57)

We have

J({\bm{B}},{\bm{L}}):=-\log\det{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}-\log\det\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}+\log\det{\bm{A}}+\log\det{\bm{C}}\kern 5.0pt.

(58)

Step 2: define
{\bm{M}}
—

Define the
2m\times 2m
block matrix

{\bm{M}}:=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{B}}&{\bm{L}}\\
-{\bm{L}}&{\bm{B}}\end{pmatrix}\kern 5.0pt.

(59)

Since
{\bm{B}}
and
{\bm{L}}
are diagonal, they commute, hence
{\bm{B}}{\bm{L}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{B}}
. Using
{\bm{B}}^{2}+{\bm{L}}^{2}={\bm{I}}
, we compute

{\bm{M}}{\bm{M}}^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{B}}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{L}}{\bm{L}}&-{\bm{B}}{\bm{L}}+{\bm{L}}{\bm{B}}\\
-{\bm{L}}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{L}}&{\bm{L}}{\bm{L}}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{B}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{I}}&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&{\bm{I}}\end{pmatrix}={\bm{I}}_{2m}\kern 5.0pt.

(60)

Thus
{\bm{M}}
is orthonormal, so
\det{\bm{M}}=\pm 1
.

Step 3: define
\mathcal{B}
—

Consider the block-diagonal matrix
\mathrm{diag}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e})
and conjugate by
{\bm{M}}
:

\mathcal{B}:={\bm{M}}\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}&{\bm{0}}\\[4.0pt]
{\bm{0}}&\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}\end{pmatrix}{\bm{M}}^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{A}}&{\bm{S}}\\[4.0pt]
{\bm{S}}^{\top}&{\bm{C}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\bm{S}}:={\bm{L}}\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}{\bm{B}}-{\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{L}}\kern 5.0pt.

(61)

Because
{\bm{M}}
is orthonormal and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}\succ{\bm{0}}
, the matrix
\mathcal{B}
is symmetric positive definite (SPD). In particular,
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{C}}
are also SPD (as they are principal blocks of a SPD matrix) and thus
{\bm{A}}^{-1}
exists.

Step 4: Schur complement of
{\bm{A}}
in
\mathcal{B}
—

For a symmetric block matrix
\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{A}}&{\bm{S}}\\
{\bm{S}}^{\top}&{\bm{C}}\end{pmatrix}
with
{\bm{A}}\succ{\bm{0}}
, the Schur complement of
{\bm{A}}
is
{\bm{C}}-{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}
, and the Schur’s formula yields

\det\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{A}}&{\bm{S}}\\
{\bm{S}}^{\top}&{\bm{C}}\end{pmatrix}=\det(\mA)\,\det\big( \mC- \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS\big)\kern 5.0pt.

(62)

Applying this to
\mathcal{B}
and using multiplicativity of determinant and the fact that
\det{\bm{M}}=\pm 1
gives

\det(\mSigma_x)\det(\widetilde\mSigma_e)=\det(\mathcal B)=\det(\mA)\,\det\big( \mC- \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS\big)\kern 5.0pt.

(63)

Step 5: derive a matrix inequality —

Because
\mathcal{B}\succ{\bm{0}}
and
A\succ{\bm{0}}
, it follows from the Schur complement characterization of positive definiteness that
{\bm{C}}-{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}\succ{\bm{0}}
.
In particular, we have
\det(\mC- \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS)>0
.
Moreover, since
{\bm{A}}\succ{\bm{0}}
, we have
{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}\succeq{\bm{0}}
, and thus
{\bm{C}}-{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}\preceq{\bm{C}}
.

Step 6: derive a determinant inequality —

If
{\bm{0}}\preceq{\bm{X}}\preceq{\bm{Y}}
and
{\bm{Y}}\succ{\bm{0}}
, then conjugating by
{\bm{Y}}^{-1/2}
shows
{\bm{0}}\preceq{\bm{Y}}^{-1/2}{\bm{X}}{\bm{Y}}^{-1/2}\preceq{\bm{I}}
. Thus, all eigenvalues of
{\bm{Y}}^{-1/2}{\bm{X}}{\bm{Y}}^{-1/2}
lie in
[0,1]
, so their product satisfies
\det(\mY^{-1/2} \mX\mY^{-1/2})\leq 1
. Hence
\det(\mX)\leq\det(\mY)
.
Applying this to
{\bm{X}}:={\bm{C}}-{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}
and
{\bm{Y}}:={\bm{C}}
yields

\det\big( \mC- \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS\big)\leq\det(\mC)\kern 5.0pt.

(64)

Step 7:
J({\bm{B}},{\bm{L}})\geq 0
—

From Steps 4 and 6, we get

\det(\mSigma_x)\det(\widetilde\mSigma_e)=\det(\mA)\,\det\big( \mC- \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS\big)\leq\det(\mA)\det(\mC)\kern 5.0pt.

(65)

Taking natural logarithms and rearranging gives

\log\det(\mA)+\log\det(\mC)\;\geq\;\log\det(\mSigma_x)+\log\det(\widetilde\mSigma_e)\kern 5.0pt,

(66)

which is precisely
J({\bm{B}},{\bm{L}})\geq 0
.

Step 8: equality condition —

The equality
J({\bm{B}},{\bm{L}})=0
holds iff

\det\big( \mC- \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS\big)=\det(\mC)\kern 5.0pt.

(67)

Write
{\bm{K}}:={\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\big({\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}\big){\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\succeq{\bm{0}}
. We have

\displaystyle\det\big( \mC- \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS\big)

\displaystyle=\det(\mC)\det\big( \mI- \mC^{-1} \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS\big)

(68)

\displaystyle=\det(\mC)\det\big( \mC^{-\frac 12} \mC^{\frac 12} - \mC^{-\frac 12} \mC^{-\frac 12} \mS^\top\mA^{-1} \mS\mC^{-\frac 12} \mC^{\frac 12} \big)

(69)

\displaystyle=\det(\mC)\det\big( \mC^{-\frac 12} \big)\det(\mI- \mK)\det\big( \mC^{\frac 12} \big)

(70)

\displaystyle=\det(\mC)\det(\mI- \mK)\kern 5.0pt.

(71)

Thus, the equality condition is
\det(\mI- \mK)=1
. From Step 5, we know that
{\bm{0}}\preceq{\bm{C}}-{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}\preceq{\bm{C}}
. Conjugating by
{\bm{C}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
gives
{\bm{0}}\preceq{\bm{I}}-{\bm{K}}\preceq{\bm{I}}
, so each eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
of
{\bm{K}}
satisfies
0\leq\lambda_{i}\leq 1
. The eigenvalues of
{\bm{I}}-{\bm{K}}
are
1-\lambda_{i}\in[0,1]
, hence

\prod_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})=\det(\mI- \mK)=1\kern 5.0pt.

(72)

The equality thus holds iff
\lambda_{i}=0
for all
i
, hence
{\bm{K}}={\bm{0}}
.
Since
{\bm{C}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},{\bm{A}}^{-1}\succ{\bm{0}}
, we get
{\bm{S}}={\bm{0}}
. Conversely,
{\bm{S}}={\bm{0}}
clearly forces equality. Therefore, the equality occurs exactly when

{\bm{S}}={\bm{L}}\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}{\bm{B}}-{\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{L}}={\bm{0}}\kern 5.0pt,

(73)

i.e.

\forall i,j:\qquad b_{i}l_{j}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}=l_{i}b_{j}(\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e})_{ij}\kern 5.0pt.

(74)

This completes the proof.

B.6
Conditions for strict positivity of the likelihood-based criterion

From now on, we assume that the model is not degenerate in the sense that disturbances have a positive variance, that is
l_{i}=\mathrm{var}(e_{i})>0
for all
i
.

The criterion
\mathrm{LR}
derived in Appendix
B.4
is used to determine the causal direction from its sign, with
\mathrm{LR}=0
indicating that the two directions cannot be distinguished.
Lemma
8
shows that
\mathrm{LR}=0
if and only if

{\bm{L}}\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}{\bm{B}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{L}}\kern 5.0pt.

(75)

Entrywise, this is equivalent to

\forall i,j:\qquad l_{i}b_{j}(\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e})_{ij}=b_{i}l_{j}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}\kern 5.0pt.

(76)

Let us fix
i,j\in[\![1,m]\!]
.

Case 1:
b_{i}=0
and
b_{j}=0
—

The equality immediately holds.

Case 2:
b_{i}=0
and
b_{j}\neq 0
—

The right-hand side in Eq.
76
is equal to 0. Since
l_{i}=\mathrm{var}(e^{i})>0
and
b_{j}\neq 0
, we must have
(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}=0
.

Case 3:
b_{i}\neq 0
and
b_{j}=0
—

The left-hand side in Eq.
76
is equal to 0. Since
l_{j}=\mathrm{var}(e^{j})>0
and
b_{i}\neq 0
, we must have
({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}=0
.

Case 4:
b_{i}\neq 0
and
b_{j}\neq 0
—

Multiplying Eq.
76
by
\frac{l_{j}}{b_{i}b_{j}^{2}}
, and using
l_{j}^{2}=1-b_{j}^{2}
, we obtain

\frac{l_{i}l_{j}}{b_{i}b_{j}}(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}=\bigg(\frac{1}{b_{j}^{2}}-1\bigg)({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}\kern 5.0pt.

(77)

Switching the roles of
i
and
j
, and using the symmetry of
(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}
and
({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}
, yield

\frac{l_{i}l_{j}}{b_{i}b_{j}}(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}=\bigg(\frac{1}{b_{i}^{2}}-1\bigg)({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}\kern 5.0pt.

(78)

hence

b_{i}^{2}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}=b_{j}^{2}\;({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}\kern 5.0pt.

(79)

Similarly, exchanging the roles of
(b_{i},b_{j},({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij})
and
(l_{i},l_{j},(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij})
yields

l_{i}^{2}(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}=l_{j}^{2}\;(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}\kern 5.0pt.

(80)

Now suppose
({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}\neq 0
or
(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}\neq 0
.
From Eq.
79
and Eq.
80
it follows that
b_{i}^{2}=b_{j}^{2}
or
l_{i}^{2}=l_{j}^{2}
. Since
b_{i}^{2}+l_{i}^{2}=1
, both equalities hold simultaneously:

|b_{i}|=|b_{j}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad l_{i}=l_{j}

(81)

where the asbolute value can be dropped for
l_{i}
because
l_{i}=\mathrm{var}(e_{i})>0
.
Plugging these equalities into Eq.
77
yields

l_{j}^{2}(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}=\pm(1-b_{j}^{2})({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}

(82)

hence

|(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}|=|({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}|\kern 5.0pt.

(83)

Moreover, for Eq.
76
to hold we must also have the sign equality

\mathrm{sign}(b_{i})\;\mathrm{sign}(b_{j})=\mathrm{sign}(({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij})\;\mathrm{sign}((\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij})\kern 5.0pt.

(84)

Altogether, either both
({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}
and
(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}
vanish, or the following four conditions hold:

\displaystyle|(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}|

\displaystyle=|({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}|\neq 0

(85)

\displaystyle|b_{i}|

\displaystyle=|b_{j}|

(86)

\displaystyle l_{i}

\displaystyle=l_{j}

(87)

\displaystyle\mathrm{sign}(b_{i})\;\mathrm{sign}(b_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sign}(({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij})\;\mathrm{sign}((\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij})\kern 5.0pt.

(88)

Conversely, if
({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}=(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}=0
, or if conditions Eq.
85
–Eq.
88
are satisfied, then Eq.
76
holds.

Conclusion —

By contraposition, Eq.
75
is violated whenever there exists
(i,j)
such that one of the following cases holds:

•

b_{i}=0
,
b_{j}\neq 0
, and
(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}\neq 0

•

b_{i}\neq 0
,
b_{j}=0
, and
({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}\neq 0

•

b_{i}\neq 0
,
b_{j}\neq 0
,
({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}\neq 0
or
(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}\neq 0
, and at least one of the conditions Eq.
85
–Eq.
88
is not met.

In particular, a simple sufficient condition is

\exists i,j:\qquad\big(b_{i}\neq 0\qquad\text{or}\qquad b_{j}\neq 0\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad|(\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e})_{ij}|\neq|({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x})_{ij}|\kern 5.0pt.

(89)

which is the basis of Assumptions
1
and
2
given in the main paper.

Appendix C
Cross-covariance-based criterion

Consider the setting described in Appendix
B.1
.

C.1
Derivation of the cross-covariance-based criterion

A simple approach to infer the causal direction is to exploit the assumption that root variables and residuals are independent. Specifically, if the true direction is
x\rightarrow y
, then
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{e}}
are independent; if the true direction is
y\rightarrow x
, then
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{d}}
are independent.
This implies that the cross-moment matrix
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{e}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}]
should vanish when
x\rightarrow y
, while
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{d}}{\bm{y}}^{\top}]
should vanish when
y\rightarrow x
.
Comparing the Frobenius norms of these matrices therefore provides a natural criterion, based solely on cross-covariances:

\mathrm{FC}:=\left\|\mathbb{E}[{\bm{d}}{\bm{y}}^{\top}]\right\|_{F}-\left\|\mathbb{E}[{\bm{e}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}]\right\|_{F}\kern 5.0pt.

(90)

Intuitively, if this criterion is positive we deduce that
x\rightarrow y
, and if negative we deduce that
y\rightarrow x
.

C.2
Conditions for strict positivity of the cross-covariance criterion

Assume without loss of generality that the true causal direction is
x\rightarrow y
, and let us analyze the sign of the criterion
\mathrm{FC}
.

Given that
x\rightarrow y
, the true model is

{\bm{y}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}}

(91)

where
{\bm{B}}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{cov}(x^{1},y^{1}),\dots,\mathrm{cov}(x^{m},y^{m}))
, and
{\bm{e}}
is independent from
{\bm{x}}
. So, we have

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}{\bm{e}}^{\top}]={\bm{0}}\kern 5.0pt.

(92)

In the wrong direction, the residuals take the form

{\bm{d}}={\bm{x}}-{\bm{C}}{\bm{y}},\qquad{\bm{C}}={\bm{B}}\kern 5.0pt.

(93)

The cross-covariance between observations
{\bm{y}}
and residuals
{\bm{d}}
is then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{y}}{\bm{d}}^{\top}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{y}}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{B}}{\bm{y}})^{\top}\big]

(94)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[({\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}})({\bm{x}}-{\bm{B}}({\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}}))^{\top}\big]

(95)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2})\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{e}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2}))\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}{\bm{e}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{e}}{\bm{e}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}\big]

(96)

\displaystyle={\bm{B}}\;{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}\;({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2})-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}\;{\bm{B}}

(97)

where
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}\big]
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{e}}{\bm{e}}^{\top}\big]
.

From Eq.
92
and the non-negativity of the Frobenius norm, we see that
\mathrm{FC}
is always non-negative. Moreover, using Eq.
97
, we deduce that
\mathrm{FC}=0
if and only if

{\bm{B}}\;{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}\;({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2})-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}\;{\bm{B}}={\bm{0}}\kern 5.0pt.

(98)

Note that the fact that
l_{i}>0
, for all
i
, makes
{\bm{L}}
invertible. So, using the identity
{\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{2}={\bm{L}}^{2}
, and multiplying on the right by
{\bm{L}}^{-1}
, we obtain

{\bm{B}}\;{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}\;{\bm{L}}={\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}\;{\bm{B}}\;{\bm{L}}^{-1}

(99)

hence

{\bm{B}}\;{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}\;{\bm{L}}={\bm{L}}\;\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}\;{\bm{B}}

(100)

since
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}={\bm{L}}\;\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}\;{\bm{L}}
, and
{\bm{B}}
and
{\bm{L}}
commute.

This condition coincides exactly with Eq.
75
.
In particular, this shows that the likelihood-based criterion and the cross-covariance-based criterion share the same consistency assumptions.

Let

denote any two component indices such that
{\bm{x}}_{j}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{j^{\prime}}
.
Then, there exist two views
i
and
i^{\prime}

1.

\mathrm{corr}({\bm{x}}_{j}^{i},{\bm{x}}_{j^{\prime}}^{i})\neq 0\ \text{or}\ \mathrm{corr}({\bm{x}}_{j}^{i^{\prime}},{\bm{x}}_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}})\neq 0

3.

At least one of the four following inequalities is met:

where

denotes the correlation coefficient and
\mathrm{sign}
denotes the sign function.

These conditions can only be met for indices
i\neq i^{\prime}
, so they can be interpreted as a need for correlation and diversity across views. Because they may be hard to interpret at first glance, we next visualize in Figure
6
what these conditions mean for the causal graph.

D.2
An example for interpreting the SOS-based identifiability condition in Assumption
2

Consider one view and two variables denoted for notational simplicity
x
and
y
. We have

y^{1}=b^{1}x^{1}+e^{1}

(101)

and assume for simplicity that
x^{1}
and
y^{1}
have been standardized.

Now, suppose we observe two datasets from this one view, denote them by
(x^{1},y^{1})
and
({x^{1}}^{\prime},{y^{1}}^{\prime})
, which have identical distributions and are independent of each other.
Crucially, all this data is from one view.
However, we could artificially generate a new “view” by adding those two datasets together:

x^{2}=x^{1}+{x^{1}}^{\prime}

(102)

and likewise for
y^{2}
and
e^{2}
. Clearly the new data follows the SEM with the same
b
:

y^{2}=b^{1}x^{2}+e^{2}

(103)

since we are simply adding each of the terms separately in the two datasets.

But this “multi-view” data is degenerate. In fact, the correlation coefficients are

\text{corr}(x^{1},x^{2})=\frac{\text{cov}(x^{1},x^{1})+\text{cov}(x^{1},{x^{1}}^{\prime})}{\text{std}(x^{1})\text{std}(x^{2})}=\frac{\text{var}(x^{1})}{\sqrt{2}\text{std}(x^{1})\text{std}(x^{1})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\kern 5.0pt.

(104)

An exactly identical calculation applies for
\text{corr}(e^{1},e^{2})
which is also equal to
1/\sqrt{2}
.

Thus, we see that this case violates the simple identifiability condition in Assumption
2
. This is understandable since no new information is created when computing the variable in the second view, and the two views are thus degenerate.

D.3
Proof of Theorem
3

We proceed in three steps. (i) We show that the LiMVAM model always admits at least one root variable and that our LR and FC criteria recover one consistently. (ii) We prove a
recursive residuals
lemma: removing a recovered root and regressing on it preserves the model form and the causal ordering, which implies that the full (partial) ordering is consistently recoverable. (iii) Conditional on the recovered ordering, each view-specific strictly lower-triangular system can be estimated consistently, hence the adjacency matrices are identifiable.

Preliminaries

For clarity, we unpack the “shared ordering” assumption. This assumption is equivalent to the existence of a
main
DAG
\mathcal{G}
that is the union of view-specific DAGs
\{\mathcal{G}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
: an edge appears in
\mathcal{G}
if it appears in at least one
\mathcal{G}^{i}
. Root variables are therefore common candidates across views. Furthermore, in terms of vocabulary, we distinguish (a)
directed paths
(a sequence of edges with consistent direction) from (b)
direct edges
(one edge between adjacent variables). An
indirect path
is a directed path with at least two edges.

D.3.1
Recovering a root variable

Let observations follow LiMVAM in Eq.
3
. Since all views share an ordering, there exists a (possibly non-unique) permutation
{\bm{P}}
such that

{\bm{T}}^{i}={\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}^{i}{\bm{P}}\quad\text{is strictly lower triangular for all }i\in[\![1,m]\!]\kern 5.0pt.

(105)

Reordering the observations and disturbances with
{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{P}}{\bm{x}}^{i}
and
{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}={\bm{P}}{\bm{e}}^{i}
, the model is

{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{T}}^{i}{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}+{\bm{e}}^{\prime i},\qquad i\in[\![1,m]\!]\kern 5.0pt.

(106)

The first row of each
{\bm{T}}^{i}
is zero, so
x^{\prime i}_{1}
is exogenous; hence a root variable always exists.

To
find
a root, consider distinct indices
(j,k)
and define
M_{jk}
to be the criterion from Eq.
5
or Eq.
6
(with
M_{kj}=-M_{jk}
). Stack these into
{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
with zero diagonal, and analyze signs under Assumption
6
(which concerns only direct relations):

1.

Direct edge:

{\bm{x}}_{j}\to{\bm{x}}_{k}
or
{\bm{x}}_{k}\to{\bm{x}}_{j}
. Under the bivariate conditions derived from Assumption
6
,
M_{jk}>0>M_{kj}
when
{\bm{x}}_{j}\to{\bm{x}}_{k}
, and
M_{jk}<0<M_{kj}
when
{\bm{x}}_{k}\to{\bm{x}}_{j}
.

2.

Indirect path:

{\bm{x}}_{j}\to\dots\to{\bm{x}}_{k}
or
{\bm{x}}_{k}\to\dots\to{\bm{x}}_{j}
.
Consider the reduced form of the model in Eq.
4
,
{\bm{x}}^{i}={\bm{A}}^{i}{\bm{e}}^{i}
with
{\bm{A}}^{i}=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}^{i})^{-1}
. The entry
A^{i}_{kj}
is the
total causal effect
of
x_{j}^{i}
on
x_{k}^{i}
,
i.e.
the sum over all directed paths from
j
to
k
of the products of edge coefficients. Hence for each view
i
,

x_{k}^{i}=A^{i}_{kj}x_{j}^{i}+\epsilon^{i}

(107)

where
\epsilon^{i}
collects all terms not caused by
x_{j}^{i}
and is independent of
x_{j}^{i}
. Thus, the pair
(x_{j}^{i},x_{k}^{i})
behaves as if there were a direct edge with coefficient
A^{i}_{kj}
, and the sign analysis of Item 1 applies but with non-strict inequalities:
M_{jk}\geq 0\geq M_{kj}
when
{\bm{x}}_{j}\to\dots\to{\bm{x}}_{k}
(and conversely). If the bivariate conditions hold for
(j,k)
, the inequalities are strict; however, we do not require this here.

3.

No relation, no common ancestor:
then
{\bm{x}}_{j}
and
{\bm{x}}_{k}
depend on disjoint disturbance sets and are independent, hence
M_{jk}=M_{kj}=0
.

4.

No relation but with a common ancestor.

If
{\bm{x}}_{j}
and
{\bm{x}}_{k}
are not connected by any directed path but share at least one ancestor, then their pairwise criteria
M_{jk}
and
M_{kj}
may take mixed signs. However, since each of
{\bm{x}}_{j}
and
{\bm{x}}_{k}
has at least one parent, there exist variables
{\bm{x}}_{j^{\prime}}
and
{\bm{x}}_{k^{\prime}}
such that
M_{jj^{\prime}}<0
and
M_{kk^{\prime}}<0
. Thus, even if the signs of
M_{jk}
and
M_{kj}
are not determined, the
j
-th and
k
-th rows of
{\bm{M}}
necessarily contain negative entries.
For instance, with three variables,
{\bm{x}}_{1}\to{\bm{x}}_{2}
and
{\bm{x}}_{1}\to{\bm{x}}_{3}
makes
{\bm{x}}_{2}
and
{\bm{x}}_{3}
siblings: there is no directed path between them, but both rows
2
and
3
of
{\bm{M}}
contain negative entries due to their direct parent
{\bm{x}}_{1}
.

Consequently,
{\bm{x}}_{j}
is a root (no incoming edges)
if and only if
the
j
-th row of
{\bm{M}}
has only nonnegative entries. Hence a root exists and is consistently detectable. If multiple roots exist, we pick one at random.

D.3.2
Recovering the causal ordering

We now show the recursive step.

Lemma 9

(Residuals preserve LiMVAM and the ordering)

Let
j
be a root variable. Regress every other variable on
{\bm{x}}_{j}
(within each view) and remove
{\bm{x}}_{j}
. The residual vector still follows a LiMVAM model with the
same
ordering over the remaining variables.

Proof.

The proof is a direct adaptation of
Shimizu et al. [35, Lemma 2 and Corollary 1]
to the LiMVAM model: the original result is in the single-view setting with non-Gaussian disturbances.

Choose a permutation
{\bm{P}}
that places
{\bm{x}}_{j}
first for simplicity (since
{\bm{x}}_{j}
is a root variable, this permutation exists). In the permuted system we write

{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{T}}^{i}{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}+{\bm{e}}^{\prime i},\qquad{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{P}}{\bm{x}}^{i},\;\;{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}={\bm{P}}{\bm{e}}^{i}

(108)

where each
{\bm{T}}^{i}
is strictly lower triangular and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{1}={\bm{x}}_{j}
.
Equivalently, the model can be expressed in reduced form

{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{A}}^{i}{\bm{e}}^{\prime i},\qquad{\bm{A}}^{i}:=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{T}}^{i})^{-1}

(109)

where the matrix
{\bm{A}}^{i}
is lower triangular with unit diagonal.

The entry
A^{i}_{k1}
(
k\geq 2
) quantifies the dependence of
x^{\prime i}_{k}
on
e^{\prime i}_{1}
. Since
x^{\prime}_{1}=e^{\prime i}_{1}
is exogenous, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[x^{\prime i}_{k}x^{\prime i}_{1}\right]=A^{i}_{k1}\,\mathbb{E}\left[(x^{\prime i}_{1})^{2}\right]

(110)

so the least-squares regression coefficient of
x^{\prime i}_{k}
on
x^{\prime i}_{1}
equals
A^{i}_{k1}
.
Therefore, regressing each
x^{\prime i}_{k}
(
k\geq 2
) on
x^{\prime i}_{1}
yields residuals
{\bm{r}}^{i}_{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
such that

{\bm{r}}^{i}_{(1)}={\bm{x}}^{\prime i}_{2:p}-{\bm{A}}^{i}_{2:p,1}\,x^{\prime i}_{1}={\bm{A}}^{i}_{2:p,:}{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}-{\bm{A}}^{i}_{2:p,1}e^{\prime i}_{1}={\bm{A}}^{i}_{2:p,2:p}\,{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}_{2:p}\kern 5.0pt.

(111)

Here
{\bm{A}}^{i}_{2:p,2:p}
is again lower triangular with unit diagonal, and
{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}_{2:p}
has mutually independent components. Hence
{\bm{r}}^{i}_{(1)}
follows a LiMVAM model on
p-1
variables. Moreover, since
{\bm{A}}^{i}_{2:p,2:p}
is precisely the submatrix of
{\bm{A}}^{i}
obtained by deleting the first row and column, the relative structure among the remaining variables is unchanged. In other words, the causal ordering of variables
2,\dots,p
is preserved.
∎

Applying Lemma
9
recursively — find a root via
{\bm{M}}
(under Assumption
6
), regress out its effect, remove it, and repeat — recovers the full (partial) causal ordering consistently.

D.3.3
Recovering the adjacency matrices

Sections
D.3.1
and
D.3.2
showed that, under Assumption
6
, the algorithms PairwiseLiMVAM and DirectLiMVAM consistently recover the causal ordering. Equivalently, they recover a permutation matrix
{\bm{P}}
that makes the
{\bm{T}}^{i}={\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}^{i}{\bm{P}}
strictly lower triangular. So, we can reorder the variables as follows:

{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{T}}^{i}{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}+{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}\;,\qquad i\in[\![1,m]\!]\kern 5.0pt.

(112)

Fix
j\in[\![1,p]\!]
. We have

x^{\prime i}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}T^{i}_{jk}x^{\prime i}_{k}+e^{\prime i}_{j}\;,\qquad i\in[\![1,m]\!]\kern 5.0pt.

(113)

Because
e^{\prime i}_{j}
is independent of
(x^{\prime i}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime i}_{j-1})
, the regressors are exogenous. This is a system of linear regressions across views with potentially cross-view–correlated errors. Feasible GLS is consistent in this setting: once a consistent estimate of the cross-view error covariance is obtained, the GLS estimator converges to the true coefficients
[
13
]
. Therefore,
\{T^{i}_{jk}\}_{k<j}
are consistently estimated for every
(i,j)
, hence each
{\bm{T}}^{i}
is consistently recovered. Finally,
{\bm{B}}^{i}={\bm{P}}{\bm{T}}^{i}{\bm{P}}^{\top}
, so the view-specific adjacency matrices are identifiable. This concludes the proof.

Appendix E
SOS-based Algorithms for LiMVAM

E.1
Consistent estimator of the likelihood-based criterion

Assume we have observed
n
independent samples, concatenated as
{\bm{X}}=[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}]
and
{\bm{Y}}=[{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{n}]
, with sample index
j
.

In practice, the four covariance matrices in Eq.
45
can be estimated by consistent estimators from the samples in
{\bm{X}}
and
{\bm{Y}}
.
Since
b_{i}=c_{i}=\mathrm{cov}(x_{i},y_{i})
for all
i
, we define

\forall i:\qquad\hat{b}_{i}:=\hat{c}_{i}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{ij}\,y_{ij}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{cov}(x_{i},y_{i})=b_{i}=c_{i}

(114)

so that
\hat{\bm{b}}:=(\hat{b}_{1},\dots,\hat{b}_{m})^{\top}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\bm{b}
and
\hat{{\bm{c}}}:=(\hat{c}_{1},\dots,\hat{c}_{m})^{\top}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}{\bm{c}}
.
Using these vectors, we define the empirical covariances of the residuals

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\bm{y}}_{j}-\hat{\bm{b}}\odot{\bm{x}}_{j})({\bm{y}}_{j}-\hat{\bm{b}}\odot{\bm{x}}_{j})^{\top}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}

(115)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{d}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\bm{x}}_{j}-\hat{\bm{b}}\odot{\bm{y}}_{j})({\bm{x}}_{j}-\hat{\bm{b}}\odot{\bm{y}}_{j})^{\top}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d}

(116)

and we also define the empirical covariances of the observations

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{x}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{j}{\bm{x}}_{j}^{\top}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}

(117)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{y}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bm{y}}_{j}{\bm{y}}_{j}^{\top}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{y}\kern 5.0pt.

(118)

We can then evaluate the criterion in these estimators.

\widehat{\mathrm{LR}}:=\mathcal{L}(\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{x},\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e};x\rightarrow y)-\mathcal{L}(\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{y},\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{d};y\rightarrow x)=-\log|\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{x}|-\log|\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e}|+\log|\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{y}|+\log|\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{d}|

(119)

and it follows that this empirical criterion is a consistent estimator of the true criterion:

\mathcal{L}(\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{x},\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{e};x\rightarrow y)-\mathcal{L}(\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{y},\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{d};y\rightarrow x)\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{L}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{x},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{e};x\rightarrow y)-\mathcal{L}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{y},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{d};y\rightarrow x)=:\mathrm{LR}\kern 5.0pt.

(120)

E.2
Consistent estimator of the cross-covariance-based criterion

Assume we have observed
n
independent samples, concatenated as
{\bm{X}}=[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}]
and
{\bm{Y}}=[{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{n}]
, with sample index
j
.

In practice, the matrices
\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{e}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}\big]
and
\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{d}}{\bm{y}}^{\top}\big]
are estimated from the data.
Performing univarite least-squares regression of
{\bm{y}}
on
{\bm{x}}
(resp.
{\bm{x}}
on
{\bm{y}}
) yields the residual matrices
\widehat{{\bm{E}}}=[\widehat{{\bm{e}}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{{\bm{e}}}_{n}]
(resp.
\widehat{{\bm{D}}}=[\widehat{{\bm{d}}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{{\bm{d}}}_{n}]
).
The corresponding cross-covariance estimators are

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widehat{{\bm{e}}}_{j}{\bm{x}}_{j}^{\top}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{e}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}\big]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widehat{{\bm{d}}}_{j}{\bm{y}}_{j}^{\top}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{d}}{\bm{y}}^{\top}\big]\kern 5.0pt.

(121)

Consequently, the empirical criterion

\widehat{\mathrm{FC}}:=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widehat{{\bm{d}}}_{j}{\bm{y}}_{j}^{\top}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widehat{{\bm{e}}}_{j}{\bm{x}}_{j}^{\top}\right\|_{F}

(122)

is a consistent estimator of the population criterion:

\widehat{\mathrm{FC}}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\left\|\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{d}}{\bm{y}}^{\top}\big]\right\|_{F}-\left\|\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{e}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}\big]\right\|_{F}=:\mathrm{FC}\kern 5.0pt.

(123)

E.4
How to find the root variable

Assume we have a scalar criterion of the causal direction between two variables
{\bm{x}}_{j}=(x_{j}^{1},\dots,x_{j}^{m})^{\top}
and
{\bm{x}}_{k}=(x_{k}^{1},\dots,x_{k}^{m})^{\top}
, such that the criterion is positive if
{\bm{x}}_{j}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}_{k}
and negative in the opposite direction. Let
M_{jk}
denote the criterion obtained for the pair
(j,k)
, and collect all pairwise criteria in a matrix
{\bm{M}}
with a zero diagonal.
We now have a matrix
{\bm{M}}
whose entries determine the causal direction by their sign:
M_{ij}>0
indicates
{\bm{x}}_{i}
causes
{\bm{x}}_{j}
, and
M_{ij}<0
indicates that
{\bm{x}}_{j}
causes
{\bm{x}}_{i}
.
In this setting,
{\bm{x}}_{j}
is a root variable if and only if the
j
-th row of
{\bm{M}}
contains only non-negative entries.
We follow
Hyvärinen and Smith [17, Section 3.2.2]
to derive a principled way for finding the root variable given
{\bm{M}}
. We next recall their argument.

Suppose that the entries in
{\bm{M}}
follow a normal distribution, so that
M_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{ij},\sigma^{2})
where
\sigma^{2}
models the estimation error due to finite samples (and is supposed to be constant for simplicity). Then, the log-likelihood of
{\bm{x}}_{i}
being the root variable is

\displaystyle\log\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{P}(\mu_{ij}>0|M_{ij})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{\mu_{ij}-M_{ij}}{\sigma}>\frac{-M_{ij}}{\sigma}\Big|M_{ij}\bigg)

(129)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log\Phi\bigg(\frac{M_{ij}}{\sigma}\bigg)\approx-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\min(0,M_{ij})^{2}

(130)

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of a standardized Gaussian, whose log we then approximated. We therefore obtain the index of the root variable using

\displaystyle\arg\min_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\min(0,M_{ij})^{2}\kern 5.0pt.

(131)

E.5
One-step Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS)

The FGLS procedure begins with an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression in each view to obtain residuals
(e^{\prime 1}_{j},\dots,e^{\prime m}_{j})
, which are then used to estimate the cross-view covariance of the disturbances. In a second step, the OLS coefficients are adjusted via Generalized Least Squares using this estimated covariance. In the population limit, this estimator coincides with OLS but is asymptotically more efficient whenever disturbances are correlated across views, as is the case here.

The estimation proceeds as follows.
Assume we have observed
n
independent samples. Here, we consider the model

{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{T}}^{i}{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}+{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}\;,\qquad i\in[\![1,m]\!]

(132)

where
{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}
and
{\bm{e}}^{\prime i}
are the reordered observations and disturbances, respectively, and
{\bm{T}}^{i}
are strictly lower triangular matrices. By construction, each variable
x^{\prime i}_{j}
only depends on its predecessors
{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}_{<j}:=(x^{\prime i}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime i}_{j-1})
.

Fix a variable index
j\in[\![1,p]\!]
. In view
i
, the structural equation reads

x^{\prime i}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}T^{i}_{jk}\,x^{\prime i}_{k}+e^{\prime i}_{j}\kern 5.0pt.

(133)

Stacking all views, we obtain the block regression model

{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}={\bm{X}}_{<j}\,{\bm{T}}_{<j}+{\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{j}

(134)

where

•

{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}=\big(x^{\prime 1}_{j},\dots,x^{\prime m}_{j}\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
stacks all samples across the
m
views,

•

{\bm{X}}_{<j}=\mathrm{diag}\big({\bm{x}}^{\prime 1}_{<j},\dots,{\bm{x}}^{\prime m}_{<j}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times m(j-1)}
is block-diagonal and contains the predecessors,

•

{\bm{T}}_{<j}=\big({\bm{T}}^{1}_{j,<j},\dots,{\bm{T}}^{m}_{j,<j}\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m(j-1)}
collects the coefficients,

•

{\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{j}=\big(e^{\prime 1}_{j},\dots,e^{\prime m}_{j}\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
are disturbances.

The disturbance vector
{\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{j}
has covariance

\Omega_{j}:=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{j}({\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{j})^{\top}\big]={\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{e}}_{j}}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}

(135)

where
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{e}}_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
captures cross-view covariances for component
j
.

The one-step FGLS procedure is:

1.

OLS step:

\widehat{{\bm{T}}}_{<j}^{\text{OLS}}=\big({\bm{X}}_{<j}^{\top}\,{\bm{X}}_{<j}\big)^{-1}{\bm{X}}_{<j}^{\top}\,{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}

(136)

yielding residuals
\hat{\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{j}={\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}-{\bm{X}}_{<j}\,\widehat{{\bm{T}}}_{<j}^{\text{OLS}}
.

2.

Covariance estimation:

\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{e}}_{j}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\hat{\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{j,k}(\hat{\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{j,k})^{\top}

(137)

where
k
denotes the sample index.

3.

FGLS update:

\widehat{{\bm{T}}}_{<j}^{\text{FGLS}}=\big({\bm{X}}_{<j}^{\top}\,\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{e}}_{j}}^{-1}\,{\bm{X}}_{<j}\big)^{-1}{\bm{X}}_{<j}^{\top}\,\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{e}}_{j}}^{-1}\,{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}\kern 5.0pt.

(138)

This estimator reduces to OLS if
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{e}}_{j}}
is diagonal (no cross-view correlation). More generally, it achieves asymptotic efficiency by exploiting cross-view correlations.

E.6
Computational complexity

The computational complexity of Algorithm
1
can be broken down across the different steps.

In Step 1, the preprocessing is in
O(m\cdot p\cdot n)
.

In Step 2.a., we solve
O(p^{2})
LS regression problems, each of which has complexity
O(m\cdot n)
.

In Step 2.b., we compute
O(p^{2})
entries of a matrix. Each entry is computed either using the cross-covariance-based criterion in Algorithm
3
which is in
O(m^{2}\cdot n)
, or using the likelihood-based criterion in Algorithm
2
which is in
O(m^{2}\cdot n+m^{3})
due to
computing
log determinants.
So the total complexity of this step is
O(m^{2}\cdot p^{2}\cdot n)
or
O(m^{2}\cdot p^{2}\cdot n+m^{3}\cdot p^{2})
.

In Step 2.c., we make
O(p^{2})
operations.

In Step 2.d., we simplify redefine a matrix.

These steps 2.a-d., are repeated
O(p)
times, until all variables are removed, so this yields a complexity in either
O(m^{2}\cdot p^{3}\cdot n)
or
O(m^{2}\cdot p^{3}\cdot n+m^{3}\cdot p^{3})
.

In Step 2.e., we reorder a matrix, which costs
O(m\cdot p\cdot n)
operations.

In Step 3, we run the one-step Feasible GLS procedure, which is in
O(m^{3}\cdot p^{3}\cdot n)
. It scales cubically in the
m
views and
p
dimensions as it involves matrix multiplications and inversions. It then does
m
matrix multiplications. Each of them involves permutation matrices which has a quadratic cost
O(p^{2})
. So the cost of this step
is dominated by
O(m^{3}\cdot p^{3}\cdot n)
.

The total computational complexity of the algorithm is thus
O(m^{3}\cdot p^{3}\cdot n)
.

Identifiability of LiMVAM with shared disturbances

{\bm{x}}^{i}={\bm{B}}^{i}{\bm{x}}^{i}+{\bm{D}}^{i}{\bm{s}}+{\bm{n}}^{i}\;,\qquad i\in[\![1,m]\!]

Proof of Theorem

In particular, Assumption

\Theta=({\bm{\Sigma}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{\Sigma}}^{m},{\bm{P}},{\bm{T}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{T}}^{m})

G.1
ICA-based Algorithm

Algorithm 4

ICA-LiMVAM

1.

Estimate the adjacency matrices

(a)

Estimate the unmixing matrices

and the noise variance matrices

The algorithm returns an estimate of the true unmixing matrix

H.1.4
Testing Assumptions
1
and
2

To assess whether Assumption
2
(or, in the bivariate setting, Assumpion
1
) holds in practice, we developed a simple score taking values between 0 and 1. A score close to 0 indicates that the assumption is unlikely to hold, whereas a score close to 1 suggests that it is satisfied.

In more detail, once one of our algorithms has recovered the permutation
{\bm{P}}
, variables can be reordered as
{\bm{x}}^{\prime i}={\bm{P}}{\bm{x}}^{i}
, such that each variable
x^{\prime i}_{j}
depends only on its predecessors
x^{\prime i}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime i}_{j-1}
. We then decompose the problem into a collection of bivariate subproblems: for each cause-and-effect pair
({\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j-1},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j})
, we quantify to what extent Assumption
1
is satisfied for the direction
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j-1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}
. Specifically:

1.

For each view
i
, we measure cross-variable correlation by computing the scalar
\mathrm{corr}(x_{j-1}^{i},x_{j}^{i})
. Intuitively, this quantity is represented by one of the two vertical arrows in Figure
2
. These scalars are stored in a length-
m
vector, from which we compute: a) the
\ell_{2}
-norm, to quantify the presence of cross-variable correlation, and b) the standard deviation, to quantify the diversity of these correlations.

2.

For each pair of views
(i,i^{\prime})
, we regress
x^{\prime i}_{j}
on
x^{\prime i}_{j-1}
to get residuals
e^{\prime i}_{j}
, and similarly for view
i^{\prime}
. We then measure cross-view correlation by computing
|\mathrm{corr}(x_{j-1}^{i},x_{j-1}^{i^{\prime}})|
and
|\mathrm{corr}(e_{j}^{i},e_{j}^{i^{\prime}})|
. Intuitively, these quantities are represented by the horizontal arrows in Figure
2
. The resulting scalars are stored in two different
m\times m
matrices. We then compute: a) their
\ell_{2}
-norms, to quantify the presence of cross-view correlations, and b) the standard deviation (or the
\ell_{2}
-norm) of their difference, to quantify the diversity of these correlations.

3.

Finally, these quantities (
\ell_{2}
-norms and standard deviations) are aggregated (multiple aggregation schemes can be considered) and standardized to produce a final score between 0 and 1.

We emphasize that this score is only heuristic and could likely be improved in several ways. First, it only considers consecutive pairs of variables
({\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j-1},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j})
rather than comparing
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}
to all of its predecessors. Second, it relies on Assumption
2
, which itself is a simplification of Assumption
6
. Finally, several design choices remain heuristic. Nevertheless, the score appears informative in the following simulation study.

H.2.1
Details on the preprocessing of MEG data

The MEG data measures participants’ responses to auditory and visual stimuli. The auditory stimuli were binaural pure tones.
The visual stimuli were checkerboards presented both to the left and right of a central fixation for 34-ms duration.
This task leads to strong signal power modulations during the motor preparation and motor execution.

The original MEG data were acquired with 306 sensors, recorded at 1000 Hz, and band-pass filtered between 0.03 and 330 Hz.
All MEG processing was done using the MNE-Python library
[
11
,
12
]
and we largely followed the pre-processing steps used in
Power et al. [29]
.
We applied a Maxwell filter
[
40
]
to improve data quality and a band-pass filter between 8 and 27 Hz to focus on power effects spread over the alpha and beta bands of the brain.
This range of waves is supposed to be particularly active in sensorimotor tasks, especially during movement preparation and execution, and it typically shows a characteristic suppression (event-related desynchronization) during movement, followed by a rebound (event-related synchronization) after movement cessation, which is thought to reflect sensorimotor processing and inhibitory mechanisms.
In particular, the suppression and rebound are reflected in the energies of the signals, not raw signals.

The data were then parsed into trials synchronized to each button press, with a duration of 4.5 s, including a 1.5 s pre-movement interval.
The 4.5 s window length was selected to ensure a sufficient post-movement interval to capture the entire beta rebound response.
Trials were excluded if the button press occurred more than 1 s after the audiovisual cue (indicating poor task performance) or if another button press occurred within the time window.
Then, a baseline correction was applied using the pre-movement interval
(-1.5\,\text{s},-1\,\text{s})
. The procedure led to about 60 trials per participant on average.

Figure 14
:
Example of time series obtained after the preprocessing. We averaged the data across subjects and trials, resulting in 10 time series, one for each brain region.

Next, we performed a cortical projection.
First, each participant’s MRI (additionally given in the Cam-CAN database) was segmented using FreeSurfer
[
8
]
.
The segmentation provided a digitization of the cortical surface for source estimation, a transformation to the average brain (
i.e.
fsaverage) for spatial normalization and group statistics, and a boundary element model of the head to provide more accurate calculation of the forward solution.
The inverse solution, based on the MNE method
[
14
]
, allowed to consider cortical region activations for further analysis.

We used the cortical parcellation from
[
18
]
to divide the cortical mantel (both hemispheres) into 448 distinct regions and summarized each region by an averaged time course.
Then, we selected 10 of the 448 regions based on their known importance in sensorimotor tasks.
Specifically, we picked for each hemisphere three regions in the motor cortex (two parcels in the “postcentral” and one in the “precentral”; visible in blue in Fig.
5(a)
), one region in the auditory cortex (“superiortemporal” parcel; highlighted in pink), and one region in the visual cortex (“pericalcarine” parcel; not visible in the figure).
Note again that the task for the participant is active as right index button presses are triggered by audiovisual stimulations.

For each participant, we thus extracted one time series for each of the 10 regions and fixed the number of trials to 40.
Participants with less than 40 trials were discarded and extra trials were averaged.
Participants for whom some of the parcels did not have any vertex in the source space were also discarded, resulting in a total of 98 available participants.
We performed a Z-score normalization of the time series to correct the depth bias and, importantly, computed the Hilbert
envelope
of the signals to study modulations in cortical source power.
Finally, the signals were centered and downsampled from 1000 Hz to 10 Hz due to the slowly changing nature of the envelopes (energies).
The final dataset consisted of 98 subjects, 10 brain regions, and 1760 time points.

Figure
14
shows the typical time series obtained after preprocessing. For visualization, we averaged the time courses across participants and trials. As expected, we observe a prominent peak in the “superiortemporal” auditory regions shortly before the button press (
t=0
s), reflecting the stimulus onset. In the motor regions, a characteristic rebound pattern emerges: the signals decrease around the time of the button press, consistent with event-related desynchronization of beta rhythms, and subsequently increase after approximately 0.3 seconds, indicating beta resynchronization. This figure also labels the ten cortical parcels selected for analysis.

Appendix I
Distinction between multi-view and multi-domain frameworks

Several frameworks study causal discovery from multiple related datasets that share the same causal structure. While
multi-view
methods (like ours) model
correlated
views arising from a joint (non-factorial) distribution,
multi-domain
methods treat datasets as
independent
domains drawn from related but distinct distributions. Consequently, multi-domain methods cannot leverage cross-view correlations and instead rely exclusively on distributional shifts across domains.

Multi-environment
methods are a special case of multi-domain methods, where distributional shifts arise from
interventions
(whether designed by the practitioner or arising from uncontrolled environment differences) on the causal mechanisms. In the following, we review some multi-domain/multi-environment methods that are related to our work.

Ghassami et al. [10]
propose a multi-domain method that, like ours, studies linear causal discovery from multiple datasets that share the same underlying DAG in the causally sufficient setting and without a non-Gaussianity assumption. However, they assume that causal mechanisms
\mathbb{P}(x^{i}_{j}|\mathbf{PA}^{i}_{j})
are
independent
(both within and across domains), where
\mathbf{PA}^{i}_{j}
denotes the parents of
x^{i}_{j}
. Our approach does not require such an independence assumption. For identifiability of the true DAG, their method further assumes that noise variances or causal weights vary across domains, and one of their two criteria additionally requires these changes to be sparse. In our paper, we show that such changes in noise variances are sufficient for identifiability (see Assumption
6
), but not necessary when the views exhibit diverse correlations.

Adams et al. [1]
study multi-domain identifiability in the more complex setting of
latent confounders
. Their main contributions are necessary and sufficient conditions (“bottleneck” and “strong non-redundancies”) that are specific to the confounded setting and vanish in our causally sufficient case. In particular, in this causally sufficient case, their conditions reduce to assuming heterogeneous variances (see their Theorem 1), which is precisely one of the sufficient conditions in our Assumption
6
. Moreover, they require the causal weights
\mathbf{B}^{i}
to
remain constant
across domains, which is a strong assumption we do not make. Thus, in the causally sufficient case, our method encompasses theirs and goes much further by leveraging correlations (across views and within a view), while allowing causal weights to differ. They suggested, in fact, that their assumptions were too strong in Section 3: “Note that this theorem gives sufficient conditions; our empirical results suggest that they are not necessary.”

Peters et al. [26]
propose a multi-environment method that aims to identify the parents of
a single target variable
rather than recovering the full graph, while assuming purely
Gaussian
distributions. Their approach relies on
invariance
of the target’s causal mechanism across environments, which implicitly requires knowing where interventions occurred (and that the target was not intervened upon), an assumption that is often unrealistic in practice.

Similarly,
Perry et al. [25]
propose another multi-environment method that leverages invariance and can be more easily applied to causal discovery of the whole structure. However, their method considers the more complex case of
nonlinear
relationships.
Like
Ghassami et al. [10]
, they rely on changes in
\mathbb{P}(x^{i}_{j}|\mathbf{PA}^{i}_{j})
across environments, but instead of measuring independence between mechanisms, they count how often these mechanisms change and select the DAG that minimizes this number. Their identifiability results require sparsity in the number of changes; in particular, not all causal mechanisms are allowed to differ across environments. In contrast, our theory accommodates arbitrarily many changes in
\mathbf{B}^{i}
and in the distribution of
\mathbf{e}^{i}
; moreover, these changes are not required for identifiability if cross-view correlations are diverse.

We finally briefly mention two further works that are related to the multi-domain setting.
A general framework, allowing for various kinds of interventions, was proposed by
Mooij et al. [22]
, but this was not claimed to improve identifiability.
Sturma et al. [39]
proposed a related “multi-context” approach with the goal of estimating a representation as well as in causal representation learning (CRL).

In neuroimaging, this distinction between the two frameworks makes multi-view methods particularly well-suited to experiments centered around stimulus onsets (”phase-locked”, as in our MEG and fMRI experiments), while multi-domain methods are more naturally aligned with resting-state experiments (as in the fMRI experiment of
Ghassami et al. [10]
).
