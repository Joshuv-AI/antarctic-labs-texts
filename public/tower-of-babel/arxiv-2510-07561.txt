Correlation Lengths for Stochastic Matrix Product States
Lubashan Pathirana, Albert H Werner

Abstract
We introduce a general model of stochastically generated matrix product states (MPS) in which the local tensors share a common distribution and form a strictly stationary sequence, without requiring spatial independence. Under natural conditions on the associated transfer operators, we prove the existence of thermodynamic limits of expectations of local observables and establish almost-sure exponential decay of two-point correlations. In the homogeneous (random translation-invariant) case, for any error tolerance in probability, the two-point function decays exponentially in the distance between the two sites, with a deterministic rate. In the i.i.d. case, the exponential decay still holds with a deterministic rate, with the probability approaching one exponentially fast in the distance. For strictly stationary ensembles with decaying spatial dependence, the correlation decay quantitatively reflects the mixing profile: ρ \rho –mixing yields polynomial bounds with high probability, while stretched-exponential (resp. exponential) decay in ρ \rho (resp. β \beta ) yields stretched-exponential (resp. exponential) decay of the two-point function, again with correspondingly strong high-probability guarantees. Altogether, the framework unifies and extends recent progress on stationary ergodic and Gaussian translation-invariant ensembles, providing a transfer-operator route to typical correlation decay in random MPS.

Introduction

1.1
Matrix Product States

Matrix product states (MPS) play a central role in quantum many-body theory and quantum information. They provide efficient representations of not-too-strongly entangled quantum states and form the basis of powerful numerical methods, such as the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG)
[
Whi92
,
Sch05
]
and its descendants. MPS and tensor-network methods have become key tools for understanding quantum many-body systems, for instance, in the many-body localized phase where constrained entanglement growth preserves computational tractability
[
Aba+19
,
Orú19
]
. MPS and their multidimensional generalization—tensor networks (TN)—also appear prominently in variational quantum algorithms (VQA)
[
Hag+22
,
Rud+23
,
WFU24
]
. A key structural feature of MPS is exponential clustering of correlations in the thermodynamic limit, a property that underlies their algorithmic usefulness. For
deterministic, translation–invariant
MPS, exponential clustering is well understood via the spectral theory of transfer operators
[
Orú14
,
Has07
]
.

Random MPS arise naturally in the study of noisy quantum circuits, statistical ensembles of tensor networks, and models of disordered quantum matter such as many-body localized phases. The behavior of
random
MPS has only recently begun to be systematically explored
[
Gar+10
,
Haf+21
,
Aba+19
,
MS22
,
LP21
,
Che+24
]
. Understanding their typical correlation properties is crucial for bridging quantum information theory with statistical mechanics in disordered settings. Moreover, randomized MPS have proved useful for analyzing the presence (and absence) of barren plateaus in quantum machine learning
[
Gar+22
,
Liu+22
]
.

Motivated by these developments, and building on recent advances in the mathematical theory of tensor networks
[
Cir+21
,
BC17
,
Orú14
,
Orú19
]
, we introduce and analyze a general model of
stochastically generated matrix product states
. In this model the local order-three tensors
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{D})^{\otimes 2}
share a common marginal law
\lambda
but may be arbitrarily correlated across sites. This framework unifies random translation-invariant, i.i.d., and more general correlated ensembles, extending prior results for ergodic sequences of MPS
[

]
and Gaussian homogeneous (translation-invariant) MPS
[

]
, and complementing studies of Haar-random states
[

]
. Furthermore, our analysis of transfer operators directly interfaces with open-system dynamics realized by repeated interactions with a stochastic environment
[

,

,
BJM08
,
BJM10
,

The Random Model

Fix integers
d,D\geq 2
and consider the space of rank–three local tensors with physical index

\mathbb{C}^{d}\,\otimes\,(\mathbb{C}^{D})^{\otimes 2}.

A

Definition 1.1

(Stochastically Generated Local Tensors)
.

We say that

\ten

Remark 1.1

We use the term “stochastically generated” because strict stationarity precisely means that there exists a measure–preserving dynamical system

\mathcal{A}_{n}=\mathcal{S}\circ\theta^{n}

Given a realization

(1.1)

It is not a priori assumed that

Strict stationarity encompasses, as extreme instances, the following two regimes:
At one extreme,
maximal
correlation is realized by placing the
same
random tensor at every site, yielding a

At the other extreme, the tensors are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), producing the least–correlated ensemble (

Between these poles lie intermediate families in which spatial stochastic correlations decay with the distance separating sites (e.g.

\beta

Thus, the present framework simultaneously covers random translation–invariant and i.i.d. models, as well as ensembles with progressively weaker correlations as the separation increases.
Furthermore, by taking the marginal to be a Dirac mass, this setting also allows one to study completely deterministic TI-MPS.

Transfer Maps and Standing Assumptions (informal)

To each site
n
, we associate the completely positive (not necessarily trace–preserving) transfer superoperator

\phi^{\omega}_{n}:\mathbb{M}_{D}\to\mathbb{M}_{D},\qquad\phi^{\omega}_{n}(X)=\sum_{p=1}^{d}A^{(n),\omega}_{p}\,X\,(A^{(n),\omega}_{p})^{\dagger},

and we write
\Phi^{\omega}_{[a,b]}:=\phi^{\omega}_{b}\circ\cdots\circ\phi^{\omega}_{a}
for forward compositions. The analysis proceeds under the following mild structural assumptions:

(A1)

Neither the transfer maps nor their adjoints annihilate any quantum state.

(A2)

Finite forward compositions of transfer operators become positivity improving after some (random but a.s. finite) depth.

These assumptions are directly implied by the assumptions used in
[
MS21
]
.
In the homogeneous Gaussian models of
[

]
, these conditions are satisfied implicitly; a self–contained verification is provided in our
Appendix

B

1.3
Norm Conventions and Matrix Representations

Throughout, let
\mathbb{M}_{D}
denote the space of all
D\times D
complex matrices.
We equip
\mathbb{M}_{D}
with the Schatten–
p
norms for
p\in[1,\infty]
.
For
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\rho\in\mathbb{M}_{D}
,

\norm{\rho}_{p}\;=\;\big(\operatorname{tr}\left[|\rho|^{\,p}\right]\big)^{1/p}\qquad\text{with}\qquad|\rho|:=\sqrt{\rho^{\dagger}\rho},

and the Schatten–
\infty
norm (spectral norm) is

\norm{\rho}_{\infty}\;=\;\sup\{\|\rho u\|_{2}:u\in\mathbb{C}^{D},\ \|u\|_{2}=1\}.

We use
\langle\,\cdot\,|\,\cdot\,\rangle
for the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product on
\mathbb{M}_{D}
,

\langle A|B\rangle\;=\;\operatorname{tr}\left[A^{\dagger}B\right]\qquad(A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{D}).

(1.2)

Let

\states\;:=\;\{\rho\in\mathbb{M}_{D}:\ \rho\geq 0,\ \operatorname{tr}\left[\rho\right]=1\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\states^{\mathrm{o}}\;:=\;\{\rho\in\states:\ \rho>0\},

denote, respectively, the set of
D
–dimensional density matrices and the subset of strictly positive definite density matrices.
We write
\partial\states:=\states\setminus\states^{\mathrm{o}}
for the boundary (rank–deficient states).
For
\rho\in\states^{\mathrm{o}}
let
\eta(\rho)
denote its smallest eigenvalue.

Let
\mapspace
denote the space of linear maps
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{D}\to\mathbb{M}_{D}
.
We endow
\mapspace
with the induced operator norm (the
p\!\to\!p
norm):

\norm{\phi}_{p\to p}\;=\;\sup\{\norm{\phi(\rho)}_{p}:\rho\in\mathbb{M}_{D},\ \norm{\rho}_{p}\leq 1\}\qquad(1\leq p\leq\infty).

Every
\phi\in\mapspace
admits a unique
D^{2}\times D^{2}
matrix representation
K(\phi)
via the standard vectorization map
\text{vec}(\,\cdot\,):\mathbb{M}_{D}\to\mathbb{C}^{D^{2}}
:

\text{vec}(\phi(\rho))\;=\;K(\phi)\,\text{vec}(\rho)\qquad(\rho\in\mathbb{M}_{D}).

The assignment
\phi\mapsto K(\phi)
is a linear isomorphism
\mapspace\to\mathbb{M}_{D^{2}}
, often called the
natural (Liouville) representation
.
When the context is clear, we do not distinguish notationally between a superoperator and its Liouville matrix.

We define the
superoperator trace
by

\Tr{\phi}\;=\;\operatorname{tr}\left[\big(K(\phi)\right],

where
\operatorname{tr}\left[\,\cdot\,\right]
on the right is the usual trace on
\mathbb{M}_{D^{2}}
.
To avoid ambiguity, we reserve
\Tr{\,\cdot\,}
for the trace of a superoperator and
\operatorname{tr}\left[\,\cdot\,\right]
for the trace on
\mathbb{M}_{D}
.
Equivalently, for any orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}
of
\mathbb{C}^{D}
,

\Tr{\phi}:=\operatorname{tr}\left[K(\phi)\right]=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{tr}\left[\ket{e_j}\bra{e_i}\phi(\ket{e_i}\bra{e_j})\right].

In particular, if

\phi(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}A_{i}\,\rho\,B_{j}^{T},

where
(\,\cdot\,)^{T}
denotes matrix transpose, then, with the convention
\text{vec}(A\rho B^{T})=(B\otimes A)\text{vec}(\rho)
,

K(\phi)\;=\;\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}B_{j}\otimes A_{i},\qquad\Tr{\phi}\;=\;\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\operatorname{tr}\left[A_{i}\right]\,\operatorname{tr}\left[B_{j}\right].

Given
\phi\in\mapspace
, its adjoint
\phi^{\dagger}\in\mapspace
(with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product) is the unique map satisfying

\langle\phi(A)|B\rangle\;=\;\langle A|\phi^{\dagger}(B)\rangle\qquad\text{for all }A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{D},

(1.3)

and the Liouville representation respects adjoints:

K(\phi^{\dagger})\;=\;K(\phi)^{\dagger}.

For background on matrix norms and operator theory, see
[
HJ85
,
Bha97
]
; for superoperators, see
[
Wat18
]
.

Matrix Product State (MPS)

Transfer Operators

For each local tensor
\mathcal{A}_{n}=(A^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,A^{(n)}_{d})\in\ten
, the

at site

is the linear map

\phi_{n}^{\omega}:\mathbb{M}_{D}\to\mathbb{M}_{D},\qquad\phi_{n}^{\omega}(X)\;=\;\sum_{p=1}^{d}A^{(n)}_{p}\,X\,(A^{(n)}_{p})^{\dagger},

(1.4)

which is completely positive but not necessarily trace-preserving.
With the Liouville convention from §
1.3
, the

K(\phi_{n}^{\omega})\;=\;\sum_{p=1}^{d}\overline{A^{(n)}_{p}}\,\otimes\,A^{(n)}_{p}.

(1.5)

Observable-dependent Transfer Operators

Let
\mathbb{O}_{n}
be a local observable supported at site

\mathcal{O}_{n}:\mathbb{M}_{D}\to\mathbb{M}_{D},\qquad\mathcal{O}_{n}(X)\;=\;\sum_{p,q=1}^{d}(\mathbb{O}_{n})_{p,q}\,A^{(n)}_{p}\,X\,(A^{(n)}_{q})^{\dagger},

K(\mathcal{O}_{n})\;=\;\sum_{p,q=1}^{d}(\mathbb{O}_{n})_{p,q}\,\overline{A^{(n)}_{q}}\,\otimes\,A^{(n)}_{p}.

In particular, setting

1.5
Assumptions

As established above, normalized expectation values of local observables in random matrix product states (MPS) can be expressed in terms of the associated transfer superoperators.
We now record the standing assumptions under which our main analytical results are derived.
First, we need the following notational convention.

Distributional Stationarity

We also work on model where
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
have a common marginal law on
\ten

and we further assume that the sequence
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is
strictly stationary
: for every
r\in\mathbb{Z}
the finite–dimensional distributions of
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are invariant under the shift
n\mapsto n+r
.

Assumption 1
.

With probability one,

\ker\left(\phi_{0}\right)\cap\states=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\ker\left(\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\right)\cap\states=\emptyset.

1
ensures that neither
\phi_{0}
nor
\phi_{0}^{\dagger}
(hence, almost surely, none of the
\phi_{n}
nor
\phi_{n}^{\dagger}
) annihilates any state.
Since the local tensors
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are stationary, we have that the sequence of transfer operators
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is also stationary, thus the kernel property in
1
holds for all
\phi_{n}
.
So, with probability
1
, none of the transfer operators nor their adjoints annihilates any quantum state.

For
\omega\in\Omega
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the
n
–fold forward composition

\Phi^{(n)}_{\omega}\;:=\;\phi^{\omega}_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi^{\omega}_{1}\circ\phi^{\omega}_{0},

(1.9)

with the convention
\Phi^{(0)}_{\omega}:=\mathrm{Id}_{\mapspace}
.
Here and below, a linear map
\Psi:\mathbb{M}_{D}\to\mathbb{M}_{D}
is called
strictly positive
(or
positivity improving
) if
\Psi(X)\in\mathbb{S}_{D}^{\mathrm{o}}
for every
X\in\mathbb{M}_{D}
with
X\geq 0
and
X\neq 0
.

Assumption 2
.

Almost surely there exists
n_{*}(\omega)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Phi^{(n_{*}(\omega))}_{\omega}
is strictly positive.

2
asserts that, with full probability, some finite composition of the local transfer maps is positivity improving.
In particular (see
3.1
), once strict positivity is attained, it persists: for all
k\geq n_{*}(\omega)
,
\Phi^{(k)}_{\omega}
is strictly positive.

Entry Time to Strict Positivity

Given
2
, define the (a.s. finite) random time

\tau(\omega)\;:=\;\inf\{\,n\in\mathbb{N}:\ \Phi^{(n)}_{\omega}\ \text{is strictly positive}\,\}.

(1.10)

Then
\pr\{\tau>n\}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
In concrete models, one may have a deterministic bound
\pr\{\tau>\mathbf{N}\}=0
for some
\mathbf{N}<\infty
(such as
random TI MPS and i.i.d. MPS obtained by sampling tensors from absolutely continuous distributions from
\ten
such as multivariate Gaussian with a positive covariance matrix (
[
Mui82
,
Eat07
]
). See
Appendix

B
for details),
or quantitative tails
\pr\{\tau>n\}
decaying to
0
at model–dependent rates.

2
Main results

With the standing assumptions of the preceding section in place, we state our main limit theorem for random matrix–product states built from identically distributed local tensors (common marginal law on
\ten
).
The theorem below asserts that if the associated transfer maps satisfy
1
and
2
, then the thermodynamic limit of the normalized expectation of any local observable exists almost surely.

Theorem A
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a strictly stationary sequence with a common marginal law on

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})\right]\leq C_{\text{iid}}e^{-n\delta}\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

4.2
Mixing Coefficients for Random Systems

In a system described by random objects, there are various
measures
of stochastic correlation/de-correlation given by so-called mixing coefficients.
Five of such classical mixing coefficients are described in this section.
Before we discuss the mixing coefficients, we start with the stochastic correlation between two random variables.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\Pr)
be a probability space. For real–valued random variables
X,Y
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\Pr)
, the
maximal stochastic correlation
is

\rho(X,Y)\ :=\ \sup\bigl\{\,|\mathrm{corr}(f(X),g(Y))|\ :\ f,g\in L^{2},\ f\neq 0,\ g\neq 0\,\bigr\}.

(4.2)

It is classical that
X\!\perp\!\!\!\perp Y
if and only if
\rho(X,Y)=0
. The notion extends to random elements with values in measurable spaces. Writing

\mathcal{A}:=\sigma(X),\qquad\mathcal{B}:=\sigma(Y),

where
\sigma(\,\cdot\,)
denotes the generated
\sigma
-algebra.
One has the equivalent formulation

\rho(X,Y)=\rho(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\ :=\ \sup\bigl\{\,|\mathrm{corr}(U,V)|\ :\ U\in L^{2}(\mathcal{A}),\ V\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B}),\ U\neq 0,\ V\neq 0\,\bigr\}.

(4.3)

Beyond
\rho
there are standard mixing coefficients for pairs of sub–
\sigma
–algebras
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
:

\displaystyle\psi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\sup\Bigl\{\Bigl|1-\frac{\Pr(A\cap B)}{\Pr(A)\Pr(B)}\Bigr|:\ A\in\mathcal{A},\ B\in\mathcal{B},\ \Pr(A),\Pr(B)>0\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\sup\bigl\{\,|\Pr(B\mid A)-\Pr(B)|:\ A\in\mathcal{A},\ \Pr(A)>0,\ B\in\mathcal{B}\,\bigr\},

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\sup\bigl\{\,|\Pr(A\cap B)-\Pr(A)\Pr(B)|:\ A\in\mathcal{A},\ B\in\mathcal{B}\,\bigr\},

\displaystyle\beta(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\sup\biggl\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\bigl|\Pr(A_i\cap B_j)-\Pr(A_i)\Pr(B_j)\bigr|\biggr\},

where the last supremum is over all finite partitions
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I}\subset\mathcal{A}
and
\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}\subset\mathcal{B}
.
These satisfy the (one–sided) hierarchy (see
[
Bra05
,
Bra07
]
)

\rho(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq\psi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}),\qquad\rho(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq\varphi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})^{1/2},\qquad\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}).

(4.4)

Now these mixing coefficients generalize further to a random system described by a sequence of random objects
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
(for our case, the random local tensors) as follows:
Define the forward and backward filtrations

\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{n}:n\leq k),\qquad\mathcal{F}^{k}:=\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{n}:n\geq k).

(4.5)

The
maximal spatial
\rho
–profile
is

\rho_{n}\ :=\ \sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\ \rho(\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathcal{F}^{k+n})\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

(4.6)

We say the sequence is
\rho
–mixing
if
\rho_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
(cf.
[
KR60
]
).
Analogously define spatial profiles
\psi_{n},\varphi_{n},\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}
by replacing
\rho
in (
4.6
) with the corresponding coefficient.

Remark 4.2
.

If
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is strictly stationary (our standing assumption), then
\rho(\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathcal{F}^{k+n})
does not depend on
k
, hence

\rho_{n}=\rho(\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}^{n})\ \ \text{and similarly for }\psi_{n},\varphi_{n},\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}.

Thus, the “
\sup_{k}
” in (
4.6
) can be dropped without changing the value.

With these definitions in place, we are now ready to prove the remaining results.
We start with the following practical proposition and the lemma.

Proposition 4.3
.

Under Assumption
1
-
2
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[c(\Phi^{(n)})\bigr]=0
.

Proof.

Using
0\leq c(\Phi^{(n)})\leq 1
,
c(\Phi^{(n)})\to 0
almost surely, and the Dominated convergence theorem produces the result.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(
[
Dou94
, §1.2 Thm. 3]
,
[
Bra07
, Thm. 3.9]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
are sub–
\sigma
–algebras,
U\in L^{2}(\mathcal{A})
and
V\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B})
, then

|\mathrm{cov}(U,V)|\ \leq\ \rho(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\,\|U\|_{L^{2}}\,\|V\|_{L^{2}}\,.

The following lemma shall be helpful in the subsequent analysis.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a strictly stationary sequence of local tensors.
Then we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i+q+r)})\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})\right]\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(r)})\right]\ +\ \rho_{q}\,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})\right]\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(r)})\right]}\qquad\text{for all }i,q,r\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.7)

Proof.

Fix
i,q,r\in\mathbb{N}
. By submultiplicativity and the bound
\mathrm{c}(\,\cdot\,)\leq 1
,

\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i+q+r)})\ \leq\ \mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})\,\mathrm{c}(\phi_{i+q+r-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{i+q}),

hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i+q+r)})\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\,\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})\,\mathrm{c}(\phi_{i+q+r-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{i+q})\,\right].

Here
\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})
is
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
–measurable and
\mathrm{c}(\phi_{i+q+r-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{i+q})
is
\mathcal{F}^{\,i+q}
–measurable. Applying
Lemma

4.4
with
U=\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})
and
V=\mathrm{c}(\phi_{i+q+r-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{i+q})
, and using
(\mathrm{c}(\,\cdot\,))^{2}\leq\mathrm{c}(\,\cdot\,)
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i+q+r)})\right]\\
\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})\right]\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\phi_{i+q+r-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{i+q})\right]\ +\ \rho_{q}\,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})\right]\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\phi_{i+q+r-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{i+q})\right]}.

(4.8)

By
strict stationarity
of
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, the distribution of any length–
r
block is translation invariant, so

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\phi_{i+q+r-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{i+q})\right]\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(r)})\right].

Thus (
4.8
) reduces to

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i+q+r)})\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})\right]\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(r)})\right]\ +\ \rho_{q}\,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(i)})\right]\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(r)})\right]},

(4.9)

proving
4.7
.
∎

4.3
Random MPS with Vanishing Spatial Stochastic Correlations

In scenarios where the maximal spatial stochastic correlation
\rho_{n}
(as defined in (
4.6
)) decays to zero as

, we obtain quantitative decay estimates on the stochastic expectation of the contraction coefficient
\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})
.
Specifically, the decay can be shown to be faster than any prescribed polynomial rate:

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a
strictly stationary
bi–infinite sequence of random local tensors satisfying
1
-
2

\rho_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Then for each

there exists a constant

such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})\right]\ \leq\ \frac{C_{p}}{n^{p}}\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Proof.

Since
\rho_{n}\to 0
and, by
4.3
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})\right]\to 0

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(3N_{0})})\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(N_{0})})\right]^{2}+\rho_{N_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(N_{0})})\right]\ \leq\ 4^{-p}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(N_{0})})\right].

(4.12)

Next, setting
i=r=3N_{0}
and
q=N_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(7N_{0})})\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(3N_{0})})\right]^{2}+\rho_{N_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(3N_{0})})\right].

Using (
4.12

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(7N_{0})})\right]\ \leq\ 4^{-2p}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(N_{0})})\right].

Define

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(M_{k+1})})\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(M_{k})})\right]^{2}+\rho_{N_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(M_{k})})\right]\ \leq\ \bigl(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(M_{k})})\right]+4^{-p}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(M_{k})})\right].

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(M_{k})})\right]\ \leq\ 4^{-(k-1)p}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(N_{0})})\right]\qquad(k\geq 1).

Now let

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(M_{k})})\right]

4.4
Sufficiently Fast
\rho
-mixing

In the previous section, we worked under the minimal decorrelation hypothesis
\rho_{n}\to 0
, which yielded (super) polynomial decay estimates for
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})\right]
.
We now strengthen the assumption to a stretched–exponential or exponential spatial
\ rho
–mixing profile and show how this accelerates the contraction bootstrapping accordingly.
Throughout this subsection we assume that there exist constants
C_{\rho},c_{\rho}>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1]
such that

\rho_{n}\ \leq\ C_{\rho}\,\exp\{-c_{\rho}\,n^{\gamma}\}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

(4.15)

We present the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Assume (
4.15
). Then for every
\alpha\in(0,\gamma)
and every
\theta\in(0,1)
there exists
L_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
L\geq L_{0}
,

C_{\rho}\,\exp{-\tfrac{c_\rho}{2}\,(\theta L)^{\gamma}}\ \leq\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\exp{-2L^\alpha}\,.

(4.16)

Proof.

Inequality (
4.16
) is equivalent to

\frac{c_{\rho}}{2}\,\theta^{\gamma}\,L^{\gamma}-2L^{\alpha}\ \geq\ \ln(2C_\rho).

A sufficient condition is to enforce simultaneously

\frac{c_{\rho}}{2}\,\theta^{\gamma}\,L^{\gamma}\ \geq\ 4L^{\alpha},\qquad 2L^{\alpha}\ \geq\ \bigl(\ln(2C_\rho)\bigr)^{+},

(4.17)

where
x^{+}:=\max\{x,0\}
. Since
\alpha\in(0,\gamma)
, the first inequality in (
4.17
) is implied by

L\ \geq\ \left(\frac{4}{\tfrac{c_{\rho}}{2}\,\theta^{\gamma}}\right)^{\!1/(\gamma-\alpha)}\ =\ \left(\frac{8}{c_{\rho}\,\theta^{\gamma}}\right)^{\!1/(\gamma-\alpha)}.

The second is implied by

L\ \geq\ \left(\frac{(\ln(2C_\rho))^{+}}{2}\right)^{\!1/\alpha}.

Therefore the choice

L_{0}\ :=\ \Biggl\lceil\,\max\!\left\{\left(\frac{8}{c_{\rho}\,\theta^{\gamma}}\right)^{\!1/(\gamma-\alpha)},\ \left(\frac{(\ln(2C_\rho))^{+}}{2}\right)^{\!1/\alpha},\ 1\right\}\,\Biggr\rceil

ensures (
4.17
) for all
L\geq L_{0}
, which in turn yields (
4.16
).
∎

Next, we present a simple yet useful comparison of stretched–exponential rates.

Lemma 4.8
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
there exists
\theta\in(0,1)
and
L_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{3}{2}\,\exp\{-2L^{\alpha}\}\ \leq\ \exp\{-((2+\theta)L)^{\alpha}\}\qquad\text{for all }L\geq L_{0}.

(4.18)

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and choose
\theta\in(0,1)
so small that
(2+\theta)^{\alpha}<2
, e.g.

\theta\ :=\ \min\!\left\{\frac{2^{1/\alpha}-2}{2},\,\frac{1}{2}\right\}\in(0,1).

Since the map
x\mapsto e^{-x}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\infty)
, (
4.18
) is equivalent to

2L^{\alpha}-((2+\theta)L)^{\alpha}\ \geq\ \ln(3/2).

By the choice of
\theta
the coefficient
2-(2+\theta)^{\alpha}
is strictly positive. Therefore, it suffices to take

L_{0}\ \geq\ \left(\frac{\ln(3/2)}{\,2-(2+\theta)^{\alpha}\,}\right)^{\!1/\alpha},

which yields the desired bound for all
L\geq L_{0}
.
∎

Equipped with the lemmas above, we are now ready to prove the following result.

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose there are constants
C_{\rho},c_{\rho}>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1]
such that

\rho_{n}\ \leq\ C_{\rho}\,\exp\{-c_{\rho}n^{\gamma}\}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Then for every
\alpha\in(0,\gamma)
there exist
C_{\alpha},c_{\alpha}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})\right]\ \leq\ C_{\alpha}\,\exp\{-c_{\alpha}n^{\alpha}\}\qquad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,\gamma)
. Choose
\theta\in(0,1)
as in
Lemma

4.8
, so that
(2+\theta)^{\alpha}<2
. Increase
L_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
so that simultaneously:

1.

Lemma

4.7
holds for all
L\geq L_{0}
(i.e.
C_{\rho}e^{-\tfrac{c_{\rho}}{2}(\theta L)^{\gamma}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-2L^{\alpha}}
),

2.

Lemma

4.8
holds for all
L\geq L_{0}
(i.e.
\tfrac{3}{2}e^{-2L^{\alpha}}\leq e^{-((2+\theta)L)^{\alpha}}
),

3.

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(L_{0})})\right]\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
,

4.

\theta L_{0}\geq 2
.

Define recursively
L_{k+1}:=2L_{k}+\lfloor\theta L_{k}\rfloor
for
k\geq 0
, and abbreviate
c(n):=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})\right]
.

We start by establishing the following claim.

Claim 1.
There exists
K_{0}
such that
c(L_{K_{0}})\leq e^{-L_{K_{0}}^{\alpha}}
.

Proof of Claim 1:

c(L_{k+1})\ \leq\ c(L_{k})^{2}+\rho_{q}\,c(L_{k}).

Since
\theta L_{k}\geq 2
, we have
\lfloor\theta L_{k}\rfloor\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta L_{k}
, whence

\rho_{q}\ \leq\ C_{\rho}\,\exp{-c_\rho\bigl(\dfrac{\theta L_k}{2}\bigr)^\gamma}\ \leq\ C_{\rho}\,\exp{-\dfrac{c_\rho}{2}\,\theta^\gamma L_k^\gamma}\ \leq\ \dfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-2L_{k}^{\alpha}},

by
Lemma

4.7
and
2^{-\gamma}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
. Using
c(L_{k})\leq 1
and
c(L_{k})>e^{-L_{k}^{\alpha}}
,

c(L_{k+1})\ \leq\ c(L_{k})^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}e^{-2L_{k}^{\alpha}}\ \leq\ \dfrac{3}{2}\,c(L_{k})^{2}.

Iterating this with
c(L_{0})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
gives

c(L_{k})\ \leq\ \left(\dfrac{3}{2}\right)^{2^{k}-1}c(L_{0})^{2^{k}}\ \leq\ \dfrac{2}{3}\,\left(dtfrac{3}{4}\right)^{2^{k}}\ =\ \frac{2}{3}\,\exp{-\ln(4/3)\,2^k}.

(4.19)

On the other hand,
L_{k+1}\leq(2+\theta)L_{k}
implies
L_{k}\leq L_{0}(2+\theta)^{k}
, and hence, by the standing assumption
c(L_{k})>e^{-L_{k}^{\alpha}}
,

c(L_{k})\ >\ \exp\{-L_{k}^{\alpha}\}\ \geq\ \exp\{-L_{0}^{\alpha}(2+\theta)^{\alpha k}\}.

(4.20)

Since
(2+\theta)^{\alpha}<2
, the two bounds in
4.19
and
4.20
contradict each other for large
k
. This proves Claim 1.
∎

Having obtained
K_{0}
so that
c(K_{0})\leq e^{-L_{K_{0}}^{\alpha}}
we claim the following:

Claim 2.
For all
k\geq K_{0}
one has
c(L_{k})\leq e^{-L_{k}^{\alpha}}
.

Proof of Claim 2.

Assume
c(L_{k})\leq e^{-L_{k}^{\alpha}}
for some
k\geq K_{0}
. Then, as above,

c(L_{k+1})\ \leq\ c(L_{k})^{2}+C_{\rho}\exp{-\dfrac{c_\rho}{2}\theta^\gamma L_k^\gamma}\ \leq\ \exp{-2L_k^\alpha}+\dfrac{1}{2}\exp{-2L_k^\alpha}\ =\ \dfrac{3}{2}\,\exp{-2L_k^\alpha}.

By
Lemma

4.8
,
\dfrac{3}{2}e^{-2L_{k}^{\alpha}}\leq\exp{-((2+\theta)L_k)^\alpha}
. Since
L_{k+1}=2L_{k}+\left\lfloor\theta L_{k}\right\rfloor\leq(2+\theta)L_{k}
and
x\mapsto e^{-x}
decreases,

c(L_{k+1})\ \leq\ \exp{-((2+\theta)L_k)^\alpha}\ \leq\ \exp{-(L_{k+1})^\alpha}.

Thus, the property propagates by induction to all
k\geq K_{0}
.
∎

Finally, let
n
be large and choose
k\geq K_{0}
with
2L_{k}<n\leq 2L_{k+1}
. Writing
n=2L_{k}+q
with
q>0
, (
4.7
) yields

c(n)\ \leq\ c(L_{k})^{2}\,c(q)+\rho_{q}\,c(L_{k})\ \leq\ 2\,c(L_{k})\ \leq\ 2\,\exp{-L_k^\alpha}.

Since
n\leq 2(2+\theta)L_{k}
, we have
L_{k}\geq n/(2(2+\theta))
, hence

Thus,

Therefore on the full-probbaility event
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}E_{n}
we have, for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\ker\left(\phi_{n}\right)\cap\states=\emptyset=\ker\left(\phi_{n}^{\dagger}\right)\cap\states
. This establishes
1
for the IID process under the product measure
\pr
.

2
:

We prove that, under
\nu\ll\mathrm{Leb}
on
\tens
, there exists a finite length

L_{\star}\ :=\ 2\Big\lceil\log_{d}D\Big\rceil

such that for
\nu^{\otimes L_{\star}}
-almost every
length-
L_{\star}
block

\boldsymbol{A}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{L_{\star}-1})\in\tens^{L_{\star}}
the composed transfer map

\Phi^{(L_{\star})}:=\phi_{L_{\star}-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{0}
is
strictly positive
. By stationarity of the IID sequence, this yields
2
.
The technique closely follows the proof in
[
JC24
]
.

Step 1:

Let
S=\{1,\ldots,d\}
and denote by
S^{L_{\star}}_{\mathrm{words}}
the words of length
L_{\star}
on
S
.
By the “sweeping words” construction
[
KŠ16
]
, there exist
D^{2}
words

w_{1},\ldots,w_{D^{2}}\in S^{L_{\star}}_{\mathrm{words}}
and a
d
-tuple

\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{d})\in\tens
such that

\big\{\,w_{\ell}(\mathcal{B})\,:=\ B_{w_{\ell}^{(L_{\star})}}\cdots B_{w_{\ell}^{(1)}}\ \big\}_{\ell=1}^{D^{2}}\quad\text{is linearly independent in }\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C}),

hence spans
\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})
.

Step 2:

For a block
\boldsymbol{A}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{L_{\star}-1})
, with
a_{j}=(A_{1}^{(j)},\ldots,A_{d}^{(j)})\in\tens
,
define the
length-
L_{\star}
Kraus products

K_{w}(\boldsymbol{A})\ :=\ A_{w^{(L_{\star})}}^{(L_{\star}-1)}\cdots A_{w^{(1)}}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad w\in S^{L_{\star}}_{\mathrm{words}}.

Form the
D^{2}\times D^{2}
matrix of vectorized products

M(\boldsymbol{A})\ :=\ \big[\,\mathrm{vec}\,K_{w_{1}}(\boldsymbol{A})\ \ \cdots\ \ \mathrm{vec}\,K_{w_{D^{2}}}(\boldsymbol{A})\,\big],\qquad P(\boldsymbol{A})\ :=\ \det M(\boldsymbol{A}).

Each entry of
M(\boldsymbol{A})
is a polynomial in the entries of the
A_{i}^{(j)}
, hence
P
is a
polynomial on
\tens^{L_{\star}}
. Evaluating at the
homogeneous
block

\boldsymbol{B}\ :=\ (\mathcal{B},\ldots,\mathcal{B})\in\tens^{L_{\star}}

gives
K_{w_{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{B})=w_{\ell}(\mathcal{B})
; by the choice of
\{w_{\ell}\}
and
\mathcal{B}
we have

P(\boldsymbol{B})\neq 0
. Therefore
P\not\equiv 0
and its zero set
\{P=0\}
has Lebesgue measure zero in
\tens^{L_{\star}}
.

Since
\nu\ll\mathrm{Leb}
on
\tens
, the product law
\nu^{\otimes L_{\star}}
is absolutely
continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\tens^{L_{\star}}
. Consequently,

\nu^{\otimes L_{\star}}\!\left\{\,\boldsymbol{A}\in\tens^{L_{\star}}:\ \mathrm{span}\{K_{w_{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{A})\}_{\ell=1}^{D^{2}}\ =\ \mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})\,\right\}\ =\ 1.

(B.1)

Step 3:

Applying
Lemma

B.1
below to the Kraus family
\{K_{w_{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{A})\}_{\ell=1}^{D^{2}}
, the event (
B.1
) implies that for
\nu^{\otimes L_{\star}}
-a.e.
\boldsymbol{A}
the composed map

\Phi^{(L_{\star})}\ :=\ \phi_{L_{\star}-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{0}

is strictly positive.
Now on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr)=(\tens^{\mathbb{Z}},\mathcal{B}^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}},\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}})
, every consecutive block

(\mathcal{A}_{n},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{n+L_{\star}-1})
has law
\nu^{\otimes L_{\star}}
. Thus, for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\pr\!\left\{\phi_{n+L_{\star}-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{n}\ \text{is strictly positive}\right\}\ =\ 1.

Taking the countable intersection over
n\in\mathbb{Z}
yields probability one for the event
that
all
such length-
L_{\star}
translates are strictly positive. This proves
2
.
∎

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\{K_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R}\subset\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})
satisfy
\mathrm{span}\{K_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R}=\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})
and define

\Psi(X):=\sum_{r=1}^{R}K_{r}XK_{r}^{\dagger}
. Then
\Psi
is
strictly positive
:
for every non-zero positive semi-definite
X\in\matrices
, we have
\Psi(X)
is strictly positive definite.

Proof.

Suppose
\Psi(X)\not\geq 0
for some
X\geq 0
with
X\neq 0
.
Then there exists
v\neq 0
with

0\ =\ v^{\dagger}\Psi(X)v\ =\ \sum_{r=1}^{R}\norm{\,X^{1/2} K_r\adj v\,}_{2}^{2},

so
X^{1/2}K_{r}^{\dagger}v=0
for all
r
. Hence
K_{r}^{\dagger}v\in\ker\left(X\right)=\ker\left(X^{1/2}\right)
for all
r
and, by linearity,

Z^{\dagger}v\in\ker\left(X\right)
for every
Z
in the span of
\{K_{r}\}
, i.e. for all
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})
.
But
Z\mapsto Z^{\dagger}v
is surjective.
Thus
\{Z^{\dagger}v:Z\in\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})\}=\mathbb{C}^{D}
, whence

\ker\left(X\right)=\mathbb{C}^{D}
, i.e.
X=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Alternative.

We may use the same method in
[
San+10
, prop. 1]
to prove this for the inhomogeneous case:

Recall that there is a one-to-one correspondence between super operators
\Psi\in\mapspace
and their Choi matrix:

\Psi(\,\cdot\,)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}K_{r}(\,\cdot\,)K_{r}^{\dagger}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{J}(\Psi)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\text{vec}(K_{r})\text{vec}(K_{r})^{\dagger}

Hence
\rank\{\mathcal{J}(\Psi)\}=\dim{\mathrm{span}\{{\text{vec}(K_{r}):1\leq r\leq R\}}\}}
.
By the hypothesis
\mathrm{span}\{K_{r}\}=\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})
and the linear isomorphism
\mathrm{vec}:\mathbb{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}^{D^{2}}
, we obtain
\rank\,\mathcal{J}(\Psi)=D^{2}
, so
\mathcal{J}(\Psi)
is full-rank.
Now, for any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{D}
the evaluation identity holds:

\Psi(\ket{v}\bra{v})\ =\ (I\otimes\bra{\overline{v}})\,\mathcal{J}(\Psi)\,(I\otimes\ket{\overline{v}}).

Thus
\Psi(\ket{v}\bra{v})
is also full-rank.
This improves to any non-zero positive semi-definite matrix
X
by the spectral decomposition
X=\sum_{j}\lambda\ket{v_j}\bra{v_j}
.
∎
