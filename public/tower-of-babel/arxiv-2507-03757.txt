Kappa distributions in the language of superstatistics
Sergio Davis, Biswajit Bora, Cristian Pavez, and Leopoldo Soto

Abstract
The kappa distribution of velocities appears routinely in the study of collisionless plasmas present in Earth’s magnetosphere, the solar wind among other contexts where particles are unable to reach thermal equilibrium. Originally justified through the use of Tsallis’ non-extensive statistics, nowadays there are alternative frameworks that provide insight into these distributions, such as superstatistics. In this work we review the derivation of the multi-particle and single-particle kappa distributions for collisionless plasmas within the framework of superstatistics, as an alternative to the use of non-extensive statistics. We also show the utility of the superstatistical framework in the computation of expectation values under kappa distributions. Some consequences of the superstatistical formalism regarding correlations, temperature and entropy of kappa-distributed plasmas are also discussed.

Introduction

The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution is the probability density of particle velocities observed in systems that reach thermal equilibrium. For a particle with mass
m
at temperature

T
, the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution is the three-dimensional Gaussian

P(\bm{v}|\beta,m)=\left(\frac{m\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta m\bm{v}^{2}}{2}\right),

(1)

with
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=1/(k_{B}T)
the inverse temperature. On the other hand, collisionless plasmas such as the ones present in the Earth’s magnetosphere
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
, the solar wind
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
and the interstellar medium
[
8
,
9
]
are unable to reach
thermal equilibrium, and in that case what is commonly observed is that particle velocities are best described by the kappa distribution
[
10
,
11
]
, a generalization
of (
1
) given by

P(\bm{v}|\kappa,v_{\text{th}})=\left[\pi\left(\kappa-\frac{3}{2}\right)v_{\text{th}}^{2}\right]^{-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{\Gamma(\kappa-\frac{1}{2})}\left[1+\frac{1}{\kappa-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\bm{v}^{2}}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\right]^{-(\kappa+1)},

(2)

where
\kappa
is referred to as the
spectral index
and
v_{\text{th}}
is the
thermal velocity
. Outside space plasmas, kappa distributions have only recently been
proposed
[
12
,
13
]
as plausible models for velocity distributions in laboratory plasmas such as Z-pinch discharges, even when power law distributions in such
configurations have been reported previously
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. It is particularly interesting to explore the existence of kappa
distributions in plasma focus discharges
[
19
,
20
]
, where phenomena such as jets and filaments similar to the ones observed in astrophysical environments, have
been observed
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

In the limit
\kappa\rightarrow\infty
, the kappa distribution in (
2
) reduces to a Maxwellian as in (
1
) with temperature
T
given by

k_{B}T=\frac{mv_{\text{th}}^{2}}{2}.

(3)

The thermodynamic foundations leading to the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution in (
1
) are well understood. Let us consider a system of
N
classical particles with microstate

\bm{\Gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\bm{P},\bm{R})
, where
\bm{P}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{N})
are the kinetic degrees of freedom and
\bm{R}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N})
the configurational degrees of freedom, and such that the Hamiltonian of the system is

\mathcal{H}(\bm{P},\bm{R})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\Phi(\bm{R}),

(4)

with
\Phi
the interaction (potential) energy. The distribution of microstates
p(\bm{\Gamma})
that maximizes the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy

\mathcal{S}[p]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-k_{B}\int d\bm{\Gamma}p(\bm{\Gamma})\ln p(\bm{\Gamma})

(5)

subject to the constraint of fixed mean energy

\big<\mathcal{H}\big>_{p}=\int d\bm{\Gamma}p(\bm{\Gamma})\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma})=\overline{E}

(6)

is known as the canonical ensemble, the equilibrium distribution given by

P(\bm{P},\bm{R}|\beta)=\frac{\exp\big(-\beta\mathcal{H}(\bm{P},\bm{R})\big)}{Z(\beta)}.

(7)

The equilibrium distribution of momenta
P(\bm{P}|\beta)
is then obtained by integration of the configurational degrees of freedom
\bm{R}
in
(
7
), that is,

P(\bm{P}|\beta)=\int\frac{d\bm{R}}{Z(\beta)}\,\exp\left(-\beta\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\Phi(\bm{R})\right]\right)

(8)

which reduces to

P(\bm{P}|\beta)=\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi\tilde{m}}\right)^{\frac{3N}{2}}\;\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}\right),

(9)

with
\tilde{m}
the geometric mean mass,

\tilde{m}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}=m_{A}^{N_{A}/N}\cdot m_{B}^{N_{B}/N},

(10)

where in the last equality we have assumed, for simplicity, a two-species plasma with species
A
and
B
, having masses
m_{A}
and
m_{B}
, and number of particles
N_{A}
and
N_{B}

respectively, such that
N=N_{A}+N_{B}
.

The distribution in (
9
) is universal, in the sense that it does not depend on the interaction potential
\Phi
. Moreover, it clearly factors as the product of
N

independent, single-particle momentum distributions,

P(\bm{P}|\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P(\bm{p}_{i}|\beta),

(11)

where

P(\bm{p}_{i}|\beta)=\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi m_{i}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}\right).

(12)

The single-particle momentum distribution
P(\bm{p}|\beta)
is equivalent to (
1
) if we recall that
\bm{p}=m\bm{v}
and use the scaling property

P(\bm{v}|\beta)=m^{3}\,P(\bm{p}=m\bm{v}|\beta).

(13)

Taking this procedure as a basis, an early theoretical attempt
[
24
]
to explain the origin of the kappa distribution lead to Tsallis’ non-extensive statistics
[
25
]
, which postulates that the functional that must be maximized under appropriate constraints is not (
5
) but the more general
q
-entropy,
defined by

S_{q}[p]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{k_{B}}{q-1}\left(1-\int d\bm{\Gamma}\,p(\bm{\Gamma})^{q}\right),

(14)

where
q
is known as the
entropic index
, and such that
S_{q}
reduces to the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy in (
5
) in the limit
q\rightarrow 1
. However, Tsallis
statistics has not been free of criticisms, due to the fact that its application can lead to inconsistencies
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
.

Moreover, nowadays there exist alternative formalisms that can explain the existence of kappa distributions without the need to postulate a different definition of entropy. Among them,
one of the most promising is the theory of superstatistics
[
30
,
31
]
, where the inverse temperature
\beta
is promoted from a constant to a random variable having its
own probability density. This mechanism cleanly produces non-equilibrium ensembles, including the power laws such as (
2
) commonly associated with non-extensive statistics,
using instead mixtures of canonical ensembles.

In this work we will review the derivation of the kappa distribution from the point of view of superstatistics, using an invariant parameterization of the distribution of inverse
temperatures, as well as derive new results regarding correlations, entropy and temperature of kappa-distributed plasmas.

2
Superstatistics

The formalism of superstatistics
[
30
,
31
]
is an alternative to non-extensive statistics that effectively recovers non-canonical ensembles in an elegant manner.
It has been successfully applied to plasma physics
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
, condensed matter systems
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
, high-energy physics and gravitation
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, among other fields
[
43
,
44
]
.

In superstatistics the inverse temperature
\beta
is not a constant but a random variable, having a probability density
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
where
\bm{\lambda}
represents the parameters
defining a particular ensemble. The canonical ensemble in (
7
) is then replaced by a joint distribution
P(\bm{\Gamma},\beta|\bm{\lambda})
of the microstates
\bm{\Gamma}
and the inverse temperature
\beta
, and this joint distribution is factorized as

P(\bm{\Gamma},\beta|\bm{\lambda})=P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})P(\bm{\Gamma}|\beta,\bm{\lambda})

(15)

using the product rule of probability theory. Because
\bm{\lambda}
represents knowledge about
\beta
, we see that the state of knowledge
(\beta,\bm{\lambda})
can be replaced by
\beta

itself and thus we can replace
P(\bm{\Gamma}|\beta,\bm{\lambda})
by
P(\bm{\Gamma}|\beta)
as given by (
7
). Therefore, we can write (
15
) as

P(\bm{\Gamma},\beta|\bm{\lambda})=P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})\frac{\exp\big(-\beta\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma})\big)}{Z(\beta)},

(16)

and the superstatistical distribution of microstates
P(\bm{\Gamma}|\bm{\lambda})
as the integral over
\beta
of (
16
),

P(\bm{\Gamma}|\bm{\lambda})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})\frac{\exp\big(-\beta\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma})\big)}{Z(\beta)}.

(17)

Because
\beta
and
\Gamma
are correlated in (
16
), the marginal distribution
P(\bm{\Gamma}|\bm{\lambda})
is, in general, different from the canonical ensemble. This
provides us with a mechanism to obtain non-canonical ensembles by postulating different inverse temperature distributions without the need to replace the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy.

The canonical ensemble is a particular case of superstatistics where
\beta
becomes again a constant, that is, when the uncertainty over
\beta
vanishes. This is of course achieved for

P(\beta|\beta_{0})=\delta(\beta-\beta_{0}),

(18)

as in this case
P(\bm{\Gamma}|\bm{\lambda})
in (
17
) reduces to (
7
) with
\beta=\beta_{0}
.

We will now define two quantities of interest for superstatistical steady states. First, the mean inverse temperature of the ensemble, denoted by
\beta_{S}
, will be the mean value of

\beta
under the distribution
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
, that is,

\beta_{S}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<\beta\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})\beta.

(19)

Secondly, we will define

\mathcal{U}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<(\delta\beta)^{2}\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})\big(\beta-\beta_{S}\big)^{2},

(20)

as the variance of
\beta
under the distribution
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
. The relative variance
u
is then given by

u\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\mathcal{U}}{(\beta_{S})^{2}}.

(21)

The distribution
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
has been shown
[
45
]
to be invariant under the choice of subsystems in a composite system, therefore its moments
\beta_{S}
and
\mathcal{U}
are
promising candidates for any parameterization of a superstatistical state. The same is true of
u
, being a combination of
\mathcal{U}
and
\beta_{S}
. In particular, using
u
as one of the
superstatistical parameters is useful because the limit
u\rightarrow 0
turns
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
into a Dirac delta distribution, therefore being the canonical (equilibrium) limit of
superstatistics.

3
Kappa distribution as a superstatistical mixture of Maxwellians

Let
\bm{V}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots,\bm{v}_{N})
be the
3N
-dimensional vector containing the velocities of
N
particles. The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution for
\bm{V}
at
inverse temperature
\beta
is the equivalent in velocity space of the distribution of momenta in (
9
). It is given by

P(\bm{V}|\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P(\bm{v}_{i}|\beta,m_{i})=\left(\frac{\tilde{m}\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{3N}{2}}\exp\big(-\beta K(\bm{V})\big),

(22)

with
P(\bm{v}_{i}|\beta,m_{i})
the single-particle Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution in (
1
) and

K(\bm{V})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{m_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{2}}{2}

(23)

the kinetic energy of the
N
-particle system. From (
22
), the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of kinetic energy is obtained as

P(K|\beta)=\int d\bm{V}\,P(\bm{V}|\beta)\,\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{m_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{2}}{2}-K\right)=\frac{\exp(-\beta K)\Omega_{K}(K;N)}{Z_{N}(\beta)}

(24)

where
\Omega_{K}
is the density of states of
K(\bm{V})
, defined by

\Omega_{K}(K;N)=\int d\bm{V}\,\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{m_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{2}}{2}-K\right)=\frac{K^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}\right)}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{m}}\right)^{\frac{3N}{2}}

(25)

and

Z_{N}(\beta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dK\,\exp(-\beta K)\Omega_{K}(K;N)=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{m}\beta}\right)^{\frac{3N}{2}}.

(26)

Replacing (
25
) into (
24
) we see that
P(K|\beta)
is a gamma distribution, namely

P(K|\beta)=\frac{\beta^{\frac{3N}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}\right)}\exp(-\beta K)K^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}.

(27)

In order to move from the Maxwellian to the kappa distribution within the superstatistical framework, we need to postulate a distribution
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
of inverse temperatures.
The particular distribution that achieves this is a gamma distribution,

P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})=\frac{1}{u\beta_{S}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\right)^{\frac{1}{u}-1},

(28)

as parameterized in Ref.
[
46
]
. This fixes our superstatistical parameters to be
\bm{\lambda}=(u,\beta_{S})
, with
u
and
\beta_{S}
as defined in (
21
) and
(
19
), respectively. The most probable value of
\beta
under this distribution is

\beta^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\beta_{S}\,(1-u),

(29)

while the mean and variance of
\beta
are given by

\displaystyle\big<\beta\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{S},

(30a)

\displaystyle\big<(\delta\beta)^{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}

\displaystyle=u(\beta_{S})^{2},

(30b)

as expected. If we replace (
28
) into (
17
) and introduce the variable
t\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\beta/(u\beta_{S})
, we obtain

P(\bm{V}|u,\beta_{S})=\frac{(u\beta_{S})^{\frac{3N}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}\left(\frac{\tilde{m}}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{3N}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3N}{2}-1}\exp\left(-t\Big[1+u\beta_{S}K(\bm{V})\Big]\right),

(31)

and after performing the integral in
t
and using (
26
), we recover the multi-particle kappa distribution
[
47
]
,

P(\bm{V}|u,\beta_{S})=\frac{C_{N}(u)}{Z_{N}(\beta_{S})}\Big[1+u\beta_{S}K(\bm{V})\Big]^{-\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3N}{2}\right)}

(32)

with a size-dependent spectral index

\kappa_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3N}{2}-1,

(33)

where we have defined

C_{N}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{u^{\frac{3N}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}+\frac{1}{u}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}.

(34)

Note that

\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}C_{N}(u)=1

(35)

for all
N
, and also

\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}\Big[1+u\beta_{S}K\Big]^{-\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3N}{2}\right)}=\exp(-\beta_{S}K),

(36)

thus confirming that the limit
u\rightarrow 0
of (
32
) yields the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution in (
22
) with

\beta=\beta_{S}
. For a single particle, (
32
) reduces to

P(\bm{v}|u,\beta_{S})=\left(\frac{mu\beta_{S}}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{u}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}\left[1+u\beta_{S}\frac{m\bm{v}^{2}}{2}\right]^{-\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3}{2}\right)}

(37)

which is exactly the kappa distribution as defined in (
2
), under the substitutions

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{2},

(38a)

\displaystyle\frac{mv_{\text{th}}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\beta^{*}}.

(38b)

One of the advantages of the superstatistical framework is that in many cases it simplifies the computation of expectation values. For instance, we can easily obtain all the moments of
the
N
-particle energy distribution associated to (
32
) without computing the distribution explicitly, by using the moments of the canonical energy distribution in
(
27
), which is a gamma distribution. Recalling that for a gamma distribution of the form

P(X|r,\theta)=\frac{\exp(-X/\theta)X^{r-1}}{\Gamma(r)\theta^{r}}

(39)

the
n
-th moment is given by

\big<X^{n}\big>_{r,\theta}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(r)\theta^{r}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dX\exp(-X/\theta)X^{r+n-1}=\theta^{n}\,\frac{\Gamma(r+n)}{\Gamma(r)},\qquad n>-r,

(40)

we obtain the canonical energy moments as

\big<K^{n}\big>_{\beta}=\beta^{-n}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}+n\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}\right)},\qquad n>-\frac{3N}{2}.

(41)

Taking expectation over
P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})
we have

\big<K^{n}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\big<\beta^{-n}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}+n\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}\right)}

(42)

and by using the fact that
P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})
in (
28
) is also a gamma distribution, we use (
40
) to easily get

\big<\beta^{n}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=(u\beta_{S})^{n}\;\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}+n\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}.

(43)

Replacing (
43
) into (
42
) we obtain the moments of
P(K|u,\beta_{S})
as

\big<K^{n}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=(u\beta_{S})^{-n}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}+n\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}-n\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)},\qquad-\frac{3N}{2}<n<\frac{1}{u}.

(44)

The upper bound for
n
imposes an upper bound for
u
(lower bound for
\kappa
). For instance, in order for the kinetic energy to have a well-defined variance, we must have
u<1/2

(equivalently,
\kappa>5/2
). On the other hand, the existence of higher moments of
K
imposes an even more strict bound on
u
.

Replacing
n=1
in (
44
) and using the property
z\Gamma(z)=\Gamma(z+1)
we obtain the mean kinetic energy of the system,

K_{S}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<K\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\frac{3N}{2\beta_{S}(1-u)}=\frac{3N}{2\beta^{*}},

(45)

and we see that the
N
-particle kappa distribution follows a form of equipartition theorem with equipartition temperature

k_{B}T_{\text{equip}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{k_{B}T_{S}}{1-u}.

(46)

Similarly, using (
44
) with
n=2
and some algebra we obtain the variance of
K
,

\big<(\delta K)^{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\left(\frac{3N}{2\beta^{*}}\right)^{2}\frac{(3N-2)u+2}{3N(1-2u)},

(47)

and we see that the relative variance

\frac{\big<(\delta K)^{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}}{(K_{S})^{2}}=\frac{(3N-2)u+2}{3N(1-2u)}

(48)

does not vanish in the thermodynamic limit unless
u=0
, due to the uncertainty in
\beta
. For
N=1
, the mean and variance of the single-particle kinetic energy are given by

\displaystyle k_{S}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<k\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\frac{3}{2\beta^{*}},

(49a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<(\delta k)^{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\frac{u+2}{3(1-2u)}(k_{S})^{2},

(49b)

respectively, thus from
k_{S}
and
\sigma_{k}^{2}
we can determine
u
and
\beta_{S}
as

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{3\sigma_{k}^{2}-2k_{S}^{2}}{6\sigma_{k}^{2}+k_{S}^{2}},

(50a)

\displaystyle\beta_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2(1-u)k_{S}}.

(50b)

On the other hand, for
u<1/3
, that is,
\kappa>7/2
, we can also define the single-particle kinetic energy
skewness
,

s(u)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{3}}\left<\left(\frac{m\bm{v}^{2}}{2}-k_{S}\right)^{3}\right>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\frac{4(1+2u)}{1-3u}\sqrt{\frac{1-2u}{3(2+u)}}.

(51)

Not only is the skewness
s(u)
non-negative, implying that the kinetic energy tails always extend to the right, it increases monotonically with
u
from its minimum value at
u=0
,
which is
s(0)=2\sqrt{2/3}
.

4
Correlation between particle kinetic energies

The kinetic energies of particles in a multi-particle kappa distribution such as (
32
) are correlated due to the presence of inverse temperature variations, and in
fact, the existence of a linear dependence between kinetic energies has been shown to be a sufficient condition to obtain the kappa distribution
[
46
]
.

In this section we will evaluate this correlation between the energies of two trial particles with energies
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, through the covariance

\big<\delta k_{1}\delta k_{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<k_{1}k_{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}-\big<k_{1}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}\big<k_{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}.

(52)

First we use the fact that kinetic energies in the canonical ensemble are uncorrelated, therefore
\big<\delta k_{1}\delta k_{2}\big>_{\beta}=0
and

\big<k_{1}k_{2}\big>_{\beta}=\big<k_{1}\big>_{\beta}\;\big<k_{2}\big>_{\beta}.

(53)

Now, because the single-particle kinetic energies are identically distributed,

\big<k_{1}\big>_{\beta}=\big<k_{2}\big>_{\beta}=\big<k\big>_{\beta}=\frac{3}{2\beta}

(54)

and we have

\big<k_{1}k_{2}\big>_{\beta}=\left(\frac{3}{2\beta}\right)^{2},

(55)

hence taking expectation under
P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})
we have

\big<k_{1}k_{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\left<\big<k_{1}k_{2}\big>_{\beta}\right>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2}\big<\beta^{-2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\left(\frac{3}{2\beta_{S}}\right)^{2}\;\frac{1}{(1-u)(1-2u)}

(56)

where we have used (
43
) for
n=-2
. Therefore the covariance between
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
is

\big<\delta k_{1}\delta k_{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\frac{u}{1-2u}(k_{S})^{2}.

(57)

We see that the covariance is positive for
0<u<1/2
and moreover, increases strictly with
u
, being zero only for
u=0
. From (
57
) we can compute the Pearson correlation, which
yields

\rho_{k_{1},k_{2}}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\big<\delta k_{1}\delta k_{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}}{\big<(\delta k)^{2}\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}}=\frac{3u}{u+2},

(58)

and also strictly increases with
u
. However, a more robust indicator of correlation is the mutual information
I_{k_{1},k_{2}}
between
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, defined by

I_{k_{1},k_{2}}(u,\beta_{S})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left<\ln\left[\frac{P(k_{1},k_{2}|u,\beta_{S})}{P(k_{1}|u,\beta_{S})P(k_{2}|u,\beta_{S})}\right]\right>_{u,\beta_{S}}.

(59)

From (
37
) we readily obtain the distribution of single-particle kinetic energies

\begin{split}P(k|u,\beta_{S})&=\int d\bm{v}P(\bm{v}|u,\beta_{S})\delta\left(k-\frac{m\bm{v}^{2}}{2}\right)\\
&=\frac{2(u\beta_{S})^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{u}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}\Big[1+u\beta_{S}k\Big]^{-\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3}{2}\right)}\sqrt{k},\end{split}

(60)

which is shown in Fig.
2
. Similarly, from (
32
) we can compute the joint distribution of
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
as

\begin{split}P(k_{1},k_{2}|u,\beta_{S})&=\int d\bm{v}_{1}d\bm{v}_{2}P(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}|u,\beta_{S})\delta\left(k_{1}-\frac{m_{1}\bm{v}_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)\delta\left(k_{2}-\frac{m_{2}\bm{v}_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\\
&=\frac{4(\beta_{S})^{3}\,(1+u)(1+2u)}{\pi}\Big[1+u\beta_{S}(k_{1}+k_{2})\Big]^{-\frac{1}{u}-3}\sqrt{k_{1}\;k_{2}}.\end{split}

(61)

Upon replacing (
61
) and (
60
) into (
59
), we can finally evaluate
I_{k_{1},k_{2}}
as

I_{k_{1},k_{2}}(u)=2F\Big(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3}{2}\Big)-F\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)-F\Big(\frac{1}{u}+3\Big),

(62)

where we have defined

F(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=z\psi(z)-\ln\,\Gamma(z)

(63)

with
\psi
the digamma function,
\psi(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\Gamma^{\prime}(z)/\Gamma(z)
. Because
F^{\prime}(z)=z\psi^{\prime}(z)>0
for
z>0
we can show that
I_{k_{1},k_{2}}(u)
strictly increases with
u
,
and by using the asymptotic approximation

F(z)\approx z+\frac{1}{2}\ln z-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\ln\,(2\pi)+1\Big)

(64)

for large values of
z
we can verify that

\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}I_{k_{1},k_{2}}(u)=0,

(65)

therefore we confirm that
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are uncorrelated only in the canonical limit. Fig.

shows
I_{k_{1},k_{2}}
and
\rho_{k_{1},k_{2}}

5
Observable temperature for kappa distributions

It has been established
[
48
]
as a general property of non-canonical superstatistics that no function
\hat{\beta}(\bm{\Gamma})
exists being interchangeable with
\beta
in
the sense that

\big<G\big(\hat{\beta}(\bm{\Gamma})\big)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}=\big<G(\beta)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}

(66)

for an arbitrary function
G
. A consequence of this theorem is that
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
cannot be written as a sampling distribution, in other words, there is no function

\hat{\beta}(\bm{\Gamma})
such that

P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})=\big<\delta\big(\hat{\beta}(\bm{\Gamma})-\beta\big)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}.

(67)

One way to understand this impossibility theorem in the case of kappa distributions, is to notice that the uncertainty in
\beta
does not vanish even when knowing the velocities

\bm{V}
of all the particles. In fact, if such a function
\hat{\beta}(\bm{V})
existed for which (
67
) with
\bm{\Gamma}=\bm{V}
and
\bm{\lambda}=(\bm{V},u,\beta_{S})

would hold, it would imply

P(\beta|\bm{V},u,\beta_{S})=\delta\big(\beta-\hat{\beta}(\bm{V})\big).

(68)

In the case of our multi-particle kappa distribution, following Ref.
[
49
]
we will compute the conditional distribution of
\beta
given
\bm{V}
using the product rule of probability,

P(\beta|\bm{V},u,\beta_{S})=\frac{P(\bm{V},\beta|u,\beta_{S})}{P(\bm{V}|u,\beta_{S})}.

(69)

The superstatistical joint distribution of velocities and inverse temperature
P(\bm{V},\beta|u,\beta_{S})
is, according to (
16
) and (
28
),

P(\bm{V},\beta|u,\beta_{S})=\left(\frac{\tilde{m}\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{3N}{2}}\frac{1}{u\beta_{S}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\Big[1+u\beta_{S}K(\bm{V})\Big]\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\right)^{\frac{1}{u}-1},

(70)

thus replacing (
70
) and (
32
) into (
69
) yields

P(\beta|\bm{V},u,\beta_{S})=\frac{\Big[1+u\beta_{S}K(\bm{V})\Big]^{\frac{3N}{2}+\frac{1}{u}}}{u\beta_{S}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}+\frac{1}{u}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\Big[1+u\beta_{S}K(\bm{V})\Big]\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\right)^{\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3N}{2}-1}.

(71)

This is again a gamma distribution, but with mean

\big<\beta\big>_{\bm{V},u,\beta_{S}}=\left(1+\frac{3Nu}{2}\right)\frac{\beta_{S}}{1+u\beta_{S}K(\bm{V})},

(72)

and with relative variance

\frac{\big<(\delta\beta)^{2}\big>_{\bm{V},u,\beta_{S}}}{\big<\beta\big>^{2}_{\bm{V},u,\beta_{S}}}=\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3N}{2}\right)^{-1},

(73)

which is in general greater than zero and less than
u
, vanishing in the limit
u\rightarrow 0
(
\kappa\rightarrow\infty
), that is, when the kappa distribution reduces to the Maxwellian, and also in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
for
u>0
. In the latter case, we can approximate

\big<\beta\big>_{\bm{V},u,\beta_{S}}\approx\frac{3N}{2K(\bm{V})},

(74)

and, together with
\big<(\delta\beta)^{2}\big>_{\bm{V},u,\beta_{S}}\rightarrow 0
, we are led to conclude that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}P(\beta|\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots,\bm{v}_{N},u,\beta_{S})=\delta\big(\beta-\hat{\beta}_{\infty}\big),

(75)

then agreeing with (
68
), where

\hat{\beta}_{\infty}(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{3N}{2K(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots)}.

(76)

In fact this means that only in the thermodynamic limit, there is a unique value of inverse temperature given the set of velocities
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots
, namely
\hat{\beta}
in
(
76
). It follows by taking expectation under
P(\bm{V}|u,\beta_{S})
on both sides of (
75
) and recognizing that
P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})
is independent of
N
, that

P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})=\left<\delta\left(\beta-\hat{\beta}_{\infty}\right)\right>_{u,\beta_{S}},

(77)

which is precisely the result by Gravanis
et al

[
50
]
on the interpretation of the superstatistical inverse temperature in the thermodynamic limit. We can rewrite
the right-hand side of (
77
) to read

P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\Big<\delta\Big(\beta-\frac{3N}{2K}\Big)\Big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\;\frac{3N}{2\beta^{2}}\,P\Big(K=\frac{3N}{2\beta}\Big|u,\beta_{S}\Big),

(78)

and this result can be validated by replacing the distribution

P(K|u,\beta_{S})=\frac{C_{N}(u)}{Z_{N}(\beta_{S})}\Big[1+u\beta_{S}K\Big]^{-\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3N}{2}\right)}\Omega_{K}(K;N),

(79)

into the rightmost-hand side of (
78
), upon which we obtain

\begin{split}P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})&=\frac{1}{u\beta_{S}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2\,\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}+\frac{1}{u}\right)}{3N\,\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}\right)}\left(\frac{3Nu\beta_{S}}{2\beta}\right)^{\frac{3N}{2}+1}\Big[1+\frac{3Nu\beta_{S}}{2\beta}\Big]^{-\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{3N}{2}\right)}\\
&=\frac{1}{u\beta_{S}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\right)^{\frac{1}{u}-1},\end{split}

(80)

exactly recovering
P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})
as in (
28
).

6
Entropy of particle velocities in a collisionless plasma

We can compute the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy, as defined in (
5
), associated to the superstatistical joint distribution of velocities and inverse temperature in
(
70
), as the integral

\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{V}}(u,\beta_{S})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,\int d\bm{V}P(\bm{V},\beta|u,\beta_{S})\ln P(\bm{V},\beta|u,\beta_{S}).

(81)

Replacing (
70
) into (
81
), and using (
30a
) and (
41
) with
n=1
to obtain

\big<\beta K\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\left<\big<\beta K\big>_{\beta}\right>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\frac{3N}{2},

(82)

together with

\big<\ln\beta\big>_{u,\beta_{S}}=\ln\,(u\beta_{S})+\psi\left(\frac{1}{u}\right),

(83)

we finally have, after replacing
\tilde{m}
according to (
10
), that

\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{V}}(u,\beta_{S})=\frac{3N}{2}\Big[1+\ln\,(2\pi)-\frac{N_{A}}{N}\ln m_{A}-\frac{N_{B}}{N}\ln m_{B}-\psi\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)-\ln\,(u\beta_{S})\Big]+\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(u,\beta_{S}),

(84)

where

\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(u,\beta_{S})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})\ln P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})=\frac{1}{u}-F\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)+\psi\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)+\ln\,(u\beta_{S})

(85)

is the entropy of the superstatistical inverse temperature distribution in (
28
). We see that the entropy
\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{V}}
is in general non-additive,
due precisely to this last term that reflects the uncertainty in
\beta
. For instance, for two particles the entropy can be written as

\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}}=\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{v}_{1}}+\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{v}_{2}}-\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(u,\beta_{S}).

(86)

The derivative of the entropy in (
84
) with respect to
u
is

\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{V}}(u,\beta_{S})}{\partial u}=\frac{(3N-2)u+2}{2u^{3}}\;\Big(\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)-u\Big)

(87)

which is non-negative, thus entropy increases monotonically with
u
. On the other hand, we can evaluate the derivative

\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{V}}}{\partial K_{S}}\right)_{u,N}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{V}}(u,\beta_{S})}{\partial\beta_{S}}\cdot\frac{\partial\beta_{S}}{\partial K_{S}}=\left(\frac{3N-2}{3N}\right)\beta_{S}\,(1-u),

(88)

hence

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{\beta,\bm{V}}}{\partial K_{S}}\right)_{u,N}=\beta^{*},

(89)

consistent with the fact that
\beta^{*}
plays the role of the inverse equipartition temperature.

7
Concluding remarks

We have reviewed the derivation of the multi-particle and single-particle kappa distribution from the assumption of a gamma distribution of inverse temperatures in superstatistics.
This is not only a promising route to explain the origin of the kappa distribution in collisionless plasmas, a superstatistical perspective also brings analytical tools from probability
theory that allow us to simplify the computation of observables in superstatistical states. We have illustrated through the computation of the Pearson correlation and the mutual
information between kinetic energies of two distinct particles in a kappa distribution that those energies are always positively correlated, and that their correlation increases with
the distance to equilibrium, as measured by
u
. We are able to interpret the result by Gravanis
et al
on the interpretation of the superstatistical temperature in kappa plasmas
as the vanishing of the uncertainty about
\beta
given knowledge of all particle velocities in the thermodynamic limit.
