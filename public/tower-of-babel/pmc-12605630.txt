Tracing the enteric neural crest cell pathway from origin to colonization: insights into Hirschsprung’s disease
Authors: Huifang Lin, Jingyi You, Luwen Hao, Ziyi Zheng, Jie Zhou, Jun Xiao, Jiexiong Feng, Feng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe enteric nervous system (ENS) is a highly organized network of neurons and glial cells within the intestinal wall, which orchestrates the precise regulation of gastrointestinal motility, nutrient absorption, and mucosal barrier function through interconnected ganglia and plexuses. Originating from enteric neural crest cells (ENCCs), the ENS develops as ENCCs undergo a coordinated migratory process to colonize the entire gastrointestinal tract. Defective migration of ENCCs leads to neurodevelopmental disorders, such as Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR), in which the distal intestine remains aganglionic. In recent years, breakthroughs in single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have significantly advanced our ability to resolve gut cellular networks, uncovering spatiotemporal heterogeneity and lineage diversification during ENS development. Nevertheless, the inherent complexity of intestinal tissues—including diverse cell types, microenvironmental interactions, and multiscale regulatory networks—continues to impede mechanistic insights and precision therapeutics for enteric neurodevelopmental disorders. This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of molecular mechanisms underlying ENS migration, encompassing critical signaling pathways, migratory dynamics, and multicellular regulatory networks. We further discuss emerging technologies and unresolved questions in this rapidly evolving field.Keywords: Enteric nervous system, Enteric neural crest cells, Migration, Regulatory networks, Hirschsprung's disease

Introduction

The enteric nervous system (ENS), as the autonomous regulatory center of the gastrointestinal tract, is a complex neural network system composed of neurons and glial cells. This system not only independently coordinates fundamental physiological functions such as digestion, absorption, gastrointestinal motility, and mucus secretion but also maintains intestinal microenvironmental stability by regulating water-electrolyte balance, immune barrier homeostasis, and microbiota interactions [1]. As the largest peripheral autonomic nervous system in humans, the ENS possesses fully independent signal-processing capabilities, with approximately 200–600 million neurons and 5–7 times more glial cells forming a complete neural regulatory network spanning from the esophagus to the anus [2].

The ENS contains approximately 20 neuronal subtypes, functionally categorized into three classes: (1) motor neurons, which mediate excitatory (via acetylcholine) or inhibitory (via nitric oxide/vasoactive intestinal peptide) smooth muscle contractions; (2) intrinsic primary afferent neurons, which detect luminal mechanical stretch, chemical stimuli, and other signals, integrating them via interneurons to regulate epithelial secretion and blood flow; and (3) interneurons, which act as relay stations for neural signaling, balancing sensory inputs and motor outputs [1, 3]. This precise excitatory-inhibitory regulatory mechanism underlies the rhythmic peristalsis of the gut.

The normal development of the ENS relies on spatiotemporal crosstalk between neural crest-derived cells and the intestinal microenvironment. During embryogenesis, neural crest-derived precursor cells undergo directional migration, proliferation, and differentiation to establish the neural network architecture [2]. Notably, the craniocaudal migration of neural crest cells along the gut axis is critical; disruptions in this process lead to congenital disorders such as Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR). HSCR, characterized by the absence of ganglia in the distal bowel, occurs in approximately 1/5000 live births and is closely linked to defective neural crest cell migration [4–6] (Fig. 1).

This article explores the developmental origins of the ENS and elucidates the pathological mechanisms of HSCR. By integrating advances in ENCCs migration research with emerging technologies like single-cell sequencing, we aim to uncover novel regulatory mechanisms underlying ENCCs migration, providing a theoretical foundation for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Origin of the ENS

During early embryonic development, as the germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm) form, the ectoderm overlying the notochord thickens to form the neural plate [7]. The neural folds then fuse, creating the neural tube, which will develop into the spinal cord. Simultaneously, a subset of neural crest cells (NCCs), known as vagal neural crest cells (vNCCs), emerges from the closing neural folds and migrates, giving rise to the main proportion of ENCCs [8]. The remaining ENCCs originate from sacral neural crest cells (sNCCs) and display a posterior-to-anterior gradient in their distribution across the hindgut [9].

According to conventional theory, sNCCs contribute to the innervation of the distal colon and rectum. However, recent lineage-tracing studies have revealed that the ENS in mice is entirely derived from vNCCs, leaving the contribution of sNCCs to murine ENS development a subject of ongoing debate [10]. Remarkably, the contribution of sNCCs to the ENS exhibits species-specific characteristics. While sNCCs are involved in the formation of the ENS in birds, their role in mammals remains unknown [11].

Beyond vNCCs and sNCCs, Schwann cell precursors (SCPs) significantly contribute to ENS development. In mice, SCPs originating from somites 1–2 invade the intestinal wall alongside extrinsic nerve fibers, differentiating into approximately 20% of enteric neurons and supporting ENS maturation [12]. In basal vertebrates such as lampreys, the entire ENS arises from trunk neural crest-derived SCPs, as vagal neural crest cells are absent [13]. Notably, SCPs are not only involved in normal ENS development but also demonstrate regenerative potential under pathological conditions. SCPs isolated from both HSCR patients and mouse models can be expanded in vitro and, upon transplantation, differentiate into functional neurons and restore colonic neuromuscular activity [14]. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis further reveals that Schwann-like glial cells in the aganglionic segments of HSCR patients exhibit upregulation of neural development-related genes, suggesting a possible dedifferentiation process that activates neurogenesis or gliogenesis, thereby representing an intrinsic compensatory mechanism [15]. In the transition zone of HSCR mouse models—the critical interface between ganglionic and aganglionic bowel regions—SCPs have been shown to drive neurogenesis independently of the RET signaling pathway [16]. Furthermore, in ret mutant zebrafish, the selective 5-HT4 receptor agonist prucalopride promotes the differentiation of SCPs into HuC/D⁺ neurons, increases the number of distal enteric neurons, and partially rescues the HSCR phenotype [15]. These findings indicate that SCPs, by virtue of their RET-independent neurogenic capacity and endogenous presence, hold significant therapeutic potential for short-segment HSCR patients carrying heterozygous RET mutations.

Neurotransmitters serve as the molecular basis for the classification of neuronal subtypes, while neuronal subtypes determine the synthesis and release of neurotransmitters [17]. The differentiation of distinct neuronal subtypes exhibits remarkable spatiotemporal heterogeneity. Temporally, each neuronal population emerges within strictly defined developmental windows: starting with the appearance of choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) at embryonic day 8 (E8), followed by serotonergic neurons at E10–11.5, and neurons expressing enkephalin, neuropeptide Y, vasoactive intestinal peptide, and calcitonin gene-related peptide from E10 onwards. Tyrosine hydroxylase-positive and nitric oxide synthase 1-expressing neurons appear around E14, with the differentiation process culminating around birth (P0) as calretinin-positive neurons reach their peak abundance [18, 19]. Spatially, the emergence of submucosal neurons generally lags behind that of myenteric neurons and can extend into the postnatal period [19]. In HSCR patients, the expression level of ChAT gradually decreases from the aganglionic to the ganglionic intestinal segments, while nNOS expression shows a negative correlation with it. This complementary dysregulation may originate from impairments during the critical periods of enteric nervous system development (E10–E13 in mice/EW12–14 in humans) [20, 21]. Disruption of these developmental windows can directly lead to the loss or functional impairment of such specific neuronal subpopulations, thereby compromising intestinal motility and secretory homeostasis.

Phases of ENCCs migration

The migration of ENCCs is a multi-stage, highly coordinated dynamic process. Despite the complexity of their migratory trajectories, ENCCs follow a specific spatiotemporal program, primarily undergoing three phases of migration: craniocaudal migration, radial migration, and transmesenteric migration.

Craniocaudal migration

The establishment of the ENS relies on the precisely orchestrated craniocaudal migration of vNCCs along the intestinal axis (Fig. 2A). This process achieves efficient colonization through synergistic coordination between dynamic chain-structure remodeling and wavefront proliferation-differentiation equilibrium [22]. During embryogenesis, wavefront ENCCs display distinct behavioral characteristics: upon reaching the cecal region, the cell population attains peak proliferative activity to sustain migratory potential, ensuring continuous advancement toward the colorectum [23]. Live imaging demonstrates that ENCCs within 200 μm of the wavefront exhibit low directional persistence and slow caudal movement, whereas trailing cells adopt a rapid random-walk pattern. This behavioral dichotomy suggests impaired polarity sensing at the wavefront. Interestingly, wavefront cells migrate via chain-like structures at high speeds, significantly outpacing trailing cells organized in network configurations [24, 25].

This velocity disparity correlates with spatial gradients of protein kinase A (PKA) activity—low PKA activity in the wavefront region promotes migration, whereas high PKA activity in the trailing region reduces Rac1 activity and migration speed [26]. At the molecular level, the dynamic equilibrium between JNK and ERK signaling pathways regulates ENCCs migration. Dual-specificity phosphatase 6 (DUSP6) is specifically upregulated in the wavefront, maintaining migratory phenotypes via negative feedback regulation of ERK hyperactivation. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of DUSP6 disrupts hindgut wavefront colonization and reduces midgut and postnatal neuroblast migration capacity [27]. Furthermore, retinoic acid (RA) signaling plays a pivotal role in the chain-like migration of ENCCs. RA promotes the collective migratory expansion of chain-like by upregulating the expression of the Meis3 transcription factor and RET tyrosine kinase receptor. Concurrently, RA sustains migratory activity by reducing the intracellular accumulation of phosphatase and tensin homolog in migrating cells [28].

Radial migration

The ENS is primarily composed of two layers of ganglia: the myenteric plexus and the submucosal plexus [29, 30]. Within the myenteric ganglia, some cells undergo radial migration (inward migration) to form the submucosal plexus, which further develops into the intrinsic and extrinsic innervation of the mucosal region [31] (Fig. 2B).

The radial migration of ENCCs is regulated by the spatiotemporal coordination of multi-source signaling factors. The expression of Netrin1/3 in the epithelium, extracellular matrix, and pancreatic buds attracts ENCCs towards the mucosal direction [32]. Concurrently, Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) derived from the epithelium prevents ENCCs from over-invading the epithelial barrier, while BMP4 expressed late in the mesenchyme suppresses the excessive attraction of Netrin, maintaining the stability of the migratory pathway [33]. This balance of attraction and repulsion explains the directional migration capacity of ENCCs within the complex microenvironment. Despite their robust migratory potential, the distribution of ENCCs descendants within ganglionic layers exhibits significant spatial confinement. Progeny of individual ENCCs is restricted to specific regions within the myenteric or submucosal layers. The lack of long-distance migratory capacity among ENCCs descendants suggests the presence of microenvironment-dependent differentiation constraints [34].

Neurons in both the myenteric and submucosal ganglia originate from specific progenitor cells that are confined to their respective ganglionic layers, although the mechanisms underlying this directional confinement remain unclear [35]. The formation of these ganglionic layers may depend on the early determination of ENCCs migratory pathways. Radial migration of ENCCs relies on complex intercellular interactions and the function of cell adhesion molecules. However, our current understanding of how intercellular interactions influence ENCCs migration remains limited. Key areas that require further investigation include: (i) the role of cell adhesion in regulating cell polarity and directional migration; (ii) the coordinated effects of different chemokines on ENCCs migration; and (iii) the impact of interactions between ENCCs and surrounding tissues or cell populations on migratory patterns and pathways.

Transmesenteric migration

In mice, some vNCCs bypass the traditional craniocaudal migration pathway by taking a shortcut to the hindgut via the mesentery, located between the proximal colon and distal small intestine [36]. These cells differentiate into two populations: mesenteric neural crest cells (mNCCs), which travel along the mesentery, and ENCCs, which invade the foregut and migrate along the gut mNCCs contribute to the pool of ENCCs and ultimately form a crucial part of the hindgut ENS (Fig. 2C-E). This mesenteric migration process also offers a novel embryological perspective for understanding segmental Hirschsprung disease (SSHD). SSHD is a rare subtype of HSCR characterized by the presence of aganglionic skip areas in the intestinal tract, which are typically surrounded by normally ganglionated intestinal segments [37].

Further investigations have revealed significant differences in the ability of various gene-deficient mouse models to recapitulate the phenotypes of SSHD. A phenotype similar to SSHD is observed in the majority of Ednrb−/− mice, whereas this phenomenon is not evident in Ret knockout Ret−/− mice [38]. The RET/GDNF signaling pathway is not only essential for the migration of ENCCs along the gut but also for the entry of mNCCs into the gut. The RET signaling pathway plays a crucial role in modulating the relative abundance of ENCCs and mNCCs. The segmental pathological features of SSHD may arise from localized imbalances in the functionality of the EDNRB/EDN3 or RET/GDNF pathways.

Different migration form

Individual cell migration

The single-cell migration ability of ENCCs relies on a precise and coordinated mechanism involving polarity establishment, cytoskeletal dynamics, and signal integration. Initially, the initiation of migration requires the establishment of an anterior-posterior polarity axis: small GTPases such as RhoA, Rac1, and Cdc42 regulate actin remodeling through spatially specific activation [39, 40]. Specifically, Rac1 drives the extension of leading edge lamellipodia [41], RhoA mediates the formation of contractile fibers at the rear end [42], and Cdc42 maintains the directional arrangement of microtubule organizing centers, allowing the microtubule network to serve as tracks for polarized transport of proteins (Fig. 3A). Additionally, external guidance signals are essential for achieving directional migration of individual cells [39, 43]. GDNF is a typical chemotactic factor and a key signal regulating ENCCs’ single-cell migration. GDNF forms a chemotactic gradient in the extracellular environment, with higher concentrations of GDNF at target locations. ENCCs sense this gradient through their receptors and migrate towards higher concentrations of GDNF, ensuring directional movement during intestinal colonization [44] (Fig. 3C).

The migration of individual ENCCs is guided not only by chemotactic factors but also dynamically regulated by intercellular interactions. Isolated ENCCs at the migratory wavefront exhibit biased random walk behavior, yet their trajectory directionality is significantly dependent on contact signals from neighboring cells. Live imaging has revealed that the advancement of the expanding front is primarily driven by cells within 300 μm of the leading wavefront [25, 39]. These cells form mechanical coupling with other ENCCs through alternating parallel movements or reverse migrations, thereby collectively propelling the wavefront.

Notably, isolated cells located behind the wavefront dynamically participate in the expansion of the front through attachment-detachment processes—some cells transiently attach to the front and transmit mechanical stress, while others detach from the group to form secondary migratory paths. This heterogeneous behavior ensures the coverage of neural crest cells along the entire length of the gut [45].

As the distance from the wavefront increases, ENCCs movement becomes progressively restricted, and trajectory diversity increases, suggesting that the intensity of intercellular interactions decays with spatial distance [25]. More critically, ENCCs at the expanding front spontaneously aggregate into nodes through an attractant-repellent signaling network (such as Ephrin/Eph-mediated contact repulsion and Netrin/DCC-mediated chemotaxis) and subsequently branch to form new migratory paths. This self-organizing behavior may underlie the morphogenesis of the ganglion network in the ENS [46].

Collective cell migration

In the process of intestinal colonization, ENCCs form collective migration through dynamic intercellular adhesion connections [47]. Leading cells explore the microenvironment through plate-like pseudopodia, while subsequent cells follow their path for directional migration [48]. Although the migration trajectory of leading cells is highly random, the group as a whole maintains directional consistency. Notably, the migration speed of individual cells is significantly lower than that of the group, indicating that cell aggregation enhances migration efficiency by reducing movement resistance. This “group acceleration effect” may be related to intercellular mechanical transmission and signal synchronization. The collective migration of ENCCs not only relies on the autonomous motility of cells but is also precisely regulated by a complex molecular network [24].

Research has shown that Phactr4 negatively regulates integrin signaling through the RhoA/ROCK pathway and coordinates protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) with catenin activity to regulate cytoskeletal dynamics, which is essential for directional collective cell migration in vivo [49].

Additionally, C3a/C3aR signaling enhances N-cadherin-mediated lateral cell adhesion, maintaining the integrity of the chain structure. The crosstalk between N-cadherin-mediated adhesion and C3a/C3aR signaling may control the interactions during the collective migration process of ENCCs in the developing intestine [50] (Fig. 3B).

Molecular mechanisms affecting migration

Molecules affecting ENCCs of vagal spinal cell origin

The development of the enteric nervous system is a dynamic process precisely regulated by a multi-tiered signaling network (Fig. 4). The spatiotemporal migration of vNCCs is orchestrated by key signaling pathways including RET/GDNF, EDN3/EDNRB, and Hedgehog, along with intricate cell-cell interactions. Through the binding of RET receptors to ligands (GDNF, NRTN, ARTN) and co-receptors (GFRα1–4), this axis activates downstream pathways including PI3K-AKT, RAS-MAPK, and PLCγ, thereby driving the proliferation, differentiation, and directional migration of ENCCs [51, 52]. Studies have shown that reducing RET expression to approximately one-third of normal levels is sufficient to induce aganglionosis in the colorectum [53], while complete RET deficiency leads to extensive apoptosis of ENCCs in the foregut [54, 55]. Furthermore, conditional ablation of either RET or GFRα1 during late gestation, even after gut colonization is complete, results in the death of already colonized neurons [56].

The EDN3/EDNRB signaling pathway is also essential for the development of the ENS in the colon. This pathway enhances ENCCs-ECM adhesion by activating β1-integrins, promoting ENCCs migration [35]. The interactive network between ENCCs and neighboring cells further involves ephrin-Eph and Wnt-Frizzled signaling. Ephrin-Eph-mediated contact repulsion prevents path overlap, whereas the Wnt-Frizzled pathway guides directional migration for injury repair via non-canonical routes [57, 58]. The Hedgehog signal, another classic pathway, is initiated by the endoderm Shh and regulated by Gli transcription factors (Gli1/2/3) to control GDNF expression and cell proliferation, with overexpression of Gli1 inhibiting migration [59–61]. In the downstream effector systems, JNK is required for normal ENCCs migration speed but dispensable for directional persistence, while cAMP signaling modulates ENCCs migration speed, directionality, and adhesion [62].Our team has demonstrated that EMB has been identified as an evolutionarily conserved regulator during the development of the enteric nervous system (ENS), which promotes the development of ENCCs by activating the PI3K-AKT signaling axis [63]. Meanwhile, BMI1 can activate the Wnt signaling pathway through epigenetic silencing of Axin2, thereby enhancing the proliferation and migration capabilities of cells in models of HSCR [64].Furthermore, in the Ednrb−/− mouse model, blocking EP2-mediated PGE2 signaling exacerbates the pathological severity of HSCR. Further mechanistic studies have revealed that inhibition of EP2-mediated PGE2 signaling can promote the migration of ENCCs by enhancing p38 activity [65].Collectively, intracellular signaling pathways differentially regulate the velocity and directional precision of migrating ENCCs. Any imbalance in these signaling pathways can lead to migration arrest and ganglion cell deficiency, triggering ENS developmental disorders such as HSCR.

Molecules influencing sacral neural crest cell-derived ENCCs

The migration and positioning of ENCCs originating from sNCCs are jointly modulated by transcriptional control, cell adhesion, chemorepellent signals, and tissue mechanical properties. The transcription factor Sox10 serves as a central regulatory molecule, driving ENCCs migration through dual mechanisms. On one hand, it directly upregulates the expression of Itgb1, enhancing the adhesion of ENCCs to the extracellular matrix (ECM) and thereby promoting migration efficiency [66]. On the other hand, Sox10 activates cadherin-19, which forms cadherin-catenin complexes that couple to the cytoskeleton, maintaining intercellular mechanical connections during migration and ensuring coordinated collective movement [67].

Moreover, the distal hindgut microenvironment finely regulates the spatiotemporal positioning of ENCCs through gradient chemorepulsive signals. Semaphorin 3 A (Sema3A), highly expressed in the hindgut mesenchyme, forms a repulsive signaling axis by binding to its receptor Neuropilin-1 (Nrp1), restricting the invasion range of ENCCs and preventing excessive dispersion. In zebrafish models, Sema3A knockout leads to abnormal aggregation of ENCCs. The chemorepulsive action of Sema3A complements the mechanical propulsive function of the Sox10-Itgb1 axis—while the former delineates migration boundaries, the latter provides the driving force, together ensuring the precise colonization of ENCCs [68, 69].

Role of the extracellular microenvironment

The microenvironment surrounding the intestine plays a pivotal role in the development of the ENS. ENCCs express abundant cell adhesion molecules on their surface to regulate interactions with the ECM [70, 71]. These adhesion molecules, including collagen VI, collagen XVIII, and agrin, have gradually been characterized for their functional roles (Fig. 5). Collagen regulation exhibits bidirectional effects: increased collagen VI levels reduce ENCCs migration speed, likely mediated by altering intestinal microenvironment stiffness, whereas collagen XVIII facilitates cell migration by remodeling the ECM at the migration wavefront [72, 73].

Key regulators of ENCCs development also include the ECM glycoproteins tenascin-C and agrin [74]. ENCCs remodel their microenvironment via tenascin-C production to promote migration, whereas agrin strongly inhibits ENCCs migration by restricting the migratory domain through blockade of laminin-integrin interactions. Additional studies have identified METRN and TPX2—genes enriched in wavefront ENCCs—as regulators of ENCCs migration, though their precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated [22]. Synergistic effects of intercellular adhesion molecules are equally critical: L1CAM promotes chain-like migration of neural crest cells in the embryonic murine gut by enhancing cell-cell adhesion and coordinating movement. In vitro disruption of L1CAM leads to fragmented migration chains at the ENCCs wavefront, resulting in isolated ENCCs with slower intestinal migration and ultimately colonic aganglionosis [47].Although N-cadherin is non-essential, it cooperates with β1-integrin to regulate migratory path selection [75]. β1-integrin, a primary ECM receptor, modulates ENCCs migration by altering cell adhesion via interactions with the cecal ECM. Laminin provides physical support and directional cues to guide ENCCs migration, while 37/67-laminin integrin enhances migration speed by recognizing the LG domain of laminin-1 [76, 77]. Additional molecules capable of modifying the extracellular matrix or mediating direct intercellular adhesion—including COL5A2, LAMC1, and CDH2—facilitate ENCC migration by altering the physical properties of the intestinal wall or forming adhesive bridges with neighboring cells. Notably, gut microbial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through the GPR43 receptor to promote ENCCs proliferation and migration, suggesting a cross-kingdom regulatory axis linking microbiota, microenvironment, and neural development [78].

Currently, the mechanisms by which microenvironmental components regulate ENCCs migration remain incompletely understood [79, 80]. Key unanswered questions include the role of intestinal mechanical forces and mechanosensitive channels in ENCC migration, the impact of the microenvironment on ENCC regeneration, and the spatiotemporal influence of microbial metabolites and ECM components on ENS formation. Elucidating these mechanisms will provide new strategies for cell-based therapies targeting ENS developmental disorders.

Intestinal organoids and co-culture techniques

Recent advances in human intestinal organoid (HIO) technology have significantly advanced our understanding of the ENS. In a novel transwell-based co-culture system, Puzan and colleagues demonstrated that the ENS regulates intestinal stem cell fate by isolating mouse small intestinal stem cells for organoid culture alongside ENS neurons/glia or subepithelial myofibroblasts [81]. Workman et al. established an innovative protocol involving 4-week in vitro differentiation of human embryonic stem cells (ESCs) into ENS cells, followed by co-culture with HIOs derived from human ESCs or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). This process progresses through definitive endoderm and mid/hindgut spheroid stages to ultimately generate ENS-containing organoids [82]. After transplantation into mice, these organoids developed ganglion structures morphologically resembling the human myenteric and submucosal plexuses. Another study successfully constructed a multicellular organoid model by integrating iPSC-derived enteric glia, mesenchymal cells, and epithelial precursors [83]. Furthermore, co-culture of myenteric and submucosal plexus neurons with small intestinal organoids has generated innervated co-culture systems, providing new platforms for investigating regulatory mechanisms in gastrointestinal diseases [84]. Recently, our team established a co-culture system comprising primary intestinal epithelial organoids, intestinal mesenchymal cells, and ENCCs to directly interrogate the migratory behavior of ENCCs [63]. However, current intestinal organoid models cannot fully recapitulate the complex intestinal microenvironment in vivo and inadequately reproduce multidimensional communication between different cell types [85]. The absence of vascular systems, smooth muscle, associated interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC), fibroblast-containing connective tissue, nervous systems, and immune systems restricts its applications in disease mechanism investigation and drug screening. Therefore, future research should expand co-culture systems to incorporate crucial components such as smooth muscle cells and ICC to enhance physiological relevance and simulation accuracy.

Discussion

Single-cell RNA sequencing has been employed to map neuronal populations during enteric nervous system development and to predict cell type-specific expression of genes associated with HSCR [86]. Concurrently, integrated single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have delineated the coordinated morphogenesis of neural, vascular, mesenchymal, and immune lineages in the developing gut, with temporal data resolution enabling the investigation of disease gene expression throughout development [87]. Breakthroughs in single-cell and spatial omics technologies have identified ENCCs subpopulations with distinct migratory capacities and molecular signatures, revealing the central role of cellular diversity in the precise spatiotemporal colonization of the gut. These findings provide a new interpretative framework for the segmental aganglionic pathology observed in HSCR. Despite substantial progress in understanding early ENS formation, many fundamental questions remain. For instance, how intrinsic signaling within ENCCs specifically encodes neuronal subtype identity and functional circuitry requires elucidation through lineage tracing and real-time imaging techniques. The quantitative models linking ENCCs heterogeneity, revealed by multi-omics, to spatiotemporal remodeling of the microenvironment have yet to be established. The molecular mechanisms by which the perinatal gut microbiota regulates ENCCs network maturation through the metabolic-immune axis, such as the short-chain fatty acid-Treg cell pathway, urgently need clarification, offering potential targets for early intervention in neurogastrointestinal diseases.

Current research has shifted from single-molecule mechanisms to integrative multi-omics analyses, emphasizing a holistic perspective on spatiotemporal dynamics and microenvironmental interactions. Future work should further integrate spatial metabolomics and epigenetic regulation data to decipher mechanisms underlying ENCCs migration. Existing research models struggle to fully replicate the complex human gut microenvironment. Combining 3D bioprinted ENS organoids with in vivo imaging technologies to recapitulate HSCR pathological phenotypes may represents an emerging approach. In summary, ENS development research is undergoing a paradigm shift from descriptive analysis to mechanism-driven studies, with the potential to provide a theoretical basis for precision diagnosis and treatment of neurogastrointestinal diseases.

Conclusion

The development of the ENS begins with the coordinated migration of ENCCs and SCPs. ENCCs achieve precise colonization along the entire length of the gut through three distinct phases: craniocaudal, transmesenteric, and radial migration. During this process, ENCCs exhibit both collective and individual migration modes, and their migratory behavior is regulated by a multi‑level signaling network as well as by the ECM. These spatiotemporally ordered migratory mechanisms collectively ensure the structural integrity of the ENS network, laying the foundation for its physiological functions such as motility regulation and barrier maintenance.

Current research has shifted from studying isolated molecular mechanisms toward integrated multi‑omics analyses, emphasizing a holistic understanding of the spatiotemporal dynamics of ENS development and its interactions within the microenvironment. Future studies should further incorporate technologies such as spatial transcriptomics and proteomics to precisely map the interaction networks among different cell types in the HSCR gut, thereby deepening the mechanistic insight into ENCC migration. By leveraging cutting‑edge tools like ENS organoids and light‑sheet imaging, it will be possible to delineate ENS developmental trajectories and identify key regulatory nodes, ultimately providing potential interventional strategies for targeted HSCR therapy.
