Postoperative displacement as a predictor of early elbow motion limitation following fixation of pediatric lateral condyle fractures
Authors: Shin Woo Choi, Jae-Kwang Hwang, Sang Min Kang, Byong-Gon Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRecovery of elbow motion is a primary concern following fixation of pediatric lateral condyle fractures, yet the influence of postoperative displacement on early motion outcomes remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between postoperative displacement magnitude and early elbow motion limitation.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 63 pediatric patients (mean age 5.4 years) who underwent surgical fixation for lateral condyle fractures between 2015 and 2025. Immediate postoperative displacement was measured on postoperative radiographs. Early elbow motion limitation was defined as ROM <140° within the first year. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors, and ROC curve analysis determined the optimal displacement threshold.ResultsTwelve of 63 patients (19.0%) demonstrated early elbow motion limitation. This group had significantly greater postoperative displacement (1.53 ± 0.72 mm vs. 0.79 ± 0.62 mm, p = 0.002) and older age (6.5 ± 2.1 vs. 5.2 ± 2.1 years, p = 0.038) than the normal ROM group. Multivariable regression identified postoperative displacement as the sole independent predictor (OR 5.95; 95% CI 1.44–24.56; p = 0.014). ROC analysis demonstrated moderate predictive ability (AUC = 0.782). Using a 1.0 mm cutoff, early motion limitation was significantly more frequent in patients with displacement ≥1.0 mm than <1.0 mm (32.3% vs. 6.3%, p = 0.011).ConclusionsImmediate postoperative displacement independently predicted early elbow motion limitation after lateral condyle fracture fixation. A threshold of approximately 1.0 mm may serve as a practical reference for anticipating early motion recovery. Minimizing residual displacement may be beneficial, though longer follow-up studies are warranted.Keywords: lateral condyle fracture, pediatric elbow, postoperative displacement, early elbow motion limitation, range of motion

Introduction

Humeral lateral condyle fracture is the second most frequent type of pediatric elbow fractures, comprising approximately 12–20% of all childhood elbow injuries.1,2 These fractures are intra-articular injuries that involve the cartilaginous portion of the distal humeral physis, and insufficient management may lead to complications including nonunion, malunion, cubitus valgus deformity, and delayed ulnar nerve palsy.3–5

Temporary elbow stiffness is a well-recognized and almost universal finding after lateral condyle fractures of the humerus, and managing the expectations of patients and families during recovery is a common clinical challenge. Bernthal et al. prospectively followed 141 patients treated both operatively and nonoperatively for pediatric lateral condyle fractures, demonstrating that temporary elbow stiffness is an almost universal finding after these injuries and that the greatest gains in ROM occur within the first 12 weeks after injury, with continued recovery for up to one year. 6 Older age, longer duration of immobilization, and greater fracture severity were identified as independent predictors of delayed motion recovery. Importantly, however, this cohort included patients managed without surgery, and the quality of fracture reduction achieved at the time of surgical fixation was not examined as a potential determinant of this early postoperative course.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate whether the magnitude of immediate postoperative displacement is associated with early elbow motion limitation in surgically treated pediatric lateral condyle fractures. We use the term “early elbow motion limitation” to describe ROM deficits observed within the first year after surgery, a period during which postoperative recovery is known to be ongoing. We hypothesized that greater residual displacement on immediate postoperative radiographs would be associated with a higher risk of early limitation of elbow ROM. In addition, we sought to identify a clinically relevant displacement threshold that could serve as a practical intraoperative target for optimizing early functional outcomes.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital, which waived the requirement for informed consent given the retrospective nature of the study. We retrospectively reviewed 63 pediatric patients who underwent surgical fixation for lateral condyle fractures of the humerus at our institution between March 2015 and August 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients under 12 years of age, (2) isolated lateral condyle fractures of the distal humerus treated with either ORIF or CRPP, (3) availability of postoperative radiographs obtained during the entire follow-up period, and (4) minimum follow-up of 3 months after surgery, as previous studies have shown that the majority of elbow motion recovery occurs within the first 12 weeks following pediatric lateral condyle fractures, 6 and that the average time to return to full range of motion is approximately 82–113 days, 7 supporting 3 months as the threshold beyond which persistent motion deficits represent a clinically meaningful outcome. Exclusion criteria included open fractures, associated elbow dislocations, pathological fractures, combined injuries, or cases without adequate follow-up radiographs.

Surgical procedures

All procedures were performed by two orthopedic surgeons, who are co-authors of this study. The choice of surgical method was based on intraoperative assessment of fracture displacement and articular congruity under fluoroscopy. For CRPP, reduction was achieved by varus stress and supination of the forearm, and when necessary, a Kirschner wire (K-wire) was used as a joystick to manipulate the fragment. In ORIF, the lateral approach was used to expose the fracture site, and fixation was achieved with K-wires. After closed or open reduction, fractures were stabilized with either two or three smooth K-wires. The choice of pin number was based on intraoperative stability after reduction. Postoperative immobilization was maintained with a long-arm cast until radiographic evidence of union was confirmed, after which the cast was removed and active range-of-motion exercises were initiated. The mean duration of cast immobilization was approximately 6 weeks.

Clinical and radiographic assessment

Clinical variables included age, sex, duration of follow-up, cast duration, time to surgery, and postoperative elbow ROM. Radiographic variables included preoperative fracture type according to the Jakob classification system, union time, and immediate postoperative displacement. Surgical variables included number of pins and reduction method (open or closed reduction). Immediate postoperative displacement was defined as the maximal gap or step-off along the entire fracture line, measured on both the anteroposterior and internal oblique radiographs obtained immediately after surgery; the larger of the two values was recorded as the displacement (Figures 1 and 2). All displacement measurements were performed by a single fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon, and the values from the first measurement were used for all analyses. To assess intra-observer reliability, the same observer repeated all measurements on the same radiographs after an interval of more than 6 months, blinded to the initial values and to the patients’ clinical outcomes. Radiographic union was defined as the presence of bridging callus and cortical continuity across the fracture site on anteroposterior, lateral, and oblique radiographs. 3 The primary outcome was early elbow motion limitation, defined as a clinically meaningful ROM deficit occurring within the first year after surgery, the recognized primary window of postoperative recovery. 6 ROM was assessed based on chart documentation at the final follow-up visit within 12 months postoperatively; for patients with a follow-up duration exceeding 12 months, ROM was evaluated at the last available visit within this period. Patients whose medical records described elbow motion as “full ROM” or documented a ROM ≥ 140° were classified as having normal ROM, reflecting our institutional documentation practices. In cases of suspected motion limitation based on comparison with the contralateral unaffected elbow, the exact measured ROM values were explicitly recorded in the chart, and these values were used for analysis. A ROM threshold of 140° was used to operationally define early elbow motion limitation, based on previously reported normative values for pediatric elbow motion.8,9

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between the normal ROM group and the early motion limitation group. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test due to the small sample size. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of early elbow motion limitation. The variables entered into the model included age, sex, pin number, reduction method, postoperative displacement, preoperative fracture classification (Jakob classification), cast duration, and time to surgery. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of postoperative displacement and to determine the optimal threshold value. Intra-observer reliability of displacement measurement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement of single measurements. An ICC above 0.90 was considered to indicate excellent reliability. The optimal cutoff value was determined using Youden’s index to maximize the combined sensitivity and specificity. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

The analysis comprised 63 patients, with 51 in the normal ROM group and 12 in the early elbow motion limitation group (19.0%). The mean follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 6.8 ± 6.4 months (range, 3–48 months). Four patients had a follow-up duration exceeding 12 months (14, 18, 18, and 48 months); all four demonstrated full ROM (≥140°) at their last assessment within the 12-month postoperative period. The mean ROM at final follow-up was 127.5° ± 4.5° in the early elbow motion limitation group. All 63 fractures achieved radiographic union at a mean of 5.87 ± 1.42 weeks.

The baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Intra-observer reliability of displacement measurement was excellent, with an ICC of 0.977 (95% CI, 0.96 to 0.99). Patients in the early elbow motion limitation group had greater immediate postoperative displacement (1.53 ± 0.72 mm vs. 0.79 ± 0.62 mm, p = 0.002). Patients in the early motion limitation group were also older than those in the normal ROM group (6.5 ± 2.1 years vs. 5.2 ± 2.1 years, p = 0.038). No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding sex distribution, number of pins, union time, Jakob classification, reduction method, cast duration, or time to surgery.

Among the total patients, 19 underwent open reduction and 44 underwent closed reduction. The immediate postoperative displacement was 1.1 ± 0.5 mm in patients treated with open reduction and 0.9 ± 0.8 mm in those treated with closed reduction, showing no significant difference between the two approaches (p = 0.223).

Multivariable logistic regression identified postoperative displacement as the only independent predictor of early elbow motion limitation (OR 5.95, 95% CI 1.44–24.56; p = 0.014), whereas age, sex, pin number, reduction method, preoperative fracture classification, cast duration, and time to surgery were not significant (Table 2). In multivariable linear regression for union time, none of the tested factors showed a significant association.

ROC analysis revealed that immediate postoperative displacement had a moderate ability to predict early elbow motion limitation with an area under the curve of 0.782. The statistically optimal cutoff determined by Youden’s index was 0.90 mm, with a sensitivity of 91.7% and specificity of 56.9%. For clinical applicability, a cutoff of 1.0 mm was adopted, yielding a sensitivity of 83.3% and a specificity of 58.8% (Figure 3). Using this 1.0 mm threshold, the early motion limitation rate was markedly higher in patients with displacement ≥1.0 mm (10/31, 32.3%) than in those with displacement <1.0 mm (2/32, 6.3%, p = 0.011).

No major complications were observed in this series. All fractures achieved union without nonunion, deep infection, or nerve injury. Minor complications included pin site infection in two patients, which resolved with local wound care without requiring surgical intervention. Radiographic findings such as spur formation were noted in several patients; however, these did not lead to functional limitations. No patient experienced severe elbow stiffness requiring physical therapy referral or additional procedures. Additionally, no patient in either group had associated elbow dislocation, or other findings consistent with gross elbow instability.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that immediate postoperative displacement was the most significant predictor of early elbow motion limitation in pediatric lateral condyle fractures. Although older age was associated with early motion limitation on univariate analysis, it was not retained as an independent predictor in the multivariable model. This indicates that the quality of articular reduction, objectively measured on immediate postoperative radiographs, is a critical factor in achieving optimal functional recovery. Furthermore, our threshold analysis identified 1.0 mm as a clinically practical reference value for postoperative displacement, below which the risk of early motion limitation was significantly reduced. Although the absolute difference in displacement between the two groups was less than 1 mm (1.53 ± 0.72 mm vs. 0.79 ± 0.62 mm, p = 0.002), the corresponding outcome difference was substantial: the rate of early motion limitation in patients with displacement ≥ 1.0 mm was 32.3% compared with 6.3% in those below this threshold (p = 0.011).

The present results should be interpreted in the context of how postoperative motion limitation has been defined across the literature. Limitation of elbow motion following treatment of lateral condyle fractures exists on a clinical spectrum. At the severe end, postoperative stiffness requiring physical therapy referral or surgical intervention has been reported in approximately 21% of surgically treated patients, with older age, open reduction, and concomitant elbow dislocation identified as independent risk factors; notably, that cohort included patients with concurrent elbow dislocations and more severe fracture patterns, which likely contributed to the higher rate of clinically significant stiffness requiring additional intervention. 10 The present study, by contrast, was restricted to isolated lateral condyle fractures without associated dislocations, and none of the patients in our cohort exhibited severe stiffness of this magnitude. At the milder end, transient motion deficits are commonly observed after these injuries; prospective data from a mixed operative and nonoperative cohort demonstrate that meaningful ROM recovery continues for up to one year after injury, with older age, longer immobilization, and greater fracture severity predicting a slower rate of recovery. 6 By focusing on this milder end of the spectrum in a surgically treated cohort, the present study identified immediate postoperative displacement as the primary independent predictor of early elbow motion limitation, with older age showing a significant association on univariate but not multivariable analysis.

In our study, maintaining postoperative displacement below 1.0 mm was associated with a markedly lower early motion limitation rate (6.3%) than displacement ≥1.0 mm (32.3%). Notably, this threshold is consistent with the findings of Salgueiro et al., who demonstrated that postoperative displacement exceeding 1.0 mm was independently associated with delayed fracture healing (OR = 4.78), suggesting that 1.0 mm may represent a clinically meaningful boundary for fracture union. 11 Song et al. demonstrated that even completely displaced and rotated fractures can be successfully managed with closed techniques when meticulous fluoroscopic confirmation is employed to achieve reduction within 2 mm, underscoring the shared principle that reduction quality is central to favorable outcomes. 12 Xie et al. similarly reported mean postoperative displacements of 1.1–1.2 mm with no complications in severely displaced fractures treated with closed reduction and percutaneous pinning. 2 Taken together, these findings consistently support the importance of minimizing residual displacement for achieving favorable outcomes, including restoration of elbow motion, union, and avoidance of complications, even in fractures with severe initial displacement.

An important consideration in the treatment of these fractures is the choice between open and closed reduction. Open reduction is typically indicated when closed reduction fails or when fracture displacement is severe, with the advantage of direct visualization for more accurate anatomical restoration of the articular surface. However, in our series, open reduction did not result in better reduction quality than closed reduction, and immediate postoperative displacement did not show any significant difference between the two approaches (p = 0.223). This finding may be explained by selection bias, as cases with greater initial displacement or rotational instability are more likely to be treated with an open approach, making an anatomic reduction inherently more challenging. Even with direct visualization, achieving precise reduction can be technically demanding, particularly in cases with substantial comminution or rotational instability. Consistent with our findings, Xie et al. compared outcomes between CRPP and ORIF and found no significant differences between the two groups in postoperative displacement, and both methods yielded satisfactory outcomes. 2 In a subsequent analysis of fractures displaced more than 4 mm, they also reported no significant difference in postoperative fracture gap between CRPP and ORIF (1.2 ± 0.5 mm vs 1.0 ± 0.5 mm, p = 0.10). 13 These findings collectively support that open reduction does not necessarily guarantee superior reduction quality and that a meticulously performed closed reduction can achieve comparable alignment with less iatrogenic soft-tissue injury.

While our findings emphasize the potential importance of achieving optimal reduction, another related issue in the surgical management of pediatric lateral condyle fractures is the choice of internal fixation method, particularly between K-wires and cannulated screws. Our study cohort uniformly utilized K-wires, which precludes a direct comparative analysis of outcomes associated with alternative modalities, such as screw fixation. This comparison is clinically relevant, as K-wires and screws exhibit different complication profiles and biomechanical properties. K-wires are often favored due to the advantage of minimal risk of physeal damage and the ability for non-operative removal in an outpatient setting, thereby avoiding the need for a second surgical procedure and general anesthesia. 14 However, recent comparative meta-analyses indicated that screw fixation may provide greater stability through compression and yields better results regarding specific complications. 15 Specifically, the risk of elbow motion limitation was found to be significantly higher in the K-wire group compared with the screw fixation group in one quantitative meta-analysis (risk ratio for motion limitation = 3.75; p < 0.01). 16 This observation is not necessarily in conflict with our central finding; rather, it may reflect that more rigid fixation better preserves the reduction quality achieved intraoperatively throughout the healing period, suggesting that reduction precision and fixation stability may act as sequential determinants of early motion recovery.

Regarding fracture union, all fractures in our series achieved radiographic consolidation, and no significant association was found between union time and displacement, age, reduction method, or other variables examined. This finding aligns with previous reports indicating that union is generally reliable in pediatric lateral condyle fractures when adequate stability is achieved, irrespective of the degree of initial displacement or surgical approach.17,18 However, the absence of a significant association in our study should be interpreted with caution given the relatively small sample size and the limited statistical power to detect associations with union time. It is likely that union is influenced by multiple interacting factors, including reduction quality, 11 fixation stability, 14 and vascularity, 3 and larger-scale studies are needed to better characterize the determinants of union in this population.

Taken together, these findings reaffirm the importance of immediate postoperative displacement as an outcome-relevant parameter following surgical fixation of pediatric lateral condyle fractures. While the 1.0 mm threshold may serve as a useful reference for assessing reduction quality and counseling families regarding expected recovery, it should not be interpreted as a strict intraoperative target mandating repeated reduction attempts or conversion to open reduction, particularly given the generally transient nature of early motion limitation.

This study had several limitations. Firstly, its retrospective design and relatively small cohort may have compromised the statistical power and external validity. Secondly, the uniform use of K-wire fixation across the cohort precluded a comparative analysis with alternative fixation modalities such as cannulated screws, as discussed above. Thirdly, although intra-observer reliability of displacement measurement was excellent, all measurements were performed by a single observer and inter-observer reliability was not assessed; the reproducibility of the proposed threshold across different raters therefore remains to be confirmed. Finally, the relatively short mean follow-up duration (7 months) may not have captured the full trajectory of motion recovery, and the deficits identified in the present study may not represent permanent functional impairment. Future studies with longer follow-up periods are warranted to validate whether our proposed displacement threshold is consistently associated with long-term functional outcomes.

In conclusion, immediate postoperative displacement was independently associated with early elbow motion limitation after fixation of pediatric lateral condyle fractures. In our cohort, patients with postoperative displacement below 1.0 mm demonstrated a lower rate of early motion limitation. These findings suggest that minimizing residual displacement may contribute to improved early elbow motion recovery.
