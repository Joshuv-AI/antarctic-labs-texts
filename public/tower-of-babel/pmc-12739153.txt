Evaluating postoperative quality of life and satisfaction in physicians undergoing small incision lenticule extraction
Authors: Necip Kara, Serhat Ayla, Şaban Büyükbayram, Tansu Muazzez Baran, Nilufer Yesilirmak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall Incision Lenticule Extraction (SMILE) offers a minimally invasive refractive surgery option. We evaluated the impact of SMILE on visual satisfaction and quality of life among 75 physicians—non-surgeons (n = 45), microscopic surgeons (n = 15), and non-microscopic surgeons (n = 15)—by comparing pre- and postoperative questionnaire responses. A self-developed survey with strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.89) was used, and data were analysed with ANOVA and non-parametric tests. Postoperative satisfaction after SMILE was significantly higher than preoperative satisfaction with spectacles and contact lenses among non-surgeons (p < 0.001) and microscopic surgeons (p = 0.016 vs. spectacles; p = 0.003 vs. lenses). Among non-microscopic surgeons, satisfaction after SMILE was greater than with contact lenses (p = 0.001) and showed a trend versus spectacles (p = 0.060). All subgroups reported marked quality-of-life improvements, including reduced visual discomfort, increased self-confidence, and enhanced performance in daily tasks. No significant differences were detected between specialty subgroups. These findings demonstrate that SMILE surgery substantially improves self-reported visual satisfaction and quality of life in physicians with high visual demands and support its clinical applicability in similarly demanding professional settings. Larger, prospective studies incorporating objective visual metrics are warranted to confirm and extend these results.Keywords: Small incision lenticule extraction, Quality of life, Physician satisfaction, Postoperative outcomesSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introductıon

Refractive surgery has become increasingly popular among healthcare professionals due to its potential to significantly improve visual acuity and overall quality of life. Among the various refractive surgery techniques, SMILE has gained attention for its minimally invasive nature and promising outcomes1,2. SMILE involves the use of a femtosecond laser to create a lenticule within the corneal stroma, which is then removed through a small incision, minimizing the disruption to the corneal surface3.

Several studies have demonstrated that SMILE is associated with high patient satisfaction and significant improvements in visual quality4,5. Additionally, SMILE has been associated with lower incidences of dry eye symptoms and faster recovery times, further contributing to patient satisfaction6,7.

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting the efficacy of SMILE, there is a lack of focused research on the specific population of physicians who undergo this procedure. Physicians, due to their demanding professional environments, may experience unique benefits and challenges following refractive surgery. Assessing the impact of SMILE on their quality of life and professional satisfaction is crucial, as improved visual acuity can directly influence their clinical performance and job satisfaction8,9.

This study aims to evaluate the postoperative quality of life and satisfaction among physicians who have undergone SMILE surgery. By addressing this specific population, we seek to fill a gap in the existing literature and provide insights that could inform clinical practice and patient counseling10,11.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a prospective observational cohort design to evaluate the postoperative quality of life and satisfaction among physicians who underwent SMILE surgery. A total of 75 physicians participated in the study. Participants were categorized into three groups based on their surgical practice: non-surgeons (n = 45), microscopic surgeons (n = 15), and non-microscopic surgeons (n = 15). Participants were excluded if they were nonphysicians, had previously undergone refractive surgery or ocular surgery in either eye, or had any history of ocular disease that could limit visual outcomes. Inclusion criteria required participants to have undergone SMILE surgery at single center with same surgeon and to have completed both preoperative and postoperative assessments.

The primary endpoint was the total satisfaction score at 3 months postoperatively, derived from the 32-item questionnaire. Secondary endpoints included subgroup comparisons (non-surgeons, microscopic surgeons, non-microscopic surgeons), quality-of-life domain scores, and time to return to work.

Data collection

Data were collected using a validated 32-question survey designed to assess both visual satisfaction and quality of life, incorporating items from the Quality of Life Impact of Refractive Correction (QIRC) and Quality of Vision (QoV) questionnaires (Table 1). The questionnaire was administered during routine follow-up visits at the clinic and included assessments of satisfaction with visual aids (glasses and contact lenses) prior to surgery, as well as satisfaction with visual outcomes following SMILE surgery. Postoperative questionnaires were administered at the 3-month follow-up visit, allowing sufficient time for visual recovery and stabilization of satisfaction levels.

Participants were asked to report their gender, age, and specialty. The survey assessed visual discomfort by asking participants to rate symptoms such as glare, halos, blurred vision, difficulty focusing, and dry eye before and after the surgery. Quality of life was evaluated by inquiring about the impact of visual changes on daily activities, including work performance and general daily tasks.

In the surgical satisfaction survey, each of the 32 questions was rated on a scale from 1 to 5, where 1 represented complete dissatisfaction and 5 represented complete satisfaction. Responses marked as ‘I don’t know/use, cannot evaluate’ were treated as missing values and excluded from total score calculations. The total satisfaction score for each participant was calculated by summing the individual scores from all 32 questions. These total scores were then used to compare overall satisfaction levels across the different groups.

A psychometric analysis of the adapted 32-item questionnaire was conducted to assess its reliability and structural validity. Cronbach’s alpha was used to evaluate internal consistency, while exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with principal component extraction and Varimax rotation was applied to identify underlying dimensions.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the baseline characteristics and outcomes for each group.

Descriptive statistics were aligned with distributional assumptions: mean ± SD for normally distributed data and median [IQR] for non-normally distributed variables.

Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, the Kruskal-Wallis test—a robust non-parametric alternative to one-way ANOVA—was utilized to compare differences across the three groups.

To compare preoperative and postoperative satisfaction levels (using glasses, contact lenses, and SMILE) within each group, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was utilized, as it is suitable for non-normally distributed paired data. This analysis allowed us to assess the changes in satisfaction before and after SMILE surgery within each group.

For categorical variables such as return-to-work time (≤ 2 days and ≤ 1 week), Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation was applied to account for small subgroup sizes.

Between-group analyses were adjusted for baseline preoperative SEQ and cylinder values to account for potential confounding related to initial refractive error.

Missing responses were handled using a complete-case approach, and all analyses were conducted on a per-protocol basis, including only participants who completed both preoperative and postoperative assessments.

Given the relatively small subgroup sizes, non-parametric rank-based tests were chosen instead of multivariable regression models to maintain parsimony and robustness.

Effect sizes (rank-biserial r) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for within-subject pre/post comparisons to complement p-values and quantify the magnitude of change.

Statistical significance was set at a p-value of less than 0.05 for all analyses, with all reported p-values being two-tailed.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Gaziantep University (Approval number:2023/159; Approval date: May 15, 2023). Participant recruitment and data collection were conducted between May 2023 and March 2024. Prior to participation, all subjects provided written informed consent. Confidentiality and privacy of participant data were rigorously protected, with all identifying information anonymized and securely stored.

Results

All postoperative data reflect responses collected at the 3-month follow-up visit after SMILE surgery. A total of 75 physicians were included in the study, divided into three groups: non-surgeons (n = 45), microscopic surgeons (n = 15), and non-microscopic surgeons (n = 15). The mean (± SD) age was 30.96 ± 4.88 years for the non-surgeon group, 30.09 ± 2.84 years for the microscopic surgeon group, and 31.93 ± 4.45 years for the non-microscopic surgeon group.

In terms of gender distribution, the non-surgeon group consisted of 44.4% males and 55.6% females. In the microscopic surgeon group, 80% were male and 20% were female. The non-microscopic surgeon group included 53.3% males and 46.7% females. No significant differences in age distribution or gender proportions were observed among the groups (p > 0.05). (Table 2)

Preoperative spherical equivalent (SEQ) differed significantly among subgroups (p < 0.001, Kruskal–Wallis test), with the microscopic surgeon group having the highest degree of myopia. Preoperative UDVA also varied significantly between groups (p = 0.002), reflecting baseline differences in uncorrected visual acuity. However, all other preoperative and postoperative parameters, including postoperative SEQ, cylinder, UDVA, and CDVA, were comparable among the three physician subgroups (all p > 0.05; Table 3). Within-subject analysis demonstrated significant improvement in refractive and visual outcomes after SMILE across all groups (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). The within-subject pre/post comparisons showed large effect sizes (rank-biserial r > 0.6, 95% CI 0.48–0.71), consistent with a strong magnitude of visual and refractive improvement across subgroups. Median postoperative SEQ values approached emmetropia, and postoperative UDVA and CDVA values indicated satisfactory visual recovery across subgroups.

Descriptive statistics revealed that the mean satisfaction scores were highest post-SMILE compared to preoperative glasses and contact lenses across all groups. Specifically, the mean satisfaction scores for SMILE were 109.87 (SD = 11.89), significantly higher than those for glasses (Mean = 87.43, SD = 23.04) and contact lenses (Mean = 78.94, SD = 34.28). Because significant differences in preoperative SEQ and UDVA were identified across groups, an additional analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to control for these covariates.

After adjustment, postoperative satisfaction scores remained significantly different among the three groups (F(2, 47) = 8.566, p = 0.001, partial η² = 0.267).

Neither preoperative SEQ (p = 0.124) nor preoperative UDVA (p = 0.419) significantly affected satisfaction.

The internal consistency of the 32-item questionnaire was found to be high (Cronbach’s α = 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.89). Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using principal component extraction and Varimax rotation identified three underlying factors—visual discomfort, daily functionality, and emotional well-being—which together explained 68.2% of the total variance. These findings support the reliability and construct validity of the adapted instrument used in this study.

Dıscussıon

Our study’s findings align with the broader literature on the efficacy and patient satisfaction associated with SMILE surgery, particularly in high-demand professional populations. Consistent with previous studies, our results indicate that SMILE surgery leads to significant improvements in visual satisfaction and quality of life across different surgical groups.

Wei et al. (2020) reported high levels of patient satisfaction post-SMILE, with similar visual outcomes to those observed in our study12. This study emphasized the importance of visual quality and stability, particularly in professional settings where rapid recovery and minimal complications are essential. Similarly, Bokhary et al. (2022) demonstrated that SMILE provided comparable refractive outcomes to other refractive surgeries, with patients reporting high satisfaction levels due to the minimal postoperative discomfort and stable refractive outcomes over time13.

The comparison with another study further supports our findings, as this study highlighted that SMILE and LASIK both resulted in high patient satisfaction and quality of life improvements14. However, SMILE had an edge in terms of fewer postoperative complications like dry eye, a finding echoed in our study where physicians reported minimal postoperative symptoms, contributing to their high satisfaction rates.

In contrast, Ma, Pillar, & Krueger (2020) focused on the long-term outcomes of SMILE, showing that the procedure maintains its efficacy and patient satisfaction over several years15. This long-term stability is crucial for professionals who rely heavily on their vision for their daily work. Our findings support this notion, with similar high satisfaction rates observed even months after the procedure.

Moreover, Han et al. (2016) provided insights into the specific quality of life improvements following SMILE surgery, which our study also confirmed16. The use of standardized questionnaires like QIRC and QoV in both studies allows for a robust comparison of patient-reported outcomes, further validating the improvements in quality of life observed in our study.

Additionally, Lee et al. (2012)examined the satisfaction levels of physicians who underwent corneal refractive surgeries, including LASIK and LASEK, and found high satisfaction rates with no significant differences between physicians and other healthcare workers​17. Their study also highlighted that even among surgeons who perform delicate manual tasks, there were no significant differences in satisfaction levels post-surgery. This finding aligns with our results, reinforcing the notion that SMILE is a reliable option for professionals who require high visual acuity and rapid recovery, even in visually demanding roles.

The clinical implications of these findings are significant. SMILE surgery may be considered a viable option for individuals in high-demand professions, such as physicians, where optimal visual acuity and rapid recovery are paramount. The consistency of the positive outcomes reported across different studies, including ours, highlights the procedure’s reliability and effectiveness.

This study provides useful descriptive data on the self-reported satisfaction and quality of life among physicians following SMILE surgery; however, several limitations temper the generalizability of the findings. The prospective observational cohort design does not permit conclusions regarding causality, and the reliance on self-reported measures introduces potential for bias. The subgroup sizes were small and imbalanced, limiting the power of statistical comparisons. Although adapted from previously validated instruments, the questionnaire was not formally validated in this population. However, internal consistency was confirmed via Cronbach’s alpha (Cronbach’s α = 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.89). Future research should consider larger, prospective studies incorporating objective visual metrics to strengthen the evidence base.

Moreover, comparative studies that include other refractive surgeries, such as LASIK and PRK, in similar professional cohorts could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relative benefits of each procedure. Finally, exploring the psychological and cognitive impacts of improved vision in high-stakes professions could offer new perspectives on the broader implications of refractive surgery.

The single-center design, with all surgeries performed by the same surgeon, may also limit generalizability, as variations in surgical experience or technique could influence postoperative satisfaction.

In addition, while the study incorporated objective refractive and visual parameters (e.g., SEQ, cylinder, UDVA, and CDVA), it relied primarily on subjective self-reported outcomes without including other objective visual quality metrics such as contrast sensitivity or higher-order aberrations. Future studies should integrate both subjective and comprehensive objective measures to more fully characterize postoperative visual performance.

Satisfaction and quality-of-life outcomes were assessed at 3 months postoperatively, which likely captures the stabilization phase of visual recovery but may not reflect long-term adaptation.

Another potential limitation relates to regression to the mean, as participants with higher baseline refractive errors or lower preoperative visual acuity may have shown greater postoperative gains. We partly mitigated this risk by adjusting postoperative satisfaction for baseline SEQ and UDVA using ANCOVA; however, residual regression-to-the-mean and ceiling effects may persist—particularly among participants with mild preoperative errors or near-normal visual function, who have limited room for improvement. These statistical phenomena should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of postoperative changes in satisfaction and quality-of-life scores.

Future studies should include longer follow-up intervals to evaluate sustained outcomes and potential long-term changes in satisfaction and visual function.

Conclusıon

This study offers preliminary evidence that SMILE surgery may enhance physicians’ self-reported visual satisfaction and quality of life, particularly in visually demanding fields. While the findings are promising, they should be interpreted with caution due to methodological limitations, including the use of a self-developed questionnaire with demonstrated internal consistency and a relatively modest sample size. Future research involving standardized assessment tools and larger, more diverse populations is recommended to further validate and expand upon these findings.
