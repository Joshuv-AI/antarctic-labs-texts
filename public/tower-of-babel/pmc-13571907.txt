IL-1 Blockade to Resolve Persistent Inflammation After STEMI
Authors: Marco Giuseppe Del Buono, Michele Golino, Antonio Abbate
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the report by Graesser et al,1 showing in a large ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) cohort that leukocyte counts persistently elevated at 72 hours, rather than the magnitude of the initial inflammatory peak, identify patients with larger infarcts, worse left ventricular function, and substantially higher 1- and 5-year mortality. This translational study reframes nonresolving inflammation, rather than peak, as the clinically actionable target after MI.

This aligns with our findings using interleukin-1 (IL-1) blockade with anakinra in STEMI. In a pooled analysis of the VCU-ART trials, anakinra produced a significantly greater reduction in white blood cell and neutrophil counts at 72 hours vs placebo, persisting at 14 days and resolving by 3 months after discontinuation.2,3 Anakinra-treated patients who later had heart-failure events showed a blunted reduction in leukocyte and neutrophil counts at 72 hours and 14 days vs those without such events,2 the same "nonresolving" phenotype Graesser et al1 now link to mortality, but observed within a treated population. This suggests on-treatment leukocyte kinetics could serve as an early pharmacodynamic biomarker of IL-1 blockade response.

We also showed that anakinra inhibits spontaneous ex vivo IL-6 production by STEMI leukocytes without impairing toll-like receptor–mediated antibacterial responses,4 suggesting IL-1 blockade selectively dampens sterile inflammation rather than causing global immunosuppression, a relevant safety consideration if leukocyte resolution becomes a therapeutic target. The same pooled analysis2 showed anakinra also transiently increased eosinophil counts at 72 hours and 14 days, no longer apparent at 3 months. We later found a dose-duration–dependent rise in eosinophils in anakinra-treated heart failure patients that correlated with improved cardiorespiratory fitness and lower natriuretic peptides,5 consistent with a reparative role for eosinophils postmyocardial infarction and suggesting IL-1 blockade reshapes the leukocyte differential rather than simply lowering counts.

Together, these data suggest the unfavorable “nonresolving inflammation” phenotype identified by Graesser et al1 is not only a risk marker but a modifiable target. We propose IL-1 blockade with anakinra, by accelerating resolution of leukocytosis and neutrophilia while preserving eosinophilic and antibacterial responses, as a candidate strategy to blunt nonresolving inflammation after STEMI.
