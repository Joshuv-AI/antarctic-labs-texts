Targeting Hyaluronan Signaling Overcomes Primary Effusion Lymphoma Cells Resistance to Rapamycin
Authors: Jungang Chen, Margaret Qin, Jiaojiao Fan, Shengyu Mu, Lu Dai, Zhiqiang Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The oncogenic γ-herpesvirus known as the Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV) is a principal causative agent of several cancers arising in patients with compromised immune systems. One of KSHV-related malignancies, primary effusion lymphoma (PEL), comprises transformed B cells harboring the viral episome and arises preferentially within the pleural or peritoneal cavities of patients including those with HIV infection. PEL is a rapidly progressing malignancy with a median survival time of several months even with conventional chemotherapy. One of the major reasons causing PEL chemotherapy failure is multidrug chemoresistance of lymphoma cells, however, there are only limited data exploring the mechanisms of PEL chemoresistance. In the current study, we demonstrate the role of hyaluronan (HA) signaling activation in rapamycin resistance in PEL cells, and that targeting HA signaling by small HA oligosaccharides (oHA) can augment rapamycin efficacy against PEL expansion in vivo. Introduction

The oncogenic γ-herpesvirus known as the Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV) is a principal causative agent of several cancers arising in patients with compromised immune systems. One of KSHV-related malignancies, primary effusion lymphoma (PEL), comprises transformed B cells harboring the viral episome and arises preferentially within the pleural or peritoneal cavities of patients including those with HIV infection [1]. PEL is a rapidly progressing malignancy with a median survival time of approximately 6 months even with conventional chemotherapy [2]. One of the major reasons causing PEL chemotherapy failure is multidrug chemoresistance of lymphoma cells, however, there are only limited data exploring the mechanisms of PEL chemoresistance and about the development of overcome strategies.

Hyaluronan (HA, also named as hyaluronic acid) is a large, linear glycosaminoglycan composed of repeating disaccharides of glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine, which plays a structural role through its unique hydrodynamic properties and interactions with other extracellular matrix (ECM) components [3]. Moreover, HA has an instructive role in signaling that is implicated in numerous aspects of cancer progression including chemoresistance, through binding to several cellular receptors such as CD44 and LYVE-13. HA-receptor interaction has an important role in a variety of activities crucial to multidrug chemoresistance, including regulation of activities of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), ATP-binding cassette (ABC) drug efflux transporters, and monocarboxylate transporters (MCT) [4]. There are several approaches for targeting HA-receptor interactions and attenuating downstream signaling, including small HA oligosaccharides (oHA), which contain 6–18 sugar residues and compete for endogenous polymeric HA, thus replacing high affinity, multivalent and cooperative interactions with low affinity, low valency receptor interactions [4, 5]. Our previous work demonstrated that there were higher levels of HA production and HA synthase genes (Has1, Has2 and Has3) expression in chemoresistant PEL cell lines (BCBL-1, BCP-1) than in chemosensitive ones (BC-1, BC-3) [6]. We also found that oHA treatment could effectively resensitize resistant PEL cells to paclitaxel or doxorubicin especially at their low-dose treatments [6]. In the current study, we showed that HA production and signaling were involved in PEL cells resistance to rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor used for PEL treatment [7]. Furthermore, oHA treatment significantly increased the antitumor potency of rapamycin to chemoresistant PEL cell lines in vitro and in vivo.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Reagents

KSHV-infected PEL cells (BCBL-1) was kindly provided by Dr. Pinghui Feng (Cleveland Clinic Research) and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) with supplements as described previously [6]. BCP-1 and BC-1 were purchased from were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and cultured as recommended by the manufacturer. BC-1Rap, a new rapamycin-resistant PEL cell line, was generated as reported previously [8], by exposing the parental cell line BC-1 to an increasing dose of rapamycin (from 1 nM to 1 µM) for around 15 weeks. Clones were obtained by extracting and expanding a single colony from a colony formation assay. Clones were maintained at 100 nM of rapamycin. Hyaluronan oligosaccharides (oHA) were prepared as described previously [9]. All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Cell Viability Assays

Cell viability was assessed using both WST-1 and trypan blue exclusion assays. For WST-1 assays (Roche), after the period of treatment of cells, 10 µL/well of cell proliferation reagent, WST-1 (4-[3-(4-Iodophenyl)-2-(4-nitro-phenyl)-2 H-5-tetrazo-lio]-1,3-benzene disulfonate), was added and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The absorbance of samples was measured by using a microplate reader at 490 nm. Data was normalized as the inhibition relative to the vehicle control. For trypan blue exclusion assays, cells were incubated with 0.4% trypan blue (MP Biomedicals) and observed under light microscopy. Relative cell viability was determined after assessment of at least 1000 cells per condition for each experiment using the following formula: (#live cells/#total cells for experimental conditions)/ (#live cells/#total cells for vehicle-treated control cells).

Western Blot

Total cell lysates (20 µg) were resolved by 10% SDS–PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and immunoblotted with antibodies to phosphor (p)-Akt/total (t)-Akt, p-mTOR/t-mTOR, Emmprin and Tubulin as a loading control (Cell Signaling). Immunoreactive bands were identified using an enhanced chemiluminescence reaction (Perkin-Elmer), and visualized by autoradiography.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated by using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen), and cDNA was synthesized using a SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix Kit (Invitrogen). Specific primers used for amplification of individual target gene were listed in Table S1. The amplification was carried out using an iCycler IQ Real-Time PCR Detection System, and cycle threshold (Ct) values were tabulated in triplicate for each gene of interest in each experiment. “No template” (water) controls were used to ensure minimal background contamination. Using mean Ct values tabulated for each gene, and the paired Ct values for β-actin gene as a loading control, the fold changes for experimental groups relative to assigned control groups were calculated by using automated iQ5 2.0 software (Bio-rad).

ELSA for HA

Concentrations of HA in culture supernatants or plasma from patients were determined using HA enzyme-linked sorbent assay (ELSA) kit (Echelon) according to the manufacturers’ instructions as described previously [10].

PEL Xenograft Models

NOD/SCID mice, 6–8-week old, male (Jackson Laboratory), 1 × 107 BCBL-1 cells in 200 µL RMPI-1640 without FBS were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) and then mice were randomized into treatment groups of 4 mice as described previously (Ref). Rapamycin (Rap, 2.0 mg/kg), oHA (0.5 mg/kg), Rap + oHA or vehicle were administered i.p. initially at 48 h after BCBL-1 injections, 3 days/week for 3 weeks. Weights were recorded weekly as a surrogate measure of tumor progression.

Statistical Analysis

Significant differences between experimental and control groups were determined using the two-tailed Student’s t-test. The 50% Cytotoxicity Concentrations (CC50) were calculated from the dose-response curves using GraphPad Prism 10.

Results and Discussion

oHA Treatment Overcomes PEL Cells Resistance to Rapamycin In Vitro

PEL has a highly active mTOR pathway, which makes mTOR a potential therapeutic target [8]. However, several recent studies also reported the resistance of PEL cells to rapamycin [7, 8, 11]. Thus, we first treated chemoresistant PEL cell lines (BCBL-1 and BCP-1) by a dose range of rapamycin with or without oHA (100 µg/mL) as described previously [6]. Our results showed that the combination of oHA significantly increased the antitumor potency of rapamycin to BCBL-1 and BCP-1 cells even at low concentrations (Fig. 1A and B). Mechanistically, we found that the combination of oHA greatly reduced the phosphorylation of Akt and mTOR kinases in rapamycin treated PEL cells by using western blot (Fig. 1C).

The Hyaluronan Signaling Activities are Greatly Increased in Rapamycin-resistant BC-1Rap Cell Line

Next, we established a new rapamycin-resistant PEL cell line, BC-1Rap, which displaying > 1000 folds of CC50 to rapamycin when compared to the parental cell line BC-1 (Fig. 2A and B). BC-1Rap cell line was generated as described in the Methods. Interestingly, we found that BC-1Rap significantly increased HA production when compared to the parental cell line BC-1 (Fig. 3A), by using an enzyme-linked sorbent assay (ELSA) as described previously [10]. BC-1Rap also significantly increased the transcriptional levels of HA synthase genes (Has1, Has2 and Has3) as well as Emmprin, a multifunctional glycoprotein (also named as CD147) which has been reported to regulate HA production from PEL cells [6, 10] (Fig. 3B). Our western blot results confirmed the expression of Emmprin glycoforms was greatly increased in BC-1Rap when compared to the parental cell line BC-1 (Fig. 3C).

oHA Treatment Augments Rapamycin Efficacy Against PEL Expansion In Vivo

We have established a PEL xenograft model using non-obese diabetic/severe-combined immunodeficiency (NOD/SCID) mice [12]. Intraperitoneal injection of 10 [7] BCBL-1 cells into the peritoneal cavity of 6–8 week-old male NOD/SCID mice resulted in rapid tumor growth and massive ascites within 3–4 weeks when compared to the PBS injected control group [12]. Using this animal model, we found that oHA (0.5 mg/kg) combined with rapamycin (2.0 mg/kg) treatments augmented the suppression of PEL expansion in mice when compared to rapamycin alone treated group (Fig. 4A). However, oHA alone treatment showed no effects on PEL growth in mice. At the end of the treatment period, we collected the ascites from sacrificed mice for a comparison. Our results showed that oHA combined with rapamycin treatments caused more reduction of ascites formation than rapamycin alone treatment in mice (Fig. 4B). Again, oHA alone treatment did not affect ascites formation in mice.

In summary, our findings support the role of HA signaling activation in rapamycin resistance in PEL cells, and that targeting HA signaling by oHA can augment rapamycin efficacy against PEL expansion in vivo. Currently, combinational chemotherapy is the standard of care for PEL [13, 14], however, the myelosuppressive effects of systemic cytotoxic chemotherapy synergize with those caused by antiretroviral therapy or immune suppression [13, 15]. Therefore, our results provide the framework for the development of HA-targeted therapies clinical trials for PEL patients, which may represent promising adjuncts to reduce chemotherapy toxicity, improve treatment and prolong patients’ survival.
