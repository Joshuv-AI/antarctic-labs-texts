Paint Outside the Box: Synthesizing and Selecting Training Data for Visual Grounding
Zilin Du, Haoxin Li, Jianfei Yu, Boyang Li

Abstract
Visual grounding aims to localize the image regions based on a textual query. Given the difficulty of large-scale data curation, we investigate how to effectively learn visual grounding under data-scarce settings in this paper. To address the data scarcity, we propose a novel framework, POBF (Paint Outside the Box and Filter). POBF synthesizes images by inpainting outside the box, tackling a label misalignment issue encountered in previous works. Furthermore, POBF leverages an innovative filtering scheme to select the most effective training data. This scheme combines a hardness score and an overfitting score, balanced by a penalty term. Extensive experiments across four benchmark datasets demonstrate that POBF consistently improves performance, achieving an average gain of 5.83% over the real-data-only method and outperforming leading baselines by 2.29%–3.85% in accuracy. We validate the robustness and generalizability of POBF across various generative models, training data sizes, and model architectures.

Problem Definition

Visual grounding is the task of identifying the target region
R\subseteq I
within the image that a natural language
T
refers to. Typically,
R
is represented as a bounding box
B=(x,y,w,h)
, where
(x,y)
specifies the box center coordinates, and
w
and
h
define its width and height. In this paper, we investigate the problem of learning visual grounding in data-scarce scenarios, namely data-scarce visual grounding, where only a very limited amount of annotated real training data is available. Following
Xue et al. (2024)
;
Chen et al. (2022)
;
Jiang and Zheng (2023)
, we simulate this setting by randomly sampling a small subset of the full training set.

4
Methodology

To address the data-scarce visual grounding problem, we introduce a novel, model-agnostic framework called POBF (Paint Outside the Box and Filter). As illustrated in Figure
2
, POBF consists of four stages. (a) Our approach begins with a data generation process, where we synthesize images and captions using image inpainting and captioning techniques. This process utilizes the image generator and captioner pretrained on large-scale image-caption pairs. (b) Next, we train a teacher model using the limited real data. (c) We then apply a filtering scheme to select effective synthetic images. Specifically, the well-trained teacher network assigns a hardness score, an overfitting score, and a penalty term to each synthetic image. (d) Finally, we retain the synthetic images with high scores and combine them with real data to train the student model. The proposed POBF framework is simple, effective, and does not rely on any auxiliary models pretrained with dense annotated data.

4.1
Cross-modality Data Generation

Image Generation with Inpainting.
We begin by synthesizing images through image inpainting, a process that regenerate masked regions within an image based on a given prompt, while keeping the unmasked areas intact. To address the label misalignment problem, we propose to paint outside of the bounding box instead. For each data point (
I,T,B
), we first use a captioning model
\mathcal{G}_{T}
to generate a caption
C
for
I
. The bounding box
B
, image
I
, and caption
C
are then input into the inpainting model
\mathcal{G}_{I}
, yielding a generated image
I^{\prime}
. As shown in Figure
2
(a), the red bus within the bounding box remains unchanged, making it strictly align with the ground truth bounding box after generation. The synthetic image
I^{\prime}
can be paired with the original text
T
and bounding box
B
to form a new training sample (
I^{\prime},T,B
). By generating
K
synthetic images per real sample, denoted as
\{I^{\prime}_{1},I^{\prime}_{2},\dots,I^{\prime}_{K}\}
, the training dataset effectively increases in size by a factor of
K
. A key hyperparameter in the inpainting process is
strength
, which controls the amount of noise added to the base image and, consequently influences the similarity between the generated output and the original image. To encourage data diversity, we set the
strength
parameter to a high value.

Text Generation with Captioning.
To augment the original text
T
, we feed the cropped image within the bounding box
B
to the captioning model
\mathcal{G}_{\text{T}}
, which generates a new caption
T^{\prime}
describing the object. The generated caption
T^{\prime}
can be paired with
B
and either the real image
I
or the generated image
I^{\prime}
to create a new training sample. In this work, we employ
p
-sampling
Holtzman et al. (2019)
to improve diversity.

4.2
Filtering Scheme

Figure 3:
Illustration of the inputs used to compute each score for a generated image. Here,
B
and
\hat{B}
denote the ground truth and predicted bounding box, respectively.

Recognizing that not all synthetic samples contribute positively to model performance
Chen et al. (2024a)
;
Chen et al. (2024e)
;
Malach and Shalev-Shwartz (2017)
;
Mahmoud et al. (2024)
, we propose a novel filtering scheme to select the most effective images for training. Initially, we use the limited real training dataset to train a teacher model
\mathcal{T}
. Then,
\mathcal{T}
evaluates synthetic images based on two scores and one penalty term to determine their effectiveness as training samples. We provide an illustration in Figure
3
to demonstrate the input used when calculating each score for a synthetic image. For clarity in the following discussions, we assume a real data sample
(I,T,B)
and denote the set of images generated from it as
\{I^{\prime}_{1},I^{\prime}_{2},\dots,I^{\prime}_{K}\}
.

Hardness Score.
We propose to filter harmful synthetic images via computing hardness. Intuitively, in our data-scarce regime, it is difficult to model outliers effectively as fundamental patterns may not be fully captured
Sorscher et al. (2022)
. Since the model requires easiest examples to provide coarse-grained information about the target function, we prioritize synthetic images that are less challenging for the teacher model
\mathcal{T}
. We define the hardness score for each of the synthetic image
I_{j}^{\prime}
, where
j\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}
, as follows:

S_{1}(I_{j}^{\prime})=\text{IOU}(\mathcal{T}(I_{j}^{\prime},T),B)

(1)

where
\mathcal{T}(I_{j}^{\prime},T)
is the predicted bounding box, and IOU (Intersection over Union) is the ratio of the intersection of two areas to their combined area.

Overfitting Score.
Additionally, we introduce another score to filter out synthetic images that may hinder training by exacerbating overfitting. Given the gigantic model architecture relative to the limited number of real data, the model is prone to overfit by relying on image background features rather than the actual object in the bounding box
Beery et al. (2018)
;
Xiao et al. (2020)
. To mitigate this, we assess the potential of each generated image to contribute to overfitting. Specifically, we modify each generated image by masking the area within the bounding box and input this variant into
\mathcal{T}
. If
\mathcal{T}
can still correctly predict the bounding box, it indicates that
\mathcal{T}
may leverage unintended features present in the generated background. Incorporating such data into training dataset risks the model learning these unintended features, thereby exacerbating overfitting. We define an overfitting score to filter these problematic samples. The score for each generated image
I_{j}^{\prime}
, where
j\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}
, is defined as:

S_{2}(I_{j}^{\prime})=1-\text{IOU}(\mathcal{T}(\text{Mask}(I_{j}^{\prime},B),T),B)

(2)

where
\text{Mask}(I_{j}^{\prime},B)
is a operation that sets all pixel values within the bounding box
B
to zero.

Penalty Term.
To balance between preserving easy samples and the mitigation of overfitting risk, we introduce a penalty term. The rationale is that overly easy images may not challenge the model enough to learn complex patterns. Conversely, images where the background outside the bounding box does not offer any useful information may introduce model bias. To balance between the first two scores, we define a penalty term for each generate image
I_{j}^{\prime}
, where
j\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}
, as follows:

P(I_{j}^{\prime})=\text{IOU}(\mathcal{T}(I_{j}^{\prime},\emptyset),B)

(3)

where
\emptyset
denotes an empty textual string.
This term can be regarded as measuring the target-related visual prior in the generated image. Specifically, it measures how strongly the generated image alone supports the ground-truth box, independent of the referring expression. A high value of
P
indicates that the target remains visually identifiable from language-independent visual evidence. Together with
S_{2}
,
P
acts as a regularizing term by penalizing synthetic samples whose target region contains insufficient visual evidence, through assigning them a lower overall score. In this way, the selected samples are encouraged to retain strong target-related visual cues while avoiding excessive reliance on the synthesized background.

The three scores are individually normalized to have zero mean and unit standard deviation. Then The final score for a generated image
I_{j}^{\prime}
is calculated as:

\text{Score}(I_{j}^{\prime})=\lambda_{1}S_{1}(I_{j}^{\prime})+\lambda_{2}S_{2}(I_{j}^{\prime})+\lambda_{P}P(I_{j}^{\prime})

(4)

where
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
, and
\lambda_{P}
are hyperparameters controlling the weights of each component.

Out of the
K
generated images
\{I^{\prime}_{1},I^{\prime}_{2},\dots,I^{\prime}_{K}\}
from each real data point (
I,T,B
), we retain one synthetic image
I^{\prime}_{m}
with the highest score, where
m=\arg\max_{j}\text{Score}(I^{\prime}_{j})
and
j\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}
. This yields a synthetic data point (
I^{\prime},T,B
). By incorporating the selected synthetic data points into the training set, we effectively double its size. Finally, we train a student model
\mathcal{S}
, where the real text
T
is randomly replaced with the corresponding generated caption
T^{\prime}
with probability
q
during training.

Baselines.
We compare our method with three leading baselines under the data-scarce visual grounding setting. (i) X-Paste
Zhao et al. (2023)
uses an ensemble of pretrained segmentation models to identify instances from generated images, which are then copied into real images. (ii) Gen2Det
Suri et al. (2024)
employs a grounded editing diffusion model to edit objects in real images. (iii) GeoDiffusion
Chen et al. (2024c)
utilizes geometric control via text prompts to enhance text-to-image diffusion models for direct object detection data generation. For X-Paste and Gen2Det, we use adapted variants that do not rely on models pretrained on large-scale densely annotated datasets, enabling a more controlled comparison with POBF. For GeoDiffusion, we follow its standard configuration, which uses a model pretrained on COCO-Stuff annotations. Implementation details are in the supplementary material.

Evaluation Metric.
Following previous works
Deng et al. (2021)
;
Deng et al. (2023)
, we use top-1 accuracy as the evaluation metric, where a prediction is considered correct if the IoU between the predicted bounding box and the ground-truth exceeds 0.5.

Implementation Details.
For both the teacher and student networks, we utilize TransVG
Deng et al. (2021)
, one of the most popular models for visual grounding without any bells or whistles. We utilize the Stable Diffusion XL model
Podell et al. (2023)
for image inpainting, with 45 denoising steps, a strength of 0.9, and a guidance scale of 7.5. BLIP ViT-L
Li et al. (2022)
is employed as the image captioner, with nucleus sampling (
p
=0.9). The hyperparameters
\lambda
in filtering scheme is tuned using grid search on the validation set, with values of 1.0 or 0.5. The selected
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{P})
values are
(0.5,0.5,1.0)
for RefCOCO and ReferIt, and
(1.0,0.5,0.5)
for RefCOCO+ and RefCOCOg. Each of score in the filtering scheme is independently standardized across the entire dataset. For each real data point, we generate 4 synthetic images and retain one with highest score. During training, generated texts replace real captions with a probability of
q=0.3
. Following
Zhang et al. (2023)
;
Wang et al. (2024c)
;
Yang et al. (2024)
, we adopt a consistent training schedule (batch size × steps) across all experiments.

We analyze each score in the filtering process, excluding the influence of generated text. Inspired by recent advances in data selection
Xia et al. (2022)

;
Rose et al. (2023)
;
Yang et al. (2022c)
;
Tang et al. (2023)
;
He et al. (2024b)
, we adapt several existing methods to fit within our teacher-based selection framework. Specifically, (i)
Random teacher
: randomly selects synthetic images as training data. (ii)
No teacher
: uses all synthetic data indiscriminately for training. (iii)
CLIP teacher
: a commonly used approach in prior works
Chen et al. (2024a)
;
Rose et al. (2023)
;
Yang et al. (2022c)
;
Tang et al. (2023)
;
He et al. (2024b)
, which selects synthetic images with the highest cosine similarity with the real text. (iv)
Moderate-Loss

Xia et al. (2022)
: defines the score as the difference between a sample’s loss and the class mean loss, prioritizing samples with scores near the median. (v)
Moderate-DS

Xia et al. (2022)
: replaces the score in
Moderate-Loss
with the Euclidean distance between a sample’s embedding and the class center embedding, again favoring samples near the median. (vi)
Difficult-Loss

Sorscher et al. (2022)
: prioritizes hard examples with higher loss values. In addition, we include two variants of our method that select training samples based on the negative hardness score (
-S_{1}
) and negative overfitting score (
-S_{2}
), respectively.

Qualitative examples illustrating the effectiveness of the two proposed scores. The green score indicates the synthetic image with the higher corresponding score.

Generative models may occasionally produce ambiguous examples. They can also generate synthetic images that closely resemble real ones, increasing the risk of overfitting. In Figure
5
, we show two examples illustrating how the hardness (
S_{1}
) and overfitting (
S_{2}
) scores help to mitigate these two issues and select the effective data.

In Figure
5
(a), the second synthetic image contains a small cat in the background, which may confuse the model when attempting to localize the target object
small cat
. Since this example is difficult for the teacher model to localize correctly, it receives a low
S_{1}
and could thus be filtered out. In Figure
5
(b), the background of the second generated image is visually very similar to the real image — the color, position, and posture of the two buses closely resembles those in the real image. Including such a similar sample in training data may increase the risk of overfitting. In contrast, the first generated image background presents a bus with a noticeably different appearance and placement compared to the real image. Although
S_{2}
does not directly measure image similarity, the visually more similar second image receives a lower
S_{2}
, which is consistent with our intuition that reproducing training-specific visual patterns may increase the risk of overfitting.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced POBF, a novel framework for effectively learning visual grounding in data-scarce scenarios. Specifically, we proposed a new data generation strategy—paint outside the box—which addresses the label misalignment issues commonly encountered in previous approaches. Additionally, to select the most effective synthetic samples, we introduced a novel filtering scheme tailored for inpainted data. This scheme integrates a hardness score, an overfitting score, and a penalty term. Experimental results demonstrate that POBF surpasses leading baselines, with accuracy gains ranging from 2.29%–3.85%. We further validated the robustness and generalizability of POBF across different generative models, training data sizes, and model architectures.
