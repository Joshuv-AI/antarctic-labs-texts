The L 2 L^{2} Norm of the Interior Cauchy Transform: Beyond the First Dirichlet Eigenvalue
David Kalaj

Abstract
. We study sharp L 2 L^{2} bounds for the interior Cauchy transform C D C_{D} on a bounded planar domain D D and clarify its connection with the Dirichlet spectrum. We analyze an approach that replaces fractional Dirichlet powers on D D by Euclidean Fourier multipliers after extension by zero, and show that this substitution can change the optimal constants. In particular, we construct an explicit endpoint counterexample on the unit disk to a Fourier-weighted inequality appearing in [ 5 ] . This identifies a gap in the derivation of the conjectured identity ‖ C D ‖ L 2 → L 2 = 2 / λ 1 ​ ( D ) \|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}=2/\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)} . We then identify the correct sharp constant for the endpoint Fourier weight | ξ | − 1 |\xi|^{-1} in terms of the top eigenvalue of a natural positive potential-type operator on D D . Finally, we show that testing C D C_{D} on the first Dirichlet eigenfunction already exceeds the spectral threshold, so ‖ C D ‖ L 2 → L 2 > 2 / λ 1 ​ ( D ) \|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}>2/\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)} for simply connected domains and also for annuli A ⁡ ( r , R ) A(r,R) , and we prove a rigidity result: equality with the spectral value occurs if and only if D D is a disk.

1.
Introduction

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded domain. The (interior) Cauchy transform

(1)

(C_{D}f)(z):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{D}\frac{f(w)}{z-w}\,dA(w),\qquad z\in D,

is a basic singular integral operator in planar function theory and provides a
canonical
L^{2}
–solution operator to
\bar{\partial}u=f
on
D
. It also appears
naturally alongside Bergman-type projections and in questions on spectral and
smoothing properties of integral operators (see, for instance,

[
2
,
5
,
12
,
15
]
).

A fundamental point is that the global Cauchy transform on
\mathbb{C}
is not bounded on

L^{2}(\mathbb{C})
, yet its restriction to a bounded domain may admit sharp
L^{2}
bounds.
In their seminal work
[
1
]
, Anderson and Hinkkanen
considered the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
and proved that if
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is
extended by zero outside
\mathbb{D}
, then
C_{\mathbb{D}}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and

(2)

\|C_{\mathbb{D}}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\leq\frac{2}{j_{0,1}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})},

where
j_{0,1}
is the first positive zero of the Bessel function
J_{0}
; moreover,
the constant
2/j_{0,1}
is sharp. Since
\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{D})=j_{0,1}^{2}
for the
Dirichlet Laplacian on the disk, (
2
) can be rewritten as the spectral
identity

\|C_{\mathbb{D}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{D})}}.

This striking formula suggests a possible general principle: that the
L^{2}
norm
of
C_{D}
might be determined by the first Dirichlet eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(D)
.

In
[
5
]
, a natural extension of this principle was proposed: for
bounded domains with sufficiently regular boundary,

(3)

\|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}}.

Beyond this conjectural sharp-constant identity,
[
5
]
contains
several valuable contributions on the spectral side of the subject, including
results and estimates connected to Dirichlet eigenvalues and their asymptotic
behavior, and it helped stimulate further interest in linking singular integral
bounds to Laplacian spectra. The present paper was directly motivated by this
spectral viewpoint, and our goal is to clarify precisely which parts of this
heuristic picture can be made rigorous.

The reasoning in
[
5
]
implicitly uses the heuristic identification

(-\Delta_{D})^{-\alpha}f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\big(|\xi|^{-2\alpha}\big)\,\mathcal{F}(f\mathbf{1}_{D}),

i.e. it treats fractional Dirichlet powers of
-\Delta_{D}
as Euclidean Fourier
multipliers after extending
f
by zero outside
D
. As we explain in this paper,
this Fourier-multiplier formula does not hold in general on bounded domains
without additional structure, and relying on it can lead to incorrect sharp
constants.

What we prove.

Our first contribution is an explicit endpoint counterexample (already for the
unit disk) to the Fourier-weighted inequality that underlies the proof of
(
3
); see Section
3
. This pinpoints exactly where
the spectral identification breaks down.

Our second contribution is to give the correct sharp constant for the Fourier
weight
|\xi|^{-1}
in full generality. We show that the quadratic form

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,d\xi

is governed not by
\lambda_{1}(D)
, but by the positive self-adjoint integral
operator

(S_{D}f)(x)=\int_{D}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}\,dy,

and the best constant equals
(2\pi)^{-1}\|S_{D}\|
; see
Theorem
2
.

Finally, returning to the Cauchy transform itself, we show that the disk is the
unique domain for which the spectral value
2/\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}
can be
attained. More precisely, for a simply connected bounded domain
D
with smooth
boundary and first Dirichlet eigenfunction
u
, we prove

\frac{\|C_{D}u\|_{L^{2}(D)}}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}}>\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}},

and consequently

\|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)}>\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}}\qquad\text{unless $D$ is a disk.}

Remark that the inequality

\|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)}\geq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}},

has been already proved in
[
1
]
.

Equivalently,

\|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\text{ is a disk}.

At the heart of the proof is an
L^{2}
orthogonal decomposition of
C_{D}u
into
the
L^{2}
–minimal
\bar{\partial}
–solution and a nontrivial holomorphic
component, together with a rigidity argument showing that the holomorphic
component vanishes identically only in the disk case.

Organization.

In Section
3
we present the Fourier-weighted counterexample on the disk. Moreover in the same section we identify the correct sharp constant via the operator
S_{D}
.
The final part of the paper, Section
4
establishes the strict inequality for the Cauchy
transform on noncircular domains and proves the disk rigidity statement.

2.
Background, motivation, and main results

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded domain. We study the (interior) Cauchy transform

(4)

(C_{D}f)(z):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{D}\frac{f(w)}{z-w}\,\,\mathrm{d}A(w),\qquad z\in D,

initially defined for
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(D)
. The operator
C_{D}
is a first-order
complex singular integral and is a natural
L^{2}
–solution operator for

\bar{\partial}u=f
on
D
, with connections to Bergman-type operators and
spectral questions (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
14
,
19
]
).

2.1.
A Fourier-multiplier pitfall on bounded domains

A key step in
[
5
]
is to extend
f\in L^{2}(D)
by zero to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
then to treat fractional Dirichlet powers of
-\Delta_{D}
as though they were given by the
free-space Fourier multiplier
|\xi|^{-2\alpha}
. This leads one to consider quadratic
forms of the type

(5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2\alpha}}\,d\xi,

which correspond to the operator
(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}
acting on the zero extension.
However, (
5
) is not, in general, the Dirichlet quadratic form
for
(-\Delta_{D})^{-\alpha}
. In particular, there is no reason to expect the sharp bound

(6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2\alpha}}\,d\xi\;\leq\;\lambda_{1}(D)^{-\alpha}\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2},

where
\lambda_{1}(D)
denotes the first Dirichlet eigenvalue.

At the endpoint
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
this becomes the (wrong) inequality

(7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,d\xi\;\leq\;\lambda_{1}(D)^{-1/2}\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2},

and we give an explicit counterexample on the unit disk to the Fourier-weighted estimate
invoked in
[
5
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Motivated by the sharp disk result of Anderson–Hinkkanen, it was asserted in

[
5
]
that the
L^{2}
operator norm of
C_{D}
is determined exactly
by
\lambda_{1}(D)
:

Theorem 1

(Claimed in
[
5
]
)
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded domain with piecewise
C^{1}
boundary. Then
C_{D}

extends boundedly to
L^{2}(D)
and

(8)

\|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}},

where
\lambda_{1}(D)>0
is the smallest eigenvalue of the Dirichlet problem

(9)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda u&\text{in }D,\\
u|_{\partial D}=0.&\end{cases}

The proof in
[
5
]
uses the Fourier-weighted inequality mentioned
in §
2.1
. Since that inequality is false, the argument cannot
establish (
8
).

3.
A counterexample to the (
6
) at
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
and the correct sharp constant for the
\left|\xi\right|^{-1}
Fourier weight

Throughout, we use the Fourier normalization

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Let
D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ |x|\leq 1\}
be the unit disk and set
f=\mathbf{1}_{D}
.
Then

\int_{|x|\leq 1}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-ir|\xi|\cos\theta}\,r\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\,\mathrm{d}r=2\pi\int_{0}^{1}J_{0}(r|\xi|)\,r\,\,\mathrm{d}r=2\pi\,\frac{J_{1}(|\xi|)}{|\xi|},

and hence

(10)

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\frac{J_{1}(|\xi|)}{|\xi|}.

Writing
r=|\xi|
and using
\,\mathrm{d}\xi=r\,\,\mathrm{d}r\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta
, we obtain

(11)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\left|\widehat{f}(\xi)\right|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(J_{1}(r)^{2}/r^{2}\big)}{r}\,r\,\,\mathrm{d}r\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J_{1}(r)^{2}}{r^{2}}\,\,\mathrm{d}r.

Using the classical identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J_{1}(r)^{2}}{r^{2}}\,\,\mathrm{d}r=\frac{4}{3\pi},

it follows that

(12)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\left|\widehat{f}(\xi)\right|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{8}{3}.

On the other hand,

\int_{D}|f(x)|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x=|D|=\pi,\qquad\lambda_{1}(D)=j_{0,1}^{2},

so

(13)

\lambda_{1}(D)^{-1/2}\int_{D}|f(x)|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\pi}{j_{0,1}}<\frac{8}{3}.

Therefore

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\left|\widehat{f}(\xi)\right|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi>\lambda_{1}(D)^{-1/2}\int_{D}\left|f(x)\right|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,

and the estimate
[
5
, Lemma 2.2]
fails on the unit disk for
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Here,
J_{k}
denotes the Bessel function of the first kind of order
k
, defined by the series

J_{k}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!\,(m+k)!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m+k},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots

j_{0,1}
denotes the first positive zero of
J_{0}
, i.e.

J_{0}\!\left(j_{0,1}\right)=0,\qquad 0<j_{0,1}<j_{0,2}<\cdots,

and numerically

j_{0,1}\approx 2.4048255577.

Theorem 2

(Sharp
L^{2}
constant for the
|\xi|^{-1}
Fourier weight)
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be bounded and let
f\in L^{2}(D)
, extended by
0
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Define the Fourier transform by

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Let
S_{D}:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
be the integral operator

(S_{D}f)(x)=\int_{D}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}\,\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in D.

Then
S_{D}
is positive, self-adjoint and compact on
L^{2}(D)
and for every

f\in L^{2}(D)
,

(14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\left\langle S_{D}f,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}.

Consequently, the sharp constant in

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi\leq C\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\qquad(f\in L^{2}(D))

is

C_{\mathrm{sharp}}(D)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\|S_{D}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\lambda_{\max}(S_{D}).

Moreover, by Schur’s test,

C_{\mathrm{sharp}}(D)\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}\frac{1}{|x-y|}\,\,\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.

With the above normalization, Plancherel holds in the form

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\|\widehat{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
, and the convolution identity is

\widehat{g*h}=2\pi\,\widehat{g}\,\widehat{h}
.
Let
k(x)=|x|^{-1}
. In the sense of tempered distributions in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\widehat{k}(\xi)=\frac{1}{|\xi|}.

Define
Tf:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\big(|\xi|^{-1}\widehat{f}(\xi)\big)
. Then

\widehat{Tf}(\xi)=\frac{1}{|\xi|}\widehat{f}(\xi)=\widehat{k}(\xi)\,\widehat{f}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\widehat{k*f}(\xi),

hence
Tf=\frac{1}{2\pi}(k*f)
. Since
f
is supported in
D
,

Tf(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{D}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}\,\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{2\pi}(S_{D}f)(x)\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in D.

Therefore, using Plancherel, let
g(\xi):=|\xi|^{-1}\widehat{f}(\xi)
. Then
\widehat{\mathcal{F}^{-1}g}=g
, so

\big\langle\mathcal{F}^{-1}g,\,f\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\big\langle g,\,\widehat{f}\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|\xi|}\,\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{f}(\xi)}\,d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,d\xi.

Thus

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\left\langle\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{1}{|\xi|}\widehat{f}\Big),f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\left\langle Tf,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left\langle S_{D}f,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(D)},

which proves (
14
).

The operator
S_{D}
is bounded on
L^{2}(D)
and is self-adjoint and positive since
its kernel
|x-y|^{-1}
is real and symmetric. It is compact because

|x-y|^{-1}\in L^{2}(D\times D)
(the singularity is integrable in two dimensions),
so
S_{D}
is Hilbert–Schmidt. Hence

Using (
14
), the sharp constant in the Fourier inequality is

C_{\mathrm{sharp}}(D)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\|S_{D}\|=\frac{1}{2\pi}\lambda_{\max}(S_{D})
.

Finally, Schur’s test gives

\|S_{D}\|_{2\to 2}\leq\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}\frac{1}{|x-y|}\,\,\mathrm{d}y,

and multiplying by
1/(2\pi)
yields the stated bound for
C_{\mathrm{sharp}}(D)
.

Schur’s test (used here).

Let
(Tf)(x)=\int_{D}K(x,y)f(y)\,dy
with
K\geq 0
. Schur’s test with the weight
m\equiv 1
gives

\|T\|_{2\to 2}\leq\Bigl(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}K(x,y)\,dy\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sup_{y\in D}\int_{D}K(x,y)\,dx\Bigr)^{1/2}.

If
K(x,y)=K(y,x)
is symmetric, then the two suprema coincide, hence

\|T\|_{2\to 2}\leq\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}K(x,y)\,dy.

Applying this to
S_{D}
with
K(x,y)=1/|x-y|
yields

\|S_{D}\|_{2\to 2}\leq\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}\frac{1}{|x-y|}\,dy,

and multiplying by
1/(2\pi)
gives the corresponding bound for

C_{\mathrm{sharp}}(D)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\|S_{D}\|.

∎

Remark 3
.

By the proof of the previous theorem, if
D=\mathbb{D}

4.
First-eigenfunction test and disk rigidity

Theorem 4
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded simply connected domain with piecewise
C^{1}
boundary.
Let
u
be the (real-valued) first Dirichlet eigenfunction on
D
, normalized arbitrarily, with

-\Delta u=\lambda_{1}(D)\,u\quad\text{in }D,\qquad u|_{\partial D}=0.

Set

v_{0}:=-\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}(D)}\,\partial_{z}u,\qquad\text{and}\qquad w:=C_{D}u.

Then
h:=w-v_{0}
is holomorphic on
D
and

(15)

\|w\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}=\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|h\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}.

Moreover,

(16)

\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}}\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

Consequently,

\frac{\|C_{D}u\|_{L^{2}(D)}}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}}\geq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}},

with strict inequality whenever
h\not\equiv 0
(equivalently,
C_{D}u\not\equiv v_{0}
). In particular,

\|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)}\geq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}},

and it is strictly larger if
D
is not a disk (see Theorem
6
below).

Proof.

Since
\Delta=4\,\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}
and
-\Delta u=\lambda_{1}u
, we have

\partial_{\bar{z}}v_{0}=-\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}}\partial_{\bar{z}}\partial_{z}u=-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\Delta u=u.

On the other hand,
\partial_{\bar{z}}(C_{D}u)=u
in
D
, hence

\partial_{\bar{z}}(w-v_{0})=0,

so
h:=w-v_{0}
is holomorphic on
D
.

Next, compute
\|v_{0}\|_{2}
. Since
u
is real-valued,
|\partial_{z}u|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}|\nabla u|^{2}
, and therefore

\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}=\frac{16}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}\int_{D}|\partial_{z}u|^{2}\,dA=\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}\int_{D}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dA.

By Green’s identity and
u|_{\partial D}=0
,

\int_{D}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dA=\int_{D}u(-\Delta u)\,dA=\lambda_{1}\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2},

which gives (
16
).

Finally, we show
v_{0}\perp h
in
L^{2}(D)
. Using
\overline{\partial_{z}u}=\partial_{\bar{z}}u
,

\langle v_{0},h\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}=-\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}}\int_{D}(\partial_{\bar{z}}u)\,h\,dA.

A
\bar{\partial}
–integration by parts yields

\int_{D}(\partial_{\bar{z}}u)\,h\,dA=\frac{i}{2}\int_{\partial D}u\,h\,dz-\int_{D}u\,(\partial_{\bar{z}}h)\,dA=0,

because
u|_{\partial D}=0
and
\partial_{\bar{z}}h=0
. Hence
\langle v_{0},h\rangle=0
, and
(
15
) follows. The stated inequalities are immediate from
(
15
) and (
16
).
∎

Lemma 5

(Exterior holomorphy and decay of the Cauchy transform)
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be bounded and let
f\in L^{1}(D)
. Define

F(z):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{D}\frac{f(w)}{z-w}\,dA(w),\qquad z\in\Omega:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{D}.

Then
F
is holomorphic on
\Omega
and, as
z\to\infty
,

F(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{M_{0}}{z}+\frac{M_{1}}{z^{2}}+\frac{M_{2}}{z^{3}}+\cdots\right),\qquad M_{k}:=\int_{D}f(w)\,w^{k}\,dA(w),

in particular
F(z)=O(1/z)
.

Proof.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be measurable and bounded,
f\in L^{1}(D)
, and define

F(z):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{D}\frac{f(w)}{z-w}\,dA(w),\qquad z\in\Omega:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{D}.

Fix
z_{0}\in\Omega
. Set

\delta:=\dist(z_{0},D)>0.

Choose
r\in(0,\delta/2)
. Then for all
z\in B(z_{0},r)
and all
w\in D
,

|z-w|\geq|z_{0}-w|-|z-z_{0}|\geq\delta-r\geq\delta/2.

Hence the kernels are uniformly dominated on
B(z_{0},r)
:

\Big|\frac{f(w)}{z-w}\Big|\leq\frac{2|f(w)|}{\delta},\qquad\Big|\frac{f(w)}{(z-w)^{2}}\Big|\leq\frac{4|f(w)|}{\delta^{2}},

and the dominating functions
2|f|/\delta
,
4|f|/\delta^{2}
are in
L^{1}(D)
.

For each fixed
w
, the map
z\mapsto(z-w)^{-1}
is holomorphic on
B(z_{0},r)
with derivative

-(z-w)^{-2}
. The domination above allows differentiation under the integral sign (e.g. via dominated
convergence applied to difference quotients), giving

F^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{D}\frac{f(w)}{(z-w)^{2}}\,dA(w),\qquad z\in B(z_{0},r).

Since
z_{0}
was arbitrary,
F
is holomorphic on
\Omega
.

Let
D
be bounded and pick
R>0
such that
D\subset\{\,|w|\leq R\,\}
. If
|z|>2R
, then

|w/z|\leq 1/2
for
w\in D
, and

\frac{1}{z-w}=\frac{1}{z}\,\frac{1}{1-w/z}=\frac{1}{z}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{w}{z}\Big)^{k},

with absolute (and uniform in
w\in D
) convergence.

Moreover,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big|\frac{w^{k}}{z^{k+1}}\Big|\leq\frac{1}{|z|}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{R}{|z|}\Big)^{k}\leq\frac{1}{|z|}\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/2}=\frac{2}{|z|}.

Thus

\Big|\frac{f(w)}{z-w}\Big|\leq|f(w)|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big|\frac{w^{k}}{z^{k+1}}\Big|\leq\frac{2|f(w)|}{|z|},

which is integrable over
D
. This justifies termwise integration:

F(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{z^{k+1}}\int_{D}f(w)\,w^{k}\,dA(w)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{M_{0}}{z}+\frac{M_{1}}{z^{2}}+\frac{M_{2}}{z^{3}}+\cdots\right),

where

M_{k}:=\int_{D}f(w)\,w^{k}\,dA(w).

In particular,

|F(z)|\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{D}\frac{|f(w)|}{|z|-|w|}\,dA(w)\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{|z|-R}\,\|f\|_{L^{1}(D)}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{|z|}\right),

so
F(z)=O(1/z)
as
z\to\infty
, and the Laurent series above is valid for
|z|>2R
.

If
M_{0}=M_{1}=\cdots=M_{m-1}=0
, then the expansion starts at
z^{-(m+1)}
and

F(z)=O(|z|^{-(m+1)})
.

∎

Theorem 6

(Rigidity)
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded, simply connected domain with
C^{1,\alpha}
boundary.
Let
u
be the first Dirichlet eigenfunction,

-\Delta u=\lambda_{1}(D)\,u\quad\text{in }D,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial D,

and define

v_{0}:=-\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}(D)}\,\partial_{z}u.

Then

C_{D}u\equiv v_{0}\quad\text{in }D

if and only if
D
is a disk.

Proof.

Assume first that
C_{D}u\equiv v_{0}
in
D
, where
v_{0}=-(4/\lambda_{1})\partial_{z}u
.
Let
\Omega:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{D}
and consider the exterior Cauchy transform

F(z):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{D}\frac{u(w)}{z-w}\,dA(w),\qquad z\in\Omega.

By Lemma
5
,
F
is holomorphic on
\Omega
and satisfies
F(z)=O(1/z)
as
z\to\infty
.

For smooth
u
and
\partial D
, the same integral defines a continuous function on
\mathbb{C}
, and its restriction
to
D
coincides with
C_{D}u
. Hence the identity
C_{D}u=v_{0}
on
D
implies that
v_{0}
has a holomorphic
extension to
\Omega
, given by

\partial_{z}u(w):=-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4}\,F(w),\qquad w\in\Omega.

In particular, this extension is holomorphic on
\Omega
and

(17)

\partial_{z}u(w)=O(1/w)\qquad(w\to\infty).

Boundary identity.

On
\partial D
we have
u=0
, so
\nabla u
is normal:

\nabla u=(\partial_{n}u)\,n,

where
n=n_{x}+in_{y}
is the unit outward normal. Using
\partial_{z}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y})
, we obtain

\partial_{z}u=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{n}u)\,\overline{n}\qquad\text{on }\partial D.

Let
\gamma=\gamma(s)
be an arclength parametrization of
\partial D
. Then
|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|=1
and

n(s)=-i\,\gamma^{\prime}(s),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\overline{n(s)}=i\,\overline{\gamma^{\prime}(s)}=\frac{i}{\gamma^{\prime}(s)}.

Consequently,

\partial_{z}u(\gamma(s))\,d\gamma=\partial_{z}u(\gamma(s))\,\gamma^{\prime}(s)\,ds=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{n}u)\,\overline{n}\,\gamma^{\prime}(s)\,ds=\frac{i}{2}(\partial_{n}u)\,ds,

so the complex differential
\partial_{z}u\,d\gamma
has vanishing real part on
\partial D

(equivalently, it is purely imaginary along
\partial D
).

Rigidity via the exterior conformal map.

Let
\Phi:\Omega\to\{|\zeta|>1\}
be the exterior conformal map normalized by
\Phi(\infty)=\infty
,
and write

Z(\zeta):=\Phi^{-1}(\zeta).

Define

\omega(\zeta):=\bigl(\partial_{z}u\bigr)\bigl(Z(\zeta)\bigr)\,Z^{\prime}(\zeta),\qquad|\zeta|>1.

Then
\omega
is holomorphic for
|\zeta|>1
, and (
17
) together with the normalization

Z(\zeta)=a\zeta+b+O(1/\zeta)\qquad(\zeta\to\infty)

implies

\omega(\zeta)=O(1/\zeta)\qquad(\zeta\to\infty),

so
F_{1}(\zeta):=\zeta\,\omega(\zeta)
is holomorphic on
|\zeta|>1
and bounded at
\infty
.

On
|\zeta|=1
we have
Z(\zeta)\in\partial D
and
dZ=Z^{\prime}(\zeta)\,d\zeta
, hence

\omega(\zeta)\,d\zeta=\bigl(\partial_{z}u\bigr)\bigl(Z(\zeta)\bigr)\,dZ.

The boundary identity above shows that this differential has vanishing real part on
|\zeta|=1
; therefore,
for
\zeta=e^{it}
(so
d\zeta=i\zeta\,dt
) we obtain

\Re\!\bigl(F_{1}(e^{it})\,i\,dt\bigr)=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F_{1}(e^{it})\in\mathbb{R},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus
F_{1}
extends holomorphically across
|\zeta|=1
by Schwarz reflection and is an entire bounded
function; hence
F_{1}
is constant, say
F_{1}(\zeta)\equiv c\in\mathbb{R}
, and thus

\omega(\zeta)=\frac{c}{\zeta}.

Unwinding the definition of
\omega
shows that
|Z^{\prime}(\zeta)|
is constant on
|\zeta|=1
.
Since
\log|Z^{\prime}(\zeta)|
is harmonic on
|\zeta|>1
, constant boundary values imply
\log|Z^{\prime}|
is constant
on
|\zeta|>1
, so
Z^{\prime}(\zeta)
is constant and
Z(\zeta)
is a Möbius map. Consequently
\partial D
is a circle
and
D
is a disk.

Conversely.

If
D
is a disk, the first eigenfunction is radial and a direct computation (e.g. in polar coordinates)
shows that
C_{D}u=-\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}(D)}\,\partial_{z}u
holds identically.
∎

Remark 7
.

The rigidity theorem above is formulated for smooth domains (e.g.
C^{1,\alpha}
) in order to use
boundary identities and conformal mapping. The next example has a different role: it is a purely
interior computation on the unit square, showing directly that
C_{D}u\not\equiv v_{0}
by evaluating at
a single point. In particular, it yields

\frac{\|C_{D}u\|_{L^{2}(D)}}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}}>\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}},

and hence
\|C_{D}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)}>\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}}
. Thus the claimed norm identity (
3
)
cannot hold for the square.

Example 8
.

Let
D=(0,1)^{2}
, for which
\lambda_{1}(D)=2\pi^{2}
, and take

u(x,y)=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).

Then
u_{z}(0,0)=0
, hence
v_{0}(0)=0
. On the other hand, evaluating the Cauchy transform at
z=0
gives

(C_{D}u)(0)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)}{x+iy}\,dy\,dx,

and writing
(x+iy)^{-1}=(x-iy)/(x^{2}+y^{2})
shows

\Re\,(C_{D}u)(0)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\,\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\,dy\,dx<0.

Thus
(C_{D}u)(0)\neq v_{0}(0)
, hence
h\not\equiv 0
in Theorem
4
, and therefore

\frac{\|C_{D}u\|_{L^{2}(D)}}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}}>\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(D)}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}.

Theorem 9
.

Let
A=A(r,R)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ r<|z|<R\}
with
0<r<R
, and let
f
be the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of

-\Delta
on
A
, i.e.

-\Delta f=\lambda_{1}(A)\,f\quad\text{in }A,\qquad f=0\quad\text{on }\partial A,

normalized arbitrarily and chosen positive in
A
. Define the Cauchy transform

C_{A}g(z):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{A}\frac{g(w)}{z-w}\,d\mu(w),\qquad d\mu=du\,dv,

and set

v_{0}:=-\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}(A)}\,\partial_{z}f.

Then
f
is radial and

C_{A}f(z)=\frac{2}{z}\int_{r}^{|z|}f(\rho)\,\rho\,d\rho,\qquad z\in A.

Moreover, one has the exact identity

C_{A}f(z)=-\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}(A)}\,\partial_{z}f(z)\;+\;\frac{2r\,f^{\prime}(r)}{\lambda_{1}(A)}\,\frac{1}{z},\qquad z\in A,

and the orthogonality relation

\left\langle v_{0},\frac{1}{z}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(A)}=0.

Consequently,

\|C_{A}f\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}=\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}+\left|\frac{2r\,f^{\prime}(r)}{\lambda_{1}(A)}\right|^{2}\left\|\frac{1}{z}\right\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}>\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2},

and since
\Delta=4\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}
one has

\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}=\frac{16}{\lambda_{1}(A)^{2}}\|\partial_{z}f\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}=\frac{16}{\lambda_{1}(A)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}=\frac{4}{\lambda_{1}(A)}\|f\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}.

Therefore,

\frac{\|C_{A}f\|_{L^{2}(A)}}{\|f\|_{L^{2}(A)}}>\frac{\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(A)}}{\|f\|_{L^{2}(A)}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(A)}}.

Proof.

By simplicity of
\lambda_{1}(A)
and rotational invariance of
A
, the first eigenfunction is radial.
For radial
f
, write
z=\rho e^{i\theta}
and
w=se^{i\varphi}
. Then

C_{A}f(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{r}^{R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{f(s)\,s}{\rho e^{i\theta}-se^{i\varphi}}\,d\varphi\,ds.

A residue computation yields

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{\rho e^{i\theta}-se^{i\varphi}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{\rho e^{i\theta}}=\frac{2\pi}{z},&s<\rho,\\[4.0pt]
0,&s>\rho,\end{cases}

hence

C_{A}f(z)=\frac{2}{z}\int_{r}^{|z|}f(s)\,s\,ds.

In particular,
\lim_{|z|\downarrow r}C_{A}f(z)=0
. Set
\lambda=\lambda_{1}(A)
and

v_{0}=-(4/\lambda)\partial_{z}f
. Using
\Delta=4\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}
and
-\Delta f=\lambda f
,
we have
\bar{\partial}v_{0}=f
in
A
. Also
\bar{\partial}(C_{A}f)=f
in
A
, hence

h:=C_{A}f-v_{0}
is holomorphic in
A
. Since
f
is radial,
h(e^{it}z)=e^{-it}h(z)
, thus
h(z)=c/z

for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Multiplying by
z
and letting
|z|\downarrow r
gives

c=\lim_{|z|\downarrow r}\bigl(zC_{A}f(z)-zv_{0}(z)\bigr)=-\lim_{|z|\downarrow r}zv_{0}(z).

For radial
f
,

\partial_{z}f(\rho e^{i\theta})=\frac{e^{-i\theta}}{2}f^{\prime}(\rho),\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad zv_{0}(z)=-\frac{2\rho}{\lambda}f^{\prime}(\rho),

hence
c=(2r/\lambda)f^{\prime}(r)
and

C_{A}f=v_{0}+\frac{c}{z}.

Next,

\begin{split}\left\langle v_{0},\frac{1}{z}\right\rangle&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{r}^{R}\left(-\frac{2}{\lambda}e^{-i\theta}f^{\prime}(\rho)\right)\left(\frac{e^{i\theta}}{\rho}\right)\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta\\
&=-\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}\int_{r}^{R}f^{\prime}(\rho)\,d\rho=-\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}\bigl(f(R)-f(r)\bigr)=0.\end{split}

Therefore
v_{0}\perp 1/z
in
L^{2}(A)
and

\|C_{A}f\|_{2}^{2}=\|v_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+|c|^{2}\left\|\frac{1}{z}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
f^{\prime}(r)\neq 0
by Hopf’s lemma (because
\Delta f<0
),
c\neq 0
, and moreover

\left\|\frac{1}{z}\right\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}=\int_{A}\frac{1}{|z|^{2}}\,dA=2\pi\int_{r}^{R}\frac{1}{\rho}\,d\rho=2\pi\log\frac{R}{r}>0,

we obtain
\|C_{A}f\|_{2}^{2}>\|v_{0}\|_{2}^{2}
.

Finally,

\|\partial_{z}f\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\lambda\|f\|_{2}^{2},

which yields
\|v_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=(4/\lambda)\|f\|_{2}^{2}
and the stated strict inequality.
∎

Remark 10
.

It is also worth emphasizing that there is a closely related Cauchy-type transform arising from the
Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian for which the sharp constant
\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(\Omega)}}

does
hold on arbitrary bounded domains. Following Astala–Iwaniec–Martin, let
G(z,\tau)
denote
the Green function of a bounded domain
\Omega
, and for
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
define the Green
potential

(18)

v=L_{\Delta}(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}G(z,\tau)\,\varphi(\tau)\,d\tau,

so that
v|_{\partial\Omega}=0
in the sense of distributions. The associated Cauchy transform is

(19)

\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}(\varphi)(z)=v_{z}=\int_{\Omega}G_{z}(z,\tau)\,\varphi(\tau)\,d\tau.

It was proved in
[
13
]
that

(20)

\|\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(\Omega)}},

where
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
-\Delta
on
\Omega
.
