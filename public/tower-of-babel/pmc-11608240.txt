Creep characterisation and microstructural analysis of municipal solid waste incineration fly ash geopolymer backfill
Authors: Lijuan Su, Siyao Wu, Guosheng Fu, Wancheng Zhu, Xiangdong Zhang, Bing Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, an alkali-activated municipal solid waste incineration (MSWI) fly ash-based filling material was prepared with MSWI fly ash as the raw material and slag as the auxiliary material. The filling body experiences long-term creep, which may have a direct effect on the stability of the overlying strata of the mine goaf. The long-term mechanical properties of the fly ash-based filling materials were tested with a triaxial rheological apparatus. First, uniaxial creep testing was carried out at five levels of axial stress: 50%, 60%, 70%, 80% and 90% of the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS). Then, triaxial creep testing was carried out by considering the geological environment of the goaf. The creep characteristics of fly ash-based filling materials under a three-dimensional stress state were explored. The results indicate that (1) under different stress levels, the creep curves of fly ash-based filling materials can be divided into three types: decelerated creep‒stable creep, decelerated creep‒constant creep, and decelerated creep‒constant creep‒accelerated creep. (2) The total creep deformation of the fly ash-based filling material is 0.46 ~ 0.78%, which is similar to the creep deformation of soft rock. The instantaneous deformation during loading contributes most of the total deformation. (3) The polymerization products generated in the fly ash-based filling material system can effectively cement the raw material particles, and the presence of gel can effectively delay the accelerating creep process of the material. (4) A nonlinear fractional-order model composed of an Abel dashpot can fully describe the complete process of decelerating creep-constant creep-accelerating creep.Keywords: Municipal solid waste incineration (MSWI) fly ash, Filling material, Creep, Fractional order model, Pore structureSubject terms: Materials science, Engineering

Introduction

Owing to the continuous growth of the urban population, the output of municipal solid waste clearly increases. The incineration of domestic waste can reduce the municipal solid waste volume by approximately 90% and its mass by 70%, so it is an ideal method for waste disposal1–5. At present, the treatment of MSWI fly ash in China is still mainly based on safe landfills, and its utilization rate as a resource is still low. The rational use of MSWI fly ash is an inevitable trend to solve the problem of fly ash stacking6–11. The use of MSWI fly ash after alkali excitation treatment in the filling treatment of mine goafs can not only solve the land occupation problem of landfill schemes but also make full use of the potential activity of fly ash to prepare filling materials12–15. The filling body is subjected to long-term and stable pressure from the overlying strata during the service process, and its long-term mechanical properties are related to the stability of the entire goaf. Therefore, it is very important to study the creep characteristics of MSWI fly ash-based filling materials to facilitate the utilization of fly ash16.

At present, there are few studies on the creep behavior of filling materials in mine goafs17,18. Cheng et al.19,20 prepared a filling body with iron tailings and composite Portland cement as the main raw materials, studied the creep hardening–damage characteristics of the filling body and deduced a nonlinear constitutive model of filling body creep. Chen et al.21,22 prepared filling paste samples with fly ash and cement as cementitious materials and analyzed the variation in the instantaneous elastic modulus during creep. Zhou et al.23 prepared a water-rich filling material by mixing cement, gypsum and lime with water and deduced a one-dimensional creep damage model of the material. Most of the above studies include uniaxial creep tests, and the corresponding constitutive models are one-dimensional models. In practical engineering, in addition to the axial pressure imposed by the overlying strata, the filling body is also subjected to the confining pressure of the surrounding soil or rock, and the real working environment is closer to a triaxial stress state. In addition, previous studies have mostly adopted the multistage loading method to carry out creep tests on filling materials. This loading method cannot eliminate the influence of early cumulative damage on later loading.

Although there are few studies on the creep behavior of filling materials in goafs, research on the long-term mechanical properties of soft rock and clay materials has been relatively abundant. Owing to the diversity of raw materials for the preparation of filling, at present, most filling are prepared from solid waste. Therefore, the different components, different material sources and preparation processes used to prepare the filling significantly affect the creep performance of the filling. Zhu et al.24 described the parameters in the Merchant model by using the variable modulus method and proposed a three-dimensional creep model of clay related to the stress level of soft clay at the bottom of a caisson foundation. Guo et al.25 introduced a nonlinear SP element based on the Burgers model and established a three-dimensional creep constitutive model that can fit the shear creep curve of marine soft soil. The filling material is similar to soft rock and clay in terms of its working environment, bearing capacity and failure mode. The creep behavior of MSWI fly ash-based filling materials can be studied by referring to relevant theories.

So far, scholars have less research on the characteristics of MSWI fly ash filler. Therefore, according to the microscopic characteristics and pore characteristics of MSWI fly ash filling material, the author has realized the stability of the filling material. In this work, MSWI fly ash is used as the main raw material to prepare goaf filling material, and the creep behavior of the filling body under uniaxial and ordinary triaxial loading is studied. The experimental study fully combines the existing uniaxial creep test of the filling body and the triaxial creep theory of rock and derives a three-dimensional fractional creep constitutive model that can describe the whole creep process of MSWI fly ash-based filling material. The research results provide a reference for the application of MSWI fly ash in the field of mine goaf filling.

Microscopic characteristics of the samples

Analysis of geological polymerization reactions

Figure 2 shows the sample preparation process, microscopic morphology, and formation of the main gel product. The preparation process of the materials, stirring, sample molding, and curing are shown in Fig. 2. After the material forms a solidified body, it is scanned by an electron microscope to obtain the microscopic morphology of the gel material, which is roughly judged to exhibit a flocculent and three-dimensional network structure. Geopolymers form large amounts of silicate gel and aluminosilicate gel substances through the depolymerization‒condensation of Si‒O tetrahedra and Al‒O tetrahedra26,27. This gel product has a stable structure and fixes unreacted MSWI fly ash in an interwoven, entangled, and wrapped manner. Water glass provides stable active SiO2, and NaOH provides an alkaline environment during the adjustment of the water glass modulus, allowing many OH- ions to enter the material interior, breaking the surface glass of the material and releasing active Si4+, Al3+, Ca2+, and Na+ions into the Si‒O‒Si bond28. Through depolymerization‒condensation reactions, new gel substances are formed, as shown in the microscopic molecular structure morphology in Fig. 2.

Schematic diagram of the sample preparation process, microscopic morphology, and formation of the main gel product.

The combination of NaOH and water glass excites slag cementitious materials to produce fewer cracks on the surface, with products distributed in the structure in a flocculent and clustered form, stabilizing the material structure and presenting a regular arrangement29,30. Owing to the continuous intervention of Na+ ions, a small amount of N-A-S–H (sodium aluminosilicate gel) is generated in the polymerization product, which has a three-dimensional network structure and is entangled with the abovementioned gel substances, making it difficult to distinguish.

The analysis of fine-scale tests on municipal solid waste incineration (MSWI) fly ash-based inorganic cementitious materials revealed that the predominant reaction products, including C-(A)-S–H gel, consisted of spherical and nanoscale particles31. The organic matter in the material reacts with the slag based polymer to form a new phase, which is named C-(A)–S–H/polymer gel. C-(A)-S–H/polymer gel has a high degree of polymerization, such as three-dimensional network structure, so it can bridge cracks in multiple directions and further inhibit crack propagation32,33. The simultaneous addition of MSWI and slag not only formed a network between organic and inorganic, but also formed a network structure of organic matter, mainly C-(A)-S–H/polymer gel. This leads to the cross distribution of different products on the micro surface, resulting in the formation of an interpenetrating network structure of organic–inorganic composite cementitious materials in three-dimensional space. The cross distribution of C-(A)-S–H/polymer gel, C-(A)-S–H gel and unreacted slag forms a three-dimensional network structure, which is more conducive to bridging cracks and improving toughness34,35.

Pore structure characteristics

Figure 3 shows a schematic diagram of the CT scan of the test sample. The scanning sections of the top layer, middle layer and bottom layer are shown in Fig. 3, and fine pores can be clearly observed. The pores in a sample are generally small and unevenly distributed. According to Fig. 3, the top layer of the section has more pores, the middle layer has fewer pores, and the bottom layer has fewer pores, which indicates that the internal gas in the material is discharged upward as a whole due to vibration and other reasons during the production process of the sample, resulting in this pore stratification phenomenon. CT scanning revealed that the pore distribution of the test sample was uneven, but the number of pores was small, which had little effect on the stability of the overall structure. Owing to the large number of pores in the top layer, the top bearing capacity of the sample is reduced, and then a creep test is carried out to evaluate the long-term stability of the sample.

Schematic diagram of the CT scanning section of a sample.

With the increase of hydrogen in the sample, the pore structure changes to a coarser distribution. The shrinkage of particles and the formation of pores may be caused by the evaporation of water in geopolymer matrix. Geopolymers are mostly composed of zeolite minerals with three-dimensional pore structure. Geopolymer molecular sieves have the same cell in a short time, but the pores do not change significantly in a long time. There is unreacted interlayer water in the geopolymer structure. Through external evaporation, the water in the sample volatilizes, and gradually forms some small pores. Some fine pores can be explained that the space filling trend of precipitated nano zeolites is related to the energy loss of covalent bonds between the crystal boundaries of nano zeolites: the more water used in synthesis, the more space for precipitation, so due to the energy loss, the smaller the trend of crystal boundary connection. This may be a valuable property of Geopolymer in some applications.

Creep characteristics and discussion of MSWI fly ash-based filling materials

Uniaxial creep characteristics

The strain‒time curve of uniaxial creep is shown in Fig. 4. The curve in the figure shows that there are three main forms of creep curves for alkali-activated MSWI fly ash-based filling materials under uniaxial compression. The specific characteristics of the curve are as follows. ① As shown in Fig. 4 (a), (b) and (c), when the axial stress level is low, the creep curve is stable. At the moment of loading, the curve rises rapidly and then enters the decelerating creep stage. The deformation of a sample mainly occurs in the early stage of loading. When the sample is compacted, the creep curve enters the stable stage, and the increase range is small. Creep increases with time and remains constant, indicating stable creep. ② As shown in Fig. 4 (d), when the stress level increases to a certain level (80% of the UCS), the creep curve begins to change. After decelerating creep, the strain continues to increase, and the strain is linearly related to time, indicating metastable creep. ③ As shown in Fig. 4 (e), when the stress level reaches 90% of the UCS, the upper load is close to the bearing limit of the sample. With increasing time, the creep curve experiences a decelerating creep stage, a constant creep stage and an accelerating creep stage. When the creep amount accumulates to a certain extent, the curve rises rapidly, the sample is destroyed, and the creep curve shows unstable creep.

Creep curves of the samples under uniaxial stress.

Figure 4(e) shows a summary of the creep curves of different materials at different stress levels. The creep curve cluster reveals that the total creep amount of the fly ash-based filling material is 0.46 ~ 0.78%, which is similar to the creep deformation of soft rock36. The instantaneous creep amount is between 0.40% and 0.62%, accounting for 80.21 ~ 92.51% of the total creep amount of the sample. The instantaneous deformation of a sample during loading contributes most of the total deformation during the whole creep process. Figure 4(e)-(f) shows that the axial deformation of the fly ash-based filling material is different from that of ordinary rock when it is near failure. As described in Reference37, the deformation of ordinary rock is small when it is close to failure, and the failure process is short. In contrast, the failure of fly ash-based filling materials is relatively slow. When the axial pressure is 80% of the UCS, the material does not fail after 72 h, and the particles near the diagonal of the sample obviously move but are not separated. When the stress reaches 90%, although the sample eventually fails, it has experienced more than 10 h from the accelerating creep stage to material failure, and the strain in the accelerating creep stage exceeds 0.1%. This is mainly because the waste incineration fly ash-slag system generates gel products internally after alkali activation, and these polymerization products closely bond the raw material particles together. When subjected to external loads, the gel products between the raw material particles are compressed, which plays a role in cementation and buffering so that the particles do not separate too quickly while moving.

Triaxial creep characteristics

The conventional triaxial creep results of the MSWI fly ash-based filling materials under different confining pressures are shown in Fig. 5. Under the confining pressures tested, most of the samples did not fail, which indicated that the presence of confining pressure was beneficial to the stability of the filling body. There are three main types of triaxial creep test curves used in this study: ‘decelerating creep‒stable creep’, ‘decelerating creep‒constant creep’, and ‘decelerating creep‒constant creep‒accelerating creep’. As shown in Fig. 5 (c) and (d), when the confining pressure is 0.4 MPa and 0.5 MPa, the creep curves first decelerate creep and then stabilize creep. This is because the higher confining pressure provides lateral restraint for the sample so that the sample can obtain a higher bearing capacity in the axial direction, and its stability can be guaranteed. As shown in Fig. 5 (b), when the confining pressure is 0.3 MPa and the axial pressure is 2.25 MPa, the filling material enters the constant creep stage after decelerating creep, and the axial strain increases slowly, but the sample is not destroyed. As shown in Fig. 5 (a), when the confining pressure is low (0.2 MPa) but the axial pressure is high (2.25 MPa), the sample enters the accelerating creep stage after experiencing decelerating creep and stable creep and finally fails. This is because under the action of a higher axial stress, the lower confining pressure is not enough to restrain the development of internal cracks in the material, and the internal structure of the sample is destroyed after undergoing continuous deformation.

Creep deformation rate analysis

The straight-line segments in the ε‒log(t) curves of the samples under different confining pressures are drawn in Fig. 8. Under different confining pressures, as the axial stress increases, the slope of the ε-log(t) curve of the sample increases. Under different axial stresses, with increasing confining pressure, the slope of the ε-log (t) curve first increases but then decreases. When the confining pressure is 0.2 MPa, the slope of the ε-log (t) curve reaches a maximum. The results show that when the samples enter creep, the main load is the friction force between the solid particles in the samples. As the gap between particles gradually narrows, the strength of the samples is essentially stable. Under the action of each level of axial stress, the soil will form a corresponding strength according to the magnitude of axial stress, which is consistent with the above analysis conclusion of the relationship between the instantaneous elastic modulus and stress.

Conclusion

In this work, an alkali-activated MSWI fly ash-based filling material was prepared with MSWI fly ash as the raw material and slag as the auxiliary material. To evaluate the long-term stability of the fly ash-based filling materials, the creep properties were tested. First, the uniaxial creep test was carried out at five levels of axial compression: 50%, 60%, 70%, 80% and 90% of the UCS. The triaxial creep test was subsequently carried out on the basis of the uniaxial creep test, and the creep characteristics of the fly ash-based filling materials under a three-dimensional stress state were explored. Finally, the following conclusions were obtained.

(1) When the MSWI fly ash-based filling material was subjected to a constant axial load, three forms of creep were observed: stable creep, metastable creep and unstable creep. When the axial load reached 90% of the UCS, the internal damage accumulation of the material accelerated. Therefore, the upper load of the MSWI fly ash-based filling material should not exceed 90% of the axial peak stress in actual application processes.

(2) The total creep amount of the MSWI fly ash-based filling material was 0.46 ~ 0.78%, similar to the creep deformation of soft rock. The instantaneous deformation of the sample during loading contributed most of the total deformation. The polymerization products generated in the MSWI fly ash-based filling material system could effectively cement the raw material particles, and the presence of the gel could effectively delay the accelerating creep process of the material.

(3) The Burgers model can describe decelerating creep and stable creep but cannot directly describe accelerating creep. The Abel sticky pot with a switch was connected in series with the Burgers model to obtain a fractional order model that can reasonably describe the whole process of decelerating creep, stable creep and accelerating creep of MSWI fly ash-based mine filling materials. When the fractional order of the Abel sticky pot was greater than 1, the model could describe the accelerating creep stage.
