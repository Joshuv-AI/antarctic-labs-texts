A study on factors influencing music enthusiast’s behavioral intention to use generative AI for music composition based on an expanded technology acceptance model
Authors: Yongxin Zhou, Yuhui Wang, Jiayu Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to understand the intentions of music enthusiasts in using generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) for music composition and to elucidate the factors influencing those intentions. A covariance-based structural equation modeling approach is applied to construct a technology acceptance model (TAM). A total of 429 valid questionnaire responses were analyzed to explore music enthusiasts’ acceptance mechanisms regarding GenAI tools. The sample encompasses diverse age groups, educational backgrounds, and experience levels, thus ensuring the broad applicability of the findings. The results show that relationships within the original TAM structure remain robust. Specifically, perceived AI risk negatively impacts intention to use (IU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU); however, it does not correlate significantly with perceived usefulness (PU). Perceived trust positively influences PEOU and PU but does not directly affect IU. Content quality is positively correlated with PEOU, PU, and IU. Social influence is correlated only with PU and does not affect PEOU or IU. Perceived enjoyment enhances both PEOU and PU but has no effect on IU. Demographic analysis reveals no differences in GenAI tool evaluations among music enthusiasts in terms of gender, age, or education level. Overall, this report provides essential theoretical and practical insights for applying GenAI in music creation by integrating traditional and novel factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28532-2.Keywords: Music composition, Music enthusiast, Generative artificial intelligence, Technology acceptance model, Intension to useSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) encompasses technical systems that simulate intelligent human behavior through computational algorithms. Its core capabilities include natural language processing, pattern recognition, autonomous decision-making, and continuous learning from experience.1,2 Generative AI (GenAI) is a cutting-edge branch of AI involving technologies that learn from vast amounts of data to create new content. GenAI employs deep learning models, such as generative adversarial networks (GANs), variational autoencoders (VAEs), and transformer-based architectures (e.g., the GPT series), to mimic human creativity and generate diverse outputs, including text, images, audio, video, and code.3,4 The essence of this technology lies in understanding data distributions and generating novel outputs that adhere to logical or visual patterns through prediction or completion.5

In recent years, with the breakthrough advancements in machine learning and neural network technologies, GenAI has been undergoing a revolutionary transformation in music composition, shaping the spectrum of potential creative possibilities.6,7 Deep learning-based GenAI systems can analyze vast amounts of music data to identify patterns and generate musical fragments that imitate specific styles or create new sounds.8 As a result, it can produce original scores or enable innovative music creation by blending different styles, automatically generating soundtracks, or composing complete works. From technical support to artistic collaboration, AI is reshaping the paradigm of music creation while fostering continuous innovation within the industry.

Although GenAI offers innovative tools for music creation, its practical application for this purpose has been limited due to several complex challenges. The ambiguity of rights ownership raises issues regarding the legitimacy of training data sources, originality boundaries, and intellectual property rights allocations for the generated content.9–11 Meanwhile, some research efforts have focused on redefining standards of artistic originality.12 Moreover, this technology-driven “style collage” reduces artistic diversity.6 A more nuanced risk involves the power dynamics in human–machine collaboration.13 Over-reliance on AI-generated melodic frameworks or arrangements may shift aesthetic decision-making power from the artist to the algorithm, thus potentially impacting emotional depth and cultural value in musical works.14 Furthermore, privacy concerns and technological trust issues increase barriers to AI acceptance among musicians15. notably, the training of AI models often requires access to musicians’ personal creative libraries or unpublished materials. The lack of transparency in data collection and use, therefore, raises privacy concerns.16 Moreover, the inconsistent quality of AI-generated content, e.g., flawed melodic logic and mechanical emotional expression, has eroded music creators’ trust in such technology and made them hesitate to incorporate it into their creative processes.17.

Many studies have explored the acceptance of GenAI in artistic fields, such as image creation,18–20 research on the fundamental factors influencing the intention of music enthusiasts to use GenAI remains limited. Exploring the differences in GenAI acceptance among music enthusiasts and the factors influencing their attitudes can not only reveal the key barriers hindering the practical implementation of the technology but also inform the design of AI-assisted systems that better align with musicians’ creative workflows. This study adopts the TAM as the foundational theoretical framework due to its extensively validated robustness in explaining and predicting the adoption of emerging information technologies21,22. As one of the most widely used foundational models in market research, TAM centers on the core constructs of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, which are also fundamental components of user experience23. The model is particularly suitable for systems that emphasize practical functionalities and target novice or intermediate users who typically lack advanced expertise. A substantial body of research has demonstrated TAM’s adaptability across various contexts. As a novel intelligent system, AI-generated music composition tools require broader user acceptance, making TAM an appropriate choice for examining user behavior in this domain.

Furthermore, for an exploratory study in the early stages of AI music composition research, the TAM model offers several advantages: its structure is concise, the relationships between variables are clearly defined, and its measurement scales are well-established. These characteristics enable researchers to rapidly construct a framework with strong reliability and validity, while also providing dimensions that are easy to interpret and apply in future industry-oriented applications. Building upon this foundation, the present study extends the classic TAM model to reflect the specific context of music composition. The aim is to retain TAM’s robust explanatory power while more accurately capturing the core factors influencing music enthusiasts’ adoption of AI-assisted composition tools.

Specifically, five additional core constructs were introduced. Perceived Enjoyment reflects intrinsic motivation during the creative process and addresses TAM’s original limitations in capturing hedonic factors. Social Influence acknowledges that music composition is fundamentally a social and cultural practice, and that technology adoption is often shaped by peers, audiences, and the broader industry ecosystem. Perceived AI Risk and Perceived Trust respond to the inherent uncertainties of AI technologies, together forming a key mechanism for users’ risk assessment and confidence-building. Content Quality moves beyond the traditional emphasis on information accuracy in information systems, and instead contextualizes quality as the extent to which AI-generated music aligns with creators’ artistic intentions and cultural expectations. This expanded framework not only enhances TAM’s explanatory capacity in creative domains, but also offers a more comprehensive theoretical lens for understanding the complex interactions between music enthusiasts and emerging generative technologies.

Section 3 details the expanded TAM-based model and the research hypotheses. Section 4 describes the methodology used in this study. Section 5 presents the analytical results, highlighting variable relationships. Section 6 discusses the significance and implications of the study findings and provides a comprehensive interpretation of the results. Related research

TAM

The TAM, which was proposed by Davis21 based on the theory of reasoned action (TRA), aims to explain user adoption decisions regarding information technology systems. The model posits that user acceptance is driven by behavioral intentions, which are influenced by an individual’s attitude toward use. These intentions are shaped by two key cognitive dimensions: perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU).21 Perceived usefulness refers to a user’s belief that technology enhances their task performance, whereas perceived ease of use reflects the difficulty associated with using the technology. Together, these factors comprise the cognitive basis for technology adoption through positive reinforcement.22 TAM is a leading theoretical framework in information systems research and an essential platform for studying technology acceptance across various fields, including business,24 education,25 sports and fitness,26 and cultural creativity.27 Owing to its wide recognition in technology acceptance research,28 TAM helps scholars effectively explain and evaluate system or technology acceptance or rejection.29.

The TAM has been widely used in studies of AI system acceptance, thus demonstrating its effectiveness in assessing new technologies.30–33 Research applying TAM to explore AI acceptance has primarily focused on customer service,34,35 education,36 healthcare,37,38 organizational management,39 and consumer goods.40 These studies consistently confirm the strong predictive power and stable explanatory capacity of the TAM in AI system research. Its core constructs, PU and PEOU, continue to form the central explanatory pathways for user behavioral intention. One of TAM’s advantages lies in its excellent extensibility and inclusiveness, allowing for the easy integration of additional constructs or even the incorporation of entirely new theoretical models to explain technology acceptance behaviors in specific domains more precisely.41 Previous studies extending TAM by incorporating new variables have been widely validated and empirically tested.27,42,43 Therefore, in this study, we introduce several additional variables as an extension of TAM, aiming to gain a deeper understanding of music enthusiasts behavioral intentions toward using AI-assisted music composition applications.

Acceptance of AI systems in art and creativity

In the realm of artistic creativity, the issue of acceptance toward AI and GenAI systems has been extensively explored in prior research. These investigations have encompassed not only general user populations but also professionals in the creative industries, such as designers, architects, and music students. In the field of music, Li44 extended the TAM to investigate the factors influencing Chinese music majors’ intention to use intelligent music platforms. The study identified service quality, perceived enjoyment, and perceived cost as influencing factors. In addition, Zhang45 employed an extended UTAUT framework that incorporated personal innovativeness and perceived value to explore Chinese creators’ acceptance of AI-driven music creation tools. The results showed that personal innovativeness, performance expectancy, and effort expectancy influenced user intentions. Correia46 examined Portuguese listeners’ acceptance of AI-generated music by analyzing how consumption habits, interactions with AI technology, and genre preferences affected their attitudes.

In other creative fields, Lin and Xu47 explored the factors influencing the use of AI-based architectural design tools operated by robotic architects. Based on the TAM, they proposed an extended acceptance model that incorporates the constructs of diversity and controllability. Xu et al.20 developed an extended TAM to investigate the factors affecting ordinary users’ acceptance of an AI-based painting system in China. They identified the influence of hedonic motivation and perceived trust. Xu et al.48 then combined TAM with the Expectation Confirmation Model (ECM) to explore the factors influencing Chinese users’ continued intention to use AI-based painting tools. The findings highlighted the impact of emotional experience, followed by satisfaction, attitude, perceived usefulness, and perceived trust.

In addition, Yin et al.49 surveyed creative industry professionals in China to better understand their acceptance of AI by integrating AI anxiety into the UTAUT2. The study identified performance expectancy, social influence, hedonic motivation, habit, and AI anxiety as predictors of users’ intention to adopt AI-generated content. Li19 also extended the UTAUT model to investigate the intention of design students and experienced design professionals to use AI-generated content (AIGC) tools in creative design. The study incorporated additional constructs of perceived anxiety and perceived risk. Similarly, Wang and Zhang4 explored the factors influencing China’s Generation Z in adopting generative AI for artistic design, identifying optimism, creativity, effort expectancy, price value, and hedonic motivation as predictors of usage intention.

Previous research offers valuable insights into the acceptance of GenAI in music creation. Musicians are key players in terms of artistic creativity, and their intentions to use GenAI are often influenced by multiple factors, including PTR, perceived risk, content quality, perceived enjoyment, and social influence. For example, the balance between perceived trust and risk can shape musicians’ views on AI tool reliability, while content quality impacts their recognition of the artistic value of generated works. Perceived enjoyment may enhance their willingness to use GenAI through emotional satisfaction or innovative experiences during creation, at the same time, social influence can exert external pressure or incentives via peer adoption or industry trends. These factors may also be considered in the context of music creation.

Proposed model and hypotheses

Given the broad applicability and scalability of the TAM41 and its relevance to complex technologies such as AI,30–33 this study aims to expand and explore this model further. We extend the original TAM by adding five constructs: Perceived Enjoyment (PE), Social Influence (SI), Perceived AI Risk (PAIR), Perceived Trust (PTR), and Content Quality (CQ). Intention to Use (IU) serves as the dependent variable, and the independent variables include PE, SI, PAIR, PTR, CQ, PU, and PEOU. Perceived AI risk

Perceived AI risk refers to users’ subjective assessments of potential negative consequences and uncertainties associated with AI technology applications.19,50 Although AI technology can enhance creative efficiency,51 it also poses challenges related to intellectual property protection, personal privacy, and ethics.19 In particular, the legal uncertainties regarding the copyright and ownership of AI-generated music remain topics of debate.52 Musicians’ attitudes toward these risks can influence their willingness to adopt AI technology, thus further affecting their evaluation of the system’s functionality.53 When musicians harbor concerns about copyright disputes or ethical controversies, their evaluations of AI tools’ ability to enhance creative quality and efficiency may decline. The potential for legal costs and reputational damage can offset the productivity gains associated with AI.54 Moreover, complex risk assessments may impose additional cognitive load and operational steps, thus undermining perceptions of system ease of use.55 The uncertainty induced by perceived risks can also trigger anxiety, impeding users’ fluid exploration of system functions and, consequently, diminishing their behavioral intention to adopt the technology.56,57 Thus, we propose the following hypotheses.

H1

Perceived AI Risk has a negative impact on the perceived ease of use of the AI music app.

H2

Perceived AI Risk has a negative impact on the intentions to use the AI music app.

H3

Perceived AI Risk has a negative impact on the perceived usefulness of the AI music app.

Perceived trust

Perceived trust is a user’s belief in a system’s ability to reliably and safely assist them in achieving their goals amid technological uncertainty.58 Studies have shown that trust is important for AI technology adoption because it directly influences use intentions38,59 and enhances PU28,60 while optimizing PEOU.61 When users trust that an AI system can consistently generate high-quality content, handle data transparently, and adhere to ethical standards, their recognition of the AI’s value and effectiveness as a “creative partner” increases.62 Furthermore, trust can simplify users’ decision-making processes during operation.62 When users believe that the system will responsibly execute commands and safeguard sensitive information such as personal data, they are relieved from the need to monitor risks or adjust complex settings. This sense of “peace of mind” reduces cognitive load and facilitates a more seamless and intuitive interaction with the system. The same is true for AI-driven music creation, i.e., when musicians have confidence in the quality of the music generated by AI, the transparency of data use, and the ethical compliance, they are more likely to view AI as a “creative partner” rather than a “tool replacement”. This perspective deepens their acceptance of and willingness to use such technologies.52 Thus, we propose the following hypotheses.

H4:

Perceived Trust has a positive impact on the perceived ease of use of the AI music app.

H5:

Perceived Trust has a positive impact on the intentions to use the AI music app.

H6:

Perceived Trust has a positive impact on the perceived usefulness of the AI music app.

Content quality

Content quality can be defined as the extent to which learning contents are designed to fit workers’ needs.63 At its core, content quality concerns whether an informational product effectively supports the user’s goals. This concept emphasizes that quality assessment should be contextualized by both task objectives and users’ cognitive needs, rather than relying on abstract artistic standards. Accordingly, in the context of generative AI for music creation, we define content quality as the degree to which AI-generated musical output aligns with the creator’s artistic intent in terms of technical accuracy, expressive coherence, and cultural appropriateness. When AI-generated musical compositions are regarded as having greater emotional resonance, they are more likely to bridge the gap with traditional audiences who value the emotional connection with human composers.52 Thus, content quality directly influences their evaluations of AI tools and may indirectly strengthen their intentions to use those tools by improving the creative effectiveness. Additionally, previous studies have shown that when users perceive the content quality to be high, their perception of the system’s usefulness also enhanced, which in turn increases their intention to adopt the technology.63,64 Also, a positive correlation between CQ and PEOU was demonstrated.65 Thus, we propose the following hypotheses.

H7:

Content Quality has a positive impact on the perceived ease of use of the AI music app.

H8:

Content Quality has a positive impact on the intentions to use the AI music app.

H9:

Content Quality has a positive impact on the perceived usefulness of the AI music app.

Social influence

Social influence refers to the degree to which individuals feel that others expect them to adopt a specific technology and how these pressures affect their acceptance and use of the technology.66 Today, AI-generated music has moved beyond experimental technology to become a mainstream phenomenon, forming a widely recognized presence in public music consumption. In May 2023, the AI-generated version of Stefanie Sun’s Love in BC garnered over two million plays and frequently appeared on the Hot 100 chart, demonstrating the disruptive potential of AI in music production.67 By 2025, the globally renowned AI virtual band Velvet Sundown reached 1.3 million monthly active listeners, surpassing Chinese pop icon Faye Wong (1.25 million), with their AI-generated single Dust on the Wind dominating Spotify charts across multiple countries.68 Meanwhile, Chinese AI music platform Mureka saw its global traffic surge by 86.5% in March 2025 to 3.33 million monthly visits, the fastest growth among all AI music platforms worldwide. Such explosive growth compels musicians to confront the sociocultural presence and impact of generative AI in the music industry. In creative industries, social influence regarding AIGC applications can be shaped by the perceptions of teachers, colleagues, friends, and/or family.49 Studies have shown that social influence can enhance technology adoption through positive support and encouragement,69 i.e., more substantial social influence is correlated with a higher willingness to adopt. Furthermore, some research has revealed that when people think (or are told) that classical music pieces are composed by AI, they may not prefer these pieces as much.70 Thus, we propose the following hypotheses.

H10:

Social Influence has a positive impact on the perceived ease of use of the AI music app.

H11:

Social Influence has a positive impact on the intentions to use the AI music app.

H12:

Social Influence has a positive impact on the perceived usefulness of the AI music app.

Perceived enjoyment

Perceived enjoyment refers to the pleasure derived from using a particular system.71 This concept highlights the positive experiences users have with technology due to the inherent fun of these activities. This construct is particularly prominent in the AI-driven music composition scenario. When musicians create, arrange, or improvise musical compositions using AI tools, the pleasure brought by the system stems from both the final output and the technical and human–machine interactions during the creative process.6 In information systems research, perceived enjoyment influences users’ cognitive evaluations and behavioral intentions; specifically, it directly enhances PU72,73 and indirectly promotes technology acceptance by improving PEOU.74,75 Thus, we propose the following hypotheses.

H13:

Perceived Enjoyment has a positive effect on the perceived ease of use of the AI music app.

H14:

Perceived Enjoyment has a positive effect on the intentions to use the AI music app.

H15:

Perceived Enjoyment has a positive effect on the perceived usefulness of the AI music app.

Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use

PEOU and PU are key components of the TAM.21 PEOU refers to individuals’ subjective evaluations of the cognitive effort needed to use a system, and PU reflects users’ judgments about a system’s potential to improve their work efficiency. Studies have shown that PEOU positively influences PU in AI applications.76 Furthermore, PU has been identified as the primary predictor of use intentions.77 Additionally, studies on AI have confirmed that PEOU positively affects use intentions.78 Performance and expected investment are also expected to affect music creators’ acceptance of AI tools.45 Thus, we propose the following hypotheses.

H16:

Perceived Ease of Use has a positive influence on the Perceived Usefulness of the AI music app.

H17:

Perceived Ease of Use has a positive influence on the intentions to use the AI music app.

H18:

Perceived Usefulness has a positive influence on the intentions to use the AI music app.

Method design

Materials

This study used the AI music module (SkyMusic) in the Skywork AI app as the experimental material. SkyMusic is an AI system developed by China’s Kunlun Tech company, and it is China’s first music SOTA model. The Skywork 3.0 super model can quickly create music in various styles. Users can input their own lyrics, select a genre, and specify requirements, thus allowing the AI to generate melodies autonomously. Alternatively, users can let the AI create both the lyrics and the melody based on chosen genres. Currently, this module supports only song generation; instrumental options are not available. The generated works include vocals and instrumental arrangements, which may result in complete songs. The public beta testing of the SkyMusic model began in April 2024.

Questionnaire development

The questionnaire used in this study is divided into two parts. The first part focuses on gender, age, education, and experience. The second part asks users to rate the AI music app across eight dimensions, i.e., in terms of PU, PEOU, IU, PAIR, PTR, CQ, SI, and PE. To ensure validity and minimize participant fatigue,79 we designed a concise template based on a standardized questionnaire with 4–5 items per construct. A total of 35 items were created using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Some items were screened and rephrased for this study. For example, PU (e.g., “Using the AI music app is useful for my creative work”) was adapted from Davis21; PEOU (e.g., “I find the AI music app easy to use”) followed reports by Davis21 and Venkatesh71; IU (e.g., “I am willing to try the AI music app in the future”) was adapted from Liu80 and Dholakia81; PE (e.g., “I find using the AI music app fun”) was adapted from Koufaris82 and Moon and Kim83; PTR (e.g., “I believe the services provided by the AI music app are clear and reliable”) was adapted from Ponte et al.84 and Choung et al.85; CQ(e.g., “I believe the AI music app enhances my overall quality of composition”) was adapted from Almulla86; SI (e.g., “My teacher thinks that I should use the AI music app”) was adapted from Venkatesh et al.66; and PAIR (e.g., “I am concerned that using the AI music app may lead to copyright disputes”) was adapted from Wolfinbarger et al.87 and Ponte et al.84.

Pilot study

After establishing the questionnaire structure and candidate items, we invited five experts in GenAI software to evaluate the survey. They assessed the consistency of each item’s content, concept, and wording. The expert group provided suggestions, including revising the PTR item statements to “I believe the services provided by the AI music app are clear and reliable” and “I believe the AI music app is competent in providing music composition services” to fit the research scenario better.

The developed tool was then pre-tested with ordinary users to verify item comprehension and experimental procedures. Fifty music professionals and enthusiasts were randomly selected from social media platforms (Weibo, WeChat) to complete the survey. All participants had prior experience using the SkyMusic platform. Among the 50 participants, 15 were under 18. During the pre-screening phase, we conducted brief interviews to confirm that these younger participants possessed basic knowledge of music composition and AI tool operation. We also ensured that they had a foundational understanding of core questionnaire concepts such as PEOU and PU. Moreover, data from the 6th Report on Internet Use Among Chinese Minors indicate that 19% of underage internet users have used generative AI tools,88 supporting the inclusion of this demographic as a reasonable representation of potential users of AI music tools. All participants completed the survey. They demonstrated a clear understanding of the 7-point Likert scale and were able to respond based on their initial impressions. The majority reported that the items were clearly worded and easy to comprehend. The completion time ranged from approximately 1 to 5 min. Two participants suggested that some statements should be more specific, and they were refined as a result. Three participants recommended adding dimension names before each question for greater clarity regarding question organization. This feedback was incorporated by adding dimension names and rewording some items. The final survey used in this study is presented in the supplementary material.

Participants

Given that AI music operates primarily as a consumer-facing system, this study focuses on intermediate users—namely, music enthusiasts—as the target population. Furthermore, the age design of the questionnaire considered the fact that AI music technology is still very new, it takes some time to accept and learn how it works, and AI music software is relatively professional software. To avoid small samples of some age groups, which could affect the validity of the statistical analysis, we did not further subdivide the “30 years old and above” age group. In addition, “under 18 years old” corresponds to minors in the legal sense; however, due to the unique requirements of the music major, many students who want to enter conservatories or study music at university must start learning music earlier. Some music majors (e.g., piano) even require students to start learning from a young age. Therefore, even if the participants in this age group are under 18 years of age, they likely already have music creation experience. Accordingly, this group was retained to fully capture the behavioral intentions of music enthusiasts and professionals at different stages of life. We recruited 530 Chinese users online, all of whom signed an informed consent form. A total of 511 responses were collected; however, 34 respondents answered “no” to the question “Do you consider yourself a music enthusiast (including either an amateur or a professional)?” and were excluded from the study. Furthermore, 48 samples were invalidated due to various criteria: completion times under one minute (n = 22), item repetition rates over 85% (n = 24), and outliers identified in SPSS analysis (n = 2). This left a total of 429 valid samples. The sample size exceeded ten times the number of items to ensure reliable covariance-based structural equation modeling results.89,90 Demographic data (Table 1) indicated that there were 189 male and 240 female participants. Most participants were aged 26–30 years (46.9%), followed by 18–25 years (27.7%). Additionally, a majority held a bachelor’s degree (53%) and had heard of or used SkyMusic (59.7%). Only 5.6% had never before encountered this app.

Procedures

This study consisted of several steps. First, the researcher posted recruitment advertisements on social media (Rednote, Weibo, WeChat, QQ) and offline at music schools and music production companies. Recruited participants read the experiment introduction and signed an informed consent form. Second, participants accessed the introduction page via a link or QR code to review the experiment instructions. Third, they watched a 447 s screencast demonstrating how the app is used (i.e., tutorials). Fourth, participants completed an online questionnaire through a provided link. Finally, upon receiving the completed questionnaire, the researcher checked for missing data; participants who completed the questionnaire received 5 RMB.

Results

Two software packages were used for data analysis in this study: the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS v.26) and analysis of moment structure (AMOS v.28). Data cleaning and screening were performed, and all data used in the analysis were complete with no missing values (invalid data were removed). Before analysis, the Jarque–Bera (skewness-kurtosis) test was used to check whether the data was normally distributed. According to Tabachnick et al.,91 the threshold for the skewness-kurtosis test is 2.58; thus, all items in the data set (Table 2) were normally distributed (i.e., < 2.58).

Measurement model

According to the results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.924, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.8, indicating a high degree of correlation among the variables and demonstrating that the dataset was highly suitable for factor analysis. The Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded a statistically significant result (χ2 = 8506.178, df = 595, p < 0.001), further supporting the appropriateness of conducting factor analysis. For factor extraction, principal component analysis was employed, and factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were retained. After rotation, a total of eight factors were extracted, with a cumulative variance explanation rate of 71.118%, suggesting that the extracted common factors effectively account for the majority of the information contained in the original variables.

We evaluated the convergent validity, discriminant validity, and goodness of fit of the model. According to standard guidelines, factor loadings ≥ 0.70 indicate good reliability; composite reliability (CR) and Cronbach’s α > 0.70 indicate sufficient internal consistency; average variance extracted (AVE) values ≥ 0.50 indicate adequate convergent validity; and the square root of each AVE should exceed the corresponding inter-construct correlations to demonstrate sufficient discriminant validity.90,92 As shown in Table 3, Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.859 to 0.893, all exceeding the target threshold. The CR and AVE for all structures met the recommended thresholds of 0.7 and 0.5 respectively. The standardized factor loadings were all greater than 0.7, indicating strong internal consistency.92 Table 4 shows that the average square root of all structures was greater than their respective inter-construct estimates, thus confirming that each structure was more closely related to its own measurement than those of others. Thus, this study exhibits discriminant validity.92 In addition, the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio was calculated to further assess discriminant validity. As shown in Table 5, all constructs demonstrated HTMT values below the commonly recommended threshold of 0.90, indicating strong discriminant validity. Furthermore, multicollinearity diagnostics were conducted via regression analysis. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all predictors ranged from 1.312 to 1.650, which are well below the commonly accepted cutoff value of 5, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a concern.

Discussion

This study investigated Chinese music enthusiasts’ behavioral intentions to use GenAI for music creation based on an extended TAM comprising seven key factors: PU, PEOU, PAIR, PE, PTR, SI, and CQ. Path analysis revealed that 13 hypotheses were supported, and five were not. The original TAM structure remained robust. Perceived AI risk negatively affected IU and PEOU but was not significantly correlated with PU. Perceived trust was positively correlated with PEOU and PU, but not IU. CQ had a positive effect on PEOU, PU, and IU. However, SI was only related to PU and had no significant effect on PEOU or IU. Perceived enjoyment positively affected PEOU and PU, but not IU. In addition, there were no differences in evaluations of the AI music app among music enthusiasts based on gender, age, or educational background.

Perceived Al risk and perceived trust

The results confirmed that in the context of AI music composition applications, PAIR significantly and negatively affects PEOU and IU but has no significant effect on PU. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that perceived risk hinders behavioral intention.19 Notably, PAIR also negatively impacts PEOU, potentially due to the fluid nature of music composition requiring immediate feedback. Risk perception (e.g., data privacy leaks, unclear copyright ownership of creative outputs, or uncontrollability of AI-generated content) may raise users’ concerns about the safety and stability of the tool, which indirectly increases their perception of operational complexity.93 For example, creators might fear needing to apply extra effort to learn risk-averse practices or verify compliance of AI-generated content, leading them to perceive a greater burden, rather than ease of use. In addition, PEOU encompasses the tool’s interface intuitiveness as well as users’ confidence in its efficiency and reliability, and PAIR may undermine this confidence. In contrast, PU is more directly related to the core functional value of the tool, e.g., whether AI can quickly stimulate inspiration, optimize creative efficiency, or expand creative boundaries. Users may evaluate these functional benefits independently of risk perception. Even if creators acknowledge potential risks, they may still recognize AI’s usefulness if it significantly improves creation quality or efficiency. The negative emotions stemming from risks can directly or indirectly influence an individual’s intention to use a tool by affecting the operating experience and psychological acceptance. This relationship highlights the unique nature of AI tool selection for artistic creation purposes. Creators often prioritize functional utility to fulfill their creative needs; however, concerns about risks may reduce their willingness to continue using a tool due to increased psychological pressure or operational friction.

Notably, the results showed that PTR significantly and positively affects both PEOU and PU, consistent with previous studies on AI system acceptance.20,48,85 However, unlike earlier research,48,94 this study indicates that PTR does not significantly impact IU. This may be because PTR influences IU indirectly by enhancing users’ perceptions of the tool’s functional value (e.g., via ease of use and usefulness), rather than directly driving behavior. For example, even if users trust an AI system’s reliability, technical instability or discrepancies between its output and the user’s expectations can undermine this trust and fail to increase IU. Moreover, the intentions to use GenAI for music composition might stem from diverse motives, including professional needs and/or personal artistic expression, rather than solely from trust in technology. Additionally, PTR could interact with other variables or have intermediary effects that dilute its direct impact. For example, the negative influence of PAIR may weaken the potential positive effect of PTR on IU.

Content quality, social influence and perceived enjoyment

The results show that CQ has significant positive effects on PEOU, IU, and PU. These results align with previous studies on technology acceptance.63,65 High CQ suggests that AI-generated music better meets creators’ aesthetic standards and professional needs; as a result, users intuitively recognize the tool’s utility. Additionally, high CQ reduces perceived complexity in operating the tool; in other words, when the output quality is high, users may find the tool’s design more “natural” or “suitable”, which enhances their ease-of-use evaluations. Moreover, high-quality content can boost creators’ confidence and motivation while minimizing frustration from technical limitations, thereby increasing their willingness to continue using the tool.

Surprisingly, SI had a significant effect on only PU but did not affect PEOU or IU. These results are in contrast to previous studies on AI art creation systems that found that SI significantly impacted IU.19,49 This may be because, in music composition, SI (e.g., recommendations from industry leaders or teachers) may indirectly strengthen the user’s perception of the practical value of the tool. When creators see others successfully using AI to improve their work, they are more likely to recognize its functional benefits for achieving creative goals. However, PEOU relies more on the tool’s design and the user’s technical adaptability than on external use behaviors. Thus, SI is less effective in enhancing users’ subjective evaluations of the tool’s intuitiveness or functionality. In general, IU depends not only on recognizing the tool’s value, but also on personal experiences (e.g., risk perception) and immediate creative needs. Music creators may prioritize whether the tool can address their creative challenges over its popularity among others.

In addition, the results showed that PE significantly and positively influences PEOU and PU but has no significant effect on IU. These results suggest that when users find AI-assisted music composition enjoyable, they are more likely to perceive the technology as helpful and easy to use. However, these perceptions do not necessarily translate into a willingness to adopt the technology. This finding is in contrast to previous research, which indicated that enjoyment directly affects use intentions.44,48,49 The formation of IU may depend on numerous factors, including immediate pleasure, functional recognition, actual utility, potential risks, alignment with creative goals, and individual autonomy in using the technology. For example, musicians might have privacy concerns regarding their personal compositions. Such users may hesitate to share their compositional processes or unpublished works with third-party services. Thus, even if the tool offers short-term enjoyment or appears useful, privacy concerns could hinder its adoption.

Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use

In the context of AI-assisted music creation, the study results indicate that both PEOU and PU significantly enhance IU. These findings are consistent with previous research on AI systems.30,48,95,96 The influence of PU on IU is particularly strong, with an impact coefficient of β = 0.306, which is significant at the p < 0.001 level. This indicates that the more useful users find AI music creation tools, the more likely they are to intend to use these tools to create. Similarly, PEOU positively affects IU with a coefficient of β = 0.145 at p < 0.05, indicating that easier-to-use technologies encourage user adoption. In addition, PEOU significantly impact PU, meaning that users who find the technology easy to use are more likely to also perceive it as valuable. Overall, these relationships show that in AI music composition, both PEOU and PU directly promote user IU by indirectly improving user perceptions of AI tool usefulness. Therefore, developing user-friendly and intuitive AI-driven music composition tools can effectively boost user acceptance and willingness to engage. Overall, the strong predictive power of PU on IU underscores the integration of instrumental rationality and artistic expression in music creation. When AI tools are shown to enhance both efficiency and expressive potential, users—even in inspiration-driven artistic domains—tend to follow a rational cost–benefit decision-making framework. In contrast, the relatively weaker influence of PEOU suggests that in professional creative contexts, technical complexity is not the primary barrier; domain experts may be more willing to tolerate cognitive load in pursuit of high-value outcomes.

Demographics

The study results indicated no significant differences in how demographic variables (e.g., gender, age, and educational background) influenced music enthusiasts’ evaluations of AI music apps. This may be due to the universal core needs of music composition and the adaptability of AI tools across different groups. First, essential aspects of music composition, such as inspiration and artistic expression, are consistent across genders and ages.97,98 However, further subgroup SEM analyses by gender revealed subtle distinctions: among female users, PE influenced both IU and PU, whereas no such effects were observed among male users. This suggests that female music enthusiasts may place greater emphasis on the entertainment and hedonic aspects of AI tools, which in turn meaningfully shape their evaluations of system functionality. When AI tools are viewed as neutral aids in creation, music enthusiasts from diverse backgrounds tend to assess their technical features based on professional relevance, rather than personal traits. For example, all musicians might evaluate the originality of an AI-generated melody using industry-standard music theory instead of generational aesthetic preferences.99 Moreover, perceived risk appears to be a critical factor, particularly among users with higher levels of prior experience. PAIR can influence both PU and PEOU, indicating heightened sensitivity to uncertainties, especially those related to copyright and legal concerns. Such perceived risks may trigger skepticism and inhibit seamless exploration of system features, thereby negatively impacting usability. The moderating effect of experience on the relationship between PAIR and IU further reinforces this interpretation.

Furthermore, with the rise of digital natives, the impact of educational background on digital tool use has diminished owing to widespread technology adoption.100 It is reasonable to assume that all modern musicians, whether formally trained or self-taught, possess a basic level of digital literacy. As a result, educational disparities may not affect evaluations of AI tools because users can rely on their technical intuition. Additionally, AI music composition software is designed to be rather inclusive, with user-friendly features that cater to various groups’ needs. This inclusivity helps reduce evaluation biases related to gender, age, or education level.

Conclusions, limitations, and future work

This study explores the key factors influencing music enthusiasts’ adoption of GenAI-assisted music composition. We applied an extended TAM to systematically analyze the mechanisms driving music enthusiasts’ acceptance and use of an AI music composition application. The main research objectives included the following. First, we aimed to verify the explanatory power of seven core variables (i.e., PTR, PAIR, CQ, PE, SI, PU, and PEOU) on music enthusiasts’ behavioral intentions to use an AI music composition application. Second, we aimed to investigate whether demographic factors, such as gender, age, and education level, moderate these relationships. By introducing five new constructs into the TAM, this study broadens its theoretical framework to facilitate a more balanced discussion on the pros and cons of AI music creation systems. This approach was applied to better explain and predict music enthusiasts’ behavioral intentions toward using GenAI, while also informing the development of AI music composition software.

The conclusions of this study are primarily based on empirical findings from music enthusiasts, and its theoretical contribution lies in overcoming the limitations of traditional TAM in explaining user behavior in AI-driven creative contexts. By introducing contextualized variables such as PE and CQ, the study uncovers a synergistic mechanism between instrumental cognition and intrinsic motivation, and confirms the mediating roles of PE, CQ, PTR, and PAIR in shaping both PEOU and PU in AI-based music creation. Additionally, this study constructs a risk–trust framework for the AI music domain, revealing that concerns about PAIR are mainly concentrated on the erosion of artistic uniqueness and conventional privacy issues, while trust tends to be established through the quality and relevance of the generated content. This deepens the understanding of how perceived trust is formed in human–AI collaboration. It is worth noting that if the target population were extended to professional musicians, significant differences might arise: professionals, with higher demands for artistic originality and industry reputation, may exhibit stronger sensitivity to content quality, and the weight of social influence in their decision-making could increase due to competitive industry pressures. These group-level differences suggest possible moderating effects across varying levels of musical expertise and offer a valuable comparative benchmark for future research. Accordingly, the theoretical framework not only defines the current conclusions’ applicability within amateur and semi-professional user groups, but also lays a practical and operational foundation for future studies targeting professional creators.

The practical significance of this study lies in providing multi-dimensional strategic guidance for AI technology developers and music industry practitioners. First, the negative impact of PAIR on music enthusiasts’ IU and PEOU, as well as the findings from the moderation analysis, suggest that developers should prioritize system transparency, data privacy protection, and error feedback mechanisms. Visualizing algorithm processes or offering risk assessment tools can alleviate users’ concerns about losing control over the technology or their creative sovereignty, thus lowering psychological barriers. Second, strong correlations between PTR and both PEOU and PU underscore the importance of brand reputation and technical reliability. Developers can build a professional image through third-party certification, user case demonstrations, and ongoing technical improvements, while enhancing software interface design to help users quickly gain confidence when using the system. CQ positively affects IU, PU, and PEOU. Thus, technology optimization should focus on improving sound quality accuracy, style diversity, and flexibility in human–machine collaboration with the AI-driven music composition tool. Developers are advised to incorporate feedback from professional music enthusiasts, build a comprehensive sound feature database, and design flexible parameter controls to support personalized remixing. In addition, SI was only significantly related to PU. This indicates that promotion strategies should focus on demonstrating AI’s practical value in tasks. Over-reliance on social pressure or authoritative endorsements should be avoided. In addition, the role of PE in promoting PEOU and PU indicates that music enthusiasts appreciate the entertaining aspects of technology and the joy of creation. Incorporating gamification elements into software design can enhance improvisation fun without compromising professionalism. Although PE does not directly impact IU, its indirect effects on PU and PEOU offer a potential pathway for increasing willingness to use. Finally, no significant differences were found among demographic variables, suggesting that technology adoption strategies should prioritize optimizing the tool functions and addressing general user psychological needs, rather than creating distinct solutions for specific groups.

Despite the successful validation of multiple theoretical hypotheses through the extended TAM in this study, its limitations warrant careful consideration. First and foremost, the geographical limitation of the sample is significant. The study focused exclusively on Chinese music enthusiasts. However, music composition, as a culturally embedded creative practice, is inherently shaped by the interplay of regional cultural values, aesthetic traditions, and socio-technical environments.101,102 From a cultural value perspective, traditional Chinese artistic philosophy, particularly the notion of "the unity of man and nature" emphasizes the uniqueness of the creator’s subjectivity and emotional expression.103 This emphasis on the irreplaceability of human creativity may lead to subtle tensions in Chinese music enthusiasts’ acceptance of AI-generated content. When AI is perceived merely as a ‘technical mediator’ rather than an ‘emotional subject,’ its instrumental value is more likely to be embraced. In contrast, if AI’s generative process is interpreted as a form of 'subject-less creation,' it may trigger ontological questions about the nature of art itself. By comparison, research from other regions104,105 often frames AI as a co-creative partner, a view that may align with postmodern Western notions of decentralization. Therefore, the conclusions of this study may be limited in their generalizability due to the cultural specificity of the sample, and their applicability should be validated across musician groups in other cultural contexts. The discussion section should further engage in a critical evaluation of this cultural boundary to clarify the scope of applicability of the findings. Ideally, future research should conduct cross-cultural comparisons or validation studies in regions with significant differences in musical traditions and attitudes toward technology, in order to more comprehensively reveal the applicability and limitations of technology acceptance models across diverse cultural contexts.

Additionally, the lack of diversity among the participant population may weaken the comprehensiveness of the conclusions. This study focused on music enthusiasts as research subjects, however, professional musicians may differ significantly in their evaluations of content quality, perceived risk, and even core TAM variables. Future research should conduct subgroup analyses to test the model’s applicability across different musician identities. By refining sample labels and categorizing participant roles more precisely, researchers can better capture intergroup differences and generate richer cross-group insights. Furthermore, although a pilot test was conducted to ensure that underage participants could understand the core concepts, and they were considered an important emerging user group, it must be acknowledged that they may differ systematically from experienced music enthusiasts in dimensions such as creative expectations, reliance on technology, and risk perception. For example, teenagers may focus more on the entertainment value and ease of use of AI tools, whereas professionals may be more concerned with issues like copyright ownership and artistic originality. While such group heterogeneity reflects the real-world diversity of user composition, it may also introduce potential variations in the interpretation of model path coefficients. Future studies could employ stratified sampling or multi-group analysis to further examine how age and professional experience moderate relationships within the technology acceptance model.

In terms of research methods, the study’s reliance on self-reported data and the limitations of the cross-sectional design restricted the depth and accuracy of causal inferences. Self-reported data are susceptible to social desirability bias. Especially in a context where GenAI has become a focus of public discussion, music enthusiasts may tend to adjust their responses to conform to the mainstream narrative of “high technology acceptance” or “concerns about technology risks”, rather than reflecting their true personal use intentions or behavioral motivations. Additionally, cross-sectional designs can capture only static data at a single point in time. Although they can verify some hypotheses, such as the negative impact of PAIR on PEOU, they cannot clearly identify the specific sources of risk perception. Future research can enhance the reliability of causal inference through multidimensional methodological improvements. First, in terms of data collection, behavioral indicators and objective logs can be introduced—for example, by collaborating to obtain music enthusiasts’ actual usage records of AI tools, as well as the proportion of AI-generated content in their works. These behavioral data can then be cross-validated with self-reported attitude data. Second, a longitudinal tracking design can be adopted, collecting data at multiple stages over the study period to observe the dynamic trajectories of technology acceptance constructs. Key events can also be marked to identify triggering factors and evolution mechanisms of risk perception. Additionally, qualitative validation methods may be integrated, for instance, through in-depth interviews or participatory observation, to uncover the latent motivations behind self-reported data.

Finally, the limitations of the theoretical model itself also warrant deeper reflection. Although the extended TAM attempts to adapt to the music composition context by incorporating variables such as perceived AI risk and CQ, its core framework still inherits structures from adjacent fields (e.g., education, visual design, and general AI systems). It remains insufficiently tailored to the specificities of music creation. Specifically, music composition is not merely the application of technical tools, but a complex interaction involving emotional expression, the shaping of personal style, and the construction of artistic identity. Critical driving and inhibiting factors, such as creative autonomy, artistic identity, or algorithmic bias, have yet to be fully integrated into the existing model. Musical cognition plays a critical role in shaping users’ evaluation of AI-generated music, as it determines their aesthetic judgment and ability to refine harmonic structures, formal coherence, and emotional expression. At the same time, creative autonomy influences users’ sense of ownership and artistic control over the final work. This is particularly important for professional composers who value personal style and artistic integrity. Future studies should incorporate theoretical perspectives from both musicology and cognitive science to build extended models that reflect the unique characteristics of music creation. Variables such as creative autonomy, artistic identity, and algorithmic bias, which are highly specific to this domain, should be introduced to better explain the complex mechanisms underlying user behavior in AI-assisted composition.

Due to limitations in scope and the composition of the preliminary sample, the present study primarily focused on a broad group of music enthusiasts. It did not delve into the complex cognitive regulation processes or the core demand for autonomy experienced by highly professional music enthusiasts during collaboration with AI systems. These overlooked constructs potentially influence users’ behavioral intentions and are crucial to understanding the essence of the “tension between human expressiveness and machine-generated output” in human–machine collaboration. This tension is not merely a matter of technology acceptance. However, rather, it is a concentrated manifestation of the collision between the “human” subjectivity and the “machine” coldness in artistic creation. Future research could explore the construction of a human–AI co-creativity model, enabling AI to participate in creative decision-making processes and collaborate with professional musicians during their ideation stage. Another promising direction involves incorporating affective computing into AI systems by capturing musicians’ physiological signals and creative contexts in real time. This approach would enhance the AI’s understanding of the user’s emotional state and artistic environment, allowing it to dynamically adjust the emotional tension and structural complexity of the generated content. Such advancements would facilitate a shift from mere functional responsiveness to genuine emotional resonance in AI-assisted music creation.

Future research may consider moving beyond the unidirectional “technology acceptance” framework by constructing empirical analytical paths grounded in collaboration theory106 and a theory of copyright authorship.107 For instance, ethnographic observation could be employed to document music enthusiasts’ long-term creative interactions with AI tools. Creative logs and interview data could complement this to analyze the dynamic mechanisms of human-AI negotiation. Alternatively, a multi-case comparative method could be applied by selecting creators from various music genres who have established stable collaborative models with AI, and examining shared patterns and divergences in terms of authorship distribution and hybrid creativity traits. At the same time, socio-technical systems theory108 could be introduced to explore how the technical features of AI tools and the broader music industry ecosystem jointly shape the legitimacy boundaries of this emerging mode of music creation.
