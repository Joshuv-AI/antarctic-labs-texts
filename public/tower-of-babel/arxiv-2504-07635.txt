Generative Artificial Intelligence for Internet of Things Computing: A Systematic Survey
Fabrizio Mangione, Claudio Savaglio, Giancarlo Fortino

Abstract
. The integration of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) and the Internet of Things (IoT) is attracting significant attention due to their rapid, individual evolution and their widespread joint adoption across domains such as Healthcare, Manufacturing, and Smart Cities. Consequently, their increasing popularity has catalyzed extensive research to understand the interplay between GenAI and IoT, and how their synergy can advance the state-of-the-art in both domains. However, despite the increasing prominence of GenAI for IoT Computing, much of the existing research remains focused on specific, narrowly scoped applications. This fragmented approach highlights the need for a more comprehensive analysis of the potential, challenges, and implications of GenAI integration within the broader IoT ecosystem. The survey exactly aims to address this gap by providing a holistic overview of the opportunities and issues arising from the convergence of these mainstream paradigms. Our contribution is a systematic literature review following the PRISMA methodology: a comparison framework is presented, and well-defined research questions are outlined to comprehensively explore the current and future directions of GenAI-aided IoT Computing.

2.
Background

2.1.
Towards Intelligent Internet of Things Computing

The IoT defies a straightforward definition due to its expansive and evolving nature, which encompasses diverse technologies, applications, and use cases
(
Fortino et al., 2021
)
. At its core, IoT refers to a network of uniquely identifiable physical or virtual entities, known as
Things
, equipped with sensors, actuators, and embedded computing. These
Things
communicate, exchange data, and act autonomously or semi-autonomously, bridging the physical and digital realms to create adaptive environments hosting context-aware processes
(
Fortino et al., 2018
)
. IoT systems range in complexity from basic implementations to large-scale, self-managed ecosystems, enabling ubiquitous connectivity, interoperable communication, self-management and sophisticated service delivery
(
Casadei et al., 2025
)
. Given such complexity and versatility, it is not surprising that
the interpretation of IoT varies significantly among stakeholders, such as businesses, academic researchers, and standardization bodies, each adopting perspectives shaped by their priorities and expertise.

Regardless of the specific interpretation or domain of interest, however, the IoT Computing (intended as the paradigm dealing with all the broader computational aspects within IoT systems, including not only the software but also the data, architectural and infrastructural aspects)
encompasses three main dimensions
(
Atzori et al., 2010
)
:
Internet
,
Things
, and
Semantic
one.
The
Internet-Centric perspective
emphasizes robust connectivity and advanced communication technologies, which are essential for linking diverse devices into a global infrastructure. The focus is on standardized protocols and innovative networking solutions ensuring interoperability, scalability, and reliability across heterogeneous systems. Differently, the
Things-Centric perspective
highlights the
Things
and the technologies required for the provision of the cyber-physical services enabling novel forms of interplay between the real and the virtual worlds as well as among humans and (smart) objects. Finally, the
Semantic-Centric perspective
addresses the challenge of ensuring data relevance and usability, leveraging semantic frameworks to establish intelligent systems capable of understanding relationships and context within vast datasets. This facilitates adaptability, situational awareness, and informed decision-making. Integrating these three dimensions transforms IoT Computing into a holistic framework that seamlessly bridges physical and digital systems, also enhancing the overall scalability, robustness, and intelligence.

Driven by the shift toward Intelligent IoT Computing, since 2012 AI has been leveraged to analyze in real-time vast volumes of sensor data, facilitate the optimization of processes, devices and networks, enhance the user experiences, and improve operational efficiency across multiple sectors, including Healthcare, Smart Cities, and Industry 4.0
(
Wu et al., 2025
)
. Where to deploy AI to engineer intelligent IoT systems opened, however, another discussion. Initially,
Cloud
services have been instrumental in supporting this convergence by providing the computational capacity
required for training sophisticated AI models and storing extensive IoT data streams. However, exclusive reliance on Cloud services introduces significant network latency, efficiency and privacy issues, which presents a critical bottleneck for several IoT applications
(
Sheikh et al., 2025
)
. To mitigate these challenges, the paradigm of
Edge AI

(
Barbuto et al., 2023
;
Cordova-Cardenas et al., 2025
)
has emerged as a compelling solution. Edge AI entails deploying AI capabilities closer to the data source, at the network’s periphery, thereby facilitating more rapid data processing and decision-making. By performing computations locally, Edge AI reduces the need to transmit data to distant cloud servers, significantly diminishing latency while simultaneously enhancing data privacy and security. Notwithstanding its advantages, Edge AI is not without its limitations. The computational and energy resources available at edge nodes are typically far more constrained than those in centralized cloud environments, thereby limiting the complexity and scale of the AI models that can be executed at the edge
(
Ali and Dornaika, 2025
)
. Additionally, maintaining distributed intelligence across heterogeneous edge devices poses substantial challenges in terms of coordination, scalability, and the continuous updating of AI models.
As such, for harnessing the full potential of AI-enabled IoT ecosystems is of paramount importance:
(i)
to achieve an effective balance between cloud and edge processing to spread intelligence in the computing continuum, and
(ii)
to propose novel solutions to mitigate inherent IoT challenges related to poor data quality, not intuitive human interaction, complex device management, and unpredictable system reliability
(
Byabazaire et al., 2020
;
Hamdan et al., 2020
)
.
Exactly there, as explored in the rest of the article,
GenAI
possesses capabilities that distinguish it from both traditional AI and Edge AI (mostly exploiting deterministic approaches rather than probabilistic ones), thereby fostering a uniquely different contribution to IoT Computing for the engineering of intelligent systems
(
Wang et al., 2024c
)
.

2.2.
Deep Generative Models - DGMs

Today, the terms DGMs and GenAI are frequently used interchangeably as synonyms. However, DGMs should be used when referring to a specific class of models, while GenAI should be used in a broader context to describe the practical implementation of DGMs and other techniques or multimodal architectures to create real-world applications.
Driven by rapid advancements in Neural Networks (NNs) and significant increases in computational power, DGMs has emerged as a central area of study within Machine Learning (ML) and AI, particularly in its ability to approximate complex data distributions and generate synthetic data that closely resemble real-world instances. Its applications span a broad spectrum of domains,
encompassing classical ML modalities, such as Text analysis, Image analysis, and Audio analysis
(
Team et al., 2024
;
Hurst et al., 2024
)
, to emerging IoT applications in areas such as Healthcare
(
Kung and others, 2023
)
, Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS)
(
Mangione et al., 2024
)
, and Smart Cities
(
Li et al., 2024c
)
. As opposed to
Discriminative AI models
, which focus solely on partitioning and categorizing data points, DGMs attempt to capture the
underlying
probabilistic distribution
, thereby facilitating the synthesis of new, high-fidelity examples. In fact, from a mathematical standpoint, the main objective of DGMs is to learn a representation of an unknown and often intractable probability distribution in a high-dimensional space. Rather than explicitly modeling the probability itself, the focus is on learning a generator that transforms samples from a simple and well-understood distribution (typically Gaussian noise) into data points that closely resemble the observed data. However, learning such a generator is generally impractical or infeasible for most real-world datasets, and even when it is possible, it remains a challenging task. Consequently, it has become standard practice to approximate the generator by using generic functions like NNs with multiple hidden layers. This approach forms the core design principle in DGMs, where the generator is represented by a feed-forward Deep Neural Network (DNN)
(
Tomczak, 2024
)
.
To fully grasp the potential of DGMs, two fundamental concepts must be considered:
uncertainty
and
understanding
. Consider a traditional AI classification task that categorizes objects into two classes. If a model is trained only to separate classes (the discriminative approach), it learns where to draw the boundary between them. When a new sample falls clearly on one side of that boundary, the model confidently assigns it to a class, even if the sample looks very unusual compared to what it has seen during training . When the model also learns how the data are distributed (the joint or generative perspective), it not only decides which class a sample belongs to, but also whether the sample looks plausible at all. In this case, a sample may be far from the boundary but still lie in a region where neither class typically appears. Practically, this allows the model to recognize that the input is unfamiliar or unlikely, leading to more cautious decisions, better uncertainty awareness, and improved handling of out-of-distribution or anomalous data
(
Bond-Taylor et al., 2021
;
Bishop and Nasrabadi, 2006
)
.
So, albeit in the literature DGMs are frequently regarded primarily as mechanisms for generating new data that mimics human creativity, behavior, or appearance, we advocate for a broader perspective, where the real value lies in learning how data are distributed
(
Kingma and Welling, 2022
)
. This broader use of DGMs turns them into a core building block for realizing AI systems that are more reliable, robust, and trustworthy in real-world scenarios.

The process through which DGMs capture the underlying probabilistic distribution of data is central to their ability to generate realistic outputs. This objective is accomplished using a variety of methodologies, each offering distinct approaches to learning and representing data distributions. Among the most prominent strategies are Adversarial Training (as utilized in GANs
(
Goodfellow et al., 2014
)
) and Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015b
)
, which forms the theoretical foundation for Diffusion-based methods. Both are widely adopted and impactful strategies in generative modeling. For the purpose of this discussion, we focus on DGMs that operate based on the principle of MLE (i.e., to choose the model parameters to maximize the probability assigned to the observed data, ensuring an accurate representation of the underlying distribution): indeed, MLE isn’t universal for DGMs, but many can adapt to it
(
Theis et al., 2015
)
.

2.3.
Taxonomy of DGMs and IoT-related implications

Having emphasized the critical importance and wide-ranging applicability of DGMs, in the following we examine how such models are formulated, according the taxonomy shown in Fig.
1
and presented in
(
Goodfellow, 2017
)
. The green rectangle represents the overarching category of DGMs, while the orange rectangles denote high-level subclasses that further branch into more specific model types (in light blue rectangles). Indeed, even based on the same MLE principles, DGMs differ in their approach to representing or approximating the likelihood.
Specifically, Explicit density models (left branch of Fig.
1
), construct a probability density providing an explicit likelihood that can be maximized. Within these models, the density may be computationally tractable, or it may require Variational or Monte Carlo approximations to optimize the likelihood. On the other hand, Implicit density models (right branch of Fig.
1
) do not directly define a probability distribution over the data space. Instead, they interact indirectly with the distribution, often sampling from it. Some implicit models use Markov Chains to stochastically transform one sample into another, while others generate samples in a single step without input. Albeit GANs can theoretically define an explicit density, their training relies on solely on sampling, aligning them with implicit models that directly sample from the distribution.
Therefore, in the following, we provide a concise overview of the two main categories of DGMs, i.e., Explicit and Implicit density models, along with their respective subcategories. 2.3.1.
Explicit density

The primary challenge in explicit density models lies in designing architectures capable of capturing the full complexity of the data while preserving computational tractability
(
Tomczak, 2024
)
. Two main strategies address this issue:
(i)
constructing models with carefully designed structures that inherently ensure computational tractability, as exemplified by the class of Tractable density Models, and
(ii)
employing models that allow for tractable approximations of the likelihood and its gradients, as represented by the class of Approximate density Models.
The
Tractable density
class includes DGMs that explicitly define a probability density function, enabling direct likelihood evaluation. This category comprises Auto Regressive models, which decompose the joint distribution into sequential dependencies, and Flow-based models, which use invertible transformations to ease likelihood computation.

Auto Regressive Models (ARMs)

These models represent the distribution of data sequentially, predicting each element based on the preceding ones. Modeling all possible conditional distributions individually is impractical, as it would require building a separate model for each element, with increasing complexity due to the growing number of conditions.
A potential solution to the issue is utilizing a single, shared model for the conditional distribution. The initial approach to reducing complexity involves assuming a finite memory constraint. Specifically, that each variable depends on no more than two other variables. This method holds particular relevance for IoT applications, which are frequently characterized by resource-constrained devices incapable of managing computationally intensive tasks. However, this approach has an obvious drawback that is the limited memory range. A possible solution to short-range memory is the application of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), like the Long Short Term Memory (LSTM)
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997
)
, that allow long-range dependency learning. This approach gives a single parametrization, thus it is efficient and also solves the problem of a finite memory. However, is sequential, hence slow, and due to the application of RNNs, problems like exploding or vanishing gradients need to be addressed. An alternative approach utilizes Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) rather than RNNs to model the probability distribution. This approach offers distinct advantages, specifically the use of shared kernels and improved process parallelization. However, this solution is inefficient when there is a need to sample a new object. CNNs are generally more computationally expensive and heavier than simple RNNs, especially in terms of parameter count, memory consumption and computation complexity, limiting their use in IoT applications. Another important class of models constitutes Transformers
(
Vaswani et al., 2023
)
, which uses self-attention layers instead of causal convolutions. Like LSTMs, Transformer architectures are capable of handling distant information. Unlike LSTMs, Transformers do not rely on recurrent connections, making them easier to parallelize and more efficient. They consist of stacked Transformer blocks that map input sequences to output sequences of the same length. Each block combines linear layers, feed-forward networks, and, most importantly, self-attention. Self-attention captures contextual relationships by integrating information across tokens between layers, enabling progressively richer and more contextualized representations throughout the network.
In conclusion, the main advantage of ARMs is that they can learn long range statistics and, in a consequence, powerful density estimators. However, their drawback is that they are parameterized in an auto regressive manner, hence sampling is a slow process. Moreover, they lack a latent representation, therefore, it is not obvious how to manipulate their internal data representation. In IoT domains, the application of Transformer-based models have gained a considerable interest motivated by the enhanced data processing, thanks to the self attention mechanism, and the improved decision making, that leads to more adaptive and intelligent systems
(
Dong et al., 2024
;
Sehad et al., 2024
)
. However, IoT devices often have limited computational resources, making it challenging to deploy resource-intensive Transformer models. Furthermore, their effectiveness can be hindered by the limited availability of labeled data in specific IoT applications, affecting their performance
(
Dong et al., 2024
)
.

Flow-based Models

The change of variables formula provides a principled manner of expressing a density of a
random variable by transforming it with an invertible transformation.
The key point of Flow-based models, is to seek for such NNs
that are both invertible and the logarithm of the determinant of a Jacobian matrix is relatively easy to calculate. The resulting models, that consists of invertible transformation with tractable
determinant of a Jacobian matrix, are referred to as Normalizing Flows or Flow-based Models. Despite the success of Normalizing Flows models, in estimating high-dimensional densities, certain limitations persist in their design.
First, the latent space onto which input data is projected is not lower-dimensional, meaning Flow-based models do not inherently support data compression and are computationally demanding, limiting their use in IoT applications. Flow-based models also exhibit notable challenges in estimating the likelihood of out-of-distribution samples, namely samples that originate from distributions different from the training set. This could represent a significant problem in IoT applications wherein there is a huge data variability
(
Atzori et al., 2010
)
. One of the most compelling features of normalizing flows is the invertibility of their learned bijective mapping. This property arises from specific design constraints in models, which theoretically ensure invertibility. The integrity of the inverse map is crucial for the applicability of the change-of-variable theorem, accurate computation of the Jacobian, and reliable sampling. However, in practice, numerical errors can break invertibility, causing the inverse mapping to diverge
(
Sehad et al., 2024
)
.

2.3.2.
Approximate density

To bypass the design constraints of models with tractable density functions, alternative approaches have been developed: they retain explicit density functions, but accept intractability, necessitating the use of approximations for likelihood maximization. These models can be broadly categorized into two groups: those employing deterministic approximations, typically via variational methods, and those relying on stochastic approximations, commonly utilizing Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques.

Variational Auto-Encoder

The models in this group are based on the assumption of a lower-dimensional latent space coupled with an underlying generative process. In this context, latent variables represent hidden factors that explain the observed data, while the conditional relationship between latent variables and observations can be interpreted as a generator. For this reason, these approaches are commonly referred to as Latent Variable Models. A well-known example is probabilistic Principal Component Analysis (pPCA)
(
Tipping and Bishop, 1999
)
, in which both the latent variables and the observed data are modeled using Gaussian distributions, and their relationship is assumed to be linear.
In IoT applications, data collected from heterogeneous sensors frequently involve intricate nonlinear correlations. Since pPCA assumes a linear dependency structure, it may fail to capture the underlying variability in such datasets making alternative nonlinear techniques more suitable.
A non-linear extension of the pPCA with arbitrary
distributions is the Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE) framework
(
Kingma and Welling, 2022
)
. VAEs consist of a set of variational posterior distributions that approximate the true posterior and act as a stochastic encoder. They also include a stochastic decoder that maps latent variables back to the data space, along with a prior distribution over the latent variables.
The objective is the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), a surrogate for the log-likelihood: a tighter ELBO yields a more accurate approximation of the data distribution, improving both sample quality and latent representations.
Unlike Flow-based Models, VAEs do not require NNs to be invertible, allowing for flexibility in the choice of architectures for both encoders and decoders. In contrast to ARMs, VAEs learn a low-dimensional latent representation of the data, providing control over the model’s information bottleneck. However, VAEs encounter several challenges, including posterior collapse

(
Bowman et al., 2016
)
, the hole problem
(
Rezende and Viola, 2018
)
, difficulties in handling out-of-distribution samples
(
Le Lan and Dinh, 2021
)
, and a gap between the ELBO and the true log likelihood. These challenges, particularly the difficulty in handling out-of-distribution data, may compromise the applicability of VAEs in IoT domains. Nevertheless, the remarkable flexibility of this class of DGMs, exemplified by the absence of fixed NNs for both the encoder and decoder, presents a promising solution for various IoT applications
(
Tomczak, 2024
)
.

Energy-based Models

An Energy-based Model (EBM)
(
Lecun et al., 2006
)
is a framework in which an energy function quantifies the relationships between variables in a system. The observed inputs are represented by one set of variables, while the possible outputs or targets form another set. The energy function assigns a numerical score to each input–output configuration, with lower values indicating higher compatibility and higher values indicating lower compatibility. A Boltzmann Machine
(
Ackley et al., 1985
)

(
Smolensky, 1986
)
is a specific type of EBM, where these energy scores can be transformed into probabilities, resulting in the Boltzmann (or Gibbs) distribution over possible configurations.
In practice, most energy functions yield intractable partition functions, which represent the primary computational bottleneck when training Energy-based Models. A natural extension of Boltzmann Machines are models with a deep architecture or Hierarchical Boltzmann Machines. However, training such models is even more challenging due to the complexity of the partition function, limiting their use in IoT applications
(
Tomczak, 2024
)
. Despite the computational challenges associated with EBMs, their unconstrained nature offers a significant advantage in terms of flexibility. Specifically, the energy function is not restricted to a particular form, allowing it to be modeled by a wide variety of functions. This adaptability promotes NNs to parameterize the energy function, resulting suitable to capture highly complex relationships within the data, typical in IoT applications
(
Tomczak, 2024
)
. Such flexibility makes Boltzmann Machines a versatile tool for representing complex systems, highlighting their potential beyond the inherent training difficulties.

Diffusion Models

Probabilistic models have historically faced a fundamental trade-off between two competing objectives: tractability and flexibility. Tractable models, such as Gaussian or Laplace distributions, allow for analytical evaluation and straightforward fitting to the data. However, these models are inherently limited in their ability to capture the intricate structures present in complex datasets. In contrast, flexible models are capable of representing the rich and diverse structures in arbitrary data but often sacrifice tractability, making their evaluation and optimization computationally challenging.
Diffusion Models
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015a
)

overcome the traditional trade-off between flexibility and tractability in generative modeling. Inspired by non-equilibrium statistical physics, they first transform data into a simple prior distribution (e.g., Gaussian noise) through a forward diffusion process, then learn a reverse process that progressively removes noise to reconstruct the original data distribution. The reverse process is parameterized by NNs that predict denoising steps. By accurately approximating this process, Diffusion Models provide a flexible and tractable framework that supports efficient learning, rapid sample generation, and probability estimation, even in architectures with thousands of diffusion steps.
Moreover, Diffusion Models support conditional and posterior probability computation under the learned distribution, making them particularly versatile for a wide range of generative tasks
(
Tomczak, 2024
)
(e.g., predictive sensor data reconstruction, anomaly-free signal synthesis in industrial settings, or synthetic telemetry generation for network traffic modeling). Hence, they excel in IoT applications, if their inherent complexities are properly handled.

2.3.3.
Implicit density

Certain models can be trained without explicitly specifying a density function; rather, they interact indirectly with the underlying data distribution, most commonly via sampling procedures,
and fall under the second branch of the generative model taxonomy illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Within this category, some implicit models employ a Markov Chain transition operator, which must be iteratively applied multiple times to generate a sample from the model. However, Markov Chains often struggle to scale in high-dimensional spaces and incur significant computational costs. GANs were specifically designed to address these limitations.

Generative Adversarial Networks - Direct

The Direct class includes DGMs that learn to map a simple latent distribution to complex data distributions without relying on explicit likelihood estimation. The primary example is Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
(
Goodfellow et al., 2014
)
, which employ an adversarial training framework to generate high-quality samples through a competition between a generator and a discriminator.
GANs consist of two NNs, which are trained in a min-max game, such as:
(i)
the generator
G
,
(ii)
and the discriminator
D
.
G
produces synthetic data samples, while the
D
learns to distinguish between real data and the synthetic data generated by
G
. Through this adversarial process, the generator learns to create increasingly realistic samples by minimizing an adversarial loss, which measures the discriminator’s ability to correctly differentiate real and generated data. This framework allows GANs to learn complex data distributions without explicitly modeling the underlying probability density. GANs are specifically designed to overcome certain limitations associated with other types of DGMs, such as the ability to generate samples in parallel and the flexibility in designing the generator function with minimal constraints. For IoT applications, GANs have emerged as a powerful tool for data augmentation, anomaly detection, and privacy-preserving learning
(
Ferrag et al., 2023b
;
Hamouda and others, 2024
;
Tomczak, 2024
)
. Given that IoT systems often suffer from limited, imbalanced, or noisy datasets, GANs can generate synthetic sensor data to augment training sets, improving the robustness of machine learning models deployed in resource-constrained environments. Despite these advantages, several challenges hinder the widespread adoption of GANs in IoT. The instability of adversarial training, including mode collapse and vanishing gradients, makes training highly sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, which can be problematic in dynamic IoT environments
(
Tomczak, 2024
)
; furthermore, GANs are computationally expensive.

Markov Chain

A Markov Chain is a stochastic process used to generate samples through an iterative procedure, where each new sample is drawn based on the current one according to a defined transition rule. However, this convergence process can be exceedingly slow, particularly in high-dimensional spaces where the efficiency of Markov Chains diminishes significantly. Moreover, a critical challenge lies in the inability to definitively determine whether the chain has reached convergence. As a result, practitioners often utilize samples prematurely, before the Markov Chain has sufficiently mixed, leading to potentially biased samples that do not accurately represent
p_{model}(x)
. Markov Chains have broad applications across various fields, such as statistical physics, Bayesian inference, and Machine Learning, particularly in methods like MCMC. However, the practical challenges, including slow convergence and inefficiency in high dimensions, highlight the need for advanced techniques (e.g., Hamiltonian Monte Carlo or Variational approximations) to address these limitations and improve the scalability in modern computational problems. In the context of IoT applications, Markov Chains can be leveraged for modeling temporal dependencies in sensor data, anomaly detection, and predictive maintenance by capturing sequential patterns in time-series data
(
Shamim et al., 2023
)
. Their ability to model probabilistic transitions makes them particularly useful for systems where state evolution follows a stochastic process. However, the practical use of Markov Chains in IoT is constrained by challenges such as computational inefficiency, sensitivity to initial conditions, and difficulty in ensuring proper mixing
(
Liu et al., 2024b
)
.

3.
Research Methodology

We conducted an initial informal search, supplemented by our personal experience: this confirmed the existence of a substantial number of contributions to the integration of GenAI and IoT, indicating the need for a systematic review and providing also valuable information to guide the subsequent, manual search process. Consequently, a survey of articles exploring the interaction between GenAI and IoT was conducted over a six-year period, from January 2020 to January 2026, following PRISMA guidelines, as illustrated in Fig.
2
. This timeline aligns with the emergence and growing prominence of GenAI, as its widespread adoption and in-depth exploration began to gain traction only after 2020. Following the record screening and report selection processes, we analyzed a total of 325 studies, which were reduced to 78 after the article’s review phase. The search plan undertaken is detailed in the following subsections.

3.1.
Objectives

This article seeks to present a comprehensive overview of the current state of the art in the exploitation of GenAI for IoT Computing. To this end, a systematic survey was conducted to identify prevailing research trends, elucidate key challenges, and address the following

Research Questions
(RQs):

•

(
RQ1
) What are the current scopes of GenAI for IoT applications?

•

(
RQ2
) Which are the main GenAI models and architectures that support IoT Computing?

•

(
RQ3
) What are the main challenges and research gaps in applying GenAI in IoT Computing?

•

(
RQ4
) Which are the future research directions?

3.2.
Search Strategy

An exhaustive search of the articles regarding GenAI in IoT was performed in January 2026 by
two of the authors on the following digital libraries: ScienceDirect, Scopus, IEEEXplore,
ACM Digital Library, and Web of Science. The search focused on retrieving scientific
publications proposing solutions (i.e., models, techniques, approaches, architectures) that
aim to understand the role of GenAI in IoT.
The keyword search string was defined according to two key concepts:
(i)
GenAI and
(ii)
IoT. Considering such key terms, and their synonyms, the following search string was identified:

(1)

(“Generative AI” OR “Generative-AI” OR “GenAI” OR “GAI” OR “Generative modeling’)

(2)

AND (“Internet of Things” OR “IoT” OR “Smart devices” OR “Connected devices”)

3.3.
Eligibility criteria

The articles were eligible for selection if they met all the following inclusion criteria:
(a)

a model or at least a definition of GenAI for IoT applications is proposed or adopted;
(b)
GenAI techniques are exploited as the main element of the proposed solution;
(c)
the work is an article, literature review, survey, or mapping study that specifically delves into the application of GenAI to the IoT realm.
Articles were excluded from selection if they met one of the following exclusion criteria:
(a)
the terms “GenAI” and related synonyms, are contained only in the title, abstract, or keywords and are missing in the main body of the article;
(b)
the concept of GenAI or one of its synonyms is either defined or used improperly;
(c)
the presented GenAI techniques are not significant, or their contribution is negligible or marginal;
(d)
The paper does not truly address topics, applications, or use cases related to the IoT.

3.4.
Study Selection

Fig.
2
illustrates the flow chart summarizing the approach used to select articles according to the PRISMA guidelines
(
Sarkis-Onofre et al., 2021
)
. The initial search across digital libraries using the specified query retrieved
124

(i)
exclusion of certain publication types such as editorials, short articles, posters, theses, dissertations, brief communications, commentaries, and unpublished works;
(ii)
removal of articles not partially or fully written in English; and
(iii)
exclusion of papers lacking full-text availability. This initial step eliminated 46 articles, leaving 78 publications. In the first screening phase, two authors independently analyzed each article’s title, abstract, and keywords based on the eligibility criteria. Any article deemed relevant by at least one author was transferred to the next phase for full-text evaluation. This phase resulted in 53 articles being selected for further review. Risk of bias was assessed during the final selection phase: all authors reviewed the full-text articles and evaluated their relevance, rigor, credibility, and quality. Manuscripts were excluded if "GenAI" was mentioned only superficially, such as in the title, abstract, or related work section, without representing a core component of the proposed solution. Inclusion decisions were made by consensus, and discussions resolved disagreements; no competing financial interests or personal relationships have influenced the study selection. Ultimately, 43 articles were selected for review. In addition, we conducted an extensive snowballing process to identify other relevant studies not captured by the initial query. This included backward (reference-based) and forward (citation-based) searches of the selected articles. We also explored gray literature, such as technical reports, Ph.D. theses, patents, and company white papers, often published by government or professional organizations. As a result, 35 additional studies were analyzed in detail. Therefore, we ended-up with 78 selected records which are analyzed in the following review.

The comparative analysis suggests that the field is currently in a transition phase from proof-of-concept architectures toward deployable AI-enabled IoT systems. Across all categories of studies, performance emerges as the most mature dimension, with the majority of approaches achieving high effectiveness regardless of their architectural orientation. Similarly, scalability is generally well addressed through distributed processing, model partitioning, and decentralized intelligence mechanisms, indicating that large-scale operation has become a primary design objective. In contrast, energy efficiency remains a significant challenge, as the computational demands of LLMs, diffusion models, cloud offloading, and continuous data processing often result in low or moderate efficiency levels. Reproducibility represents the weakest aspect of the surveyed literature, with many works lacking publicly available code, datasets, or experimental artifacts, thereby limiting independent validation and comparative assessment. Deployment readiness also remains relatively low, as most solutions are evaluated through simulations, conceptual studies, or prototype implementations. Notably, all the approaches exhibit comparable performance levels, suggesting that the main differences among them lie in their trade-offs between scalability, energy consumption, reproducibility, and practical maturity rather than in raw effectiveness. Finally, the few studies that achieve both high reproducibility and high deployment readiness are typically those providing open-source implementations and undergoing practical validation, highlighting the key role of open science practices in boosting the transition from experimental to operational GenAI-enabled IoT systems.

5.
Bridging the Gap: Solutions for the Practical Challenges of Generative AI in IoT Computing

As outcome of the discussion of the previous Section
4
, it can be concluded that the development of GenAI-aided IoT systems presents both substantial opportunities and considerable challenges. Following the tripartite framework classification, a summary of main, high-priority challenges for each vision include:

Challenges in Thing-oriented vision.
The integration of GenAI into IoT ecosystems introduces a fundamental conflict between model complexity and the capabilities of edge hardware and IoT devices, making it challenging to meet the computational, memory, and energy demands required by GenAI models and the peculiar time constraints required by industrial applications. Hence, to improve efficiency while maintaining DGM accuracy is crucial
(
Cai et al., 2022
)
.

Challenges in Network-oriented vision.
Ensuring scalability, interoperability across heterogeneous platforms, and efficient network support for applications that are intrinsically cloud-centric are well-known IoT challenges. However, these are further exacerbated by GenAI workloads, that significantly increase network traffic and place stringent demands on communication reliability, bandwidth availability and end-to-end latency
(
Liu et al., 2023b
)
.

Challenges in Semantic-oriented vision.
The quality and quantity of the data used for GenAI training or fine-tuning is not a negligible aspect, but, at the same time, sensitive domains like Smart Healthcare involve privacy-sensitive data, limiting the feasibility of centralized data collection and training. Moreover, unlike general-purpose domains that benefit from massive public repositories, the IoT landscape suffers from severe data scarcity and fragmentation, since real-world sensor data are often proprietary, sensitive, or, in the case of fault detection, statistically rare, making it difficult to construct balanced datasets for supervised learning. These limitations directly impact the ability of GenAI models to learn robust semantic representations of physical-world processes
(
Xu et al., 2024a
)
.

Furthermore, deploying GenAI-aided IoT systems presents itself as a significant,
cross-vision challenge
: specific implementation, benchmarking, and regulation aspects still remain largely unexplored, and relevant topics pertaining, for example, ethical concerns,
explainability, trust, and security, scarcely addressed.
Therefore, moving beyond the study-specific findings of RQ4, this section examines

5.1.
Model compression techniques

DGMs are sophisticated architectures characterized by billions of parameters, designed to adhere to the scaling law. This principle posits that achieving higher levels of accuracy and performance requires increasingly larger model architectures. To speed up the time required for inference, reduce the number of computational resources needed, and consequently reduce the environmental impact of such models, a number of model compression techniques have been proposed. A summary is then provided by Tab.
10
.

Pruning

Parameter pruning encompasses several approaches that differ in granularity and hardware implications. Fine-grained pruning
(
Han et al., 2015
)
removes individual elements, such as weights, from data structures like tensors. Owing to its high level of granularity, this approach enables selective pruning and can achieve substantial compression ratios while largely preserving model accuracy. Pattern-based pruning
(
Niu and others, 2020
)
represents a specialized variant of fine-grained pruning, in which predefined sparsity patterns are exploited to facilitate hardware acceleration through compiler-level optimizations. In contrast, coarse-grained pruning operates at a higher level by removing entire tensor blocks, thereby improving hardware efficiency and enabling direct acceleration on GPUs when standard deep learning libraries are used. However, this reduced granularity typically comes at the cost of lower accuracy compared to fine-grained pruning.

Conventional pruning schemes are more compatible with hardware architectures, leading to reduced energy consumption and enhanced inference acceleration making them suitable in adapting DGMs for IoT resource-constrained environments. In contrast, irregular pruning schemes tend to better preserve model accuracy at equivalent compression rates, but resulting in a higher hardware resource consumption. However, with advances in specialized hardware accelerators
(
Chen et al., 2019
)
and compiler-based optimization techniques
(
Li et al., 2023a
)
, significant acceleration can also be achieved for irregular pruning methods, also making such techniques suitable for IoT applications. Once the pruning granularity has been determined, the selection of weights to be pruned is critical to the performance of the pruned model. While numerous methodologies have been proposed, they all adhere to the fundamental principle of removing less significant weights based on predefined criteria. The most straightforward heuristic relies on magnitude, where weights with larger absolute values
|W|
are considered more critical
(
Han et al., 2017
)
.
Alternative criteria include second-order derivatives
(
Hassibi and Stork, 1992
)
, loss approximation via Taylor expansion
(
Molchanov et al., 2017
)
, and output sensitivity
(
Engelbrecht, 2001
)
. Directly removing weights from DGMs, will affect the accuracy of the models. To mitigate the risk of improperly pruning essential weights,
Dynamic pruning

(
Guo et al., 2016
)
(integrating connection splicing as a continuous network maintenance task) and
Runtime pruning

(
Lin et al., 2017
)
(adjusting pruning ratios based on input samples) have been proposed. Both techniques, albeit through different mechanism, transform pruning from a static procedure into a dynamic one. Although pruning-supported techniques have been employed in IoT applications for compressing Deep Neural Networks (DNNs)
(
Prakash et al., 2022
;
Shang et al., 2021
)
, their application to DGMs in IoT environments remains unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, no existing studies have investigated the use of pruning techniques for DGMs in this context.

Low Rank Approximation (LoRA)

LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2021
)
utilizes the concept of approximating the original weight matrix by decomposing it into two or more smaller matrices with lower dimensions, then reducing the size and complexity of DGMs. To mitigate potential information loss, various techniques have been developed, broadly classified into two categories:

Training-required methods
, which require fine-tuning the entire model either during or after the application of LoRA to restore or improve overall performance
(
Ben Noach and Goldberg, 2020
)
; and
Training-free methods
, which prioritize selecting the least
significant matrices for decomposition, thereby reducing complexity without requiring
additional training
(
Hua et al., 2022
)
.

A widely applied technique is Truncated Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
(
Golub and Van Loan, 2013
)
. The LoRA approximation does not require specialized hardware for implementation and execution, making it a highly suitable solution for applications in IoT domains.
From the analyzed literature, it emerges that one study employs LoRa technique
(
Huang et al., 2023
)
. However, recent studies have yet to explore the compression of DGMs using LoRA for deployment on IoT devices.

Quantization

Network quantization reduces memory requirements and computational costs by decreasing the number of bits needed to represent weights in a DNNs. Early approaches
(
Gordon et al., 2020
)
utilized
k-means
clustering to identify shared weight representations for each layer within a trained NN. Therefore, weights falling into the same cluster were assigned a shared value. Changing bit precision affects the trade-off between model size and accuracy. Lower bit precision (e.g. FP16) allows the model size to be reduced, but may cause a loss of accuracy. Another quantization schemes involves INT8, in which both weights and activations are represented as 8-bit integers
(
Jacob et al., 2017
)
. This technique is widely adopted for integer-arithmetic-only inference, enabling acceleration on CPUs and GPUs.
Models with even lower precision include
Ternary Weight Networks

(
Li et al., 2022
)
, which quantize weights to
\{-1,0,+1\}
. To mitigate the resulting loss of accuracy,
quantization-aware training
anticipates inference-time quantization during training: it simulates low-precision behavior in the forward pass, while retaining full precision in the backward pass to preserve reliable gradient updates. This strategy maintains model accuracy while preparing DGMs for efficient deployment on resource-constrained IoT devices.
Both post-training quantization and quantization-aware training rely on access to the training dataset to achieve optimal performance, which may not always be feasible in privacy-sensitive scenarios, typical of some IoT domains. To overcome this limitation,
Data-free quantization

(
Nagel et al., 2019
)
has been introduced. This technique allow for the reduction of bit precision without requiring access to training data, offering a solution for privacy-constrained applications. Even with the most advanced quantization techniques, the maximum model compression ratio is inherently constrained by the smallest achievable bit width. Therefore, to further reduce model size and enable deployment over IoT devices, it is essential to integrate quantization methods with other compression strategies. However, quantization-supported techniques have been used in IoT applications for compressing DNNs
(
Prakash et al., 2022
;
Shang et al., 2021
)
and DGMs
(
He et al., 2024
;
Lai et al., 2024
)
.

Knowledge distillation (KD)

KD
(
Hinton et al., 2015
;
Buciluǎ et al., 2006
)

is a methodology that facilitates the transfer of knowledge from a complex model, referred to as the
teacher model
, to a more compact model, the
student model
. KD techniques generally necessitate training or fine-tuning to achieve effective knowledge transfer, thereby increasing the computational cost associated with their implementation.
KD can be categorized into
white-box
and
black-box
approaches, depending on the level of access to the teacher model.
White-box KD
provides access to the teacher’s architecture and internal representations, enabling the student to learn both outputs and intermediate features through feature-based losses. This structured guidance improves convergence and preserves key generative properties such as style and content consistency.
Black-box KD
treats the teacher as an opaque model, training the student only on its final outputs. It is suitable when the teacher is proprietary or computationally expensive, but typically yields less accurate feature replication and may require more data.
KD reduces model complexity while maintaining performance, making it well suited for resource-constrained and edge environments. However, limited research has explored KD for DGMs in IoT contexts, and existing frameworks often lack the generalization needed for dynamic, heterogeneous IoT environments.

Automated design: Compression and Neural Architecture Search

Model compression techniques rely heavily on hand-crafted heuristics and domain expertise to balance trade-offs among size, latency, energy, and accuracy. Because parameter sensitivity varies across layers, compression must be customized, making manual approaches time-consuming and often suboptimal. To overcome this, automated methods such as
Automated Pruning

(
He et al., 2018
)
and
Automated Quantization

(
Wang et al., 2019
)
use Reinforcement Learning (RL) to efficiently explore the design space and determine optimal pruning levels or bit-widths per layer.
In the context of IoT, where device capabilities, network conditions, and application requirements may change frequently over time, relying on static, manually crafted designs becomes particularly limiting. A similar need for automation therefore arises in the design of NNs themselves. Designing NNs is inherently complex and has traditionally depended on expert intuition and trial-and-error. To address this challenge,
Neural Architecture Search (NAS)

(
Zoph and Le, 2017
)
extends the idea of automated design beyond compression by systematically identifying high-performing architectures without human intervention. NAS typically consists of three components:
(i)
the search space, defining candidate architectures;
(ii)
the search strategy, exploring them; and
(iii)
the performance estimation, evaluating each configuration. When combined with model compression, NAS can be leveraged to automatically generate and adapt IoT-aware DGMs that are specialized for heterogeneous hardware profiles and evolving resource constraints, significantly accelerating deployment and reconfiguration without requiring continuous human intervention.

5.2.
On device training, fine-tuning and inference

Various techniques have been proposed to optimize the training phase directly on the devices, towards the goal of efficient on-device learning
(
Menghani, 2023
;
Li et al., 2023b
)
. One such method is
gradient checkpoint
, designed to reduce the memory required for training activations by removing intermediate activations during the forward pass. These discarded activations are recomputed during the backward pass to calculate the gradients. Another approach,
activation pruning
, reduces the size of the activation by removing non-critical neurons, similar to weight pruning, thus reducing the memory footprint and computational costs.
Low-bit training
, on the other hand, involves training with quantizing weights, activations, and gradients, significantly reducing training cost. The techniques mentioned above primarily focus on scenarios in which NNs are trained from scratch, assuming a sufficient number of data samples. However, in on-device learning contexts, where data availability is limited, training DGMs from scratch becomes challenging due to the small dataset size. An alternative approach is to transfer pre-trained DGMs to the target device. This strategy leverages the advantages of existing, well-designed pre-trained DGMs that have been developed with extensive human expertise and significant computational resources, and adapt these to resource-constrained environments. Nevertheless, in dynamic IoT environments, newly collected data often deviates from previously learned distributions. Consequently, fine-tuning is frequently required to adapt DGMs effectively to new unseen data. Fine-tuning techniques can be broadly classified in: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning
(PEFT)

(
Ding et al., 2023
)
,
Memory-Efficient Fine-Tuning (MEFT)

(
Liao et al., 2023
)
, and
Data-Efficient Fine-Tuning (DEFT)

(
Zha et al., 2023
)
. A summary of the proposed techniques is provided by Tab.
10
.

•

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning
: PEFT reduces the computational cost of fine-tuning by selecting only a subset of essential parameters in DGMs for tuning. PEFT methods can be further divided in:
(i)

Addition-Based Approach
, which introduces small neural network modules into the DGMs;
(ii)

Specification-Based Approach
, that designates a sub-set of parameters for fine-tuning while freezing the rest;
(iii)

Reparameterization-Based Approach
, that transforms the weight matrices into more efficient forms through LoRA. While PEFT significantly lowers computational costs, it still imposes a considerable memory footprint, restricting its use in IoT environments.

•

Memory-Efficient Fine-Tuning
: MEFT minimizes the memory footprint during fine-tuning by employing various strategies as:
(i)
avoiding storage of large input vectors,
(ii)
utilizing low-energy optimizers,
(iii)
combining gradient computation and update operations. MEFT offers a lower memory requirement compared to PEFT, but may take longer to complete and more energy consuming, which largely limit its use on IoT devices.

•

Data-Efficient Fine-Tuning
: DEFT focuses on achieving efficient fine-tuning by utilizing only a small fraction of the data. These techniques are often integrated with PEFT or MEFT approaches to enhance fine-tuning efficiency, particularly in scenarios where data is scarce, making DEFT suitable for IoT applications.

To reduce the computational and memory footprint during inference, various techniques based on preprocessing input data have been proposed. Specifically, Chevalier et al.
(
Chevalier et al., 2023
)
utilize pre-trained LLMs to compress prompts with long contexts into shorter summary vectors, effectively reducing the overall computation and memory requirements. From a hardware optimization perspective, cross-processor inference has been introduced to enhance on-device inference efficiency. This approach involves distributing the modules of DGMs across multiple onboard processors, allowing parallel execution to improve efficiency. However, while general task-parallelism strategies have the potential to enhance performance, they are predominantly designed for server-side inference environments with homogeneous computational units. Finally, in IoT ecosystems, the available resources at runtime can be highly dynamic due to various factors such as device heterogeneity, energy constraints, and network variability. Consequently, the configuration of DGMs must be dynamically adjusted to adapt to these fluctuating resource conditions in real-time, ensuring effective and efficient on-device inference. Only a limited number of studies have explored the adaptation of DGMs, such as the work by
Sheng et al.

(
Sheng and others, 2023
)
, which proposes techniques to tailor DGMs to various hardware resources. However, these approaches are primarily designed for resource-rich, server-scale systems.

Multi-device systems: Federated Learning (FL) approach

FL, despite its extensive study and application in recent years, has primarily been implemented in scenarios involving models of significantly smaller scales compared to contemporary DGMs, which are often characterized by billions of parameters. This highlights a gap in the current state of FL research and applications. Importantly, FL has the potential to address two critical challenges effectively:

•

Privacy Preservation
: FL facilitates a decentralized training approach, ensuring that sensitive data remains local on IoT devices. This method minimizes the risk of data breaches and complies with strict privacy regulations by avoiding the need to transfer raw data to centralized servers.

•

Scalable and Distributed Model Training
: By enabling multiple devices to collaboratively train smaller fractions of a large-scale model, FL offers a solution to the computational and communication challenges inherent in training massive DGMs. This approach not only optimizes resource utilization but also ensures that diverse, distributed data sources can contribute to model development without necessitating data centralization.
To this end,
Wen et al.

(
Wen et al., 2022
)
propose a simple approach to enable FL on resource-constrained devices.
Alam et al.

(
Alam et al., 2023
)
propose FedRolex, a partial training-based approach that enables model-heterogeneous FL, and can train a global server model larger than the largest client model. Lastly,
Dun et al.

(
Dun et al., 2022
)
propose a novel asynchronous FL framework that utilizes dropout regularization to handle IoT device heterogeneity in distributed settings.

So far, FL has been primarily presented as a solution to unlock the potential of GenAI within the IoT ecosystem. This synergy emphasizes FL’s role in enabling privacy-preserving and distributed training for large-scale GenAI models, which often rely on data from multiple edge devices. However, the relationship between FL and GenAI is not unidirectional. GenAI techniques can also play a transformative role in enhancing the performance of current FL methodologies. As an example,
Zhang et al.

(
Zhang et al., 2024c
)
propose a GPT-FL, a generative pre-trained model-assisted FL framework.
To translate these theoretical advantages into real-world deployments, the gap between model size and edge constraints is currently being bridged by a specialized software stack. Optimized inference engines such as TensorFlow Lite Micro, ONNX Runtime, and NVIDIA TensorRT, provide essential quantization and pruning capabilities to fit DGMs onto constrained devices
(
Ratul et al., 2025
;
Zhou et al., 2023
)
. Also, libraries specifically targeting LLMs at the edge, such as llama.cpp and MLC LLM, have emerged to enable 4-bit quantization on consumer-grade hardware
(
Chen et al., 2025a
)
.

5.3.
Offloading

Given the constrained memory and computational capabilities of IoT devices, many of them may be unable to execute the most efficient DGMs, even when leveraging all the optimization techniques introduced thus far. In such scenarios, it becomes essential to offload the execution of the entire model or specific portions of it to external resources. However, the success of this offloading approach hinges on addressing two critical challenges:
(i) efficient workload partitioning
and
(ii) efficient communication
. These challenges become significantly more difficult to address in contexts involving DGMs due to their large-scale dimensions, which amplify the complexity of workload partitioning and place an even greater burden on communication efficiency. A summary is then provided by Tab.
10
.

Efficient workload partitioning.

Workload Partitioning refers to the division of a DGM’s computational tasks between resource-constrained IoT devices and nearby resource-rich devices as edge servers or cloud infrastructure. This enables the distributed execution of the model, leveraging the strengths of each system. However, this process is inherently complex due to the varying computational, memory, and energy capabilities of IoT devices, edge servers, and cloud resources. Existing approaches, to workload partitioning, can be categorized into two main types:
(i) Heuristic-Based methods
that rely on predefined rules or experience-driven strategies to partition workloads and
(ii) Learning-Based methods
, which use historical workload data to train models that identify patterns and relationships between tasks and resources, enabling them to determine optimal partitions for new, unseen scenarios.

Efficient communication.

Communication between IoT devices and cloud infrastructure is typically conducted over wireless channels, where bandwidth is often limited. Ensuring the timely exchange of offloaded workloads while minimizing bandwidth usage and power consumption is therefore critical. To address these challenges, several techniques have been proposed, including:
(i) Message Compression
(i.e., reducing the size of transmitted data to conserve bandwidth),
(ii) Data Sampling
(i.e., selecting representative data points to minimize the volume of communication),
(iii) Efficient Communication Protocols
(i.e., optimizing data transmission methods for resource-constrained environments), and
(iv) Edge Caching
(i.e., storing frequently used data at edge servers to reduce repeated transmissions).

5.4.
Solutions for Robust Deployments

In parallel to the design solutions discussed so far, the deployment of IoT systems is undergoing a paradigm shift towards High-performance embedded computing (HPEC), aimed at bridging the gap between cloud and edge by utilizing powerful accelerators, such as the NVIDIA Jetson Orin family, or dedicated Neural Processing Units (NPUs) integrated into modern SoCs. Unlike standard IoT nodes, these platforms are engineered to support energy-intensive matrix operations, enabling the local execution of DGMs while maintaining millisecond-level latency
(
Navardi et al., 2025
;
Cordova-Cardenas et al., 2025
)
. However, the physical accessibility of edge nodes introduces severe security risks. Unlike cloud data centers, IoT devices are susceptible to direct attacks and are generally less protected than other kind of infrastructure. Consequently, the deployment framework must prioritize hardware-backed security, to prevent attacks where proprietary DGMs weights are stolen, or poisoning attacks where the model’s behavior is maliciously altered.

Relying on secured HPEC for addressing the computational hurdles, however, solves only one side of the deployment equation. Even with the adoption of powerful edge accelerators, the efficacy of a GenAI model is fundamentally bounded by the quality and quantity of the data used for its training or fine-tuning. Unlike general-purpose domains, that benefit from massive public repositories, the IoT landscape suffers from severe data scarcity and fragmentation. Real-world sensor data is often proprietary, sensitive, or in the case of fault-detection statistically rare. Thus, to construct balanced IoT datasets for supervised learning is not straightforward. To establish baselines, researchers frequently rely on a handful of standard open-source repositories, such as the Bot-IoT or NSL-KDD datasets for network intrusion, C-MAPSS for industrial predictive maintenance, and PAMAP2 or MHEALTH for wearable sensor data. However, these static datasets could fail to capture the dynamic, heterogeneous nature of modern, real-world IoT deployments.

To overcome these limitations, GenAI provides a powerful alternative by generating synthetic data that realistically simulates complex, evolving environments. Beyond simply augmenting sparse datasets, this generative capability addresses privacy and regulatory challenges associated with deploying AI in constrained environments. By leveraging synthetic datasets, practitioners can train downstream models on statistically representative data without exposing raw, personally identifiable information, thereby facilitating compliance with regulations in smart home or healthcare applications
(
Alabdulwahab et al., 2024
;
Goyal and Mahmoud, 2024
)
. This is increasingly achieved through specialized data generation tools and libraries, as: Synthetic Data Vault (SDV), DataSynthesizer or CTGAN, which are tailored to handle the complex, multivariate time-series data typical of IoT sensors. While synthetic data generation addresses privacy by removing personally identifiable information, it raises distinct ethical concerns regarding "bias amplification"
(
Wyllie et al., 2024
)
. If a generative model is trained on a limited, non-representative sample of IoT data, it risks hallucinating synthetic datasets that reinforce existing biases. In healthcare or smart city applications, this can lead to automated decisions that are statistically skewed against specific demographics, directly eroding user trust. Therefore, data frameworks must now include bias-detection loops alongside standard validation
(
Koul et al., 2025
)
. Furthermore, establishing standardized benchmarks remains a critical hurdle for robust deployment. While the IoT community utilizes established hardware-centric benchmarks like MLPerf Tiny or AIoTBench to evaluate the inference latency and energy efficiency of traditional discriminative models on edge accelerators, evaluating GenAI introduces new complexities. The industry currently lacks a unified benchmark suite that simultaneously assesses the edge
-
hardware efficiency of a DGM and the statistical fidelity of its outputs. As a result, researchers often adapt traditional generative evaluation metrics to time
-
series sensor data, underscoring the need for dedicated GenAI
-
IoT evaluation frameworks.

6.
Conclusions

The question of how AI should support IoT opens a significant debate in current computer science and engineering. Over the years, Discriminative AI models have been instrumental in supporting the design and implementation of intelligent IoT applications: tools (such as ML and DL) effectively extract patterns and correlations from large datasets, being focused solely on partitioning and categorizing data points in order to produce a probabilistic classification or decision. In a broader perspective, the estimation of probability underpinning the GenAI can serve as a foundational element to comprehensively address inherent semantic-, networking- and things-related issues featuring the IoT, by
simultaneously handling uncertainty and understanding and, hence, to generate outputs creatively.

Such a research direction is still mostly unexplored, and motivated this systematic survey in providing both a quantitative and qualitative analysis of the body of knowledge related to the integration of GenAI within IoT Computing. As takeaways of this survey, we recognized that comprehensive and domain-independent secondary studies on the GenAI-IoT duo are very few, while,
among the application-oriented primary studies, DGMs are widely employed in both their explicit and implicit density modeling forms. On the explicit density side, ARMs, particularly those leveraging Transformer-based architectures, are the most commonly adopted to process sequential sensor data, predict future states, and enhance automation (particularly, LLMs have primarily and largely been exploited for interpreting network traffic profile or implementing natural language interfaces for Smart Devices, thus originating the conflation of conversational applications with GenAI). On the implicit density side, GANs dominate the IoT landscape due to their ability to generate high-fidelity data without requiring explicit likelihood estimation. Nevertheless, other generative approaches such as VAEs and Diffusion models also feature prominently in the literature.

Despite the specific family of DGM, the majority of studies emphasize the challenges associated with deploying these models on resource-constrained devices, which remains a critical barrier to their full adoption in real-world IoT scenarios. In response to these limitations, various optimization techniques (ranging from model compression to edge-aware adaptations) have been outlined but not yet fully explored in IoT Computing. Conversely, a significant barrier remains the lack of standardized evaluation frameworks. While benchmarks like MLPerf Tiny exist for discriminative models, the industry lacks a unified standard to evaluate the hallucinations or energy efficiency of generative models in IoT loops. Currently, researchers often repurpose datasets such as NASA’s C-MAPSS for predictive maintenance to test GenAI robustness, but dedicated frameworks for validating synthetic data quality in safety-critical systems remain an open research challenge. Moreover, the "black box" nature of DGMs presents a critical challenge regarding Explainable AI (XAI). In safety-critical IoT loops, such as autonomous braking or industrial control, operators require more than just a prediction; they require interpretability but current edge frameworks often lack lightweight XAI tools.

To conclude, as articulated in this survey, we strongly believe that as IoT grows, GenAI will play a crucial role in scaling and managing its complexity, thus outlining an exciting and multidisciplinary area for future work.
