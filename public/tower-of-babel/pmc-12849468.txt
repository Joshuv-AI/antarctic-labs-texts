Roles of exosomes in microenvironment-related premature ovarian insufficiency: mechanisms and therapeutic intervention
Authors: YanFeng Yang, BingJie Rui, Ji Hyang Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Exosomes are nanoscale vesicles that traffic bioactive cargo and modulate cell-cell communication within the ovarian niche. They are pivotal mediators in ovarian microenvironment-related premature ovarian insufficiency (omePOI): pathogenic exosomes propagate injury, whereas therapeutic Exosomes restore homeostasis to shape the niche and influence disease onset and course. However, omePOI still lacks sensitive predictive biomarkers and disease-modifying therapies; moreover, the complexity of the ovarian niche-encompassing extracellular matrix, stromal and immune compartments, vasculature, and metabolic-redox balance-poses substantial translational challenges. In this Review, we first summarize the current clinical challenges in diagnosing and managing omePOI. We then focus on reported correlations between exosomal alterations and omePOI, and postulate mechanisms by which these vesicles influence disease biology across apoptosis/mitochondrial injury, senescence, inflammation and innate-adaptive crosstalk, angiogenesis, fibrosis/extracellular matrix, and metabolic-redox pathways. Finally, we highlight the potential value of exosomal changes as biomarkers for predicting omePOI and discuss exosomal interventions, including mesenchymal stromal cell–derived and engineered Exosomes, as well as exosome–biomaterial composites together with design principles from ovarian tissue engineering.Graphical abstract Exosomes–ovarian microenvironment-therapy overview in omePOI. Introduction

Primary ovarian insufficiency (POI), formerly termed premature ovarian failure (POF), refers to loss of ovarian function before age 40 and is characterized by amenorrhea/oligomenorrhea, infertility, and elevated gonadotropin levels [1]. Reported prevalence varies across populations, with an overall frequency of 1% and higher rates in selected risk groups [2]. Etiologies are heterogeneous, spanning genetic defects, autoimmune mechanisms, iatrogenic injury, and environmental exposures, yet 90% of cases remain idiopathic [3, 4]. The ovarian microenvironment comprises the extracellular matrix (ECM), stromal and immune cells with their cytokines/chemokines, vascular and lymphatic networks, neural and hormonal inputs, and metabolic factors. Disruption of this niche drives ovarian damage and dysfunction [5]. For example, in blood samples from women with POI, full-length transcriptome sequencing revealed immune-cell signatures; these findings suggest that defects or exhaustion in specific immune-cell subsets may accompany early ovarian failure, indicating an immune contribution to POI pathogenesis [6]. Current therapeutic options are limited. Hormone replacement therapy relieves symptoms but does not restore fertility and carries long-term risks [7]. Thus, there exists an urgent imperative to deepen our understanding of the ovarian microenvironment of POI and to create new pathways for early diagnosis and intervention.

In this review, we use the term “microenvironment-related primary ovarian insufficiency” (omePOI) to emphasise cases in which disruption of the ovarian niche, rather than intrinsic depletion of the oocyte pool alone, plays a central role in follicular failure. Classical etiologic classifications of POI (for example, genetic, autoimmune, iatrogenic, and idiopathic) are clinically useful, but they do not fully capture convergent microenvironmental insults such as cellular senescence and apoptosis, stromal and ECM remodelling, vascular and angiogenic changes, immune activation or imbalance, redox–metabolic stress, and exosome-mediated intercellular communication. We therefore define omePOI as a mechanistic construct in which these ovarian microenvironmental pathologies act as primary or convergent drivers of follicular loss and endocrine failure across heterogeneous etiologies. Importantly, omePOI as a conceptual framework to integrate microenvironment-focused mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

Small secreted vesicles were first described in the late 1960 s as matrix vesicles that bud from the plasma membrane of chondrocytes [8]. In the early 1980 s, Exosomes were defined as intraluminal vesicles released via fusion of multivesicular endosomes with the plasma membrane during reticulocyte maturation, mediating selective externalization of cargo such as the transferrin receptor [9, 10]. Over the past decade, exosome research has developed rapidly. Exosomes encapsulate a diverse array of bioactive constituents and are actively secreted by virtually all cell types, playing a crucial role in intercellular communication and the transfer of molecular signals [11]. They are now widely studied as disease biomarkers and therapeutic agents [12]. To systematically standardize extracellular vesicle (EV) research, the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) issued the ‘Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles’ (MISEV) guidelines (2014–2023) for isolation, characterization, and reporting [13].

Emerging evidence highlights the dual role of Exosomes in modulating the ovarian microenvironment. Pathogenic Exosomes, for example, those derived from POI ovaries carry altered miRNA profiles that induce granulosa cell (GC) death [14], whereas therapeutic Exosomes, particularly stem cells–derived vesicles, deliver protective factors that promote follicle growth and angiogenesis in the ovary [15]. These dual abilities raise the question of whether endogenous Exosomes carrying ‘harmful’ cargo contribute to omePOI pathogenesis and whether therapeutic Exosomes counteract microenvironmental failure to preserve ovarian function.

In this Review, we (i) summarize clinical challenges specific to omePOI; (ii) synthesize evidence linking exosomal alterations to apoptosis, senescence, inflammation, angiogenesis, stromal fibrotic remodeling, and metabolic dysregulation within the ovarian niche; and (iii) discuss translational opportunities, including biomarker development and therapeutic interventions using mesenchymal stem-cell (MSC)-derived, engineered, and biomaterial-delivered Exosomes.

Methods

This narrative review is based on a comprehensive literature search of PubMed and Web of Science, supplemented by targeted queries in Google Scholar, to identify original studies, reviews and other relevant articles on “primary ovarian insufficiency”, “premature ovarian failure”, “ovarian microenvironment”, “granulosa cell”, “Exosomes”, and “extracellular vesicles”. We prioritised more recent, peer-reviewed studies with clear mechanistic, clinical, or translational relevance; animal studies were included when they provided complementary insight into exosome biology or microenvironmental changes in the oavry. Non–peer-reviewed items (except authoritative guidelines or consensus statements) and records lacking relevant mechanistic or outcome data were excluded, and the reference lists of key papers were hand-screened to identify additional pertinent studies.

Clinical challenges posed by OmePOI

Diagnosis of OmePOI

Diagnosing omePOI is challenging because early manifestations are subtle and heterogeneous. Traditional diagnostic criteria rely on menstrual disturbance, as well as elevated serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and low estrogen levels. There are currently no sensitive biomarkers that can predict POI before overt hormonal abnormalities. For instance, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) reflects ovarian reserve but has high individual variability and is not specific for impending ovarian failure [16]. Unlike conventional POI driven largely by follicle depletion, omePOI is mechanistically linked to microenvironmental derangements, including local ovarian inflammation, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and cellular senescence, that can precede massive follicle loss and create a diagnostic blind spot when relying purely on systemic hormone changes [17, 18]. ongoing efforts to identify biomarkers for the prediction or early diagnosis of omePOI are crucial.

Because exosomal microRNAs (miRNAs) and proteins are detectable in accessible biofluids and participate in intercellular signaling [19], they are attractive noninvasive indicators of early microenvironmental disruption (Fig. 1). Measurable differences relative to healthy controls have been reported. A proof-of-concept study found higher urinary exosomal miR-4516 in idiopathic and Turner-related POI [20]. In follicular-fluid (FF), lower miR-320a/miR-19b have been linked to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction affecting follicle development and embryo quality, whereas miR-21, miR-34c, and miR-143-3p are increased and miR-155-5p, miR-339-5p, and miR-424-5p are dysregulated in associated with GC apoptosis and inflammatory signaling [21, 22]. By contrast, FF-Exos from healthy women are enriched for miRNAs involved in follicular maturation, including members of the miR-29 and miR-99/100 families and the miR-132/212 cluster [23]. Studies also show cell-type–specific miRNA differences in ovarian cell–derived Exosomes, many of which are closely linked to cell proliferation and apoptosis [24]. Moreover, Ovarian cell–derived Exosomes and FF-Exos show overlapping miRNA profiles and functions relevant to folliculogenesis [25]. These cargo signatures may therefore provide noninvasive readouts of oocyte competence and early indicators of ovarian niche health. However, no predictive biomarkers for any omePOI have been integrated into routine clinical practice, highlighting the need for larger, longitudinal studies and standardized EV in line with MISEV recommendations.

Treatment strategies for OmePOI

Beyond genetic and idiopathic causes, there is growing recognition that the ovarian microenvironment (stromal tissue, immune cells, vasculature) plays a critical role in follicle survival. Below we summarize current established therapies for POI and emerging approaches targeting these microenvironmental factors (Fig. 2). International guidelines, including those from the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE), the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM), and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), recommend hormone replacement therapy (HRT) as first-line management until the usual age of natural menopause. HRT effectively alleviates estrogen-deficiency symptoms and helps prevent associated complications; however, it has little effect on ovulation induction or achieving live birth [26]. Close surveillance of bone mineral density, cardiovascular risk, and thyroid/autoimmune comorbidity is recommended [27]. Low-dose transdermal testosterone may considered for persistent hypoactive sexual desire, but evidence of benefit is limited and routine use is not endorsed [28, 29]. Thus, there is an urgent need for novel strategies that can restore ovarian function and improve fertility while minimizing treatment-associated risks. For omePOI, a range of innovative strategies targeting dysregulation of the local ovarian microenvironment have been under active investigation in recent years.

Within the follicular niche, pro-inflammatory signaling (notably tumor necrosis factor-α, TNF-α) is heightened, whereas anti-inflammatory interleukin-10 (IL-10) is relatively deficient [30–32]. A bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis indicated that lower circulating IL-10 is associated with increased POI risk [33]. Inflammasome dependent inflammation contributes to aging-associated follicular depletion [34]. In POI, the NLRP3/caspase 1/interleukin 1β (IL-1β) axis is upregulated in GCs, and pharmacologic inhibition of NLRP3 with MCC950 improved ovarian reserve and pregnancy rates in mice models [35]. Mechanistically, TNF-α and related stimuli amplify inflammasome signalling and promote caspase-1-dependent maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, as described in the inflammation Sects [36–38]. These data support therapeutic exploration of cytokine antagonists and inflammasome inhibitors to mitigate chronic ovarian inflammation and potentially slow functional decline.

Beyond inflammation, ovarian stromal fibrosis is another pathological hallmark of POI, characterized by excessive fibroblast activation, overproduction of extracellular matrix, and collagen cross-linking [39]. Interventional studies have shown that antifibrotic agents, such as pirfenidone and BGP-15 eliminate fibrotic collagen and restore ovulation in reproductively old and obese mice, with dampened M2 macrophage polarization and upregulated matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP13), providing evidence that ovarian fibrosis may be reversible [40]. In clinical specimens, ovarian tissues from POI patients often exhibit marked stromal fibrosis accompanied by elevated TGF-β [41]. Targeted inhibition of the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling pathway attenuates these fibrotic alterations, highlighting a potential antifibrotic target [42]. Adequate follicular perfusion is essential for sustaining ovarian function. Moderate activation of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/VEGF receptor (VEGFR) signaling pathway and the stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1)/C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) axis enhances ovarian microcirculation, improving local blood supply and oxygen delivery [43–45]. Therefore, antifibrotic agents in combination with proangiogenic therapies hold promise for optimizing the ovarian microenvironment.

Links between exosomes and OmePOI

Exosome biogenesis and secretion mechanisms

Exosomes, also known as small extracellular vesicles, are nanosized vesicles of 30–200 nm in diameter. They form as intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) in eukaryotic cells. Early endosomes budding inward from the plasma membrane, then mature into late endosomes or MVBs [46]. When an MVB fuses with the cell’s plasma membrane, the ILVs are released into the extracellular space as Exosomes [47] (Fig. 3). This release is an active, regulated process involving complex molecular machinery. Cargo selection and ILV budding proceed is coordinated by the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) system consisting of ESCRT-0, -I, -II, -III subcomplexes and the ATPase VPS4 and ESCRT-independent routes, including the neutral sphingomyelinase 2 (nSMase2)-ceramide axis and tetraspanin-organized microdomains [48–53].

After secretion, Exosomes can interact with target cells via at least three routes: ①receptor/ligand interactions-exosomal surface proteins or lipids bind cognate receptors to trigger outside-in signaling [54]; ②endocytosis-clathrin/caveolin-mediated uptake, macropinocytosis, or phagocytosis, followed by endosomal/lysosomal trafficking and cargo release [55]; ③direct membrane fusion-exosomal and plasma membranes fuse, releasing luminal cargo directly into the cytosol [56]. These mechanisms enable Exosomes to deliver their molecular cargo with a degree of specificity determined by surface ligands and receptors. Notably, Exosomes’ lipid bilayer confers stability, protecting proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites from extracellular enzymatic degradation [57]. Together with selective cargo packaging, Exosomes mediate long-range intercellular communication that shapes the ovarian niche in omePOI.

Exosomes as double-edged players in omepoi: controversies and bidirectional effects

Several lines of evidence indicate that specific exosome populations confer ovarian protection by enhancing cell survival and tissue repair. Many of these beneficial effects are attributed to Exosomes derived from MSCs or other supportive cells; their cargo often includes anti-apoptotic microRNAs and pro-angiogenic factors that rejuvenate the ovarian microenvironment. For example, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (HucMSC‑Exos) enriched with miR-126-3p activate the PI3K/Akt pathway [15], while bone marrow MSC Exosomes (BMSC-Exos) deliver miR-644-5p to repress p53, thereby protecting GCs and improving follicular morphology in POI animal models [58]. BMSC-Exos have also been reported to restrain TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, reducing local inflammation and oxidative stress [59].Collectively, these findings support that MSC-Exos may function as pro-survival modulators that help buffer apoptotic, inflammatory and oxidative stress in omePOI.

In contrast, other data implicate Exosomes as drivers of injury within the ovarian niche, thereby contributing to omePOI pathogenesis and progression. Follicular fluid (FF) Exosomes from older or menopausal transition ovaries appear increased in number and enriched in stress-associated molecules, consistent with heightened oxidative stress and inflammaging [60]. In a chemotherapy-induced POI mouse model, ovarian Exosomes enriched for miR-122-5p (targeting BCL9) drive GC apoptosis, whereas normal ovarian Exosomes are protective and miR-122-5p inhibition reverses this effect [14]. Similarly, in rabbits, plasma-derived Exosomes from POI animals were enriched in miR-10a-5p; upon uptake by GCs, this miRNA suppressed proliferation and induced apoptosis by targeting brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) through the TrkB/Akt/mTOR pathway [61]. Beyond cell survival, exosome-associated latent TGF-β1 can cooperate with abundant extracellular-matrix cofactors (e.g., thrombospondins, kallikreins, integrins) in aging ovaries to amplify profibrotic activity and sustain a hyperfibrotic, inflammation-prone stromal milieu [60]. Taken together, these observations suggest that Exosomes released from damaged or ageing ovaries and from the systemic circulation in POI can act as pathogenic messengers that propagate apoptotic, profibrotic and inflammatory signalling within the ovarian niche, thereby accelerating omePOI progression. However, the number and identity of exosomal populations carrying convergent pathogenic signals, and the ways in which MSC-Exos counterbalance them, remain incompletely understood. These observations underscore a central paradox: Exosomes can be both guardians and enemies of ovarian function. Their role in omePOI appears to be highly context-dependent, varying with the cellular source of Exosomes, the physiological or pathological state of that source, and the molecular cargo carried (Fig. 4). For instance, Exosomes from young, healthy MSCs contain regenerative signals that protect ovaries, whereas Exosomes from a damaged ovary carry stress signals that can further injury. This duality has sparked debate and careful consideration in the field. Selective targeting of harmful exosome pathways might be necessary, while augmenting beneficial Exosomes could help repair ovarian damage. Achieving this balance in a clinical setting remains a major challenge. There are also gaps in understanding the exact triggers that switch exosome content from protective to harmful. Factors such as oxidative stress, estrogen deficiency, and immune activation likely influence exosomal cargo selection and release [62–64]. Therefore, elucidating the specific mechanisms by which Exosomes interact with the ovarian microenvironment in the context of omePOI, and identifying strategies to manipulate exosomal pathways in favor of ovarian protection, is of critical importance for the development of effective therapeutic interventions for POI.

Apoptosis and mitochondrial injury

In POI, GCs frequently undergo excessive apoptosis with mitochondrial dysfunction [65]. Exosomes released from pathological or stressed ovarian sources carry apoptotic cargo that undermines GC viability. Transcriptomic and RT-qPCR profiling implicated exosome-mediated regulation of GC adhesion, migration, and proliferation [66]. In women with DOR, FF-exosomal miRNAs showed enrichment for modules linked to PI3K/Akt, actin-cytoskeleton, and cell-death pathways [67]. Urinary exosomal miR-4516 from POI patients targeted STAT3 and shifts the p53/BAX axis toward apoptosis [20]. A central convergence is p53/p21 signaling axis. For instance, In GCs, p53 activation leads to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (via Bax/Bak) and release of cytochrome c, which activates caspase-9 and then the executioner caspase-3 [68, 69]. Oxidative stress (e.g., hydrogen peroxide) stabilizes p53 via JNK, triggering p53-dependent apoptosis [70]. Weakened antioxidant defenses (as seen with exosomal suppression of NRF2/SLC7A11) [71] permit reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation that triggers DNA damage and p53 activation, amplifying apoptotic programs. These findings suggest that Exosomes from pathological ovarian can tip the balance of p53-dependent signaling toward GC apoptosis, especially under conditions of oxidative stress and impaired antioxidant defense, and thus contribute to follicular loss in omePOI.

In contrast, protective MSC-derived Exosomes (MSC-Exos) counter apoptosis by engaging pro-survival and pro-metabolic pathways, notably PI3K/Akt, TGF-β/Smad, Hippo (YAP/TAZ), and AMPK/mTOR. HucMSC‑Exos have been reported to activate YAP in GCs and promote follicle growth [72]. BMSC-Exos delivering miR-144-5p repress PTEN, unleashing Akt-mediated survival and improving follicle support [73]. Exosomes derived from menstrual blood stromal cells delivered thrombospondin-1 (TSP1) and, via TGF-β/Smad3 and Akt, increased BCL-2/MDM2 and reduced p53 and cleaved caspases, rescuing follicle development in toxin-induced POI rats; pharmacologic Smad3 blockade abrogated these effects, indicating Smad-Akt cross-talk [74]. Through SIRT3/PGC-1α, MSC-Exos stabilize GC mitochondrial dynamics and membrane potential, reduce ROS and lipid peroxidation, and avert ferroptosis [75]. Thus, pathogenic Exosomes propagate apoptotic signaling that contributes to ovarian dysfunction, whereas therapeutic Exosomes preserve mitochondrial function and limit apoptosis, enhancing GC survival. However, current evidence derives predominantly from toxin- or chemotherapy-induced rodent models and in vitro cultures, with substantial heterogeneity in EV isolation and quantification protocols, marked species-specific differences in ovarian architecture, and dynamic changes in exosomal cargo driven by cell source and culture conditions. Longitudinal human cohort studies linking apoptotic exosomal signatures to POI onset and progression are still lacking, so the true magnitude of exosome-mediated apoptosis in clinical omePOI remains uncertain.

Cellular senescence and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)

Senescent-cell accumulation occurs in aging ovaries [76]. Similarly, In mice, Cdkn1a (p21Cip1) and Cdkn2a (p16INK4a) are upregulated with increased pro-inflammatory/pro-fibrotic SASP mediators and lipofuscin deposits, correlating with reduced ovarian function [18]. Although cell-cycle–arrested, senescent cells remain metabolically active and continuously secrete SASP factors (cytokines, chemokines and proteases). These signals can also be encapsulated and delivered via Exosomes and other EVs, influencing the fate of neighbouring or distant cells by modulating survival, apoptosis, differentiation and fibrotic responses, and amplifying inflammatory and tissue-damaging signals within the microenvironment [77, 78]. Studies show senescent cells release senescence-associated Exosomes (SA-Exos) enriched with specific miRNAs or signaling molecules capable of inducing senescence in neighboring young cells. For example, SA-Exos from radiation-induced senescent MSCs activate the TGF-β signaling in normal fibroblasts, inducing a myofibroblast-like phenotype with increased α-SMA and matrix deposition [79]. In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), senescent fibroblasts secrete more Exosomes enriched in MMP-1, engaging PAR1 and activating PI3K/Akt/mTOR, thereby promoting fibrogenesis [80]. This highlights the potential pro-pathogenic role of SA-Exos in driving ovarian fibrosis.

In contrast, MSC-Exos exhibit anti-senescent activity. Exosomal miR-146a-5p, often referred to as “anti-SASP” miRNAs, targets TRAF6, suppresses NF-κB, and reduces SASP output [81]. Moreover, MSC-Exos can deliver autophagy-regulating proteins and RNAs. Human umbilical cord MSC-derived EVs (HucMSC-EVs) carrying IGF-1 activate the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, normalize excessive GC autophagy, and improve ovarian function in cyclophosphamide-induced POI mice [82]. HucMSC-Exos enriched with let-7a-5p inhibit theca-interstitial cell autophagy via the AMPK/mTOR, leading to improved ovarian function in POI rats and highlighting the mechanistic control of autophagy within the ovarian stromal compartment [83]. Similarly, Exosomes derived from adipose stem cells (ADSC-Exos) alleviate POI by suppressing autophagy through the same AMPK/mTOR axis, reinforcing the conserved role of this pathway across different stem cell sources [84]. These findings indicate that MSC-Exos enhance the autophagic clearance capacity of ovarian stromal cells, thereby attenuating the proliferation-inhibitory influence of senescent cells and restoring the proliferative and differentiation potential of stromal cells. Overall, Exosomes in the context of ovarian aging can act as carriers of deleterious senescence signals that exacerbate microenvironmental imbalance and functional decline, yet they also hold therapeutic potential as delivery vehicles capable of reversing senescence-associated phenotypes.

Inflammation and innate-adaptive crosstalk

Chronic sterile inflammation is a hallmark of POI. Transcriptomic data indicate early activation of inflammatory programs in ovarian aging, exemplified by CD38 upregulation with concomitant NAD⁺ depletion; importantly, CD38 blockade mitigates inflammation, improves fertility, and reduces stromal collagen in aged ovaries [85]. Autoimmune disease is a recognized POI etiology, supporting chronic, non-infectious inflammation as a core feature in many cases [86].

Exosomes released from injured tissues are increasingly recognized as danger signals, acting as damage associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that engage ovarian innate immune receptors and drive overactivation of inflammatory pathways [87, 88]. In androgen stimulated preantral GCs, exosomal miR379-5p is transferred to ovarian macrophages suppresses the PDK1–AKT axis, promotes M0 to M1 polarization, and inhibits the M2 phenotype [89]. Exosomal DAMPs or RNAs can also act directly on ovarian parenchymal cells: after uptake, these vesicles initiate intracellular inflammatory signaling cascades that culminate in apoptosis or senescence of granulosa and stromal cells. For example, FF Exosomes from PCOS activate the NFκB and assembled the NLRP3 inflammasome in GCs, increasing IL-1β/IL-18 release and amplifying sterile inflammation within the ovarian parenchyma [90].

Exosomes derived from immune cells are important mediators of immune modulation in ovarian ageing [91]. For example, miR-155 marks M1 polarization [92]and within regulatory T cells (Tregs), it targets SOCS1to enhance IL-2/STAT5 signaling and Treg fitness [93]. In inflammatory contexts, M1 exosomal miR-155 can impede Tregs induction and favor STAT3-linked Th17 responses, weakening tolerance and exacerbating inflammation [94]. Conversely, immunosuppressive cells such as M2 macrophages and Tregs release Exosomes enriched in anti-inflammatory mediators that help preserve local immune balance; for example, Tregs derived vesicles carry CD73, CTLA4, and TGF-β, which suppress local inflammation and promote tissue repair [95]. MSC-Exos have also been shown to down-regulated the NLRP3/Casp1/GSDMD pyroptosis and inhibited NF-κB activation in ovarian cells, attenuating chemotherapy-induced inflammatory injury [96]. Overall, Exosomes in the ovarian inflammatory network have dual potential: they can fuel immune dysregulation under pathology, yet therapeutic Exosomes can re-establish innate–adaptive balance. However, these exosome-mediated immunomodulatory effects have been characterised mainly in non-POI inflammatory models, EV isolation and quantification methods differ substantially between studies, and longitudinal human data are lacking; as a result, the contribution of exosome-driven inflammation to human omePOI remains only partially defined.

Angiogenesis and microvascular integrity

The ovary is a highly vascular organ in which the microvasculature supplies developing follicles with nutrients and transports steroid hormones. Whole-organ imaging revealed age-related declines in ovarian blood-vessel density and angiogenic activity, with single-cell data implicating endothelial aging; GC-derived vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) was identified as a key driver of ovarian angiogenesis [97].The Sema3E-Plexin‐D1 pathway regulates ovarian angiogenesis by acting on Plexin-D1-expressing vascular endothelial cells to modulate proliferation, differentiation, adhesion, and migration [98]. Independently, another study showed that Sema3E/Plexin-D1 suppresses VEGFA-induced Delta‐like 4 (Dll4)‐Notch signaling to limit endothelial cell differentiation during vessel sprouting [99]. Building on this vascular paradigm, ovarian angiogenesis is governed by aging of the vascular endothelium and by granulosa cell derived factors such as VEGFA.

Exosomes are emerging important regulators of ovarian angiogenesis. HucMSC Exosomes preconditioned by hypoxia (Hypo-Exos) enriched in miR-205-5p enhance angiogenesis via PTEN/PI3K/Akt/mTOR [100]. In polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) models, adipocyte-derived Exosomes carrying miR-30c-5p are internalized by ovarian microvascular endothelial cells, relieve SOCS3 restraint of STAT3, upregulate VEGFA, and triggered a robust pro-angiogenic response [101]. Beyond RNAs, exosomal surface ligands (e.g., heparan-sulfate proteoglycan -, integrin-, and tetraspanin-based) facilitate endothelial interactions; delivery of sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) or sphingosine kinase activates S1PR1/PI3K/Akt/eNOS, enhancing microvascular resilience and barrier function in ischemic models [102–104]. In vitro, MSC-Exos promote human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) migration and capillary-like tube formation [105]. Thus, exosome-mediated activation of ovarian angiogenic programs, together with preservation of microvascular integrity, represents a viable strategy to improve perfusion and protect follicles in omePOI. However, these observations are based almost entirely on short-term rodent models, in which ovarian vascular architecture and remodelling dynamics differ from those of humans. There is substantial heterogeneity in EV isolation, quantification and functional assays, and angiogenic exosomal cargo is strongly influenced by donor-cell state and culture conditions. To date, no longitudinal human imaging or biomarker studies have linked exosome-derived angiogenic signals to microvascular decline or recovery in POI, so the relevance of these angiogenic pathways to human omePOI remains to be validated.

Fibrosis and ECM remodeling

Excess stromal fibrosis is a histologic hallmark of POI, with collagen deposition and sclerosis. Physiologically, ovarian ECM remodels cyclically; in POI, a pro-inflammatory, senescence-associated milieu biases toward fibrogenesis, driving myofibroblast activation, excessive collagen cross-linking, and stromal stiffening [5]. Human tissue studies link IFN-γ/TNF-α–JAK/STAT1 and NF-κB signaling to GC expression of CTGF and CYP19A1, promoting atresia and dysfunction [106]. Consistently, chronic activation of these inflammatory pathways by cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) and SASP factors sustains a pro-fibrotic stromal programme that converges on TGF-β/Smad and CTGF signalling, yielding α-SMA-positive myofibroblasts and a stiffer ovarian matrix [107, 108].

Exosomes from “pathogenic” niches plausibly carry profibrotic signals. In other organs, hypoxic tubular epithelial-cell Exosomes bearing TGF-β1 mRNA promoted fibroblast proliferation, α-SMA expression, F-actin assembly, and collagen I production; blocking exosomal TGF-β1 abrogated these effects [109]. Cancer-cell Exosomes presenting latent TGF-β activate SMAD2/3 phosphorylation and myofibroblast conversion, which is attenuated by TGF-β or betaglycan inhibition [110]. Not only epithelial or cancer cells, but even fibroblasts in diseased tissue can propagate fibrosis via Exosomes. Exosomes from fibrotic-muscle fibroblasts increased α-SMA, collagen I/III, and fibronectin in normal fibroblasts [111], SA-Exos in radiation/drug-induced ovarian senescence carry miRNAs that target TGF-β/Smad genes, potentially sustaining paracrine pro-fibrotic signaling [79]. Beyond TGF-β, persistent YAP/TAZ activity is fibrogenic; tumor-cell Exosomes can activate YAP1 and promote a cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) phenotype [112], while YAP/TAZ upregulates ECM genes and drives myofibroblast conversion [113]. Conversely, therapeutic exosomes show anti-fibrotic potential. Amniotic fluid-derived xosomes (AF-Exos) modulate Smad4/Smad6, an inhibitor of TGF-β signalling, and reduce ovarian collagen deposition in POI rats [114]. miR-21-5p, abundant in MSC-Exos, modulate Hippo (LATS1/YAP) and LOXL2 signaling and to enhance steroidogenic gene expression in autoimmune POI, consistent with anti-fibrotic and microenvironment-restorative effects [115, 116]. Conceptually, delivering anti-fibrotic cargo via Exosomes could rebalance ECM turnover-reducing pathologic collagen while preserving essential scaffold-to create a better mechanical and nutritional environment for follicle growth; however, this remains a hypothetical strategy based primarily on preclinical data and has not yet been validated in humans.

Metabolic stress and redox homeostasis

Beyond infertility, POI often presents with postmenopausal-like metabolic abnormalities. Clinical studies indicate that increased visceral adiposity, central obesity, and insulin resistance, and atherogenic lipid profiles, reflecting estrogen-deficiency effects on adipose and hepatic metabolism [117]. Mitochondria are central to ovarian bioenergetics: oocytes and GCs rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for ATP generation from glucose and lipids. In POI and ovarian aging, mitochondrial dysfunction, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage, and excess ROS are common. Under stress, accumulated ROS further injures membrane lipids and both nuclear DNA and mtDNA, establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies oxidative damage [118]. Clinically, circulating oxidative markers (d-ROMs, reflecting hydroperoxides) are significantly higher in POI patients than in controls without a compensatory rise in antioxidant capacity [119]. In POI models, ATP depletion and lipid peroxidation rise. For example, in cisplatin-treated rats, ovarian malondialdehyde (MDA, a lipid-ROS product) is high while superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and ATP content are low [120], and hydrogen peroxide triggers GC apoptosis via ROS/JNK/p53 signaling [121]. Conversely, antioxidant signaling (Nrf2, SIRT1) is downregulated in POI models, linking redox imbalance to gene regulation of cell survival [122].

Follicles are metabolically coupled units. Oocytes have limited glycolytic capacity and rely on cumulus and mural GCs for metabolites via Cx37/Cx43 gap junctions, regulated by oocyte-secreted factors such as GDF9 and BMP15 [123, 124]. Disruption of this coupling in Cx37-null mice impairs ovulation, highlighting the need for intercellular metabolite transfer in follicle maturation [125]. The theca-granulosa “two-cell, two-gonadotropin” system furnishes androgen precursors from the theca/stroma that GCs aromatize to estradiol, while the stroma, vasculature, and ECM shape oxygen and nutrient delivery and paracrine inputs that support-but do not directly execute-oocyte metabolism [126, 127]. Therefore, GCs and ovarian stromal fibroblasts are key targets of metabolic and oxidative injury.

In merPOI, local glucose- and lipid-metabolic disturbances within follicles are frequent and accompany redox imbalance. Label-free CARS imaging links ovarian aging to altered oocyte lipid profiles, membrane/mitochondrial ultrastructure damage, and reduced exosome secretion, correlating with implantation failure in older mice [128]. Exosomal cargo has emerged as a driver of metabolic reprogramming across GCs, theca/stromal cells, and even oocytes. Physiologically, Exosomes coordinate follicle development and oocyte maturation by shuttling metabolic signals. Li et al. found that MSC-Exos delivering circDennd2a bound lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), enhanced NADH-to-NAD⁺ conversion, and sustained GC glycolysis [129]. Chen et al. reported that FF-Exos deliver carried miR-21-5p to target BTG2 and activate IRS1/Akt, increasing glucose uptake [130]. Conversely, FF-Exos delivering miR-143-3p and miR-155-5p suppressed HK2, PKM2, and LDHA, constraining glycolysis [22]. Together, these findings underscore that exosomal cargo can either preserve or constrain GC glycolysis in a cargo-dependent manner.

Exosomal communication between cumulus–oocyte complexes and GCs represent a new molecular axis for oocyte competence. In porcine models, GC-derived Exosomes (GC-Exos) miR-148a-3p targets DOCK6, increasing the cumulus expansion index, lowering ROS, and elevating mitochondrial membrane potential [131]. Likewise, FF-Exos miR-339-5p regulates ERK1/2 activity via SFPQ, thereby increasing ER content and lipid-droplet number, and reducing ROS, droplet size, and abnormal cortical-granule patterns [132]. Under pathology, exosome-mediated delivery of miRNAs and lncRNAs disrupts oocyte-granulosa energy coupling. For example, plasma exosomal miR-10a-5p is elevated in POI and represses BDNF-TrkB/Akt/mTOR signaling in GCs, precipitating metabolic insufficiency and atresia [61];FF-Exos the long noncoding RNA LIPE-AS1 (elevated in PCOS) sponges miR-4306 to upregulate LHCGR, modulating steroidogenesis and GC proliferation and apoptosis [133]. Beyond ncRNAs, exosomal proteins have also been implicated in POI. In an avian model, FF-Exos from atretic follicles enriched in heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) disrupted iron/redox homeostasis, increasing Fe²⁺, ROS, and MDA, consistent with iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and GC dysfunction [134]. Conversely, therapeutic Exosomes can mitigate metabolic stress; for example, amniotic MSC-Exos miR-320a modulates sirtuin signaling in oocytes, restored OPA1, and preserved mitochondrial function [135].These findings indicate that Exosomes act as conduits of metabolic regulation in the ovary, transmitting and perpetuating signals through energy metabolism and redox pathways such that pathological vesicles propagate nutrient imbalance and oxidative injury, whereas therapeutic vesicles restore metabolic homeostasis and antioxidant defenses, thereby inducing or relieving local metabolic stress.

Predictive and therapeutic potential of exosomes

Biomarkers

Due to Exosomes reflect disease state changes in cellular secretory profiles, they are increasingly explored as early predictive and diagnostic biomarkers for omePOI. Preliminary clinical evidence indicates that, compared with women with normal ovarian function, patients with ovarian dysfunction exhibit distinct exosomal miRNA profiles in peripheral blood that allow for accurate diagnostic discrimination. In a cohort of 107 women with PCOS and 101 controls, plasma exosomal miR-4488 and miR-151a-5p were upregulated while miR-223-3p was downregulated; the areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) for single markers were 0.889, 0.871, and 0.664, respectively, and combining these three with AMH increased the AUC to 0.967 with 93% sensitivity and 91% specificity [136]. Additionally, Exosomes from follicular fluid and urine also show disease specific miRNA alterations linked to local ovarian pathology and have been proposed as noninvasive early biomarkers that can signal impending changes in the ovarian tissue microenvironment [20, 137]. These data provide a foundation for developing predictive models. We hypothesize that profiling Exosomes multimolecular signatures in patient biofluids (serum, urine, follicular fluid), combined with machine learning, could yield multiplex panels that predict high risk of omePOI. Exosome-derived therapeutic strategies for microenvironment-related ovarian dysfunctions

However, several key issues must be resolved before exosomal biomarkers can be translated into clinical practice. First, prospective cohort designs should collect samples at multiple time points-before injury, during exposure or treatment, and during follow-up-to track exosomal biomarker trajectories and capture preclinical signals [184]. Second, technical rigor requires standardized exosomal isolation and characterization procedures and harmonized analytics across qPCR, RNA sequencing, and mass spectrometry, with cross-validation to ensure reproducibility [13]. Third, exosomal readouts should be linked to clinically meaningful endpoints, including follicle development by ultrasound, hormone levels such as AMH and FSH, and reproductive outcomes like pregnancy rates and embryo quality, to establish clinical relevance [7]. Only after large, multi-center validation studies define optimal marker panels and thresholds can Exosomes be advanced as early-warning and individualized risk-assessment tools for omePOI.

Therapeutic strategies

MSC-Exos

A large body of animal studies demonstrates that MSC-Exos show robust therapeutic activity in POI models. Sources include MSCs from umbilical cord, adipose tissue, bone marrow, and placenta, as well as induced pluripotent stem cell derived MSCs. Mechanistically, MSC-Exos deliver miRNAs such as miR-21 and miR-664-5p that suppress apoptosis and inflammation in ovarian cells, dampen p53 and NF-κB activity, and thereby reduce follicular atresia [58, 152]. In parallel, they are enriched in pro-angiogenic and anti-fibrotic mediators (e.g., VEGF, PARP1), improving ovarian perfusion and limit stromal fibrosis, enhancing tissue repair capacity [185, 186]. Exosomal membranes also carry chaperone component such as HSPA8 that can enter target cells, repair mitochondrial damage and inhibit cellular ROS production, mitigating ovotoxicity [187]. Across mice and rat POI models-including cyclophosphamide chemotherapy, radiation injury, and autoimmune oophoritis-multiple MSC exosome preparations increase the number of residual follicles, decrease stromal collagen deposition, restore estradiol and AMH levels, and improve fertility outcomes. For example, administration of HucMSC-Exos increased primordial and growing follicle counts in POI mice and yielded higher oocyte recovery and litter size after superovulation [96, 188].

Delivery routes explored include intravenous (IV), intraperitoneal (IP), and intraovarian (local) injection. IV infusion is most common in preclinical ovarian studies, and a first-in-human trial in ovarian insufficiency has selected IV delivery (single infusion) for simplicity, safety, and cost [189]. Nevertheless, IV delivery warrants careful biodistribution assessment to ensure effective ovarian exposure. By analogy, IV-infused MSCs exhibit a pronounced pulmonary first-pass effect (> 80% initially lodging in the lung microvasculature with 24 h persistence), with only a small fraction reaching distal organs [190, 191]. In contrast, locally injected MSC-Exos tend to remain within the ovary rather than distributing systemically [192]. The clear benefit of intraovarian delivery is maximal ovarian enrichment of Exosomes and higher efficacy in restoring function [189]. However, a key safety consideration is that the human ovary lacks the protective “cystic” bursa seen in rodents; consequently, intraovarian injection would entail direct deposition into the stromal or medullary parenchyma, and excessive injection volume or pressure may damage the fragile ovarian architecture [193]. In preclinical POI studies, IP administration of Exosomes is far less common than IV or intraovarian delivery, and while IP can convey therapeutic Exosomes to ovaries, current evidence is too sparse to establish its comparative potency relative to the other routes [194]. Optimizing exosome delivery may involve hybrid strategies, such as systemic administration coupled with ovary specific targeting ligands to enhance homing, or the use of biomaterial scaffolds to retain Exosomes within the ovary; in all cases, the least invasive route that achieves the therapeutic goal is preferred. Overall, MSC-Exo therapy is well supported in preclinical POI models and appears positioned for early-phase clinical evaluation.

Engineered exosomes

To enhance the efficacy and specificity, Exosomes can be engineered. One approach is cargo loading, whereby specific RNAs or proteins are introduced via electroporation, co-incubation, sonication, or chemical conjugation [195]. In ovarian models, engineered vesicles co-delivering cytotoxic drugs with siRNA or redox-activated payloads increase ovarian accumulation and antitumor activity, demonstrating the targeting gain achievable with exosome carriers [196, 197]. Another strategy is surface engineering, genetic or chemical display of ovarian-relevant ligands (e.g., follicle-homing peptides or folate-receptor ligands) enhances tissue homing and positions signals within the ovarian niche. Notably, HucMSC- exos were engineered to deliver the PI3K/Akt agonist peptide 740Y-P, which promoted follicle activation [198]. Folate‑functionalized Exosomes enhanced drug uptake by folate receptor (FR)‑positive ovarian cells [199–201]. These oncology studies illustrate that surface-modified Exosomes can markedly increase targeting efficiency; in principle, analogous ligand strategies could be adapted for ovarian tissues relevant to POI.

For POI, Exosomes decorated with ovarian-specific recognition ligands (e.g., peptides for follicular-surface markers) achieve selective ovarian homing and more potent, lower effective doses, multi-pathway interventions while preserving native biocompatibility and low immunogenicity. Key challenges include achieving high loading efficiency without compromising membrane integrity, modifying the surface without perturbing pharmacokinetics and biodistribution, and navigating an unsettled regulatory classification that spans drug, gene therapy, and biologic paradigms. Despite these hurdles, engineered Exosomes represent a forward-looking precision-medicine strategy for POI.

Exosome–biomaterial composites

Given the relatively short in vivo half-life and limited tissue retention of Exosomes, recent work has combined Exosomes with biomaterials to create injectable sustained-release systems [202]. Hydrogel scaffolds can be loaded with Exosomes to form “exosome gels” for peri-ovarian injection, enabling in situ release over time [203]; alternatively, degradable microspheres enriched with Exosomes can be placed on the ovarian surface for continuous local delivery [204]. Hydrogels and related matrices have high water content and a porous, ECM-like architecture that permits high local loading and gradual release while supporting tissue repair [205]. In rodents, ovarian cortex ECM (OvaECM) hydrogel preserved native physicochemical properties and supported murine follicle development and ovarian regeneration [206]. Coupling microfluidic nanopore electroporation with in situ hydrogel sequestration achieved on-demand EV loading, primordial follicle activation, and functional ovarian rejuvenation in aged mice [207]. Exosome-loaded self-healing hydrogels prolonged exosome residence and improved ovarian function compared with bolus injection, reducing off-target hepatic and pulmonary uptake and clearance, increasing ovarian exposure, extending therapeutic duration, and lowering dosing frequency.

This strategy of self-healing hydrogels also integrates well with tissue engineering. Lee et al. engineered a platelet-derived factor-encapsulated fibrin hydrogel (PFH) that rebuilt stromal architecture to support follicle engraftment and promoted angiogenesis [208]. Similarly, Shikanov et al. embedded basement membrane-binding (BMB)-functionalized electrospun dextran fibers within poly (ethylene glycol) (PEG) hydrogels to mimic fibrous ECM architecture and sustain follicle culture. Together, these studies indicate that restoring stromal organization is a central determinant of cell–biomaterial crosstalk in ovarian tissue engineering [209]. Beyond biochemical and architectural cues, matrix mechanics and degradability critically govern stromal cell fate via integrin-cytoskeleton-YAP/TAZ mechanotransduction; permissive degradability and viscoelasticity, rather than stiffness alone, better support ovarian repair [210]. Conceptually, such biocompatible, degradable matrices could in the future be adapted for minimally invasive delivery (e.g., ultrasound-guided peri-ovarian injection or laparoscopic placement) [211], but these approaches remain at an exploratory, preclinical stage, and no studies have yet evaluated their safety, degradation kinetics, or ability to deliver Exosomes in a controlled manner in human POI.

Conclusions

Exosomes act as context-dependent messengers that either propagate microenvironmental injury or restore homeostasis in omePOI. Under pathological conditions, ovarian-derived Exosomes disseminate harmful cues between cells, destabilizing the ovarian microenvironment and accelerating functional decline. In contrast, therapeutically sourced Exosomes, particularly from stem cells, can remodel this microenvironment and confer protection across multiple axes, including apoptosis, cellular senescence, inflammation, angiogenesis, and metabolism. This duality underpins two complementary translational avenues: selective attenuation of deleterious exosomal signals and augmentation or engineering of reparative vesicles, including MSC-derived products and exosome-biomaterial composites that improve ovarian retention and dosing efficiency. Overall, Exosomes act as extracellular messengers that are pivotal in the onset and modulation of ovarian dysfunction. As mechanistic mapping advances, it should be possible to delineate the exosome-regulated networks that govern ovarian homeostasis and to harness these natural vesicles for safe, effective diagnostics and therapeutics for POI.

Nevertheless, current POI models comprise a single cell type and murine in vivo, which is probably not identical to the human ovarian microenvironment. The mice ovarian bursa (absent in humans) enables intra-bursal/intra-ovarian dosing and can inflate local exposure [212]; these routes are invasive and not feasible in routine care. Beyond this anatomic difference, divergent hormone profiles, estrous vs. menstrual cycle timing, and ovulation patterns shift biomarker windows and treatment timing [213]. Species-specific theca, pericyte, and epithelial architecture, together with generally milder stromal fibrosis in this model [214]. Methodological heterogeneity in exosome isolation presents significant challenges in reproducibility and translation. Even vesicles from the same source display variable sizes, densities, viscoelastic properties and distinct molecular cargos. If this substructure is ignored, mechanism assignment and clinical translation can be confounded [215].

First, large longitudinal human cohorts are needed to validate the clinical performance of exosome-based biomarkers. Registered trials in other diseases show that serum Exosomes can support early detection; analogous designs should be implemented for omePOI with prespecified clinical endpoints [216]. Such studies are essential to determine whether exosome-derived signatures truly predict disease onset, progression or treatment response, rather than reflecting non-specific tissue injury, and to define clinically meaningful cut-offs. Second, standardize isolation and detection to consistently define EV subpopulations and separate biological signal from method bias [217]. At present, heterogeneity in sample handling, EV enrichment and normalisation strategies hampers comparison across studies and prevents robust assay validation. Without methodological harmonisation, it will be difficult to obtain regulatory approval or to implement exosome-based tests in routine clinical practice. Third, most efficacy data come from short-term rodent models, and practical delivery and safety barriers must be addressed before exosome-based therapies can be translated. Clear definition and control of exosomal cargo are critical to ensure that therapeutic exosomes can be delivered safely and effectively in humans. Finally, ethical issues should be addressed early. Although classification is jurisdiction-specific, native EVs from unmodified cells and EVs from genetically modified cells that do not carry transgene products are regulated as biological medicinal products, whereas EVs that contain and deliver transgene products are typically classified as gene therapy products. Robust consent, privacy safeguards for omics, source governance and long-term reproductive follow-up are essential for clinical translation [218].
