Profile of Susan Goldin-Meadow
Authors: Sandeep Ravindran
Published: 2024

Susan Goldin-Meadow wants to know where language comes from. Her deep fascination with language led her to pursue a career in science. “If you aren’t exposed to a language, would you reinvent one? That really was my question to start,” says Goldin-Meadow.

Susan Goldin-Meadow. Image credit: The University of Chicago/Robert Kozloff (photographer).

Pursuing this question led Goldin-Meadow to a long and distinguished career encompassing linguistics, child development, and cognition. She has made major contributions to the understanding of nonverbal communication—specifically gestures—and of how language develops in early childhood. In her Inaugural Article (IA), Goldin-Meadow brings two strands of her research together in collaboration with behavioral and computational scientists (1). Goldin-Meadow, a professor of psychology at the University of Chicago, was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 2020.

Learning from Home-Signers

Goldin-Meadow went to Smith College in Massachusetts for her undergraduate studies in 1967. The college was down the street from the Clark School for the Deaf, a renowned “oral” school, which was focused on training deaf children to produce and understand spoken language. “But, behind the teachers’ backs, the children at the school used their hands to communicate with one another,” she says. “I wanted to study that spontaneously invented communication,” says Goldin-Meadow.

The gestures invented by deaf children are called “homesigns,” and studying them provided Goldin-Meadow the opportunity to investigate whether individuals who are not exposed to language could develop one. After graduating from Smith College, Goldin-Meadow went to the University of Pennsylvania in 1971 and worked on this question with now-deceased psychologist Lila Gleitman and another graduate student, Heidi Feldman.

Goldin-Meadow initially sought out children within the deaf community. However, she soon realized that her intended study group had to be composed of deaf children born to hearing parents who did not know sign language, given the challenges of acquiring spoken language while deaf. In addition, the children Goldin-Meadow sought were not exposed to sign language.

Goldin-Meadow and her colleagues started observing deaf children at local oral schools in the Philadelphia area and got permission to videotape the children interacting with their hearing parents and with the researchers at home. “We were interested in seeing whether the children would use their hands to communicate with the hearing speakers in their homes and, if so, whether the gestures they produced would resemble language,” says Goldin-Meadow. “We found that they did on both counts, and we called the children’s linguistic achievements the resilient properties of language—the properties of language that can be developed without a language model,” she says (2).

The homesigns invented by deaf children who did not know each other and had never interacted with each other had many structural similarities. The structural properties were also found in the speech of hearing children who were learning spoken language from their hearing parents. The resilient properties of language are so fundamental that they can be developed even by children who are not exposed to language. “So we started to compile and think about which linguistic properties can be developed without a language model,” says Goldin-Meadow. She continued this line of research at the University of Chicago, which she joined in 1976.

Homesigns and Language Emergence Across Cultures

At the University of Chicago, Goldin-Meadow expanded her research to other countries and cultures (3–5). “We started to look at deaf kids of hearing parents in Taiwan, Turkey, and Nicaragua, just to see how similar they were or weren’t,” she says. “We found many, many similarities, despite the fact that the gestures hearing speakers use in these cultures aren’t all the same,” says Goldin-Meadow.

Goldin-Meadow also found that the children’s homesigns were not modeled on gestures that their parents used when talking with them—the so-called “co-speech gestures.” “We found no evidence that the parents’ gestures were influencing the children’s homesigns. The children were coming up with their own linguistic structures,” she says (6).

Together with colleagues, Goldin-Meadow has also explored the emergence of sign language from homesigns in Nicaragua (5). “Forty years ago, people like the deaf kids I studied were brought together for the very first time in a school designed to teach them Spanish. They didn’t learn Spanish, but they did share their homesign systems, and a new sign language—Nicaraguan Sign Language—was born,” she says. “Over time, new deaf kids entered the school and changed the language. We now have the opportunity to compare Nicaraguan homesign to the later stages of Nicaraguan Sign Language and see how language changes when it is shared within a group and when it is transmitted to new learners,” explains Goldin-Meadow.

Goldin-Meadow’s work on homesigns sparked her interest in the spontaneous gestures that accompany speech, and one focus of her research became deciphering what the cospeech gestures reveal. “We have found that these gestures often convey ideas not found in the speech they accompany—gestures can tell us what speakers are thinking, even when the speakers don’t express those thoughts in their words,” she says. For example, a child might verbally say that one container is “tall” and another is “short” while, at the same time, indicating with their hands that the tall container is thin and the short container is wide—the difference in the containers’ widths compensates for the difference in their heights, which we learn about only by paying attention to the child’s words and hands (7).

“Gestures that reveal our thoughts are found in both speaking and signing cultures and are distinct from sign language,” Goldin-Meadow says. Gesture is even present in congenitally blind children and adults, who have never seen anyone gesture (8). “It speaks to the robustness of gesturing,” says Goldin-Meadow. “We’re not just waving our hands—we’re revealing our thoughts for all to see,” she says.

Resilient Property of Language

Goldin-Meadow has also studied language acquisition in hearing children. She is the principal investigator of the Language Development Project, a 20-year longitudinal study launched in 2002 to explore the language-learning process in 60 typically developing children and 40 children with prenatal or postnatal brain injury (9). “The unique thing about this sample is that it is the same kids videotaped interacting naturally with their parents at home from 14 to 58 months,” she says. “It is the perfect data to compare to my homesigners—language developed with language input from hearing parents, compared to language developed without this type of input,” says Goldin-Meadow.

Goldin-Meadow’s IA brings together the findings on homesigns and the longitudinal data on hearing children (1). Homesigns provide solid evidence that a determiner–noun combination can be invented by a child without linguistic input—the homesigners point at a cup and then produce an iconic gesture for cup (moving a C-shaped hand to the mouth). The children’s hearing parents do not combine pointing gestures with iconic gestures to specify an object when they talk to their deaf children (4). Data from the homesigners reveal that children can invent determiner–noun combinations.

In the IA, Goldin-Meadow and her colleagues examined what happens when children get input about determiner–noun phrases from the language their parents produce. The team looked at the onset of the determiner phrases and the trajectory followed by each child in the longitudinal sample of English learners. In addition to this behavioral analysis, the team computationally modeled the acquisition of determiner–noun phrases in the English learners.

The computer model provides a way to examine the development of determiner–noun phrases with known inputs. “Unlike children, who hear much more language than the parent speech we have in our sample, the computer gets only the input we give it. We know exactly what information the computer was fed,” she says. “So we can tell when the computer is going beyond the input it receives. To the extent that there are parallels between the developmental trajectories of child and computer, we have reason to attribute the productivity that we see in the computer to the child,” says Goldin-Meadow.

The team’s eventual goal is to try giving the computer model different amounts of linguistic input. “Now that we have this model, which does a good job of capturing what children’s development looks like then they get naturalistic input from their parents, we can play with how much input is actually needed—children may use linguistic input when it’s there, but they may not need it,” she says. “How little determiner–noun input does the model need to arrive at the developmental trajectories we see?”

Gestures and Thoughts

Goldin-Meadow has written several books about her research. Her latest book, “Thinking with your Hands: The Surprising Science Behind How Gestures Shape Our Thoughts,” describes some practical uses of gestures for parenting, medicine, and education. For example, she has found that children’s gestures can provide early hints at their readiness to learn language, math, or scientific concepts (7, 10). “It’s useful for teachers and parents to pay attention to a learner’s gestures not only to figure out what a learner knows but also to help them decide what kind of input the learner needs next,” says Goldin-Meadow. In addition, encouraging children to gesture during a lesson can make them more likely to profit from that lesson. Further, gesturing is useful for teachers and parents; the gestures capture the child’s attention and convey information in an accessible format. “Gestures are often easier to understand than the words they accompany,” she says.

Goldin-Meadow’s work has come a long way over the past 50 years, from recording kids on videotapes to using computer models. She recently has gone back to the old reel-to-reel tapes of the homesigners and is redigitizing them to gather additional information from those early studies. “Ruthe Foushee, who is an author on the Inaugural Article, and I are going to look carefully at parent–child interactions to determine if and how the parents support the development of communication in the homesigners. These analyses of hearing parent–deaf child interactions can then be situated within comparable analyses of hearing parent–hearing child interactions,” she says.

Looking back, Goldin-Meadow reflects on how her work has changed the study of gestures. “Gestures have become a more acceptable area of study both in linguistics and in psychology,” she says. “We move our hands all the time when we talk. It’s time to really look at what we are saying with them.”

Goldin-Meadow continues to embrace new technology to answer these questions and has recently explored functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), a noninvasive method that uses near-infrared light propagating through the scalp to image brain hemodynamics. “We want to see what’s going on in your head when you’re saying one thing and gesturing another. Speakers can move naturally when imaged with fNIRS. As a result, we can look at the brain when speakers’ spontaneous gestures reinforce what they’ve said in speech, compared to when their gestures reveal ideas not found in their speech,” she says. “That’s what’s on the horizon for me.”
