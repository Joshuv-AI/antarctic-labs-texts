On spooky action at a distance and conditional probabilities
Henryk Gzyl

Abstract
The aim of this exposé is to make explicit the analogy between the classical notion of non-independent probability distribution and the quantum notion of entangled state. To bring that analogy forth, we consider a classical systems with two dependent random variables and a quantum system with two components. In the classical case, afet observing one of the random variables, the underlying sample space and the probability distribution change. In the quantum case, when and event pertaining to one of the components is observed, the post-measurement state captures, both, the change in the state of the system and implicitly the new probability distribution. The predictions after a measurement in the classical case and in the quantum case, have to be computed with the conditional distribution given the value of the observed variable.



Keywords
: Action at a distance, prediction, conditional probabilities as predictors, Measurement and state change.

1
Introduction and preliminaries

About 90 years ago Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen published a paper,
[
1
]
in which they proposed a criticism of quantum mechanics as a model for the intrinsic randomness in microscopic phenomena. The core of their argument consists in considering a system with two components, in an initial state in which the variables used to characterize the state (in their case the momenta of the individual particles) were not statistically independent. In current terminology, the state was entangled.
They argued that when one of the momenta was measured, since the system was prepared in a state of known total momentum, the momentum of the other particle was known. They claimed that this contradicted Heisenberg uncertainty principle, for then one could measure the position of the second particle, and this measurement does not have infinite variance as required by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.

The terms “entangled” and “entanglement; had already appeared in Schrödinger’s
[
12
]
, mentioned in a way that suggests statistical non-independence, but was not precisely defined for today standards. By the way, it is in that same work that the famous cat example came up. Two references at an expository level are
[
14
]
or
[
13
]
. See as well
[
8
]
for detailed mathematical formulations of the notion of entanglement, especially in the Heisenberg representation.

About 10 years later, in further discussing with Max Born the notion of physical reality and the use of quantum mechanics to understand it, Einstein wrote:
I cannot seriously believe in it because the theory cannot be reconciled with the idea that physics should represent a reality in time and space, free from spooky action at a distance
,
[
2
]
. That phrase “spooky action at a distance” is the title of an expository paper about entanglement, see
[
4
]
, which inspired this work.

The aim of this work is to tend a bridge between quantum measurement theory and classical probabilistic prediction theory. We shall see that the underlying mathematical concept is the same in both cases, namely, that after an observation is made, new predictions are to be made with a conditional probability distribution.

In the quantum case, it is the state of the system that changes after a measurement, and further prediction should be made using this post-measurement state. In classical probability it is the sample space that changes, which makes the joint distribution become a conditional distribution.

This conceptual equivalence may be of interest in current applications of quantum mechanics, as in quantum computing say. There the role of measurement and prediction with a post measurement state plays an essential role. See
[
11
]
for example. For an application to understand why the EPR critique of quantum mechanics is not valid, consider
[
5
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In the remainder of this section we recall some basic notations along the lines of the didactical paper
[
4
]
. In section 2, we reconsider his example and prove that prediction in the post-measurement stated amounts to prediction with respect to quantum conditional probabilities as introduced in
[
3
]
, for example. Actually, in our setup, the technicalities described in
[
3

Then, in Section 3, we describe the exact analogue of the example considered in Section 2, but now in the classical probabilistic setup. After that, in Section 4 we add a few comments related to statistical independence.

The basic building block consists of a (complex) Hilbert space
H,
in which the scalar product between to vectors
\psi
and
\phi
is denoted by
\langle\psi,\phi\rangle,
and
|\langle\psi,\phi\rangle|^{2}
is interpreted as the probability of the system being in state
\psi
when it is in state
\phi.
The probabilistic interpretation requires states to be represented by normalized vectors, that is
\|\psi\|^{2}=|\langle\psi,\psi\rangle|^{2}=1.
Observables, (the analogue of classical random variables), are represented by self-adjoint (with respect to the given scalar product), bounded operators.
Their collection is denoted by
{\mathcal{L}}(H).

In the quantum mechanical formalism, events are represented by projection operators
{\boldsymbol{P}},
that is, self-adjoint operators such that
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{2}={\boldsymbol{P}},
and the probability of observing such event when the system is in stated
\psi
is given by

{\mathcal{P}}_{\psi}({\boldsymbol{P}})=\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle.

(1.1)

And, after the observation of an event
{\boldsymbol{P}}
is made, then the post-observation state of the system is

\psi^{\prime}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi}{\|{\boldsymbol{P}}\Psi\|}

(1.2)

Where,
\|{\boldsymbol{P}}\Psi\|=\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle^{1/2}.
To finish the list of preliminaries, when describing a composite system, whose quantum states are vectors in the Hilbert spaces
H_{1}
and
H_{2},
one forms the tensor product space
H=H_{1}\otimes H_{2},
in which the scalar product of two vectors
\psi_{1}\otimes\psi_{2}
and
\phi_{1}\otimes\phi_{2},
in Dirac’s bra-ket notation, is given by

\langle\psi_{1}\otimes\psi_{2},\phi_{1}\otimes\phi_{2}\rangle=\langle\psi_{1},\phi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{2},\phi_{2}\rangle.

If
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}
is an observable of the first subsystem, to be proper when thinking of it as an observable of the joint system we should write it as
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{2},
where
{\mathbb{I}}_{2}
is the identity operator in
H_{2}.
Such tensor product operator acts on the product state
\psi\otimes\phi
as follows:

({\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{2})\psi\otimes\phi={\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}\psi\otimes\phi.

A similar comment applies for operators acting on the second subsystem. After this preamble, we move onto the next topic.

2
Prediction after a measurement and conditional probabilities

Here we provide a small variation on the basis free example in Section 2 of
[
4
]
.
As initial, entangled state we consider
\eta=a\psi\otimes\phi+b\phi\otimes\psi,
where

0<|a|^{2},|b|^{2}<1
and
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1,
and we suppose that
\langle\psi,\phi\rangle=0.
Consider events represented by projectors
{\boldsymbol{P}},

{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}={\mathbb{I}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{P}}
on
H_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}={\mathbb{I}}_{2}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}
on
H_{2}.
Let us now compute the joint probability distribution of these events.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})=\langle\eta,{\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta\rangle=\;\;

\displaystyle|a|^{2}\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\phi\rangle+\bar{a}b\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\psi\rangle+a\bar{b}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\psi\rangle+|b|^{2}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\phi\rangle\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\psi\rangle..\;\;\;

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c})=\langle\eta,{\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\eta\rangle=\;\;

\displaystyle|a|^{2}\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\phi\rangle+\bar{a}b\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\psi\rangle+a\bar{b}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\psi\rangle+|b|^{2}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\phi\rangle\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\psi\rangle.\;\;\;

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}^{c},{\boldsymbol{Q}})=\langle\eta,{\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta\rangle=\;\;

\displaystyle|a|^{2}\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\phi\rangle+\bar{a}b\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\psi\rangle+a\bar{b}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\psi\rangle+|b|^{2}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\phi\rangle\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\psi\rangle.\;\;\;

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}^{c},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c})=\langle\eta,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\eta\rangle=\;\;

\displaystyle|a|^{2}\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\phi\rangle+\bar{a}b\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\psi\rangle+a\bar{b}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\psi\rangle+|b|^{2}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\phi\rangle\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\psi\rangle.\;\;\;

(2.4)

Notice that these can be thought of as the diagonal elements of the density matrix

|\eta\rangle\langle\eta|
in the basis
{\mathcal{P}}\psi\otimes{\boldsymbol{Q}}\phi,{\mathcal{P}}^{c}\psi\otimes{\boldsymbol{Q}}\phi,{\mathcal{P}}\psi\otimes{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\phi,{\mathcal{P}}^{c}\psi\otimes{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}\phi
on which the projections project the state vector
\eta.

Now, if we use the assumption that
\langle\psi,\phi\rangle=0,
a similar but simpler computation yields that:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}})=\langle\eta,{\boldsymbol{P}}\eta\rangle=|a|^{2}\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle+|b|^{2}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\phi\rangle\;\;

(2.5)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{Q}})=\langle\eta,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta\rangle=|a|^{2}\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\phi\rangle+|b|^{2}\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\psi\rangle\;

(2.6)

And, furthermore, notice that the following consistency relationship hold:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}})={\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c})+{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c})\;\;\;

(2.7)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{Q}})={\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})+{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}^{c},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\;\;\;

(2.8)

Skimming a number of technicalities (explained in
[
GU1
]
), since in our case the operators
{\boldsymbol{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
commute because they operate on different components of the product space, the notion of classical and quantum conditional probabilities coincide and we have, for example, that the conditional probability of
{\boldsymbol{P}}
(or of
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}
) given that
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
occurs, is:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}|{\boldsymbol{Q}})=\frac{{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{Q}})}{{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{Q}})}\;\;

(2.9)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}|{\boldsymbol{Q}})=\frac{{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}{\boldsymbol{Q}})}{{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{Q}})}\;\;

(2.10)

On account of (
2.7
), clearly
{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}|{\boldsymbol{Q}})+{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}|{\boldsymbol{Q}})=1.
That is, the conditional probability distribution is a true probability distribution.

Next, we show that (
2.9
) or (
2.10
) are the probabilities of the events
{\boldsymbol{P}}
or
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}
in the state
\eta_{Q}
obtained from
\eta
according to (
1.2
) if the event
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
is observed. To begin with, note that
\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta\|
is given by (
2.8
), and that

\eta_{Q}=\frac{{\mathbb{I}}_{1}\otimes{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta}{\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta\|}=\frac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta\|}\big(a\psi\otimes{\boldsymbol{Q}}\phi+b\phi\otimes{\boldsymbol{Q}}\psi).

(2.11)

We leave it for the reader to do the arithmetic and verify that

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{Q},{\boldsymbol{P}}\eta_{Q}\rangle={\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}|{\boldsymbol{Q}})\;\;\;

(2.12)

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{Q},{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\eta_{Q}\rangle={\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}|{\boldsymbol{Q}})\;\;\;

(2.13)

That is, the probability of the event
{\boldsymbol{P}}
in the state
\eta_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}
is the (quantum) conditional probability of
{\boldsymbol{P}}
given
{\boldsymbol{Q}}.

3
The classical case

Let us consider a very simple case. Two binary random variables taking values in

\{0,1\}.
The blocks of the probabilistic model are: the sample space
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{2},

the possible events are all subsets of
\Omega
and a probability distribution
{\mathcal{P}}(i,j)
from which the probability of any event
A
can be obtained as
{\mathcal{P}}(A)=\sum_{(i,j)\in A}{\mathcal{P}}(i,j).

Random variables are real valued functions defined on
\Omega.
we are interested in

X
and
Y
defined by
X(i,j)=i
and
Y(i,j)=j.
Think of them as coordinate maps if you prefer. From the probability
{\mathcal{P}}
on the events, one obtains, for example,

{\mathcal{P}}\big((X,Y)\in A\big)=\sum_{(i,j)\in A}{\mathcal{P}}(i,j)={\mathcal{P}}(A).

(3.1)

Observe that, for example, since
\{X=i\}=\{(X,Y)\in\{i\}\times\{0,1\}\},
then
{\mathcal{P}}(\{X=i\})={\mathcal{P}}(i,0)+{\mathcal{P}}(i,1).
All of this can be summarized in Table
1
:

X\backslash Y

0

1

Row sum

0

{\mathcal{P}}(0,0)

{\mathcal{P}}(0,1)

{\mathcal{P}}(X=0)

1

{\mathcal{P}}(1,0)

{\mathcal{P}}(1,1)

{\mathcal{P}}(X=1)

Column sum

{\mathcal{P}}(Y=0)

{\mathcal{P}}(Y=1)

{\mathcal{P}}(\Omega)=1

Table 1:
Joint probability distribution

The rightmost column and the last rows contain the predictors of the observations of one of the variables. The focus should be in the reduction of the sample space. If we observe, say, that
\{Y=1\}
has occurred, then the only events compatible with this observation are
\{(0,1),(1,1)\}.
This reduction of the sample space is the analogue of applying a projection operator to the initial state of the system, which reduced the state space to a subspace of the original Hilbert space. It is not the result of some action at a distance of some agent acting on the system. There is no reason to be spoked about that.

To continue, once we know that
\{Y=1\}
has occurred, the sample space for the next possible predictions changes, and the probabilities have to be recalculated. In terms of the available microscopic configurations, it goes as follows: Even though the number of microscopic configurations in each cell stays the same, since our sample space is smaller (it consists of the configurations compatible with
\{Y=1\}
), it is the total number of available microscopic configurations that has changed because the sample space consists of the cells in the given column. Therefore, the probabilities in the cells have to be readjusted to:

{\mathcal{P}}(i|1)=\frac{{\mathcal{P}}(i,1)}{{\mathcal{P}}(\{Y=1\})},\;\;\;i=0,1.

(3.2)

That is, the prediction of the frequency of occurrence of any event related to the observation of
X
has changed. These are the classical analogue of (
2.9
)-(
2.10
).

4
Final remarks: On entanglement and dependence

Had we started with an initial state like
\eta=\psi\otimes\phi,
we would have obtained that

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{Q},{\boldsymbol{P}}\eta_{Q}\rangle={\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}})\;\;\;\;

(4.1)

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{Q},{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}\eta_{Q}\rangle={\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}^{c})\;\;\;

(4.2)

which we interpret as saying that the observation of
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
does not affect the probabilities of occurrence (or non-occurrence) of the event
{\boldsymbol{P}}.
Also

{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{Q}})={\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}}){\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{Q}})=\langle\psi,{\boldsymbol{P}}\psi\rangle\langle\phi,{\boldsymbol{Q}}\phi\rangle.

(4.3)

That is, the probability of the event
{\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}
is the product of the probabilities of observing the two events. A similar expression is valid replacing either
{\boldsymbol{P}}
by
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{c}
or
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
by
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{c}.
Observe that the right hand side of (
4.3
) is independent of the ordering of
{\boldsymbol{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}},
and in our setup, so is the right hand side since
{\boldsymbol{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
commute because they act on different factors of the product space.

A restricted commutativity of
{\boldsymbol{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
is obtained if we require that, first measuring
{\boldsymbol{P}}
and predicting
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
is the same as first measuring
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
and then predicting
{\boldsymbol{P}}.
To make it explicit, let
\eta
be any state, entangled or not, of the composite system. Let
{\boldsymbol{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
be to events and let

\eta_{\boldsymbol{Q}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta}{\sqrt{{\mathcal{P}}({\boldsymbol{Q}})}}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\eta_{\boldsymbol{P}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{P}}\eta}{\sqrt{{\mathcal{P}}({\boldsymbol{P}})}}

be, respectively, the post-measurement states after observing the events
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
and
{\boldsymbol{P}}
respectively. Suppose now that we require that:

\langle\eta_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}},{\boldsymbol{P}}\eta_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}\rangle=\langle\eta_{{\boldsymbol{P}}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta_{{\boldsymbol{P}}}\rangle.

(4.4)

Then we have:

\frac{\langle\eta,{\boldsymbol{Q}}{\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}\eta\rangle}{{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{Q}})}=\frac{\langle\eta,{\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}{\boldsymbol{P}}\eta\rangle}{{\mathcal{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{P}})}.

(4.5)

This reduces to (
4.3
) when
{\boldsymbol{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
commute.

The exact analogue of this for the classical case corresponds to assuming that

{\mathcal{P}}(\{X,Y)=(i,j)\})={\mathcal{P}}(\{X=i\}){\mathcal{P}}(\{Y=j\}),\;\;\;\mbox{for any}\;\;\;i,j=0,1.

(4.6)

In this case one says that
X
and
Y
are independent, and as above, observing one variable, does not change the predictions about the other.

We end up stressing a conceptual issue: Statistical independence implies zero correlation, but the converse may not be true. That is, two random variables might be uncorrelated, but dependent. This is a homework for students of basic probability. A simple quantum analogue may go as follows. Consider some observable
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H)
of a quantum system with Hilbert space
H,
written as
{\boldsymbol{A}}=\sum_{j}a_{j}|a_{j}\rangle.
The
|a_{j}\rangle
are the eigenvectors of
{\boldsymbol{A}}.
For any state
\psi,
we put
p_{\psi}(j)=|\langle a_{j}|\psi\rangle|^{2}
for the probability that
{\boldsymbol{A}}
has a value
a_{j}
in
\psi.
Let now
f({\boldsymbol{A}})=\sum_{j}f(a_{j})|a_{j}\rangle\langle a_{j}|,
and suppose that
\psi
is such that

\sum_{j}a_{j}p_{\psi}(j)=0;\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\sum_{j}a_{j}f(a_{j})p_{\psi}(j)=0.

Then,

\langle\psi\big(f({\boldsymbol{A}})-\langle f(A)\rangle_{\psi}\big)\big({\boldsymbol{A}}-\langle A\rangle_{\psi}\big)\psi\rangle=0.

WE uses the obvious shorthand
\langle f(A)\rangle_{\psi}=\langle\psi|f(A)|\psi\rangle
and
\langle A\rangle_{\psi}=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle.
This happens, for example, if
{\boldsymbol{A}}
has eigenvalues of opposite sign occurring with the same probability. Then, the observables
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
f({\boldsymbol{A}})
are uncorrelated, but they are clearly not statistically independent.

Our setup can be rapidly expended to the systems with a finite number of levels, in which the Hilbert spaces are finite dimensional and observables correspond to Hermitian matrices. The technicalities for the general case are elaborate, but the conceptual analysis holds.

The results presented above can be restated in the Heisenberg representation. Instead of working with vectors in a Hilbert space, one would consider density matrices. In this domain there is a larger collection of states between the pure states and the entangled states. See
[
8
]
for a tour of that zoo. But the final correspondence between classical prediction using conditional probabilities and the quantum prediction using the post-measurement state is the same.
