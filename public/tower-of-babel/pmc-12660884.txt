Integrated micro and nano-silica in advanced composite high-strength mortar for TRM system
Authors: Manisha Shewale, R Siva Chidambaram, Gobinath Ravindran, Bhagyashree Khartode, Nitin Shinde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents the development of a high-strength composite mortar for Textile Reinforced Mortar (TRM) systems through the synergistic integration of micro-silica, nano-silica, silica sand, and hybrid fibers. The combination of steel and basalt fibers significantly enhanced the mechanical and bonding performance of the mortar. The optimized mix achieved compressive, split tensile, and flexural strengths of 70.335 MPa, 8.03 MPa, and 19.63 MPa, respectively, while demonstrating improved bonding behavior with an 80.5% increase in splitting bond strength, direct pull-off bond strength of 8.5 MPa, and pull-out bond strength of 7.0 MPa. These improvements are attributed to the densified microstructure and refined interfacial transition zone resulting from the pozzolanic reactions of micro- and nano-silica. The findings confirm that the combined use of multi-scale silica materials and hybrid fibers produces superior mechanical strength, durability, and adhesion, making the developed mortar a promising solution for retrofitting and strengthening applications in modern construction.Keywords: Textile reinforced mortar, TRM systems, Inorganic mortar, Supplementary cementitious materials, Compatibility, Splitting tensile bond strength, Slant shear test, Direct pull-off bond strength, Pull-out strengthSubject terms: Civil engineering, Materials science

Introduction

There is an increasing emphasis on ensuring the sustainability of existing infrastructure. Rehabilitating and strengthening deteriorated or underperforming reinforced concrete (RC) structures is essential to meet current design standards and extend service life1. Fiber-reinforced polymers (FRPs) have been widely used as external reinforcement for deficient structures2,3; however, their dependence on epoxy resins leads to several limitations—high costs, poor performance at elevated temperatures, incompatibility with certain substrates, and poor adhesion to damp surfaces4–6.

To overcome these issues, researchers have proposed replacing the organic epoxy matrix with an inorganic cementitious mortar, giving rise to textile-reinforced mortar (TRM) and fabric-reinforced cementitious matrix (FRCM) systems5,6. These systems incorporate high-strength fiber meshes within inorganic binders, offering enhanced bond conditions, superior fire resistance, and compatibility with both concrete and masonry substrates7–10. Triantafillou10 and Bournas11 demonstrated the successful application of TRM in strengthening and retrofitting concrete and masonry structures, while recent studies have expanded its use in prefabricated elements and heritage rehabilitation12–14.

TRM systems are valued for their cost-effectiveness, environmental compatibility, and superior thermal stability—an advantage highlighted in recent studies on hybrid fiber-reinforced magnesium oxychloride cement-based composites, which demonstrated notable improvements in fire resistance and thermal performance15. Similarly, research on seismic safety assessments emphasizes the urgent need for resilient, sustainable materials that can enhance structural safety in earthquake-prone regions16. These studies underscore the broader importance of developing multifunctional materials capable of withstanding both mechanical and environmental stresses.

Recent advancements in high-performance cement-based composites, such as textile-reinforced concretes (TRCs) and FRCMs, have shown significant improvements in load-bearing and deformation capacities17–20. These composites typically employ high-strength mortars integrated with textile sheets composed of woven or knitted fibers impregnated with cementitious matrices. The inorganic matrix—composed of hydraulic binders and fine aggregates—can also be modified with supplementary materials such as micro-silica and nano-silica to improve densification and interfacial bonding. Incorporating multiple fiber types and silica additives has been shown to enhance strength, ductility, and crack resistance in cementitious systems21.

In this study, we investigate a high-strength mortar incorporating a unique combination of various fiber types, micro-silica, and nano-silica to improve mechanical properties such as compressive, split tensile, and flexural strength. Unlike prior research, which has primarily focused on single-fiber systems or isolated silica modification, this study adopts a multi-scale and multi-fiber approach to synergistically enhance performance. The integration of micro- and nano-silica further refines the mortar’s microstructure, offering improved bond strength and reduced porosity.

The novelty of this research lies in its comprehensive evaluation of hybrid fiber–silica-modified high-strength mortars, aiming to develop next-generation construction materials that exhibit superior mechanical performance, durability, and adaptability. The findings contribute to advancing sustainable and resilient construction materials for modern infrastructure rehabilitation.

Assessing the compatibility between substrate concrete and repair mortar requires evaluating their bond. Several testing methods can be utilized for this assessment, including slant shear bond strength, flexural bond strength, direct pull-off bond strength, and bond splitting tensile strength. For the purposes of this research, we opted to employ three specific techniques: slant shear, splitting tensile, and direct pull-off tests.

Fourteen different proportions of high-strength mortar underwent mechanical testing at curing intervals of 3, 14, and 28 days. A total of 420 specimens were cast and tested, with 27 specimens per proportion for mechanical properties evaluation and 3 additional specimens per proportion for compatibility assessment.

Substrate concrete cube specimens measuring 100 mm and cylinders of 100 mm diameter and 200 mm height were prepared. After curing for 28 days, cube specimens were split for the tensile strength test. The failed surface of substrate concrete was cleaned, and the repaired area was remolded. Fresh repair mortar was then poured into the molds.

Splitting tensile bond tests were conducted using cube and cylindrical specimens to comprehensively assess the mortar-substrate interface. These tests allow evaluation of bond strength in different orientations and conditions, providing insights into repair mortar performance and compatibility with substrate concrete. This approach contributes valuable information to the overall evaluation of repair system effectiveness and durability.

In the splitting tensile bond test, the specimens were subjected to axial force that was applied in a direction that was parallel to the bond interface. Results and discussions

The mechanical properties of different mortar samples were examined at three different curing periods: 3 days, 7 days, and 28 days.

Compressive strength

Figure 9 displays the compressive strength measurements for various mortar samples at 3, 7, and 28 days. The substantial variation in compressive strength across samples highlights the impact of different mix ratios and additives on mortar performance. As expected for cement-based materials, the compressive strength generally increases with time, showing higher values at 7 days than at 3 days, and peaking at 28 days. Notably, samples 5 and 6 demonstrate high early-age strength, reaching 15 MPa and 17.8 MPa respectively at 3 and 7 days. This suggests their composition promotes rapid strength development, which is beneficial for projects requiring quick strength gain. At 28 days, samples 11 through 14 exhibit exceptionally high compressive strength compared to other samples, indicating superior long-term strength properties that could be advantageous in high-load structural applications. The observed variations in compressive strength among samples may be attributed to the presence of additives such as micro silica, SBR latex, plasticizer, steel fibers, glass fibers, basalt fibers, and nano silica. These additives can influence hydration kinetics, microstructure formation, and interfacial bonding within the mortar matrix, ultimately affecting its mechanical characteristics. Samples 7 to 10 show consistent compressive strength across different age intervals, suggesting stable and predictable performance over time. The highest strength of 72.99 MPa is observed in sample 11. Such consistency could be desirable in applications where uniform strength development is crucial.

The superior performance of Sample 12 can also be attributed to the synergistic behavior of hybrid fibers, particularly the combination of steel and basalt fibers. Basalt fibers, with their high tensile strength and thermal stability, bridge microcracks and improve the matrix’s crack-arresting ability. Mortars intended for use with textile sheets incorporate additives or fibers to boost flexural and tensile strength. This is essential for ensuring the necessary structural integrity, particularly in scenarios where the material may experience bending or tensile forces. The mortar’s bonding properties are improved by incorporating fibers, such as steel and basalt, into the mixture. Bonding agents and fibers collaborate to create a network within the mortar, enhancing its adherence to both the textile sheet and substrate material. To achieve optimal adhesion, proper surface preparation of the substrate material and textile sheet is crucial. Techniques such as priming, cleaning, or roughening the surfaces can enhance the connection by providing the mortar with a suitable substrate for attachment.

Microstructural analysis (SEM and XRD)

Although the present study primarily focuses on the mechanical and bond performance of the developed mortars, the underlying mechanisms responsible for these improvements can be more clearly understood through microstructural characterization.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) enables visualization of the mortar’s internal structure, including the fiber–matrix interface and pore distribution. The incorporation of micro-silica and nano-silica leads to a denser and more homogeneous matrix by filling microvoids and refining the interfacial transition zone (ITZ). SEM images typically reveal reduced porosity and enhanced bonding around fibers, corroborating the observed increase in compressive and flexural strength.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis can further confirm the formation of additional calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel due to the pozzolanic reactions of micro- and nano-silica. Peaks corresponding to portlandite (Ca(OH)₂) decrease, while those associated with C–S–H intensify, indicating improved hydration and matrix densification.

Future work will include detailed SEM and XRD characterization to establish quantitative relationships between silica-based microstructural refinement and mechanical performance.

The present findings align with previous research on nano-silica and hybrid fiber integration in cementitious systems, which report compressive strength improvements of 15–30% compared to control mixes due to enhanced pozzolanic reactivity and reduced microcracking25,27. Similar observations were made in studies on macro-synthetic fiber–reinforced concrete containing e-waste aggregates, where fiber addition improved tensile and flexural capacities while reducing brittleness31,32. Moreover, the micro-silica–nano-silica synergy observed in this study supports the conclusions given by Ahmad et al., 202532, which emphasize the benefits of multi-scale particle systems in achieving dense and durable matrices. These correlations validate the mechanical improvements achieved in the present study and demonstrate the material’s potential for advanced repair and retrofitting applications.
