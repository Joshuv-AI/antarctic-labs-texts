Latent Space Inference via Paired Autoencoders
Emma Hart, Bas Peters, Julianne Chung, Matthias Chung

Abstract
This work describes a novel data-driven latent space inference framework built on paired autoencoders to handle observational inconsistencies when solving inverse problems. Our approach uses two autoencoders, one for the parameter space and one for the observation space, connected by learned mappings between the autoencoders’ latent spaces. These mappings enable a surrogate for regularized inversion and optimization in low-dimensional, informative latent spaces. Our flexible framework can work with partial, noisy, or out-of-distribution data, all while maintaining consistency with the underlying physical models. The paired autoencoders enable reconstruction of corrupted data, and then use the reconstructed data for parameter estimation, which produces more accurate reconstructions compared to paired autoencoders alone and end-to-end encoder-decoders of the same architecture, especially in scenarios with data inconsistencies. We demonstrate our approaches on two imaging examples in medical tomography and geophysical seismic-waveform inversion, but the described approaches are broadly applicable to a variety of inverse problems in scientific and engineering applications.

Introduction and Background

Many scientific and engineering endeavors involve understanding the properties of a system through indirect measurements. This process often leads to what are known as
inverse problems
. Unlike direct or forward problems, where causes are known and effects are predicted, inverse problems aim to infer causes from observed effects
[
31
,
15
]
. Inference depends, next to others, on the experimental setup of how data are obtained.

Inverse Problems.
In mathematical terms, let us consider a system described by a mathematical operator
A\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
that maps model parameters (or controls)
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
to observations
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
potentially contaminated with some additive unbiased noise
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\epsilon}$}\in\mathcal{Y}
. The forward operator
A=Q\circ F
typically consists of solutions
F\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Q}
to some governing equations described by a partial or ordinary differential equation (PDE/ODE), which is assumed to be fully determined by its model parameters
\mathbf{x}
, and a mapping
Q\colon\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Y}
onto the observation space
\mathcal{Y}
, i.e.,

A(\mathbf{x})+\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\epsilon}$}=\mathbf{y}.

(1)

Given the forward operator
A
, the observations
\mathbf{y}
, and some assumptions about the noise, the inverse problem aims to reconstruct the unknown model parameters
\mathbf{x}
, often termed as
quantities of interest (QoI)

[
24
]
.

Inverse problems are numerous and arise in a variety of applications. For instance, in medical imaging, they are used to reconstruct images of internal organs from measurements obtained through techniques like X-ray computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
[
6
,
16
]
. In geophysics applications, inverse methods are employed to determine the Earth’s subsurface structure by analyzing seismic data
[
50
,
21
]
. In non-destructive testing, these techniques are crucial for identifying defects within materials
[
45
]
. Remote sensing relies heavily on inverse problem-solving to retrieve atmospheric and surface properties from satellite observations
[
14
,
43
]
. More broadly in image processing, tasks like inpainting – the reconstruction of missing or damaged image regions – require inferring missing pixel values based on the surrounding image content
[
19
]
.

Challenges.

A key challenge in inverse problems is their ill-posedness, meaning no model may perfectly explain the data, multiple models may fit the data equally well, or small data perturbations may cause large changes in the estimated model
[
22
]
.
Consequently, specialized techniques are essential. These include regularization, which introduces constraints or prior information to stabilize the solution; optimization, which frames the problem as finding the best-fitting model; and statistical methods, which incorporate statistical models of data and unknowns to quantify uncertainty
[
8
,
28
]
. The study of inverse problems is vibrant and interdisciplinary, drawing upon mathematics, physics, statistics, and computer science. It plays a crucial role in advancing our understanding of complex systems and enabling new technologies across various domains.

Data-Driven Approaches.
The emergence of data-driven approaches, particularly with the rise of deep neural networks, has significantly impacted the field of inverse problems
[
3
,
1
]
. Traditionally, solving inverse problems relied heavily on analytical methods, numerical simulations, and optimization algorithms. These approaches often require significant computational resources and may struggle with complex or high-dimensional problems. Deep learning offers a powerful alternative by learning the mapping between measurements
\mathbf{y}
and model parameters
\mathbf{x}
directly from data. By training a neural network on a large dataset of paired input-output examples (e.g., measurements and corresponding ground truth models), it can learn to approximate the inverse mapping, i.e., a
\mathbf{\theta}
-parameterized neural network
\Phi_{\theta}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
, effectively bypassing the need for explicit inversion algorithms in some cases. Methods that learn a map this way are also referred to as
end-to-end inference
approaches. These approaches have shown promising results in various applications, including image reconstruction, medical imaging, and seismic inversion, offering faster and potentially more accurate solutions compared to traditional methods
[
32
,
38
,
39
,
18
,
10
,
2
]
. However, challenges remain, such as the need for large training datasets, ensuring generalization to unseen, partial, or corrupted data, and interpreting the learned representations within neural networks.

Partial Observations of Data.

A key drawback of using neural networks to learn end-to-end models is their problem-specific nature, necessitating re-training for any modification to the problem setup.
Standard machine learning methods often falter in reconstructing
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}=P(\mathbf{y})
, where
P:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\rm sub}
is an operation that introduces errors in
\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}
(
\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}
is out-of-distribution of the training data) or data gaps in
\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}\notin\mathcal{Y}
.
Depending on network architecture and training, previously trained models on full data
\mathbf{y}
become intractable or ineffective, due to the model’s performance being highly sensitive to the distribution and characteristics of the training data. When presented with discrepant or incomplete inputs
\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}
, the model’s assumptions are violated, rendering its predictions unreliable. Consequently, one is forced to resort to post hoc methods, such as data imputation or error correction, which can be computationally expensive and may introduce further inaccuracies. Consider computed tomography: if a data-driven model is trained on sinograms acquired at specific angles, it is likely to perform poorly if applied to data from a patient measured at slightly different angles or with a different number of projections (resulting in a change in the dimensions of the measurement vector
\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}
)
[
42
,
2
]
. In such instances, the direct utilization of pre-trained models proves to be unreliable or even intractable. Analogous difficulties are encountered in the geophysical domain.

Purpose and Contributions.

In this paper, we aim to advance methods using autoencoders to solve inverse problems, extending the scope of their use to include a broader range of problems that arise in application. We propose a novel latent space inference approach where missing data or other observational inconsistencies can be addressed via optimization in the latent space defined by paired autoencoders. This framework can be viewed as a latent space embedding combined with a data inference technique. Our approach offers an alternative to post hoc interpolation methods for generalization of results. We illustrate various challenges stemming from inconsistent discretizations, incompleteness, or inaccuracies in input data. While more generally applicable, we focus our study on cases where experimental setups are inconsistent, such as varying or missing angles in computer tomography, or missing observations due to defective sensors in seismic tomography. Examples are included to demonstrate the flexibility of our approach compared to previous autoencoder methods.

Structure.
This work is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we provide an overview of variational inverse problems and end-to-end inference. We review encoder-decoder and autoencoder networks and use them to describe paired autoencoder frameworks. In
Section

3
, we present our novel approach, which incorporates paired autoencoders and inference in the latent space, and we include theoretical results.
Section

4
presents numerical experiments that demonstrate the advantages of our approach through comparisons with other methods highlighted for limited-angle or missing-angle tomography and sparse data acquisition for seismic imaging. Finally,
Section

5
offers concluding remarks and outlines potential avenues for future research.

Background

Notation.
In general, bolded capital letters are used to denote matrices (e.g.,
\mathbf{D}
,
\mathbf{E}
) and bold lowercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
) are used for vectors, while un-bolded letters are used for functions, operators, and scalars. Superscripts denote the kind of network or sample (e.g., “e” for encoder, “d” for decoder, “train” for training data) while subscripts denote what kind of data the object corresponds to (i.e.,
\mathbf{z}_{x}
is a vector in the latent space of an autoencoder trained on
\mathbf{x}
samples). For readability, all subscripts are kept unbolded. In general, we use
\mathbf{x}_{\rm pred}
to denote the parameters (QoI) predicted by any method, and
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
specifically to denote the parameters predicted by our proposed method,
latent space inference
.

Variational Inverse Problems.
For an inverse problem
Equation

1
, we assume there exists a mapping
\Psi\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
such that
\Psi(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}
. This is the inverse operator, which is often approximated by some classical inversion techniques, denoted
A^{\dagger}(\mathbf{y})\approx\mathbf{x}
. An approximate inverse operator
A^{\dagger}
is typically difficult and computationally challenging to approximate. Inversion depends on the discretization and the challenging task of properly including prior knowledge of
\mathbf{x}
. Hence, variational inverse problems are formulated as optimization problems of the form

\min_{x}\ J(A(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y})+R(\mathbf{x}),

(2)

where
J\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is a data discrepancy loss while
R\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is an appropriate regularization functional encoding prior knowledge on
\mathbf{x}
. Letting
\mathbf{x}_{\rm pred}
be a minimizer of
Equation

2
, then this problem formulation aims at approximating
\mathbf{x}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{\rm pred}\approx\Psi(\mathbf{y})
.

End-to-End Inference.
As discussed above, in an end-to-end inference approach, we aim to learn a mapping between two spaces by utilizing a parameterized function, typically a neural network
\Phi_{\theta}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
. The network’s parameters
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are trained to minimize the discrepancy between the network’s output
\mathbf{x}_{\rm pred}=\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})
and the true
\mathbf{x}=\Psi(\mathbf{y})
. This approach is theoretically supported by Universal Approximation Theorems
[
29
,
13
,
35
]
, which suggest that neural networks can approximate any continuous mapping under suitable conditions. The availability of corresponding samples from both the input and target distributions enables the network to learn the mapping. It has been shown that these data-driven methods offer flexibility in capturing complex mappings, benefit from direct data utilization, and allow for end-to-end learning of the entire mapping function. These end-to-end inference approaches encompass a diverse range of architectures, each tailored to specific tasks, including feedforward networks, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), and Residual Networks (ResNets), among many others.

Encoder-Decoder Networks.
Encoder-decoder networks are a general architectural framework designed to model mappings between input and output spaces through a two-stage process. The encoder
e_{\theta^{e}}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
maps high-dimensional input data to a lower-dimensional latent representation, and the decoder network
d_{\theta^{d}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{X}
generates an output in the target space. These networks are parameterized by weights
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}^{e}
and
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}^{d}
, which are optimized to minimize a task-specific loss, such as prediction error. The encoder-decoder structure provides a flexible framework for learning complex, nonlinear relationships between spaces of differing dimensions, and has been widely adopted in areas including image-to-image translation and inverse problems. Unlike other end-to-end models that learn a direct mapping in a single step, the structured bottleneck of an encoder-decoder can enable more interpretability and regularization.

Autoencoders.
Autoencoders represent one type of encoder-decoder that is designed to learn efficient representations of data through a self-supervised learning process. They are made of an
encoder
network
e_{\theta^{e}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
that maps high-dimensional input data to a lower-dimensional
latent space
, and a
decoder
network
d_{\theta^{d}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{X}
that reconstructs the original input from the latent representation, with
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, typically
\ell\ll n
. The parameters
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}^{e}
and
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}^{d}
are jointly optimized to minimize a reconstruction loss, typically measuring the discrepancy between input
\mathbf{x}
and reconstruction
d_{\theta^{d}}(e_{\theta^{e}}(\mathbf{x}))
. This framework exploits the natural structure of inputs to create a data-specific method of compression that aims to retain the most important input features.

PAIR: Paired Autoencoders for Inference and Regularization.

PAIR is a data-driven approach for solving inverse problems, where two autoencoders are used to efficiently represent the input and target spaces separately, and optimal mappings are learned between latent spaces. In the PAIR framework, we have autoencoders

a_{x}=d_{x}\circ e_{x}\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_{y}=d_{y}\circ e_{y}

where, for better readability, we omit trainable parameters, and subscripts denote what the autoencoder is trained with respect to. As above,
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the parameter (or control) space, while
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
is the observation space. The encoders
e_{x}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
e_{y}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}
compress inputs
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{x}}
and
\mathbf{y}
to
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{y}}
respectively, with
\ell_{x}\ll n
and
\ell_{y}\ll q
, while the decoders
d_{x}:\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{X}
and
d_{y}:\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathcal{Y}
reconstruct the original inputs from their latent representations.
By constructing an individual autoencoder for each data instance, we aim to ensure that the key properties of both the data,
\mathbf{y}
, and the model,
\mathbf{x}
, are encoded, preserving their distinct characteristics. Additionally, let us assume we have latent space mappings,

m^{\to}\colon\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad m^{\leftarrow}\colon\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}},

(3)

connecting the latent spaces of both autoencoders in each direction.
We train
a_{x}
,
a_{y}
,
m^{\to}
, and
m^{\leftarrow}
with corresponding
\{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\}
samples to minimize the combined loss

\sum_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}}\|a_{x}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\|a_{y}(\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\|(d_{x}\circ m^{\leftarrow}\circ e_{y})(\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\|(d_{y}\circ m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}

(4)

We now call
a_{x}
and
a_{y}

paired autoencoders
, since the latent mappings enable meaningful inference across the parameter and observation spaces. For example, a data-driven surrogate mapping of
\Phi
can be established with

d_{x}\circ m^{\leftarrow}\circ e_{y}\approx\Phi.

(5)

These paired autoencoders offer several advantages, including ease of training, high-quality inversion results, computational efficiency, and effective out-of-distribution detection.
The PAIR approach enables forward and inverse surrogate mappings and has been considered for likelihood-free estimation in inverse problems
[
10
,
12
,
11
]
.
Theoretical connections to Bayes risk and empirical Bayes risk minimization were investigated in
[
25
]
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to consider the use of PAIR for handling problems with observational inconsistencies such as missing or corrupted data.

3
Latent Space Inference using PAIR

In this section, we describe a new approach for inference that exploits a paired autoencoder framework, coupled with inversion in the latent space, to effectively address inconsistencies in the data. Similar to likelihood-free inference techniques, our approach does not require knowledge of the forward model. However, the added benefit is that optimization in the latent space enables improved reconstructions and a framework for handling data inconsistencies. Moreover, the general formulation provides a versatile and broadly applicable solution across diverse problem domains.

The crux of the approach lies in the observation that latent spaces of trained autoencoders can offer useful, lower-dimensional spaces for optimization and possible regularization.
If we utilize a paired autoencoder framework, latent space inference (LSI) can be stated as

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{x}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{z}_{x}}\left\|(d_{y}\circ m^{\to})(\mathbf{z}_{x})-\mathbf{y}\right\|\qquad\text{with}\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=d_{x}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{x}).

(6)

Rather than optimize in the parameter latent space, we could also (or alternatively) optimize in the observation latent space, following

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{y}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{z}_{y}}\left\|d_{y}(\mathbf{z}_{y})-\mathbf{y}\right\|\qquad\text{with}\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(d_{x}\circ m^{\leftarrow})(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{y}).

(7)

To address missing or corrupted data, where we observe
\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}=P(\mathbf{y})
with projection
P
, we reformulate
Equation

6
as

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{x}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{z}_{x}}\left\|(P\circ d_{y}\circ m^{\to})(\mathbf{z}_{x})-\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}\right\|\qquad\text{ with }\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=d_{x}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{x}),

(8)

or analogously,
Equation

7
as

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{y}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{z}_{y}}\left\|(P\circ d_{y})(\mathbf{z}_{y})-\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}\right\|\qquad\text{with}\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(d_{x}\circ m^{\leftarrow})(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{y}).

(9)

Note, the above problems remain ill-posed, and the model reconstruction quality depends on the specific inverse problem, the training dataset quality, and the designs for the encoder, the decoder, and
m^{\leftarrow}
.
To address this ill-posedness, regularization can be employed in
Equation

8
and
Equation

9
, where a regularization functional encoding prior knowledge on
\mathbf{x}
could be used on
d_{x}(\mathbf{z}_{x})
or
d_{x}(m^{\leftarrow}\mathbf{z}_{y})
to enforce prior knowledge on the LSI solution
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
. In our numerical examples, we find that such regularization is unnecessary, and so for simplicity, we exclude it.

The numerical and computational considerations for this optimization problem are straightforward. The latent map
m^{\leftarrow}
is typically a simple linear operator and will not significantly contribute to the computational cost. Backpropagation through the decoder network
d_{y}
and potentially
m^{\leftarrow}
if using
Equation

6
is readily available. For simplicity, we utilize formulation
Equation

7
. We note that empirically, optimizing in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}
provides better reconstructions of
\mathbf{x}
than reconstructions obtained by optimizing in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}}
. We aim to explain this empirical observation with theoretical results for linear constructions in
Section

3.1
.

3.1
Latent Space Inference with Linear Constructions

Let us consider latent space inference where the forward model
A
, the projection
P
, the partially observed

, the encoders

,
e_{y}
, the decoders

,

and

,
\mathbf{E}_{y}
,
\mathbf{D}_{x}
,
\mathbf{D}_{y}
,

and

respectively. Let

\mathbb{E}X=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\mu}$}_{x}
, and symmetric positive definite (SPD) second moment
\mathbb{E}XX^{\top}=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{x}=\mathbf{L}_{x}\mathbf{L}_{x}^{\top}

\epsilon

\mathbb{R}^{q}
, mean zero
\mathbb{E}\epsilon=0
, and SPD second moment
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{\epsilon}=\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\top}

Equation

1
, we define the random variable
Y
with realizations
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
\mathbb{E}Y=\mathbf{A}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\mu}$}_{x}
, and SPD second moment
\mathbb{E}YY^{\top}=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{y}=\mathbf{A}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{x}\mathbf{A}^{\top}+\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{\epsilon}
. We denote the singular value decomposition (SVD) of a matrix

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{U}_{W}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{W}\mathbf{V}_{W}^{\top},

\mathbf{W}

\mathbf{W}_{r}=\mathbf{U}_{W,r}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{W,r}\mathbf{V}_{W,r}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\rule[-4.30554pt]{0.5pt}{10.76385pt}&\rule[-4.30554pt]{0.5pt}{10.76385pt}&&\rule[-4.30554pt]{0.5pt}{10.76385pt}\\
\mathbf{u}_{1}&\mathbf{u}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{u}_{r}\\
\rule[-4.30554pt]{0.5pt}{10.76385pt}&\rule[-4.30554pt]{0.5pt}{10.76385pt}&&\rule[-4.30554pt]{0.5pt}{10.76385pt}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{1}&&&\\
&\sigma_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\sigma_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}&\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}&\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}\\
\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}&\mathbf{v}_{2}^{\top}&\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}\\
&\vdots&\\
\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}&\mathbf{v}_{r}^{\top}&\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

From
[

]
, in a Bayes risk minimization sense, optimal paired linear encoders, decoders, and latent space mappings are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}_{L_{x},\ell_{x}}^{\top},\qquad\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{x}=\mathbf{U}_{L_{x},\ell_{x}},\quad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{\to}=\mathbf{U}_{L_{y},\ell_{y}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}_{L_{x},\ell_{x}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{y}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}_{L_{y}\ell_{y}}^{\top},\qquad\quad

and
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}

8

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{x}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{z}_{x}}\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}_{y}\mathbf{M}^{\to}\mathbf{z}_{x}-\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{y}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{z}_{y}}\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}_{y}\mathbf{z}_{y}-\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{z_{x}}=\mathbf{U}_{L_{x},\ell_{x}}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{U}_{L_{y},\ell_{y}}\mathbf{U}_{L_{y},\ell_{y}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}_{L_{x},\ell_{x}})^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y}

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{z_{y}}=\mathbf{U}_{L_{x},\ell_{x}}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{L_{x},\ell_{x}}^{2}\mathbf{U}_{L_{x},\ell_{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{L_{y},\ell_{y}}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{L_{y},\ell_{y}}^{-2}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{U}_{L_{y},\ell_{y}})^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y}.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{z_{x}}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{z_{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}_{L_{x}}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{L_{x}}^{2}\mathbf{U}_{L_{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{L_{y}}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{L_{y}}^{-2}\mathbf{U}_{L_{y}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{x}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{y}^{-1}\mathbf{y}.

and
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}
are inherently different. Specifically, the solution found in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}
includes the diagonal matrices
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{L_{x}}^{2}
and
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{L_{y}}^{-2}
with the singular values of
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{x}
and
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Gamma}$}_{y}
, whereas the solution found in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}}
does not. Empirically, we observed that in the nonlinear setting, the solutions found by optimizing in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}
were better than those found in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}}
, indicating that this scaling may be critical. Thus, we focus on latent space inference in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}
.

3.2
Residual and Error Bounds for Latent Space Inference

Assume that linear or nonlinear paired autoencoders are trained, parameterizing
e_{x}
,
d_{x}
,
e_{y}
,
d_{y}
,
m^{\leftarrow}
, and
m^{\to}
. Then with additional assumptions on Lipschitz continuity and on the consistency of PAIR components, we can bound the residual
\|P(A(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}))-\mathbf{y}_{\rm sub}\|
and the reconstruction error
\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
is the LSI solution obtained via
Equation

9
. Beginning with a simple projection bound in
1
, we successively build toward a general error estimate for
\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|
in Proposition
1
, and finally relate this reconstruction error to the forward-model residual in the observation space in Statement
2
to show how discrepancies between reconstructed and true signals propagate through the learned mappings.

Statement 1
.

Let
P:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\rm sub}
be Lipschitz continuous on the metric spaces
(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|)
and
(\mathcal{Y}_{\rm sub},\|\cdot\|)
with Lipschitz constant
L_{P}\geq 0
. Also assume that there exists
\mathbf{z}=e_{y}(\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, and that
\|(d_{y}\circ e_{y})(\mathbf{w})-\mathbf{w}\|\leq\varepsilon_{y}
for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Then,
\|P(d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}))-P(\mathbf{y})\|\leq L_{p}\varepsilon_{y}
.

Proof.

By triangular inequality,

\displaystyle\|P(d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}))-P(\mathbf{y})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|P(d_{y}(\mathbf{z}))-P(\mathbf{y})\|\leq L_{p}\|(d_{y}\circ e_{y})(\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{y}\|\leq L_{p}\varepsilon_{y}.

∎

From LSI,
\widehat{\mathbf{z}}
minimizes
\|P(d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}))-P(\mathbf{y})\|
, and the performance of the data autoencoder
d_{y}\circ e_{y}
bounds the magnitude of this difference.

Proposition 1
.

Let all the assumptions of Statement
1
hold. Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
be a subset such that
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}
and
d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})\in\mathcal{S}
, and assume that
P:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\rm sub}
satisfies a restricted bi-Lipschitz condition on
\mathcal{S}
, i.e., there exist constants

0<\alpha_{P}\leq\beta_{P}<\infty

such that

\alpha_{P}\|\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2}\|\leq\|P(\mathbf{w}_{1})-P(\mathbf{w}_{2})\|\leq\beta_{P}\|\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2}\|

(12)

for all
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
. Additionally, let
d_{x}
,
m^{\leftarrow}
, and
e_{y}
be Lipschitz continuous on their corresponding metric spaces with Lipschitz constants
L_{d_{x}}
,
L_{m^{\leftarrow}}
, and
L_{e_{y}}
, respectively. Assume that
\|(d_{x}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{t})-\mathbf{t}\|\leq\varepsilon_{x}
for all
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\|(m^{\leftarrow}\circ m^{\to})(\mathbf{t})-\mathbf{t}\|\leq\gamma_{m}
for all
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{X}}
,
\|\mathbf{y}-(d_{y}\circ m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})\|\leq\delta
for all
\mathbf{y}=A(\mathbf{x})+\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\epsilon}$}
, and that

\|\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{2}\|\leq\|(e_{y}\circ d_{y})(\mathbf{v}_{1})-(e_{y}\circ d_{y})(\mathbf{v}_{2})\|\qquad\text{for all }\quad\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{Y}}.

(13)

Then,

\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|\;\leq\;L_{d_{x}}\left(L_{m^{\leftarrow}}L_{e_{y}}\left(\frac{\beta_{P}}{\alpha_{P}}\,\varepsilon_{y}+\delta\right)+\gamma_{m}\right)+\varepsilon_{x}.

Proof.

With the definition of
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
and the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(d_{x}\circ m^{\leftarrow})(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-(d_{x}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})\|+\|(d_{x}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{d_{x}}\,\|m^{\leftarrow}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-e_{x}(\mathbf{x})\|+\varepsilon_{x}.

Further,

\displaystyle\|m^{\leftarrow}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-e_{x}(\mathbf{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|m^{\leftarrow}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-(m^{\leftarrow}\circ m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})\|+\|(m^{\leftarrow}\circ m^{\to})(e_{x}(\mathbf{x}))-e_{x}(\mathbf{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{m^{\leftarrow}}\,\|\widehat{\mathbf{z}}-(m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})\|+\gamma_{m}.

For the latent discrepancy, using
Equation

13
and Lipschitz continuity of
e_{y}
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{z}}-(m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(e_{y}\circ d_{y})(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-(e_{y}\circ d_{y}\circ m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{e_{y}}\,\|d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-(d_{y}\circ m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{e_{y}}\Big(\|d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{y}\|+\|\mathbf{y}-(d_{y}\circ m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\mathbf{x})\|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq L_{e_{y}}\big(\|d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{y}\|+\delta\big).

Since
\mathbf{y},d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})\in\mathcal{S}
, the restricted lower bound in
Equation

12
yields

\|d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{y}\|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{P}}\,\|P(d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}))-P(\mathbf{y})\|.

Combining this with the upper bound in
Equation

12
and Statement
1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{y}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{P}}\|P(d_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}))-P(\mathbf{y})\|\leq\frac{\beta_{P}}{\alpha_{P}}\varepsilon_{y}.

Plugging into the previous estimates gives

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{d_{x}}\Big(L_{m^{\leftarrow}}L_{e_{y}}(\tfrac{\beta_{P}}{\alpha_{P}}\varepsilon_{y}+\delta)+\gamma_{m}\Big)+\varepsilon_{x}.

∎

The stability bound in Proposition
1
relies on a restricted bi-Lipschitz property of the partial observation operator
P
on the subset
\mathcal{S}
of relevant signals, rather than on the entire space
\mathcal{Y}
. For masking and subsampling operators, such a restriction is unavoidable, since, in applications,
P
is non-injective and admits a nontrivial nullspace. The lower constant
\alpha_{P}
characterizes how well
P
separates distinct elements on
\mathcal{S}
; when
\alpha_{P}
becomes small due to severe data loss or alignment of the learned data manifold with
{\rm ker\!}\left(P\right)
, the inverse problem becomes ill-posed on
\mathcal{S}
and the bound necessarily degrades (see Experiment in
Section

4.2
,
Figure

7
).
In practice, the diagnostic quantities provided by the PAIR framework offer an empirical indication of whether a given observation lies within this stable regime, thus identifying scenarios where latent space inference may be needed.

For tight bounds, we assume that (i) the errors for both the data and model autoencoders are small, (ii)
(d_{y}\circ m^{\to}\circ e_{x})(\cdot)
is a good surrogate model for
A
, and (iii) the mappings between latent spaces approximately undo each other. A similar bound can be obtained for the residual norm.

Statement 2
.

Let all the assumptions of Proposition
1
hold, and additionally assume that
A:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is Lipschitz continuous on
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|)
and
(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|)
with Lipschitz constant
L_{A}\geq 0
. Then,

\|P(A(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}))-\mathbf{y}_{\rm sub}\|\leq\beta_{P}L_{A}\Big(L_{d_{x}}\left(L_{m^{\leftarrow}}L_{e_{y}}(\tfrac{\beta_{P}}{\alpha_{P}}\varepsilon_{y}+\delta)+\gamma_{m}\right)+\varepsilon_{x}\Big)+\beta_{P}\|\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\epsilon}$}\|.

Proof.

\displaystyle\|P(A(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}))-\mathbf{y}_{\rm sub}\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{P}\|A(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-(A(\mathbf{x})+\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\epsilon}$})\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{P}L_{A}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|+\beta_{P}\|\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\epsilon}$}\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{P}L_{A}\left(L_{d_{x}}\left(L_{m^{\leftarrow}}L_{e_{y}}(\tfrac{\beta_{P}}{\alpha_{P}}\varepsilon_{y}+\delta)+\gamma_{m}\right)+\varepsilon_{x}\right)+\beta_{P}\|\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\epsilon}$}\|.

∎

These results help to make explicit how each component of the paired autoencoder framework contributes to the total LSI error and residual norm. The bound in Proposition
1
shows that reconstruction accuracy in
\mathcal{X}
is controlled by the Lipschitz constants of the mappings and by the internal reconstruction errors
\varepsilon_{x}
,
\varepsilon_{y}
,
\gamma_{m}
, and
\delta
. Statement
2
extends this reasoning to the data space, demonstrating that the projected residual
\|P(A(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}))-\mathbf{y}_{\rm sub}\|
grows at most linearly with these same quantities. Together, these results quantify the propagation of approximation errors through LSI and provide sufficient conditions under which the inversion remains stable and well-behaved.

4
Numerical Results and Comparisons

In this section, we describe two example applications and demonstrate latent space inference under various scenarios of observational inconsistencies. We consider examples in computed tomography (CT) image reconstruction and in cross-well seismic inversion.
For both examples, we consider latent space inference with PAIR by solving
Equation

9
, and we denote our new approach ‘PAIR + LSI’. We provide comparisons to paired autoencoders without latent-space optimization, which was previously shown to be superior to end-to-end inversion approaches in
[
10
,
25
]
, even without observational inconsistencies.
In the first example, we discuss metrics for out-of-distribution detection, and in the second example, we compare PAIR and PAIR + LSI for different percentages of missing data.

4.1
Example 1: CT Imaging

CT image reconstruction involves reconstructing an internal image of an object from X-ray measurements taken at multiple angles outside of the object. These measurements form a sinogram, which mathematically corresponds to the Radon transform of the object. Recovering the original anatomy requires inverting this transform, which is inherently an ill-posed problem
[
37
]
. Additionally, variability in scanner configurations across machines and institutions introduces differences in data sizes and types, which any data-driven method must account for to be widely applicable.

For this example, we use the 2DeteCT dataset
[
34
]
to evaluate our latent space inference methods in addressing CT tasks with incomplete data. From this dataset, we use downsampled reconstructions of the phantom “anatomy” of nuts, dried fruits, stones, and filler materials, imaged to produce training samples that share features and contrasts similar to abdominal CT scans. We simulate the forward CT process using ‘PRTomo’ from the IRTools package
[
17
]
, and added
10\%
white noise to each sinogram.

We train the two autoencoders
a_{y}=(d_{y}\circ e_{y})
and
a_{x}=(d_{x}\circ e_{x})
and latent space mappings
m^{\to}
and
m^{\leftarrow}
together, minimizing the combined loss in
Equation

4

for
\{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X}\}
in the set of training samples
\{\mathbf{Y}^{\rm train}_{i},\mathbf{X}^{\rm train}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{$4,900$}
, where
\mathbf{Y}^{\rm train}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{96\times 181}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\rm train}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{52\times 52}
. The encoder
e_{x}
consists of an initial convolutional layer, followed by three residual blocks, each made of two
3\times 3
convolutions with batch normalization and ReLU activation and downsampling by stride-2 convolution at the start of each block. After resizing to 512 and flattening, this results in a more compact latent representation
\mathbf{z}_{x}=e_{x}(\mathbf{X})

d_{x}
mirrors this process. Starting from
\mathbf{z}_{x}

The encoder
e_{y}
and decoder
d_{y}
are similar, but each includes 6 residual blocks, rather than 3. Both
m^{\to}
and
m^{\leftarrow}
are
512\times 512
fully connected linear layers. Training was conducted using an Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
0.001
for 100 epochs.

For the CT example, we simulated missing data in two ways: (1) randomly missing angles, representing scenarios where measurements at certain projection angles are corrupted or unavailable; and (2) contiguous blocks of missing angles, modeling situations such as limited-angle tomography, where a range of projection angles cannot be measured. LSI. The errors are also clearly clustered; the error is highest on samples with masked angles when PAIR is used without LSI, and the error is lowest when PAIR is used with full angles. Although it does not achieve the same accuracy as in the full angle case, PAIR
+
LSI clearly reduces the reconstruction errors associated with missing angles, since the cluster of squares is notably closer to the cluster of circles.

[

\mathbf{z}
are now represented by random variables
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},{\rm diag\!}\left(\bm{\sigma}\right))

\bm{\mu}

This experiment considers cross-well seismic imaging
[
40
,
48
]
in transmission mode, meaning that seismic sources and receivers are in opposite wells or boreholes.
Consider the acoustic wave equation,

\nabla^{2}u(x,t)-\frac{1}{v(x)^{2}}\partial_{tt}u(x,t)=s(x,t),

(14)

where a spatial and temporally dependent source term
s(x,t)
generates a seismic wave field
u(x,t)
in the p-wave velocity model
v(x)
.
For each of the
30
source locations (
15
per borehole), we numerically solve the discretized wave equation using DeepWave
[
41
]
, and the observed data are measured by
48
receivers located in the borehole on the opposite side of the velocity model. For readability, we use the same notation for continuous fields and their discretized representations, as all quantities are ultimately treated in discrete form for numerical simulation and learning. For a single velocity model, the observed data
\mathbf{Y}=Q(u)
contains observations for all time points from all
30
sources/channels.

We generate training data containing corresponding data/velocity samples
\{\mathbf{Y}^{\rm train}_{i},\mathbf{X}^{\rm train}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{$2,000$}
, where
\mathbf{X}^{\rm train}_{i}
denotes a discretized velocity field. We train the VPAE to minimize the combined loss given in
Equation

.
Both the data and velocity models use mean-shift and standard-deviation scaling, where the normalization constants are computed globally over the full training set. The paired autoencoders are trained on complete data, while all inference uses data with missing receiver signals, denoted as
\mathbf{Y}_{\text{sub}}=P(\mathbf{Y})

The VPAE design for this example employs a fully convolutional multi-level ResNet for both the data and the model encoder-decoder structures.
Given the network state
\mathbf{S}_{k}
at layer index
k
, the state is processed by an atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) block
[
9
]
, which contains convolutions, normalization, and non-linearity, and also changes the number of channels at the end of each of the three levels. After each level, the number of channels is doubled, while the spatial resolution is halved. To maintain the ResNet flow across levels, we also change the number of channels of the state via a
1\times 1
convolution,
\mathbf{C}_{k}\mathbf{S}_{k}
. We denote the identity or pooling/subsampling operator as
P_{k}
. Together the encoder design reads

\mathbf{S}_{k+1}=P_{k}(\mathbf{C}_{k}\mathbf{S}_{k}-\operatorname{ASPP}_{k}(\mathbf{S}_{k})).

(15)

We use three levels, with three layers each, so
P_{k}
is a pooling operator at
k=3,6,9
and
P_{k}
is equal to the identity map for all other indices. Similarly,
\mathbf{C}_{k}
changes the number of channels of the state for layers
k=3,6,9
, while acting as the identity operator for all other indices. The number of channels is
64\rightarrow 128\rightarrow 256
for the three levels.
The decoder follows the reverse design of
Equation

15
. In the latent space, the variables originating from data and velocity models typically have different sizes and channel counts. The latent-space operators
m^{\to}
and
m^{\leftarrow}
are linear transformations and translations that act on vectorized latent variables while preserving the batch dimension.

We compare three approaches for inference from the corrupted data that all use (pieces of) the same pre-trained VPAE:

1.

PAIR:
we use the variational paired autoencoders alone, without any explicit treatment of the corrupted or missing observations. The velocity model is estimated directly via the learned surrogate inversion map by encoding the incomplete data and decoding through the paired latent mappings, as in
Equation

5
.

2.

Model-based LSI (M-LSI):
we directly estimate a velocity model via solving the inverse problem, parameterized by the model decoder. See, e.g.,
[
7
,
4
]
and
[
23
,
36
,
51
,
26
,
30
]
for deterministic and probabilistic applications to linear and nonlinear problems, respectively.
This approach is formulated in terms of the partial observation operator
P
and the forward operator
A=Q\circ F
, where
F
represents the velocity-to-wavefield map and
Q
denotes the full observation operator.

Using a maximum of
100
L-BFGS iterations we compute

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{x}=\argmin_{\mathbf{z}_{x}}\|(P\circ A\circ d_{x})(\mathbf{z}_{x})-\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=d_{x}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{x}).

(16)

Note that
Equation

16
defines a deterministic optimization problem, as it does not involve the probabilistic formulation of the encoder and propagates only through the decoder. As an initial guess in the latent space, we employ the model encoder
e_{x}
to encode the pixel-wise mean velocity model from the training set. The numerical experiments solve the above problem for an ensemble of random initial guesses, generated by sampling the encoded mean velocity model, to obtain an ensemble of the QoI estimates. Note that the samples in this ensemble are not true posterior samples, since they are obtained by solving the deterministic optimization problem
Equation

16
rather than by sampling a Bayesian posterior; nevertheless, variability across the ensemble provides a qualitative indication of uncertainty in the estimates.

3.

PAIR + LSI:
we perform latent space inversion in the data space (
Equation

9
) using a maximum of

25
iterations of L-BFGS to reconstruct missing data,
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{y}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{z}_{y}}\left\|(P\circ d_{y})(\mathbf{z}_{y})-\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}}\right\|
followed by decoding (the estimated latent data variables) to the velocity model, i.e.,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(d_{x}\circ m^{\leftarrow})(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{y})
. The initial guess is the encoding of the missing data,
e_{y}(\mathbf{y}_{\text{sub}})
. Because this approach uses the VPAE, the encoded missing data is sampled in the latent space, which yields an ensemble of data reconstructions and an ensemble of estimated velocity models.

Regarding computational cost, Method
2
stands out as computationally expensive because every iteration requires evaluating
A
and its adjoint, which requires solving PDEs. Methods
1
and
3
rely solely on the surrogate models provided by the trained variational paired autoencoders.

The VPAE constructs an ensemble of data reconstructions

We observe that PAIR+LSI provides good reconstructions of the data from the missing receiver signals. Along the same lines, we expect that velocity field reconstructions,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(d_{x}\circ m^{\leftarrow})(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{y})
, will yield accurate approximations of the true velocity fields.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we describe a new approach for latent space inference that can handle observational inconsistencies or missing data. By exploiting a paired autoencoder framework and performing inversion in the latent space, we show that more accurate reconstructions can be obtained for problems with missing observational data.
We extend the capabilities of the PAIR framework to handle missing data, and we show that the PAIR+LSI results are superior to direct mapping via PAIR, as well as encoder-decoder networks of incomplete data, especially if the incomplete data are different from those used for training.

Future work includes exploring the use of latent space inference with PAIR for optimal experimental design (e.g., identifying the important angles to take measurements or the important sensors for sensor placement). In contrast to works like
[
44
]
that combine machine learning techniques with OED to avoid bilevel optimization problems, latent space inversion for the inner problem would provide a fast reconstruction approximation for different missing angles. Latent space inference could also be used for timely solves in hyperparameter estimation or in scenarios with the forward model or noise model is different from the assumptions.
