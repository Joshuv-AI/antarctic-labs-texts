A dataset on morphology and composition of saltation-sandblasting dust particles from major Chinese source regions
Authors: Long Zhang, Tafeng Hu, Yingpan Song, Youjia Yuan, Feng Wu, Yu Huang, Daizhou Zhang, Junji Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Large quantities of dust particles are emitted from the arid and semi-arid regions of China, influencing atmospheric processes and climate from regional to global scales. The morphology and composition of dust particles govern their optical properties, reactivity, and transport behavior; thus, accurate single-particle information is essential for improving model representations and understanding their environmental effects. Here, we present the Dust Morphology and Composition from China’s Source Regions (DMC-China) dataset. The dataset includes 541,074 individual dust particles generated via saltation-sandblasting processes from 55 surface soils collected from major dust source regions. The samples represent surface cover types, including gobi (gravel/desert pavement), sand dune, saline, desertified grassland and alluvial sediment surfaces. The particles were analyzed using computer-controlled scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (CCSEM-EDX), and strict quality control procedures ensured consistency and reliability across samples. Provided in accessible XLSX format with detailed metadata, DMC-China delivers comprehensive source-region profiles of nascent dust particle size, shape, and composition, offering a valuable foundation for studies of dust emission, long-range transport, and dust-related effects on climate and the environment.

Background and summary

Mineral dust emitted from arid and semi-arid regions exerts profound impacts on Earth’s climate system, atmospheric chemistry, and biogeochemical cycles1–3. Its optical, hygroscopic, and chemical properties are strongly governed by particle morphology and composition, which influence processes such as radiative forcing, cloud formation, and heterogeneous chemistry4–6. Despite extensive field campaigns and simulation experiments in East Asia7–10, most existing analyses rely on bulk measurements or a limited number of particle samples collected either from desert air or surface soils, which restricts a comprehensive understanding of dust particles released from source regions. No systematic dataset currently exists for the morphology and composition of source region-emitted dust particles in China.

To address this knowledge gap, we developed the Dust Morphology and Composition from China’s Source Regions (DMC-China) dataset, which provides detailed information on the morphology and composition of 541,074 individual dust particles from 55 surface soil samples through the saltation-sandblasting processes. These samples were collected from major Chinese desert source regions, including the Taklimakan Desert in the Tarim Basin, the Gobi Desert of the Alashan Plateau, the Hunshandake Sandy Land in eastern Inner Mongolia, and the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. Dust particles were generated from the soil samples using a laboratory-constructed resuspension system that simulates the saltation-sandblasting processes of natural wind erosion, following the operational principle of the Portable In-Situ Wind Erosion Laboratory (PI-SWERL)11. The size, shape, and elemental composition of each particle were determined using computer-controlled scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (CCSEM–EDX). The combination of PI-SWERL and CCSEM–EDX allows for large-scale, automated, and reproducible single-particle characterization across multiple dust source regions12,13.

The DMC-China dataset consists of four interlinked data tables: (1) a Raw Data file containing direct CCSEM–EDX measurements of morphology and elemental composition; (2) a Site Information file documenting regional settings, coordinates, elevations, and surface cover types; (3) a Mineral Classification Rules file defining compositional thresholds and empirical formulas for mineral type identification; and (4) Processed Data files providing derived morphological parameters (e.g., equivalent circular diameter, aspect ratio, form factor, and roundness) and two mineralogical classification schemes: Case1_IEE (Institute of Earth Environment) and Case2_Kandler (a typical particle classification method for dust developed by Kandler14). Each table is linked by a unique Sample ID, forming a relational database structure that ensures internal consistency and facilitates integrated analyses. Strict quality control, including visual particle screening, unit harmonization, error checking, and outlier removal, was implemented to guarantee data reliability and reproducibility. DMC-China provides the most extensive and standardized single-particle dataset from Asian dust source regions to date. It serves as a benchmark resource for investigating the relationships between dust morphology, composition, and environmental behavior, supporting improved parameterizations in transport and climate models (e.g., WRF-Chem, GEOS-Chem, CESM). Potential applications include assessing dust–pollution interactions, simulating dust optical and hygroscopic properties of mineral aerosols, and evaluating their health and environmental impacts. By adhering to FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable) data principles, this dataset enables broad reuse and integration with field observations, laboratory experiments, and remote-sensing retrievals, promoting cross-disciplinary research on dust–climate–environment interactions. This dataset has the potential to bridge the gap between source-region soil properties and atmospheric dust characteristics, enabling quantitative linkage for modeling and remote sensing studies.

Methods

Surface soil samples

This dataset covers four dust source regions in China, encompassing diverse geomorphological and climatic settings: the Taklimakan Desert (TK) in the Tarim Basin, the Gobi Desert (GD) on the Alashan Plateau, the Hunshandake Sandy Land (HS) in eastern Inner Mongolia, and the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau (TP) in western China (Fig. 2). At each sampling point, surface crusts or loose surface materials were collected after removing surface gravel or plant debris. The sampling captured the entire uppermost layer (typically a few millimeters to approximately 1 cm in thickness) using clean plastic shovels to collect materials susceptible to aeolian entrainment. The sampling covered diverse surface cover types, including gobi surfaces sand dune, alluvial sediment, desertified grassland, and saline land (Figure S1). Each sample was stored in an individual self-sealed plastic bag. Sampling locations and elevations were recorded with a GPS (GPSMAP 67i, Garmin International Inc., USA), and all sites were selected at least 1 km away from human disturbances. A summary of the sampling information is provided in Table S1.

The Tarim Basin (35.17°–42.08° N, 75.08°–90.17° E), located in northwestern China, is the largest inland basin and one of the most important dust sources in East Asia. The Taklimakan Desert, situated within the basin, lies in the mid-latitude westerly belt and accounts for approximately 35% of the total annual dust aerosol emissions from East Asia15,16. In the region, we collected 13 surface soil samples: seven from sand dune cover, four from gobi (gravel) cover, and two from desertified grassland cover.

The Gobi Desert (37.17°–44.83° N, 97.33°–117.50° E) extends eastward from the Tarim Basin and spans a broad region bordered by the Altai Mountains to the north, the Hexi Corridor to the south, the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau to the west, and the Mongolian Plateau to the east17,18. Thirteen surface soil samples from this region were analyzed, covering the Badain Jaran Desert, the Tengger Desert and the Ulan Buh Desert. These subregions differ in sediment texture and geomorphological setting, representing a wide range of gobi-soil surfaces across northern China. In total, seven samples were collected from sand cover, five from gobi cover, and one from saline cover.

The Hunshandake Sandy Land (41.93°–44.40° N, 112.37°–117.95° E) is located at the southeastern part of the Inner Mongolian Plateau, forming a transitional zone between desert and steppe surface cover type19. It consists of semi-fixed dunes and grass-covered sandy surfaces and serves as an important dust source for northern China20. In the region, seven surface soil samples were collected, including six from sand dune cover and one from desertified grassland cover.

The Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, known as the “Roof of the World,” is characterized by high elevation, complex topography, and strong climatic gradients21, with crucial impacts on global dust transport in the upper troposphere22. In this region, 22 surface soil samples were collected and categorized into three subregions based on topographic and environmental characteristics: (1) the Qiangtang Plateau (29.67°–37.80° N, 78.30°–93.79° E) in the central–western plateau (4500–5200 m a.s.l.), including one sand dune, one gobi, one sediment, two desertified grasslands. (2) the Qaidam Basin (35.00°–39.33° N, 90.27°–99.27° E), including six sand dunes and four gobi soils in the northeastern plateau (2700–3200 m a.s.l.). (3) the Yarlung Tsangpo River Valley included two sand dune cover and four gobi cover and one desertified grassland cover (28.00°–31.50° N, 88.00°–96.00° E) along the southern plateau margin (2900–3600 m a.s.l.).

Laboratory dust generation

In the laboratory, the soil samples were dried and stored at room temperature without sieving or chemical pretreatment. The dust emission process was simulated by a self-made Portable In-Situ Wind Erosion Laboratory (PI-SWERL)23,24, which is a device designed based on the principles of natural wind erosion processes25,26. It generates controlled shear stress on the soil surface through a rotating annular blade, which mobilizes particles and suspends them within a chamber, thereby simulating dust aerosol formation under natural conditions26,27.

The cylindrical chamber (57 cm diameter, 25 cm height) was supplied with clean23, dry air filtered through a High Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filter (99.97% efficiency at 0.3 μm; Whatman International Ltd., Maidstone, UK). For each soil sample, approximately 5.0 g of material was evenly spread on the aluminum base plate, and the system was initialized by setting the rotation speed at 500 rpm for 5 minutes to remove possible suspended particles that might disturb mobilized dust. Following the purge process, the rotation speed was gradually increased to 1200 rpm, corresponding to friction velocities of 0.54 m s-126. Previous studies have demonstrated that dust generated using the laboratory suspension method under these operating conditions exhibits particle-size distributions and chemical compositions that are consistent with field observations23,24,26. Dust particles (TSP) were collected using Mini-Vol air samplers (Airmetrics Corp., Springfield, OR, USA) onto 47-mm polycarbonate Nuclepore filters (0.2 μm pore size; Whatman International Ltd., UK) at a flow rate of 5 L min−1. Sampling durations, typically ranging from tens of seconds to a few minutes, were determined according to particle concentrations monitored by a DustTrak device (DustTrak 8533 DRX Aerosol Monitor, TSI Inc., Shoreview, MN, USA). In addition, these varying durations were applied to avoid particle overlap and to ensure enough particles for CCSEM analysis.

Before each experiment, residual soil on the inner surfaces, base plate, ventilation ducts and removable components was removed using compressed air. The chamber interior and base plate were subsequently wiped with lint-free wipes to clean adherent particles. All internal surfaces, including the chamber walls, base plate, ventilation inlets, ducts and dust outlet, were then cleaned with alcohol and Kimtech wipers to ensure the removal of fine residues and potential contaminants. Finally, all components were thoroughly dried with compressed air, reassembled, and prepared for the next sample.

Single-particle analysis using CCSEM-EDX

Particles were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (FESEM, MAIA3, Brno, Czech Republic) equipped with two energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectrometers (EDX, XFlash6 I 60, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany). Morphological factors and elemental composition of individual particles were obtained using automated particle analysis based on computer-controlled scanning electron microscope analysis software (CCSEM, IntelliSEM, RJ Lee Group, Inc., Monroeville, USA). The instrument was operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 keV with a beam current of ~1 nA and a working distance of ~7.5 mm. Secondary electron (SE) signals were used to distinguish particles from the substrate material. Automated particle recognition was performed by the EPAS software using a gray-level threshold algorithm, with a minimum detectable particle size of approximately 0.2 μm due to the pore size of the filters.

For analysis, a small rectangular section (~10 × 10 mm) was cut from the center of each polycarbonate filter and mounted on a CCSEM stub using conductive carbon tape. The stub was then coated with carbon for 6 s to enhance the electrical conductivity of particles. If more than 20% of the particles on the filter were overlapping, the sample was re-collected to obtain a more uniform particle distribution.

The perimeter (pepe) was determined by tracing the outer contour of each particle and summing the lengths of all boundary pixels.

Elemental composition was determined from EDX spectra through a sequential analytical process. Peak fitting based on Gaussian distribution was applied to deconvolve overlapping peaks and obtain net peak areas (counts) for each element. ZAF correction (atomic number, absorption, and fluorescence corrections) was then used to convert the net counts into weight fractions (wt%). Because the polycarbonate filter contributed substantial carbon background, especially for smaller particles, carbon was excluded from the subsequent normalization. The selected non-carbon elements (O, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, K, Ca, Ti, and Fe) were normalized to 100% to derive relative weight fractions. The 0.1 wt% value was used as a post-normalization reporting threshold for mineral classification, rather than as an absolute instrumental detection limit. Elements with normalized relative weight fractions exceeding 0.1 wt% were retained for classification.

Data processing

A systematic workflow was applied to ensure data quality and consistency (Fig. 3). Raw data were obtained from 55 representative dust source sites, encompassing diverse surface cover types such as gobi, sand dune, alluvial sediment, desertified grassland, and saline land. Although the EPAS system provides automated particle recognition, occasional misidentifications (e.g., incomplete boundaries, background artifacts, non-particle features) were observed. To ensure dataset reliability, all detected particles were visually inspected, and unsuitable detections were manually excluded, retaining only well-defined particles. When more than 20% of particles were manually excluded during screening, the corresponding sample was re-prepared and re-analyzed. In this study, the retained particle ratio averaged 90.36 ± 7.11%.Fig. 3Data processing workflow for the DMC-China dataset.

Particle classification was based on the relative weight percentages of detected elements, following empirical formulas for typical dust minerals. Reference values for the classification, including relative weight ratios of elements and corresponding empirical formulas obtained from online databases at https://duffy.princeton.edu/mineralogy-and-crystallography-databases. This classification rule has been reported in our previous studies24,28. The mineral classification criteria and the corresponding mineralogical results are provided in the auxiliary data files as an example case (Case1_IEE). In cases of ambiguity, particles were classified according to the prioritization scheme shown in Figure S4 (e.g., quartz > gypsum > …). Representative micrographs of dust particles and their corresponding original CCSEM-EDX spectra are presented in Figure S2.

The particle classification scheme can be further refined and optimized to meet the requirements of diverse research applications. To demonstrate the applicability of our data for particle mineralogical classification in other research, we randomly selected one sample from each of the four regions and analyzed it using Kandler’s mineral classification method as an alternative example (Case2_Kandler)14.

To account for the irregular morphology of dust particles, additional shape descriptors, including aspect ratio, roundness, and form factor, were derived from their two-dimensional projections.

Data Record

The dataset “Dust Morphology and Composition from China’s Source Regions (DMC-China)30” is available from Figshare repository (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.30768710). It provides comprehensive particle-level data on the morphology, elemental composition, and mineralogical classification of dust generated from 55 representative source sites across northern China. These sites span diverse surface cover types, including gobi, sand dune, alluvial sediment, desertified grassland, and saline land, representing the major dust-producing regions.

A total of 541,074 particles were analyzed. This dataset offers the most extensive record of dust particle properties from Asian source regions to date. All data are provided in XLSX format, ensuring accessibility and interoperability for diverse applications.

The raw data, site information, and processed data files are linked by an identified Sample ID, which serves as the unique identifier across all data layers. This indexing system allows users to merge particle-level measurements with site metadata and processed classifications, enabling integrated analyses across spatial, mineralogical, and morphological dimensions.

Technical Validation

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the dataset, a series of technical validation procedures and quality control measures were implemented across data collection, sample analysis, and data processing as the following aspects:

Dust particles were generated from surface soils using a PI-SWERL-type system, developed based on the saltation–induced sandblasting mechanism and has been widely employed to simulate the dust emission process13,26,31. In addition, particle characterization was conducted using CCSEM, enabling automated single-particle analysis and has been applied in atmospheric aerosol research14,32. The CCSEM-EDX instrument was regularly calibrated with standard reference materials to maintain measurement accuracy and instrumental stability.

To ensure robustness, a series of quality control procedures were applied. First, raw CCSEM outputs were screened to remove particles with incomplete spectra, abnormally low counts, or unstable beam signals. Elements below detection thresholds were excluded from the calculation of weight percentages. Second, all parameters were harmonized into consistent units (µm for size, wt% for elemental composition) and checked for internal consistency. Outliers were identified and removed based on threshold criteria for unrealistic morphological or compositional values. Third, particle classification into mineralogical groups was conducted using a rule-based decision tree grounded in established literature, ensuring comparability with previous datasets.

In addition, we used 0.8-µm polystyrene microspheres (PSL, Duck Scientific Corp., USA) to calibrate the threshold settings and validate measurement accuracy, as this size falls within the typical particle size range (0.5–1.0 µm) generated by saltation-sandblasting in our experiment24,28. Internal validation was carried out using replicate analyses within each sample. For each sample, two replicate protocols were applied: one consisted of three repeated CCSEM analyses within the same field of view to evaluate analytical repeatability under identical observation conditions, and the other consisted of analyses at three different fields of view within the same sample to assess the variability associated with within-sample heterogeneity. In this study, “same” denotes three repeated analyses conducted within the same field of view, whereas “different” denotes replicate analyses performed at three different fields of view within the same sample.

The reproducibility of the elemental composition data was first assessed using the number fractions of particles containing mineral elements. To evaluate external validity, our results were compared with published single-particle datasets from Asian dust studies. The relative abundances of quartz, feldspar, clays, carbonates, and salts in the DMC-China dataset fall within the ranges reported for Taklimakan and Gobi dust by previous literature24,28. For example, clay minerals dominate Asian dust particles, typically accounting for approximately 60% of the total particles, whereas quartz and feldspar generally represent smaller fractions (<10%), in agreement with mineralogical distributions reported in previous single-particle analyses. Dust particle sizes are also comparable to those observed in source-region samples generated by the saltation-sandblasting processes, with most particles typically falling within the 0.5–1.0 µm range. This agreement suggests that the experimental dust generation approach used here can realistically reproduce the physical characteristics of naturally emitted mineral dust particles. Differences observed in saline or gypsum-rich samples reflect known geochemical heterogeneity of specific dust source regions, thereby enhancing the dataset’s representativeness.

The procedures adopted here are repeatable, allowing the methodology to be applied in other dust source regions. All data are provided in XLSX format with clear variable definitions and sample identifiers that link raw and processed files, enabling traceability and re-analysis by other researchers. Together, these validation steps ensure that the dataset is reliable, reproducible, and broadly applicable for atmospheric modeling, climate assessments, and environmental studies.

Usage Notes

By employing automated CCSEM analysis on samples generated by saltation-sandblasting simulations, this dataset delivers detailed single-particle morphology and composition. These data offer valuable insights for research on long-range dust transport, atmospheric evolution, and environmental effects. Users should consider the following points when using the dataset:

Dust emission mechanism

The dust particles in this dataset were generated based on saltation-sandblasting processes, focusing on fine particles that significantly affect long-distance transport. Dust particles emitted in source regions can also be produced through suspension processes.

Surface cover type selection bias

The dataset emphasizes the dominant surface cover types within each major desert and dust source region, ensuring representativeness of typical dust-emitting surfaces. However, it does not provide exhaustive coverage of all possible surface cover types (e.g., farmlands or other disturbed surfaces), and users should be cautious when extrapolating results to surface cover types not included in the dataset.

Mineral classification

The raw single-particle data include fundamental elemental information, enabling users to define mineral groups according to their specific research objectives. Instead of imposing a fixed mineral classification scheme, we provide two representative frameworks, namely Case1_IEE and Case2_Kandler, to demonstrate how different element-ratio criteria can be applied. Users may adopt one of these schemes or design their own classification rules to support analyses related to heterogeneous chemistry, aerosol optics, or dust transport. This flexibility highlights the value of releasing the original elemental dataset, which allows re-analysis, future methodological refinement, and cross-comparison among different dust classification approaches. These two classification rules based on EDX elemental composition are largely derived from empirical mineral formulas rather than definitive mineralogical identification.

Morphological descriptor limitation

The particle morphological parameters in this dataset are derived from two-dimensional SEM projections using the EPAS rotated-Feret method. These descriptors mainly characterize particle size, aspect ratio, roundness, and form factor, but are less sensitive to concavity and internal structural complexity. This limitation may be particularly important for irregular dust particles with fragmented shapes, concave outlines, and complex boundaries generated from the saltation-sandblasting process. Moreover, as these measurements are obtained from two-dimensional projections, they are inherently subject to orientation bias and cannot fully represent the actual three-dimensional morphology of the particles. Consequently, morphological variability associated with surface relief, thickness, and spatial structure may not be completely resolved.

Recommended applications

The DMC-China dataset can be applied in a variety of research contexts to represent Asian dust particles with strong potential for long-distance transport. It can be directly applied to atmospheric models (e.g., WRF-Chem, GEOS-Chem) for dust emission and size distribution parameterizations, as well as to studies of dust optical properties, radiative forcing, and cloud interactions (CCN, INPs, heterogeneous chemistry). The dataset also provides compositional information for assessing environmental and health impacts. Integration with complementary observations (e.g., satellite products, ground-based measurements) is encouraged to maximize research outcomes.
