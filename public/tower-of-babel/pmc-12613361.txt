Experiences of individuals undergoing maintenance hemodialysis during the COVID-19 pandemic in China: a qualitative study
Authors: Hao Dai, Sijian Li, Tao Liu, Qingyan Wang, Qiuping Yin, Yan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic, coupled with China’s stringent “zero-COVID” policy, posed unprecedented challenges for individuals undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD). Access to dialysis care was significantly disrupted by lockdowns, transportation limitations, and the reallocation of healthcare resources. Despite these systemic barriers, the interaction between such constraints and patients’ personal experiences has not been thoroughly investigated. Therefore, this study seeks to explore the lived experiences of individuals receiving MHD in China during the pandemic, with particular attention to their access to care, psychosocial well-being, and coping mechanisms.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was carried out at a tertiary hospital in Chongqing, China. Sixteen adult participants undergoing MHD were purposively recruited to take part in ten semi-structured interviews and one focus group discussion involving six participants. Data collection occurred between November and December 2023, and the information obtained was analyzed using thematic analysis based on Braun and Clarke’s framework to identify major themes and corresponding subthemes.FindingsThe analysis led to the identification of five major themes and sixteen subthemes that captured the lived experiences of individuals receiving MHD during the COVID-19 pandemic. These themes encompassed systemic barriers to accessing care, perceived uncertainty about infection control and environmental safety, mental health challenges, self-management approaches, and the influence of family, social, and professional support. The findings highlighted the multifaceted difficulties faced by hemodialysis patients during the pandemic, underscoring the vital importance of personalized coping mechanisms and strong social support systems in sustaining treatment adherence and promoting mental well-being.ConclusionChina’s pandemic control measures created significant barriers to dialysis access and contributed to heightened psychological distress among patients. In response, many individuals employed self-management strategies to reduce the impact of these disruptions. The findings highlight the need for patient-centered interventions, particularly those aimed at enhancing transportation accessibility, incorporating mental health support, and addressing disparities in rural healthcare. Future studies should adopt integrated qualitative and quantitative methodologies to better inform pandemic preparedness and response strategies for dialysis-dependent populations.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04568-4.

Background of study

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects a growing proportion of the Chinese population, increasing from 10.8% in 2012 to 12.5% in 2022, with an estimated 850,000 individuals requiring maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) due to kidney failure in China [1, 2]. Kidney failure, defined by a glomerular filtration rate below 15 ml/min/1.73 m² or the necessity for dialysis, represents the terminal stage of CKD and is linked to high rates of morbidity and mortality [3]. As CKD advances to kidney failure, patients must undergo kidney replacement therapy (KRT), which includes hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplantation, or opt for conservative management to sustain life [3, 4]. KRT plays a crucial role in eliminating metabolic waste and excess fluid, correcting electrolyte imbalances, and supporting survival when renal function is severely impaired. The latest kidney disease: improving global outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines advocate for a patient-centered, individualized approach to initiating and managing KRT [5]. Hemodialysis, in particular, is a life-sustaining intervention that typically requires at least three sessions per week to ensure adequate dialysis clearance [6].

The outbreak of COVID-19 in Wuhan in late 2019 and its rapid spread throughout China prompted the government to adopt stringent measures and implement a “zero-COVID-19” policy aimed at halting viral transmission. This policy remained in effect until January 8, 2023 [7]. The pandemic introduced additional complications as authorities enforced lockdowns, prioritized COVID-19 treatment, and restricted public transportation services [8–10]. At the onset of the pandemic, regions including mainland China, Australia, and New Zealand enacted strict containment measures to minimize infection risks and prevent healthcare system overload. Although the “zero-COVID” strategy was intended to safeguard public health and reduce viral spread, it simultaneously created substantial challenges in healthcare accessibility for individuals undergoing dialysis. In August 2021, the concept of “dynamic zero-COVID” emerged, signaling a shift toward a more flexible and adaptive policy framework in mainland China [11].

These evolving policies had a particularly profound impact on individuals with chronic conditions such as kidney failure. Research indicates that dialysis-dependent patients experienced elevated mortality rates, psychological distress, and greater barriers to accessing medical care during the pandemic [12, 13]. For many people undergoing dialysis in China, the COVID-19 crisis brought complex trade-offs between maintaining consistent treatment and adhering to infection-control requirements. This included intermittent facility closures, heightened fears of infection, and diminished social interaction. Despite these challenges, many patients demonstrated remarkable resilience in sustaining their treatment routines. However, such perseverance often concealed a significant burden of physical symptoms and psychosocial strain. Studies have shown that individuals receiving hemodialysis frequently experience intense fatigue, pain, and sexual dysfunction, symptoms that are not only common but also characterized by considerable severity, frequency, and emotional distress [14]. This research highlights the multidimensional nature of symptom burden, particularly among older adults and female patients [14]. Similarly, McKie and colleagues emphasize that individuals on hemodialysis often face unmet supportive care needs, including loss of independence, diminished quality of life, and difficulties sustaining social and family relationships. The demanding treatment schedule and lifestyle restrictions inherent to dialysis further contribute to feelings of dependency and reduced spontaneity, resulting in considerable psychological distress [15]. Collectively, these studies underscore that the resilience displayed by individuals undergoing hemodialysis is often accompanied by substantial and multifaceted burdens, an issue that calls for greater recognition and intervention from both clinicians and policymakers.

International qualitative research has revealed varied dialysis experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic across different healthcare systems. In Canada, patients receiving in-center hemodialysis reported transportation barriers, reduced caregiver support, and heightened anxiety; however, their resilience was strengthened through stable staff relationships and consistent communication [16]. In Europe, both patients and healthcare professionals emphasized treatment schedule disruptions, infection-related fears, and psychosocial strain, while organizational reliability and strong patient–staff relationships served as protective factors against distress [17]. Comparative studies have also shown that home-based dialysis modalities were linked to greater autonomy and quality of life compared to in-center treatments, suggesting that the type of dialysis influenced the extent of pandemic-related disruptions [18].

Within this global context, China’s “dynamic zero-COVID” policies, characterized by mass testing, permit-based mobility, and test-dependent access to medical facilities, likely exacerbated barriers to care even as viral transmission was controlled. In Ethiopia, patients faced similar difficulties related to transportation and social isolation, though without the additional challenge of widespread testing [19]. Conversely, in Mexico, individuals on automated peritoneal dialysis supported by telemedicine experienced greater continuity of care and improved self-management, illustrating how digital innovations can mitigate psychosocial distress [20]. The present study builds upon this international evidence by offering context-specific insights into the experiences of in-center MHD patients during China’s prolonged containment period. It highlights both commonalities, such as transportation and scheduling challenges, anxiety, and dependence on support networks, and distinct differences shaped by policy, including mobility permits, testing-related delays, and urban–rural disparities.

The interaction between China’s pandemic control measures and dialysis care accessibility remains insufficiently examined, as does the influence of family and institutional support systems. To address these gaps, this study adopted a patient-centered qualitative approach aimed at uncovering the unmet needs of individuals undergoing dialysis and generating insights to guide more effective crisis response strategies in the future.

Aim and objectives, the significance of the study

The purpose of this study was to investigate the experiences of individuals receiving MHD in China during the COVID-19 pandemic, emphasizing access to care, psychosocial well-being, and coping strategies. The specific objectives were:

To identify the challenges associated with dialysis access, infection risk, and psychological distress; and.To explore the coping mechanisms and support systems utilized by patients to manage these challenges.

This study is among the first to foreground patient perspectives in examining how China’s “zero-COVID” policy influenced the continuity and quality of life-sustaining dialysis care. By investigating both the obstacles faced and the coping strategies employed, the findings offer context-specific insights that can inform strategies to enhance dialysis service delivery during future public health emergencies.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive qualitative study design was deemed appropriate for exploring the experiences of patients undergoing MHD during the COVID-19 pandemic. This methodology enables a deep exploration of patients’ perceptions, emotions, and responses to the distinctive challenges posed by this unprecedented public health crisis. Qualitative inquiry is especially valuable for capturing the subtle dimensions of patients’ lived experiences, providing insights that can guide both clinical practice and policy development [21]. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) guidelines [22].

Setting and participants

This study focused exclusively on in-center MHD. Home-based therapies were excluded, as peritoneal dialysis involves distinct domestic workflows beyond the study’s scope, and home hemodialysis was unavailable locally, remaining rare nationwide due to infrastructure, training, and policy limitations. All dialysis treatments in our hospital were conducted within the main hemodialysis unit, as no satellite facilities operate under its management.

Purposive sampling was used to recruit adult participants (≥ 18 years) receiving in-center MHD at a tertiary hospital in Hechuan District, Chongqing, China. Eligible participants were those who underwent dialysis during the COVID-19 pandemic and were members of a hospital-managed WeChat communication group comprising patients and caregivers affiliated with the dialysis unit. This sampling strategy was well-suited to the study’s goal of obtaining detailed, context-rich accounts from individuals with firsthand experience of the phenomenon under investigation [23]. Individuals unable to provide informed consent or participate in interviews due to cognitive impairment were excluded. Recruitment took place through the WeChat group, where a nurse liaison (Author 5) introduced the study and invited eligible members. Those who expressed interest were contacted privately, received detailed study information, and provided written informed consent.

Data collection methods

Both semi-structured and focus group interviews were selected as data collection methods because they represent two of the most widely used approaches in qualitative research, allowing investigators to capitalize on the distinct strengths of each. Semi-structured interviews offer depth and flexibility, facilitating open, individualized discussions that promote personal expression, whereas focus group discussions (FGDs) harness group interaction to generate diverse viewpoints and foster validation of shared experiences. The integration of these methods enriches the depth, breadth, and credibility of the qualitative data collected [24].

Semi-structured interviews served as the primary method of data collection due to their adaptability and ability to yield detailed, meaningful narratives. This approach enables researchers to address predetermined topics while also providing participants the freedom to articulate their experiences and emotions in their own words [25]. The interactive and flexible nature of semi-structured interviews encourages dynamic dialogue, allowing unanticipated themes and insights to emerge beyond the original research scope.

A focus group discussion was also conducted to capture collective insights from participants. This method fostered interaction among participants, enabling them to share experiences and perspectives that enriched the data through dialogue and mutual reflection [26]. The focus group approach was particularly valuable for examining social dynamics and collective responses to the pandemic, offering a broader contextual understanding of individual experiences [24].

The semi-structured interview and focus group discussion guides were developed based on the study objectives, an extensive literature review, and data obtained from a retrospective analysis. Both guides were designed specifically for this study and are publicly available on Figshare, a digital repository for academic resources (see reference [27]). Data for the retrospective review were collected from a WeChat communication group spanning from August 10, 2021 (the earliest retrievable date) to January 8, 2023 (the final adjustment to China’s COVID-19 prevention policies). The group was established to promote communication and information sharing between healthcare providers and patients. It consisted of 199 members, including 26 unit staff (one head of the hemodialysis unit, four physicians, one head nurse, and twenty nursing staff) as well as 173 patients and their caregivers. Both verbal and written communications within the group were reviewed and systematically organized. The initial version of the interview guide was drafted by the research team and subsequently reviewed by the second author (LSJ, PhD, female), an expert in qualitative research, who provided feedback on language precision and conceptual coherence. Revisions were made accordingly to enhance clarity and relevance. Additionally, a pilot interview was conducted with a dialysis patient who was not included in the final sample to assess the clarity, logical flow, and suitability of the questions. Minor refinements were made to improve the structure and phrasing prior to commencing formal data collection. During the study period, the second author was a senior teaching fellow at The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. Given her expertise, prior experience in qualitative research, and previous work in chronic disease management, she led the training of another five novice researchers before the commencement of interviews. This training focused on enhancing their communication skills, ethical considerations, and ability to manage participant dynamics [28–30]. Furthermore, the fifth research member (YQP, RN, female) was a nurse from the hemodialysis department of the study hospital. After the study began, this nurse facilitated communication between the research team and participants.

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (Approval Code: HSEARS20230816005). Before participation, all individuals were provided with both verbal and written explanations outlining the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks and benefits. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant prior to data collection. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were explicitly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without penalty or consequence. In the event of withdrawal, all corresponding data from that participant would have been excluded from the final analysis.

Data collection procedures

Data collection was carried out in two primary phases, which included a retrospective review followed by semi-structured interviews and a subsequent focus group discussion. The procedures for each phase are described in detail below.

Phase 1: semi-structured interviews

Face-to-face individual interviews were conducted at patients’ bedsides during their dialysis sessions. Interviews were scheduled 2–3 days in advance, and each session began with collecting participants’ demographic information, including age, gender, education level, dialysis duration, occupation, type of medical insurance, and family income. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ informed consent. This phase of data collection was completed between November 26 and 27, 2023, by five authors (DH, RN, male; LT, RN, female; WQY, RN, female; YQP, RN, female; ZY, RN, female). A total of ten patients were interviewed, with individual sessions lasting between 22 and 53 min (average duration: 38 min).

Phase 2. Focus group discussion

A single focus group discussion was conducted with six participants who were invited based on their availability. The session took place on December 10, 2023, in a health education room selected for its comfortable and private environment. The facilitator explained the purpose of the discussion and emphasized confidentiality before beginning. The FGD encouraged interactive dialogue, allowing participants to reflect on and respond to one another’s perspectives [31]. Along with recording the discussion, non-verbal cues, such as gestures, tone, and facial expressions, were also observed and documented. The discussion lasted for 82 min.

All participants took part in the interviews, and no repeat sessions were required. Interview recordings were transcribed verbatim and returned to participants for review and validation. Throughout the data collection process, both interviews and the focus group discussion were continuously analyzed to assess thematic saturation. After the tenth individual interview and the completion of the focus group discussion, no new themes or perspectives emerged, indicating that further data collection was unlikely to generate additional insights. This confirmed that data saturation had been achieved, ensuring the findings were both comprehensive and analytically robust [32].

Thematic analysis method

Thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke [33], was utilized to systematically identify, analyze, and report recurring patterns (themes) within the qualitative data. This analytical approach was selected for its flexibility in capturing the depth and complexity of patients’ lived experiences and its appropriateness for exploratory research conducted in relatively understudied contexts. Employing an inductive framework, this method allowed themes to emerge organically from participants’ narratives, ensuring that the analysis was grounded in their authentic perspectives rather than predetermined conceptual categories.

Following Braun and Clarke’s [33] six-phase framework, the thematic analysis was conducted as follows:

Systemic barriers to care during the pandemic

During the pandemic, participants encountered substantial difficulties in obtaining medical care, largely stemming from systemic challenges such as lockdown restrictions (“locked out” and “locked in” situations), transportation disruptions, limited healthcare resources, and the burdens of mass testing and repeated retesting procedures.

Locked out, locked in

Lockdown measures severely restricted participants’ mobility, creating obstacles to accessing healthcare services and heightening psychological distress. Many participants described their daily lives as a constant countdown, overshadowed by the instability brought about by the pandemic. While some urban participants managed to find alternative means of transportation, such as riding bicycles, to attend their dialysis sessions, those in rural regions often had no choice but to walk long distances. Mass testing and retesting

Mandatory COVID-19 testing led to significant treatment delays, as participants were often required to wait for their test results or repeat tests when errors occurred. Discussion

The current findings align with previous studies on dialysis disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic, while also highlighting several China-specific aspects, including systemic barriers, psychological distress, and uneven resource allocation [34, 35]. By incorporating a rich array of illustrative quotations, this study captured the nuanced experiences of patients across urban and rural settings, different age groups, and both genders. The findings underscore not only the vulnerabilities of dialysis recipients but also their remarkable resilience in maintaining treatment amid prolonged lockdowns, repeated testing, and infection concerns. These challenges reflect the compounded burden of kidney failure and the broader impact of a prolonged public health emergency that disrupted access to care, social connections, and emotional stability.

Major challenges in adhering to Dialysis treatment

Public health messaging during the pandemic emphasized the importance of control measures to protect public health and reduce mortality, shaping the context in which individuals receiving dialysis sought care [10]. Domestic and international travel faced strict restrictions, including mandatory quarantines and limitations on movement between urban and rural areas [10]. Policies also involved extensive mass testing campaigns, with entire populations in affected regions tested repeatedly. Individuals who tested positive were required to quarantine, often in designated facilities [9].

Chinese healthcare facilities implemented rigorous infection control measures, including mandatory temperature checks, health screenings, and the use of personal protective equipment. However, the execution of these measures varied across regions, particularly between urban and rural areas [31]. Patients reported heightened anxiety about infection risks during dialysis sessions. The uncertainty surrounding COVID-19 and the potential for exposure in healthcare settings contributed to substantial stress among dialysis patients in China, influenced by regional and systemic factors [36, 37]. These findings highlight that variability in implementation, such as differences in testing pathways and staff redeployment, can significantly affect both access to care and perceptions of safety.

Fear of infection or cross-infection and mental health struggles

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the prevalence of anxiety and depression among maintenance dialysis patients in China ranged from 22.33% to 34.89% for anxiety and 30.02% to 55.1% for depression, with data reported from Yibin [38], Hohhot [39], and Chongqing [40]. In Shenzhen, the rates were 35.6% and 38.9%, respectively [41]. These findings highlight a substantial psychological burden, consistent with Brooks et al. [42], who described quarantine-related distress including fear, frustration, boredom, inadequate supplies, financial loss, and stigma.

In contrast, studies from Western countries reported more limited disruption. Bonenkamp and colleagues, in the Netherlands (DOMESTICO study), found no significant deterioration in patients’ mental health, attributing this stability to continuity of care and robust professional support [43]. Similarly, Noce et al. in the USA reported stable care and emphasized the value of consistent communication and continuity with healthcare staff [44]. These findings demonstrate international heterogeneity: where service continuity and clear communication were maintained, dialysis routines remained relatively stable with minimal psychological decline; where transport restrictions and testing barriers were more pronounced, anxiety and depression were more evident. Such contrasts underscore the critical role of health system capacity and social support structures in shaping psychological outcomes among dialysis patients during the pandemic.

Coping strategies to deal with systematic barriers to access to care, fear of infection and mental health struggles

The fear of contracting COVID-19 has notably impacted the mental health of individuals receiving MHD and influenced their adherence to treatment sessions. Interestingly, some participants in the current study expressed greater concern about complications arising from their underlying kidney disease than about the risks associated with COVID-19. They reported feeling confident in managing physical discomfort, psychological distress, polypharmacy issues, dietary and fluid intake, arteriovenous fistula care, and transportation challenges. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies.

Prior research has also highlighted that individuals receiving MHD employ a variety of coping strategies. Tao and colleagues [45] noted that these patients often experience physical discomforts, including fatigue, pain, and cramping. Participants reported using over-the-counter pain relief medications and engaging in gentle physical activity or light exercise as effective strategies to alleviate discomfort, manage fatigue, and enhance overall well-being [45].

In addition, psychological distress is common among individuals undergoing dialysis, often intensified by the chronic nature of their condition [46]. Various coping techniques are employed, including participation in support groups, which allow patients to share experiences and reduce feelings of isolation. Many patients also practice mindfulness, meditation, or relaxation exercises to manage anxiety and stress [46].

Moreover, individuals receiving MHD often manage multiple medications to treat comorbid conditions. These complex regimens frequently involve taking 10 to 12 medications daily, which increases the risk of confusion, adverse interactions, and poor adherence [47]. To manage these challenges, patients commonly use pill organizers, set reminders, and seek guidance from healthcare providers [48]. While these difficulties are well documented, the COVID-19 pandemic introduced additional significant barriers. Lockdowns and public health restrictions disrupted access to pharmacies and in-person consultations. Studies have reported issues such as medication shortages, pharmacy closures, and limited access to professional guidance [49, 50]. Our findings align with these reports, showing that some patients had to adjust their medication routines, often relying on digital communication with healthcare providers or informal networks to resolve access problems.

Similarly, adhering to dietary and fluid restrictions became more challenging under pandemic conditions. Previous research has emphasized the importance of following renal dietary guidelines, which are often complicated by cultural food preferences and availability [51]. During COVID-19, strict lockdowns and supply chain disruptions affected access to appropriate foods. In Canada and Ethiopia, patients reported reduced availability of renal-friendly foods and limited support from dietitians [16, 19]. Our study mirrored these challenges: some individuals relied on family members who were unfamiliar with dietary requirements, while others restricted intake excessively out of fear of fluid overload or missing dialysis sessions.

Family support, a critical factor in treatment adherence, was also affected by the pandemic. While many patients continued to benefit from strong familial involvement, new constraints emerged. Hospital policies designed to prevent infection often prohibited caregivers from accompanying patients, leaving individuals to attend dialysis sessions alone and potentially heightening feelings of vulnerability [17]. Family caregivers themselves faced increased stress, balancing work, childcare, and concerns about infection [52]. Sousa and colleagues similarly reported that the emotional and logistical burdens on family members intensified during lockdowns [49]. Collectively, these factors undermined the effectiveness of family support during the pandemic, despite its ongoing importance. Evidence from automated peritoneal dialysis with telemedicine monitoring in Mexico demonstrated that structured remote follow-up supported self-management, timely adjustments, and a sense of connection during lockdowns, providing a useful contrast to in-center MHD and highlighting the value of integrated digital supports [20].

Importantly, some studies have examined interventions to support caregivers during the pandemic. For example, a six-week stress management program significantly reduced perceived stress and caregiver burden among family members supporting individuals on hemodialysis [53]. These findings underscore the need for structured psychoeducational interventions to complement informal support systems and protect the well-being of both patients and caregivers during public health emergencies.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, selection bias may be present, as urban participants could overrepresent certain experiences. Second, recall bias is possible because retrospective accounts may lack accuracy. Third, the findings may not reflect the diversity of regions or policies, which limits generalizability. Fourth, the study did not include interviews with families of deceased dialysis patients, restricting the ability to capture the full spectrum of the pandemic’s impact on dialysis recipients and their families. Finally, interviewer positionality may have influenced participants’ openness, although reflexivity statements were used to mitigate this potential effect. Future research should address these limitations by including multiple sites and collecting data longitudinally.

Implications to policy

The findings underscore the urgent need for targeted measures to ensure continuity of dialysis care and provide mental health support during public health emergencies. Drawing on patient experiences and international evidence, three priority recommendations can be identified:

Emergency transport permits for dialysis patients: Patients should be pre-registered with dialysis units and relevant health authorities, with digital or paper permits issued specifically for dialysis days. Establishing a “prioritized entry pathway” or “green channel” at community and hospital checkpoints can help minimize delays caused by lockdown restrictions.Tele‑mental‑health support integrated into dialysis care: Routine screening for anxiety and depression (e.g., using PHQ‑2 and GAD‑2) should be integrated into dialysis visits, with prompt referral to tele-psychology services as needed. Remote counseling, crisis hotlines, and brief CBT-informed sessions can be delivered via phone or digital platforms to provide timely mental health support.Satellite dialysis nodes for rural areas: Pilot county-level satellite centers or mobile dialysis units linked to tertiary hospitals for quality oversight. These centers should provide essential infrastructure, pooled transportation, and tele-supervision protocols to reduce urban–rural disparities and ensure continuity of treatment.

Conclusion

This study illuminated the multifaceted challenges faced by individuals undergoing MHD in China during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly regarding disrupted access to care, psychological distress, and the need for adaptive coping strategies. The findings emphasize the importance of patient-centered approaches that incorporate flexible dialysis scheduling, accessible mental health resources, and community-based support systems. These insights can guide future public health emergency responses to more effectively support this vulnerable population.
