New uniqueness results for a mean field game of controls Thanks: National Science Foundation under NSF Grant DMS-2045027.
P. Jameson Graber, Elizabeth Matter

Abstract
. We propose a new approach to proving the uniqueness of solutions to a certain class of mean field games of controls. In this class, the equilibrium is determined by an aggregate quantity Q ⁡ ( t ) Q(t) , e.g. the market price or production, which then determines optimal trajectories for agents. Our approach consists in analyzing the relationship between Q ⁡ ( t ) Q(t) and corresponding optimal trajectories to find conditions under which there is at most one equilibrium. We show that our conditions do not match those prescribed by the Lasry-Lions monotonicity condition, nor even displacement monotonicity, but they do apply to economic models that have been proposed in the literature.



1.
Introduction

Mean Field Games (MFG) were introduced in
[
LL07
]
to describe Nash Equilibria of a large population of players in differential games. Each individual seeks to optimize their outcome, while playing against the distribution of all the other players. In a mean field game of controls, each player’s cost depends on the distribution of both states and controls. One motivating example we consider in this paper is the Cournot mean field game of controls, a model of the production of an exhaustible resource by a continuum of producers. Much recent work has been done with this model, e.g. in
[
CLT24
,
CS17
,
CS14
,
GB18
,
GS23
,
HHS10
,
JGIN21
,
Kob22b
,
Kob22a
]
.

An important question in mean field games is if there exists a unique Nash Equilibrium. Lasry and Lions introduced the
Lasry-Lions monotonicity condition
as a sufficient condition to show uniqueness of equilibria. This condition has been the most popular criterion for establishing uniqueness in the literature on mean field games. In fact, it has also been shown in situations where the Lasry-Lions condition does not hold, there is non-uniqueness of equilibria for long time horizon
[
BF19
]
. In such a case, one way to recover uniqueness is by limiting the time horizon. However, in this paper we will focus on showing uniqueness for an arbitrarily long time horizon. For mean field games of controls, Cardaliaguet and Lehalle in
[
CL18
]
proved uniqueness under the Lasry-Lions condition. More recently
displacement monotonicity
has also been used in proving uniqueness (see
[
GM22
,
GMMZ22
,
MM24
,
BGY19
,
BGY23
,
Ahu16
]
). In
[
GM23
]
, the authors introduced two new types of monotonicity, and showed each of the four conditions were in dichotomy with one another. Since none of these conditions can fully describe all classes of mean field games which have a unique equilibrium, it is natural to ask what doors these new monotonicity conditions open that were closed before.

In this paper we further explore the monotonicity condition
(\Sigma)
used in
[
GM23
]
.
Conceptually, condition
(\Sigma)
applies to any mean field game in which Nash equilibrium is equivalent to finding a trajectory, which we call here
Q(t)
, that is a fixed point of the map

Q(t)\mapsto\text{distribution of optimal states and controls}\ \mu_{Q}(t)\mapsto F(\mu_{Q}(t)).

(Think, for example, of market price or aggregate production.)
If the
error map

E[Q]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=Q-F(\mu_{Q}(\cdot))
is strictly monotone, then the Nash equilibrium is unique.
We prove that this monotonicity condition is met under certain assumptions and show that examples under these assumptions do not have to meet the Lasry-Lions or even displacement monotonicity condition.
The assumptions are met by a class of examples inspired by the Cournot competition model from
[
CS17
]
, in which the Nash equilibrium is given by a market clearing condition.

The rest of this introduction will be spent describing the model we plan to analyze. In Section
2
we will show sufficient conditions under which the error map is monotone. It is convenient to consider separately two cases.
In the first case, the Lagrangian does not depend on the position variable, and we find that the game has a special structure that we exploit to prove uniqueness in a surprisingly simple way; the second case is more general. We conclude the paper with the crucial Section
3
, which is devoted to examples. We show that mean field games such as the one considered in
[
CS17
]
satisfy the
(\Sigma)
condition but are not Lasry-Lions monotone. This observation leads us to believe that recent results on Cournot competition
[
JGIN21
,
GS23
]
could be improved by dispensing with the “smallness condition” used to prove uniqueness. Nevertheless, in this paper we deal only with first-order models without boundary conditions or state constraints, thus avoiding the heavy technicalities and focusing solely on the issue of monotonicity.

1.1.
Description of the Model

Our goal is to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions to the following system:

(1.1a)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}u+H(x,D_{x}u,Q)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{in}~{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times(0,T),

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m-\nabla\cdot\mathinner{\left(D_{p}H(x,D_{x}u,Q)\right)}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{in}~{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times(0,T),

(1.1c)

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}D_{p}H(x,D_{x}u(x,t),Q(t))m(x,t)\dif x

\displaystyle=Q(t)

\displaystyle\text{in}~(0,T),

(1.1d)

\displaystyle m(x,t)=m_{0}(x),\quad u(x,T)

\displaystyle=g(x),

\displaystyle\text{in}~{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

System (
1.1
) is a type of MFG of controls, inspired by market competition models. From the Lagrangian
L
, we define the Hamiltonian
H
by

(1.2)

H(x,p,Q)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathinner{\left(p\cdot v-L(x,v,Q)\right)}.

It corresponds to a game in which players seek to minimize

(1.3)

\int_{0}^{T}L\mathinner{\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t),Q(t)\right)}\dif t+g(x(T))

treating the function
Q(t)
as given.
Equation (
1.1c
) is the equilibrium condition.
It says that
Q(t)
is the average of all optimal velocities played against
Q(t)
itself (e.g. the aggregate equilibrium production).
An alternative way to determine
Q(t)
is to compute optimal trajectories using the Euler-Lagrange equations:

(1.4a)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{t}}D_{v}L\mathinner{\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t),Q(t)\right)}

\displaystyle=D_{x}L\mathinner{\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t),Q(t)\right)},

(1.4b)

\displaystyle x(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0},

(1.4c)

\displaystyle D_{v}L\mathinner{\left(x(T),\dot{x}(T),Q(T)\right)}

\displaystyle=-Dg(x(T)).

The solution of System (
1.4
) will be denoted
x(t;x_{0},Q)
. The assumptions in Section
2
will ensure that the Euler-Lagrange equations are solvable, in particular the convexity of
L
with respect to the pair
(x,v)
. We realize such an assumption will limit the generality of the result. Nevertheless, we are able to prove uniqueness in cases that have already been proposed in the literature for which Lasry-Lions and Displacement Monotonicity may not be applicable.

Note that
x(t;x_{0},Q)
depends on
Q
is a nonlocal way, i.e. not on
Q(t)
but on the entire trajectory
Q\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Equation (
1.1c
) is equivalent to

(1.5)

Q(t)=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dot{x}(t;x_{0},Q)\dif m_{0}(x_{0}).

Define the error map
E[Q]
to be

(1.6)

E[Q](t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=Q(t)+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dot{x}(t;x_{0},Q)\dif m_{0}(x_{0}).

This is the map which shows how far
Q
is from being a fixed point of the right hand side of equation (
1.5
). In order to solve System (
1.1
), we first solve (
1.5
) by arguing that the error map is strictly monotone with respect to
Q
in
L^{2}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

2.
Monotonicity of the Error Map

2.1.
When the Lagrangian does not depend on
x

We aim to show the monotonicity of
E
first for the case where there is no
x
dependence in the Lagrangian. This case has interesting properties and is relevant to the Cournot model where the Lagrangian is
L(v,Q)=vP(-v+\varepsilon Q)
, which we will discuss further in Section
3
. We will consider Lagrangians which meet the following assumptions.

Assumption 2.1
.

The Lagrangian
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{2}
and satisfies the following:

•

L(v,Q)
is strictly convex with respect to
v
.

•

\mathinner{\left(D^{2}_{vv}L(v,Q)\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(-D^{2}_{vQ}L(v,Q)\right)}+I>0
for all
v,Q
. (We say an
n\times n
matrix
A>0
in the sense that
x^{T}Ax>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathinner{\left\{0\right\}}
. We denote by
I
the identity matrix.)

Lemma 2.2
.

If
L
satisfies
2.1
, then there exists a unique solution of the Euler-Lagrange equations (
1.4
). Its velocity is given by

(2.1)

\dot{x}(t;x_{0},Q)=D_{p}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q(t)).

Proof.

If
L
does not depend on
x(t)
then (
1.4
) becomes

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{t}}D_{v}L\mathinner{\left(\dot{x}(t),Q(t)\right)}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.2b)

\displaystyle x(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0},

(2.2c)

\displaystyle D_{v}L\mathinner{\left(\dot{x}(T),Q(T)\right)}

\displaystyle=-Dg(x(T)).

Then
D_{v}L\mathinner{\left(\dot{x}(t),Q(t)\right)}=-Dg(x(T))
, and because
D^{2}_{vv}L\mathinner{\left(\dot{x}(t),Q(t)\right)}>0

(2.3)

\dot{x}(t;x_{0},Q)=D_{p}H(-Dg(x(T)),Q(t)).

Now we show the dependence of
x(T)
on
x_{0}
and
Q
.

(2.4)

x(T)-x(0)=\int_{0}^{T}\dot{x}(t;x_{0},Q)dt=\int_{0}^{T}D_{p}H(-Dg(x(T)),Q(t))\dif t

so we define
x_{T}(x_{0},Q)=x(T;x_{0},Q)
implicitly by the following equation

(2.5)

x_{T}(x_{0},Q)-\int_{0}^{T}D_{p}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q(t))\dif t=x_{0}.

Note this is solvable as the left side of (
2.5
) is a monotone function of
x_{T}
by Assumption
2.1
.
Therefore the optimal velocity played against
Q(t)
is given by (
2.1
).
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
D^{2}_{pQ}H(p,Q)+I>0
for all
p,Q
. Then if
Q(t)
solves

(2.6)

Q(t)=-\int D_{p}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q(t))\dif m_{0}(x_{0}),

Q(t)
is constant.

Proof.

Suppose
Q(t)
solves equation (
2.6
). Since
x_{T}
depends on the entire trajectory of
Q
, not
Q(t)
, differentiating with respect to
t
yields

(2.7)

\dot{Q}(t)=-\int D^{2}_{pQ}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q(t))\dot{Q}(t)\dif m_{0}(x_{0}).

This becomes

(2.8)

\mathinner{\left(I+\int D^{2}_{pQ}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q(t))\dif m_{0}(x_{0})\right)}\dot{Q}(t)=0.

So if
D^{2}_{pQ}H(p,Q)>-I
, then
Q(t)
must be constant.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Since
D^{2}_{pQ}H(p,Q)=\mathinner{\left(D^{2}_{vv}L(v,Q)\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(-D^{2}_{vQ}L(v,Q)\right)}>-I
for
v=D_{p}H(p,Q)
, the optimal control
Q(t)
under the assumptions
2.1
must be constant.

Now we call
Q(t)=Q
for constant
Q
. Our next step is to show the monotonicity of the map

(2.9)

E[Q]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=Q+\int D_{p}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q)\dif m_{0}(x_{0}).

Lemma 2.5
.

If
D_{pQ}^{2}H(p,Q)>-I
for all
p,Q
, and
L
satisfies the assumptions of
2.1
,

D_{Q}x_{T}(x_{0},Q)=\mathinner{\left(I+TD^{2}_{pp}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q)D^{2}g(x_{T}(x_{0},Q))\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(TD^{2}_{pQ}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q)\right)}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
,
Q
is constant so equation (
2.5
) becomes

(2.10)

x_{T}(x_{0},Q)-TD_{p}H(-Dg(x_{T}(x_{0},Q)),Q)=x_{0}.

Taking the implicit partial derivative with respect to
Q
yields

(2.11)

D_{Q}x_{T}+TD^{2}_{pp}H(-Dg(x_{T}),Q)D^{2}g(x_{T})D_{Q}x_{T}-TD^{2}_{pQ}H(-Dg(x_{T}),Q)=0.

Solving for
D_{Q}x_{T}
completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose
L
satisfies the assumptions of
2.1
, and
D^{2}g>0
. Then
E
is monotone in the sense that for all
Q_{0},Q_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
Q_{0}\not=Q_{1}
,

(2.12)

\langle E[Q_{1}]-E[Q_{0}],Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle>0

Proof.

Let
Q_{1},Q_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then

(2.13)

\langle E[Q_{1}]-E[Q_{0}],Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle=\langle DE[\tilde{Q}](Q_{1}-Q_{0}),Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle

for some
\tilde{Q}
between
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
. Using Lemma
2.5
,

(2.14)

\begin{split}DE[\tilde{Q}]&=I+\int\mathinner{\left(D_{pQ}^{2}H(-Dg(x_{T}),\tilde{Q})-D^{2}_{pp}H(-Dg(x_{T}),\tilde{Q})D^{2}g(x_{T})D_{Q}x_{T}\right)}\dif m_{0}(x_{0})\\
&=I+\int\mathinner{\left(I+TD^{2}_{pp}H(-Dg(x_{T}),\tilde{Q})D^{2}g(x_{T})\right)}^{-1}D^{2}_{pQ}H(-Dg(x_{T}),\tilde{Q})\dif m_{0}(x_{0})\end{split}

Now since
D^{2}_{pQ}H(p,Q)>-I
and
D^{2}g(x_{T})>0
,

(2.15)

\langle E[Q_{1}]-E[Q_{0}],Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle>0

for all
Q_{1},Q_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
Q_{1}\not=Q_{0}
.
∎

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose
L
satisfies the assumptions of
2.1
,
D^{2}_{vv}L(v,Q)\leq MI
for some
M<\infty
, and
D^{2}g\geq cI>0
. Then
E
is monotone in the sense that for all
Q_{1},Q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(2.16)

\langle E[Q_{1}]-E[Q_{0}],Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle>\mathinner{\left(1-\delta\right)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2},

where
\delta<1
.

Proof.

We make use of the proof of Theorem
2.6
through equation (
2.14
).

Now since
D^{2}_{vv}L(v,Q)\leq MI
,
D^{2}_{pp}H(p,Q)\geq\frac{1}{M}I>0
. We also have that
D^{2}_{pQ}H(p,Q)>-I
and
D^{2}g(x_{T})\geq cI>0
, so

(2.17)

\langle E[Q_{1}]-E[Q_{0}],Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle>\mathinner{\left(1-\frac{1}{1+T\frac{c}{M}}\right)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2}.

Thus (
2.16
) holds with
\displaystyle\delta=\frac{1}{1+T\frac{c}{M}}
.
∎

2.2.
When the Lagrangian depends on
x

Now we treat the case where the Lagrangian does depend on
x(t)
. As before our goal is to compute the derivative of
E
. However, because of the
x
-dependence, we cannot explicitly solve for the optimal velocity of the Euler-Lagrange equations. Instead we use the following approach:

Suppose that
y(t),\dot{y}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy the following equations:

(2.18)

y(t)=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\delta x}{\delta Q}\mathinner{\left(t,x_{0},Q\right)}(s)\tilde{Q}(s)\dif s

and

(2.19)

\dot{y}(t)=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\delta\dot{x}}{\delta Q}\mathinner{\left(t,x_{0},Q\right)}(s)\tilde{Q}(s)\dif s.

Then letting
X(t)=(x(t),\dot{x}(t),Q(t))
and taking the derivative with respect to
Q
of (
1.4
), we have the system:

(2.20)

\begin{split}\dfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{t}}\mathinner{\left[D_{vx}^{2}L\mathinner{\left(X(t)\right)}y(t)+D_{vv}^{2}L(X(t))\dot{y}(t)+D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(t))\tilde{Q}(t)\right]}&=D_{xx}^{2}L\mathinner{\left(X(t)\right)}y(t)+D_{xv}^{2}L(X(t))\dot{y}(t)\\
&+D_{xQ}^{2}L(X(t))\tilde{Q}(t),\\
y(0)&=0,\\
D_{vx}^{2}L\mathinner{\left(X(T)\right)}y(T)+D_{vv}^{2}L(X(T))\dot{y}(T)+D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(T))\tilde{Q}(T)&=-D^{2}g(x(T))y(T).\end{split}

Now we seek to show the existence and uniqueness of
x(t)
which solves (
1.4
) then use estimates on
y(t)
from (
2.20
) to tell us about the derivative of the error map. Before we proceed we make the following assumptions on
L
:

Assumption 2.8
.

The Lagrangian
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{2}
and satisfies:

•

D_{x}L(x,v,Q)\not=0

•

D^{2}_{(x,v)}L\geq c>0
.

•

c^{2}>2M^{2}
, where
M=\max\mathinner{\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(t))\right\rVert}_{L_{\infty}},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D_{xQ}^{2}L(X(t))\right\rVert}_{L_{\infty}}\right\}}
.

Remark 2.9
.

Assumption
2.8
means that the Lagrangian’s dependence on
Q
does not overpower its convexity with respect to
(x,v)
. This does not necessarily imply small dependence on
Q
, since in some applications
c
need not be small.

Lemma 2.10
.

If
L
satisfies Assumption
2.8
and
D^{2}g(x)\geq 0
, there exists a unique
x(t)
which solves (
1.4
).
Likewise, given
x(t)
, there exists a unique solution
y(t)
which solves (
2.20
).

Proof.

In order to prove this theorem, we make use of results from Chapter 6 of Cannarsa and Sinestrari’s book
[
CS04
]
. The strict convexity of
L
and
D^{2}g(x)\geq 0
imply the criteria that ensure there exists a solution satisfying the Euler-Lagrange Equations. Additionally the strict convexity of
L
means that such a solution is unique.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

Suppose
L
satisfies Assumption
2.8
and
D^{2}g(x)\geq 0
. If
y(t)
solves the system (
2.20
) then for
\varepsilon>0
small enough

(2.21)

\mathinner{\left(c-2M^{2}\varepsilon\right)}\int_{0}^{T}|\dot{y}(t)|^{2}\dif t\leq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}|\tilde{Q}(t)|^{2}\dif t.

Proof.

By multiplying the first equation of system (
2.20
) by
y(t)^{T}
on the left, integrating with respect to
t
, and applying the boundary conditions we get the following equation:

(2.22)

\begin{split}-y(T)^{T}D^{2}g(x(T))y(T)-\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left[\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vx}^{2}L(X(t))y(t)+\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vv}^{2}L(X(t))\dot{y}(t)+\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(t))\tilde{Q}(t)\right]}\dif t\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left[y(t)^{T}D_{xx}^{2}L(X(t))y(t)+y(t)^{T}D_{xv}^{2}L(X(t))\dot{y}(t)+y(t)^{T}D_{xQ}^{2}L(X(t))\tilde{Q}(t)\right]}\dif t\end{split}

Now by rearranging we have

(2.23)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left[y(t)^{T}D_{xx}^{2}L(X(t))y(t)+y(t)^{T}D_{xv}^{2}L(X(t))\dot{y}(t)+\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vx}^{2}L(X(t))y(t)+\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vv}^{2}L(X(t))\dot{y}(t)\right]}\dif t\\

Since
L
is uniformly convex in
x
and
v
,

(2.24)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left[y(t)^{T}D_{xx}^{2}L(X(t))y(t)+y(t)^{T}D_{xv}^{2}L(X(t))\dot{y}(t)+\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vx}^{2}L(X(t))y(t)+\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vv}^{2}L(X(t))\dot{y}(t)\right]}\dif t\\
\geq c\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dot{y}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert y(t)\right\rvert}^{2}\right)}\dif t.\end{split}

Also, note

(2.25)

\begin{split}&-\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left[\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(t))\tilde{Q}(t)+y(t)^{T}D_{xQ}^{2}L(X(t))\tilde{Q}(t)\right]}\dif t\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathinner{\left(\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(t))+y(t)^{T}D_{xQ}^{2}L(X(t))\right)}\tilde{Q}(t)\right\rvert}\dif t\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left[\varepsilon\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(t))+y(t)^{T}D_{xQ}^{2}L(X(t))\right\rvert}^{2}+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tilde{Q}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}\right]}\dif t.\end{split}

Putting the inequalities together,

(2.26)

\begin{split}c\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dot{y}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert y(t)\right\rvert}^{2}\right)}\dif t&+y(T)^{T}D^{2}g(x(T))y(T)\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left[\varepsilon\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dot{y}(t)^{T}D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(t))+y(t)^{T}D_{xQ}^{2}L(X(t))\right\rvert}^{2}+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tilde{Q}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}\right]}\dif t.\end{split}

Since
M=\max\mathinner{\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D_{vQ}^{2}L(X(t))\right\rVert}_{L_{\infty}},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D_{xQ}^{2}L(X(t))\right\rVert}_{L_{\infty}}\right\}}<\infty
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

. The inequality becomes

(2.27)

\mathinner{\left(c-2M^{2}\varepsilon\right)}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dot{y}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert y(t)\right\rvert}^{2}\right)}\dif t+y(T)^{T}D^{2}g(x(T))y(T)\leq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tilde{Q}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}\dif t

∎

Now we can use the estimates on
\dot{y}(t)
to prove the monotonicity of the error map.

Theorem 2.12
.

If
L
satisfies Assumption
2.8
and
D^{2}g\geq 0
, then
E
is monotone in the sense that for all
Q_{0}(t),Q_{1}(t)\in L^{2}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

(2.28)

\langle E[Q_{1}]-E[Q_{0}],Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle\geq(1-\delta)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2}

for some
\delta<1
.

Proof.

(2.29)

\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left(E[Q_{1}](t)-E[Q_{0}](t)\right)}\cdot\mathinner{\left(Q_{1}(t)-Q_{0}(t)\right)}\dif t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}DE[Q_{\lambda}](t)\mathinner{\left(Q_{1}(t)-Q_{0}(t)\right)}\dif\lambda\cdot\mathinner{\left(Q_{1}(t)-Q_{0}(t)\right)}\dif t

where
Q_{\lambda}=\lambda Q_{1}+(1-\lambda)Q_{0}
. The integral becomes

(2.30)

\begin{split}&=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\delta\dot{x}}{\delta Q}\mathinner{\left(t,x_{0},Q_{\lambda}\right)}(s)\mathinner{\left(Q_{1}(s)-Q_{0}(s)\right)}\dif s\dif\lambda\cdot\mathinner{\left(Q_{1}(t)-Q_{0}(t)\right)}\dif t\\
&=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}\dot{y}(t,x_{0},Q_{\lambda})\dif\lambda\cdot\mathinner{\left(Q_{1}(t)-Q_{0}(t)\right)}\dif t\\
&\geq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dot{y}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Q_{1}(t)-Q_{0}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}\right]}\dif t\dif\lambda\\
&\geq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\left(1+c^{*}\right)}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Q_{1}(t)-Q_{0}(t)\right\rvert}^{2}\dif t\dif\lambda\\
&=\mathinner{\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\left(1+c^{*}\right)}\right)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2}\end{split}

So
E
is monotone if
c^{*}<1
, where
c^{*}=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon\mathinner{\left(c-2M^{2}\varepsilon\right)}}
from (
2.27
). By Assumption
2.8
,
c^{2}>2M^{2}
, so by taking
\varepsilon=\frac{c}{4M^{2}}
we get
c^{*}=\frac{2M^{2}}{c^{2}}<1
.
∎

2.3.
Existence and Uniqueness

The monotonicity of the error map, combined with standard results on uniqueness for Hamilton-Jacobi equations and continuity equations (see, for instance,
[
Car13
, Section 4]
), immediately implies uniqueness of solutions to the mean field game of controls (
1.1
).
For completeness, in this section we collect two results that show the system is well-posed.

Theorem 2.13
.

Suppose the Lagrangian
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{2}
and satisfies Assumption
2.1
,
D^{2}_{vv}L(v,Q)\leq MI
for some
M<\infty
, and
D^{2}g\geq cI
for some
c>0
. Then there exists a unique solution to system (
1.1
).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.6
, the error map
E\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as defined in (
1.6
) satisfies the condition that for all
Q_{1},Q_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(2.31)

\langle E[Q_{1}]-E[Q_{0}],Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle>\mathinner{\left(1-\delta\right)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{0}\right\rVert}^{2},

where
\delta<1
. Notice this implies
E
is strictly monotone and coercive.
E
is also bounded and continuous. Applying Minty-Browder theorem yields existence and uniqueness of a fixed point
Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of (
1.5
). Then by standard results there exists a unique solution to to system (
1.1
). In particular, the Hamilton-Jacobi equation has a classical solution and the continuity equation has a solution in the sense of distributions.
∎

Theorem 2.14
.

Suppose the Lagrangian
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}

By Theorem

3.
Examples and Counterexamples

We use this section to emphasize the dichotomy between the monotonicity condition used in this paper with the Lasry-Lions condition and even displacement monotonicity. Due to the simplicity of this first example, we are able to explicitly solve for the optimal trajectory and then the fixed point
Q(t)
.

Example 3.1
.

Suppose
L(\dot{x},Q)=\ell(-\dot{x}+\varepsilon Q)
,
D^{2}\ell>0
, and
D^{2}g\geq 0
, then
Q(t)
which solves

(3.1)

Q(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}-\dot{x}(t;x_{0},Q)\dif m_{0}(x_{0})

is unique.

The Euler Lagrange equations for this case are:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{t}}\mathinner{\left[-D\ell(-\dot{x}(t)+\varepsilon Q(t))\right]}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle x(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0},

(3.4)

\displaystyle-D\ell(-\dot{x}(T)+\varepsilon Q(T))

\displaystyle=-Dg(x(T)).

This implies

(3.5)

-D\ell(-\dot{x}(t)+\varepsilon Q(t))=-Dg(x(T)).

Then since
D^{2}\ell>0
,
D\ell
is invertible, so

(3.6)

-\dot{x}(t)+\varepsilon Q(t)=D\ell^{-1}(Dg(x(T))).

To solve for
x(T)
, take the integral over
\mathinner{\left[0,T\right]}
to get

(3.7)

\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}(x(T))=\int_{0}^{T}\varepsilon Q(s)\dif s+x_{0}.

So

(3.8)

x(T)=\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(\int_{0}^{T}\varepsilon Q(s)\dif s+x_{0}\right)},

where invertibility comes from
D^{2}\ell,D^{2}g\geq 0
. Now

(3.9)

-\dot{x}(t)=-\varepsilon Q(t)+D\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\circ\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(\int_{0}^{T}\varepsilon Q(s)\dif s+x_{0}\right)}.

Next we solve for
Q
such that

(3.10)

Q(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathinner{\left[-\varepsilon Q(t)+D\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\circ\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(\int_{0}^{T}\varepsilon Q(s)\dif s+x_{0}\right)}\right]}\dif m_{0}(x_{0}).

We see

(3.11)

(1+\varepsilon)Q(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathinner{\left[D\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\circ\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(\int_{0}^{T}\varepsilon Q(s)\dif s+x_{0}\right)}\right]}\dif m_{0}(x_{0}).

Note that the right side of the equation has no dependence on
t
, therefore
Q(t)
must be constant. Applying this fact and rearranging we see

(3.12)

\begin{split}0=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathinner{\left[D\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\circ\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(T\varepsilon Q+x_{0}\right)}-(1+\varepsilon)Q\right]}\dif m_{0}(x_{0})\\
=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathinner{\left[-\frac{1}{T\varepsilon}\mathinner{\left((1+\varepsilon)I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}\circ\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(T\varepsilon Q+x_{0}\right)}+\frac{1+\varepsilon}{T\varepsilon}x_{0}\right]}\dif m_{0}(x_{0})\end{split}

Note
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathinner{\left((1+\varepsilon)I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}\circ\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}^{-1}
is continuous, strictly monotone and coercive, so by the Minty-Browder theorem our solution is unique and we get

(3.13)

Q=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{T\varepsilon}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\left(I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}\circ\mathinner{\left((1+\varepsilon)I+TD\ell^{-1}\circ Dg\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left((1+\varepsilon)x_{0}\right)}-x_{0}\right)}\dif m_{0}(x_{0})

Remark 3.2
.

For a counter-example showing
L(\dot{x},Q)=\ell\mathinner{\left(-\dot{x}+\varepsilon Q\right)}
is not in general Lasry-Lions monotone under the same assumptions, see Section (3.1) of
[
GM23
]
.

The next example uses the Cournot model. Previous papers such as
[
GS23
]
have required
\varepsilon
to be very small to guarantee uniqueness of solutions, but we show monotonicity of

which give uniqueness without requiring
\varepsilon
to be so small.

Example 3.3
.

Let
s\in\mathinner{\left(-1,0\right)}
, and let
P
be given by

P^{\prime}(q)=-q^{s}.

The Lagrangian
L(q,Q)=-qP(q+\varepsilon Q)
is not Lasry-Lions monotone or displacement monotone. However, the error map
E
from (
1.6
) is monotone provided
\varepsilon<2
.

To see that
E
is monotone, first note the following:

(3.14)

P^{\prime}(q)<0,\ P^{\prime\prime}(q)\geq 0,\ P^{\prime}(q)+qP^{\prime\prime}(q)<0.

We now see that
L
is strictly convex in
q
:

(3.15)

\begin{split}D^{2}_{qq}L(q,Q)&=-2P^{\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)-qP^{\prime\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)\\
&\geq-2P^{\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)-(q+\varepsilon Q)P^{\prime\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)\\
&>0.\end{split}

Next, we show
D^{2}_{pQ}H(p,Q)>-1
. Setting
q=D_{p}H(p,Q)
we have

(3.16)

\begin{split}D^{2}_{pQ}H(p,Q)&=-\frac{D_{qQ}^{2}L(q,Q)}{D_{qq}^{2}L(q,Q)}\\
&=-\frac{\varepsilon\mathinner{\left(-P^{\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)-qP^{\prime\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)\right)}}{-2P^{\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)-qP^{\prime\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)}\\
&\geq-\frac{\varepsilon\mathinner{\left(-P^{\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)-qP^{\prime\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)\right)}}{2\mathinner{\left(-P^{\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)-qP^{\prime\prime}(q+\varepsilon Q)\right)}}\\
&=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}>-1.\end{split}

Now we can say by Theorem
2.6
,

(3.17)

\langle E[Q_{1}]-E[Q_{0}],Q_{1}-Q_{0}\rangle>0.

To see that
L
is not Lasry-Lions Monotone, we show that the sign of

(3.18)

-\int_{q\geq 0}q\mathinner{\left(P\mathinner{\left(q+\varepsilon\int\beta\dif\mu_{1}(\beta)\right)}-P\mathinner{\left(q+\varepsilon\int\beta\dif\mu_{2}(\beta)\right)}\right)}\dif\mathinner{\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)}(q)

changes depending on choice of
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
. We see (
3.18
) becomes

(3.19)

-M\int\int_{0}^{1}qP^{\prime}\mathinner{\left(q+M_{\lambda}\right)}\dif\lambda\dif\mathinner{\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)}(q)

where
M=\varepsilon\int\beta\dif\mathinner{\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)}(\beta)
and
M_{\lambda}=\varepsilon\int\beta\dif\mathinner{\left(\lambda\mu_{1}-(1-\lambda)\mu_{2}\right)}(\beta)
. Now define

(3.20)

F(q)=-M\int_{0}^{1}qP^{\prime}\mathinner{\left(q+M_{\lambda}\right)}\dif\lambda.

Suppose
\mu_{1}=c_{1}\delta_{q_{1}}+(1-c_{1})\delta_{\overline{q_{1}}}
and
\mu_{2}=\delta_{q_{2}}
. Then (
3.18
) becomes

(3.21)

c_{1}F(q_{1})+(1-c_{1})F(\overline{q_{1}})-F(q_{2}).

We see that for our choice of
P
, and
M>0
,
F
is increasing and concave for
0<q
. Then choose
0<q_{1}<q_{2}<\overline{q_{1}}
so
q_{2}=tq_{1}+(1-t)\overline{q_{1}}
for some
t\in\mathinner{\left(0,1\right)}
. Since
F
is concave,

(3.22)

F(q_{2})>tF(q_{1})+(1-t)F(\overline{q_{1}}).

Then for
c_{1}\in\mathinner{\left(0,t\right)}

(3.23)

\begin{split}c_{1}q_{1}+(1-c_{1})\overline{q_{1}}&=\overline{q_{1}}-c_{1}(\overline{q_{1}}-q_{1})\\
&>\overline{q_{1}}-t(\overline{q_{1}}-q_{1})=q_{2}\end{split}

so that
M=\varepsilon\mathinner{\left(c_{1}q_{1}+(1-c_{1})\overline{q_{1}}-q_{2}\right)}>0
. Now for choice of
c_{1}
close enough to
t
we get

(3.24)

c_{1}F(q_{1})+(1-c_{1})F(\overline{q_{1}})-F(q_{2})<0.

To see that (
3.18
) could be positive, take
\mu_{1}=\delta_{q_{1}}
and
\mu_{2}=\delta_{q_{2}}
where
0<q_{2}<q_{1}
so that
M>0
, then since
F
is increasing
F(q_{1})-F(q_{2})>0
.

By a similar argument we can see that
L
is not displacement monotone. Let
\xi^{1},\xi^{2}
be random variables with
\mathcal{L}_{\xi^{i}}=\mu_{i}
. Supposing
Q_{i}=\mathbb{E}[\xi^{i}]=\int\xi\dif\mu_{i}(\xi)
, we aim to show that the sign of

(3.25)

\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\left[\mathinner{\left(D_{q}L(\xi^{1},Q_{1})-D_{q}(\xi^{2},Q_{2})\right)}\mathinner{\left(\xi^{1}-\xi^{2}\right)}\right]}

changes depending on choice of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
. Now (
3.25
) becomes

(3.26)

\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\left[\mathinner{\left(-\xi^{1}P^{\prime}(\xi^{1}+\varepsilon Q_{1})-P(\xi^{1}+\varepsilon Q_{1})+\xi^{2}P^{\prime}(\xi^{2}+\varepsilon Q_{2})+P(\xi^{2}+\varepsilon Q_{2})\right)}\mathinner{\left(\xi^{1}-\xi^{2}\right)}\right]}

Suppose for some
q_{1},\overline{q_{1}},q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mu_{1}=\lambda\delta_{q_{1}}+(1-\lambda)\delta_{\overline{q_{1}}}
such that

, and
\mu_{2}=\delta_{q_{2}}
. Then define

(3.27)

F(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-xP^{\prime}(x+\varepsilon y)-P(x+\varepsilon y).

Then (
3.25

(3.28)

\mathinner{\left(\lambda F(q_{1},q_{\lambda})+(1-\lambda)F(\overline{q_{1}},q_{\lambda})-F(q_{2},q_{2})\right)}(q_{\lambda}-q_{2})

where

is increasing and strictly concave with respect to the first variable and continuous with respect to the second variable. Fix

, and choose

For

\begin{split}\lambda q_{1}+(1-\lambda)\overline{q_{1}}&=\overline{q_{1}}-\lambda(\overline{q_{1}}-q_{1})\\
&>\overline{q_{1}}-t(\overline{q_{1}}-q_{1})=q_{2}.\end{split}

Now by the strict concavity of

in the first variable and the continuity of

in the second variable, we may choose

close enough to

Therefore combining the inequalities in (

\mathinner{\left(\lambda F(q_{1},q_{\lambda})+(1-\lambda)F(\overline{q_{1}},q_{\lambda})-F(q_{2},q_{2})\right)}(q_{\lambda}-q_{2})<0.

3.25

On the other hand, if we choose any

such that

, we get that (

\int\mathinner{\left(L\mathinner{\left(x,v,\int a\dif\mu_{1}(\xi,a)\right)}-L\mathinner{\left(x,v,\int a\dif\mu_{2}(\xi,a)\right)}\right)}\dif\mathinner{\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)}(x,v)

.

Example 3.7
