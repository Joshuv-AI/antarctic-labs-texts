Analysis of the mechanism of roof water hazards caused by fault activation
Authors: Xingyu Miao, Wenping Li, Qiqing Wang, Guojie Ma, Yuchu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFault activation is a common phenomenon during coal seam mining in proximity to faults. To better understand the mechanism of roof water hazards caused by fault activation, numerical simulation was employed. By coupling FLAC3D with PFC3D, this simulation is mainly analyzed from four aspects: the number of contact forces, the evolution of vertical displacement, the development of the plastic zone, and vertical stress. Based on field test and numerical simulation, this study reveals that fault activation becomes more intense as the working face advances from the hanging wall to the footwall of the fault. In addition, fault activation exhibits distinct phases, each characterized by different mechanical behaviors. Furthermore, combined with the decreasing trend in the number of contact forces, the height of the water-conducting fracture zone shows a pattern of initial increase followed by subsequent decrease. These findings reveal that analyzing the mechanism of roof water hazards caused by fault activation provides a theoretical basis and valuable insights for preventing and controlling roof water hazards during coal mining operations near faults.Keywords: Fault activation, Roof water hazards, Coupling FLAC3D with PFC3DSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Natural hazards

Introduction

With the increasing intensity of coal mining and the continuous extension of mining depth, the threat of water hazards to safe coal extraction has become increasingly prominent1–3. Among various water hazards, roof water hazards are one of the most prevalent types in Chinese coal mining practices, often leading to sudden roof water inrush accidents that seriously affect mine production safety4. Statistics show that approximately 80% of water inrush incidents are closely related to fault activation induced by mining disturbances5,6. Notably, the activation of faults during coal extraction is not a random process but is closely associated with the engineering geological conditions of the mining area, such as rock mass properties, fault occurrence characteristics, and in-situ stress state7–9. Therefore, studying the mechanism of fault activation under specific engineering geological backgrounds is crucial for improving the prevention and control level of mine water hazards and ensuring safe mining operations.

In recent decades, mine water hazards triggered by fault activation have caused massive economic losses and casualties worldwide10. Fault activation refers to the shear slip and destabilization of pre-existing geological faults under the combined action of in-situ tectonic stress and mining-induced stress disturbance, which destroys the original sealing state of fault zones and forms connected water-conducting channels11–14. Accordingly, numerous scholars have carried out extensive physical and numerical simulations to reveal the mechanism of fault activation. For instance, Zhu et al.16 adopted COMSOL Multiphysics numerical modeling to investigate fault reactivation characteristics and stress evolution patterns. Sun et al.17 utilized FLAC3D numerical simulation and similar material tests to establish a stress–displacement response model of faults under mining conditions. Xia et al.18 studied the mining-induced fault reactivation mechanism associated with sublevel caving in metal mines. Zhu et al.19 revealed the mechanism and evolution law of delayed water inrush caused by fault activation during mining. Chen et al.20 established a mechanical model for surrounding rock instability and fracture induced by fault activation under mining influence. Cai et al.21 revealed the mechanism of fault activation inducing coal bursts under the superposition of static and dynamic stresses, proposed two activation modes, and provided monitoring and prevention methods. Chen et al.22 studied the mechanism of fault activation and water inrush disasters caused by mining, divided activation stages, identified water-conducting channels, and realized risk early warning. Rong et al.23 solved the surrounding rock instability of roadways crossing collapse columns, and put forward a combined technology of blasting pressure relief and enhanced support to ensure safe crossing. Xing and Han24 simulated fault stick-slip activation in multi-seam mining, clarified the influencing factors, assessed seismic risk, and guided safe mining. These studies have laid an important theoretical foundation for the prevention and control of mine water hazards.

However, the above studies have failed to systematically analyze the intrinsic mechanism of fault activation from both microscopic and macroscopic perspectives. Moreover, the above studies do not take into account the initiation stress criterion and the stress field coupling mechanism. Fault activation is affected not only by coal mining but also by the in-situ stress field.

Therefore, the analysis and prevention of fault activation must fully consider the actual engineering geological conditions of the study area, and combine the initiation stress criterion and stress field coupling mechanism to achieve accurate risk assessment and effective control. This study primarily analyzes the mechanism of roof water hazards caused by fault activation in the Xinhu Mine Coal. Accordingly, we adopt a novel approach to establish the numerical model. In this model, PFC3D is utilized to simulate the Quaternary aquifer, while FLAC3D is employed to simulate the rock strata, thereby achieving the coupling of the two. The findings of this research can provide reference value for future mine water hazards caused by fault activation.

Engineering background

The 815 working face at Xinhu Coal Mine is a large-scale mining face within the Huaibei Mining Group, producing 3 Mt of high-quality coking coal annually. As shown in Fig. 1a, Xinhu Coal Mine is located within Bozhou City, Anhui Province, China. The coal seam in the 815 working face is stably preserved. The 8 coal seam has a total thickness ranging from 5.18 to 8.92 m, with an average thickness of 7.17 m. A high-angle normal fault named F16 is developed within the 815 working face. As shown in Fig. 1b, the mining direction of the 815 working face progressed from the upper panel to the lower panel, with a total excavation length of 310 m.

Geographic location map of the 815 working face.

On November 5, 2023, a roof water hazard occurred during mining operations at the 815 working face, with a large volume of mud and sand surging into the working face. According to data provided by the mine, the water disaster was caused by fault activation when the mining face advanced 310 m from the hanging wall to the foot wall of the fault. The working face is still in the stage of mud and sand cleanup. As shown in Fig. 2, due to a mud-sand inrush across the entire working face, many workers are still underground clearing the mud and sand. According to on-site geological survey data, this roof water inrush incident was caused by the activation of the F16 fault in the 815 working face. The water source of this sudden water inrush primarily originated from the Quaternary aquifer, causing extensive damage to the 815 working face and resulting in the suspension of production at the mine.

Methods

PFC3D-FLAC3D coupled modeling approach

In this study, a discrete-continuous coupling method based on boundary control walls was employed to address the problem by coupling FLAC3D-PFC3D. During the coupled analysis, FLAC3D simulates the mechanical behavior of the medium within the continuous domain at the macroscopic level. Meanwhile, PFC3D focuses on simulating the mechanical behavior of the medium within the discrete domain from a microscopic perspective. The mutual coupling interaction between the two primarily occurs at the contact boundary between the continuous and discrete domains25,26. As shown in Fig. 3, during this process, computational data between different domains is efficiently and accurately transmitted and exchanged via the Socket I/O interface, ensuring the coordinated operation of the entire coupled system and seamless data interaction.

Principles of FLAC-PFC coupling calculation.

The FLAC3D continuum model transfers stress and displacement fields to the PFC3D discrete element model through the coupling interface, while PFC3D feeds back contact forces and particle displacements to FLAC3D for boundary condition updating. A stable data exchange scheme is adopted at each time step to ensure force balance and displacement compatibility at the interface, and time-step matching and iterative convergence criteria are used to guarantee numerical stability of the coupled calculation.

During the coupling process, when nodes or zones in FLAC3D are subjected to forces, velocities, and displacements are calculated based on the equations of motion. Particle states are updated, and wall positions are determined27. Subsequently, particles, walls, and regions synchronously update velocities and displacements, with data transferred between the two models. This process repeats in subsequent cycles, recalculating contact forces between walls and particles at their new positions28. As illustrated by Eq. (2), this method facilitates the transfer of information that includes coordinates, velocities, and contact forces between the two models, thereby achieving coupling29.

Implementation of PFC3D-FLAC3D coupled numerical modeling

To achieve coupling between FLAC3D and PFC3D, the PFC module from FLAC3D version 6.0 was utilized. In this study, PFC3D is employed to simulate the Quaternary aquifer, while FLAC3D models the bedrock strata. To optimize computational efficiency, the model domain is defined as 700 m in length, 5 m in width, and 200 m in height. The model comprises 59,079 particles and 38,233 zones configured with the Mohr-Coulomb model. Subsequently, the Quaternary aquifer is modeled as a void space, with particles distributed using a linear contact model configuration. As shown in Fig. 6, this model configuration effectively analyzes the mechanism of roof water hazards caused by fault activation, consistent with actual conditions.

Initial configuration of the FLAC3D-PFC3D coupling numerical simulation.

Model setup

Based on the geological conditions of Xinhu Coal Mine, the overlying strata are mainly composed of clay with a density of 2000 kg/m3 and a total thickness of approximately 400 m. Accordingly, a uniformly distributed equivalent vertical load of 8 MPa is applied on the top boundary of the coupled model to simulate the vertical stress induced by the overlying strata. The bottom boundary is fully fixed with velocity constraints, while the four lateral boundaries are free of displacement constraints and imposed with stress boundary conditions based on mine field test data. The lateral pressure coefficient of the in-situ stress field is set to 0.4. Combined with monitored water pressure data of the Quaternary aquifer in adjacent mining areas and the actual average water level elevation of approximately 1 m near the 815 working face, the Quaternary aquifer water pressure is defined as 4 MPa in the numerical model30,31. Coal seam excavation commenced at X = 100 m, proceeding in 10 steps of 25 m each. Following the fault crossing, excavation continued in 3 steps of 20 m each. Particle density was set at 2000 kg/m³, with a damping coefficient of 0.7 and porosity of 0.4. The normal fault F16 was simulated in the 815 working face, featuring a dip angle of 78° and a displacement of 7.5 m. As shown in Table 1, physical and mechanical parameters for rock and coal strata are derived from actual field geological exploration data, while fault parameters were provided by the mine32–34.

Results and discussion

As coal seam mining progresses, stress redistribution in surrounding rock strata may activate faults35. Numerical simulation is essential for investigating the mechanisms of fault activation. This simulation is mainly analyzed from four aspects: the number of contact forces, the evolution of vertical displacement, the development of the plastic zone, and vertical stress.

The number of contact forces reflects the quantity of mechanical contact points generated by the interaction between particles and between particles and rock strata during coal mining in the Quaternary aquifer. As shown in Fig. 7, the number of contact forces in the Quaternary aquifer gradually decreases as the working face advances. Figure 7a indicates that when the working face advances 75 m and is located on the hanging wall of the fault, the number of contact forces within the Quaternary aquifer reaches 295,860. As shown in Fig. 7b, when the working face advances to 225 m and gets closer to the fault, this value decreases to 237,543. Overall, the decline rate of contact forces remains relatively stable with the continuous advancement of the working face. In contrast, Fig. 7c reveals that the final number of contact forces drops sharply to 108,796.

The evolution of contact forces.

As shown in Fig. 8, when the working face advances from 25 m to 50 m, the number of contact forces remains basically stable. As the working face advances from 50 m to 250 m, the number of contact forces shows a continuous downward trend, with a cumulative reduction of 99,120. When the working face advances further to 310 m, the number of contact forces decreases more significantly, with an overall reduction of 96,080. Overall, within the hanging wall of the fault, the number of contact forces decreases at an average rate of 397 per meter. In contrast, the attenuation in the footwall is markedly more pronounced, averaging a reduction of 1,601 per meter. This indicates that when the working face advances from the hanging wall to the footwall of the fault, the attenuation rate of contact forces accelerates significantly.

The number of contact forces at different advancing distances of the working face.

As shown in Fig. 9, the displacement contour map of the overlying strata roughly resembles an arch bridge. During mining operations, the influence zone of mining activities continuously expands, with the greatest subsidence occurring beneath the immediate roof of the coal seam36–38. Figure 9a and b show that the working face is currently advancing over the hanging wall of the fault, with maximum subsidence ranging from 0.12 m to 0.63 m. However, Fig. 9c shows that the maximum subsidence reaches 2.92 m when the working face advances to 310 m and is located in the footwall of the fault. When the working face advances to 310 m, its maximum subsidence is approximately 4.63 times that at 225 m.This indicates that fault activation becomes more intense as the working face advances from the hanging wall to the footwall of the fault.

Vertical displacement diagram at different advancing distances of the working face.

The mine has adopted airborne Light Detection and Ranging mounted on an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle and has completed full scanning of the 815 working face along planned routes. With a scanning frequency of 240 Hz, on-site point cloud data has been collected and processed to obtain the maximum subsidence.

A goodness-of-fit test is performed using Eq. (6). The coefficient of determination R2 is found to be 0.999, indicating an excellent linear correlation (approaching 1.0) between the numerical simulation and the field test. This result confirms the high accuracy and reliability of the established numerical model.

As shown in Fig. 10, both numerical simulation and field test indicate that as the working face advances from the hanging wall to the footwall of the fault, fault activation becomes more intense. The maximum subsidence increases progressively with the advancement of the working face. The average subsidence of the hanging wall per unit mining length along the coal seam is 0.0036 m, while that of the footwall is 0.034 m. There is a significant difference in subsidence between the two walls, and the average subsidence of the footwall is approximately 9.4 times that of the hanging wall.

Maximum subsidence at different advancing distances of the working face.

The plastic zone within the roof above the excavated coal seam, where shear failure occurs continuously, is defined as the water-conducting fracture zone. Its height is defined as the vertical distance from the top of this plastic zone to the roof of the coal seam. The numerical model uses 1 × 10⁻⁵ as the convergence tolerance. Once the calculation meets this convergence criterion, the development of the water-conducting fracture zone is considered to have stabilized.

As shown in Fig. 11, fault activation does not occur simultaneously across the entire structure but proceeds in distinct phases. According to the continuity of the plastic zone and the locations of shear failure in the fault during the advancement of the working face, the activation process can be summarized into three phases: upper activation phase (a), near-coal seam activation phase (b), and overall activation phase (c). Specifically, during the advance of the working face, shear failure first occurs in the upper part of the fault. Subsequently, as coal mining approaches the fault zone, failure develops near the coal seam. Finally, the newly generated failure continues to develop, connecting with previously established failures, leading to the overall activation of the fault. Figure 11c shows that the plastic zone has already developed into the weathering zone.

Developmental process of the plastic zone.

As shown in Fig. 12, borehole monitoring shows the initial water level of the Quaternary aquifer at the 815 working face is about 1 m. The water level drops 13–21 m within 14 h, proving this aquifer is the main source of the roof water hazard. Combined with the fracture development characteristics from numerical simulation, the mechanical damage pattern, and field test data, the water-conducting fracture zone is inferred to eventually connect to the Quaternary aquifer, resulting in water inrush and sand collapse.

The water level elevation monitoring of the Quaternary aquifer in the 815 working face.

As shown in Fig. 13, during coal mining, the continuous development of the compression-tension stress transition zone above the fault indicates that fault activation starts at the upper fault section. Based on the comprehensive analysis, the three stages of fault activation exhibit distinctly different characteristics.

Vertical stress distribution at different advancing distances of the working face.

When the working face advances to 75 m, the fault activation enters its initial phase, where shear failure begins to occur in the upper section of the fault, and a relatively low compressive stress zone of approximately 2 MPa develops compared to other regions. As the working face continues to advance, both the extent and magnitude of this compressive stress zone gradually increase, with the compressive stress reaching 2.5 MPa when the working face advances 150 m. Upon advancing to 225 m, the fault activation enters the second stage, characterized by the onset of shear and tensile failures near the coal seam. At this stage, a significant tensile stress zone of approximately 4.62 MPa develops in the upper portion of the fault, while a substantial compressive stress zone peaking at approximately 55.56 MPa emerges near the lower section. Finally, when the working face reaches 310 m, the fault enters the third stage and undergoes overall activation. The upper section exhibits a pronounced tensile stress zone of approximately 19.14 MPa, whereas the high compressive stress zone previously observed near the fault disappears.

Using direct current sounding and transient electromagnetic methods, the mine has investigated resistivity variations within the 815 working face to monitor the height of the water-conducting fracture zone.

A goodness-of-fit analysis was performed based on Eq. (6). The coefficient of determination R2 reached 0.999, demonstrating a nearly perfect linear correlation between numerical simulation outputs and field measurements. The above result further validates the superior precision and credibility of the constructed numerical model.

During coal mining, the reduction in the number of contact forces induces stress redistribution. These two factors jointly control fault activation intensity and the development of the water-conducting fracture zone. As shown in Fig. 14, the height of the water-conducting fracture zone shows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. When the working face advances on the hanging wall of the fault, the reduction in the number of contact forces triggers stress redistribution. The stress above the fault gradually changes from a compressive stress of 2 MPa to a tensile stress of 4.62 MPa, which provides mechanical conditions for the overall activation of the fault, and the final height of the water-conducting fracture zone is approximately 140 m. In contrast, when the working face advances on the fault footwall, the number of contact forces decreases more rapidly, and the redistributed stress above the fault eventually reaches a tensile stress of 19.14 MPa. However, with the coal seam elevation rising by 7.5 m, the final height of the water-conducting fracture zone reaches approximately 94.5 m.

Height of the water-conducting fracture zone at different advancing distances of the working face.

Conclusions

This study employs coupled numerical simulation based on FLAC3D and PFC3D to analyze the mechanism of roof water hazards caused by fault activation. The detailed conclusions are summarized as follows:

As the working face advances, the number of contact forces in the Quaternary aquifer gradually decreases. When the working face advances from the hanging wall to the footwall of the fault, the attenuation rate of the contact forces accelerates significantly, and intense stress redistribution occurs near the fault, providing favorable mechanical conditions for fault activation. As the working face advances, the maximum subsidence increases progressively. Both the maximum subsidence and the subsidence rate per unit distance are greater in the footwall than in the hanging wall, indicating more pronounced fault activation in the footwall.

Applicability and limitations

This study provides a basis for preventing fault activation during future coal seam mining operations, thereby reducing the occurrence of roof water hazards. However, it should be noted that this study also has its own applicability and limitations, which are specifically elaborated as follows.

Based on field tests and numerical simulations, fault activation becomes more intense when the working face advances from the hanging wall to the footwall of the fault, leading to an increased probability of roof water hazards. The conclusion can provide a basis for mine water hazard early warning, helping to identify high-risk sections and prevent water inrush accidents. Meanwhile, designating the fault footwall as a key area for monitoring and reinforcement can guide the optimization of on-site engineering layout, improve prevention and control efficiency, reduce engineering costs, and offer technical references for the safe mining of mines with similar geological conditions.
