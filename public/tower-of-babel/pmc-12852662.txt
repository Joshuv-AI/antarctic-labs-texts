A cross-sectional study investigating optimism and mental resilience among Greek healthcare professionals
Authors: Georgia-Nektaria Porfyri, Konstantina Karanasi, Theocharis Konstantinidis I, Pavlos Sarafis, Aikaterini Toska, EC Fradelos, Elpida Stratou, Aikaterini Gamvroula, Maria Saridi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare professionals experience high levels of burnout and chronic stress, which further jeopardizes their optimism and mental resilience.MethodsThe study involved 175 health professionals (46 doctors, 56 nurses, 27 nursing assistants, 23 ward assistants, 23 paramedical workers) of the Sismanogleio General Hospital of Athens, aged 26-65. Participants were administered an electronic questionnaire, which included socio-demographic data and work-related information, the Revised Life Orientation Test (LOT-R) and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISK-25). The data analysis was conducted using SPSS, with Descriptive and Inferential Statistics methods.ResultsThe results reveal moderate levels of mental resilience (Mean=65.23, Std. Dev.=15.44) and moderate levels of optimism (Mean=12.75, Std. Dev.=2.89) among participants. Health professionals’ levels of optimism are positively correlated with their levels of mental resilience (rho = 0.208, p = 0.006). The levels of optimism and mental resilience do not differ according to gender, marital status, or years of work experience (p = 0.612, p = 0.458, p = 0.214, respectively), but differ according to age (p < 0.001). Health professionals aged 60+ show the highest levels of mental resilience, while paramedical workers demonstrate greater optimism compared to physicians.ConclusionsThe development of targeted training and support programs aimed at strengthening optimism and mental resilience among healthcare professionals may be beneficial.

Introduction

Healthcare workers are at increased risk for mental disorders, long-term stress, and burnout in comparison to other professions1,2. The challenging nature of their work, marked by long hours, high patient volume, and exposure to life-endangering circumstance, creates a high-pressure environment and intensifies mental stress3,4. Furthermore, beyond their professional duties, healthcare workers are called to fulfill personal and social commitments, like parenting, financial pressure, and relationships5,6. During the COVID-19 pandemic, these stressors were amplified, declining the quality of both personal and professional defensive mechanisms, growing susceptibility to depression, stress, and other emotional disorders7,8. According to literature, 22% to 32% of nurses considered stepping away due to emotional and physical exhaustion, as well as professional burnout9. Thus, exploration of defensive mechanisms like optimism and mental resilience among healthcare workers is crucial.

Optimism is defined as a cognitive disposition encompassing the expectation that future events will unfold favorably, shaping how individuals perceive and approach experiences and challenges10. It is worth noting that optimism is not limited to the concept of a passive hope, but it is an active cognitive style that influences motivation and coping strategies11. More specifically, in healthcare workers, optimism functions as a protective factor against occupational stress, facilitating work-related difficulties management, and preserving job satisfaction12. Especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, optimism among healthcare workers was revealed as a positive factor for professional engagement and quality of work and simultaneously as a negative factor for work-related anxiety13,14.

Mental resilience refers to the individual’s capacity of adapting, coping with, and recovering from stressful situations15. Notably, resilience is conceptualized not only as a static characteristic but as a dynamic and developmental process shaped by the interaction of individual, social, and environmental factors16. More precisely, it includes cognitive flexibility, emotional regulation, and the ability to find meaning in adversity. In high-pressure settings such as hospitals, resilience empowers health professionals to withstand occupational stress, preventing burnout syndrome.

Numerous studies reveal the significant interrelationship between optimism and mental resilience, with optimism fostering motivation to confront adversity and enabling positive reconstruction following negative experiences17. For healthcare providers, this synergistic relationship is vital, as an optimistic outlook reinforces resilience and alleviates the pressure of occupational stress and burnout18. The enhancement of these psychological resources is essential for both improving quality of life and optimizing professional performance, especially in environments marked by frequent exposure to suffering and loss19.

Perceived level of adequate training, working in structured units, clear communication and support from supervisors, colleagues, family and friends have been reported as important factors influencing both resilience and optimism in healthcare professionals20.

This study presents the optimism and mental resilience levels among the healthcare personnel of one of the largest hospitals in Greece. Using two appropriate questionnaires, we demonstrate that healthcare workers experience moderate levels of both mental resilience and optimism, with older ones showing the highest resilience and paramedic staff showing the greatest optimism. This highlights the need for appropriate training programs to enhance optimism and resilience in healthcare personnel despite their educational level, age, or work position.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional study in which all healthcare professionals employed at Sismanogleio General Hospital were invited to participate via institutional e-mail. Of approximately 620 invitees, 175 responded (46 doctors, 56 nurses, 27 nursing assistants, 23 ward assistants, and 23 paramedical workers), corresponding to a response rate of 28.2%. The >96% item completion threshold was set a priori to ensure high data quality and reliability of the results. The extent of missing data was minimal (<2% overall), and listwise deletion was applied. It is worth noting here that Sismanogleio General Hospital is one of Greece’s largest public hospitals and a major healthcare facility in northern Athens, with a resource capacity of 540 beds and 620 workers, while it specializes in pulmonology and respiratory medicine21. It also played a key role during the COVID-19 pandemic serving as a COVID-19 reference hospital22.

After the approval of the study protocol by the Institutional Review Board of Sismanogleio General Hospital (approval number: 6445/15.03.2024) health professionals were invited to participate in the research voluntarily and anonymously, having provided informed consent during the initial briefing for the survey. The study was conducted from 24 March to 22 May 2024. This was a voluntary response sampling, as the survey was sent to all healthcare professionals of the Sismanogleio General Hospital.

Considering that an online survey can provide a large amount of data within a short period, the questionnaires were provided through electronic devices in an online form on Google forms. The inclusion criteria were: i) acceptance to participate, ii) being a healthcare worker from Sismanogleio General Hospital of Athens, iii) completion of over 96% of survey questions. The authors have obtained written consent to publish the details.

Measures

Socio-demographic and work-related information questionnaire

Participants were asked to provide anonymous demographic information on their age, gender, marital status, education, work experience, and profession.

Connor-Davidson resilience scale (CD-RISC-25)

They were also given the “Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale” (CD-RISC-25); a 25-item self-administered questionnaire used to evaluate psychological resilience, initially developed by Connor and Davidson in 200323. The scale is rated on a five-point Likert scale (0–4), with total scores ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate greater resilience. The CD-RISC-25 consists of five subscales (personal competence/tenacity, trust in one’s instincts/tolerance of negative affect, positive acceptance of change/secure relationships, control and spiritual influences). For example, one item reads: “I am able to adapt when changes occur.” In the present study, only the total score of the CD-RISC-25 was used for all analyses. The Greek CD-RISC-25 was validated by Dimitriadou and Stalikas in 201224, showing high internal consistency. In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.93.

Revised life orientation test (LOT-R)

Participants were also given the “Revised Life Orientation Test” (LOT-R); a 10-item self-report measure (6 scored items) assessing dispositional optimism, rated from 0 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”), with a total score range of 0 to 24. The LOT-R includes items reflecting both optimism and pessimism. An example item is: “In uncertain times, I usually expect the best.” The scale was originally developed by Scheier and Carver25. and adapted into Greek by Lyrakos et al.26. The internal consistency in the current sample was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.70).

Statistical analysis

Demographic and occupational variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies. Descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviations) were calculated for the main variables. Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Both main variables (optimism and psychological resilience) showed significant deviations from normality (p < 0.05), and therefore all group comparisons and correlation analyses were performed using nonparametric tests. Spearman rho correlations were selected to examine associations, consistent with the distributional properties of the data. The level of statistical significance was set at 5% (p < 0.05). Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0.

Sample size determination

To estimate the required sample size, a priori power analysis was conducted. The primary hypothesis of the study concerned the association between optimism (LOT-R) and psychological resilience (CD-RISC-25). Assuming an effect size of r = 0.20, a significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed), and desired power of 0.80, the required number of participants was estimated at approximately N = 194. For comparisons across five professional groups (one-way ANOVA as an approximate equivalent of the Kruskal-Wallis test), and with a medium effect size (f = 0.25), the required sample size was estimated at N = 196. Given that 175 individuals participated in the study, the achieved power for detecting a correlation of r = 0.20 was ~0.76. This indicates that the study had sufficient power to detect medium-sized associations, but limited capacity to detect very small differences or weak correlations.

Levels of psychological resilience and optimism

The participants in this study demonstrated moderate levels of psychological resilience, as measured by the total CD-RISC-25 score (range: 25–100; M = 65.23, SD = 15.44). These findings reveal an overall positive, though not particularly high, level of resilience among the healthcare professionals in the sample.

The levels of optimism among healthcare professionals were subsequently assessed. Participants expressed a neutral stance toward the relevant statements, with mean values ranging from 2.09 to 3.04. More specifically, they most strongly agreed with the statements that they enjoy spending time with their friends (M = 3.04, SD = 1.02), expect more good things than bad to happen to them (M = 2.78, SD = 1.06), believe it is important to stay constantly occupied (M = 2.77, SD = 1.00), and feel consistently optimistic about their future (M = 2.63, SD = 1.04). The total optimism scores ranged from 3 to 23, with a mean value of 12.75 and a standard deviation of 2.89, indicating moderate levels of optimism in the study sample.

Associations between optimism and psychological resilience

Optimism demonstrated a small but statistically significant positive association with overall psychological resilience, as measured by the total CD-RISC-25 score (rho = 0.208, p = 0.006) (rho = 0.208, p < 0.01). This indicates that individuals with higher levels of optimism tend to report greater resilience, although the strength of the association is low. Discussion

The aim of the present study was to explore optimism and mental resilience levels among healthcare professionals at Sismanogleio General Hospital of Athens and to investigate their inter-relationship. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating both optimism and mental resilience in a sample of frontline healthcare workers in Greece.

However, to exhibit the scientific contribution of our study, we must accurately present the mental health state data of Greek healthcare staff during the recent years. Following the fear of Covid-19 pandemic’s impact, numerous studies exploring the mental health of Greek healthcare professionals emerged, with their results being quite disturbing and calling for action. More specifically, in a study conducted among occupational therapists, it was revealed that 50% of the participants had no psychological resilience27, while in another study conducted in six public hospitals, 50% of healthcare professionals experienced symptoms of depression and 61.5% showed symptoms of anxiety28. Finally, in a study among Greek primary healthcare professionals, results showed that 1 in 4 was suffering from burnout29.

In this context, the results of our study revealing moderate levels of both mental resilience and optimism among participants, represent a significant finding. Also, the positive correlation between optimism and psychological resilience found in our study suggests a modest association, indicating that health professionals with higher levels of optimism tend to exhibit greater resilience in the face of adversity. The correlation coefficient was rho = 0.208, p = 0.006, reflecting a weak effect size, implying that while optimism is related to resilience, other factors are also likely to play an important role. This observation aligns with previous studies that highlight the predictive role of optimism in fostering resilience among healthcare professionals30.

No statistically significant differences in optimism or resilience were found based on gender, marital status, or years of professional experience (p = 0.612, p = 0.458, p = 0.214). This contrasts with earlier studies conducted in Greece which detected associations between these factors and psychological resilience. These discrepancies should be interpreted with caution, as the referenced studies included larger samples or were systematic reviews, whereas the present study involved a smaller convenience sample from a single center. In more detail, in a study among 271 dentistry and nursing students, male postgraduate students reported significantly higher resilience compared to females31, aligning with another study among 288 nursing students which also showed greater resilience in males32. On the contrary, different research conducted among 584 nurses revealed that females’ resilience was higher than males, while those with children showed higher resilience compared to those without children. In addition, a higher educational level predicted a greater resilience with nurses holding a PhD/MSc being more resilient than those without a PhD/MSc33.

However, in our study, age emerged as a differentiating factor of mental resilience, with professionals aged 60 years and over showing significantly higher levels compared to all younger age groups (p = 0.0003). This finding is consistent with studies according to which strong stress management skills develop with age, potentially due to accumulated socio-economic and personal challenges, while further contributing to the shaping of a mental maturity accompanied by mental resilience34. Nevertheless, other studies support higher resilience in younger age groups35 suggesting that the age-resilience relationship may vary depending on the sample or study conditions.

In addition, numerous studies reveal that professional background can have a different impact on optimism levels36 an observation that aligns with our research’s results showing that paramedical staff show greater optimism compared to doctors, nurses and support staff (p = 0.024). This finding could be explained by the existence of a more optimism-orientated training -in comparison to physicians or nurses- as well as a more enriched psychological first aid education or simply by the necessity to professionally perform with hope even in times of crisis. On the contrary, as regards resilience, no statistically significant differences were revealed between professional groups; in contradiction with previous studies demonstrating different levels of resilience between physicians and nurses37.

Interventions to enhance resilience may be beneficial in healthcare working groups, especially for those who appear to not have a supportive framework.

On a structural level, governmental authorities could consider planning psychological aid services destined to healthcare professionals at risk of mental health issues38. On an educational level, targeted training and support programs centered on optimism and mental resilience among healthcare professionals might be useful, including mindfulness-based coping strategies like breathing exercises39. Training of this kind incorporated during the first years of healthcare studies could potentially contribute to cultivating a mentality of hope, perseverance, and companionship in future healthcare professionals. Finally, on an individual level, healthcare professionals may also benefit from engaging in self-care practices and maintaining a healthy work-life balance40.

This study is subject to certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the use of the convenience sampling method and the relatively small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings to the wider population of health professionals. It is suggested that future research should be conducted using representative probability sampling methods and a larger sample size to enhance the external validity of the results. Secondly, a limitation of the present study concerns the sample size in relation to power analysis. A priori power analysis indicated that approximately 194 participants would be required to detect a small-to-moderate correlation (rho = 0.208) with 80% power, whereas our sample comprised 175 participants. Consequently, the absence of certain statistically significant differences may be attributed to limited statistical power. Future studies with larger samples could confirm or further clarify our findings, particularly with respect to small or borderline correlations.

On addition, data collection was conducted through self-report tools, which may affect the accuracy and objectivity of the responses, as it is based on the participants’ assessment of their mental state. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences, and the single-center setting (one general hospital in Athens) may limit the generalizability of findings to other healthcare environments. Another limitation of the present study concerns the use of multiple post hoc pairwise comparisons. Although we have applied Bonferroni corrections and reported adjusted p-values as well as effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals, the possibility of Type I error cannot be fully excluded, especially given the relatively modest sample size. Therefore, some of the reported associations should be interpreted with caution, and replication in larger samples is warranted. Therefore, future studies could utilize mixed methodological approaches (quantitative and qualitative) to yield a more comprehensive and in-depth understanding of the phenomenon of mental resilience and optimism among health professionals.

Conclusions

Our findings show improved levels of optimism and mental resilience compared to those of previous studies in Greek students and healthcare professionals. However, they indicate that there is still room for progress to strengthen the mental health of health professionals regardless of their educational level, work experience or work position, with reforms such as educational and social measures. Implications of our results and potential avenues for further study are included.
