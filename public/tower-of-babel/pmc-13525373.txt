Reduction of CXCR3+CCR6+ CD4+ T Cells and IL‐17+ RORγt+ CD4+ T Cells During Secukinumab Treatment Identifies Patients With Axial Spondyloarthritis Responsive to Subsequent Biologic Therapy
Authors: Addison Pacheco, Patricia Remalante‐Rayco, Nigil Haroon, Denis Poddubnyy, Robert D Inman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe recently reported an immune profile that stratifies patients with axial spondyloarthritis based on clinical response to secukinumab. We now undertake an exploratory study on the secukinumab nonresponder patients to determine if immunologic changes occur during their initial secukinumab treatment that could determine their next biologic response.MethodsAt the clinician's discretion, 18 patients who did not respond to secukinumab were either switched to a tumor necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi; n = 8) or underwent secukinumab dose escalation to 300 mg monthly or 150 mg biweekly (n = 10). Flow cytometry–derived immune cell subset frequencies and NanoString gene expression profiling of CD45RO+CD45RA‐CD4+ T cells obtained during initial secukinumab treatment were stratified and reanalyzed according to subsequent treatment response based on the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index at week 24.ResultsMost patients were either subsequent secukinumab responders (sSecu‐Rs; n = 5/10, 50%) or subsequent TNFi responders (sTNFi‐Rs; n = 6/8, 75%). During the initial secukinumab treatment, there was a significant decrease in the frequency of TH17.1 cells (P < 0.05) and interleukin‐17 positive (IL17+) RORγt+ CD4+ T cells (P < 0.05) in sSecu‐Rs and sTNFi‐Rs, but not in patients who failed all three biologics (subsequent non responder [sNR]). Multidimensional scaling of NanoString data revealed that subsequent nonresponders (sNRs; before and after secukinumab) clustered distinctly from other groups. Following the initial secukinumab therapy, but not before it, sSecu‐Rs and sTNFi‐Rs showed lower expression of IL12A, IL12RB1, IFNGR1, and JAK1 compared with sNRs.ConclusionSecukinumab treatment induces immunologic changes associated with reduced inflammation, even in patients who demonstrate an inadequate clinical response. Notably, patients who display these immunologic profiles are more likely to respond to subsequent biologic therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Biologics such as tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFis), interleukin‐17 inhibitors (IL17is), and JAK inhibitors (JAKis) are commonly employed to reduce inflammation and pain in patients with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA). Although the biologics have contributed to a significant improvement in disease symptoms, it is currently unknown why approximately 40% of patients do not respond to each of the biologic classes. Our recent findings in patients with axSpA receiving secukinumab treatment revealed that type 1 interferon (IFN)–regulated genes are higher in secukinumab nonresponders (SEC‐NRs) compared with secukinumab responders (SEC‐R) after treatment. 1 This elevated type 1 IFN signature has also been described in patients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) who are unresponsive to TNFi. 2 The up‐regulation of IFN‐α receptor 1 (IFNAR1) on monocytes mediated by increased allograft inflammatory factor 1 drives this increased type 1 IFN signature. This suggests that alternative immune pathways, specifically the type 1 IFN pathway, may contribute to treatment resistance in AS.

Current perspectives on treatment‐refractory patients may be overly simplistic, leading to the assumption that the therapeutic target is incorrect. The target itself may be valid while factors such as timing, dosing, or administration may be suboptimal for an individual patient. We therefore completed an analysis on SEC‐NR patients who also did not respond to a prior TNFi to determine whether a new biologic target was required for improving patient outcomes. Additionally, we sought to define whether immunologic changes occurred in previous biologic usage that hinted toward subsequent biologic response.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patient and public involvement

The SEC‐NR cohort (n = 19) described in our previous Arthritis & Rheumatology article 1 was analyzed retrospectively after determining patient subsequent biologic response. One SEC‐NR patient had no subsequent biologic response data and was therefore excluded. Any subsequent biologic was at clinician discretion and included either switching to TNFi or changing the secukinumab dosing. Patients who had altered secukinumab dosing were either given 300 mg monthly or 150 mg biweekly (Supplementary Table 1). This strategy was previously demonstrated to have sustained improvements in disease activity and retention for patients with axSpA with active disease after receiving 150 mg. 3 Patients provided written consent to participate in the study approved by the University Health Network ethics board (REB# 20‐5095). Patients with AS fulfilled the modified New York AS criteria, and patients with nonradiographic axSpA (nr‐axSpA) fulfilled the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS) classification criteria for nr‐axSpA. 4 , 5 Patient response to initial secukinumab treatment was defined as either ≥50% by a reduction in the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) or a decrease of >1.1 in the BASDAI‐based Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score (BASDAS), a surrogate score for ASDAS‐CRP. 6 , 7 Inadequate response to subsequent biologics was determined through a shared decision between the physician and patient and when the BASDAI exceeded 4 after 24 weeks of treatment. Patient clinical characteristics for the analyzed cohort are provided in Supplementary Table 1 and summarized in Table 1.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation and flow cytometry staining

Isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and staining was described in our previous publication, 1 and counts were reanalyzed after reclassifying patients based on their response to the third biologic attempt. To summarize, PBMCs were isolated using Ficoll‐Paque Plus and were frozen in 50% DMSO and 50% complete media (RPMI + L‐Glu, 10% fetal bovine serum [FBS], 10% Penicillin/Streptomycin, and 2 μL/500 mL β‐mercaptoethanol) in liquid nitrogen. Isolated frozen PBMCs were thawed and rested in complete media for 4 hours before flow cytometry experiments. If measuring intracellular cytokines, cells were stimulated with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) and ionomycin 4 hours before staining. GolgiPlug was supplemented 30 minutes after the addition of PMA and ionomycin. Cells were stained with BD live/dead for 20 minutes at 4°C and then blocked for 10 minutes with BioLegend human TruStain FcX. Cells were then stained for CXCR3 and CCR6 for 30 minutes at 37°C before staining for other surface antigens at 4°C for 30 minutes. Cells were fixed and permeabilized using the eBioscience FOXP3 Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set before intracellular staining of transcription factors and cytokines. When RORγt was included in the staining panel, intracellular staining was done overnight at room temperature. Data was acquired using a BD Canto flow cytometer or BD Symphony A3. Antibodies used are provided in Supplementary Table 2 in our previous publication. 1

Fluorescent‐activated cell sorting and RNA extraction for NanoString analysis

The isolation of CD45RO+CD45RA‐ CD4+ T cells were sorted using fluorescent‐activated cell sorting as described in our previous publication. 1 Cells were sorted into 2‐mL DNA LoBind tubes containing 50% FBS complete media using a BD Aria. Total RNA was extracted from sorted cells using a PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit and 200 ng of RNA was hybridized using an nCounter messenger RNA assay. Raw RNA transcript counts were assessed for quality control and analyzed using Rosalind's nCounter analysis.

Data analysis

All experimental procedures were completed for our previous publication 1 and reanalyzed with reclassification of patients based on their subsequent biologic response. PBMCs were counted using flow cytometry data using FlowJo software (version 10.8.0) and represented as mean ± SD. Paired analyses were analyzed using two‐tailed Wilcoxon signed rank test with P = 0.05 used as the threshold. These data were visualized using GraphPad Prism (V10.5). NanoString data from fluorescence‐activated cell–sorted CD45RO+CD45RA‐CD4+ T cells were analyzed using Rosalind's nCounter analysis, and heatmaps were revisualized using the pheatmap package 8 in R 4.4.0 from a matrix of mean subtracted normalized log2 expression. Genes used for hierarchical clustering in heatmaps were filtered based on fold change >1.5 and unadjusted P values <0.05, determined using a generalized linear model assuming a negative binomial distribution. The multidimensional scaling (MDS) plot was visualized using ggplot2. The supporting data are available from the corresponding author upon request.

RESULTS

Most patients respond to the third biologic, regardless of class

Following nonresponse to TNFi as a first biologic and IL17i (secukinumab) as a second biologic, our SEC‐NR cohort (N = 18) attempted a third biologic strategy consisting of either secukinumab dose escalation or switching to a TNFi (Figure 1A). Clinical response was observed in most patients, with benefit seen following secukinumab dose escalation (n = 5 of 10, 50%) or TNFi switching (n = 6 of 8, 75%). These findings suggest that symptom control was ultimately achievable but often required either a longer period on treatment or higher dosing of the drug.

To determine whether clinical or inflammatory signals during prior secukinumab exposure could distinguish patients who would later respond to a third biologic, we reclassified SEC‐NR patients according to their response to third‐line therapy (Figure 1A). We then reanalyzed our flow cytometry and NanoString databased on this reclassification. Patients who proved to be nonresponders to the third‐line therapy were classified as subsequent nonresponders (sNRs, n = 7), those who responded to dose escalation of secukinumab were classified as subsequent secukinumab responders (sSecu‐Rs, n = 5), and those who achieved adequate response when switching to TNFi were classified as subsequent TNFi responders (sTNFi‐Rs, n = 6).When stratified by these groups, no significant differences were observed in BASDAI (Figure 1B) or BASDAS (Figure 1C) during the 24‐week period of second‐line secukinumab treatment. CRP levels were also normal in most patients (Figure 1D). Therefore, neither clinical disease activity measures nor CRP levels during prior secukinumab exposure discriminated patients who would later respond to the subsequent biologic.

Second‐line secukinumab treatment–induced TH17.1 and IL‐17A+ CD4+ T cell frequency decreases in patients who subsequently respond to the next biologic

In our prior study, 1 increased TH1 frequency and TH1.17 frequencies before secukinumab were associated with a lower likelihood of secukinumab response. Strikingly, the secukinumab‐treated patients who subsequently responded to a third‐line biologic were found to have a reduction in TH17.1 frequency during secukinumab treatment (Figure 1E). The decrease in TH17.1 frequency was not seen among sNR patients. On the contrary, the frequency of other immune subsets, TH1 and TH17, did not change significantly during secukinumab treatment (Figure 1F and G). These changes were concurrent with a CXCR3 median fluorescence intensity decrease in CD4+ T cells (Figure 1H). The cells that express RORγt also appeared to decrease in frequency in sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients, but this did not reach statistical significance (Figure 1I). Because secukinumab targets IL‐17A, we checked if IL‐17A–producing CD4+ T cell subset frequency changed during secukinumab treatment for any of these reclassified patients. The frequency of IL‐17A+ in CD4+ T cells had a decreasing trend in sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients (Figure 1J). When gating on RORγt+CD4+ T cells instead, the frequency of IL‐17A–producing cells was significantly decreased in sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients, but one patient in this group had an increase (Figure 1K). This suggests that although SEC‐NR patients did not meet clinical response criteria or demonstrated a decrease in markers of systemic inflammation, some elements of their immune profile were being reduced by their prior secukinumab treatment.

Transcriptome clustering of second‐line SEC‐NRs distinguishes third‐line biologic responders from nonresponders

We further explored differences between patients characterized as sNR and subsequent responders (sSecu‐R or sTNFi‐R) through reanalysis of the NanoString count data of CD45RO+CD45RA‐CD4+ T cells. MDS of NanoString data revealed that sNR patients (before and after secukinumab) clustered distinctly from other groups (Figure 2A). Interestingly, one patient in sNR clustered closely with sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients. This patient stopped secukinumab treatment before the week 24 time point after reporting lack of improvement and expressed the desire to switch to a TNFi. After nonresponse to TNFi, the patient subsequently responded to a JAKi during their fourth attempt at advanced therapy. When categorizing the clusters based on second‐line secukinumab time point, baseline and week 24 appeared to group separately for sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients (Figure 2B), suggesting transcriptional changes occurring during second‐line secukinumab treatment. Before second‐line secukinumab treatment, sNR patients appeared to have increased transcripts of CTSW, IL5, and AHR and decreased transcripts of PDCD1, PDCD1LG2, EOMES, TIGIT, and TCF4 compared with sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients (Figure 2C). Following second‐line secukinumab treatment, IL‐5 transcripts decreased and checkpoint inhibitor markers CD244 and PDCD1 increased in sNR (Figure 2D).

Although these patients did not respond to three different biologic attempts, some immune differences appeared to occur. However, these immunologic differences were reduced compared with the changes seen in sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients. Following the second‐line secukinumab therapy, sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients showed lower expressions of IL18R1, IL12RB1, IFNGR1, STAT4, and JAK1 compared with sNR patients (Figure 2E). This suggests that prior secukinumab treatment slightly reduces markers of inflammation in sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients, which may support response to a subsequent biologic. Some of these markers did not appear significantly different in our paired comparison for sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients, but IL‐18R1 and STAT4 are important genes that were significantly reduced (Figure 2F and G). IL‐18 and STAT4 signaling play an important role in IFNγ production in synergy to IL‐12 or type 1 IFNs. These data suggest that type 1 IFN signaling may influence treatment resistance, but this resistance could be managed through increased dosing of IL17i or combination therapies, not strictly by targeting the type 1 IFN pathway.

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates that SEC‐NR patients who respond to a subsequent third‐line biologic have a reduction in IL‐17A+ RORγt+ CD4+ T cells and TH17.1 cell frequencies during their second‐line secukinumab treatment. Secukinumab also appeared to induce transcriptional changes that reflect reduced markers of inflammation involved in IFNγ signaling such as IL18R1, STAT4, and JAK1.

The elevated frequency of TH17.1 cells observed in SEC‐NR patients and the important reduction of this subset in individuals who respond to a third biologic highlights a potential role for this subset in sustaining inflammatory burden in axSpA. Although TH17.1 cells produce less IL‐17A than conventional TH17 cells, they retain the capacity to express IFNγ. This TH17.1 subset has been demonstrated to activate synovial fibroblasts and drive inflammation in an IFNγ‐independent manner. Interestingly, blocking IL‐17A only partially inhibits synovial fibroblast activation, whereas broader inhibition of T cell–derived cytokines can fully abrogate this response. 9 These findings support the concept that CCR6+ T helper subsets, including TH17.1 cells, contribute to inflammation through redundant and IL‐17A–independent mechanisms. In this context, the persistence of TH17.1 cells during secukinumab treatment may reflect an incomplete suppression of inflammatory pathways, thereby contributing to inadequate clinical response. Conversely, their reduction in patients who later respond to subsequent biologic therapy suggests that progressive modulation of these cells may be required to achieve meaningful disease control. This aligns with our observation that immunologic changes can occur in the absence of immediate clinical response and may still predict future therapeutic success.

This work suggests that the inflammatory burden is elevated in some patients and the cycling within or switching between biologic classes allows for the eventual reduction of inflammation to a level that reduces perceived pain. Although cycling within a biologic class was found to have higher drug‐retention rate, 10 these data suggest that up‐titration of a biologic can address inadequate clinic response. Although anti‐IL17A is not thought to specifically target the TH1 immune pathways, targeting of one immune pathway may engage host immune regulation by allowing the focus on one inflammatory pathway instead of two. The role of anti‐IL17A monoclonal antibodies was previously explored in mice primary cell coculture. It was determined that monoclonal antibodies targeting IL‐17A increased the function of Treg cells and indirectly suppressed TH1 responses while promoting TH2 responses. 11 The expansion of Treg cells and decrease of TH1 has also been observed following TNFi therapy in rheumatoid arthritis. 12 These studies were limited to CD4+ T cells, and immune changes in monocytes or dendritic cells during this transition would provide a better understanding of the immunoregulation that occurs during biologic treatment. Alternatively, the elevation of type 1 IFNs in SEC‐NRs may represent an immunoregulatory mechanism that limits excessive IFNγ production following IL‐17A suppression by secukinumab treatment. 13 This would not explain the correlation found between type 1 IFN scoring and TNFi response at the pretreatment time point.

Our study has the limitation of not mandating magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) during protocol visits; therefore, the objective evidence of inflammation in the axial skeleton could not be fully assessed. Consequently, we refrained from classifying our patients as treatment‐refractory according to the ASAS definition, which requires objective confirmation of the inflammatory nature of symptoms. 14 Treatment failure in axSpA can result either from persistent inflammation or from symptoms related to nonnociceptive pain and coexisting conditions rather than inflammation. It is possible that sSecu‐R and sTNFi‐R patients responded because their initial treatment failure was because of a suboptimal control of inflammation, whereas sNR patients may have had pain unrelated to inflammation. Alternatively, the observed responses could reflect distinct inflammatory mechanisms, as described in the reports by Song et al and ourselves. 2 Interestingly, type 1 IFNs can promote nociceptive pain via neuron sensitization mediated through the ERK/MAPK‐interacting kinase (MNK) signaling axis, rather than the JAK/STAT pathway, thereby supporting the rationale for targeting IFNAR1 over JAK inhibition. 15 Future studies should clarify whether the differences between third‐line biologic responders and nonresponders are driven by persistent inflammation in the former group and noninflammatory pain mechanisms in the latter. Because CRP, as a marker of systemic inflammation, does not always reflect inflammatory activity in the sacroiliac joints or spine, MRI assessment would better address this question in future studies. Additionally, our study lacks sufficient statistical power and makes our findings susceptible to chance variation. The study can therefore only be considered exploratory but serves as an interesting observation for future considerations. Understanding the determinants of response and nonresponse to different biologic and targeted synthetic disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs will advance precision medicine by enabling optimized therapeutic regimens, refining the timing of interventions, and identifying novel therapeutic targets.

In summary, we propose that type 1 IFN has an important role in treatment‐refractory patients and may provide alternative pro‐inflammatory signals that overwhelm immune regulation. However, at this point we cannot propose that JAKi is reliably the answer for these patients because JAK1 appears to be down‐regulated with secukinumab treatment. Although JAKi targets the type 1 IFN pathway, we suggest that using JAKi in biologic unresponsive patients may lead to the premature conclusion that JAKi is superior to other treatments, when patients may reach adequate response regardless of the third biologic taken. We demonstrate that secukinumab treatment induces immunologic changes associated with reduced inflammation, even in patients who demonstrate an inadequate clinical response. Notably, patients who display these immunologic profiles are more likely to respond to subsequent biologic therapy. Additionally, JAKi would not improve type 1 IFN–induced nociceptive pain because neuronal sensitization is driven through ERK/MNK signaling. The efficacy of JAKi in patients who do not respond to TNFi and IL17i requires further study. Considering the data published and presented, we propose that some treatment‐refractory patients experience increased inflammatory burden contributed by IFNAR1 overexpression. These patients may require increased dosage, combination therapy, or cycling of different targeted therapies to achieve clinical response. In baseball and biologics, two strikes do not necessarily predict defeat—sometimes that third swing delivers the breakthrough hit.
