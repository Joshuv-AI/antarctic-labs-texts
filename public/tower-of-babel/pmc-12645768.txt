Effects of blood flow restriction combined with low resistance training on post-activation performance enhancement in male volleyball players
Authors: Xuanxi Wei, Xin Zheng, Tong Jiang, Ziren Zhao, Hua Zheng, Tailin Wang, Kaixiang Zhou, Jingtao Du, Lei Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood flow restriction combined with low-load resistance training (BFR-RT) may effectively induce post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) in team sport athletes. However, the effectiveness of its application in male volleyball players is unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effect of BFR-RT on PAPE in male volleyball players between 15 s and 20 min after barbell squats.MethodsSixteen male collegiate volleyball players were randomly assigned to two groups, the BFR-RT group and the control group. The BFR-RT group performed two sets of 30 repetitions of barbell squats at 30% of their one-repetition maximum (1RM) with an average pressure of approximately 200 mmHg. The control group did squats at 90% 1RM. PAPE was assessed by measuring countermovement jump (CMJ) height, peak power output (PPO), vertical ground reaction force (vGRF), rate of force development (RFD), and EMG root-mean-square (RMS) of the biceps femoris (BF), vastus lateralis (VL), rectus femoris (RF), vastus medialis (VM), gastrocnemius (GA), and tibialis anterior (TA) at baseline and 15 s, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 min after the squats. A two-factor repeated-measures ANOVA was used for statistical analysis of the metrics.ResultsThe BFR-RT elicited PAPE in male volleyball players, with significant improvements observed in CMJ height. CMJ height significantly increased from 4 to 20 min after the conditioning activity (p < 0.01), with a between-group difference favoring BFR-RT at 16 min. For PPO, vGRF, and RFD, no significant time × group interactions were detected; however, time main effects indicated significant increases across both BFR-RT and RT groups (p < 0.05). Moreover, RMS amplitudes significantly increased over time for six muscles (BF, VL, RF, VM, GA, and TA; all p < 0.01), with no significant between-group differences.ConclusionsBFR-RT may induce acute PAPE in male volleyball players: CMJ exceeded RT at 16 min, PPO favored BFR-RT at 8–20 min, while vGRF, RFD, and EMG rose over time without consistent between-group effects. Given the small sample size, these preliminary findings require larger studies to confirm the potential of BFR-RT compared to traditional resistance training.Keywords: Blood flow restriction, Post-activation performance enhancement, Volleyball players

Background

Introduction

Warm-up exercises are crucial for enhancing the athletic performance of athletes during both training and competition. Typical warm-up routines encompass various types of exercises, including low-intensity aerobic activities, dynamic stretching, movement preparation, and sport-specific drills [1–4]. The acute enhancement of athletic performance after warm-up (e.g., higher jump height) is referred to as post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) [5–7], a phenomenon that most commonly occurs after the completion of maximal or near-maximal intensity conditioning activities (CAs) such as squats, power cleans, and jumps [8–11]. For example, Lamberto et al. found that performing squats at 90% 1RM significantly improves CMJ performance among elite female volleyball players [12]. The rationale for this phenomenon might be related to residual post-activation potentiation (PAP) in its earliest stages after a conditioning activity (CA) and other mechanisms such as enhanced muscle activation, increased muscle temperature, and/or increased shortening velocity triggered by fiber water content [8]. Meta-analyses indicate that high-load (>85% 1RM) resistance training is an effective method for inducing PAPE [7]. For example, Heynen et al. suggested that squats performed at 85% 1RM before competition significantly improved jump performance in elite volleyball players [13]. However, volleyball players are generally tall, and high-load resistance exercises, like barbell squats, may increase the risk of skeletal muscle injuries, which can hinder PAPE. There is increasing interest in alternative methods that can enhance acute performance while reducing mechanical stress.

In recent years, Blood Flow Restriction (BFR) training has emerged as a potential new strategy to enhance athletic performance by generating a stress response similar to high-load resistance training through low-load stimulation [14–16]. BFR training involves the use of an external restraint device, such as a blood pressure cuff or elastic bandage, which is applied to the proximal muscles of the limb to restrict arterial blood flow and slow down venous return while performing the exercise [17]. Vechin et al. [18] found that BFR combined with low-load resistance training (BFR-RT [leg press exercises at 20–30% 1RM]) was as effective as training at 70–80% 1RM for improving leg strength in older adults. In contrast, Cook et al. [19] reported that performing squats at 30% 1RM, combined with BFR, did not significantly improve jump height in college students. One meta-analysis found that >60% of Arterial Occlusion Pressure (AOP) + 40%-70% 1RM BFR resistance training was more likely to promote an optimal protocol for upper limb PAPE [19]; but another meta-analysis indicated that BFR-RT with 50% AOP + 30% 1RM was the optimal protocol to induce PAPE in participants [20]. The inconsistency may be due to differences in the populations, training programs, BFR applications, and training loads. Moreover, there is a lack of empirical studies examining the role of BFR combined with resistance training in inducing PAPE in male volleyball players. Based on the BFR training consensus, low-load BFR-RT (approximately 20–40% of 1RM) should be paired with higher repetitions to ensure sufficient metabolic stimulation [17]. The most commonly prescribed protocol in practice involves accumulating approximately 75 repetitions per exercise (30-15-15-15, 2–4 sets), a sufficient volume to induce adaptation [17, 21]. Brad J. Schoenfeld et al. observed that lifting heavy loads (80–100% 1RM) for 1 to 5 repetitions per set is optimal for maximum strength gains [22].

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to examine the effects of BFR-RT on male volleyball players’ CMJ height, peak power output (PPO), vertical ground reaction force (vGRF), and rate of force development (RFD). In addition, we investigated the effects of BFR-RT on skeletal muscle EMG activity. We hypothesized that the BFR-RT group would produce PAPE effects similar to high-intensity (90% 1RM) resistance training.

Methods

Sixteen male collegiate badminton players participated in this study. Participants were randomly assigned to either the BFR-RT group(n = 8; age: 20.75 ± 2.05 years; height: 191.88 ± 6.83 cm; body mass: 85.81 ± 4.90 kg; training experience: 7.88 ± 1.55 years) or the RT group(n = 8; age: 21.75 ± 2.31 years; height: 189.94 ± 7.51 cm; body mass: 80.46 ± 10.57 kg; training experience: 9.00 ± 3.25 years) using a computer-generated block randomization sequence (block size = 4). All participants had no reported history of lower limb injuries within the preceding three months, no diagnosed cardiovascular diseases, and were proficient in executing barbell deep squat resistance exercises. Before testing, participants were instructed to abstain from high-intensity physical activity, caffeine, and alcohol consumption for at least 24 h, to maintain adequate hydration, and to observe a four-hour fasting period. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of North University of China and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (20240803). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before participating in the experiment.

Post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.7, Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Kiel, Germany) for a repeated-measures ANOVA (2 groups × 7 time points; group × time interaction), with α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and a total sample size of 16. The correlation among repeated measures was set at 0.5. The analysis indicated that the present study was adequately powered to detect interaction effects with a minimum effect size of f ≥ 0.28, which corresponds to a partial eta squared () of approximately 0.07, considered a moderate effect [23].

Procedures

This study used a randomized controlled trial. The experimental procedure consisted of three phases: a familiarization session, a 1RM squat test, and the experimental session. Before the 1RM squat test, participants were informed of the experimental procedures and provided written informed consent. They then completed a standardized 10-minute warm-up, which included submaximal cycling, dynamic stretching, and vertical jumps. After the warm-up, a 5-minute recovery period was given before taking the baseline measurements. Participants underwent a resistance activation protocol (Fig. 1), followed by CMJ assessments conducted at 15 s and at 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 min post-resistance activation. The intervention and testing were conducted in an indoor sports training facility (temperature 24 °C) between 13:30 and 16:30.

PAPE protocol

This study used the relative pressure method to determine each participant’s personalized pressure value for the compression band, setting the inflatable cuff’s pressure based on their specific thigh circumference. Previous research indicates that the relative pressure method enhances muscle adaptation more effectively than applying fixed pressure levels [22]. Thigh circumference was measured with a tape at the 33% point between the proximal border of the patella. and the inguinal crease [23]. To ensure accuracy, we referenced prior datasets and performed a correlation analysis between thigh circumference and AOP [24] (Table 1). Although this method may not be perfect, it provides a relative estimate of BFR stimulus. Before the intervention, participants were instructed to stand upright, with the BFR cuffs (BSTRONG, Utah, USA) placed approximately 1–2 cm above the inguinal fold on each thigh [25, 26]. To ensure safety, pressure was gradually increased to reach the target level. The final inflation pressure was an average of 200 mmHg, approximately 60% AOP [24] (Table 1). Before beginning the barbell squat, all participants completed a 10-minute warm-up (5 min on a cycling ergometer and 5 min of dynamic stretches), followed by a 5-minute rest. Then, the BFR-RT performed two sets of 30 barbell squat repetitions at 30% of their 1RM. The RT group completed two sets of 5 repetitions at 90% of 1RM, with 1-minute rest intervals between sets [27]. This design was chosen because the BFR training consensus, low-load BFR-RT (approximately 20–40% of 1RM), should be paired with higher repetitions to ensure sufficient metabolic stimulation [17]. The most commonly prescribed protocol in practice involves accumulating approximately 75 repetitions per exercise (30-15-15-15, 2–4 sets), a sufficient volume to induce adaptation [17, 21]. Brad J. Schoenfeld et al. observed that lifting heavy loads (80–100% of 1RM) for 1 to 5 repetitions per set is optimal for maximum strength gains [22]. The strength coach stood at each end of the barbell during the workout to ensure proper and safe squat execution.

Measurement

The primary outcomes were CMJ height and PPO. Secondary outcomes included vGRF, RFD, and RMS electromyographic activity.

CMJ height and joint angles were recorded using a VICON infrared 3D motion analysis system (OML Inc., UK) at 100 Hz. CMJ height were determined by comparing the height change of the right anterior superior iliac spine (RASI) at the peak of the jump with that at static stance.

MyoResearch (version: 3.6.32, Noraxon Corporation, USA) was utilized to rectify and low-pass filter (10 Hz) the data from the vertical direction of the floor of the force platform. This study focused on the VL, RF, VM, BF, GA, and TA muscles, which are highly activated during jumping and squatting. These muscles are key for knee and hip extension, as well as ankle movements, and are closely associated with lower-limb explosive performance [24–26]. Surface EMG was collected using a 16-channel wireless system (Noraxon, USA; sampling rate 1500 Hz) with bipolar Ag/AgCl electrodes (inter-electrode distance 20 mm). Electrode placement followed SENIAM: sites were identified by palpating each muscle belly and verified during submaximal isometric contractions. To reduce crosstalk, electrodes were aligned with muscle fibers, positioned at least 2 cm from adjacent muscles, and applied after shaving, light abrasion, and alcohol cleaning to ensure low impedance; procedures followed current EMG reporting checklists. Signals were imported into Visual3D V6 (C-Motion) and processed with a zero-lag fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter (10 to 500 Hz), full-wave rectification, and a 6 Hz low-pass filter to obtain the linear envelope. For normalization, a task-specific dynamic method was used: for each muscle, the mean of the peak amplitudes across three CMJ trials served as the reference rather than MVIC. Statistics

All variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was assessed with Levene’s test. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical package (version 25.0, IBM Statistics, Chicago, IL). A two-way repeated measures ANOVA design was conducted to examine both within- and between-group differences. The effect size was expressed as , with thresholds defined as small (0.01 ≤ ≤ 0.06), moderate (0.06 ≤ < 0.14), and large (≥ 0.14) [23]. When significant interaction or main effects were observed, post hoc analyses were performed, and p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni method. In cases where the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Cohen’s d was calculated to quantify effect sizes (ES) for within-group pre–post comparisons and between-group comparisons, with the following thresholds: trivial (ES < 0.2), small (0.2 ≤ ES ≤ 0.6), moderate (0.6 ≤ ES < 1.2), large (1.2 ≤ ES < 2.0), very large (2.0 ≤ ES < 4), extremely large (ES ≥ 4) [27]. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CMJ height

A significant interaction effect between time and group was observed (F = 3.03, p = 0.03). Simple effects analysis indicated that significant increases from baseline were observed in the BFR-RT group at 4 min (p < 0.01, d = 1.54, large effect), 8 min (p < 0.01, d = 1.05, large effect), 12 min (p = 0.01, d = 1.26, large effect), 16 min (p < 0.001, d = 1.84, large effect) and 20 min (p = 0.04, d = 1.12, large effect). No significant changes were observed at 15 s (p = 0.32, d = 0.25, small effect). Conversely, significant increases were observed in the RT group only at the 4 min (p < 0.001, d = 0.54, moderate effect), 8 min (p = 0.03, d = 0.26, small effect) and 12 min (p = 0.006, d = 0.49, small effect), with no other significant differences observed from baseline at 15 s (p = 0.32, d = 0.12, trivial effect), 16 min (p = 0.33, d = 0.17, trivial effect), or 20 min (p = 0.72, d = 0.07, trivial effect).

Between-group comparisons revealed that a significant difference was observed at 16 min (p = 0.04, d = 1.13, large effect). However, no significant differences were observed between the BFR-RT and RT groups at 15 s (p = 0.89, d = 0.07, small effect), 4 min (p = 0.47, d = 0.37, small effect), 8 min (p = 0.32, d = 0.52, moderate effect), 12 min (p = 0.52, d = 0.33, small effect), or 20 min (p = 0.19, d = 0.69, moderate effect) (Fig. 2).

PPO

A non-significant interaction effect between time and group was observed (F = 0.90, p = 0.49, =0.06, moderate effect). The time main effects analysis showed that significant increases in PPO were observed in both the BFR-RT and RT groups compared to baseline (F = 3.66, p<0.003, =0.21, large effect). The group main effects analysis showed that significant differences were observed between the BFR-RT group and the RT group (F= 4.85, p=0.045, =0.26, large effect) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study examined the effects of BFR-RT on the CMJ height, PPO, vGRF, and RFD in male volleyball players. The findings may indicate that BFR-RT has the potential to induce PAPE in male volleyball players. In addition, BFR-RT may produce effects on CMJ height and PPO similar to high-intensity resistance training.

This study observed that CMJ height significantly increased within a 4 to 20-minute window following barbell squat activation in the BFR-RT group, relative to baseline. This observation extends beyond the commonly reported PAPE time window of 0 to 15 min, as documented in prior studies [28, 29]. The time course and magnitude of PAPE depend on the post-CA balance between enhancement and fatigue: performance improves when enhancement predominates and is limited when fatigue prevails [30]. Additionally, the interval duration is a critical regulatory factor: intervals that are too short tend to let fatigue dominate, while too long cause the gain to diminish [31].To date, there is still no consensus on the “time window” for inducing PAPE. Previous studies have found that short (≈ 5 min), medium (≈ 8–12 min), and long (≈ 18.5 min) intervals may all induce PAPE [32]. A meta-analysis suggests that the optimal interval duration may relate to the load of the concurrent training protocol [33]. Most studies indicate that a 7 to 10-minute interval after CA is optimal for increasing power output. Individual traits greatly affect performance. Those with higher training levels experience PAPE more quickly and intensely [34]. Therefore, the relatively long window of 4–20 min observed in this study may be partially attributable to the player’s high level of training. However, this phenomenon is likely the result of multiple factors acting in concert (such as the BFR protocol, CA type, etc.) and requires further validation.

In this study, although the time × group interaction for PPO was insignificant, we observed a significant main effect of group, indicating that BFR-RT showed higher PPO overall compared to RT across time points. Previous research indicates that combining BFR with resistance training can enhance performance within 12 min post-exercise, depending on the training protocol and intensity. For example, Doma et al. observed increased jump height and power output with BFR during lunge exercises [35]. Wilk et al. reported increased power and bar velocity in bench press exercises [36]. The mechanism behind PAPE after BFR-RT is not fully understood. Metabolites like lactate and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are often linked to performance gains [37]. Lactate may induce metabolic stress associated with short-term performance improvements [37], although its exact impact is unclear. Similarly, while ROS are generally connected to long-term muscle growth, they also support acute performance in BFR training by activating specific recovery pathways [37]. Further research is needed to clarify the roles of these metabolites in performance enhancement. Together, they may contribute to the CMJ height and PPO improvements seen with BFR + RT; however, we emphasize that this interpretation is speculative. Additionally, this study employed a protocol of two sets of 30 repetitions, leading to a significant PAPE response in vertical jump performance. This effect may be due to the alignment of kinematic characteristics between the conditioning contraction and the vertical jump, as research indicates that similar characteristics enhance PAPE induction [38, 39]. This study’s two-set, 30-repetition protocol was essential for generating sufficient metabolic stress. Future research should consider different repetition rhythms and higher external loads (e.g., >30% 1RM) to enhance the PAPE effect further.

In this study, RFD and vGRF did not significantly improve compared to the RT group, which differs from previous findings [21, 35, 40]. This may stem from an inadequate protocol that couldn’t detect subtle changes, especially due to the low load of 30% 1RM and the small sample size, which could have limited the neural response and statistical power for significant improvements. Moreover, the neural responses from the BFR-RT in our study may not align with those in earlier studies. For example, Doma et al. (2020) used lunges with BFR, engaging different muscle groups and movement patterns [35], which could produce different neural responses. These factors may explain the lack of significant changes in RFD and vGRF in our findings.

This study also found that EMG activity in lower limb muscle groups (e.g., RF, VM, GA, TA) significantly increased in the BFR-RT group and RT group during the 4–20 min period. However, no significant differences were observed between groups. This finding is inconsistent with the results reported by Sun et al., who observed higher standardized RMS values in lower limb muscles under BFR-RT compared to non-BFR, with values increasing across sets [21]. Given the differences between the two studies in CA type, training load, and AOP settings, coupled with the fact that the 90% 1RM high-load in the control group of this study was sufficient to induce a significant PAPE effect, this resulted in suppressed intergroup differences. Additionally, Loenneke et al. (2015a) observed a significant increase in activation from 40% to 50% AOP in the lower limbs, with no further enhancement from 50% to 60% AOP [41], suggesting diminishing returns at higher AOP levels. Our findings indicate that EMG results do not support attributing the CMJ and PPO advantages of BFR-RT solely to intergroup differences in surface EMG amplitude. This inferred mechanism requires validation through higher neurophysiological assessments. Additionally, some outcomes showed substantial effect sizes despite non-significant p-values (e.g., VL: p = 0.10, =0.17), suggesting limited power due to small sample size; Given this study post-hoc sensitivity analysis threshold (≈0.07), the non-significant interactions likely reflect limited power for small effects rather than no effect. Future studies should increase the sample size to detect smaller effects.

Limitations

The interpretation of the present findings should be considered in light of several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size substantially reduced the statistical power, so the results should be interpreted cautiously; future research should employ larger samples to validate and extend these findings. Second, the study involved multiple comparisons. However, a Bonferroni correction was applied to reduce the risk of Type I error; this approach cannot eliminate the issue and may increase the likelihood of Type II error. Consequently, some results may still reflect false-positive findings due to multiple testing. Third, results with borderline findings should be treated with caution, especially given the limited statistical power and the potential influence of confounding factors. Fourth, as all participants were male volleyball players, this may not extend to female athletes. Additionally, BFR pressure was determined based on thigh circumference, which may not precisely reflect individualized arterial occlusion pressure. Finally, although EMG data were collected from several key muscles, important contributors to jumping performance, such as the gluteus maximus, were not assessed, constraining the interpretation of overall muscle activation patterns.

Conclusions

This study preliminarily indicates that BFR-RT may induce acute PAPE in male volleyball players: CMJ exceeded RT at 16 min, PPO favored BFR-RT at 8–20 min, while vGRF, RFD, and EMG rose over time without consistent between-group effects. Due to sample size and measurement limitations, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should employ larger samples and include female participants to systematically examine how training load, occlusion pressure, and rest interval duration influence the individualized expression of PAPE.
