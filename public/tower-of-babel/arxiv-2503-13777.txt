8-Calves Image dataset
Xuyang Fang, Sion Hannuna, Neill Campbell

Abstract
We introduce the 8-Calves dataset, a benchmark for evaluating object detection and identity preservation in occlusion-rich, temporally consistent environments. Comprising a 1-hour video (67,760 frames) of eight Holstein Friesian calves with unique coat patterns and 900 static frames, the dataset emphasizes real-world challenges like prolonged occlusions, motion blur, and pose variation. By fine-tuning 28 object detectors (YOLO variants, transformers) and evaluating 23 pretrained backbones (ResNet, ConvNextV2, ViTs), we expose critical architectural trade-offs: smaller models (e.g., ConvNextV2 Nano, 15.6M parameters) excel in efficiency and retrieval accuracy, while pure vision transformers lag in occlusion-heavy settings. The dataset’s structured design—fixed camera views, natural motion, and verified identities—provides a reproducible testbed for object detection challenges (mAP50:95: 56.5–66.4%), bridging synthetic simplicity and domain-specific complexity. The dataset and benchmark code are all publicly available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/tonyFang04/8-calves . Limitations include partial labeling and detector bias, addressed in later sections.

Introduction

Benchmark datasets are indispensable tools for advancing machine learning, offering standardized environments to evaluate model robustness, generalization, and efficiency. While large-scale datasets like ImageNet
[
1
]
and COCO
[
2
]
drive progress in general-purpose vision systems, they often obscure foundational challenges—such as occlusion handling, temporal consistency, and identity preservation—within the noise of variable domains or massive annotation requirements. Conversely, overly simplistic datasets like MNIST
[
3
]
fail to capture real-world dynamics. The 8-Calves dataset bridges this gap with a compact benchmark for testing detection and vision models under real-world constraints.

The dataset captures 67,760 frames from a 1-hour video of eight Holstein Friesian calves in a barn, alongside 900 static frames, emphasizing natural behaviors such as feeding, occlusion, and fast motion. Unlike DanceTrack, which focuses on choreographed human movement, the 8-Calves dataset introduces unstructured, prolonged sequences that mimic real-world scenarios, as detailed in Section II. By fixing camera angles and object counts while preserving unique coat patterns, it enables focused analysis of detection robustness and identity preservation—a balance not achieved in existing benchmarks like 3D POP (pigeons)
[
4
]
, which lack distinct identities or temporal continuity.

Our benchmarking of 28 object detectors and 23 pretrained vision banckbones (Section 3 and 4) reveals critical insights for the community. While all models achieve near-ceiling performance on lenient metrics (95–98% mAP50), fine-grained detection (mAP50:95) remains challenging, with performance ranging widely from 56.5% to 66.4%. Notably, smaller architectures like ConvNextV2 Nano
[
5
]
(15.6M parameters) outperform larger counterparts in efficiency and accuracy, underscoring their viability for edge deployment. Conversely, pure transformer backbones (e.g., ViT
[
6
]
) struggle in occlusion-heavy settings, reinforcing the need for hybrid architectures. These findings highlight the dataset’s ability to expose model limitations and provide practical guidance for architectural trade-offs in real-world applications.

The dataset’s design intentionally avoids domain-specific complexity, aligning it with foundational ML challenges rather than agricultural applications. As discussed in Section 5, its closest counterpart, DanceTrack
[
7
]
, shares superficial similarities (unique identities in motion) but diverges in critical ways: 8-Calves offers longer sequences ( 1 hour vs. minutes) and natural motion patterns, making it uniquely suited for evaluating temporal consistency. Limitations—such as partial labeling (1 of 9 raw videos annotated) and detector bias—are addressed in later sections, alongside proposals for future work, including multi-view expansions and semi-supervised labeling.

By prioritizing simplicity and focus, the 8-Calves dataset complements existing resources, offering researchers a tool to refine occlusion handling, temporal reasoning, and efficiency—challenges often masked by large-scale benchmarks. All experiments were completed on either one NVIDIA RTX2080 or A100 GPU with 8-32 cores of Intel(R) Xeon(R) Silver 4112 CPU @ 2.60GHz or AMD EPYC 7543 32-Core Processor.

2
8-Calves dataset

Our raw data consists of nine 1-hour videos of the same 8 Holstein Friesian calves in a barn during feeding times. The resolution and frame rate of these videos are 600x800 and 20 respectively. During feeding time, the calves frequently occlude each other and change poses, orientations, and aspect ratios. Sometimes they move at fast speed which results in blurs. Our original plan was to get the ground truth bounding boxes and identities for all 9 videos. But due to the time-consuming nature of manual labelling, we only obtained them for one video. While this limits the dataset’s temporal diversity, the annotated hour still captures occlusion-rich interactions of the calves during their feeding hours.

We aimed to obtain both bounding boxes and identities while minimizing manual labelling. Hence, we applied a state-of-the-art Multi-Object Tracker to our videos and manually corrected tracking errors. We selected ByteTrack
[
8
]
, an open-source tracker ranked in the top 12 and 14 on MOT20 and MOT17 leaderboards
[
9
]
. As a track-by-detection algorithm, ByteTrack
[
8
]
requires integration with an Object Detector. Therefore, we fine-tune Object Detectors provided by Ultralytics
[
10
]
, an easy-to-use open-source library. It also provides a built-in ByteTrack
[
8
]
tracker that can be integrated with their detectors. To obtain the data for fine-tuning, we randomly selected 900 frames, 100 from each of the 9 raw videos. We hand labelled the bounding boxes. We fine-tuned the Ultralytics YOLOV8m object detector
[
11
]
using these data.

We combined our detector with ByteTrack
[
8
]
, and applied them to our raw data. However, due to the time consuming nature of manual labelling, we only eliminated the false positives, swapped identities and miss tracks for one video. Nevertheless, this still results in 537908 correct bounding boxes and identities, verified by our manual inspection. Our manual inspection revealed
\sim
3000 missing boxes (0.56% of total labels). This would minimally impact training and evaluating object detectors, whilst have no effect on cow identification.

3
Benchmarking object detectors on temporal cow detection

We benchmarked state-of-the-art detectors after fine-tuning them on a small portion of our dataset. For thorough evaluation, we used all 67,760 frames from the labeled video as our test set. To avoid data leakage, we only used 600 out of the 900 randomly selected and hand labelled frames as training and validation data. We sorted these 600 frames chronologically, and selected the first 500 as the training set, the rest as the validation set, which we used for early stopping. For each model, we repeated the training, validation and testing process 3 times to compute the averages and standard deviation of the model performance.

We chose 28 models to benchmark. These included YOLOV6 (n, s, m, l, x)

For data augmentation during training, we chose rotate 90, horizontal flip, brightness contrast and elastic transform. We avoided augmentations that would change the fixed number of cows per image and alter the data distribution, such as copy paste, mosaic, perspective, mixup, crop fraction, erasing and grey scale. Due to time constraint, for all models, we fixed the probability of rotate 90, horiziontal flip, brightness contrast and elastic transform to 1.0, 0.5, 0.4, and 0.5, with the
\alpha

We highlighted the benchmarks of all models in Table

However, the significant drop in mAP75 (61.6-78.9%) and mAP50:95 (56.5-66.4%) demonstrates the challenge of fine-grained detection that our dataset poses. And the wilder spread of mAP75 and mAP50:95 displays our dataset’s capability to expose performance disparities between different models.

The top 2 performed models at fine-grained detection were YOLOV6m (66.4% mAP50:95, 78.9% mAP75) and YOLOV8x (65.7% mAP50:95, 77.8% mAP75). YOLOv9c (64.9% mAP50:95, 77.0% mAP75) respectively. YOLOv9t is the most efficient, with only 2.1 million parameters, the smallest among all variants. However, it offers the best parameter-to-performance ratio, making it the best edge deployment. Comparison between transformer based models underscores the struggles of models with pure vision transformer backbone (YOLOS Small), and reinforces the necessity of hybrid architecture (Conditional and Facebook DETR).

The YOLOv6 and v10 series both exhibit signs of overfitting as model size scales exponentially (see Figure

While YOLOv6m (52M parameters) achieves the best mAP50:95 (66.4%) in its family, larger variants like YOLOv6l (110.9M parameters, 63.6%) and YOLOv6x (173.1M parameters, 62.3%) show declining performance. Similarly, YOLOv10x (31.8M parameters, 60.2%) lags behind YOLOv10l (25.9M parameters, 62.2%).

Benchmarking vision models on temporal cow identification

Our 537908 Ground Truth bounding boxes with Ground Truth IDs allowed us to evaluate transfer learning capabilities of existing vision models. We extracted all of our bounding boxes from our video, reshaped them to 224 x 224, and then forward passed them through all 23 models without standardising to ImageNet’s
[
1
]
mean. This gave us 23 embedding spaces. We used these embeddings to complete two Downstream tasks: Temporal Linear Classification and Temporal K-Nearest-Neighbours (KNN). The former tested the global structure of the embedding space: whether it was clear enough for a linear classifier to distinguish between the 8 calves. The latter evaluated the local geometry of the embedding space: whether embeddings of the same calf were indeed closely together.

For both tasks, we split each embedding space chronologically. For temporal classification, we used a 30/30/40 train, validation and test split, where the validation set is used for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping. We used a batch size of 32, AdamW optimiser, a learning rate of 0.005, cross entropy loss, total training epoch of 100, early stopping patience of 10 epochs, and variance scaling to initialise the parameters in the classifier. For KNN, we split our dataset into 50/50 train and test. To improve the inference speed, we used FAISS KNN with IndexIVFPQ quantiser. We set number of subquantisers to 8, bits per subquantiser to 8, and number of probes to 10. For each model, we repeated the training, validation and testing process 2 times to compute the averages and standard deviation of the model performance.

The ConvNextV2 Nano model (15.6M parameters) stands out as the top performer in Table
2
, achieving the highest accuracy (73.35%) and KNN Top-1 retrieval (50.82%), demonstrating that smaller, optimized architectures excel in occlusion-heavy calf identification. However, this success does not mask the overfitting of ConvNextV2 as model size scales up. The performance of larger variants collapses catastrophically, with the Huge model (657.5M parameters) plummeting to just 42.28% accuracy—a 31% drop.

In contrast, other architectures exhibit diminishing returns with scale, though less drastically (see Table
2
and Figure
3
). For example, Swin Transformers show modest gains: the Large variant (197.0M parameters) achieves 70.47% accuracy, only 3.8% higher than Swin-Tiny (66.59%), despite being 6.9x larger. Similarly, ResNet101 (64.56% accuracy) improves marginally over ResNet18 (60.45%) at 3.8x the parameter cost. Pure ViTs scale poorly across the board, with ViT Large (56.12% accuracy) barely surpassing ViT Base (54.70%) despite 3.5x more parameters.

As model sizes scale up, most model families display drops in KNN Top-1 and Top-5, whilst accuracy increases (see Figure
4
and
5
). This implies currently, smaller models retain finer local details critical for retrieval, while larger models focus on broader contextual patterns.

At Base size, ConvNextV2 and Swin have marginally different performances. However, they both significantly outperform ViT, even with the same pretraining method and data. This reinforces the struggle of pure ViT backbone against the hybrid CNN+transformer backbone (Swin and ConvNextV2). The performance gap between ResNet family, and ConvNextV2 and Swin are likely due to the pretraining data disparities, as the former were only pretrained on ImageNet1K.

BEiT, pretrained via masked image modeling on ImageNet1K, achieves the highest accuracy (67.61%) and KNN performance (Top-1: 42.40%, Top-5: 63.33%), underscoring its effectiveness in learning discriminative features for fine-grained identification. The results emphasize that pretraining strategies prioritizing semantic feature learning (e.g., BEiT’s masked modeling) outperform those reliant on scale (DINOv2) or low-level reconstruction (MAE).

5
Related works

The 8-Calves dataset aligns most closely with 3D-POP
[
4
]
, a benchmark featuring groups of pigeons filmed in closed environments. Both datasets share three core characteristics: 1) Fixed groups of objects: A consistent number of individuals (pigeons in 3D-POP
[
4
]
, calves in ours) with unique visual patterns. 2) Temporal continuity: Recordings span multiple days, though 3D-POP
[
4
]
sequences are shorter (
\sim
10 minutes per video) compared to our 1-hour sequences. 3) Natural motion dynamics: Both emphasize unstructured, ground-based behaviors—pigeons in 3D-POP
[
4
]
primarily walk, while calves engage in feeding and occlusion-heavy interactions. However, 3D POP’s
[
4
]
utility for identity distinction is limited by subtle pigeon coat patterns, which are far less visually discernible than the distinct markings of calves.

DanceTrack
[
7
]
, while sharing superficial similarities (fixed camera angles and multi-object sequences), diverges in critical ways. Its focus on choreographed human motion (e.g., dance, gymnastics) introduces domain-specific constraints: individuals often wear identical outfits or obscure facial features (e.g., masks, hats), eliminating unique visual identifiers. Additionally, DanceTrack
[
7
]
sequences are brief (minutes vs. our 1-hour recordings) and prioritize scripted motion, contrasting with the natural, unstructured behaviors captured in our work.

MNIST
[
3
]
and CIFAR10
[

6
Conclusion

The 8-Calves dataset presented in this work serves as a compact yet challenging benchmark for evaluating object detection and vision backbone models in temporally consistent, occlusion-rich environments. Designed as a ’sanity check’ tool for machine learning pipelines, it bridges the gap between simplistic synthetic datasets (e.g., MNIST) and large-scale, domain-specific benchmarks. With 537,908 annotated bounding boxes from a 1-hour video and 900 hand-labelled static frames, the dataset emphasizes real-world challenges such as motion blur, pose variation, and identity preservation under occlusion—all within a controlled setting of fixed camera views and fixed object counts.

Our benchmarking reveals critical insights for the vision community. Despite near-ceiling mAP50 scores (95.2–98.9%), mAP50:95 (56.5–66.4%) and mAP75 (61.6–78.9%) expose stark performance gaps, particularly in occlusion-heavy scenarios. YOLOv6m (66.4% mAP50:95) and YOLOv8x (65.7% mAP50:95) emerged as top performers, while transformer-based models (e.g., YOLOS Small: 56.5% mAP50:95) lagged significantly, underscoring the need for hybrid architectures. Smaller models like YOLOv9t (2.1M parameters) and ConvNextV2 Nano (15.6M parameters) achieved competitive accuracy with minimal computational overhead, making them ideal for edge deployment. Conversely, larger YOLOv6/YOLOv10 variants exhibited overfitting, with performance declining as parameters scaled exponentially. Transfer learning experiments highlighted ConvNextV2 Nano’s superior temporal linear classification accuracy (73.35%), outperforming Swin Transformers and ResNets, while pure ViTs (54.7% accuracy) struggled with occlusion-induced identity shifts.

The dataset’s unique contributions lie in its temporal continuity and domain-agnostic design. While DanceTrack shares similarities (unique identities in motion), 8-Calves introduces distinct challenges: longer sequences ( 1 hour vs. minutes) and naturalistic, non-choreographed motion patterns (e.g., feeding calves vs. dancers). These features make it particularly suited for evaluating temporal consistency and robustness to gradual appearance changes.

Key limitations include the partial annotation of raw data (1 of 9 hours labeled) and inherent detector bias toward YOLO architectures, which may skew comparisons with transformer-based models. Future work could expand annotations to additional hours, incorporate multi-view recordings, and explore semi-supervised labeling to mitigate manual effort. For the broader ML community, this dataset provides a pragmatic testbed for refining models in occlusion handling, temporal reasoning, and efficiency—challenges often masked by large-scale benchmarks. By prioritizing simplicity and focus over domain specificity, 8-Calves aims to complement existing resources in advancing foundational vision capabilities.
