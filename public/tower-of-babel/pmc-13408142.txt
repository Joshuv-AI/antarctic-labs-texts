An integrative in silico and in vitro synergy of Cinnamomum verum essential oil and cinnamaldehyde with imipenem against extensively drug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria
Authors: Saoussen Jilani, Malek Besbes, Mohamed Ferjeni, Salwa Ahmed Lotfi, Kholoud Al-Shammery, Zhaojun Ban, Haya Rashid Mohammed AlTamimi, Amr Farouk, Walid Ben Selma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae bacterial pathogens are associated with multidrug-resistant hospital-acquired infections. This study aimed to assess the phytochemical composition, antibacterial efficacy, synergistic interactions, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics of the essential oil Cinnamomum verum (Civ-EO) and its major compound. Cinnamaldehyde was identified as the major component of Civ-EO (70.15%). Disc diffusion and MBC/MIC showed advanced antibacterial activity of Civ-EO against extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains of Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Synergism between Cinn-EO and imipenem was revealed by combined disc diffusion and confirmed by checkerboard assays against all XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates (FICI˂0.5). Cinnamaldehyde displayed important antibacterial effects alone and in combination with imipenem (FICI˂0.5) against all used XDR and susceptible strains. Molecular docking demonstrated the highest interaction between cinnamaldehyde and undecaprenyl pyrophosphate synthase enzymes and D-alanine: D-alanine ligase. According to molecular dynamics simulations, the stabilizing effects of ligand binding on the target enzyme reduce its activity. The current study demonstrates the highly antibacterial effects of cinnamaldehyde in combination with imipenem against XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae, which could constitute an interesting strategy for combating these bacteria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50224-8.Keywords: Antibiotic resistance, Cinnamaldehyde, Cinnamomum verum essential oil, XDR-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, Synergism, Molecular docking, Dynamic simulation

Introduction

Due to the rapid and widespread emergence of antimicrobial resistance and the scarcity of new antibiotics, infections, especially those caused by Gram-negative bacteria, are becoming increasingly challenging for clinicians and a significant problem for global public health1–3. Thus, the World Health Organization (WHO) categorizes carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter and Enterobacteriaceae as urgent global threats1.

Carbapenems are an important class of antibiotics that are first-line treatments for infections caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens. Based on its extensive antibacterial activity, including coverage against Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and anaerobic bacteria, imipenem is widely considered an important member of the carbapenem class of antibiotics. Moreover, it is widely used in the treatment of various severe infections, such as infective endocarditis, bone and joint infections, abdominal infections, urinary tract infections, reproductive system infections, lower respiratory tract infections, septicemia, and severe infections caused by unidentified pathogens1. Historically, multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) Gram-negative bacterial strains of Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii have been detected worldwide and have numerous classical mechanisms of antibiotic resistance4. Therefore, the world is currently facing a substantial and increasing threat from the rapid and easy emergence of bacterial strains that have acquired different mechanisms of resistance to almost all available antibiotics5. As reported by the Infectious Diseases Society of America in the “Bad Bugs, No Drugs” paper, “as antibiotic discovery stagnates, a public health crisis brews”6.

Acinetobacter baumannii, a Gram-negative bacterium, is classified in the Gram-negative ESKAPE pathogens accountable for MDR nosocomial infections; Pseudomonas aeruginosa (carbapenem-resistant), Acinetobacter baumannii (carbapenem-resistant), Enterobacter spp. (third-generation cephalosporin-resistant), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (third-generation cephalosporin-resistant)7. These bacteria are frequently associated with pneumonia, urinary tract infections, meningitis, bacteremia, and wound infections, particularly in burn patients8. Moreover, this bacterial pathogen developed intrinsic resistance to various antibiotic families; additional acquired resistance mechanisms have spread during the past three decades9. The resistance of Acinetobacter baumannii to imipenem increased to 85% in certain areas, posing a severe public health risk. Previous studies reported that hospital death rates are high, ranging from 50 to 64%10.

Klebsiella spp. is a Gram-negative, rod-shaped bacterium belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family and is one of the determinants of nosocomial infections11. It was reported that Klebsiella pneumoniae has become, in the last two decades, an important pathogen associated to healthcare and being related with approximately 14–20% of the infections of the urinary tract, respiratory tract, surgical wounds, and lower biliary duct and is mostly the causative agent correlated with a high fatality amount if the infection remains untreated in hospital’ health care units1.

The increasing amount of emerging isolates of Klebsiella pneumoniae resistant to antibiotics is a foremost problem worldwide12. The XDR Klebsiella pneumoniae, characterized by resistance to multiple antibiotic classes, including cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones, has been reported in several studies1. Moreover, the World Health Organization has recently published a list of priority pathogens, including XDR-Klebsiella pneumoniae, for which the development of new antibiotics is urgently needed1. The Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates that are resistant to carbapenem are related to a simultaneous presence of one or more of the following mechanisms: low outer membrane permeability, altered penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), efflux pump overexpression, stable derepression of AmpC, or synthesis of class B metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), and carbapenem-hydrolysing class D oxacillinases13,14.

To address this global issue, the WHO has announced an urgent need to encourage the research and development of new drugs or strategies1. Thus, numerous studies have been conducted to discover novel antimicrobials for clinical use in the eradication of MDR bacteria. However, as no new antimicrobial has been found to combat MDR Gram-negative bacteria, other strategies are proposed and evaluated in vitro15–19. These strategies primarily recovered from the use of antibiotics and other non-antibiotic compounds, commonly known as antibiotic adjuvants. Therefore, they were assessed as a discovery approach to enhance the activity of conventional antibiotics and increase the susceptibility of MDR bacterial strains. This approach is based on investigating natural products due to their great diversity and relative abundance. Hence, natural products, such as essential oils, which comprise a wide range of chemical compounds, are becoming an attractive and popular mainstream platform for researchers in drug discovery20,21. Therefore, previous studies have shown that various plant essential oils, such as Thymus capitatus17, Cinnamomum zeylanicum22, Thymus algeriensis23, etc., have significant antimicrobial potential due to their potent bactericidal activity against a wide range of bacterial pathogens. Thus, evidence from multiple previous studies suggests that the essential oils of cinnamon bark, clove basil, and oregano disrupt bacterial membranes, ultimately killing the target bacteria24.

The genus Cinnamomum belongs to the large Lauraceae family, which comprises 250 species, mainly in Asia, China, and Australia25. Cinnamomum verum is an important spice of the Cinnamomum genus, with cinnamaldehyde as a major component26. Cinnamomum verum has been utilized for insulin receptor functions in diabetes treatment27, antibacterial and antibiofilm properties28, and in vitro and in vivo anticancer activity against hepatocellular carcinoma and colorectal adenocarcinoma cells29. These diverse effects result from its rich phytochemical composition, primarily cinnamaldehyde.

Carbapenems are one of the last lines of defense against antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections and are now the treatment of choice for serious infections caused by ESKAPE bacterial pathogens. However, the resistance of these bacteria to this family of antibiotics renders this optional treatment ineffective. Thus, the objectives of this study are to (i) determine the composition of Cinnamomum verum essential oil by conventional technique; (ii) evaluate the antimicrobial activities of Cinnamomum verum against XDR strains of Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae; (iii) check for plausible synergism between imipenem and Cinnamomum verum essential oil and its major compound could restore this antibiotic activity against XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae; (iv) estimate by molecular docking the interaction between Cinnamomum verum essential oil and ten vital enzymes as targets to inhibit bacterial growth.

Materials and methods

Ethics declarations

The current study and all experimental protocols were approved by the ethical committee CER-SVS/ of the Higher Institute of Biotechnology of Monastir, with the reference number “003/2022”. The ethical committee CER-SVS waived the need for consent to participate because no human participants were included. All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the ethical committee CER-SVS.

Bacterial isolates

The study was conducted on bacterial isolates of Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae obtained from specimens used for routine bacteriological analysis in the Microbiological Laboratory at the University Hospital Taher Sfar, Mahdia, Tunisia. Clinical specimens from the urinary tract, ascites, drainage fluid, cerebrospinal fluid, lower respiratory tract secretions, blood, and wounds of hospitalized patients in the critical care unit yielded isolates. Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates were identified presumptively through microscopic inspection and biochemical testing, including Gram staining.

The XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae were characterized as isolates resistant to aminoglycosides, carbapenems, cephalosporins, and fluoroquinolones. Isolates were included in this study if they were resistant to imipenem by the broth microdilution method as recommended by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI)30, with the criteria of minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of ≥ 4 μg/ml for imipenem as recommended by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST)31.

As a reference for susceptibility testing, susceptible bacterial strains of E. coli ATCC 25,922, Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 17,978, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 were included as the quality control organisms.

Antibacterial susceptibility assay

The disk diffusion assay (Oxoid, USA) was used to evaluate antibacterial susceptibility testing on Mueller–Hinton agar plates (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as previously recommended30,31. The conventional guidelines were used as a reference to interpret the obtained results30. All bacterial isolates included in this study were resistant to β-lactam (Aztreonam, Ceftazidim, Cefixime, Cefepime, Cefoxitin), carbapenems (meropenem, ertapenem, imipenem), β-lactam combination with inhibitor agents (Piperacillin-Tazobactam, Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid), penicillins (ampicillin, ticarcillin, piperacillin), cephems (cefotaxim, ceftazidime), fluoroquinolones and quinolones (ciprofloxacin, nalidixic acid), and aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin). The antibiotic resistance profile of the selected strains was presented in Supplementary file 2.

Phenotypic detection of carbapenemase production

All isolates resistant to carbapenem (imipenem and/or ertapenem) were tested for carbapenemase production by the modified carbapenem Inactivation method (mCIM)30. About 1–4 loops full of each bacterial isolate were suspended in 2 mL of tryptic soy broth, and a 10 μg meropenem disk was submerged in each tube, which was incubated at ambient temperature for 4 h ± 15 min. Fresh carbapenem-susceptible ATCC 25,922 Escherichia coli, ATCC 17,978 Acinetobacter baumannii, and ATCC 700,603 Klebsiella pneumoniae were suspended in sterile saline, checked for an equivalent 0.5 McFarland with a densitometer, and spread onto Mueller–Hinton agar. Then, meropenem was removed from each tube, placed on Mueller–Hinton agar, and incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 18–24 h, and the results were interpreted according to the CLSI 2023 guideline30. Measure of inhibition zone with a diameter of 6–15 mm or pinpoint colonies in 16–18 mm were determined as positive for carbapenemase enzyme production; and a zone of inhibition ≥ 19 mm was considered to be negative for carbapenemase enzyme production. We used Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603, Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 17,978, and an Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 17,978 non-carbapenemase producer as the quality control strains.

Extraction of essential oil

Cinnamomum verum Blume was purchased at a local market in Mahdia, a city in Tunisia’s central region. To extract the essential oil, 100 g of the samples were ground to obtain small particles, ensuring the complete extraction of the bioactive compounds. They were then immersed in 700 mL of distilled water. The extraction was performed by hydrodistillation using a Clevenger apparatus for 2 h, as previously reported23. The whitish distillate obtained by hydrodistillation was treated with sodium sulfate to dehydrate the water and separate and collect the essential oil molecules. Next, the essential oil was stored in closed bottles at 4 °C in the dark until further use.

Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry Analysis

An Agilent-Technologies model 5890 II GC gas chromatograph system coupled to mass spectrometry (Agilent 5977B GC/MSD) containing a HP-5MS capillary column (0.25 mm film thickness, 30 m, 0.25 mm i.d.) was used to analyze the phytochemical components of essential oil’ Cinnamomum verum. The chromatographic separation protocol was performed as previously described23. The Wiley 6th. edition mass spectral library, standards, and conventional published data were used to identify peaks in the obtained chromatogram. For each phytocompound, a Kovats index was determined based on retention times of C6-C26 n-alkanes and matched to the standards reported in the literature32.

Preparation of stock solutions for the antimicrobial assays

Imipenem and cinnamaldehyde were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO). Fresh stock solutions of essential oil’ Cinnamomum verum, cinnamaldehyde, and imipenem were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Mueller–Hinton II cation-adjusted (MH-II) broth, respectively, to obtain a final concentration of 100 mg/mL for each compound. Working concentrations for assays were prepared by serial two-fold dilution in cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton II broth; final concentration ranges tested were 0.078–10 mg·mL⁻1 for Cinnamomum verum essential oil, 1–2000 µg·mL⁻1 for cinnamaldehyde, and 2–4096 µg·mL⁻1 for imipenem. The concentration of DMSO did not exceed 0.05% in the solutions of Cinnamomum verum essential oil, and this level is non-toxic to bacteria33.

Bacterial inoculum

For every bacterial strain included in this study, CFU counts were used to prepare the bacterial inoculum according to previous recommendations30. Thus, a 1 × 105 CFU/mL bacterial inoculum was used for microdilution assays, prepared from overnight cultures, diluted to 0.5 McFarland (≈1 × 108 CFU/mL), then diluted 1:1000.

Antimicrobial resistance/susceptibility test

The disk diffusion assay (Oxoid, USA) on Mueller–Hinton agar plates (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), as previously recommended, was used to evaluate the antimicrobial resistance/susceptibility testing30,31. The results obtained were compared and analyzed according to the conventional published guidelines31. Susceptibility or resistance of bacterial strains was evaluated to these antibiotics’ β-lactam (Aztreonam, Cefoxitin, Ceftazidim, Cefepime, Cefixime,), penicillin’s (ampicillin, ticarcillin, piperacillin), β-lactam combination agent (Piperacillin-Tazobactam, Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid), fluoroquinolones and quinolones (ciprofloxacin, nalidixic acid), carbapenems (imipenem, ertapenem, meropenem), cephems (cefotaxim, ceftazidime), and aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin). XDR bacterial strains are defined as those of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii that show phenotypic susceptibility to one antibiotic class (Supplementary file 2).

Determination of the MICs of imipenem

The microdilution technique was used to determine the MICs of imipenem (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) against selected bacteria, including Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii, as well as susceptible strains, as previously recommended30,31. Sterilized Mueller Hinton II cation-adjusted broth was placed in 96-well microplates (SARSTEDT AG & Co. KG, Numbrecht, Germany). Fresh solution of ciprofloxacin was diluted from 2048 to 0.125 µg/mL using a two-fold dilution method. Then, 100 µL of 105 CFU/mL XDR bacterial isolates and Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 17,978, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 were added to the wells. All of these bacterial isolates were tested simultaneously. After overnight incubation, wells showing visible bacterial growth at concentrations of 4 µg/mL or greater were classified as resistant to imipenem, following the recommendations of EUCAST31.

Disc diffusion

The antibacterial activity of Cinnamomum verum essential oil was determined according to the protocol described by Ben Selma and coworkers against XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumonia17,34. Briefly, a microbial suspension at 105 CFU/mL was spread onto Mueller–Hinton (MH) agar (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in a Petri plate. Sterilized discs (6 mm diameter) were impregnated with 10 µL of essential oil’ Cinnamomum verum of 10 mg·mL⁻1, and the discs were then placed at the center of the plates. Then, the Petri plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. Three independent experiment repetitions were performed.

To determine the antibacterial activity of Cinnamomum verum essential oil, the inhibition zone around the disc was measured, and the results were classified as follows: extremely sensitive = diameter ≥ 20 mm; very sensitive = diameter varying between 15 and 19 mm; sensitive = diameter varying between 8 and 14 mm; not sensitive = diameter ˂8 mm16,17.

Microdilution determination of MIC and MBC

The minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of Cinnamomum verum essential oil and cinnamaldehyde against XDR and sensitive bacterial isolates was determined by broth microdilution test following the recommendations of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute27,28. Experiments were conducted in triplicate using XDR strains of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii. Briefly, the cinnamaldehyde and Cinnamomum verum essential oil were diluted by microdilution assay in a sterile ELISA plate at concentrations ranging from 0.125 to 2048 μg/mL and 0.078 to 10 mg/mL, respectively. In each culture well, 100 μL of the105 CFU/mL bacterial suspension was added. The control wells received only 100 μL of culture medium. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that inhibited bacterial growth, with negative controls to ensure no contamination and positive controls to demonstrate normal bacterial growth.

The minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) of Cinnamomum verum essential oil and cinnamaldehyde against each bacterial strain was determined by spreading 10 µL from each well without clear bacterial growth onto a Petri plate, which was then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The concentration at which no microbial growth occurred was identified as the MBC16,17. Each test was performed in triplicate in two independent experiments. The Cinnamomum verum essential oil and cinnamaldehyde were characterized as having bactericidal activity when the MBC/MIC ratio was < 416,35.

Combined use of imipenem and Cinnamomum verum essential oil against XDR bacterial strains

Combined disk diffusion and checkerboard assays were used to evaluate the simultaneous interaction between Cinnamomum verum essential oil and imipenem against XDR bacterial strains.

Combined disc diffusion

As previously described, a combined disc diffusion assay was used to assess potential synergism between Cinnamomum verum essential oil and imipenem against XDR bacterial strains16,17. Briefly, a suspension of each bacterial strain at 105 CFU/mL was spread onto MH agar (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in a Petri plate. Then, a commercial disk containing imipenem at 10 µg/mL (Oxoid, France) was placed on the inoculated plates. Next, 10 μL of essential oil from Cinnamomum verum was added to each disk, and the plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The obtained results were interpreted as a synergistic interaction between the two antibacterial agents if the difference in the imipenem inhibition zone in the presence or absence of the essential oil was ≥ 4 mm.

Checkerboard assay

The checkerboard method, as we have previously described16,17, was used to analyze the effects of the simultaneous use of Cinnamomum verum essential oil and imipenem against XDR strains of Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae. A prepared 96-well plate with combined serial twofold dilutions in MH-II broth of imipenem along the horizontal rows and Cinnamomum verum essential oil along the vertical rows. The concentrations of Cinnamomum verum essential oil and imipenem ranged from 10 to 0.009 mg/mL and from 2048 to 0.007 µg/mL, respectively. Then, 100 μL of bacterial suspension at 105 CFU/mL was added. Finally, plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Synergism between cinnamaldehyde and imipenem

All imipenem-resistant bacterial isolates of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii, as well as the Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 17,978, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 strains, were used to evaluate an eventual interaction between cinnamaldehyde and imipenem. The synergistic activity of these antimicrobial agents was investigated using a checkerboard assay, as we previously reported16,17. Briefly, the antibiotic combination was cross-diluted in the wells of a 96-well microplate using Mueller–Hinton II cation-adjusted broth. The imipenem was diluted vertically, with concentrations ranging from 1024 to 0.125 μg/L. On the other hand, the cinnamaldehyde was diluted horizontally, with concentrations ranging from 125 to 0.125 μg/L. The bacterial inoculum (105 CFU/mL) was added to the wells, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The results were analyzed according to the FICI value, as stated below: FICI = FIC imipenem + FIC cinnamaldehyde.

The results were analyzed using the FICI, defined as the ratio of the MIC of the combined substances to the MIC of the isolated substances. The results were interpreted and categorized as synergism (FICI ≤ 0.5), additive (0.5 < FICI ≤ 4), or antagonist (4 < FICI).

Molecular docking

Crystal structures of isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (PDB ID: 6LDK), DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 6FM4), dihydropteroate synthase (PDB ID: 1AJ2), Sortase A (PDB ID: 1T2P), penicillin-binding protein 3 (PDB ID: 4KQR), IV topoisomerase (PDB ID: 7LHZ), dihydrofolate reductase (PDB ID: 6XG5), D-alanine: D-alanine ligase (PDB ID: 2ZDQ), and undecaprenyl pyrophosphate synthase (PDB IDs: 2E98 and 1X06) were used as receptors. These structures were found in the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/, accessed on August 17, 2022, December 11–12, and December 25–26, 2023). Ligands were retrieved from PubChem (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed January 24, 2024). Ions, co-crystallized ligands, and water molecules were removed from the protonated receptors using Pymol software version 2.5.1. The three-dimensional structure of each ligand was optimized using Avogadro Software version 1.2.0. Blind docking between ligands and receptors was conducted using CB-DOCK2 (http://clab.labshare.cn/cb-dock/php/, accessed January 24, 2024), following a previously published protocol36. The AutoDock Vina was used to dock the top five binding cavities further. The best-docked complexes were subsequently analyzed using Discovery Studio software, as previously reported37.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

MD simulations were performed using the Desmond simulation package from Schrödinger LLC. The NPT ensemble was maintained at 300 K and 1 bar for 100 ns, with a relaxation time of 1 ps38. The OPLS_2005 force field was applied, and long-range electrostatics were calculated using the particle-mesh Ewald method with a 9.0 Å cutoff39. Water molecules were explicitly modeled using the simple point-charge model. Pressure was controlled using the Martyna–Tuckerman–Klein scheme with a 2.0 ps coupling constant, and temperature was regulated by the Nosé–Hoover method. Nonbonded forces were calculated using the r-RESPA integrator, and trajectories were saved every 4.8 ps for subsequent analysis. Ligand–protein interactions were evaluated using the Simulation Interaction Diagram tool in Desmond, and simulation stability was monitored using root-mean-square deviation (RMSD).

The AMBER 14 package was employed with the ff99 force field for energy minimization, counterion addition, and periodic box molecular dynamics simulations in explicit water (TIP4P) for the ligands40. Ligand structures were optimized using density functional theory with the 6-31G basis set and GAFF force field parameters, facilitating flexible protein–ligand-water systems. Ten independent simulations of 10 ns each were conducted with a 0.001 ps timestep to ensure effective sampling of conformational space. Data analysis was performed using the cpptraj program from the AMBER Tools distribution. Pre-relaxation and equilibration steps were implemented to ensure the system started from a stable configuration, thereby providing reliable dynamic behavior.

Statistical analysis

Antibacterial analyses were performed using three tests per bacterial strain. The obtained values were stated as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All calculations were performed using the SPSS program (version 29.0; SPSS) with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. In the statistical analysis, significance was considered when p ≤ 0.05.

Results and discussion

Cinnamomum verum essential oil chemical composition

The hydrodistilled Cinnamomum verum bark essential oil yield was 4.8 ± 0.04% with a clear yellow color. The yield of the present study was higher than that reported by Phu et al.24, who reported 1.42% for Cinnamomum verum bark grown in Vietnam.

The qualitative and quantitative analysis of Cinnamomum verum essential oil was performed by GC–MS. The analysis identified 53 compounds, accounting for 97.02% of the entire mixture (Table 1). The major constituent of Cinnamomum verum essential oil is E-cinnamaldehyde (70.15%), followed by Z-cinnamaldehyde (5.36%), β-caryophyllene (4.91%), terpinolene (4.84%), δ3-carene (2.35%), and cinnamyl acetate (2.1%). Other constituents are present at levels below 1%, including α-terpinene, camphene, methoxy aldehyde, and cinnamic acid. The current results are consistent with previous studies in the Mediterranean region that identified cinnamaldehyde as the major component of Cinnamomum verum essential oil41,42. Different qualitative and quantitative compositions arise from various factors, including plant organ type, variety, extraction method, cultivar type, packaging, storage conditions, and geographic origin43.

Antibacterial activities of Cinnamomum verum essential oil

Susceptibility assay

Interestingly, the diameters of the inhibition zones of essential oil’ Cinnamomum verum were greater than 20 mm against all tested XDR Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and susceptible control strains (Table 2). This finding indicated that the bacterial strains tested were highly susceptible to the antibacterial activities of Cinnamomum verum essential oil. Moreover, the diameters of inhibition zones varied from 25 to 40 mm (mean of diameter of growth inhibition zone = 30 mm) for XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and from 28 to 42 mm (mean of diameter of growth inhibition zone = 34 mm) for XDR Klebsiella pneumoniae (Table 2).

MIC and MBC assays

Table 2 presents the MIC and MBC values of Cinnamomum verum essential oil against all XDR Acinetobacter baumannii, XDR Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 susceptible control strains tested in this study. Our results demonstrated that Cinnamomum verum essential oil could inhibit the growth of all tested XDR bacterial strains and the sensitive strain at low concentrations in a liquid medium, with MIC values varying from 0.312 to 5 mg/mL. Moreover, low concentrations of Cinnamomum verum essential oil are needed to kill the same bacterial strains with MBC values ranging from 0.625 to 10 mg/mL. Additionally, the MBC/MIC ratio ranged from 1 to 2 across the bacterial strains tested in this study, indicating that this essential oil exhibited bactericidal activity against these strains (p < 0.05).

Interaction between imipenem and Cinnamomum verum essential oil

Table 2 summarizes the results of the diameters of clear zones revealed after simultaneous use of Cinnamomum verum essential oil and conventional imipenem disc against all XDR Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii isolates, and the susceptible control strain. Notably, the simultaneous use of these two antibacterial elements increased the inhibition zones compared to those obtained with the single use of the essential oil tested across all bacterial strains in this study.

A synergetic interaction between imipenem and Cinnamomum verum essential oil could be considered when the difference in the inhibition zone of imipenem in the absence or presence of the essential oil is ≥ 4 mm. In this study, for most bacterial strains tested (12 of 15 strains, 80%), the imipenem inhibition zone in the presence or absence of the essential oil was 4 mm or greater (Table 2; p˂0.05). Thus, these current results appear to be of great interest in arguing for a plausible synergism between imipenem and Cinnamomum verum essential oil against XDR and susceptible bacterial strains. To our knowledge, the current study is the first to show these findings.

Previous studies indicated that essential oils extracted from medicinal plants may be promising adjuvants to other antimicrobials against multidrug-resistant bacteria due to their potent penetrability. In this setting, we recently reported that the essential oil of Thymus capitatus grown in Tunisia, combined with cefotaxime, increased the susceptibility of Extended-Spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL)- producing Klebsiella pneumoniae17, but no study has examined the combination of Cinnamomum verum essential oil and imipenem against XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae strains.

In this work, we first reported that checkerboard assays showed that simultaneous use of Cinnamomum verum essential oil and imipenem against all XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae strains significantly decreased the antibiotic MICs and the essential oil’s MICs in different combinations (Table 3). Moreover, the calculated FICI values for all XDR and control bacterial strains were under 0.5. These findings indicated synergism between these two antibacterial agents (Table 3).

All XDR Gram-negative bacterial isolates and the sensitive control strain tested were sensitive to cinnamaldehyde; the response intensity was the same against all clinical isolates and sensitive strains (Table 4). Interestingly, cinnamaldehyde showed MIC values of 128 μg/mL against all XDR bacterial isolates and sensitive control strains. The results of the current study were similar to those reported in previous studies44,45. However, other studies reported MIC values for cinnamaldehyde exceeding 128 μg/mL46,47. These differences in the results of these studies could be related to the technical particulars of the investigations and the purity of the cinnamaldehyde used.

Synergistic effects between cinnamaldehyde and imipenem

Table 4 shows that FICI values confirmed a complete synergistic effect between cinnamaldehyde and imipenem, ranging from 0.01 to 0.27. These results demonstrate their synergistic inhibitory activity against imipenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, including Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae. This combination decreases the individual MICs up to 1024-fold for imipenem and up to 1024-fold for cinnamaldehyde.

Accordingly, several previous studies have reported the mechanism of action of cinnamaldehyde on bacterial strains. Its antimicrobial activity involves disruption of the bacterial cell membrane’s integrity by increasing permeability, thereby causing the seepage of intracellular contents48. Moreover, in Klebsiella aerogenes, the cinnamaldehyde carbonyl group binds to proteins, preventing decarboxylase activity49,50, and gaining access to the periplasmic space and the inner cellular contents of Gram-negative bacteria through the outer membrane and porous proteins51. Thus, based on these findings, we suggest that the synergistic effects of cinnamaldehyde and imipenem are related to alterations in the bacterial cell membrane. These mechanisms may allow imipenem to enter the bacterial cytoplasm rapidly and to reach its target within the cell.

Molecular docking analysis

This study used molecular docking to examine how cinnamaldehyde, the main volatile compound in Cinnamomum verum, interacts with key bacterial enzymes that play pivotal roles in the biosynthesis and repair of cell walls, nucleic acids, and proteins. The research also examined enzymes associated with antibiotic resistance, including isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase, undecaprenyl pyrophosphate synthase, sortase A, penicillin-binding protein, dihydrofolate reductase, dihydropteroate synthase, DNA gyrase, topoisomerase 4, and D-alanine: D-alanine ligase. Imipenem was used as a control (Fig. 1).

Molecular docking scores of cinnamaldehyde and the bacterial key metabolic enzymes as receptors: dihydropteroate synthase (PDB ID: 1AJ2), sortase A (PDB ID:1T2P), undecaprenl pyrophosphate synthase (PDB IDs:1X06 and 2E98), D-alanine: D-alanine ligase (PDB ID: 2ZDQ), penicillin-binding protein 3 (PDB ID:4KQR), DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 6FM4), isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (PDB ID:6LDK), IV topoisomerase (PDB ID: 7LHZ), and dihydrofolate reductase (PDB ID: 6XG5).

Cinnamaldehyde has the highest binding free energy with D-alanine: D-alanine ligase (PDB ID: 2ZDQ). This enzyme is an essential ATP-dependent bacterial enzyme involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis. The molecular docking process in the 2ZDQ pocket yielded a binding energy of -7.0 kcal/mol. The previous finding is higher than that reported by Gupta and collaborators52, who reported a lower docking score for cinnamaldehyde and 2ZDQ (− 4.1 kcal/mol).

Undecaprenyl pyrophosphate synthase (PDB IDs: 1X06 and 2E98) is crucial for peptidoglycan formation in Staphylococcus aureus cell wall biosynthesis. The enzyme converts farnesyl pyrophosphate and isopentenyl pyrophosphate into undecaprenyl pyrophosphate, a lipid carrier for this process. Cinnamaldehyde exhibited binding free energies of − 6.2 kcal/mol with 1X06 and − 5.4 kcal/mol with 2E98, surpassing many natural phytochemicals, such as gambogic acid (− 5.38 kcal/mol), but falling short of neogambogic acid (− 7.46 kcal/mol) from the same source53. DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 6FM4) is a crucial target for antibacterial agents as it manages DNA topology during transcription and replication. Molecular docking was performed with the 6FM4 pocket to investigate interactions with cinnamaldehyde (Fig. 1), yielding a binding energy of − 5.9 kcal/mol. This is higher than the interaction between the same ligand and another enzyme (PDB ID: 1KZN) reported by Gupta and collaborators52. Comparatively, citronellal (− 5.7 kcal/mol) and terpinene-4-ol (− 5 kcal/mol) showed weaker interactions, indicating cinnamaldehyde’s greater potential against DNA gyrase54.

The current study reports a higher docking score for cinnamaldehyde with Dihydrofolate reductase (PDB ID: 6XG5) at − 5.8 kcal/mol, compared to Gupta et al. (− 4.4 kcal/mol) using PDB ID: 3SRW52. Dihydrofolate reductase is essential for thymidine synthesis, making it a target for anticancer and antimicrobial agents. Cinnamaldehyde also demonstrated greater binding free energy than eugenol (− 4.8 kcal/mol) and eucalyptol (− 4.6 kcal/mol), the primary monoterpenes in clove and cardamom oils, respectively52. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases are vital for protein synthesis by charging tRNA with amino acids and are considered potential drug targets for infectious diseases. This study found that cinnamaldehyde (− 5.7 kcal/mol) from Cinnamomum verum binds more strongly to isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (PDB ID: 6LDK) compared to another variant (PDB ID: 1JZQ), which has a binding affinity of − 5.3 kcal/mol52.

Cross-linked peptidoglycan is vital for bacterial cell walls, and its formation is regulated by penicillin-binding proteins, which contain penicillin-sensitive transpeptidase and transglycosylase domains. Disabling these proteins stops cell wall development and bacterial growth. In silico analysis indicated that cinnamaldehyde (− 5.7 kcal/mol) has a greater binding affinity for penicillin-binding protein 3 (PDB ID: 4KQR) than other monoterpenes like 1,8-cineole (− 4.9 kcal/mol) and thymol (− 5.3 kcal/mol), as noted by Aouf et al.55. The binding free energy between cinnamaldehyde and sortase A (PDB ID: 1T2P) is − 5.6 kcal/mol. Sortases are extracellular transpeptidases in Gram-positive bacteria that are crucial in sorting proteins for cell wall assembly and bacterial pathogenicity. Lupeol, a triterpenoid, has a similar docking score of − 5.54 kcal/mol, while phytosterols like β-sitosterol (− 6.4 kcal/mol) and stigmasterol (− 6.98 kcal/mol) show even higher scores, as noted by Bibiso and colleagues56.

Topoisomerase IV (PDB ID: 7LHZ) in Gram-positive bacteria facilitates the separation of the daughter strands after replication. Molecular-docking analysis showed cinnamaldehyde binds with an affinity of − 5.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 1), while sesquiterpenes exhibited even higher affinities. Gupta and collaborators reported similar results with cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, methyl eugenol, and eugenol acetate showing binding affinities of − 5.3, − 5.5, − 5.2, and − 5.3 kcal/mol, respectively52. Sesquiterpenes like β-caryophyllene and α-humulene had even higher binding energies of − 6.0 and − 6.3 kcal/mol, as noted by Aouf et al.55. Dihydropteroate synthase (PDB ID: 1AJ2) is crucial for folate biosynthesis in bacteria and primitive eukaryotes due to the absence of the folate pathway in higher eukaryotes. This makes it a promising target for antimicrobial drug development, as inhibiting dihydrofolic acid formation affects DNA synthesis. In a study by Aouf et al.55, essential oils from Salvia officinalis and Thymus vulgaris showed affinities of − 4.5 to − 5.1 kcal/mol, while cinnamaldehyde demonstrated a comparable affinity of − 4.7 kcal/mol.

Figure 2A, B shows the interaction of the potent cinnamaldehyde, revealing the highest docking scores with the crystal structures of D-alanine: D-alanine ligase (PDB ID: 2ZDQ) and isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (PDB ID: 6LDK). The strong binding affinity of cinnamaldehyde with 2ZDQ (–7.0 kcal/mol) can be explained by conventional hydrogen bonding. In this case, the hydroxyl group of SER A:160 donates a hydrogen atom, while the aldehyde group of cinnamaldehyde accepts it (Fig. 2A). In addition, π–π stacking hydrophobic interactions occur between the aromatic rings of PHE A:151 and PHE A:272 and the phenyl ring of cinnamaldehyde (Fig. 2A).

A similar pattern of interactions was observed when cinnamaldehyde was docked with 6LDK, producing a binding energy of –5.7 kcal/mol (Fig. 2B). Here, hydrogen bonding was established between the amino group of VAL A:184 (donor) and the aldehyde group of cinnamaldehyde (acceptor). π–π stacking was again detected, this time between PHE A:341 and cinnamaldehyde. Additional stabilization came from π–alkyl interactions between the alkyl group of LEU A:182 and the cinnamaldehyde phenyl ring. Unlike the earlier docking results, this complex also revealed a distinct π–σ hydrophobic interaction between the C–H group of THR A:419 and the π-orbitals of cinnamaldehyde (Fig. 2B).

Hydrogen bonds are among the most important intermolecular forces in biological systems. Of these, N–H…O interactions are the most common, followed by O–H…O and N–H…N interactions. Their bond energies typically range from –1.5 to –4.7 kcal/mol57. Aromatic ring interactions are also widespread in both chemistry and biology. Three major stacking arrangements between π-systems have been described: T-shaped, edge-to-face, and parallel-displaced, with interaction energies ranging from –1.48 to –2.48 kcal/mol. In line with the present findings, about half of all π-stacking interactions involve phenylalanine residues paired with aromatic rings in ligands58. Finally, π–alkyl interactions are known to play a critical role in facilitating ligand intercalation into receptor binding pockets, as highlighted by Arthur59 et al.

Threonine C-H donors of the C-H…π-interaction observed in the current study could be involved as Cα-H, Cβ-H, and Cγ-H. A surprising result is that nearly 50% of all aromatic rings act as acceptors60.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation study

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted for 100 ns to study the cinnamaldehyde/D-alanine: D-alanine ligase (PDB ID: 2ZDQ) complex with the highest docking score, as shown in Fig. 1. The simulations analyzed protein and complex structures through various computational methods, focusing on structural, dynamic, and thermodynamic aspects. Studies assessed its behavior in bound and unbound states using backbone RMSD, RMSF, protein secondary structure, protein–ligand contacts, and ligand properties, including Rg, SASA, H-bonds, and MolSA analyses.

The stability of the protein–ligand complex was evaluated using root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) to assess atomic motion and backbone conformational changes. RMSD changes in the range of 1–3 Å are generally considered acceptable for small, globular proteins, whereas larger deviations suggest significant conformational changes during the simulation. As shown in Fig. 3, after approximately 5 ns of simulation, the RMSD of the complex stabilizes at 1.50–1.75 Å. The similarity between the RMSD values for the complex and the protein indicates that the ligand likely remains bound to its initial binding site61.

The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of protein, ligand, and complex.

Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) was used to characterize local fluctuations along the protein chain. Figure 4A displays peaks that correspond to regions of the protein exhibiting the greatest fluctuations during the simulation. The N- and C-terminal tails show higher fluctuations compared to other regions. Secondary structure elements such as alpha helices and beta strands, highlighted in red and blue, are typically more rigid than unstructured regions and therefore exhibit less fluctuation than loop regions. Protein residues interacting with the ligand are indicated by green vertical bars. Secondary structure elements (SSE), including α-helices and β-strands, were monitored throughout the simulation (Fig. 4B). The plot summarizes the SSE composition at each trajectory frame, indicating a stable system62.

The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of the complex (A) and the protein secondary structure elements (SSE) changes during the MD process (B).

Protein interactions with the ligand can be monitored throughout the simulation (Fig. 5). These interactions can be categorized by type and summarized as hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, ionic interactions, and water bridges. Each interaction type comprises more specific subtypes, which can be examined in the ‘Simulation Interactions Diagram’ panel. The stacked bar charts are normalized across the simulation trajectory; for instance, a value of 0.7 indicates that a specific interaction persists for 70% of the simulation duration. Values exceeding 1.0 may occur because certain protein residues can form multiple contacts of the same subtype with the ligand. Conventional hydrogen bonding and water bridges, which are hydrogen-bonded protein–ligand interactions mediated by a water molecule, exhibit the highest interaction fractions and are indicative of stronger binding affinity (Fig. 5). The current geometric criteria for a protein–ligand hydrogen bond are as follows: a distance of 2.5 Å between donor and acceptor atoms (D–H···A); a donor angle of at least 120° between donor-hydrogen-acceptor atoms (D–H···A); and an acceptor angle of at least 90° between hydrogen-acceptor-bonded atom atoms (H···A–X).

Our study has limitations. We evaluated only two Gram-negative ESKAPE pathogens, and although the MICs were similar, differences between strains could yield more comprehensive insights into the interactions between Cinnamomum verum essential oil, cinnamaldehyde, and imipenem. Additionally, we did not perform a pharmacokinetic evaluation in mice, which could have provided valuable insights into their mechanism of action. This gap restricts our understanding of the synergy between these agents. Despite these limitations, our findings hold promise for pharmaceutical development and therapeutic strategies against antibiotic-resistant infections.

Moreover, our results seemed to encourage researchers to study the in vivo interaction between cinnamaldehyde and imipenem in order to check for any eventual tolerance or toxic effects on any organ of the animals before translating to human medicinal application of this interesting result of synergistic effects as an antibiotic adjuvant to help alleviate the global public health problem of XDR, potentially.

Conclusions

This is the first report of antimicrobial activities of Cinnamomum verum essential oil combined with imipenem against XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae strains. Furthermore, the molecular docking showed that the major component of Cinnamomum verum EO, Cinnamaldehyde, has the highest binding free energy with D-alanine: D-alanine ligase and undecaprenyl pyrophosphate synthase implicated in biosynthesis and survival of the XDR Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae. The MD simulations revealed that the stabilizing effects of ligand binding on the D-alanine: D-alanine ligase enzyme impaired its function. The current results reveal that combining Cinnamomum verum essential oil and imipenem is an interesting therapeutic strategy for addressing current clinical challenges in combating XDR Gram-negative strains. Overall, this study provides a starting point for further research on the simultaneous use of cinnamaldehyde and imipenem as a new strategy to treat severe infections caused by XDR Gram-negative bacteria.
