Senescent endothelial cell-derived extracellular vesicles promote neoadjuvant chemoresistance in colorectal cancer via GPX4
Authors: Kunpeng Jia, Yuxin Ding, Menglei Jin, Qi Yang, Xinyu Wang, Yujie Yan, Jiao Xiang, Ruoqi Zhang, Jun Fang, Jingxin Jiang, Danting Wang, Fengbo Huang, Jianming Hu, Jian Huang, Yeting Hu, Zhigang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemotherapy resistance remains a major challenge in advanced colorectal cancer (CRC), with chemotherapy-induced senescence playing a key role. While cancer cell senescence has been extensively studied, the contribution of stromal components, particularly vascular endothelial cells, remains unclear. Here, we analyze 77 CRC patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy and find that favorable responders exhibit well-defined vascular lumens, whereas poor responders show an increase in senescent endothelial cells (senEndo), as revealed by single-cell transcriptomics and immunofluorescence. Chemotherapy-induced senescence reduces GPX4 ubiquitylation in senEndo, leading to its accumulation and extracellular vesicle transfer to CRC cells, where it inhibits ferroptosis and promotes chemoresistance. In vitro and in vivo models demonstrate that targeting GPX4 with the ferroptosis activator RSL3 restores chemotherapy sensitivity. These findings identify a novel mechanism of chemoresistance mediated by senEndo-derived GPX4 in extracellular vesicles and highlight the potential of ferroptosis induction, alone or in combination with senolytic agents, to improve 5-Fu/oxaliplatin-based CRC therapies.Subject terms: Cancer, Colorectal cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health challenge, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1, with over 1.9 million new cases and approximately 900,000 deaths annually2. For patients with locally advanced or metastatic disease, systemic chemotherapy—most commonly oxaliplatin (Oxa) and 5-fluorouracil (5-Fu)—is a cornerstone of treatment in both neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings3–5. However, despite initial responses, many patients ultimately develop chemoresistance, resulting in treatment failure and poor outcomes. The limited rates of durable response to chemotherapy highlight the urgent need to elucidate the mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance and to identify more effective treatment strategies6,7.

A growing body of evidence implicates chemotherapy-induced senescence (CIS) as a paradoxical driver of therapy resistance and tumor relapse8. Senescence, a state of stable growth arrest triggered by genotoxic and cellular stress, is accompanied by the acquisition of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)9,10. Through the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases, SASP factors can modify the tumor microenvironment (TME) in a manner that promotes cancer cell survival, invasion, and relapse11,12. This SASP can exert potent non-cell-autonomous effects, paradoxically promoting the proliferation, invasion, and survival of neighboring cancer cells, thereby undermining therapeutic efficacy13–18. Clinical data have linked the accumulation of senescent cells in post-treatment tumors with adverse prognosis19,20. Furthermore, preclinical studies show that senolytic elimination of senescent cells can restore chemosensitivity21,22. More recently, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as critical SASP components that mediate long-range, non-cell-autonomous communication between senescent cells and cancer cells23. These nanosized vesicles, secreted by senescent cells, can transfer a cargo of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids to recipient cancer cells, effectively reprogramming their phenotype to confer chemoresistance24,25. In CRC, EVs have been recognized as potent regulators of drug response by transferring molecular cargo that reprograms recipient tumor cells26,27. EV-mediated transfer of hsa_circ_0004085 from Fusobacterium nucleatum-infected CRC cells has been shown to induce resistance to Oxa/5-Fu28. It also had been reported that, senescent CRC cells secrete EVs enriched in SERPINE1 to activate NF-κB signaling and promote survival29. These findings underscore the diverse mechanisms through which EVs act as carriers of pro-resistance signals within the TME.

Concurrently, evasion of ferroptosis has emerged as a critical mechanism of chemoresistance30. The antioxidant enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a central suppressor of ferroptosis and is often upregulated in therapy-resistant cancer cells31. Although connections between EVs and ferroptosis are increasingly appreciated32,33. Whether SASP-derived EVs modify ferroptotic sensitivity during CIS remains largely unexplored. In particular, the possibility that senescent stromal cells could deliver anti-ferroptotic signals to tumor cells through EV cargo has not been addressed. Most CIS research has focused on tumor cells themselves, yet chemotherapy profoundly affects the broader TME. However, the TME is a complex ecosystem where non-malignant stromal cells are also profoundly affected by chemotherapy and play a critical role in dictating treatment responses34. Endothelial cells, which form the vascular interface responsible for nutrient supply and drug delivery, are constantly exposed to cytotoxic agents and are likely to undergo senescence35,36. The functional consequences of endothelial senescence for tumor behavior, especially in the context of chemoresistance, remain poorly understood. Whether senescent endothelial cells can modulate ferroptosis sensitivity in adjacent CRC cells—and whether EVs serve as the mediators of this crosstalk—represents a critical gap in current knowledge.

In this study, we address this gap by investigating how chemotherapy-induced senescent vascular endothelial cells (senEndo) influence CRC chemoresistance. We hypothesized that chemotherapy triggers endothelial senescence, which in turn promotes tumor survival by delivering pro-resistance EV cargo that suppresses ferroptotic cell death.

Result

senEndo was increased in neoadjuvant chemotherapy-resistant CRC patients

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) plays a crucial role in treating37 advanced CRC, yet the pathological response rate to standard chemotherapy regimens, such as 5-Fu and Oxa, remains controversial due to small sample sizes in existing studies38. To address this, we conducted a meta-analysis of publicly available clinical trial data, identifying thirteen eligible studies involving 2252 locally advanced CRC patients (LACRC)39–51 (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Tables 1–4). The analysis revealed a pathological response rate of approximately 13% (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating resistance to these regimens in most CRC patients undergoing NAC. To investigate the role of endothelial cells in chemotherapy resistance, we analyzed data from the National Colorectal Cancer Cohort (NCRCC, NCT04287335) (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Tables 5 and 6) of 77 CRC patients who underwent NAC. Patients were categorized based on tumor regression grades (TRGs): complete or near-complete regression (CR, TRG 0/1), partial regression (PR, TRG 2), and no regression (NR, TRG 3)52. Clinical evidence suggests that a strong chemotherapy response, such as CR, is associated with reduced tumor proliferation and increased apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Interestingly, histological analysis of tumor sections from PR and NR patients revealed increased blood vessel accumulation near tumor cells (Fig. 1B), suggesting that neoadjuvant therapy may remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME). Notably, CR patients exhibited well-formed, open lumens within their vasculature, whereas blood vessels in poor responders appeared collapsed. To determine whether these vascular alterations were due to an increased number of endothelial cells, IHC analysis was performed. The results indicated no significant difference in blood vessel count among NR, PR, and CR patients (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. 4–6; Supplementary Table 7). These findings suggest that NAC-induced therapeutic effects may be mediated not by endothelial cell quantity but by phenotypic alterations in these cells.

To explore potential phenotypic changes in endothelial cells, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data from CRC patients treated with NAC, available in the China National GeneBank Database. This analysis identified 358,041 single-cell transcriptomes, from which 24 distinct cell clusters, including endothelial cells (Endos), were identified (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 7). No significant differences in CD31+ endothelial cells were observed across NR, PR and CR (Fig. 1D). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of these enriched genes showed significant overrepresentation in categories related to cellular senescence and p53 signaling (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Data 1). Further investigation using Seurat software revealed a subset of endothelial cells (Clusters 1 and 11) exhibiting senEndo (Fig. 1F, G). The proportion of senEndo was significantly higher in the NR and PR groups compared to the CR group (Fig. 1H). Gene expression analysis of senEndo revealed upregulation of senescence-related genes, including CDKN1A (p21), and SASP genes such as IL1A, IL1B, CXCL1, CXCL10, CCL11, MMP12, TIMP1, TGFB1, and TGFB3, as well as the senescence marker lysosomal β-galactosidase (GLB1) (Fig. 1I). Immunofluorescence staining of patient tissue sections showed that senEndo levels were markedly higher in poor responders (TRG 2 + 3) than in favorable responders (TRG 0 + 1) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Fig. 1J, K). Longitudinal analysis using paired pre- and post-treatment tissue samples further confirmed that senEndo accumulation increased significantly following chemotherapy in TRG 2 + 3 patients, whereas only minimal changes were observed in TRG 0 + 1 patients (Supplementary Fig. 8). Consistent with these clinical findings, tumor-bearing mouse models demonstrated that endothelial cells within chemotherapy non-responsive tumors also exhibited a substantially elevated prevalence of senEndo (Fig. 1L). These findings suggest that senEndo plays a key role in chemotherapy resistance in CRC, supporting its importance as a therapeutic target to overcome resistance in clinical settings.

senEndo promotes chemotherapy resistance in CRC

To investigate whether senEndo contributes to chemotherapy resistance, we developed an in vitro co-culture system to observe interactions between chemotherapy-induced senEndo and CRC cells (Fig. 2A). The characteristics of senescent endothelial cells were confirmed by senescence-associated biomarkers, including pRb, p53, p21, and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity53,54. These data showed a significant increase in senescent cells in chemotherapy-treated human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), with a marked elevation in SA-β-gal-positive cells following treatment with 5-Fu and Oxa, similar to the positive control with doxorubicin (Dox) (Fig. 2B). Western blot analysis demonstrated a decrease in pRb expression in HUVECs following treatment with Dox, 5-Fu, and Oxa, while p53 and p21 levels were significantly upregulated (Fig. 2C, D). Consistently, RT-qPCR results confirmed these findings, showing a significant increase in p21 and p53 expression in HUVECs treated with Dox, 5-Fu, and Oxa (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Immunofluorescence staining for Rb, p53, and p21 further confirmed the induction of senescence by these chemotherapy agents (Supplementary Fig. 9B–D). To further characterize the molecular changes associated with chemotherapy-induced senescence, we performed transcriptome sequencing on HUVECs treated with 5-Fu compared to untreated controls. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of cellular senescence, reinforcing the conclusion that chemotherapy induces endothelial cell senescence (Supplementary Fig. 10A).

Next, we examined whether chemotherapy-induced senEndo promotes drug resistance in CRC cells. Chemotherapy-treated senescent HUVECs were co-cultured with CRC cell lines (SW480 and HT-29) for 48 h, and drug resistance (IC50) to 5-Fu and Oxa was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. The results demonstrated that co-culturing CRC cells with chemotherapy-induced senEndo led to significantly higher resistance to 5-Fu and Oxa compared to non-senescent HUVECs (Fig. 2E). To explore the in vivo effects of senEndo on tumor growth, we used a xenograft mouse model in which HT-29 cells were mixed with chemotherapy-treated HUVECs and implanted subcutaneously into mice. Starting on day 14 after implantation, 5-Fu (50 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally every four days. On day 28, tumor size and volume were significantly larger in the group with chemotherapy-induced HUVECs compared to the control group (Fig. 2F, G), indicating that senEndo promoted tumor chemoresistance in vivo.

Given the link between senEndo and increased chemotherapy resistance, we evaluated whether clearing chemotherapy-induced senescent endothelial cells could reverse this resistance. Characteristics of senescent cells include increased levels of cell cycle inhibitors such as p16 and p21, as well as resistance to apoptosis. Senolytic drugs remove senescent cells by targeting their defining characteristics. ABT-263, an inhibitor of Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, effectively eliminates senescent cells by blocking their anti-apoptotic functions55. Notably, RNA sequencing analysis revealed a significant enrichment of the Bcl-2 signaling pathway in chemotherapy-induced endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 10B). Treatment of senescent HUVECs with ABT-263 markedly reduced the number of SA-β-gal-positive cells (Fig. 2H, I). Additionally, co-culture of ABT-263-treated HUVECs with CRC cells demonstrated that ABT-263 mitigated chemotherapy resistance induced by senescent endothelial cells (Fig. 2J, K). To further validate these findings in vivo, we administered ABT-263 to tumor-bearing mice and monitored tumor progression. Consistent with our in vitro observations, ABT-263 treatment significantly reduced tumor volume, supporting the contribution of senescent endothelial cells to chemoresistance and highlighting the therapeutic potential of senolytic intervention (Supplementary Fig. 11).

In summary, our findings suggest that chemotherapy-induced senescence in vascular endothelial cells enhances resistance to common chemotherapy regimens in CRC. Targeting senescent endothelial cells with senolytic agents, such as ABT-263, could potentially reduce chemotherapy resistance and improve treatment efficacy.

senEndo promotes 5-Fu/Oxa resistance via extracellular vesicles in CRC cells

To investigate the mechanisms by which senEndo promotes drug resistance in CRC, we treated CRC cell lines (SW480 and HT-29) with conditioned medium from untreated HUVECs (Con-CM) and 5-Fu/Oxa chemotherapy-induced HUVECs (5-Fu-CM or Oxa-CM). Compared to CRC cells treated with Con-CM, those exposed to 5-Fu-CM or Oxa-CM exhibited significantly higher resistance to the standard chemotherapy regimen (5-Fu and Oxa), as indicated by an increased IC50 fold change (Fig. 3A–E).

It is known that senescent cells release cytokines, which may contribute to chemoresistance56,57. To investigate whether EVs in the conditioned medium from senEndo were involved, we used ultra-high-speed centrifugation to remove EVs from the conditioned medium. We then treated CRC cells (HT-29 and SW480) with the EV-free conditioned medium. The results showed that the EV-free conditioned medium significantly reduced the chemotherapy resistance of CRC cells compared to the intact 5-Fu-CM/Oxa-CM, suggesting that EVs from senEndo contribute to chemoresistance in CRC cells (Fig. 3F–H, Supplementary Fig. 12).

To further validate this hypothesis, we isolated and purified EVs from 5-Fu-CM and Oxa-CM. Extracellular vesicles presence was confirmed by Western blot analysis using the exosome markers CD63, HSP70, and TSG101, as well as by scanning electron microscopy and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (Supplementary Fig. 13). When CRC cells (HT-29 and SW480) were treated with purified EVs from 5-Fu-CM (5-Fu-EV) and Oxa-CM (Oxa-EV), we observed that 5-Fu-EV and Oxa-EV significantly increased chemotherapy resistance in CRC cells, compared to their EV-free counterparts (Fig. 3I–L). Finally, we validated these findings in vivo using a tumor-bearing mouse model. Extracellular vesicles released by chemotherapy-induced senEndo increased chemotherapy resistance in CRC tumors. Tumor size and volume were significantly larger in mice treated with senEndo-derived EVs compared to the PBS-treated control group (Fig. 3M, N). In conclusion, our results demonstrate that senEndo enhances CRC cells’ resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs primarily through the release of extracellular vesicles, rather than cytokines.

SenEndo-derived extracellular vesicles transfer GPX4 and regulate chemotherapy resistance of CRC cells

To investigate the mechanism by which EVs derived from chemotherapy-induced senEndo promote drug resistance in CRC cells, we performed proteomic analysis to compare the composition of EVs released by senEndo treated with three different chemotherapeutic drugs. A total of 438 proteins were identified in EVs from chemotherapy-treated senEndo, but not in control EVs (Con-EV) (Fig. 4A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of these proteins revealed a significant enrichment in proteins involved in ferroptosis (Fig. 4B). Among these, GPX4, a protein associated with drug resistance, was detected in EVs derived from HUVECs treated with 5-Fu and Oxa (5-Fu-EV/Oxa-EV), but not in Con-EV. Previous studies have shown that GPX4 is upregulated in various tumors and plays a critical role in regulating ferroptosis, tumor development, and drug resistance57. We hypothesized that EVs released by chemotherapy-induced senEndo, exhibiting elevated GPX4 expression, are taken up by CRC cells, leading to ferroptosis resistance and contributing to chemotherapy resistance. To test this hypothesis, we confirmed that chemotherapeutic drug treatment increased GPX4 expression in EVs derived from HUVECs by Western blot (Fig. 4C). To validate this phenomenon in patients, we collected serum from three CRC patients before and after chemotherapy and analyzed the expression of GPX4 in extracellular vesicles (Fig. 4D).

To examine whether extracellular vesicles derived from senEndo cells can be taken up by CRC cells, extracellular vesicles from senEndo cells were labeled with the PKH67 dye. When PKH67-labeled EVs were added to the culture medium of CRC cells, green fluorescence was observed within the CRC cells using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results suggest that exosomes derived from senEndo cells are internalized by CRC cells. Next, we aimed to determine whether GPX4 in endothelial cells can be transferred to tumor cells. To test this, we transfected HUVECs with mCherry-labeled GPX4 and co-cultured these cells with CRC cells (SW480 and HT-29) for 48 h. The presence of a red fluorescence signal in CRC cells treated with mCherry-GPX4 confirmed that GPX4 released by HUVECs was successfully internalized by the CRC cells (Fig. 4E). Additionally, we observed elevated GPX4 protein levels in CRC cells treated with 5-Fu-EV and Oxa-EV (Fig. 4F, G, Supplementary Fig. 15). The data suggested that chemotherapy significantly increased GPX4 expression in exosomes post-treatment.

To further investigate whether senEndo-induced chemoresistance in CRC cells was mediated by GPX4 in EVs, we silenced GPX4 in HUVECs using siRNA. HUVECs with silenced GPX4 (siGPX4) were co-cultured with CRC cells, and drug resistance assays revealed that silencing GPX4 significantly reduced the chemotherapy resistance of CRC cells to 5-Fu and Oxa compared to the control group (siScr) (Fig. 4H–J). Additionally, treatment with the GPX4-specific inhibitor RSL3 also reduced chemotherapy resistance in CRC cells to 5-Fu and Oxa (Fig. 4K–M). In contrast to the physiological profile of healthy individuals, where plasma EVs predominantly originate from cancer-associated fibroblasts and macrophages58,59, we co-cultured chemotherapy-induced senescent endothelial cells with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and macrophages (THP-1 cells). Western blot analysis for GPX4 showed no detectable increase in GPX4 levels in either CAFs or macrophages. Furthermore, EV treatment alone did not upregulate GPX4 expression in these cell types (Supplementary Fig. 16, 17). These results indicate that the EV-mediated GPX4 transfer is specific to cancer cells in our model.

These results suggest that chemotherapy sensitivity in CRC cells to 5-Fu and Oxa can be enhanced by silencing GPX4 in senEndo, thereby reducing the protective effect of GPX4-containing EVs on CRC cells.

GPX4 enhanced the 5-Fu/Oxa chemoresistance of CRC cells via ferroptosis inhibition

Numerous studies have identified ferroptosis as a novel pharmacological mechanism associated with resistance to several anticancer drugs, including 5-Fu and Oxa60. Cellular defense against ferroptosis is largely mediated by the GPX4 signaling pathway31. As a glutathione peroxidase, GPX4 converts lipid hydroperoxides into lipid alcohols, preventing lipid peroxidation and thus inhibiting ferroptosis61. To explore the role of GPX4 in the development of chemotherapy resistance in CRC cells, we first examined the effect of GPX4 on ferroptosis. We silenced GPX4 in CRC cells and assessed their sensitivity to chemotherapy drugs by measuring cell viability. Our findings demonstrated that inhibiting GPX4 in CRC cells increased their sensitivity to 5-Fu and Oxa (Fig. 5A, B).

To further investigate the role of ferroptosis in GPX4-mediated resistance to 5-Fu/Oxa, we knocked down the GPX4 gene in CRC cells and treated them with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 following 5-Fu/Oxa treatment. Cell survival, iron concentration, and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were used to evaluate efficacy. The results showed a decreased survival rate in 5-Fu/Oxa-treated cells after GPX4 knockout, while Fer-1 treatment significantly improved cell survival (Supplementary Fig. 18). Additionally, the number of ferroptotic cells and lipid peroxidation levels were significantly reduced in the group treated with both 5-Fu/Oxa and Fer-1 (Fig. 5C, D). Similar findings were observed for reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, with Fer-1 reducing ROS levels post-chemotherapy treatment following GPX4 silencing (Fig. 5E, F). As ACSL4 is a key driver of ferroptosis by promoting lipid peroxidation62 while RGS4 acts as a protective suppressor of this process63, we observed that Fer-1 decreased the expression of ACSL4 and increased RGS4 in CRC cells treated with 5-Fu/Oxa (Fig. 5G, H, Supplementary Fig. 19). These results indicate that inhibiting GPX4 in CRC cells can induce ferroptosis, thereby enhancing their sensitivity to 5-Fu and Oxa chemotherapy drugs.

To directly demonstrate that the protective effects observed in CRC cells are mediated by EV-delivered GPX4, we knocked down GPX4, treated recipient CRC cells with senEndo-derived EVs, and subsequently exposed them to 5-Fu/Oxa. Ferroptotic stress was quantified using MDA levels and ROS measurements. Lipid peroxidation (MDA) levels were significantly reduced in CRC cells receiving both chemotherapy and senEndo-EVs, even under GPX4 knockdown conditions. Likewise, ROS accumulation was markedly diminished following senEndo-EV treatment (Supplementary Fig. 20). These results demonstrate a functional rescue effect, consistent with the transfer of GPX4 via senEndo-derived EVs. Together, our data provide direct evidence that endothelial-derived EVs can compensate for endogenous GPX4 loss and suppress chemotherapy-induced ferroptosis in CRC cells.

GPX4 accumulation in senEndo-derived extracellular vesicles is caused by reduced GPX4 ubiquitylation

To investigate the mechanism underlying the accumulation of GPX4 in senEndo, we first examined the mRNA expression of GPX4 in HUVECs following chemotherapy treatment. The results showed that chemotherapy did not affect the mRNA levels of GPX4 in HUVECs, while the protein expression of GPX4 was significantly upregulated (Fig. 6A, B). These findings suggest that chemotherapy may influence the post-translational regulation of GPX4, specifically its protein synthesis or degradation.

To further explore this, we treated HUVECs with cycloheximide (CHX) to inhibit GPX4 protein synthesis for 12 h. Protein expression of GPX4 was assessed at different time points (0, 4, 8, and 12 h) in HUVECs treated with 5-Fu (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Fig. 21). The data indicated that inhibition of protein synthesis did not significantly affect GPX4 levels in HUVECs exposed to 5-Fu/Oxa, suggesting that chemotherapy-induced accumulation of GPX4 is not dependent on new protein synthesis.

The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway is the main way for the degradation of endogenous proteins. Next, we used MG132 to inhibit the 26S proteasome complex and prevent GPX4 degradation. We immunoprecipitated GPX4 using a specific antibody and analyzed its ubiquitination status (Fig. 6D). In the presence of MG132, we observed a significant reduction in the ubiquitination of GPX4 in HUVECs following 5-Fu/Oxa chemotherapy, indicating that chemotherapy treatment might reduce the proteasomal degradation of GPX4. To further investigate the regulation of GPX4 ubiquitination after chemotherapy, we employed mass spectrometry to analyze GPX4-interacting proteins in HUVECs treated with 5-Fu/Oxa. Ubiquitination modification involves a series of reactions catalyzed by the ubiquitin-activating enzyme E1, the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2, and the ubiquitin ligase E364. Among these, E3 ubiquitin ligase plays a critical role by specifically recognizing target proteins and facilitating the transfer of ubiquitin to substrate proteins, making it the key enzyme in the ubiquitination process65. Mass spectrometry analysis identified 11 E3 ligase proteins whose levels were altered in response to chemotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 22). Notably, we detected TRIM4, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, among these proteins (Fig. 6E). As anticipated, our Co-IP assay confirmed the interaction between TRIM4 and GPX4 in HUVEC cells (Fig. 6F). TRIM4 is known to promote the proteasomal degradation pathway via Lys48 (K48)-linked ubiquitination66. Further investigation revealed that K48-linked ubiquitination of GPX4 was significantly reduced following chemotherapy treatment (Fig. 6G).

Taken together, these results suggest that the loss of TRIM4-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination contributes to the accumulation of GPX4 in senEndo cells, which is subsequently incorporated into EVs.

GPX4 inhibitor enhances the sensitization of CRC to 5-Fu/Oxa in vivo

To investigate whether senescent endothelial cells influence CRC sensitivity to 5-Fu via GPX4-containing EVs, we conducted in vivo experiments using a xenograft mouse model established with HT-29 CRC cells. Mice were administered EVs derived from senescent endothelial cells and treated with a combination of the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 and 5-Fu (Fig. 7A).

RSL3 significantly enhanced the antitumor effects of 5-Fu, leading to a more pronounced reduction in tumor volume compared to 5-Fu alone (Fig. 7B–D). Clinical evidence suggests that a strong chemotherapy response, such as CR, is associated with reduced tumor proliferation and increased apoptosis. Histological analysis confirmed that the combination of RSL3 and 5-Fu induced greater tumor cell apoptosis and reduced proliferation compared to 5-Fu alone, suggesting a synergistic effect (Fig. 7E, Supplementary Fig. 23, 24). These data highlight a novel therapeutic approach that integrates ferroptosis induction with conventional chemotherapy, offering a promising avenue to overcome drug resistance in CRC and improve clinical outcomes (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

Chemotherapy resistance remains a major barrier to effective treatment of CRC, particularly in advanced disease, where tumor progression is rapid67. While much of the focus has been on intrinsic resistance mechanisms in cancer cells, the contribution of stromal components, particularly endothelial cells, is often overlooked. These cells, which line the blood vessels that line the tumor vasculature, are indispensable for maintaining blood supply and facilitating drug delivery. Yet, they are also highly vulnerable to chemotherapy-induced stress. Our study demonstrates that these cells undergo CIS and acquire a pro-tumorigenic phenotype that actively drives therapy resistance.

Anti-angiogenesis has long been a strategy in CRC treatment, aiming to starve tumors by limiting their blood supply68. However, the onset of endothelial cell senescence complicates the effectiveness of such therapies. Senescent endothelial cells retain the capacity to form new blood vessels and may even secrete factors that support tumor growth and survival, despite anti-angiogenic treatments. This suggests that targeting senEndo could enhance the efficacy of these therapies by preventing their survival and function. Our findings reveal a previously underappreciated mechanism by which chemotherapy-induced endothelial senescence contributes to CRC chemoresistance -- through the transfer of GPX4 via EVs.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy using 5-Fu and Oxa remains a cornerstone for treating advanced CRC, yet response rates remain suboptimal. In our patient cohort, poor responders exhibited a marked upregulation of senescence-associated genes (e.g., CDKN1A, IL1A, CXCL1, MMP12) specifically within senEndo. These molecular signatures, combined with our in vitro and in vivo data, indicate that CIS in the vascular compartment establishes a microenvironment that protects CRC cells from chemotherapy-induced cytotoxicity. Tumors in chemotherapy-treated mice exhibited accelerated growth when senEndo were present, confirming their role in promoting resistance.

A central discovery of this study is that senEndo secretes EVs enriched with GPX4, a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, thereby directly transferring ferroptosis resistance to CRC cells. We show that EV-mediated GPX4 delivery reduces lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species accumulation, thereby protecting cancer cells from ferroptotic death during chemotherapy. Knocking down GPX4 in endothelial cells significantly reversed this protective effect. These data identify GPX4 transfer from senEndo as a critical stromal-derived mechanism of chemoresistance. The EV-mediated delivery of GPX4 from senEndo thus constitutes a targeted, adaptive countermeasure to the ferroptotic pressure imposed by chemotherapy. To illustrate the therapeutic relevance of this pathway, we show that combining the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 with 5-Fu enhances antitumor efficacy in xenograft models. Nonetheless, systemic ferroptosis induction presents considerable toxicity challenges. GPX4 is essential for normal tissue homeostasis, and its systemic inhibition is lethal in mice. Therefore, clinical application of GPX4 inhibitors will require tumor-targeted delivery systems or next-generation, TME-selective ferroptosis modulators. Systemic loss of GPX4 is embryonically lethal in mice69,70, and its conditional knockout in adult animals leads to multi-organ failure71–73, highlighting its essential role in protecting normal tissues from lipid peroxidation. Therefore, systemic administration of potent GPX4 inhibitors like RSL3 is likely to have a narrow therapeutic window and significant on-target, off-tumor toxicity. In our preclinical study, RSL3 serves as a valuable proof-of-concept tool to validate the ferroptosis pathway as a therapeutic vulnerability74. For clinical translation, future strategies must focus on mitigating this toxicity. This could involve the development of tumor-targeted delivery systems (e.g., antibody-drug conjugates or lipid nanoparticles) to concentrate the ferroptosis inducer at the tumor site, or the design of next-generation inhibitors with improved pharmacokinetic properties and a wider therapeutic index.

While senolytic agents like ABT-263, which selectively clear senescent cells, could be an ideal adjunct to reduce chemotherapy resistance by eliminating EV-secreting senescent endothelial cells, we acknowledge the insightful point that their broader impact on the TME remains to be fully elucidated. The BCL-2 family proteins, targeted by ABT-263, are critical regulators of apoptosis across a wide range of cell types75. ABT-263 may exert complex, context-dependent effects—both beneficial and detrimental. For example, it may deplete tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes but also eliminate senescent or immunosuppressive stromal elements such as CAFs or exhausted T cells. Future studies employing single-cell transcriptomics and multiplex spatial profiling will be essential to dissect these pleiotropic effects and guide rational therapeutic use of senolytics. Senolytics and ferroptosis inducers each carry distinct advantages and limitations. Senolytics target the upstream source of EV-mediated resistance by eliminating senescent “factory cells”, whereas ferroptosis inducers directly target the downstream survival mechanism in CRC cells. Ultimately, an optimal therapeutic strategy may combine or sequentially integrate both approaches—for example, first clearing senEndo to suppress GPX4-rich EV production, followed by targeted ferroptosis induction to eliminate the now-vulnerable cancer population.

In conclusion, our study identifies senescent vascular endothelial cells as a critical driver of chemoresistance in CRC through EV-mediated GPX4 transfer and ferroptosis inhibition. This study expands the conceptual framework of CIS beyond cancer cells, revealing a stromal–epithelial crosstalk mechanism that shapes chemotherapeutic outcomes. Therapeutically, targeting either the source (senEndo) or the mechanism (ferroptosis resistance) represents a promising avenue to enhance chemotherapy efficacy. Future preclinical and clinical studies are warranted to refine these strategies and translate them into improved treatments for patients with CRC.

Methods

Meta-analysis of neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in colorectal cancer

A systematic review of the literature was conducted from 2010 to 2024 using PubMed, EMBASE, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Library. Studies eligible for inclusion were those that reported the use of preoperative (neoadjuvant) chemotherapy in non-metastatic locally advanced colon cancer (LACC) or locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC). The search strategy included keywords such as “preoperative chemotherapy,” “neoadjuvant chemotherapy,” and “locally advanced colon cancer”, while the corresponding keyword was replaced by “rectum” for rectal cancer. Inclusion Criteria: Studies were included if they reported on patients with non-metastatic LACC or LARC who underwent preoperative chemotherapy, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies. Data extracted from these studies included trial design, patient selection criteria, number of patients, patient demographics, radiological stage, chemotherapy regimens (pre- and post-operative), chemotherapy completion rates, and oncological outcomes. The primary endpoint assessed was tumor regression grade (TRG), which was classified into three categories: little/no response, moderate response, and complete response based on the presence of residual tumor cells in resection specimens. Risk of Bias Assessment: For RCTs, the quality of studies was independently assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool. The risk of bias for each study was classified as low (over 5 domains with “low” risk), moderate (3 or 4 domains with “low” risk), and high (less than 2 domains with “low” risk). For non-randomized studies, quality was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Statistical Analysis: Data from included studies were pooled using a random-effects model due to significant clinical heterogeneity between studies. The odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated to assess the relationship between TRG and chemotherapy response. Heterogeneity across studies was quantified using I² statistics. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager 5.4, and the final calculations for odds ratios and 95% CIs were performed in Excel.

Tumor regression grade (TRG) and intratumoral microvessel density analysis

Patients were recruited from the National Colorectal Cancer Cohort (NCRCC) study (Clinical Trial ID: NCT04287335). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2024-1554). Informed consent was obtained from all the patients. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Comprehensive clinical and pathological data were collected, including patient demographics, tumor stage, tumor site, treatment regimens, histological type, pathological grade, and oncological outcomes. The primary endpoint was the tumor regression grade (TRG) following neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). TRG was categorized into four grades based on the extent of residual tumor cells in the resection specimen. Intratumoral microvessel density (IMVD) was assessed in “hot spots” within the tumor area, defined as regions with the highest density of CD31-positive cells. IMVD was quantified under a high-power field using immunohistochemical staining for CD31.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Paraffin-embedded sections from patients with different tumor regression grades (TRG) were used for CD31 immunohistochemical analysis. Tumor microvascular density was assessed using an immunohistochemical kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sections were dewaxed, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked with 3% BSA-PBST for 30 min at room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited with 0.3% H2O2 for 10 min at room temperature. Membrane permeabilization was performed using 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD31 antibodies (1:400 dilution), followed by a secondary incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000) for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing, DAB staining and hematoxylin counterstaining were performed, and images were acquired using an optical microscope.

Cell culture and treatment

Human colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines SW480 and HT-29, along with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), were obtained from ATCC. Cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely checked.

To induce senescence in HUVECs, cells were treated with doxorubicin (0.5 μM), 5-fluorouracil (1.0 μM), or oxaliplatin (10 μM) for 24 h, followed by a 48 h recovery in drug-free medium. To evaluate the effect of ABT-263, senescent cells were treated with 0.1 μM ABT-263 for 6 h. A non-contact co-culture system was established using transwell inserts (0.4 μm pores), with HUVECs (4 × 10⁵ cells/mL) seeded in the lower chamber and CRC cells (2 × 10⁵ cells/mL) in the upper chamber.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining

SA-β-gal staining was performed using a commercial kit (Beyotime, Cat# C0602) per the manufacturer’s protocol. Following treatment, cells were washed with PBS, incubated with the staining solution, and visualized under a bright-field microscope. Senescent cells were quantified as the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells in randomly selected fields.

Antibodies and reagents

Primary antibodies were obtained from Proteintech (p53, GPX4, CD63, TSG101, HSP70, Rb, phospho-Rb, GLB1, Ki67, cleaved caspase-3), ABclonal (β-actin, K48, TRIM4, ACSL4, RGS4), and Abcam (CD31). Chemotherapeutic agents (Dox, 5-Fu, Oxa) were purchased from Aladdin, and RSL3 was obtained from MCE. Specific information is listed in Supplementary Table 8.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Yeasen), and cDNA was synthesized with a reverse transcription kit (Yeasen). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green (Yeasen) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast system. Relative gene expression was analyzed using the 2^-∆∆Ct method, with β-actin as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 9.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed, and proteins were quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (GlpBio). Equal protein amounts (20 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Proteins were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (GlpBio) and analyzed using a Tanon imaging system.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, antigen-retrieved, permeabilized, and blocked with 3% BSA. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cambridge, UK). Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were obtained via confocal microscopy.

CCK-8 assay for chemosensitivity

Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 kit (GlpBio). CRC cells were treated with different concentrations of 5-Fu and Oxa for 48 h, followed by CCK-8 incubation. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured to determine IC50 values.

Extracellular vesicle isolation and characterization

Extracellular vesicles were isolated via ultracentrifugation. Conditioned media were sequentially centrifuged at 2000 × g (10 min), 10,000 × g (30 min), and 110,000 × g (70 min). Extracellular vesicles were resuspended in PBS and characterized by TEM and western blot analysis for CD63, TSG101, and HSP70.

In vivo tumorigenesis

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Zhejiang Chinese Medicine University Animal Care and Ethics Committee (IACUC-20240415-04). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment with a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50–60%, a 12-h light/dark cycle, and free access to food and water.

To establish the subcutaneous xenograft model, 1 × 10⁶ HT-29 cells were suspended in 100 μL of PBS and injected into the right flank of each mouse. When the average tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups (n = 5 per group) using a computer-generated random sequence to ensure unbiased allocation. The sample size was determined based on our previous experience with this tumor model to ensure sufficient statistical power while minimizing animal use. Any mice that died unexpectedly during the experimental period were excluded from the final analysis.

The treatment groups received 5-Fu (50 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection every 4 days, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. Senescent and normal HUVECs were co-injected with CRC cells to assess their impact on tumor growth. Additionally, extracellular vesicles from senescent HUVECs (10 μg) and RSL3 (5 mg/kg) were injected. For ABT-263 treatment, mice were randomly assigned to two cohorts: the control group received vehicle treatment via oral gavage, while the ABT-263 group received ABT-263 (50 mg/kg) daily through oral gavage for seven consecutive days.

Tumor volume was monitored every 4 days and calculated using the formula: V = (width² × length)/2. Outcome assessment was conducted in a blinded manner, where the researchers measuring tumor dimensions were unaware of the specific group assignments to minimize potential confounders. At the end of the study, mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation, and tumors were harvested for further histological analysis.

Single-cell sequencing data analysis

The single-cell RNA sequencing data used in this study were publicly available and downloaded from the China National GeneBank Database (CNGBdb) with the accession number CNP000413837. Dimensional reduction, clustering, and cell-type identification were performed using Seurat (v4) in R (v4.2.1). Ggplot2 (v3.4.4) was used to analyze the proportions of different cell types in the CR, PR, and NR groups. The endothelial cell ratio analysis was visualized using GraphPad Prism (v9.5). ComplexHeatmap (v2.14.0) was employed to analyze the expression differences of senescence-related genes.

Measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS)

DCFH-DA (10 μM final, diluted 1:1000 in PBS/HBSS) was loaded onto cells (37 °C, 20 min), washed 3× with PBS, stimulated with ROS inducer (e.g., Rosup, 20–30 min), and imaged via confocal microscopy (488/525 nm).

Lipid peroxidation malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

Lipid peroxidation levels were assessed by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) using a lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit (Beyotime). Cells were homogenized or lysed in cell lysis buffer (Beyotime) and centrifuged at 10,000–12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for MDA determination. A standard curve was generated by diluting the standard with distilled water to concentrations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, and 50 μM. For the assay, 0.1 mL of sample or standard was mixed with 0.2 mL of MDA detection solution. The mixture was heated at 100 °C for 15 min, then cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min. The absorbance of 200 μL of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader. The MDA content in the samples was calculated using the standard curve.

Iron measurements

Total iron content was measured using the colorimetric Iron Assay Kit (Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reagents, working standards, and samples were prepared as directed. Briefly, 5 μL of iron reducer was added to each standard and sample well, followed by mixing and incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 100 μL of iron probe containing the iron standard was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a microplate reader. The mean absorbance of the blank was subtracted from all standard and sample readings. A standard curve was generated from the absorbance values of the standards, and the total iron content in each sample was calculated and normalized.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) and plasmid DNA transfection

HUVECs were transfected with GPX4 siRNA (sequences in Supplementary Table 10) using Lipofectamine 2000. Gene knockdown was confirmed via Western blot. For overexpression, the GPX4 plasmid (pLVX-mCherry-C1/GPX4) was transfected using Lipofectamine 3000, and extracellular vesicles were isolated post-transfection.

Proteomic analysis of senEndo-EVs

To prepare the samples, an appropriate amount of reagent was added to the human, mouse, and rat serum samples after removing high-abundance serum proteins. Protein extraction was performed using SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.6), and the protein concentration was quantified using the BCA method. 20 µg of protein from each sample was combined with 5X loading buffer and boiled in a water bath for 5 min. The proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250.

For proteomic analysis, an appropriate amount of protein from each sample was subjected to tryptic enzymatic hydrolysis using the Filter-Aided Sample Preparation (FASP) method. The resulting peptides were desalted using a C18 cartridge, lyophilized, and redissolved in 40 µL of 0.1% formic acid solution. Peptide quantification was performed by measuring the absorbance at OD280.

Peptide separation was performed using a NanoElute HPLC system at a nanoliter flow rate. Buffer A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and buffer B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (99.9%). The chromatographic column (C18, 25 cm, ID 75 μm, 1.9 μm) was equilibrated with 95% buffer A, and peptides were separated at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The chromatographic fractions were then analyzed using a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer. The detection mode was positive ion mode with an ion source voltage of 1.5 kV. Both MS and MSMS were performed, with a mass spectrum scanning range of 100–1700 m/z.

Data acquisition was conducted in Parallel Accumulation Serial Fragmentation (PASEF) mode, where one primary mass spectrum corresponded to 10 secondary spectra. The ion mobility was set to 0.6–1.6 Vs/cm². The dynamic exclusion time for tandem mass spectrometry scans was set to 24 s to avoid repeated scanning of parent ions.

LC-MS/MS analysis

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometry (Bruker) that was coupled to an Evosep One system liquid chromatography (Denmark). The peptides were loaded onto a C18-reversed phase analytical column (15 cm long, 150 μm inner diameter, 1.9 μm resin) in buffer A (0.1% Formic acid in water) and separated with a linear gradient of buffer B (99.9% acetonitrile and 0.1% Formic acid) at a flow rate of 220 nL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode. The electrospray voltage applied was 1.6 kV. Precursors and fragments were analyzed at the TOF detector over a mass range of m/z 100–1700. The timsTOF Pro was operated in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. PASEF mode data collection was performed based on the following parameters: Ion mobility coefficient (1/K0) value was set from 0.75 to 1.35 Vs cm2; 1 MS and 8 MS/MS PASEF scans. Active exclusion was enabled with a release time of 24 s.

Identification and quantification of proteins

The MS raw data for each sample were combined and searched using the MaxQuant 1.6.14 software for identification and quantification analysis.

Immunoprecipitation assay

For the immunoprecipitation assay, HUVECs were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (weak) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. The cell supernatant was then incubated overnight at 4 °C on a rotator with the GPX4 antibody. Protein A/G plus magnetic beads were added to the mixture, and the reaction was further incubated at 4 °C for 2 h to allow for the antibody-protein complex to form. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors to remove unbound proteins. The protein complexes were then eluted from the magnetic beads by boiling in 2× SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 5 min. Immunoblotting was performed to detect the protein expression of GPX4 and other relevant proteins.

Proteomic analysis of GPX4 binding protein

The protein precipitated from HUVEC cells following chemotherapy was analyzed using mass spectrometry. The protein samples were first subjected to overnight digestion at 37 °C with trypsin (Sigma, part number SLBF7596V) and from Beijing Shengxia Proteins (part number HLS GLU001C). The resulting peptide mixture was desalted and then separated using liquid chromatography on an UltiMate 3000 system (Thermo Scientific), employing a self-packed C18 analytical column. The peptides were eluted during a 60 min gradient analysis, utilizing buffer A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and buffer B (100% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid).

Following separation, peptides were analyzed by capillary high-performance liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry using a Q-Exactive Plus system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Prior to analysis, a standard calibration solution was applied for instrument calibration. The parent ion scanning range was set to 300–1500 m/z, and data acquisition was performed in Information Dependent Acquisition (IDA) mode. Following each full scan (MS1), the 20 most intense fragment ions (MS2) were analyzed. The fragmentation was performed using high-energy collision dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 28 and a dynamic exclusion time of 25 s. The MS1 resolution was set to 70,000 at m/z 200, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3e6 and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. For MS2, the resolution was set to 17,500, the AGC target was 1e5, and the maximum injection time was 50 ms. Specific information is listed in Supplementary Table 11.

Data analysis was performed using the Q-Exactive Plus system software, and the mass spectrometry analysis was conducted by Shanghai Ouyi Biotechnology Company, China.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), while RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Library preparation was performed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, and hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted in R (v3.2.0) to visualize gene expression patterns across different groups. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was carried out using the hypergeometric distribution, and significantly enriched pathways were identified.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Extracellular vesicles derived from isolated HUVECs were characterized using SEM. Samples were fixed with 3.7% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 15 min, washed twice with PBS, and sequentially dehydrated using graded ethanol solutions. Following ethanol evaporation, samples were air-dried at room temperature, mounted on aluminum stubs, and sputter-coated. SEM imaging was performed using a Tescan scanning electron microscope.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

Extracellular vesicles from HUVECs were pelleted and resuspended in 300 μL of PBS. Prior to analysis with the NanoSight LM10 instrument, the solution was diluted 50-fold. The instrument uses a finely focused laser beam directed through a glass prism to illuminate the sample. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, with two one-minute videos recorded per run. Instrument settings were kept consistent across experiments. Size distribution curves were averaged across video repeats for each sample and subsequently across all samples to generate a representative size distribution profile.

Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Mouse tumor tissues were assessed for pathological features using H&E staining. Fresh tumor samples were harvested, immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut into 4-μm-thick slices and stained following the manufacturer’s protocol. Stained sections were visualized and imaged using an optical microscope.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data from at least three independent experiments are presented as the mean ± SEM. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was employed. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Specific details on sample sizes and the number of replicates are listed in the corresponding figure legend.
