Patent Ductus Arteriosus Spasm During Percutaneous Device Occlusion in Infants and Children
Authors: Hani M. Adel, Ashraf K. Mahmoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpasm of ductus arteriosus is an uncommon but significant phenomenon associated with transcatheter patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) occlusion. It can lead to procedural failure, improper device sizing, or even embolization of the occluder device as the spasm subsides post-procedure. Aim of this study is to report the frequency, features, management and outcome of PDA spasm during device closure in a single Egyptian center.Patients and methodsOver about 3 years, 200 infants and children underwent percutaneous device occlusion. The echocardiographic and catheterization data of patients who developed spasm of PDA before or during or shortly after anesthesia induction or upon catheter entry into PDA were recorded and analyzed. Upon detecting the spasm, the anesthetist managed it by increasing the depth of anesthesia through a higher minimum alveolar concentration of inhaled sevoflurane and administering a secondary opioid dose (fentanyl 0.5 mcg/kg) to reduce stress level. The outcome of this treatment protocol was recorded.ResultsPDA spasm occurred in 8 (4 %) of the patients between 8 months and 7 years. Complete resolution of spasm was attained after 10–45 min of anesthesia management. All 8 ducts were successfully occluded but one of them had significant residual shunt after device deployment, so larger device was used instead. No cases were referred for surgery.ConclusionsDuctal spasm at the start of anesthesia induction can be effectively managed, allowing the procedure to be safely completed, by following a straightforward anesthetic protocol.

Introduction

Device occlusion of PDA is now a widely performed as effective less complication in comparison to surgery [1].

We consider device closure for all patients with significant left-to-right shunting, as indicated by echocardiography as evidence of left side overload [2].

The awareness of ductal spasm as a complication arose from two early cases of attempted transcatheter PDA closures. The first case was not initially recognized as ductal spasm until they did the angiography where the duct appeared “closed”, then spontaneously reopened after the patient returned to the ward, where it was recognized by reappearance of a continuous murmur was once. In the second case, the spasm was identified during catheterization, but due to concerns about accurate ductal sizing―particularly when using coils for closure and he did surgical ligation of PDA. These experiences raised concerns that ductal spasm is underdiagnosed, potentially leading to unnecessary surgical referrals [3].

Batlivala et al., outlined five major consequences of ductal spasm [4].

Suboptimal closure with a residual shunt due to PDA measurement and assessment during partial spasm.

Device embolization due to same mechanism as previously.

This study aimed to report the frequency, features, management and outcome of PDA spasm during percutaneous occlusion at the catheter lab of Alexandria University New Smouha Children’s Hospital, over about 3 years. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report from Egypt on this issue.

Patients and methods

During the period from 2022 to September 2025, two-hundred infants and children underwent PDA device closure in our centre. All patients who developed ductal spasm before, during or shortly after anesthesia induction or upon catheter entry into the ductus arteriosus were enrolled in the study. The clinical, echocardiographic, angiographic and hemodynamic data of the patients were recorded and statistically analysed. The ductal spasm was managed by an anesthetic protocol and the outcome was recorded. The patients were followed up for 3 months.

As regard interobserver variability in echocardiographic measurements and the timing of assessment, the echocardiography done the day before the procedure and at the day of the intervention before anesthesia induction and this echo done by 3 different consultant pediatric cardiologists and mostly the same results for all.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethical committee of Alexandria Faculty of Medicine. Informed consent was obtained from the parents of all patients, and the confidentiality of the data was ensured.

Transthoracic echocardiography using Philips EPIQ 7 echocardiography system S 9-2or X 5-1 TTE probes (Andover, MA, USA) or Mindray M9 (Shenzhen, China) echocardiography was done for all cases. Part of the anesthesia time was devoted to echocardiographic assessment of the PDA, as most cases were performed via a venous approach without angiography. This duration increased further in cases of PDAspasm, as we waited from a few minutes up to 45 min for spontaneous resolution, followed by detailed echo-Doppler reassessment.

The transcatheter occlusion [1] was performed under general anesthesia for all cases. The following data were monitored as standard ECG, percutaneous pulse oximetry, End Tidal Co2 (EtCo2), noninvasive blood pressure and temperature.

Sevoflurane inhalational induction was done according to Minimum Alveolar Concentration (MAC) then endotracheal intubation according to body weight.

The following drugs were given atracurium muscle relaxant was given in dose of 0.5 mg/kg IV, fentanyl 1 mcg/kg and paracetamol 10 mg/kg IV.

Ventilator data were adjusted as the following (tidal volume 8 ml/kg, respiratory rate according to age, positive end expiratory pressure 5 mmHg and Fio2 was 1.

The venous access was obtained in all cases and venous and arterial accesses were obtained in 2 cases and in all cases the deployment of the device was through the venous approach without angiography except in 2 cases where it was done through the arterial approach after angiography. Aortic angiography was done in lateral and right anterior oblique views and done through the pig tail catheter 4 or 5 Fr at the mid thoracic aorta and isthmus junction and the wires used were 0.035 inchs ordinary and Terumo soft hydrophilic wires. The amount of the contrast was 1.5 ml/kg and after that we advance the multi-purpose catheter 4 Fr over the terumo wire and exchange it with Amplatzer stiff wire and then advance the long delivery sheath over it. The choice of the devices or coils was based on the ductal shape and length, the patient’s weight, and the size of isthmus of aorta opposite to ampulla of PDA by Echo. If Amplatzer duct occluders (ADOs) or Lifetech device were used, size selected was usually 2–3 mm larger than the pulmonary end of PDA.

Complete spasm means total occlusion of the pulmonary end of PDA and no flow across the PDA during the spasm and partial spasm means the pulmonary end of PDA become smaller than the pre-procedure echo but still there is flow. PDA spasm of our cases was detected at different times and by different ways as:

Ductal spasm and the resultant changes in PDA diameter were monitored by echocardiography till relief of spasm before starting intervention as whether it was complete spasm (5 cases) or partial spasm (3 cases).

When PDA spasm occurred the anesthetist managed it by increasing the depth of anesthesia through a higher MAC of inhaled sevoflurane and administering a secondary opioid dose (Fentanyl 0.5 mcg/kg) to reduce stress levels.

Follow-up protocol included chest x-ray and echocardiography, 24 h after the procedure.

Discussion

Failing to compare the PDA size before and during a spasm can lead to an underestimation of its diameter, resulting in incorrect conclusions. This is why important echo assessment of the PDA before the procedure should be done to detect PDA spasm at the start of anesthesia. While the absence of a murmur confirms a complete spasm, its presence does not always rule out the possibility of a partial spasm [3].

Ductal spasm occurring during the occlusion of a PDA is a well-documented phenomenon, particularly in prematurely born infants. Research by Batlivala et al. [4] has shown that this condition is primarily observed in ex-premature children. If unrecognized, ductal spasm poses significant risks, including occlusion failure due to the mistaken belief that the ductus has permanently closed, as well as suboptimal closure, which can result in a residual shunt or even device embolization due to incorrect device sizing which occurred in one of our cases [4].

Although most PDA closures are performed without major complications, ductal spasm occurring before or during the procedure remains a significant challenge. Batlivala et al. highlighted this issue in their study of 331 PDA occlusions over a decade, reporting an incidence of 3.6% and underscoring its potential impact on procedural success [4].

In another series of 267 PDA closure over 12.5 years, 10 cases (3.7%) of PDA spasm were encountered [3].

Case report of 2 cases of PDA spasm and one of them died after the device embolized to iliac bifurcation [5].

Alkamali et al., reported 2 cases of PDA spasm; 11 month old infant and PDA closed successfully and other case, 16 month old had large PDA but they consider it small during the spasm and closed it twice using Flipper cook coil and in each time the coil embolized to left pulmonary artery and finally referred for surgical closure as the coil cannot be snared from the groin and surgery did cut down and remove it and angiography showed it was a large tubular PDA [6].

Embolization of small devices is explained by case reports by Guzeltas et al. [7] and Yates et al. [8].

Guzeltas and colleagues identified PDA spasm in five premature infants out of 150 transcatheter ductal closure procedures, giving an incidence of approximately 3%. In one patient, inappropriate device sizing secondary to ductal spasm resulted in embolization of the occluder into the aorta. A comparable event was reported by Yates, where ductal spasm led to underestimation of ductal diameter and subsequent migration of a Gianturco coil into the left pulmonary artery.

Various management protocols have been explored to address PDA spasm, including modifications in anesthesia and the continuous infusion of intravenous prostaglandin E1 [4]. It has been suggested that inhaled anesthesia contributes to the development of spasm and should be discontinued to facilitate spasm relief. However, this alone may not be sufficient to fully resolve the spasm. Studies indicate that the initiation of a continuous alprostadil infusion results in the ductus expanding to its maximum pre-procedural dimensions within minutes. Interestingly, findings suggest that the ductus in ex-premature infants remains highly responsive to prostaglandin E1 even up to 7.3 years (87 months) of age [3].

A search of published reports involving children older than the typically accepted upper age threshold of 50 days identified only a single case. Kashani and colleagues described an 11-month-old infant with a patent ductus arteriosus who developed spontaneous ductal spasm that resolved following administration of prostaglandin E1 [9].

Our observations indicate that ductal spasm may be precipitated by factors such as the initiation of anesthesia prior to establishing venous or arterial access, as well as mechanical irritation of the ductus from catheter or guidewire manipulation.

Review of published studies indicates that a range of physiological, hemodynamic, and drug-related influences can initiate or exacerbate ductal spasm during transcatheter closure of a patent ductus arteriosus [3].

It is noteworthy that local anesthesia used at the access site does not affect ductal patency. However, if administered before general anesthesia, the pain from the injection might contribute to ductal spasm [3].

Additionally, there is evidence of increased sensitivity of the ductus in preterm infants during the immediate postnatal period to endogenous prostaglandin E2. This sensitivity may contribute to prolonged ductal patency and resistance to medical closure using agents such as ibuprofen. This theory could also explain why the ductus in ex-preterm infants remains highly responsive to intravenous prostaglandin E1, even at an older age when the patient undergoes ductal closure. A high level of clinical suspicion is necessary to detect PDA spasm, particularly in prematurely born children undergoing percutaneous occlusion, regardless of their age at the time of the procedure [10,11].

Careful cardiac auscultation should be performed in all patients prior to induction of anesthesia. Nevertheless, detection of a murmur does not exclude the possibility of ductal spasm, as the ductus may be only partially constricted. For this reason, a targeted echocardiographic assessment before anesthesia is recommended. Transthoracic imaging should verify re-expansion of the ductal lumen and demonstrate continuous-wave Doppler gradients comparable to those documented on the pre-catheterization study.

In our study, immediate management of ductal spasm involved deepening anesthesia with a higher MAC of inhaled sevoflurane and administering an additional opioid dose (Fentanyl 0.5 mcg/kg) to reduce stress.

Minimizing mechanical manipulation of the ductus is essential. Recovery from ductal spasm should be followed using transthoracic echocardiography, and in cases where angiography was performed prior to the onset of spasm, a lateral angiographic view may be used for confirmation once echocardiographic features return to their precatheterization state.

To minimize the risk of embolization, selecting the safest occlusion device is essential. This may involve choosing a slightly oversized device or opting for an occluder instead of a coil.

Routine assessments should confirm the suitability for percutaneous closure. A post-procedural echocardiogram must be performed before the patient exits the catheterization laboratory.

Conclusion

Ductal spasm occurring during transcatheter occlusion procedure can be effectively managed, allowing safe completion of the procedure. This can be achieved through deepening anesthesia by increasing the minimum alveolar concentration of inhaled sevoflurane and administering fentanyl at a dose of 0.5 mcg/kg to help minimize stress level during the intervention.

Recommendations

Additional research is needed to fully understand the specific mechanisms contributing to ductal spasm, as this would aid in developing preventive strategies.

Limitation of the study

In our study the limited number of the cases was the limit of the significance of the anesthesia intervention which is done in cases of the PDA spasm. Also it is observational study.
