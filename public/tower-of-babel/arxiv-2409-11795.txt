1.
Introduction

1.1.
Self-similar fragmentation processes

Fragmentation processes
are stochastic processes
modelling the evolution of a particle breaking up as time passes.
One may think of particles in a cement mixer, the fragmentation of a hard drive,
or fragments of onion on a cook’s chopping board.
The theory of fragmentation processes dates back at least as far as
Kolmogorov
[
19
]
, but it was not until the 2000s that Bertoin
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
and
Berestycki
[
2
]
initiated
a systematic study and the rigorous mathematical theory.

Formally, a fragmentation process is a stochastic process
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}

taking values in the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}:=\left\{\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)\,:\,s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\ldots\geq 0,\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}\leq 1\right\}.

We think of
X(t)=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)
as a list (in decreasing order) of the sizes of the fragments in the system at time
t\geq 0
. In other words,
s_{1}
is the size of the
largest fragment,
s_{2}
is the size of the second largest fragment, and so on.

In developing a mathematically tractable theory of fragmentation processes,
we usually take the process to be Markovian, and require that, roughly speaking, fragments behave independently of other fragments in the system.
Outlining this here in the so-called
finite activity case
,
fragmentation processes are
characterised in terms of a suitably nice rate function
r:(0,1]\to[0,\infty)
and a probability measure
\tilde{\nu}
on
\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}

as follows.
We begin at time zero with a single fragment of size
1
. This fragment has an exponential lifetime with rate
r(1)
, then splits into a random (possibly infinite) number of pieces of sizes
(S_{1},S_{2},\ldots)
, where
(S_{1},S_{2},\ldots)
is distributed according to
\tilde{\nu}
.
The process proceeds by the following rule: independently of all other
fragments in the system, a fragment of some size
u>0
has an exponential lifetime
with rate
r(u)
, and at the end of its lifetime breaks into pieces of sizes
(uS_{1},uS_{2},\ldots)
, where

(S_{1},S_{2},\ldots)
is a random variable distributed according to
\tilde{\nu}
and independent of all other fragments in the system.

1.2.
Self-similar fragmentation processes and the infinite activity case

In the present article, we will be interested in
self-similar fragmentation processes
,
which are characterised by the property that a fragment of size
u\in(0,1]
breaks
at rate
r(u)=\lambda u^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the index of
self-similarity, and
\lambda>0
is some constant.

Self-similarity is a natural physical assumption.
Take for example, the fragmentation of pieces of onion on the (stochastic) cook’s chopping board. One might argue that this is a fragmentation process with index
\alpha=1/3
: the rate at which an onion of mass
m

gets hit by the knife is proportional to its diameter, which is proportional to
m^{1/3}
.
(Though of course, any good chef is likely to cut many pieces of onion at once,
while our formal stochastic processes experience no simultaneous fragmentations.)
Of course, different exponents may be associated with various physical models:
the
\alpha=1
case arises naturally in one dimension, and the
\alpha=2/3

case has even been considered as a model for polymer degradation
[
1
]
.

When
\alpha>0
, self-similarity entails that larger fragments break more quickly than
smaller fragments, and as such there is a regularising effect in the range of sizes of
the fragments. This effect may be regarded as serendipitous for anyone who chops
onions and wants all of their onion fragments small: the large
pieces of onion are more likely to get hit by the cook’s knife,
and as such are more likely to break to ‘catch up’ to the small sizes of the other
pieces of onion.

Of course, the
\alpha<0
case is characterised by the opposite behaviour:
smaller fragments break more quickly than larger fragments.
In fact, in the
\alpha<0
case, the process has quite dramatic behaviour:
initially a portion, and then the entire system, is guaranteed to
fragment into dust in finite time
[
14
,
15
]
.

In the finite activity case, we can package
\lambda
and
\tilde{\nu}
into a finite
measure (not necessary a probability measure) by setting
\nu:=\lambda\tilde{\nu}
.
The measure
\nu
is called the
dislocation measure
. The dynamics are then
summarized by the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

In a self-similar fragmentation process with index
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

and dislocation measure
\nu
, fragments behave independently of one another,
and at rate
u^{\alpha}\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s})
, each fragment of size
u

breaks into fragments of sizes
u\mathbf{s}=(us_{1},us_{2},\ldots)
.

One of the major technical achievements of the theory of fragmentation processes is
the development of the
infinite activity case
, which allows for the “crumbling”
of fragments continuously in time. This is accounted for by allowing the dislocation measure

\nu
to be an infinite measure, albeit with most of its concentration on elements

(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)
of
\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}
where
1-s_{1}
is small, so that while events may occur
at an arbitrarily high rate, most of these small events involve a tiny amount of mass being
chipped off the size of each fragment to produce smaller fragments. The precise technical
condition necessary to make sense of such processes is the requirement that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}(1-s_{1})\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s})<\infty.

In other words, the expected instantaneous loss of size of a given fragment is finite, where we are making the natural association between a parent fragment and its largest child fragment at a fragmentation event.
For a detailed construction and introduction to the topic we refer the reader
to
[
6
]
.

Finally, through the article, in order to simplify the discussion, we will assume that the
process is
conservative
. In other words, the measure
\nu
is supported on the
subset of
\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}
consisting of sequences
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)
such that

\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}=1
. In the case where the index
\alpha
of self-similarity is
positive, this property is sufficient to ensure that the sum of masses of all fragments
in the system is equal to
1
at all times. We emphasise that this assumption is in force
for the remainder of the article. To avoid trivialities, we also assume throughout that

\nu
is a nonzero measure that has no mass on
\{(1,0,\ldots)\}
.

1.3.
The size of the largest fragment in self-similar fragmentation processes of positive index

Given a self-similar fragmentation process with positive index of self-similarity,
it is natural to enquire about the size of the largest fragment in the
system at large times
t\geq 0
, as it describes the speed at which the object that
undergoes the fragmentation breaks down.

The most general previous result on this front is due to Bertoin
[
5
]
,
stating that if
e^{-m_{t}}
is the size of the largest fragment in the system at time
t\geq 0
, then

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}m_{t}/\log t=-1/\alpha\qquad\text{almost-surely}.

We should mention that in earlier work, Brennan and Durrett
[
9
,
10
]

proved (
1.2
) in the special finite activity case in which at
rate
\lambda u^{\alpha}
, a fragment of size
u

breaks into two pieces of sizes
Zu
and
(1-Z)u
, where
Z
is a uniform random variable
on
[0,1]
.

In the recent work
[
13
]
(whose authors overlap those of the present article)
the simplest possible fragmentation mechanism is considered, namely the mechanism in
which each fragment of size
m
splits into
k
equally sized pieces at rate
m^{\alpha}
.
In other words, this corresponds to the case where

\displaystyle\nu:=\delta_{(1/k,\ldots,1/k,0,\ldots)}\qquad\text{(with $k$ copies of $1/k$)},

where
\delta_{x}
refers to the Dirac mass at
x
.
Note then that, starting with a single initial fragment of size
1
,
every fragment in the system at any time
t
has size
k^{-n}
for some positive integer
n
.

It is shown in
[
13
]
that if
k^{-\tilde{m_{t}}}
is the size of the largest fragment in the system at time
t\geq 0
, then for ‘most’ large times
t
, we have

(1.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{m}_{t}=\lceil\kappa\left(\log t-\log\log t+\log(\alpha)\right)\rceil\qquad\kappa=1/(\alpha\log k),

with high probability as
t\to\infty
. By ‘most’ large times
t
, we mean
t
is such that the quantity inside the ceiling function is not too close to an integer; see
[
13
, Theorem A]
for a precise statement.

The authors also prove a statement similar to (
1.3
) on the behaviour of the
exponent
\tilde{M}_{t}
governing the size
k^{-\tilde{M}_{t}}
of the
smallest
fragment at
large times.

The main tool used in
[
13
]
is to relate the process to a branching random walk
by considering the genealogy of the fragment process.

Namely, for each
n
, one may consider the
k^{n}
different times at which a fragment of size
k^{-n}
fragmented into
k
fragments of size

k^{-(n+1)}
.
One can think of each of these
k^{n}
times as the position of a particle in
generation
n
, and thereby relate the fragmentation process to a time-inhomogenous discrete-time branching random walk, where the jumps become exponentially larger as the generations progress. The exponential growth of the jumps accounts for the concentration of (
1.3
). We use a continuous-space analogue of this association — with the Lévy process playing the role of the random walk — to prove our finite activity result, Theorem
1.2
.

1.4.
Main results

In the article at hand, we prove fine estimates for the size
e^{-m_{t}}
of the
largest fragment in the system at large times in a broad class of self-similar
fragmentation processes with conservative dislocation measures. These estimates take the form

\displaystyle m_{t}=\left(\log t-(1-\theta)\log\log t\right)/\alpha+O(\log\log t),\qquad\text{almost-surely for large $t$},

where the
O(\log\log t)
terms are explicit. Here
\theta\geq 0
is a parameter
describing how “erosive” the dislocation measure is.

Over the course of the article we will consider two cases. First, we consider the
finite activity case where
\nu(\mathcal{P}_{\rm m})<\infty
. Thereafter, we consider the
infinite activity case under the mild regularity condition that the dislocation measure
takes the form

(1.4)

\nu(\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}:1-s_{1}>\delta\})=\delta^{-\theta}\ell(1/\delta),

for some
\theta\geq 0
and
\ell\colon[0,\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
which
is slowly varying at
+\infty
.
Recall that the latter means that for any positive
\lambda
,

\ell(\lambda x)/\ell(x)\to 1
as
x\to\infty
.
We call
\theta
in (
1.4
) the
crumbling index
.
We note that for
\nu
satisfying (
1.4
),
\theta
is the infimum over

\phi\geq 0
such that the strong version

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}(1-s_{1})^{\phi}\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s})<\infty

of (
1.1
) holds. Consequently, in order for (
1.4
) to hold,
we must have
\theta\in[0,1]
.

If the process has finite activity, then (
1.4
) holds with
\theta=0
.
It is, however, possible for (
1.4
) to hold with
\theta=0
but for the
process to nonetheless have infinite activity (e.g.
\ell(1/\delta)=\log(1/\delta)
).
We emphasise that it is also possible for (
1.4
) to hold with
\theta=1
,
provided
\ell
satisfies the integrability condition
\int_{0}^{1}\ell(1/t)\mathrm{d}t/t<\infty
.
Since these are edge cases whose treatment would demand substantial adaptations of our tools,
for the sake of concision we rule these two special cases out of our consideration.
In the finite activity case where
\nu
has mass
\lambda
, we make the very mild assumption that,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\text{There exist $C,c>0$ such that $\nu(\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}:1-s_{1}>\delta\})\leq C\delta^{c}$ for all $\delta\in[0,1]$}.

We now state our main results.

Theorem 1.2

(The finite activity case)
.

Let
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a self-similar fragmentation process with dislocation
measure
\nu
and index of self-similarity
\alpha>0
. Let
e^{-m_{t}}
be the
size of the largest fragment in the system at time
t
. Suppose that
\nu
is a
finite measure satisfying (
1.5
), and such that,
writing
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)=(e^{-h_{1}},e^{-h_{2}},\ldots)
,

\nu
is not concentrated on a set for which
(h_{i},i\geq 1)
take values in a lattice.
Let

\lambda=\nu(\mathcal{P}_{\rm m})<\infty
be the total mass of
\nu
.
Define the deterministic function

(1.6)

\displaystyle g(t):=\left(\log(t)-\log\log t+\log(\alpha\lambda)\right)/\alpha,\qquad t\geq 0.

Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}(m_{t}-g(t))=0,

almost-surely.

This result gives a treatment of self-similar fragmentation processes with finite activity.
The non-lattice assumption is purely technical. In fact, since
g
in (
1.6
) is
slowly varying, one may expect to get a similar result in the lattice case
by examining
g
along the smallest lattice containing
(h_{i},i\geq 1)
, c.f. (
1.3
). We would like to highlight in particular that, remarkably perhaps, the equation (
1.6
) states that after accounting for the total mass,
\lambda>0
, of the dislocation measure, the asymptotic behaviour of
(m_{t})_{t\geq 0}
does not depend on the dislocation measure.

We turn to treating the case where the fragmentation mechanism takes the
form (
1.4
) for some
\theta\in(0,1)
.
We ultimately prove the following.

Theorem 1.3

(The infinite activity case)
.

Let
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a self-similar fragmentation process with
dislocation measure
\nu
and index of self-similarity
\alpha>0
.
Let
e^{-m_{t}}
be the size of the largest fragment in the system at
time
t
.
Suppose
\nu
satisfies (
1.4
) for some
\theta\in(0,1)
.
Define the deterministic function

(1.7)

\displaystyle g(t):=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{\log t-(1-\theta)\log\log t-\log\ell\left(\log t~\ell\left(\log t\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)+c(\alpha,\theta)\right\},

where
c(\alpha,\theta):=\log\alpha-(1-\theta)\log(1-\theta)-\log\Gamma(1-\theta)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}(m_{t}-g(t))=0,

almost-surely.

Our second result draws a clear connection between the singularity of
\nu
at

\{s_{1}=1\}
and the asymptotic behaviour of
m_{t}
.

It can also be shown that this connection holds without requiring a detailed
asymptotic expansion
of
\nu({1-s_{1}>\delta})
. For instance if one knows only that

\nu({1-s_{1}>\delta})=\delta^{-\theta+o(1)}

without any regularity assumptions on
o(1)\to 0
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
one should
still be able to show,
by applying our strategy and stochastic dominance, that

m(t)=\left(\log t-(1-\theta)\log\log t\right)/\alpha+o(\log\log(t)).

We finally note that the
\log\log t
order term in equation (
1.7
) matches
that of (
1.6
) as
\theta
decreases to zero.

1.5.
Idea of Proof

Our proof hinges on the idea that we study the total number of distinct particles to ever
have size of order
O(e^{-h})
, and find that the number of such particles is
O(e^{h})
. For this task, we rely on the theory of Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes.
Of course, there are certain technical challenges associated with making sense of a ‘particle’ in the
infinite activity case, as fragmentation events are occuring continuously in time.
Our work in this case is related to that of Bertoin and Martinez
[
7
]
, who associate the
process of fragmentation with an energy cost, and estimate the amount of energy required to
break up material into smaller pieces.

Thereafter, we track the size of one such particle of around this size, and study the rare
event that it undergoes little to no fragmentation for a long period of time. Here, we
appeal to an idea appearing in
[
6
]
which involves following a size-biased
fragment forward in time. More precisely, we follow a single fragment, and when it undergoes
a fragmentation, we choose a size-biased fragment to continue following after the fragmentation.
Again, this turns out to be a sound notion even in the infinite activity case.

In any case, the pertinent
fact here is that if
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the size of this size-biased fragment process
(see Section
3.1
for a detailed explanation),
then we have the distributional identity

\displaystyle(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}\overset{(d)}{=}\left(e^{-\xi_{\rho(t)}}\right)_{t\geq 0},

where
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a non-decreasing Lévy process with an explicit Laplace
mechanism that can be described in terms of
the dislocation measure
\nu
(see (
3.3
) below), and
\rho(t)
is a
Lamperti-type time change defined implicitly via

(1.8)

\displaystyle t=\int_{0}^{\rho(t)}e^{\alpha\xi_{s}}\mathrm{d}s,\qquad t\geq 0.

Since there are roughly
O(e^{h})
particles of size
O(e^{-h})
, we then search for the
largest values of
t
such that

(1.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(e^{-\xi_{\rho(t)}}\leq e^{-h}\right)\sim e^{-h},

where the precise meaning of (
1.9
) will be made apparent in the sequel.
In making technical sense of (
1.9
) we use a many-to-one formula for
fragmentation processes to prove an upper bound, and the limit theory of
Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes to prove a lower bound.

In the finite activity case, the largest values of
t
for which
(
1.9
) holds are due to events where the path of
\xi_{\rho(t)}
travels to a
height
h-\varepsilon
in a fairly typical time, and then spends an unusually long
period of time in the small space window
[h-\varepsilon,h]
.
This leads to our proof of
Theorem
1.2
, which may be regarded as a continuous-space generalisation of
Theorem A of
[
13
]
, which is concerned with the dislocation measure

\nu=\delta_{(1/k,1/k,\ldots,1/k,0,0,\ldots)}
associated with splitting into
k

equally sized pieces.

The more interesting — and substantially more technically delicate — case is the
infinite activity setting of Theorem
1.3
, where the dislocation measure has
crumbling index
\theta\in(0,1)
. Here we find that the hitting times of the underlying
Lévy process have the superexponential tails

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{t}<1)\sim\exp\left(-t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\tilde{\ell}(t)\right),

for an explicit slowly varying function
\tilde{\ell}
. There is a
delicate balance between the stochastic clock in (
1.8
) wanting the process

(\xi_{\rho(t)})_{t\geq 0}
to spend as much time as possible in the region
[h-O(1),h]

where the clock runs slowest, and the superexponential tails in (
1.10
)
penalising such behaviour. Letting
\rho(x)
be an expression for the density of
time spent by the untime-changed process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
at height
h-x
,
we ultimately arrive at the following variational problem, where we would like to
maximise

\displaystyle I[\rho]:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha(h-x)}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x

subject to the constraint

\displaystyle J[\rho]:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(x)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\mathrm{d}x=h.

By solving this maximisation problem, we ultimately obtain a fine
1+o(1)
expression
for the largest
t>0
for which
(
1.9
) holds,
and this leads to the fine estimate in Theorem
1.3
of
m_{t}
up to
o(1)
terms.
Implicit in our proof is that if
\rho_{*}(y)
is the maximiser, the historical size

e^{-h(t)}
of the largest fragment existing at some time
t
has size closely tracking
the inverse function of
x\mapsto e^{\alpha x}\rho_{*}(h-x)
.

1.6.
Overview

The remainder of the article is structured as follows.

In Section
2
we review the basics of Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes and
their limit theory. We then discuss a natural projection of finite activity fragmentation
processes onto Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes, and thereafter show that even in the
infinite activity case, one can obtain a Crump–Mode–Jagers branching process by viewing an
infinite activity self-similar fragmentation process at certain discrete stochastic times.

In Section
3
we review the spine construction of a tagged fragment in a
self-similar fragmentation process due to Bertoin
[
6
]
. Thereafter, we use
tools from Jain and Pruitt
[
18
]
to study the rare events that a spine particle does
not lose too much size over a large time interval.

In Section
4
we use a many-to-one type formula to prove an upper bound for
the expected number of fragments of size greater than
e^{-h}
at time
t
, and, for a
given
h
, study the asymptotics of the times
t(h)
at which this expectation becomes
o(1)
.

In Section
5
we use our comparison with a Crump–Mode–Jagers branching
process together with a delicate spine argument to show that with high probability there
exist fragments of a reasonably large size at certain times.

In the final Section
6
, we tie our work together to prove our main results,
Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Correspondences with Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes

2.1.
Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes

In this section we introduce Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes
(C–M–J processes for short). Let

D
be a random point process on

(0,\infty)
. For the sake of simplicity, we will assume throughout that

D

has at least one point in
(0,\infty)
almost-surely.

In the Crump–Mode–Jagers
branching process, we start at height zero with a single particle. This particle
reproduces offspring particles at heights

0<\mathcal{H}(1)\leq\mathcal{H}(2)\leq\ldots
according to a copy of the
point process

D
. If the reproductive point process has only
k
points on

(0,\infty)
, we use the convention that
\mathcal{H}(j)=\infty
for all
j>k
.
Thereafter, the
j^{\text{th}}
child of the initial particle reproduces
children at heights

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}(j1),\mathcal{H}(j2),\ldots):=(\mathcal{H}(j)+d_{j1},\mathcal{H}(j)+d_{j2},\ldots),

where
0<d_{j1}\leq d_{j2}\leq\ldots
are the points (listed in increasing order)
of an independent copy of the point process
D
(if the process has only
k
points,
set
d_{j\ell}:=+\infty
for all
\ell>k
). More generally, using the Ulam–Harris
labelling convention, each particle in the process is associated with a word
w
in the set

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{N}^{n}.

If a particle associated with a word
w
has height
\mathcal{H}(w)
, then the children
of this particle occur at heights

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{H}(w1),\mathcal{H}(w2),\ldots\right)=\left(\mathcal{H}(w)+d_{w1},\mathcal{H}(w)+d_{w2},\ldots\right),

where
(d_{w1},d_{w2},\ldots)
are the points (labelled in nondecreasing order)
of an independent copy of
D
. In essence, the Crump–Mode–Jagers process is a random function

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{T}\to[0,\infty]

and is an example of a branching random walk. We refer the reader to
[
17
]
for an extensive literature review on the topic of C–M–J processes.

2.2.
Limit theory for supercritical C–M–J processes

We now develop some central properties of supercritical C–M–J processes,
following Nerman
[
20
]
. Firstly, we define the reproduction intensity
\mu

of the reproductive point process
D
to be the measure
\mu
on
(0,\infty)
given
by

\displaystyle\mu(A):=\mathbb{E}[D(A)]:=\mathbb{E}[\#\{\text{Points of $D$ in $A$}\}]

for any Borel subset
A\subset(0,\infty)
.

Through the remainder of this section we will operate under the following three assumptions:

(i)
\mu
is not concentrated on any lattice
\{0,h,2h,\ldots\}
.

(ii) There exists a Malthusian parameter
\kappa>0
with the property that

\displaystyle 1=\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-\kappa h}\mu(\mathrm{d}h).

(iii) The first moment of
e^{-\kappa h}\mu(\mathrm{d}h)
is finite, i.e.

(2.1)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,\infty)}he^{-\kappa h}\mu(\mathrm{d}h)<\infty.

Nerman
[
20
]
goes on to define a random variable

\Omega_{\kappa}
as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\kappa}:=\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-\kappa h}D(\mathrm{d}h).

We say the C–M–J process satisfies the
Kesten–Stigum condition
if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Omega_{\kappa}\log_{+}\Omega_{\kappa}\right]<\infty.

Given
a>0
, define a random variable

\displaystyle Z_{h}^{a}:=\#\{w\in\mathbb{T}:\mathcal{H}(w)\in(h-a,h]\},

and for short right
Z_{h}:=Z_{h}^{\infty}
.

Then Doney
[
12
,
11
]
showed that under (
2.2
), the random variable
Z_{h}e^{-\kappa h}
converges to a nondegenerate random limit
W
.
Moreover, if
D
has the property that it has at least one point in
(0,\infty)
almost-surely, then
W>0
almost-surely also.

Nerman then shows, again under (i), (ii), (iii) above, together with (
2.2
), that

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{h}^{a}e^{-\kappa h}}{Z_{h}e^{-\kappa h}}\to\frac{1}{\kappa}(1-e^{-\kappa a}).

2.3.
Crump–Mode–Jagers from self-similar fragmentations

There is a natural way of generating a Crump–Mode–Jagers branching process from a self-similar
fragmentation process with finite activity, namely one simply associates each fragmentation event
with an increase in generation. In this section however, we will seek to find a more robust
connection between self-similar fragmentation processes and Crump–Mode–Jagers processes that is
also suitable for the infinite activity case. Our trick here is to observe the self-similar
fragmentation process at certain discrete and particle-dependent times.

We may construct a Crump–Mode–Jagers branching process
\{\mathcal{H}(w)\,:\,w\in\mathbb{T}\}

from a self-similar fragmentation process with possible infinite activity as follows. Each

\mathcal{H}(w)
will represent a fragment of size
e^{-\mathcal{H}(w)}
living at some
time
\mathcal{T}(w)
in the process. Thus, we will construct a collection

\{\mathcal{T}(w)\,:\,w\in\mathbb{T}\}
of times
\mathcal{T}(w)
associated to words

w\in\mathbb{T}
.

The construction is as follows. Set
\mathcal{T}(\varnothing):=0
and
\mathcal{H}(\varnothing):=0
.
The word
\varnothing
is associated with the initial fragment of size
1=e^{-\mathcal{H}(\varnothing)}

existing at time
\mathcal{T}(\varnothing)=0
. Consider now viewing the descendent fragments of this
initial fragment at time

1=e^{\alpha\mathcal{H}(\varnothing)}
. These fragments have
sizes
(X_{1}(1),X_{2}(1),\ldots)
listed in decreasing order. We define the variables

\{\mathcal{H}(i)\,:\,i\geq 1\}
by setting
e^{-\mathcal{H}(i)}:=X_{i}(1)
. We also define
first generation times by setting
\mathcal{T}(i):=1
for all
i\geq 1
. Thereafter, consider a
word
w\in\mathbb{T}
associated with a fragment existing at a moment
\mathcal{T}(w)
in time
and size
e^{-\mathcal{H}(w)}
. Consider viewing the descendants of this fragment at time

\mathcal{T}(w)+e^{\alpha\mathcal{H}(w)}
, and write
(e^{-\mathcal{H}(w1)},e^{-\mathcal{H}(w2)},\ldots)

for the sizes of these descendants. Set
\mathcal{T}(wi):=\mathcal{T}(w)+e^{\alpha\mathcal{H}(w)}

for all
i\geq 1
.

By the self-similarity property,
\{\mathcal{H}(w):w\in\mathbb{T}\}
is a Crump–Mode–Jagers
branching process. Let
\mu
be the reproduction intensity and
D
be the associated reproductive
point process. Since the process is conservative, the sums of sizes of descendants of a given
fragment at a later time is equal to the size of that fragment. That is, for every
w\in\mathbb{T}
,

\displaystyle e^{-\mathcal{H}(w)}=\sum_{i\geq 1}e^{-\mathcal{H}(wi)}.

In particular, by taking
w=\emptyset
, we have

\displaystyle 1=\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-h}D(\mathrm{d}h)\qquad\text{almost-surely}.

Therefore, the Malthusian parameter
\kappa=1
.
In fact, we emphasise that
\Omega_{1}=1
almost-surely, as opposed to merely

\mathbb{E}[\Omega_{1}]=1
. Consequently, the Kesten–Stigum condition (
2.2
) holds trivially.

We now seek a lower bound on the number of particles existing in the Crump-Mode-Jagers process associated with our fragmentation processes at large heights.

We begin by noting that provided the fragmentation process itself is nonlattice (which always happens automatically in the infinite activity case, and is one of our assumptions in Theorem
1.2
in the finite activity case), the associated Crump-Mode-Jagers process is nonlattice.

However, there is an issue we need to take care of in the infinite activity case:
the integrability condition (iii) above (equation (
2.1
)) fails in general.
With this in mind, in order to sidestep any concerns about integrability,
we simply truncate our process by ignoring any particles born in the Crump-Mode-Jagers process
that has a height
M
greater than its parents. This is equivalent to ignoring any fragment of size smaller than
e^{-M}
at time
1
, and thereafter doing similarly under the time change. This procedure induces a second Crump-Mode-Jagers process
\mathcal{H}_{M}:=\{\mathcal{H}_{M}(w):w\in\mathbb{T}\}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{M}(wi):=\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}(wi)&\text{if $\mathcal{H}(wi)-\mathcal{H}(w)\leq M$}\\
+\infty&\text{if $\mathcal{H}(wi)-\mathcal{H}(w)>M$}.\end{cases}

The associated reproduction intensity measure
\mu_{M}
is then supported on
(0,M)
. If
M
is sufficiently large,
\mathcal{H}_{M}
is still not concentrated on a lattice so that (i) holds. Moreover, there exists
\kappa_{M}<1
such that

\displaystyle 1=\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-\kappa_{M}h}\mu_{M}(\mathrm{d}h),

thus (ii) holds. Finally, (iii) holds trivially by virtue of the fact that
\mu_{M}
is supported on
(0,M)
. Again, the Kesten Stigum condition (
2.2
) holds, since
\Omega_{\kappa}\leq 1
almost-surely in the truncated process. (We had
\Omega_{\kappa}=1
almost-surely in the un-truncated process.)

We note that as
M\uparrow\infty
,
\kappa_{M}\uparrow 1
. Let us write

\displaystyle\kappa_{M}=1-\varepsilon_{M},

so that
\varepsilon_{M}\downarrow 0
as
M\uparrow\infty
.

In any case, the
M
-truncated Crump-Mode-Jagers process clearly satisfies all of the conditions of Section
2.2
. As before let
Z_{h,M}:=\#\{w\in\mathbb{T}:\mathcal{H}_{M}(w)\leq h\}
. Then
Z_{h,M}e^{-\kappa_{M}h}
converges to a nondegenerate limit random variable
W
, which is strictly positive almost-surely.
Moreover, setting
Z_{h,M}^{a}:=\#\{w\in\mathbb{T}:\mathcal{H}_{M}(w)\in(h-a,h]\}
, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle Z_{h,M}^{a}e^{-\kappa_{M}h}\to W\frac{1}{\kappa_{M}}(1-e^{-\kappa_{M}a}),

where
W
is an almost-surely positive random variable.
We will extract from these results the following rough lower bound on the number of particles in our Crump-Mode-Jagers branching process who ever have heights
\mathcal{H}(w)
in
(h-a,h]
:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
a>0,M>0
. Then, there exists a random
h(a,M)
such that for all
h\geq h(a,M)
we have

\displaystyle Z_{h,M}^{a}\geq e^{(1-2\varepsilon_{M})h}.

Proof.

The convergence (
2.3
) entails that there exists a random
h_{1}(a,M)
such that

\displaystyle Z_{h,M}^{a}e^{-\kappa_{M}h}\geq W\frac{1}{2\kappa_{M}}(1-e^{-\kappa_{M}a})=:c_{a,M}W.

for all
h\geq h_{1}(a,M)
. Moreover, since
W
is an almost-surely positive random variable, there exists another random
h_{2}(a,M)
such that

\displaystyle c_{a,M}W\geq e^{-\varepsilon_{M}h}

for all
h\geq h_{1}(a,M)
. It follows that for all
h\geq h(a,M):=\max\{h_{1}(a,M),h_{2}(a,M)\}
we have

\displaystyle Z_{h,M}^{a}e^{-\kappa_{M}h}\geq e^{-\varepsilon_{M}h}.

Using
\kappa_{M}=1-\varepsilon_{M}
and rearranging, we obtain the result.
∎

2.4.
Independent sets in Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes

In the sequel, we would like to use a variant of our previous lower bounds for C–M–J
processes to show that there exist particles that exhibit certain properties with high
probability. In this direction, we would like to use a variant of Proposition
2.1

to show that there are a certain number of fragments of size between
e^{-h}
and
e^{-(h-s)}

existing at various times in the process. However, we would like to rule out the possibility
that one of these fragments is parent, grandparent or ancestor to the other, so that
we can consider independent events. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

A subset
\mathbb{S}
of
\mathbb{T}
is an
antichain
if and only if for every
distinct pair of elements
w
and
w^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{S}
, neither
w
not
w^{\prime}
is ancestor to the other.

Suppose that we have a self-similar fragmentation process with index
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
of self-similarity. Suppose that the process is either finite activity such that the log-sizes of fragments do not take values in a lattice, or such that the process is infinite activity in the regime (
1.4
). The following result guarantees large antichains of fragments at large heights:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\delta>0,\log(2)>a>0
. Then there exists a random
h_{0}(a,\delta)
such that for all
h\geq h_{0}(a,\delta)
, there exists a subset
\mathbb{S}
of
\mathbb{T}
such that

(1)

\mathbb{S}
is an antichain,

(2)

\mathcal{H}(w)\in(h-a,h]
for all
w\in\mathbb{S}
,

(3)

The cardinality of
\mathbb{S}
satisfies
\#\mathbb{S}\geq e^{(1-\delta)h}
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{M}(h-a,h]:=\{w\in\mathbb{T}:\mathcal{H}_{M}(w)\in(h-a,h]\}.

and write
\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]:=\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(h-a,h]
for the associated set where there is no truncution. Then for any
M>0

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{M}(h-a,h]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]

Choose
M=M_{\delta}
sufficiently large so that
\varepsilon_{M}\leq\delta/4
. Then by Proposition
2.1
there exists
h_{0}(a,\delta):=h_{0}(a,M_{\delta})
such that for all
h\geq h_{0}(a,\delta)
we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle Z_{h}^{a}\geq Z_{h,M}^{a}\geq e^{(1-2\varepsilon_{M})h}\geq e^{(1-\delta/2)h}\qquad h\geq h_{0}(a,\delta).

We now show that with high probability,
\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]
contains an antichain of size at least
e^{(1-\delta)h}
.
To this end define let
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(h-a,h]
denote the set of first ancestors of
(h-a,h]
. This is the subset of
\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]
consisting of those elements with no descendent also in
\mathcal{Q}_{M}(h-a,h]
. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(h-a,h]:=\{w\in\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]:\mathcal{H}(v)<h-a~\text{for every strict ancestor $v$ of $w$}\}.

Plainly
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(h-a,h]
is an antichain. We now seek to bound below the cardinality
Z_{h}^{\prime a}
of
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(h-a,h]
.
In this direction, let
w\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(h-a,h]
and define the random variable

\displaystyle N_{w}:=\#\{v\in\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]:v\text{ descendant of $w$}\}.

We control
N_{w}
. Recall that from the conservativeness of the underlying fragmentation process we have

(2.5)

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{i\geq 1}e^{-(\mathcal{H}(wi)-\mathcal{H}(w))}.

Note that since
a<\log(2)
, (
2.5
) tells us that each
w
in
\mathbb{T}
may have at most one descendent
wi
with
\mathcal{H}(wi)-\mathcal{H}(w)<a
. Let
p(a)\in(0,1)
denote the probability of this occuring, i.e.

\displaystyle p(a):=\{\exists i:\mathcal{H}(wi)-\mathcal{H}(w)<a\}.

It follows that for each
w
in
\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]
,
N_{w}
is stochastically dominated by a geometric random variable with success probability
p(a)
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{w}|w\in\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]]\leq 1+\frac{1}{1-p(a)}=:c(a).

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{h}^{a}|Z_{h}^{\prime a}]\leq c(a)Z_{h}^{\prime a}.

By conditional Markov’s inequality, it follows that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{h}^{a}\geq e^{\delta h/2}Z_{h}^{\prime a})\leq c(a)e^{-\delta h/2}.

Combining (
2.4
) with (
2.6
) and using the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it follows that there exists a random
h_{1}(a,\delta)
such that

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(h-a,h]\geq e^{(1-\delta)h}\qquad\text{for all $h\geq h_{1}(a,\delta)$ such that $h=na$ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

To transfer this result to
h
sufficiently large taking values in the lattice
\{n\delta:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
to all sufficiently large
h
, note that any interval of the form
(h-a,h]
contains an subinterval of the form
((n-1)a/2,na/2]
. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(h-a,h]\supseteq\mathcal{Q}\left(n\frac{a}{2}-\frac{a}{2},n\frac{a}{2}\right]\qquad\text{for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

It thus follows that for
h\geq h_{2}(a,\delta):=h_{1}(a/2,\delta)
we have

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(h-a,h]\geq e^{(1-\delta)h}.

Thus for all real
h
greater than some random height
h_{2}(a,\delta)
, the antichain
\mathbb{S}:=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(h-a,h]
of first ancestors in
(h-a,h]
has cardinality at least
e^{(1-\delta)h}
, thus completing the proof.

∎

3.
Spines, Lévy processes, and tails of hitting times

To describe the behaviour of the largest fragment in a self-similar fragmentation process
we will relate the log-sizes of the fragments to a time-changed branching Lévy process.
The large deviation estimates for Lévy processes are therefore a natural tool to analyse
the extremes, i.e. the size of the largest fragment. In this section we will describe the
connection between self-similar fragmentations and Lévy processes. The deviation estimates
for the time-changed process that we present at the end of this section will be utilized in
the proof of our main results.

3.1.
A tagged fragment as a Lévy process

We will now describe the spine construction appearing in
[
6
, Chapters 3.2.2 and 3.3.2]
.
Since this process is described in detail there, we will be content to give a brief verbal description.
Assume from now on that the fragmentation process in question starts with a single object of mass one,
i.e.
X(0)=\mathbf{1}=(1,0,\ldots)
. One may think that
X
describes the fragmentation of
the interval
[0,1)
into subintervals. More precisely
X_{i}(t)
is the size of the
i
th largest
subinterval present in the system at time
t>0
.
Let
U
be a random variable with a uniform distribution on
[0,1)
independent form
X
and let
Z_{t}

denote the size of the subinterval
I(t)
containing
U
at time
t
.
The process
Z_{t}
describes the evolution of the mass of the fragment which contains a point
tagged at random according to the mass distribution of the fragment; it will therefore often be
referred to as the process of the
tagged fragment
.
The usefulness of
Z_{t}
can be seen form the following many to one formula

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\geq 1}f(X_{i}(t))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{f(Z_{t})}{Z_{t}}\right]

valid for all measurable
f
with support bounded away form zero. It is therefore beneficial to
analyze
Z_{t}
in further detail.
Conditionally on
X
, for every
t>0
,
U
will have a uniform distribution on
I(t)
,
the Markov property suggest that for any
t,t^{\prime}>0
,

Z_{t+t^{\prime}}/Z_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z^{\prime}_{Z_{t}^{\alpha}t^{\prime}}

where
Z^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
Z
.
This therefore suggest that
Z
is an exponent of a time-changed Lévy process.
Now consider

\rho_{Z}(t)=\inf\left\{s\geq 0\,:\,\int_{0}^{s}Z_{r}^{\alpha}{\rm d}r>t\right\},\qquad t\geq 0.

Then,
\xi(t)=-\log Z_{\rho_{Z}(t)}
is a
Lévy process with
Lévy measure
\Lambda
such that for any measurable
f\colon(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
,

(3.2)

\int_{(0,+\infty)}f(x)\>\Lambda({\rm d}x)=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}f(-\log(s_{i}))\>\nu(\rm d\mathbf{s}),\qquad\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots).

More precisely,
\xi
has Laplace exponent given by

(3.3)

\Phi(q)=-\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-q\xi_{1}}\right]=\int_{(0,+\infty)}\left(1-e^{-qx}\right)\Lambda(\rm dx)=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\left(1-\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}^{q+1}\right)\>\nu({\rm d}\mathbf{s}),

for
q\geq 0
.
Note that if
\nu
is a finite measure, then
\xi
is a pure jump Lévy process;
we refer to
[
6
, Theorem 3.2 and 3.3]
for more details.
Naturally, one can recover
Z
from the Lévy process
\xi
by using the time change

\rho(t):=\inf\left\{u\geq 0\,:\,\int_{0}^{u}e^{\alpha\xi_{r}}\mathrm{d}r>t\right\},\qquad t\geq 0,

via
Z_{t}=\exp\{-\xi_{\rho(t)}\}
. We can thus recast (
3.1
) as

(3.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\geq 1}f(X_{i}(t))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\xi_{\rho(t)}}f\left(e^{-\xi_{\rho(t)}}\right)\right].

3.2.
Tail bounds for Lévy processes

In order to study the behaviour of the largest fragments at certain times,
it is natural to study the probability that a size-biased fragment does not decrease
too much in size over a certain period in time. Putting the time change to one side for a moment,
our goal in this section is to study the asymptotics of the quantity

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{t}\leq w)

for large
t
and small
w/t
.

The large-
t
asymptotics of the probabilities
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{t}\leq 1)
associated with a Lévy process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are intimately related to the upper tails of exponential functions
I:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi_{t}}\mathrm{d}t
of Lévy processes, for which there is a rich literature
[
16
,
21
]
. However, the exact asymptotics we require do not appear in this body of work, and thus we now state a pair of bespoke results on the asymptotics of these probabilities. Our proofs are based on tail bounds appearing in Jain and Pruitt
[
18
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Asymptotics for finite activity spine processes)
.

Suppose that
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Lévy process with Laplace exponent

\Phi(q)
given in (
3.3
), where
\nu
is a finite activity dislocation measure.
Then, for any
t\geq 0
and
w\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{t}\leq w)=\exp\left(-\lambda t+O(t^{\beta}w^{1-\beta})\right)

for some
\beta<1
.

Theorem 3.2

(Asymptotics for infinite activity spine processes)
.

Suppose that
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Lévy process with Laplace exponent

\Phi(q)
given in (
3.3
), and that the dislocation measure

\nu
satisfies (
1.4
) for some
\theta\in(0,1)

and slowly varying function
\ell
. Then, there is a bounded function
r(x)
satisfying

\lim_{x\downarrow 0}r(x)=0
such that

\displaystyle F(w,t)\leq-\log\mathbb{P}(\xi_{t}\leq w)\leq F(w,t)+cF(w,t)^{1/3},

where the function
F(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as

\displaystyle F(w,t)=L(t/w)t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}w^{-\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}

and
L(\cdot)
is the slowly varying function given by

\displaystyle L(s):=(1+r(s))\mathcal{C}_{3}(\theta)\ell\left(s^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell\left(s^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}.

Here
\mathcal{C}_{3}(\theta):=(1-\theta)\theta^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\Gamma(1-\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
.

The main external tool we invoke in proving Theorem
3.2

is the following result from Jain and Pruitt
[
18
]
.

Lemma 3.3

(Lemma 5.2 of
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose that
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Lévy process with Laplace
exponent
\Phi(q)
given in (
3.3
). For any

x\in\left(0,\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}]\right)
, let
q_{x}
be determined
by
\Phi^{\prime}(q_{x})=x
, where
\Phi^{\prime}
is the derivative of
\Phi
. Define
R(q):=\Phi(q)-q\Phi^{\prime}(q)
. Then

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{t}\leq tx)\leq e^{-tR(q_{x})}.

In addition, there are constants
c,t_{0},x_{0}>0
depending on
\Phi
,
such that for any
t\geq t_{0}
and
x\leq x_{0}
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{t}\leq tx)\geq e^{-tR(q_{x})-c(tR(q_{x}))^{1/3}}.

In (
3.6
), we have given a slightly adjusted statement of that given in
Lemma 5.2 of
[
18
]
. To obtain (
3.6
) from the statement there,
set
\varepsilon=(tR(q_{x}))^{-2/3}
.

With a view to using Lemma
3.3
in our setting with small
x
,
we need to understand the large
q
asymptotics of
\Phi(q)
and its derivative.
In this direction we have the following pair of results, which in accordance with
our later requirements, have different degrees of precision. The former result is
a blunter, qualitative result concerning the asymptotics of
\Phi
for finite activity
processes. The latter result makes a fine estimate of
\Phi
for infinite activity
processes satisfying (
1.4
).

Lemma 3.4

(
\Phi
asymptotics in finite activity case)
.

Let
\Phi(q)
be the Laplace exponent given in (
3.3
),
where
\nu
is a finite activity dislocation measure with total
mass
\lambda
and satisfying (
1.5
).
Then,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\Phi(q)=\lambda-f(q),

where
f(q)
is a smooth, decreasing, nonnegative function satisfying
f(q)=O(q^{-c/2})
.

Lemma 3.5

(
\Phi
asymptotics in infinite activity case)
.

Let
\Phi(q)
be the Laplace exponent given in (
3.3
)
such that the infinite dislocation measure
\nu
satisfies (
1.4
)
for some
\theta\in(0,1)
, i.e. there is a slowly varying at zero function
\ell
,
such that
\nu(\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}:1-s_{1}>\delta\})=\delta^{-\theta}\ell(1/\delta)
.
Then, there exist functions
\varepsilon_{1}(q)
and
\varepsilon_{2}(q)

tending to zero as
q\to\infty
such that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Phi(q)=(1+\varepsilon_{1}(q))\mathcal{C}_{1}(\theta)q^{\theta}\ell(q),

and

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(q)=(1+\varepsilon_{2}(q))\mathcal{C}_{2}(\theta)q^{\theta-1}\ell(q).

where
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\theta):=\Gamma(1-\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{-\theta}\mathrm{d}s
,
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\theta):=\theta\mathcal{C}_{1}(\theta)
.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

By virtue of (
3.3
) we have

\displaystyle\Phi(q)-\lambda=-\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}^{q+1}\>\nu({\rm d}\mathbf{s})=:-f(q).

Using
s_{1}\geq s_{i}
to obtain the first inequality below, and then the fact that
\nu
is supported on
\{\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}=1\}
to obtain the follow equality, we have

\displaystyle|f(q)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{1}^{q}s_{i}\>\nu({\rm d}\mathbf{s})\leq\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}s_{1}^{q}\>\nu({\rm d}\mathbf{s}).

In particular, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
we have
|f(q)|\leq\lambda(1-\delta)^{q}+\nu(\{s_{1}>1-\delta\})
. By (
1.5
) we have
|f(q)|\leq e^{-\delta q}+C\delta^{c}
. Setting
\delta=q^{-1/2}
we obtain
|f(q)|=O(q^{-c/2})
, as required.

∎

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

First we prove an upper bound for
\Phi(q)
. Plainly

\Phi(q)\leq\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\left(1-s_{1}^{q+1}\right)\nu(\rm d\mathbf{s}).

To analyze the integral on the right hand side just integrate by parts to get

\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\left(1-s_{1}^{q+1}\right)\nu(\rm d\mathbf{s})=\int_{0}^{1}(q+1)t^{q}\nu(s_{1}<t)\rm dt=\int_{0}^{1}(q+1)(1-t)^{q}t^{-\theta}\ell(1/t)\rm dt.

We write the last integral as

q^{\theta}\frac{q+1}{q}\ell\left(1/q\right)\int_{0}^{q}\left(1-\frac{s}{q}\right)^{q}s^{-\theta}\frac{\ell(qs)}{\ell(q)}\rm ds.

By an appeal to Potter bounds
[
8
, Theorem 1.5.6(i)]

for sufficiently large
q
,

\ell(qs)/\ell(q)\leq 2s^{-(1-\theta)/2}

for all
s\in(0,1)
. Using a simple estimate
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for
x\in(0,1)
we can appeal to
the dominated convergence theorem and slow variation of
\ell

to show that the integral in question tends to
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\theta)
as
q\to\infty
.
This concludes a proof of a upper bound for
\Phi(q)
.

As for the lower bound for
\Phi(q)
, write

\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\left(1-\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}^{q+1}\right)\nu(\rm d\mathbf{s})\geq\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\left(1-s_{1}^{q+1}-(1-s_{1})^{q+1}\right)\nu(\rm d\mathbf{s}),

using the fact that
s_{2}^{q+1}+s_{3}^{q+1}+\ldots\leq(1-s_{1})^{q+1}
whenever

\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}\leq 1
.
We have already checked that the integral of
1-s_{1}^{q+1}
has the desired asymptotic.
To conclude the proof it suffices to check that
the integral

\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}(1-s_{1})^{q+1}\nu(\rm d\mathbf{s})=(q+1)\int_{0}^{1}t^{q-\theta}\ell(t)\rm dt

is bounded for
q\geq 1
. This is indeed true, since by yet another appeal to
Potter bounds
[
8
, Theorem 1.5.6(ii)]
,

\ell(t)\leq{\rm C}/t
for some positive constant
{\rm C}
and all
t\in(0,1)
.
Thus the last integral is bounded above via

C(q+1)/(q-\theta)
. We obtain (
3.8
).

We turn to examining
\Phi^{\prime}(q)
. Note that by (
3.2
) and (
3.3
),
we have

\Phi^{\prime}(q)=\frac{\rm d}{\rm dq}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\left(1-\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}^{q}\right)\nu(\rm d\mathbf{s})=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm m}}\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}^{q+1}\log(1/s_{i})\nu(\rm d\mathbf{s}).

Since
\Phi^{\prime}
is monotone, we can use the already established asymptotic of
\Phi

in combination with the monotone density Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.7.2]

to get (
3.9
).
∎

We are now equipped to prove Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

By (
3.7
),
\Phi^{\prime}(q)=-f^{\prime}(q)
, where
f(q)
is a
smooth function decaying to zero as
q\to\infty
,
either exponentially or polynomially quickly. In particular, the solution

q_{x}
to
\Phi^{\prime}(q)=-x
has as
x\downarrow 0
,

\displaystyle q_{x}=(1+o(1))\log(1/x)\qquad\text{or}\qquad q_{x}\sim x^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}(1+o(1))}

for some
\beta
. A calculation tells us that for

x\downarrow 0
we have

\displaystyle R(q_{x})=\lambda-O(x\log(1/x))\qquad\text{or}\qquad R(q_{x})=\lambda-O\left(x^{\beta^{\prime}}\right).

in the latter case for some
\beta^{\prime}
in
(0,1)
. Either way,

R(q_{x})=\lambda-O\left(x^{\beta^{\prime}}\right)
for some
\beta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
.
Setting

, we obtain the result by using Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Before the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we need to introduce a few notions from the theory of slowly varying functions
[
8
]
.
Given a slowly varying function
L
, there exists a slowly varying function
L^{\#}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
known as a
de Bruijn conjugate
, that satisfies the relation

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}L^{\#}(x)L(xL(x))=1.

The following result is from
[
8
]
.

Proposition 3.6

(Proposition 1.5.15 of
[
8
]
)
.

Let
a,b>0
. Let
L
be slowly varying at infinity and let
L^{\#}
be its de Bruijn conjugate. Suppose we have a bijective function
f:[x_{0},\infty)\to[y_{0},\infty)
of the form

\displaystyle f(x)=x^{ab}L(x^{b})^{a}.

Then the inverse function
g(x)
of
f(x)
satisfies

\displaystyle g(x)\sim x^{\frac{1}{ab}}L^{\#}(x^{1/a})^{1/b},

where
L^{\#}
is a de Bruijn conjugate.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Note that with
R(q)=\Phi(q)-q\Phi^{\prime}(q)
as in
the setting of Lemma
3.3
, using Lemma
3.5
we may write

\displaystyle R(q)=(1+\varepsilon_{3}(q))\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)q\Phi^{\prime}(q)\qquad q\to\infty.

Accordingly, with
q_{x}
the chosen so that
\Phi^{\prime}(q_{x})=x

as in the statement of Lemma
3.3
, we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle R(q_{x})=(1+\varepsilon_{3}(q_{x}))\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)xq_{x}\qquad\qquad x\downarrow 0,

where we note that
x\downarrow 0
entails
q_{x}\to\infty
.

It remains to appraise the asymptotics of
q_{x}
as
x\downarrow 0
.
According to (
3.9
) we have

\displaystyle x=(1+\varepsilon_{2}(q_{x}))\mathcal{C}_{2}(\theta)q_{x}^{\theta-1}\ell(q_{x})=q_{x}^{\theta-1}L_{1}(q_{x}),

where

(3.11)

\displaystyle L_{1}(y):=(1+\varepsilon_{2}(y))C_{2}(\theta)\ell(y)

is slowly varying at infinity. Let
L_{2}(y):=L_{1}(y)^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
. Using Proposition
3.6
with
a=-\frac{1}{1-\theta}
and

we have

\displaystyle q_{x}=x^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}L_{2}^{\#}\left(x^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right).

We now seek to unravel
L_{2}^{\#}
in terms of our original slowly varying function
\ell
. Using the definition of
L_{2}^{\#}
to obtain the the first relation below, the
L_{2}(y):=L_{1}(y)^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
to obtain the second, rearranging to obtain the third, using (

) to obtain the fifth, and then further rearranging and using the fact that

is slowly varying to obtain the sixth, we have

\displaystyle L_{2}^{\#}(y)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{L_{2}(yL_{2}(y))}=\frac{1}{L_{1}(yL_{1}(y)^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}})^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}}=L_{1}(yL_{1}(y)^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}

\displaystyle\sim C_{2}(\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell\left(yC_{2}(\theta)^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell(y)^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\sim C_{2}(\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell\left(y\ell(y)^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}.

In particular, plugging in

we obtain

\displaystyle q_{x}=(1+\varepsilon_{4}(x))C_{2}(\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell\left(x^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell\left(x^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}x^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}},

where
\varepsilon_{4}(x)\to 0
as

. Now using (
3.10
) it follows that we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle R(q_{x})=L(x)x^{-\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}},

where, as in the statement of Theorem
3.2
we have

\displaystyle L(x):=(1+\varepsilon_{5}(x))\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)C_{2}(\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell\left(x^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell\left(x^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}.

Recall from the statement of Lemma
3.5
that
C_{2}(\theta)=\theta\Gamma(1-\theta)
, so that a brief calculation tells us that
(1/\theta-1)C_{2}(\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}=(1-\theta)\theta^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\Gamma(1-\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}=C_{3}(\theta)
, as in the statement of Theorem
3.2
.

Now by (

) and (

) we have

\displaystyle tR(q_{x})\leq-\log\mathbb{P}(\xi_{t}\leq tx)\leq tR(q_{x})+c(tR(q_{x}))^{1/3}

for some constant

.
Replacing

with

, using (

∎

4.
Upper bounds for expected numbers of particles

4.1.
A slight change in perspective

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a fragmentation process
with dislocation measure given by
\nu
.
In the remainder of the paper, we will define the following functions.
When
\nu
has finite total mass
\lambda
, we consider the function

\displaystyle F_{0}(h):=he^{\alpha h}/\lambda.

When
\nu
is infinite with crumbling index
\theta

and slowing variance function
\ell
, we associate the function

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(h):=(\theta/\alpha)^{\theta}h^{1-\theta}e^{\alpha h}L(h^{1-\theta})^{-(1-\theta)},

where as in the statement of Theorem
3.2
,

L
is defined through
\ell
via

\displaystyle L(s)=(1+r(1/s))(1/\theta-1)(\theta\Gamma(1-\theta))^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\ell(s^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}},

for some function
r
tending to zero as
x
goes to zero.

The motivation for these functions is as follows.
One may verify that if
F^{-1}_{0}
and
F^{-1}_{\theta}
are the
inverse functions of
F_{0}
and
F_{\theta}
, then with
g(t)
as in the
statement of Theorem
1.2
we have

\displaystyle F^{-1}_{0}(t)=g(t)+O(\log\log t/\log t),

and with
g(t)
as in the statement of Theorem
1.3
, we have

\displaystyle F^{-1}_{\theta}(t)=g(t)+O(\log\log t/\log t).

Thus we are instead at liberty to study the asymptotics of the

inverse function
of the stochastic process

(m_{t})_{t\geq 0}
capturing the size
e^{-m_{t}}
of the largest fragment.
In other words, we are interested in tackling the problem of, given a large
h
,
finding the largest times
t(h)
for which there exists a fragment of size
greater than
e^{-h}
. What we find in the finite activity case is
that with high probability the last fragment of size greater than
e^{-h}
existing
in the process last existed at a time between
(1-\delta)F_{0}(h)
and
(1+\delta)F_{0}(h)
. The analogous result holds in the infinite activity case with
F_{\theta}(h)
in place of
F_{0}(h)
.

This motivates our work in the next two sections. In the remainder of
Section
4
we prove that if
t\geq(1+\delta)F(h)
(with
F=F_{0}
or
F=F_{\theta}
),
the expected number of fragments of size greater than
e^{-h}
at time
t
decays
exponentially in
h
. (Section
5
is dedicated to a corresponding lower bound.) Define

\displaystyle E(t,h):=\mathbb{E}\left[\#\{\text{Fragments at time $t$ of size at least $e^{-h}$}\}\right].

Our chief task in this section is to prove the following pair of results.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
F_{0}(h)=he^{\alpha h}/\lambda
be associated with a
finite activity fragmentation process. Then for every
\delta>0

there exists
c_{\delta}>0
and
h_{\delta}
such that whenever
h\geq h_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle E((1+\delta)F_{0}(h),h)\leq e^{-c_{\delta}h}.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
F_{\theta}(h):=(\theta/\alpha)^{\theta}h^{1-\theta}e^{\alpha h}L(h^{1-\theta})^{-(1-\theta)}

be associated with an infinite activity fragmentation process
with crumbling index
\theta
. Then, for every
\delta>0
there exists

c_{\delta}>0
and
h_{\delta}
such that whenever
h\geq h_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle E((1+\delta)F_{\theta}(h),h)\leq e^{-c_{\delta}h}.

4.2.
A general tool

We now develop a general upper bound for
E(t,h)
that we will exploit in the
proofs of both Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
.
Setting
f(x)=\mathrm{1}_{\{x\geq e^{-h}\}}
in the
many-to-one formula (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle E(t,h):=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\xi_{\rho(t)}}\mathrm{1}_{\{\xi_{\rho(t)}\leq h\}}\right]\leq e^{h}\mathbb{P}[\xi_{\rho(t)}\leq h].

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Then there exists
h_{0}(\delta)
and
s_{0}(\delta)
such that whenever

h\geq h_{0}
and
s:=te^{-\alpha h}\geq(\log h)^{2}\wedge s_{0}(\delta)
, we have

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq Ce^{-h}+e^{(1+\delta)h}J((1-\delta)s,\varepsilon),

where

\displaystyle J(u,\varepsilon):=\sup\left\{\prod_{i\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(\tau^{(\varepsilon)}>\varepsilon y_{i})\,:\,(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}\mbox{ such that }\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 1}y_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq u\right\}

and with
\tau^{(\varepsilon)}\overset{(d)}{=}\inf\{t>0\,:\,\xi_{t}\geq\varepsilon\}
.

In the course of proving Proposition
4.3
we will require the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
A_{0},\ldots,A_{N-1}
be independent non-negative random variables. Then, for
s>N
,

\mathbb{P}(A_{0}+\ldots+A_{N-1}>s,A_{i}\leq s\mbox{ for every }i=0,\ldots,N-1)\\
\leq s^{N}\sup\left\{\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}>y_{i})\,:\,(y_{0},\ldots,y_{N-1})\in[0,\infty)^{N}\mbox{such that }\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}y_{i}>s-N\right\}.

Proof.

Letting
\lfloor A_{i}\rfloor
denote the integer part of
A_{i}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}A_{i}>s,A_{i}\leq s,\forall i=0,\ldots,N-1\right)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\lfloor A_{i}\rfloor>s-N,A_{i}\leq s,\forall i=0,\ldots,N-1\right)\\
\\
\leq\sum_{(a_{0},\ldots,a_{N-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{N}:a_{i}\leq s,\ s-N<\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}a_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}>a_{i}).

The result follows by bounding the sum using its supremum,
and extending the range of the supremum from integers
a_{i}\in[0,s)
to reals
y_{i}\in[0,\infty)
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

For
i\geq 0
define the random variable

\displaystyle X_{i}=X_{i}(h,\varepsilon):=\mathrm{Leb}\{t:\xi_{\rho(t)}\in(h-(i+1)\varepsilon,h-i\varepsilon)\}

to be the amount of time spent by the time-changed Lévy
process
(\xi_{\rho(t)})_{t\geq 0}
in the interval
[h-(i+1)\varepsilon,h-i\varepsilon)
.
With a large integer
N\geq 1
to be determined later, we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{Leb}\{t\,:\,\xi_{\rho(t)}<h\}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}+Y_{N}

where

\displaystyle Y_{N}:=\mathrm{Leb}\{t:\xi_{\rho(t)}<h-N\varepsilon\}.

Also,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho(t)}\leq h)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}X_{i}(h,\varepsilon)+Y_{N}>t\right).

Recall
\tau^{(\varepsilon)}=\inf\{t>0:\xi_{t}\geq\varepsilon\}.

While
\xi_{\rho(t)}
is contained inside the interval

[h-(i+1)\varepsilon,h-i\varepsilon)
, it behaves like the Lévy process

(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
but with its clock slowed down,
though the clock runs at least as fast as

e^{\alpha(h-i\varepsilon)}
. In particular, we have the stochastic domination

\displaystyle X_{i}\leq^{(d)}e^{\alpha(h-i\varepsilon)}\tau^{(\varepsilon)}.

In other words, for all
w\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{i}>w)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(e^{\alpha(h-i\varepsilon)}\tau^{(\varepsilon)}>w\right).

Also, we have the stochastic domination

\displaystyle Y_{N}\leq^{(d)}e^{\alpha(h-N\varepsilon)}\tau^{(h-N\varepsilon)}\leq^{(d)}e^{\alpha(h-N\varepsilon)}\tau^{(h)}.

More generally, while the
(X_{i})_{i\geq 0}
and
Y_{N}
are not independent random variables,
if
(\tau^{(\varepsilon)}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are independent random variables
identically distributed like
\tau^{(\varepsilon)}
; and
\tau^{(h)}
is a further
independent random variable distributed like the hitting time of
h
,
we have the stochastic domination of sums

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}X_{i}(h,\varepsilon)+Y_{N}\leq^{(d)}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{\alpha(h-i\varepsilon)}\tau_{i}+e^{\alpha(h-N\varepsilon)}\tau^{(h)}.

In summary, it follows that with
(\tau_{i})_{i\geq 0}

i.i.d. with
\tau_{i}\sim\tau^{(\varepsilon)}
we have

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq e^{h}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{\alpha(h-i\varepsilon)}\tau_{i}+e^{\alpha(h-N\varepsilon)}\tau^{(h)}>t\right).

Writing
t=e^{\alpha h}s
we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq e^{h}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}+e^{-\alpha N\varepsilon}\tau^{(h)}>s\right).

In particular, since

(4.2)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}+e^{-\alpha N\varepsilon}\tau^{(h)}>s\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(\tau^{(h)}>h^{2})+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>s-e^{-\alpha N\varepsilon}h^{2}\right),

by writing
s_{1}:=s-e^{-\alpha N\varepsilon}h^{2}
and using Lemma
3.7
, we have

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq Ce^{-h}+e^{h}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>s_{1}\right)

Take
N\geq\lceil c(\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta)\log h\rceil

for some sufficiently large constant
c(\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta)
.
Then whenever
s\geq 1
, we have
s_{1}\geq s_{2}:=(1-\delta/2)s
.

We now seek better control over the sum in (
4.2
). By the union bound, we have

(4.3)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>s_{2}\right)\leq\\
\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}\left(e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>s_{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>s_{2},e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}\leq s_{2}~\forall i=0,\ldots,N-1\right)\\
\leq N\mathbb{P}\left(\tau^{(\varepsilon)}>s_{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>s_{2},e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}\leq s_{2}~\forall i=0,\ldots,N-1\right).

Now we note that
N=O(\log h)\leq e^{\delta h}/2

for all sufficiently large
h
, and
\mathbb{P}(\tau^{(\varepsilon)}>s_{2})\leq J(s_{2},\varepsilon)

(this follows by restricting to the case where
y_{0}=s_{2}/\varepsilon
, and
y_{i}=0
for all
i\geq 1

in the definition of
J(s_{2},\varepsilon)
). Thus, by definition of
N
and
s_{2}
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle N\mathbb{P}\left(\tau^{(\varepsilon)}>s_{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{\delta h}J((1-\delta/2)s,\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{\delta h}J((1-\delta)s,\varepsilon)

for all sufficiently large
h
.

As for the sum on the right-hand side of (
4.3
),
by applying Lemma
4.4
with

A_{i}=e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>s_{2},e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}\leq s_{2}~\forall i=0,\ldots,N-1\right)\\
\leq s_{2}^{N}\sup\left\{\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}(e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>y_{i})\,:\,(y_{0},\ldots,y_{N-1})\in[0,\infty)^{N},\>\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}y_{i}>s_{2}-N\right\}.

Now provided
s
is sufficiently large, i.e.
s>(\log h)^{2}
, since
N=O(\log h)

we have
s_{2}-N=s(1-\delta/2)-N\geq(1-\delta)s
.
Replacing
y_{i}
with
y_{i}e^{\alpha i\varepsilon}/\varepsilon
, it thus follows that

(4.5)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}>s_{2},e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\tau_{i}\leq s_{2}~\forall i=0,\ldots,N-1\right)\\
\leq s^{N}J((1-\delta)s,\varepsilon)\leq e^{\delta h}J((1-\delta)s,\varepsilon)/2

for all sufficiently large
h
. Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) in (
4.3
),
and then finally in (
4.1
), we obtain the result.
∎

4.3.
Proof of Proposition
4.1

The proof of Proposition
4.1
is
now
reasonably straightforward.
For the purposes of proving Proposition
4.1
, we will only
require the
\varepsilon=1
case of Proposition
4.3
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Let
\delta>0
. According to the case
\varepsilon=1
of
Proposition
4.3
there exists
h_{0}(\delta)
and
s_{0}(\delta)

such that whenever
h\geq h_{0}
and

s:=te^{-\alpha h}\geq(\log h)^{2}\wedge s_{0}(\delta)
, we have

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq Ce^{-h}+e^{(1+\delta)h}J((1-\delta)s,1).

where

\displaystyle J(u,1)=\sup\left\{\prod_{i\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(\tau^{(1)}>y_{i})\,:\,(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}\mbox{ such that }\sum_{i\geq 1}y_{i}e^{-\alpha i}\geq u\right\}

where
\tau^{(1)}\overset{(d)}{=}\inf\{t>0:\xi_{t}\geq 1\}
.
Now according to Theorem
3.1
,
we have
J(u,1)\leq e^{-K(u,1)}
where

\displaystyle K(u,1):=\inf\left\{\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(\lambda y_{i}-Cy_{i}^{\beta}\right)\,:\,(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}\mbox{ such that }\sum_{i\geq 0}y_{i}e^{-\alpha i}\geq u\right\}.

for some
C>0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
.

We now claim that for large
u
,
K(u,1)=\lambda u-O(u^{\beta})
.
To see this, one may use Lagrange multipliers: for a free parameter
\mu

we look at simultaneous solutions to the equations

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\sum_{j\geq 0}\left(\lambda y_{j}-Cy_{j}^{\beta}\right)-\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\sum_{j\geq 0}y_{j}e^{-\alpha j},

ultimately finding that the minimiser takes the form

\displaystyle y_{i}=\left(\frac{\beta C}{\lambda-\mu e^{-\alpha i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}},

for some
\mu<\lambda
. Note that

y_{0}=y_{0}^{\mu}:=\left(\frac{\beta C}{\lambda-\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}
.
Moreover, for all
\mu<\lambda
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 0}y_{i}e^{-\alpha i}\leq y_{0}^{\mu}+\left(\frac{\beta C}{\lambda-\mu e^{-\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}\leq y_{0}^{\mu}+C(\beta,\alpha,\lambda).

In particular, given that the minimiser
(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)
satisfies

\sum_{i\geq 0}y_{i}e^{-\alpha i}\geq u
, its first coordinate must satisfy

y_{0}=\lambda u-O(1)
, so that
K(u,1)\geq\lambda u-O(u^{\beta})
.
Moreover, by considering
y_{0}=u,y_{i}=0
for
i\geq 1
, we get

K(u,1)=\lambda u-O(u^{\beta})
for large
u
.

By Proposition
4.3
and the fact that

J(u,1)\leq e^{-\lambda u+Cu^{\beta}}
, there exist
h_{0}(\delta),s_{0}(\delta)

such that for all sufficiently large
h
and
s
we have

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq Ce^{-h}+e^{(1+\delta)h-\lambda(1-\delta)s+Cs^{\beta}}

where
s=te^{-\alpha h}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle E((1+2\delta)F_{0}(h),h)\leq e^{-\delta h}.

Replacing
2\delta
with
\delta
, that completes the proof.
∎

4.4.
Infinite activity case

In this section we work towards proving Proposition
4.2
,
which is an upper bound for the expected number of large particles in
infinite activity fragmentation processes.
We search for a good upper bound on
E(t,h)
in the infinite activity case.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
We now use Theorem
3.2
to control the term
J(u,\varepsilon)
occurring in
the setting of Proposition
4.3
. Here we have
J(u,\varepsilon)\leq e^{-K(u,\varepsilon)}
where

(4.6)

\displaystyle K(u,\varepsilon):=\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}L(y_{i})y_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\,:\,(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}},\>\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}y_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq u\right\},

where we recall from the statement of Theorem
3.2
that
L(\cdot)
is
a slowly varying function at infinity.
Before giving our main result on the asymptotics of
K(u,\varepsilon)
, we present the following
lemma which describes a minimisation problem that provides a proxy for the asymptotics
of
K(u,\varepsilon)
after correct normalisation for the terms depending on
u
.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have

C(\theta,\alpha,\varepsilon):=\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}:(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\mbox{ such that }\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1\right\}\\
=\left(\frac{1-e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon/\theta}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}.

Moreover, the coordinates of the sequence

(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
attaining the minimum take the form

\displaystyle z_{i}=\frac{1-e^{-\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}}{\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon(1/\theta-1)}.

Proof.

This follows from a simple Lagrange multipliers calculation,
solving

0=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}\left(\sum_{i\geq 0}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}-\mu\sum_{i\geq 0}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\right)

for a given parameter
\mu
, and using the fact
that the minimiser
(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)
satisfies

\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1
.
∎

In order to relate the asymptotics of
K(u,\varepsilon)
to the variational
problem in Lemma
4.5
, we will require the following technical lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f_{n}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a sequence of functions
such that there exist a constant
C
so that for each
n
,

f_{n}
is bounded on
[0,1]
, and

f_{n}(x)\geq Cx^{-\delta_{n}}
for
x\geq 1
, for some
sequence
\delta_{n}\to 0
.
Suppose further that
f_{n}(x)\to 1
, uniformly for
x
in
compact subsets
[a,A]
of
(0,\infty)
. Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1}\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}f_{n}(z_{i})\geq C(\theta,\alpha,\varepsilon),

where
C(\theta,\alpha,\varepsilon)
is given in Lemma
4.5
.

Proof.

Let

J_{n}:=\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}f_{n}(z_{i}):(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}},\>\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1\right\}.

First we claim that there exists
A>0
and
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,
we may restrict our intention to the case where the infimum is attained by
sequences with coordinates
z_{i}
taking values in a compact interval

[0,A]
. To see this, note that since, for
x\geq 1
,

f_{n}(x)\geq Cx^{-\delta_{n}}
for some sequence

(\delta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
\delta_{n}\to 0
, it follows that
for all sufficiently large
n
,

z^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}f_{n}(z)\geq z^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
.
In particular, if some
z_{i}>A
, we have

\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}f_{n}(z_{i})>\ \varepsilon A^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
. Thus

\displaystyle J_{n}=I_{n}\wedge\varepsilon A^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{1-\theta}}.

where

(4.7)

\displaystyle I_{n}=I_{n}(A):=\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}f_{n}(z_{i}):(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,A]^{\mathbb{N}}.\>\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1\right\}.

Choose
A
sufficiently large so that

A^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{1-\theta}}>C(\theta,\alpha,\varepsilon)
.
It remains to examine
I_{n}=I_{n}(A)
for such
A
.
Now let
a\in(0,1)
be small, and consider the contribution to the sum

\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}

from terms with
z_{i}<a
and from terms with
z_{i}\geq a
.
Plainly we have

\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}<a\}}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon a\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}=\tilde{C}a

where
\tilde{C}=\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}
.
In particular it follows that for small
a
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1-\tilde{C}a.

Thus picking up from (
4.7
), we have

(4.8)

I_{n}\geq\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}f_{n}(z_{i}):(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,A]^{\mathbb{N}},\>\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1-\tilde{C}a\right\}\\
\geq\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}f_{n}(z_{i}):(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,A]^{\mathbb{N}},\>\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1-\tilde{C}a\right\}

Moreover, since the sequence of functions
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converge
uniformly to
1
on the compact set
[a,A]
, for all
a
there exists

n_{0}(a)
such that whenever
n\geq n_{0}(a)
,

f_{n}(x)\geq 1-a
for all
x\in[a,A]
.
Using this fact in (
4.8
) to obtain the first inequality below,
and then rescaling the
z_{i}
with
z_{i}/(1-\tilde{C}a)
,
to obtain the following equality, for all
n\geq n_{0}(a)
we have

(4.9)

I_{n}/(1-a)\geq\\
\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}}\,:\,(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,A]^{\mathbb{N}},\>\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1-\tilde{C}a\right\}\\
=(1-\tilde{C}a)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\,:\,(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,A]^{\mathbb{N}},\>\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1\right\}.

Finally, we note that

(4.10)

\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}}\,:\,(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,A]^{\mathbb{N}}\mbox{ with }\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathrm{1}_{\{z_{i}\geq a\}}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1\right\}\\
=\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\,:\,(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in\left(\{0\}\cup[a,A]\right)^{\mathbb{N}}\mbox{ with }\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1\right\}\\
\geq\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\,:\,(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}\mbox{ with }\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1\right\}=C(\alpha,\theta,\varepsilon).

Thus by (
4.9
) and (
4.10
),
given
a>0
, for all
n\geq n_{0}(a)
we have

\displaystyle I_{n}\geq(1-a)(1-\tilde{C}a)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}C(\theta,\alpha,\varepsilon).

Since
a>0
was arbitrary, that completes the proof.
∎

We now use the previous lemma to describe the asymptotics of
K(u,\varepsilon)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

For all
\delta>0
there exists
u_{\delta}

such that whenever
u\geq u_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle K(u,\varepsilon)\geq(1-\delta)L(u)u^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}C(\theta,\alpha,\varepsilon).

Proof.

Note that
K(u,\varepsilon)
may be written

\displaystyle K(u,\varepsilon):=L(u)u^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\inf\left\{\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}f_{u}(z_{i})z_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\,:\,(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}},\>\varepsilon\sum_{i\geq 0}z_{i}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon}\geq 1\right\},

where

\displaystyle f_{u}(z)=L(uz)/L(u).

Since
L(\cdot)
is a slowly varying function,

f_{u}
converges pointwise to
1
.
Moreover, by the uniform convergence theorem
(
[
8
, Section 1.2]
), this convergence is
uniform on compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
. It is also possible to show
using Karamata’s representation theorem
[
8
, Section 1.3]
or Potter bounds
that
f_{u}(z)\geq Cz^{o_{u}(1)}
for
z\geq 1
.
Proposition
4.2
now follows
from applying the previous lemma to
(f_{u})
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let
\delta>0
.
By Proposition
4.3
, for all sufficiently large

s=te^{-\alpha h}
and
h
we have

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq Ce^{-h}+e^{(1+\delta)h}J((1-\delta)s,\varepsilon).

Now as noted above, by Theorem
3.2
we
have
J(u,\varepsilon)\leq e^{-K(u,\varepsilon)}
where

K(u,\varepsilon)
is as in (
4.6
).
Now by Lemma
4.7
for all
s
sufficiently large we have

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq Ce^{-h}+\exp\left\{(1+\delta)h-(1-\delta)L(s)s^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}C(\theta,\alpha,\varepsilon)\right\}.

Note that

\displaystyle C(\theta,\alpha,\varepsilon)=\left(\frac{1-e^{-\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\to(\alpha/\theta)^{\theta/(1-\theta)},\qquad\mbox{ as }\varepsilon\rightarrow 0,

in an increasing way. Thus, we may choose
\varepsilon
sufficiently
small so that
C(\alpha,\theta,\varepsilon)\geq(1-\delta)(\alpha/\theta)^{\theta/(1-\theta)}
. Then

\displaystyle E(t,h)\leq Ce^{-h}+\exp\left\{(1+\delta)h-(1-\delta)^{2}L(s)s^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(\alpha/\theta)^{\theta/(1-\theta)}\right\}.

After a brief calculation, one may check that after
choosing
c=c_{\alpha,\theta}
sufficiently large,

E((1+c\delta)F_{\theta}(h),h)\leq e^{-\delta h}
.
Replacing
c\delta
with
\delta
, we obtain Proposition
4.2
as written.
∎

5.
Lower bounds for existence of large particles

In this section it will be useful to introduce the following notation. Let

\displaystyle D(t,h):=\mathbb{P}\left(\text{At time $t$ there are no fragments of size greater than $e^{-h}$}\right).

Over the course of this section, we prove the following pair of results,
which complement Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
F_{0}(h)=he^{\alpha h}/\lambda
be associated with a finite
activity fragmentation process with total mass
\lambda
.
Then for every
\delta>0
there exists
c_{\delta}>0
and
h_{\delta}

such that whenever
h\geq h_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle D((1-\delta)F_{0}(h),h)\leq e^{-c_{\delta}h}.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
F_{\theta}(h):=(\theta/\alpha)^{\theta}h^{1-\theta}e^{\alpha h}L(h^{1-\theta})^{-(1-\theta)}

be associated with an infinite activity
fragmentation process with crumbling index
\theta
.
Then for every
\delta>0
there exists
c_{\delta}>0
and

h_{\delta}
such that whenever
h\geq h_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle D((1-\delta)F_{\theta}(h),h)\leq e^{-c_{\delta}h}.

In the proofs of both Proposition
5.1
and
Proposition
5.2
, we will use tools
developed in Section
2
.
Recall that in the context of Section
2.4
that
we say that a collection of fragments existing at various
points of time is
independent
if no fragment is ancestor
to any other fragment in the collection. We prove in that
Section
2.4
that when
h
is large, one can choose a
large independent set of fragments of sizes between
e^{-h}
and

e^{-(h-\varepsilon)}
. Independent fragments of course behave independently.

5.1.
Proof of Proposition
5.1

The proof of Proposition
5.1
is reasonably straightforward
using the tools we have developed thus far:

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Setting
\delta=a=\varepsilon
in Theorem
2.3
, we see that there
exists a random
h_{0}(\varepsilon)
such that for all

h\geq h_{0}(\varepsilon)
, there is an antichain of cardinality
at least
e^{(1-\varepsilon)h}
such that each fragment in this set has
size between
e^{-h}
and
e^{-(h-\varepsilon)}
. Given a fragment
in this set, the probability it undergoes no fragmentation
for a period of time
t
is at least

e^{-\lambda e^{-\alpha(h-\varepsilon)}t}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle 1\geq\limsup_{h\to\infty}D(t,h)\left(1-e^{-\lambda e^{-\alpha(h-\varepsilon)}t}\right)^{-e^{(1-\varepsilon)h}}\geq\limsup_{h\to\infty}D(t,h)\exp\left\{e^{(1-\varepsilon)h-\lambda e^{-\alpha(h-\varepsilon)}t}\right\}.

Let
t=(1-3(1+\alpha)\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\lambda}he^{\alpha h}
.
Then for all
h
large enough, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Every fragment at time $t$ is smaller than $e^{-h}$ }\right)\leq\exp(-\varepsilon h).

Setting
\delta:=3(1+\alpha)\varepsilon
, we complete the proof.

∎

5.2.
Proof of Proposition
5.2

The proof of Proposition
5.2
is substantially more
delicate than its finite activity counterpart, Proposition
5.1
.
The main idea is that we construct an event under which the time-changed
Lévy process
(\xi_{\rho(t)})_{t\geq 0}
takes a path which leads to a large particle at time
t
.

Let
t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1}>0
be a collection of times.
Define
q_{0}:=0
and
q_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{N-j}
, so that
q_{j}-q_{j-1}=t_{N-j}

for
1\leq j\leq N
. We begin by observing that since
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
Markov process with stationary increments, for any starting position
-x
with
x\in[0,\varepsilon]
we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{-x}\left(\xi_{q_{j}}\in[(j-1)\varepsilon,j\varepsilon]~\text{for all $j=1,\ldots,N$}\right)\geq\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}f_{\varepsilon}(t_{i}),

where

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(t):=\inf_{u\in[0,1]}\mathbb{P}_{-u\varepsilon}(\xi_{t}\in[0,\varepsilon])=\inf_{u\in[0,1]}\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}(\xi_{t}\in[0,(1+u)\varepsilon])-\mathbb{P}_{0}(\xi_{t}\in[0,u\varepsilon])\right).

Our next result provides a lower bound for
f_{\varepsilon}(t)
using Theorem
3.2
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For every
\delta>0
there exists a

t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
such that whenever
t\geq t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq\exp\left(-(1+\delta)L(t)t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\varepsilon^{-\theta/(1-\theta)}\right).

Proof.

For large
t
, we now use Theorem
3.2
to bound

f_{\varepsilon}(t)
from below. We consider

F(w,t)=L(t/w)t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}w^{-\theta/(1-\theta)}

as in the statement of Theorem
3.2
, by using both the
upper and lower bound in Theorem
3.2
we have

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq\inf_{u\in[0,1]}\left(e^{-A_{t}(1+u)-cA_{t}(1+u)^{1/3}}-e^{-A_{t}(u)}\right),

where we are using the shorthand
A_{t}(p):=F(p\varepsilon,t)
. Now

\displaystyle A_{t}(1+u)=t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\varepsilon^{-\theta/(1-\theta)}L(t/(1+u)\varepsilon)(1+u)^{-\theta/(1-\theta)}.

Note that
L(st)/L(t)
converges uniformly, for

s\in[1/\varepsilon,2/\varepsilon]
, to
1
as
t\to\infty
.
In particular, for
A_{t}(1+u)
converges to infinity at a speed uniformly
bounded below for
u\in[0,1]
. Choose
\delta>0
arbitrarily.
Now choose
t=t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
so that

A_{t}(1+u)+cA_{t}(1+u)^{1/3}\leq(1+\delta)A_{t}(1+u)
. Then we have

(5.2)

f_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq\inf_{u\in[0,1]}e^{-(1+\delta)A_{t}(1+u)}\inf_{u\in[0,1]}(1-e^{-B_{t}(u)})\\
\geq\inf_{u\in[0,1]}e^{-(1+\delta)A_{t}(1+u)}(1-e^{-\inf_{u\in[0,1]}B_{t}(u)}),

where
B_{t}(u):=A_{t}(u)-(1+\delta)A_{t}(1+u)
.
Now

\displaystyle B_{t}(u)=t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\varepsilon^{-\theta/(1-\theta)}\left(L(t/u\varepsilon)u^{-\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}-(1+\delta)L(t/(1+u)\varepsilon)(1+u)^{-\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\right).

by the uniform convergence theorem
[
8
, Section1.2]
, there exists

t_{\delta}>0
such that for all
u\in[0,1]
,

L(t/(1+u)\varepsilon)\leq(1+\delta)L(t)
.
Moreover, by Karamata’s representation theorem
[
8
, Section 1.3]
,
for every
\kappa>0
, there exists
t_{\kappa}
such that for all

t\geq t_{\kappa}
,
L(st)/L(t)\geq s^{-\kappa}
for
s\geq 1/\varepsilon
.
In particular, for sufficiently large
t\geq t_{\delta}\vee t_{\kappa}\vee t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle B_{t}(u)\geq t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\varepsilon^{-\theta/(1-\theta)}L(t)\left(\varepsilon^{\kappa}u^{\kappa-\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}-(1+\delta)^{2}(1+u)^{-\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\right).

Choose
\kappa\leq\theta/2(1-\theta)
such that
\varepsilon^{\kappa}\geq 1-\delta
.
Then, for sufficiently large
t
, there is a constant
c(\theta)
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in[0,1]}B_{t}(u)\geq c(\theta)t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\varepsilon^{-\theta/(1-\theta)}L(t).

Using this fact in (
5.2
) it follows that for all
\delta>0
,
there exists
t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
such that

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq\exp\left(-(1+2\delta)L(t)t^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\varepsilon^{-\theta/(1-\theta)}\right),

completing the proof.
∎

With
t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
as in the statement of Lemma
5.3
,
let
t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1}\geq t_{\delta,\varepsilon}/\varepsilon
, and define

(5.3)

\displaystyle T(t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1}):=e^{\alpha(N-2)\varepsilon}\varepsilon\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}t_{j}e^{-\alpha\varepsilon j}.

Lemma 5.4
.

Under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
, let
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the Lévy process
of Section
3.1
, and let
\rho(t)
be the time change
defined by
t=\int_{0}^{\rho(t)}e^{\alpha\xi_{s}}\mathrm{d}s
.
Let
\delta,\varepsilon,N>0
be given.
Suppose
t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1}\geq t_{\delta,\varepsilon}/\varepsilon
.
Then with
T=T(t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1})
as in (
5.3
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq N\varepsilon\right)\geq\exp\left\{-(1+\delta)\varepsilon\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}L(t_{i})t_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1}\geq t_{\delta,\varepsilon}/\varepsilon

be a set of large times, where
t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
is as in the statement of Lemma
5.3
.
Define
q_{j}:=\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{N-i}
for
0\leq j\leq N
.
Now combining Lemma
5.3
with (
5.1
)
for any
x\in[0,\varepsilon]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{-x}\left(\xi_{q_{j}}\in[(j-1)\varepsilon,j\varepsilon]~\text{for all $j=1,\ldots,N$}\right)\geq\exp\left(-\varepsilon(1+\delta)\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}L(\varepsilon t_{i})t_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right).

Now on the event

\Gamma:=\{\xi_{q_{j}}\in[(j-1)\varepsilon,j\varepsilon]~\text{for all $j=1,\ldots,N$}\}
,
since
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a non-decreasing stochastic process,
we plainly have
\xi_{t}\geq(j-1)\varepsilon
for all
t\in[q_{j},q_{j+1}]
.
In particular, if we set
r:=\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}t_{i}=q_{N}
we have

\displaystyle I(r)=\int_{0}^{r}e^{\alpha\xi_{s}}\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\int_{q_{j}}^{q_{j+1}}e^{\alpha\xi_{s}}\mathrm{d}s\geq\varepsilon\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}t_{N-(j+1)}e^{\alpha(j-1)\varepsilon}=T(t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1}),

where the final equality above follows from reindexing the sum and using the
definition (
5.3
) of
T(t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1})
.

Since
I
is the inverse function of
\rho
, and both functions are strictly increasing,
on the event
\Gamma
we have
r\geq\rho(T)
. It follows that on the event
\Gamma
,

\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq\xi_{r}\leq N\varepsilon
. In particular,

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq N\varepsilon)\geq\mathbb{P}(\Gamma)
.
To obtain the result as written, note that given
\delta>0
, for each
t_{i}

is sufficiently large compared to
\varepsilon
, we have

L(\varepsilon t_{i})\leq(1+\delta)L(t_{i})
. Now replace
\delta
as necessary.
∎

Given a fixed
T_{0}
, it is natural to try to optimise the bound occurring
in Lemma
5.4
to find values
(t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1})
minimising

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}L(\varepsilon t_{i})t_{i}^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}

subject to the condition
T(t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1})=T_{0}
. In light of Lemma
4.5

a natural candidate to consider is taking
t_{i}:=Qe^{-\alpha i\varepsilon(1-\theta)/\theta}

for some sufficiently large
Q
. We use this idea to prove the following result.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
p>0
. For each
\delta>0
there exists
T_{\delta}

such that whenever
T\geq T_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq p)\geq\exp\left\{-(1+\delta)L(T)(Te^{-\alpha p})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(1-e^{-\alpha p/\theta})^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(\alpha/\theta)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\right\}

Proof.

In the setting of Lemma
5.4
, take

t_{i}=Qe^{-\alpha\varepsilon i(1-\theta)/\theta}
. Then

(5.4)

\displaystyle T_{Q}:=T(t_{0},\ldots,t_{N-1})=Qe^{\alpha(N-2)\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon}{1-e^{-\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}}(1-e^{-N\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}).

Now provided
Qe^{-\alpha\varepsilon N(1-\theta)/\theta}

is sufficiently large, by Lemma
5.4
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq N\varepsilon)\geq\exp\left\{-(1+\delta)\varepsilon Q^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}L(Qe^{-\alpha\varepsilon i(1-\theta)/\theta})e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon/\theta}\right\}.

By the uniform convergence theorem
[
8
, Section 1.2]
,
for all
Q
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle L(Qe^{-\alpha\varepsilon i(1-\theta)/\theta})\leq(1+\delta)L(Q),

so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq N\varepsilon)\geq\exp\left\{-(1+\delta)^{2}L(Q)\varepsilon Q^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon/\theta}\right\}.

Since

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{-\alpha i\varepsilon/\theta}\leq\frac{1}{1-e^{-\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}}
, we obtain

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq N\varepsilon)\geq\exp\left\{-(1+\delta)^{2}L(Q)\frac{\varepsilon}{1-e^{-\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}}Q^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right\}.

We now note that
T
and
Q
are related to one another via (
5.4
),
so that using (
5.4
) to express
Q
in terms of
T
in (
5.5
),
we obtain, for all sufficiently large
T\geq T_{0}(\delta,\varepsilon,N)
we have

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq N\varepsilon)\geq\exp\left\{-(1+\delta)^{3}L(T)\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1-e^{-\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}}\right)^{-\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}(Te^{-\alpha(N-2)\varepsilon})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(1-e^{-N\alpha\varepsilon/\theta})^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right\}.

Letting
p=N\varepsilon
, we may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho(T)}\leq p)\geq\exp\left\{-(1+\delta)^{3}L(T)(Te^{-\alpha p})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(1-e^{-\alpha p/\theta})^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(\alpha/\theta)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}m_{\alpha,\theta}(\varepsilon)\right\}

where

\displaystyle m_{\alpha,\theta}(\varepsilon):=e^{\frac{2\alpha\varepsilon}{1-\theta}}\left(\frac{\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}{1-e^{-\alpha\varepsilon/\theta}}\right)^{-\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}.

which converges to
1
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Choose
\varepsilon
sufficiently small so that
m_{\alpha,\theta}(\varepsilon)\leq 1+\delta
.
Then after replacing
(1+\delta)^{4}
with
1+\delta^{\prime}
for some suitably chosen
\delta^{\prime}
,
we obtain the result as written.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
5.2
.

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

Choose
p>0
. Let
h^{\prime}=h-p
.
Setting
\delta=a=\varepsilon
in the setting of Theorem
2.3
, there exists almost-surely a random time
h_{0}(\varepsilon)
such that for all
h\geq h_{0}(\varepsilon)
there is an antichain of cardinality at least
e^{(1-\varepsilon)h^{\prime}}
of particles of sizes between

e^{-(h-p)}
and
e^{-(h-p-\varepsilon)}
existing at various points in time.

Take one such particle in the aforementioned collection.
The probability that this particle has a descendant of size greater
than
e^{-h}
at time
T
is at least the probability that the spine
particle initiated at time zero from a particle with initial size at
between
e^{-(h-p)}
and
e^{-(h-p-\varepsilon)}
has size greater
than
e^{-h}
at time
T
, which is at least

\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\rho(Te^{-\alpha(h-p-\varepsilon)})}\leq p\right)
.

Provided
Te^{-\alpha(h-p-\varepsilon)}\geq T_{\varepsilon}
is sufficiently large,
by Corollary
5.5
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\rho(Te^{-\alpha(h-p-\varepsilon)})}\leq p\right)\geq\\
\exp\left\{-(1+\varepsilon)L(Te^{-\alpha(h-p-\varepsilon)})(Te^{-\alpha(h-\varepsilon)})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(1-e^{-\alpha p/\theta})^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(\alpha/\theta)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\right\}\\
\geq\exp\left\{-(1+c_{\alpha,\theta}\varepsilon)L(Te^{-\alpha(h-p-\varepsilon)})(Te^{-\alpha h})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(1-e^{-\alpha p/\theta})^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(\alpha/\theta)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\right\}.

Choose
p_{0}:=p_{0}(\varepsilon)
sufficiently large so that

(1-e^{-\alpha p/\theta})^{-\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\leq(1+\varepsilon)
,
and thereafter suppose
Te^{-\alpha h}
sufficiently large so that

L(Te^{-\alpha(h-p-\varepsilon)})\leq(1+\varepsilon)L(Te^{-\alpha h})
.
Then changing the constant
c_{\alpha,\theta}
if necessary, we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\rho(Te^{-\alpha(h-p-\varepsilon)})}\leq p_{0}\right)\geq\exp\left\{-(1+c_{\alpha,\theta}\varepsilon)L(Te^{-\alpha h})(Te^{-\alpha h})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(\alpha/\theta)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\right\}.

In particular, using the inequality
(1-u)\leq e^{-u}
together with (
5.6
),
provided that

Te^{-\alpha(h-p-\varepsilon)}\geq T_{\varepsilon}

is sufficiently large,
for all sufficiently large
Te^{-\alpha h}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{There exists no particle of size $\geq e^{-h}$ at time $t$})\leq\exp\left\{-e^{S(T,h,p_{0})}\right\}

where

\displaystyle S(T,h,p_{0}):=(1-\varepsilon)(h-p_{0})-(1+c_{\alpha,\theta}\varepsilon)L(Te^{-\alpha h})(Te^{-\alpha h})^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}(\alpha/\theta)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}.

For a sufficiently large constant
C=C_{\alpha,\theta}>0
set

(5.7)

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}(h):=(1-C\varepsilon)e^{\alpha h}\frac{h^{1-\theta}}{L(h^{1-\theta})^{1-\theta}}(\theta/\alpha)^{\theta}.

Then provided
C
is chosen sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle S(T,h,p_{0})\geq c^{\prime}_{\alpha,\theta}\varepsilon h.

In summary, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists a deterministic

h_{0}(\varepsilon)
such that for all

h\geq h_{0}(\varepsilon)
, the probability that there is no particle
of size greater than
e^{-h}
at time
T_{\varepsilon}(h)
(with
T_{\varepsilon}(h)
as in (
5.7
))
is at most
e^{-e^{c^{\prime}\varepsilon h}}
.
It follows from the Borel-Cantelli lemma that there exists
h_{1}(\varepsilon)
such that for every
h\geq h_{1}(\varepsilon)
of the form
h=n+i/n
with
0\leq i\leq n-1
there exists a particle of size greater than
e^{-h}
at time
T_{\varepsilon}(h)
.
∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3

To complete the proof of our main results, we first make some general
observations about certain functions and their inverses.
We say that a function
f(h)
is
nice
if and only if it takes the form

\displaystyle f(h)=e^{\alpha h}h^{\beta}G(h)

for some
\beta>0
and slowly varying
G
.

A brief calculation tells us that the inverse function of a nice function takes the form

(6.1)

\displaystyle f^{-1}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\log t-\beta\log\log t+\beta\log\alpha-\log G(\log t)\right)+O(\log\log t/\log t).

We note from (
6.1
) that if
f_{\delta}(h):=(1+\delta)f(h)
,
we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle f^{-1}_{\delta}(t)=f^{-1}(t)-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log(1+\delta)+O(\log\log t/\log t).

We now have the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an increasing stochastic
process satisfying
M_{0}=0
. For
h\geq 0
, define

\displaystyle\tau_{h}:=\sup\{t\geq 0:M_{t}\leq h\}.

Suppose there is a nice function
f(h)
such that
for all
\delta>0
, there exist
h_{\delta}>0
and

c_{\delta}>0
such that, for all
h\geq h_{\delta}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{h}\notin[(1-\delta)f(h),(1+\delta)f(h)]\right)\leq e^{-c_{\delta}h}.

Then
M_{t}-f^{-1}(t)
converges to zero almost-surely.

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
. We will prove that

M_{t}-\frac{1}{\alpha}(\log t-\beta\log\log t-\log G(\log t)+\beta\log\alpha)\geq-\delta

for all sufficiently large
t
. Define

\displaystyle h_{i}=i\delta\qquad\text{and}\qquad t_{i}:=f(h_{i})=e^{\alpha i\delta}(hi)^{\beta}G(hi).

By the Borel–Cantelli lemma, with probability one,

\tau_{h_{i}}\leq(1+\delta)t_{i}
for all sufficiently large
i
.
Since
h\mapsto\tau_{h}
is a non-decreasing stochastic process, it follows that

(6.3)

\displaystyle h\in(h_{k},h_{k+1}]\implies\tau_{h}\leq\tau_{h_{k+1}}\leq(1+\delta)f(h_{k+1})

for all sufficiently large
k
.
Note that for a sufficiently large
c>0
, we have

(6.4)

\displaystyle(1+\delta)f(h_{k+1})\leq(1+c\delta)f(h),\qquad\qquad~\forall h\in(h_{k},h_{k+1}].

In particular, combining (
6.3
) and (
6.4
),
it follows that
\tau_{h}\leq(1+c\delta)f(h)
for all
sufficiently large
h
. Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, it follows that

\displaystyle\tau_{h}\leq f_{\delta}(h):=(1+\delta)f(h)

for all sufficiently large
h
. It then follows that for all
sufficiently large
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle M_{t}\geq f_{\delta}^{-1}(t).

Now using (
6.2
), it follows that for all
\delta>0

there exists
t_{0}(\delta)
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}(\delta)
we have

\displaystyle M_{t}-f^{-1}(t)\geq-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log(1+\delta)+O(\log\log t/\log t).

Thus for all
\delta>0
, there exists
t_{0}(\delta)
such that

M_{t}-f^{-1}(t)\geq-\delta
for all
t\geq t_{0}(\delta)
.

A similar argument may be used to prove that there exists
t_{0}^{\prime}(\delta)

such that
M_{t}-f^{-1}(t)\leq\delta
for all
t\geq t_{0}^{\prime}(\delta)
.
Thus
M_{t}-f^{-1}(t)
converges to zero.
∎

We now prove our main results, Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\tau_{h}:=\sup\{t\geq 0:m_{t}\leq h\}

and
F_{0}(h):=\frac{1}{\lambda}he^{\alpha h}
. Combining the statements of
Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
5.1
,
for all
\delta>0
there exist
c_{\delta}>0
and
h_{\delta}>0
such that whenever
h\geq h_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{h}\notin((1-\delta)F_{0}(h),(1+\delta)F_{0}(h)\right)\leq e^{-c_{\delta}h}.

In particular, we are in the setting of Lemma
6.1

for the process
(m_{t},t\geq 0)
with
F_{0}(h)=\frac{1}{\lambda}he^{\alpha h}
.
By (
6.1
) (setting
\beta=1
and
G(h)=1/\lambda
) the associated inverse function takes the form

\displaystyle F_{0}^{-1}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\log t-\log\log t+\log\alpha-\log\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+O(\log\log t/\log t).

The proof now follows from Lemma
6.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\tau_{h}:=\sup\{t\geq 0:m_{t}\leq h\}
and

F_{\theta}(h)=(\theta/\alpha)^{\theta}L(h^{1-\theta})^{-(1-\theta)}h^{1-\theta}e^{\alpha h}.

Combining the statements of Proposition
4.2
and
Proposition
5.2
, for all
\delta>0

there exists
c_{\delta}>0
and
h_{\delta}>0
such that whenever
h\geq h_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{h}\notin((1-\delta)F_{\theta}(h),(1+\delta)F_{\theta}(h)\right)\leq e^{-c_{\delta}h}.

In particular, we are in the setting of Lemma
6.1

with
f=F_{\theta}(h)
. By (
6.1
) (setting
\beta:=1-\theta
and

G(h):=(\theta/\alpha)^{\theta}L(h^{1-\theta})^{-(1-\theta)}
) the associated inverse function takes the form

F_{\theta}^{-1}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\log t-(1-\theta)\log\log t+(1-\theta)\log\alpha-\log\left((\theta/\alpha)^{\theta}L((\log t)^{1-\theta})^{-(1-\theta)}\right)\right)+o(1)\\
=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\log t-(1-\theta)\log\log t+\log\alpha+\theta\log\theta-\log\left(L((\log t)^{1-\theta})\right)^{1-\theta}\right)+o(1),

where of course the
o(1)
terms are in fact
O(\log\log t/\log t)
.

We now consider the term
\theta\log\theta-\log\left(L((\log t)^{1-\theta})\right)^{1-\theta}
. Using the definition of
L(s)
given in the statement of Theorem
3.2
to obtain the first equality below, and the fact that
C_{3}(\theta)=(1-\theta)\theta^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\Gamma(1-\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
to obtain the second,

\displaystyle\theta\log\theta-\log\left(L((\log t)^{1-\theta})\right)^{1-\theta}

\displaystyle=\theta\log\theta-(1-\theta)\log C_{3}(\theta)-\log\ell\left(\log t~\ell\left(\log t\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=-(1-\theta)\log(1-\theta)-\log\Gamma(1-\theta)-\log\ell\left(\log t~\ell\left(\log t\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right).

Thus

\displaystyle F_{\theta}^{-1}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{\log t-(1-\theta)\log\log t-\log\ell\left(\log t~\ell\left(\log t\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\right)+c(\alpha,\theta)\right\}+o(1),

where
c(\alpha,\theta):=\log\alpha-(1-\theta)\log(1-\theta)-\log\Gamma(1-\theta)
.
Theorem
1.3
now follows from Lemma
6.1
.
∎
