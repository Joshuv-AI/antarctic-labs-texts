Memory-Conditioned Flow-Matching for Stable Autoregressive PDE Rollouts
Victor Armegioiu

Abstract
Autoregressive generative PDE solvers can be accurate one step ahead yet drift over long rollouts, especially in coarse-to-fine regimes where each step must regenerate unresolved fine scales. This is the regime of diffusion and flow-matching generators: although their internal dynamics are Markovian, rollout stability is governed by per-step conditional law errors. Using the Mori–Zwanzig projection formalism, we show that eliminating unresolved variables yields an exact resolved evolution with a Markov term, a memory term, and an orthogonal forcing, exposing a structural limitation of memoryless closures. Motivated by this, we introduce memory-conditioned diffusion/flow-matching with a compact online state injected into denoising via latent features. Via disintegration, memory induces a structured conditional tail prior for unresolved scales and reduces the transport needed to populate missing frequencies. We prove Wasserstein stability of the resulting conditional kernel. We then derive discrete Grönwall rollout bounds that separate memory approximation from conditional generation error. Experiments on compressible flows with shocks and multiscale mixing show improved accuracy and markedly more stable long-horizon rollouts, with better fine-scale spectral and statistical fidelity.

Introduction

Many physical systems are modeled by time-dependent partial differential equations (PDEs)

Where memory enters: Mori–Zwanzig and missing-frequency generation.

2
Background and setup

We study autoregressive surrogate modeling for time-evolving PDEs under partial (resolved) observations.
The key object is the
rollout law
: the model is iterated in physical time, so small one-step law defects can compound.
We fix notation for (i) the PDE and resolved operator, (ii) Mori–Zwanzig’s memory/noise decomposition, (iii) conditional
flow-matching / rectified-flow transitions, and (iv) the Littlewood–Paley cutoff viewpoint used in the theory.

2.1
PDE setting, resolved observations, and distributional rollouts

Let
D
be either a flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
or a bounded
C^{1}
domain.
Let
\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
be the (fine) state space and consider

\partial_{t}u(t)=\mathcal{F}(u(t)),\quad u(0)=u_{0}\in\mathcal{H},

with (when relevant) boundary conditions appropriate to the PDE.
We denote by
S_{t}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
the (possibly weak) solution operator, so
u(t)=S_{t}u_{0}
when well-defined.

Statistical solutions / evolving laws.

Given an initial law
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
, the evolving law is
\mu_{t}:=(S_{t})_{\#}\mu_{0}
.

Resolved operator and autoregressive model.

Fix a bounded linear observation operator
\Pi_{R}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{Y}
into a resolved space
\mathcal{Y}
and define

y(t):=\Pi_{R}u(t)
.
We discretize physical time with step
\Delta t>0
and
t_{n}=n\Delta t
.
A learned
distributional
solver produces a Markov chain
(\widehat{u}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
via

\widehat{u}_{n+1}\sim\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\,\cdot\,\mid y_{n},\widehat{m}_{n},t_{n}),\quad y_{n}:=\Pi_{R}\widehat{u}_{n},

where
\widehat{m}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}
is a learned memory state updated causally from the trajectory (Section
4
).
We compare the rollout law
\widehat{\mu}_{n}:=\operatorname{Law}(\widehat{u}_{n})
to the true law
\mu_{n}:=\operatorname{Law}(u(t_{n}))
.

2.2
Mori–Zwanzig: exact memory and orthogonal forcing after elimination

Mori–Zwanzig (MZ) formalizes the effect of eliminating unresolved variables.
For exposition, consider a finite-dimensional discretization
H
and induced ODE
\dot{u}=F(u)
with flow
S_{t}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a class of smooth observables
\psi:H\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
and define the Liouville operator

(L\psi)(u):=D\psi(u)\,F(u)
so that
(e^{tL}\psi)(u_{0})=\psi(S_{t}u_{0})
.
Fix a projection
P:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
corresponding to “resolved information” and set
Q:=I-P
.
Then for any
\psi\in\mathcal{A}
one has the identity

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\,Pe^{tL}\psi

\displaystyle=Pe^{tL}PL\psi+\int_{0}^{t}Pe^{(t-s)L}\,PL\,e^{sQL}\,QL\psi\,ds

\displaystyle+Pe^{tQL}\,QL\psi.

The first term is Markovian, the integral term is explicit
history dependence
(memory), and the last term is an

orthogonal forcing
generated by unresolved dynamics
(
11
)
.
Unless the latter two vanish, any closure depending only on the instantaneous resolved state is intrinsically misspecified.
This motivates augmenting (
2.1
) with a memory state, consistent with empirical findings in
(
21
)
.

2.3
Conditional flow matching / rectified flows as stochastic transitions

We implement
\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}
by conditional transport in an internal time
\tau\in[0,1]
.
Let
c:=(y,m,t)
denote conditioning.
Given a base law
\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
and a learned vector field

v_{\theta}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{H}
, define

\frac{d}{d\tau}U_{\tau}=v_{\theta}(\tau,U_{\tau};c),\quad U_{0}\sim\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c),

and set
\widehat{u}:=U_{1}
.
This induces a conditional kernel

\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\cdot\mid c):=\operatorname{Law}(\widehat{u}\mid c)=(\Phi^{c}_{\theta})_{\#}\,\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c),

where
\Phi^{c}_{\theta}
is the time-
1
flow map of (
2.3
).
This includes flow matching and related stochastic-interpolant viewpoints
(
14
;
2
;
27
)
,
and rectified-flow variants that emphasize efficient ODE sampling
(
3
;
28
)
.

Why internal time matters for PDE rollouts.

In coarse-to-fine settings, part of the state is effectively
prior-driven
at intermediate
\tau
(low SNR), so the choice of
conditional prior–and thus the conditioning variables–directly affects one-step defects and rollout stability
(Section
3
).

2.4
Littlewood–Paley cutoff and the “transport difficulty” viewpoint

We now specialize to the spectral-cutoff regime used in the theory.
For clarity take
D=\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
.

Dyadic projectors and resolved/unresolved split.

Let
(\Delta_{j})_{j\geq-1}
be a Littlewood–Paley decomposition on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and fix a cutoff
J\in\mathbb{N}
.
Define

S_{J}u:=\sum_{j\leq J}\Delta_{j}u,\quad Q_{J}u:=u-S_{J}u=\sum_{j>J}\Delta_{j}u.

We take
\Pi_{R}:=S_{J}
so that each state decomposes as

u=y+z,\quad y:=S_{J}u\in\mathcal{H}_{\leq J},\quad z:=Q_{J}u\in\mathcal{H}_{>J}.

In coarse-to-fine generation we condition on
y
and must sample the tail
z
.

Why memory changes the difficulty of populating missing shells.

A memoryless generator typically uses a generic conditional tail prior (e.g. isotropic noise on
\mathcal{H}_{>J}
).
Let
\pi_{J}(\cdot\mid y)
denote such a base tail prior, and write the true conditional tail law as
\operatorname{Law}(z\mid y)
.
The mismatch

\delta_{J}(y):=W_{2}\!\big(\operatorname{Law}(z\mid y),\,\pi_{J}(\cdot\mid y)\big)

lower bounds how much transport the internal-time dynamics must perform before it can match the true tail.
A memory state
m
refines the conditioning, replacing
\operatorname{Law}(z\mid y)
by
\operatorname{Law}(z\mid y,m)
; in general, conditioning reduces
best-achievable prediction error for tail summaries and shrinks uncertainty.
This is precisely the mechanism formalized in Section
3
:
(i) disintegration localizes
W_{2}
mismatch to
\mathcal{H}_{>J}
when low modes are pinned,
(ii) conditioning on
m
reduces optimal
L^{2}
errors for tail statistics, and
(iii) if the true conditional kernel depends on
m
, then memoryless kernels incur an irreducible conditional gap.
These effects translate into smaller one-step law defects and hence more stable rollouts.

3
Theory: memory-shaped priors and stable autoregressive rollouts

We analyze diffusion/flow-matching generators used
autoregressively
as PDE solvers.
In coarse-to-fine regimes, each step conditions on resolved content and must regenerate unresolved structure (typically high
frequencies). Small per-step law defects can therefore compound and destabilize long rollouts.

Our theory isolates one mechanism and propagates it to a rollout bound:
(i) flow matching / rectified flows evolve samples in an
internal time

\tau
, where high-frequency shells can be

prior-driven
(low SNR); (ii) Mori–Zwanzig predicts that the correct tail prior is
history dependent
;
(iii) a finite-dimensional memory can approximate the relevant history convolution, shaping a structured tail prior and reducing
an intrinsic Wasserstein mismatch that lower bounds the transport needed to populate missing shells.

Proofs.
All statements in this section are proved in Appendix
A
, under the (tacit) assumptions justified in Appendix
A.1
.

3.1
Internal time and Mori–Zwanzig: prior-driven shells require a history convolution

We work in the spectral-cutoff setting of Section
2.4
.
Fix a Littlewood–Paley decomposition
(\Delta_{j})_{j\geq-1}
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and a cutoff
J
.
At physical step
n
, decompose the fine state as

u_{n}=y_{n}+z_{n},\quad y_{n}:=S_{J}u_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{\leq J},\quad z_{n}:=Q_{J}u_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{>J}.

The unresolved tail
z_{n}
influences the next state; in a Mori–Zwanzig reduction, this influence appears through memory plus an
orthogonal forcing term (Section
2.2
).

A minimal linear-Gaussian tail surrogate.

Let
Z_{n}:=z_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{>J}
and let
E_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{E}}
be a resolved embedding extracted from
y_{n}

(e.g. a UNet bottleneck feature),
E_{n}:=\mathrm{Enc}(y_{n})
.
Assume a stable linear state-space update

Z_{n+1}=AZ_{n}+BE_{n}+\eta_{n},

where
A:\mathcal{H}_{>J}\to\mathcal{H}_{>J}
has spectral radius
<1
,
B:\mathbb{R}^{d_{E}}\to\mathcal{H}_{>J}
is bounded, and

(\eta_{n})_{n}
is i.i.d. centered Gaussian in
\mathcal{H}_{>J}
.

Internal time as corruption level.

In diffusion/flow-matching, intermediate internal times correspond to Gaussian corruption of what is being generated.
For
\tau\in[0,1]
, model this by

X^{(\tau)}_{n+1}:=Z_{n+1}+\sigma(\tau)\,\xi,\quad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\ \text{independent}.

When
\sigma(\tau)
dominates the signal in shell
j
, that shell is low SNR and denoising must fall back on a conditional prior.

Theorem 3.1

(Shell-wise history prior in the optimal denoiser)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma(E_{0},\dots,E_{n})
. Assume that, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, each shell covariance is isotropic:

\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\Delta_{j}Z_{n+1}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)=s_{j,n+1}^{2}\,I_{j},\quad j>J.

Define the history term (conditional mean)

M^{\star}_{n+1}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Z_{n+1}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=A^{n+1}Z_{0}\;+\;\sum_{k=0}^{n}A^{\,n-k}BE_{k}.

Fix
\tau\in[0,1]
and set
X:=X^{(\tau)}_{n+1}
. Then for every
j>J
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_{j}Z_{n+1}\,\big|\,X,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\kappa_{j,n+1}(\tau)\,\Delta_{j}X

\displaystyle+\bigl(1-\kappa_{j,n+1}(\tau)\bigr)\,\Delta_{j}M^{\star}_{n+1},

where

\kappa_{j,n+1}(\tau):=\left(1+\frac{\sigma(\tau)^{2}}{s_{j,n+1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\in[0,1].

In particular, in low-SNR shells (i.e.
s_{j,n+1}^{2}\ll\sigma(\tau)^{2}
), the denoiser is prior-driven and governed by

\Delta_{j}M^{\star}_{n+1}
.

Why this forces memory.

The term
M^{\star}_{n+1}
is a causal convolution of
(E_{k})_{k\leq n}
with weights
A^{n-k}
.
Thus, precisely in the shells where internal-time denoising cannot “see” the signal, generation must rely on a history-shaped
prior. A learned memory state is a natural approximation class for this convolution, and shaping the tail prior
before

transport reduces the mismatch that a memoryless generator must correct by moving mass across missing shells.

3.2
Rollouts: stability reduces everything to a one-step law defect

Let
\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
and let
S_{\Delta t_{n}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be the PDE solution operator over the

n
-th physical step. For laws
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
, the true evolution satisfies

\mu_{n+1}=(S_{\Delta t_{n}})_{\#}\mu_{n}
.
A learned autoregressive model produces rollout laws
(\widehat{\mu}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and we track

d_{n}:=W_{2}(\mu_{n},\widehat{\mu}_{n})
. Define the one-step law defect

\epsilon_{n}:=W_{2}\!\bigl((S_{\Delta t_{n}})_{\#}\widehat{\mu}_{n},\ \widehat{\mu}_{n+1}\bigr).

(1)

Lemma 3.2

(Error recursion)
.

Under Assumption
A.1
,

d_{n+1}\leq e^{\alpha_{n}}d_{n}+\epsilon_{n}.

Remark 3.3

(Relation to invariant-measure regularization)
.

Invariant-measure objectives (e.g. MMD between stationary laws) target a fixed point of the time-step operator.
By contrast, our analysis controls the operator itself via one-step conditional law errors.
In particular, if the true coarse-grained Markov operator is contractive/mixing in a weak metric (e.g. an IPM/MMD),
then the stationary-law discrepancy is bounded by the one-step kernel discrepancy; see Appendix
K
.

3.3
Disintegration: with low modes fixed, mismatch localizes to the tail

Fix a cutoff
J
and write
u=y+z
with
y=S_{J}u\in\mathcal{H}_{\leq J}
and
z=Q_{J}u\in\mathcal{H}_{>J}
.
Disintegrating a law
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
along
y
yields conditionals
\mu(\cdot\mid y)
and the decomposition

\mu(du)=\int\mu(du\mid y)\,\mu_{\leq J}(dy)
, where
\mu_{\leq J}=(S_{J})_{\#}\mu
.

Lemma 3.4

(
W_{2}
reduction under pinned low modes)
.

Fix
y\in\mathcal{H}_{\leq J}
and let
\rho,\tilde{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{>J})
.
If
\mu=\delta_{y}\otimes\rho
and
\tilde{\mu}=\delta_{y}\otimes\tilde{\rho}
, then

W_{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})=W_{2}(\rho,\tilde{\rho}),

with the right-hand
W_{2}
computed in
\mathcal{H}_{>J}
.

This formalizes: once resolved content is fixed, all remaining uncertainty (and mismatch) lies in the missing-frequency tail.

3.4
Memory reduces tail mismatch

Mori–Zwanzig predicts that unresolved influence is history dependent, so conditioning only on
Y
is generically insufficient.
Two complementary statements capture why memory helps: it reduces the best achievable tail prediction error, and it prevents an
irreducible conditional-kernel gap when the truth varies with history.

Lemma 3.5

(Conditioning reduces
L^{2}
projection error)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a Hilbert space and
G(Z)\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{G})
.
Set
\Pi_{Y}:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid Y]
and
\Pi_{Y,M}:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid Y,M]
.
If
\sigma(Y)\subset\sigma(Y,M)
, then

\|G(Z)-\Pi_{Y,M}G(Z)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|G(Z)-\Pi_{Y}G(Z)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Interpretation.

If
G(Z)
encodes shell energies, flux proxies, or subgrid-stress summaries, then conditioning on a memory
M
can only improve
their best
L^{2}
prediction. Through
Theorem

3.1
, these are exactly the quantities that internal-time
denoising must supply in low-SNR shells; hence memory can reduce the tail mismatch
before
transport.

Proposition 3.6

(Irreducible mismatch without memory)
.

Fix
y
and suppose there exist
m_{1}\neq m_{2}
such that

K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m_{1})\neq K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m_{2})
.
Let

\delta_{y}:=W_{2}\!\left(K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m_{1}),\ K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m_{2})\right).

Then for any memoryless kernel
K(\cdot\mid y)
,

\max_{i\in\{1,2\}}\ W_{2}\!\left(K(\cdot\mid y),\ K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m_{i})\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta_{y}.

This shows memory is not merely beneficial: if the true conditional law varies with history, a memoryless model has an intrinsic
one-step defect lower bound.

3.5
From conditional mismatch to step defect, and a rollout bound

Let
\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\cdot\mid y,m)
denote the learned conditional transition kernel and
K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m)
the true
conditional law. We separate
(i)
memory approximation
(using
\widehat{m}_{n}
instead of an ideal
m_{n}^{\star}
) and
(ii)
conditional generation
at fixed memory.

Proposition 3.7

(Conditional defect decomposition)
.

For fixed
n
and
y
, define

\widehat{K}_{n}^{\,y}(m):=\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\cdot\mid y,m),\quad K_{n}^{\star,y}(m):=K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m),

and

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{gen},n}(y,m):=W_{2}\!\bigl(\widehat{K}_{n}^{\,y}(m),\ K_{n}^{\star,y}(m)\bigr).

Then, under Assumption
A.2
, it holds that

\displaystyle W_{2}\!\bigl(\widehat{K}_{n}^{\,y}(\widehat{m}_{n}),\ K_{n}^{\star,y}(m_{n}^{\star})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathrm{mem}}\|\widehat{m}_{n}-m_{n}^{\star}\|

\displaystyle+\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{gen},n}(y,m_{n}^{\star}).

To pass from conditional mismatch to the law-level defect
\epsilon_{n}
, we use a standard mixture-coupling estimate that bounds

between mixtures by the root-mean-square of conditional

distances (stated and proved in Appendix
A
).

Theorem 3.8

(Rollout bound)
.

Assume
A.1
and the mixture-coupling estimate of Appendix
A
.
Let
\beta_{k,n}:=\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}
with
\beta_{n,n}=0
. Then

d_{n}\leq e^{\beta_{0,n}}\,d_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{\beta_{k+1,n}}\,\epsilon_{k},

and each

satisfies

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathrm{mem}}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{m}_{k}-m_{k}^{\star}\|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\ \Bigl(\mathbb{E}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{gen},k}(Y_{k},M_{k}^{\star})^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2},

where
(Y_{k},M_{k}^{\star})
denotes the step-
k
conditioning pair under the model rollout.

4
Method and experimental protocol: memory-conditioned flow matching for autoregressive PDE rollouts

We learn
stochastic
next-step transition laws for time-dependent PDEs and deploy them
autoregressively
as surrogate
solvers. At test time, sampled predictions are fed back for 10–20 steps, and we impose
structured lead-time schedules

(uniform, sparse, increasing
\Delta t
) that are
not
seen as such during training. This explicitly targets an
out-of-distribution (OOD) deployment regime in which (i) the input distribution drifts under feedback and (ii) the temporal
pattern of lead times differs from training.

We focus on coarse-to-fine conditioning in compressible Euler: each step must regenerate unresolved content from a degraded
current state. To represent the history dependence induced by cross-scale interactions (Mori–Zwanzig viewpoint,
Section
2
), we maintain a compact memory state updated online and inject it into the generative backbone at the
bottleneck (see Appendix
J.2.1
). The generator itself remains Markovian in the
internal
flow-matching time, while the overall surrogate is
non-Markovian in
physical
time through memory.

4.1
Task: autoregressive time stepping as conditional generation

Let
u_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
denote the PDE state at physical time
t_{n}
. In our benchmarks,

\mathcal{X}=L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{4})
and
u=(\rho,\rho v_{x},\rho v_{y},E)
consists of density, two momentum components, and total energy.

A learned solver is deployed autoregressively: given
(u_{n},\Delta t_{n})
it samples a next state
\widehat{u}_{n+1}
and feeds it
back as input at the subsequent step. Even if one-step prediction is accurate under ground-truth conditioning, long rollouts can
drift because the conditioning distribution shifts: repeated sampling progressively degrades fine-scale content, so later steps
effectively become coarse-to-fine reconstructions from self-generated inputs. We view each step as learning a conditional next-state law. Let
\mathcal{I}_{n}
denote the information used at step
n
. The
target object is the true conditional kernel

K^{\star}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})\;\equiv\;\operatorname{Law}(u_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{I}_{n}).

Motivated by Mori–Zwanzig,
K^{\star}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})
is generically
not
Markov in instantaneous resolved
information alone once unresolved scales are eliminated; we therefore augment conditioning with a learned memory state.

4.2
Memory state and bottleneck injection

We maintain a per-trajectory memory
m_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}
updated online from a compact embedding of the current step:

e_{n}=\mathsf{E}_{\psi}(u_{n},\Delta t_{n}),\quad m_{n+1}=\mathsf{M}_{\psi}(m_{n},e_{n}).

Here
\mathsf{E}_{\psi}
is an encoder producing a low-dimensional step summary, and
\mathsf{M}_{\psi}
is a stable state-space
recurrence (SSM/S4 in our implementation) designed to capture multi-timescale history.

Bottleneck fusion.

Let
h_{n}
denote the UNet bottleneck tensor produced when processing the (possibly degraded) current state and conditioning
signals. We inject memory at the bottleneck via a gated fusion

\widetilde{h}_{n}=\mathsf{Fuse}(h_{n},m_{n}),

and decode
\widetilde{h}_{n}
to drive the denoising/transport dynamics. This forces history dependence to live in the same latent
representation space that controls generation, rather than being appended as a shallow conditioning token.

4.3
Conditional flow matching step (rectified-flow instantiation)

Our framework is compatible with diffusion/score models and flow-matching variants; in experiments we instantiate it with a
rectified-flow-style flow matching objective.

Conditional kernel.

Let
c_{n}:=(u_{n},m_{n},\Delta t_{n})
denote the conditioning at step
n
. The generator defines a conditional transition kernel

\widehat{K}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{n})\;:=\;\operatorname{Law}(\widehat{u}_{n+1}\mid c_{n}).

Internal time
\tau
(corruption/SNR level).

Flow matching evolves samples in an
internal time

\tau\in[0,1]
controlling the interpolation/corruption level between a
simple base draw and the target. Importantly,
\tau
is
not
a frequency index; spectral notions are handled separately
(e.g. via Littlewood–Paley shells in Section
2.4
and in the theory of Section
3
).

Flow matching loss.

Given a supervised pair
(u_{n},u_{n+1})
with conditioning
c_{n}
, sample a base draw

u_{0}\sim\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c_{n})
and
\tau\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
. Form the interpolation

u_{\tau}=(1-\tau)u_{0}+\tau u_{n+1}
and regress the velocity field
v_{\theta}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta,\psi)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|v_{\theta}(\tau,u_{\tau};c_{n})-(u_{n+1}-u_{0})\big\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\Big].

At inference, we sample
u_{0}\sim\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c_{n})
and integrate the induced ODE in
\tau
from
0
to
1
to obtain

\widehat{u}_{n+1}
.

All architectural and optimization details (UNet widths, SSM parameters, optimizer and schedule) are reported in
Appendix
I
.

4.4
Training protocol: one-step pairs + short randomized windows

Training combines broad one-step coverage with limited rollout-awareness.

Single-step pairs (variable lead times).

We sample transitions
(u_{n},u_{n+1})
with lead times
\Delta t_{n}\in[0.05,0.30]
to cover a range of horizons. This provides
one-step supervision across
\Delta t
and enables evaluation binned by lead time.

Short unrolls (teacher forcing on randomized windows).

To partially align with autoregressive deployment while keeping training stable, we also train on randomized trajectory windows
of length at most 5 steps. Concretely, we unroll the model for 3–5 steps, update
m_{n}
online, and apply teacher forcing with
probability
p_{\mathrm{TF}}
(reported in Appendix
I
); otherwise we feed back sampled predictions. This creates a
controlled form of distribution shift during training while remaining far shorter than the 10–20 step rollouts used at test
time (see Appendix
J.2
).

Downsampled-input degradation.

To mimic the progressive loss of fine scales under feedback, we optionally degrade the conditioning input
u_{n}
by

2\times 2
average pooling followed by bilinear upsampling, while keeping targets
u_{n+1}
at full resolution. This directly
targets robustness to autoregressive degradation (details and ablations in Appendix
J
,
J.4
).

4.5
Benchmarks, deployment schedules, and metrics

Benchmarks.

We evaluate on two families of 2D compressible Euler trajectories exhibiting shocks and multiscale structure:
(i) Richtmyer–Meshkov-type shock–interface interaction (CE-RM), and
(ii) four-quadrant Riemann problems with interacting waves (CE-RP/CRP2D).
We use datasets from the PDEgym collection on the Hugging Face Hub; the dataset cards document the numerical setups and train/val/test splits
(
5
)
. See also
(
10
)
(Appendix
B
).

Explicit OOD deployment: long rollouts and structured lead-time patterns.

Training uses one-step pairs and short randomized windows (up to 5 steps). In contrast, evaluation rolls out autoregressively for
10–20 steps, feeding back sampled predictions throughout, and uses
structured
lead-time schedules such as:
(i) uniform
\Delta t=0.05
,
(ii) sparse
\Delta t=0.10
,
and (iii) increasing schedules (e.g.
\Delta t_{i}=0.05+0.025\,i
).
These schedules stress temporal robustness under deployment shift: the model must remain stable when the sequence pattern of
lead times is fixed and long-range, unlike the randomized short contexts seen in training. See Appendix
J.2
for precise algorithmic details.

Metrics.

We report (i) one-step relative
L^{2}
errors (binned by
\Delta t
), and (ii) final-time relative
L^{2}
errors after 10–20 step
autoregressive rollouts under the above schedules. To localize multiscale behavior, we additionally report Fourier-band /
spectral diagnostics (see also Appendix
J
).

Conclusions.

We proposed
memory-conditioned
diffusion/flow matching for autoregressive PDE rollouts, explicitly targeting the deployment regime where learned solvers often fail: long horizons under self-generated input drift and lead-time patterns that differ from training. The core idea is to keep the generator Markovian in internal time while enabling non-Markovian behavior in physical time by maintaining a compact online memory and injecting it into the backbone bottleneck, so that history shapes the conditional prior for unresolved content. Across two compressible Euler benchmarks, memory consistently improves long-horizon rollout stability, with gains that grow with horizon and concentrate on the physically consequential low and mid frequencies. Degraded-conditioning augmentation complements memory by improving robustness to degraded autoregressive inputs and can further amplify improvements under structured lead-time schedules. Overall, the results support memory-conditioned flow matching as a practical route to stable, multi-scale autoregressive PDE surrogates in rollout-OOD settings.

A.1
Regularity assumptions

The rollout analysis separates two distinct sources of instability:
(i) how strongly the
true
resolved dynamics amplifies small perturbations of the current law over one physical step, and
(ii) how sensitively the
learned
conditional transition law reacts to perturbations of the injected memory state.
The following assumptions formalize these effects in
W_{2}
.
(We restate variants of these assumptions later, near the lemmas where they are used, to keep the local derivations self-contained.)

Assumption A.1

(One-step law stability)
.

There exists
\alpha_{n}\geq 0
such that for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
,

W_{2}\!\bigl((S_{\Delta t_{n}})_{\#}\mu,\ (S_{\Delta t_{n}})_{\#}\nu\bigr)\leq e^{\alpha_{n}}\,W_{2}(\mu,\nu).

Why Assumption
A.1
is reasonable.

Assumption
A.1
is the law-level analogue of a pointwise Lipschitz estimate for the step map

S_{\Delta t_{n}}
.
If
S_{\Delta t_{n}}
is
L
-Lipschitz on
\mathcal{H}
with
L\leq e^{\alpha_{n}}
, then pushing forward an optimal coupling yields
(
A.1
) (see Lemma
B.1
below for the general statement).
For nonlinear PDEs,
S_{\Delta t_{n}}
is typically not globally Lipschitz uniformly in time (e.g. shocks and strong interactions may
create large transient amplification). The point here is not contractivity, but a
stepwise amplification bookkeeping
: the
rollout bound will amplify each one-step defect by factors
e^{\alpha_{k}}
, so
\alpha_{n}
is precisely the quantity that predicts
when long rollouts are fragile.

Assumption A.2

(Memory-Lipschitz kernel)
.

We assume there exists
L_{\mathrm{mem}}\geq 0
such that for all
y
and
m,m^{\prime}
,

W_{2}\!\bigl(\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\cdot\mid y,m),\ \widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\cdot\mid y,m^{\prime})\bigr)\leq L_{\mathrm{mem}}\|m-m^{\prime}\|.

Why Assumption
A.2
is the right notion of memory-regularity.

The role of memory is to modulate the conditional transition law in a way that captures history dependence.
For the theory, we need this modulation to be
stable
: if the learned kernel were discontinuous in
m
, then small memory
approximation errors would lead to uncontrolled changes in the predicted law.
Assumption
A.2
is exactly the stability condition that converts a memory approximation error

\|\widehat{m}_{n}-m_{n}^{\star}\|
into a one-step kernel discrepancy.
Later, Section
G
shows how such a bound follows from standard Lipschitz regularity of the rectified-flow vector field and
(base-law) continuity in
m
.

Summary.

Assumption
A.1
quantifies how the
true
one-step operator transports laws,
while Assumption
A.2
quantifies how the
learned
operator responds to memory perturbations.
They are the two “stability constants” that govern how one-step errors compound.

A.2
Main statements referenced by the text

This section collects the auxiliary results that are cited in the main text.
They fall into three groups:

1.

Internal-time posterior structure
(Theorem
A.3
): explains why, in low-SNR/high-frequency shells,
the optimal denoiser is prior-driven and therefore benefits from a history-dependent prior mean (which memory provides).

2.

Why memory helps at all
(Lemma
A.4
and Proposition
A.6
): conditioning can reduce
irreducible MMSE for tail observables, and if the true conditional law depends on history, then any memoryless kernel suffers an
intrinsic one-step mismatch lower bound.

3.

Rollout bookkeeping
(Lemma
A.8
–Theorem
A.11
and Lemma
A.9
):
turns conditional mismatch into a step defect
\epsilon_{n}
, and then
\epsilon_{n}
into a long-horizon bound via discrete Grönwall.

Theorem A.3

(Internal-time shellwise posterior mean)
.

Fix a cutoff
J
and let
\mathcal{H}_{>J}
be the unresolved subspace.
Assume the tail evolves by the linear recursion

Z_{n+1}=AZ_{n}+BE_{n}+\eta_{n},

where
(\eta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are i.i.d. centered Gaussians independent of the resolved embeddings
(E_{k})_{k\leq n}
.
Define the history-dependent mean

M^{\star}_{n+1}:=A^{n+1}Z_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}A^{n-k}BE_{k}.

(2)

Fix a diffusion/flow-matching noise scale
\sigma(\tau)\geq 0
and define the corrupted observation

X^{(\tau)}_{n+1}:=Z_{n+1}+\sigma(\tau)\,\xi,\qquad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\ \text{independent}.

Let
(\Delta_{j})_{j>J}
denote Littlewood–Paley blocks on
\mathcal{H}_{>J}
with
\mathcal{H}_{j}:=\Delta_{j}\mathcal{H}_{>J}

and orthogonal projector
I_{j}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{j}
.
Assume shellwise block-diagonal/isotropic conditional covariance:

\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(Z_{n+1}\,\middle|\,\sigma(E_{0},\dots,E_{n})\right)=\sum_{j>J}s_{j,n+1}^{2}\,I_{j},\qquad s_{j,n+1}^{2}\geq 0.

(3)

Then for each
j>J
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_{j}Z_{n+1}\,\middle|\,X^{(\tau)}_{n+1},\,E_{0},\dots,E_{n}\right]=\kappa_{j,n+1}(\tau)\,\Delta_{j}X^{(\tau)}_{n+1}+\big(1-\kappa_{j,n+1}(\tau)\big)\,\Delta_{j}M^{\star}_{n+1},

(4)

where the gain is

\kappa_{j,n+1}(\tau):=\frac{s_{j,n+1}^{2}}{s_{j,n+1}^{2}+\sigma(\tau)^{2}}\in[0,1].

In particular, if
s_{j,n+1}^{2}\ll\sigma(\tau)^{2}
then
\kappa_{j,n+1}(\tau)\approx 0
and the posterior mean is

prior-driven
:
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{j}Z_{n+1}\mid\cdots]\approx\Delta_{j}M^{\star}_{n+1}
.

How to read Theorem
A.3
.

In rectified flows/flow matching, internal time
\tau
interpolates between noise and data. High-frequency components often remain
low-SNR for much of the trajectory, meaning
\sigma(\tau)
dominates the intrinsic tail variance in those shells
(
s_{j,n+1}^{2}\ll\sigma(\tau)^{2}
). In that regime, the
optimal
denoiser does not trust the noisy observation and falls back
to the prior mean
M^{\star}_{n+1}
.
Thus, if we do not provide the history-dependent prior mean to the model (via memory), the denoising drift must “manufacture” the

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

This section fixes notation used throughout the appendix. It can be skimmed on first pass and used as a reference when needed.

B.1
Domains, state spaces, and solution operators

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be either the flat torus

(periodic) or a bounded

domain with boundary
\partial D
.
Fix

and define

\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}),\qquad\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}}:=\int_{D}u(x)\cdot v(x)\,dx.

Remark (MZ on PDE phase space).

To avoid functional-analytic issues, Mori–Zwanzig identities are presented on a finite-dimensional Galerkin truncation
(or numerical discretization), where the flow map is a smooth ODE. This is standard: MZ is an identity for ODE flows, and the PDE
interpretation is obtained by applying it to a consistent truncation and then passing to limits on cylindrical observables.

\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|S_{J}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|Q_{J}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

Appendix C
Mori–Zwanzig: derivation and specialization

This appendix records the Mori–Zwanzig (MZ) identity in the form used in the main text and specializes it to a
resolved/unresolved split.
The role of this section is conceptual: it explains why, after eliminating unresolved variables,
history dependence is generic
.
This motivates our choice to keep the generator Markovian in internal time (sampling) while injecting a compact physical-time memory
state that can approximate the induced history terms.

C.1
Liouville operator and Koopman semigroup

Let
H
be finite-dimensional and consider
\dot{u}(t)=F(u(t))
with flow map
S_{t}
.
For smooth observables
\varphi:H\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
, define the Liouville operator

(L\varphi)(u)=D\varphi(u)\,F(u).

Then
(e^{tL}\varphi)(u_{0}):=\varphi(S_{t}u_{0})
defines the Koopman semigroup and

\frac{d}{dt}(e^{tL}\varphi)=e^{tL}L\varphi
.

C.2

Fix a projection
P
on observables and set
Q:=I-P
. Let
e^{tQL}
be the semigroup generated by
QL
.

Lemma C.1

(Dyson/Duhamel identity)
.

For every
\psi
and
t\geq 0
,

e^{tL}\psi=e^{tQL}\psi+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)L}\,PL\,e^{sQL}\psi\,ds.

Proof.

Standard Duhamel formula for the perturbed generator
L=QL+PL
.
∎

C.3
Mori–Zwanzig identity

Theorem C.2

(Mori–Zwanzig identity)
.

For every
\psi
and
t\geq 0
,

\frac{d}{dt}\,Pe^{tL}\psi=Pe^{tL}PL\psi+\int_{0}^{t}Pe^{(t-s)L}\,PL\,e^{sQL}\,QL\psi\,ds+Pe^{tQL}\,QL\psi.

Proof.

Differentiate
Pe^{tL}\psi=Pe^{tL}PL\psi+Pe^{tL}QL\psi
and apply Lemma
C.1
to

.

C.4
Specialization to a resolved/unresolved split

Assume
H=Y\oplus Z
with

by

C.5

The MZ memory term is a history integral. In practice we do not represent an entire function of time; instead we compress history

The following observation explains why state-space models are a natural choice: exponential-kernel memory corresponds exactly to a

Assume a memory contribution can be written (exactly or approximately) as

Appendix E
Subgrid forcing at a cutoff: what memory should predict

This section makes the cutoff viewpoint concrete by writing down an explicit “resolved equation + remainder” identity.
Even if the generator predicts the next resolved state directly (rather than predicting a forcing term), this computation explains
what statistical information about the unresolved tail is relevant: it is exactly what controls the subgrid remainder.

For incompressible Navier–Stokes on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,

\partial_{t}u+\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)=\nu\Delta u,\qquad\nabla\cdot u=0,

define
y=S_{J}u
and

. Then the resolved equation becomes

\partial_{t}y+\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot S_{J}(y\otimes y)=\nu\Delta y+\mathcal{R}_{J}(u),

\mathcal{R}_{J}(u):=-\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot S_{J}\big(y\otimes z+z\otimes y+z\otimes z\big).

Even with
y
fixed,
\mathcal{R}_{J}
depends on tail statistics of
z
and its correlations.

Lemma E.1

(Bernstein)
.

There exists
C=C(d)
such that for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

\|S_{J}f\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}},\qquad\|\nabla S_{J}f\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\,2^{J}\|f\|_{L^{2}}.

Moreover, for
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
,

\|S_{J}f\|_{L^{q}}\leq C\,2^{Jd(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})}\|f\|_{L^{p}}.

Proof.

Standard Bernstein estimates for low-frequency Fourier multipliers.
∎

Proposition E.2

(Subgrid forcing controlled by unresolved tail)
.

Let
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with
y=S_{J}u
and
z=Q_{J}u
. Then

\|\mathcal{R}_{J}(u)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\,2^{J}\Big(\|y\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|z\|_{L^{2}}+\|z\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\Big),

with
C=C(d)
.

Proof.

Use
\|\mathbb{P}g\|_{2}\leq\|g\|_{2}
and
\|\nabla S_{J}f\|_{2}\lesssim 2^{J}\|f\|_{2}
, then bound

\|y\otimes z\|_{2}\leq\|y\|_{\infty}\|z\|_{2}
and
\|z\otimes z\|_{2}=\|z\|_{4}^{2}
.
∎

\frac{d}{d\tau}U_{\tau}=v_{\theta}(\tau,U_{\tau};y,m,t),\qquad U_{0}\sim\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid y,m,t),

and set
\widehat{u}:=U_{1}
. This induces the conditional kernel

\widehat{K}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y,m,t)=(\Phi^{y,m,t}_{\theta})_{\#}\,\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid y,m,t),

where
\Phi^{y,m,t}_{\theta}
is the time-
1
flow map.

G.2
Sensitivity of the flow map to memory

Assumption G.1

(Lipschitz regularity)
.

There exist
L_{u},L_{m}\geq 0
such that for all
\tau\in[0,1]
,

\|v_{\theta}(\tau,u;y,m,t)-v_{\theta}(\tau,u^{\prime};y,m,t)\|\leq L_{u}\|u-u^{\prime}\|,\qquad\|v_{\theta}(\tau,u;y,m,t)-v_{\theta}(\tau,u;y,m^{\prime},t)\|\leq L_{m}\|m-m^{\prime}\|.

Lemma G.2

(Flow-map sensitivity in memory)
.

Under Assumption
G.1
, for fixed
(y,t)
and fixed initial state
u_{0}
,

\|\Phi^{y,m,t}_{\theta}(u_{0})-\Phi^{y,m^{\prime},t}_{\theta}(u_{0})\|\leq\Big(\frac{e^{L_{u}}-1}{L_{u}}\Big)L_{m}\,\|m-m^{\prime}\|,

with
(e^{L_{u}}-1)/L_{u}=1
if
L_{u}=0
.

Proof.

Let
U_{\tau},\widetilde{U}_{\tau}
solve the ODE with memories
m,m^{\prime}
and same
u_{0}
.
Then
\frac{d}{d\tau}\|U_{\tau}-\widetilde{U}_{\tau}\|\leq L_{u}\|U_{\tau}-\widetilde{U}_{\tau}\|+L_{m}\|m-m^{\prime}\|
.
Integrate after multiplying by
e^{-L_{u}\tau}
.
∎

G.3
Kernel sensitivity in memory

Assumption G.3

(Base-law continuity in memory)
.

There exists
L_{\nu}\geq 0
such that for all
y,t
and
m,m^{\prime}
,

W_{2}\big(\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid y,m,t),\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid y,m^{\prime},t)\big)\leq L_{\nu}\|m-m^{\prime}\|.

Proposition G.4

(Kernel Lipschitz bound in memory)
.

Under Assumptions
G.1
–
G.3
,

W_{2}\big(\widehat{K}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y,m,t),\widehat{K}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y,m^{\prime},t)\big)\leq\Big[\Big(\frac{e^{L_{u}}-1}{L_{u}}\Big)L_{m}+e^{L_{u}}L_{\nu}\Big]\|m-m^{\prime}\|.

Proof.

Triangle inequality:

W_{2}(\Phi_{m}\#\nu_{m},\Phi_{m^{\prime}}\#\nu_{m^{\prime}})\leq W_{2}(\Phi_{m}\#\nu_{m},\Phi_{m^{\prime}}\#\nu_{m})+W_{2}(\Phi_{m^{\prime}}\#\nu_{m},\Phi_{m^{\prime}}\#\nu_{m^{\prime}}).

Bound the first term by Lemma
G.2
using a shared
U_{0}\sim\nu_{m}
.
Bound the second by Lemma
B.1
with Lipschitz constant
e^{L_{u}}
and Assumption
G.3
.
∎

Assumption G.5

(Kernel Lipschitz in memory)
.

There exists
L_{\mathrm{mem}}\geq 0
such that for all
(y,t)
and
m,m^{\prime}
,

W_{2}\big(\widehat{K}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y,m,t),\widehat{K}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y,m^{\prime},t)\big)\leq L_{\mathrm{mem}}\|m-m^{\prime}\|.

Remark.

Assumption
G.5
holds with

L_{\mathrm{mem}}=\big(\frac{e^{L_{u}}-1}{L_{u}}\big)L_{m}+e^{L_{u}}L_{\nu}
under Proposition
G.4
.
(Compare also Assumption
A.2
, which is the same condition stated in the local notation of the rollout section.)

G.4
One-step kernel decomposition

Let
K^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m,t)
be the true conditional transition law given
(y,m,t)
.
Define the RF approximation error

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RF}}(y,m,t):=W_{2}\big(\widehat{K}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y,m,t),\,K^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m,t)\big).

Then for any candidate memory input
\widehat{m}
,

W_{2}\big(\widehat{K}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y,\widehat{m},t),\,K^{\star}(\cdot\mid y,m,t)\big)\leq L_{\mathrm{mem}}\|\widehat{m}-m\|+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RF}}(y,m,t),

(11)

by the triangle inequality and Assumption
G.5
.

Interpretation.

Inequality (
11
) is the clean separation used throughout the paper:

memory approximation
(
\|\widehat{m}-m\|
) and
conditional generation
(
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RF}}
) enter additively.
This is exactly why it is meaningful to talk about “memory helps more under OOD schedules”: if
\widehat{m}
drifts under compounding
errors, the first term grows, and the sensitivity constant
L_{\mathrm{mem}}
quantifies how much that drift matters at the level
of predicted laws.

G.5
Transport budget and “RF works harder”

If
(\rho_{\tau})_{\tau\in[0,1]}
satisfies
\partial_{\tau}\rho_{\tau}+\nabla\cdot(b_{\tau}\rho_{\tau})=0
on
\mathcal{H}
, then

W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\|b_{\tau}(u)\|^{2}\,d\rho_{\tau}(u)\,d\tau

(Benamou–Brenier, Theorem in 4.1.5
(
7
)
). For deterministic RF,
b_{\tau}=v_{\theta}(\tau,\cdot;c)
and
\rho_{\tau}=\operatorname{Law}(U_{\tau})
.
Combined with Lemma
A.5
, if low modes are pinned and only the tail is randomized, then the
W_{2}
mismatch (hence the
minimal transport energy) is exactly the mismatch of the tail laws.

Connection back to Theorem
A.3
.

When internal-time denoising is prior-driven in many shells, the burden shifts to providing a good prior mean/structure for the tail.
Memory provides precisely such structure (through a learned summary of the past resolved trajectory), reducing the transport needed to
populate missing frequencies.

Appendix H
Conditional-to-law lifting: from conditional mismatch to
\epsilon_{n}

This appendix provides the disintegration/coupling step that turns conditional
W_{2}
bounds into the law-level one-step defect

\epsilon_{n}
in (
6
). Concretely: the rollout bound needs
\epsilon_{n}
, whereas the model is trained and analyzed
in terms of conditional transition laws (kernels). The bridge between the two is that the rollout law is a
mixture
over the
conditioning variable, and mixtures can be coupled componentwise.

Let
\widehat{\mu}_{n}
be the model law at time
t_{n}
and define a conditioning variable

C_{n}:=\Gamma_{n}(\widehat{u}_{n})
in some Polish space
\mathsf{C}
.
Let
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}:=\operatorname{Law}(C_{n})
and disintegrate:

\widehat{\mu}_{n}(du)=\int\widehat{\mu}_{n}(du\mid c)\,\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(dc).

Define the true conditional next-step law under the current rollout distribution by

K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid c):=\operatorname{Law}\big(S_{\Delta t_{n}}(\widehat{u}_{n})\,\big|\,C_{n}=c\big),

so that
(S_{\Delta t_{n}})_{\#}\widehat{\mu}_{n}=\int K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid c)\,d\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(c)
.
The model step defines
c\mapsto\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\cdot\mid c)
so that

\widehat{\mu}_{n+1}=\int\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\cdot\mid c)\,d\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(c)
.
Therefore, by Lemma
A.9
,

\epsilon_{n}^{2}=W_{2}^{2}\!\big((S_{\Delta t_{n}})_{\#}\widehat{\mu}_{n},\widehat{\mu}_{n+1}\big)\leq\int W_{2}^{2}\big(K_{n}^{\star}(\cdot\mid c),\widehat{K}_{\theta,n}(\cdot\mid c)\big)\,d\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(c).

(12)

Summary.

Equation (
12
) is the point where the “kernel view” meets the “rollout view”:
a bound on conditional mismatch (inside the integral) immediately yields a bound on the law-level defect
\epsilon_{n}
.
Plugging that into Lemma
A.8
and Theorem
A.11
yields the final long-horizon control.
In particular, if the conditional mismatch is decomposed as in (
11
), then (
12
) yields a
rollout-relevant bound that separates memory approximation from conditional generation, averaged over the
rollout
distribution
of conditioning variables.

Appendix I
Model and implementation details

This appendix summarizes the model components and training protocol used in all experiments, at a level sufficient to reproduce the
implementation. Unless stated otherwise, the memory-conditioned model and its memoryless baseline share the same backbone,
parameter count up to the memory module, and optimization settings. Throughout,
u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
denotes the state at
physical time step
n
,
\Delta t_{n}
the physical time increment, and
\tau\in[0,1]
the internal rectified-flow time.

I.1
Rectified-flow parameterization and conditioning interface

Rectified-flow ODE and learned vector field.

For each physical step
n
, the model defines a conditional distribution for
u_{n+1}
by sampling the rectified-flow ODE

\frac{d}{d\tau}u_{\tau}\;=\;v_{\theta}\!\big(\tau,u_{\tau};\,\tilde{u}_{n},m_{n},\Delta t_{n}\big),\qquad u_{\tau=0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\qquad\widehat{u}_{n+1}:=u_{\tau=1},

(13)

where
\tilde{u}_{n}
is the conditioning state provided to the model at step
n
(ground truth under teacher forcing, otherwise a
model prediction), and
m_{n}
is the memory context. The memoryless baseline corresponds to
m_{n}\equiv 0
.

Time embeddings.

Both the internal time
\tau
and the physical time step
\Delta t_{n}
are embedded with sinusoidal features and injected via
feature-wise modulation (FiLM/AdaLN-style) into each residual block. Concretely, the embeddings are mapped through learned MLPs to
produce scale/shift parameters applied to normalized activations.

Backbone architecture.

The vector field
v_{\theta}
is parameterized by a U-Net-style multi-resolution convolutional network
(
22
)
:

•

Base width:

d=64
with channel multipliers
(1,2,4)
(feature widths
[64,128,256]
).

•

Down/up-sampling:
factor-
2
pooling/upsampling between levels (three resolutions).

•

Attention:
self-attention at the bottleneck only (4 heads, head dimension 32).

•

Blocks:
residual blocks with two
3\times 3
convolutions per block.

•

Nonlinearity:
GELU.

•

Normalization:
GroupNorm (8 groups) before each convolution.

•

Dropout:

0.1
.

Conditioning interface.

At each resolution level, we concatenate the noised state
u_{\tau}
and the conditioning state
\tilde{u}_{n}
channel-wise and feed the
result to the corresponding U-Net block. For CE-RM (
C=4
channels), this yields an 8-channel input (4 from
u_{\tau}
and 4 from

\tilde{u}_{n}
). Conditioning is thus available throughout the multi-resolution path; memory is injected only at the bottleneck (below).

I.2
Memory module: embedding, temporal aggregation, and injection

The memory mechanism maintains a compact temporal context
m_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}
with
d_{m}=128
.
Operationally, it is built from (i) a per-step embedding
e_{n}
extracted from the conditioning input
\tilde{u}_{n}
and (ii) a short
history of past embeddings processed by an S4 state-space layer
(
9
)
.

Embedding.

At physical step
n
we compute

e_{n}\;=\;\mathsf{E}_{\psi}(\tilde{u}_{n},\Delta t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}},

(14)

where
\mathsf{E}_{\psi}
is a convolutional encoder producing bottleneck features, followed by global average pooling and a linear
projection to
\mathbb{R}^{128}
. The scalar
\Delta t_{n}
is embedded sinusoidally and concatenated before the final projection.

Temporal aggregation (finite history).

We maintain a FIFO buffer of the most recent
L_{\max}=16
embeddings,

\mathcal{B}_{n}:=(e_{n-\ell+1},\ldots,e_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}})^{\ell},\qquad\ell=\min\{n+1,L_{\max}\}.

To obtain a memory context we apply an S4 layer to the padded/truncated sequence
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and read out the last position:

m_{n}\;=\;\mathsf{Readout}\Big(\mathsf{S4}(\mathsf{PadTo}_{L_{\max}}(\mathcal{B}_{n}))\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}.

(15)

We use a standard S4 configuration (HiPPO-style initialization and bilinear discretization), followed by LayerNorm and a learned
projection back to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}
. Because
L_{\max}
is small, recomputing the S4 outputs over the buffer is inexpensive and keeps the
implementation simple and deterministic.

Injection into the vector field.

Memory is injected at the U-Net bottleneck via gated fusion. Let
h\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d_{h}\times H^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}}
denote the bottleneck
features (
d_{h}=256
) and
m\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d_{m}}
the memory context.
We first broadcast
m
spatially and project to
d_{h}
channels:

m_{\mathrm{sp}}:=\mathrm{Repeat}(m[:,:,None,None])\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d_{m}\times H^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}},\qquad m_{\mathrm{proj}}:=\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times 1}(m_{\mathrm{sp}})\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d_{h}\times H^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}}.

We then compute gates
g_{h},g_{m}\in(0,1)^{B\times d_{h}\times H^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}}
and fuse:

g_{h}:=\sigma(\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times 1}(h)),\qquad g_{m}:=\sigma(\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times 1}(m_{\mathrm{proj}})),\qquad\widetilde{h}:=h\odot g_{h}\;+\;m_{\mathrm{proj}}\odot g_{m}.

(16)

This exposes the vector field to temporal context at every internal-time evaluation
\tau
during sampling, while keeping the memory
update frequency aligned with the physical time steps (see below).

I.3
Base law

The base distribution for flow matching is a fixed isotropic Gaussian on the data tensor space:

\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid\tilde{u}_{n},m_{n},\Delta t_{n})\equiv\mathcal{N}(0,I)\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}.

We also experimented with memory-conditioned Gaussian priors, but did not observe consistent improvements on our benchmarks; all
results therefore use the fixed base law.

I.4
Training objectives and data regimes

Training mixes (i) single-step pairs and (ii) short autoregressive windows. The memoryless baseline uses the same batches and losses,
with memory disabled.

Single-step (A2A) supervision.

Given a pair
(u_{n},u_{n+1})
with lead time
\Delta t_{n}
, we draw
u_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and
\tau\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
and form
the linear interpolant

u_{\tau}:=(1-\tau)u_{0}+\tau u_{n+1}.

The rectified target velocity is
v^{\star}:=u_{n+1}-u_{0}
, and we minimize

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|v_{\theta}(\tau,u_{\tau};\,u_{n},m,\Delta t_{n})-(u_{n+1}-u_{0})\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big],

(17)

where
m=0
in A2A mode (and always in the memoryless baseline).

Sequence supervision and teacher forcing.

For sequence windows of length
3
–
12
, we unroll autoregressively to expose the model to compounding errors. At each physical step
within the window we choose the conditioning state
\tilde{u}_{n}
by teacher forcing: with probability
p_{\mathrm{TF}}
we set

\tilde{u}_{n}=u_{n}
(ground truth), otherwise
\tilde{u}_{n}=\widehat{u}_{n}
(model prediction). We then compute
(m_{n},e_{n})
from
\tilde{u}_{n}

and sample
\widehat{u}_{n+1}
with a cheap internal-time integrator (Algorithm
6
).
The per-step loss is still a flow-matching loss against the
ground truth
next state
u_{n+1}
.

Degraded-conditioning (evaluation-time) variant.

In a designated set of experiments, we evaluate robustness under degraded conditioning by replacing the
conditioning input

\tilde{u}_{n}
with a downsample–upsample version

\tilde{u}_{n}^{\mathrm{deg}}:=\mathrm{Upsample}\!\big(\mathrm{AvgPool}_{2\times 2}(\tilde{u}_{n})\big),

while keeping targets
u_{n+1}
at full resolution. We do
not
retrain a separate model for this variant unless explicitly
stated; it is a test-time modification of the conditioning channel.

I.5
Optimization and sampling

Optimization.

We use Adam with gradient clipping (
\|\nabla\|_{2}\leq 1
) and a cosine-annealing learning-rate schedule with warm restarts.
Sequence batches backpropagate through the autoregressive chain (truncated BPTT). To prevent unstable long-range gradients through
memory, we store
detached
embeddings in the FIFO buffer during sequence training (Algorithm
6
).

Sampling and update timing.

At evaluation, sampling integrates the rectified-flow ODE (
13
) from
\tau=0
to
\tau=1
using either Euler or Heun
(RK2) with
K
internal-time steps per physical step.
The memory buffer is updated
once per physical step
, using the embedding extracted from the (possibly degraded) conditioning
state
\tilde{u}_{n}
; it is
not
updated on internal-time substeps.

Appendix K
From flow-matching to invariant-measure error control via one-step kernels

This appendix records a simple but useful implication chain:

\text{small flow-matching (FM) regression risk}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{small one-step conditional law error}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{small multi-step / invariant-measure discrepancy (under mixing).}

The point is conceptual as well as technical: invariant-measure regularizers (e.g. MMD penalties on long rollouts)
target a fixed point of the Markov operator, whereas conditional generative transition models (rectified flows, diffusion forecasters)
target the operator itself. Under standard mixing assumptions, controlling the operator in a suitable weak metric already controls
the induced invariant measure in the same topology.

Notation.

We write the conditioning context as

c:=(u_{n},m,\Delta t)\in\mathcal{C},

where
u_{n}
is the resolved current state,
m
is the memory state, and
\Delta t
is the physical step.
The next-step
true
conditional law is a Markov kernel
K^{\star}:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U})\to[0,1]
,
with
K^{\star}(c,\cdot)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
.
The learned model defines
K_{\theta}(c,\cdot)
analogously.

To keep this appendix self-contained, we use the following safe macros (they do not overwrite existing ones).

K.1
Markov kernels, operators, and IPM discrepancies

We work on a measurable state space
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U}))
and denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
the set of probability measures on it.
A (conditional) Markov kernel is a map
K:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U})\to[0,1]
such that
A\mapsto K(c,A)
is a probability measure
for each
c
, and
c\mapsto K(c,A)
is measurable for each
A
.

Induced Markov operator.

Given a distribution
\pi
on the conditioning contexts
c
(e.g. the distribution of contexts encountered along a rollout),
one obtains an operator on measures on
\mathcal{U}
by integrating out the context.
To avoid notational clutter, we state the operator form for a single “current state” variable
u\in\mathcal{U}
;
the context-dependent case is identical after enlarging the state space to include
(m,\Delta t)
.

Concretely, let
K(u,\cdot)
be a kernel on
\mathcal{U}
and define
P:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
by

(P\mu)(A):=\int_{\mathcal{U}}K(u,A)\,\mu(du),\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U}).

(18)

The learned model induces
P_{\theta}
from
K_{\theta}
by the same formula.

Integral probability metrics (IPMs).

Fix a class
\mathcal{G}
of bounded measurable functions
g:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Define the IPM

D_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{U}}g\,d\mu-\int_{\mathcal{U}}g\,d\nu\right|.

(19)

The canonical example in this appendix is MMD, where
\mathcal{G}
is the RKHS unit ball.

One-step kernel discrepancy.

Define the worst-case conditional discrepancy (the “one-step error”)

\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}:=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}D_{\mathcal{G}}\bigl(K(u,\cdot),K_{\theta}(u,\cdot)\bigr).

(20)

(If the kernel depends on context
c
, replace
\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}
with
\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
.)

Lemma K.1

(Operator discrepancy is controlled by the one-step discrepancy)
.

Assume
u\mapsto D_{\mathcal{G}}(K(u,\cdot),K_{\theta}(u,\cdot))
is measurable.
1
1

1

This holds under standard separability
assumptions on
\mathcal{G}
; for MMD it is immediate when the kernel is measurable and bounded.

Then for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
,

D_{\mathcal{G}}(P\mu,P_{\theta}\mu)\leq\int_{\mathcal{U}}D_{\mathcal{G}}\bigl(K(u,\cdot),K_{\theta}(u,\cdot)\bigr)\,\mu(du)\leq\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}.

(21)

Proof.

For
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\int g\,d(P\mu)-\int g\,d(P_{\theta}\mu)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\int g\,dK(u,\cdot)-\int g\,dK_{\theta}(u,\cdot)\Big)\,\mu(du).

Taking absolute values, bounding by the integral of absolute values, and then taking
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
yields the first inequality;
the second is immediate from the definition of
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}
.
∎

K.2
Mixing turns one-step error into multi-step and invariant-measure error

To keep the bounds explicit, we present a clean contractive (Doeblin-like) regime.
The same message persists under weaker ergodicity hypotheses (Harris recurrence, geometric ergodicity, spectral gaps),
at the expense of more technical constants.

Assumption K.2

(Contractivity in an IPM)
.

There exists
\rho\in[0,1)
such that for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
,

D_{\mathcal{G}}(P\mu,P\nu)\leq\rho\,D_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu,\nu).

(22)

Theorem K.3

(Finite-horizon perturbation bound)
.

Under Assumption
K.2
and the measurability hypothesis of Lemma
K.1
,
for any initial law
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
and all integers
n\geq 1
,

D_{\mathcal{G}}\bigl(P^{n}\mu_{0},\ P_{\theta}^{n}\mu_{0}\bigr)\leq\frac{1-\rho^{n}}{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}.

(23)

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{n}:=D_{\mathcal{G}}(P^{n}\mu_{0},P_{\theta}^{n}\mu_{0})
.
Then
\Delta_{0}=0
and

\Delta_{n+1}=D_{\mathcal{G}}\bigl(P(P^{n}\mu_{0}),P_{\theta}(P_{\theta}^{n}\mu_{0})\bigr)\leq D_{\mathcal{G}}\bigl(P(P^{n}\mu_{0}),P(P_{\theta}^{n}\mu_{0})\bigr)+D_{\mathcal{G}}\bigl(P(P_{\theta}^{n}\mu_{0}),P_{\theta}(P_{\theta}^{n}\mu_{0})\bigr).

The first term is
\leq\rho\,\Delta_{n}
by contractivity, and the second is
\leq\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}
by
Lemma
K.1
with
\mu=P_{\theta}^{n}\mu_{0}
. Hence
\Delta_{n+1}\leq\rho\Delta_{n}+\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}
,
so
\Delta_{n}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\rho^{j}=\frac{1-\rho^{n}}{1-\rho}\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}
.
∎

Corollary K.4

(Invariant-measure discrepancy bound)
.

Assume in addition that
P
and
P_{\theta}
admit invariant measures
\mu^{\star}
and
\mu_{\theta}^{\star}

(i.e.
P\mu^{\star}=\mu^{\star}
and
P_{\theta}\mu_{\theta}^{\star}=\mu_{\theta}^{\star}
).
Then

D_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu^{\star},\mu_{\theta}^{\star})\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}.

(24)

Proof.

Using invariance and the triangle inequality,

D_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu^{\star},\mu_{\theta}^{\star})=D_{\mathcal{G}}(P\mu^{\star},P_{\theta}\mu_{\theta}^{\star})\leq D_{\mathcal{G}}(P\mu^{\star},P\mu_{\theta}^{\star})+D_{\mathcal{G}}(P\mu_{\theta}^{\star},P_{\theta}\mu_{\theta}^{\star})\leq\rho\,D_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu^{\star},\mu_{\theta}^{\star})+\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}},

and rearrange.
∎

Remark K.5

(Interpretation for “MMD invariant-measure regularization”)
.

If
D_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathrm{MMD}_{k}
, then (
24
) states that, in a mixing regime,
controlling the
one-step conditional MMD
controls the stationary MMD.
In particular, a transition model trained to match the one-step conditional law can be seen as optimizing a stronger target:
it controls the operator (kernel), which then controls the fixed point (invariant measure) as a consequence.

K.3
From rectified-flow / flow-matching risk to one-step kernel error

This section provides the front-end implication needed to connect the training objective of rectified flows
to the kernel discrepancy
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}
appearing in Sections
K.1
–
K.2
.

Conditional rectified-flow kernel.

Fix a context
c\in\mathcal{C}
. The learned model generates a random next state by sampling

X_{0}\sim\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c)
and integrating the ODE

\dot{X}_{\tau}=v_{\theta}(\tau,X_{\tau};\,c),\qquad\tau\in[0,1],

(25)

to obtain
X_{1}
. This defines the learned kernel
K_{\theta}(c,\cdot)=\operatorname{Law}(X_{1})
.
At the population level, flow matching posits (and identifies) a target vector field
v^{\star}(\tau,\cdot;\,c)

whose induced transport recovers the true conditional law
K^{\star}(c,\cdot)
.

To make the link precise, it is convenient to phrase FM in terms of a reference path law.

Assumption K.6

(Flow-matching reference path and population target)
.

For each
c\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists a family of measures
(\pi_{\tau}^{c})_{\tau\in[0,1]}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
such that

\pi_{0}^{c}=\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c),\qquad\pi_{1}^{c}=K^{\star}(c,\cdot),

and a measurable vector field
v^{\star}(\tau,x;\,c)
such that
(\pi_{\tau}^{c})
solves the continuity equation

\partial_{\tau}\pi_{\tau}^{c}+\nabla\cdot\bigl(v^{\star}(\tau,\cdot;\,c)\,\pi_{\tau}^{c}\bigr)=0\quad\text{in the sense of distributions on }(0,1)\times\mathcal{U}.

(26)

Moreover, the flow-matching population risk is measured along this reference path:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(c):=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\pi_{\tau}^{c}}\bigl\|v_{\theta}(\tau,Z;\,c)-v^{\star}(\tau,Z;\,c)\bigr\|^{2}\,d\tau.

(27)

Remark K.7
.

Assumption
K.6
is the standard “population” viewpoint on flow matching:

\pi_{\tau}^{c}
is the marginal of the interpolation/coupling used in training (for rectified flow, typically linear interpolation
between base noise and data), and
v^{\star}
is the population regression target (conditional expectation of the velocity under that coupling).

Assumption K.8

(Uniform Lipschitz well-posedness)
.

For each fixed
c
, the maps
x\mapsto v_{\theta}(\tau,x;\,c)
and
x\mapsto v^{\star}(\tau,x;\,c)
are globally Lipschitz with a constant
L_{c}
,
uniformly in
\tau\in[0,1]
, and have at most linear growth so that the ODEs are well posed and moments are finite.

Theorem K.9

(FM risk controls conditional
W_{2}
error)
.

Fix a context
c\in\mathcal{C}
and assume Assumptions
K.6
–
K.8
.
Let
\Phi_{\tau}^{\theta}(\cdot;\,c)
and
\Phi_{\tau}^{\star}(\cdot;\,c)
be the flows of (
25
) with drifts

and
v^{\star}
, respectively, initialized at
\tau=0
.
Define the terminal laws

q_{1}^{c}:=(\Phi_{1}^{\theta}(\cdot;\,c))_{\#}\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c)=K_{\theta}(c,\cdot),\qquad p_{1}^{c}:=(\Phi_{1}^{\star}(\cdot;\,c))_{\#}\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c).

(28)

In particular, if

(as intended by Assumption

), then

W_{2}\bigl(K^{\star}(c,\cdot),K_{\theta}(c,\cdot)\bigr)\leq e^{L_{c}}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(c)}

Let

and define the coupled processes

and

Then
\operatorname{Law}(X_{1}^{\star})=p_{1}^{c}
and
\operatorname{Law}(X_{1}^{\theta})=q_{1}^{c}

,

W_{2}(p_{1}^{c},q_{1}^{c})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl\|X_{1}^{\theta}-X_{1}^{\star}\bigr\|^{2}.

(29)

Set
\Delta_{\tau}:=X_{\tau}^{\theta}-X_{\tau}^{\star}

\dot{\Delta}_{\tau}=v_{\theta}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta};\,c)-v^{\star}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\star};\,c)=\bigl[v_{\theta}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta};\,c)-v_{\theta}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\star};\,c)\bigr]+\bigl[v_{\theta}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\star};\,c)-v^{\star}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\star};\,c)\bigr].

By Lipschitz continuity of

\|\dot{\Delta}_{\tau}\|\leq L_{c}\|\Delta_{\tau}\|+\bigl\|v_{\theta}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\star};\,c)-v^{\star}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\star};\,c)\bigr\|.

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz in

\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{1}\|^{2}\leq e^{2L_{c}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\bigl\|v_{\theta}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\star};\,c)-v^{\star}(\tau,X_{\tau}^{\star};\,c)\bigr\|^{2}\,d\tau.

Finally,

so the right-hand side equals

Combine with (

) to obtain (
28
).
∎

Remark K.10

(What is (and is not) being assumed)
.

The key structural requirement is that the FM population target
v^{\star}
transports
\nu_{0}(\cdot\mid c)
to the true conditional law,
and that the FM risk is measured along the corresponding path marginals
\pi_{\tau}^{c}
.
This is precisely the regime in which flow matching is an operator-learning method: it learns a drift whose induced pushforward matches the target law.
The bound (
28
) is intentionally “first-principles” and does not rely on KL/TV machinery.

K.3.1
From
W_{2}
to MMD (and hence to Appendix-K mixing bounds)

We now convert
W_{2}
control into MMD control for common choices of kernels.
The only ingredient is that RKHS unit-ball functions are Lipschitz (with a kernel-dependent constant).

Lemma K.11

(RKHS unit ball is uniformly Lipschitz under a gradient bound)
.

Let
k:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{1}
positive definite kernel on
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
Assume there exists
L_{k}<\infty
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}}\ \bigl\|\nabla_{x}k(x,\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq L_{k}.

(30)

Then every
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
satisfies
\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq L_{k}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
.
Consequently,

\mathrm{MMD}_{k}(\mu,\nu)\leq L_{k}\,W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq L_{k}\,W_{2}(\mu,\nu).

(31)

Proof.

For
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
x\in\mathcal{U}
, the reproducing property gives

\partial_{x_{i}}f(x)=\bigl\langle f,\ \partial_{x_{i}}k(x,\cdot)\bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}},

hence
|\partial_{x_{i}}f(x)|\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\,\|\partial_{x_{i}}k(x,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
.
Summing over coordinates and using (
30
) yields
\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq L_{k}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, uniformly in
x
,
so
\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq L_{k}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
.

For
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1
,
f
is
L_{k}
-Lipschitz, hence by the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality,

\left|\int f\,d\mu-\int f\,d\nu\right|\leq L_{k}\,W_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Taking the supremum over
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1
gives
\mathrm{MMD}_{k}(\mu,\nu)\leq L_{k}W_{1}(\mu,\nu)
, and
W_{1}\leq W_{2}
yields (
31
).
∎

Corollary K.12

(FM risk controls conditional MMD)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
K.9
and Lemma
K.11
,

\mathrm{MMD}_{k}\bigl(K^{\star}(c,\cdot),K_{\theta}(c,\cdot)\bigr)\leq L_{k}\,e^{L_{c}}\,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(c)}.

(32)

Proof.

Combine Theorem
K.9
with Lemma
K.11
.
∎

From conditional bounds to
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Define the worst-case conditional FM risk and Lipschitz constant over a relevant set of contexts
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{test}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{\sup}:=\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{test}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(c),\qquad L^{\sup}:=\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{test}}}L_{c}.

Then (
32
) implies

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MMD}_{k}}:=\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{test}}}\mathrm{MMD}_{k}\bigl(K^{\star}(c,\cdot),K_{\theta}(c,\cdot)\bigr)\leq L_{k}\,e^{L^{\sup}}\,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{\sup}}.

(33)

Remark K.13

(Supremum vs. average and what the training loss actually controls)
.

The population training loss typically controls an
average
of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(c)
over contexts
c

drawn from the training distribution (and in sequence training, from the rollout-induced distribution under teacher forcing).
To use (
33
) with a supremum, one may either:
(i) restrict
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{test}}
to a compact/high-probability set and invoke uniform generalization,
or (ii) replace
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MMD}_{k}}
by an averaged kernel discrepancy, which is already sufficient for many operator perturbation bounds
(via Lemma
K.1
without the final
\leq\varepsilon_{\mathcal{G}}
step).
This appendix keeps the supremum form for clarity, but the same mechanism works with averages.

K.4
Putting it together: FM yields invariant-measure control (in MMD)

Combining the bounds yields a direct implication from the FM training target to the invariant-measure discrepancy.

Corollary K.14

(FM
\Rightarrow
invariant-measure bound in MMD under mixing)
.

Assume:
(i) the mixing/contractivity hypothesis of Assumption
K.2
holds for
D_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathrm{MMD}_{k}
with constant
\rho\in[0,1)
;
(ii) the FM-to-MMD bound (
33
) holds on
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{test}}
;
and (iii)
P
and
P_{\theta}
admit invariant measures
\mu^{\star}
and
\mu_{\theta}^{\star}
.
Then

\mathrm{MMD}_{k}(\mu^{\star},\mu_{\theta}^{\star})\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MMD}_{k}}\leq\frac{L_{k}\,e^{L^{\sup}}}{1-\rho}\,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{\sup}}.

Proof.

Apply Corollary
K.4
with
D_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathrm{MMD}_{k}
and substitute (
33
).
∎

Remark K.15

(Relation to MMD-regularized training baselines)
.

MMD-regularized baselines typically attempt to directly reduce
\mathrm{MMD}_{k}(\mu^{\star},\mu_{\theta}^{\star})
by matching long-run statistics.
Corollary
K.14
formalizes that, in a mixing regime, learning the one-step kernel via flow matching
already upper bounds the stationary MMD error (up to the amplification factor
(1-\rho)^{-1}
), and does so in the same topology.
This does
not
claim that the contractivity constant
\rho
is easy to estimate for high-dimensional turbulent dynamics;
rather, it states the structural implication: operator (kernel) accuracy is a stronger control knob than fixed-point matching.
