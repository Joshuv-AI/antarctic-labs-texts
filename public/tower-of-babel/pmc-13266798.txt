m6A target gene signatures reveal immune-related diagnostic biomarkers in systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension
Authors: Yansheng Jin, Wen Xu, Maoxiao Fan, Xiaozhong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundN6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is implicated in the development of systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (SSc-PAH). This study aimed to identify and characterize m6A-modified gene signatures in SSc-PAH and elucidate their association with immune dysregulation.MethodsTranscriptome expression profiles from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets were analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) linked to SSc-PAH. Functional enrichment analysis was performed. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted to identify hub genes. m6A target gene expression was assessed via single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). Feature genes were identified through the application of machine learning algorithms. A nomogram was constructed. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed with GeneMANIA. Immune infiltration was assessed through ssGSEA. Drug-gene interactions were explored using the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb).ResultsA total of 371 DEGs were identified in SSc-PAH, predominantly enriched in immune- and inflammation-related pathways. WGCNA revealed SSc-PAH-associated modules strongly correlated with immune activation. Integration with validated m6A target genes identified 20 overlapping genes, indicating altered m6A-related transcriptional activity in SSc-PAH. The derived five-gene signature (KRT10, TNFAIP3, PDK4, TLR4, and YES1) exhibited high diagnostic accuracy across multiple cohorts and was closely associated with immune cell infiltration.ConclusionsA five-gene signature was derived from m6A-related targets, which shows reliable diagnostic potential for SSc-PAH and correlates with the immune microenvironment. These findings provide the first integrative transcriptomic evidence connecting m6A-related regulation with immune dysfunction in SSc-PAH, offering mechanistic insight and potential diagnostic biomarkers.

Key findings

• The study identified a five-gene N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-related signature with high diagnostic accuracy for systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (SSc-PAH), which is closely linked to immune cell infiltration. These findings provide the first evidence connecting m6A modification with immune dysfunction in this disease.

What is known and what is new?

• Immune dysregulation and m6A methylation are involved in pulmonary arterial hypertension. Reliable biomarkers for early diagnosis of SSc-PAH remain limited.

• This study confirms that m6A modification and immune dysregulation are involved in SSc-PAH pathogenesis. It adds the discovery of a novel five-gene diagnostic signature derived from m6A targets and provides the first integrative evidence linking m6A directly to immune dysfunction in this disease.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings imply that targeting the m6A-immune axis could be a novel therapeutic strategy for SSc-PAH. Further validation of the five-gene signature in clinical cohorts and functional studies on the identified genes are urgently needed.

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by progressive fibrosis of the skin and internal organs, driven by persistent vascular injury and immune dysregulation (1,2). Among its complications, pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is one of the most fatal, affecting 8% to 15% of patients (3). Despite advances in diagnostic imaging and hemodynamic assessment, SSc-PAH often remains underdiagnosed until advanced stages due to overlapping symptoms and heterogeneous disease presentation (4). Early and accurate identification is therefore crucial, as timely intervention markedly improves outcomes (5). Current biomarkers, including N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide, lack sufficient specificity and sensitivity for early detection (6), highlighting the need for novel molecular indicators that better reflect disease mechanisms. The search for improved biomarkers is an active area of research, exploring a wide range of candidates from readily available hematological markers like red cell distribution width in related connective tissue diseases to complex molecular signatures as proposed herein (7).

Recent research has revealed that immune cell infiltration plays an important role in the vascular remodeling of SSc-PAH (8). Persistent activation of T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells promotes inflammatory and fibrotic cascades surrounding remodeled pulmonary arteries (9). Such immune-mediated vascular injury not only drives pathological remodeling but also links systemic autoimmunity to pulmonary vascular dysfunction, suggesting that immune-related molecular perturbations may represent promising diagnostic and therapeutic targets (10). Although immune activation is evident, the upstream transcriptional mechanisms governing these disturbances remain elusive, and epitranscriptomic regulation may play an essential role in this process (11).

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, a reversible and abundant RNA methylation mark, has emerged as a critical post-transcriptional regulator of gene expression, influencing messenger RNA (mRNA) stability, splicing, translation, and immune signaling through coordinated actions of “writers”, “erasers”, and “readers” (WER) enzymes (12). m6A modification is known to regulate T-cell differentiation, macrophage activation, and cytokine mRNA stability, thereby linking RNA methylation to immune homeostasis and inflammatory signaling (13). Growing evidence implicates m6A dysregulation in pulmonary vascular remodeling and immune homeostasis in idiopathic and hypoxia-induced PAH (14-16). In addition, altered expression of m6A regulators has been associated with immune infiltration and cytokine signaling in various forms of PAH (17-19). A recent study systematically investigated m6A RNA methylation patterns and their relationship with the immune microenvironment in idiopathic PAH (IPAH), providing a foundational framework for understanding m6A-related mechanisms in PAH (17). Unlike IPAH, which arises without an identifiable underlying condition, SSc-PAH is driven by systemic autoimmunity and profound immune dysregulation. Nevertheless, whether m6A-dependent transcriptional networks contribute to the pathogenesis or immune landscape of SSc-PAH has not been systematically explored.

Given the intertwined roles of immune dysregulation and epitranscriptomic remodeling in PAH, elucidating the expression patterns of m6A target genes may reveal molecular signatures that distinguish patients with SSc-PAH from healthy individuals. We therefore integrated multiple transcriptomic datasets to identify m6A-related gene signatures specific to SSc-PAH, evaluated their diagnostic performance, and examined their association with immune cell infiltration. To our knowledge, no prior study has jointly analyzed m6A-related signatures and immune infiltration in SSc-PAH. This work addressed a critical gap by linking m6A-mediated regulatory mechanisms to immune dysfunction in SSc-PAH and by nominating potential biomarkers for its early and accurate diagnosis. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0313/rc).

Methods

Data source and preprocessing

Transcriptome expression data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Two peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) microarray datasets, GSE33463 and GSE22356, were integrated to construct the training cohort. The GSE33463 dataset included 42 SSc-PAH and 41 healthy controls (20). The GSE22356 dataset contained 10 SSc-PAH samples without matching control subjects (21). To maximize statistical power, the 10 SSc-PAH samples from GSE22356 were merged with the 42 SSc-PAH samples from GSE33463, forming a combined SSc-PAH group (n=52). The 41 healthy samples from GSE33463 were used as the unified control group.

Batch effects between datasets were corrected using the “ComBat” function in the “sva” package. Post-correction principal component analysis (PCA) confirmed that SSc-PAH samples from both datasets exhibited comparable global expression profiles, supporting their integration for downstream analyses. Probes were aligned with gene names, and blank probes were excluded. The median value was used as the representative expression level for genes linked to multiple probes. Data were normalized using the normalizeBetweenArrays function to ensure cross-sample comparability.

GSE48149 and GSE73674 were employed as exploratory assessment in independent small cohorts sets. GSE48149 contains lung tissue samples from 10 SSc-PAH patients and 9 normal donors used in the present study for validation (other subgroups were not analyzed) (22,23). GSE73674 includes endothelial progenitor cell (EPC)-derived endothelial cells from SSc-PAH (n=6), SSc-non-PAH (n=20), and healthy individuals (n=9), each profiled under hypoxic and basal conditions. For validation, we used 6 SSc-PAH and 9 healthy controls under both conditions. Sample details are summarized in Table 1. In total, the exploratory assessment in independent small cohorts included 10 SSc-PAH and 9 control samples in GSE48149 and 6 SSc-PAH and 9 control samples in GSE73674.

m6A WER target genes in humans and mice were collected, organized, and analyzed by the m6A2Target database (http://rm2target.canceromics.org/#/home), resulting in a set of 701 experimentally validated m6A target genes for the human genome assembly hg38 as documented in research articles. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Differential expression analysis

In the training cohort, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with SSc-PAH were identified using “Limma”. Genes meeting the criteria of |log fold change (FC)| >0.5 and P value <0.05 were defined as DEGs, which are widely accepted thresholds in transcriptomic analyses to ensure both statistical significance and biological relevance (24,25).

Enrichment analysis

Using the R package ClusterProfiler, we examined enrichment in Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Adjusted P<0.05 was considered significant.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

A co-expression network was developed using “WGCNA” in R to explore gene modules linked to SSc-PAH. The top 50% of genes showing the greatest variance in the training cohort were selected to focus on the most dynamically expressed genes and minimize computational noise from low-variance features. The ideal soft threshold was identified using the scale-free topology criterion, followed by the construction of a weighted connectivity matrix and an overlap matrix. Gene modules with a similarity merging threshold of 0.25 and containing over 30 genes were identified using hierarchical clustering. Unique colors were assigned to each module. Modules underwent additional screening by assessing their correlation with phenotype. Hub genes were selected based on |module membership (MM)| >0.6 and |gene significance (GS)| >0.5. The intersection of DEGs, hub genes, and m6A target genes was subjected to further analysis.

m6A_targetScore calculation

Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was conducted to evaluate the expression of the intersecting genes in each sample. An m6A_targetScore was calculated using “GSVA” as an expression-based proxy for m6A-related transcriptional activity, which reflects downstream effects rather than direct methylation levels. Based on the median m6A_targetScore, samples were classified into high- and low-score groups, with stratification visualized through PCA. KEGG pathway differences between these groups were analyzed using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) with “ClusterProfiler” in R.

Identification of feature genes

To prioritize feature genes and minimize algorithm-specific bias, feature genes were identified from the overlapping genes using three complementary machine learning approaches: support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and random forest (RF). These methods were chosen for their complementary strengths: SVM-RFE is effective for high-dimensional data by progressively eliminating the least important features (26); LASSO regression selects key variables by shrinking less relevant ones to zero using L1 regularization (27); RF identifies important features by aggregating results from multiple decision trees and can capture complex, non-linear relationships (28).

SVM-RFE was performed using the caret R package. The recursive feature elimination process was implemented with a radial basis function kernel SVM (method = “svmRadial” in the rfe function), and model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, as specified within rfeControl (functions = “caretFuncs”). The optimal feature subset was determined as the one yielding the highest classification accuracy.

LASSO regression was conducted using the glmnet package. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined according to the “minimum mean cross-validated error plus one standard error” (1-SE) criterion to prevent overfitting. Genes with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were selected as candidate feature genes.

For the RF algorithm, the randomForest R package was used to rank gene importance. The model error stabilized when the number of trees reached 3,000 (ntree =3,000), and the minimum error was achieved when the number of variables tried at each split was 2 (mtry =2). These parameters were therefore selected for the final RF model. Genes with a mean decrease in Gini index >1 were selected. This threshold was determined empirically based on the distribution of importance scores in our data, as it provided a clear cutoff point to distinguish influential genes from those with minimal contribution to model purity (29,30). Because the absolute magnitude of Gini importance is not directly comparable across datasets, the >1 cutoff was determined empirically based on the distribution of importance scores that distinguished informative from low-importance genes.

To reduce the potential bias from any single method, only genes identified by all three algorithms were defined as feature genes (31).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis

The diagnostic value of the feature genes was evaluated using ROC curves with the pROC package. For each gene in each dataset, the optimal cutoff threshold was determined using Youden’s J statistic, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity. This approach provides an optimal trade-off between true positive and false positive rates. Alternative thresholds produced comparable area under the ROC curve (AUC) trends, confirming that the diagnostic performance of the selected genes was robust to the choice of cutoff.

Nomogram construction

The Rms package in R was utilized for constructing and evaluating the nomogram. Feature gene expression was incorporated to develop the nomogram, and visualization was achieved using the Regplot function from the Rms package. Clinical decision-making performance was evaluated using calibration curves generated with “Calibrate” in the Rms package, along with decision curve analysis (DCA) curves created with “ggDCA”.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks

PPI networks were developed to investigate the relationships between feature genes and functionally related genes, utilizing GeneMANIA (http://www.genemania.org) for network construction.

Immune infiltration assessment

The enrichment scores of 28 immune cell subtypes were acquired using ssGSEA as previously reported (32). The ssGSEA analysis employed “GSVA” to transform individual sample gene expression data into matrices that represent expression values for predefined gene sets. Immune cell abundance differences between the two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test. Pearson correlation was applied to assess the associations between immune cells and feature genes.

Drug-gene interaction

Relationships between feature genes and drugs were investigated using the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb, www.dgidb.org). Cytoscape (v3.9.1) was employed to visualize the interaction network (33).

Statistical analysis and visualization

Analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.0). PCA and its visualization were conducted with the FactoMineR and Factoextra packages. Heatmaps were generated using “Pheatmap”. Venn diagrams were generated with “Ggvenn”. ROC curves were displayed using “pROC”. Gene correlations were assessed and displayed with “Corrplot”. Gene positions on chromosomes were visualized using RCircos. Unless otherwise specified, “Ggplot2” or the base plot function was used for plotting. Pearson correlation tested associations. The Wilcoxon test compared continuous variables between groups. Significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

DEG screening and characterization

DEGs associated with SSc-PAH were identified by analyzing PBMC samples from 52 SSc-PAH cases and 51 controls (41 healthy, 10 SSc-non-PAH). Separate clustering patterns were observed among different datasets prior to batch effect correction (Figure 1A,1B). Following batch effect correction, samples from different datasets clustered together (Figure 1C,1D). Differential gene expression analysis revealed 371 DEGs (197 upregulated, 174 downregulated) in SSc-PAH relative to the controls (Figure 1E, table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037jtd-2026-1-0313-1.xlsx). The top 40 DEGs’ expression profiles are displayed for each sample in Figure 1F. As shown in Figure 1G and the online table (available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037jtd-2026-1-0313-2.xlsx), the DEGs were highly associated with GO terms linked to immune response, such as “response to virus” and “cytokine receptor binding”. The most significant enriched pathways were also immune-related, including “Influenza A” as well as “NOD-like” and “Toll-like” receptor signaling pathways (Figure 1H, table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037jtd-2026-1-0313-3.xlsx).

Identification of hub genes associated with SSc-PAH through WGCNA

Hub genes related to SSc-PAH were identified by conducting WGCNA on the training cohort. One outlier was excluded from the initial sample clustering (Figure S1A). The soft threshold β was set to 5 to achieve optimal scale-free topology and connectivity (Figure S1B). The DEGs were categorized into 14 modules by hierarchical clustering (Figure S1C), with the turquoise (R=0.67) and the blue (R=−0.71) modules exhibiting the most positive and negative correlations with SSc-PAH, respectively (Figure S1D). A total of 309 hub genes with |GS| >0.5 and |MM| >0.6 were identified in the blue module (Figure S1E, table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037jtd-2026-1-0313-4.xlsx) and 506 in the turquoise module (Figure S1F, table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037jtd-2026-1-0313-5.xlsx).

Differential expression of m6A target genes and assessment of m6A_targetScore

Differentially expressed m6A target genes were identified to explore m6A modification patterns in SSc-PAH. Integration of WGCNA hub genes with DEGs and m6A target genes revealed 20 overlapping genes (Figure 2A), with the chromosomal distribution displayed in Figure 2B. The m6A_targetScore, representing an expression-derived indicator of m6A-related transcriptional activity, was calculated for each sample in the training cohort using the ssGSEA algorithm. The SSc-PAH group exhibited significantly lower scores compared to the control group, indicating a global reduction in the expression-based activity of m6A-regulated genes (Figure 2C). Furthermore, an interaction network revealed a complex regulatory network between the WER of m6A marks and the overlapping genes (Figure 2D). This core set of 20 genes, residing at the intersection of differential expression, co-expression relevance, and m6A regulation, constitutes a high-confidence candidate pool for subsequent machine learning–based identification of diagnostic signatures in SSc-PAH.

The correlation of m6A_targetScore with immune cell infiltration and molecular pathway activation in SSc-PAH

To examine the relationship between the m6A target gene expression, immune cell infiltration, and molecular pathway activation in SSc-PAH, we stratified the training cohort into high and low m6A_targetScore groups based on the median score. The heatmap illustrated differential gene expression patterns between the two groups (Figure 3A). PCA further demonstrated a clear separation of the groups (Figure 3B). The immune infiltration analysis showed notable variations in eight immune cell types, including upregulation in eosinophils, central memory CD4+ T cells, activated CD4+ T cells, natural killer (NK) T cells, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, Th1 cells, and Th2 cells, as well as downregulation in gamma delta (γδ) T cells (Figure 3C). A notably negative correlation was observed between m6A_targetScore and γδ T cells, while strong positive correlations were observed with Th2, activated CD4+ T, and Tfh cells (Figure 3D). GSEA of KEGG pathways between the two groups showed activation of several key pathways in the high-score group, including AMPK signaling and apoptosis (Figure 3E, table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037jtd-2026-1-0313-6.xlsx).

Identification of m6A target gene signatures for SSc-PAH

To identify the most robust biomarkers among the 20 candidate m6A target genes, we applied three machine learning algorithms: LASSO regression, SVM-RFE, and RF, which generated 8, 19, and 10 features, respectively (Figure 4A-4E). By intersecting genes concurrently identified by all three algorithms, we derived a high-confidence five-gene signature comprising Keratin 10 (KRT10), tumor necrosis factor alpha-induced protein 3 (TNFAIP3), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4), Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), and YES proto-oncogene 1 (YES1) (Figure 4F). Among them, KRT10, TNFAIP3, and YES1 were significantly downregulated, whereas PDK4 and TLR4 were upregulated in SSc-PAH compared with controls (Figure 4G). These five genes represent a robust m6A-related diagnostic signature for SSc-PAH validated through multi-model convergence.

Diagnostic potential of feature genes in multiple SSc-PAH datasets

The five-gene signature demonstrated excellent diagnostic potential in the training cohort, with all genes achieving AUCs greater than 0.8 (Figure 5A), indicating high predictive accuracy.

To assess its clinical relevance in primary disease tissue, we evaluated the signature in an independent lung tissue dataset (GSE48149). Notably, KRT10, TNFAIP3, and PDK4 maintained strong diagnostic performance (AUCs >0.7), whereas TLR4 and YES1 showed moderate accuracy (AUCs =0.567 and 0.644, respectively) (Figure 5B). This finding suggests potential tissue-specific variation in biomarker expression and highlights the stronger translational relevance of KRT10, TNFAIP3, and PDK4 in pulmonary pathology.

In another external cohort using EPC-derived endothelial cells (GSE73674), the signature’s predictive ability varied under different microenvironmental conditions. Under hypoxic exposure, KRT10 and TNFAIP3 achieved moderate AUCs of 0.611 and 0.667, while under basal conditions, their performance improved markedly (AUCs =0.796 and 0.704, respectively) (Figure 5C,5D). These results indicate that environmental factors such as hypoxia may influence the diagnostic power of m6A-associated biomarkers in vascular cells. Detailed performance metrics, including AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cutoffs for each ROC analysis, are summarized in the online table (available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037jtd-2026-1-0313-7.xlsx).

Furthermore, to confirm that the observed performance was not an artifact of dataset merging, we evaluated the five genes in the individual training datasets. All genes consistently exhibited strong discriminative capacity in both GSE33463 (AUCs >0.8) and GSE22356 (AUCs >0.7), validating the robustness and reproducibility of the gene signature across different patient cohorts (Figure 5E,5F).

Lastly, a nomogram integrating all five feature genes demonstrated excellent calibration and clinical applicability in the training cohort (Figure 6A,6B). DCA further confirmed the superior clinical benefit of the combined model compared to single-gene predictors (Figure 6C). Exploratory assessment in independent small cohorts of the nomogram was not performed due to dataset limitations, which will be addressed in future studies.

Feature gene interactions and their correlation with immune cell infiltration

Enrichment scores of 28 immune cell subpopulations were compared between SSc-PAH patients and controls from the training cohort to investigate their immunological disparities, with 16 immune cell types exhibiting significant differences (Figure 6D). The immune cell distribution in each sample is shown in Figure 6E.

The correlations among feature genes are visualized via a PPI network (Figure 7A). Correlation analysis revealed significant interconnectivity among all feature genes (Figure 7B). In the training cohort, significant associations were observed between immune cells and feature genes (Figure 7C). For instance, TNFAIP3 positively correlated with activated CD4 T cells and negatively correlated with macrophages (Figure 7D,7E). Drug-gene interaction network analysis

To elucidate the pharmacological relevance of the feature genes, we used the DGIdb database to map out the interactions between these genes and various drugs. As shown in Figure 8 and the online table (available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037jtd-2026-1-0313-8.xlsx), YES1, TLR4, PDK4, and TNFAIP3 were associated with various drugs. For instance, TNFAIP3 was linked to TNF-alpha inhibitors. These data suggest that YES1, TLR4, PDK4, and TNFAIP3 are potential targets for drug repurposing or the development of novel therapeutic strategies in SSc-PAH treatment.

Discussion

This work represents the first integrative transcriptomic investigation connecting m6A targets with immune cell infiltration in SSc-PAH. Our findings delineate a potential epitranscriptomic–immune axis underlying vascular remodeling in autoimmune PAH. By combining multi-cohort transcriptomic profiling and machine learning-based feature selection, we identified KRT10, TNFAIP3, PDK4, TLR4, and YES1 as m6A-related biomarkers capable of distinguishing SSc-PAH patients from healthy individuals. These genes demonstrated diagnostic potential across multiple datasets and are associated with various drugs, suggesting their relevance as therapeutic targets for SSc-PAH.

This study establishes a critical link between m6A-mediated epitranscriptomics and immune dysfunction in SSc-PAH. However, it is important to recognize that the pathogenesis of PAH is multifactorial, involving not only genetic and immune pathways but also systemic factors such as metabolic and nutritional status. For example, emerging evidence suggests a significant association between vitamin deficiencies and the risk or severity of PAH, highlighting another potential axis for therapeutic intervention (34). By applying the ssGSEA algorithm, we derived the m6A_targetScore, an expression-based proxy for m6A-related transcriptional activity. SSc-PAH patients showed significantly lower scores compared to controls, suggesting reduced activity of an m6A target gene signature. This expression-based approach effectively captures downstream regulatory perturbations of m6A-related pathways in patient-derived PBMCs. The observed downregulation of m6A-related transcriptional activity may reflect enhanced activity of certain m6A “readers” such as YTHDF2, which promote mRNA decay, or altered balance among m6A “writers” and “erasers” that collectively reduce transcript stability in immune-related genes. The relationship between m6A modification and PAH is complex and seems to vary depending on the specific context and study. Zeng et al. have shown increased m6A methylation levels in lung tissues of monocrotaline-induced PAH in rats. However, the dysregulation of m6A-related enzymes, including downregulated FTO and ALKBH5 as well as increased METTL3 and YTHDF1, points to a complex regulatory mechanism of m6A modification in PAH (14). The study by Shen et al. on SSc in a mouse model has revealed complex alterations in m6A methylation, showing both increases and decreases across 843 mRNAs (35). This complexity in methylation changes implies a nuanced role of m6A modification in SSc-PAH pathogenesis.

SSc-PAH is known to involve dysregulation of the immune system (36). To better understand the immunological aspects of the disease and identify novel therapeutic targets or biomarkers for patient stratification and personalized treatment, we explored whether m6A target gene activity is correlated with immune cell infiltration. We found that the m6A_targetScore in SSc-PAH correlated negatively with γδ T cells and positively with Th2 cells, activated CD4 T cells, and Tfh cells. This is consistent with the observed changes in immune cell abundance between SSc-PAH patients and controls, including an elevation in γδ T cell abundance and a reduction in activated CD4, Th2, and Tfh cell abundance in SSc-PAH patients with lower m6A_targetScores compared to the controls with higher m6A_targetScores. This observation also aligns well with results from our previous study, which highlighted distinct immune profiles between patients with SSc-PAH and SSc-non-PAH. Specifically, SSc-PAH patients exhibited an increased proportion of monocytes and neutrophils, along with reduced naïve and inactive memory CD4 T cells, as well as γδ T cells (37). This shift in immune cell proportions in SSc-PAH underscores a dysregulated immune landscape, where alterations in m6A methylation might drive an imbalance in immune cell dynamics, thus exacerbating the disease pathology.

γδ T cells are involved in immune surveillance, inflammation, and tissue homeostasis (38). In SSc, γδ T cells may produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, promoting fibroblast activation, collagen deposition, and vascular remodeling, which are hallmarks of the disease (39). Additionally, the aberrant activation or dysregulation of γδ T cells could influence vascular endothelial cell function (40), contributing to the vascular pathology observed in SSc-PAH. While specific studies directly linking γδ T cells to SSc-PAH pathogenesis are limited, our data suggest that γδ T cells potentially mediate immune responses and vascular remodeling associated with PAH.

Activated CD4, Th2, and Tfh cells are intricately involved in orchestrating immune responses, including antibody production, cytokine secretion, and modulation of other immune cells, thereby contributing to vascular remodeling, fibrosis, and inflammation in SSc-PAH (41-43). Studies have shown that m6A methylation can regulate the immune system, influencing T cell development, differentiation, and function. The depletion of the m6A demethylase ALKBH5 leads to an expansion of γδ T cells through Jag1/Notch2 signaling regulation (44). Inhibiting METTL3, an m6A writer, exacerbates systemic lupus erythematosus by activating CD4+ T cells and disrupting effector T cell balance (45). METTL3 deletion in CD4+ T cells disrupts Tfh cell differentiation by impairing the expression of key Tfh signature genes (46). METTL3 mitigates Th2 cell differentiation in type 2 asthma by modulating SOX5 expression through m6A methylation (47). Thus, m6A methylation via WER may modulate T cell-mediated immune responses, influencing disease pathogenesis in SSc-PAH. To our knowledge, this study is the first to observe an association between m6A target gene expression and immune cell infiltration in SSc-PAH clinical samples.

The study highlighted KRT10, TNFAIP3, PDK4, TLR4, and YES1 as feature genes in SSc-PAH. KRT10, TNFAIP3, and YES1 showed decreased expression, while PDK4 and TLR4 were upregulated in SSc-PAH patients. These genes, particularly KRT10 and TNFAIP3, demonstrated diagnostic efficacy across diverse datasets in distinguishing SSc-PAH from healthy controls. It is worth noting that the diagnostic performance of selected genes may vary slightly depending on the cutoff strategy. In this study, we adopted Youden’s J statistic to determine the optimal threshold, as it balances sensitivity and specificity. Although alternative methods, such as maximizing specificity or minimizing FDR, may shift the trade-off between true positive and false positive rates, the feature genes demonstrated consistently high predictive accuracy across multiple datasets, supporting the reliability of our findings.

Moreover, these genes are linked to specific immune responses and drug interactions. KRT10 is implicated in SSc pathogenesis, with its expression linked to interferon signaling modulation in keratinocyte populations (48). In addition, KRT10 has been identified as a significantly upregulated gene in hypoxic lung tissue, suggesting its involvement in the pathophysiological response to hypobaric hypoxia and potentially as a biomarker for hypoxia-induced lung conditions (49). YES1 belongs to the Src family of tyrosine kinases, playing an important role in cell growth, differentiation, and survival. In PAH, YES1 is involved in pathological vascular remodeling (50). However, neither KRT10 nor YES1 has been reported in SSc-PAH. In our study, we novelly identified KRT10 and YES1 as key DEGs with substantial diagnostic potential for SSc-PAH, providing a new avenue for understanding the disease mechanism.

TNFAIP3 encodes the A20 protein, modulating inflammation and immune responses by suppressing NF-κB signaling and regulating ubiquitination (51). Consistent with our study, Xu et al. have identified TNFAIP3 as one of the hub genes in SSc-PAH (52). TNFAIP3 polymorphism rs5029939 is significantly associated with SSc susceptibility, particularly affecting subphenotypes like diffuse cutaneous SSc, fibrosing alveolitis, and PAH (53). Our analysis further revealed that TNFAIP3 expression correlated positively with activated CD4+ T cells and negatively with macrophages, consistent with its function as a negative regulator of NF-κB signaling (54). The downregulation of TNFAIP3 in SSc-PAH may therefore contribute to NF-κB hyperactivation, leading to enhanced T-cell activation and proinflammatory macrophage polarization, which could mechanistically underlie the immune cell imbalances observed in our study.

Among the feature genes, PDK4 and TLR4 exhibited increased expression in SSc-PAH patients. PDK4 catalyzes the phosphorylation and subsequent inhibition of pyruvate dehydrogenase. In SSc-PAH, PDK4 inhibition by TNFα may contribute to increased pyruvate dehydrogenase activity, potentially influencing right ventricular remodeling and myocardial energy substrate utilization (55). PDK4 has been shown to promote proinflammatory macrophage polarization and support M1 differentiation through metabolic reprogramming (56). In our analysis, PDK4 expression correlated positively with macrophage abundance, consistent with its function as a metabolic checkpoint linking inflammation and energy metabolism. Elevated PDK4 in vascular and immune cells may thus contribute to the inflammatory remodeling observed in SSc-PAH (57). In addition, TLR4 has been identified as a potential therapeutic target in SSc-PAH through gene microarray data analysis due to its role in mediating pro-inflammatory responses (58). The upregulation of TLR4 and its correlation with specific immune cell populations in our data align with its canonical function in innate immune activation (59). Overexpression of TLR4 may potentiate downstream cytokine signaling and skew T cell polarization, thereby contributing to the elevated inflammatory infiltrates in SSc-PAH.

It is important to contextualize our findings within the broader landscape of m6A research in PAH. Gao et al. recently conducted a comprehensive analysis of m6A RNA methylation patterns and immune infiltration in IPAH, identifying distinct m6A regulator signatures and their correlation with immune cell subsets (17). While our study shares methodological similarities with their work, both employing WGCNA, m6A-related gene analysis, machine learning, and ssGSEA based immune infiltration assessment, the disease contexts are fundamentally different. IPAH is a diagnosis of exclusion with no identifiable underlying cause, whereas SSc-PAH arises in the setting of systemic autoimmunity, characterized by widespread immune dysregulation, chronic inflammation, and fibrotic vascular remodeling. These pathophysiological differences are reflected in the distinct gene signatures and immune infiltration patterns observed between the two studies. For instance, while Gao et al. identified m6A regulators such as METTL3 and YTHDF2 as key players in IPAH, our study highlights m6A target genes including KRT10, TNFAIP3, and PDK4 as central to SSc-PAH pathology. By comparing these signatures, our study builds upon the framework established by Gao et al. and provides disease-specific insights into the autoimmune subtype of PAH. This comparison underscores the importance of considering underlying etiology when interpreting m6A-related transcriptional programs in PAH and suggests that therapeutic strategies targeting the m6A immune axis may need to be tailored to specific disease subtypes.

The results of exploratory assessment in independent small cohorts revealed variability in the diagnostic performance of the feature genes in different datasets and sample types. While KRT10, TNFAIP3, and PDK4 maintained strong discriminative power in lung tissue samples (GSE48149), TLR4 and YES1 exhibited relatively lower AUC values, and their predictive ability varied under hypoxic and basal conditions in EPC-derived endothelial cells (GSE73674). This heterogeneity, while initially appearing as a limitation, provides a biologically meaningful insight into the tissue-dependent nature of these biomarkers. Validating a PBMC-derived signature in lung tissue and endothelial cells inherently introduces biological variability, as systemic immune circulation and local vascular microenvironments possess distinct transcriptomic landscapes. PBMC-derived signatures primarily reflect systemic immune alterations circulating in peripheral blood, whereas lung tissue and endothelial cells capture local vascular remodeling processes occurring within the pulmonary microenvironment. The superior performance of TLR4 and YES1 in PBMCs coupled with their moderate accuracy in lung tissue suggests that these genes may be more indicative of systemic immune activation than of local parenchymal pathology. This tissue-specific discrepancy illustrates that systemic immune alterations may not fully parallel local vascular events, underscoring the complexity of SSc-PAH pathogenesis. PBMC-derived transcriptomes primarily capture systemic immune alterations, whereas lung tissue and endothelial cells reflect local vascular remodeling and hypoxia-related responses. Environmental conditions such as hypoxia further modulate gene activity, as hypoxic stress can reprogram metabolic and inflammatory pathways in endothelial cells (60). Collectively, these findings highlight the tissue- and context-dependent nature of m6A-related transcriptional signatures in SSc-PAH and emphasize the importance of multi-tissue validation in future studies.

Limitations of the study

The study is limited by the use of PBMC transcriptomic data, which may not fully reflect lung-specific molecular changes in SSc-PAH. Sample sizes in some datasets were small, particularly the external cohorts GSE73674 and GSE48149, which limits the generalizability of our five-gene signature. Functional validation of identified genes was not performed. While the drug-gene interaction analysis offers potential therapeutic insights, clinical applicability remains to be established. Moreover, immune cell infiltration estimated by ssGSEA is an inference based on transcriptomic profiles rather than direct cell quantification methods such as flow cytometry, and therefore warrants further experimental validation. We also acknowledge that the m6A_targetScore is an indirect measure that does not directly reflect RNA methylation status. Our approach was designed as an in silico hypothesis-generating tool to prioritize promising m6A-related gene signatures. To address this limitation, we propose future experimental validation using methylated RNA immunoprecipitation combined with quantitative polymerase chain reaction (MeRIP-qPCR) or methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) to directly assess the m6A modification status of the five signature genes (KRT10, TNFAIP3, PDK4, TLR4, YES1) in independent SSc-PAH patient samples. Additionally, RNA immunoprecipitation combined with quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RIP-qPCR) experiments are planned to investigate the binding relationships between key m6A writers and erasers including METTL3 and FTO and these target genes to establish direct mechanistic links. Lastly, as this study is based on correlative transcriptomic analysis, it cannot determine causal relationships between m6A regulation, gene expression, and immune infiltration. Whether m6A dysregulation drives immune alterations or reflects secondary immune activation remains to be clarified.

Conclusions

In summary, our study identified m6A target genes associated with SSc-PAH, revealing dysregulated pathways related to immune response. The diagnostic potential of feature genes was validated in multiple datasets, supporting their clinical relevance. Furthermore, the correlation between feature gene expression and immune cell infiltration suggests a complex interplay between epitranscriptomic regulation and immune dysfunction in SSc-PAH. While these findings provide insight into m6A-associated molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets, further mechanistic and experimental studies are needed to validate these signatures and clarify the precise role of m6A modification in SSc-PAH pathogenesis.
