Permuton and local limits for the Luce model
Jacopo Borga MIT Sourav Chatterjee Stanford University Persi Diaconis Stanford University

Abstract
We investigate the asymptotic properties of permutations drawn from the Luce model, a natural probabilistic framework in which permutations are generated sequentially by sampling without replacement, with selection probabilities proportional to prescribed positive weights. These permutations arise in applications such as ranking models, the Tsetlin library, and related Markov processes. Under minimal assumptions on the weights, we establish a permuton limit theorem describing the global behavior of Luce-distributed permutations and derive an explicit density of the limiting permuton. We further compute limiting pattern densities and analyze the differences between exact Luce permutations and their permuton approximations. We also study the local convergence of these permutations, proving a quenched Benjamini–Schramm limit and a central limit theorem for consecutive pattern occurrences. Finally, we prove a central limit theorem for the number of inversions.

Introduction

1.1
The Luce model on permutations

We consider the Luce model on permutations defined as follows. Fix some positive real weights
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
with total sum
w_{n}=\theta_{1}+\dots+\theta_{n}
and consider the function

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}}}\right):=\frac{\theta_{1}}{w_{n}}\times\frac{\theta_{2}}{w_{n}-\theta_{1}}\times\frac{\theta_{3}}{w_{n}-\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}}\times\cdots\times\frac{\theta_{n}}{\theta_{n}}.

(1.1)

Note that the weights
\theta_{i}
may depend on
n
, and could therefore be written as
\theta_{i}^{n}
. For simplicity, we will suppress this dependence in our notation and write simply
\theta_{i}
throughout.
We consider the following distribution, called the
Luce distribution
, on the set
\mathcal{S}_{n}
of permutations of
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
: for all
\sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{n})=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{\sigma_{n}^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{\sigma_{n}^{-1}(n)}}}\right).

(1.2)

Sometimes, we write
\Luce(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
for the law of a random Luce-distributed permutation of size
n
with weights
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
. Note that multiplying all weights by a fixed constant does not change the Luce distribution. We will therefore freely rescale when convenient (e.g., replacing
\theta_{i}
by
\theta_{i}/n
, or normalizing the
\theta_{i}
so that
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=n
or
1
).

Sampling and clarification on inverses.

One can sample a Luce-distributed permutation
\sigma_{n}
as follows.
Place
n
numbered balls with weights
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
into an urn.
At each step, withdraw one ball (sampling without replacement) with probability proportional to its weight relative to the remaining balls. Record the order in which the balls are withdrawn, which we denote (in one-line notation; see below for more details on notation) by

\tau=\tau_{1}\,\tau_{2}\,\dots\,\tau_{n},

where
\tau_{j}
is the label of the
j
-th ball drawn.
From this draw order we obtain a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
by declaring

\sigma(\tau_{j})=j\quad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,n,

that is,
\sigma(i)
is the
position
at which label
i
is drawn.
Equivalently,
\sigma^{-1}(j)=\tau_{j}
gives the label drawn at step
j
.
Thus, in (
1.2
), the argument of
p
is simply the sequence of weights in the actual draw order.

Example.

Suppose we have three balls labeled
1,2,3
with weights
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}>0
.
The probability that the draw order is

\tau=312,

i.e. ball
3
is drawn first, then ball
1
, and finally ball
2
, is

\frac{\theta_{3}}{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}}\;\times\;\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}}\;\times\;\frac{\theta_{2}}{\theta_{2}}\;=\;\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{3}}{(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3})(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})}.

This corresponds to the permutation
\sigma
with

\sigma(3)=1,\ \sigma(1)=2,\ \sigma(2)=3
, i.e.
\sigma=231
.
Note that evaluating (
1.2
) with

(\theta_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\theta_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\theta_{\sigma^{-1}(3)})=(\theta_{3},\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

yields exactly the same probability.

Remark 1.1
.

Note that our definition in (
1.2
) differs from the one provided, for instance, in
[
CDK23
]
. More precisely, if
\sigma\sim\Luce(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
is defined as in (
1.2
), then
\sigma^{-1}
is
\Luce(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
-distributed according to the definition in
[
CDK23
]
. We made this choice to simplify some later notation. In this paper, we will consistently use our definition in (
1.2
) and translate the results from
[
CDK23
]
in terms of our definition when we refer to them.

[
CDK23
, Section 2]
provides a literature review. Several applied problems are shown to give rise to the Luce model, ranging from the Tsetlin library problem; the stationary distribution of a Markov chain that, at each step, selects a card labeled
i
with probability proportional to
\theta_{i}
and moves it to the top; certain problems in psychology, as well as applications to poker and horse racing. The main results of
[
CDK23
]
determine the limiting distribution of
\sigma^{-1}(1)\dots\sigma^{-1}(k)
(the labels on the
top
k
cards) and
\sigma^{-1}(n-k)\dots\sigma^{-1}(n)
(the labels on the
bottom
k

\sigma

\sigma^{-1}(1)\dots\sigma^{-1}(k)
are approximately i.i.d. picks from the weights;
\sigma^{-1}(n-k)\dots\sigma^{-1}(n)

We also recall that one standard choice for the weights
\theta_{i}
is the one given by the Sukhatme weights

, see
[

for further explanations.

1.2
Limits of random permutations: local and permuton convergence

A classical (but somewhat ill-posed) question in the study of random permutations is:

What does a typical large permutation drawn from a given model look like?

Traditionally, efforts to address this question have focused on the convergence of various permutation statistics, especially in the case of uniformly random permutations. Examples include the number of cycles, the number of inversions, and the length of the longest increasing subsequence, among many others.

In recent years, however, a more geometric perspective has gained interest. Rather than analyzing specific statistics, this approach aims to understand the permutation as a whole – searching for a global or local description of its “limiting shape”. This is the point of view adopted in the present work, where we will focus on the case of Luce permutations.

We first introduce the notion of global and local limits for permutations after some reminders on permutation patterns, then in the next subsection we give an overview of our main results.

Notation for patterns in permutations.

The occurrences of various patterns in a permutation are classical fare, both in applications and in probabilistic combinatorics. For example, the number of inversions, descents, and peaks has been extensively studied. More recently, pattern avoidance has become a hot topic
[
Bón08
,
Kit11
,
Vat15
]
. We begin by introducing notation for general patterns.

Recall that we denote by
\mathcal{S}_{n}
the set of permutations of size
n\geq 1
and set
\mathcal{S}:=\cup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
We sometimes write permutations of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
in one-line notation as
\sigma=\sigma(1)\sigma(2)\dots\sigma(n).
If
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
is a sequence of distinct numbers, let
\text{std}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be the unique permutation
\pi
in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
that is in the same relative order as
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},
i.e.
\pi(i)<\pi(j)
if and only if
x_{i}<x_{j}.

Given a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and a subset of indices
I\subset[n]
, let
\pat_{I}(\sigma)
be the permutation induced by
(\sigma(i))_{i\in I},
namely,
\pat_{I}(\sigma)\coloneqq\text{std}\big((\sigma(i))_{i\in I}\big),
where the
\sigma(i)
are listed in increasing value of the index.
For example, if
\sigma=87532461
and
I=\{2,4,7\}
then
\pat_{\{2,4,7\}}(87532461)=\text{std}(736)=312
.

Given two permutations,
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
for some
k\leq n,
and a set of indices
I=\{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\}
, we say that
\sigma(i_{1})\ldots\sigma(i_{k})
is an
occurrence
of
\pi
in
\sigma
if
\pat_{I}(\sigma)=\pi
(we will also say that
\pi
is a
pattern
of
\sigma
). If the indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
form an interval, then we say that
\sigma(i_{1})\ldots\sigma(i_{k})
is a
consecutive occurrence
of
\pi
in
\sigma
(we will also say that
\pi
is a
consecutive pattern
of
\sigma
).
We also denote intervals of integers as
[n,m]
for some
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
n\leq m
.
For example, the permutation
\sigma=1532467
contains an occurrence (but no consecutive occurrence) of
1423
and a consecutive occurrence of
321
. Indeed,
\pat_{\{1,2,3,5\}}(\sigma)=1423
but no interval of indices of
\sigma
induces the permutation
1423.

We denote by
\occ(\pi,\sigma)
the number of occurrences of
\pi
in
\sigma
, that is,

\occ(\pi,\sigma)\coloneqq\#\big\{I\subseteq[n]\big|\#I=k,\pat_{I}(\sigma)=\pi\big\},

(1.3)

where
\#
denotes the cardinality of a set.
Moreover, we denote by
\widetilde{\occ}(\pi,\sigma)
the proportion of occurrences of
\pi
in
\sigma,
that is,
\widetilde{\occ}(\pi,\sigma)\coloneqq\occ(\pi,\sigma)/\binom{n}{k}
.
Similarly, we denote by
\cocc(\pi,\sigma)
the number of consecutive occurrences of
\pi
in
\sigma
, that is,

\cocc(\pi,\sigma)\coloneqq\#\Big\{I\subseteq[n]\Big|\#I=k,\;I\text{ is an interval, }\text{pat}_{I}(\sigma)=\pi\Big\},

(1.4)

and we denote by
\pcocc(\pi,\sigma)
the proportion of consecutive occurrences of
\pi
in
\sigma,
that is,
1
1

1

Another natural choice for the denominator would be
n-k+1
. Note that for every fixed
k
there are no differences in the asymptotics when
n
tends to infinity.

\pcocc(\pi,\sigma)\coloneqq\cocc(\pi,\sigma)/n
.

Permuton convergence.
A
permuton
is a Borel probability measure
\mu
on the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
with two uniform marginals, that is,
\mu([a,b]\times[0,1])=\mu([0,1]\times[a,b])=b-a
for all
0\leq a<b\leq 1
. Permutons are natural objects to describe the scaling limits of random permutations and have been studied quite intensively in the past decade; see
[
Bor21b
, Section 2.1]
or
[
Mél
, Section 2]
for an introduction to the theory of permutons and, in particular, to the notion of permuton limit.

For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we define the
permuton associated with
\sigma
to be the measure
\mu_{\sigma}
on
[0,1]^{2}
which is equal to
n
times the Lebesgue measure on

\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{j-1}{n},\frac{j}{n}}}\right]\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sigma(j)-1}{n},\frac{\sigma(j)}{n}}}\right].

(1.5)

Let
\sigma_{n}
be a random permutation of size
n
.
Moreover, for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
{I}_{n,k}
be a uniform random subset of
[n]
with
k
elements, independent of
\sigma_{n}
. We recall (see for instance
[
BBF+20
, Theorem 2.5]
) that the following assertions are equivalent:

(a)

(\mu_{\sigma_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in distribution w.r.t. the permuton topology to a random permuton
\mu
, that is,

\int_{[0,1]^{2}}f\,d\mu_{\sigma_{n}}\rightarrow\int_{[0,1]^{2}}f\,d\mu,

for every (bounded and) continuous function
f:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

The random infinite vector
\big(\widetilde{\occ}(\pi,\sigma_{n})\big)_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}}
converges in distribution w.r.t. the product topology to a random infinite vector
(\Lambda(\pi))_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}}
.

(c)

For every
\pi
in
\mathcal{S}
, there exists
\Delta_{\pi}\geq 0
such that

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\occ}(\pi,\sigma_{n})]\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\Delta_{\pi}.

(d)

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
\big(\pat_{{I}_{n,k}}(\sigma_{n})\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random permutations converges in distribution to some random permutation
\rho_{k}
.

Whenever these assertions are verified, we have for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
,

\mathbb{P}(\rho_{k}=\pi)=\Delta_{\pi}=\mathbb{E}[\Lambda(\pi)].

The theory of permutons was first developed in
[
HKM+13
]
. Permuton convergence has been studied in various models, including Erdős–Szekeres permutations
[
Rom06
]
, Mallows permutations
[
Sta09a
]
, exponential families on permutations
[
Muk16
]
, fixed pattern densities
[
KKRW20
]

and many others.
For random pattern-avoiding permutations, the limiting permutons are often random
[
BBF+20
,
Bor21c
]
. In this paper, we further contribute by analyzing the case of Luce-distributed permutations.

Local convergence.
We recall from
[
Bor20
, Theorem 2.32]
that we say that a sequence of random permutations
(\sigma_{n})_{n}
(quenched)
Benjamini–Schramm converges
if one of the following equivalent conditions holds:

(a)

There exists a family of non-negative real random variables
(\Gamma^{h}_{\pi})_{h\geq 1,\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2h+1}}
such that for
i_{n}
a uniform random index in
[n]
independent of
\sigma_{n}
, then

\Big(\mathbb{P}\big(\pat_{[i_{n}-h,i_{n}+h]\cap[1,n]}(\sigma_{n})=\pi\,\big|\,\sigma_{n}\big)\Big)_{h\geq 1,\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2h+1}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{d}}\big(\Gamma^{h}_{\pi}\big)_{h\geq 1,\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2h+1}},

w.r.t. the product topology;

(b)

There exists an infinite vector of non-negative real random variables
(\Lambda_{\pi})_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}}
such that

\big(\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})\big)_{h\geq 1,\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{h}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{d}}\big(\Lambda_{\pi}\big)_{h\geq 1,\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{h}}

w.r.t. the product topology.

In particular, if one of the two conditions holds (and so do both of them), then

\Lambda_{\pi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\Gamma^{h}_{\pi},\quad\text{for all}\quad\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2h+1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda_{\pi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\sum_{m=1}^{2h+1}\Gamma^{h}_{\pi^{*m}},\quad\text{for all}\quad\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2h},

where
\pi^{*m}\coloneqq\text{std}(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k},m-1/2)
.

The theory of local convergence for permutations was introduced in
[
Bor20
]
and subsequently investigated across several models, including those studied in
[
Bev19
,
BBFS20
,
Bor21a
,
BM22
,
PR24
]
.

2.1
The permuton limit of Luce-distributed permutations

Theorem 2.1

(Permuton limit)
.

Set
f_{n}(y):=\theta_{\lfloor yn\rfloor}
for all
y\in[0,1]
and assume that
f_{n}\to f
almost everywhere for some positive, finite, measurable function
f
on
[0,1]
.
Let also
\mu
be the law of the pair

(X,Y):=\Big(U,F\big(E/f(U)\big)\Big),

(2.1)

where
U
is a uniform random variable on
[0,1]
,
E
is an independent exponential random variable of parameter 1, and

F(x):=1-\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-xf(y)}\,\text{d}y,\quad\text{for all $x\geq 0$.}

(2.2)

If
\sigma_{n}\sim\Luce(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
, then the sequence
(\sigma_{n})_{n}
converges in probability in the permuton sense to the deterministic permuton
\mu
.

For example, if
\theta_{i}=1
for all
n\geq 1
and
i\in[n]
, then
f(x)=1
for all
x\in[0,1]
, and hence
Y=1-\mathrm{e}^{-E}
.
Thus,
(X,Y)
is a pair of independent Uniform
([0,1])
random variables in this case, as expected.

On the other hand, for the Sukhatme weights, we have
\theta_{i}=(n-i+1)/n
.
In this case,
f_{n}(x)\to f(x)=1-x
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Since
f(x)>0
for a.e.
x
, we can apply
the theorem and get that

Y=1-\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-x)E/(1-X)}\,\text{d}x=1-\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-E/(1-X)}}{E/(1-X)},

where
E
is a standard exponential random variable, independent of
X
, and
X\sim\text{Uniform}([0,1])
. Note the (a priori non-trivial) fact that
Y
is Uniform
([0,1])
, since
\mu
is a permuton. See
Figure

1
on page
1
for a picture.

We now turn to the proof of the theorem.
Let
\sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
be a permutation from the Luce model with weights
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
. Let
(U^{\alpha}_{n})_{\alpha\in[2]}
be two i.i.d. uniform random variables on
[n]
independent of
\sigma_{n}
, and define for
\alpha\in[2]=\{1,2\}
,

X^{\alpha}_{n}:=\frac{U^{\alpha}_{n}}{n},\qquad Y^{\alpha}_{n}:=\frac{\sigma_{n}(U^{\alpha}_{n})}{n}.

Then, to prove that
\sigma_{n}
converges in probability to
\mu
in the permuton sense, it is enough to show that

the vector
(X^{\alpha}_{n},Y^{\alpha}_{n})_{\alpha\in[2]}
converges in distribution to
(X^{\alpha},Y^{\alpha})_{\alpha\in[2]}
,

(2.3)

where the
(X^{1},Y^{1})
and
(X^{2},Y^{2})
are i.i.d. copies of the random variable in (
2.1
). Indeed, thanks to
[
BDMW24
, Remark 1.5]
, to show that
\sigma_{n}
converges in probability to
\mu
, it is enough to show that for every (bounded and) continuous function
f:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,

P_{n}(f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i}{n},\frac{\sigma_{n}(i)}{n}}}\right)\xlongrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}f\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\mathbb{E}[f(X^{1},Y^{1})].

(2.4)

But (
2.3
) immediately gives us that as
n
tends to infinity,

\mathbb{E}[P_{n}(f)]\to\mathbb{E}[f(X^{1},Y^{1})]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[P_{n}(f)^{2}]\to\mathbb{E}[f(X^{1},Y^{1})]^{2}

(2.5)

and so a standard second moment argument shows that (
2.3
) implies (
2.4
).

Proof of
2.1
.

Let
E_{n,1},\dots,E_{n,n}
be independent exponential r.v.’s with
\mathbb{E}[E_{n,i}]=1/\theta_{i}
. By the exponential representation of the Luce model (see for instance
[
CDK23
, Theorem 2.1]
and recall
Remark

1.1
), we can express
\sigma_{n}(i)
as

\sigma_{n}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{E_{n,j}\leq E_{n,i}\}.

Note that for
j\neq i
,

\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}\leq E_{n,i}\mid E_{n,i})=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\theta_{j}E_{n,i}}.

Consequently, conditioning on
E_{n,i}
,
\sigma_{n}(i)
is 1 plus a sum over
j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}
of independent Bernoulli random variables with
means
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\theta_{j}E_{n,i}}
. Thus, if we let

Z_{n,i}:=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\theta_{j}E_{n,i}}),

an application of Hoeffding’s inequality gives us that for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\sigma_{n}(i)}{n}-Z_{n,i}}}\right|\geq t\,\,\middle|\,\,E_{n,i}}}\right)\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{-nt^{2}/2}.

Since the bound does not depend on
E_{n,i}
, the unconditional probability is also bounded by the
same quantity. Taking a union bound over
i\in[n]
and applying the Borel–Cantelli lemma, this
shows that almost surely as
n\to\infty
,

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\sigma_{n}(i)}{n}-Z_{n,i}}}\right|\to 0.

Now let
W^{\alpha}_{n}=Z_{n,U^{\alpha}_{n}}
. By the above display, the vector
(Y^{\alpha}_{n}-W^{\alpha}_{n})_{\alpha\in[2]}
converges to the zero vector almost surely as
n\to\infty
.
Thus, it suffices to study the convergence of the vector

(X^{\alpha}_{n},W^{\alpha}_{n})_{\alpha\in[2]}
. Moreover, from now on, we can also assume that
U^{1}_{n}\neq U^{2}_{n}
. Indeed, the probability of the complement event tends to zero as
n\to\infty
and so the limit (if it exists) of the vector

(X^{\alpha}_{n},W^{\alpha}_{n})_{\alpha\in[2]}
under the conditional law that
U^{1}_{n}\neq U^{2}_{n}
is the same as the one obtained under the unconditional law.

Note that, conditioning on
U^{\alpha}_{n}=i^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in[2]
with
i^{1}\neq i^{2}
, the law of
(W_{n}^{\alpha})_{\alpha\in[2]}
is the same as that of

(Q^{\alpha}_{n,i^{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in[2]}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\theta_{j}E^{i^{\alpha}}/\theta_{i^{\alpha}}})}}\right)_{\alpha\in[2]}

where the
(E^{i^{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in[2]}
are independent exponential random variables with mean
1
, which are also independent of
(U^{\alpha}_{n})_{\alpha\in[2]}
. Since

X^{\alpha}_{n}=U^{\alpha}_{n}/n
, this shows that the joint law of
(X^{\alpha}_{n},W^{\alpha}_{n})_{\alpha\in[2]}
is the same as the joint law of
(X^{\alpha}_{n},Q^{\alpha}_{n,U_{n}^{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in[2]}
. So, our task reduces to identifying the limiting distribution of
(X^{\alpha}_{n},Q^{\alpha}_{n,U_{n}^{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in[2]}
. Since the random variables
(E^{i^{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in[2]}
and
(U^{\alpha}_{n})_{\alpha\in[2]}
are all jointly independent, and we have the assumption that
U^{1}_{n}\neq U^{2}_{n}
, we have that
(X^{1}_{n},Q^{1}_{n,U_{n}^{1}})
and
(X^{2}_{n},Q^{2}_{n,U_{n}^{2}})
are independent. Therefore, recalling that the limiting random variables
(X^{1},Y^{1})
and
(X^{2},Y^{2})
are also independent, in order to conclude, it is enough to show that
(X_{n},Q_{n,U_{n}})
converges in distribution to
(X,Y)
, where
(X_{n},Q_{n,U_{n}})
is distributed as
(X^{1}_{n},Q^{1}_{n,U^{1}_{n}})
and
(X,Y)
is distributed as
(X^{1},Y^{1})
.
Now, notice that since
f_{n}(x)=\theta_{\lfloor xn\rfloor}
,

Q_{n,U_{n}}=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-E}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\theta_{j}E^{i^{\alpha}}/\theta_{U_{n}}})=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E}}{n}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f_{n}(x)E/\theta_{U_{n}}})\,\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
E
is an exponential random variable with mean
1
, which is independent of
U_{n}
.

Consequently, for any bounded continuous function
g:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(X_{n},Q_{n,U_{n}})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{n},\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E}}{n}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f_{n}(x)E/\theta_{U_{n}}})\,\,\mathrm{d}x}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i}{n},\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E}}{n}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f_{n}(x)E/\theta_{i}})\,\,\mathrm{d}x}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{0}^{1}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{n}(y),\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E}}{n}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f_{n}(x)E/f_{n}(y)})\,\,\mathrm{d}x}}\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}y}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-z}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{n}(y),\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-z}}{n}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f_{n}(x)z/f_{n}(y)})\,\,\mathrm{d}x}}\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z,

where
h_{n}(y):=\frac{\lfloor yn\rfloor}{n}
.
Now, given any
y
such that
f_{n}(y)\to f(y)>0
, and any
z>0
, we have that for almost
every
x\in[0,1]
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f_{n}(x)z/f_{n}(y)})=1-\mathrm{e}^{-f(x)z/f(y)}.

Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem, we have that for any
y
and
z
as above,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f_{n}(x)z/f_{n}(y)})=\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f(x)z/f(y)})\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

But again,
f_{n}(y)\to f(y)>0
for almost every
y\in[0,1]
. Thus, by the boundedness and
continuity of
g
, and the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[g(X_{n},Q_{n,U_{n}})]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-z}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{n}(y),\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-z}}{n}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f_{n}(x)z/f_{n}(y)})\,\,\mathrm{d}x}}\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-z}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y,\int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-f(x)z/f(y)})\,\,\mathrm{d}x}}\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z.

The last expression equals
\mathbb{E}[g(X,Y)]
, where
X
and
Y
are as in the statement of the theorem.
This completes the proof.
∎

2.2
Properties of the Luce permuton

We now investigate some properties of the permuton
\mu
in
2.1
.

Recall that given a permuton
\mu
, one can sample
n
independent points
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
in the unit
square
[0,1]^{2}
according to
\mu
. These
n
points induce a random permutation
\sigma
: for any
i,j\in[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
\sigma(i)=j
if the point with
i
-th lowest
x
-coordinate has
j
-th lowest
y
-coordinate (this is well-defined since the marginals of a permutons are uniform, and so, almost surely there are no points with the same
x
- or
y
-coordinates). We denote this permutation by
\Perm(\mu,n)
and call it the
random permutation induced by the permuton
\mu
of size
n
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The permuton
\mu
in
2.1
is uniquely determined by its pattern densities, defined for all
k\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
by

\widetilde{\occ}(\pi,\mu):=\mathbb{P}\big(\Perm(\mu,k)=\pi\big)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Luce\big(f(U^{1}),\dots,f(U^{k})\big)=\pi\,\,\Big|\,\,U^{1},\dots,U^{k}}}\right)\Big],

(2.6)

where
(U^{1},\dots,U^{k})
are the order statistics of
k
i.i.d. uniform random variables in
[0,1]
.

Moreover, the permuton
\mu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the uniform permuton
\Leb_{[0,1]^{2}}
and it has the following density:

\rho(x,y):=\frac{f(x)\mathrm{e}^{-f(x)F^{-1}(y)}}{\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\mathrm{e}^{-f(t)F^{-1}(y)}\,\text{d}t},\quad\text{for all $(x,y)\in(0,1)^{2}$},

(2.7)

where
F
is as in (
2.2
).

Before proving the proposition above, we provide some remarks and examples.

We start by noting that the first part of the proposition statement heuristically says that the random permutation induced by the Luce permuton
\mu
of size
k
is distributed as the average of an exact Luce-distributed permutation with
random
weights
f(U^{1}),\dots,f(U^{k})
.

If
f(x)=1-x
, i.e.
\mu
is the permuton limit of Luce-distributed permutations with Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
, then
F(x)=\frac{x-1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{x}
. Hence, denoting by
\varphi(x)
the inverse of
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}+x-1}{x}
(which does not have an explicit closed form but can be easily computed numerically), we get that the density of
\mu
is given by

\rho(x,y):=\frac{(1-x)\mathrm{e}^{-(1-x)\varphi(y)}}{\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\mathrm{e}^{-(1-t)\varphi(y)}\,\text{d}t}=\frac{(1-x)\varphi(y)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{x\varphi(y)}}{\mathrm{e}^{\varphi(y)}-\varphi(y)-1},\quad\text{for all $(x,y)\in(0,1)^{2}$}.

(2.8)

The graph of the density
\rho(x,y)
is plotted in the second panel of
Figure

.

We highlight two important features of the Luce permuton
\mu
with Sukhatme weights:

•

Its density
\rho(x,y)
is singular at
(x,y)=(1,1)
. Indeed, noting that
\frac{x-1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{x}=1-1/x+o(1/x)
, we get
\varphi(x)\sim_{x\to 1}1/(1-x)
and so
\lim_{x\to 1^{-}}\rho(x,x)=+\infty
. We note that this singular behavior is different from the behavior of other well-studied limiting permutons: the Mallows permutons
(\mu_{\beta})_{\beta>0}
, i.e. the permuton limits of Mallows distributed permutations
2
2

2

We recall that in the Mallows model on permutations, the probability of a permutation is proportional to a real parameter

raised to the power of the number of inversions of the permutation.
with parameter

[
Sta09b
]
. Indeed, it is simple to see that the densities

\rho_{\beta}(x,y)=\frac{\beta/2\sinh[\beta/2]}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\beta/4}\cosh[\beta/2(x-y)]-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta/4}\cosh[\beta/2(x+y-1)]}}\right)^{2}},

(2.9)

of the Mallows permutons

are bounded for all

•

The density

Proof of
2.2
.

We first prove the second claim in the proposition statement.
Recalling from
2.1
that
F(x)=1-\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-xf(y)}dy
, we get that for all
x>0
,

F^{\prime}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}f(y)\mathrm{e}^{-xf(y)}\,\text{d}y>0.

Therefore,
F
is a (strictly) increasing diffeomorphism from the positive real line to
(0,1)
. In particular,
F
is invertible.

Let
g
be a non-negative measurable function defined on the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
. We have that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g(U,F(E/f(U)))}}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x,F(s/f(x)))\mathrm{e}^{-s}\,\text{d}s\,\text{d}x.

Using the change of variables
y=F(s/f(x))
and
s=f(x)F^{-1}(y)
, and noting that

\text{d}s=f(x)(F^{-1})^{\prime}(y)\,\text{d}y=\frac{f(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\mathrm{e}^{-f(t)F^{-1}(y)}\,\text{d}t}\,\text{d}y,

we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g(U,F(E/f(U)))}}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}g(x,y)\mathrm{e}^{-f(x)F^{-1}(y)}\frac{f(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\mathrm{e}^{-f(t)F^{-1}(y)}\,\text{d}t}\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}x,

finishing the proof of the second part of the proposition.

We now prove the first part of the proposition. Fix
k\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
. We first compute
\widetilde{\occ}(\pi,\mu):=\mathbb{P}\big(\Perm(\mu,k)=\pi\big)
. By definition of
\mu
and
\Perm(\mu,k)
we have that

\mathbb{P}\big(\Perm(\mu,k)=\pi\big)\\
=\mathbb{P}\Big(F\big(E_{1}/f(U^{\pi^{-1}(1)})\big)<F\big(E_{2}/f(U^{\pi^{-1}(2)})\big)<\dots<F\big(E_{k}/f(U^{\pi^{-1}(k)})\big)\Big),

where
(E_{i})_{i}
are independent exponential random variables of parameter 1, and
(U^{1},\dots,U^{k})
are the order statistics of
k
i.i.d. uniform random variables in
[0,1]
also independent of
(E_{i})_{i}
.
Since
F
is a (strictly) increasing diffeomorphism (as shown above), the previous expression rewrites as

\mathbb{P}\big(\Perm(\mu,k)=\pi\big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(E_{1}/f(U^{\pi^{-1}(1)})<E_{2}/f(U^{\pi^{-1}(2)})<\dots<E_{k}/f(U^{\pi^{-1}(k)})\Big).

Conditioning on
(U^{1},\dots,U^{k})
, the random variables
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i}/f(U^{\pi^{-1}(i)})}}\right)_{i}
are independent exponential random variables of parameters
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(U^{\pi^{-1}(i)})}}\right)_{i}
, respectively. Hence, (
2.6
) immediately follows from
[
CDK23
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Finally, the fact that the pattern densities
(\widetilde{\occ}(\pi,\mu))_{k\geq 1,\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}
uniquely determine the permuton
\mu
is a consequence of the fact that
\Perm(\mu,k)
converges in probability to
\mu
in the permuton sense (see, for instance,
[
HKM+13
, Lemma 4.2]
). We conclude by the uniqueness of the limit.
∎

2.4
Differences between exact Luce-distributed permutations and permutations sampled from the corresponding Luce permuton

The permuton limit of a sequence of permutations encodes well the “global properties” of the sequence of permutations, such as the proportion of patterns, as we saw in the previous section. Local limits and a CLT for consecutive occurrences

In the previous sections, we looked at the permuton limit for Luce-distributed permutations. Here, we focus on the natural counterpart of local limits, following the framework introduced in
[

]
. These are extensions of the classical descents.

3.1
Statement of the main results

For the Luce model, the quenched Benjamini–Schramm convergence follows from the following result, whose proof is postponed to the end of the section.

Theorem 3.1

(Local limit)
.

Fix
k\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
. Assume that
\sigma_{n}\sim\Luce(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
and that

\Lambda(\pi):=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)

(3.1)

exists.
Then, the following convergence in probability holds

\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})\to\Lambda(\pi).

The assumption in (
3.1
) is a regularity assumption for the weights
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
that will be explicitly verified in certain examples in the next two subsections. For instance, if
\pi=21
, i.e. the case of descents, then (
3.1
) requires the existence of a finite limit for:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\frac{\theta_{i+2}}{\theta_{i+1}+\theta_{i+2}}.

We also have the following stronger result (whose proof is also postponed to the end of the section). It gives a central limit theorem for these local statistics with a Berry–Esseen type error estimate.

Theorem 3.2
.

Fix
k\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
. Assume that
\sigma_{n}\sim\Luce(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
and let

\displaystyle(\nu_{n}(\pi))^{2}:=

\displaystyle\Var\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)-p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-h}\Bigg(\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)},E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)

\displaystyle-p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\Bigg),

where
(E_{i})_{i}
are independent exponential random variables of parameter
(\theta_{i})_{i}
respectively.
Then, the following holds

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\cocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\cocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})}}\right]}{\nu_{n}(\pi)}\leq w}}\right)-\Phi(w)}}\right|\leq 32(1+\sqrt{6})\frac{nk^{2}}{(\nu_{n}(\pi))^{3}},

where
\Phi(w)
denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

Note that when
\pi=21
, i.e. the case of descents, then, setting
\widetilde{\theta}_{i}=\frac{\theta_{i+2}}{\theta_{i+1}+\theta_{i+2}}
, we have that

\displaystyle(\nu_{n}(21))^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\theta}_{i}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\theta}_{i}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-3}\Bigg(\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+3}<E_{i+2}<E_{i+1}}}\right)-\widetilde{\theta}_{i}\cdot\widetilde{\theta}_{i+1}\Bigg).

Before proving
3.1
and
3.2
, we provide some applications, including explicit computations for the quantities appearing in these two theorems.

3.2
Application 1: permutations with Sukhatme weights are locally uniform

We consider the canonical example of Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix
k\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
. Consider the case of Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
. Then

\Lambda(\pi)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)=\frac{1}{k!}.

Proof.

Note that
n-i-k+1\leq\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(\ell)}\leq n-i
for all
0\leq i\leq n-k
and
\ell\in[k]
. Moreover,

(k-\ell)(n-i)+k(-k+1)\\
\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+1}+\dots+\theta_{i+k}}}\right)-(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}+\dots+\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(\ell)})\leq\\
(k-\ell)(n-i)-\ell(-k+1),

for all
0\leq i\leq n-k
and
\ell\in[k]
. As a consequence, by the definition of the function
p
in (
1.1
), we get that

\frac{(n-i-k+1)^{k}}{k!(n-i)^{k}+o((n-i)^{k})}\leq p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\leq\frac{(n-i)^{k}}{k!(n-i)^{k}+o((n-i)^{k})}

(3.2)

for all
0\leq i\leq n-\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
. Now, recalling that

\Lambda(\pi):=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right),

we write

\Lambda(\pi)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)+O(1/\sqrt{n})}}\right).

Finally, noting that from (
3.2
) we have that
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\to\frac{1}{k!}
for all
0\leq i\leq n-\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
, we conclude from the Stolz–Cesàro theorem that
\Lambda(\pi)=\frac{1}{k!}
.
∎

Consequently, we deduce that if
\sigma_{n}
is Luce-distributed with Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
, then it behaves locally as a uniform permutation. More precisely, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\sigma_{n}
be a Luce-distributed permutation of size
n
with Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
. Then

\big(\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})\big)_{h\geq 1,\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{h}}\xrightarrow{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{h!}}}\right)_{h\geq 1,\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{h}}

w.r.t. the product topology. In particular,
\sigma_{n}
quenched Benjamini–Schramm converges.

Proof.

Since the limits are deterministic, pointwise convergence in probability is equivalent to convergence in probability in the product topology. Hence, it suffices to show that for any fixed
h\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
,

\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})\xrightarrow{P}\frac{1}{h!}.

This follows by combining
3.3
and
3.1
.
∎

One obtains the following technical result using the same types of arguments used for
3.3
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Fix
k\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
. Consider the case of Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
. Set for all
k\geq 1
and
1\leq h\leq k-1
,

\zeta_{\pi}(h,k):=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{\pi^{-1}(k)},\,\,E_{h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right),

(3.3)

where
(E_{i})_{i}
are i.i.d. exponential random variables of parameter 1, and

\nu_{\infty}(\pi)=\frac{1}{k!}+\frac{1-2k}{(k!)^{2}}+2\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\zeta_{\pi}(h,k).

(3.4)

Then, as
n\to\infty
,

(\nu_{n}(\pi))^{2}\sim n\cdot\nu_{\infty}(\pi).

We obtain the following stronger result by combining
3.5
and
3.2
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\sigma_{n}
be a Luce-distributed permutation of size
n
with Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
. Fix
k\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
. Then

\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})-\frac{1}{k!}}{\sqrt{\nu_{\infty}(\pi)}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(3.5)

where
\nu_{\infty}(\pi)
is defined as in (
3.4
).

We conclude by showing the explicit values for
\nu_{\infty}(\pi)
for all patterns of size 2 and 3 (with some matching numerical simulations shown in
Figure

2
):

\displaystyle\nu_{\infty}(12)=\nu_{\infty}(21)=\frac{1}{12},

\displaystyle\nu_{\infty}(123)=\nu_{\infty}(321)=\frac{23}{180},\qquad\nu_{\infty}(132)=\nu_{\infty}(312)=\nu_{\infty}(213)=\nu_{\infty}(231)=\frac{7}{90}.

Note that these are the same values one obtains for uniform permutations.
3.6
is reminiscent of results showing that local statistics – such as descents – have limiting distributions that do not change between uniform and non-uniform models. For example, Kammoun
[
Kam22
]
proves such universality for the Mallows models, and Kim and Li
[
KL20
]
show that descents are asymptotically normal when restricted to permutations in a fixed conjugacy class. See also
[
Bor21a
,
GK23
,
FK24
]
for other similar universality results.

One could also investigate the finer properties of local patterns. For instance, it would be nice to study whether, in the above setting, the descents form the same determinantal point process as those for descents in uniform permutations; see
[
BDF10
, Theorem 5.1]
for more details.

.

(E_{i})_{i}

\alpha^{-i}

\nu_{\infty}(\pi):=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha^{-\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\alpha^{-\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)+(1-2k)p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha^{-\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\alpha^{-\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)^{2}+2\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\eta_{\pi}(h,k),

n\to\infty

(\nu_{n}(\pi))^{2}\sim n\cdot\nu_{\infty}(\pi).

k\geq 1

\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
,

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha^{-\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\alpha^{-\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right).

P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)},E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\\

(E_{i})_{i}

(E_{i})_{i}

\alpha^{-i}

3.4
Proofs of the main results

We now turn to the proofs of
3.1
and
3.2
.

Proof of
3.1
.

Note that by the nature of the Luce model (recall
[
CDK23
, Theorem 2.1]
and
Remark

1.1
)

\cocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\mathds{1}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pat_{[i+1,i+k]}(\sigma_{n})=\pi}}\right\}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\mathds{1}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right\}},

where
(E_{i})_{i}
are independent exponential random variables of parameter
(\theta_{i})_{i}
, respectively. As a consequence,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})}}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\\
=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,\theta_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\to\Lambda(\pi),

where in the last step, we used our assumption.
Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})^{2}}}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\\
+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq n-k}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)},E_{j+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{j+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right).

Noting that when the indexes
i<j
satisfy
j-i\geq k
then the events

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E_{j+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{j+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right\}

are independent, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{h=k}^{n-k}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-h}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-h}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)},E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})}}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{h=k}^{n-k}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-h}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-h}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right).

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\Var\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)-\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-h}\Bigg(\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)},E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad-\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\Bigg).

(3.12)

Since all the terms in the sums above are upper bounded by 1, we conclude that

\Var\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pcocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})}}\right)\leq\frac{n+2kn}{n^{2}}\to 0.

The theorem statement follows from a standard application of the Second-moment method.
∎

Proof of
3.2
.

Note that from (
3.4
) we have that

\displaystyle\Var\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cocc(\pi,\sigma_{n})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{j+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{j+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)-\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{j+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{j+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,+2\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-h}\Bigg(\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)},E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad-\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(1)}<\dots<E_{i+h+\pi^{-1}(k)}}}\right)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=(\nu_{n}(\pi))^{2}.

Then, the theorem statement follows from applying
[
BR89
, Corollary 2]
.
∎

4
A central limit theorem for the number of inversions

Section

3
developed central limit theorems for local statistics under the Luce model. In this section, we prove a similar theorem for global patterns, at least for the most well-known special case: inversions. The argument is a variant of the Hoeffding-Hajek projection method.
We believe it generalizes to more complex patterns.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{n}
be positive real numbers. Let
\sigma_{n}\sim\Luce(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
and recall that
\occ(21,\sigma_{n})
denotes the number of inversions in
\sigma_{n}
.
Then

\mathbb{E}(\occ(21,\sigma_{n}))=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{\theta_{j}}{\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}},

and

\Var(\occ(21,\sigma_{n}))=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})^{2}}+\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}\frac{4\theta_{i}\theta_{j}^{2}\theta_{k}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{k})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{k})(\theta_{j}+\theta_{k})}.

Lastly, define
a:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\Var(\occ(21,\sigma_{n}))}-n}}\right)^{+}
, where
x^{+}
denotes the positive part of a real number
x
.
Then

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\mathbb{P}\biggl(\frac{\occ(21,\sigma_{n})-\mathbb{E}(\occ(21,\sigma_{n}))}{\sqrt{\Var(\occ(21,\sigma_{n}))}}\leq t\biggr)-\Phi(t)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Cn^{4}}{a^{3}}+\frac{Cn}{a}+\frac{Cn^{2/3}}{a^{2/3}},

where
C
is a universal constant and
\Phi(t)
denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

The above theorem shows that as long as
\Var(Z)\gg n^{8/3}
,
Z
is approximately normally distributed. In typical situations, where all the
\theta_{i}
’s are all of comparable size, the above formula for
\Var(Z)
shows that it is of order
n^{3}
, and hence this condition is satisfied. This holds, for example, for the Sukhatme weights as well as for uniform random permutations.

In particular, for the Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
, using the standard asymptotic expansion for harmonic numbers

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k}=\log m+\gamma+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{m}}}\right),

and standard Riemann sum approximations, we get

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{occ}(21,\sigma_{n})]=n^{2}\int_{0}^{1}t\log\frac{1+t}{2t}\,dt+O(n\log n)=\frac{1-\log 2}{2}n^{2}+O(n\log n).

and

\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{occ}(21,\sigma_{n}))=V\cdot n^{3}+O(n^{2}\log n),

where (the final approximation is a numerical approximation)

V=\int_{0<t<u<v<1}\frac{4\,t\,u^{2}\,v}{(t+u+v)(t+u)(t+v)(u+v)}\,dt\,du\,dv\approx 0.0181166.

4.1
in this specific setting are shown in

.

Proof of
4.1
.

Let
E_{1},\ldots,E_{n}
be independent random variables, with
E_{i}
having an exponential distribution with mean
1/\theta_{i}
. Let

Z:=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}1_{\{E_{i}>E_{j}\}}.

Then, as usual,
\occ(21,\sigma_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}Z
by the exponential representation of the Luce model (see for instance
[
CDK23
, Theorem 2.1]
).
Define

\displaystyle f_{ij}

\displaystyle:=1_{\{E_{i}>E_{j}\}},

\displaystyle g_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}(f_{ij}|E_{i})=1-e^{-\theta_{j}E_{i}},

\displaystyle h_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}(f_{ij}|E_{j})=e^{-\theta_{i}E_{j}},

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}(f_{ij})=\frac{\theta_{j}}{\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}}.

Then note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mu_{ij}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{\theta_{j}}{\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}}.

Next, note that

\displaystyle\Var(Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j,\,i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}\mathrm{Cov}(f_{ij},f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}).

Take any
i<j
and
i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}
. If
\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}=\emptyset
, then clearly,
\mathrm{Cov}(f_{ij},f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})=0
. If
|\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}|=2
, then
i=i^{\prime}
and
j=j^{\prime}
. In this case, the covariance is
\mu_{ij}(1-\mu_{ij})
. Lastly, suppose that
|\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}|=1
. There are several possibilities. First, we may have
i=i^{\prime}<j<j^{\prime}
. In this case,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(f_{ij}f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{i}>E_{j}>E_{j^{\prime}})+\mathbb{P}(E_{i}>E_{j^{\prime}}>E_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{j^{\prime}}\theta_{j}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})}+\frac{\theta_{j}\theta_{j^{\prime}}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(f_{ij},f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(f_{ij}f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})-\mathbb{E}(f_{ij})\mathbb{E}(f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{j^{\prime}}\theta_{j}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})}+\frac{\theta_{j}\theta_{j^{\prime}}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})}-\frac{\theta_{j}\theta_{j^{\prime}}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\theta_{j^{\prime}}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})}.

The same holds if
i=i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}<j
. Next, suppose that
i<j=i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}
. In this case,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(f_{ij},f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{i}>E_{j}>E_{j^{\prime}})-\mathbb{P}(E_{i}>E_{j})\mathbb{P}(E_{j}>E_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{j^{\prime}}\theta_{j}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})}-\frac{\theta_{j}\theta_{j^{\prime}}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\theta_{j^{\prime}}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{j}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})}.

The next possibility is
i^{\prime}<i<j=j^{\prime}
. In this case

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(f_{ij}f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{i^{\prime}}>E_{i}>E_{j})+\mathbb{P}(E_{i}>E_{i^{\prime}}>E_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{j}\theta_{i}}{(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{i})}+\frac{\theta_{j}\theta_{i^{\prime}}}{(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{j}}{\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(f_{ij},f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{j}}{\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}}-\frac{\theta_{j}^{2}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{i}\theta_{i^{\prime}}\theta_{j}}{(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{j})}.

The same holds if
i<i^{\prime}<j=j^{\prime}
. Finally, the last possibility is
i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}=i<j
. This is symmetrical with the case
i<j=i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}
, which shows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(f_{ij},f_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\theta_{i^{\prime}}\theta_{j^{\prime}}\theta_{j}}{(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{j^{\prime}}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i^{\prime}}+\theta_{j^{\prime}})(\theta_{j^{\prime}}+\theta_{j})}.

Combining all cases, we get

\displaystyle\Var(Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\mu_{ij}(1-\mu_{ij})+\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{2\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\theta_{k}}{\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{k}}\biggl(\frac{1}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{k})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{j}+\theta_{k})}+\frac{1}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{k})(\theta_{j}+\theta_{k})}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\frac{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})^{2}}+\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{4\theta_{i}\theta_{j}^{2}\theta_{k}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{k})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{k})(\theta_{j}+\theta_{k})}.

(4.1)

Now, let

\displaystyle\eta_{ij}:=f_{ij}-g_{ij}-h_{ij}+\mu_{ij}.

Clearly,
\mathbb{E}(\eta_{ij})=0
. If
\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}=\emptyset
, then
\eta_{ij}
and
\eta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
are independent, and so,
\mathbb{E}(\eta_{ij}\eta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})=0
. We claim that
\mathbb{E}(\eta_{ij}\eta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})=0
even if
|\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}|=1
. To prove this, consider the case
i=i^{\prime}<j<j^{\prime}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\eta_{ij}|(E_{k})_{k\neq j})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(f_{ij}|(E_{k})_{k\neq j})-g_{ij}-\mathbb{E}(h_{ij}|(E_{k})_{k\neq j})+\mu_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(f_{ij}|E_{i})-g_{ij}-\mathbb{E}(h_{ij})+\mu_{ij}=0.

Since
\eta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
is a function of
(E_{k})_{k\neq j}
in this case, this proves that
\mathbb{E}(\eta_{ij}\eta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})=0
. The other cases are proved similarly. Thus, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(\sum_{i<j}\eta_{ij}\biggr)^{2}\biggr]=\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{E}(\eta_{ij}^{2})\leq n^{2}.

(4.2)

But,

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\eta_{ij}

\displaystyle=Z-\mathbb{E}(Z)-W,

where

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}(g_{ij}+h_{ij}-2\mu_{ij})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i},

with

W_{i}:=\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}(e^{-\theta_{j}E_{i}}-\mu_{ji})-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(e^{-\theta_{j}E_{i}}-\mu_{ji}).

Note that
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}
are independent centered random variables, and
|W_{i}|\leq n
for each
i
. Thus, by the Berry–Esseen theorem for sums of independent and non-identically distributed centered random variables,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\mathbb{P}\biggl(\frac{W}{\sqrt{\Var(W)}}\leq t\biggr)-\Phi(t)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|W_{i}|^{3}}{2(\Var(W))^{3/2}}\leq\frac{n^{4}}{2(\Var(W))^{3/2}},

where we recall that
\Phi
denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Thus, by (
4.2
), we have that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z\leq\mathbb{E}(Z)+t\sqrt{\Var(Z)})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(W\leq(t+\varepsilon)\sqrt{\Var(Z)})+\mathbb{P}(|Z-\mathbb{E}(Z)-W|>\varepsilon\sqrt{\Var(Z)})

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\biggl(\frac{(t+\varepsilon)\sqrt{\Var(Z)}}{\sqrt{\Var(W)}}\biggr)+\frac{n^{4}}{2(\Var(W))^{3/2}}+\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\Var(Z)}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(t)+a(t)\biggl|\frac{\sqrt{\Var(Z)}}{\sqrt{\Var(W)}}-1\biggr|+\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{\Var(Z)}}{2\sqrt{\Var(W)}}+\frac{n^{4}}{2(\Var(W))^{3/2}}+\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\Var(Z)},

where

a(t):=|t|\max\biggl\{|\Phi^{\prime}(s)|:s\text{ lies between }t\text{ and }\frac{t\sqrt{\Var(Z)}}{\sqrt{\Var(W)}}\biggr\}.

Optimizing over
\varepsilon>0
, and applying (
4.2
) through the inequality

|\sqrt{\Var(Z)}-\sqrt{\Var(W)}|\leq\sqrt{\Var(Z-W)}\leq n,

we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z\leq\mathbb{E}(Z)+t\sqrt{\Var(Z)})

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(t)+a(t)\biggl|\frac{\sqrt{\Var(Z)}}{\sqrt{\Var(W)}}-1\biggr|+\frac{n^{4}}{2(\Var(W))^{3/2}}+\frac{C_{0}n^{2/3}}{(\Var(W))^{1/3}}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(t)+\frac{a(t)n}{\sqrt{\Var(W)}}+\frac{n^{4}}{2(\Var(W))^{3/2}}+\frac{C_{0}n^{2/3}}{(\Var(W))^{1/3}},

(4.3)

where
C_{0}
is a universal constant.
Next, by (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(Z-\mathbb{E}(Z)-W)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{E}(\eta_{ij}^{2})=\sum_{i<j}\Var(f_{ij}-g_{ij}-h_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq 3\sum_{i<j}(\Var(f_{ij})+\Var(g_{ij})+\Var(h_{ij}))

\displaystyle\leq 9\sum_{i<j}\Var(f_{ij}),

where, in the second inequality, we used the law of total variance.
Now recall from (
4
) that

\Var(Z)=\sum_{i<j}\Var(f_{ij})+\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{4\theta_{i}\theta_{j}^{2}\theta_{k}}{(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j}+\theta_{k})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{j})(\theta_{i}+\theta_{k})(\theta_{j}+\theta_{k})}.

Since the second term is nonnegative, we conclude that

\sum_{i<j}\Var(f_{ij})\leq\Var(Z),

and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(Z-\mathbb{E}(Z)-W)^{2}]\leq 9\Var(Z).

Thus,

\displaystyle\Var(W)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Var(Z)+2\Var(Z-W)\leq 11\Var(Z).

A consequence of this inequality is that
a(t)
is bounded above by a universal constant. Thus, by (
4
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z\leq\mathbb{E}(Z)+t\sqrt{\Var(Z)})

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(t)+\frac{n^{4}}{2(\Var(W))^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{C_{1}n}{\sqrt{\Var(W)}}+\frac{C_{0}n^{2/3}}{(\Var(W))^{1/3}},

where
C_{1}
is another universal constant. A similar argument shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z\leq\mathbb{E}(Z)+t\sqrt{\Var(Z)})

\displaystyle\geq\Phi(t)-\frac{n^{4}}{2(\Var(W))^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{C_{1}n}{\sqrt{\Var(W)}}-\frac{C_{0}n^{2/3}}{(\Var(W))^{1/3}}.

Combining these two bounds, and applying (
4.2
) via the inequality

\sqrt{\Var(W)}\geq\sqrt{\Var(Z)}-\sqrt{\Var(Z-W)}\geq\sqrt{\Var(Z)}-n

completes the proof.
∎

5
Comments and open questions

The Luce model is simply the most basic model of sampling from an urn without replacement. Thus, any reasonable question is worth studying. Extensive references in this direction are in
[
CDK23
]
.

5.1
Statistical applications

We have not emphasized it here, but as an
n-1
parameter statistical model, the Luce model is frequently fit to permutation data. The R package
PlackettLuce
is open-source R code with its own examples and guidebook; see
https://hturner.github.io/PlackettLuce/
or
https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/PlackettLuce/index.html
.

Of course, other models are used as well. The book by Marden
[
Mar96
]
is a useful source. This is grist for our mill. How can one test whether the Luce model is better than the Mallows model? Here, if
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a metric on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the Mallows model is:

\mathbb{P}_{\beta,\sigma_{0}}(\sigma)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\cdot d(\sigma,\sigma_{0})}}{Z}

with
\beta
a scale parameter and
\sigma_{0}
a location parameter to be estimated from the data (
Z
is a normalization constant).

In a fascinating study
[
AA25
]
, the Luce and Mallows models were each fitted to basketball ranking data. Mallows seemed better suited for predicting new data. This is surprising since the Luce model has
n-1
free parameters, and the version of Mallows used had only two free parameters (they used an
\ell^{p}
-metric and fit
p
,
\beta
, and
\sigma_{0}
). But Mallows has a discrete parameter
\sigma_{0}
. How should that be factored in?
This is
not
a standard statistical testing problem. One way to distinguish models is to look at “features”, such as descents and inversions, where limit theory can be used. This motivates the development of new limit theorems for both models. Our paper contributes to that. The problems below show how much remains to be understood.

5.2
Cycle structure

Here is a frustrating open problem for the Luce model: Pick
\sigma
from the Luce distribution on
S_{n}
. What is the limiting distribution of
c_{1}(\sigma)
– the number of fixed points of
\sigma
, in the presence of a permuton limit
\mu
(with density
\rho
) for
\sigma
?

It is natural to conjecture that
c_{1}(\sigma)
should be approximately
\text{Poisson}(\theta)
, with

\theta=\int_{0}^{1}\rho(x,x)\,dx,

when the above integral is finite. But one can immediately see that for the standard Sukhatme weights
\theta_{i}=n-i+1
, the density computed in (
2.8
) (recall that it is singular at
(1,1)
) leads to an infinite integral. In this case, we believe the number of fixed points tends to infinity, but do the number of fixed points still converge to the Poisson distribution after an appropriate rescaling? Some numerical simulations are shown in
Figure

6
.

Similarly, the joint distribution of
\{c_{i}(\sigma)\}_{i}
, where
c_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the number of
i
-cycles, and even the total number of cycles, is open.

5.3
Inverses and longest increasing subsequences

For a
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, if
\sigma(i)
is the label of the card at position
i
, then
\sigma^{-1}(i)
is the position of the card labeled
i
. How is
\sigma(i)
distributed if
\sigma
has a Luce distribution? In this paper, we looked at “typical” values of
i
; however, what about the case
i=1
?

This is important in applications, e.g., to horse racing. Of course, if
n
is small (e.g.,
n=5
or
7
in horse racing applications), the computer can blast it out. But we do not know limit theorems establishing what the limiting distribution of the label of the card at position 1 is (but recall that
[
CDK23
, Section 4]
gives the distribution of the position of the card labeled 1).

Permuton limit theory does not seem very useful here. This underlines a general question:

What functions on permutations are continuous in the permuton topology?

The huge literature on probabilistic combinatorics is filled with results which give open questions for the Luce model.
One that we would love to see solved is:

What is the limit distribution for the length of the longest increasing subsequence for a Luce-distributed permutation?

The theory developed in
[
Dub23
]
seems to be a good place to start.
