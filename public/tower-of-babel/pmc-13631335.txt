Lyapunov exponents explain disorder-induced polarization and soliton jumping in a mechanical Markov system
Authors: Will Stephenson, Nan Cheng, Kai Sun, Xiaoming Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing a mapping between spatial disorder and temporal stochasticity, we develop a new framework using Lyapunov exponents to explain exotic wave localization and mobility phenomena in disordered one-dimensional (1D) mechanical systems that can be constructed via a spatial analog of a Markov process, which we call “mechanical Markov systems.” We show that disorder induces robust polarization of zero modes (ZMs) in these mechanical Markov systems, and this phenomenon is explained using Lyapunov exponents. Remarkably, these ZMs become mobile solitons in the nonlinear regime despite the disorder-controlled localization of all other modes, and display a set of new nonlinear dynamics features including reflectionless chirality-dependent jumping, which can also be explained using Lyapunov exponents. Our results establish the Markov formalism as a powerful tool to explain and design localization and dynamics in disordered mechanical systems, opening opportunities for programmable metamaterials with novel linear and nonlinear responses.Subject terms: Condensed-matter physics, Physics

Introduction

Mapping between space and time has been central to many fronts of physics, from Thouless pumping1, Floquet topological states2,3, time crystals4, quantum criticality5,6, polymer—quantum mechanics mapping7, temporal imaging8, synthetic non-hermicity9, to AdS/CFT correspondence10. The identification of spatial order with temporal coherence reveals deep mathematical structures behind complex phenomena. In light of these classical theories, it is natural to wonder: can we extend this mapping to disordered systems?

Disorder is ubiquitous in nature, and has been increasingly utilized in engineered materials for their robustness against structural defects and fracture11–14, unusual mechanical responses15–17, control18,19, and ease of scalable manufacturing20,21. However, properties, such as wave propagation, are difficult to predict in disordered materials 22–24. In one dimension (1D), even infinitesimal disorder localizes all eigenstates due to Anderson localization, suppressing long-distance transport25, and where a given mode localizes depends sensitively on the disorder realization and is generally difficult to predict.

In this paper, utilizing a mapping between spatial disorder and temporal stochasticity, we discover a class of exotic wave phenomena originating from disorder. We report a one-dimensional (1D) Maxwell mechanical system with strong disorder where a polarization, analogous to the Kane-Lubensky topological polarization26,27, emerges from disorder. This polarization localizes a zero-frequency mode (ZM, a set of vertex displacements that result in no edge tension) to one end of the chain, depending on the construction. In the nonlinear regime, this ZM is mobile throughout the system, despite the localization of all other waves under disorder. Furthermore, this system demonstrates a set of striking effects, ranging from long-distance soliton transport that is robust against disorder to reflectionless chirality-dependent jumping, in which the soliton either skips across a finite segment of the chain and re-emerges from a distance, or nucleates a distant, counter-propagating partner, depending on the signal’s chirality.

Crucially, we explain these phenomena via a space-to-time mapping that identifies the 1D Maxwell mechanical system with a Markov process28–30. Disorder induces a pseudo-time steady state with a well-defined Lyapunov exponent that sets this disorder-induced polarization. This contrasts with the topological polarization extensively studied in ordered lattices, where the bulk bands determine the topological winding number and thus the polarization26. Although it has recently been shown that topological mechanical winding numbers can be extended to systems with disorder and aperiodicity31–33, the phenomena reported here are fundamentally different, where the polarization comes from disorder itself, without dependence on a topological winding number. The polarization is instead induced by disorder and protected by stochasticity. Interestingly, disorder also causes fluctuations from this steady state in the form of “defects,” analogous to kink-antikink pairs34,35. Within these defects, the Lyapunov exponent becomes positive, indicating pseudo-time chaos. We show that each of these kink-antikink pairs results in a specially separated, yet coupled, “quasi” ZM and “quasi” state of self-stress (where a true state of self-stress is a set of edge tensions that result in no vertex displacements). When real-time dynamics are included, the ZM becomes mobile and can traverse the system—despite the localization of all linear modes—and the defects underlie soliton jumping.

We show that the extension of space-time mapping to disordered systems offers a realm of new correspondences between temporal stochasticity and spatial disorder. As we discuss in this paper, such correspondences demonstrate that disorder can be utilized as a design rule to program novel linear and nonlinear responses that are absent in ordered systems. Recent advances in metamaterials revealed the rich space of properties, such as Poisson’s ratio, bandgap, and dissipation, that can be computationally engineered through disorder36. Our work provides a distinct example in which disorder produces behaviors beyond those of ordered systems, while remaining amenable to analytic treatment.

Results

Model and phenomena

We start from a 1D chain of rotors, which we will demonstrate is a simple mechanical Markov system (Fig. 1a). The rotor chain consists of equally spaced pivot points (separation distance a), around which perfectly stiff rotors of length r may freely rotate26,27. The free ends of each rotor arm are connected to the free ends of its two adjacent rotor arms by a spring of length l. The angle of each rotor arm (θn) is measured counter-clockwise (clockwise) for every odd (even) rotor, per convention27.

From an initial rotor with given angle θ1, we can add additional rotors as long as the free ends of these two rotors are separated by a distance l. Appropriate geometric parameters (a < l < r + a/2) ensure that there will always be two possible values of θn+1 that meet the spring length constraint for any value θn, allowing indefinite addition of rotors to a chain ("spinner phase” from27). The possible angles of additional rotors can be found by solving the following geometric equation for θn+1 as a function of θn (geometry shown by the dashed lines in Fig. 1a),

We call these two solutions fa(θn) and fb(θn), where the former is distinguished from the latter by having the lesser value mod 2π at θn = 0 (as in Fig. 1b). The analytic forms of fa and fb are shown in the Supplementary Note 2.

We construct disordered rotor chains by selecting an initial angle θ1 uniformly from [0, 2π], and then choosing from the two available angle solutions (fa, fb), with probabilities pa + pb = 1 for each subsequent rotor, as demonstrated in a cobweb plot shown in Fig. 1b. Different levels of order and disorder can be achieved by choosing between these solutions with different probabilities. For example, the perfectly-ordered Kane-Lubensky chain26 requires pa = 1 or 0. Since this construction determines the value of a new degree of freedom (θn+1) as a function of only its previous degree of freedom (θn), it is exactly analogous to a Markov process, as we show later. For this reason, we call this class of systems “mechanical Markov systems.”

Having one more degree of freedom than constraint (N rotors, N − 1 springs), any finite-length rotor chain with free boundary conditions, like other 1D Maxwell lattices, has exactly one ZM for the entire lattice37. When a rotor chain system is spatially ordered, such that all of its angles are equal (as in Fig. 1a), it is well understood that this ZM must exponentially localize to one of the ends (except for the special case when geometric parameters permit a configuration where all rotors are vertical or horizontal), forming a Kane-Lubensky rotor chain with a topological edge mode26.

Remarkably, these disordered rotor chains, where the angles are chosen stochastically between two branches, also have ZMs that exponentially localize to one end consistently (Fig. 1c). The conventional topological theory, which depends on long-range order and momentum space, does not apply to this disordered chain. The ZM polarization, as we discuss later, is a new emergent phenomenon from disorder, which we call “disorder-induced polarization”. Furthermore, one novel feature that arises due to disorder is “kink-antikink pairs” (Fig. 1e where small sections of the chain exhibit the opposite polarization, leaving [+] and [−] “quasi topological charges” at the interfaces between sections, corresponding to localized quasi ZMs and quasi states of self-stress (SSSs), as we discuss in detail later. Unlike in ordered KL chains, where kink-antikink pairs require special construction involving defining different rest lengths35, the disordered chain here is constructed following the same rule from left to right, and these kink-antikink pairs spontaneously arise.

It is known that in ordered rotor chains, in addition to well-understood, topologically protected edge ZMs, there are also topologically protected zero-energy solitons27,33,35,38,39. That is, when a rotor in an ordered chain is given some infinitesimal angular velocity, a soliton will uniformly travel through the chain, appearing as a domain wall between two oppositely polarized sub-chains27.

Interestingly, we find that disordered rotor chains exhibit similar soliton transport, where zero-energy solitons remain mobile despite the strong disorder, despite lacking topologically protected polarization. Moreover, the aforementioned kink-antikink pairs give rise to unique “defect” regions in the soliton dynamics of these disordered chains (see Supplementary Movie 1 for demonstration). These regions either result in solitons traveling slowly with every individual rotor completing nearly a full rotation (such as in Fig. 1d, defect I, t ≈ 2), or solitons traveling nearly instantly with every individual rotor hardly rotating (such as in Fig. 1d, defect II, t ≈ 4). Note that defect II presented in Fig. 1 is chosen to be unusually large for the sake of visual clarity.

This exponential localization of the ZM at the initial end of the chain, due to a steady state probability distribution of configurations, defines the aforementioned “disorder-induced polarization.” In the usual context of discrete time dynamical systems, 1/λ¯ represents a characteristic convergence/divergence time28. Here, it represents a “convergence/divergence” length. In other words, a localization length. Lyapunov exponents have previously been used in describing non-directional Anderson localization of electron wave functions in lattices with random potentials40. Here, we introduce a different mapping where we focus on zero modes only, supporting a strict forward-time propagation. This allows us to show the emergence of an overall system-spanning polarization from disorder.

For an ordered chain constructed exactly at a fixed point (all angles equal, pa = 0 or 1), λ¯ is simply equal to the local Lyapunov exponent at that fixed point. Here, the two stable fixed points have λa,b(θf.p.) = −0.570, giving the ordered decay rate of δθn/δθ1 = e−0.570(n−1) (slopes equal because θn → π − θn and θn → 2π − θn do not physically change the system, see Supplementary Note 1 for further details). This method of linearization around only the fixed points of the transfer functions as a means of explaining the ZMs of ordered rotor chains has been shown before by Zhou et al.35.

While the analysis so far has focused on the rotor chain for its familiarity, this process is in no way limited to only rotor chain systems, and can be applied to any 1D mechanical Markov system (such as chains of interlocking gears, see Supplementary Note 13 and Supplementary Movies 3 and 4 for physical demonstration). It is important to note that the expected mode localization of disordered Markov systems, while appearing qualitatively similar to the topologically protected edge modes of ordered systems, is not topological in origin. Expected ZM localization in disordered Markov systems comes from configurations with differing localizations being exponentially improbable, not fundamentally impossible (Fig. 2d). For this reason, we introduce the term “stochastic protection” to refer to robust properties of disordered materials that are not necessarily topologically guaranteed, but are specifically exponentially unlikely to occur. Stochastic protection is therefore an ensemble-level statistical protection, as opposed to the singular system-level guarantee of topological protection. For discussion on the robustness of this stochastic protection under perturbed geometry, see Supplementary Note 4.

Kink-antikink pairs and design principles

This stochastic protection of the exponential localization of the ZM to the generating end (left, due to λ¯<0) of the chain allows us to define the aforementioned concept of “disorder-induced polarization.” Furthermore, although the chain at large scales consistently exhibit a clear polarization towards the generating end (left, due to λ¯<0), at smaller scales, we find isolated short sections of the chain where random fluctuations allow for the ZM locally increase towards the right, displaying the opposite polarization (Fig. 1e). These sections give rise to kink-antikink pairs (following the notion of Ref. 35 for ordered chains), with [−] and [+] being localized quasi-topological charges. Here, we discuss the properties of these quasi-topological charges induced by fluctuations in ZM polarization and demonstrate a ZM localization design principle enabled by deterministic control over ZM polarization (Fig. 3).

By choosing repeated solutions from fα(β), the ZM amplitude is guaranteed to decrease (increase). Physically, this means that rotor rotation following the profile θα causes an extension profile described by eα, and the amplitude is proportional to ωα. For each low-frequency singular value, we have a C matrix right singular vector that is a “quasi ZM,” and a C matrix left singular vector that is a “quasi SSS.” As shown in Fig. 4c, they are spatially apart, with the quasi ZM coinciding with the max of the ZM and the quasi SSS coinciding with the min of the ZM. These quasi-ZMs and SSSs sharply define the kink-antikink pairs ([+] and [−] charges) we mentioned above. This is because kink-antikink pairs in 1D ordered Maxwell chains are defined as ZM and SSS interfaces35. Here, we extend their meaning to include low-frequency quasi ZMs and SSSs. Note that these topological charges and quasi-topological charges do not have charge magnitude, but merely a sign denoting whether they exist in the left or right singular space of C. Although they do have frequencies, as defined by Eq. (13), the reason why these quasi ZMs and SSSs coincide with the local maxima and minima of the topological ZM will be explained in the next section.

The fact that configurations that defy the stochastically protected ZM localization of disordered rotor chains are not technically impossible, only improbable to realize by random chance, alludes to the possibility of a clever design principle that allows for the creation of designed systems with unique mechanical properties. For specific configurations, to control ZM growth/decay for the next specific degree of freedom, one can always choose from the available local Lyapunov exponents (Eq. (8), choosing a λi∈[m](xn) for each n). However, this does not necessarily guarantee the availability of desired local Lyapunov exponents at each step needed to create desired steady-state behavior. Here, we will demonstrate a simple design process to create different possible steady-state localizations, which can be combined to create any possible sequence of ZM growth/decay in a mechanical Markov system.

From our two solution functions fa, fb, we can construct two piecewise solution functions fα, fβ with local Lyapunov exponents λα, λβ, respectively. We want to create these piecewise functions such that successive choices from fα (fβ) deterministically result in a negative (positive) Lyapunov exponent λ¯α (λ¯β) and therefore ZM decay (growth). Finding closed-form solutions for the invariant probability density of a given iterating function is generally difficult, as these probability distributions are usually fractal41. Therefore, knowing where to splice fα, fβ to necessarily and sufficiently create negative (positive) λ¯α(λ¯β) is also generally difficult. However, a merely sufficient condition for λ¯α<0,λ¯β>0, is if there are absorbing subsets42 (subsets that will “absorb,” as in contain indefinitely, all trajectories originating from any point in the domain) u, v of the domain for fα, fβ respectively, in which ∣fα′(u)∣<1,∣fβ′(v)∣>1. For our rotor chain functions with stable/unstable fixed points, this sufficient condition is approximately (not exactly) that one constructs fα(fβ) to have only stable (unstable) fixed points. “Trajectories” of new rotor angle solutions would then deterministically converge (diverge) around the stable (unstable) fixed points, giving λ¯α<0(λ¯β>0).

In the case of the rotor chain system, this can be achieved by splicing fα, fβ together at θ = π/2, 3π/2 (see Fig. 3a), resulting in u = 1.12 ∪ 2.01 (two stable fixed points), v = [3.39, 6.03], λ¯α≃−0.570 (the slopes at the two fixed points in u are equal via symmetry, see Supplementary Note 1), and λ¯β≃+0.489. By alternating between choosing solutions for subsequent rotor angles from fα and fβ, we can construct a chain that alternates between ZM decay and growth in any desired sequential pattern (see Fig. 3b, c). Such a process is possible for any 1D mechanical Markov system with at least two solution branches with appropriate absorbing subsets with all positive/negative slopes.

As this framework describes the behavior of systems that can be constructed via a Markov process, it applies naturally to self-assembled or synthesized materials where repeated additions of identical individual units need only be compatible with the unit they are connecting to. As these components can be added without a specific top-down design plan, it is common for them to realize structurally disordered or complex systems43, making their ZM localization heretofore highly challenging to study. For potentially controlling ZM localization in self-assembled systems, the simplicity in control over ZM growth/decay, as demonstrated in the rotor chain with a simple Boolean (fα or fβ), gives promising potential to simple and reliable customization in self-assembled systems. In real self-assembled systems, hypothetical examples of possible methods to control this boolean include the presence/absence of a catalyst or magnetic field, or by having two different sets of components that attach to only one solution or the other. The actual implementations of these control schemes are beyond the scope of the present work.

Perturbation theory for low-frequency displacement modes

As mentioned above, we find that positive (negative) quasi topological charges, defined at the local maxima and minima of the ZM, correspond exactly to qualitatively low-but-not-zero frequency displacement (stress) modes in the right (left) singular space of C (Fig. 4a, b). The modes exponentially localize to local maxima (minima) in the topological ZM, shown in Fig. 4c (note here that these springs have a finite stiffness k = 1 while rotors are perfectly stiff). These quasi ZMs and quasi SSSs, unique to disordered systems, are of great interest due to their close relation with soliton dynamics, as pairs of low-frequency modes appear at the middle and far ends of defect regions. Qualitatively, perturbation theory applies provided that two distinct, “somewhat” floppy regions (ZM maxima) along the rotor chain are separated by a “somewhat” rigid region (a ZM minimum). In this case, perturbation theory concludes that these floppy regions can “somewhat” act independently of each other, to an extent determined by how floppy they are compared to the intermediate rigid region.

Lyapunov exponents for explaining soliton propagation

As discussed in relation to Fig. 1d, e, we find that anomalous soliton dynamics occur in “defect” regions around quasi-topological charges defined in the previous section. This includes either slow soliton propagation, where each intermediate rotor completes nearly a full 2π rotation, or fast soliton jumping, where each intermediate rotor hardly rotates. We will now establish why these defects appear and why they are fundamentally related to quasi-topological charges.

Assuming that there is no defect at the beginning of a rotor chain, as rotor 1 initially rotates, λ2,1 increases. λ2,1 will necessarily eventually become positive at a later time, as both fa, fb have regions of their domain with a slope of magnitude greater than 1. This results in rotor 2 having a higher angular velocity than rotor 1, becoming the location of the soliton as the rotor with the highest angular velocity. Rotor 2 will now rotate until λ3,2 necessarily becomes positive, and so on. This pattern will continue through any region of the rotor chain with uniform effective polarization. However, this pattern gets interrupted when the soliton is at a rotor m, and a distant rotor n has a greater relative Lyapunov exponent λn,m than any rotor between n and m. In this case, rotor n will initially rotate more than m + 1, explaining why defects occur between distant rotors with a relative Lyapunov exponent (λn,m) of approximately zero (equivalent to the condition δθn ≈ δθm introduced in Fig. 1).

For a distant rotor n to have a higher Lyapunov exponent relative to a given rotor m than rotor m + 1 has relative to m, there must necessarily be a region of increasing λi,m with respect to i, for some range of i ∈ [m + 1, n]. This is exactly a region of opposite disorder-induced polarization. Assuming that this region of opposite effective polarization does not extend to the end of the chain, there must be at least one local minima and one local maxima in the ZM amplitude between rotors m and n. These extrema are exactly quasi topological charges (also low frequency stress and displacement modes) from the previous section, explaining why we always find quasi topological charges in regions of defective soliton transport.

Having now established why Lyapunov exponents explain the locations of defects (rotors between n, m when λn,m ≈ 0), and how defects are intrinsically linked to quasi topological charges, we now explain why these defect regions produce the two distinct types of modified transport and how these quasi topological charges interact with the transporting soliton. There are two types of modified transport, one where soliton velocity is decreased, and each intermediate rotor completes nearly a full rotation ("slow transport”), and one with nearly instantaneous soliton transport and each intermediate rotor hardly rotating ("fast soliton jump”). Interestingly, during slow transport, the positive and negative quasi charges associated with the defect in question “annihilate” with each other, while during fast transport, the positive quasi charge jumps in the opposite direction of the true topological charge (Fig. 5a, b). Quasi-charges annihilating during “slow” transport should not be totally surprising, as each rotor in the bulk of a defect having very high angular velocity during slow transport corresponds to a large relative Lyapunov exponent of these rotors with respect to rotor m, eliminating the local minimum/maximum pair in this region.

In order for the local minimum/maximum pair in λn,1 to annihilate, they must spatially move towards each other until they meet, as seen in quasi-charge locations during slow defects in Fig. 5a, c and Supplementary Movie 2. During a “fast jump,” the global maxima in λn,1 change from the local maxima at one end of a defect to the local maxima at the other end of the defect, corresponding to the soliton “jumping” across the defect. The promotion of a non-global maxima (quasi-topological charge) to a global maxima (topological charge) necessarily requires the demotion of the other local maxima from the global maxima to a mere non-global maxima, resulting in the quasi-charge “jumping” in the opposite direction of the soliton.

Returning to the fact that defects are the result of a distant rotor moving more than a local one (λn,m ≈ 0), one may wonder why all modified soliton transport is not a fast soliton jump, as in all cases of modified transport, the soliton does, at least briefly, quickly send some energy from rotor m to n. However, the range of values that θm must sweep out in order to complete this soliton transport to rotor n does not necessarily leave all intermediate rotors k ∈ (m, n) unchanged. where the function dθkdθm has a slope with a magnitude of approximately zero over most of its domain, with a small region of very large magnitude slope where θk completes nearly a full rotation, as demonstrated in Fig. 5d, e. Therefore, if transporting a soliton from rotor m to n requires θm to pass through one of these “narrow rapid cycles” in the function θk(θm), the soliton will have to wait for each intermediate rotor to complete nearly a full cycle before traveling to rotor n + 1. Each of these sequential full cycles requires the conserved total kinetic energy to be mostly concentrated into the single rapidly rotating rotor, making this process take a long time, hence the transport being “slow.” The existence of these rapid cycles is related to the fact that λk,m − λn,m < 0, where the further negative this quantity is (ie, the larger the defect), the narrower the rapid cycle (in θm space). For further discussion related to the relation between λk,m and the existence of rapid cycles, see Supplementary Note 10.

We can now see why returning solitons necessarily have oppositely modified transport. As θn+1(θn) is monotonic, any angles swept out by an incoming soliton (moving right to left) are mutually exclusive with the angles by the outgoing (moving left to right) soliton. Therefore, if a narrow rapid cycle is traversed by the outgoing soliton, it cannot be traversed by the incoming soliton. Similarly, solitons of opposite chirality (θ1 rotating clockwise or counter-clockwise) must experience opposite modified transport, as changing the sign of θ°1 necessarily changes the sign of all θ° from the monotonicity of θn+1(θn). If an initially clockwise soliton would have caused θm to traverse from θm,ti to θm,tf along a path with a narrow rapid cycle, an initially counter-clockwise soliton would then have to traverse between those angles in the other direction in mod2π space, avoiding the narrow rapid cycle. The differences between outgoing/incoming and clockwise/counterclockwise solitons can be seen in Fig. 5a, c. For discussion relating to soliton chirality through the lens of spin-orbit coupling, see Supplementary Note 11.

Because low-frequency modes have significantly increased frequency during slow transport (quasi-topological charges get annihilated) and decreased frequency during fast jump transport (as shown in Fig. 5b), we show how one can predict the type of modified soliton transport analytically without simulating any dynamics by using the Hellmann-Feynman theorem (Supplementary Note 12). In combination with using relative Lyapunov exponents to identify the locations of defects, one can completely predict the time-dependent behavior of a disordered rotor chain using only parameters known to its static configuration. This one-way chirality-dependent transport is highly unusual for mechanical systems in general, and is even unusual in rotor chains, as its appearance is dependent on intrinsic disorder. Here, we have made major progress towards analytically understanding the complex dynamics of these disordered systems.

Crossover between order and disorder

The rotor chains discussed above are strongly disordered in the sense that a random binary sequence is used to generate the chain, with solution branch a (b) chosen with probability pa (pb = 1 − pa). It is useful to examine the phenomena we observe here as functions of a continuously varied level of disorder.

It is worth noting first that disorder is a high-dimensional space of configurations: there are infinitely many ways to splice the two possible solution branches to create an alternative version of (fa, fb); after choosing a solution-branch splice, one can choose any correlated or uncorrelated binary sequence. Here, we just sample this high-dimensional space by choosing three parameters to quantify disorder in these rotor chains.

The first one is pa ∈ [0, 1] which controls the switch between the two natural branches of the solution, where pa = 0 or 1 gives the ordered Kane-Lubensky chain, and pa = 1/2 is the unbiased strong disorder limit. As shown in Fig. 6a (blue), the mean Lyapunov exponent λ¯ varies continuously between the Kane-Lubensky chain value λ¯≃−0.570 and the maximum λ¯≃−0.311 at pa = 1/2. Along this parameter, disorder-induced polarization always exists.

The second parameter is pα ∈ [0, 1], which controls the switch between two spliced functions with negative and positive Lyapunov exponents. As shown in Fig. 6a (yellow), the mean Lyapunov exponent λ¯ varies continuously from the Kane-Lubensky chain value λ¯≃−0.570 at pα = 0 to positive values. The switch from negative to positive around pα ≃ 0.7 marks the interruption of disorder-induced polarization when the positive λ branch is picked too frequently.

The third parameter is p~, a new parameter we choose to represent correlated disorder in the binary sequence. We start from an arbitrary initial branch fa or fb. At each step of adding a new rotor n + 1, with probability p~, we switch to the opposite branch from which rotor n was generated, and stay on the same branch otherwise. In other words, at p~=0 we always stay on the same branch and get the Kane-Lubensky chain, and at p~=1 we always switch to the opposite branch for rotor n + 1 compared to rotor n. Note that p~=0 is equivalent to pa = 0 or 1 (depending on the first choice).

It is interesting to notice that pa = 1/2, pα = 1/2, and p~=1/2 all represent the exact same ensemble of disordered configurations: when it is equally probable to choose either branches, it does not matter how we splice the function. This is the most disordered case, which was used as the main system in our discussions above. p~=1/2 is equivalent to pa = 1/2, but all other values of p~ represent correlated disorder not covered by the pa or pα parameter. As shown in Fig. 6a (green), the mean Lyapunov exponent increases as a function of p~, where the three curves cross at 1/2 as we expected. We plot the inverse participation ratio of the modes at p~ values of 0, 0.5, and 1 in Fig. 6d–f, showing how disorder induces localization and merges gaps at the same time.

The rotor chain at p~=1 is a particularly interesting case: it is an ordered chain with quasi-periodicity: the rotor angle evolves monotonically (+ or − depending on the initial choice), but the change of 2π typically does not occur over an integer number of rotors, leading to an incommensurability between rotors and rotor angle across 2π. This incommensurability places these rotor chains in the class of incommensurate Aubry-André-Harper (AAH) models44–46, which are known to exhibit fractal spectra called “Hofstadter butterflies,” as can be seen in Fig. 6b. As disorder is introduced decreasing p~ from p~=1, this AAH spectra starts to “melt,” showing interesting features of modes moving from bulk bands to the gaps, and becoming more localized at the same time (Fig. 6c).

It is now worth clarifying the different possible interpretations of “disorder” in these ensembles. What we have been referring to as a “maximally” disordered rotor chain ensemble is an ensemble that generates binary solution choice sequences of the highest block entropy47 (pa=pα=p~=1/2). As shown in Fig. 6a, this is quite different from the chaos theory interpretation of order and disorder (loosely λ¯ being negative or positive, respectively). pα = 0, 1 both have perfectly ordered binary sequences, yet have nearly opposite Lyapunov exponents. In terms of block entropy, the pα = 0, 1 systems are equally ordered, while one has converging and the other diverging trajectories through θn space. Different from both of these interpretations, one could measure the “structural” disorder as the variance of some physical properties throughout a system. There are many ways to define such a metric (such as variation of shear modulus48), but they do not necessarily apply to all physical systems. In Supplementary Note 5, we show the variance of λ(θ) as a function of pa, pα, and p~. The extent of any relationship between these different understandings of disorder is an interesting question for future work.

It is useful to place these results in the broader context of how disorder affects phonon spectra, where it is known that bands typical of ordered crystals broaden as a result of disorder, causing bandgaps to blur and close49–51. Although it is not entirely straightforward to compare this rotor chain system to these studies, given the special features of the rotor chain, e.g., the lack of acoustic phonons due to rotor pinning, it is of interest for future work to fully explore and quantify disorder effects in these systems.

Discussion

We report a new class of disorder-induced wave phenomena in 1D using a simple mechanical Markov chain model. By identifying the Lyapunov exponent via a space-to-time mapping as the key quantity governing localization, we demonstrate that disorder—rather than destroying coherent behavior—induces a form of stochastic polarization: ZMs are exponentially unlikely to delocalize away from the one particular end of the chain depending on the order of construction. This provides a powerful predictive tool for both disordered and designed systems, and allows us to utilize the concept of Lyapunov exponents to engineer arbitrary localization patterns, a functionality important for many engineering problems52–55.

Beyond the linear regime, we show that disorder gives rise to rich nonlinear dynamics that have not been observed before, including defect-controlled soliton transport and fast jumping phenomena that are sensitive to chirality. These behaviors highlight how disorder can serve as a design principle, generating responses unattainable in ordered systems while remaining analytically tractable.

More broadly, the framework we establish here suggests new pathways for harnessing disorder for novel wave phenomena. The Markov-process perspective naturally applies to self-assembled or bottom-up synthesized systems, where a large class of systems follows particle-by-particle attachment type of growth, resembling the Markov construction56,57. In these systems, stochasticity is unavoidable yet may be exploited for robust function. Future work may extend these ideas to higher dimensions through methods similar to causal 2D Markov fields58, to active or non-Hermitian systems59 where Lyapunov spectra carry additional richness, and to experimental platforms where disorder can be precisely engineered60. Taken together, our results point to a new paradigm in which Lyapunov exponents provide a systematic language to design, predict, and control the emergent properties of disordered systems.
