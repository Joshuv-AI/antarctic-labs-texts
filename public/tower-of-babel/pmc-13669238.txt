Longitudinal Validity of the DUNDRUM-1 Triage Security Scale in a Canadian Not Criminally Responsible Cohort: A Retrospective Study of 85 Patients
Authors: Alexandre Hudon, Fanie Chainey, Catherine Degré, Anne-Renée Courtemanche, Marc-André Meunier, Allyson Fortier, Camille Bourque, Loriane Boulé, Vincent Latreille, Sophie Mongeon, Jeanne-Marie Allard, Stéphanie Borduas Pagé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the longitudinal stability of DUNDRUM-1 Triage Security Scale recommendations among individuals found not criminally responsible on account of mental disorder (NCRMD) receiving care within a Canadian forensic psychiatric service.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults found NCRMD who received a DUNDRUM-1 assessment at a tertiary academic forensic psychiatric centre in Quebec between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2024. Eligible participants were aged ≥18 years and had a minimum of 12 months of follow-up. Demographic, forensic, and clinical variables were extracted from electronic medical records. Primary outcomes included changes in level of therapeutic security within 12 months. Secondary outcomes included psychiatric rehospitalization and transitions across DUNDRUM security categories. Descriptive statistics, Fisher's exact tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, and exploratory logistic regression analyses were performed.ResultsEighty-five individuals met inclusion criteria. The cohort was predominantly male (91.8%) with a mean age of 39.0 years (SD = 11.9). Schizophrenia spectrum disorders were the most common primary diagnosis (83.5%), while substance-related disorders (74.1%) and personality disorders (49.4%) were frequent comorbidities. Initial DUNDRUM recommendations included low security (56.5%), medium security (35.3%), open settings (4.7%), and maximum security (3.5%). Overall, 72 participants (84.7%) remained within their initially recommended level of care over 12 months, while 13 (15.3%) required a change in therapeutic security. Psychiatric rehospitalization occurred in 12 participants (14.1%). Exploratory analyses identified the mean violence-related DUNDRUM score as the only significant predictor of changes in level of care (OR = 2.88, 95% CI: 1.03-8.00, p = .043). In exploratory random forest analysis, SHapley Additive exPlanations ranked the mean violence-related DUNDRUM-1 score as the most influential model feature.ConclusionsDUNDRUM-1 recommendations demonstrated substantial longitudinal stability within this Canadian NCRMD cohort, supporting the ecological validity of the instrument and highlighting the potential importance of violence-related domains in predicting medium-term forensic outcomes.Keywords: not criminally responsible on account of mental disorder (NCRMD), forensic psychiatry, DUNDRUM-1, therapeutic security, review board, structured professional judgment, violence risk assessment, psychiatric rehospitalization

Introduction

In Canada, individuals found not criminally responsible on account of mental disorder (NCRMD) occupy a unique position at the intersection of the mental health and criminal justice systems. 1 Under section 16 of the Canadian Criminal Code, an individual may be found NCRMD when a mental disorder rendered them incapable of appreciating the nature and quality of their actions or of knowing that they were wrong at the time of the offence. 2 Following an NCRMD verdict, individuals become subject to provincial or territorial Review Boards, which make dispositions that are necessary and appropriate in the circumstances, with public safety as the paramount consideration. 3 In Quebec, these decisions are made by the Commission d’examen des troubles mentaux (CETM), which generally reviews dispositions every 12 months, with earlier review possible when warranted, to determine whether individuals should remain detained, transition to less restrictive settings, or receive conditional or absolute discharge. Consequently, forensic psychiatric services are continually required to balance public safety with recovery, autonomy, and community reintegration.4,5

Determining the appropriate level of therapeutic security is therefore a central challenge in forensic psychiatry.6,7 Therapeutic security encompasses physical, relational, and procedural elements designed to support treatment while mitigating risks related to violence, self-harm, absconding, and clinical deterioration. 6 Historically, placement decisions have relied largely on clinical judgment and local practices, contributing to variability across jurisdictions.8,9 To address this issue, the Dundrum Toolkit was developed in Ireland as a structured professional judgment framework intended to support decision-making across the forensic continuum of care. The DUNDRUM-1 Triage Security Scale, one component of the toolkit, consists of 11 items assessing domains including seriousness of violence, immediate risk, specialist forensic needs, absconding risk, institutional behaviour, victim sensitivity, and legal considerations. 10 Together, these items provide a structured approach for recommending levels of therapeutic security and promoting greater consistency and transparency in forensic decision-making.

Over the past two decades, studies conducted in Europe and the United Kingdom have demonstrated that DUNDRUM-1 scores are associated with levels of therapeutic security and can discriminate between individuals requiring varying intensities of forensic care.11-13 Additional investigations have supported the instrument's reliability and construct validity and have suggested associations between DUNDRUM measures and progression through forensic systems, recovery trajectories, and discharge outcomes.11-14 Collectively, these findings indicate that structured approaches to therapeutic security may improve consistency in placement decisions and provide a common framework for discussing treatment needs and risk within forensic mental health services.

Despite growing international evidence, Canadian data remain limited. DUNDRUM-1 has demonstrated utility in determining hospital admission and security needs among provincially detained women in Ontario, while a recent New Zealand study examined its utility for forensic placement decisions.15,16 However, no study has examined the longitudinal stability of DUNDRUM-1 recommendations in a Canadian NCRMD population. Existing Canadian studies have primarily focused on implementation, measurement-based care and feasibility rather than evaluating whether DUNDRUM-1 recommendations remain stable over time. 17 Consequently, it remains unclear whether the instrument performs similarly among Canadian NCRMD populations or whether specific clinical and forensic characteristics are associated with subsequent changes in therapeutic security.

Establishing whether these recommendations remain stable over time is particularly important in the Canadian context. Stable recommendations would provide support for the ecological validity of the instrument, whereas frequent transitions in care could suggest limitations in its ability to capture evolving clinical and forensic needs.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the longitudinal stability of DUNDRUM-1 recommendations in a retrospective cohort of individuals found NCRMD receiving care within a Canadian forensic psychiatric service. Specifically, we sought to determine the prevalence of changes in level of care within 12 months following an initial DUNDRUM-1 assessment, identify DUNDRUM-1 factors associated with these changes, examine the role of psychiatric and forensic characteristics, and explore rates of psychiatric rehospitalization following discharge. By examining real-world trajectories within a Canadian NCRMD cohort, this study seeks to contribute to the growing literature on structured professional judgment in forensic psychiatry and inform the use of DUNDRUM-1 within Canadian forensic mental health systems.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study examined the longitudinal stability of DUNDRUM-1 recommendations among individuals found NCRMD receiving care within a Canadian forensic psychiatric service. The study was conducted at the Institut universitaire en santé mentale de Montréal, a tertiary academic psychiatric centre providing specialized forensic mental health services across inpatient, rehabilitation, and community settings. Individuals found NCRMD are reviewed by the CETM, which determines the necessary and appropriate disposition based on clinical status, risk, and rehabilitation needs. DUNDRUM-1 assessments are routinely integrated into clinical decision-making to inform recommendations regarding therapeutic security. Eligible participants were identified between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2024. Data were extracted from electronic medical records following approval from the institutional research ethics board. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement for cohort studies. 18

Participants

Participants were identified through a review of institutional forensic psychiatry records. Individuals were eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years of age or older, had received an NCRMD verdict under the Canadian Criminal Code, had a completed DUNDRUM-1 assessment documented in their medical record, and had a minimum of 12 months of available follow-up data after the initial DUNDRUM-1 assessment. Patients were excluded if DUNDRUM-1 data were incomplete or unavailable at baseline. All consecutive NCRMD patients meeting eligibility criteria during the study period were included.

A total of 85 individuals met inclusion criteria and were included in the final cohort. All participants were under the jurisdiction of the CETM at the time of their initial DUNDRUM-1 assessment. The resulting sample reflects a naturalistic cohort of individuals receiving routine forensic psychiatric care within a Canadian academic centre.

Measures and Variables

Demographic, forensic, and clinical variables were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized data collection form. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, educational attainment, and employment status at the time of the index offence. Forensic variables comprised the NCRMD index offence, prior criminal history, and the number of NCRMD verdicts. Index offences were harmonized into Canadian Criminal Code categories, including violent, sexual, property, administration of justice, and mixed offences, while prior criminal history was classified as absent, violent, or mixed. Primary psychiatric diagnoses were categorized according to DSM-5-TR diagnostic categories. Secondary psychiatric comorbidities and physical health conditions were harmonized into binary clinical domains to facilitate statistical analyses and improve interpretability of findings across clinically relevant categories.

DUNDRUM-1 Assessment

The DUNDRUM-1 Triage Security Scale is a structured professional judgment instrument designed to guide decisions regarding the level of therapeutic security required in forensic psychiatric settings. The instrument comprises 11 items scored on a five-point ordinal scale, with higher scores indicating greater need for therapeutic security. The 11 items assess seriousness of violence, seriousness of self-harm, immediate risk of violence, immediate risk of self-harm, specialist forensic need, absconding risk, institutional behaviour, preventing access, victim sensitivity and public confidence, complex risk of violence, and legal process considerations. Assessments were completed by a psychiatrist and another trained mental health professional; scoring discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Item scores informed, but did not mechanically determine, the security-level recommendation; no fixed total-score cut-offs were used.

In addition to individual item scores, several derived measures were calculated for analytical purposes. These included the total DUNDRUM-1 score, the overall mean score, and total and mean scores excluding self-harm Items 2 and 4; the latter mean was termed the violence-related score. Given the low variability of self-harm scores in this predominantly violence-related cohort, analyses excluding Items 2 and 4 were conducted as sensitivity analyses.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was a change in level of care within 12 months of the initial DUNDRUM-1 assessment. A change in level of care was defined as any documented transition between levels of therapeutic security or treatment intensity during the follow-up period.

Secondary outcomes included psychiatric rehospitalization among individuals discharged from hospital, changes in DUNDRUM security categories in the following year, and the level of therapeutic security documented within 12 months following the initial assessment. Therapeutic security was categorized as maximum security (intensive forensic inpatient care), medium security (specialized forensic inpatient care), low security (general psychiatric inpatient care), or open setting (community or residential care). These security levels reflected clinical placement and were distinct from CETM legal dispositions. For individuals who experienced multiple transitions during follow-up, the highest level of therapeutic security reached during the observation period was retained for analysis. Initial placements were concordant with DUNDRUM-1 recommendations for all participants. Rehospitalization was assessed following any hospital discharge during follow-up.

For participants who experienced psychiatric rehospitalization, clinical documentation surrounding the admission was additionally reviewed to characterize the documented precipitating circumstances. These circumstances were summarized descriptively and included clinical deterioration, treatment disengagement or nonadherence, substance use, escalating behavioural or safety concerns, aggression or new offending, and other documented concerns. Because multiple factors could contribute to a single admission, categories were not considered mutually exclusive. No attempt was made to retrospectively classify rehospitalizations as strictly proactive or reactive.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Continuous variables are reported using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

Given the relatively small sample size and limited number of primary outcome events, non-parametric statistical methods were preferentially employed. Comparisons between participants who did and did not experience a change in level of care within 12 months were conducted using Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous and ordinal variables. Given only 13 primary outcome events, exploratory logistic regressions were restricted to univariate models. Predictors (total and violence-related DUNDRUM-1 scores, substance-related and personality disorders, and mixed criminal history) were selected based on clinical relevance and Canadian NCRMD trajectory literature, particularly the National Trajectory Project.1,3

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, analyses were repeated using psychiatric rehospitalization within 12 months as the outcome variable. Second, DUNDRUM-1 scores excluding self-harm items were examined to determine whether associations differed when the assessment focused exclusively on violence-related domains. All analyses were conducted using a two-sided significance threshold of p < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.3) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 31).

An exploratory random forest analysis examined potentially nonlinear associations with change in level of care using total and violence-related DUNDRUM-1 scores, mixed criminal history, substance-related disorder, personality disorder, number of NCRMD verdicts, schizophrenia spectrum disorder, age, chronic physical illness, and sex, selected based on clinical relevance and Canadian NCRMD trajectory literature. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a model-explainability method, quantifies how much each variable shifts an individual prediction from the model's baseline; aggregated absolute SHAP values were used to rank overall feature importance. Given the small sample and limited events, analyses were considered hypothesis-generating.

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 85 individuals found NCRMD met inclusion criteria and were included in the study. The cohort was predominantly composed of individuals with schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders, which accounted for 71 participants (83.5%), followed by bipolar and related disorders (12.9%) and neurodevelopmental disorders (3.5%). Secondary psychiatric comorbidities were common, with substance-related and addictive disorders identified in 74.1% of participants and personality disorders in 49.4%. Approximately one-third of the sample presented with cardiometabolic disease (29.4%), while musculoskeletal (23.5%) and neurological (15.3%) conditions were also frequently documented. Overall, 84.7% of participants had at least one chronic physical health condition.

The cohort exhibited substantial psychosocial vulnerability. Educational attainment was generally low, with most participants having completed secondary education or less, and unemployment at the time of the index offence was highly prevalent. Forensically, the majority of participants had index offences involving interpersonal violence, while approximately one-third had no prior history of recorded criminal offences. Mixed offending histories were observed in 38.8% of participants, highlighting the complexity of the forensic presentations encountered in routine clinical practice. Initial Therapeutic Security Placement

Nearly three-fifths of participants were initially assigned to low-security settings (56.5%), corresponding to general psychiatric care, while approximately one-third were recommended for medium-security forensic units (35.3%). Only four participants (4.7%) were managed in open settings, and three individuals (3.5%) required maximum-security placements.

Twelve-Month Outcomes

At 12-month follow-up, most participants remained clinically stable with respect to their level of therapeutic security. A total of 72 individuals (84.7%) experienced no change in level of care during the observation period, while 13 participants (15.3%) underwent at least one transition across levels of therapeutic security or treatment intensity.

Twelve participants (14.1%) were rehospitalized following discharge during the 12-month follow-up period. Review of the circumstances surrounding these admissions indicated that rehospitalization was heterogeneous and frequently multifactorial. Documented precipitants included worsening psychiatric symptoms (n = 4), escalating behavioural or safety concerns (n = 2), treatment disengagement or medication nonadherence (n = 2), substance use or relapse (n = 1), and aggression or new offending-related concerns (n = 3). In several cases, admission occurred in response to emerging clinical or behavioural concerns before a more serious adverse event had occurred, whereas others followed more overt clinical deterioration or risk escalation. Because these circumstances frequently overlapped, rehospitalizations could not be reliably dichotomized as proactive versus reactive. Among those requiring rehospitalization, several returned to higher levels of therapeutic security, although the overall number of such events remained limited. When considering the highest level of therapeutic security reached during follow-up, medium-security forensic settings represented the most common category, reflecting the persistence of specialized forensic needs among a substantial proportion of the cohort. Predictors of Change in Level of Care

Exploratory univariate logistic regression analyses identified the mean violence-related DUNDRUM-1 score as the only statistically significant predictor of changes in level of care within 12 months (OR = 2.88, 95% CI: 1.03-8.00, p = .043). Total DUNDRUM-1 scores demonstrated a non-significant trend toward association (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.99-1.24, p = .086). Neither substance-related disorders, personality disorders, nor mixed criminal histories were significantly associated with changes in therapeutic security (Table 4).

Exploratory Analyses

Exploratory analyses of psychiatric rehospitalization revealed findings consistent with the primary outcome. Twelve participants (14.1%) experienced psychiatric rehospitalization within 12 months following discharge. Rehospitalized individuals had descriptively higher total and violence-related DUNDRUM-1 scores. In the exploratory random forest analysis, SHAP ranked the mean violence-related DUNDRUM-1 score as the most influential model feature. Longitudinal analyses demonstrated substantial stability in therapeutic security over the 12-month follow-up period. Initially, 56.5% of participants were assigned to low-security settings, 35.3% to medium-security forensic units, 4.7% to open settings, and 3.5% to maximum-security care. Overall, 72 participants (84.7%) remained within their initial DUNDRUM category, whereas 13 (15.3%) experienced at least one transition. Among those requiring changes in care, movement toward higher levels of therapeutic security was more frequent than progression to less restrictive environments, with transfers from general psychiatry to specialized forensic services representing the most common pattern. Nevertheless, several individuals successfully transitioned to community-based settings. Given the clinical complexity of the cohort, characterized by high rates of psychotic disorders, substance use disorders, personality pathology, and chronic physical illness, these findings provide preliminary support for the ecological validity and longitudinal stability of DUNDRUM-1 recommendations in Canadian forensic psychiatric practice.

Discussion

Approximately 85% of NCRMD patients remained in their initially recommended level of care over 12 months. This finding provides preliminary evidence supporting the longitudinal stability of DUNDRUM-1 recommendations within a Canadian forensic psychiatric setting. Only 15.3% of participants required a change in level of care, while 14.1% experienced psychiatric rehospitalization. Exploratory analyses further suggested that violence-related DUNDRUM-1 domains, particularly the mean violence-related score, were more strongly associated with changes in therapeutic security than the overall DUNDRUM-1 score.

These findings are consistent with previous international studies supporting the validity of the DUNDRUM Toolkit and extend the existing literature by examining longitudinal rather than cross-sectional outcomes.11-17 Although direct comparisons are limited by differences in legal and organizational frameworks, our results suggest that DUNDRUM-1 performs similarly within the Canadian context. The observed stability may reflect appropriate identification of therapeutic security needs, but may also partly reflect the 12-month Review Board cycle and relatively stable forensic care trajectories.

The finding that violence-related DUNDRUM scores outperformed total scores is particularly noteworthy. Given that individuals in this cohort were under Review Board jurisdiction for offences involving interpersonal violence, violence-related criteria may possess greater prognostic relevance than self-harm domains when evaluating medium-term outcomes.19,20 Importantly, this score excluded self-harm items but retained broader domains including forensic need, absconding, institutional behaviour, victim sensitivity, and legal considerations. DUNDRUM-1 therefore complements rather than replaces violence risk instruments such as the HCR-20V3, as it addresses therapeutic security needs rather than violence risk alone. 10

From a clinical perspective, these findings support further evaluation of DUNDRUM-1 within Canadian forensic services. Structured approaches to therapeutic security may improve transparency, facilitate communication with Review Boards and other stakeholders, and inform resource allocation in systems characterized by limited forensic capacity. 21 Psychiatric rehospitalization occurred in approximately one in seven participants. The circumstances surrounding rehospitalization were heterogeneous, ranging from emerging clinical or behavioural concerns prompting preventive intervention to more overt psychiatric or risk deterioration. This heterogeneity underscores that rehospitalization in forensic populations does not necessarily represent a uniform adverse outcome or failure of community placement. Consistent with previous forensic literature, schizophrenia spectrum disorders, substance use disorders, and personality disorders were highly prevalent within the cohort.22-23

This study represents, to our knowledge, the first Canadian investigation examining the longitudinal stability of DUNDRUM-1 recommendations among individuals found NCRMD. Strengths include the use of real-world clinical data, comprehensive characterization of the cohort, and the availability of 12-month follow-up data. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged, including the single-centre design, modest sample size, retrospective methodology, and limited number of outcome events. Additionally, factors such as social supports, treatment adherence, and evolving legal considerations were not systematically captured. Also, although disagreements were resolved by consensus, formal interrater reliability coefficients were not available.

Future studies should replicate these findings across larger, multicenter Canadian NCRMD cohorts and examine longer-term trajectories of therapeutic security. These trajectories may provide important complementary information on patients’ treatment progress and recovery, which are central objectives of forensic psychiatric care. In this context, the finding that most patients remained at the same level of security after 12 months warrants further investigation. Stability may reflect appropriate continuity of care for some patients, but among individuals with relatively low baseline security needs, it is important to determine whether greater progression toward less restrictive settings or conditional discharge might reasonably be expected over time.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates substantial longitudinal stability of DUNDRUM-1 recommendations and provides preliminary support for their ecological validity within a Canadian NCRMD population. The finding that approximately 85% of participants remained within their initially recommended level of care over a 12-month period suggests that the instrument captures clinically meaningful determinants of therapeutic security. Moreover, violence-related DUNDRUM domains appeared to be more strongly associated with medium-term outcomes than aggregate scores, highlighting their potential value in informing clinical decision-making. Although these findings require replication, they support the continued integration of structured professional judgment approaches into Canadian forensic psychiatric practice.

Future research should evaluate DUNDRUM-1 across multiple Canadian forensic programs and longer follow-up periods to confirm its longitudinal performance and identify predictors of therapeutic security trajectories.
