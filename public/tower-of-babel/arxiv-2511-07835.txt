Testing noisy low-degree polynomials for sparsity
Yiqiao Bao Anindya De Shivam Nadimpalli University of Pennsylvania University of Pennsylvania MIT yiqiaob@seas.upenn.edu anindyad@cis.upenn.edu shivamn@mit.edu Rocco A. Servedio Nathan White Columbia University University of Pennsylvania rocco@cs.columbia.edu nathanlw@cis.upenn.edu

Abstract
We consider the problem of testing whether an unknown low-degree polynomial p p over ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} is sparse versus far from sparse, given access to noisy evaluations of the polynomial p p at randomly chosen points . This is a natural property-testing version of various well-studied problems about learning low-degree sparse polynomials in the presence of noise, and is a generalization of the work of Chen, De, and Servedio [ 13 ] , on testing noisy linear functions for sparsity, to the more challenging setting of low-degree polynomials. Our main result gives a precise characterization of when sparsity testing for low-degree polynomials can be carried out with constant sample complexity independent of dimension, along with a constant-sample algorithm for this problem in the parameter regime where this is possible. Introduction

The field of property testing investigates algorithms that, given query access to an unknown function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, determine whether (i)
f
belongs to some known class of functions
\mathcal{C}
(i.e.,
f
has the property
\mathcal{C}
), versus (ii)
f
is “far” from any function
g\in\mathcal{C}
(i.e.,
f
is “far” from having property
\mathcal{C}
).
Starting with the seminal works of
[
8
,
9
,
41
]
, several fundamental properties
\mathcal{C}
have been intensively studied from this perspective, including
low-degree polynomials
[
8
,
38
,
1
]
, juntas
[
21
,
7
]
, monotonicity
[
23
,
22
,
12
]
, and geometric classes such as halfspaces
[
33
,
27
,
16
]
, to name a few.
From the point of view of complexity theory, this framework has been highly influential, most notably through its applications to probabilistically checkable proofs (PCPs) and hardness of approximation.

Despite its success, the classical framework rests on two assumptions that limit its relevance to contemporary data analysis and machine learning.
The first is that most function classes
\mathcal{C}
traditionally studied in theoretical computer science are Boolean-valued.
This choice is natural from the viewpoint of complexity theory but is less suited to statistical and machine learning settings, where the outputs—or “response variables”—are typically real-valued.
It is therefore natural to consider classes
\mathcal{C}
of real-valued functions.

An arguably bigger chasm is the assumption on how the testing algorithm accesses the function
f
.
Motivated by applications to PCPs, hardness of approximation, and coding theory, the classical framework assumes that the tester has
query access
to
f
.
In contrast, settings arising in statistics and machine learning typically provide much weaker access—namely,
random samples
labeled according to
f
.
Property testing in this sample-based model is significantly more challenging from an algorithmic standpoint.
We refer the reader to
[
10
,
28
,
24
]
for some results on sample-based testing of Boolean functions.
A recurring theme in this line of work is that the guarantees achievable in the sample-based model are often much weaker than those in the query model.
As an example, for the celebrated problem of linearity testing over
\mathsf{GF}[2]
, there are query algorithms that use only
\Theta(1)
queries
[
8
]
, but the same problem requires
\Theta(n)
samples in the sample-based testing model
[
24
]
.

Background: Testing sparse linear functions from samples.

The most relevant prior work for the present paper is the work of Chen, De and Servedio
[
13
]
.
In their model, the algorithm gets independent
labeled examples of the form

(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}=w\cdot\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\eta})\,,

where each
\boldsymbol{x}
is drawn from some distribution
{\cal D}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
w
is an unknown vector, and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is a “noise distribution” supported over
\mathbb{R}
.
The goal in this model is to distinguish between the following two cases: (i)
w
is
s
-sparse, versus (ii)
w
is at least
\varepsilon
-far from every
s
-sparse vector (in the sense that at most
1-\varepsilon
of the Euclidean norm of
w
comes from its
s
largest coefficients). In other words,
[
13
]
studies the problem of “testing noisy linear functions for sparsity.”

Roughly speaking,
[
13
]
show as their main result that if (i)
{\cal D}
is an i.i.d. distribution
{\cal D}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
(where
\boldsymbol{X}
is known to the testing algorithm), (ii) the noise distribution
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is known to the testing algorithm (and independent of
\boldsymbol{x}
), and (iii)
{\cal D}
is not a Gaussian distribution, then there is an algorithm for this problem whose sample complexity depends only on the sparsity parameter
s
, the error parameter
\varepsilon
, and the real-valued distribution
\boldsymbol{X}
. Crucially, the sample complexity is
dimension independent
, i.e., does not depend on the ambient dimension
n
.
Furthermore,
[
13
]
established that if any of the three conditions above are relaxed, the sample complexity of the problem becomes
\widetilde{\Omega}(\log n)
.

This work: Testing sparse low-degree polynomials from samples.

We study a natural and challenging extension of the problem considered in
[
13
]
.
In particular, we investigate the problem of “testing
low-degree polynomials
for sparsity”—thus, the results of
[
13
]
correspond to the special case of degree-1 polynomials.
In more detail, in our model the algorithm gets independent labeled examples of the form

(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}=p(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\eta})\,,

where
p
is now promised to be a degree
d
polynomial.
The goal is to distinguish between the two cases that (i)
p
is
s
-sparse, versus (ii)
p
is “far from
s
-sparse” (we make this notion precise shortly).
As in
[
13
]
, and in keeping with the usual “gold standard” for efficient property testing algorithms, our main goal is to determine conditions under which this can be achieved with a sample complexity that is
completely independent
of
n
.

Since this problem is more challenging than the one in
[
13
]
, we will (at least) require the same assumptions as
[
13
]
.
In particular, we will assume that (i)
{\cal D}
is an i.i.d. product distribution
\mathcal{D}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
; (ii) the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is known (and independent of
\boldsymbol{x}
); and (iii)
{\cal D}
is not Gaussian.
However, these conditions alone turn out to be insufficient to obtain
dimension-independent
sample complexity (i.e., independent of
n
) for the problem of testing sparsity of low-degree polynomials.
To see this, and to motivate the additional assumptions that we will introduce, we consider two illustrative examples:

Example #1:
Consider two polynomials
p_{1}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1
and
p_{2}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2})/n
.
Although
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are distinct as polynomials, we have
p_{1}(x)=p_{2}(x)
for every
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
.
Since
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
have very different sparsities, it follows that—even with infinitely many noiseless samples of the form
(\boldsymbol{x},p(\boldsymbol{x}))
where
\boldsymbol{x}
is supported on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
—one cannot distinguish whether
p
is
1
-sparse versus
n
-sparse.
To rule out such degeneracies, we will restrict our attention to
multilinear
polynomials—i.e., polynomials whose degree in each variable at most one.
The multilinearity restriction ensures that any polynomial
p
(and consequently, its sparsity) is completely determined by its values on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
.

Example #2:

Even with the additional restriction of multilinearity, achieving the kind of sharp testing guarantees (
s
-sparse versus
\varepsilon
-far from
s
-sparse) obtained in
[
13
]
for linear functions is no longer possible for degree-
d
polynomials when
d>1
.
More precisely, our results imply that for the uniform distribution over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, the task of distinguishing

•

degree-
2
multilinear polynomials which are
1
-sparse, versus

•

degree-
2
multilinear polynomials which are
c
-far from
3
-sparse (for some absolute constant
c>0
),

requires
\Omega(\log n)
samples.
(Looking ahead, this is an immediate consequence of
Theorem

7
and the lower bound on “
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),2}(1)
” given in
63
.)
Thus, even for a simple background distribution
{\cal D}
such as the uniform distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, the problem of distinguishing
s
-sparse degree-2 multilinear polynomials from
\varepsilon
-far-from-
s
-sparse degree-2 multilinear polynomials cannot be solved with dimension-independent sample complexity.

Thus, we turn to a natural “gapped” version of the sparsity testing problem, which is as follows:
Given a background distribution
{\cal D}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
and the noise distribution
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, the algorithm receives noisy labeled samples of the form
(\boldsymbol{x},p(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\eta})
and must distinguish between (i)
p
being
s
-sparse, versus (ii)
p
being
\varepsilon
-far from
T
sparse, for some value
T>s
.
Our focus will be on the setting where
\boldsymbol{X}
has finite support, and our goal is to obtain sharp bounds on the “gap value”
T
such that a dimension-independent sample complexity
is
achievable for this testing problem.

Before proceeding, we give a brief heuristic reason for why the degree-
d
case is substantially more challenging than the linear (i.e., degree-
1
) case considered by
[
13
]
.
Any degree-
d
multilinear polynomial over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be viewed as a linear form over an expanded feature space of
{n\choose\leq d}
many “meta-features” corresponding to all degree-at-most-
d
multilinear monomials. However, when
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is endowed with the i.i.d. product measure
\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
, the induced measure on this expanded feature space fails to satisfy the key conditions shown to be necessary in
[
13
]
: not only is it not i.i.d., it is not even 3-wise independent.
Consequently, there is no simple reduction to the linear case handled in previous work.

Other related work.

Several prior works in the area of property testing have studied the testability of function classes admitting sparse representations. This includes a long line of work on junta testing
[
20
,
7
,
11
,

]
, testing sparse polynomials over finite fields

[

.
However, as is standard in property testing, the access model considered in all these works is via queries, whereas in the current paper the form of access we consider is significantly weaker, namely, random samples.

Sparse low-degree polynomials have also been studied from the point of view of machine learning. In particular, in the same model as the current paper,
[
2
]
designed an efficient algorithm to recover an underlying degree-
d

s
-sparse polynomial

for two different choices of background distributions (
\boldsymbol{X}=N(0,1)
and the uniform distribution on

).
The key contribution of

was an algorithm whose running time scales as a fixed polynomial in

(as opposed to a polynomial whose exponent on

or the sparsity parameter

).
In contrast with that work, our focus is on testing rather than learning, and our goal is to achieve sample complexity
completely independent
of

.

A bit farther afield, sparse low-degree polynomials are also connected to other tasks in machine learning; for e.g.,
[
6
]
studied the problem of learning such polynomials in the context of sparse hierarchical regression.
Connections have also been made between the problem of learning sparse polynomials and the problems of graph and hypergraph sketching

1.1
Our results and techniques

Let us define the notion of
distance to
s
-sparsity
that we will use:

Definition 1
.

Given a degree-
d
multilinear polynomial

p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{S\in{[n]\choose\leq d}}\widehat{p}(S)\prod_{i\in S}x_{i},

we write
\|p\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}
for

{\left(\sum_{S}\widehat{p}(S)^{2}\right)}^{1/2},

the square root of the sum of squares of
p
’s coefficients.
Given a nonzero degree-
d
multilinear polynomial
p
, we define its
distance to
s
-sparsity
to be

~\mathrm{dist}(p,s\text{-sparse})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{q\text{~is an $s$-sparse degree-$d$ multilinear polynomial}}{\frac{\|p-q\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}}{\|p\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}}};

(1)

this can be viewed as the fraction of the coefficient-norm of
p
that comes from the coordinates that are not among the
s
largest-magnitude ones (see
Lemma

9
for a precise statement).

We remark that for the case of
d=1
(linear functions),
Definition

1
coincides with the notion of “distance to
s
-sparsity” used in
[
13
]
.

1.1.1
First result: A coarse upper bound

As described earlier in Example #2, unlike the case for linear polynomials, once the degree
d
of the unknown polynomial is greater than
1
we can no longer test whether the polynomial
p
is
s
-sparse versus
\varepsilon
-far from
s
-sparse with a number of samples that is independent of
n
. Instead, we seek to design algorithms with constant sample complexity to distinguish between the cases that (i)
p
is
s
-sparse, versus (ii)
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse for some
T>s
. As alluded to earlier, we seek to understand the exact
threshold
T
for which such a constant sample complexity tester is possible. But en route to this sharp result, both as a conceptual first step (but also a key technical ingredient in our sharp result), we will first get a
coarse upper bound
.

Before going further, let us define the precise algorithmic problem we are interested in:

Definition 2

(Sparsity testing problem with gap parameter
T
)
.

Fix a finitely supported random variable
\boldsymbol{X}
over
\mathbb{R}
, a degree bound
d
, a noise distribution
\boldsymbol{\eta}
over
\mathbb{R}
, a sparsity parameter
s\in\mathbb{N}
, a gap value
T\in\mathbb{N}
, and a real error parameter
\varepsilon>0
.

The
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem
is the following:
An algorithm is given as input access to i.i.d. samples
(\boldsymbol{x}\sim\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n},\boldsymbol{y}=p(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\eta})
, where
p
is promised to be a multilinear degree-
d
polynomial with
1/K\leq\|p\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}\leq K
. The task for the algorithm is to satisfy the following criteria:

•

If
\mathrm{sparsity}(p)\leq s
, then with probability at least
9/10
the algorithm must output “
s
-sparse”;

•

If
p
’s distance to
T
-sparsity is at least
\varepsilon
, then with probability at least
9/10
the algorithm must output “
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse.”

Note that the algorithm is allowed to depend on
\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,K
, and
\varepsilon
; in particular, we may assume that the algorithm “knows” the distributions of
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and “knows” the values of
d,s,T,K,
and
\varepsilon
.

Our first result is an algorithm for the above problem, with constant sample complexity, for
T
chosen as a certain function of
s
. While this gap between
s
and
T
may not be the best possible, an advantage of this result is that the value of
T
(as a function of
s
and
d
) is quite explicitly given.
Before stating this algorithmic result, we remark that throughout the paper we assume that the background distribution
\boldsymbol{X}
satisfies
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\boldsymbol{X}]=0
and
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[\boldsymbol{X}]=1
. We impose these normalization conditions on
\boldsymbol{X}
to avoid a proliferation of parameters, though we expect that our arguments can be extended to the general case without significant difficulty.

Theorem 3

(The first algorithm: A coarse but explicit upper bound)
.

Fix any finitely supported random variable
\boldsymbol{X}
over
\mathbb{R}

which has
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\boldsymbol{X}]=0
and
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[\boldsymbol{X}]=1
.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be the i.i.d. product distribution
\mathcal{D}\equiv\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}
be a real random variable corresponding to a noise distribution over
\mathbb{R}
which is such that all its moments are finite.
Let
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon),
where

\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)={\frac{(O_{\boldsymbol{X},K}(1))^{d^{2}}\cdot s^{d}}{\varepsilon^{2d}}}.

(2)

Then there is an algorithm for the
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem that uses a number
m
of samples that is at most

\exp{\left(\mathrm{poly}{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon},s,(O_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(1))^{d}\right)}\right)}\cdot m_{[1,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\ell}\,,

(3)

where
\ell=(O_{\boldsymbol{X},K}(1))^{d}\cdot s/\varepsilon
and
m_{[1,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
is defined in
Section

2.2
;
in particular, it is independent of
n
.
We refer to such an algorithm as an
(\varepsilon,T)
-tester for
s
-sparsity under
\mathcal{D}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
with sample complexity
m
.

Note that while
Theorem

3
is potentially non-optimal vis-a-vis the gap between
T
and
s
, the sample complexity is completely explicit.
This feature will be useful in designing our
optimal
testing algorithm, which is discussed in
Section

1.1.2
.

Techniques for the coarse upper bound (
Theorem

3
).

Before turning to
Section

1.1.2
, though, let us discuss the techniques we use to prove
Theorem

3
.
To explain the main idea, we assume for simplicity that
K=1
; in other words, we are promised that
\|p\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}=1
.
It is an easy observation that any such
s
-sparse multilinear degree-
d
polynomial
p
must take values in the interval
[-\sqrt{s}\cdot M^{d},\sqrt{s}\cdot M^{d}]
with probability
1
, where
M
is an upper bound on the values in
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{X})
, the support of
\boldsymbol{X}
.
In a central technical result of this paper,
Theorem

30
, we establish a
robust converse
to this observation.
Specifically, we show that if
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from
T
sparse where
T=\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)
, then
|p(\boldsymbol{x})|
takes values “noticeably larger” than
\sqrt{s}\cdot M^{d}
with “noticeable probability” when
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
.

This result alone would suffice for an
(\varepsilon,T)
-tester if the algorithm had access to noise-free labeled samples
(\boldsymbol{x},p(\boldsymbol{x}))
.
However, in our setting, the algorithm gets noisy samples of the form
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}=p(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\eta})
.
Because of the added noise
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, even in the case when
p
is
s
-sparse, the labels
\boldsymbol{y}
may
take values larger than
\sqrt{s}\cdot M^{d}
or any other pre-specified threshold.
Thus, it is not enough to simply “look for large values.”
Instead, we show that when
p
is far from
s
-sparse, the
higher-order moments
of
p(\boldsymbol{x})
differ noticeably from those of any
s
-sparse polynomial.
In more detail, if
p
is
s
-sparse, then the
\ell^{\text{th}}
moment of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is bounded in magnitude by
(\sqrt{s}\cdot M^{d})^{\ell}
(as
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is always bounded in
[-\sqrt{s}\cdot M^{d},\sqrt{s}\cdot M^{d}]
).
In contrast, we use
Theorem

30
to show that if
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from
T
sparse where
T=\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)
, then the
\ell^{\text{th}}
moment of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is noticeably larger than
(\sqrt{s}\cdot M^{d})^{\ell}
provided
\ell
is sufficiently large (
Corollary

31
).
The remaining challenge then is whether these moment can be reliably estimated from noisy samples of the form
(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n},\boldsymbol{y}=p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})+\boldsymbol{\eta})
.

To handle this, we work with the
cumulants
of the random variable
\boldsymbol{y}
(see
Section

2.7
for the definition of cumulants).
In particular, for any
\ell\geq 1
, since the random variables
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are independent, the
\ell^{\text{th}}
cumulant (denoted by
\kappa_{\ell}(\cdot)
) satisfies

\kappa_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})+\boldsymbol{\eta})=\kappa_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))+\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

Since the distribution
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is known to the algorithm, this means that given the
exact
cumulants of the label
\boldsymbol{y}
, we can also compute the
exact
cumulants of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
.
Finally, since the first
\ell
cumulants of a random variable uniquely determine the first
\ell
moments and vice versa, this means that given the exact moments of the label
\boldsymbol{y}
, we can also compute the exact moments of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
.

While exact computation of moments of
\boldsymbol{y}
is not feasible in our model,
since the algorithm gets access to samples of
\boldsymbol{y}
it can compute the moments of
\boldsymbol{y}
to high accuracy, and these can be used (together with knowledge of the noise distribution
\boldsymbol{\eta}
) to get high accuracy estimates of the moments of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
.
The details of this estimation procedure are somewhat technical and deferred to
Section

3
.
Summarizing this discussion, our coarse algorithm can distinguish between the cases when the polynomial
p
is
s
-sparse versus
\varepsilon
-far from
T
sparse, where
T
and the number of samples used are as given in
Theorem

3
.

As noted above, the main technical workhorse underlying this algorithm is
Theorem

30
, which establishes a lower bound on the tail of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
if
p
is far from
T
-sparse.
In the special case where
\boldsymbol{X}
is uniformly distributed over
{\pm 1}
, this theorem can be derived from Theorem 7 of
[
19
]
.
We present a generalization of their result beyond the Boolean setting to arbitrary finitely supported mean-
0
distributions, stated as
Theorem

16
.
The proof of
Theorem

16
is technically involved: although it follows the broad outline of
[
19
]
, extending the argument to arbitrary finitely supported distributions requires us to go beyond the arguments in
[
19
]
at a number of points.
In particular, our use of a noise operator with a
negative
“noise rate”
\rho
(
Definition

17
) does not have a counterpart in
[
19
]
.

1.1.2
Main result: A sharp characterization of constant-sample testability

The main drawback of
Theorem

3
is that it only distinguishes between the cases where the unknown polynomial
p
is
s
-sparse versus
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse where
T=\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)
.
As discussed in Example
\#2
previously (see also
63
and
Theorem

46
), achieving constant sample complexity may require
T
to exceed

; however, the optimal “gap” between
s
and
T
that permits constant-sample distinguishability has remained unclear.

Our main result gives an exact characterization of this gap.
For any finitely supported distribution
\boldsymbol{X}
with
\mathbf{E}[\boldsymbol{X}]=0
and
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[\boldsymbol{X}]=1
, we give a
precise characterization
of the “gap parameter”
T
for which constant sample complexity is achievable.
Formally, we define a parameter
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
(and give an algorithm to compute its value) with the following guarantee:

•

If
T\geq\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, there exists an algorithm whose sample complexity is independent of the ambient dimension
n
that distinguishes between the case (i)
p
is
s
-sparse, versus (ii)
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse, even in the presence of noise.

•

On the other hand, if
T<\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, then even in the absence of noise, distinguishing between cases (i) and (ii) requires
\Omega(\log n)
samples.

Equivalently, in the formulation of
Definition

2
, we show that the
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem can be solved with
O(1)
samples (i.e., a number of samples with no dependence on
n
, rather depending only on
\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T
and
\varepsilon
) if

T\geq\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
.
On the other hand, if
T<\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, then the
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem necessarily requires
\Omega(\log n)
samples.

Furthermore, we do not just show the
existence
of this “sharp threshold” for the gap parameter, but, as mentioned earlier, we also show that the threshold value
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
is
computable
.
We do this by providing an algorithm which, given
\boldsymbol{X}
,
d
and
s
, exactly computes the value of
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
.
From an algorithmic standpoint, this yields a complete characterization of the
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem for finitely supported
\boldsymbol{X}
.
Specifically, we first compute
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
: if
T\geq\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, then we give a constant sample complexity algorithm for the
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem.
On the other hand, if
T<\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, we can
certifiably
conclude that the
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem requires
\Omega(\log n)
samples.

Defining and computing the
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
function.

It turns out to be the case that the
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
function is tightly connected to the following question: when can there exist two polynomials
p
and
q
with
different sparsities
but
identical output distributions
when each input coordinate is drawn i.i.d. from
\boldsymbol{X}
?
This motivates the following definition:

Definition 4
.

Fix a distribution
\boldsymbol{X}
over
\mathbb{R}
that has finite support. For any degree
d\geq 1
, define the function
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
to be the largest natural number
t
such that:

1.

There exist two degree-at-most-
d
multilinear polynomials
p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, where
\mathrm{sparsity}(p)=s
,
\mathrm{sparsity}(q)=t
, such that the distributions
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
and
q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
are exactly identical; but

2.

There are no two degree-at-most-
d
polynomials
p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
with
\mathrm{sparsity}(p)=s,

\mathrm{sparsity}(q)>t
such that the distributions
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
and
q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
are exactly identical.

Since we can take
q=p
in item (1) above, we clearly have that
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)\geq s
for any
\boldsymbol{X}
and
d
.
Simple examples also show that we may have
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)>s
; see
Appendix

A
for a simple argument establishing that taking
\boldsymbol{X}=\mathrm{Unif}(\{-1,1\})
, for any
s\geq 1
we have
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)\geq 4^{d-1}s.

The following result, which is proved in
Section

6
,
gives an upper bound on
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
and hence shows that
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
is well-defined:

Theorem 5

(
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
is well-defined)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a finitely supported distribution that takes at most
\ell
distinct output values,
and let
d\geq 1
be any natural number. Then we have that

\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)\leq\Phi(d,s,\ell)\qquad\text{where}~\Phi(d,s,\ell)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2^{2d^{2}\cdot(\ell^{ds}+3)}\,.

Roughly speaking, the proof proceeds by giving both (i) an
upper
bound on the number of distinct output value that an
s
-sparse degree-
d
multilinear polynomial
p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
can assume when its input is drawn from
\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
, as well as (ii) a
lower
bound on the number of distinct output values that a degree-
d
multilinear polynomial
q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
with
\mathrm{sparsity}(q)=T

must
take under the same input distribution.
If
T
is large enough so that the lower bound in (ii) exceeds the upper bound in (i), then the distributions of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
and
q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
cannot be the same, and hence such a value of
T
is an upper bound on
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
.
The proof of (ii) builds on an upper bound, originally due to Nisan and Szegedy
[
35
]
, on the number of variables that any degree-
d

Boolean-valued
function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
can depend on.

The next theorem, which we prove in
Section

7
, builds on
Theorem

5
and shows that in fact there is an algorithm to compute
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
:

Theorem 6

(Algorithm for computing
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
)
.

There is an algorithm, which we call
Compute-Max-Sparsity-Gap
, with the following performance guarantee:
Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a mean-zero variance-one finitely supported distribution that puts probability
\alpha_{i}>0
on
v_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
for
i=1,\dots,\ell
, where each
\alpha_{i}
and
v_{i}
is a rational number whose numerator and denominator each have bit length at most
L
.
The
Compute-Max-Sparsity-Gap
algorithm is given as input a description of
\boldsymbol{X}
, a positive integer
d
, and a positive integer
s
.
It runs in time
d^{O(dL\Phi(s,d,\ell)^{2})}
and outputs the value of
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
.

We remark that by
Theorem

6
, the value of
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
is computable in time
O(1)
for any fixed base distribution
\boldsymbol{X}
, any constant degree bound
d
, and any sparsity bound
s
.
(Note that none of these parameters depend on
n
—indeed,
n
does not appear in the definition of
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
.)

Before turning to our characterization of constant-sample testability, we give a proof overview of
Theorem

6
.
To compute
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, it suffices to decide, given values
s
and
t
, whether there exist two polynomials
p
and
q
with
\mathrm{sparsity}(p)=s
,
\mathrm{sparsity}(q)=t
and identical output distributions when the input coordinates are drawn i.i.d. from
\boldsymbol{X}
.
Note that the total number of variables in either
p
or
q
is at most
k\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max\{s,t\}\cdot d
.
As the distribution
\boldsymbol{X}
is finitely supported, so are the distributions of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
and
q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
.
Hence the distributions of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
and
q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
are identical if and only if their sufficiently high moments match (see
38
).
Furthermore, any moment of

p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
(respectively, of
q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
) can be expressed as a polynomial in the coefficients of

p
(respectively, of
q
).
From these observations, with a little work we can express the problem of finding two polynomials
p
and
q
such that
\mathrm{sparsity}(p)=s
,
\mathrm{sparsity}(q)=t
with identical distributions for
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
and
q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
as a system of constraints (equalities and inequalities) defined by polynomials.
Consequently, we can employ known decision procedures for the existential theory of the reals
[
42
,
39
]
to algorithmically find such polynomials
p
and
q
if they exist, or certify that no such pair exists.

The main result: Sharp upper and lower bounds for constant-sample testability at
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
.

With the definition of
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
in place, and the assurance that it is both well-defined and computable, we can now precisely characterize when the polynomial sparsity testing problem admits constant sample complexity.
This characterization is provided by the following pair of theorems:

Theorem 7

(Lower bound on the polynomial sparsity testing problem)
.

Fix any finitely supported mean-zero variance-one distribution
\boldsymbol{X}
over
\mathbb{R}
, any degree bound
d
, any sparsity parameter
s\in\mathbb{N}
, and a gap value
T\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}
be the noise distribution which always outputs 0 (i.e. there is no noise).

If
T\leq\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)-1
, then
there exists a real error parameter
\varepsilon>0
, independent of
n
, such that
any algorithm for the
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem must use
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{X},d,s}(\log n)
samples.

Theorem 8

(Upper bound on the polynomial sparsity testing problem)
.

Fix any finitely supported mean-zero variance-one distribution
\boldsymbol{X}
over
\mathbb{R}
, any degree bound
d
, any sparsity parameter
s\in\mathbb{N}
, any noise distribution
\boldsymbol{\eta}
over
\mathbb{R}
all of whose moments are finite, a gap value
T\in\mathbb{N}
, and a real error parameter
\varepsilon>0
.

If
T\geq\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, then there is an algorithm
Test-Sparsity
for the
(\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,\varepsilon)
polynomial sparsity testing problem that uses
O(1)
samples (where the
O
notation hides “constant” factors that depend only on
\boldsymbol{X},d,\boldsymbol{\eta},s,T,K,
and
\varepsilon
, but with no dependence on
n
).

We stress that
Theorems

7
and
8
together establish a “sharp cutoff” in the sample complexity of sparsity testing.
At the critical value
T=\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, the required number of samples transitions abruptly: if
T
decreases by even one, the complexity jumps from
O(1)
to
\Omega(\log n)
for distinguishing whether
p
is
s
-sparse or
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse.
We now give a brief overview of the proofs of
Theorems

7
and
8
.

Proof overview of
Theorem

7
.

Let
p
and
q
be two degree-
d
polynomials over
\mathbb{R}^{k}
where
\mathrm{sparsity}(p)=s
and
\mathrm{sparsity}(q)=\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
. Note that
for any
T
which is strictly less than
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s)
, the polynomial
q
is
\varepsilon
-far from any
T
-sparse polynomial for some
\varepsilon>0
. Now, suppose the algorithm get samples from
(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k},r(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k}))
where

1.

In the “YES” case,
r=p
;

2.

In the “NO” case,
r=q
, i.e., the polynomial
r
is
\varepsilon
-far from any
T
-sparse polynomial.

The goal of the algorithm is to distinguish which of the two cases it is in. An easy observation is that if the algorithm is only allowed to look at the label, i.e.
r(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
, then no finite number of samples is sufficient, as the distribution of
r(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
is identical in the “YES” and the “NO” cases.

To transform this observation into a real lower bound for our problem (since in the problem we consider, the algorithm receives as input both the example points drawn from
\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k}
as well as their labels
r(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k})
), we consider the following construction. First, split
n
into
n/k
blocks of size
k
each. Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{[i]}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\boldsymbol{X}_{(i-1)k+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{ik})
denote the
i^{\text{th}}
block of variables (note that the distribution of each
\boldsymbol{X}_{[i]}
is the same as
\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes k}
).
We construct “YES” and “NO” instances as follows:

1.

In the “YES” case, we sample
1\leq\boldsymbol{i}\leq n/k
uniformly at random. We then set
r=p(\mathbf{X}_{[\boldsymbol{i}]})
, so in this case
the algorithm gets samples from
(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n},p(\boldsymbol{X}_{[\boldsymbol{i}]}))
.

2.

In the “NO” CASE, we again sample
1\leq\boldsymbol{i}\leq n/k
uniformly at random. We then set
r=q(\mathbf{X}_{[\boldsymbol{i}]})
, so in this case the algorithm gets samples from
(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n},q(\boldsymbol{X}_{[\boldsymbol{i}]}))
.

In
Theorem

7
, we show that to distinguish between the “YES” and the “NO” cases, any algorithm must use
\Omega(\log n)
samples. The rough intuition is that there are
n/k=\Theta(n)
possible choices of
i
, and until the algorithm gets
\Theta(\log n)
samples, it does not have enough information to infer the value of
i
; without knowledge of
i
, the algorithm can essentially just look at the label
r(\boldsymbol{X}_{[i]}).
As we have described earlier, merely looking at the label is insufficient to distinguish between the “YES” and the “NO” cases.
While this is the intuition behind the lower bound, the actual proof proceeds by calculating the total variation distance between a sequence of samples drawn from the “YES” case versus a sequence of samples drawn from the “NO” case, and showing that as long as we have fewer than
\Theta(\log n)
samples, the total variation distance is negligible.

Proof overview of
Theorem

8
.

The proof of
Theorem

8
proceeds in multiple phases, which we describe below.
We first recall our setting: we are given samples of
(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n},p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))
, and the goal is to distinguish whether
p
is
s
-sparse versus
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse, where
T\geq\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(s).

•

Phase Zero:
If
T\geq\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)
(where
\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)
is as in
Equation

2
), we can simply run our algorithm for the “coarse upper bound,” i.e., the algorithm from
Theorem

3
, and if
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse, then the algorithm from
Theorem

3
rejects
p
with high probability.

•

Phase One:
For the ensuing discussion, it is useful to have some notation. Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of polynomials which are
s
-sparse and
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
denote the set of random variables realizable as
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
where
p\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathcal{P}({\varepsilon})
be the set of polynomials which are both
\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)
-sparse and
\varepsilon
far from
T
-sparse, and let
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon))
denote the set of random variables realizable as
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
for some
p\in\mathcal{P}({\varepsilon})
. We use a compactness argument to show that there is some
c_{\varepsilon}>0
such that any element of
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon))
is at least
c_{\varepsilon}
-far in Wasserstein distance from
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
(
Lemma

54
).
Algorithmically, in Phase 1 we estimate the minimum Wasserstein distance between any element of
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon))
and
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
and this will be used to set a parameter
\varepsilon^{\prime}\ll\varepsilon
whose purpose we explain next.

•

Phase Two:
We now run the “coarse upper bound” algorithm from
Theorem

3
again, but this time with its closeness parameter set to the value
\varepsilon^{\prime}
mentioned above. If the algorithm rejects then we reject the polynomial
p,
and if the algorithm does not reject then we know that
p
must be
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-close to
\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)
-sparse.
Let
\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
denote the set of polynomials which are both
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-close to
\Upsilon_{K,\boldsymbol{X}}(s,d,\varepsilon)
-sparse, and let
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon))
denote the corresponding set of random variables. At this point, the remaining task is to distinguish whether
p\in\mathcal{P}
versus
p\in\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}).

•

Phase Three:
Now, note that both
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
and
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon))
are compact sets which are
c_{\varepsilon}
-far from each other in Wasserstein distance. Using a connection between moments and Wasserstein distance (
Lemma

56
), this implies that the distributions in these sets can be robustly specified using their low-degree (degree independent of
n
) moments. We now explain the role of the
\varepsilon^{\prime}
parameter: this parameter is chosen so that every element of
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}))
is close (in terms of its low-degree moments) to some element of
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon))
. Algorithmically, in Phase 3 we construct a net for
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
(here we are again using the fact that this is a compact set), and for each element (distribution) in the net, we compute its low-degree moments (we call this its “moment profile”).

•

Phase Four:
We estimate the low-degree moments of the given random variable

p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
and check if this moment profile is sufficiently close to that of any element in the net for
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
. If this is the case then we accept, and otherwise we reject.

Note that if
p
is
s
-sparse, then by construction, the distribution of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is close in Wasserstein distance to an element in the net for
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
.
This closeness also implies that the moment profile of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is close to that of an element in the same net.
Hence, with appropriately chosen parameters, any
s
-sparse polynomial
p
will pass the above test.

On the other hand, if the polynomial
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from
T
-sparse and additionally survives Phase Two, then the distribution of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
must be
c_{\varepsilon}
-far in Wasserstein distance from every element of
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
.
For sufficiently small
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, it follows that
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
also has a moment profile that is “far” from the moment profile of every element in the net for
\mathrm{RV}(\mathcal{P})
(see
Lemma

58
).
Such a polynomial will therefore be rejected, completing the proof overview of
Theorem

8
.

\boldsymbol{X}

\mathbf{Inf}_{i}[p]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[{\left\|\frac{\partial p(\boldsymbol{X})}{\partial x_{i}}\right\|}^{2}\right]}=\sum_{S\ni i}\widehat{p}(S)^{2}\,.

and we define

2.5
Wasserstein distance and moment distance

Some of our arguments will use the notion of Wasserstein distance. Given two real random variables
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
, we recall that the
Wasserstein distance between
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
is

W_{1}(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Z})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime})}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[|\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime}|\right]},

(7)

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime})
of
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
(so the marginal distribution
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
of
(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime})
is distributed according to
\boldsymbol{Y}
, and likewise for
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
).

Another notion of the distance between two real random variables
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
(with finite moments) that we will need is the “

-th order moment distance,” which we denote
\mathrm{Mom}_{k}(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Z}).

This is defined as

\mathrm{Mom}_{k}(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Z})=\|m(\boldsymbol{Y})-m(\boldsymbol{Z})\|_{2},

(8)

where for a real random variable
\boldsymbol{Z}
, we write
m(\boldsymbol{Z})
to denote the
k
-dimensional vector whose
i
-th coordinate is
m_{i}(\boldsymbol{Z})
.

2.7
Cumulants

In this section we recall some necessary background on cumulants and how they are related to moments.

Definition 11
.

The
cumulants
of a real random variable
\boldsymbol{Y}
are defined by the cumulant generating function
K(t)
, which is the natural logarithm of the moment generating function
M(t)=\operatorname{{\bf E}}[e^{t\boldsymbol{Y}}]
:

K(t)=\ln\operatorname{{\bf E}}[e^{t\boldsymbol{Y}}],\quad\quad\text{or equivalently,}\quad\quad e^{K(t)}=\operatorname{{\bf E}}[e^{t\boldsymbol{Y}}].

For
\ell>0
the cumulants of
\boldsymbol{Y}
, which are denoted
\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})
, are the coefficients in the Taylor expansion of the cumulant generating function about the origin:

K(t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y}){\frac{t^{\ell}}{\ell!}},\quad\quad\text{or equivalently,}\quad\quad\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})=K^{(\ell)}(0).

An easy consequence of the definition of cumulants is that they are additive for independent random variables:

Fact 12
.

If
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
are independent random variables then
\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y}+\boldsymbol{Z})=\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})+\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Z})
.

Recall that in our framework, the input data consists of labeled examples
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}=p(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\eta})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is independently drawn from a noise distribution.
Our algorithms work by estimating cumulants of the real random variable
\boldsymbol{y}
; by
12
, we can subtract out the effect of the noise random variable
\boldsymbol{\eta}
on
\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})
and we are left with (an estimate of)
\kappa_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{x})).

It is well known that for any
n\geq 1
, there is a one-to-one mapping between the first
n
moments and the first
n
cumulants, which can be derived by relating coefficients in the Taylor series expansions of the cumulant and moment generating functions (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
):

Fact 13
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Y}
be a random variable with mean zero.
Then

\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})=m_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}{\ell-1\choose j-1}\kappa_{j}(\boldsymbol{Y})\cdot m_{\ell-j}(\boldsymbol{Y}).

(10)

Cumulants can be expressed in terms of moments and vice-versa:

m_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}B_{\ell,k}\bigg(\kappa_{1}(\boldsymbol{Y}),\ldots,\kappa_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{Y})\bigg)

(11)

and

\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!B_{\ell,k}\bigg(m_{1}(\boldsymbol{Y}),\ldots,m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{Y})\bigg),

(12)

where
B_{\ell,k}
are incomplete
Bell polynomials
,

B_{\ell,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell-k+1})=\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{x_{\ell-k+1}}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}},

where the summation is over all non-negative sequences
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-k+1})
that satisfy

j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell-k+1}=k\text{ and }j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+(\ell-k+1)j_{\ell-k+1}=\ell.

Equation

10
can be used to give a upper bound on
\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})
in terms of the absolute moments of
\boldsymbol{Y}
:

Claim 14
.

For any random variable
\boldsymbol{Y}
with mean zero and any positive integer
\ell
, we have
|\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})|\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[|\boldsymbol{Y}|^{\ell}]\cdot e^{\ell}\cdot\ell!
.

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix A.1 in
[
13
]
.
∎

7.2

We will use the following simple result on equivalence of finitely many moments implying equivalence of finitely supported random variables. While we believe that this is a well-known folklore result (see e.g.

Claim 38

Let
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}

are identical (and if
m_{j}(\boldsymbol{X})\neq m_{j}(\boldsymbol{Y})

\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
are not identical).

Proof.

The parenthesized second claim is obvious so it suffices to prove the first claim, that moment-matching implies identity of distributions.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}
be the set containing all the support points of either
\boldsymbol{X}
or
\boldsymbol{Y}
, so
|Z|\leq 2k
.
The
j
-th raw moment of
\boldsymbol{X}
is
m_{j}(\boldsymbol{X})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{z\in Z}p_{\boldsymbol{X}}(z)z^{j}

is the probability mass that
\boldsymbol{X}
puts on
a
, and likewise
the
j
-th raw moment of
\boldsymbol{Y}
is

.
By the matching-moments condition on
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
, we have that

\text{for~}j=0,\dots,2k-1,\quad\quad m_{j}(\boldsymbol{X})=m_{j}(\boldsymbol{Y}).

(43)

But for any set

for all

and

9.4
Connecting Wasserstein distance and moment distance

We will use a result of

(

for an absolute constant

.∎

We mainly use an easy corollary of Lemma

.

Let
\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Z}
be two real random variables whose support is contained in

Taking the constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
as in

56

Proof.

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{Z}/L

and

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Mom}_{k}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(m_{i}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})-m_{i}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime}))^{2}}

The main idea is that we will get a high accuracy estimate of

\widetilde{p}_{\mathrm{coeff}}

We start by observing that

Thus, using
Lemma

15

to accuracy

\boldsymbol{X}

K

d
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}

{\left|\widetilde{m}_{2}(p)-\|p\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}^{2}\right|}\leq\tau_{0}.

{\left|\widetilde{p}_{\mathrm{coeff}}-\|p\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}\right|}\leq\frac{\tau_{0}}{\|p\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}}.

We give upper and lower bounds on

Observation 63
.

For
\boldsymbol{X}=\mathrm{Unif}(\{-1,1\})
and any
d\geq 1
, we have
4^{d-1}\leq\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\boldsymbol{X},d}(1)\leq 2^{O(d^{2})}
.

Proof.

Up to scaling by a non-zero constant and renaming of variables, a multilinear polynomial
p
of sparsity
s=1
and degree

must be
p_{r}(x)=x_{1}\cdots x_{r}
. Without loss of generality, assume that the coefficient of the single term in the 1-sparse polynomial is 1; then
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is distributed as
\mathrm{Unif}(\{-1,1\}).

To prove the lower bound, it suffices to give an example of a degree-
d
polynomial
p
such that
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is distributed identically to
\boldsymbol{X}
(i.e. uniform over
\{\pm 1\}
).
Such a polynomial is given, for example, by a complete decision tree of depth
d
, with
2^{d}
leaf bits,
2^{d}-1
internal nodes, and
d
variables on each path, in which every variable occurs at only one node and leaf bits alternate between 1 and
-1

p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is balanced, and an easy induction shows that the sparsity of
p
is exactly

For the upper bound, let
p
be any degree-
d
polynomial such that
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
is distributed identically to
\boldsymbol{X}
. Such a polynomial
p
computes a
\{\pm 1\}
-valued function over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. As reported in
[
14
]
, Wellens has shown that any degree-
d
polynomial computing a
\{\pm 1\}
-valued function over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
must depend on at most
4.416\cdot 2^{d}

{4.416\cdot 2^{d}\choose\leq d}=2^{O(d^{2})}
-sparse polynomial.
∎

and having
q(x)
be the
(s(2^{d}-1))

s
variable-disjoint copies of the decision tree witnessing the

lower bound of
63
.

q(x)=\widehat{q}(\emptyset)+\sum_{i}\widehat{q}(i)x_{i}+\sum_{i<j}\widehat{q}(i,j)x_{i}x_{j};

since
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})]=\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\mathrm{Unif}(\{-1,1\}^{n})]=0,
we have
\widehat{q}(\emptyset)=0.

We further note that
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})^{2}]^{1/2}=\|q\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}=1
, so by Parseval’s identity we have that
\sum_{i}\widehat{q}(i)^{2}+\sum_{i<j}\widehat{q}(i,j)^{2}=1.

If
\widehat{q}(i,j)=0
for all
i<j
then it is easy to see that
q=p_{1}
(up to renaming of variables), so let
i<j
be such that the coefficient
\widehat{q}(i,j)
is non-zero. Notice that for any
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
we have that
|q(x)-q(x^{\oplus i})-q(x^{\oplus j})+q(x^{\oplus\{i,j\}})|=4|\widehat{q}(i,j)|\in\{0,2,4\}
, as
q(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
takes values in
\{-1,1\}
. If
\widehat{q}(i,j)=1
then
q=p_{2}
and we are done, so we may suppose that all the quadratic coefficients
\widehat{q}(i,j)
have magnitude
1/2.

We further observe that for any
i
, we have
|q(x)-q(x^{\oplus i})|=2|\widehat{q}(i)\pm\sum_{\ell<i}\widehat{q}(\ell,i)\pm\sum_{r>i}\widehat{q}(i,r)|\in\{0,2\}
.
Since from above we know that all nonzero
A_{\ell,i}
and
A_{i,r}
values must have magnitude 1/2, this implies that
|\widehat{q}(i)|\in\{0,\frac{1}{2}\}
.

Thus, we have argued that all the non-zero coefficients of
q
must have magnitude
\frac{1}{2}
. Together with the fact that
\|q\|_{\mathrm{coeff}}=1
,
q
needs to have exactly 4 non-zero coefficients. Thus, we have that
\mathrm{Max\text{-}Sparsity\text{-}Gap}_{\mathrm{Unif}(\{-1,1\}),2}(1)=4
and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 67

(Estimating noiseless moments of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})
given noiseless data)

For any
\varepsilon,\delta>0

Claim 68

(Bounds on noisy moments of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})+\boldsymbol{\eta}
)
.

For any positive integer
\ell
,
we have that

|m_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})+\boldsymbol{\eta})|\leq 2^{\ell}\cdot\left(\ell^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d(1-\ell)/2}\cdot K^{\ell}+m_{[\ell,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})+1\right).

Proof.

Expanding the
\ell^{th}
moment, we have

\displaystyle|m_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})+\boldsymbol{\eta})|=

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\left(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right)^{\ell}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}{\ell\choose i}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\left(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})\right)^{i}\right]\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\ell-i}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}{\ell\choose i}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\left|p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})\right|^{i}\right]\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[|\boldsymbol{\eta}|^{\ell-i}\right].

In the case when
\ell
is even, by Hölder’s inequality, the above quantity is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}{\ell\choose i}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\left|p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})\right|^{i}\right]\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[|\boldsymbol{\eta}|^{\ell-i}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}{\ell\choose i}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\left(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})\right)^{\ell}\right]^{i/\ell}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\ell}\right]^{1-i/\ell}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}{\ell\choose i}\cdot\max\{m_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})),m_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\ell}\cdot\left(m_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))+m_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\ell}\cdot\left((\ell-1)^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d-d\ell/2}\cdot K^{\ell}+m_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\ell}\cdot\left(\ell^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d-d\ell/2}\cdot K^{\ell}+m_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)

where the second to the last inequality follows from
66
.

In the case when
\ell
is odd, again by Hölder’s inequality, we obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}{\ell\choose i}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\left|p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})\right|^{i}\right]\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[|\boldsymbol{\eta}|^{\ell-i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}{\ell\choose i}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\left(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})\right)^{\ell+1}\right]^{i/(\ell+1)}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}\left[\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\ell+1}\right]^{1-(i+1)/(\ell+1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}{\ell\choose i}\cdot\max\{m_{\ell+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})),m_{\ell+1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\}^{\ell/(\ell+1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\ell}\cdot\left(m_{\ell+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{\ell/(\ell+1)}+m_{\ell+1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\ell/(\ell+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\ell}\cdot\left((\ell^{d\ell/2+d/2}\cdot\lambda^{d/2-d\ell/2}\cdot K^{\ell+1})^{\ell/(\ell+1)}+m_{\ell+1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\ell/(\ell+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\ell}\cdot\left(\ell^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d(\ell-\ell^{2})/(2\ell+2)}\cdot K^{\ell}+m_{\ell+1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\ell/(\ell+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\ell}\cdot\left(\ell^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d(1-\ell)/2}\cdot K^{\ell}+m_{\ell+1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})+1\right).

Combining the two cases, we must have

|m_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})+\boldsymbol{\eta})|\leq 2^{\ell}\cdot\left(\ell^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d(1-\ell)/2}\cdot K^{\ell}+m_{[\ell,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})+1\right).\qed

Lemma 70

(Estimating noisy cumulants of
p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})+\boldsymbol{\eta}
given noisy data)

There is an algorithm with the following property:
For any
\delta>0
,
1>\varepsilon>0

\ell

m=\mathrm{poly}(\ell^{d\ell^{2}},K^{\ell^{2}},1/(\delta\varepsilon),1/\lambda^{d\ell^{2}},m_{[1,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\ell})
many independent random samples of
\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}=p(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})+\boldsymbol{\eta}
where the
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}
’s are i.i.d. according to
\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}
and each
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is independent of everything else. The algorithm outputs an estimate
\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})
of
\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})
that with probability at least
1-\delta
satisfies

{\left|\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})-\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})\right|}\leq\varepsilon.

(55)

Proof.

First we apply
Lemma

69
to obtain estimates
\tilde{m}_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)})^{k}/m
for each
1\leq k\leq\ell
such that with probability at least
1-\delta/\ell
, we have

|\tilde{m}_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})-m_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime},

for some
1>\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
we will define later. Our estimates of
\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})
is as follows:

\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!B_{\ell,k}\left(\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}),\tilde{m}_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}),...,\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)

where

are incomplete Bell polynomials

and the sum is taken over all non-negative tuples
(j_{1},...,j_{{\ell-k+1}})
satisfying

j_{1}+\cdots+j_{{\ell-k+1}}=k\text{ and }j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+(\ell-k+1)j_{{\ell-k+1}}=\ell.

Fix such a tuple
(j_{1},...,j_{{\ell-k+1}})
; we give a bound for
\left(\frac{\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}
. Notice that since
|\tilde{m}_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})-m_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
for all

there exists a set of sign assignments
\sigma_{k}=\pm 1
for all
k\in[\ell]
such that

\left(\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\leq\left(m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})+\sigma_{1}\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})+\sigma_{\ell-k+1}\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}.

Let
M_{L}=\max_{\alpha\in[\ell]}\{|m_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{y})|,1\}
. We expand and further bound the above expression by

\displaystyle\left(m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}+\sum_{i=0}^{j_{1}-1}{j_{1}\choose i}m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{i}(\sigma_{1}\varepsilon^{\prime})^{j_{1}-i}\right)

\displaystyle\cdots\left(m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}+\sum_{i=0}^{j_{\ell-k+1}-1}{j_{\ell-k+1}\choose i}m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{i}(\sigma_{\ell-k+1}\varepsilon^{\prime})^{j_{\ell-k+1}-i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\{0,1\}^{\ell-k+1}\\
b\neq\vec{1}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\ell-k+1}\left(m_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\alpha}}\right)^{b_{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{j_{\alpha}-1}{j_{\alpha}\choose i}m_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{y})^{i}(\sigma_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\prime})^{j_{\alpha}-i}\right)^{1-b_{\alpha}}

(expanding the product)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\{0,1\}^{\ell-k+1}\\
b\neq\vec{1}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\ell-k+1}\left|m_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{y})\right|^{j_{\alpha}\cdot b_{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{j_{\alpha}-1}{j_{\alpha}\choose i}|m_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{y})|^{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{j_{\alpha}-i}\right)^{1-b_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\{0,1\}^{\ell-k+1}\\
b\neq\vec{1}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{j_{\alpha}\cdot b_{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{j_{\alpha}-1}{j_{\alpha}\choose i}(M_{L})^{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{j_{\alpha}-i}\right)^{1-b_{\alpha}}

(follows from the definition of
M_{L}
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\{0,1\}^{\ell-k+1}\\
b\neq\vec{1}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{j_{\alpha}\cdot b_{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{j_{\alpha}-1}{j_{\alpha}\choose i}(M_{L})^{i}\right)^{1-b_{\alpha}}

(since each term in the summation contains a positive power of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}<1
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\{0,1\}^{\ell-k+1}\\
b\neq\vec{1}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{j_{\alpha}\cdot b_{\alpha}}\left(1+M_{L}\right)^{j_{\alpha}(1-b_{\alpha})}

(binomial expansion)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\{0,1\}^{\ell-k+1}\\
b\neq\vec{1}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{j_{\alpha}}(2M_{L})^{j_{\alpha}}

(since
b_{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}
and
M_{L}\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell-k+1}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{2j_{\alpha}}2^{j_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell+1}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{2k}.

By a similar argument, we derive a lower bound for the estimate

\left(\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\geq m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell+1}}-\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{2k}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})-\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})|=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\left(B_{\ell,k}(\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}),...,\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})-B_{\ell,k}({m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}),...,{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y}))\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left(\left(\frac{\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}-\left(\frac{{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\right)\right|

(by definition of Bell polynomials)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(k-1)!\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}

\displaystyle\left|\left(\frac{\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}-\left(\frac{{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\right|

(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(k-1)!\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}

\displaystyle\left|\left(\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}-\left({m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left({m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(k-1)!\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{2k}

(by the previous argument)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(k-1)!\cdot 2^{\ell}\cdot\ell!\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell-k+1}(M_{L})^{2k}

(bound the number of possible sequences
j_{1},...,j_{\ell-k+1}
by
2^{\ell}
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell!\cdot 2^{\ell}\cdot\ell!\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell}(M_{L})^{2\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot(\ell!)^{2}\cdot 2^{2\ell}\cdot(M_{L})^{2\ell}.

By
68
, we have that

M_{L}=\max_{\alpha\in[\ell]}\{|m_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{y})|,1\}\leq 2^{\ell}\cdot\left(\ell^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d(1-\ell)/2}\cdot K^{\ell}+m_{[1,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})+1\right).

Therefore,
|\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})-\kappa_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{y})|
is bounded above by
\varepsilon
when taking

\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon}{(\ell!)^{2}\cdot 2^{2\ell^{2}+2\ell}\cdot\left(2^{\ell}\cdot\left(\ell^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d(1-\ell)/2}\cdot K^{\ell}+m_{[1,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})+1\right)\right)^{2\ell}}.

To achieve the
\varepsilon^{\prime}
error,

m=\mathrm{poly}(\ell^{d\ell^{2}},K^{\ell^{2}},1/(\delta\varepsilon),1/\lambda^{d\ell^{2}},m_{[1,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\ell})
samples suffices.
∎

of
\kappa_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})
for every

satisfy

|\tilde{\kappa}_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})-\kappa_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}

Since for independent random variables

Equation

11
expresses
m_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))
in terms of
\kappa_{i}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))
for
i\in[\ell]
.
Naturally, let our estimate of

\tilde{m}_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}B_{\ell,k}\bigg(\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})),\ldots,\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))\bigg).

Then we have the estimation error bound

\displaystyle|\tilde{m}_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))-m_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}B_{\ell,k}\left(\bigg(\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})),\ldots,\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))\bigg)-\bigg({\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})),\ldots,{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))\bigg)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}\left(\frac{1}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{1}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{\ell-k+1}}-{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{1}}\cdots{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}\left(\frac{1}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{1}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{\ell-k+1}}-{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{1}}\cdots{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\right|

where the second summation is taken over all tuples
(j_{1},...,j_{\ell-k+1})
of non-negative satisfying

j_{1}+\cdots+j_{{\ell-k+1}}=k\text{ and }j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+(\ell-k+1)j_{{\ell-k+1}}=\ell.

Notice that since for all
i\in[\ell],
the estimate
\tilde{\kappa}_{i}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))
is within an additive
\varepsilon^{\prime}
to
{\kappa}_{i}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))
, by an argument analogous to that when bounding
\tilde{m}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots\tilde{m}_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell-k+1}}
in terms of
m_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{1}}\cdots m_{\ell-k+1}(\boldsymbol{y})^{j_{\ell+1}}
in
Lemma

70
, we obtain the bounds

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{\ell-k+1}}

\displaystyle\in\left[{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{1}}\cdots{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\pm\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell-k+1}\mathcal{K}^{2k}\right]

where
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{\alpha\in[\ell]}\{|\kappa_{\alpha}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))|,1\}
. By
14
,
|\kappa_{\alpha}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))|\leq m_{\ell}(|p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n})|)\cdot e^{\ell}\cdot\ell!.
Together with
66
,

\mathcal{K}\leq\ell^{d\ell/2}\cdot\lambda^{d-d\ell/2}\cdot K^{\ell}\cdot e^{\ell}\cdot\ell!.

Combining the above, we obtain

\displaystyle|\tilde{m}_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))-m_{\ell}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}\left(\frac{1}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{1}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{\ell-k+1}}-{\kappa}_{1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{1}}\cdots{\kappa}_{\ell-k+1}(p(\boldsymbol{X}^{\otimes n}))^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum\frac{\ell!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{\ell-k+1}!}\left(\frac{1}{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{1}{(\ell-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{\ell-k+1}}\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell-k+1}\mathcal{K}^{2k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell\cdot 2^{\ell}\cdot\ell!\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell}\cdot\mathcal{K}^{2\ell}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot\ell^{d\ell^{2}}\cdot\lambda^{-d\ell^{2}}\cdot K^{2\ell^{2}}\cdot e^{2\ell^{2}}\cdot\ell^{3\ell^{2}}.

Letting
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\tau}{\ell^{d\ell^{2}}\cdot\lambda^{-d\ell^{2}}\cdot K^{2\ell^{2}}\cdot e^{2\ell^{2}}\cdot\ell^{3\ell^{2}}}
satisfies the error guarantee. Hence taking

m=\mathrm{poly}(\ell^{d\ell^{2}},K^{\ell^{2}},1/(\delta\tau),1/\lambda^{d\ell^{2}},m_{[1,\ell+1]}(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\ell})

suffices to produce the estimation guarantee.
