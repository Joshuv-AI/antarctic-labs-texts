PRIMEdit: Probability Redistribution for Instance-aware Multi-object Video Editing with a New Benchmark Dataset
Samuel Teodoro, Agus Gunawan, Soo Ye Kim, Jihyong Oh, Munchurl Kim, School of Electrical Engineering, Korea Advanced Institute of Science, Technology

Abstract
Recent Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based video editing has enabled users to edit videos through simple text prompts, significantly simplifying the editing process. However, recent zero-shot video editing techniques focus on global or single-object edits, which can lead to unintended changes in other parts of the video. When editing multiple objects only within their localized regions, existing methods face challenges, such as unfaithful editing, editing leakage, and lack of suitable evaluation datasets and metrics. To overcome these limitations, we propose P robability R edistribution for I nstance-aware M ulti-object Video Edit ing ( PRIMEdit ). PRIMEdit is a zero-shot framework that introduces two key modules: (i) Instance-centric Probability Redistribution (IPR) to ensure precise localization and faithful editing and (ii) Disentangled Multi-instance Sampling (DMS) to prevent editing leakage. Additionally, we present the MIVE dataset for multi-instance video editing, including its subsets categorized by instance counts and sizes for controlled analysis across different levels of scene complexity. We also introduce the Cross-Instance Accuracy (CIA) Score that is firstly proposed to evaluate the editing leakage in multi-instance video editing tasks, which exhibits strong correlation with human evaluation as measured by Spearman’s ρ = 0.86 \rho=0.86 . Our extensive qualitative, quantitative, and user study evaluations demonstrate that PRIMEdit significantly outperforms recent state-of-the-art methods in terms of editing faithfulness, accuracy, and leakage prevention, setting a new benchmark for multi-instance video editing.

for the
j\text{-th}
text embedding
e_{j}
.
IPR is motivated by two observations:
(i) Modifying the padding token attention
A_{i,j\in\boldsymbol{P}}
causes artifacts, so we keep them unchanged;
(ii) Increasing the probability of the start-of-sequence token
S
reduces editing faithfulness, while decreasing
S
and reallocating it to
\boldsymbol{T}
and
E
improves faithfulness.
Hence, for
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{E}},j}
, we set
\boldsymbol{T}
and
E
to zero and reallocate their values to
S
to prevent edits outside the mask (blue dashed box in Fig.
4
).
In addition, for
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j}
, we reduce
S
and redistribute it to
\boldsymbol{T}
and
E
(red dashed box in Fig.
4
), enhancing faithfulness.

More specifically, we reduce
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j=S}
by
\lambda_{S}
which linearly decays to 0 from
t=T
to
t=1
, as follows:

\begin{gathered}\resizebox{20122815}{}{$\lambda_{S}=(t\mathbin{/}T)\cdot(\text{min}(\text{mean}(A_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j=S}),\text{min}(A_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j=S}))+W)$}\end{gathered}

(4)

where
W
, which improves editing fidelity for small objects, is a warm-up value that is linearly decayed from
\lambda
to 0 in the early sampling steps
t<0.1T
.
Increasing
\lambda
enhances editing faithfulness but may introduce artifacts.
Empirically, we find
\lambda=0.5
provides the best trade-off between faithfulness and artifacts.
We then redistribute
\lambda_{S}
to
\boldsymbol{T}
and
E
with ratios
\lambda_{r}
and
1-\lambda_{r}
, respectively.
The attention probabilities,
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j\in\boldsymbol{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j=E}
, of
T
and
E
, are updated as follows:

\begin{gathered}\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j\in\boldsymbol{T}}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j\in\boldsymbol{T}}+\lambda_{S}\cdot\lambda_{r}\mathbin{/}N_{\boldsymbol{T}}\\
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j=E}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\text{I}},j=E}+\lambda_{S}\cdot(1-\lambda_{r})\end{gathered}

(5)

where
N_{\boldsymbol{T}}
denotes the number of text tokens.
Increasing
\lambda_{r}
enhances editing details for specific tokens, while decreasing it improves overall fidelity.
We empirically set
\lambda_{r}=0.5
for all experiments.
We compare the attention map visualizations for the vanilla cross-attention
[
5
]
, DenseDiffusion
[
27
]
and our IPR in Sec.
V-F
(Fig.
14
).

III-D

Disentangled Multi-instance Sampling (DMS)

To reduce attention leakage, we propose Disentangled Multi-instance Sampling (DMS), which edits each instance independently and fuses the resulting denoised latents in the final sampling steps, reducing the undesirable cross-instance interference.
As shown in Fig.
3
, DMS consists of two strategies: (i) Series Noise Sampling (SNS, yellow box) and (ii) Parallel Noise Sampling (PNS, blue box), preceded by latent fusion (green box) and re-inversion (pink box).
In SNS, we independently edit each instance
i
using its caption
c_{i}
and mask
\boldsymbol{m_{i}}
by first estimating the instance noise
\hat{n}_{i}^{1:N}
for
\boldsymbol{f}=f^{1:N}
.

\hat{n}_{i}^{1:N}
is obtained by:

\hat{n}_{i}^{1:N}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\{z_{t}^{1:N}\}_{i},c_{i},\boldsymbol{m_{i}},e,t),

(6)

where
\{z_{t}^{1:N}\}_{i}
is the noisy latent of the
i
-th instance at timestep
t
, and
e
represents the depth condition obtained via MiDas
[
70
]
.
Then, we compute the background noise
\tilde{n}^{1:N}
using the inverted latents as:

\tilde{n}^{1:N}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\tilde{z}_{t}^{1:N},c_{\varnothing},t),

(7)

where
c_{\varnothing}
is an empty caption to reconstruct the background that is not intended to be edited.
We then fuse the estimated instance noise
\hat{n}_{i}^{1:N}
with the inverted latent noise
\tilde{n}^{1:N}
to obtain edited instance noise
\{\bar{n}_{t-1}^{1:N}\}_{i}
using dilated masks
\delta(\boldsymbol{m_{i}})
.

\{\bar{n}_{t-1}^{1:N}\}_{i}
is computed as:

\{\bar{n}_{t-1}^{1:N}\}_{i}=\hat{n}_{i}^{1:N}\odot\delta(\boldsymbol{m_{i}})+\tilde{n}^{1:N}\odot(1-\delta(\boldsymbol{m_{i}})),

(8)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication, and
\delta(\boldsymbol{m_{i}})
is obtained by applying a
5\times 5
dilation kernel
\delta
to
\boldsymbol{m_{i}}
, enlarging the instance regions and producing more coherent boundaries.
We apply
\delta
because when
\boldsymbol{m_{i}}
is smaller than the target edit for the
i
-th instance, some pixels from the boundaries of the original instance remain unedited, as they are treated part of the background.
We then perform one DDIM
[
31
]
denoising step from
t\rightarrow t-1
to obtain instance latents
\{\hat{z}_{t-1}^{1:N}\}_{i}
as:

\{\hat{z}_{t-1}^{1:N}\}_{i}=\text{DDIM}(\{\hat{z}_{t}^{1:N}\}_{i},\{\bar{n}_{t-1}^{1:N}\}_{i},t).

(9)

SNS requires the edited instance
i
to appear within
\boldsymbol{m_{i}}
, as the background is replaced with the inverted latent noise at each step to ensure it remains unchanged.
Our proposed IPR (Sec.
III-C
) provides this necessary spatial control.

While we can perform multi-instance VE by solely using SNS until the end of denoising, the edited frames suffer from temporal inconsistency (see Table
VI
).
Thus, we propose a two-step approach to mitigate this as follows:

(i) Step 1 - Latent Fusion
: As shown in the green box of Fig.
3
, we fuse the multiple instance latents
\{\dot{z}_{t}^{1:N}\}
sampled independently from SNS via:

\dot{z}_{t}^{1:N}=\Sigma_{i=1}^{M}(\{\hat{z}_{t}^{1:N}\}_{i}\odot\delta(\boldsymbol{m_{i}})+\tilde{z}_{t}^{1:N}\odot\boldsymbol{m_{B}},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{m_{B}}=\boldsymbol{1}-\Sigma_{i=1}^{M}\delta(\boldsymbol{m_{i}})
denotes background masks for
\boldsymbol{f}=f^{1:N}
;

(ii) Step 2 - Re-inversion
: We propose re-inversion as shown in the pink box of Fig.
3
by performing DDIM inversion for
l
steps after Step 1 to obtain
\ddot{z}_{t+l}^{1:N}
followed by PNS (discussed below) from timestep
t+l\rightarrow t
to yield the final fused latent
\ddot{z}_{t}^{1:N}
as follows:

\ddot{z}_{t}^{1:N}=\text{DDIM}(\ddot{z}_{t+l}^{1:N},t).

(11)

After mitigating the temporal inconsistency, we perform PNS for the remaining denoising steps.
PNS harmonizes the edited instance latents from SNS while maintaining decoupled sampling with their respective target captions.
In this strategy, we use the re-inverted fused latents
\ddot{z}_{t}^{1:N}
to estimate each instance noise
\hat{n}_{i}^{1:N}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\ddot{z}_{t}^{1:N},c_{i},\boldsymbol{m_{i}},e,t)
using instance caption
c_{i}
.
We estimate the background noise as
\tilde{n}^{1:N}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\tilde{z}_{t}^{1:N},c_{\varnothing},t)
using the inverted latents
\tilde{z}_{t}^{1:N}
and empty caption
c_{\varnothing}
.
We combine
\hat{n}_{i}^{1:N}
and
\tilde{n}^{1:N}
to a single noise via masking, and perform one DDIM step to obtain
\hat{z}_{t-1}^{1:N}
as:

\hat{z}_{t-1}^{1:N}=\text{DDIM}(\hat{z}_{t}^{1:N},\Sigma_{i=1}^{M}(\hat{n}_{i}^{1:N}\odot\delta(\boldsymbol{m_{i}}))+\tilde{n}^{1:N}\odot\boldsymbol{m_{B}},t).

(12)

Lastly, we decode
\hat{z}_{t-1}^{1:N}
using VAE decoder
\mathcal{D}
to obtain the final edited frames
\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}=\mathcal{D}(\hat{z}_{0}^{1:N})
as shown in Fig.
3
.

MIVE Dataset Construction

Existing datasets
[
73
,
55
,
24
,
20
,
74
]
are unsuitable for multi-instance video editing (VE) tasks.
TGVE
[
73
]
and TGVE+
[
55
]
contain few videos, limited objects with few instances per object class, and no instance masks.
VideoGrain’s
[
24
]
dataset similarly includes few objects and limited instances per class.
GAV’s
[
20
]
dataset is only partially available, and DAVIS
[
74
]
does not distinguish multiple instances within the same class, making it unsuitable for multi-instance VE.

To address this gap, we introduce MIVE, the
first
evaluation dataset for multi-instance VE tasks.
MIVE is built from VIPSeg
[
75
]
, a video panoptic segmentation dataset, with each video center-cropped to
512\times 512
.
We retain clips that contain at least 12 frames and where all instances remain visible across frames.
To ensure diversity, we remove videos containing only one of the 40 most frequently occurring object classes (e.g., only persons).
This results in a final set of 200 videos.

As VIPSeg lacks source captions, we generate them using a visual language model, LLaVA
[
71
]
, and an LLM, Llama 3
[
72
]
.
We show our four-step caption generation pipeline in Fig.
5
:
(1) Image Captioning,
(2) Text Summarization,
(3) Manual Fix and Annotation, and
(4) Target Caption Generation.
As shown in Fig.
5
, we first prompt LLaVA to describe each frame while including known objects, then select the caption covering the most instances as the representative video caption.
Next, we prompt Llama 3
[
72
]
to summarize the caption with fewer words and provide the output in JSON format (omitted in Fig.
5
for clarity).
As some objects may be missed, we manually refine the captions and insert start/end tags for each instance to serve as reference for their segmentation masks.

In addition to the source captions, we generate a target caption for each instance using Llama 3 to prompt edits such as retexturing or swapping, similar to
[
11
]
.
We instruct Llama 3 to generate five candidate target captions for each instance, and randomly select one for the final target instance caption.
We then modify the
global source caption
by replacing its
instance captions
with the selected targets to form the global target caption.
Although we focus on “
thing
” instances, we also generate captions for the “
stuff
” and background elements to enable future extensions with LLM-based target edits.
Sample frames and captions are provided in the
Appendix
.

We further categorize MIVE dataset by (i) the numbers of instances to edit and (ii) the sizes of instances.
For the number of instances per video, we define four difficulty levels:

(
\text{MIVE}_{E}

Medium
(
\text{MIVE}_{M}

Hard
(
\text{MIVE}_{H}

eXtreme
(
\text{MIVE}_{X}

For instance size, we follow the COCO Dataset convention
[
76
]
: small (
<32^{2}
pixels), medium (
32^{2}
–
96^{2}
), and large (
>96^{2}
).
MIVE dataset contains 69 small, 297 medium, and 434 large instances, with each instance area averaged across frames.
Under both criteria, PRIMEdit consistently outperforms baselines on key multi-instance VE metrics (see Tables
VII
and
VIII
).

compares MIVE with existing video editing datasets (top) and summarizes MIVE by the number of edited instances per video (bottom).
Compared to prior datasets, MIVE provides the largest number of evaluation videos and greater diversity in objects per video and instances per object class.
MIVE also covers a wide range of instance sizes, from small objects of 25 pixels (

IV-B

Cross-Instance Accuracy (CIA) Score

Fig. 6:

An illustration of Cross-Instance Accuracy (CIA) Score computation. We calculate the Local Textual Faithfulness (LTF) between each cropped instance and all the instance captions. For each row, we assign 1 to the maximum LTF (shown in
red
) and 0 to the rest. The CIA Score is calculated as the mean of the diagonal elements (shown in
green
).

We propose a new metric, the Cross-Instance Accuracy (CIA) Score, to address limitations of existing VE metrics, particularly their inability to capture editing leakage, often referred to as attention leakage.
Global Textual Faithfulness (GTF)
[
24
,
17
,
16
,
19
]
and Frame Accuracy (FA)
[
18
]
fail to detect cases where a prompt for one instance affects others, as they cannot capture the nuances of individual instances within the global captions and frames.
Instance Accuracy (IA), defined as the percentage of instances more similar to their target instance caption than source instance caption, only measures target caption alignment and ignores interference from other instance captions.
Local Textual Faithfulness (LTF)
[
28
,
62
]
evaluates alignment between a cropped instance and its target instance caption but also overlooks leakage from other captions.
Background Preservation (BP) measures background changes but cannot detect leakage affecting instances.
We further observe cases where the LTF between a cropped instance and another instance’s target caption exceeds the score with its own instance caption, revealing cross-instance leakage.

To address all the above issues on GTF, LTF, FA, IA and BP, our CIA Score is defined as follows:
For each cropped instance
i
and each target instance caption
j
, we obtain the corresponding CLIP image embedding
I_{i}
and text embedding
C_{j}
, respectively.
We compute the cosine similarity
S(I_{i},C_{j})
between all pairs of image embeddings
I_{i}
and the text embeddings
C_{j}
for all
m
instances
(i,j\in\{1,...,m\})
, producing an
m\times m
matrix given by
\mathbf{S}=[S(I_{i},C_{j})]
for
i,j=1,2,...,m
.
Fig.
6
visualizes matrix
\mathbf{S}
and illustrates how CIA is computed for both VideoGrain
[
24
]
and our PRIMEdit.

For each row of
\mathbf{S}
, we assign a value of 1 to the highest similarity score and 0 to the rest, ideally yielding a matrix with ones along the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
We adopt this binary formulation because CIA evaluates exclusive instance–caption alignment rather than similarity magnitude.
Although LTF provides continuous similarity scores, even the sum or average of diagonal LTF values may fail to reveal severe cross-instance leakage.
As shown in Fig.
6
, VideoGrain achieves an average diagonal LTF of 0.2196, comparable to PRIMEdit’s 0.2210, yet in several rows, the off-diagonal similarities for VideoGrain exceed the diagonal entries, indicating incorrect caption dominance.
Consequently, continuous diagonal aggregation masks such violations.
By enforcing a winner-takes-all decision per row, CIA directly penalizes these cases, which is reflected in the average CIA scores (0.3333 for VideoGrain versus 1.0 for PRIMEdit), clearly distinguishing the methods despite similar diagonal LTF averages.

The CIA Score is calculated as the mean of the diagonal elements as:

\text{CIA}=(1\mathbin{/}m)\cdot\Sigma_{i=1}^{m}S(I_{i},C_{i}).

(13)

As illustrated in Fig.
6
, CIA clearly captures editing leakage in VideoGrain. For example, the second cropped instance is more influenced by the caption “yellow washing machine,” resulting in a CIA value of 0.

We compare the performance of PRIMEdit against VideoGrain under noisy instance masks.
To systematically evaluate robustness to mask imperfections, we conduct five experiments on the full MIVE dataset using synthetically corrupted masks:

(i) Dilated masks
, where masks are randomly dilated using kernels with sizes selected from {5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15} pixels;

(ii) Eroded masks
, where masks are randomly eroded using kernels with sizes selected from {5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15} pixels;

(iii) Jittered masks
, where boundary perturbations are introduced to simulate imperfect annotations.
Specifically, we compute the Euclidean distance transform of each binary mask and define a narrow boundary band (5 pixels) around the object contour.
Gaussian noise (smoothed with a Gaussian kernel) is added within the boundary band.
The perturbed distance map is thresholded to obtain a binary mask, producing locally irregular contour distortions while preserving overall object structure.
We set the jitter strength to 30;

(iv) Hole-injected masks
, where we simulate missing annotations by removing irregular interior regions from the foreground.
For each mask, we sample a Gaussian noise, smooth it with a
7\times 7
Gaussian kernel, and threshold it at 0.20 to obtain a binary pattern.
Pixels where both the original mask and the thresholded noise have values equal to 1 are set to 0, producing spatially irregular holes of varying shapes and sizes within the object.

(v) Mixed-noise masks
, where dilation, erosion, boundary jittering, and hole injection are randomly combined to simulate more severe and realistic segmentation errors.

Table
X
and Fig.
13
present quantitative and qualitative results, respectively.
From Table
X
, PRIMEdit maintains higher LTC, LTF, and IA scores across all mask perturbation types.
As corruption intensifies from dilated, eroded, and jittered to hole-injected and mixed-noise settings, VideoGrain shows more pronounced drops in IA, particularly under hole-injected and mixed-noise masks.
This reflects increased inconsistency in applying the target attributes within the intended instance.
In contrast, PRIMEdit exhibits smaller IA reductions and shows more stable LTC and LTF values, indicating that local temporal coherence and instance-caption alignment remain intact even when masks are distorted or incomplete.

The qualitative results directly reflect these trends.
Under eroded masks in Fig.
13
, VideoGrain leaves portions of the cat partially unedited, producing visible inconsistency across the object boundaries.
This corresponds to the lower IA values observed in the table.
Under hole-injected and mixed-noise masks, patchy editing appears more frequently in VideoGrain outputs, which aligns with the larger IA degradation.
PRIMEdit, however, maintains more uniform editing across the cat and bench, visually confirming its higher IA and stable local metrics.

Regarding leakage sensitivity, the CIA scores provide the clearest distinction.
The differences in CIA are most pronounced under corruption types that introduce irregular or fragmented mask boundaries, such as jittered and hole-injected masks.
In these settings, PRIMEdit maintains a clearer advantage, indicating stronger resistance to cross-instance interference when mask precision is disrupted in non-uniform ways.
Even in corruption types where the relative percentage gaps are smaller (e.g., dilated or eroded masks), PRIMEdit still consistently achieves higher raw CIA scores than VideoGrain.
This pattern suggests that cross-instance leakage is more strongly triggered by irregular mask perturbations than by uniform boundary scaling.

Overall, the combined quantitative (shown in the last column of Table
X
) and qualitative evidence demonstrates that PRIMEdit is less sensitive to mask inaccuracies (i.e., strong robustness against noisy masks enabled by the complementary interaction of IPR and DMS, where IPR stabilizes instance-level attention while DMS mitigates boundary distortions and missing regions).
It preserves local scores, maintains stronger instance-level alignment, and exhibits better leakage control across different perturbation types, especially under severe corruption such as hole-injected and mixed-noise masks.

Limitations of our PRIMEdit and other SOTA multi-instance VE methods in editing scenes with reflective surfaces (top) and an excessive number of instances (bottom).

Fig.
15
shows the limitations of our PRIMEdit and other SOTA methods in editing scenes with reflective surfaces and an excessive number of instances.

As shown in the top row of Fig.
15
-(d), PRIMEdit struggles in scenes with reflective surfaces, where it cannot edit reflections because the masks cover only the instance itself.
This leads to inconsistencies between the edited instance and its reflection.
As shown in (b) and (c) in the top part of Fig.
15
, other SOTA methods exhibit the same limitation.
To address this, future works may incorporate a prior technique
[
80
]
to enforce the consistency between instances and their reflections.

Moreover, PRIMEdit and the other baselines struggle to handle complex scenes containing small objects to be edited.
As shown, they fail in scenes with many instances (18 objects in the bottom scene of Fig.
15
).
This limitation arises from the inherent difficulty of U-Net–based LDMs in handling small objects, as the input is compressed into low-resolution latents by the VAE encoder and further downsampled within the U-Net.
As a result, very small objects and their masks may vanish within the low-resolution U-Net features, hindering accurate editing.
Editing in the pixel space or a higher-resolution latent space could mitigate this issue at the cost of increased computational cost.
Both the reflection consistency and the small-sized object editing in complex scenes remain open problems for future research in multi-instance VE.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we present PRIMEdit, a novel multi-instance video editing framework, featuring Instance-centric Probability Redistribution (IPR) and Disentangled Multi-instance Sampling (DMS), to elaborately handle instance-level video editing.
PRIMEdit produces faithful, disentangled edits with minimal attention leakage, outperforming existing SOTA methods in qualitative, quantitative, and user studies on our
newly
proposed MIVE dataset, the
first
large-scale dataset dedicated to multi-instance video editing tasks.
We further propose a new Cross-Instance Accuracy (CIA) Score to quantify the attention leakage from one instance to others, which often occurs in multi-instance video editing but has been overlooked by existing video editing metrics.
Our user study supports the effectiveness of the CIA Score, showing the quality consistency with human’s visual perception.
