Abstract
. The qualitative behavior of the Rabinowitz unbounded continuum of subcritical Gelfand problems is well known on balls in any dimension. We don’t know of any such sharp and detailed description otherwise, which is our motivation to look for a new approach to the problem. The underlying idea is to describe solutions of Gelfand problems via suitably defined constrained problems of free boundary-type arising in plasma physics and to replace the usual L ∞ L^{\infty} norm of the solution with the energy of the plasma. Toward this goal, we first solve a long standing open problem of independent interest about the uniqueness of solutions of Grad-Shafranov type equations. Thus, we exploit these unique solutions to detect a curve containing both minimal and non minimal solutions of the associated Gelfand problem. In other words we come up with a new global parametrization of the Rabinowitz continuum, the monotonicity of the energy along the branch providing a meaningful generalization of the classical pointwise monotonicity property of minimal solutions, suitable to describe non minimal solutions as well. On a ball in any dimension, we come up as expected with a bell-shaped profile of the full branch of solutions of the Gelfand problem.



Keywords
: Rabinowitz continuum, Gelfand problems, Grad-Shafranov type equations,
free boundary problems, uniqueness.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
be a bounded domain of class
C^{3}
and set

p_{{}_{N}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}+\infty,\;N=2\\
\frac{N}{N-2},\;N\geq 3.\end{array}\right.

For
1<p<p_{{}_{N}}
we wish to develop a general method to describe the qualitative behavior of the Rabinowitz unbounded continuum (
[
29
]
)
of classical solutions of the Gel’fand problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v=\mu(1+v)^{p}\quad\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega\\
v>0\quad\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega\\
v=0\quad\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.\qquad{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}

It is well known (see e.g.
[
13
]
) that there exists
\mu_{*}=\mu_{*}(\Omega,p)>0
such that
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
has at least two solutions for
\mu<\mu_{*}
(one of which is a minimal solution, the other one a mountain-pass solution), exactly one solution for
\mu=\mu_{*}
and no solution at all for
\mu>\mu_{*}
, the well known branch of minimal solutions being smooth and monotone as a function of
\mu
.
However results of this sort are concerned with the counting of solutions and not with the connectivity and/or with the qualitative behavior of the set of non-minimal solutions.
Besides the general result in
[
29
]
, the existence of continua of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
containing both minimal and non-minimal solutions is well-known for any subcritical
p<\frac{N+2}{N-2}
and convex domains, see
[
24
]
.
The claim in that case is about the existence of a continua of solutions containing minimal solutions for any
\mu\leq\mu_{*}
and non-minimal solutions characterized by the fact that they diverge as
\mu\to 0^{+}
, but with no reference in general to the monotonicity of
\|v_{\mu}\|_{\infty}
along the branch.

If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a ball it is well-known that solutions are radial (
[
16
]
) and that
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
has exactly two solutions for
\mu<\mu_{*}
for any
p\leq\frac{N+2}{N-2}
(
[
19
,
21
]
).
In particular all solutions are contained in a unique curve originating from
(\mu,v)=(0,0)
which makes only one turn to the left and then diverges as
\mu\to 0^{+}
, see
[
21
]
.
Since
v_{\mu}(0)
is monotonic increasing along the branch, this shows that the global branch has, in the plane
(\mu,v_{\mu}(0))
, the classical bell-shaped profile, see for example
[
24
,
28
]
.
In dimension
N=2
, a general result in
[
18
]
states that if
\Omega
is symmetric and convex with respect to coordinate directions, then all the solutions lie on a curve of the form
t\mapsto(\mu(t),v_{\mu(t)}(0))
, where
v_{\mu(t)}(0)
is strictly increasing in
t
.
However, with the unique exception of the above mentioned results on balls, it seems that the monotonicity of
\mu(t)
is not known so far, neither in dimension
N=2
for
\Omega
symmetric and convex with respect to coordinate directions.
See
[
7
]
for a rather general result about the qualitative behavior of the Rabinowitz
unbounded continuum, including the global behavior of
\mu(t)
, in case
N=2
for the exponential nonlinearity.

These are our motivations to try to develop a new general method for the description of the qualitative behavior
of the Rabinowitz unbounded continuum of classical, minimal and non minimal solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
with
p<p_{{}_{N}}
, extending somehow the classical results about minimal solutions in
[
13
]
.
Toward this goal we pursue the constrained problem,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\psi=[\alpha+{\lambda}\psi]_{+}^{p}\quad\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega\\
\\
\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\limits_{\Omega}{[\alpha+{\lambda}\psi]_{+}^{p}}=1\\
\\
\psi=0\quad\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.\qquad{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}

for
p<p_{{}_{N}}
,
\lambda\geq 0
and the unknowns
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\in C^{2,r}_{0}(\overline{\Omega}\,)
.
If
(\alpha_{\lambda},\psi_{\lambda})
solves
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
for some
\lambda>0
and
\alpha_{\lambda}>0
, then
v^{(\lambda)}=\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{\lambda}}\psi_{\lambda}
solves
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
for
\mu=\mu_{\lambda}=\lambda\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-1}
. Assuming for simplicity
|\Omega|=1
, the underlying idea of the connection with
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
is thus to gather enough information to follow
“the” curve of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
from
\lambda=0
and
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\alpha_{0}=1
, up to “the first value” of
\lambda
where
\alpha_{\lambda}

approaches
\alpha=0
from above, while keeping in particular the monotonicity of a suitably defined energy.

Let
q
be the Hölder conjugate of
p
, i.e.
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
.
For a fixed
(\alpha_{\lambda},\psi_{\lambda})
which solves
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, we will adopt the following notations,

\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=[\alpha_{\lambda}+\lambda\psi_{\lambda}]_{+}^{p}\quad\mbox{\rm and }\quad{(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}}=[\alpha_{\lambda}+\lambda\psi_{\lambda}]_{+}^{p-1}.

Problem
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
is of independent interest due to its relevance to Tokamak’s plasma physics (
[
14
,
20
,
30
]
),
which motivated the lot of work done to understand existence, uniqueness, multiplicity of solutions and existence/non-existence/structure of the free boundary
\partial\{x\in\Omega\,:\,\alpha+\lambda\psi>0\}
of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
,
see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
4
,
8
,
10
,
12
,
15
,
22
,
26
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
and references quoted therein.
Actually
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
is equivalent to the well-known Grad-Shafranov type equation, see
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{F}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{I}}}
in section
7
.
In particular due to the fundamental results in
[
8
]
, it is well known that for any
\lambda>0
there exists at least one solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
.
The variational formulation adopted in
[
8
]
is closely related to minimal free energy principles with non-extensive entropy, where
-\lambda
plays the role of the inverse statistical temperature, see Appendix A in
[
4
]
and
[
11
]
for further details.

On the other side, due to the constraint in
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, we miss the classical notion of minimal solutions (
[
13
]
) for this problem.
Remark that this is not just a technical point, as we shortly discuss hereafter.
First of all it has been recently shown in
[
7
,
4
]
that in a neat interval of values of
\lambda
there exists a unique positive (i.e.
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}>0
) solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
forming a branch
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega)

along which
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is decreasing while a suitably defined energy is increasing, see Theorems A and B below. Actually, still due to the constraint, it is not true in general that any first eigenfunction of the associated linearized operator (see (
16
) below) must be simple and neither that if the first eigenvalue is positive then the maximum principle holds, see
[
5
]
for an example illustrating this point.
As a consequence, unlike classical branches of Gelfand problems emanating from
(\mu,v)=(0,0)
(
[
13
]
), it is not at all clear that
\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\lambda\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{+}^{p}
is pointwise
monotonic increasing along
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega)
.
A workaround to this problem has been recently found in
[
6
,
7
,
4
]
, yielding suitable monotonicity property through the bifurcation analysis of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
for solutions with
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}>0
, again referring to Theorems A and B below.
However, in particular for
N\geq 3
, the requirement of the positivity of
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
causes a major obstacle in the understanding of the uniqueness and continuation of branches of solutions.
Motivated by the analysis of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
, we solve this problem here by introducing a weighted first eigenvalue in the same spirit of
[
27
]
, where the weight, which is
(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}=[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\lambda\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{+}^{p-1}
, is allowed to vanish in a “large” set.
This in turn allows the description of a branch of stable (i.e. with positive weighted first eigenvalue) solutions for
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, see Theorem
1.1
below.

We follow the traditional convention to assume that the domain has unit volume,
|\Omega|=1
.
In particular
\mathbb{D}_{N}
denotes a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
centered at the origin of unit volume
|\mathbb{D}_{N}|=1
, whose radius is denoted by
R_{N}
.
For fixed
t\geq 1
we define

\Lambda(\Omega,t)=\inf\limits_{w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),w\equiv\!\!\!\!/\;0}\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}}{\left(\int_{\Omega}|w|^{t}\right)^{\frac{2}{t}}}\,,

which is related to the best constant in the Sobolev embedding
\|w\|_{p}\leq\mathcal{C}_{S}(\Omega,p)\|\nabla w\|_{2}
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{S}(\Omega,p)=\Lambda^{-1/2}(\Omega,p)\quad\mbox{ for }p\in[1,2p_{{}_{N}}).

Definition.

We say that a solution
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
is
positive

[
resp.
non-negative
]
if

\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}>0

[
resp.
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\geq 0
].

It has been recently proved in
[
4
]
that the following quantity is well-defined and strictly positive:

\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)=\sup\left\{\delta>0\,:\,\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}>0\mbox{ for any solution }(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\mbox{ of }{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}\mbox{ with }\lambda<\delta\right\}.

Remark.

We will use the fact that
\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
is always finite, as readily follows
by the variational formulation of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
. See also

[
4
, Appendix A]
.

Definition.

Let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
be a solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
.
The
energy
of
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
is

E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\rm$\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}$}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Let us define

\lambda_{0}(\Omega,p):=\frac{1}{p}\Lambda(\Omega,2p),\quad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},\;N\geq 2,

and

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,p):=\left(\frac{8\pi}{p+1}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{2p}}\Lambda^{\frac{p+1}{2p}}(\Omega,p+1),\quad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The following results about positive solutions have been recently proved in
[
7
,
4
]
.

Theorem A
(
[
7
]
).
Let
N\geq 2
and
p\in[1,p_{N})
.
For any
\lambda<\lambda_{0}(\Omega,p)
there exists a unique positive solution to
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
,
defining a real analytic curve
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega)
,
such that
\alpha_{0}=1
,
2E_{0}=2E_{0}(\Omega)
is the torsional rigidity of
\Omega
and

\frac{d\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}<0,\quad\frac{dE_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}>0,\quad\forall(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\in\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega).

In particular,

\lim\limits_{\underset{\lambda\to\lambda^{-}_{0}(\Omega,p)}{(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\in\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega)}}\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=0

if and only if
p=1
.

The situation is better for
N=2
, where we have a sharp "positivity" estimate and, as a consequence, an unconditional uniqueness result.

Theorem B
(
[
7
,
4
]
).
Let
N=2
and
p\in[1,+\infty)
, then
\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega,p)

where the equality holds if and only if either
p=1
or
\Omega=\mathbb{D}_{2}
.
Moreover, for any
\lambda<\min\{\lambda_{0}(\Omega,p),\lambda_{1}(\Omega,p)\}
there exists a unique solution to
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
,
defining a
real analytic curve of positive solutions which we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega)
,
such that
\alpha_{0}=1
,
2E_{0}=2E_{0}(\Omega)
is the torsional rigidity of
\Omega
and

\frac{d\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}<0,\quad\frac{dE_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}>0,\quad\forall(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\in\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega).

We tend to believe that
\lambda_{1}(\Omega,p)>\lambda_{0}(\Omega,p)
, which is always true at least for
p
large enough and for
\Omega=\mathbb{D}_{2}
, see
[
4
]
.
It is not clear whether or not the sharp positivity estimates about
\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
obtained in Theorem B can be extended to higher dimensions
N\geq 3
.
For later purposes we also recall the following result in
[
4
]
. Let
u_{0}
be the unique (
[
16
]
) solution of the Emden equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u_{0}=u_{0}^{p}\quad\mbox{in}\;\;B_{1}\\
u_{0}>0\quad\mbox{in}\;\;B_{1}\\
u_{0}=0\quad\mbox{on}\;\;\partial B_{1},\end{array}\right.

and set

I_{p}=\int\limits_{B_{1}}{\displaystyle{u}_{0}^{p}}\dx.

Note that
R_{N}
denotes the radius of
\mathbb{D}_{N}
and let us set,

(4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}(\mathbb{D}_{N},p)={I_{p}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}{R_{N}^{-\frac{N}{p}(1-\frac{p}{p_{{}_{N}}})}}.

Then we have,

Theorem C
(
[
4
]
).
Let
\Omega=\mathbb{D}_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 3
. Then, for any
p\in(1,p_{{}_{N}})
,
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
admits a unique solution. Moreover

\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}>0
if and only if
\lambda<\lambda_{+}(\mathbb{D}_{N},p)
.

However, in particular for
N\geq 3
, it is still not clear whether or not the unique positive solutions in Theorem A are in fact the unique solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, although this is indeed the case for
\lambda>0
sufficiently small (
[
4
]
).
We solve this problem here by refined spectral estimates for the linearized problem naturally associated with
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
.

First of all it can be shown (see section
3
and in particular (
16
)), that the non-local
eigenvalue problem associated to the linearized equation for
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
evaluated at a fixed solution
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
takes the form

-\Delta\phi-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\sigma(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

where, here and in the sequel,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
denotes an eigenvalue relative to
\phi
,

\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda p,

and

\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\coloneqq\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\phi}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}},\qquad[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\coloneqq\phi\,-\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\qquad\forall\,\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega).

We can prove that, for any fixed solution
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
, the weighted first eigenvalue is well defined, which we will always denote by
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
, see Lemma
3.1
.
Moreover, let us define

\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)\coloneqq\sup\left\{\overline{\lambda}>0\,:\,\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0\mbox{ for any solution }(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\mbox{ of }{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}\mbox{ with }\lambda<\overline{\lambda}\right\}.

Our first result is a spectral estimate of independent interest, which implies in particular that the unique positive solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
in Theorem A are in fact the unique solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
.
At least to our knowledge, this is the first unconditional uniqueness result about Grad-Shafranov type equations, solving a long standing open problem which dates back to the pioneering results in
[
8
]
, see
[
7
,
4
]
and references quoted therein.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N\geq 2
and
p\in[1,p_{N})
, then
\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)\geq\lambda_{0}(\Omega,p)
where the equality holds if and only if
p=1
and
for any
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p))
there exists a unique solution to
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
,
defining a
C^{1}
curve of solutions, denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{*}(\Omega)
, such that
\alpha_{0}=1
,
2E_{0}=2E_{0}(\Omega)
is the torsional rigidity of
\Omega
and

\frac{d\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}<0,\quad\frac{dE_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}>0,\quad\forall(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\in\mathcal{G}_{*}(\Omega).

In particular
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{*}(\Omega)
where
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\Omega)\equiv\mathcal{G}_{*}(\Omega)
if and only if
p=1
.

Along the branch
\mathcal{G}_{*}(\Omega)
we have
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
which is why
we say that
\mathcal{G}_{*}(\Omega)
is a branch of
stable
solutions. In other words, a solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
is said to be
stable if the first eigenvalue of the linearized
equation is positive. Remark also that, although we don’t know much about the pointwise monotonicity of
\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
, as far as

\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
we have that

\frac{dE_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}>0
and
\frac{d\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}<0
, see Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
below.

As a corollary of Theorem
1.1
we come up with a new approach relevant to the understanding of the qualitative behavior of the branch of solutions of

{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
starting at
(\mu,v)=(0,0)
, also known as the Rabinowitz continuum (
[
29
]
), including non minimal solutions. Indeed we reduce the problem of the existence of a
global strictly monotone
(in terms of
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
) parametrization to an estimate about
\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)
and
\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
N\geq 2
and let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
be the unique solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
for

\lambda<\min\{\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p),\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)\}.

Then:

(i)

The pair
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
, as well as
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
, are real analytic in
\lambda
, with

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}<0,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}>0,

\displaystyle\forall\lambda\in[0,\;\min\left\{\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p),\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)\right\}).

(ii)

The function
v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})}=\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
solves
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
for
\mu=\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{p-1}
, and the energy
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
takes the form,

(6)

\displaystyle E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(1+v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})})^{p}v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})}.

Furthermore,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{+}=\{(\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})})\,:\,\lambda\in[0,\min\{\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p),\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)\})\}

is a real analytic branch of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
.

(iii)

In case
\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)\geq\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
, as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
we have that,

(8)

\displaystyle\alpha\searrow 0^{+},

\displaystyle\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\to 0^{+},

\displaystyle\|v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})}\|_{\infty}\to+\infty,

and in particular
\mathcal{R}_{+}
is unbounded.

As far as
\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)\geq\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
, it is useful to denote

(9)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{G}_{+}}(\Omega,p)\coloneqq\mbox{ the curve of unique solutions of }{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}\mbox{ with }0\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)

and

(10)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{+}(\Omega,p)\coloneqq\mbox{ the portion of }\overline{\mathcal{G}_{+}}(\Omega,p)\mbox{ with }0\leq\lambda<\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p).

Due to Theorem
1.1
and since
\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
is always finite, the proof of Theorem
1.2
is deduced just by following
the curve of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
as far as either
\lambda<\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)
or else all along
{\mathcal{G}}_{+}(\Omega,p)
up to
\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
,
which is “the first value” of
\lambda
where
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

approaches
\alpha=0
from above, while keeping in particular the monotonicity of
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
.

Remark 1.3
.

In particular, since
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is the half of the mean value of
\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
with respect to the weight
\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
, we think that the monotonicity of
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is a good extension of the classical notion
(
[
13
]
)
of monotonicity of minimal solutions,
which is of particular interest whenever solutions on
\mathcal{G}_{*}(\Omega)
does not anymore correspond via

v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})}=\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
to minimal but in fact to
non-minimal solutions
of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
.
We also observe that the quantity
\frac{v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})}}{\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}
is monotonically strictly increasing along
\mathcal{G}_{*}
.
Actually the result shows that, as far as
\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)\geq\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
, the global
parametrization
\mathcal{R}_{+}
of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}

built with the stable solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
along
{\mathcal{G}}_{+}(\Omega,p)
includes
a full branch of non-minimal solutions (since of course minimal solutions
v_{\mu}
vanish
(
[
13
]
)
in the limit
\mu\to 0^{+}
). However, as far as
\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)\geq\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
, we also have the monotonicity of the solutions all along the branch, including
non-minimal solutions
. We are not aware of any criteria of this sort suitable to catch the monotonicity of the solutions behind the minimal branch.

Clearly, for those domains for which
\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)\geq\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
,
\mathcal{R}_{+}
would be just the Rabinowitz unbounded (positive) continuum
(
[
29
]
)
relative to
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
and therefore would coincide, for
\Omega
convex, with the continuum obtained in Theorem 2.2 in
[
24
]
. We recall that, as mentioned at the very beginning of the introduction, the monotonicity all along the branch of the

L^{\infty}
norm of the solutions is well known on balls (see
[
21
]
and references quoted therein) and on planar domains symmetric and convex w.r.t. coordinate directions (
[
18
]
).

The underlying idea of this new method is that a careful analysis of a subcritical, superlinear equation with one constraint, which is
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, is effective in describing the continuum of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
with Morse index not larger than one.
Indeed, the continuum of solutions described in
[
24
]
contains a full branch of non-minimal solutions, which in turn can be
obtained by a careful use of the mountain pass theorem, see
(
[
13
]
)
.
However, under suitable non-degeneracy assumptions, it is well-known that mountain pass solutions have Morse index not larger than one
(
[
17
]
)
. This is why we refer to the branch
(\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})})
as the
branch of constrained-stable solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
.

Therefore, as a consequence of Theorem
1.2
, we are naturally interested in finding sufficient conditions which guarantee
\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)\geq\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)
, which in turn would yield the existence of a monotone (in terms of
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
) global parametrization of the Rabinowitz unbounded continuum
\mathcal{R}_{+}
.
An elementary estimate shows that
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
as far as
\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}(\Omega,p)
(see Proposition
3.5
below) which is unfortunately far from enough for our purposes.

2.
Preliminary estimates for constrained solutions

Since
|\Omega|=1
, by the constraint in
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
we have that
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\leq 1
for any non-negative solution. Here we have,

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p\in[1,p_{{}_{N}})
. For any
\overline{\lambda}>0
there exist
\overline{\alpha}=\overline{\alpha}(\beta,\Omega,\overline{\lambda},p,N)>-\infty
and
C_{1}=C_{1}(\beta,\Omega,\overline{\lambda},p,N)<+\infty
such that

(11)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\geq\overline{\alpha},

\displaystyle\|\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\|_{C^{2,r}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C_{1}

for any solution
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
with
\lambda\in[0,\overline{\lambda}\,]
.

This extends the estimates for positive solutions in
[
7
, Lemma 4.2]
and will be crucial for later bifurcation analysis.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

We can assume w.l.o.g.
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}<0
, otherwise
0\leq\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\leq 1
and the proof for
[
7
, Lemma 4.2]
works fine.
Because of the constraint
\int_{\Omega}[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\lambda\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{+}^{p}=1
, it is well-known (
[
25
]
) that for any
t\in[1,\frac{N}{N-1})
there exists
C=C(t,N,\Omega)
such that
\|\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\|_{W_{0}^{1,t}(\Omega)}\leq C(t,N,\Omega)
for any solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
.
Thus, by the Sobolev inequalities, for any
1\leq s<p_{N}=\frac{N}{N-2}
we have
\|\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\|_{L^{s}(\Omega)}\leq C(s,N,\Omega)
, for some
C(s,N,\Omega)
.
As a consequence, since
p<p_{N}
, we have

(12)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\lambda\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{+}^{pm}\leq\lambda^{pm}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{pm}\leq\overline{\lambda}^{pm}C(m,N,\Omega)

for any
1<m<\frac{p_{N}}{p}
, and
\lambda\leq\overline{\lambda}
.
By standard elliptic estimates,
\|\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\|_{W_{0}^{2,m}(\Omega)}\leq C_{0}(p,N,\overline{\lambda},\Omega)
, for any
\lambda\leq\overline{\lambda}
.
Thus by the Sobolev embedding we find
\|\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\|_{W_{0}^{1,t}(\Omega)}\leq C(t,N,\Omega)
for any
t\in[1,\frac{Nm}{N-m}]
.

At this point, since
\frac{Nm}{N-m}>\frac{N}{N-1}
and
\Omega
is of class
C^{2,r}
, by a bootstrap argument we find that

(13)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\|_{C^{2,r}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C_{1}(r,\Omega,\overline{\lambda},p,N)

for any
\lambda\leq\overline{\lambda}
.
As a consequence, if
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
were not uniformly bounded from below, we would have
\alpha_{n}\to-\infty
along some sequence
\lambda_{n}\leq\overline{\lambda}
, which would be a contradiction to
\int_{\Omega}[\alpha_{n}+\lambda_{n}\psi_{n}]_{+}^{p}=1
.
∎

3.
Spectral setup of the linearized problem

For fixed
\lambda\geq 0
and
p\in[1,p_{{}_{N}})
, let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
be a solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, we will adopt the following notation,

\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\phi}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}}\quad\mbox{and }\quad[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\phi\,-\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\quad\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Let us recall that we are always concerned with classical solutions, whence in particular

(14)

\displaystyle(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}=[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\lambda\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{+}^{p-1}\in C^{0}(\Omega).

The support of
\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is by definition
\overline{\Omega_{+}}
,
whence, if
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\geq 0
then
\Omega_{+}=\Omega
while if
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}<0
then, since
\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is continuous, we have
\Omega_{+}\Subset\Omega
.

Consider the linear operator
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\colon C^{2,\beta}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})\to C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
defined by

(15)

\displaystyle L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\phi]=-\Delta\phi-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

with
\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda p
.
We say that
\sigma=\sigma(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\in\mathbb{R}
is an eigenvalue of
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
if the equation

(16)

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\sigma(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

admits a non-trivial weak solution
\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and the corresponding eigenspace is denoted by
\Eigen(L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};\sigma)
.
Note that
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
involves not only nonlocal terms but also a weight which may vanish on a large set.
This is in great contrast to
[
7
]
; although the structure of the spectral theory goes along the same ideas
in
[
7
]
, a relevant
part of the argument differs essentially.
Now it is crucial to understand the spectral properties of
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
.
Although the main properties for general weighted operators are considered in
[
27
]
,
here we need a refined information about the spectrum and, in particular, about the eigenfunctions.

The eigenvalue equation (
16
) can be written as follows,

(17)

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi=(\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\sigma)(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

If we let
G
denote the Green’s function for
-\Delta
with Dirichlet boundary condition and apply it to (
16
), then we see that,

(18)

\displaystyle\phi=\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\sigma}{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\;G*\left(\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right).

As in
[
7
]
we consider the operator
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\colon H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
defined as follows,

(19)

\displaystyle T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\phi)\coloneqq G*\left(\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right),

so that (
16
) is equivalent to

(20)

\displaystyle T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\phi)=\mu\phi,\qquad\mbox{ with }\mu=\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\sigma}.

In other words,
\phi\in\Eigen(L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};\sigma)
iff
\phi\in\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\sigma})
.
Thus it suffices to understand the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
, where the advantage is that it is a linear self-adjoint compact operator on the Hilbert space
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
equipped with the inner product

(21)

\displaystyle\left<\xi,\eta\right>_{H^{1}_{0}}=\int_{\Omega}\left<\nabla\xi,\;\nabla\eta\right>,\qquad\forall\xi,\eta\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

Indeed,

(22)

\displaystyle\left<T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\xi),\;\eta\right>_{H^{1}_{0}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left<\nabla T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\xi),\nabla\eta\right>=\int_{\Omega}(-\Delta T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\xi))\eta

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\eta=\int_{\Omega}\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\eta]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

which is symmetric in
\xi
and
\eta
, hence
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is self-adjoint.
The compactness of
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
follows immediately from the compactness of
G
.
As a consequence, the spectrum of
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
, say
\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
, consists of countably many real nonzero eigenvalues and perhaps zero.
Each nonzero eigenvalue has finite multiplicity, and the nonzero eigenvalues have zero as the unique accumulation point.
From (
17
) it follows that
\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\sigma\geq 0
, hence
\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
We may thus list the nonzero eigenvalues as follows,

(24)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\mu_{3}\geq\cdots>0,\quad\lim_{j\to+\infty}\mu_{j}=0,

and denote the corresponding eigenfunctions by
\phi_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, which satisfy

(25)

\displaystyle\delta_{jk}=\left<\phi_{j},\phi_{k}\right>_{H^{1}_{0}}=\int_{\Omega}\left<\nabla\phi_{j},\;\nabla\phi_{k}\right>,\qquad\forall j,k\geq 1.

Introducing the bilinear form,

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\xi,\eta)\coloneqq\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\eta]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\qquad\forall\xi,\eta\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

then it is well known (see
[
27
]
) that the eigenvalues can be characterized as follows :

•

for
j=1
:

(27)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\mathcal{B}(\phi,\phi)\mid\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\;\left<\phi,\phi\right>_{H^{1}_{0}}=1\right\}

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{B}(\phi_{1},\phi_{1}),

•

for
j\geq 2
:

(29)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\mathcal{B}(\phi,\phi)\mid\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\;\left<\phi,\phi\right>_{H^{1}_{0}}=1,\;\left<\phi,\phi_{k}\right>_{H^{1}_{0}}=0,\mbox{ for }1\leq k\leq j-1\right\}

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{B}(\phi_{j},\phi_{j}).

We collect the set of nonzero eigenvalues in
\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\setminus\left\{0\right\}
and denote

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}\coloneqq\overline{\oplus_{j\geq 1}\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};\mu_{j})}=\overline{\Span\left\{\phi_{j}\mid j\geq 1\right\}}

where the closure is taken in the
H^{1}_{0}
norm.
This is a closed subspace of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

(i)

0\in\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
iff
\alpha<0
;

(ii)

If
\alpha\geq 0
, then

(32)

\displaystyle\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=\left\{\mu_{j}\mid j\geq 1\right\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)=\mathcal{H}_{1};

(iii)

If
\alpha<0
, then

(33)

\displaystyle\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=\left\{0\right\}\cup\left\{\mu_{j}\mid j\geq 1\right\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)=\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}.

Moreover,

(34)

\displaystyle\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)=\left\{\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\mid\phi|_{\Omega_{+}}=\mbox{const}\right\}

and the restriction of any function in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to the set
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}}
is harmonic.

Proof.

(i) Taking the Laplacian of
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\phi)=\mu\phi
gives

(35)

\displaystyle-\mu\Delta\phi=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

and testing against
\phi
we have that

(36)

\displaystyle\mu\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}.

If
\alpha\geq 0
, then
\Omega_{+}=\Omega
by the maximum principle and
if
\mu=0
were an eigenvalue with an eigenfunction
\phi\neq 0
, then
[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\equiv 0
, whence
\phi=\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
would be constant, which should be necessarily zero since
\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. This is the desired contradiction, showing the first implication.

Conversely, if
\alpha<0
then
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}}
is a nonempty open subset and any
0\neq\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}})
is an eigenfunction of
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
.

(ii) If
\alpha\geq 0
, then we see that
\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=\left\{\mu_{j}\mid j\geq 1\right\}
doesn’t contain
0
, and the normalized eigenfunctions constitutes a complete orthonormal basis for
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
by standard results.

(iii) If
\alpha<0
, then
\Spect{T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}=\left\{0\right\}\cup\left\{\mu_{j}\mid j\geq 1\right\}
, and
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)=\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}
.
Since we have seen that
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}})\subset\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)
, the eigenvalue
0
has infinite multiplicity.

Let
\phi_{0}\in\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)
, namely,

(37)

\displaystyle 0=T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\phi_{0})=G*\left(\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{0}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right),

hence
(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{0}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\equiv 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
It follows that
[\phi_{0}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\equiv 0
a.e. in
\Omega_{+}
or equivalently,
\phi_{0}=\left<\phi_{0}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is constant a.e. in
\Omega_{+}
.
Again, since
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}})\subset\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)
,
\phi_{0}
can be arbitrary in
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}}
as long as it is
H^{1}
and has trace
0
on
\partial\Omega
.

For
\mu_{j}>0
, the eigenfunction
\phi_{j}
satisfies

(38)

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi_{j}=\frac{1}{\mu_{j}}\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

and the right hand side vanishes in
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{+}
, namely
\phi_{j}|_{\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}}}
is necessarily harmonic.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In the orthogonal decomposition, we will use

(39)

\displaystyle P_{0}\colon H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad P_{1}\colon H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{H}_{1}

to denote the corresponding orthogonal projections.
More precisely, for any
\psi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have that

(40)

\displaystyle\psi=P_{0}\psi+P_{1}\psi=P_{0}\psi+\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\beta_{j}\phi_{j}

with the Fourier coefficients

(41)

\displaystyle\beta_{j}=\left<\psi,\phi_{j}\right>_{H^{1}_{0}}=\int_{\Omega}\left<\nabla\psi,\;\nabla\phi_{j}\right>=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mu_{j}}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\psi=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mu_{j}}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\psi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Note that the presence of
\frac{1}{\mu_{j}}
is due to the normalization condition: we did not require
\int_{\omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}=1
but instead

(42)

\displaystyle 1=\int_{\Omega}\left<\nabla\phi_{j},\nabla\phi_{j}\right>=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mu_{j}}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}.

Recall that each
\mu_{j}>0
of
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
corresponds to an eigenvalue
\sigma_{j}
of
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
, related by

(43)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}=\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\sigma_{j}},\quad\mbox{ that is, }\quad\sigma_{j}=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{j}}-1\right),

sharing the same eigenfunction
\phi_{j}
.
The zero eigenvalue of
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
does not correspond to any eigenvalue of
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
.
Indeed,
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}|_{\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)}=(-\Delta)|_{\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)}
, which gives the other part of the spectrum of
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
.

Note that
0\in\Spect(L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
iff
1\in\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
0\notin\Spect(L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
, then
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\colon C^{2,\beta}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})\to C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

The assumption
0\notin\Spect(L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
guarantees that
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is injective.
For the surjectivity, let
f\in C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
, and we need to find a solution of

(44)

\displaystyle L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\varphi=-\Delta\varphi-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\varphi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=f.

Applying
G
to both sides, this is equivalent to

(45)

\displaystyle\varphi-T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\varphi)=G*f.

By projecting to the subspaces
\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
respectively, say
\varphi=\varphi_{0}+\varphi_{1}
, and
G*f=(G*f)_{0}+(G*f)_{1}
, with
\varphi_{0},(G*f)_{0}\in\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)
and
\varphi_{1},(G*f)_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and noting that
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\varphi_{0})=0
, we get

(46)

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}=(G*f)_{0},

\displaystyle(I-T)\varphi_{1}=(G*f)_{1}.

Since
1\notin\Spect(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
, by the Fredholm alternative on the Hilbert subspace
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, we see that there exists a unique
\varphi_{1}
solving the second equation in (
46
).
∎

To describe the continuous branch of solutions, we employ the implicit function theorem.
Thus consider the map

(47)

\displaystyle F\colon(-1,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times C^{2,\beta}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}\times C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega}),

(48)

\displaystyle(\lambda,\alpha,\psi)

\displaystyle\mapsto F(\lambda,\alpha,\psi)=(F^{1},F^{2}),

with

(49)

\displaystyle F^{1}(\lambda,\alpha,\psi)=-1+\int_{\Omega}[\alpha+\lambda\psi]_{+}^{p},

\displaystyle F^{2}(\lambda,\alpha,\psi)=-\Delta\psi-[\alpha+\lambda\psi]_{+}^{p}\in C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega}).

The preimage
F^{-1}(0,0)
consists exactly of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
.
The gradient w.r.t.
(\alpha,\psi)
is given by

(50)

\displaystyle D_{(\alpha,\psi)}F(\lambda,\alpha,\psi)[s,\phi]=\left(p\int_{\Omega}[\alpha+\lambda\psi]_{+}^{p-1}(s+\lambda\phi),\;-\Delta\phi-p[\alpha+\lambda\psi]_{+}^{p-1}(s+\lambda\phi)\right).

Fix
\lambda>0
and let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
be a solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, so that
F(\lambda,\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=(0,0)
.
Using the notation
\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
as above, we have

(51)

\displaystyle D_{(\alpha,\psi)}F(\lambda,\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})[s,\phi]=\left(p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}(s+\lambda\phi),\;-\Delta\phi-p(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}(s+\lambda\phi)\right).

Consider first the kernel of
D_{(\alpha,\psi)}F(\lambda,\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
.
Let
(s,\phi)
be an element in the kernel.
The vanishing of the first component in (
51
) implies
s=-\lambda\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
, hence the vanishing of the second component becomes

(52)

\displaystyle 0=-\Delta\phi-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=L_{\lambda}\phi.

If
\phi\neq 0
, then
0\in\Spect(L_{\lambda})
.
That is,
if
0\notin\Spect(L_{\lambda})
, then
\phi=0
and hence also
s=0
, so
\Ker D_{(\alpha,\psi)}F(\lambda,\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=0
.

Given
(t,f)\in\mathbb{R}\times C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
, consider the equation

(53)

\displaystyle D_{(\alpha,\psi)}F(\lambda,\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})[s,\phi]=(t,f).

Similarly, the relation for the first components gives

(54)

\displaystyle s=-\lambda\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\frac{t}{pm_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}},\qquad(\mbox{ where }m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\equiv\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\;)

while the second becomes

(55)

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=f+\frac{t}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}.

By Lemma
3.3
, there is a unique
\phi\in C^{2,\beta}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})
solving this equation, which in turn determines a unique
s
.
Thus
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is also surjective, hence an isomorphism.

One can readily check that the above argument also holds for
\lambda=0
.

Summarizing these facts, together with the implicit function theorem, we have the following local
C^{1}
regularity of the branch of solutions.
See
[
1
]
and compare with
[
7
, Lemma 2.4]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}})
be a solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
with
\lambda=\lambda_{0}\geq 0
.
If
0\notin\Spect(L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}})
, then:

(i)

D_{\alpha,\psi}\Phi(\lambda_{0},\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}})
is an isomorphism;

(ii)

There exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
(\lambda_{0},\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}})
such that the set of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
in
\mathcal{U}
is a
C^{1}
curve of solutions
J\ni\lambda\mapsto(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\in B
, for suitable neighborhoods
J
of
\lambda_{0}
and
B
of
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}})
in
\mathbb{R}\times C^{2,r}_{0}(\overline{\Omega}\,)
.

Let us equivalently write the characterization of
\mu_{1}
as follows,

(56)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}=\inf\left\{\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}(\phi,\phi)}\,:\,\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\;\left<\phi,\phi\right>_{H^{1}_{0}}=1\right\},

then it is readily seen that

(57)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}-1\right)=\inf\limits_{\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}}.

On the other hand, the standard first eigenvalue is characterized by

(58)

\displaystyle\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\coloneqq\inf\limits_{w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w^{2}}.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
is a solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
.
Then

(i)

\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\geq\Lambda(\Omega,2p)-\lambda p
,

(ii)

\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
.

In particular, if
\lambda p<\Lambda(\Omega,2p)
, then
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
.

Proof.

(i) The argument is similar to that in
[
7
]
, only noting that here
\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
can vanish in a large subset but still integrates to 1.
W.l.o.g. we consider
\lambda>0
.
Let
w\in C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega}\,)
,
w\equiv\!\!\!\!\!/\;0
then we have

(59)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w^{2}}\geq\dfrac{1}{\left(\int_{\Omega}[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\lambda\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{+}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}}\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}}{\left(\int_{\Omega}w^{2p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}=\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}}{\left(\int_{\Omega}w^{2p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}\geq{\Lambda(\Omega,2p)}

which implies that
\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\geq{\Lambda(\Omega,2p)}-\lambda p
.

(ii) Clearly
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\geq\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
.
Argue by contradiction and assume that
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
, then there exists some
\{\phi,w\}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
such that

\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}}=\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}-\lambda p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w^{2}}=\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})

However, if
\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\neq 0
, then

\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\leq\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\phi^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\phi^{2}}<\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}),

which is absurd.
Thus we have
\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=0
, which is also impossible: as an eigenfunction of
\nu_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
,
\phi
should have a sign, contradicting
\left<\phi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=0
.
∎

4.
Monotone parametrization of solutions

In this section we are concerned with the monotonicity of the energy

2E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

and of
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
for solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
with
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
(see (
57
)).
The energy monotonicity is detected by making use of the spectral setting in the previous section.
We recall that
m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}
and
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is the linearized operator defined in (
15
).
Recall by Remark
3.2
that
P_{1}\colon H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{H}_{1}
denotes the orthogonal projection on the the closed subspace
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}})
be a solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
with
\lambda_{0}\geq 0
and suppose that
0\notin\Spect(L_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}})
.
Then, locally near
\lambda_{0}
, the map
\lambda\mapsto(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
is a
C^{1}
simple curve and

(60)

\displaystyle 2E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

\displaystyle\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\coloneqq\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}

are
C^{1}
functions in
\lambda
with
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\in C^{2}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})
and

(61)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+p\int_{\Omega}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}.

If, in addition,
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
, then

(62)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\geq\sigma_{1}\frac{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{p}\left<[P_{1}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}\right>_{\lambda},

and in particular
\frac{dE_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}>0
.

The corresponding statement was obtained for positive solutions in
[
7
]
.
For solutions which are not necessarily positive, more care is needed for the spectral analysis.
We already see that there is a new subspace
\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)
appearing in this context.
It turns out that this will not affect the energy estimates since the derivative of the energy "does not see" the
\Eigen(T_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda};0)
subspace.
This will be clear in the proof and is the crucial point for the argument to work.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

That
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
is
C^{1}
in
\lambda
locally around
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda_{0}})
follows from Lemma
3.4
.
The function
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
satisfies

(63)

\displaystyle-\Delta\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+p(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+p(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda},

where
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}
can be computed by the unit mass constraint in
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, that is

p\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}=-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\left<\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}-p\left<\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Therefore, we conclude that
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is a solution of,

(64)

\displaystyle-\Delta\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+p(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Thus
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\in C^{2}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})
.
By using
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
, we also have
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}|^{2}
, and in particular it holds,

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E_{\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}\left<\nabla\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\nabla\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>=\int_{\Omega}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(-\Delta\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

which proves the first equality in (
61
).
Meanwhile integrating by parts and using (
64
) gives that,

(65)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}(-\Delta\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(-\Delta\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

which is the second equality in (
61
).

It remains to prove the monotonicity of
E_{\lambda}
.
The case
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\geq 0
is already handled with in
[
7
, Proposition 3.1]
, so we may assume
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}<0
.
We use the decomposition in Lemma
3.1
and Remark
3.2
and write

(66)

\displaystyle\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=P_{0}(\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{+\infty}\beta_{j}\phi_{j},

\displaystyle\mbox{ with }\beta_{j}=\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mu_{j}}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mu_{j}}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

(67)

\displaystyle\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=P_{0}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{+\infty}\gamma_{j}\phi_{j},

\displaystyle\mbox{ with }\gamma_{j}=\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mu_{j}}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mu_{j}}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Testing (
64
) against
\phi_{j}
and combining with (
16
) we get that,

(68)

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\qquad(\mbox{here }\sigma_{j}\equiv\sigma_{j}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}))

that is,
\sigma_{j}\gamma_{j}=p{\beta_{j}}
,
which hold true for each
j\geq 1
.
The crucial observation is that
(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[P_{0}(\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\equiv 0
and
(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[P_{0}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\equiv 0
, hence

(69)

\displaystyle p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

\displaystyle=p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\left([P_{0}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+[P_{1}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right)\left([P_{0}(\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+[P_{1}(\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right)

(70)

\displaystyle=p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[P_{1}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[P_{1}(\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

(71)

\displaystyle=p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right)\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\beta_{k}[\phi_{k}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right)

(72)

\displaystyle=p\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}

(73)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}p\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}

(74)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\sigma_{j}\gamma_{j}^{2}.

The desired estimate in (
62
) follows from

(75)

\displaystyle\left<[P_{1}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}[\phi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\gamma_{j}^{2}

which can be estimated from above, as long as

,

(76)

\displaystyle\left<[P_{1}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\sigma_{1}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\sigma_{j}\gamma_{j}^{2}=\frac{1}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\sigma_{1}}p\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Thus the first term in (
61
) is non negative, and the right hand side of (
61
) is thus positive, proving the monotonicity of
E_{\lambda}
.
∎

Next we prove the monotonicity of
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
whenever
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
.

For this and later purposes, it is convenient to denote
u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
which satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda\left[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right]_{+}^{p}\quad\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
\\
u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=0\;\;\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\\
\\
\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\limits_{\Omega}\left[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right]_{+}^{p}=1.\end{array}\right.

In terms of
u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
we have
\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\left[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right]_{+}^{p}
and
(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}=\left[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right]_{+}^{p-1}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
solve
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
with
\lambda\geq 0
.
If
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
then
\dfrac{d\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}<0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4

u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
is a
C^{1}
function of
\lambda
and
w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{du_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}\in C^{2}_{0}(\overline{\Omega}\,)
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\quad\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega\\
\\
w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=0\quad\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, taking derivative of the constraint
\int_{\Omega}\left[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right]_{+}^{p}=1
yields

(85)

\displaystyle\frac{d\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}=-\left<w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

We first prove the claim for
p>1
and
\lambda>0
.
Testing (
4
) by
w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
we find that

(86)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}|^{2}-\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

(87)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

On the other side, multiplying the equation in (

\displaystyle\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\lambda\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}{u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}},

which is equivalent to

\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{1}{p-1}(p\left<u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}-\frac{1}{\lambda(p-1)}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Substituting this expression of

Since

we thus have

\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\left<w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\geq{\lambda}{(p-1)}\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Since
\sigma_{1}>0
by assumption and

\left<w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\lambda}>0

.

For

\lambda>0

and then the conclusion follows from (

).
For

and

w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
and
\left<w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=2E_{0}

).
∎

At this point we can prove Theorem

and Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We see from Proposition
3.5
that
\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})>0
for any solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
for any

By Lemmas
2.1
and

and Lemmas
3.4

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We recall from
[
4
, Appendix A]
that
\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)<+\infty
.
To simplify the exposition we denote
\lambda_{*}=\lambda_{*}(\Omega,p)
and
\lambda_{+}=\lambda_{+}(\Omega,p)

.

In view of Theorem
1.1
, the set of solutions of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}
for
\lambda<\lambda_{+\wedge*}
is a
C^{1}
curve
\lambda\mapsto(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
which we denote by
{\mathcal{G}}_{+\wedge*}

\frac{d\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}<0
along
{\mathcal{G}}_{+\wedge*}
.
Remark that solutions are unique for
\lambda<\lambda_{+\wedge*}
, so necessarily
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\searrow 0^{+}
as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{+}
.
As solutions are positive for
\lambda<\lambda_{+}
, we have

which implies that the map

defined in (
49
) is jointly real analytic as a function of
(\lambda,\alpha,\psi)
, as far as
\lambda<\lambda_{+}
.
As a consequence, we can use the analytic implicit function theorem (see e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 4.5.4]

3.4
to deduce that in fact
\lambda\mapsto(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
is real analytic.

The variables
v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})}=\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
and
\mu=\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\lambda\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{p-1}
are also real analytic, and
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\to 0^{+}
as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{+}
since
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\searrow 0^{+}
as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{+}
.
In particular
v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})}
solves
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{Q}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\mu}}}
for
\mu=\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
and it is readily seen that

takes the form

and is real analytic as well.
However because of Lemma
2.1
we see that

for some

\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{+}
.

We are just left to prove that

as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{+}
.
Since
v^{({\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})}=\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}
and
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\to 0^{+}
as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{+}
, it is enough to prove that
\liminf_{\lambda\to\lambda_{+}^{-}}\|\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\|_{\infty}>0

If not there exist sequences

and
\psi_{n}

.
Again by Lemma

and standard elliptic estimates, possibly passing to a subsequence,

converges in

to a solution

of

(89)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\psi={\lambda_{+}^{p}}\psi^{p}\quad\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega\\
\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\limits_{\Omega}{\displaystyle\lambda_{+}^{p}\psi^{p}}=1\\

which is impossible since we would find that

.
∎

Although not needed in the proof of Theorem

, for later references we state the following formula of independent interest.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
N\geq 2
,
p\in[1,p_{N})
and assume that
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
is differentiable at some

.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}+2E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=-\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\lambda\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

Proof.

Using the notation from Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
, we have that,

(91)

\displaystyle E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}[\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{+}^{p}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}.

Recall that

satisfies (

(92)

\displaystyle\frac{dE_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}=

\displaystyle-\frac{E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\lambda}+\frac{p}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

(93)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\lambda}+\frac{p}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{\lambda}+\frac{p}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left<w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+2\lambda E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=

\displaystyle-\left<w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+2E_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+2\lambda\frac{dE_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({p+1})\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}=({p+1})\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+u_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})[w_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}

is smooth in
B_{1}(0)
, we see that
\mathscr{I}(R)
is smooth for
R>R_{2}
.

Corollary 5.2
.

The function
R=\mathscr{I}^{-1}(I)
is
C^{2}
.

Proof.

It suffices to note that
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathscr{I}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R}
never vanishes, hence
\mathscr{I}^{-1}
is as regular as
\mathscr{I}
does, whence it is of class
C^{2}
.
∎

5.2.
The higher dimensional case
N\geq 3

The same argument works for general dimensions
N\geq 3
, but for form of the harmonic part which has to be correspondingly modified. We state it here for the sake of completeness.

Recall that the volume of the unit ball
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is
|B_{1}|=\omega_{N}
and the volume of the unit sphere is
|\partial B_{1}|=N\omega_{N}
.
The value of
R_{N}>0
is fixed so that
|B_{R_{N}}|=\omega_{N}R_{N}^{N}=1
.

The unique solution
{\rm v}
, which has to be radial and radially decreasing, takes the form

•

for
\gamma\geq 0
:

(120)

\displaystyle{\rm v}(x)=\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}}}u_{0}(\frac{x}{R})

for some
R\in[R_{N},+\infty)
;

•

for
\gamma<0
:

(121)

\displaystyle{\rm v}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}}}u_{0}(\frac{x}{R}),&0\leq|x|\leq R,\\
A\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{N-2}}-\frac{1}{R^{N-2}}\right)+B,&R\leq|x|\leq R_{N},\end{cases}

for some
R\in(0,R_{N})
and
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
.
The
C^{2}
regularity of
{\rm v}
requires that,

(122)

\displaystyle A=\frac{-u_{0}^{\prime}(1)}{N-2}\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N+2}}>0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad B=0.

Again the parameter
R>0
will be determined uniquely by the integral constraints as follows,

(123)

\displaystyle R\mapsto\mathscr{I}(R)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N+2}}\int_{B_{1}}u_{0}(y)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y},&\mbox{ if }R<R_{N},\\
\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N+2}}\int_{B_{\frac{R_{N}}{R}}}u_{0}(y)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y},&\mbox{ if }R\geq R_{N}.\end{cases}

Lemma 5.3
.

The function
\mathscr{I}(R)
is
C^{2}
and monotonic strictly decreasing, with the inverse function
\mathscr{I}^{-1}
also of class
C^{2}
.

Proof.

Note that

(124)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{p-1}-N+2=\frac{N-2}{p-1}\left(p_{{}_{N}}-p\right)>0,\quad\mbox{ since }p<p_{{}_{N}},

whence
\mathscr{I}(R)
is monotonic decreasing.
Also the function
\mathscr{I}
is readily seen to be smooth in
(0,R_{N})
and in
(R_{N},+\infty)
and
we are just left to verify that it is twice differentiable at
R_{N}
.
This can be done as follows.
First consider the derivative in
(R_{N},+\infty)
:

(125)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathscr{I}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R}=

\displaystyle-\frac{N-2}{p-1}(p_{{}_{N}}-p)\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N+3}}\int_{B_{\frac{R_{N}}{R}}}u_{0}(y)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}+\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N+2}}\int_{\partial B_{\frac{R_{N}}{R}}}u_{0}(y)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s}\cdot\left(-\frac{R_{N}}{R^{2}}\right)

(126)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{N-2}{p-1}(p_{{}_{N}}-p)\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N+3}}\int_{B_{\frac{R_{N}}{R}}}u_{0}(y)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}-N\omega_{N}\frac{R_{N}^{N}}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}+3}}u_{0}(\frac{R_{N}}{R})^{p},

which is strictly negative, and observe that at
R=R_{N}
the second term vanishes in view of
u_{0}(1)=0
.
We thus see that

(127)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R}\Big|_{R=R_{N}+0}\mathscr{I}(R)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R}\Big|_{R=R_{N}-0}\mathscr{I}(R)

and hence
\mathscr{I}(R)
is a
C^{1}
function in
(0,+\infty)
.
In particular, the derivative never vanishes, so its inverse function
R_{F}(I)
is also
C^{1}
.

Then consider the second derivative of
\mathscr{I}(R)
in
(R_{N},+\infty)
:

(128)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathscr{I}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R^{2}}=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{N-2}{p-1}(p_{{}_{N}}-p)\right]\left[\frac{N-2}{p-1}(p_{{}_{N}}-p)+1\right]\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N+4}}\int_{B_{\frac{R_{N}}{R}}}u_{0}(y)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

(129)

\displaystyle+\frac{N-2}{p-1}(p_{{}_{N}}-p)N\omega_{N}\frac{R_{N}^{N}}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}+4}}u_{0}(\frac{R_{N}}{R})^{p}

(130)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{2}{p-1}+3\right)N\omega_{N}\frac{R_{N}^{N}}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}+4}}u_{0}(\frac{R_{N}}{R})^{p}

(131)

\displaystyle+pN\omega_{N}\frac{{R_{N}}^{N+1}}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}+5}}u_{0}(\frac{R_{N}}{R})^{p-1}u_{0}^{\prime}(\frac{R_{N}}{R})

(132)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{N-2}{p-1}(p_{{}_{N}}-p)\right]\left[\frac{N-2}{p-1}(p_{{}_{N}}-p)+1\right]\frac{1}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N+4}}\int_{B_{\frac{R_{N}}{R}}}u_{0}(y)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

(133)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{4}{p-1}-N+5\right)N\omega_{N}\frac{R_{N}^{N}}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}+4}}u_{0}(\frac{R_{N}}{R})^{p}+pN\omega_{N}\frac{{R_{N}}^{N+1}}{R^{\frac{2}{p-1}+5}}u_{0}(\frac{R_{N}}{R})^{p-1}u_{0}^{\prime}(\frac{R_{N}}{R})

We see that
\mathscr{I}(R)
is
C^{2}
for
R>R_{N}
.
Moreover, since
p>1
and
u_{0}(1)=0
, we have

(134)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R^{2}}\Big|_{R=R_{N}+0}\mathscr{I}(R)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R^{2}}\Big|_{R=R_{N}-0}\mathscr{I}(R)

whence
\mathscr{I}\in C^{2}(0,+\infty)
.
Consequently the inverse function
\mathscr{I}^{-1}(I)
is also
C^{2}
in
(0,+\infty)
.
∎

6.
The proof of Theorem
1.4

\lambda_{*}(\mathbb{D}_{N},p)\geq\lambda_{+}(\mathbb{D}_{N},p)

Recall that Section

is non-degenerate, namely

In this section we need to study the case that some of the eigenvalues of

is zero.
Motivated by the analysis in

For later purposes we introduce the weighted product,

(157)

\displaystyle\left<\phi,\psi\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\coloneqq\frac{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\phi\psi}{\int_{\Omega}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}},\qquad\forall\phi,\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Note that

The main ingredient is the following bending lemma of Crandall-Rabinowitz type,

Proposition 6.1

(
[
7
, Prop. 2.7.]
)
.

Let
(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})
be a positive solution of
{\textbf{(}\mathbf{P}\textbf{)}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}}

and

\sigma_{k}(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})=0

\displaystyle(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathcal{U},\qquad s\mapsto(\lambda(s),\alpha(s),\psi(s)),

(\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})

\displaystyle\left<[\phi_{k}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda(s)},\xi(s)\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda(s)}=0,\quad s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)

(160)

\displaystyle\xi^{{}^{\prime}}(0)

\displaystyle\equiv 0,

(161)

\displaystyle\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}(0)

\displaystyle=-\lambda<\phi_{k}>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

\displaystyle\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}(0)

\displaystyle=0,

(163)

\displaystyle\psi^{{}^{\prime}}(0)

•

either
\lambda(s)=\lambda
is constant in

or

\displaystyle\left<[\phi_{k}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\neq 0\mbox{ and }\left<[\phi_{k}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\mbox{ has the same sign as }\left<\phi_{k}\right>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},

(165)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sigma_{k}(s)}{\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}(s)}=\dfrac{p<[\phi_{k}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda},\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\mbox{\rm o}(1)}{<[\phi_{k}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{2}>_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}+\mbox{\rm o}(1)},\mbox{ as }s\to 0.

The assumption that the zero eigenvalue is simple and the corresponding eigenfunction has nonzero weighted average seems nontrivial.
But on the ball it is automatically guaranteed, by the following

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\Omega=B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

(ii)

(i) Argue by contradiction and assume that

\phi_{k}

(166)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta\phi_{k}=\tau_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\mbox{\Large\textrho}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\lambda})^{\frac{1}{q}}\phi_{k},&\mbox{ in }B_{R}(0),\\
