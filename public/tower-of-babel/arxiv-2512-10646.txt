Eigenfunctionals for positive operators
Nicolas Monod

Abstract
. We establish an eigenfunctional theorem for positive operators, evocative of the Krein–Rutman theorem. A more general version gives a joint eigenfunctional for commuting operators.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 47B65; Secondary 47A75, 43A07

1.
Introduction

The following “eigenfunctional theorem” is an abstract algebraic counterpart to the fundamental Krein–Rutman eigenvector theorem
[
KR48
]
, itself an infinite-dimensional version of the Perron–Frobenius principle
[
Per07
,
Fro12
]
.

Theorem A
.

Let
V
be a non-singular ordered vector space and
T\colon V\to V
any positive linear map.

For every
v\gneqq 0
with order-bounded orbit
\langle T\rangle v
there is a positive linear functional
J
on the orbit ideal
V_{\langle T\rangle v}
and a scalar
t\geq 0
such that

J(Tu)=t\,J(u)\kern 8.53581pt(\forall\,u\in V_{\langle T\rangle v})\kern 8.53581pt\text{and}\kern 8.53581ptJ(v)=1.

Moreover, we can choose
J
such that
t\leq 1
.

A first motivation for
A
is that it enters the proof of a new characterization of amenable groups that we establish in
[
Mon25
]
. That criterion characterizes amenability not by invariance, but by stationarity. Specifically, applying
A
to a Markov operator, it is shown that a group
G
is amenable if and only if
\ell^{\infty}(G)
carries a stationary “conditional mean”; see
[
Mon25
]
for this concept.

In the above theorem,
V
is
non-singular
if for every
v\gneqq 0
in
V
there exists some positive linear functional which is non-zero on
v
. This holds in most familiar examples; for instance, it holds as soon as
V
admits a Hausdorff locally convex topology compatible with the order
[
Sch99
, V.4.1]
. We emphasize however that
A
makes no topological assumption; this is a notable difference with the Krein–Rutman theorem and its generalizations, which consider
compact
continuous linear operators on topological vector spaces.

As for the
orbit ideal
, it refers to the smallest vector subspace containing the orbit
\langle T\rangle v=\{T^{n}v:n\in\mathbf{N}\}
and closed under order intervals. The orbit is said
order-bounded
if it admits some upper bound in
V
.

Remark 1
.

The mild assumptions of
A
cannot be dropped. First, without the non-singularity assumption there might not exist any non-zero positive functional
J
at all. Secondly,
J
cannot in general be defined on all of
V
; consider e.g.
v=\delta_{0}
for the right shift
T
in
V=\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{Z})
. Lastly, regarding the boundedness of the orbit, one could guess that it is assumed to ensure
t\leq 1
, but the situation is more interesting than that:

(i) Even if arbitrary eigenvalues
t
are allowed, the order-boundedness cannot be dropped completely. See
2
below.

(ii) Assuming order-bounded orbits, the additional statement
t\leq 1
is still not automatic. Consider again the right shift
T
on
V=\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{Z})
with
v=\delta_{0}
. The orbit ideal
V_{\langle T\rangle v}
consist of the finitely supported functions on
\mathbf{N}
. Now every
t\geq 0
admits a functional
J
as above, namely
J(u)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\,u(n)
, which is a finite sum.

A
can also be viewed as a non-compact version of the Markov–Kakutani theorem
[
Mar36
,
Kak38
]
. Just like for Markov–Kakutani, we can more generally consider a commuting family of operators instead of a single one. We then obtain a joint eigenfunctional:

Theorem B
.

Let
V
be a non-singular ordered vector space and let
S
be a commutative semigroup of positive linear maps
V\to V
.

For every
v\gneqq 0
with order-bounded
S
-orbit there is a positive linear functional
J
on the orbit ideal
V_{Sv}
and a semigroup homomorphism
t\colon S\to[0,1]
such that

J(Tu)=t(T)\,J(u)\kern 8.53581pt(\forall\,T\in S\ \forall\,u\in V_{Sv})\kern 8.53581pt\text{and}\kern 8.53581ptJ(v)=1.

There are however fundamental differences between our setting of positive operators and the convex compact setting of Markov–Kakutani (with linear or affine maps). For fixed points in convex compact spaces, it is straightforward to go from commutativity to, for instance, solvable groups. In fact, a group or semigroup satisfies the fixed-point property for convex compact spaces
if and only if
it is amenable. This fails dramatically for positive operators, where already metabelian groups can fail to admit eigenfunctionals; see
[
Mon25
]
and
[
Mon17
]
for an extensive discussion.

We do not even know whether the existence of eigenfunctionals is preserved under products of two groups or semigroups. We conjecture it is not.

These stark differences are due to the fact that Theorems
A
and
B
rely on a combination of positivity and compactness techniques, as opposed to only compactness. This is analogous — and in fact related — to the differences between Martin and Poisson boundaries in the theory of Markov processes.

Finally, we state a technical variant of
B
. It is more cumbersome, but slightly more general and it turns out to have applications to the study of locally compact groups (which we plan to present in a forthcoming paper).

Theorem C
.

Let
S
be a commutative semigroup of positive linear maps
V\to V
in an ordered vector space
V
and pick
v\in V^{+}
.

Suppose that there is a positive linear functional on the orbit ideal
V_{Sv}
that is bounded on the
S
-orbit
Sv
and non-zero on
v
.

Then there is a positive linear functional
J
on
V_{Sv}
and a semigroup homomorphism
t\colon S\to[0,1]
such that

J(Tu)=t(T)\,J(u)\kern 8.53581pt(\forall\,T\in S\ \forall\,u\in V_{Sv})\kern 8.53581pt\text{and}\kern 8.53581ptJ(v)=1.

The assumptions of
C
are satisfied in the setting of
B
. Indeed suppose that the orbit
Sv
of
v\gneqq 0
is order-bounded by some
w\in V
and that
V
is non-singular. Then there is a positive linear functional
\zeta
on
V
with
\zeta(v)\neq 0
. Consider the restriction of
\zeta
to
V_{Sv}
; this is bounded by
\zeta(w)
on the orbit
Sv
.

To conclude this introduction, we illustrate that we cannot drop completely from
A
the assumption that the orbit is order-bounded, even if we allow arbitrary eigenvalues and keep all other assumptions.

Example 2
.

Let
V
be the ordered vector space of all finitely supported functions on
\mathbf{N}
; it is non-singular and its dual can be identified with the space
V^{*}=\mathbf{R}^{\mathbf{N}}
of all functions. Consider the positive linear operator
T\colon V\to V
defined by
T(\delta_{n})=\delta_{0}+(n+1)\delta_{n+1}
on the standard basis and take
v=\delta_{0}
. Then the orbit ideal
V_{\langle T\rangle v}
is all of
V
. We claim that regardless of the value
t\in\mathbf{R}
, possibly greater than one, there is no positive linear functional
J
on
V
with
J(v)=1
and such that
J(Tu)=tJ(u)
holds for all
u\in V
.

Suppose otherwise and consider the non-negative sequence defined by
j_{n}=J(\delta_{n})
. Then
j_{0}=1
and we have the recurrence relation
j_{n}=(tj_{n-1}-1)/n
for all
n\geq 1
. This shows that we have

j_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\left(t^{n}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k-1)!\,t^{n-k}\right)\kern 8.53581pt(\forall\,n\geq 1).

Since each
j_{n}
is non-negative, this implies in particular
t^{n}-(n-1)!\geq 0
for all
n
. This cannot hold for any
t\in\mathbf{R}
.

In view of the formulation of
C
, it must in fact be the case that no positive linear functional
\zeta
on
V
with
\zeta(\delta_{0})\neq 0
is bounded on the orbit
\{T^{n}(\delta_{0}):n\in\mathbf{N}\}
. This can also be verified directly by showing inductively that the value at
0
of
T^{n}(\delta_{0})
satisfies

\forall n\geq 0:\kern 8.53581pt\big(T^{n+1}(\delta_{0})\big)(0)=\sum_{p=0}^{n}(n-p)!\,\big(T^{p}(\delta_{0})\big)(0),

which defines the super-exponential sequence
1,1,2,5,15,54,235,1237,7790,\ldots
(the OEIS sequence A051295); we spare the details. It follows that

\zeta\big(T^{n}(\delta_{0})\big)\geq\big(T^{n}(\delta_{0})\big)(0)\cdot\zeta(\delta_{0})

is unbounded, indeed super-exponential. (The latter also gives another proof that no eigenfunctional
J
exists.)

2.
Proof of the theorems

Obwohl dies ein rein algebraischer Satz ist, so ist mir doch sein Beweis mit den gewöhnlichen Hilfsmitteln der Algebra nicht gelungen.

Oskar Perron
[
Per07
]
, p. 262.

[Although this is a purely algebraic theorem, I have not succeeded in proving it with the usual tools of algebra.]

We have no topology on the abstract ordered vector space
V
and thus no topological assumptions on our positive linear maps
V\to V
. However, any (algebraic) dual
V^{*}
can be endowed with the weak-* topology, whose uniform structure is complete. This is because
V^{*}
is isomorphic, as topological vector space, to the product of copies of
\mathbf{R}
indexed by a basis of
V
, see e.g.
[
Bou81
, II § 6 No. 6–7]
.

We will also consider this for the ideal
V_{A}
instead of
V
, where
A=\langle T\rangle v
and more generally
A=Sv
. We recall the straightforward verification that for any set
A\subseteq V^{+}
the ideal
V_{A}
can be written explicitly as

(
*
)

V_{A}=\big\{u\in V:\exists a_{1},\ldots,a_{q}\in A\text{ with }\pm u\leq a_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}\big\}.

It follows that the order of
V
restricted to
V_{A}
is
generating
, i.e.
V_{A}=\Span(V_{A}^{+})
. In particular, the dual positive cone
(V_{A}^{*})^{+}
in the algebraic dual
V_{A}^{*}
is
proper
, which means
-(V_{A}^{*})^{+}\cap(V_{A}^{*})^{+}=\{0\}
.

The proof of the eigenfunctional theorems will involve extremal rays. Recall that even very familiar cones may fail to contain any extremal ray: for instance, the positive cone in the Hilbert space
L^{2}[0,1]
, which is weakly complete and self-dual, has no extremal ray. Choquet’s fundamental insight is that a cone
C
in a locally convex topological vector space will be the closed convex hull of its extremal rays as soon as the following holds:

for every
\lambda\in C
there is a compact convex set
B\subseteq C
containing
\lambda
and such that
C\smallsetminus B
is convex too.

Such a set
B
is what Choquet calls a
chapeau
(a hat, or cap). Indeed, it then suffices to apply the Krein–Milman theorem to every chapeau. We refer to
[
Cho63
]
for an overview and to
[
Cho69
]
or
[
Bou81
, II § 7]
for the full monty.

When the above condition holds, the cone is called
well-capped
(bien coiffé). It is apparent on the definition that this property is inherited by closed sub-cones.

We shall need the following (rather standard) fact, in which the countability assumption is essential.

Lemma 3
.

Let
V
be an ordered vector space and
A\subseteq V^{+}
a countable set. Let
C=(V_{A}^{*})^{+}
be the cone of positive linear functionals in the algebraic dual
V_{A}^{*}
of the ideal generated by
A
, endowed with the weak-* topology.

Then
C
is well-capped.

Without countability, a counter-example is
V=L^{2}[0,1]
with
A=V^{+}
. In that case,
V_{A}=V
and
C=(V_{A}^{*})^{+}\cong V^{+}
(e.g. by Klee’s theorem
[
Kle55
, Thm. B]
). Thus
C
has no extremal rays and hence is not well-capped for any topology.

Proof of
3
.

We fix
\lambda\in C
, which we can assume non-zero. We also choose an enumeration
A=\{v_{n}:n\in\mathbf{N}\}
. (If
A
is finite there will be repetitions, but that case is trivial anyways, since then
C
admits a compact base). We claim that there is
n
with
\lambda(v_{n})\neq 0
. Indeed, choose
u\in V_{A}
with
\lambda(u)\neq 0
. There are indices
n_{1},\ldots,n_{q}
with
\pm u\leq v_{n_{1}}+\cdots+v_{n_{q}}
; the claim follows by positivity of
\lambda
.

This implies that we can choose a sequence of scalars
t_{n}>0
such that
\sum_{n\geq 0}t_{n}\lambda(v_{n})=1
. We define a map

s\colon C\longrightarrow[0,+\infty],\kern 8.53581pts(\xi)=\sum_{n\geq 0}t_{n}\xi(v_{n}).

This map is additive and positively homogeneous on
C
, where this is suitably understood for
[0,+\infty]
-valued maps. We now consider the sub-level set
B=\{\xi\in C:s(\xi)\leq 1\}
. The only millinery property of
B
that is not clear from this definition is compactness.

Since
B
is contained in the complete space
C
, it suffices to justify that it is closed and bounded, both with respect to the weak-* topology
[
Cho69
, 23.11]
. We recall here that a set in
bounded
in the sense of topological vector spaces if it is absorbed by any sufficiently large rescaling of any neighbourhood of
0
. Every partial sum
\xi\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{N}t_{n}\xi(v_{n})
is weak-* continuous, and therefore the map
s
is lower semi-continuous; this shows that
B
is closed.

For boundedness, after unwinding the definitions, it suffices to show that for every
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in V_{A}
there is
r>0
such that

B\subseteq\big\{\xi\in C:|\xi(u_{j})|<r\ \forall\,j\big\}.

We thus fix some
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in V_{A}
. Using
(*)
for each
u_{j}
and grouping all sums together, we obtain indices
n_{1},\ldots,n_{q}
with

\forall\,j:\kern 8.53581pt\pm u_{j}\leq v_{n_{1}}+\cdots+v_{n_{q}}.

Given
i=1,\ldots,q
we have

\xi(v_{n_{i}})=\frac{1}{t_{n_{i}}}t_{n_{i}}\xi(v_{n_{i}})<\frac{1}{t_{n_{i}}}s(\xi)\leq\frac{1}{t_{n_{i}}},

which implies

|\xi(u_{j})|\leq\xi(v_{n_{1}})+\cdots+\xi(v_{n_{q}})<\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{t_{n_{i}}}.

This yields the desired
r
and concludes the proof.
∎

We now prove the theorems stated in the introduction. We have already recorded that
C
implies
B
which in turn contains
A
.

Since
3
fails without the countability assumption, it is natural to prove
C
first for countable semigroups
S
. This already contains the case of
A
.

Proof of
C
for
S
countable.

We recall the setting. We are given a countable commutative semigroup
S
of positive linear maps
T\colon V\to V
on an ordered vector space
V
, an element
v\geq 0
and a positive linear functional
\zeta
on the orbit ideal
V_{Sv}
such that
\zeta
is bounded on
Sv
and
\zeta(v)\neq 0
. In particular,
v\gneqq 0
. To uniformize notation, we suppose that
S
contains the identity, adding it to
S
if needed.

Each
T
restricts to a positive linear map
V_{Sv}\to V_{Sv}
on the ideal
V_{Sv}
; we still denote it by
T
. We endow the algebraic dual
V_{Sv}^{*}
of
V_{Sv}
with the weak-* topology, which is complete as mentioned earlier. It follows that the positive cone
C=(V_{Sv}^{*})^{+}
is also complete and hence weakly complete since the weak-* topology is a weak topology.

Consider the semigroup
S^{*}
of all dual maps
T^{*}\colon V_{Sv}^{*}\to V_{Sv}^{*}
as
T
ranges over
S
. That is,
(T^{*}\xi)(u)=\xi(Tu)
for
u\in V_{Sv}
and
\xi\in V_{Sv}^{*}
. Algebraically, this is the opposite semigroup to (the restriction to
V_{Sv}
of )
S
and in particular it is still a commutative semigroup with identity. Each
T^{*}
is a positive linear map and
S^{*}
leaves
C
invariant, i.e.
T^{*}(C)\subseteq C
for all
T\in S
.

We claim that
C
contains a closed
S^{*}
-invariant subcone
C_{1}\subseteq C
which is minimal among all closed
S^{*}
-invariant subcones satisfying the following property:

(\dagger)
there is
\xi\in C_{1}
with
\xi(v)=1
and with
\xi|_{Sv}\leq 2
.

We first show that any closed
S^{*}
-invariant subcone
C_{0}\subseteq C
will satisfy
(\dagger)
as soon as it satisfies the formally weaker property

(\ddagger)
there is
\xi\in C_{0}
with
\xi(v)=1
and which is bounded on
Sv
.

Indeed,
(\ddagger)
allows us to define
s=\sup_{T\in S}\xi(Tv)\geq 1
and to choose
T_{0}\in S
with
\xi(T_{0}v)\geq s/2
. Next, we observe

\sup_{T\in S}(T_{0}^{*}\xi)(Tv)=\sup_{T\in S}\xi(T_{0}Tv)\leq\sup_{T\in S}\xi(Tv)=s.

Therefore, since
C_{0}
is
S^{*}
-invariant, we can replace
\xi
by

\frac{1}{(T_{0}^{*}\xi)(v)}T_{0}^{*}\xi=\frac{1}{\xi(T_{0}v)}T_{0}^{*}\xi

which satisfies
(\dagger)
.

Next, we record that
C
itself enjoys
(\ddagger)
by assumption (upon rescaling
\zeta
) and hence also
(\dagger)
.

Now, to establish the claim, it suffices by Zorn’s lemma to show that for any chain
\mathscr{C}
of closed
S^{*}
-invariant subcones
K\subseteq C
containing an element
\xi_{K}
as in
(\dagger)
, the intersection
\bigcap_{K\in\mathscr{C}}K
still contains such an element.

For every
u\in V_{Sv}
, we can by
(*)
fix
q\in\mathbf{N}
and
T_{1},\ldots,T_{q}\in S
such that
\pm u\leq T_{1}v+\cdots+T_{q}v
. This gives for any
K\in\mathscr{C}
the bound

|\xi_{K}(u)|\leq\xi_{K}(T_{1}v)+\cdots+\xi_{K}(T_{q}v)\leq 2q,

which is uniform over
K\in\mathscr{C}
. This implies, by the criterion given in
[
Cho69
, 23.11]
, that the net
(\xi_{K})_{K\in\mathscr{C}}
is weak-* relatively compact in
C
, recalling that
C
is weak-* complete. We can therefore choose an accumulation point
\xi\in C
of this net.

We have
\xi\in\bigcap_{K\in\mathscr{C}}K
since each
K
is closed. Moreover, the properties
\xi_{K}(v)=1
and
\xi_{K}(Tv)\leq 2
(for any
T\in S
) pass to weak-* limits. This completes the proof of the claim.

Let thus
C_{1}
be minimal as claimed and choose some
T\in S
. The set

C_{T}=(\mathrm{Id}+T^{*})C_{1}=\{\eta+T^{*}\eta:\eta\in C_{1}\}

is a sub-cone of
C_{1}
. It is
S^{*}
-invariant because
S^{*}
is commutative; indeed, for any
T^{\prime}\in S
we have

T^{\prime*}C_{T}=(T^{\prime*}+T^{\prime*}T^{*})C_{1}=(\mathrm{Id}+T^{*})(T^{\prime*}C_{1})\subseteq(\mathrm{Id}+T^{*})C_{1}=C_{T}.

Let
\xi\in C_{1}
be a witness for
(\dagger)
. Then
\xi+T^{*}\xi\in C_{T}
is bounded on
Sv
and non-zero on
v
; therefore, after rescaling, it witnesses that
C_{T}
satisfies
(\ddagger)
and hence also
(\dagger)
.

We claim that
C_{T}
is closed. Indeed, if any net
(\eta_{\alpha}+T^{*}\eta_{\alpha})
(where
\alpha
ranges over some directed index set) with
\eta_{\alpha}\in C_{1}
converges in
C
, then so does the net
(\eta_{\alpha})
upon passing to a subnet because the sum map
C_{1}\times C_{1}\to C_{1}
is proper, see Cor. 1 in
[
Bou81
, II § 6 No. 8]
together with Thm. 1(c) in
[
Bou71
, I § 10 No. 2]
. This implies that the limit of
(\eta_{\alpha}+T^{*}\eta_{\alpha})
belongs to
C_{T}
.

In conclusion, the minimality of
C_{1}
shows that we have
C_{T}=C_{1}
, where
T\in S
is arbitrary.

By
3
, the cone
C
is well-capped and therefore so is
C_{1}
. It follows that
C_{1}
is the closed convex hull of its extremal rays
[
Bou81
, II § 7 No. 2 Cor. 2]
. In particular, extremal rays exist. Consider any
0\neq J\in C_{1}
such that the ray
\mathbf{R}^{+}J
is extremal. For each
T\in S
, the equality
C_{T}=C_{1}
shows that there is
\eta\in C_{1}
with
J=\eta+T^{*}\eta
. By extremality, both summands are on the ray
\mathbf{R}^{+}J
and thus we have
a,b\geq 0
with
\eta=aJ
and
T^{*}\eta=bJ
. The relation
J=\eta+T^{*}\eta
implies that
a\neq 0
and that
b=1-a
. It follows
T^{*}J=\frac{1-a}{a}J
and we set
t_{J}(T):=(1-a)/a\geq 0
.

Thus, to each
J
spanning an extremal ray of
C_{1}
corresponds a map
t_{J}\colon S\to\mathbf{R}_{\geq 0}
with

\forall\,T\in S\ \forall\,u\in V_{Sv}:\kern 8.53581ptJ(Tu)=t_{J}(T)\,J(u).

We have
J(v)\neq 0
since otherwise the above property would force
J
to vanish, being a positive functional on the ideal
V_{Sv}
. After rescaling, we assume
J(v)=1
. Next, note that
t_{J}
is a semigroup homomorphism to the multiplicative semigroup
\mathbf{R}_{\geq 0}
; indeed,

t_{J}(T_{1})t_{J}(T_{2})=t_{J}(T_{1})t_{J}(T_{2})\,J(v)=t_{J}(T_{1})\,J(T_{2}v)=J(T_{1}T_{2}v)\\
=t_{J}(T_{1}T_{2})\,J(v)=t_{J}(T_{1}T_{2}).

It remains to show that we can choose
J
such that
t=t_{J}\leq 1
; as we noted in
1
, this is not the case for all eigenfunctionals.

To that end, we enumerate
S=\{T_{i}:i\in\mathbf{N}\}
and our final claim is that for every
n\in\mathbf{N}
there exists
J_{n}
as above and such that
t_{J_{n}}(T_{i})\leq 2
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. Supposing this fails for some
n
, we pick
p\in\mathbf{N}
such that
2^{p}\geq 2n+1
. Then for each
J
as above there is
i\leq n
with
t_{J}(T_{i}^{p})\geq 2n+1
. Since
t_{J}\geq 0
, this implies

J\big(T_{1}^{p}v+\cdots+T_{n}^{p}v\big)\geq(2n+1)\,J(v)

and thus every
J\in C_{1}
on any extremal ray of
C_{1}
belongs to the closed convex set

\Big\{\zeta\in C_{1}:\zeta\big(T_{1}^{p}v+\cdots+T_{n}^{p}v\big)\geq(2n+1)\,\zeta(v)\Big\}.

On the other hand,
\xi
does not belong to that set since
\xi(T_{j}^{p}v)\leq 2\xi(v)=2
for all
j
. Let
B
be a chapeau of
C_{1}
containing
\xi
and recall that every extremal point of
B
lies on an extremal ray of
C_{1}
(this is the motivating definition of a chapeau
[
Cho62
, Lem. 2]
). This contradicts the Krein–Milman theorem, confirming the claim.

From the final claim, we can deduce that there is
J
as above but with
t_{J}(T)\leq 2
for all
T\in S
, obtained as any accumulation point of the sequence
(J_{n})
. Indeed, by the same compactness principle as for the initial claim, it suffices to justify that for every given
u\in V_{Sv}
the values
J_{n}(u)
remain bounded. We write again
\pm u\leq T_{n_{1}}v+\cdots T_{n_{q}}v
and deduce that
|J_{n}(u)|\leq 2q
holds for all
n
large enough, as desired.

Now,
t:=t_{J}
being a semigroup homomorphism,
t\leq 2
forces in fact
t(T)\leq 1
by considering
t(T^{n})
as
n\to\infty
. This concludes the proof of
C
in the countable case.
∎

It remains to reduce the general version of
C
to the case of countable semigroups. The most obvious reduction strategy, one that works in the setting of convex compact spaces, is to consider all countable sub-semigroups
S_{0}\subseteq S
and to extend functionals on
V_{S_{0}}v
to the larger space
V_{Sv}
in a coherent way. This is not always possible in the setting of positive linear maps; for instance, Hahn–Banach-like extension principles such as the Kantorovich theorem do not hold without further assumptions.

Nevertheless, a compactness argument will get around this difficulty, as follows.

Proof of
C
, general case.

There is again no loss of generality in assuming that
S
contains the identity. We denote by
\mathscr{S}
the collection of all countable sub-semigroups
S_{0}\subseteq S
containing the identity. Then
S
is the directed union (=colimit) of
\mathscr{S}
.

Given any sub-semigroup
S_{0}\subseteq S
containing the identity, we consider the following set
E(S_{0})\subseteq V_{S_{0}v}^{*}
of positive eigenfunctionals on
V_{S_{0}v}
:

E(S_{0})=\left\{J\in(V_{S_{0}v}^{*})^{+}:\begin{array}[]{l}J(v)=1\text{ and }\forall\,T\in S_{0},\ J(Tv)\leq 1\text{ and }\\
\forall\,T\in S_{0}\,\forall\,u\in V_{S_{0}v},\ J(Tu)=J(Tv)\,J(u)\end{array}\right\}.

The countable case of
C
states that this set is non-empty for all
S_{0}\in\mathscr{S}
since, in the notation of the theorem,
t(T)=J(Tv)
. We need to prove that
E(S)
is non-empty, too.

The set
E(S_{0})
is compact in the weak-* topology of
V_{S_{0}v}^{*}
because we can bound
|J(u)|
by
q
whenever
\pm u\leq T_{1}v+\cdots+T_{q}v
.

If
S_{1}
contains
S_{0}
, then
V_{S_{1}v}
contains
V_{S_{0}v}
and the restriction map
V_{S_{1}v}^{*}\to V_{S_{0}v}^{*}
maps
E(S_{1})
to
E(S_{0})
. This is a continuous map; the fact that it is a priori not surjective is the extension failure mentioned before the proof.

Nonetheless, we have obtained an inverse system of non-empty compact (Hausdorff) spaces
E(S_{0})
indexed by
S_{0}\in\mathscr{S}
, and therefore the inverse limit
E=\lim_{S_{0}\in\mathscr{S}}E(S_{0})
of that system is non-empty. There is by functoriality a unique map
E(S)\to E
compatible with all restriction maps, but so far
E(S)
is not known to be non-empty.

To complete the proof, we argue instead that there is also a well-defined map
E\to E(S)
from the non-empty space
E
. Since any element of
E
defines a compatible system of functionals on
V_{S_{0}v}
as
S_{0}\in\mathscr{S}
, it suffices to observe that
V_{Sv}
is the directed union of all
V_{S_{0}v}
. This hold because of the characterisation
(*)
of the orbit ideal
V_{Sv}
. (Functoriality now implies that
E(S)
and
E
actually coincide.)
∎

Remark 4
.

Some of the ideas in the proofs above are related to our earlier work on fixed points for group actions on cones
[
Mon17
]
. What makes the present situation very different is that a general positive map, typically non-invertible, will often not fix any non-zero point.

This difference is crucially important for the applications to amenability considered in
[
Mon25
]
, where the Markov operator will not fix a non-zero functional unless the group is amenable.
