HCA-Mamba: a hierarchical cross-attention framework combining Vision Mamba and CNN for night-time image quality assessment
Authors: Tianqi Zhang, Wu Dong, Likun Lu, Weipeng Niu, Rui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNight-time image quality assessment (NTIQA) requires jointly modeling global illumination consistency and spatially localized distortions, such as noise, glare, and halo artifacts. Existing CNN-based methods are effective at capturing local degradations but are limited in modeling long-range contextual dependencies, whereas Transformer-based approaches improve global perception at the cost of higher computational complexity. To address this challenge, we propose HCA-Mamba, a hybrid framework that combines Vision Mamba (ViM)-based global context modeling with CNN-based local distortion perception for NTIQA. Specifically, a ViM backbone is employed to capture long-range dependencies and global luminance structure, while a parallel CNN branch extracts multi-scale local degradation cues and compacts them into distortion-aware tokens through a multi-scale distortion extractor (MSDE). These tokens are then progressively injected into successive ViM layers via a local distortion injection module (LDIM), which performs gated cross-attention to enable stable interaction between global and local representations across network depths. The fused representation is finally mapped to a perceptual quality score by a regression head. Experiments on the NNID and EHNQ benchmarks, together with additional cross-dataset evaluation on NPHD, including both intra-dataset evaluation and cross-dataset generalization settings, demonstrate the effectiveness and generalization potential of the proposed method. A controlled synthetic distortion severity study further evaluates the model response to progressively intensified night-time degradations. Extensive ablation studies further verify the effectiveness of MSDE and LDIM in enhancing sensitivity to night-time distortions.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Capturing high-quality images under night-time conditions remains a significant challenge for both consumer electronics and professional vision systems1,2. Due to inherently limited illumination and complex imaging pipelines, night-time images often suffer from scene-dependent degradations such as color distortion, reduced visibility, low contrast, flare, and loss of fine details3,4. These artifacts severely compromise perceptual quality and hinder the performance of downstream vision tasks, including mobile photography, video surveillance, and autonomous driving, where preserving clear, detailed and visually faithful imagery is essential5,6.

Compared with daytime scenes captured under sufficient illumination, where distortions are generally easier to characterize and analyze, night-time images exhibit more complex and heterogeneous degradations arising from low-light imaging limitations, non-uniform illumination and sensor noise1,7,8. However, most general-purpose blind image quality assessment (BIQA) models are developed and trained on well-lit images, leading to substantial performance degradation when they are applied to night-time imagery or to results produced by recent low-light image enhancement methods4,9,10.

This performance gap has motivated increasing interest in night-time image quality assessment (NTIQA), which aims to design specialized models capable of accurately predicting the perceptual quality of low-light images and enhanced night-time results1,3,11–13.

In general, image quality assessment (IQA) methods can be categorized into full-reference (FR), reduced-reference (RR) and no-reference (NR) approaches according to the availability of reference images14,15. Among these, NR-IQA methods are the most applicable for real-world night-time scenarios, where ideal reference images are rarely available and authentic distortions are diverse1,14. Nevertheless, effectively modeling both local degradations (such as noise clusters, halos and uneven exposure) and global perceptual dependencies (such as overall luminance balance and semantic consistency) remains challenging for existing IQA frameworks. Convolutional neural network (CNN)-based methods are strong at capturing localized artifacts but struggle to model long-range context due to limited receptive fields16–20. Transformer-based models improve global context modeling via self-attention, yet their quadratic complexity and weak locality bias hinder sensitivity to subtle distortions and limit scalability on high-resolution, low-light images4,21–23. These limitations call for architectures that couple robust global modeling with fine-grained local distortion perception in a computationally efficient manner.

Recently, state-space based visual backbones, such as Vision Mamba (ViM), have emerged as promising alternatives to self-attention, achieving long-range context modeling with linear-time scaling through selective state updates and parallelized recurrence24,25. While ViM provides an efficient global prior, it offers limited inductive bias toward spatial locality, which is crucial for capturing night-specific artifacts and fine-grained distortions. This observation motivates a hybrid architecture that augments the ViM backbone with a dedicated pathway for local distortion modeling and global–local interaction.

To this end, we propose a hierarchical cross-attention Mamba (HCA-Mamba), a hybrid framework designed to synergize the efficient global modeling of ViM with the multi-scale local feature extraction of CNNs. As the core of our approach, HCA-Mamba exploits the complementary strengths of these two backbones: a ViM branch that models long-range global dependencies and illumination patterns with linear complexity, and a convolutional branch that focuses on fine-grained local distortions in dark or overexposed regions. Concretely, a ResNet50 backbone equipped with a multi-scale distortion extractor (MSDE) aggregates multi-stage CNN features into a compact set of distortion-aware tokens on a 7 × 7 grid. These tokens are injected into every ViM layer via a local distortion injection module (LDIM) that performs four-head cross-attention followed by per-layer down and up projections and learnable residual scaling, thereby enabling adaptive and stable global–local interaction throughout the network. A final multilayer regression head then maps the fused representation to the predicted image quality score. Extensive experiments on the natural night-time image database (NNID) benchmark1 demonstrate that HCA-Mamba achieves state-of-the-art performance, validating the effectiveness of synergizing Mamba-based global state-space modeling with convolutional local priors for perceptual quality assessment under challenging night-time conditions.

While prior hybrid approaches such as LoDa26 have explored the integration of CNN features into Transformer-based architectures, HCA-Mamba differs in both its global modeling backbone and its fusion mechanism. Instead of relying on quadratic self-attention, HCA-Mamba adopts a ViM backbone to model long-range illumination structure through selective state-space modeling, providing a more scalable pathway for capturing global context in night-time scenes. In addition, rather than using direct feature fusion, we introduce a progressive local distortion injection strategy through the LDIM. By combining gated cross-attention with residual modulation, LDIM allows multi-scale local distortion cues to interact adaptively with the global ViM representation across depths, so that fine-grained night-time artifacts can complement high-level pretrained priors in a more stable manner.

Related work

Night-time image quality assessment

NTIQA has attracted increasing attention because perceptual quality prediction under low-light conditions is substantially more challenging than in well-lit scenes. Night-time images usually contain authentic and scene-dependent degradations, including insufficient illumination, heavy sensor noise, low contrast, color distortion, glare, and detail loss, which jointly affect both local visibility and global perceptual consistency1,3,5,27. As a result, conventional blind image quality assessment (BIQA) models trained on daytime or synthetically distorted images often generalize poorly to real night-time photographs and enhanced night-time results1,3,8,28. These characteristics make NTIQA a distinct problem that requires dedicated datasets, task-specific feature modeling, and illumination-aware quality prediction mechanisms.

Early NTIQA studies mainly relied on handcrafted descriptors and statistical regression models. Xiang et al.1 established the NNID benchmark and proposed BNBT, which combines brightness and texture descriptors for blind night-time image quality prediction. Hu et al.5 designed a no-reference night-time BIQA metric by explicitly measuring fundamental image properties, such as brightness, saturation, sharpness, noiseness, contrast, and semantics. Xiao et al.6 further modeled night-time image quality using contrast, texture, and color cues in a perceptually motivated color space, while Song et al.11 assessed night-time image quality through the joint analysis of local and global features. Although these methods achieve reasonable performance on their target datasets, their feature expressiveness is limited and their generalization to more diverse real-world night scenes remains insufficient.

With the development of learning-based IQA, NTIQA research has gradually moved toward more data-driven designs. Li et al.29 proposed a dual-head CNN for realistic night-time images, and Jiang et al.30 introduced DDB-Net, which combines Retinex-based decomposition with bilinear pooling for night-time quality evaluation. Shi et al.13 later proposed a progressive bidirectional feature extraction and enhancement network for night-time image quality evaluation. For enhanced night-time imagery, Yang et al.8 released the EHNQ database and proposed BEHN for blind quality evaluation of enhanced night-time images. Han and Xie27 constructed the NPHD database and developed a region-selective IQA strategy for night-time photos, while Wang et al.28 introduced the RNTIEQA benchmark and a pair-wise ranking method for quality evaluation of enhanced night-time images. In addition, Zhang et al.31 proposed NLIEE for no-reference low-light image enhancement evaluation, and Li et al.32 proposed PCSNet to jointly predict and enhance night-time image quality through cross-sharing between IQA and enhancement branches. Besides deep models, recent NTIQA studies have also explored efficient descriptor-based solutions. Karimi and Nejati3,12 developed competitive lightweight frameworks based on sparse feature representations and histogram descriptors, respectively. Nevertheless, most existing NTIQA methods still emphasize either local handcrafted cues, limited-range convolutional features, or task-specific enhancement settings, and they insufficiently model global illumination distribution together with fine-grained local distortions in a unified and efficient framework.

Deep learning-based image quality assessment

Deep neural networks have become the dominant paradigm for no-reference image quality assessment because they can learn quality-aware representations directly from data. Early CNN-based methods established the patch-based learning paradigm for BIQA. Kang et al.16 predicted image quality from local patches in an end-to-end manner, and Bosse et al.33 extended this idea with a deeper architecture and learned patch-weighted pooling. Talebi and Milanfar34 further modeled the distribution of subjective scores through NIMA, while other works explored multi-branch or feature-fusion strategies to encode complementary quality cues17,18,35,36. With the release of large-scale in-the-wild datasets, CNN-based BIQA has been extended from synthetic distortions to authentic degradations in smartphone photography and other uncontrolled scenarios37–39. More recent studies improve robustness through semi-supervised learning, knowledge distillation, distorted-patch selection, and multi-scale feature fusion40–42.

To overcome the limited receptive field of CNNs, transformer-based and transformer-enhanced NR-IQA models have been introduced to strengthen global context modeling. MUSIQ23 leverages multi-scale transformer representations for image quality prediction, and MANIQA43 enhances NR-IQA by exploiting multi-dimension attention interactions. TReS44 adopts a hybrid CNN–Transformer design together with relative ranking and self-consistency constraints, demonstrating strong performance on standard IQA benchmarks. Subsequent works incorporate supervised contrastive learning, multi-task learning, or Swin-Transformer backbones to further improve generalization and quality representation learning20,45,46. In parallel, vision–language approaches such as CLIP-IQA47 use large-scale CLIP embeddings to evaluate the overall “look and feel” of images in a zero- or few-shot manner.

Since local distortions and global perceptual coherence are both essential for reliable IQA, hybrid architectures that combine convolutional inductive bias with global reasoning have drawn increasing interest. AHIQ19 introduces a ViT/CNN hybrid design for GAN-based IQA, where deformable convolution is used to better align shallow CNN features with visually salient regions. Tong et al.48 proposed a dual-stream hybrid model for BIQA, while Zeng and Kwong49 jointly combined CNN and Transformer components for full-reference and no-reference IQA. LoDa26 further injects local CNN distortion features into a frozen large pretrained transformer for efficient IQA adaptation. Overall, deep learning-based IQA has evolved toward hybrid designs that explicitly integrate local distortion perception with global perceptual reasoning. However, most such methods still rely on Transformer backbones and thus inherit the quadratic computational complexity of self-attention, which limits efficiency on high-resolution images. This limitation motivates exploring alternative global modeling backbones that preserve long-range context modeling while reducing computational overhead.

Multi-scale and hierarchical representation learning has also become an important direction in deep learning-based IQA, because image quality is affected by both local distortions and global perceptual structures. In addition to IQA studies that exploit multi-branch designs, feature fusion, or multi-scale representations17,18,23,35,36,42, related vision tasks have further demonstrated the effectiveness of multi-scale modeling for capturing degradations and contextual dependencies. For example, CGD-TraP50 introduces condition-guided diffusion with intention and interaction priors for multi-modal pedestrian trajectory prediction. MB-TaylorFormer V251 employs a multi-branch linear Transformer with multi-scale patch embedding for image restoration. ESTINet52 enhances video deraining through spatio-temporal interaction modeling, while DDMSNet53 adopts a deep dense multi-scale network with semantic and depth priors for snow removal. DBLRNet54 further exploits spatio-temporal feature learning for video deblurring. These studies suggest that multi-scale and hierarchical modeling provides useful cues for representing distortions with different spatial extents, which motivates the use of hierarchical CNN features for local distortion modeling in NTIQA.

State space models and Vision Mamba

State space models (SSMs) have recently emerged as an efficient alternative to attention-based architectures for long-sequence modeling55,56. Unlike self-attention, which explicitly computes pairwise token interactions with quadratic complexity57,58, SSMs model long-range dependencies through implicit recurrence governed by linear dynamical systems. The structured state space sequence model S455 demonstrated that carefully parameterized continuous-time state transitions can achieve strong sequence modeling ability while retaining linear computational and memory complexity55,59.

Building on this line, Gu and Dao proposed Mamba24, a selective state-space model equipped with input-dependent gating and selective scanning. By dynamically updating internal states according to the input and parallelizing recurrence through efficient kernel implementations, Mamba achieves linear-time sequence modeling with strong contextual awareness and good scalability.

For vision tasks, Vision Mamba (ViM) extends selective SSMs to 2D image modeling by treating images as patch sequences with positional embeddings and bidirectional state-space processing25. Compared with transformer backbones, ViM provides efficient global context modeling with subquadratic computation and near-linear memory in practice. It has shown strong transferability across visual tasks, including image classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation25. The state-space paradigm has also been extended to video understanding, where VideoMamba effectively models long-range spatiotemporal dependencies60. These results suggest that ViM-style backbones are promising general-purpose visual encoders for tasks requiring efficient global reasoning.

In addition to ViM, recent visual state-space backbones have further explored more spatially structured scanning mechanisms for vision tasks. For example, VMamba61 introduces a visual state-space backbone based on 2D selective scan operations to adapt Mamba-style sequence modeling to two-dimensional visual data. These developments suggest the growing relevance of Mamba-style backbones for efficient visual representation learning. Meanwhile, Mamba-based IQA has also begun to receive attention. Q-Mamba62 revisits Vision Mamba for image quality assessment and explores its use in task-specific, universal, and transferable IQA settings with a StylePrompt tuning paradigm. VISGA63 introduces a visual state-space model with graph-based feature aggregation for blind image quality assessment. Nevertheless, dedicated exploration of Mamba-style visual backbones for no-reference night-time IQA remains limited.

These observations suggest that Mamba-style backbones are promising for efficient global reasoning, but their use in NTIQA remains insufficiently explored. Since night-time IQA requires both global illumination understanding and local artifact sensitivity, we investigate a ViM–CNN hybrid design tailored to this task.

Unlike generic multi-scale feature fusion modules used for image restoration or dense prediction, MSDE is designed to construct compact quality-aware representations for no-reference night-time IQA. Experiments

Datasets and evaluation metrics

We conduct our experiments on three representative night-time IQA benchmarks, namely NNID1, EHNQ8 and NPHD27. NNID is the first large-scale benchmark specifically designed for authentic night-time photography. It contains 2,240 low-light images captured from 448 distinct scenes using three different devices: Nikon D5300, iPhone 8 Plus, and iPad Mini 2. For each scene, five images were acquired under different exposure settings to cover a broad range of perceptual quality. Each image is annotated with a normalized MOS in the range [0, 1].

In addition, we also employ the EHNQ dataset8, which is a dedicated benchmark for the quality assessment of enhanced night-time images. EHNQ consists of 1,500 enhanced images generated from 100 real night-time images using 15 enhancement algorithms, with each image associated with subjective quality annotations. Compared with NNID, which focuses on authentic night-time photographs, EHNQ provides a complementary benchmark for evaluating the generalization ability of IQA models on enhanced night-time images.

We further use the NPHD dataset27 as an external benchmark for cross-dataset generalization evaluation. NPHD is designed for blind quality assessment of real night-time photos. It contains 510 night-time photographs captured from 17 night scenes using 30 mobile devices, with subjective quality annotations obtained from human opinion ratings. In this work, NPHD provides an additional authentic night-time domain for evaluating whether the learned quality representation can transfer across different real night-time imaging conditions.

Following standard IQA protocols23,33, we report four metrics to quantify performance: Pearson Linear Correlation Coefficient (PLCC) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for prediction accuracy, and Spearman Rank-order Correlation Coefficient (SRCC) and Kendall Rank-Order Correlation Coefficient (KRCC) for ranking consistency. Implementation details

We follow a strict scene-level 80/20 train–test split on NNID, ensuring no overlap of scenes between sets to prevent data leakage. During training, images are resized to a short side of 384 pixels and randomly cropped to , with random horizontal flipping (p=0.5) for augmentation. The HCA-Mamba model is trained end-to-end using the Adam optimizer with and a weight decay of . The batch size is set to 64, and the learning rate starts at , following a cosine-annealing schedule with a five-epoch warm-up. We directly regress the MOS using the mean-squared error loss. The local branch is initialized with an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50, while the global branch uses the official ViM-Tiny checkpoint25. Unless otherwise specified, MSDE uses a grid size of g=7 (M=49 tokens) with latent width d=64, and LDIM employs H=4 heads. To reduce randomness, each configuration is evaluated with ten different random seeds, and we report the median performance. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch 2.0 on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Detailed complexity and inference-speed comparisons at 224×224 and 384×384 resolutions are reported in “Complexity and efficiency comparison”, where we analyze the accuracy–efficiency trade-off between HCA-Mamba and representative IQA baselines.

Comparison with state-of-the-art methods

We compare HCA-Mamba with a broad set of representative IQA approaches spanning five categories: (i) traditional BIQA methods, including BRISQUE65, DIIVINE66, BIQME67, and NRSL68; (ii) generic deep BIQA methods, including WaDIQaM33, GraphIQA69, and DBCNN17; (iii) recent hybrid or pretrained-model-based IQA methods, including MANIQA43, MUSIQ23, and LoDa26; (iv) Mamba-related IQA baselines, including ViM-IQA and VMamba-IQA constructed upon Vision Mamba25 and VMamba61, respectively, together with VISGA63; and (v) night-time-specific IQA methods, including BNBT1, BNIQM5, Xiao6, HNQA12, BNTI11, Karimi3, DDB-Net30, and Li29. Cross-dataset generalization

To further evaluate the generalization ability of HCA-Mamba across different night-time image distributions, we conduct cross-dataset experiments on NNID, EHNQ, and NPHD. Both NNID1 and NPHD focus mainly on authentic night-time photographs27, whereas EHNQ is designed for enhanced night-time images8. These three datasets provide a broader and more complementary setting for analyzing transfer between authentic capture-related night-time degradations and enhancement-induced distortions. In the cross-dataset setting, the model is trained on the source dataset and directly evaluated on the target dataset without fine-tuning, thereby providing a more challenging test of generalization across authentic and enhancement-induced distortions.

Table 8 summarizes both intra-dataset and cross-dataset results. Compared with the intra-dataset results, all cross-dataset settings show clear performance degradation, indicating that NTIQA models are sensitive to domain shifts among different night-time image sources. The NPHD results also indicate that authentic-to-authentic transfer can be relatively more stable than transfer between authentic and enhanced domains, especially when NPHD is used as the target dataset. However, the transfer performance is still direction-dependent, suggesting that cross-dataset NTIQA is affected not only by distortion type but also by dataset-specific imaging and annotation characteristics. These results show that cross-dataset evaluation is much more challenging than intra-dataset testing, while HCA-Mamba still achieves reasonable correlation performance in different transfer settings.

The performance degradation mainly comes from the mismatch between the degradation distributions of the source and target datasets. Authentic night-time datasets such as NNID and NPHD are dominated by capture-related degradations, including insufficient illumination, sensor noise, uneven exposure, glare, and natural detail loss. These degradations are closely related to camera sensors, exposure settings, imaging pipelines, and scene illumination conditions. In contrast, EHNQ consists of enhanced night-time images, where perceptual quality is affected not only by residual low-light degradations but also by enhancement-induced artifacts, such as over-smoothing, unnatural contrast, color shift, halo amplification, and local saturation. Therefore, a model trained on NNID or NPHD may not have sufficient exposure to enhancement-induced artifacts, which explains the performance drop when it is directly transferred to EHNQ.

The reverse transfer from EHNQ to authentic datasets also suffers from a domain gap. Although EHNQ contains diverse enhancement results, its degradation distribution is biased toward algorithm-dependent artifacts rather than natural camera noise, device-dependent imaging characteristics, and exposure variations in real night-time photographs. As a result, a model trained on EHNQ may overemphasize enhancement-related cues while being less sensitive to authentic capture-related factors. This also explains why authentic-to-authentic transfer, such as NNID → NPHD, is relatively more stable, although NNID and NPHD still differ in camera devices, scene composition, MOS distribution, and subjective annotation protocols.

Overall, the results indicate that HCA-Mamba has reasonable cross-dataset robustness, but also show that quality-aware representations learned from one night-time domain do not fully generalize when the underlying distortion sources change. This suggests that future NTIQA research should further consider domain-general training strategies and explicit modeling of both capture-related degradations and enhancement-induced artifacts.

Comprehensive structural ablation

Table 12 presents a comprehensive structural ablation of HCA-Mamba from three perspectives, namely simpler fusion strategies, LDIM simplifications, and injection depth variants.

We first examine the overall module effect. Starting from the ViM baseline, introducing MSDE improves the performance from 0.9257/0.7599/0.9277/0.0629 to 0.9449/0.7842/0.9487/0.0575, confirming that distortion-aware local tokens extracted from multi-scale CNN features are beneficial for NTIQA. Further incorporating LDIM raises the performance to 0.9536/0.8129/0.9551/0.0507, showing that the progressive interaction between local distortion tokens and global ViM features is critical.

For the simpler fusion variants, both late concatenation and late addition improve over the ViM baseline, but they remain inferior to the complete model. In particular, late concat and late add achieve SRCC values of 0.9480 and 0.9471, respectively, while token-based final-stage fusion further increases SRCC to 0.9502. However, all these simpler alternatives are still weaker than the full HCA-Mamba, indicating that merely combining local and global features at the final stage is insufficient. Instead, progressive token injection across network depth is more effective for aligning local distortions with global night-time scene representations.

For the LDIM simplifications, removing the learnable residual gate, replacing layer-specific projections with shared projections, or discarding the down/up projection design all degrade performance compared with the full model. Specifically, the variant without the residual gate achieves 0.9520/0.8087/0.9542/0.0524, the variant with shared projections achieves 0.9514/0.8069/0.9538/0.0529, and the variant without the down/up projection design further drops to 0.9507/0.8045/0.9533/0.0536. These results indicate that gated residual scaling, layer-specific projections, and compact latent-space interaction all contribute to stable and effective global–local coupling.

For the injection depth variants, all partial injection strategies underperform the full model. Injecting distortion tokens only at the final layer yields the weakest result (0.9491/0.7988/0.9524/0.0549), while restricting the injection to shallow, middle, or deep layers leads to gradual improvements. In particular, the deep-layer-only setting achieves the strongest performance among the partial variants, with 0.9518/0.8081/0.9542/0.0527, but it still remains below the full model. This trend suggests that local distortion information is most effective when it participates in feature refinement throughout multiple depths rather than being injected only once or only within a restricted stage.

Overall, the results in Table 12 consistently support the effectiveness of the proposed design. MSDE provides informative local distortion tokens, LDIM enables stable and selective global–local interaction, and all-layer injection yields the strongest overall performance.

Therefore, g=7 provides the best balance between local granularity and coupling cost.

For the latent width d, enlarging the latent space from 32 to 64 significantly improves all metrics, whereas further increasing it to 128 yields no additional gain and even causes a slight decline. Specifically, the model achieves 0.9478/0.7896/0.9485/0.0685 at d=32, 0.9536/0.8129/0.9551/0.0507 at d=64, and 0.9515/0.8103/0.9536/0.0556 at d=128. This suggests that d=64 is sufficient to support effective global–local interaction, whereas an excessively wide latent space may introduce redundant capacity.

For the number of attention heads H, the best performance is achieved at H=4. Compared with H=2, using four heads improves SRCC/KRCC/PLCC from 0.9485/0.7954/0.9476 to 0.9536/0.8129/0.9551, while reducing RMSE from 0.0672 to 0.0507. Increasing the number of heads to H=8 leads to a slight drop, resulting in 0.9506/0.8052/0.9496/0.0569. This indicates that too few heads limit the diversity of cross-attention interactions, whereas too many heads overly fragment the latent space and weaken the representation capacity of each head.

Overall, the results show a consistent trend that moderate hyperparameter settings outperform both smaller and larger alternatives, validating the default choice of g=7, d=64, and H=4.

Both the visualizations and the statistics suggest a progressive transition from local extraction to spatial realignment and contextual integration. In the CNN branch, the responses are concentrated on local edges, textures, and boundary structures. This behavior is reflected by the relatively compact activation pattern (hot area of 0.369) and the strong edge preference, with an edge bias of 15.78. After multi-scale aggregation in MSDE, the border bias is noticeably reduced to 7.55, and the response center moves slightly closer to the image center, with dist center decreasing from 0.042 to 0.035. This trend suggests that MSDE helps regularize the spatial distribution of local features before they are injected into the global branch.

The ViM backbone, guided by LDIM, exhibits a different but complementary response pattern. While preserving the centered focus established by MSDE (dist center remains 0.035), it selectively recovers sensitivity to fine structural details, as indicated by the edge bias increasing again to 15.26. This U-shaped trend in edge bias, i.e., high in the CNN branch, reduced in MSDE, and increased again in ViM, suggests that the final representation retains both global structural focus and local distortion sensitivity.

The depth-wise visualizations in Supplementary Figure S1 are consistent with this interpretation. Early ViM layers respond more broadly, middle layers gradually concentrate on the main structures, and deeper layers refine the responses toward more specific salient details. The relatively stable spread value of 0.350 further suggests that the ViM branch maintains a balance between global context modeling and local detail localization, avoiding both overly diffuse and overly concentrated responses. Overall, this case study indicates that HCA-Mamba does not simply mix features from different branches. Instead, the CNN branch extracts local evidence, MSDE reorganizes it spatially, and the ViM backbone integrates it into a globally coherent yet locally sensitive representation for night-time image quality assessment.

The high-error cases reveal that the remaining errors are mainly associated with extreme illumination and artifact distributions. Conclusion

In this paper, we presented HCA-Mamba, a hybrid framework for NTIQA that combines ViM-based global context modeling with CNN-based local distortion perception. To jointly capture scene-level illumination structure and fine-grained night-time degradations, we designed a dedicated global–local fusion pipeline in which the MSDE converts hierarchical CNN features into compact distortion-aware tokens, and the LDIM progressively injects these tokens into successive ViM layers through gated cross-attention. Experiments on the NNID, EHNQ and NPHD benchmarks, including both intra-dataset evaluation and cross-dataset generalization settings, demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method on authentic and enhanced night-time images. The synthetic distortion severity analysis further shows that HCA-Mamba responds reasonably to several progressively intensified degradations, while revealing remaining challenges under localized halo/glare artifacts. In addition, ablation experiments and visualization analyses further verify the roles of MSDE and LDIM in improving sensitivity to night-time distortions.

Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. First, although the ViM backbone provides a more scalable alternative to quadratic self-attention for global modeling, the overall dual-branch design still incurs non-negligible computational overhead, which may limit practical deployment in real-time or resource-constrained scenarios. Second, current NTIQA datasets remain relatively small and heterogeneous, and the distribution gap among authentic night images, enhanced night images, and images captured by different devices continues to hinder robust generalization. Future work will therefore focus on more efficient global–local coupling and lightweight architectures, semi-supervised and self-supervised learning to alleviate annotation scarcity, and stronger cross-device and cross-domain generalization for more robust zero-shot transfer.
