Investigation of the relationship between lead exposure in heavy metals mixtures and the prevalence of stroke: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiarong He, Wen Zhang, Fang Zhao, Ming Wang, Zhuo Wang, Chen Liang, Jing Pan, Jiaoying Jia, Mingming Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe adverse effects of environmental toxic metal exposure on human health are well-documented. However, the specific influence of heavy metal exposure on stroke prevalence remains underexplored.MethodsThis study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning from 2011 to 2018 to investigate the association between blood metal concentrations and the incidence of stroke. Four analytical approaches—logistic regression, Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS), Weighted Quantile Sum regression (WQS), and Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR)—were employed to assess the relationship, with a mediation analysis conducted to explore the role of inflammatory markers in Pb exposure-induced stroke.ResultsAmong the 9,399 participants in this project, 421 (4.4%) were diagnosed with stroke. After adjusting for covariates, a multivariable logistic regression model identified a positive association between the logarithmic concentration of Pb and the incidence of stroke. Besides, the analysis conducted using both WQS and BKMR methodologies found a consistent positive association between the composite exposure to heavy metals and the frequency of stroke cases, with Pb emerging as the predominant factor in this relationship. An evident saturation phenomenon was noted in the correlation between lead exposure and the risk of stroke. Additionally, the interplay between Pb exposure and stroke manifestation was found to be partially mediated by inflammatory markers, which were responsible for 6.9% of the observed effect (95%CI:0.01, 0.24, P = 0.03).ConclusionThese findings indicate a notable contribution of Pb exposure to stroke risk, highlighting inflammation as a significant intermediary mechanism in the Pb exposure-stroke association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-21000-y.

Introduction

In the 2022 overview provided by the World Stroke Organization, it is emphasized that stroke continues to represent a significant global health concern, ranking as the second leading cause of mortality and the third leading cause of combined mortality and disability [1]. The definition of stroke encompasses a broad spectrum of clinical and organizational criteria, which include cerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and ischemic stroke [2]. Stroke is influenced by a multitude of risk factors, with primary contributors being smoking, elevated body mass index, increased systolic blood pressure, and high fasting plasma glucose levels [3]. Recent studies have shed light on the potential role of environmental pollutants in exacerbating the prevalence of stroke. Atmospheric contaminants, including PM2.5, PM10, SO2, and NO2, have been implicated in being contributors to an increased risk of stroke [4]. Even after accounting for numerous conventional risk factors, the prevalence of stroke remains elevated. Considering the continuous progress in industrial development and the growing influence of environmental pollution, it becomes imperative to conduct comprehensive surveys into the influence of environmental toxins and heavy metals on the development of stroke.

Defined by their substantial density (≥ 5 g/cm3), heavy metals including manganese (Mn), selenium (Se), mercury (Hg), cadmium (Cd), and Lead (Pb) are recognized as significant environmental contaminants [5]. Routes of human exposure to these toxic metals encompass inhalation of polluted air, interaction with household sewage, utilization of cosmetic products, and consumption of contaminated food sources [6]. Accumulating research indicates that the presence of heavy metals within mammalian organisms is linked to a diverse array of adverse health outcomes, including but not limited to, carcinogenesis, metabolic disorders, bone density reduction, and pulmonary conditions [7, 8]. García and Zhang have elucidated the detrimental effects of heightened levels of Pb, Cd, and Hg on the functionality of the brain’s microvasculature, leading to disruptions in the regulation of cerebral blood circulation [9, 10]. Specifically, the adverse influence of Pb on endothelial cells in cerebral vessels has been established, resulting in microvascular dysfunctions and subsequent cerebral circulatory disturbances [11]. In addition, Lee et al. have revealed that chronic exposure to Pb, as evidenced by its bioaccumulation, escalates the risk of atherosclerotic plaque formation in intracranial carotid arteries, correlating with an increased incidence of cardiovascular pathologies [12]. Also, Pb exposure is implicated in the etiology of cerebral atherosclerosis through its cytotoxic and pathogenic effects, potentially contributing to a spectrum of cardiovascular disorders [13]. Nonetheless, the direct correlation between Pb exposure and stroke prevalence remains ongoing exploration, necessitating further empirical scrutiny.

Extensive research has established that inflammation and oxidative stress are key precursors to stroke development [14]. Exposure to heavy metals, such as lead, has been shown to trigger these responses, which are involved in various disease processes [15, 16]. Studies in rodent models have revealed that Pb exposure increases hepatic interleukin-1β (IL-1β) levels, a cytokine associated with inflammation and hepatocyte damage, and elevates nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) expression [17]. Pb’s interaction with amyloid β (Aβ) peptides further exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction, indicated by increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), oxidative stress markers, and apoptotic activity [18, 19]. Chronic Pb exposure has also been linked to neurodegenerative outcomes, including oxidative damage, apoptotic cell death, and motor deficits [20]. These findings support our hypothesis that Pb exposure may be a significant environmental risk factor for stroke. Additionally, the combined exposure to multiple heavy metals could lead to complex interactions affecting health, highlighting the need to explore their joint impact on stroke risk.

In our study, we applied logistic regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses to explore the association between Pb exposure and stroke occurrence. To assess the effects of exposure to mixed heavy metals on stroke risk, we utilized Weighted Quantile Sum (WQS) and Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) models. Furthermore, mediation analyses were conducted to examine the intermediary roles of inflammation and oxidative stress in the linkage between Pb exposure and stroke incidence, providing a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Research design and participant demographics

This study utilized data from four cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 2011 to 2018. The NHANES is a comprehensive, cross-sectional survey conducted biennially to evaluate the health and nutritional status of the civilian, non-institutionalized population in the United States. Conducted in accordance with the ethical standards prescribed by the Research Ethics Review Board at the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under Protocol #201,117, the survey requires written informed consent from all participants. We excluded individuals with indeterminate stroke diagnoses, incomplete data on metal levels or covariates, or those younger than 18 years. The final sample included 9,399 subjects, of whom 421 were identified as having experienced a stroke (Fig. 1).

Evaluation of stroke incidence

Stroke occurrence data were collected through a structured interview. Participants were asked, “Has a medical professional ever informed you that you have experienced a stroke?” In our study, affirmative responses were considered indicative of a prior stroke event. Participants who responded with “don’t know,” “refused,” or left the question unanswered were excluded from the analysis to ensure clarity and accuracy in stroke diagnosis data.

Heavy metal analysis

Blood specimens were preserved at -30 °C before being sent for analytical processing to the Division of Laboratory Sciences at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC/NCHS, 2018) in Atlanta, Georgia. Heavy metals, including Pb, Cd, Hg, Se, and Mn, were quantified using Inductively Coupled Plasma Dynamic Reaction Cell Mass Spectrometry (ICP-DRC-MS). Given that over 80% of the measured values fell below the detection threshold, NHANES employed a method for estimating concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD) by dividing the LOD by the square root of 2, as outlined in the CDC’s “National Report on Human Exposure to Environmental Chemicals“ [21]. We used quartiles to categorize heavy metal concentrations, including Pb, to examine their association with stroke prevalence. This method allows us to compare stroke risk across different exposure levels, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference category.

Variables and categorizations

We accounted for a range of clinically pertinent variables in our analysis, including age at the time of interview, sex, marital status, body mass index (BMI), educational attainment, family income-to-poverty ratio (PIR), alcohol consumption, tobacco use, sedentary time, diabetes presence, and hypertension history. Age was recorded in years, while sex was categorized as male or female. Marital status was classified as never married, married, or other. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized into normal weight (< 25 kg/m²), overweight (25–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m²). Educational attainment was divided into three levels: middle school or lower, high school, and college or more. Economic status was categorized using PIR into low income (PIR < 1.3), middle income (PIR 1.3–3.5), and high income (PIR ≥ 3.5). Alcohol consumption was segmented into lifetime abstainers, former drinkers, and current consumers, while tobacco use was divided into never users, past users, and current smokers. Sedentary time was measured in minutes per day. Diabetes was identified through survey responses, current diabetes medication use, or meeting any of the following criteria: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, glucose level post 2-hour meal ≥ 200 mg/dL, or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. Hypertension was identified based on self-reported diagnoses, current antihypertensive medication use, or blood pressure readings ≥ 140/90 mmHg. These variables were included to adjust for potential confounders and to examine their interactions and mediating effects in relation to stroke risk.

Interaction analysis

To examine potential interactions between heavy metal exposure and other variables, we included interaction terms in our models. Specifically, we investigated how the association between heavy metals and stroke risk might vary across different subpopulations. This included interactions between heavy metal concentrations and factors such as age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and hypertension. Interaction terms were added to the models as multiplicative terms (heavy metal * subpopulation group). This approach helps identify whether the effect of heavy metal exposure on stroke risk differs significantly based on these characteristics. We selected interactions for variables such as age, gender, BMI, and other covariates because these factors are known to influence stroke risk and may modify the relationship between heavy metal exposure and stroke.

Statistical models and covariates

The analysis employed three models to assess the association between heavy metal concentrations and stroke risk. The Crude Model provided an initial estimate without adjusting for confounding factors. Model I adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates, including age, BMI, sedentary time, gender, education level, marital status, family income-to-poverty ratio, drinking status, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension. These variables were selected as they are established risk factors for stroke and may confound the association between heavy metal exposure and stroke risk. Model II further adjusted for the presence of other heavy metals, accounting for co-exposures to better isolate the independent effects of each metal on stroke risk.

Statistical analysis

Initial comparative analyses of baseline data utilized t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square (χ²) tests for categorical variables. Categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages (n, %), whereas continuous data were presented as mean values with standard deviations (SD). Given the right-skewed nature of heavy metal concentration distributions in biological samples, a log10 transformation was applied to normalize these values, which were then divided into quartiles for further analysis. The univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to explore the associations between various covariates (age, gender, BMI, education level, marital status, PIR, sedentary time, drinking status, diabetes, smoking status, hypertension) and stroke risk, alongside heavy metal concentrations (Pb, Cd, Hg, Se, Mn). These covariates were selected based on their clinical relevance and known association with stroke risk. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to elucidate the correlations between different heavy metal concentrations in the bloodstream. To further assess the impact of individual heavy metals on stroke risk, a multivariate logistic regression model was applied, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference category. Outcomes were presented as ORs with 95% CIs. The multivariate model adjusted for potential confounders, allowing for a detailed examination of interactions and sensitivities across various subpopulations [22].

Subsequently, the analysis incorporated WQS regression to evaluate the collective influence of blood metal levels on stroke incidence. The dataset was partitioned into a training set and a validation set in a 40:60 ratio [23]. Utilizing the “gWQS” package within R software, the WQS index was calculated, representing a weighted aggregate of individual metal concentrations in the blood. This index, which varies between 0 and 1, reflects the overall exposure to metals, assigning significance to each based on non-trivial weighting. The outcome elucidates the cumulative effect of a one-quartile increase in the metal mixture on stroke risk.

Additionally, the BKMR model was implemented to discern the effects of mixed metal exposure on stroke, leveraging kernel functions to accurately delineate both singular and combined exposure effects [24]. This statistical approach allows for the modeling of complex exposure-response relationships, accommodating potential non-linearities and interactions, while controlling for confounders. Specifically, the model assessed stroke risk by evaluating the collective effect of heavy metals at different percentiles compared to the median (50th percentile). The analysis also explored individual metal contributions by increasing the concentration of one metal to the 75th percentile while holding other metals constant at the 25th, 50th, or 75th percentiles. Lastly, univariate responses of each heavy metal were examined by keeping all other metals at their median levels. By manipulating the median concentrations of the metal mix by ± 5%, the combined effect on stroke was assessed. The model determined the relative importance of each metal in the mix through Posterior Inclusion Probabilities (PIPs), with a significance threshold set at 0.5, after 20,000 iterations using the “BKMR” package in R [25].

Linear regression analysis was then applied to explore the relationship between Pb exposure and biomarkers of inflammation (alkaline phosphatase, white blood cell) and oxidative stress (γ-glutamyltransferase, bilirubin) [26, 27]. Mediation analysis, using non-parametric bootstrapping and a restricted cubic spline framework, was conducted to dissect the direct and indirect pathways linking Pb exposure to stroke risk. Given NHANES’ complex survey structure, analysis incorporated sample weights, clustering, and stratification as per NCHS guidelines. All statistical analyses were executed in R software (version 4.3.2), with statistical significance determined at a P-value threshold of 0.05.

Correlation between heavy metals and stroke risk

The relationship between stroke prevalence and quartiles of log-transformed metal concentrations was scrutinized using both univariate and multivariate regression analyses. As shown in Table S1, which reports the univariate analyses, a significant association was observed between the prevalence of stroke and elevated levels of all five investigated metals. Specifically, Pb demonstrated a pronounced association with an increased risk of stroke, exhibiting an odds ratio (OR) of 4.671 (95%CI: 3.452, 6.321, P < 0.001), indicating a strong link between higher Pb levels and stroke risk. In addition to metal concentrations, other covariates such as age, education, marital status, PIR, sedentary time, diabetes, smoking, and hypertension also showed significant associations with stroke prevalence. For example, age and sedentary time were positively associated with stroke risk, whereas higher education levels and PIR were inversely related to stroke incidence. These results underscore the importance of adjusting for multiple risk factors in evaluating the relationship between heavy metals and stroke risk.

The lowest quartile (Q1) was designated as the reference. The findings reveal that in the unadjusted analysis, elevated levels of two metals, Pb and Cd, were associated with a higher stroke prevalence, whereas levels of Hg, Se, and Mn were inversely associated with stroke prevalence (Table 2). In the adjusted Model I, a positive correlation was maintained for Pb with stroke risk (P < 0.05), while Hg and Se were inversely correlated (P < 0.05). Then, Model II; analysis indicated that the highest quartile of Pb, in comparison to the lowest, correlated with a 170% heightened stroke risk (OR for Pb = 1.70; 95% CI: 1.12, 2.56), and the highest quartile of Hg, compared to the lowest, was associated with a 55% reduction in stroke risk (OR for Hg = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.40, 0.76). Linear trend analysis confirmed a significant association between the log10-transformed concentrations of Pb and Hg and stroke prevalence across all three multivariate regression models (P for trend < 0.05).

Subgroup evaluations

Figure 2 illustrates that the direct association between Pb exposure and stroke prevalence, as noted in both the unadjusted and adjusted models, was consistent across various subgroups. Analysis of metal co-exposure using WQS regression

The study further employed WQS regression to explore the combined effect of exposure to five specific heavy metals in blood on stroke incidence. The derived WQS index indicated a statistically significant association between the combined metal exposure and stroke prevalence, with the unadjusted model showing an OR of 1.57 (95%CI: 1.42, 1.71, P < 0.001) and the adjusted Model I presenting an OR of 1.20 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.39, P = 0.041) (Table S2). Initial analysis in the unadjusted model suggested that Pb and Cd were positively associated with stroke risk, whereas Hg, Se, and Mn exhibited inverse associations with stroke risk (Fig. 3a). Upon adjustment for all covariates, the contribution of Pb to stroke risk was most obvious with a weighting of 0.593, while Cd, Se, Mn, and Hg followed with weights of 0.321, 0.078, 0.007, and 0.001 respectively, all indicating a positive direction in their association with stroke risk (Fig. 3b).

Influence of inflammatory markers on Pb’s role in stroke

The association between log-transformed blood Pb levels and the inflammatory marker alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was statistically significant in both Model I (β = 9.26, 95% CI: 7.31, 11.21, P < 0.001) and Model II (β = 9.73, 95% CI: 7.73, 11.74, P < 0.001), as reported in Table S4. Moreover, the relationship between ALP and stroke risk was established in Model I (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.01, P < 0.05) and Model II (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.01, P < 0.05) (Table S5). The mediating role of ALP in the linkage between Pb levels and stroke risk was quantified, accounting for 7.08% in the unadjusted model and 6.90% in Model II, indicating the significance of inflammatory pathways in this association (Table S6). We also explored other potential mediators in this relationship; however, no significant findings were observed with these mediators.

Given that Pb consistently demonstrated a significant association with stroke risk across various analyses (logistic regression, WQS, and BKMR), while other heavy metals did not show similar significant patterns, we focused our investigation on Pb for the non-linear analysis. An RCS analysis further explored the dose-response curve between blood Pb concentration and stroke risk, adjusting for confounding factors. The RCS model revealed a non-linear relationship between log10-transformed Pb levels and stroke risk, with a notable point of saturation at approximately 0.39 µg/dL for log10-transformed Pb, where the risk plateaued (P for non-linearity = 0.002) (Figure S4), indicating a threshold effect in the association between Pb exposure and stroke incidence.

Discussion

Our study aimed to explore the association between blood heavy metal concentrations and stroke risk using NHANES data. The findings demonstrate a significant correlation between the collective presence of heavy metals in the bloodstream and stroke occurrence, with Pb emerging as the key element driving this association. This study not only corroborates existing evidence linking Pb exposure to adverse health outcomes but also provides new insights into the role of multiple heavy metals in stroke risk. In particular, our analysis identified Pb as a significant predictor of stroke risk, contributing to increased stroke prevalence. Importantly, inflammation and oxidative stress likely mediate this relationship, as demonstrated by the positive association between Pb levels and ALP, a marker of inflammation. ALP was found to mediate approximately 6.90% of the association between Pb and stroke risk, suggesting that Pb exposure may exacerbate inflammatory pathways, increasing stroke susceptibility.

Contemporary studies have emphasized the potential health risks associated with elevated exposure to heavy metals, implicating such exposure as a contributory factor to adverse health outcomes [27]. The role of heavy metal exposure as an independent predictor in the multifaceted etiology of stroke remains a subject of ongoing investigation [28]. Understanding the link between environmental metal contamination and stroke is pivotal for unraveling the pathogenesis of stroke and for guiding public health measures aimed at mitigating exposure to hazardous metals, especially considering the widespread nature of environmental metal pollution. In contrast to previous studies [29, 30], which primarily focused on individual metal exposures, our study explores the correlation between the concentrations of various metals in the bloodstream and stroke risk using a cohort of 9,399 participants. This research provides new insights into the relationships between multiple metals in the blood and stroke risk among the US population, broadening the perspective beyond the limited focus of earlier studies.

Cadmium, often inhaled through cigarette smoke, tends to accumulate in several body organs such as the central and peripheral nervous systems, the cardiovascular system, kidneys, and liver, potentially leading to a range of health issues including stroke and coronary artery disease [31]. The mechanisms of oxidative stress and inflammation are related to the process of tissue damage, which can accelerate the development of atherosclerosis, contributing to the pathophysiology of these conditions [32, 33]. In the conducted analysis, multivariate regression did not show a statistically significant link between cadmium levels and stroke occurrence. Mercury, recognized as a global environmental contaminant [34], has been associated with raised risks of hypertension and diabetes [35]. Interestingly, research conducted in Northern Sweden suggested a protective role of mercury against acute myocardial infarction, from its relationship with stroke [36]. Similarly, a study involving women with documented mercury exposure found that higher mercury levels correlated with lower mortality rates and a decreased risk of fatal acute myocardial infarction, yet no significant association with non-fatal myocardial infarction or stroke was observed [37]. In our research, mercury demonstrated a significant inverse association with stroke, as indicated by a negative correlation with stroke prevalence after adjustments for variables like gender and age were made. The current understanding of the impact of selenium and manganese on stroke risk remains limited. Multivariate regression and heavy metal mixture analyses did not show a statistically significant connection between cadmium exposure and stroke risk. Further research is needed to clarify the potential relationships between these metals and stroke. With the reduction in the use of lead, especially in leaded gasoline, its environmental levels have declined, yet occupational exposure to Pb continues to be a significant health risk. Studies have pointed a correlation between higher blood Pb concentrations and increased blood pressure [38]. Our findings, through logistic regression analysis, also observed a positive correlation between higher blood Pb levels and increased stroke prevalence. Furthermore, the study explored the effects of simultaneous exposure to various metals on stroke risk. WQS and BKMR analyses uniformly revealed a positive correlation between the combined presence of metals in the blood and the risk of stroke, with Pb identified as the main risk factor.

Inflammation and oxidative stress are part of mechanisms in the development of stroke [39], with research indicating their significant role in its pathophysiology. Wang et al. discovered higher levels of bilirubin and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) in individuals suffering from stroke [40], suggesting a link between these biomarkers and the condition. Paulina et al. discovered the critical importance of chronic inflammation in stroke progression, illustrating a connection between higher white blood cell (WBC) and stroke incidence [41]. Laurence et al. established a positive relationship between serum ALP levels and the occurrence of atherosclerosis, further linking inflammatory markers to cardiovascular diseases [42]. Tinkov et al. have also revealed that Pb exposure contributes to heightened inflammatory responses and increased oxidative stress. Alexey et al. demonstrated that Pb could trigger the activation of the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and enhance the expression of NF-κB, playing a significant role in the inflammatory pathways associated with non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [43]. Ren et al. reported that Pb exposure markedly escalates cellular oxidative damage and enhances oxidative stress through the sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) signaling pathway. Excessive Pb exposure has been implicated in the elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1) in animal models, underlining the potential link between Pb toxicity and inflammatory processes [44]. Given these shared pathogenic pathways, it is plausible to consider that Pb exposure could raise stroke risk through mechanisms involving inflammation and oxidative stress. In our investigation, a significant positive correlation was identified between ALP levels and blood Pb concentrations, with ALP contributing to 6.90% of this association. This finding suggests that higher Pb exposure may enhance stroke susceptibility by promoting inflammatory conditions. Additionally, our study showed a saturation effect in the relationship between Pb exposure and stroke risk, with an increase in stroke risk at higher Pb concentrations, reaching a plateau at log-transformed Pb levels exceeding 0.39 µg/dL.

This research aimed to explore the association between blood heavy metal levels and the occurrence of strokes within the framework of NHANES. The outcomes of our study point to a significant correlation between the collective presence of heavy metals in the bloodstream and the increased occurrence of stroke, with Pb identified as a key element driving this association. Nonetheless, the cross-sectional nature of our study design warrants caution, as it does not preclude the possibility of reverse causation. Additionally, the NHANES dataset does not provide information on the arterial topography of the stroke, such as whether infarcts occurred in the posterior or middle cerebral artery, which may have an impact on prognosis and further analysis of stroke risk [45]. Another limitation is the inability to differentiate between ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes, as the dataset lacks specific data on stroke subtypes. This distinction is critical because the underlying mechanisms, risk factors, and outcomes may vary between the two types. Future studies should aim to address these gaps by incorporating data on stroke subtypes and using longitudinal designs to establish clearer causal pathways. The study is supported by several strengths, particularly the utilization of four statistical methodologies to dissect the association between metal exposure and stroke risk across a sizeable demographic. This approach not only enhances the depth of our analysis but also amplifies its relevance to clinical practices and public health strategies aimed at mitigating stroke risks within the U.S. population. Moreover, the analysis was adjusted for a broad array of potential confounders to bolster the reliability of our findings. However, this study had some limitations. A notable constraint is the single-time measurement of blood heavy metal levels, which may not provide a comprehensive representation of an individual’s long-term exposure to these metals. While our study accounted for a wide range of potential confounding factors, the possibility of residual confounders or unaccounted variables, such as genetic predispositions, remains a limitation that could influence the outcomes. While our study identified a significant association between heavy metal concentrations and stroke risk, a valuable area for future research would be to explore the specific impact of these metals on small vessel disease compared to other ischemic stroke subtypes. Small vessel ischemic strokes differ from other cerebral infarcts in their pathophysiology, prognosis, and clinical features, suggesting that the effects of environmental contaminants like heavy metals may also vary between subtypes [46]. Future studies should focus on examining these differences to better understand how heavy metal exposure influences different stroke mechanisms.

Conclusions

Pb, a significant environmental contaminant, has been linked to harmful effects on the central nervous system. The primary objective of this study was to examine the association between blood Pb levels and stroke prevalence using data from NHANES (2011–2018). Our findings suggest that Pb exposure may contribute to an increased risk of stroke, potentially through inflammatory mechanisms, offering new insights into stroke prevention strategies. However, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to establish a causal relationship between Pb exposure and stroke. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to better understand the causal link between Pb exposure and stroke development. Additionally, exploring the role of heavy metal concentrations in small vessel disease versus other ischemic stroke subtypes, and gene-environment interactions that may influence susceptibility to Pb toxicity, are promising avenues for future investigation. Furthermore, examining interventions and public health policies aimed at reducing Pb exposure could play a critical role in lowering stroke risk and improving overall public health.
