Unexpectedly, a Symmetry on Unlabeled Graphs
Florian Fürnsinn, Moritz Gangl, Martin Rubey

Abstract
. We exhibit the joint symmetric distribution of the following two parameters on the set of unlabeled simple connected graphs with n n vertices. The first parameter is the maximal number of leaves attached to a vertex. The second parameter is the size of the largest set of vertices sharing the same closed neighborhood minus 1 1 . Apparently, this is the first example of a natural non-trivial equidistribution of graph parameters on unlabeled simple connected graphs with a fixed set of vertices. Our proof is enumerative, using the theory of species. Exhibiting an explicit bijection interchanging the two parameters remains an open problem.



1.
Introduction

Symmetries are an essential part of human life. Some seem obvious to
us now, such as the rotational symmetry of a wheel, or the reflective
symmetry of a balance scale. Other symmetries, such as the symmetry
between area and bounce on Dyck paths are still
mysterious
[
MR2371044
, Open Problem 3.11]
.
1
1

1

Even in 2026, some people still ask why
mirrors reflect horizontally but not vertically.
In mathematical
terms, a symmetry is a bijection on a set of objects. As the preceding example demonstrates, in some
cases, we know about the existence of a symmetry but are unable to
exhibit it explicitly.

There are many known bijections on sets
classically studied in combinatorics, such as permutations, integer
partitions or Dyck paths. However, to the best of our
knowledge
2
2

2

https://mathoverflow.net/questions/313532
,
there is not a single known ‘natural’ bijection from the set of
all unlabeled simple connected graphs with a given finite
set of vertices to itself. Enlarging the domain to all graphs we are
aware of taking the complement of a graph. Alternatively, we can restrict the
domain to three-connected planar graphs and remove the constraint that the number
of vertices is preserved.
Then a celebrated result of Whitney shows that mapping a graph to its planar dual is an involution
[
10
, Thm. 11]
. Of
course, it is possible to modify these maps to obtain involutions
preserving connectivity, but such a modification does not seem to be a very natural thing to do.

In fact
3
3

3

https://mathoverflow.net/questions/312823
,
we do not even know of any pair of ‘natural’ equidistributed
parameters on unlabeled simple connected graphs appearing in the literature. This may be surprising, since many graph parameters have been considered and studied, albeit mostly in terms of inequalities satisfied among them. The
www.findstat.org
database, as of 2025, lists about
300 such statistics, without any conjectural equidistributions.

Note that any equidistribution implies the existence of a bijection that maps one of the parameters to the other.
Thus, our aim is to present a pair of non-trivial graph parameters
which have the same distribution on the set of unlabeled
simple connected graphs with a fixed number of vertices.

We consider only finite simple graphs. A graph
G
consists of a set of vertices
V(G)
and a set of edges
E(G)
. Given a
vertex
v
, the (open)
neighborhood
of
v
is the set

\Nhd(v)\coloneqq\{u\in V(G)\mid u\text{ adjacent to }v\},

whereas the set

\cNhd(v)\coloneqq\{v\}\cup\Nhd(v)

is the
closed neighborhood
of
v
.
Two vertices are
siblings
if they share the same
closed neighborhood. Finally, a
leaf
or
endpoint
is a
vertex of degree one.

We consider the following two parameters on graphs. The
sibling
number

s(G)\coloneqq\max_{v\in V(G)}\left\lvert\{u\in V(G)\setminus\{v\}\mid\cNhd(u)=\cNhd(v)\}\right\rvert

is the maximal number of other vertices sharing the
same
closed
neighborhood with any given vertex. The
tuft
number

number of leaves adjacent to the same vertex.

Our main theorem, initially conjectured by Per Alexandersson and
the third author, exhibits a non-trivial equidistribution among graph parameters.

Theorem 1
.

The sibling number and the tuft number have joint symmetric
distribution on the set of unlabeled connected graphs with
n

vertices. Put differently, the isomorphism type generating series

\sum_{G}x^{s(G)}y^{t(G)}
, where the sum is taken over all unlabeled connected graphs
with
n
vertices, is symmetric in
x
and
y
.

The sibling number and tuft number of a graph are the maximum sibling number and tuft number, respectively, of its connected components. Therefore, equidistribution of the parameters on connected graphs is a stronger property than equidistribution on the set of all graphs. The maximal number of vertices sharing the same open neighborhood and the tuft number do not have joint symmetric distribution.

Graphs without siblings and graphs without vertices sharing the same open neighborhood were studied previously by several authors. Apparently, Sumner
[
9
]
was the first to formally introduce terminology. He calls a graph
point determining
if all of its vertices have distinct open neighborhoods, and
point distinguishing
if all of its vertices have distinct closed neighborhoods. He also notes that a graph is point distinguishing if and only if its complement is point determining.

Bull and Pease
[
2
]
re-introduced point determining graphs as ‘mating graphs’, to describe possible mating types of biological species. They provide the number of unlabeled point determining graphs with at most ten vertices, and ask for a general formula. Soon after, such a formula was supplied by Read
[
8
]
, using the cycle index of graphs.

Jovović
5
5

5

cf. comments in
www.oeis.org/A004108
then observed that the number of unlabeled connected point determining graphs with
n
vertices coincides with the number of unlabeled connected leafless graphs with
n
vertices, if
n\neq 2
. This was shown bijectively by Kilibarda
[
7
]
. A proof using combinatorial species building on Read’s idea was then provided by Gessel and Li
[
4
]
. In fact, they show the following.

Theorem 2

(Kilibarda 2007, Gessel and Li 2011)
.

The number of unlabeled connected point determining graphs with
n
vertices, the number of unlabeled connected point distinguishing graphs with
n
vertices, and the number of unlabeled connected leafless graphs with
n
vertices, coincide for
n\neq 2
.

Gessel and Li used the language of combinatorial species
that was introduced by Joyal
[
6
,
5
]
, and further
developed by Bergeron, Labelle and Leroux
[
1
]
, to provide
an alternative proof of Kilibarda’s result. We follow Gessel and Li’s approach to prove
Theorem

1
. More precisely, we
express the species of graphs with sibling number at
most
s
and tuft number at most
t
as a
composition of the species of graphs without siblings and a virtual
species. It then turns out that this virtual species has an
isomorphism type generating series which is obviously symmetric
in
s
and
t
.

Since our main motivation was to exhibit a symmetry on graphs, let us mention some obstructions we encountered trying to find an explicit bijection. First of all, the sibling and the tuft number are not equidistributed on the set of (labeled) graphs. This fact also makes combinatorial species a natural tool to approach the problem. Although leaves cannot be adjacent to vertices having siblings in graphs other than
K_{2}
, it turns out that it is impossible to replace each group of siblings with a vertex having the appropriate number of leaves and vice versa. The reason is that the number of groups of siblings of maximal cardinality and the number of vertices adjacent to the maximal number of leaves are not equidistributed. In
Section

10
we describe some tentative first steps towards a bijection.

However, we are able to prove the following refinement of
Theorem

1
. Let the

reduction
of a graph – other than
K_{2}
– be the graph
obtained by repeatedly removing all leaves and contracting each group
of siblings to a single vertex. Trivially, the reduction is a
leafless graph without siblings.
Figure

3

provides an example of the reduction process.

Figure 3
.
The graph on the left is reduced in two steps, each removing leaves and then contracting siblings, to the leafless point distinguishing graph depicted on the right.

Theorem 3
.

Let
R
be a connected leafless point distinguishing graph. The
sibling number and the tuft number have joint symmetric distribution on
the set of unlabeled connected graphs with
n
vertices that
reduce to
R
.

The reduction operation has some attractive properties. For example, we show in
Theorem

30
that a graph
G
contains an induced
k
-cycle if and only if the reduction of
G
contains an induced
k
-cycle, for
k\geq 4
. Together with the previous theorem, this implies that the sibling and the tuft number also have joint symmetric distribution on the set of connected chordal graphs, that is, graphs which do not contain an induced cycle with
4
or more vertices.

In a different direction, we show in
Proposition

31
that the species of point distinguishing graphs reducing to a given leafless point distinguishing graph
R
can be expressed in terms of the species of point distinguishing graphs reducing to the single vertex graph. In particular, this implies that the enumeration of graphs with restricted sibling and restricted tuft number reducing to a graph
R
can be reduced to the case where
R
is the single vertex graph.

At the heart of our proof of
Theorem

1
is a functional equation, expressing the species of graphs in terms of the species of point distinguishing graphs. As a further application, we exhibit another enumerative coincidence. A graph is
bi-point determining
if it is simultaneously point determining and point distinguishing. Equivalently, its automorphism group contains no transposition. Combining an expression for the species of bi-point determining graphs due to Gessel and Li
[
4
, Cor. 4.7]
with our functional equation, we find the following.

Theorem 4
.

The number of unlabeled connected bi-point determining graphs with
n
vertices equals the number of unlabeled connected leafless point distinguishing graphs with
n
vertices.

We do not have a bijective proof for this theorem, either.

2.
Combinatorial Species

Combinatorial species were introduced by Joyal as a formal framework
for enumeration
[
6
,
5
]
. In this section, apart
from briefly recalling the definitions, we also introduce the species
appearing subsequently. For any details, we recommend Joyal’s
original articles and the textbook by Bergeron, Labelle and
Leroux
[
1
]
.

A
combinatorial species
is a functor from the category of
finite sets with bijections to itself. Given a (finite) set of

labels

U
, a species
F
produces a (finite) set
F[U]
of

F
-structures
. Moreover, given a bijection

\sigma:U\to V
it produces a bijection
F[\sigma]:F[U]\to F[V]
,
called the
relabeling
or
transport
of
F
-structures
along
\sigma
. Often, the precise definition of relabeling for a
particular species is obvious from context and therefore omitted. We
regard two species
F
and
G
as
(combinatorially) equal
6
6

6

cf.
[
1
, p. 21]
if there is a natural
isomorphism between
F
and
G
in the categorical sense.

The symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on the set

F[n]\coloneqq F[\{1,2,\dots,n\}]
by relabeling.
The
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-orbits under this action are called
isomorphism types of
F[n]
or

unlabeled
F
-structures
of order (or size)
n
. Accordingly,
two
F
-structures
a\in F[U]
and
b\in F[V]
are
isomorphic

if there is a bijection
\sigma:U\to V
with
F[\sigma](a)=b
.

Example 5
.

The species of
(simple) graphs
is defined as follows.
\mathcal{G}[U]
is the set of simple graphs with vertex set
U
. Unlabeled graphs are precisely the isomorphism types of
\mathcal{G}
.

The
exponential generating series
and the
isomorphism
type generating series
associated with a species
F
are

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 0}\big\lvert F[n]\big\rvert\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 0}\big\lvert\big\{\text{isomorphism types of $F[n]$}\big\}\big\rvert\;x^{n},

respectively. Both are specializations of the
cycle index series

\displaystyle Z_{F}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\fix F[\sigma]p_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}p_{2}^{\sigma_{2}}\dots,

where
\fix F[\sigma]
denotes the number of fixed points of

F[\sigma]
,
p_{k}
is the
k
-th power sum symmetric function, and

\sigma_{k}
is the number of
k
-cycles of
\sigma
.

Proposition 6
.

The exponential generating series of a species is obtained from its
cycle index series by specializing
p_{1}=x
and
p_{k}=0
for
k>1
.

The isomorphism type generating series of a species is obtained
from its cycle index series by specializing
p_{k}=x^{k}
for all

k\geq 1
.

A species
F
is a
subspecies
of a species
G
, if, for any set
of labels, every
F
-structure is also a
G
-structure, and the
relabeling bijections coincide. We denote the
restriction of a
species

F
to sets of cardinality
m
by
F_{m}
. The
restrictions to sets of cardinality other than
m
, at least
m
, at most
n
and
sets of cardinalities from
m
to
n
are denoted by

F_{\neq m},

F_{\geq m}
,
F_{\leq n}
and
F_{m\leq\bullet\leq n}
, respectively.

Perhaps the most fundamental species is the
species of sets

E
, which produces a single structure for any set of
labels. The species
E_{0}
is usually denoted by
1
, and the
species
E_{1}
is usually denoted by
X
and referred to as the

singleton species
. We have
E(x)=\exp x
,

\widetilde{E}(x)=\frac{1}{1-x}
, and

Z_{E}=\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\right).

The zero species, producing no structure for any set of labels, is denoted by
0
.

Example 7
.

Recall that a
point distinguishing graph
is a graph without siblings.
We define
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{c}}[U]
as the set of connected point distinguishing graphs with vertex
set
U
. For a given connected point distinguishing graph
D\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{c}}[U]
and a bijection

\sigma:U\to V
, the graph
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{c}}[\sigma](D)
is obtained from

D
by replacing the vertex labeled
u
with a vertex labeled

\sigma(u)
. Thus, the species of connected point distinguishing graphs
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{c}}
is a subspecies of the species

\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{c}}
of connected graphs.

A
point determining graph
is a graph without vertices sharing the same open neighborhood. A
bi-point determining graph
is a graph which is point distinguishing and point determining. Thus, the species of bi-point determining graphs
\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{c}}
is the subspecies of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{c}}
whose structures are also point determining.

A
multisort species of
k
sorts
is a functor from the
category whose objects are
k
-tuples of finite sets (short: ‘
k
-sets’) and whose morphisms are
k
-tuples of bijections, to the category of finite sets with bijections.
Given a
k
-set
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{k})
, the elements of each set

U_{i}
are referred to as the
labels of sort
i
. The concepts introduced for
1
-sort species carry over to the multisort context in the obvious way.

We denote by
X
,
Y,
and
Z
the singleton species of
three different sorts. That is, given a
3
-set
U=(U_{1},U_{2},U_{3})
,
the set
X[U]
contains a single structure if
U_{1}
has one element
and
U_{2}
and
U_{3}
are empty. Otherwise,
X[U]
is empty. For convenience,
we refer to labels of sort
1
as labels of sort
X
, etc. To emphasize that a species
F
has, for example,
3
sorts, we sometimes write
F(X,Y,Z)
. This notation is compatible with the composition of species defined below. However, we alert the reader that by abuse of notation
X
denotes both a
1
-sort singleton species and a multisort singleton species.

In the following definitions, the addition of
k
-sets always refers
to their component-wise disjoint union.

The Reduction of a Graph

In this section, we show that the reduction of a graph, as defined in
the introduction, and the reduction of the same graph with some
leaves removed coincide. In particular, we show that the reduction of the underlying
point distinguishing graph equals the reduction of the original graph.

In fact, we will prove a slight generalization of this statement.
Recall from
Section

3
that
\lambda_{L}(G)
is the graph obtained from
G
by removing a set of leaves
L
, and
\rho(G)
is the graph obtained from

G
by contracting each group of siblings to a single vertex. For
convenience, let
\lambda
without subscript denote the map removing all
leaves of a graph. Note that
\rho(G)
cannot be isomorphic to
K_{2}
and that

\lambda_{L}(G)
has at least one vertex if
G
is not isomorphic to
K_{2}
.

Theorem 25
.

Let
G
be a connected graph other than
K_{2}
and let
U
be a subset of its
leaves. Then there exists a set of leaves
V
of

\rho\circ\lambda(G)
such that

\rho\circ\lambda\circ\rho\circ\lambda_{U}(G)=\rho\circ\lambda_{V}\circ\rho\circ\lambda(G).

One can actually show that the reduction of a graph can also be
obtained by repeatedly cutting off any single leaf from a graph other
than
K_{2}
and reducing the size of any group of siblings by one, in
any order. However, we do not need this statement, so we content
ourselves with the above.

Theorem

25
is a simple consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 26
.

Let
H
be a connected graph other than
K_{2}
and let
L
be a subset of its
leaves. Then

\rho\circ\lambda_{L}(H)=\rho\circ\lambda_{L}\circ\rho(H).

Proof.

We partition the set of vertices of
\lambda_{L}(H)
into maximal
groups of siblings
\{S_{i}\mid i\in I\}
. Each set
S_{i}

corresponds to a triple
(P_{i},L_{i},R_{i})
of (possibly empty) sets
of vertices in
H
, where
L_{i}\subseteq L
is the set of leaves in
L
of
H
adjacent to
S_{i}
,
P_{i}\subseteq S_{i}
is
the set of vertices adjacent to a leaf in
L_{i}
in
H
and

R_{i}=S_{i}\setminus P_{i}
. These sets partition the set of vertices of
H
.

Let us first show that
R_{i}
is a maximal group of siblings of
H

for all
i\in I
. Let
x\in R_{i}
and let
y\neq x
be a sibling in

H
. In a graph other than
K_{2}
, siblings cannot be leaves, so

x
and
y
are both vertices in
\lambda_{L}(H)
. Moreover, in a
graph other than
K_{2}
, siblings cannot be adjacent to leaves.
Thus, the map
\lambda_{L}
preserves neighborhoods of siblings, and

x
and
y
are also siblings in
\lambda_{L}(H)
. Since

S_{i}=P_{i}\cup R_{i}
is a maximal group of siblings in
\lambda_{L}(H)
,

y\in S_{i}
. Finally,
y\not\in P_{i}
because it is a sibling of
x

in
H
.

It follows that the vertices of
\rho(H)
can be partitioned into
pairs of sets
(L_{i},S^{\prime}_{i})
, with

S^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}P_{i}&\text{if $R_{i}=\emptyset$}\\
P_{i}\cup\{r_{i}\}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
r_{i}
is the vertex obtained by contracting the maximal group
of siblings
R_{i}
. Consequently, the sets
S^{\prime}_{i}
partition

\lambda_{L}\circ\rho(H)
.

We now show that each set
S^{\prime}_{i}
is a
maximal group of siblings in this graph. Consider the surjective
map

\displaystyle\iota

\displaystyle:\lambda_{L}(H)\to\lambda_{L}\circ\rho(H)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{cases}r_{i}&\text{if $z\in R_{i}$ for some $i$}\\
z&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For any two vertices
x
and
y
in
\lambda_{L}(H)
we have that
x

and
y
are equal or adjacent if and only if
\iota(x)
and

\iota(y)
are equal or adjacent in
\lambda_{L}\circ\rho(H)
. Put
differently,

\cNhd_{\lambda_{L}(H)}(x)=\cNhd_{\lambda_{L}(H)}(y)\Leftrightarrow\cNhd_{\lambda_{L}\circ\rho(H)}(\iota(x))=\cNhd_{\lambda_{L}\circ\rho(H)}(\iota(y)).

Since each set
S^{\prime}_{i}
is a maximal group of siblings, it is contracted to a single vertex
s^{\prime}_{i}
when applying
\rho
to
\lambda_{L}\circ\rho(H)
. On the other hand, applying
\rho
to
\lambda_{L}(H)
contracts each set
S_{i}
to a single vertex

s_{i}
by definition. Finally,
s^{\prime}_{i}
and
s^{\prime}_{j}
are adjacent if and only if

s_{i}
and
s_{j}
are adjacent for any pair
i,j\in I
, because
\rho
preserves adjacency between the groups of siblings it contracts.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

25
.

If
\lambda_{U}(G)
is isomorphic to
K_{2}
then
\lambda(G)
is either isomorphic to
K_{2}
or a single vertex graph. Thus we choose
V=\emptyset

and find that both sides of the equation

(3)

\rho\circ\lambda\circ\rho\circ\lambda_{U}(G)=\rho\circ\lambda_{V}\circ\rho\circ\lambda(G).

correspond to the single vertex graph.

Otherwise, let
U^{c}
be the complement of
U

in the set of leaves of
G
. Since this is a subset of leaves of
\lambda_{U}(G)
, and
\lambda_{U}(G)
is not isomorphic to
K_{2}
, we can apply

Lemma

26
with

and obtain

(4)

\rho\circ\lambda(G)=\rho\circ\lambda_{U^{c}}\circ\lambda_{U}(G)=\rho\circ\lambda_{U^{c}}\circ\rho\circ\lambda_{U}(G).

If
\lambda_{U^{c}}\circ\rho\circ\lambda_{U}(G)
is isomorphic to

\rho\circ\lambda(G)
is the single vertex

Finally, suppose that neither

Corollary 27
.

Let

be a subset of leaves of a connected graph

Recall that each step in the reduction of

\rho\circ\lambda

Induced Cycles and Graph Reduction

An
induced subgraph
of a graph
G
consists of a subset
V
of vertices of
G
together with all edges of
G
connecting vertices within
V
. An
induced
k
-cycle
is an induced subgraph that is isomorphic to a cycle graph with
k
vertices. In this section, we show that induced cycles with at least
4
vertices
are essentially preserved by reduction.

Theorem 30
.

For
k\geq 4
, a graph
G
contains an induced
k
-cycle if and
only if the reduction of
G
contains an induced
k
-cycle.

In particular, the sibling number and the tuft number have joint
symmetric distribution on the set of connected chordal graphs,
i.e., on graphs which do not contain an induced cycle with
4
or more
vertices.

Proof.

We show that induced cycles are preserved when removing leaves and
when contracting any maximal group of siblings to a single vertex.
The former is true, because an induced cycle of a graph cannot
contain a leaf.

To prove the latter, we first show that a group of siblings
S
and
an induced
k
-cycle
C
with
k\geq 4
can have at most one vertex
in common. Let
v
be such a vertex and let
u
and
w
be its
neighbors on the induced
k
-cycle. Because
k\geq 4
the
vertices
u
and
w
cannot be adjacent. Thus, neither of them can
be a sibling of
v
.

Let
G^{\prime}
be the graph obtained by contracting the vertices in a
maximal group of siblings
S
to a single vertex. If
S
and
C

have no vertices in common,
C
is also an induced cycle of
G^{\prime}
.
If
S
and
C
have a single vertex
v
in common, contracting the
group of siblings
S
to a single vertex
v^{\prime}
, the vertices of

(C\setminus\{v\})\cup\{v^{\prime}\}
form an induced cycle of
G^{\prime}
.

Conversely,
\lambda(G)
is an induced subgraph of
G
, and

\rho(G)
is isomorphic to the subgraph induced on one representative
of each maximal group of siblings. Thus, the reduction of
G
is
isomorphic to an induced subgraph of
G
.
∎
