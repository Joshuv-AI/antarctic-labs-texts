Zero-Shot Attribution for Large Language Models: A Distribution Testing Approach Thanks: The accompanying tool, available open source, can be found at https://github.com/uddaloksarkar/anubis .
Clément L. Canonne, Yash Pote, Uddalok Sarkar

Abstract
A growing fraction of all code is sampled from Large Language Models (LLMs). We investigate the problem of attributing code generated by language models using hypothesis testing to leverage established techniques and guarantees. Given a set of samples S S and a suspect model ℒ ∗ \mathcal{L}^{*} , our goal is to assess the likelihood of S S originating from ℒ ∗ \mathcal{L}^{*} . Due to the curse of dimensionality, this is intractable when only samples from the LLM are given: to circumvent this, we use both samples and density estimates from the LLM, a form of access commonly available. We introduce 𝖠𝗇𝗎𝖻𝗂𝗌 \mathsf{Anubis} , a zero-shot attribution tool that frames attribution as a distribution testing problem. Our experiments on a benchmark of code samples show that 𝖠𝗇𝗎𝖻𝗂𝗌 \mathsf{Anubis} achieves high AUROC scores ( ≥ 0.9 \geq 0.9 ) when distinguishing between LLMs like DeepSeek-Coder, CodeGemma, and Stable-Code using only ≈ 2000 \approx 2000 samples.

Introduction

Recent developments in large language models (LLMs) have led to a surge in machine-generated content across several domains, raising critical questions about attribution and accountability. As organizations increasingly deploy multiple LLMs for distinct use cases, distinguishing between outputs of different models has become essential for verifying compliance, tracking misuse, and understanding model-specific behaviors. This challenge is compounded by the quick evolution of LLMs, where new architectures and fine-tuned variants often produce text with overlapping statistical signatures.

A number of approaches have been proposed to solve the above challenge
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
. However, long-term solutions remain elusive, as for most detectors, model updates and adversarial attacks are able to circumvent attribution. A fundamental question persists:
What are the fundamental statistical limits on the performance of tests?

In this work, we re-frame attribution as a
distribution testing
problem. To connect the task of attribution to distribution testing, we think of LLMs as distributions over all possible token sequences. Given a dataset, our goal reduces to determine whether the samples originate from a reference distribution
\mathcal{D}^{*}
(e.g., DeepSeek-Coder) or an alternative
\mathcal{D}
(e.g., Stable-code).

This abstraction allows us to leverage theoretical tools from distribution testing while addressing two practical challenges:
(1)
Dimensionality
: Direct comparison of high-dimensional distributions (with support size
\approx(5\times 10^{4})^{d}
), where
5\times 10^{4}
is the size of the token set, and
d
is the sequence length, is infeasible with standard i.i.d. sample bounds
(
Valiant & Valiant 2011
)
.
(2)
Robustness
: LLM generated code undergoes post-processing (e.g., testing, formatting, dead code removal), violating idealized distributional assumptions.

To address this issue, we adopt a framework from
oracle-enhanced
distribution testing, a sub-field of testing that focuses on the use of more powerful
oracles
beyond sampling to obtain more data-efficient statistical tests. For instance, an
evaluation oracle

\mathsf{EVAL}(\mathcal{D},x)
provides, for a given data point
x
, the probability to see
x
occur under distribution
\mathcal{D}
. Combining this with the ability to sample (modeled as a
sampling oracle

\mathsf{SAMP}(\mathcal{D})
) can, in theory, lead to much more data-efficient algorithms, bypassing the curse of dimensionality
(
Canonne et al. 2014
;
Onak & Sun 2018
)
.

This paper can thus be seen as porting these theoretical results from the distribution testing literature to the practical, high-dimensional setting of attribution to assess whether these data efficiency gains translate in the real world. This approach presents, on the one hand, some very appealing features: it is completely agnostic to post-processing and does not require any ad hoc method such as watermarking. On the other hand, it does present some significant challenges: one may wonder whether this domain-agnostic, abstracted approach may have to pay a stark efficiency price due to its very generality. Moreover, as our paper demonstrates, a significant amount of work is required to translate the theoretical guarantees obtained in the distribution testing literature (where constant factors are typically ignored, and simplifying modeling assumptions are common) to our practical setting, where the constant factors do have a significant impact on the result, and robustness to deviations from an idealized model is a necessity.

Related work

There have been a number of works that have studied the problem of identifying LLM-generated content
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
. These works can be broadly divided into three categories: (1) watermarking technology: this approach involves embedding a watermark in the text, which is later extracted by a watermark detector to determine the origin
(
Gu et al. 2022
;
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023
;
Tang et al. 2023
)
, (2) neural-network based detectors: in this approach a classifier is trained for the task of attribution
(
Uchendu et al. 2024
;
Kumarage et al. 2023
)
, and finally (3) zero-shot statistical approach
(
Mitchell et al. 2023
;
Bao et al. 2023
)
. Our work falls into the third category, where we use distribution testing techniques for the attribution task.

Preliminiaries

In this work, we are interested in discrete probability distributions over a finite domain
\Omega
, which we identify using their probability mass function (pmf), a function
\mathcal{P}:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
\sum_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{P}(x)=1
. For a subset
S\subseteq\Omega
, we let
\mathcal{P}(S)=\sum_{x\in S}\mathcal{P}(x)
. Throughout the paper, we will use calligraphic letters to denote distributions, e.g.,
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L}
.

We define notions of distance between probability distributions as follows: The
\ell_{1}
distance between two distributions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{*}
over
\Omega
is defined as
\|\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}^{*}\|_{1}=\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\mathcal{P}(x)-\mathcal{P}^{*}(x)|
; and the
\ell_{\infty}
distance is defined as
\|\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}^{*}\|_{\infty}=\max_{x\in\Sigma^{n}}|\mathcal{P}(x)-\mathcal{P}^{*}(x)|
.
Given a set
S\subseteq\Omega
with
\mathcal{P}(S)>0
, the
conditional distribution
of
\mathcal{P}
on
S
, denoted by
{\mathcal{P}}_{|{S}}
, is a distribution such that for any
x\in S
,
{\mathcal{P}}_{|{S}}(x)=\mathcal{P}(x)/\mathcal{P}(S)
.

Oracles for distributions

We will use
oracles
to formally model interaction with distributions. Hence, to draw independent samples from a distribution
\mathcal{P}
, we call the
\mathsf{SAMP}
oracle. The
\mathsf{EVAL}
(for
evaluation
) oracle provides a more powerful type of access, allowing exact evaluation of the probability of a domain element in a distribution, i.e.,
\mathsf{EVAL}(\mathcal{P},x)=\mathcal{P}(x)
.

In many cases, the exact probabilities are not available, either due to issues arising from finite precision representation or due to the computational complexity of computing exact probabilities. Hence, it is often more realistic to allow for
approximate
evaluation oracles, which return multiplicative approximations of the probability
\mathcal{P}(x)
.

Definition 2.1

(Approximate
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracle
(
Batu et al. 2005
;
Canonne & Rubinfeld 2014
;
Bhattacharyya et al. 2020
)
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a distribution over
\Omega
,
x\in\Omega
, and
\eta\in[0,1]
. An approximate
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracle takes as input
x
and returns an
\eta
-estimate of
\mathcal{P}(x)
, i.e.,
\mathsf{EVAL}(\mathcal{P},x)\in[(1-\eta)\mathcal{P}(x),(1+\eta)\mathcal{P}(x)]
. The oracle is deterministic and will return the same value for a fixed input
\mathcal{P}
and
\eta
.

Distribution testing

Distribution testing is a well-studied area of study, concerned with questions of the following type: given distribution
\mathcal{P}
, and a property
1

1

A property is a non-empty collection of distributions.

\Pi
, the goal is to decide whether
\mathcal{P}
satisfies
\Pi
, or is
far
from satisfying
\Pi
. Formally, for a distance parameter
\varepsilon_{2}
, a confidence parameter
\delta
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, a testing algorithm returns (1)
accept
if
\mathcal{P}\in\Pi
, or else (2)
reject
if
\mathcal{P}
is at distance at least
\varepsilon_{2}
from
\Pi
(“is
\varepsilon_{2}

\ell_{1}

\varepsilon_{1}
-closeness to a given distribution
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. Hence
\Pi=\{\mathcal{P}\mid\|\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}^{*}\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\}
.
Hence, for input distributions
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{*}
, and parameters
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
and
\delta
, the test returns (1)
accept
if
\|\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}^{*}\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{1}
, or else (2)
reject
if
\|\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}^{*}\|_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}
.

We will rely on the celebrated Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality, which provides a bound on the
\ell_{\infty}
distance of an empirical cumulative distribution of samples from the distribution that the samples are drawn from. The optimal constant in the DKW inequality was found by
Massart 1990
, and we use it in our analysis.

Lemma 2.2

(
Dvoretzky et al. 1956
,
Massart 1990
)
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
be the empirical distribution generated by
m

\mathcal{P}
over domain
[n]
. We have that:

\Pr\left[\max_{i\in[n]}\left|\sum_{j\in[i]}\mathcal{P}(j)-\sum_{j\in[i]}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}(j)\right|\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq 2e^{-2m\varepsilon^{2}}
.

Language Models as Distributions

We start by defining terms related to the domain of language models.
A
token
is an integer from the set
\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}=\{1,\ldots,|\Sigma_{tok}|\}
, and a sequence of
n
tokens is represented as
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\ldots\sigma_{n}
or
\sigma
when
n
is clear from context.
The
k
-length prefix of
\sigma
, denoted as
\sigma_{<k+1}
, refers to the sequence
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\ldots\sigma_{k}
. We will use
\sigma_{<1}
for the empty sequence.
A
word
is a symbol from a set of symbols called a
dictionary
, denoted by
\Sigma_{w}
. Language models are distributions over sequences of words called
text
. Formally, a language model
\mathcal{L}:\Sigma_{w}^{*}\to[0,1]
is a distribution that assigns a probability
\mathcal{L}(s)
to every
s\in\Sigma_{w}^{*}
. We define
token distribution

\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}:\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}^{*}\to[0,1]
that assigns a probability
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\sigma)
to every token sequence
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}^{*}
.

For state-of-the-art LLMs, next-token prediction is the standard sampling strategy. In the next token prediction, sequence
\sigma
is sampled from the distribution such that the probability of the
k^{th}
token in
\sigma
depends only on the preceding
k
-1 tokens, denoted as
\sigma_{<k}
.
This allows for left-to-right generation where the model samples tokens starting from
\sigma_{1}
to
\sigma_{n}
, predicting one token at a time conditional on the prefix.
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\cdot|\sigma_{<i})
denotes the marginal distribution of the
i^{th}
token conditioned on the prefix
\sigma_{<i}
. The chain rule of probability lets us factorize
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\sigma)
(the probability of a
n
-token sequence
\sigma
) as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\sigma)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\sigma_{i}|\sigma_{<i})
.
\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}
contains a special token
\mathtt{EOS}
to indicate the end-of-string, which is either the last token or is followed by another
\mathtt{EOS}
. Formally,
\forall_{i\in[n-1]}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\sigma_{i+1}=\mathtt{EOS}|\sigma_{i}=\mathtt{EOS})=1
.

A tokenizer, consisting of two functions,
\mathsf{enc}
, and
\mathsf{dec}
, serves as an interface between the token space and the word space. We adopt the formal definition of a tokenizer as presented in
Gastaldi et al. 2024
.

Definition 2.3

(Tokenizer)
.

A tokenizer from dictionary
\Sigma_{w}
to token-set
\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}
is defined as a tuple of deterministic mappings,
\mathsf{tok}=(\mathsf{enc},\mathsf{dec})
, known as the
encoder
and
decoder
, respectively. The encoder,
\mathsf{enc}:\Sigma_{w}\to\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}^{*}
, converts a word into a token sequence, while the decoder,
\mathsf{dec}:\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}\to\Sigma_{w}
, converts one token to one word. We can extend the definition of encoder and decoder to sequences of words and tokens, respectively, as
\mathsf{enc}^{*}:\Sigma_{w}^{*}\to\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}^{*}
and
\mathsf{dec}^{*}:\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}^{*}\to\Sigma_{w}^{*}
.
A tokenizer is called
bijective
if
\mathsf{enc}^{*}
is a one-to-one mapping, and
\mathsf{dec}^{*}=(\mathsf{enc}^{*})^{-1}
over
\mathsf{enc}^{*}(\Sigma_{w}^{*})
.

The process of generating text from an LLM is primarily based on drawing samples from the token distribution
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}
. The tokenizer, specifically the decoder
\mathsf{dec}
, transforms the sampled token sequence into the output text, eventually defining the distribution
\mathcal{L}
of the LLM.
We use Greek lowercase (e.g.,
\sigma
) for token sequences and lowercase letters (e.g.,
s
) for texts.

4
\mathsf{Anubis}
: a practical attribution tester

To address the problem of attribution within the framework of distribution testing, we adapt
Algorithm

1
to construct our attribution tester
\mathsf{Anubis}
.
\mathsf{Anubis}
evaluates whether or not a substantial number of code samples in a set
S
was generated by a target language model,
\mathcal{L}^{*}
. Given
\mathcal{L}^{*}
and
S
, with high confidence,
\mathsf{Anubis}
outputs
accept
if most samples are generated by
\mathcal{L}^{*}
, and
reject
otherwise. In distribution testing terms, this reduces to determining whether
S
comes from a distribution close to or distant from
\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

The primary challenges of designing
\mathsf{Anubis}
are (1) the infeasibility of directly accessing the probabilities
\mathcal{L}^{*}(s)
, which are required for the
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracle. We circumvent this by designing the
\mathsf{EVAL^{+}}
algorithm that estimates the probability of a sample
s
generated by a language model
\mathcal{L}^{*}
.
(2) Determining the number of buckets
\ell
is a significant bottleneck because, ideally, this requires knowledge about the domain size of
\mathcal{L}^{*}
. We circumvent this with the Monte Carlo approach, wherein we draw samples to estimate the number of buckets while incurring some error, which is accounted for in the analysis. To address this, we introduce a parameter called the
leftover fraction

\tau_{\Delta_{0}}
, which ensures that no more than a
\tau_{\Delta_{0}}
fraction of the samples fall into the leftover bucket
\Delta_{0}
.

4.1
Designing
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracles for language models

The
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracle is designed to estimate the probability of a text being generated by a language model. Specifically, given a model
\mathcal{L}
and a text
s
,
\mathsf{EVAL}
computes the probability
\mathcal{L}(s)
that
s
was produced by
\mathcal{L}
. However, designing an
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracle for language models is challenging due to the non-invertible nature of tokenizers.

Non-invertibility of Tokenizers

Tokenizers are typically non-injective functions
(
Sennrich 2015
)
, meaning different token sequences, such as
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}
and
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}
from the token-set
\Sigma_{\mathsf{tok}}
, can map to the same text
s_{1}s_{2}
, resulting in a collision. For instance, the tokenizer used by
GPT2

(
Radford et al. 2019
)
maps both token sequences
64\circ 14512\circ 65
and
64\circ 0\circ 28\circ 65
to the text ‘a!=b’.
This non-invertibility complicates the direct computation of
\mathcal{L}(s)
. While the
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracle provides easy access to the marginal probabilities
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\sigma_{i}|\sigma_{<i})
for a token sequence
\sigma
, calculating
\mathcal{L}(s)
from these probabilities remains a non-trivial task unless these collisions are known in advance.

To address this, we propose the algorithm
\mathsf{EVAL^{+}}
, which leverages the
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracle for the marginal probabilities
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\sigma_{i}|\sigma_{<i})
to estimate the likelihood that
s
was generated by the language model
\mathcal{L}
.
To evaluate
\mathcal{L}(s)
we provide
\mathsf{EVAL^{+}}
with the tokenizer
\mathsf{tok}
used by the model
\mathcal{L}
, token distribution
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}
, the token sequence
\sigma:=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\ldots\sigma_{n}
such that
\sigma=\mathsf{enc}(s)
, and the depth parameter
d
. If
\sigma
’s length is less than
d
, the algorithm iterates over the tokens, computing the probability of each
\sigma_{i}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{tok}}(\cdot|\sigma_{<i})
using the
\mathsf{EVAL}
oracle. However, as discussed earlier, the tokenization process can lead to multiple token sequences that decode the same text. To account for this, we introduce the function
\mathsf{getCollision}
that takes a tokenizer
\mathsf{tok}
, a token sequence
\sigma
, and the depth parameter
d
as input and returns the set of token sequences of length
d
that decode to the same text as
\mathsf{dec}(\sigma)
.
\mathsf{EVAL^{+}}
then computes the probability of each token sequence in the set returned by
\mathsf{getCollision}
and adds it to the probability of the token in the token sequence to improve the estimate of the probability
\mathcal{L}(s)
.
If
\sigma

\mathsf{EVAL^{+}}
splits the sequence into two parts and recursively processes each part.

5
Experiments

To assess the practical effectiveness of our algorithm, we implemented
\mathsf{Anubis}
to test attribution on a dataset of code samples generated by LLMs. Our goal is to investigate the following research questions:

RQ1
For a target model
\mathcal{L}^{*}
, parameters
\mathsf{LB}\%
,
\mathsf{UB}\%
, and a set of code samples
S
, how accurately can
\mathsf{Anubis}
determine whether 1) fewer than
\mathsf{LB}\%
, or 2) more than
\mathsf{UB}\%
of the samples were drawn from
\mathcal{L}^{*}
?

RQ2
How does the performance of
\mathsf{Anubis}
change with varying number of samples?

Models

As our objective revolves around testing attribution in a set of code samples, we focus on popular code LLMs.
In our experimental setup, we employed three LLMs, each chosen for a specific role within the setup. The selected versions of the models were of similar size, with parameter counts ranging from 1 to 3 billion.
(1) We used the 3-billion-parameter version of
stable-code

(
Pinnaparaju et al. 2023
)
to generate the primary code dataset.
(2) The 1.3-billion-parameter version of
deepseek-coder

(
Guo et al. 2024
)
and (3) the 2-billion-parameter version of
codegemma

(
Team et al. 2024
)
were employed as the target LLMs. These models were chosen as target models because of their smaller size which allowed us to run experiments more efficiently.

Datasets

The primary requirement for our evaluation is the availability of code samples from different sources. To generate the code datasets, we adopted a generate-and-sanitize approach. Specifically, we used prompts from the
HumanEval
dataset
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
, which contains 164 Python programming problems, each accompanied by test cases. These problems require knowledge of basic mathematics and can be solved with a few lines of code. For each prompt, we queried three LLMs to generate 2K samples, resulting in three datasets, each comprising 164 tasks with 2K samples per task. Additionally, we constructed an ‘Adversarial Set‘ by generating equivalent datasets using the target LLMs.
To evaluate
\mathsf{Anubis}
’s robustness in attribution, we simulated contamination by replacing a fraction of the original samples with adversarial ones. For each contamination level
\gamma\%
(
\gamma\in\{0,10,\ldots,90\}
) and each fresh dataset, we randomly replaced
\gamma\%
of the samples with samples from the adversarial set. We used these contaminated datasets to assess
\mathsf{Anubis}
’s performance in detecting the presence of samples from the target LLMs.

Comparison

We compared
\mathsf{Anubis}
against the SOTA zero-shot statistical approach for identifying LLM-generated text:
\mathsf{detectGPT}

(
Mitchell et al. 2023
)
.
\mathsf{detectGPT}
operates on the principle that LLM-generated samples tend to be drawn from the local maxima of the target LLM’s distribution, whereas text generated from other sources may originate from a flatter region of the same target LLM’s distribution.
Since
\mathsf{detectGPT}
is designed for use on individual code samples, we extended it for dataset-level analysis.
\mathsf{detectGPT}
computes a score called perturbation discrepancy for each sample, using which it takes a binary decision for that sample. To make
\mathsf{detectGPT}
work for the dataset, we aggregated the scores for all samples to evaluate the overall attribution for the dataset.

Evaluation metric

Following the setup in
Mitchell et al. 2023
, we used the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) as our evaluation metric. AUROC measures the ability of the algorithm to distinguish between two classes, in this case, samples generated by the target LLMs and samples that are not. An AUROC of 1 indicates perfect performance, while 0.5 is equivalent to random guessing. In our experiments, we applied
\mathsf{Anubis}
to determine whether the proportion of samples generated by the target LLMs within a set
S
was below
\mathsf{LB}\%
or above
\mathsf{UB}\%
. Accordingly, our positive class was defined as sets containing more than
\mathsf{UB}\%
of samples from the target LLMs, while the negative class was sets containing fewer than
\mathsf{LB}\%
of samples from the target LLMs.

Hyperparameters

The key hyperparameters of
\mathsf{Anubis}
are: (1) the leftover fraction
\tau_{\Delta_{0}}
, which is fixed at 0.05 in all experiments related to
Figs.

2
and
3
. In the appendix, we present an additional experiment to demonstrate the low sensitivity of
\mathsf{Anubis}
to
\tau_{\Delta_{0}}
.
(2) To maintain the algorithm’s tractability, we set the depth parameter
d
in
\mathsf{EVAL^{+}}
to 2.

We also studied the effect of the number of samples in the dataset on the performance of

\mathsf{Anubis}
