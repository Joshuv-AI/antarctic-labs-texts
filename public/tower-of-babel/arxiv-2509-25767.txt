Galton’s Law of Mediocrity: Why Large Language Models Regress to the Mean and Fail at Creativity in Advertising
Matt Keon *, Aabid Karim *, Bhoomika Lohana *, Abdul Karim *, Thai Nguyen, Tara Hamilton, Ali Abbas

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) generate fluent text yet often default to safe, generic phrasing, raising doubts about their ability to handle creativity. We formalize this tendency as a Galton-style regression to the mean in language and evaluate it using a creativity stress test in advertising concepts. When ad ideas were simplified step by step, creative features such as metaphors, emotions, and visual cues disappeared early, while factual content remained, showing that models favor high-probability information. When asked to regenerate from simplified inputs, models produced longer outputs with lexical variety but failed to recover the depth and distinctiveness of the originals. We combined quantitative comparisons with qualitative analysis, which revealed that the regenerated texts often appeared novel but lacked true originality. Providing ad-specific cues such as metaphors, emotional hooks and visual markers improved alignment and stylistic balance, though outputs still relied on familiar tropes. Taken together, the findings show that without targeted guidance, LLMs drift towards mediocrity in creative tasks; structured signals can partially counter this tendency and point towards pathways for developing creativity-sensitive models.

1
Introduction

Over the past decade, LLMs have undergone rapid and transformative development. Ground-breaking innovations such as Word2Vec
(
Mikolov et al., 2013
)
, Transformers
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
, and scaling laws
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
have reshaped natural language processing (NLP), problem solving, question answering, and computer vision tasks
(
Li et al., 2025
)
,
(
Gunter et al., 2024
)
,
(
Team et al., 2023
)
.
However, these models consistently struggle in one critical area: genuine creativity
(
Ismayilzada et al., 2024
)
, because they are trained to maximise next-token likelihood and thus privilege high-probability continuations and suppress rare or unconventional ones
(
Lu et al., 2024
)
. This bias explains what
(
Wenger and Kenett, 2025
)
call creative homogeneity: even diverse models converge on strikingly similar responses in divergent thinking tasks, a pattern they warn could narrow rather than expand human idea space. Similarly,
(
Anderson et al., 2024
)
report that while LLMs generate grammatically polished narratives, they offer little variation in perspective or style, which constrains expression when used as co-creators. Moreover, the assumption that larger datasets inherently yield deeper insights has not held true; as training corpora expand, this statistical bias becomes more pronounced
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
,
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
,
(
Muennighoff et al., 2023
)
.

We call this tendency Galton’s Law of Mediocrity. First introduced by
(
Francis Galton’s, 1886
)
, the law describes the statistical principle of regression towards the mean. When unusually extreme traits appear in a population, for example, exceptional height or intelligence, they are unlikely to persist across generations and instead drift back towards the population average. We adapt this principle to LLMs: because they are trained to maximise next-token likelihood, they privilege high-frequency, statistically probable patterns while suppressing rare or unconventional ones. Consequently, even as models scale with larger datasets, their outputs converge on safe and predictable phrases, leaving little room for genuinely novel or creative expression.
(
Pasquinelli and Joler, 2021
)
argue that this tendency reflects a deeper limitation: models do not invent but remix dominant cultural patterns, which can give the illusion of creativity without genuine novelty. Empirical studies reinforce this point.
(
Haase et al., 2025
)
find that the same model can swing between highly novel and bland responses depending on the prompt, while
(
Kumar et al., 2025
)
report that LLMs generate many ideas quickly in terms of fluency but fall short on originality and real-world usefulness. Together, these findings suggest that scale brings polish, but not the kind of originality that sustains long-run creativity. Alignment efforts add another layer to this regression.
(
Mohammadi, 2024
)
shows that reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), while improving safety and politeness, lowers entropy and reduces surprising continuations, revealing a trade-off between reliability and generative richness.
(
Franceschelli and Musolesi, 2024
)
further argues that much of what appears as creativity in LLMs reflects surface fluency rather than true conceptual innovation.

From Ada Lovelace’s claim that machines “originate nothing” to Turing’s suggestion that novelty might emerge through recombination, the question of whether machines can truly generate new ideas has remained central for nearly two centuries. This long-standing debate becomes especially vivid in advertising, a domain where emotional resonance, cultural relevance, and originality are everything. Unlike abstract tests of narrative or brainstorming, advertising provides concrete examples of how a single metaphor, image, or slogan can define success, making it an ideal setting to probe whether LLMs truly generate creativity or regress towards mediocrity. Classic campaigns like Nike’s “Just Do It”
(
Peters, 2024
)
, Apple’s “Think Different”
(
Fou, 2011
)
, and Volkswagen’s “Lemon”
(
Michael, 2025
)
are remembered not because they recombined stock phrases, but because they blended novel narratives and striking images in ways that spoke to the time. When we ask today’s LLMs to create an eco
-
friendly slogan, however, they often reply with something generic like “Go Green!” instead of inventing culturally nuanced metaphors. Even when models produce surprising combinations, such as an ad that mixes coffee and astronomy, the result often lacks value without human interpretation. This reflects what
(
Tian et al., 2024
)
identify in narrative generation, where LLMs struggle with emotional depth, and what
(
Gu and Krenn, 2024
)
observe in scientific brainstorming, where models contribute only moderately novel ideas unless curated by humans. Turning a raw output into a culturally resonant campaign like Starbucks’ Galaxy Blend therefore still requires a human creative director. To illustrate this gap more concretely, we focus on two famous car ads as our motivation, that stood out: Volkswagen’s “Darth Vader” commercial
(
Sanburn, 2015
)
and Volvo Trucks’ “Epic Split”
(
Contagious, 2014
)
. The former pairs a family sedan’s remote
-
start feature with a child in a Darth Vader costume, creating a humorous twist; the latter uses an bold stunt to show a truck’s stability. These advertisements demonstrate how much success depends on a single visual joke or stunt, an aspect that current language models often fail to include. These two advertisements, along with still images are provided in the Appendix
A
for reference. That’s why we chose advertising as our evaluation setting: it exposes the gap between the lively, surprising content humans create and the cautious outputs from today’s LLMs.

Our goal is to understand, in a systematic way, why current LLMs regress towards the average in creative domains such as advertising. We pose three questions:

1.

When we gradually simplify ads, which part lasts longest, the generic product details or the unusual creative highlights?

2.

If we give the model only the bare facts, can it fill in the creative flourishes or does it fall back on clichés?

3.

Can explicit creative cues (“markers”) improve the recovery of originality during regeneration?

To answer them, we design a structured, two-phase empirical study to be conducted using our 55mV idea database (1000+ advertising ideas). The database covers a wide range of advertising concepts that show key aspects of creativity, such as telling a story, creating an emotional connection, sparking visual imagination, and using short, memorable slogans. We run a staged forgetting experiment, asking a state
-
of
-
the
-
art LLM to simplify each ad in several steps and measuring how quickly creative content disappears. Second, we invert the process: starting from the compressed outputs of phase 1, we prompt the same model to expand them back into full ads and analyse the kinds of details it invents or omits.

Across all SOTA models, we find that forgetting erases creative content first; expansions produce surface-level novelty but fail to restore originality; and marker-driven prompting (prompts include creative markers/concepts) improves diversity by reintroducing visual, emotional, and slogan-like cues, though models still recycle familiar tropes. Our contributions are following:

•

We formalise Galton’s Law of Mediocrity for LLMs and identify the factors that enforce it.

•

We propose a two-phase creativity stress test, combining controlled forgetting and expansion, and introduce a hybrid evaluation framework that integrates quantitative metrics (cosine similarity, METEOR, entropy, n-gram uniqueness) with qualitative human + LLM judgment. The details of these metrics are provided in Section
2.3
.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows: Section 2 describes our experimental design, Section 3 presents the results, and Section 4 discusses implications and future directions.

2
Methodology

In advertising, creativity is not just about filling space with words or visuals; it is about producing ideas that stand out, surprise, and connect emotionally with audiences. Yet, large language models often default to safe, predictable outputs, demonstrating creative homogeneity that produces ideas that appear creative but lack the diversity and novelty found in human creativity
(
Wenger and Kenett, 2025
)
. To study this limitation, our experiments begin with the 55mV Idea Database, which contains more than 1000 original advertising concepts selected for their creative richness. These concepts included metaphorical phrasing, emotional tone, visual imagination, and cultural specificity, characteristics that typically challenge generative models. Representative examples from the dataset are presented in the Appendix
C.1
. All model generations are produced using a consistent sampling configuration (fixed temperature and sampling settings) to ensure comparability. We evaluate five state-of-the-art LLM families: GPT-4o, GPT-5 (main and thinking variants), GPT-OSS-120B, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4. The experiment is structured in two phases: Phase 1 (forgetting), where ads are contracted through staged reductions, and Phase 2 (expansion), where models attempt to regenerate creative detail from compressed inputs.

2.1
Phase 1 Forgetting:

In this phase, we prompt LLMs to compress original advertising ideas through three levels of reduction: mild, moderate, and extreme. At each level, the model rewrites the ad with fewer details, always starting from the original ad concept. As shown in Appendix
B
, Figure
B1
, the prompts do not specify which details should be removed or retained. This open-ended design lets us observe what the model naturally chooses to keep and what it lets go of, providing insight into its own forgetting tendencies.

•

Mild reduction (
\approx
35%):
most of the ad’s structure and creative features remain, with some details removed.

•

Moderate reduction (
\approx
70%):
only the central idea is preserved, while most creative layers are removed.

•

Extreme reduction (
\approx
95%):
the ad is compressed into one or two sentences containing only the bare core message.

This process is repeated across three independent runs per model to capture both consistent forgetting patterns and natural variation. The outcome is three versions of each original ad (three reductions), which form the dataset for our later quantitative and qualitative analyses. The goal of this phase is to test whether, as ads are simplified, creative elements such as metaphors, humor, and emotional cues are preserved or disappear faster than the factual product message.

2.2
Phase 2 Expansion:

Phase 2 reverses the forgetting procedure by testing whether lost creativity can be rebuilt through step-by-step expansion. This process follows a chained expansion design. Starting from the extreme forgetting versions produced in Phase 1, each advertisement is expanded sequentially: Mild → Moderate → Extreme. In this setup, the Phase 1 extreme output serves as the base concept for the mild expansion; the mild version is then used as input to produce the moderate expansion; and the moderate version in turn feeds into the extreme expansion. At each stage, the newly generated text becomes the input for the next, creating a chained process rather than restarting from the original. This design allows us to observe whether creativity re-emerges gradually as details are layered back in.

The same set of models used in Phase 1 are evaluated under identical prompting conditions, and the same four metrics are applied: cosine similarity for semantic fidelity, METEOR for surface overlap, entropy for lexical diversity, and 4-gram uniqueness for structural novelty. In every case, the original ads serve as a fixed benchmark, providing a reference point for recovery.

•

Plain Expansion:
The model receives the extreme forgotten version of an ad as input and expands it across mild, moderate, and extreme stages without additional guidance. This establishes a baseline for how much creativity can be recovered autonomously. An illustrative example of this process is provided in Appendix
C.3
, and the corresponding prompt is shown in Appendix
B
.

•

Marker-Driven Expansion:
Alongside the extreme forgotten version, the model is given 3–4 creative markers extracted from the original ad. These markers are short, ad-specific phrases (e.g., emotional cues, metaphors, visual details, brand references, or slogans) extracted by an LLM with a structured prompt. Sample list of markers is provided in Appendix
C.5
. Unlike a generic list, each set of markers is unique to its advertisement. They are introduced progressively: one or two at mild expansion, most at moderate, and all at extreme. This setup tests whether explicit creative cues help the model restore lost originality rather than defaulting to generic filler. An illustrative example of this process is included in Appendix
C.4
, and the corresponding prompt is shown in Appendix
B
.

2.3
Evaluation Metrics

Evaluating creativity requires multiple perspectives, since no single metric captures its full scope. We consider four complementary measures, cosine similarity, METEOR, entropy, and 4-gram uniqueness, chosen to reflect semantic fidelity, surface overlap, lexical diversity, and structural novelty. Together, these measures provide a multidimensional view of how creativity degrades during forgetting and re-emerges during expansion. For each phase, metric values are first computed for every individual advertisement at each level of forgetting or expansion (mild, moderate, extreme). These values are then averaged across the 1,045 ads in the dataset. Finally, we report the mean ± standard deviation across three independent runs per model, with the original ad serving as the baseline for comparison. The detailed computation of these metrics is provided in Appendix
D

1.

Cosine Similarity:
We compute cosine similarity between embedding representations of the generated text and the original advertisement to assess semantic fidelity. This indicates whether the core idea is preserved or diverges. In forgetting, lower similarity signals semantic drift, while higher values indicate retention. In expansion, increases suggest recovery of the original concept, whereas decreases imply elaboration without alignment.

2.

METEOR
: METEOR measures surface-level correspondence, accounting for synonyms and word order flexibility
(
Banerjee and Lavie, 2005
)
. Unlike BLEU, which penalizes variation, METEOR tolerates creative rephrasings while still capturing recognizable overlap with the original. We use it to assess whether compressed or expanded texts maintain a recognizable connection to the original creative concept, even when expressed in different wording.

3.

Entropy
We compute token-level entropy over the generated text to quantify lexical diversity. Higher entropy reflects richer and more varied word use, while lower entropy reflects reduced variety and repetitive phrasing. We use entropy because diversity in word choice is a key marker of creative expression.

4.

4-gram Uniqueness
This metric measures the proportion of novel 4-grams in the generated text relative to the original, capturing structural novelty at the lexical level. We use it to assess whether the model re-expresses ideas with fresh phrasing instead of relying on repeated or conservative wording. We use 4-grams because they are long enough to capture meaningful patterns of phrasing, but not so long that they become too rare to compare reliably.

Entropy and 4-gram uniqueness reveal the same dynamic in reverse. Entropy declines steadily from 5.3 in the originals to 3.3–4.2 at the extreme, showing how vocabulary variety eroded as detail was stripped away. GPT-5 Thinking and GPT-oss-120b hold on to more lexical diversity, while GPT-4 collapses most severely, producing the most repetitive phrasing. In contrast, 4-gram uniqueness rises sharply, climbing from near zero to 0.99 for all models at the extreme. This indicated that while the compressed outputs looked new on the surface, they were only superficially novel, paraphrased shells stripped of metaphor, humor, and sensory richness. The mild stage again shows differences: GPT-oss-120b departs earliest, introducing new phrasing almost immediately, while Gemini stays closer to the original. But by the moderate and extreme levels, every model saturates near the ceiling, suggesting a universal drift towards reworded but flattened text.

Taken together, these quantitative patterns provide clear evidence for creativity regression through forgetting. The models begin differently, some shorten conservatively, others paraphrase aggressively, but as forgetting increases, they all converge on the same endpoint: semantically thinned, lexically repetitive, and stylistically generic outputs. Instead of compressing in ways that sharpen or distill creative ideas, forgetting consistently drives the models towards mediocrity. Still, at the extreme stage, subtle differences remain: GPT-5 Thinking and GPT-OSS-120B retain slightly higher similarity and lexical diversity than the other models. This raised a key question: did these traces of richness provide an advantage when models are asked to expand ideas again? This naturally led to the question of whether better retention during forgetting enabled stronger expansions.

3.1.1
Phase1: Qualitative Analysis

To complement the quantitative metrics, we conducted a qualitative marker-based analysis of 25 advertisements sampled from the dataset. For each ad, we extracted 3–4 short “creative markers”, phrases capturing distinctive features such as metaphor, emotional tone, visual detail, brand-specific cues, or slogan-like phrasing taken as a creative hook that conveys an ad’s originality as shown in Figure
2
. To carry this out, we used a constrained prompt that identifies 3–4 markers per ad, each limited to ten words or fewer to maintain precision and comparability. We then tracked the survival of these markers across mild, moderate, and extreme forgetting, using a hybrid evaluation: an LLM provides initial judgments (present, partly present, absent), which were manually verified for accuracy. The sample chosen ensured consistency while maintaining scalability. This hybrid approach drew on established frameworks such as MT-Bench
(
Zheng et al., 2023
)
and recent work on LLMs as qualitative judges
(
Chiang and yi Lee, 2023
)
, adapted here for creative advertising content.

Figure

From the original text, markers such as “flipping the script on traditional vegetarian dishes” (metaphor), “models are the vegetarian dishes” (visual), and “vegetarian, but make it fashion” (slogan) are extracted as the key creative elements. These markers provide a consistent reference point for assessing what survives as detail is stripped away.

Together, these qualitative findings reinforce the quantitative patterns. Forgetting does not selectively distill creativity into sharper form but instead erases its most imaginative components, emotion, metaphor, and vivid imagery, while leaving behind only the safe, formulaic anchors of brand identity. This structured collapse consolidates our hypothesis that LLMs outputs under compression regress towards mediocrity.

3.2
Phase 2: Expansion

3.2.1
Plain Expansion: Quantitative Analysis

In Phase 2 we test whether the creative detail lost during forgetting can be rebuilt. Expansions began from the extreme forgotten versions produced in Phase 1, which served as the starting point for regeneration. The baseline for evaluation was always the original advertisement, and at each expansion level the regenerated text was compared back to this original. The same four metrics used in Phase 1 are applied here: for each advertisement we calculated values at every level, then averaged these across all 1,045 ads. In the main text we report the results from the final run, while Appendix
D.3
Table
D2
presents the mean ± SD across all three runs. Standard deviations remained below 0.02 for nearly all metrics, confirming that the observed patterns were stable and not an artifact of sampling variance.

As shown in the Table
3
and Figure
4
, cosine similarity and METEOR improved a little from mild to moderate expansion, but remained low across all models at the extreme level, with scores between 0.22 (Claude Sonnet-4) and 0.29 (GPT-4o, GPT-5 Thinking, GPT-oss-120b). METEOR values follow the same pattern, plateauing at 0.13–0.19 depending on the model, again well below the benchmark of 1.0. Together, these two metrics indicate that expansions introduce detail but recover only a fraction of the original meaning and surface overlap.

Entropy, by contrast, exceeded the original baseline, rising from 5.31 in the ads to between 5.60 (Gemini-2.5 Pro) and 6.20 (GPT-5 Thinking) at the extreme level. This consistent increase across models signaled richer lexical variety in the expanded outputs than in the source ads. Similarly, n-gram uniqueness reached saturation, with nearly all models producing values of 0.99–1.00 at the extreme stage, meaning that the regenerated ads are composed almost entirely of novel phrasing. At first glance this looked like strong originality, but when read together with the low cosine similarity and METEOR, it showed that the novelty is mostly in surface wording. The expansions sound fresh, yet they do not recover much of the original meaning and creative content.

Taken together, these results highlighted a consistent quantitative profile: expansions generated outputs that are lexically varied and almost entirely novel in form, but their semantic and surface alignment with the originals remained limited. Within this overall pattern, differences between models were still visible. As shown in Figure
4
, GPT-5 Thinking and GPT-OSS-120B performed better, higher on similarity and overlap, while also producing more lexically diverse expansions. GPT-4o and GPT-5 Main follow at a mid-range level, whereas Claude and Gemini consistently trail behind. Appendix
E
includes a cross-model example where expansions of the same advertisement are compared, illustrating how these quantitative differences appeared qualitatively. These outcomes reinforced the Phase 1 observation: models that retained slightly more information during forgetting were also better positioned to expand the original creative ideas.

3.3
Comparison: Plain vs. Marker-Driven Expansion

When comparing the two expansion strategies side by side, a consistent advantage emerged for the marker-driven condition. Cosine similarity and METEOR scores were systematically higher under marker guidance, indicating that expansions anchored by creative markers remained more semantically aligned with the originals and preserved more recognizable overlap. Entropy was also higher under marker-driven expansion, reflecting richer lexical variety without simple repetition. At the same time, n-gram uniqueness was lower with markers, showing that while vocabulary broadens, phrasing remained focused around key cues rather than pushing towards maximal surface novelty. These effects were consistent across runs. By contrast, plain expansion drifted towards surface-level novelty, showing higher n-gram uniqueness but lower cosine similarity and METEOR, yielding lexically diverse outputs that are less connected to the original ad ideas. These results suggested that providing models with structured creative cues reduced the burden of unconstrained generation, allowing them to recover both alignment and diversity more effectively.

3.4
Phase2: Qualitative Analysis

While the quantitative metrics in Phase 2 established how much the regenerated ads diverged from the originals, they leave open a key question: what kinds of creativity are the models actually producing? Cosine similarity and METEOR scores tell us about semantic alignment, and entropy and n-gram uniqueness measure lexical variety, but none of these capture the nature of the new ideas being introduced in generated ads. To address this gap, we conducted a qualitative analysis of the extreme expansions, the stage where the model stretches furthest from the original text. We aimed to characterize the stylistic forms of creativity that emerge and to see whether these felt genuinely novel or instead reflect familiar advertising tropes.

We randomly sampled 100 ads from the full dataset to ensure broad coverage without topical bias. For each case, we compared the original advertisement with its extreme expansion and extracted only emergent ideas, novel elements that appeared in the expansion but are absent from the original. For instance, if an ad originally described "a tea brand that helps you relax after work" and the expansion introduced "a sanctuary where every sip whispers calm into your soul," the metaphorical phrase "sanctuary where every sip whispers calm" is counted as an emergent idea. Each emergent idea was then coded into one of four categories adapted from advertising and linguistics research: Metaphorical, Emotional, Visual, or Slogan-like. This method provided a systematic way to track how LLMs express creativity across ads.

4
Discussion

Our study highlights that large language models tend to regress towards generic phrasing when challenged with creative generation, and several factors help explain this outcome. The next-token prediction objective encourages models to reproduce high-probability continuations rather than preserve rare creative features, while training corpora dominated by mainstream patterns further reinforce safe, conventional phrasing. Decoding methods also favor tokens with higher likelihood, which accelerates the loss of metaphors, emotions, and visual cues observed in our contractions. These mechanisms explain why expansions increased surface novelty but failed to restore originality: lexical diversity alone cannot counteract the statistical pull towards the mean. Marker-driven prompting improved alignment because it reintroduced structure that models would otherwise not prioritize. Building on these insights, future work could strengthen creativity recovery by designing training objectives that reward diversity and penalize clichés, by adopting adaptive sampling strategies that adjust when repetition or collapse occurs, and by incorporating richer evaluation measures such as human judgments or prototype-distance scores. Together, these refinements would make studies of creativity more robust and guide the development of models that better preserve originality during creative generation tasks.

5
Conclusion

We probed creativity loss and recovery in LLMs via a two-phase stress test on 1,045 ad concepts across six models (three runs each). In Phase 1 (forgetting), progressive contraction erased creative detail first, cosine and METEOR fell, lexical entropy dropped, and distinct 4-grams rose; in Phase 2 (expansion), plain regeneration increased length and surface variety but did not restore fidelity, producing outputs that sounded new but relied on familiar clichés, while marker-driven prompting partially recovered alignment. From these results we draw three key insights. First, regression towards generic phrasing is a consistent property across models. Second, surface novelty is not the same as true creativity, since new wording can still hide shallow ideas. Third, external structure such as creative markers can guide models towards better alignment, but they cannot fully restore originality. We interpret this pattern as regression towards a domain prototype, as described by Galton’s Law. Despite repeated runs and full mean
\pm
SD reporting, limitations include domain specificity (ads), metric/LLM-judge dependence, and the information advantage of markers. In short, structure and targeted signals can steer generation away from mediocrity, suggesting a path towards creativity-sensitive LLMs.

Appendix D

D.1
Metric Computation

This appendix describes the procedures used to compute the four evaluation metrics reported in Phases 1 and 2. Each metric was applied at the advertisement level and then averaged across the 1,045 ads in the dataset.

•

Cosine Similarity.

Semantic alignment between the regenerated text and the original advertisement was measured using TF–IDF weighted representations. Each text pair was vectorized with scikit-learn’s
TfidfVectorizer
, and cosine similarity was computed as:

\text{cosine}(x,y)=\frac{x\cdot y}{\|x\|\|y\|}

where
x
and
y
are the TF–IDF vectors. Scores range between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating stronger semantic similarity.

•

METEOR

Surface-level correspondence was assessed with METEOR,
which accounts for synonyms and word-order flexibility. The original advertisement served as the reference, and the regenerated ad as the hypothesis. Both texts were tokenized into words, and the
algorithm aligned them using exact matches, stemmed matches, and synonym matches. The METEOR score combines precision (the fraction of generated words that overlap with the reference) and recall
(the fraction of the reference captured in the generated text), with a penalty for fragmented word order. We used the implementation in NLTK’s
meteor_score.single_meteor_score

with default parameters.

•

Entropy.

Lexical diversity was estimated using Shannon entropy over the unigram distribution of each text. Word counts were collected using Python’s
Counter
, and entropy was computed as:

H=-\sum_{w\in V}p(w)\log_{2}p(w)

with
p(w)
the relative frequency of word
w
. Lower values indicate repetition and simplified language, while higher values indicate richer and more varied expression.

•

N-gram Uniqueness.

To quantify structural novelty, both texts were tokenized and segmented into sequential 4-grams using NLTK. Two sets of 4-grams were then created, one for the original and one for the generated text. The overlap between these sets was compared against their union, and uniqueness was defined as:

U=1-\frac{|N_{gen}\cap N_{orig}|}{|N_{gen}\cup N_{orig}|}

where
N_{gen}
and
N_{orig}
are the sets of 4-grams in the generated and original texts. A higher value indicates greater lexical novelty, while a lower value suggests reuse of original phrasing.
