Thyroid hormone receptor beta (THRβ1) is the major regulator of T3 action in human iPSC-derived hepatocytes
Authors: Lorraine Soares De Oliveira, Joseph E Kaserman, Anne H Van Der Spek, Nora J Lee, Hendrik J Undeutsch, Rhiannon B Werder, Andrew A Wilson, Anthony N Hollenberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThyroid hormone (TH) action is mediated by thyroid hormone receptor (THR) isoforms. While THRβ1 is likely the main isoform expressed in liver, its role in human hepatocytes is not fully understood.MethodsTo elucidate the role of THRβ1 action in human hepatocytes we used CRISPR/Cas9 editing to knock out THRβ1 in induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC). Following directed differentiation to the hepatic lineage, iPSC-derived hepatocytes were then interrogated to determine the role of THRβ1 in ligand-independent and -dependent functions.ResultsWe found that the loss of THRβ1 promoted alterations in proliferation rate and metabolic pathways regulated by T3, including gluconeogenesis, lipid oxidation, fatty acid synthesis, and fatty acid uptake. We observed that key genes involved in liver metabolism are regulated through both T3 ligand-dependent and -independent THRβ1 signaling mechanisms. Finally, we demonstrate that following THRβ1 knockout, several key metabolic genes remain T3 responsive suggesting they are THRα targets.ConclusionsThese results highlight that iPSC-derived hepatocytes are an effective platform to study mechanisms regulating TH signaling in human hepatocytes.Keywords: iPSC-derived hepatocytes, THRB, Thyroid hormone, Liver metabolism

Introduction

Thyroid Hormones (TH) are essential for normal development and intact physiologic function in adult life. Their actions are mediated by nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (THR), THRα and THRβ, which interact principally with triiodothyronine (T3) [[1], [2], [3]]. THRβ1 is the main isoform expressed in liver and is presumed to exert transcriptional effects both in the absence and presence of T3 [4,5]. In the setting of low or absent levels of T3, the THRs recruit a complex that includes the nuclear receptor corepressors, Nuclear Receptor Corepressor 1 (NCoR1) and Nuclear Receptor Corepressor 2 (SMRT), to mediate repression of known T3 targets. Increasing concentrations of T3 leads to release of the corepressor complex by THR isoforms, and recruitment of a coactivator complex promoting gene expression of T3 targets [1,3,6]. Previous studies in mice have demonstrated that T3, predominantly through interactions with Thrβ1, plays an important role regulating several hepatic metabolic pathways such as fatty acid synthesis, beta-oxidation, lipolysis, gluconeogenesis, and bile acid synthesis [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, data to identify the mechanisms by which TH signaling regulates these pathways in humans remains limited. Indeed, the field has been held back by limitations in existing model systems that faithfully reproduce TH signaling and action in human hepatocytes.

The ability to manipulate human induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPSC) has provided a novel platform that can overcome these limitations to facilitate insights into TH signaling in human cells. iPSCs and their differentiated progeny represent an inexhaustible supply of human cells that recapitulate the biology of their in vivo analogues. Additionally, the ability to manipulate iPSCs through gene-editing technology allows personalized in vitro assessments of disease mechanisms and therapeutic solutions that can be interrogated in a cell-type specific fashion [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Our group previously demonstrated that human iPSC-derived hepatocytes (iHEPs) are highly TH-responsive and they are possibly dependent upon THRβ1 to regulate hundreds of target genes [15]. Thus, they represent a potentially useful human platform to dissect TH regulation of liver metabolic pathways to provide greater insight into the mechanism of action of TH and its analogs. While in vivo models have been enlightening, they are limited by differences among species in cell-specific responses to TH [16]. Human liver cell-based models are therefore needed to illuminate TH action and signaling, especially given the recent approval of a thyroid hormone analog for the treatment of metabolic-dysfunction associated liver disease (MASLD) [17].

To further our understanding of TH action in hepatocytes, we have utilized human iHEPs and CRISPR/Cas9 endonuclease technology to knockout (KO) THRβ, allowing a direct comparison of THRβ KO cells to syngeneic wild type controls. This approach directly tested the effects of THRβ signaling in response to T3 stimulation in iHEPs. We find that THRβ is essential for modulating the T3 response across key metabolic pathways in iHEPs including carbohydrate and lipid metabolism and establish a novel platform to study homeostasis and diverse TH-related diseases.

Material and methods

Experimental approach and methods

All experiments were performed with the approval of Weill Cornell Medicine and Boston University Institutional Review Board (BUMC IRB protocol H33122). The human iPSC line BU3-10-Cr2 was obtained from Center for Regenerative Medicine, Boston University and previously published [18]. The iPSCs parental and gene-edited lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma and karyotypically normal as determined by G-band karyotyping analysis from 20 metaphases (Figure S1A). Detailed iPSCs derivation and culture protocols are available at (https://crem.bu.edu/cores-protocols/).

Maintenance of iPSCs and CRISPR Cas9 edition

Undifferentiated iPSCs (BU3-10-Cr2) were cultured in feeder-free conditions in mTeSR-1 media (StemCell Technologies) on Matrigel (Corning) coated dishes. Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (StemCell Technologies) was used for passaging. To knockout THRβ1, 2 guide RNAs flanking exon 8 were designed to excise a ∼1.5 kb fragment encompassing portions of the DNA-binding and ligand binding domains and thereby truncate expression of the isoform [19]. Both gRNAs were cloned into the same plasmid that encodes for Cas9 and for GFP. Targeted iPSC clones were isolated and screened by PCR to confirm excision, and clones positive for the excision were then sequenced. To reduce or eliminate expression of THRβ1, we selected clones showing correct targeting of both alleles to expand and ultimately determined one clone to establish our THRβ1 KO line and differentiate those iPSCs into hepatocytes. iHEPs were then treated with PBS (vehicle control), 1 nM,10 nM T3 and 100 nM of T3 according to the experiment.

Hepatocyte differentiation and exposure to T3 and GC-1

iPSC directed differentiation to hepatocytes was performed using our previously published protocol [9,20]. Briefly, the liver differentiation protocol started at day 0 in which the iPSCs were dissociated for single cell passaging with Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (StemCell Technologies), seeded at 1 × 106 cells per well on a Matrigel coated 6 well plate, and placed at 37 °C into hypoxic conditions (5% O2, 5% CO2, 90% N2). Endodermal differentiation was initiated using the STEMdiff Definitive Endoderm Kit (StemCell Technologies), according to manufacturer's instructions over 4 days, then the cells were passaged at a ratio of approximately 1:4 using Gentle Cell Dissociation reagent onto a new Matrigel-coated 12 well plate containing complete serum-free differentiation medium (cSFDM) base media which contains Iscove's Modified Dulbecco's Media (IMDM - ThermoFisher Scientific), Ham's F12 (VWR), B27 supplement minus vitamin A (ThermoFisher Scientific), N2 supplement (ThermoFisher Scientific), 7.5% Bovine Albumin Fraction V (BSA - ThermoFisher Scientifc), Glutamax (ThermoFisher Scientific), l-ascorbic acid (MilliporeSigma), 1-Thioglycerol (MTG - MilliporeSigma) supplemented with Activin A (R&D Systems), Bone Morphogenetic Protein 4 (BMP4 - R&D Systems), Fibroblast Growth Factor 2 (FGF2 - R&D Systems), Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF - R&D Systems), and Y-27632 (Tocris). Cells were refed with the same media on day 6. From day 7–12 cells were kept in the same serum free base media containing BMP4, FGF2, VEGF, Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF - R&D Systems), Transforming Growth Factor alpha (TGFα - R&D Systems), Hepatocyte Growth Factor (HGF - R&D Systems), and dexamethasone (MilliporeSigma). Then from day 13 to day 18 cells were cultured in serum free base media supplemented with FGF2, VEGF, EGF, HGF, Oncostatin M (OSM) (ThermoFisher Scientific), Phylloquinone K1 (MilliporeSigma), gamma secretase inhibitor (gSI) (MilliporeSigma), and dexamethasone. From day 19 to day 25 cells were incubated with serum free base media containing HGF, OSM, Phylloquinone K, and dexamethasone. On Day 24, cells were incubated with media (day 19 – day 25) containing Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS - Gibco) as vehicle control or T3 (MilliporeSigma) in 1 nM, 10 nM or 100 nM concentrations according to the experiment). After 24 h cells were harvested for future analysis. iHEPs were also treated with GC-1 (sobetirome) - a kind gift from Thomas Scanlan; for this experiments GC-1 was dissolved in Dimethyl sulfoxide (MilliporeSigma) to make a 10 mM stock solution and diluted with culture medium to the desired concentration before being added to the cells. On day 24, 0.25 nM, 0.5 nM, and 1 nM of GC-1 was administered to iHEPs for 24 h [21]. WT control cells were from the same genetic background as the edited THRBKO line, were treated with an equivalent volume of PBS diluted in the culture medium. All reagents are listed at Supplementary Table 1.

Flow cytometry

CKIT/CXCR4 staining for endoderm on day 5 - Definitive endoderm (DE) efficiency was quantified at day 5 using anti-human CD184 (CXCR4)-PE (StemCell Technologies) and anti-human CD117(CKIT)-APC (ThermoFisherScientific) conjugated monoclonal antibodies [9,20]. Cells were harvested, then the pellet was resuspended in PBS+ (2% FBS in PBS), after that they were incubated with CKIT and CXCR4 antibodies for 30 min on ice. AFP and FOXA1 staining for hepatocytes on day 25 - To analyze the presence of hepatocytes markers at day 25, iHEPs were fixed in 1.6% paraformaldehyde (VWR) for 20 min at 37 °C and then permeabilized for 5 min in Permeabilization Wash Buffer (BioLegend). Cells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature with AFP (Abcam) and FOXA1 (Santa Cruz Technology) antibodies, then the cells were incubated with anti-rabbit IgG-AlexaFluor488 (ThermoFisher Scientific) and anti-mouse IgG-AlexaFluor488 (ThermoFisher Scientific) secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature, respectively. Proliferation was measured by flow cytometry using the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 647 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific), as per manufacturer's instructions. Staining quantification was measured using MA900 Multi-Application Cell Sorter (Sony). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (Becton, Dickinson & Company). For all flow cytometry analysis, gating was determined using isotype-stained controls. Antibodies details are listed at Supplementary Table 2.

Immunocytochemistry

For immunocytochemistry coverslips were inserted into a 24 well plate, then 2D Matrigel was added and coated for 30 min at 37 °C 5% CO2. Matrigel was aspirated, the wells washed with DMEM/F12, and mTeSR-1 media was added for the undifferentiated iPSCs while media day 19 – day 25 was added in the wells that received iHEPs. 50,000 cells/well were seeded, and after both undifferentiated iPSCs and iHEPs were attached to the glass slide the media was removed, the wells washed with PBS, and then the cells were fixed in 4% PFA (VWR) at room temperature for 30 min. After that the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton (MilliporeSigma) for 20 min, and blocked with 10% normal donkey serum (Electron Microscopy Sciences) at room temperature for 1 h. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 1% Donkey Serum/0.1% Triton overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: Human Albumin (1:100 - Bethyl Laboratories) and HNF-4-alpha (1:100 - Cell signaling). Samples were then washed with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1h at room temperature. The following secondary antibodies were used: anti-Goat IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Alexa Fluor™ 488 (1:1000 - ThermoFisher Scientific) and anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 488 (1:1000- Abcam) (Supplementary Table 2). Prolong Gold antifade reagent with DAPI (ThermoFisher Scientific) was mounted on the glass slide and covered with a glass coverslip. Images of the cells were obtained by a fluorescent microscope (ECHO Revolve) and processed with Fiji software (National Institutes of Health). See Supplementary Table 4 for software details.

Real-time qPCR

WT and THRBKO iHEPs were collected and stored in Qiazol (Qiagen). Total RNA was isolated using an RNeasy plus mini kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. 250 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Real-time, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed in duplicate for all samples using Taqman Universal Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and QuantStudio 6 Pro system (Thermo Fisher). Predesigned TaqMan probes (Thermo Fisher) for 40 cycles were used. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using standard-curve methods and normalized to the expression level of Eukaryotic 18S rRNA Endogenous Control (VIC™ ⁄ MGB Probe – Applied Biosystems). Probes are listed at Supplementary Table 3.

RNA sequencing

iHEPs were treated with 100 nM of T3 during 24 h and on day 25 RNA was isolated from them using the RNeasy plus mini kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instruction. RNA sequencing was performed by the Core Laboratories Center — Genomics Cores at Weill Cornell Medicine. Briefly, Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep kit was performed for library preparation. 200 ng total RNA was used as input for each sample. Pooled libraries were sequenced using NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) sequencer. Raw data quality was assessed using FastQC. Sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) and counts per gene were summarized using the featureCounts function from the subread package. The edgeR package was used to import and filter the counts. Analysis with principal component analysis (PCA) and differential expression testing were conducted to compare the gene expression between different sample sets. Differentially expressed genes for each comparison were visualized using Glimma package (https://github.com/ Shians/Glimma), and FDR <0.05 was set as the threshold for determining significant differential gene expression. Enrichment analyses were carried out using Fgsea package (https://github.com/ctlab/fgsea). Data are deposited at NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): GSE270233, reviewer token = itwxkkempburzg https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE270233.

Lipid droplet accumulation

Human iHEPs were incubated in the presence of fatty acids (FA) for 24 h. Long-chain FAs, palmitic (PA) (16:0) was provided as sodium salt (Sigma-Aldrich) and oleic (OA) (18:1) as solution (Sigma-Aldrich). PA and OA were conjugated with free FA-BSA solution (stock solution 10 mM). At day 24 iHEPs were incubated with media containing a mixture of OA + PA conjugated with BSA or BSA alone as control for 24 h in ratio 2:1 at the following final concentrations: OA = 0.66 mM and PA = 0.33 mM [22]. After 24 h incubation the lipid droplet accumulation was analyzed by Oil Red O staining (Sigma-Aldrich).

Free fatty acid - BSA complex solution preparation

A 4.8 mM BSA solution was prepared by mixing free FA - BSA (Sigma) with 150 mM NaCl. Next, sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) (Fisher Scientific) was dissolved in dH2O and incubated at 65 °C, then it was mixed with PA and OA and heated at 65 °C for 2 min to make sodium salt. Following, the BSA solution was added into the tube containing PA and OA and incubated at 37 °C for 70 min. The solution was then filtered, and the concentrations measured. The stock solution can be stored at −20 °C for up to 4 weeks.

Oil Red O staining

Oil Red O stock solution was prepared by dissolving 30 mg of Oil Red O (Sigma-Aldrich) in 100 ml of 100% isopropanol (Fisher Scientific). Oil Red O working solution was done by mixing 3 parts of Oil Red O stock solution in 2 parts of dH2O. iHEPs at day 25 were washed with 1X PBS, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (VWR) for 30 min. The iHEPs were then incubated in Oil Red O working solution for 15 min, washed with distilled water three times, and the images were acquired using ECHO Revolve microscope system at 4x and 10x magnification. To convert the Oil Red O color into fluorescent images, the TRITC (emission wavelength of 570 nm), and FITC (emission wavelength of 495 nm) channels were analyzed, to confirm the only fluoresce seen was from TRITC. Lipid droplets were identified by the presence of TRITC - red color. After that the cells were lysed in 60% isopropanol for 5 min at room temperature, the supernatant was collected and pipetted into a 96 wells plate where the 490 absorbance was read, then analyzed.

Glucose production

At the day 25 iHEPs were incubated for 24 h in washout media (75% IMDM, 25% Hams F12, 50ug/mL of ascorbic acid, 1X primocin and 0.05% of BSA) to prevent activation of Akt by other components of the medium. T3 100 nM or PBS vehicle control was added to the medium and incubated for a following 24 h. After 24 h of T3 treatment cells were washed with PBS twice and incubated for 5 h in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) without glucose, glutamine or phenol red (Gibco) with 10 mM l-Lactate (MilliporeSigma) and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (ThermoFisher Scientific). Glucose was measured in supernatant using fluorescent glucose assay (Abcam) per manufacturer's instructions and normalized for total protein content in the well. Cells were harvested for protein measurement in cell lysis buffer (Cell Signaling) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Protein content was measured using BCA assay (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism, version 10. All figures show representative data of 3 or more independent biological replicates from separate differentiations with 2 technical replicates per group, specified in detail in the figure legends. The samples were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with repeated measures followed by Tukey multiple comparisons post-hoc test. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM and p values smaller than 0.05 were considered as statistically significant; ∗ (p < 0.05), ∗∗ (p < 0.01), ∗∗∗ (p < 0.001), ∗∗∗∗ (p < 0.0001).

Results

Generation of a THRβ knockout in human iPSCs

To establish a relevant human model to study TH action and signaling we first designed a targeting approach that would eliminate THRβ activity by completely excising exon 8, encompassing portions of the DNA-binding and ligand-binding domains in a human iPSC line (Figure 1A). For this we used CRISPR-Cas9 technology combined with specific gRNAs targeting the intronic regions flanking exon 8 (Figure S1A) using a protocol we have shown results in efficient editing in iPSCs [23]. The generated THRβ KO (THRBKO) line was verified and retained a normal karyotype (Figure S1B). Following CRISPR targeting, we confirmed through Sanger sequencing and PCR screening that exon 8 was excised in THRBKO iPSC compared to the Wild type (WT) iPSC used as control (Figures S1C and S1D). As THRβ is minimally expressed in iPSC, we generated iHEPs to characterize the effects of knocking out THRβ, utilizing a directed differentiation protocol we have previously shown efficiently generates hepatic-like cells that exhibit a transcriptional profile and functional characteristics similar to primary human hepatocytes and are T3 responsive [9,15,20,23]. The cells were then harvested at day 25 (Figure 1B).

To validate our targeting approach, we first confirmed that THRβ1 mRNA expression was undetectable in both undifferentiated iPSC and in day 25 iHEPs in the THRBKO line compared to WT cells (Figure 1C). As expected, we found no difference in THRα expression between THRBKO and WT iPSC and iHEPs. Surprisingly, levels of THRα were upregulated in iHEPs compared with WT iPSC, suggesting THRα may have a functional role in hepatocytes (Figure 1D). To further investigate THRα and THRβ T3 specific responses in iHEPs, we assessed the expression of KLF9 a well-known THRα target upregulated by T3 [24]. Our data revealed significantly higher induction of KLF9 by T3 in all groups, confirming that iHEPs are responsive to T3 and that THRα has the ability to regulate specific T3 targets in iHEPs (Figure 1E). Next, to compare T3 responsiveness between iHEPs we first evaluated the expression of the deiodinases (DIO1, DIO2, and DIO3) as Dio1 has been previously shown to be the proteotypic T3-responsive gene in mouse liver. DIO1 is highly expressed in iHEPs but was upregulated by T3 only in WT cells (Figure 1F). Interestingly, we found that both DIO2 and DIO3 had low levels of expression in iHEPs (Figure S2A). In contrast, in undifferentiated iPSC we observed that DIO1 and DIO2 are lowly expressed while DIO3 is moderately expressed (Figure S2B). To test for potential signaling through THRβ2, we analyzed its mRNA expression and based on the Ct mean we could detect no evidence of expression of THRβ2 in iPSC (THRβ2 – average Ct > 37) or iHEPs (THRβ2 – average Ct > 36) (Figure S2C). These data indicate DIO1 is likely a THRβ1 target and the main deiodinase expressed in human hepatocytes.

THRβ1 is dispensable for directed differentiation of iPSCs-derived hepatocytes

We next wanted to ensure that knockout of THRβ1 did not impair the ability of pluripotent iPSCs to specify to the hepatic lineage. To test this, we analyzed cells at days 5 and 25 of our differentiation protocol, which correspond to critical stages of differentiation including definitive endoderm and hepatocytes [9]. We used flow cytometry to analyze the expression of the stage specific markers CKIT and CXCR4 together with AFP and FOXA1 for definitive endoderm and hepatocytes respectively. Flow cytometric results demonstrated no differences in endodermal or hepatocyte specification between the WT and THRBKO lines (Figures 2A and B and S3A). Expression of lineage specific markers ALBUMIN, AFP, SERPINA1, TTR and HNF4 in iHEPs and POU5F1 (OCT4) in iPSC were not diminished in THRBKO compared to WT lines (Figure 2C). Immunofluorescence staining also showed presence of ALBUMIN and HNF4A expression with no difference between WT and THRBKO iHEPs (Figure 2D). Additionally, we confirmed that undifferentiated iPSCs lack features associated with hepatocyte, including no expression of AFP compared to WT and THRBKO iHEPs, as quantified by flow cytometry, and negative immunofluorescence staining of ALBUMIN and HNF4A (Figure S3A and S3B). Finally, WT and THRBKO cells displayed typical hepatocyte morphology at the end of the differentiation (Figure 3A). These results support that the absence of THRβ1 does not disturb human hepatocyte differentiation from iPSCs.

THRβ1 modulates cell proliferation of iPSCs-derived hepatocytes

Interestingly, while we did not see any impact of THRβ1 knockout on hepatocyte specification, we did observe that THRBKO iPSCs appeared to grow more slowly compared to WT iPSCs (Figure 3A). To determine whether THRβ1 alters cell proliferation, we next quantified EdU incorporation across three developmental stages of our hepatocyte differentiation protocol: undifferentiated (Day 0), hepatic progenitor (Day 11), and hepatocyte (Day 25). Supporting our initial observation, we found that the THRBKO iPSC cultures at day 0 had significantly fewer cells in S phase (DNA replication in nucleus) compared to their WT iPSC syngeneic comparators (Figure 3B). Additionally, we analyzed the expression of key genes involved in cell proliferation including Marker of Proliferation Ki-67 (MKi67) which is a proliferative gene present during all active phases of the cell cycle (G1, S, G2, and mitosis (M)) [25], Cyclin D1(CCND1), another proliferative gene that modulates the transition from G1 to S phase [26], and BTG Anti-Proliferation Factor 2 (BTG2) which is an antiproliferative gene that increases apoptosis by arresting cells at G1/S and G2/M transition [27]. Surprisingly, expression of the proliferative genes MKI67 and CCND1 was not significantly altered, although the expression of the antiproliferative gene BTG2 was increased in the THRBKO iPSC compared to the WT line (Figure 3C).

We next assessed EdU incorporation by flow cytometry at day 11 of our liver differentiation protocol, a time point at which cells have reached a hepatic progenitor stage. Similar to day 0, fewer THRBKO cells at day 11 were in S phase (Figure 3D). While the mRNA expression of MKI67 was not significantly changed at this time, CCND1 and BTG2 were decreased in THRBKO cells (Figure 3E). By day 25 the cells were no longer proliferative, as demonstrated by a lack of EdU incorporation (Figure 3F); however, the proliferative gene MKI67 was increased in the THRBKO, while CCND1 and BTG2 were not altered. Collectively, these results indicate that THRβ1 plays a role in the proliferation of undifferentiated iPSCs and in the first stages of hepatocyte differentiation, when the cells specify to definitive endoderm and then to hepatic progenitor.

THRβ1 plays a critical ligand-independent and dependent role in human hepatocytes

Previously, our group showed that WT iHEPs are highly responsive to T3 with over 1000 genes differentially expressed [15]. To understand the effects of the loss of THRβ in the global gene expression of human iHEPs, we next performed bulk RNA-sequencing in WT and THRBKO iHEPs treated for 24 h with T3 or vehicle control at day 24. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrates that WT cells separate into different clusters in the presence (purple) or absence of T3 (turquoise) consistent with our prior observations (Figure 4A). In contrast, THRBKO iHEPs (THRBKO - orange and THRBKO + T3 - blue) do not generate separate clusters in the presence of T3. Furthermore, THRBKO and WT iHEPs cluster separately in the absence of T3 (turquoise and orange), confirming a potential role for the unliganded THRβ in transcriptional regulation (Figure 4A). Formal gene expression analysis identified 951 unique genes upregulated by T3 in WT iHEPs (Figure 4B - red circle), while only 9 unique targets were upregulated in THRBKO iHEPs (Figure 4B - yellow circle). Similarly, the number of genes downregulated by T3 in WT iHEPs is higher than in THRBKO, 445 (Figure 4B - green circle) and 3 (Figure 4B - purple circle), respectively. We also observed 279 unique genes (Figure 4B - orange circle) upregulated and 218 genes (Figure 4B - blue circle) repressed in THRBKO iHEPs compared to WT iHEPs, consistent with a potential ligand-independent role of THRβ. The corresponding genes up and downregulated in the Venn Diagrams (Figure 4B) are further illustrated in the volcano plots (Figure 4C) highlighting the magnitude of differential expression of these regulated genes. Indeed, in volcano plots the dramatic effect of THRβ in the presence or absence of ligand is well-depicted (Figure 4C). However, even in the absence of THRβ, targets such as KLF9 and COQ10A were still regulated (Figure 4C – middle panel). Given the importance of T3 signaling in controlling various liver metabolic pathways, we next looked to see whether genes associated with these pathways were regulated by T3 in THRBKO iHEPs. Analysis of genes associated with fatty acid (FA) metabolism and gluconeogenesis revealed that most of these selected genes do not respond to T3 in THRBKO iHEPs. However, we did confirm that a set of genes including KLF9, COQ10A and EGR1 were still regulated by T3 in THRBKO iHEPs (Figure 4D). Taken together, these results demonstrate that THRβ1 expression is essential for T3 metabolic effects in human hepatocytes, but that a subset of genes remain T3 responsive in THRBKO cells.

T3 regulates carbohydrate metabolism in human iHEPs in a THRβ1 dependent manner

TH impacts glucose metabolism centrally via the sympathetic pathway connecting the paraventricular hypothalamus to the liver and peripherally through its actions on many organs, mostly the pancreas, muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver [8]. TH stimulates gluconeogenesis by directly increasing the expression of rate-limiting enzymes such as glucose-6-phosphatase (G6PC) [28] and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PCK1) [29]. To further explore whether the gluconeogenic program in human iHEPs is regulated by T3, we analyzed the gene expression of G6PC, PCK1, and Fructose-Bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1). T3 treatment significantly upregulated G6PC and PCK1 (Figure 5A), and downregulated FBP1 (Figure 5B) in WT iHEPs. However, these effects were lost in THRBKO iHEPs. To confirm these results, we analyzed the expression of KLF9 in iPSC and G6PC, PCK1, FBP1, and KLF9 in iHEPs treated with a range of T3 concentrations. The data revealed that T3 is able to regulate these genes even with low T3 concentrations (Figure S4A and S4B). We next analyzed glucose production in iHEPs. Consistent with our gluconeogenic gene analysis, WT iHEPs showed increased in vitro glucose production in the presence of T3, while this effect was lost in THRBKO iHEPs (Figure 5C). Thus, T3 stimulates gluconeogenesis in human hepatocytes and this effect is mediated by THRβ1.

THRβ1 regulates lipid metabolism in iPSCs-derived human hepatocytes

THRBKO iHEPs observed under the microscope exhibited a prominent presence of intracellular droplets, suggestive of lipid droplets (Figure 6A). To confirm this observation and to test if lipid metabolism was altered in iHEPs following THRβ knockout, we next analyzed lipid droplet content at steady state by Oil Red O staining. We observed significantly higher lipid droplet accumulation in THRBKO iHEPs compared to the WT iHEPs with no T3 effect (Figure 6B,C). We then performed a FA challenge using a 2:1 combination of OA, a monounsaturated FA and PA, a saturated FA [30], as these are the most abundant FA present in the diet and have different biological effects. After a 24 h exposure, increased lipid droplet accumulation was evident in both iHEPs lines. However, the amount of fat accumulation was more pronounced in the THRBKO iHEPs (Figure 6D,E) with no T3 effect seen (Figure S5A). It is well described that TH is a regulator of lipid metabolism controlling both beta-oxidation and lipogenesis [31]. To investigate whether this accumulation of lipid droplets in absence of THRβ1 could be related to T3 action, we analyzed the mRNA expression of key metabolic genes, some of which we identified in our bulk RNA-seq analysis shown earlier. T3 was a clear regulator of the lipogenic program, as shown by its ability to upregulate the expression of MLX Interacting Protein Like (MLXIPL), coding for CHREBP, as well as Fatty acid synthase (FASN), Acetyl-Coenzyme A Carboxylase Alpha (ACACA) and ELOVL Fatty Acid Elongase 6 (ELOVL6). All of these targets were regulated in a THRβ dependent manner (Figure 6F). T3 also regulated critical targets that control fatty acid oxidation (Peroxisome Proliferator Activated Receptor Delta - PPARD and Carnitine Palmitoyltransferase 1A - CPT1A) as well as cholesterol metabolism (Lipase C - LIPC) (Figure 6G), and fatty acid transport (CD36 molecule - CD36 and Fatty Acid-Binding Protein 1 - FABP1) (Figure 6H). Interestingly, while T3 dependent regulation of all of these genes was THRβ dependent, FABP1 expression was upregulated in the absence of THRβ, implying that increased FA transport could mediate the increased lipid accumulation in the THRBKO cells [32]. Collectively these results suggest THRβ1 is critical to regulate the balance of lipid metabolic pathways in hepatocytes.

A role for THRα in human hepatocytes

To confirm genes identified by our bulk RNA-sequencing that were highly differentially expressed by T3 in the THRβKO iHEPs, we proceeded with RT-qPCR to determine mRNA gene expression. Coenzyme Q10A (COQ10A), Early growth response protein 1.

(EGR1) and Transient Receptor Potential Cation Channel Subfamily M Member 6 (TRPM6) were all significantly upregulated by 100 nM T3 in both WT and THRBKO iHEPs (Figure 7A). Similarly, 3-Hydroxy-3-Methylglutaryl-CoA Synthase 2 (HMGCS2), ELOVL Fatty Acid Elongase 2 (ELOVL2) and CDC Like Kinase 1 (CLK1) were all down regulated in both iHEPs lines in the presence of T3 (Figure 7B). To further delineate whether these target genes in our iHEPs were uniquely THRβ1 targets we next treated WT and THRBKO iHEPs with GC-1 a high affinity ligand for the THR which shows a selectivity for THRβ over THRα [21]. We first administered T3 at 1 nM and 10 nM and GC-1 at 0.25 nM, 0.5 nM and 1 nM to iHEPs for 24 h and examined their effects on exclusive THRβ targets. Indeed, PCK1, G6PC and FBP1 were regulated by both T3 and GC-1 in WT iHEPs, while neither ligand was able to regulate these targets in THRBKO cells (Figure 7C). In contrast KLF9, COQ10A and ELOVL2 were all regulated by T3 and GC-1 in WT hepatocytes but only responded to T3 in THRBKO iHEPs and not to GC-1 (Figure 7D). Taken together these data suggest that in the absence of THRβ, certain T3 targets are still regulated. Thus, THRα may play a role in the regulation of these targets.

Discussion

The advent of approved THRβ analogs for the treatment of metabolic disease has underscored the need for the development of human cell models to understand their mechanism of action. It remains entirely unclear why only 30% of patients studied with MASLD respond to THRβ analog therapy [17]. Most insight into TH action has been garnered through mice studies performed over the last 30 years due to the lack of human model systems. To address these limitations, we have developed and validated a novel approach to investigate TH action and signaling in human hepatocytes derived from iPSCs. Importantly, iPSCs can be edited to further elucidate mechanistic insight into key signaling pathways. Despite ongoing efforts to optimize protocols and identify compounds and conditions that can enhance maturation of hepatocytes, currently human iPSCs-derived hepatocytes are transcriptionally similar to primary human fetal hepatocytes as indicated by persistent expression of the immature marker gene AFP. However, these cells still provide a valuable human model not previously available to study TH action [10].

Utilizing this approach, we applied CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knockout THRβ in iPSCs and the resulting iHEPs allowed us to study the role of THRβ in both hepatocyte differentiation and function. Our iPSC-derived THRBKO hepatocytes achieve the same differentiation state as WT iHEPs as demonstrated by flow cytometry (AFP and FOXA1), gene expression (ALBUMIN, AFP, SERPINA1, TTR, and HNF4), and immunofluorescence (ALBUMIN and HNF4) of hepatocyte markers. Nevertheless, we observed that the THRBKO line was initially less proliferative in the undifferentiated state (iPSCs - day 0) consistent with higher expression of the antiproliferative gene BTG2 in this group. Interestingly, the expression of both CCND1, a proliferative gene, and the BTG2 was lower in THRBKO cells at the hepatic progenitor stage (day 11), and these cells were also less proliferative compared to WT samples. By the completion of differentiation, we did not see differences between WT and THRBKO cells and the phenotype of the THRBKO cells was indistinguishable from the WT iHEPs. Importantly, T3 induces hepatocyte proliferation in rodent liver and this effect is assumed to be mediated by THRβ1 [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. Recently it was demonstrated that THRβ and T3 signaling in mice induce key genes of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, stimulating hepatocyte proliferation via CCND1 [36]. Thus THRβ1 may be essential for greater hepatocyte cell proliferation, which could play a role in recovery from primary liver disease. Further work in this area will be required to determine the significance of THRβ1 in hepatocyte development and proliferation.

While TH is a known regulator of gluconeogenesis, cholesterol and FA metabolism in humans, presumably through its actions on the liver, the signaling pathways responsible for these effects have not been well identified. Herein, we are able to show that gluconeogenic genes and glucose production are regulated in a THRβ1 specific fashion iHEPs. Whether the FoxO1 pathway is important in this process as it is in mouse liver can now be determined [37]. Similarly, THRβ1 appears to play a critical role in lipid accumulation in iHEPs. Multiple pathways could be in play as T3 target pathways involved in lipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation and fatty acid transport. Strikingly, treatment of iHEPs with T3 for 24 h did not lead to a reduction in lipid content, despite the activation of CPT1A and other genes involved in fatty acid oxidation. This suggests that a longer treatment period may be necessary to see this effect in iHEPs. Clearly, however, the ability to lipid load these cells establishes them as a model to study metabolic disease further. In addition, these findings suggest that THRβ1 is critical for normal lipid droplet accumulation in human hepatocytes, and its absence can disrupt lipid metabolism as seen in patients with hypothyroidism or resistance to thyroid hormone beta [38,39]. Impairment of THRβ1 signaling may disrupt beta-oxidation of free FA, or disturb several metabolic processes including lipophagy, lipase activity, and mitochondrial function [40].

Central to understanding TH action is the ability to discern the function of the THR isoforms both in the presence and absence of T3. It has long been postulated that hypothyroidism in humans is secondary to the unliganded repression mediated by THRβ and THRα. We demonstrate for the first time in human hepatocytes that the THRβ can both activate and repress genes in the absence of T3. By utilizing additional methodology, we should be able to discern the mechanism underlying ligand-independent activation and repression in human hepatocytes. To date, an effective model to understand how the THR activates genes in the absence of T3 has not previously been available.

Finally, the role of THRα in the liver has long been vexing, as THRβ is dominantly expressed and mutations in THRβ as found in Resistance to Thyroid Hormone Beta (RTHβ) may block all T3 signaling. Here we find that the knockout of THRβ does not remove all T3 signaling. A small number of target genes retain their response to T3. These genes (i.e., KLF9, COQ10A and ELOVL2) appear to be regulated by both THRβ and THRα, such that in WT cells they can be regulated by both T3 and the THRβ analog GC-1. However, in the absence of THRβ they respond to T3, likely through THRα. It remains unclear why THRα is only able to rescue the expression of certain target genes and whether this involves THR isoform-specific binding to response elements that control their expression. This raises the possibility that THR analogs, depending on potency, could fail to activate certain targets in the liver. Further analysis of the role of THRs in the presence of different TH analogs would be illuminating.

As the incidence of MAFLD and its complications including steatohepatitis, fibrosis and finally liver failure and cirrhosis leading to hepatocellular carcinoma [41] continue to grow and TH analogs become viable options for therapy, a better understanding of their action is needed. The availability of iPSC-derived hepatocytes that can be edited and applied for modeling provide a unique resource to understand disease mechanisms. We expect that further work utilizing this model system will provide greater insight into the molecular action of thyroid hormone and its analogs and lead to the development of new tools to potentially treat metabolic disease.
