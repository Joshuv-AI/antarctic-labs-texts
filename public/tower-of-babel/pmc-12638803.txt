Synergistic effects of rice husk ash and extracted microsilica on the performance of high-strength concrete
Authors: Muhammad Fahad Ullah, Hesheng Tang, Arshad Ullah, Kaffayatullah Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The construction industry’s urgent need for sustainable alternatives to ordinary Portland cement (OPC) has spurred significant interest in supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs). Therefore, this study investigates the synergistic effects of rice husk ash (RHA) and extracted microsilica (EMS) as partial replacements for cement in high-strength concrete (HSC). RHA was produced by controlled combustion of rice husk (RH) at 600–700 °C, while EMS was obtained through an optimized extraction process. Along with control mix (CM), three ternary mixes incorporating 15–35% RHA and 5–15% EMS were investigated to evaluate their effects on the mechanical and durability properties, as well as microstructural characteristics. Experimental results demonstrated that samples containing 20% (5% EMS + 15% RHA) and 35% (10% EMS + 25% RHA) as cement replacement exhibited enhanced performance, increasing the 28-day compressive strength by 11.56% and 5.98%, respectively, as compared to conventional concrete. Durability assessments of the optimal mixes revealed a significant reduction in water absorption (WA) and permeability. Physiochemical characterization confirmed the increased formation of dense calcium silicate hydrate (CSH) gels and a reduction in portlandite content, indicating high pozzolanic reactivity. However, the 50% replacement mix (15% EMS + 35% RHA) showed inferior performance due to the particle agglomeration and higher porosity. This study shows that the strategically formulated combinations of EMS and RHA blends (up to 35%) can produce sustainable HSC with improved mechanical properties and durability while mitigating environmental impacts. These findings provide valuable insights for advancing eco-friendly construction materials, supporting global sustainability initiatives in infrastructure development.

Introduction

Concrete, a composite blend of synthetic and natural constituents mixed with water, is considered the most substantial, versatile, durable, and widely utilized construction material worldwide. As a fundamental construction material, concrete is vital to the growth of a nation’s infrastructure and construction industry, contributing to its economic growth and social advancement. Due to rapid expansion in infrastructure development, concrete use continues to grow1. The global production of concrete is approximately 4.4 billion tons annually, and this number will increase to 5.5 billion tons by 2050, driven by the rapid urbanization in developing countries2. To achieve sustainability and effectively reduce carbon emissions, the cement and concrete manufacturing industries must improve process efficiency by incorporating waste materials into OPC blends as partial or full replacements3. According to the report of the International Energy Agency, the carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions intensity from the cement industry increased by almost 10% since 2015, highlighting the urgent need to use alternative raw materials in cement production4. Cement production has historically left a significant carbon footprint, prompting researchers to explore industrial byproducts as SCMs. In recent years, RHA has been extensively examined for its potential use as an SCM5. The increasing demand for cement has also led to a greater reliance on raw materials, particularly limestone, which is essential for cement production6. In the cement manufacturing process, the calcination reaction produces calcium oxide, releasing CO2 as a byproduct, while an additional 50% of emissions are linked to energy consumption during production7. The cement industry produces nearly 5% to 7% of the world’s CO2 emissions, with CO2 accounting for 65% of total greenhouse gas emissions worldwide8. To mitigate the carbon footprint of cement manufacturing, researchers are investigating the potential of industrial byproducts, such as SCMs9. Environmental issues associated with the construction industry can be addressed through sustainable approaches, such as substituting cement with highly pozzolanic SCMs to produce durable, high-performance, and sustainable concrete10.

In recent decades, pozzolanic materials obtained from agricultural and industrial wastes have been widely utilized as SCMs for partial replacement of cement. The inclusion of pozzolanic materials such as ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), fly ash (FA), RHA, and silica fume (SF) refines the concrete microstructure, leading to superior mechanical performance and enhanced durability11. The integration of agricultural residues such as RHA, corncob ash, and sugarcane bagasse ash, along with industrial by-products like FA, GGBS, and metakaolin (MK), has shown favorable outcomes in producing sustainable, high-performance concrete, enhancing strength while improving cost, energy, and environmental efficiency12. Moreover, using such materials presents sustainable solutions for managing industrial and agricultural waste, particularly in developing countries, where disposing of agro-waste remains a critical challenge13. Furthermore, the construction industry has increasingly explored agricultural waste materials, including RHA and bagasse ash, as sustainable and high-performing partial substitutes for cement in concrete production14. RH is a major agrarian byproduct, with a global annual production of approximately 150 million tons15. Despite this, only 17% of RHA is utilized for fertilizer, fuel, or electricity generation, while the remaining 83% is discarded or openly burned, causing water and soil pollution16. Rice, a major Kharif crop in Pakistan, plays a vital role as both a staple food and a key export commodity, contributing 3.1% value added in agriculture and 0.6% to the national GDP17. According to the literature, 20% of rice paddy is husk, with Pakistan generating approximately 1.5 million tons of RH annually. In Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province, the estimated annual rice production is approximately 147,000 tons, resulting in 30,000 tons of RH18.

RH is an agricultural waste material with low nutrient content, undergoes significant transformation when burned under controlled conditions, and produces amorphous silica with large surface area. The utilization of RHA as SCM has gained considerable attention in the building construction industry, particularly in rice-producing countries such as Pakistan5. RHA, primarily composed of non-crystalline (amorphous) SiO2, exhibits high pozzolanic activity and enhances the mechanical strength and durability performance of concrete when utilized as a substitute for cement19. Due to its economic and environmental benefits, RHA is increasingly being explored as an alternative to conventional cementitious materials20,21. Studies show that the combustion of RH at controlled temperatures generates RHA with a high silica content, typically varying between 90% and 95% depending upon the burning conditions. The pozzolanic reactivity of RHA is significantly influenced by factors including incineration temperature, duration, and oxygen availability22. The presence of unburnt carbon in RHA has been observed to impair its pozzolanic reactivity significantly, underscoring the necessity of implementing controlled incineration processes to optimize its performance as an SCM23. RHA contains amorphous silica, and minimal unburnt carbon enhances concrete properties, such as mechanical strength, reduced porosity, and improved durability24. High-quality pozzolanic materials are produced by combusting RH under controlled conditions, improving the properties of mortar and concrete25. Previous studies indicate that burning RHA at temperatures below 700 °C produces a high percentage of amorphous silica, with optimal pozzolanic reactivity achieved between 500 and 700 °C15. Research findings suggest that burning RH at 700 °C yields the highest amorphous silica content, accompanied by high pozzolanic reactivity and improved surface area20. A ferrocement furnace with a capacity of 60 kg was utilized to obtain high-quality RHA. Initially, a controlled fire source was maintained for approximately 10 min; after that, the husks gradually combusted for over a day, maintaining a temperature range of 600 to 700 °C. The ash was allowed to cool before being carefully collected, resulting in RHA with 87% amorphous silica26. Several studies have used ferrocement furnaces to obtain high-quality RH ashes with varying unburnt carbon content and a high silica content, ranging between 85% and 97%27.

Concrete production has improved by incorporating RHA as a replacement for SF, reducing environmental pollution and lowering costs. Previous study demonstrated that RHA improves concrete’s mechanical and microstructural properties while also providing excellent durability, high frost resistance, and chemical resistance28. The incorporation of RHA improved compressive strength due to its finer particle size and higher silicon-to-aluminum (Si/Al) ratio compared to GGBS29. Due to its pozzolanic nature, RHA has been blended into cement at levels of up to 30% to investigate its impact on consistency, setting time, and permeability, yielding improved results30. Concrete containing 15% or more of RHA significantly enhance compressive strength, demonstrating that reactive silica content is more critical than FA for developing high-performance geopolymers31. Earlier studies confirm that amorphous silica refines the interfacial transition zone, densifying the concrete microstructure. RHA incorporation improves hardened properties, with research determining optimal replacement levels (typically 10–20%), curing effects, and strength-resistivity relationships32. Premkumar et al.33 investigated the combined effect of RHA and SF as partial cement replacements enhances the mechanical and durability properties of concrete, reducing cement consumption, and promoting sustainable construction practices. Finely ground RHA has demonstrated pozzolanic reactivity and durability performance comparable to silica fumes, despite its lower silica content and larger particle size, due to its high specific surface area and porous structure34. RHA demonstrated durability and strength enhancements comparable to SF, with microstructural densification contributing to improved performance, highlighting its cost-effective potential for sustainable concrete in rice-producing regions35. Before advancements in concrete technology, RHA was frequently discarded in landfills and lakes, contaminating the environment36. The advancements in green materials enhanced the utilization of RHA in mortar and concrete, enabling researchers to replace cement with RHA without compromising the mechanical properties37. In the ferrosilicon industry, SF is generally obtained as a by-product in electric arc furnaces at a relatively high temperature of approximately 2000 °C38. Considering cement and other related industries, silica extraction from various inorganic sources, including silica sand, diorite sand, and waste-colored glasses, has received considerable attention. Based on literature, RHA calcination (600–700 °C) likely has a lower carbon footprint than SF production (2000 °C) due to biomass carbon neutrality and avoided industrial processing39, though chemical treatment steps require optimization. A substitution of RHA resulted in a reduction in the carbon footprint (global warming potential) compared to the use of silica fume, making it eco-friendly40. RHA was sourced locally from Charsadda, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and was combusted and ground to ash, achieving 100% passing at 75 μm particle size5. The emission factors are 0.1032 kg CO2-eq/kg for combustion41 and 0.011 kg CO2-eq/kg for grinding42. In contrast, the production of silica fumes is associated with a higher CO2 emissions footprint compared to that of RHA, primarily due to the elevated temperatures required during its manufacturing process43. In contrast, silica extraction from various inorganic sources often requires complex processing techniques, including crushing, grinding, and high-energy milling, followed by impurity purification. In some cases, high-temperature sintering is also needed44. In contrast, silica extracted from well-known biological sources such as sugarcane bagasse ash and RHA are of superior quality and energy efficient. The amorphous and chemically reactive silica extracted from RHA exhibits high purity levels, significant chemical reactivity, and homogeneity45,46. Researchers have evaluated the sustainability of several RHA applications, including microsilica (MS) extracted from RHA47. A promising alternative to SF could be the EMS, which offers both economic and environmental advantages48.

Despite the high purity, high surface area, pozzolanic reactivity, and homogeneity of extracted silica from RHA, studies exploring its potential applications in cement, mortar, and concrete remain limited. Considering these properties, further exploration is needed to evaluate their full potential in cement-based materials. The partial cement replacement with EMS at low dosages (5% and 15%) significantly improved the mechanical and microstructural performance of mortar while also mitigating alkali-silica expansion48. Cavalcante49 reported that 16% silica extracted from RHA enhanced cement paste strength at all ages, surpassing 40% MK blends by 28 days. The improvement was attributed to the silica’s high reactivity and ultra-fine particles, which densified the microstructure and accelerated hydration. Microstructural analysis confirmed its effectiveness in refining pores and boosting mechanical performance. According to the modified Chapelle test, silica gel extracted from RHA contributed to the consumption of calcium hydroxide (CH), further confirming its amorphous nature. A 5% substitution of cement with extracted silica gel resulted in significantly enhanced compressive strength compared to control cement for the same curing age46. To optimize the performance and address compatibility issues of RHA, incorporating additional SCMs with higher pozzolanic activity and high surface area, such as MS, is recommended50.

The rapid advancements in infrastructure development have increased the demand for high-resilience and HSC, focusing on a long-term goal of creating sustainable and high-quality SCMs. Therefore, this study aims to explore the combined use of RHA and EMS as high-volume (20–50%) cement replacements for developing sustainable concrete. The specific goals were to assess the mechanical performance and durability of ternary concrete mixes and to investigate their microstructural and pore structure modifications using advanced characterization techniques, highlighting the potential of RHA and EMS as viable alternatives to conventional silica-based materials in HSC.

Research significance

In response to the growing need for low-carbon construction materials, this study explores the innovative application of regionally sourced RHA and EMS as partial replacements for cement to produce more sustainable concrete. While prior research has often explored the individual effects of RHA or MS on concrete, less attention has been paid to their synergistic application, especially when using locally sourced materials and extracted silica from agricultural waste. The current study addresses this gap by evaluating the mechanical performance, durability, and microstructural characteristics of concrete incorporating varying high dosages of RHA (15%, 25%, and 35%) and EMS (5%, 10%, and 15%). The mechanical strength, durability, and microstructural behavior were examined at multiple curing ages (14, 28, and 56 days). X-ray Diffraction (XRD), X-ray fluorescence (XRF), Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and nitrogen (N2) adsorption analysis were used to understand hydration processes, pore structure, and silica reactivity. This research holds potential to influence future studies by establishing a foundation for optimized ternary binder systems using agricultural waste. Furthermore, this study promotes sustainable construction practices, supports policy efforts to reduce cement-related carbon emissions, and offers a viable, cost-effective alternative to commercial SF, especially relevant for regions with abundant biomass resources. Ultimately, the research aims to promote the industrial use of regionally available RHA as a substitute for commercial pozzolans in the production of cost-effective and high-performance sustainable concrete.

Ordinary Portland Cement type-1 from Bestway Cement, Pakistan, was utilized as per ASTM C-15051. Locally sourced fine and coarse aggregates were utilized as filler materials in concrete mixtures. The gradation curves following ASTM C13652 are displayed in Fig. 1.

Soil properties

Soil plays a pivotal role in plant and crop growth and productivity. Soil is influential in producing healthy plants and proper crop growth. Several significant elements and their oxides are found in soil, such as silicon (Si), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), potassium (K), manganese (Mn), phosphorus (P), sodium (Na), aluminum (Al), and iron (Fe) separated from parent source generally covered by igneous or sedimentary rocks which greatly influenced the production of crops. In addition, silica (SiO2) is identified as a critical part of the soil profile and a fundamental element of plant cell walls, which substantially influences the physical and chemical characteristics of soil and crops. Moreover, Alumina contributes to physicochemical soil properties, and iron oxide contributes to soil fertility; additionally, other oxides, CaO, Na2O, MgO, and K2O, are also present in trace amounts, affecting soil properties5. As per the literature, RH samples were collected from Charsadda, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, based on soil properties having the maximum amount of Silica as a major oxide. Production of RHA

In the current study, the RH was burned at a controlled temperature of 600–700 °C using a ferrocement drum for 24 h to produce high silica RHA. Subsequently, the burning of RH produced approximately 22% of the ash. After the incineration, the ash gradually turned cool and was left in the furnace before being ground in a rotary mill at 15 rpm for 12 h to obtain finer material. After grinding, the sample was packed in polythene bags for further investigation. The ash obtained was analyzed for mineralogical composition using XRD (JDX 3532, JEOL, Japan) and SiO2 content through an XRF (EDS-7000, Shimadzu, Japan) installed in Central Resource Labs, University of Peshawar. Elemental analysis and surface morphology were investigated using SEM-EDS with JSM-IT100 at the National Centre of Excellence in Geology, University of Peshawar.

SEM analysis of RHA revealed irregular morphology with visible aggregates, consistent with findings in the literature61. The fine, irregular particles confirmed the highly amorphous nature of silica, resulting in a high surface area that contributes to enhanced pozzolanic reactivity. According to EDS results, considerable amounts of silica and oxygen, and relatively low calcium suggest that RHA is more effective as a silica contributor rather than a calcium source. The high silica content promotes pozzolanic activity by reacting with Ca(OH)2 to form CSH gel, improving concrete strength and durability. Similarly, SEM of EMS showed globular structures, facilitating better dispersion in cement62. The elemental composition analysis revealed silicon and oxygen as the predominant elements, consistent with the expected SiO2 content of MS, as confirmed by XRD (Fig. 4). The minor carbon peaks observed in EDS analysis are attributed to standard sample preparation artifacts, specifically from the carbon tape used for mounting63. The reduced particle size and indistinct crystal structures indicate the amorphous nature of the sample64. EMS, with high SiO2 content and minimal impurities, demonstrated excellent pozzolanic properties. Due to its high reactivity, EMS can effectively react with Ca(OH)2 to form additional CSH gel, significantly enhancing the strength and long-term durability of concrete.

Concrete mix proportions

This study evaluated the performance of four concrete mixes, including CM and three ternary mixes incorporating EMS and RHA. In ternary mixtures, high cement replacement ranged from 20% (05% EMS + 15% RHA) to 35% (10% EMS + 25% RHA), and 50% (15% EMS + 35% RHA) was carried out. The EMS replacement levels (5%, 10%, 15%) were selected as per the optimal 05–15% range identified in prior studies for pozzolanic efficiency, while balancing mechanical performance and durability46,48,49. Similarly, the RHA replacement (15%, 25% and 35%) was selected to evaluate the progressive influence of higher replacements on mechanical and microstructural properties65. The details of these mixed compositions are presented in Table 4. The primary purpose of adding EMS to ternary mixtures with RHA is to replace the maximum amount of cement without compromising the strength and durability properties of concrete. The total amount of binder for each mixture was 450 kg/m³. Therefore, 157.5 kg/m3 of water was kept the same for all the mixtures. Furthermore, to achieve workability with slump values of 120 ± 30, an additional high-range water-reducing admixture (sikament-520) was utilized at various percentages (wt% of binder).

Testing program

Preparation of specimens

According to ASTM C19266 guidelines, concrete ingredients were mixed with the help of a power-driven rotatory mixer. Immediately after achieving the required slump, the freshly mixed concrete was cast into cylindrical molds with a diameter of 100 mm and a height of 200 mm, following ASTM C-3967. Samples were prepared for curing periods of 14, 28, and 56 days to evaluate the mechanical strength of concrete. After casting, molds were wrapped with plastic sheets and kept in a controlled environment maintained at 20 ± 1 °C and 60 ± 5% relative humidity for 24 h. Following this, concrete specimens were demolded and submerged in a water tank for the specified curing ages of 14, 28, and 56 days. Before testing, the top and bottom surfaces of the specimens were leveled with the help of an end surface grinder to ensure uniformity.

Water absorption and apparent porosity (AP)

In addition to mechanical testing, WA and AP tests were conducted on the hardened concrete samples per ASTM C94869. These tests were performed to assess the concrete’s water absorption capacity, providing valuable insight into its durability, including porosity and permeability characteristics. Cylindrical specimens (100 mm diameter, 50 mm thickness) were prepared from samples cured for 56 days. These specimens were immersed in water at 21 °C, and their weights were observed at 24 h intervals until a stabilized saturated surface dry (SSD) condition was attained. The SSD was considered to be achieved when the weight difference between two consecutive measurements was less than 0.5%. The most recent weight recorded is denoted as ‘B’. Subsequently, the samples were suspended in water, and their weight was determined to be the nearest 0.01 g, recorded as ‘A’. The specimens were dried in an oven at 100 ± 5 °C, and their weights were noted at 24 h intervals. Once the weight loss was observed to be less than 0.5% of the previous reading, the specimens were allowed to cool in a vacuum desiccator, maintaining room temperature. Concrete permeability

Water permeability test was performed on the hardened concrete specimens to investigate water penetration depth at the National University of Sciences and Technology, Islamabad, Pakistan, following BS EN 12390-870. Three cylindrical samples (300 mm diameter, 150 mm height) from each mixture, water-cured for 56 days, were evaluated under a water pressure of 500 ± 50 kPa on the bottom surface. Observations were made periodically on the unpressurized surface to monitor any changes in appearance. After 72 ± 2 h, the specimens were split using a Universal Testing Machine to investigate the depth of water penetration. The average penetration depth was calculated from the measurements taken from three cylindrical samples; all cast with the same concrete batch and evaluated simultaneously. Water penetration under exterior hydraulic pressure in partially saturated media, such as concrete, is linked to sorptivity and unsteady flow. Thus, the method quantifying water transport under a pressure gradient can be evaluated under a non-stationary regime71. The primary assumption is that the water penetration test is performed only when steady-state conditions are reached72. However, the studies presented indicate that applied pressure and measurement duration are adequate to achieve results corresponding to a fully saturated state, even for concrete with low permeability. Furthermore, the high density of tested concrete samples with minimal penetration depth relative to the specimen supports the uniaxial water penetration assumption. Consequently, this method enables us to calculate the coefficient of permeability.

Microstructure investigation

Preparation of cement pastes for microstructure investigation

CM specimen and three ternary paste specimens with different proportions of EMS and RHA (E05R15, E10R25, and E15R35) as partial cement replacements (by weight) were prepared. The mixer was thoroughly blended using a Hobart mixer. After the proper mixing, the pastes were poured into circular plastic molds 20 mm in diameter and 50 mm in height. Subsequently, the molds were sealed and kept for 28 days to cure, maintaining room temperature. After curing, the test specimens were demolded and dried using an ion exchange method to ensure the stoppage of the hydration process. The acquired powdered and thinly sliced specimens were cleaned in isopropanol for 15 min, then oven-dried at 40 ℃ for 30 min. The specimens obtained were held in polythene plastic bags till testing.

X-ray diffraction

The mineralogical investigation of well-ground cementitious samples was conducted using a specially designed sample handler for analysis of an X-ray powder diffraction machine using (XRD, JDX 3532, JEOL, Japan, with SX-Rays-Cu Ka (I = 1.5418 Å). The XRD data were obtained in the range of 2θ = 0° to 80°, installed in the Central Resource Labs, University of Peshawar. After 28 days, the cured samples were taken out of their molds and crushed into smaller fractions, which were treated with ethanol to stop further hydration73. Before analysis, the smaller fraction was further oven-dried and ground to a particle size lower than 70 μm74.

SEM-EDS analysis

The microstructural behavior of cement pastes was studied using the SEM-EDS technique on thick slices prepared from the hardened specimens with JSM-IT100 at NCEG, University of Peshawar. As discussed above, the hardened paste slices were dried through the solvent exchange method using isopropanol. The microstructural and compositional transformations of the tested specimens were examined.

Fourier transformed infrared (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was performed to characterize the different functional groups present in the cement paste samples having various percentages of EMS and RHA using an FTIR Spectrometer (Model Cary 630, Agilent Technologies, USA) in addition to the microstructural investigation of samples. The infrared spectrum was recorded between (400 cm−1 and 4000 cm−1) when the dry powder specimens were directly exposed to an infrared light source.

N2 adsorption

Besides XRD and SEM analysis, N2 adsorption tests were conducted on powdered samples (approximately 0.3 g) collected from the control and ternary-based mixes after 28 days of casting. An N2 adsorption analysis was performed to find the induced pore volume and BET surface area using (NOVA2200e, Quanta chrome, USA) analyzer, maintaining the temperature of 273 K, at the Central Research Laboratory, University of Peshawar. During the procedure, the powdered samples were initially degassed to eliminate any airborne contaminants. Similarly, the test was performed while maintaining controlled pressure and ambient temperature.

Comparison of results obtained for the water absorption and apparent porosity of control and ternary mixes

In addition to validating the strength properties of the ternary concrete mixtures under investigation, other key characteristics, such as WA and AP, were also assessed and compared with the CM. WA and AP are commonly used as indirect indicators for evaluating the durability characteristics of hardened concrete. The influence of ternary mixtures having different percentages of EMS (5%, 10%, and 15%) and RHA (15%, 25%, and 35%) on WA and AP is displayed in Figs. 11 and 12, respectively. The results revealed that the low cement replacement by EMS and RHA by 20% (E05R15) exhibited the lowest WA and AP values compared to the CM and higher replacement levels (E10R25 and E15R35), as shown in Figs. 11 and 12. The decrease in WA of the E05R15 and E10R25 mixes can be attributed to the low value of AP (Fig. 12), which is likely due to the fine particles of EMS and RHA, contributing to significant pore refinement in the concrete. The incorporation of RHA up to 20% cement replacement leads to a significant reduction in WA compared to CM84. In contrast, the higher replacement level of EMS and RHA (E15R35) resulted in increased WA and AP values compared to CM. The higher WA and AP values observed with increased cement replacement by EMS and RHA up to 50% might be attributed to the slower pozzolanic reaction rate, adversely affecting the hydration process and resulting in a more porous microstructure. The findings also confirm that the compressive and split tensile strengths of concrete samples containing EMS and RHA are higher than those of CM, except for concrete samples with 15% EMS and 35% RHA (E15R35), whose strengths were lower than CM. Higher RHA content can reduce mechanical strength and increase water absorption and porosity due to unreacted silica, which limits its participation in hydration and leads to weaker hydration products85. Moreover, high dosages of EMS tend to form agglomerates due to their ultrafine particle size, which hinders proper hydration and results in weaker products, ultimately reducing strength and durability48,86. The above results revealed that cement replacement up to 10% by EMS and 25% by RHA may be adopted for future applications, as it achieves satisfactory strength results. Additionally, replacing cement with EMS and RHA at high levels, like 50%, is not recommended, as it leads to reduced strengths at all ages compared to CM.

Comparison of results obtained for the water permeability of the control mix and ternary mixes

Water permeability is a critical property influencing the long-term durability of concrete. Higher values of water permeability result in increased water penetration, which elevates the risk of deterioration, especially when concrete is exposed to harsh environments, such as seawater or industrial water. The increased water penetration, in turn, compromises the durability of concrete structures in the long term. The results of water penetration and the permeability coefficient derived from penetration are illustrated in Table 7. The test results revealed that incorporating EMS and RHA at low percentage replacements (E05R15 and E10R25) significantly reduced the water penetration compared to CM and the high percentage replacement mixture by 50% (E15R35). Specifically, water penetration decreased by 32.67% for E05R15 and 11.63% for E10R25 compared to CM. E10R25 showed a significant reduction in water penetration as compared to the E05R15. However, its consistently higher compressive and tensile strength across all curing periods ensures superior structural performance for demanding applications. The observed water penetration reduction results from the synergistic filler effects of EMS and RHA particles, which enhance matrix density and reduce porosity relative to the control mixture. For durability-critical applications, the combination of improved mechanical properties and moderate permeability reduction makes E10R25 the optimal choice, as structural integrity often outweighs permeability considerations alone. The addition of pozzolans such as RHA and SF decreases the pore size and makes the concrete less permeable87. In contrast, the higher percentage replacement sample, E15R35, exhibited increased water penetration by 29.70% compared to CM. The increase in water penetration by adding high percentages of EMS and RHA is attributed to poor dispersion, causing agglomeration problems, adversely affecting the hydration process, and ultimately resulting in weak products. Inadequate dispersion of silica promotes agglomeration, impairing hydration, liquid penetration, and stability, particularly when combined with a high percentage of RHA88. Wu et al.89 reveal that poor dispersion of SF results in particle agglomeration, leading to weaker microstructure development and reduced stability compared to well-dispersed samples. The E05R15 ternary sample reflected superior performance in resisting water penetration, followed by E10R25, CM, and E15R35. Furthermore, all the values obtained for concrete mixtures are in the range of 10−9 to 10−10 mm/s and are desirable for concrete used in structures exposed to severe environments, such as marine and industrial conditions.

FTIR spectroscopy

The FTIR spectroscopy compares the results between the CM and mixes containing EMS and RHA substitutes with different percentages, and all samples exhibit IR bands at the exact location, while their intensities vary, as demonstrated in Fig. 17. These variations in the intensities primarily result in the production of CH and CSH, major hydration products. In the range between 450 cm−1 and 1100 cm−1, all samples exhibit a complicated collection of bands demonstrating symmetric and asymmetric stretching of the Si-O bands. In particular, the prominent peaks at 900–1100 cm−1 are linked with Si-O band vibrations in the CSH phase94. The peak shifts in these ranges indicate silica polymerization. The absorption peak recorded at 954 cm−1 in the control sample indicates the valence fluctuations of the Si-O band in a tetrahedral structure. This peak is associated with the linkage of the oxygen atom [SiO4] to the central Si-O atom, which plays a role in CSH formation. Peak shifts for all ternary mixes (EMS and RHA) substitutions from 954 to 961 cm−1, respectively. As a result of the development of additional SiO2 gel, these shifts exhibit prominent levels of silica polymerization with the incorporation of EMS and RHA. The FTIR spectroscopy results align with the EDS results, indicating that E05R15 and E10R25 samples formed denser CSH-phases having a lower Ca/Si ratio than the control sample. The increase in compressive strength for E05R15 and E10R25 mixes can be linked to the production of additional CSH gels. The observed Si-O peak shift (954 to 961 cm⁻¹) in E15R35 without enhanced polymerization reflects silica agglomeration, validated by SEM analysis, leading to compromised silica connectivity, despite higher silica content. Additionally, the intensity peaks were recorded at 711 cm−1, 872 cm−1, and 1420 cm−1 as a result of calcite formation during carbonation95.

Sustainability and practical implications

A comprehensive evaluation comparing concrete incorporating RHA and EMS with conventional silica fume-based mixes reinforces the innovative value of this study. Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of binary and ternary blends of RHA and SF in improving mechanical strength and durability. Zareei et al.32 reported that incorporating 20% RHA and 10% SF improved compressive strength and acid resistance while reducing water permeability and chloride ingress by 26% and 78%, respectively. Gupta et al.99 observed strength increases of 4.12% with 10% RHA and 13.4% with 20% SF. Premkumar et al.33 found that using 15% SF with 30% RHA enhanced compressive and flexural strength while reducing sorptivity and chloride penetration. Similarly, Xu et al.34 showed compressive strength gains of 12.8% with 10% RHA and 17% with 10% SF, along with improved microstructure and durability. Sahoo et al.35 demonstrated that mixes with 15% RHA and 15% SF enhanced acid resistance, reduced water penetration, and refined the microstructure. Amin et al.65 achieved improved mechanical performance and reduced water absorption with a blend of 33% RHA and 7% SF, with lowered carbon emissions. Maharana et al.50 also confirmed enhanced strength, durability, and cost efficiency using 10% RHA and 10% MS. In line with these findings, the present study reveals that the E05R15 and E10R25 mixes (high volume cement replacement) improved compressive strength by 11.56% and 5.98%, respectively, while achieving lower porosity and enhanced durability, confirmed by microstructural analysis showing dense CSH formation. The findings suggest that optimizing concrete with locally sourced supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), such as RHA and EMS extracted from agricultural waste, can significantly reduce CO2 emissions and production costs without compromising strength and performance. The optimized mix design with 10% EMS and 25% RHA as cement replacements enhances mechanical properties and reduces reliance on conventional cement, offering a viable solution for the construction industry by utilizing local byproducts. While focusing on material characterization and mechanical behavior, the study highlights sustainability benefits such as reduced cement use, CO2 reduction, agricultural waste valorization, and local material circularity. This makes the approach particularly viable for large-scale applications in regions like Pakistan, where sustainable construction is crucial to meet the challenges of rapid urbanization. These findings underscore the potential of industrial byproducts in developing green, high-performance concrete and inform future policies and practices toward low-carbon construction solutions.

Conclusions

This study examined the performance of RHA and microsilica chemically extracted from RHA in developing high-performance concrete. For the formation of high-performance and sustainable concrete mixes, different proportions of RHA (15%, 25%, and 35%) and EMS (5%, 10%, and 15%) were used as substitutes for cement. Finally, the combined effects of RHA and EMS as cement substitutes on mechanical, durability, and microstructural characteristics were studied.

The main conclusions drawn from the present experimental work are outlined below:

The feasibility of using EMS combined with RHA as a partial substitution of cement in concrete revealed great potential in terms of enhanced mechanical strength, durability properties, and improved microstructure. However, researchers should consider high shear mixing and ultrasonic dispersion techniques in the future, which could result in effective utilization of EMS along with RHA at high volume and with improved performance. Further studies should also aim to improve water penetration resistance while maintaining these key properties. Advanced techniques such as isothermal calorimetry and thermogravimetric analysis are recommended to quantify hydration behavior. Moreover, comprehensive life cycle assessments should be conducted to evaluate the environmental impact of EMS production, including energy consumption, CO2 emissions, and chemical usage. Pilot-scale trials are also essential to ensure process consistency across diverse RH sources and to refine reagent recovery strategies for industrial-scale implementation.
