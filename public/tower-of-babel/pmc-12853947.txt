Paradoxical gestational normotension in primary aldosteronism: a case report of diagnostic challenges and postpartum rebound hypertension
Authors: Yuzhu Fan, Bing Wang, Leilei Xing, Guiting Liang, Junhong Duan, Chunliang Wang, Yu Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary aldosteronism (PA), characterized by dysregulated aldosterone secretion, is a leading cause of endocrine hypertension. While its association with cardiovascular morbidity is well-established, pregnancy-related PA remains understudied, with limited data on hemodynamic fluctuations and diagnostic pitfalls. Transient blood pressure normalization during gestation, attributed to progesterone-mediated mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism, often masks underlying pathology, delaying diagnosis and increasing postpartum complications.Case presentationA 29-year-old woman presented with recurrent dizziness and labile hypertension (ambulatory BP: 135–163/85–108 mmHg). The initial evaluation did not include assessment for secondary hypertension, with symptoms attributed to essential hypertension. Following pregnancy confirmation, amlodipine was discontinued due to teratogenicity concerns. Paradoxically, first-trimester stage 1 hypertension (119–142/72–83 mmHg) normalized by the third trimester (108–126/72–79 mmHg) without gestational hypertensive disorders. At 6 weeks postpartum, progressive rebound hypertension (145–167/92–105 mmHg) prompted tertiary referral. Biochemical profiling revealed suppressed plasma renin (2.81 mU/L) and elevated aldosterone-to-renin ratio [ARR: 5.427(ng/dL)/(mU/L)]. Confirmatory testing (seated saline infusion and captopril suppression tests) demonstrated dysregulated aldosterone secretion. Adrenal CT identified an 8-mm right adrenal nodule, with adrenal vein sampling confirming lateralized secretion (lateralization index: 7.46). Laparoscopic right adrenalectomy achieved hypertension remission and complete biochemical remission.ConclusionPA should be suspected in hypertensive women of reproductive age, irrespective of potassium status. Pregnancy-induced blood pressure normalization does not preclude PA diagnosis, mandating systematic postpartum reassessment. Background

Primary aldosteronism (PA), defined as pathological aldosterone production independent of renin-angiotensin system regulation, establishes a distinct endocrine hypertension phenotype marked by sodium-volume expansion, suppressed plasma renin concentration (PRC), and potassium homeostasis disruption [1]. The disorder is associated with substantially elevated risks of cardiovascular morbidity, nephropathy, and metabolic dysfunction, though these complications may be mitigated through targeted pharmacological interventions or surgical management. Diagnostic evaluation follows a three-step protocol: initial screening via measurement of the aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR), subsequently confirmed through dynamic testing. Subtype differentiation requires anatomic localization using cross-sectional adrenal imaging followed by functional confirmation through adrenal vein sampling - the current gold standard for determining surgical candidacy [1].

Current evidence regarding the prevalence, diagnosis, and management of PA during pregnancy remains limited, predominantly derived from case reports and small cohort studies. Clinical manifestations exhibit profound heterogeneity, spanning from progesterone-mediated blood pressure stabilization to recalcitrant hypertension complicated by maternal-fetal morbidity. However, systematic analysis reveals elevated rates of pregnancy-related complications in PA patients compared with the general obstetric population [2]. These findings substantiate current consensus recommendations: universal PA screening should be prioritized for women of reproductive potential with hypertension contemplating pregnancy, accompanied by multidisciplinary management strategies to optimize perinatal outcomes [3].

Case presentation

A 29-year-old woman was evaluated at an external institution in August 2022 for recurrent dizziness and labile hypertension. Her chief complaint was persistent non-rotatory lightheadedness, devoid of syncope, visual disturbances, or vestibular symptoms. Blood pressure (BP) surges during symptomatic episodes measured 150–160/90–100 mmHg. Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) demonstrated sustained hypertension with a 24-hour profile of 135–163/85–108 mmHg. She denied any personal medical history or family history of hereditary disorders.​ Initial workup revealed normokalemia (serum potassium: 3.8 mmol/L), preserved renal function, and no endocrine stigmata. In the absence of secondary hypertension indicators, her condition was provisionally attributed to occupational stress-induced essential hypertension. Secondary hypertension screening was not pursued, and antihypertensive therapy was initiated with amlodipine 5 mg daily.

At the 3-month follow-up, persistently elevated BP measurements (128–149/78–95 mmHg) coincided with ongoing dizziness. Following confirmation of pregnancy in November 2022, the patient voluntarily discontinued amlodipine due to perceived teratogenic risks. ABPM documented stage 1 hypertension during the first trimester (119–142/72–83 mmHg) spontaneously normalized during the third trimester (108–126/72–79 mmHg), without evidence of preeclampsia and hypokalemia. Notably, within 6 weeks postpartum, she developed recurrent dizziness accompanied by progressive blood pressure escalation (145–167/92–105 mmHg), necessitating referral to our tertiary center for comprehensive secondary hypertension workup.

Upon admission, diltiazem 90 mg twice daily was initiated based on her blood pressure profile. Potassium supplementation was not administered due to persistent normokalemia during the postpartum period. Physical examination demonstrated a body mass index of 23.6 kg/m² (height 166 cm) with absence of cushingoid facies, thyromegaly, or peripheral edema. Cardiopulmonary auscultation revealed regular heart sounds without murmurs and clear lung fields. Abdominal examination showed no organomegaly or vascular bruits. Systematic screening including laboratory tests (Table 1), polysomnography and renal Doppler ultrasonography excluded obstructive sleep apnea, renovascular hypertension, renal parenchymal disease, thyroid disease and pheochromocytoma. Biochemical profiling under diltiazem therapy (90 mg twice daily) disclosed normokalemia (3.8 mmol/L; reference 3.5–5.1) with pathological hormonal parameters: suppressed PRC (2.81 mU/L; reference 3.11–41.2) and diagnostic ARR [5.427 (ng/dL)/(mU/L); cutoff > 3.7]. Confirmatory testing demonstrated dysregulated aldosterone secretion: seated saline infusion test (SIT, post-infusion aldosterone 14.21 ng/dL) and captopril suppression test (CST, post-dose aldosterone 13.22 ng/dL), both exceeding diagnostic thresholds (Table 2). The low-dose dexamethasone suppression test demonstrated the absence of hypercortisolism.

Unenhanced CT demonstrated a hypoattenuating nodule (3 HU) in the right adrenal gland with mild contralateral adrenal enlargement. Contrast-enhanced adrenal CT revealed an 8 × 5 mm nodule in the medial limb of the right adrenal gland (Fig. 1A). Adrenal venous sampling (AVS) before and after adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation confirmed right-sided lateralized secretion, with a pre-ACTH stimulation lateralization index (LI) of 7.46 (diagnostic cutoff > 2.0) and a post-ACTH stimulation LI of 3.07 (Table 3). The biochemical and imaging evidence satisfies the diagnostic criteria on lateralized primary aldosteronism established by the Working Group on Endocrine Hypertension of the European Society of Hypertension [1].

The patient underwent laparoscopic right adrenalectomy, with histopathology confirming an adrenal adenoma (Fig. 1B). Antihypertensive therapy was discontinued postoperatively. Follow-up evaluations at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively revealed that the patient remained asymptomatic with sustained normotension per ABPM (118–128/72–78 mmHg) and complete biochemical remission [ARR: 1.6–2.1 (ng/dL)/(mU/L); serum potassium: 3.9–4.4 mmol/L], permitting permanent discontinuation of antihypertensive therapy (Fig. 2; Table 4).

Discussion

This case exemplifies a classic diagnostic pitfall in hypertension evaluation: normokalemia with misattribution of elevated blood pressure to occupational stress delayed the diagnosis of secondary hypertension. Seminal data from the PAPY Study (n = 1,180) revealed PA prevalence reaches 11.2% in newly diagnosed hypertensive patients, establishing it as the most prevalent endocrine hypertension subtype [4]. However, a retrospective analysis from two European nations with advanced healthcare systems revealed that only 1%–2% of hypertensive individuals underwent PA screening during the subsequent decade [5]. This underdiagnosis stems partly from clinicians’ perception of PA rarity, prioritizing screening for hypokalemia-associated hypertension [6]. Crucially, a cross-sectional study demonstrated that merely 28.6% of hypokalemic patients had PA, while only 57.1% of PA patients exhibited hypokalemia, indicating substantial diagnostic gaps with selective screening [7].

The biochemical hallmark - markedly elevated ARR - necessitated confirmatory testing including SIT and CST. Methodologically, we employed a refined CST protocol with enhanced diagnostic accuracy: 2-hour post-captopril aldosterone levels > 11 ng/dL demonstrated superior performance characteristics [sensitivity 90% (95% CI, 84%-95%), specificity 90% (95% CI 83%-95%)] compared to traditional suppression thresholds [8].

A literature review reported 40 pregnancies in 38 women with primary aldosteronism. Among these women, 32.3% exhibited suboptimal blood pressure control, and 15.8% required adrenalectomy during pregnancy [9]. However, the normalization of blood pressure during pregnancy in this case represents a critical diagnostic confounder. Intriguingly, a part of PA patients with hypertension exhibit paradoxical blood pressure stabilization or reduction during gestation [10]. Placental estrogen stimulates hepatic angiotensinogen synthesis. The combined effect of angiotensinogen and estrogen induces marked increases in plasma renin concentration and activity, which in turn enhances aldosterone production [11]. However, an observational study revealed that gestational renin elevation substantially exceeds aldosterone increase, suppressing the ARR and potentially masking PA diagnosis in pregnancy [10]. Concurrently, elevated progesterone and prostacyclin antagonize aldosterone’s pressor effects, promoting blood pressure reduction or pseudonormalization in hypertension [12]. However, such transient hemodynamic “pseudo-remission” does not mitigate adverse outcomes: a case-control study demonstrated increased risk of preterm delivery in PA pregnancies compared to essential hypertension controls [13]. A systematic review found a significantly higher incidence of preeclampsia/eclampsia in primary aldosteronism (PA) patients (28.8%) compared to essential hypertension controls (18%) [3]. This finding was corroborated by a retrospective study of 19 postpartum-diagnosed PA patients, in which eclampsia was the most frequent complication (26%). Preterm delivery occurred in 5 cases, and over half underwent cesarean delivery primarily due to preeclampsia or uncontrolled maternal hypertension secondary to fetal distress [14]. Notably, elevated angiotensin II type-1 receptor autoantibodies (AT1-AAs) were detected in both PA and preeclamptic patients. However, the mechanistic relationship among PA, eclampsia, and AT1-AAs requires further elucidation [15, 16]. Furthermore, PA parturients develop rebound hypertension within 6 weeks postpartum, reflecting abrupt progesterone withdrawal and unopposed aldosterone action [10]. Our patient’s clinical trajectory precisely mirrored this pattern - paradoxical gestational normotension (108–126/72–79 mmHg) without proteinuria or hypokalemia masked underlying pathology, necessitating vigilant post-delivery reevaluation. Ultimately, we confirmed PA diagnosis through systematic workup.

Current Endocrine Society guidelines mandate AVS for subtyping PA in patients with bilateral adrenal abnormalities, particularly crucial for discerning unilateral hypersecretion in cases of microadenomas (< 1 cm) which CT imaging demonstrates limited specificity [5]. In this case, due to CT findings of a right adrenal nodule (8 × 5 mm) with contralateral hyperplasia, we performed AVS - the approach per Endocrine Society guidelines [5]. AVS revealed right-sided lateralized secretion and suppression of the left adrenal gland (Table 3). Based on the contralateral suppression phenomenon, we inferred that the left adrenal hyperplasia was nonfunctional [17]. However, the utility of ACTH-stimulated adrenal AVS in diagnosing PA and predicting outcomes remains controversial. The ADOPA trial suggested that ACTH stimulation during AVS may offer no clinical benefit [18]. The AVIS-2 study observed that cosyntropin stimulation may reduce the LI, potentially altering the interpretation of lateralization [19]. Elena et al. attributed this phenomenon to the adenoma’s preexisting high aldosterone output, which constrains its responsiveness to ACTH stimulation, whereas the contralateral adrenal gland increases aldosterone secretion. This results in LI reduction and incomplete contralateral suppression [20]. Both phenomena were observed in the present case. Definitive lateralization (baseline right lateralization index 7.46; right post-ACTH 3.07) guided successful laparoscopic right adrenalectomy, resulting in hypertension remission and complete biochemical remission. It is noteworthy that a retrospective cohort study found strong AVS lateralization may not rule out multifocal, asymmetric bilateral primary aldosteronism, highlighting the need for long-term follow-up in these patients [21].
