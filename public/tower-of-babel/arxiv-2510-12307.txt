Fully mixed virtual element schemes for steady-state poroelastic stress-assisted diffusion
Isaac Bermudez, Bryan Gómez-Vargas, Andrés E. Rubiano, Ricardo Ruiz-Baier

Abstract
We propose a fully mixed virtual element method for the numerical approximation of the coupling between stress-altered diffusion and linear elasticity equations with strong symmetry of total poroelastic stress (using the Hellinger–Reissner principle). A novelty of this work is that we introduce a less restrictive assumption on the stress-assisted diffusion coefficient, requiring an analysis of the perturbed diffusion equation using Banach spaces. The solvability of the continuous and discrete problems is established using a suitable modification of the abstract theory for perturbed saddle-point problems in Banach spaces (which is in itself a new result of independent interest). In addition, we establish optimal a priori error estimates. The method and its analysis are robust with respect to the poromechanical parameters. We also include a number of numerical examples that illustrate the properties of the proposed scheme. MSC (2010): 65N30; 65N15; 74D05. Keywords: Coupled elasticity-diffusion; Mixed virtual element methods; Stress-based formulations; Error estimates.

Introduction

Scope.

Mechanical deformations and the diffusion of solutes occur concurrently in various real-world problems, such as those found in metallurgy, geomechanics, and biomedicine. In certain specific applications, the interaction mechanisms involve stress altering the microstructure of the material. This phenomenon is referred to as stress-assisted diffusion, and a number of contributions are available for the analysis and discretisations of that problem in a variety of forms
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
. When the underlying medium is poroelastic the coupling implies that the effective poroelastic stress contains contributions from the fluid pressure and also from the diffusive quantity. The equations of poroelastic stress-assisted diffusion have been analysed numerically with mixed finite element formulations in
[
33
]
(see also a similar treatment for poroelasticity-heat equations in the recent work
[
16
]
, as well as twofold saddle-point formulations for poroelasticity equations with nonlinear permeability
[
36
,
39
]
).

The present work proposes a momentum and mass conservative, robust, and Biot-locking-free virtual element formulation for poroelastic stress-assisted diffusion systems. It constitutes an extension of the stress-assisted diffusion VEM scheme from
[
37
]
to the fully mixed poroelastic setting. The underlying model problem is based on
[
33
,
16
]
, but the formulation is simpler as it is based on the Hellinger–Reissner variational principle and imposing strong symmetry of the total Cauchy stress. For this we follow the similar works
[
1
,
3
,
22
]
.
In this context we also mention other recent polytopal discretisations for poroelasticity in mixed form, as proposed in
[
44
,
20
,
37
,
10
,
12
,
40
,
38
,
34
,
41
,
23
]
.

As the continuous formulation is also novel, we conduct its well-posedness analysis treating the linear diffusion-stress coupling terms as a perturbation of two perturbed saddle-point problems. For the stress-assisted diffusion nonlinearity we use a fixed-point argument based on Banach’s contraction mapping theorem, and combine this with two applications of the Babuška–Brezzi theory for perturbed saddle-points in
[
8
, Chapter 4]
and the Banach–Nečas–Babuška theory for global inf-sup conditions. The analysis requires uniform Lipschitz continuity on the nonlinearity as well as a small data assumption – a byproduct of using Banach fixed-point theory. However, and in contrast to previous analysis for stress-assisted diffusion and nonlinear Biot systems in
[
27
,
36
,
37
,
39
]
, here we only require the Lipschitz continuity of the inverse stress-assistance in
\amsmathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, which is a much more reasonable assumption. The price to pay is that now our diffusive flux trial and test functions are sought in
\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)
, requiring a slightly more involved inf-sup condition, and to invoke a modified abstract result for saddle-point problems in Banach spaces. However, the specific structure of this perturbed saddle-point problem does not fall in the framework of
[
19
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.4]
. These results require the main diagonal block (in our case, the flux-flux bilinear form) to be coercive on the kernel of the off-diagonal operator, and this condition is not met in our case. On the other hand, the recent result in
[
17
, Theorem 3.2]
assumes that the main form is elliptic on the entire space, which is also not the case for our flux-flux bilinear form. Nevertheless, we observe that the present formulation possesses a dual structure: the perturbation block (the lower diagonal block) is induced by a bilinear form that is elliptic on the whole space. Therefore we introduce a new abstract stability result—symmetric to
[
17
, Theorem 3.2]
—which is tailored for the structure of the present problem.

The proposed formulation for Biot–stress-assisted diffusion is robust and conservative, and at the same time has fewer unknowns than those used in
[
33
]
. Another important advantage of the analysis presented herein is that we are able to establish in a straightforward manner the uniqueness of the discrete solution. This is a difficult task as observed in previous works
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
33
]
. There, the solvability analysis of the continuous fixed-point scheme relies on regularity assumptions on the exact solutions. In particular, in that Hilbert context it was possible to control (under small data assumptions) the diffusive flux in the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}-
norm. The same holds in Banach spaces (using a primal formulation for the diffusion equation), but in the
\mathrm{L}^{\frac{2r}{2-r}}-
norm, where
r
is such that
2d/(d+1)-\varepsilon<r<2
with
\varepsilon>0
. In both situations, such bounds enabled the direct application of Banach’s Fixed Point Theorem. In contrast, at the discrete level, the additional regularity was not ensured; and as a result, we could not establish the Lipschitz continuity—and thus the contractivity—of the fixed-point operator in a direct way.

In the present work, by formulating the problem in Banach spaces and introducing a suitable fixed-point operator, we obtain bounds in the associated norms that provide the necessary control on the data. Importantly, these bounds do not rely on additional regularity assumptions and apply both at the discrete and continuous levels, thus allowing the use of more natural smallness conditions and the application of Banach’s fixed-point theory.

Outline.

The remainder of the paper has been organised in the following manner. In the rest of this section we recall usual notational convention for the domain and the used functional spaces. We also state the governing equations of Biot–stress-assisted diffusion, giving also assumptions on the nonlinear diffusion coefficient and the rest of the model parameters. Section
2
contains the derivation of the weak formulation in double saddle-point structure, it specifies the splitting of kernels of suitable operators, and it examines the properties of all bilinear forms (including stability and boundedness). This section also addresses the unique solvability of the separate Biot and mixed diffusion problems, and proving an auxiliary abstract result for perturbed saddle-point problems in Banach spaces. In Section
3
we construct the virtual element discretisation of the coupled model problem, introducing the needed discrete spaces, polynomial interpolation and projection operators, appropriate stabilisation, and discrete operators. In Section
4
we show that the scheme is well-posed using a similar fixed-point argument as in the continuous case. A priori error estimates are presented in Section
5
, and simple numerical tests are provided in Section
6
, including the verification of optimal convergence, simulation of classical benchmark tests for poromechanics, and a specific application for stress-assisted diffusion.

Lemma 2.2
(symmetry and positive semi-definiteness of diagonal forms)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})=\frac{1}{2\mu}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\tt d},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\tt d})+\frac{1}{d(2\mu+d\lambda)}(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\sigma},\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau})\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\amsmathbb{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega),

(2.4)

, the form
a_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}(\bullet,\bullet)
is positive semi-definite.

\square

Lemma 2.3

There exist constants

(2.7a)

\displaystyle c(\psi,\psi)

(2.7b)

Lemma 2.4
(continuous inf-sup conditions)

There exist positive constants
\beta_{B},\beta_{b}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\amsmathbb{V}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})}{\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}}

\displaystyle\geq\beta_{B}\|\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\,\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}\in[\mathrm{ker}(\mathbf{B}^{*})]^{\perp},

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\sup_{\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)}{\|\psi\|_{0,\Omega}}

\displaystyle\geq\beta_{b}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\operatorname*{div},\Omega).

(2.8b)

Proof.

To prove (
2.8a
), it suffices to establish the following two independent inf–sup conditions, which follow from the diagonal structure of
B(\bullet,\bullet)
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\amsmathbb{H}^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{v},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau})}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}}\geq\beta_{1}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}_{01},

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\sup_{q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{(q,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{w})}{\|q\|_{0,\Omega}}\geq\beta_{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}_{02}.

(2.9b)

For (
2.9a
), we refer to
[
26
, Lemma 2.2, eq. (14)]
, whereas (
2.9b
) holds by virtue of the existence of a constant
\widehat{\beta}_{2}>0
, such that (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Lemma 3.2]
)

\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{(q,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{w})}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}}\geq\widehat{\beta}_{2}\|q\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall\,q\in\amsmathbb{V}^{\perp}_{02}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),

and the identity given by
[
8
, eq. (4.3.18)]
, which also implies that
\beta_{2}=\widehat{\beta}_{2}
. Thus, the required inequality (
2.8a
) is obtained with
\beta_{B}=\frac{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}}{4}
.

Regarding the inf-sup condition for the bilinear form
b(\bullet,\bullet)
(cf. (
2.8b
)) let us first define
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:=|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{L}^{4/3}(\Omega)
, and consider the problem of finding
\widehat{\psi}\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,{4/3}}(\Omega)
such that

\nabla\widehat{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}\quad\text{in}\ \Omega,\qquad\widehat{\psi}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

Testing against
\nabla\zeta
for any
\zeta\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega)
we have

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\widehat{\psi}\cdot\nabla\zeta=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\nabla\zeta\qquad\forall\zeta\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega).

(2.10)

The bilinear form
\tilde{a}:\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4/3}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega)\to\amsmathbb{R}
defined as
\tilde{a}(\psi,\zeta):=\int_{\Omega}\nabla{\psi}\cdot\nabla\zeta

is bounded using Hölder’s inequality

|\tilde{a}(\psi,\zeta)|\leq\|\nabla\psi\|_{0,4/3;\Omega}\|\nabla\zeta\|_{0,4;\Omega}\leq\|\psi\|_{1,4/3;\Omega}\|\zeta\|_{1,4;\Omega},

and it satisfies the inf-sup Banach–Nečas–Babuška condition: for a fixed
\psi\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4/3}(\Omega)
,

\sup_{\zeta\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\tilde{a}(\psi,\zeta)}{\|\zeta\|_{1,4;\Omega}}=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\nabla\psi\|_{0,4/3;\Omega}\|\nabla\zeta\|_{0,4;\Omega}}{\|\zeta\|_{1,4;\Omega}}\|\gtrsim\|\psi\|_{1,4/3;\Omega},

by duality between
\mathbf{L}^{4/3}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)
and Poincaré inequality.
In turn, the linear functional
\tilde{F}:{\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega)}\to\amsmathbb{R}

defined as
\tilde{F}(\zeta):=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\nabla\zeta
is bounded
thanks to Hölder’s inequality

|\tilde{F}(\zeta)|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0,4/3;\Omega}\|\nabla\zeta\|_{0,4;\Omega}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0,4/3;\Omega}\|\zeta\|_{1,4;\Omega},

and so we obtain that there exists a unique
\widehat{\psi}\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,{4/3}}(\Omega)
satisfying (
2.10
). This is a standard consequence of the theory of elliptic operators in reflexive Banach spaces
[
25
, Theorem 2.6]
. The continuous dependence on data associated with this problem, in combination with the continuous embedding
\mathrm{W}^{1,{4/3}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
(valid for Lipschitz domains in 2D and 3D), then gives

\|\widehat{\psi}\|_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\|\widehat{\psi}\|_{1,{4/3};\Omega}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0,4/3;\Omega},

(2.11)

where the hidden constant depends only on the norm of the continuous embedding and the Poincaré constant.

Next, for any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
we choose
\psi=\widehat{\psi}
and exploiting the fact that
\widehat{\psi}\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,{4/3}}(\Omega)
, we invoke the integration by parts formula to derive

\displaystyle\sup_{\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)}{\|\psi\|_{0,\Omega}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\xi},\widehat{\psi})}{2\|\widehat{\psi}\|_{0,\Omega}}=\frac{-\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\widehat{\psi}}{2\|\widehat{\psi}\|_{0,\Omega}}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\nabla\widehat{\psi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}{2\|\widehat{\psi}\|_{0,\Omega}}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}{2\|\widehat{\psi}\|_{0,\Omega}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0,4/3;\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,4;\Omega}}{\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0,4/3;\Omega}}=\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,4;\Omega},

(2.12)

where in the second-last equality we have used (
2.10
) and in the last inequality we have used the definition of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and (
2.11
).

On the other hand, and again for any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
, we can construct
\widetilde{\psi}\equiv-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
. This straightforwardly implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)}{\|\psi\|_{0,\Omega}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\xi},\widetilde{\psi})}{2\|\widetilde{\psi}\|_{0,\Omega}}=\frac{-\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\psi}\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}}{2\|\widetilde{\psi}\|_{0,\Omega}}=\frac{\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}}{2\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,\Omega}}=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(2.13)

Finally, it suffices to add (
2.1
) with (
2.13
) to obtain the desired inf-sup condition, with constant
\beta_{b}>0
depending only on the Poincaré and continuous embedding constants.

\square

Remark 2.1

It is important to clarify that the unique solvability of the auxiliary problem (
2.10
) is understood in a weak sense and it only needs the Banach–Nečas–Babuška argument in combination with Poincaré’s inequality. Note that we are not claiming a classical solution of the Dirichlet Poisson problem with
\mathrm{L}^{s^{\prime}}(\Omega)
control, which allows (from the sharp result in
[
35
]
) a solution integrability only up to
\mathrm{W}^{1,s}(\Omega)
with
\frac{3}{2}-\epsilon<s<3+\epsilon
for
d=3
and
\frac{4}{3}-\epsilon<s<4+\epsilon
for
d=2
.

The strategy for the analysis of well-posedness of (
2.2
) is simply to decompose the problem into the poroelasticity equations (first two equations in that system) and the remaining diffusion equation in mixed form. We separate the analysis for each problem in the following sub-section.

2.2
Unique solvability of decoupled Biot equations and mixed diffusion equations

As announced in Section
1
, the following result is a
dual
to
[
17
, Theorem 3.2]
and it provides the necessary framework to establish the well-posedness of the diffusion subproblem without requiring coercivity of
a_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}(\bullet,\bullet)
.

Theorem 2.5
(Abstract result for Q-elliptic perturbed saddle-point problems)

Let H and Q be reflexive Banach spaces, and let
a:H\times H\rightarrow R
,
b:H\times Q\rightarrow R
, and
c:Q\times Q\rightarrow R
be bounded bilinear forms with boundedness constants denoted by
\|a\|
,
\|b\|
, and
\|c\|
, respectively. Assume that:

i)

a(\bullet,\bullet)
is positive semi-definite, that is
a(\tau,\tau)\geq 0
for all
\tau\in H
.

ii)

b(\bullet,\bullet)
satisfies a
transposed
continuous inf-sup condition, that is, there exists a constant
\hat{\beta}>0
such that

\sup_{v\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\frac{b(\tau,v)}{\|v\|_{Q}}\geq\hat{\beta}\|\tau\|_{H}\quad\forall\tau\in H.

iii)

c(\bullet,\bullet)
is
Q
-elliptic, that is, there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that

c(v,v)\geq\gamma\|v\|_{Q}^{2}\quad\forall v\in Q.

Then, for each pair
(F,G)\in H^{\prime}\times Q^{\prime}
, the problem: Find
(\sigma,u)\in H\times Q
such that

\displaystyle a(\sigma,\tau)+b(\tau,u)

\displaystyle=F(\tau)\quad\forall\tau\in H,

(2.14a)

\displaystyle b(\sigma,v)-c(u,v)

\displaystyle=G(v)\quad\forall v\in Q,

(2.14b)

has a unique solution. Moreover, there exists a positive constant
C
, depending only on
\|b\|
,
\|c\|
,
\gamma
and
\hat{\beta}
, such that

\|\sigma\|_{H}+\|u\|_{Q}\leq C\{\|F\|_{H^{\prime}}+\|G\|_{Q^{\prime}}\}.

(2.15)

Proof.

The proof is symmetric to that of
[
17
, Theorem 3.2]
. The argument is driven by the strong
Q
-ellipticity of the form
c(\bullet,\bullet)
. To begin, we establish existence. Indeed, the
Q
-ellipticity of
c(\bullet,\bullet)
(hypothesis iii)) guarantees, by the Banach–Nečas–Babuška theorem, for each
\zeta\in H
, the existence of a unique
u_{\zeta}\in Q
such that

c(u_{\zeta},v)=b(\zeta,v)\quad\forall\,v\in Q,

(2.16)

as well as a unique
u_{0}\in Q
such that

c(u_{0},v)=G(v)\quad\,\forall\,v\in Q.

(2.17)

The corresponding
a priori
estimates are given, respectively, by

\|u_{\zeta}\|_{Q}\leq\frac{\|b\|}{\gamma}\|\zeta\|_{H}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\|u_{0}\|_{Q}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\|G\|_{Q^{\prime}}\quad\forall\,\zeta\in H.

(2.18)

Next, we use the transposed inf-sup condition ii) to obtain for each
\zeta\in H

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}\|\zeta\|_{H}\leq\sup_{v\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\frac{b(\zeta,v)}{\|v\|_{Q}}=\sup_{v\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\frac{c(u_{\zeta},v)}{\|v\|_{Q}}\leq\|c\|\|u_{\zeta}\|_{Q}.

(2.19)

Noting from (
2.16
) that
u_{\zeta}
depends on
\zeta
, we define a new form
\Theta:H\times H\to\amsmathbb{R}
by

\Theta(\zeta,\tau):=a(\zeta,\tau)+b(\tau,u_{\zeta})\quad\forall\zeta,\tau\in H.

In what follows, we prove that
\Theta(\bullet,\bullet)
is bilinear. Indeed, for
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in H
and scalars
x,y\in\amsmathbb{R}
, we use the bilinearity of
b(\bullet,\bullet)
and
c(\bullet,\bullet)
to arrive at

c(xu_{\zeta_{1}}+yu_{\zeta_{2}},v)=xc(u_{\zeta_{1}},v)+yc(u_{\zeta_{2}},v)=xb(\zeta_{1},v)+yb(\zeta_{2},v)=b(x\zeta_{1}+y\zeta_{2},v)\quad v\in Q.

By the uniqueness of the solution to (
2.16
), we must have
u_{x\zeta_{1}+y\zeta_{2}}=xu_{\zeta_{1}}+yu_{\zeta_{2}}
.
Thus for the first argument, we have

\displaystyle\Theta(x\zeta_{1}+y\zeta_{2},\tau)

\displaystyle=a(x\zeta_{1}+y\zeta_{2},\tau)+b(\tau,u_{x\zeta_{1}+y\zeta_{2}})

\displaystyle=xa(\zeta_{1},\tau)+ya(\zeta_{2},\tau)+b(\tau,xu_{\zeta_{1}}+yu_{\zeta_{2}})

\displaystyle=xa(\zeta_{1},\tau)+ya(\zeta_{2},\tau)+xb(\tau,u_{\zeta_{1}})+yb(\tau,u_{\zeta_{2}})

\displaystyle=x\Theta(\zeta_{1},\tau)+y\Theta(\zeta_{2},\tau),

which proves the linearity in the first argument. The linearity in the second argument and the boundedness of
\Theta(\bullet,\bullet)
follow directly from the properties of
a(\bullet,\bullet)
and
b(\bullet,\bullet)
.

We now demonstrate that
\Theta(\bullet,\bullet)
is
H
-elliptic. From the definition of
u_{\zeta}
in (
2.16
), we have the identity
c(u_{\zeta},u_{\zeta})=b(\zeta,u_{\zeta})
, which, along with hypotheses i) and iii), yields

\displaystyle\Theta(\zeta,\zeta)=a(\zeta,\zeta)+b(\zeta,u_{\zeta})=a(\zeta,\zeta)+c(u_{\zeta},u_{\zeta})\geq c(u_{\zeta},u_{\zeta})\geq\gamma\|u_{\zeta}\|_{Q}^{2}\geq\gamma\biggl(\frac{\hat{\beta}}{\|c\|}\biggr)^{2}\|\zeta\|_{H}^{2}.

This proves that
\Theta(\bullet,\bullet)
is elliptic on
H
with a constant
\alpha_{\Theta}=\frac{\gamma\hat{\beta}^{2}}{\|c\|^{2}}>0
. Thus, applying again the Banach–Nečas–Babuška theorem, we conclude that there exists a unique
\sigma\in H
such that

\Theta(\sigma,\tau)=F(\tau)+b(\tau,u_{0})
for all
\tau\in H
, that is

\displaystyle a(\sigma,\tau)+b(\tau,u_{\sigma})

\displaystyle=F(\tau)+b(\tau,u_{0})\quad\forall\tau\in H,

which can be rearranged as

\displaystyle a(\sigma,\tau)+b(\tau,u_{\sigma}-u_{0})

\displaystyle=F(\tau)\quad\forall\tau\in H.

(2.20)

Now, letting
u=u_{\sigma}-u_{0}\in Q
, it follows from (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) that

c(u,v)=c(u_{\sigma},v)-c(u_{0},v)=b(\sigma,v)-G(v),

that is

b(\sigma,v)-c(u,v)=G(v)\quad\forall v\in Q,

which, together with (
2.20
), shows that
(\sigma,u)\in H\times Q
is a solution to (
2.14a
).

In turn, the a priori estimate for the solution is derived now.
First, we establish the bound for
\sigma
. From the stability of the bilinear form
\Theta(\bullet,\bullet)
, we have

\|\sigma\|_{H}\leq\frac{\|c\|^{2}}{\gamma\hat{\beta}^{2}}\left(\|F\|_{H^{\prime}}+\|b\|\|u_{0}\|_{Q}\right).

The above together with the second inequality in (
2.18
), yields

\|\sigma\|_{H}\leq\frac{\|c\|^{2}}{\gamma\hat{\beta}^{2}}\|F\|_{H^{\prime}}+\frac{\|b\|\|c\|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\hat{\beta}^{2}}\|G\|_{Q^{\prime}}.

(2.21)

Next, we establish the bound for
u
. Using the triangle inequality and the bounds for
u_{\sigma}
and
u_{0}
in (
2.18
), we get

\|u\|_{Q}\leq\frac{\|b\|}{\gamma}\|\sigma\|_{H}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\|G\|_{Q^{\prime}}.

Substituting the estimate (
2.21
) into this inequality yields

\|u\|_{Q}\leq\frac{\|b\|\|c\|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\hat{\beta}^{2}}\|F\|_{H^{\prime}}+\left(\frac{\|b\|^{2}\|c\|^{2}}{\gamma^{3}\hat{\beta}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\|G\|_{Q^{\prime}}.

(2.22)

Having proved the existence of a solution
(\sigma,u)
, it only remains to show the uniqueness, for which we let
(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{u})\in H\times Q
be such that

\displaystyle a(\tilde{\sigma},\tau)+b(\tau,\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall\tau\in H,

\displaystyle b(\tilde{\sigma},v)-c(\tilde{u},v)

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall v\in Q.

Then, taking
\tau=\tilde{\sigma}
and
v=\tilde{u}
, and then subtracting the resulting equations and using i) and iii), we get

0=a(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\sigma})+c(\tilde{u},\tilde{u})\geq\gamma\|\tilde{u}\|_{Q}^{2}.

from which
\tilde{u}=0
. In addition, it is clear from the second row of the homogeneous system and (
2.16
) that
u_{\tilde{\sigma}}=\tilde{u}
, which, invoking (
2.19
), yields
\tilde{\sigma}=0
, thus confirming the uniqueness of the solution.

Finally, (
2.21
) and (
2.22
) imply (
2.15
) and complete the proof.

\square

2.3
Unique solvability of the coupled problem via fixed-point theory

We define the following map

\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q},\quad\widehat{\varphi}\mapsto\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\widehat{\varphi})=((\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}_{1}(\widehat{\varphi}),\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}_{2}(\widehat{\varphi})),\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}_{3}(\widehat{\varphi})):=((\boldsymbol{\sigma},p),\vec{\boldsymbol{u}})=(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}),

where
(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}})\in\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}
is the unique solution of the poroelasticity equations as stated in Theorem
2.6
.
In turn, we define the solution operator associated with the mixed diffusion equations as

\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}:\amsmathbb{H}_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{sym}}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\to\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mapsto\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=(\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}_{1}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}),\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}_{2}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})):=(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi),

where
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)
is the unique solution of the diffusion equations as stated in Theorem

. These maps are well-defined and so it is the following one

\mathcal{J}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),\quad\widehat{\varphi}\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\widehat{\varphi}):=\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}_{2}(\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}_{1}(\widehat{\varphi})).

(2.25)

Finding a fixed point
\varphi
of
\mathcal{J}
is therefore equivalent to solve (
2.2
). For this we use Banach fixed point theorem and start by considering, for a generic
r>0
, the following closed ball

\mathrm{W}:=\{\widehat{\varphi}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega):\|\widehat{\varphi}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq r\},

and proceed next to show that
\mathcal{J}
maps it to itself and that
\mathcal{J}
is Lipschitz continuous.

Lemma 2.8
(ball mapping property)

Under the small data assumption

\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\leq r,

(2.26)

it follows that
\mathcal{J}\big(\mathrm{W}\big)\subseteq\mathrm{W}
.

Proof.

Given
\widehat{\varphi}\in\mathrm{W}
, by (
2.25
), (
2.26
) and the estimate given by Theorem
2.7
we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}(\widehat{\varphi})\|_{0,\Omega}=\|\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}_{2}(\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}_{1}(\widehat{\varphi}))\|_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\leq r,

which means that
\mathcal{J}\big(\mathrm{W}\big)\subseteq\mathrm{W}
.

\square

We continue the analysis with the Lipschitz-continuity property of
{\mathcal{J}}
. To this end, we recall that Theorems
2.6
and
2.7
establish the existence of positive constants
C_{\mathrm{B}}
and
C_{\mathrm{D}}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\in\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}}{{(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\neq\mathbf{0}}}}\frac{A(\vec{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}})+B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{w}})+B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})-C(\vec{\boldsymbol{w}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})}{\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}}\geq\,C_{\mathrm{B}}\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\vec{\boldsymbol{w}})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}\ \forall\,(\vec{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\vec{\boldsymbol{w}})\in\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q},

(2.27a)

\displaystyle\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\in\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}{{(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\neq\mathbf{0}}}}\hskip-2.84544pt\frac{a_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})+b(\boldsymbol{\xi},\phi)+b(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\psi)-c(\phi,\psi)}{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\geq C_{\mathrm{D}}\|(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\phi)\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\forall\,(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\phi)\in{\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

(2.27b)

Lemma 2.9
(Lipschitz continuity)

There exists a positive constant
L_{\mathcal{J}}
such that

\|\mathcal{J}(\varphi_{1})-\mathcal{J}(\varphi_{2})\|_{0,\Omega}\leq L_{\mathcal{J}}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{0,\Omega}\qquad\forall\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

(2.28)

Proof.

Given
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, we let
\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\varphi_{1})=(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})\in\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\varphi_{2})=(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})\in\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}
be the unique solutions of (
2.23
). Then, applying the inf-sup condition (
2.27a
) with
(\vec{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\vec{\boldsymbol{w}})=(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{B}}\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\in\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}}{{(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\neq\mathbf{0}}}}\frac{A(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}})+B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})+B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})-C(\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})}{\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\in\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}}{{(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\neq\mathbf{0}}}}\frac{D({\varphi}_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}})-D({\varphi}_{2},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}})}{\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{2\mu+d\lambda}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{0,\Omega}.

The bound above implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\varphi_{1})-\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\varphi_{2})\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}\leq\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{C_{\mathrm{B}}(2\mu+d\lambda)}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(2.29)

On the other hand, given
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\in\amsmathbb{H}^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
, we let
\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1})=(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1},\varphi_{1})\in\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})=(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\varphi_{2})\in\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
be the unique solutions of (
2.24
). This means

\begin{array}[]{rlllll}a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi})-a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi})&+\,b(\boldsymbol{\xi},\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})&=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega),\\[4.30554pt]

Then, by adding and subtracting the term
{a}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi})
, it follows that

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi})+b(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\psi)+b(\boldsymbol{\xi},\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})-{c}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2},\psi)={a}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi})-{c}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Thus, from (
2.27
) with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}
and
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\phi)=(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})
, and the assumptions for

(cf. (

)) we find that

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2}\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{D}}}\displaystyle\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\in\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}{{(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\neq\mathbf{0}}}}\frac{|a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi})-a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi})|}{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{D}}}\displaystyle\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\in\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}{{(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\neq\mathbf{0}}}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|(\varrho(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})^{-1}-\varrho(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1})^{-1})\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}|}{\|(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi)\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\varrho}}{C_{\mathrm{D}}}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2}\|_{0,4;\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\varrho}}{C_{\mathrm{D}}}(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}})\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\|_{0,\Omega},

which implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}_{2}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1})-\mathcal{J}_{2}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\frac{L_{\varrho}}{C_{\mathrm{D}}}(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}})\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(2.30)

Then, the estimate in (
2.28
) follows from the definition of
\mathcal{J}
(cf. (
2.25
)), the Lipschitz-continuity of
\mathcal{J}_{2}^{\mathrm{diff}}
(cf. (
2.30
)) and
\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\mathrm{Biot}}
(cf. (
2.29
)) with

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{J}}:=\frac{\,L_{\varrho}(1+\alpha d)\beta}{C_{\mathrm{B}}C_{\mathrm{D}}(2\mu+d\lambda)}(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}).

(2.31)

\square

Owing to the above analysis, we now establish the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.10
(well-posedness of the fully-coupled continuous problem)

Suppose that

(cf. (

) has a unique solution

. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\biggl(\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{2\mu+d\lambda}+1\biggr)\bigl(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\bigr)+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|g\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}+\,\|p_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}.

Proof.

We first recall that Lemma
2.8
guarantees that

maps

into itself. Then, bearing in mind the Lipschitz-continuity of

along with the fact that

3
Virtual element discretisation

This section introduces the VEM-based discrete formulation for the fully-coupled problem (
2.1
)-(
2.2
). We employ a VEM for both 2D/3D linear elasticity problems based on the Hellinger–Reissner variational principle (cf. (
1.4a
)-(
1.4c
)). The main advantage of this type of VE space is that it allows the symmetry of the discrete tensor to be enforced strongly. Moreover, its definition is unified in both 2D and 3D, taking into account that in 3D, facets correspond to the faces of the polyhedral element, while in 2D, they correspond to the edges of the polygonal element. On the other hand, the VEM employed here for mixed second order elliptic problems (corresponding to the equations (
1.4d
)-(
1.4e
) and (
1.4f
)-(
1.4g
)) requires separate definitions in 2D and 3D. In addition, we introduce appropriate polynomial projection, interpolation and stabilisation operators to guarantee computability of the discrete formulation.

We recall that the detailed construction, unisolvence in terms of the corresponding Degrees of Freedom (DoFs), additional properties of the VE spaces; as well with the properties of the polynomial spaces and the computability of the polynomial projection operators in terms of the (respective) DoFs presented in this section are provided in
[
2
,
47
,
6
,
5
]
.

Assumptions on the mesh.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a collection of polygonal/polyhedral meshes on
\Omega
and
\mathcal{F}_{h}
be the set of all facets in 3D (edges in 2D). The diameter of a polygon/polyhedron
K
is represented by
h_{K}
and the length/area of a facet
f
is represented by
h_{f}
. The maximum diameter of elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is represented by
h
. It is assumed that there exists a uniform positive constant
\eta
such that

(A1)

Every element
K
has a star-shaped interior with respect to a ball with a radius greater than
\eta h_{K}
.

(A2)

Every facet
f\in\partial K
has a star-shaped interior with respect to a ball with a radius greater than
\eta h_{K}
.

(A3)

Every facet
f\in\partial K
satisfies the inequality
h_{f}\geq\eta h_{K}
.

Polynomial spaces.

In this paper, we consider an arbitrary polynomial degree
k\geq 1
. The space of polynomials of total degree at most
k
defined locally on
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
(or facet
f\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
) is represented by
\mathrm{P}_{k}(K)
, and its vector and tensor counterparts are represented by
\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)
and
\amsmathbb{P}_{k}(K)
, respectively. We also consider the standard notation
\mathrm{P}_{-1}(K)=\{0\}
.

The spaces
\mathbf{G}_{k}(K):=\nabla(\mathrm{P}_{k+1}(K))
and
\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(K)
denote the gradients of polynomials of degree
\leq k+1
on
K
and the complement of the space
\mathbf{G}_{k}(K)
in the vector polynomial space
\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)
such that the direct sum
\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)=\mathbf{G}_{k}(K)\oplus\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(K)
holds, respectively. In particular, we select
\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(K)=\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}\mathrm{P}_{k-1}(K)
(resp.
\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(K):=\boldsymbol{x}\wedge(\mathbf{P}_{k-1}(K)
) where
\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}=(x_{2},-x_{1})^{\tt t}
in 2D (resp.
\boldsymbol{x}:=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{\tt t}
and
\wedge
the usual external product in 3D).

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{K}=(x_{1,K},x_{2,K})^{\tt t}
(resp.
\boldsymbol{x}_{K}=(x_{1,K},x_{2,K},x_{3,K})^{\tt t}
) denote the barycentre of
K
and let
\mathbf{M}_{k}(K)
be the set of vector scaled monomials as

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{k}(K):=\left\{\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{K}}{h_{K}}\right)^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}(K):\ 0\leq|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\leq k\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})^{\tt t}
(resp.
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})^{\tt t}
) is a non-negative multi-index with
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}
in 2D (resp.
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}x_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}
in 3D), with analogous definition for the scalar and tensor version
\mathrm{M}_{k}
and
\amsmathbb{M}_{k}
. Notice that the polynomial decompositions presented before hold also in terms of the scaled monomial. For example, in the 2D case, we can take the sets
\mathbf{G}_{k}(K)
and
\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(K)
as
\mathbf{M}_{k}^{\nabla}(K):=\nabla\mathrm{M}_{k+1}(K)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
\mathbf{M}_{k}^{\oplus}(K):=\mathbf{m}^{\perp}\mathrm{M}_{k-1}(K)
, with
\mathbf{m}^{\perp}:=(\frac{x_{2}-x_{2,E}}{h_{K}},\frac{x_{1,K}-x_{1}}{h_{K}})^{\tt t}
and
\mathbf{m}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{K}}{h_{K}}
, respectively; and providing the decomposition
\mathbf{M}_{k}(K)=\mathbf{M}_{k}^{\nabla}(K)\oplus\mathbf{M}_{k}^{\oplus}(K)
.

The set of polynomials that solves locally the constitutive law in linear elasticity is defined as
\widetilde{\amsmathbb{M}}_{k}(K):=\{\widetilde{\mathbf{m}}_{k}\in\amsmathbb{M}_{k}(K):\ \widetilde{\mathbf{m}}_{k}=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{m}_{k+1})\ \text{for some}\ \mathbf{m}_{k+1}\in\mathbf{M}_{k+1}(K)\}
. On the other hand, the set of scaled rigid body motions of an element
K
is given by

\displaystyle\textbf{RBM}(K)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{h_{E}}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\frac{1}{h_{E}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\frac{x_{2,E}-x_{2}}{h_{E}}\\
\frac{x_{1}-x_{1,E}}{h_{E}}\end{pmatrix}\right\}&\quad\text{in 2D},\\
&\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{h_{P}}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\frac{1}{h_{P}}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\frac{1}{h_{P}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\frac{x_{2,P}-x_{2}}{h_{P}}\\
\frac{x_{1}-x_{1,P}}{h_{P}}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\frac{x_{3,P}-x_{3}}{h_{P}}\\
\frac{x_{2}-x_{2,P}}{h_{P}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\frac{x_{3}-x_{3,P}}{h_{P}}\\
0\\
\frac{x_{1,P}-x_{1}}{h_{P}}\end{pmatrix}\right\}&\quad\text{in 3D}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

In this case, the polynomial decomposition
\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)=\textbf{RBM}(K)\oplus\textbf{RBM}^{\perp}_{k}(K)
, holds with

\displaystyle\textbf{RBM}^{\perp}(K):=\left\{\mathbf{m}_{k}\in\mathbf{M}_{k}:\ \int_{K}\mathbf{m}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{{\textbf{RBM}}}=0,\,\forall\mathbf{m}_{{\textbf{RBM}}}\in\textbf{RBM}(K)\right\}.

3.1
VEM for Hellinger–Reissner linear elasticity

The associated (conforming) VE space for
\amsmathbb{H}({\mathbf{div}},\Omega)
in both 2D and 3D locally solves the constitutive law in linear elasticity
[
2
,
47
]
and its defined by

\displaystyle\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}(K):=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{H}({\mathbf{div}},K):\

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\boldsymbol{n}|_{{f}}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}(f),\,\forall f\in\partial K,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}(K),\ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})\ \text{for some}\ \boldsymbol{v}^{*}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(K)\}.

Notice that the polynomial space
\widetilde{\amsmathbb{P}}_{k}(K):=\{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{k}\in\amsmathbb{P}_{k}(K):\ \widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{k}=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{p}_{k+1})\ \text{for some}\ \mathbf{p}_{k+1}\in\mathbf{P}_{k+1}(K)\}\subseteq\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}(K)
. To define the global discrete spaces we patch together the local spaces in the following way

\displaystyle\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{H}^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\colon\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}|_{K}\in\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}(K),\,\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{h,k}

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\colon\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}(K),\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

The associated DoFs for
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}(K)
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{U}^{h,k}(K):=\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)
are given as follows

\displaystyle\bullet\frac{1}{h_{f}}\int_{f}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k}\in\mathbf{M}_{k}(f),

\displaystyle\bullet\frac{1}{h_{K}}\int_{K}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{{\textbf{RBM}}^{\perp}},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{{\textbf{RBM}}^{\perp}}\in\textbf{RBM}^{\perp}(K),

\displaystyle\bullet\frac{1}{h_{K}}\int_{K}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k}\in\mathbf{M}_{k}(K).

3.2
VEM for perturbed mixed second-order elliptic problems

The conforming VE approximation for the space
\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
in 2D is defined locally by solving a
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits
-
\mathop{\mathrm{rot}}\nolimits
problem
[
6
]
, as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\text{2D}}^{h,k}(K):=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,K)\cap\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{rot}}\nolimits,K)\colon\

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{{f}}\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(f),\,\forall f\subset\partial K,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(K),\ \mathop{\mathrm{rot}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathrm{P}_{k-1}(K)\}.

Observe that
\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\text{2D}}^{h,k}(K)
. In turn, the global discrete space is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\text{2D}}^{h,k}

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\colon\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbf{V}_{\text{2D}}^{h,k}(K),\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

We consider the following DoFs for
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{\text{2D}}^{h,k}(K)
:

\displaystyle\bullet\text{The values of }\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\text{ at the $k+1$ Gauss--Lobatto quadrature points of each edge of $K$},

\displaystyle\bullet\frac{1}{h_{K}}\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k-1}^{\nabla},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k-1}^{\nabla}\in\mathbf{M}_{k-1}^{\nabla}(K),

\displaystyle\bullet\frac{1}{h_{K}}\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k}^{\oplus},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k}^{\oplus}\in\mathbf{M}_{k}^{\oplus}(K).

In contrast, the 3D version of the conforming VE approximation for the space
\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
locally solves a
\nabla(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits)
–
\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits
problem
[
5
]
and it is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\text{3D}}^{h,k+1}(K):=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,K)\cap\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits,K)\colon\

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{{f}}\in\mathrm{P}_{k+1}(f),\,\forall f\in\partial K,

\displaystyle\nabla(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})\in\mathbf{G}_{k-1}(K),\ \mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)\}.

Note that
\mathbf{P}_{k+1}(K)\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\text{3D}}^{h,k+1}(K)
. Then, the discrete global spaces are defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\text{3D}}^{h,k+1}

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\colon\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbf{V}_{\text{3D}}^{h,k+1}(K),\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

The set of DoFs for
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{\text{3D}}^{h,k+1}(K)
is provided next

\displaystyle\bullet\text{The values of }\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\text{ at the $k+2$ quadrature points on each face of $K$},

\displaystyle\bullet\frac{1}{h_{K}}\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k-1}^{\nabla},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k-1}^{\nabla}\in\mathbf{M}_{k-1}^{\nabla}(K),

\displaystyle\bullet\frac{1}{h_{K}}\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k+1}^{\oplus},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k+1}^{\oplus}\in\mathbf{M}_{k+1}^{\oplus}(K).

Finally, the global discrete space for the space
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is defined in general for 2D and 3D as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}

\displaystyle:=\{\psi_{h}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\colon\psi_{h}|_{K}\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(K),\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

and, for a given
\psi_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}(K):=\mathrm{P}_{k}(K)
, the DoFs for the space above are defined by

\displaystyle\bullet\frac{1}{h_{K}}\int_{K}\psi_{h}m_{k},\quad\forall m_{k}\in\mathrm{M}_{k}(K).

3.3
Polynomial projection and interpolation operators

For each element
K
, we introduce the following local polynomial projection operators:

•

The
\mathcal{C}
-energy projection is defined as
\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K}:\widetilde{\amsmathbb{S}}(K)\rightarrow\widetilde{\amsmathbb{M}}_{k}(K)
by

\displaystyle\int_{K}{\mathcal{C}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}-\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K}\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)}:\widetilde{\mathbf{m}}_{k}=0,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\widetilde{\amsmathbb{S}}(K)},\ \forall\widetilde{\mathbf{m}}_{k}\in\widetilde{\amsmathbb{M}}_{k}(K),

(3.1)

where
\widetilde{\amsmathbb{S}}(K):=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\amsmathbb{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,K):\ \boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\ \text{for some}\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(K)\}
.

•

The
\mathbf{L}^{2}
projection is defined by
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{k}^{0,K}:\mathbf{L}^{2}(K)\rightarrow\mathbf{M}_{k}(K)
where

\displaystyle\int_{K}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{k}^{0,K}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k}=0,\ \forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(K),\ \forall\mathbf{m}_{k}\in\mathbf{M}_{k}(K),

(3.2)

with an analogous definition for scalar functions.

The detailed proof of computability of these operators in terms of the respective DoFs can be found in
[
2
,
47
,
6
,
5
]
. In addition, we state a result involving classical polynomial approximation theory
[

]
. The estimate is presented for scalar functions, but it also holds in general for vector and tensor functions.

Proposition 3.1
(polynomial approximation)

Given
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, assume that
v\in\mathrm{H}^{{\overline{s}}}(K)
, with
1\leq{\overline{s}}\leq k+1
. Then, there exist
v_{\pi}\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(K)
and a positive constant that depends only on
\eta
(cf.
(A1)
-
(A3)
) such that for
0\leq\overline{r}\leq{\overline{s}}
the following estimate holds

\displaystyle|v-v_{\pi}|_{\overline{r},K}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{K}^{{\overline{s}}-\overline{r}}|v|_{{\overline{s}},K}.

Next, the nature of the space defined in Section
3.1
allow us to define locally the Fortin–like interpolation operator
\amsmathbb{F}^{k,K}:\amsmathbb{H}^{1}(K)\rightarrow\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}(K)
through the associated DoFs in a unified way for an element
K
(cf.
[

, the Fortin–like interpolation operators

and

are defined by their associated DoFs, taking into account that the element

refers to a polygon in 2D and a polyhedral in 3D. See, e.g.,
[
6
, Section 3.2]
and
[
7

for their respective constructions. Moreover, the associated commutative property holds for each operator as follows: for each

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\amsmathbb{F}^{k,K}\boldsymbol{\tau})=\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,K}_{k}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}),\quad\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\mathbf{F}_{\text{2D}}^{k,K}\boldsymbol{\xi})=\Pi_{k}^{0,K}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}),\quad\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\mathbf{F}_{\text{3D}}^{k+1,K}\boldsymbol{\xi})=\Pi_{k}^{0,K}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}).

(3.3)

Proposition 3.2
(Hellinger–Reissner VEM interpolation estimates)

Given
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, assume that
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\amsmathbb{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,K)\cap\amsmathbb{H}^{{\overline{s}}}(K)

(cf.
(A1)
-
(A3)

, the following estimate holds

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\tau}-\amsmathbb{F}^{k,K}\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{\overline{r},K}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{K}^{{\overline{s}}-\overline{r}}|\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{{\overline{s}},K}.

Proposition 3.3
(mixed VEM interpolation estimates)

Given
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}

and
1\leq{\overline{s}}\leq k+1
, there exist positive constants that depend only on
\eta
(cf.
(A1)
-
(A3)
) such that for
0\leq\overline{r}\leq{\overline{s}}
the following estimates hold

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{2D}}^{k,K}\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,\overline{l};K}\lesssim h_{K}^{{\overline{s}}-\overline{r}}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{{\overline{s}},\overline{l};K},\qquad\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{3D}}^{k+1,K}\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,\overline{l};K}\lesssim h_{K}^{{\overline{s}}-\overline{r}}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{\overline{l},\overline{s};K}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{W}^{\overline{s},\overline{l}}(K).

Remark 3.1

Typically, one also requires an interpolation property for the divergence part of the flux (or stress) space to obtain error estimates (see, for example,
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
for mixed VEM in the
L^{p}
context). Such a property calls for additional regularity for the divergence part, for example (using the notation from Proposition
3.3
in the 2D case)

\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{2D}}^{k,K}\boldsymbol{\xi})\|_{0,K}\lesssim h_{K}^{{\overline{s}}-\overline{r}}|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{\overline{s},K}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{W}^{1,1}(K)\ \text{such that}\ \mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathrm{H}^{\overline{s}}(K).

Here we proceed differently and derive estimates involving the divergence by simply using the commutativity property (
3.3
) and applying Proposition
3.1
. This avoids the assumption of more regularity for the divergence, but rather asking it for the concentration.

3.4
Discrete problem

Without losing generality we denote by

and note that the space

consists of the discrete space

equipped with the norm

in the two dimensional setting (resp.

Given

\displaystyle A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}A_{h}^{K}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\biggl[(\mathcal{C}^{-1}(\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}),\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})_{K}+S_{1}^{\mathcal{C},K}((\mathbb{1}-\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},(\mathbb{1}-\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K})\tau_{h})

The discrete version of (

This section extends the results shown in Section

we employ a discrete fixed-point argument to state the well-posedness of the fully-coupled discrete problem. We recall that, thanks to stabilisation, the discrete operators inherit the properties presented in Section

4.1
Properties of the discrete operators

Note that, for each

, given
\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}
and
\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}

\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)
,
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(K)
(see also the definition of the 2D (resp. 3D) VEM space in Section
3.2
(resp.
[
5
, Theorem 8.2]
)),
q_{h}\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(K)
, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)

\displaystyle\amsmathbb{V}_{0}^{h}:=\mathrm{ker}(\mathbf{B}|_{\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}})

\displaystyle=\{\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}:\ B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})=0,\ \forall\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}\}

\displaystyle=\amsmathbb{V}_{01}^{h}\times\amsmathbb{V}_{02}^{h}\equiv\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}:\ \mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}|_{K}=\boldsymbol{0},\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\times\{0\},

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{0}^{h}:=\mathrm{ker}(\mathbf{B}^{*}|_{\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}})

\displaystyle=\{\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}:\ B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})=0,\ \forall\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}_{01}^{h}\times\mathbf{Q}_{02}^{h}\equiv\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\times\{\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}^{h,\overline{k}}:\ \mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{w}_{h}|_{K}=0,\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

(4.1b)

On the other hand, the orthogonal spaces
(\amsmathbb{V}_{0}^{h})^{\perp}=(\amsmathbb{V}_{01}^{h})^{\perp}\times(\amsmathbb{V}_{02}^{h})^{\perp}
and
(\mathbf{Q}_{0}^{h})^{\perp}=(\mathbf{Q}_{01}^{h})^{\perp}\times(\mathbf{Q}_{02}^{h})^{\perp}
are closed subspaces of
\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}
and
\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}
, where

\displaystyle(\amsmathbb{V}_{01}^{h})^{\perp}\equiv\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}:\ (\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})_{K}=0,\ \forall\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}_{01}^{h},\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},\quad(\amsmathbb{V}_{02}^{h})^{\perp}\equiv\mathrm{Q}^{h,k},

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Q}_{01}^{h})^{\perp}\equiv\mathbf{Q}^{h,k},\quad\hbox{and}\quad(\mathbf{Q}_{02}^{h})^{\perp}\equiv\{\boldsymbol{z}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}^{h,\overline{k}}:\ (\boldsymbol{z}_{h},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})_{K}=0,\ \forall\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{Q}_{02}^{h},\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

In what follows, we prove some key properties of the discrete bilinear forms.

Lemma 4.1
(symmetry and positive semi-definiteness of discrete diagonal forms)

The bilinear forms
A_{h}(\bullet,\bullet)
and
C_{h}(\bullet,\bullet)
are symmetric and positive semi-definite; and (for a given
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}:=\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}
)
a_{h,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\bullet,\bullet)
is positive semi-definite.

Proof.

The proof reduces to employ the arguments in Lemma
2.2
together with the properties of the stabilisation operators in (
3.4
). Indeed, we can extend (
2.4
) for all
\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}
as follows

\displaystyle A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\mu}\|(\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})^{\tt d}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+S_{1}^{\mathcal{C},K}((\mathbb{1}-\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},(\mathbb{1}-\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})

\displaystyle\quad+\,\frac{s_{0}}{2}\|q_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\,+\frac{s_{0}}{d(s_{0}(2\mu+d\lambda)+2d\alpha^{2})}\|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\mathbb{\Pi}_{k}^{\mathcal{C},K}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\geq 0.

(4.2)

In addition, for a given
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}
, we have

a_{h,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})\geq\varrho_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\overline{k}}^{0,K}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+S_{3}^{0,K}((\mathbf{1}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\overline{k}}^{0,K})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},(\mathbf{1}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\overline{k}}^{0,K})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})\geq 0,

thanks to the positive semi-definitess of
S_{1}^{\mathcal{C},K}(\bullet,\bullet)
and
S_{3}^{0,K}(\bullet,\bullet)
.

\square

Lemma 4.2
(coercivity for the main discrete diagonal forms)

There exist constants
\overline{\alpha}_{A},\overline{\alpha}_{a}>0
such that

\displaystyle A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})

\displaystyle\geq\overline{\alpha}_{A}\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\|^{2}_{\amsmathbb{V}}\quad

\displaystyle\forall\,\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}_{0}^{h},

(4.3a)

\displaystyle c(\psi_{h},\psi_{h})

\displaystyle\geq\overline{\alpha}_{c}\|\psi_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\quad

\displaystyle\forall\,\psi_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}.

(4.3b)

Proof.

Note that (
4.2
) and (

) imply that for all
\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})

\displaystyle\geq\min\{\frac{1}{2\mu},C_{s1}^{-1}\}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}^{\tt d}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\frac{s_{0}}{2}\|q_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\,+\min\{\frac{s_{0}}{d(s_{0}(2\mu+d\lambda)+2d\alpha^{2})},C_{s1}^{-1}\}\|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}.

(4.4)

Thus, applying (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) to
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}
, there exist

such that

,
where

\square

Lemma 4.3
(discrete inf-sup conditions)

There exist positive constants
\overline{\beta}_{B},\overline{\beta}_{b}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})}{\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}}

\displaystyle\geq\overline{\beta}_{B}\|\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\,\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in[\mathrm{ker}(\mathbf{B}_{h}^{*})]^{\perp},

(4.5a)

\displaystyle\sup_{\psi_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\psi_{h})}{\|\psi_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}}

\displaystyle\geq\overline{\beta}_{b}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{h,\overline{k}}.

(4.5b)

Proof.

We start by recalling from
[
1
, Proposition 5.6]
the following discrete inf-sup condition

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}}\geq\overline{\beta}_{1}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in(\mathbf{Q}_{01}^{h})^{\perp}.

(4.6)

Similarly to
[
8
, Proposition 5.4.2]
, given that
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)
for all

,

\displaystyle\sup_{q_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{(q_{h},\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{w}_{h})}{\|q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}}\geq\sup_{q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{(\Pi_{k}^{0}q,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{w}_{h})}{\|\Pi_{k}^{0}q\|_{0,\Omega}}\geq\sup_{q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{(q,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{w}_{h})}{C_{\pi}\|q\|_{0,\Omega}}\geq\overline{\beta}_{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega},

(4.7)

where in the last inequality we have used the continuous inf-sup condition (

,
\Pi_{k}^{0}q:=\Pi_{k}^{0,K}q|_{K}
for all

in the

There exists a unique

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h},\psi_{h})-c(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})

Next, we define the following discrete maps

Next, we prove that

Lemma 4.6
(discrete ball mapping property)

Under the small data assumption in (

Lemma 4.7
(discrete Lipschitz continuity)

There exists a positive constant

such that

\|\mathcal{J}_{{h}}({\varphi_{1h}})-\mathcal{J}_{h}({\varphi_{2h}})\|_{0,\Omega}\leq L_{\mathcal{J}_{h}}\|\varphi_{1h}-\varphi_{2h}\|_{0,\Omega}\qquad\forall\varphi_{1h},\varphi_{2h}\in{\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}}.

Proof.

Given
\varphi_{1h},\varphi_{2h}\in{\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}}
, we let
\mathcal{J}_{h}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\varphi_{1h})=(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1h})\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\times\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}
and
\mathcal{J}_{h}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\varphi_{2h})=(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2h})\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\times\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}
be the unique solutions of (
4.8
). Then, applying the discrete version of the inf-sup condition (
2.27a
) with

, imply that
\overline{C}_{\mathrm{B}}\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\times\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})\in(\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\times\mathbf{Q}^{h,k})\setminus\{(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\}}\frac{A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})+B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2h})+B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})-C_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})}{\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})\in(\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\times\mathbf{Q}^{h,k})\setminus\{(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\}}\frac{D_{h}({\varphi}_{1h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})-D_{h}({\varphi}_{2h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})}{\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}}\leq\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{2\mu+d\lambda}\|\varphi_{1h}-\varphi_{2h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}_{1h}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\varphi_{1h})-\mathcal{J}_{1h}^{\mathrm{Biot}}(\varphi_{2h})\|_{4,\operatorname*{div};\Omega}\leq\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{\overline{C}_{\mathrm{B}}(2\mu+d\lambda)}\|\varphi_{1h}-\varphi_{2h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(4.12)

Similarly, let
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2h}\in\amsmathbb{S}^{h,k}
, such that
\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1h})=(\varphi_{1h},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1h})\in\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{h,\overline{k}}
and

be the unique solutions of (
4.9

\displaystyle a_{h,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1h},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})+a_{h,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2h},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})+b_{h}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\varphi_{1h}-\varphi_{2h})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\forall\psi_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}^{h,k},

\displaystyle b_{h}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1h}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2h},\psi_{h})-c_{h}(\varphi_{1h}-\varphi_{2h},\psi_{h})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{h,\overline{k}},

and from here we add and subtract the term

to obtain

\displaystyle a_{h,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1h}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2h},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1h}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2h},\psi_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\varphi_{1h}-\varphi_{2h})-c(\varphi_{1h}-\varphi_{2h},\psi)=a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2h},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})-a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2h},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}).

where

is the continuity constant of the projection operator

in the
\amsmathbb{L}^{2}
-norm, which implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}_{2h}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1h})-\mathcal{J}_{2h}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2h})\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\frac{L_{\varrho}\overline{C}_{\pi}}{\overline{C}_{\mathrm{D}}}(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}})\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1h}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(4.13)

Then, the estimate (
4.11
) follows from the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{h}
(
4.2
), the Lipschitz-continuity of
\mathcal{J}_{2h}^{\mathrm{diff}}
(
4.13
) and that of
\mathcal{J}_{1h}^{\mathrm{Biot}}
(
4.12
), with

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{J}_{h}}:=\frac{\,L_{\varrho}\overline{C}_{\pi}(1+\alpha d)\beta}{\overline{C}_{\mathrm{B}}\overline{C}_{\mathrm{D}}(2\mu+d\lambda)}(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}).

(4.14)

\square

We are ready to state the main result of this section which is a consequence of Lemmas
4.6
-
4.7
together with the Banach fixed-point theorem.

Theorem 4.8
(well-posedness of the fully-coupled discrete problem)

Suppose that
\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\leq r
and

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{J}_{h}}<1
(cf. (
4.14
)). Then, the coupled problem (
3.5
) has a unique solution
(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\times\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}
and
(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h},\varphi_{h})\in\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{h,\overline{k}}\times\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}
. Moreover, and similarly to the continuous case, we have

\displaystyle\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h},\varphi_{h})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{2\mu+d\lambda}+1\right)\bigl(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\bigr)+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|g\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad+\,\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}+\|p_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}.

(4.15)

5
A priori error analysis

This section is devoted to deriving the optimal a priori error estimate. The first step is to establish the Strang-type inequalities which are formulated in the theorem below.

Theorem 5.1
(quasi-optimality)

In addition to the assumptions of Theorems
2.10
and
4.8
, let
(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)\in\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h},\varphi_{h})\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\times\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{h,\overline{k}}\times\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}
be the unique solutions to (
2.2
) and (
3.5
), respectively. Under these conditions, the following error estimates hold:

\displaystyle\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}+\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}+\|\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}+\|\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle\quad+\,\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{2\mu+d\lambda}\|\varphi-\varphi_{h}\|_{{0,\Omega}},

(5.1)

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h},\varphi-\varphi_{h})\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}}+\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\overline{k}}^{0,K}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}}+\|\varphi-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi\|_{{0,\Omega}}

\displaystyle\quad+\,L_{\varrho}(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}})\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}},

(5.2)

where
\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}}:=(\amsmathbb{F}^{k,K}\boldsymbol{\sigma},\Pi_{k}^{0,K}p),\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}:=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}},p_{h}),\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}}:=(\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,K}_{k}\boldsymbol{u},\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{z}),\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}:=(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}_{\overline{k}}^{0,K}\boldsymbol{z})
, and by
\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}
we represent the Fortin operators either
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{2D}}^{k,K}
or
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{3D}}^{k+1,K}
, depending on the spatial dimension under consideration.

Proof.

We proceed in a similar way as in
[
37
]
, noting from (
2.2
) and (
3.5
) that
(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}})\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k}\times\mathbf{Q}^{h,k}
is the unique solution to

\displaystyle A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})+\,B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}})

\displaystyle\,=\,\widetilde{F}_{1}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})

\displaystyle\forall\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}\in\amsmathbb{V}^{h,k},

\displaystyle B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})-\,C_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})

\displaystyle\,=\,\widetilde{G}_{1}(\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})

\displaystyle\forall\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathbf{Q}^{h,k},

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{1}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})

\displaystyle:=A(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})-A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})+B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}})+D_{h}(\varphi-\varphi_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{1}(\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})

\displaystyle:=B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})-C(\vec{\boldsymbol{u}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})+C_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}).

By exploiting the continuous dependence on data established in Theorem
4.4
, we can deduce that

\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}})\|_{\amsmathbb{V}\times\mathbf{Q}}\lesssim\|\widetilde{F}_{1}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}^{\prime}}+\|\widetilde{G}_{1}\|_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}.

(5.3)

Now, noting that
A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})=A(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})
, by applying the continuity of the bilinear forms
A(\bullet,\bullet)
,
A_{h}(\bullet,\bullet)
,
B(\bullet,\bullet)
,
D(\bullet,\bullet)
,
C(\bullet,\bullet)
, and
C_{h}(\bullet,\bullet)
, as well as using the triangle inequality, it is possible to deduce that

\displaystyle|A(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})-A_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})|

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}+\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}})\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}\lesssim(\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}+\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}})\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}},

(5.4a)

\displaystyle|B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}},

(5.4b)

\displaystyle|D_{h}(\varphi-\varphi_{h},\vec{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{h})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{2\mu+d\lambda}\|\varphi-\varphi_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}},

(5.4c)

\displaystyle|B(\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\amsmathbb{F}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{Q}},

(5.4d)

\displaystyle|C_{h}(\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})-C(\vec{\boldsymbol{u}},\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})|

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}+\|\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\|_{\mathbf{Q}})\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}\lesssim\|(\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}+\|\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}-\vec{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\mathbf{F}}\|_{\mathbf{Q}})\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}.

(5.4e)

Upon substitution of (
5.4
) into (
5.3
), and invoking the triangle inequality, the result (
5.1
) follows. Conversely, for the diffusivity problem, it follows once more from (
2.2
) and (
3.5
) that
(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi_{h}-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi)\in\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{h,\overline{k}}\times\mathrm{Q}^{h,k}
constitutes the unique solution of

\displaystyle a_{h,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\varphi_{h}-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi)

\displaystyle\,=\,\widetilde{F}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{h,\overline{k}},

(5.5a)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\psi_{h})-c(\varphi_{h}-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi,\psi_{h})

\displaystyle\,=\,\widetilde{G}_{2}(\psi_{h})

\displaystyle\forall\psi_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}^{h,k},

(5.5b)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})

\displaystyle:=a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})-a_{h,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\varphi-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi),

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{2}(\psi_{h})

\displaystyle:=b(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\psi_{h})-c(\varphi-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi,\psi_{h}).

The continuous dependence on data shows

\|(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi_{h}-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi)\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\widetilde{F}_{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)^{\prime}}+\|\widetilde{G}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{\prime}}.

(5.6)

After adding and subtracting suitable terms, applying the continuity of the bilinear forms

a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\bullet,\bullet)
,
a_{h,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}}(\bullet,\bullet)
,
b(\bullet,\bullet)
, and
c(\bullet,\bullet)
, and invoking the triangle inequality,
we can deduce that

\displaystyle|a_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})-a_{h,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}}(\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})|

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}}+\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\overline{k}}^{0,K}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega})\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad+L_{\varrho}(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}})\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{\mathbb{\Pi}}\|_{\amsmathbb{V}}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega},

(5.7a)

\displaystyle|b(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\varphi-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\varphi-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega},

(5.7b)

\displaystyle|b(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\psi_{h})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\overline{k},K}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{4,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega}\|\psi_{h}\|_{0,\Omega},

(5.7c)

\displaystyle|c(\varphi-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi,\psi_{h})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\varphi-\Pi_{k}^{0,K}\varphi\|_{0,\Omega}\|\psi_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(5.7d)

Finally, proceeding as in the previous case, the substitution of (
5.7
) into (
5.6
), combined with the triangle inequality, yields the estimate (
5.2
).

\square

Theorem 5.2
(convergence rates)

In addition to the hypotheses of Theorem
5.1
, assume that

\frac{(1+\alpha d)\beta}{2\mu+d\lambda}+L_{\varrho}(\|\ell\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\|_{1/2,00;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}})<\frac{1}{2}.

Additionally, suppose that there exist
s\in[1,k+1]
and
\overline{s}\in[1,\overline{k}+1]
such that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\amsmathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega),p\in\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega),\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{H}^{s}(\Omega),\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{H}^{\overline{s}}(\Omega),\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{H}^{\overline{s}}(\Omega)
and

. Then, there holds

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}_{h}\lesssim h^{\mathrm{min}\{s,\overline{s}\}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{s,\Omega}+\|p\|_{s,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{s,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\overline{s},\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{\overline{s},\Omega}+\|\varphi\|_{s,\Omega}\right),

(5.8)

where

.

Proof.
The proof relies on estimates (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), in conjunction with the smallness assumption and the approximation properties of the spaces stated in Propositions
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
, and in
[
13
]
.

\square

6.3
Sleep-driven molecular clearance within brain tissue

Neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s and dementia are linked to the accumulation of proteins (functional molecules) and metabolites (intermediate or residual products of metabolism) within brain tissue. To mitigate this, the brain enhances its clearance mechanisms during sleep. Studies indicate that sleep deprivation impairs molecular clearance, and this effect cannot be compensated for by an extra night’s sleep
[
24
]
. Moreover, it has been shown that the cortical interstitial space in mice (the narrow, irregularly shaped region between neurons and blood vessels in the cerebral cortex) increases by more than
60\%
during sleep, resulting in more efficient clearance
[
48
]
.

Figure 6.5:
Example 3. Two-dimensional schematic illustration of molecular clearance in brain tissue of a fluorescent CSF tracer. The experimental setup is shown at the top middle. The MRI scans for the sleep and awake states are shown on the left and right pannels. The bottom middle panels show the expected CSF tracer concentration computational simulations in a polytopal mesh of a coronal slice of the brain with 1,999 Voronoi cells.

Experimentally, MRI scans can visualise the distribution of the fluorescent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tracer Gadobutrol within brain tissue under various conditions, including sleep and awake states
[
24
]
(see Figure
6.5
). In this example, we focus on the mathematical modelling of this process by tracking the concentration of the CSF tracer under sleep and awake states in coronal slices of the brain. The mesh originally introduced in
[
9
]
provides the geometry of the coronal slice boundary. We extend this geometry by including the left, right, and bottom ventricles, and employ the capabilities of
PolyMesher

[
43
]
to discretise the domain with 19,999 Voronoi cells.

Following
[
33
]
, we neglect convection and assume that stress-dependent diffusion is the dominant transport mechanism. This assumption is supported by experimental data indicating that transport within brain tissue occurs
5-26\%
faster than predicted by Fickian diffusion
[
45
]
. The expansion of the cortical interstitial space during sleep leads to an increase in the volume fraction of brain tissue
[
48
]
. This can be measured as a porosity of
\phi=0.14
in the awake state and
\phi=0.23
in the sleep state.

We imposed a compression condition
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}=-(p_{0}\pi/2)\boldsymbol{n}

\varphi_{D}=1

\boldsymbol{f}
,
