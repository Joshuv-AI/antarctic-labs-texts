EGFR Wild-Type Sarcomatoid Carcinoma as Acquired Resistance to Tarlatamab in Small Cell–Transformed EGFR-Mutated Lung Adenocarcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Kei Kunimasa, Keiichiro Honma, Motohiro Tamiya, Takako Inoue, Nobuaki Mamesaya, Kinnosuke Matsumoto, Yosuke Fukumoto, Soichiro Minami, Tomoko Harima, Kazumi Nishino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors are the standard treatment for EGFR-mutated lung cancer, but acquired resistance frequently develops through diverse mechanisms, including histologic transformation. We report a rare case of EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinoma with an initial SCLC component that revealed marked lineage plasticity during treatment. A 38-year-old woman underwent left upper lobectomy for EGFR exon 19 deletion–positive lung adenocarcinoma with combined SCLC. After surgery, she received osimertinib and adjuvant cisplatin plus etoposide. After 6 years, pleural dissemination progressed, and biopsy result revealed recurrent SCLC without an adenocarcinoma component. The recurrent SCLC had DLL3 expression in approximately 80% of the tumor cells. Carboplatin plus etoposide and amrubicin were ineffective, whereas third-line tarlatamab induced the disappearance of liver metastases and disease control for 10 months. At progression, biopsy result of a recurrent liver lesion demonstrated sarcomatoid carcinoma with a complete loss of DLL3 expression. Genomic analyses revealed a shared TP53 p.H168P mutation across sequential specimens, whereas the recurrent SCLC and tarlatamab-resistant sarcomatoid carcinoma lacked the original EGFR p.E746_A750del–mutant allele. This case highlights dynamic histologic and molecular evolution in EGFR-mutated lung cancer and suggests that tarlatamab resistance may involve the selection of DLL3-negative clones.Keywords: EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinoma, Small cell lung carcinoma, Tarlatamab, Sarcomatoid carcinoma, DLL3, Case report

Introduction

EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors are the standard treatment for EGFR-mutated lung cancer, but acquired resistance almost inevitably develops through mechanisms such as EGFR C797S mutation, MET amplification, and transformation to SCLC. Tarlatamab has recently entered clinical practice for extensive-stage SCLC, although its activity in SCLC arising from EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinoma remains unclear. We report a case of EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinoma with an SCLC component at initial diagnosis,1 in which recurrent SCLC developed after 6 years of osimertinib treatment and responded to tarlatamab for 10 months. At the time of progression on tarlatamab, biopsy result revealed sarcomatoid carcinoma, highlighting marked histologic plasticity during treatment.

Case Presentation

The patient’s initial clinical course was reported previously. A 38-year-old woman with a former light-smoking history (1.5 pack-years) underwent left upper lobectomy for EGFR exon 19 deletion (p.E746_A750del)–positive lung adenocarcinoma, clinically staged as cT2aN0M0 (Fig. 1A). Pathologic examination revealed pT2aN0M1a disease with pleural dissemination of the adenocarcinoma component, whereas the primary tumor was histologically diagnosed as combined SCLC and adenocarcinoma1 (Fig. 1B). Postoperatively, she received osimertinib with 4 cycles of cisplatin plus etoposide for the SCLC component. After 6 years of osimertinib treatment, left pleural dissemination progressed (Fig. 1C–E). Computed tomography–guided biopsy of the pleural lesion demonstrated SCLC without an adenocarcinoma component (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemistry results revealed DLL3 expression in approximately 80% of the tumor cells (Fig. 2B). As first-line treatment for recurrent SCLC, carboplatin plus etoposide was administered, but levels of tumor markers, including neuron-specific enolase (NSE) and progastrin-releasing peptide (ProGRP), increased after 1 cycle. Amrubicin was then administered as second-line therapy, but multiple liver metastases developed (Fig. 1F and G). As third-line therapy, tarlatamab was started for relapsed SCLC using the approved step-up dosing schedule. The liver metastases disappeared after treatment initiation (Fig. 1H and I). However, after 10 months, the liver lesions recurred (Fig. 1J and K), and biopsy result of a recurrent liver lesion revealed sarcomatoid carcinoma (Fig. 2C). DLL3 expression was completely absent in the sarcomatoid tumor (Fig. 2D). The patient was subsequently treated with nivolumab plus ipilimumab and remains on therapy at the time of writing.

As previously reported,1 whole-exome sequencing of the adenocarcinoma and SCLC components of the primary tumor demonstrated shared EGFR p.E746_A750del, TP53 p.H168P, and FAM83E p.W397∗ alterations, with suggested loss of heterozygosity of the TP53-mutant allele in the SCLC component. Whole-exome sequencing of the recurrent pleural SCLC specimen revealed loss of the EGFR p.E746_A750del alteration (Supplementary Method and Supplementary Table 1). In the tarlatamab-resistant sarcomatoid carcinoma, FoundationOne profiling identified TP53 p.H168P, TSC1 p.S836∗, and CCNE1 amplification and revealed absence of the EGFR p.E746_A750del–mutant allele (Table 1).

Discussion

This case highlights the biological heterogeneity and histologic plasticity of EGFR-mutated lung cancer during treatment. Although the SCLC component at initial diagnosis and the recurrent SCLC specimen shared TP53 p.H168P, the recurrent SCLC no longer harbored the EGFR p.E746_A750del–mutant allele. In addition, the tumor that emerged at tarlatamab resistance was pathologically diagnosed as sarcomatoid carcinoma despite the retention of the same TP53 alteration. These findings suggest branched clonal evolution under sequential therapeutic pressure.

Importantly, the absence of the original EGFR p.E746_A750del–mutant allele in the recurrent SCLC raises the possibility that this lesion was not a conventional SCLC transformation directly derived from the EGFR-mutant adenocarcinoma clone. It may instead represent de novo SCLC arising from a TP53-mutant ancestral clone or the outgrowth of a preexisting EGFR wild-type SCLC subclone. Although the shared TP53 p.H168P alteration supports a common ancestral relationship, the loss of the EGFR-mutant allele precludes a definitive conclusion regarding direct lineage transformation. Therefore, we interpret this case as reflecting branched clonal evolution and lineage diversification rather than a simple linear transformation.

The efficacy of tarlatamab in SCLC arising from EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinoma has not been established. In previously reported cases, tarlatamab monotherapy demonstrated limited activity, whereas a benefit was observed when it was combined with osimertinib.2 In our case, however, the recurrent SCLC had already lost the EGFR-mutant allele, suggesting reduced or absent EGFR dependence and a limited rationale for continuing osimertinib. Notably, tarlatamab achieved disease control for 10 months, which appears to be longer than previously reported. This may indicate that the recurrent SCLC in this case was biologically distinct from the more typical SCLC transformed from EGFR-mutated adenocarcinoma.

Resistance to tarlatamab remains poorly understood. In this case, DLL3 was expressed in approximately 80% of the cells in the recurrent SCLC specimen but was absent in the sarcomatoid tumor at progression, suggesting that the emergence or selection of a DLL3-negative clone contributed to resistance.3 Such a clone may have preexisted at a low frequency before tarlatamab exposure, supporting the presence of heterogeneous subclones with distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Another notable finding was the persistence of TP53 p.H168P across sequential specimens despite marked histologic change and loss of the EGFR-mutant allele. This suggests that a TP53-mutant ancestral clone may have been maintained throughout the disease course and served as a molecular backbone for lineage diversification.4

In conclusion, we report a rare case of EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinoma with an SCLC component at diagnosis, followed by recurrent SCLC lacking the original EGFR-mutant allele and the subsequent emergence of tarlatamab-resistant sarcomatoid carcinoma. This case suggests that resistance evolution in EGFR-mutated lung cancer may involve repeated histologic shifts and clonal selection, underscoring the importance of rebiopsy with integrated pathologic and genomic assessments.
