Single-cell transcriptomic dissection of two waves of endothelial-hematopoietic transition in a murine yolk sac
Authors: Zhe Chen, Chaojie Wang, Xupeng Chen, Yang Yang, Yandong Gong, Yingpeng Yao, Yanli Ni, Zongcheng Li, Bing Liu, Yu Lan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The yolk sac drives vertebrate embryonic hematopoiesis through primitive hematopoiesis and endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition (EHT) waves. However, dynamic cellular and molecular changes during EHT of the yolk sac remain to be elucidated. We built a comprehensive atlas of early endothelial and hematopoietic development in the yolk sac by integrating single-cell transcriptomic data from mouse embryos (E6.75–E11.0). Focusing on the yolk sac (E7.5–E9.5), we established a refined atlas capturing key cell populations of EHT in the yolk sac. This enabled the identification of distinct hemogenic endothelial cell (HEC) subpopulations and revealed 2 fundamentally distinct waves of yolk sac hemogenesis via EHT that differed in temporal emergence, cellular origin, molecular signature, and lineage bias. The first EHT wave, emerging around E8.0, originated from primordial endothelial cells and exhibited a bias toward the generation of erythromyeloid progenitors. In contrast, the second EHT wave, emerging around E8.5, originated from maturing yolk sac endothelial cells, expressed key intraembryonic HEC markers (Hlf, Nupr1, Gfi1), and showed a hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell fate bias. Furthermore, molecular dynamics analysis of the pseudo-trajectory during the 2 waves of EHT in mouse yolk sacs revealed different dynamic changes in several pathways, particularly the ribosome and metabolic pathways. The yolk sac endothelial and hematopoietic atlas is accessible from an interactive web server (https://lllab.shinyapps.io/ysshinyapp/). Collectively, this study provides novel insights into the multi-wave nature of yolk sac hematopoiesis, clarifies the fundamental principles of yolk sac EHT at a single-cell resolution, and offers potential guidance for in vitro blood cell regeneration strategies.

1. INTRODUCTION

The embryonic hematopoietic system is primarily established through hematopoietic stem cell (HSC)-independent mechanisms. While definitive HSCs emerge later in the aorta-gonad-mesonephros (AGM) region, functional hematopoiesis during early embryogenesis is overwhelmingly driven by specialized progenitors generated prior to and independently of HSCs.1–3 The yolk sac constitutes the earliest and most indispensable extraembryonic site for HSC-independent hematopoiesis, producing progenitors essential for embryonic survival, organogenesis, and sustained function in adulthood.3

Multiple hematopoietic waves occur in the yolk sac. Primitive hematopoiesis arises from blood islands through the direct differentiation of mesodermal precursors around embryonic day (E)7, followed by definitive hematopoiesis, which is thought to proceed via endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition (EHT), including the emergence of erythromyeloid progenitors (EMPs) at E8.5, and lymphomyeloid progenitors (LMPs) at E9.5.3 After E9.5, pre-hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (pre-HSPC) populations emerge, as evidenced by their differentiation into common myeloid progenitors (CMPs) and granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (GMPs), governed by Evi1 expression.2 Definitive hematopoiesis has been shown to originate from hemogenic endothelial cells (HECs), which are specialized vascular endothelial cells in the embryo.2,3 In the yolk sac, HECs located in different vascular beds exhibit varying hematopoietic differentiation potentials. EMPs can be generated from HECs present in large vessels and capillaries,4 and the production of HSPCs with lymphoid potential is mainly detected in arterial vessels.5 Despite this significant progress, the molecular basis underlying the heterogeneity of HECs and distinct EHT procedures within the yolk sac remains largely unexplored.

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have profoundly elucidated the complexity and dynamics of hematopoietic development in mouse yolk sacs. Pioneering work by Pijuan-Sala et al6 established a comprehensive atlas from E6.5 to E8.5, revealing the critical role of the yolk sac in primitive erythropoiesis and early vasculogenesis while unexpectedly identifying rare megakaryocyte and myeloid populations, demonstrating spatial heterogeneity in endothelial cell transcriptomes across anatomical sites. When the temporal resolution was further extended to E9.5, the endothelial-hematopoietic diversification and complex maturation process of primitive erythrocytes and the transient wave of EMPs were reconstructed.7 The current evidence indicates that EMPs are heterogeneous and may follow distinct differentiation pathways. For instance, some studies suggested that multipotent EMPs in the E9.5 yolk sac can initiate megakaryocyte differentiation directly, potentially bypassing the canonical megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitor (MEP) stage, as MEPs (expressing CD131) tend to emerge later at E10.5.8 At earlier stages (E7.5–E8.5), primitive EMPs (pEMPs) are identified and subdivided into Csf1r⁻ (transiently contributing to endothelial cells and giving rise to Csf1r⁺ pEMPs) and Csf1r⁺ pEMPs (primarily producing microglia), alongside definitive EMPs (dEMPs).9 Moreover, scRNA-seq refined our understanding of the hematopoietic potential of the yolk sac endothelial cells. Both arterial and non-arterial endothelial populations have been reported to display lymphoid potential as early as at E8.5, independent of pronounced arterial identity.10 Using Neurl3 as a marker for enriching HECs with multilineage potential, it was revealed that yolk sac HECs, exhibiting weaker arterial signatures than their intraembryonic counterparts, could be partitioned into subsets biased toward arterial or venous characteristics, with the arterial-like subset enriched for lymphoid programs, which were further validated using the Unc5b reporter mouse model.11

Although the aforementioned scRNA-seq studies have been instrumental in defining the heterogeneity of endothelial and hematopoietic populations and establishing broad trajectories for primitive and definitive hematopoiesis within the yolk sac, the precise cellular evolution and molecular changes orchestrating the EHT underlying yolk sac definitive hematopoietic waves remain inadequately resolved. To address this gap, we integrated datasets from multiple developmental stages in early mouse embryos, specifically covering E6.75 to E11.0, to construct a comprehensive atlas of the endothelial and hematopoietic development of the yolk sac. By fine-grained clustering of yolk sac cells from E7.5 to E9.5, we carefully established a yolk sac hemato-endothelial atlas that delineated the critical events during 2 distinct waves of EHT: an early wave originating from primordial endothelial cells and generating EMPs with erythromyeloid potential, and a late wave originating from maturing endothelial cells and producing pre-HSPCs with multilineage differentiation potential. We further mapped the transcriptional dynamics and the underlying biological processes driving the specification of both EHT waves. This study provides critical insights into the spatiotemporal orchestration of EHT in the yolk sac, revealing previously unappreciated heterogeneity in the origins and programming of early hematopoietic ontogeny.

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1. Mice and embryos

The mice were bred in strict accordance with the institutional guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Center of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from SPF (Beijing, China) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and housed at the Experimental Animal Center of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences in accordance with the institutional guidelines. All mouse experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute. The yolk sac regions were separated according to the anatomical sites of the E9.5 (23–26 somite pairs) and E11.0 (41–45 somite pairs) embryos. The yolk sac was the portion remaining after removing the embryo proper and extraembryo vitelline arteries and veins. The fresh tissues were placed on ice in a sterilized 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube containing 200 μL of 10% fetal bovine serum/α minimum essential medium. Following removal of the culture medium by brief centrifugation, primary embryonic single-cell suspensions were prepared by digestion with 0.1% type I collagenase.

2.2. Flow cytometry

Cells were analyzed and sorted by flow cytometry using FACSAria II and the data were analyzed using FlowJo™ software (BD Life Sciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey). The cells were stained with the following antibodies: CD41 (MWReg30; eBioscience, San Diego, California) and CD45 (30-F11; eBioscience). 7-Aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; eBioscience) was used to exclude dead cells.

2.3. Cell preparation for scRNA-seq

The fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) sorting strategy for yolk sac cells at E9.5 and E11.0 was as follows: CD41 in combination with CD45 divided all yolk sac cells into 2 major categories, namely hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic cells. Cells from these 2 categories were pooled in specific proportions. The sorted cells were then pooled for sequencing.

2.4. Droplet-based single-cell RNA-seq library construction

The yolk sacs from embryos at the same stage were pooled to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells from each stage were sorted and pooled. Libraries were produced using a chromium system (10× Genomics, PN120263) following the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform in a 150 bp paired-end manner (sequenced by Berry Genomics).

2.5. Pre-processing of scRNA-seq data

Sequencing data generated with the 10× Genomics platform were processed for each sample using Cell Ranger software (version 7.1.0). Reads were aligned to the mm10 mouse reference genome obtained from the official 10× Genomics repository. Quantitative gene expression matrices based on unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were generated for each sample. Expression values were normalized using the transformation log(TPM/100 + 1), as the UMI number of most of our samples sequenced using the 10× Genomics method was of the order of 10,000 transcripts.

2.6. Quality control of sequencing data

Cells with fewer than 1000 UMI counts, 500 detected genes, and a mitochondrial gene proportion >10% were considered low-quality cells and were removed from these datasets. Doublets in each library were evaluated using DoubletFinder (version 2.0.4).12

2.7. Integration, dimensionality reduction, and clustering

To merge and analyze the different sequencing time points and datasets, we used the Scanpy package (version 1.9.8)13 for pre-processing and dimensionality reduction. Briefly, data normalization and log transformation were performed using scanpy.pp.normalize_total and scanpy.pp.log1p functions. We identified the top 2000 highly variable genes across batches using scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes function with the parameter batch_key=“batch” to account for batch-specific variance. Principal component analysis was conducted using scanpy.tl.pca function to capture the main sources of variation. To further correct for batch effects, we applied the BBKNN algorithm (scanpy.external.pp.bbknn). Subsequently, we used uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP; scanpy.tl.umap) for nonlinear dimensionality reduction and visualized the embeddings (scanpy.pl.umap). Unsupervised clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm, and clusters were annotated based on established marker gene expression.

To further subdivide the endothelial and hematopoietic populations, batch correction was performed in R (version 4.3.2) using the fastMNN function of the SeuratWrappers package (version 0.3.0; https://github.com/satijalab/seurat-wrappers).14 Dimensionality reduction was achieved using RunUMAP function, followed by unsupervised clustering using FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Cell subpopulations were annotated by integrating the feature gene expression, original data source information, and external reference datasets.

2.8. Differential gene expression analysis

To detect differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in different dataset clusters, we used the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat (version 4.4.0).15 Only genes that met certain criteria (fold change >1.5, adjusted p value <0.050) were retained as DEGs.

2.9. Partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) analysis

PAGA graphs of yolk sac HEC-related cells were generated based on UMAP projections using Scanpy with default parameters. Edges were weighted according to the connectivity between clusters, and low-connectivity edges with a weight below 0.4 were filtered out for visualization.

2.10. Gene functional enrichment analysis

To determine the biological significance of the feature genes in each cluster, gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.8.2)16 in R, based on the DEGs of each cluster. The top GO terms were selected and visualized using the scplotter package (version 0.1.1; https://github.com/pwwang/scplotter).

2.11. Gene set scores

Lymphoid, myeloid, and HSC gene sets were obtained from published studies.17–20 Single-cell scoring was conducted using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat to systematically quantify the expression levels of the gene sets at single-cell resolution.

2.12. Construction of single-cell trajectories and identification of dynamically changed genes

To reconstruct the developmental trajectories of individual cells or cell populations, we used 2 distinct computational approaches: Mpath (version 1.0)21 and Monocle 3 (version 1.3.4).22 In Mpath analysis, we began by selecting highly variable genes. Next, the cell labels obtained from the clustering analysis were assigned to each cell as landmark cluster information, whereas all other parameters were maintained at their default values, as specified by Mpath. When performing the trajectory analysis with Monocle 3, we strictly followed the official tutorial. Specifically, we used the UMAP embeddings generated by Seurat to construct a trajectory graph and ordered cells along this graph. To more accurately identify trajectory branches and assign pseudotime values, we applied the “order_cells” function to manually specify the root node on the trajectory graph. Root node selection was informed by sampling stage information along with insights gained from other trajectory analyses. We separately constructed trajectories of the 2 waves of hemogenic endothelial development. The graph_test function was used to identify genes with significant changes in expression along each developmental trajectory. Subsequently, gene heat maps and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway visualizations were generated using the ClusterGVis package (version 0.1.2; https://github.com/junjunlab/ClusterGVis).

2.13. Differential fate bias estimation and visualization

To estimate the fate bias of yolk sac HEC-related cells, CellRank (version 2.0.5)23 was used to quantify the lineage bias. EMP, pre-HSPC, and LMP were used as the terminal clusters, and the default settings were used for the parameters. The computed probabilistic quantification of the fate bias was further visualized for the yolk sac hemogenic endothelium-related cells.

2.14. Transcriptional regulation analysis

Gene regulatory network analysis was conducted using pySCENIC (version 0.12.1)24 with the default parameters. Subsequent data exploration and visualization were performed using the SeuratExtend (version 1.0.2, https://huayc09.github.io/SeuratExtend/) package in R.

2.15. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R programming language. To compare the gene counts, transcript numbers, gene expression levels, and feature scores between 2 specified cell clusters, a 2-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied.

2.16. Data and code availability

The raw sequence data reported in this article have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (Genomics, Proteomics & Bioinformatics 2021) at the National Genomics Data Center (Nucleic Acids Res 2022), China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GSA: CRA028922), and are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa.25,26 Previously published raw data were obtained from the GEO database under accession numbers GSE139389 and GSE164336 and from the ArrayExpress database under accession numbers E-MTAB-6967, E-MTAB-6970, and E-MTAB-11763. A detailed list of all the datasets used is provided in Table S1, https://links.lww.com/BS/A137. The processed single-cell datasets are accessible for interactive exploration through our web portal (https://lllab.shinyapps.io/ysshinyapp/) and can be downloaded from the Science Data Bank (https://www.scidb.cn/) at https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.28974. However, this study does not report on the original code. Any additional information required to reanalyze the data reported in this article is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

3. RESULTS

3.1. A hemato-endothelial atlas of early yolk sacs of mouse embryos

We assembled an integrative single-cell atlas focusing on yolk sac hemato-endothelial lineages from E6.75 to E11.0, through combining de novo scRNA-seq data with previously published datasets (Fig. 1A–C and Fig. S1, https://links.lww.com/BS/A132). It primarily included the endothelial and hematopoietic cells spanning E6.75 to E8.5, which were in silico purified from whole-embryo sampling cells,6 and spatially resolved yolk sac cells from E8.5 to E9.57 (Fig. 1A and C). Both datasets were densely sampled at continuous 0.25-day embryonic intervals (Fig. 1A). To enhance the precise identification of hematopoietic subpopulations, we specifically isolated hematopoietic cells from E9.5 and E11.0 yolk sacs for scRNA-seq (see Materials and Methods) and integrated these data into the atlas (Fig. 1A and C). Furthermore, we incorporated published datasets encompassing FACS-sorted endothelial cells, the extraembryonic mesoderm, EMPs, primitive erythroid cells, megakaryocytes, and macrophages27,28 (Fig. 1A and C), which could be used to validate the association between transcriptomically defined cell clusters and immunophenotypic populations (Fig. S1, https://links.lww.com/BS/A132). Following rigorous quality control, the final integrated dataset comprised 82,453 cells predominantly originating from the yolk sac, spanning from E6.75 to E11.0 (Fig. 1B and C). This comprehensive atlas enables the delineation of yolk sac endothelial and hematopoietic heterogeneity and their dynamic changes during early mouse embryonic development at a single-cell resolution.

After batch correction and unsupervised clustering, we identified 13 major cell types and assigned their identities based on the expression profiles of population-specific marker genes (Fig. 1B, D, and E). Within the endothelial and hematopoietic developmental trajectories, it included mesoderm (Mesp1), hemato-endothelial progenitors (HEP: T, Etv2), blood cell populations mainly derived from the yolk sac primitive hematopoietic wave (primitive blood progenitors, BP: Gata1, Hbb-bh1; erythrocytes: Hba, Klf1; megakaryocytes: Itga2b, Gp5, Gp9; and macrophages: C1qa, C1qb, Cx3cr1), endothelial cells (primordial EC: Etv2, Kdr; maturing EC: Pecam1, Cdh5; hemogenic EC: Runx1, Cdh5), and yolk sac definitive hematopoietic progenitors (HPC: Myb, Kit).6,9,29–32 Other cell types, including mesenchymal cells (Col1a1 and Col1a2), visceral endoderm (Apoa1 and Ttr), and parietal endoderm (Lamb1 and Lamb2)6,7 were identified and excluded from downstream analyses. These immunophenotypically sorted populations were consistent with their transcriptomically defined identifications, supporting the accuracy of our population assignment (Fig. S1, https://links.lww.com/BS/A132).

Focusing on hemato-endothelial lineages, we reconstructed the complete dynamic developmental trajectory from a mesodermal origin through endothelial and hematopoietic diversification from E6.75 to E9.5 (Fig. 1F). Quantitatively, primitive hematopoietic cells, mainly erythrocytes, dominated the cellular fraction (~80%) and served as the principal executors of embryonic oxygen transport and energy support. Endothelial lineages began to emerge at E7.5. Next, we aimed to dissect the cellular evolution and molecular roadmap of EHT in the yolk sac at a single-cell resolution.

3.2. Two temporally and molecularly distinct waves of HECs emerge in mouse yolk sacs

We performed unsupervised clustering of the endothelial compartment comprising primordial cells, maturing cells, and HECs, and divided them into 12 refined subpopulations (Fig. 2A). We note that the endothelial compartment included a portion of non–yolk sac-derived cells, primarily originating from whole-embryo samples (E6.75–E8.5) or inadvertent inclusion of adjacent tissues during yolk sac dissection (E8.5–E9.5) (Fig. 2A). Fortunately, using site-specific markers for the yolk sac (Lyve1, Npl), embryo proper (Cd24a), and allantois (Dlk1) endothelial cells32–34 (Fig. 2B), we assigned the presumed tissue origin to the clusters. Then, based on maturational status, hemogenic potential, and tissue origin, we annotated these endothelial subpopulations as: yolk sac-derived primordial endothelial cells (YS_primordial_EC: Etv2, Npl), maturing endothelial cells (YS_EC1/EC2: Lyve1, Cdh5), arterial endothelial cells (YS_aEC: Lyve1, Gja5), and HECs and their precursors (YS_HEC1/HEC2 and YS_preHEC1/preHEC2: Lyve1, Runx1); embryo proper-derived primordial endothelial cells (EP_primordial_EC: Etv2, Cd24a) and maturing endothelial cells (EP_EC: Cd24a, Cdh5); and allantois-derived primordial endothelial cells (EP_primordial_EC: Etv2, Dlk1) and maturing endothelial cells (EP_EC: Dlk1, Cdh5)32–34 (Fig. 2A, B; Fig. S2, https://links.lww.com/BS/A133). Integrated analyses of cells derived from embryo proper and allantoic tissues at E8.25 further confirmed the accuracy of our spatial origin annotations for endothelial subpopulations6 (Fig. S3, https://links.lww.com/BS/A134). Moreover, a series of site-specific DEGs was identified (Fig. 2C), reflecting their adaptation of molecular specialization to distinct microenvironments.

Leveraging the yolk sac endothelial atlas of unbiased sampling from E7.5 to E9.5, we observed sequential endothelial specification events: YS_primordial_EC emerged at E7.5, differentiated into YS_EC1 at E8.25, and subsequently matured into YS_EC2 and YS_aEC (Fig. 2D and E). Meanwhile, we observed 2 distinct hemogenic specification events: for precursors of HECs, YS_preHEC1 appeared at E7.5 but regressed at E8.75, and YS_preHEC2 emerged at E8.5 and persisted until E9.5; for HECs, which subsequently emerged, YS_HEC1 arose at E8.0, peaked at E8.5, and then declined, whereas YS_HEC2 initiated at E8.75 and was dominated by E9.5 (Fig. 2D and E). Consistent with the cellular evolution patterns along the developmental time described earlier, the PAGA and Mpath trajectory analysis results suggested the existence of 2 waves of hemogenic specialization pathways, from YS_primordial_EC to YS_preHEC1 and then to YS_HEC1, as well as from maturing YS_EC1 to YS_preHEC2 and then to YS_HEC2 (Fig. 2E and F).

Compared to YS_HEC1, YS_HEC2 uniquely expressed HSC-primed HEC markers (eg, Hlf, Nupr1, and Gfi1)28 and elevated expression of ribosomal genes (Fig. 2G). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that YS_HEC2 was highly enriched for ribosome biogenesis and protein translation, mimicking the features of HSC-primed HECs28 (Fig. 2G). A series of surface marker genes, potentially discriminating between YS_HEC1 and YS_HEC2, was identified for future isolation and functional experiments (Fig. 2H). Comparative analyses of HEC precursors showed that YS_preHEC1 was primarily enriched in ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, ribosome biogenesis, and cytoplasmic translation, whereas YS_preHEC2 was unexpectedly enriched in lymphoid and myeloid differentiation programs, indicating enhanced hematopoietic priming (Fig. 2I). Lineage potential scoring further confirmed the stronger lymphoid/myeloid competence of the second wave of hemogenic populations (Fig. 2J).

3.3. Heterogeneity of yolk sac definitive hematopoietic progenitors

We reanalyzed yolk sac HPCs (Fig. 1B) and divided them into 9 distinct hematopoietic subpopulations (Fig. 3A), including precursors of HSPCs (pre-HSPCs: Hlf, Hoxa9), EMPs (EMP/EMP-E/EMP-M: Kit, Myb, Cd34, Itga2b), monocyte progenitors (MonoP: Irf8, Csf1r), LMP (Flt3, Il7r, Spi1), dendritic cell precursors (pre-DC: Cd74, Ccr2), mast cell precursors (pre-MC: Cpa3, Hdc), and neutrophil precursors (pre-Neu: Elane, Cebpe)4,35–40 (Fig. 2B). Notably, LMP and the 3 myeloid lineage progenitors (pre-DC, pre-MC, and pre-Neu) remained scarce until E9.5 but significantly expanded at E11.0, as shown in the hematopoietic cell-enriched dataset (Fig. 3C).

Among the 3 EMP-related subpopulations, EMP likely represented an upstream, relatively unbiased stage, showing moderate erythroid-megakaryocytic and myeloid potential and harboring stronger cell migration (Fig. 3D and E; Fig. S4, https://links.lww.com/BS/A135). The erythroid-biased EMP (EMP-E) demonstrated a pronounced bias toward erythroid-megakaryocytic potential, as evidenced by the high expression of erythroid-megakaryocytic lineage genes (Gata1 and Pf4)8 and enrichment of blood coagulation and platelet activation (Fig. 3D–F). However, myeloid-biased EMP (EMP-M) possessed a strong bias toward myeloid potential, with increased expression of myeloid-associated genes (Cebpb and Plac8)39 and enrichment of myeloid cell differentiation (Fig. 3E and F). We identified several surface marker genes that could potentially discriminate between the 3 EMP-related subpopulations for future validation of EMP heterogeneity (Fig. 3F). In contrast, pre-HSPCs and LMP exhibited limited erythroid-megakaryocytic potential but strong myeloid and lymphoid potential17,18,38 (Fig. 3D). Pre-HSPCs exhibited the highest HSC signature score compared to any EMP subpopulation and LMP, indicating that the pre-HSPC population represents a cell compartment with heightened stemness compared to EMPs and LMPs19 (Fig. 3G).

3.4. Two waves of EHT procedures in mouse yolk sacs

Building on the cellular annotations described earlier, we constructed a refined atlas of yolk sac endothelial and hematopoietic populations (Fig. 4A). We made it accessible online at https://lllab.shinyapps.io/ysshinyapp/ using ShinyCell.41 We observed that EMP populations (EMP, EMP-E, and EMP-M) emerged from E8.5 onward following the appearance of the first wave of HECs (YS_HEC1). These EMPs progressively dominated the hematopoietic compartment of the yolk sac (83% at E9.5). In contrast, pre-HSPCs emerged later (starting at E8.75) following the appearance of the second wave of HECs (YS_HEC2), maintaining a stable proportion in the yolk sac through E9.5 (6% at E9.5) (Fig. 4B). This temporal kinetic pattern suggested that YS_HEC1 and YS_HEC2 underwent different lineage commitments to generate EMPs and pre-HSPCs, respectively. Mpath trajectory and lineage bias analyses further supported this hypothesis: YS_preHEC1 and YS_HEC1 exhibited a propensity toward the EMP fate, while YS_preHEC2 and YS_HEC2 were biased toward the HSPC lineage (Fig. 4C and D).

To dissect the transcriptional differences underlying the 2 distinct EHT waves, we performed SCENIC analysis to identify differentially activated transcription factor regulons across the key cell populations involved in the stepwise specification. Most regulons exhibited stage-specific activity patterns. Early endothelial populations from both waves shared vasculogenesis- and proliferation/differentiation-associated transcription factors (Sox17 and Atf3),42,43 which were highly active in YS_primordial_EC and YS_EC1. In contrast, hemogenic endothelial precursors displayed wave-specific regulon activity, and the first EHT wave (YS_HEC1) showed pluripotency-linked programs (Nanog and Thap11),44,45 whereas the second EHT wave (YS_HEC2) exhibited enriched activity of hematopoietic and cell cycle regulators (Gata2 and E2f6).46,47 Notably, YS_HEC2 uniquely activated Rxrb regulons (Retinoid X Receptor Beta, a key modulator of EHT).48 Downstream EMPs and pre-HSPCs showed myeloid-related regulatory activity (Cebpa and Cebpb),39 but pre-HSPCs distinctly expressed stem/progenitor identity factors (Hoxa7, Hoxa9, and Hoxa10)49 (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these transcriptional dynamics delineate a hierarchical regulatory framework underpinning the 2 waves of EHT.

3.5. Divergent molecular dynamics of two waves of EHT in mouse yolk sacs

To further delineate the developmental trajectories of the 2 yolk sac EHT waves, we performed pseudotime analysis using Monocle 3 (Fig. 5A). We first examined the dynamics of canonical molecular signatures associated with EHT, including endothelial, arterial, hemogenic, and hematopoietic signatures, along developmental trajectories. Our previously established DA EHT trajectory from primordial endothelial cells to T1 pre-HSCs is shown for comparison.28 Notably, while the first yolk sac EHT wave was initiated with low endothelial characteristics, both the second yolk sac EHT and the DA EHT waves began with pronounced endothelial features. However, the second yolk sac EHT wave exhibited a more rapid loss of endothelial identity. In contrast, the DA wave was preceded by a distinct arterial specification phase, a feature absent in both yolk sac EHT waves, although the second yolk sac EHT wave showed subtle arterial marker upregulation, which is consistent with our previous findings.10,11 Across all 3 trajectories, the hemogenic and hematopoietic signatures gradually increased (Fig. 5B).

Five gene expression patterns were identified based on the top 500 dynamically altered genes along the developmental trajectories of both yolk sac EHT waves (Fig. 5C and D). Angiogenesis-related pathways (Wnt, Apelin, and HIF-1 signaling)50,51 progressively declined in both waves. However, metabolism-related pathways (eg, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and biosynthesis of amino acids) diverged between waves. The first yolk sac EHT wave, initiated with a relatively low metabolic activity, was upregulated at the HEC stage (YS_HEC1) and attenuated in EMPs. In contrast, the second yolk sac EHT wave activated metabolism earlier at the pre-HEC and HEC stages (YS_EC2 and YS_preHEC2) but was suppressed upon hematopoietic commitment (pre-HSPC). These observations indicate stage-specific metabolic demands during the yolk sac EHT. As expected, the second wave of yolk sac EHT preferentially yielded multipotent progenitors with enhanced lymphoid potential, consistent with our observation of progressive upregulation of hematopoietic cell lineage and lymphoid activation-related genes during development. Intriguingly, ribosomal genes were enriched in multiple dynamic patterns in both EHT waves, suggesting that ribosomes may play significant and multifaceted roles in the EHT process.52,53 These findings demonstrate that the stage-specific activation and repression of genetic programs during EHT orchestrates the production of lineage-restricted hematopoietic outputs.

4. DISCUSSION

Although the yolk sac is recognized as the primary site for HSC-independent embryonic hematopoiesis, scRNA-seq studies have largely focused on primitive and single definitive waves. However, accumulating evidence suggests that definitive yolk sac hematopoiesis occurs in multiple waves, yielding temporally distinct progenitor populations. Crucially, the cellular and molecular characteristics underlying these successive definitive waves remain unresolved at a single-cell resolution.

Our study addresses this gap by deconvoluting yolk sac-definitive hematopoiesis into 2 temporally and molecularly distinct EHT waves, each with unique origins, transcriptional signatures, and hematopoietic outputs. The first EHT wave (initiating at ~E7.75) originates directly from primordial endothelial cells (YS_primordial_EC) and predominantly gives rise to EMPs, including less-differentiated EMP, erythroid-biased EMP-E, and myeloid-biased EMP-M. The second EHT wave (initiating at ~E8.75) originating from maturing endothelial cells (YS_EC1) exhibited enhanced HSC-priming signatures (eg, Hlf, Gfi1, and Nupr1) and specifically generated multipotent HSPCs with expanded lymphoid-myeloid potential (Fig. S5, https://links.lww.com/BS/A136). Notably, we observed significant differences in ribosome-related genes between the 2 EHT waves and among the EHT-related populations. Ribosomal function has been reported to play a critical role in the intraembryonic EHT,52 and the functional significance of ribosomal activation in the extraembryonic EHT warrants further investigation.

Although pseudotime trajectory analyses and molecular profiling support the existence of 2 largely independent EHT pathways originating from distinct endothelial precursors (primordial endothelial cells for wave 1 and maturing endothelial cells for wave 2), questions remain regarding the potential branching points within these developmental routes. We observed both temporal proximity and transcriptional overlap between YS_HEC1 and YS_HEC2 (Fig. 2). Previous in vitro studies have proposed that the arterialization of HECs is essential for activating the lymphomyeloid program, raising the possibility of a fate transition from YS_HEC1 (with limited lymphoid potential) to YS_HEC2 (with robust lymphoid potential).54 However, because of the inherent limitations of computational trajectory inference, which reflects transcriptomic similarity rather than true lineage relationships, we cannot exclude the possibility that a subset of YS_HEC1 contributes to YS_HEC2.

This study integrated extraembryonic datasets from multiple developmental stages in mice to define the transcriptomic landscapes and lineage biases of 2 successive yolk sac EHT waves, providing valuable directions for future validation. However, this study had several limitations. First, we must emphasize that our conclusions were derived from in silico analyses and lack in vivo functional validation. Accordingly, the validation of inferred trajectories and fate predictions using fate-mapping approaches (eg, lineage tracing or spatially resolved tracing55,56) will be required in future studies. Second, although preliminary screening identified surface markers that could distinguish the HEC populations from the 2 waves, experimental validation is needed to enable robust enrichment strategies. Third, the predicted key transcription factors and ribosome-associated genes were not functionally validated; therefore, perturbation assays are necessary to establish their causal roles in the 2 EHT waves.

In summary, this study established that spatially and temporally segregated EHT waves drive the sequential production of functionally distinct hematopoietic progenitors in the yolk sac, thereby reshaping our understanding of early hematopoietic ontogeny. Future efforts to integrate functional validation with multidimensional omics will be crucial for fully dissecting the molecular cues and lineage decisions governing these waves.
