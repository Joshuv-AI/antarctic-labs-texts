Soil microbial community composition by crop type under rotation diversification
Authors: Donglin Zong, Yefu Zhou, Jing Zhou, Yanan Zhao, Xiaokang Hu, Tao Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCrop rotation is an important agricultural practice that often affects the metabolic processes of soil microorganisms through the composition and combination of crops, thereby altering nutrient cycling and supply to the soil. Although the benefits of crop rotation have been extensively discussed, the effects and mechanisms of different crop combinations on the soil microbial community structure in specific environments still need to be analyzed in detail.Materials and methodsIn this study, six crop rotation systems were selected, for which the spring crops were mainly tobacco or gramineous crops: AT (asparagus lettuce and tobacco rotation), BT (broad bean and tobacco rotation), OT (oilseed rape and tobacco rotation), AM (asparagus lettuce and maize rotation), BM (broad bean and maize rotation), and OR (oilseed rape and rice rotation). All crops had been cultivated for > 10 years. Soil samples were collected when the rotation was completed in spring, after which the soil properties, composition, and functions of bacterial and fungal communities were analyzed.ResultsThe results indicate that spring cultivated crops play a more dominant role in the crop rotation systems than do autumn cultivated crops. Crop rotation systems with the same spring crops have similar soil properties and microbial community compositions. pH and AK are the most important factors driving microbial community changes, and bacteria are more sensitive to environmental responses than fungi. Rotation using tobacco systems led to soil acidification and a decrease in microbial diversity, while the number of biomarkers and taxonomic indicator species differed between rotation patterns. Symbiotic network analysis revealed that the network complexity of OT and BM was the highest, and that the network density of tobacco systems was lower than that of gramineous systems.ConclusionsDifferent crop rotation combinations influence both soil microbial communities and soil nutrient conditions. The spring crops in the crop rotation systems had stronger dominating effects, and the soil bacteria were more sensitive than the fungi were to environmental changes. The tobacco rotation system can cause soil acidification and thereby affect soil sustainability, while the complexity of soil microbial networks is lower than that of gramineous systems. These results provide a reference for future sustainable applications of rotation crop systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03580-2.

Introduction

Cultivation is one of the most intensive land uses for humans, and repeated cultivation has a significant impact on soil sustainability [1]. In particular, continuous monocultures often lead to an imbalanced nutrient supply and increased risk of diseases and pests, ultimately leading to continuous cropping obstacles [2]. In continuous monoculture cropping systems, plant roots repeatedly release the same types of exudates over the years, leading to autotoxicity in many crops [3, 4], and studies have shown that water and ethanol extracts from tobacco rhizosphere soil significantly inhibit seed germination [5]. Meanwhile, succession cropping leads to a decrease in soil fertility and microbial diversity, increasing barriers to severe soil-borne diseases [6], and decreasing yield and quality, especially for gramineous crops (maize and rice) [7]. However, long-term crop rotation accumulates more soil carbon and microbial biomass than monoculture, which is beneficial to soil health and sustainable agricultural production [8, 9]. Although the benefits of crop rotation in terms of yield, resource utilization, and soil improvement have been noted in studies, an evaluation of the consequences of alternative rotations is crucial for determining the optimal crop combinations and understanding the relationships between crop rotation systems and crop production.

In crop rotation systems, the cultivation of each crop type causes different soil disturbances. Monocropping alters C and N cycling, which is correlated with microbiome differences [10]. Frequent tillage can alter the properties of topsoil, such as mixing additional oxygen, which directly affects the composition and activity of the soil microbial community [11]. Different crops often have unique rhizosphere microbes, and rotation can increase crop yield and improve soil conditions; as such, crop rotation is recognized as the key to improving crop sustainability. For example, planting soybean plants before rapeseed significantly increased the population density of microorganisms that can inhibit Plasmodiophora brassicae [12]. Maize and soybean yield and biomass were greater in more diverse rotations, and crop biomass and yield were better predicted by the microbiome than by nutrients [13]. Additionally, rhizosphere metabolites affect soil microbial diversity and community composition, and influence the soil microbial population through C–N–P–S cycles [14]. Overall, crop rotation, as an effective soil management practice, plays an important role in maintaining and improving soil fertility and promoting crop growth. In agricultural ecosystems, the positive relationship between biodiversity and ecosystem function is driven by the interaction between crop rotation diversity and soil microbial diversity [8]. Crop rotations increase microbial biomass by an average of 21% according to meta-analysis of 122 studies [15] A diverse crop rotation system can maintain soil microbial communities, and positively impact soil organic matter and soil fertility by increasing the quantity, quality, and chemical diversity of residues [8]. Therefore, it is important to understand how crop types and their combinations in crop rotation affect the composition and function of soil microorganisms.

Crop types or their different genotypic characteristics may be important driving factors for changes in soil microbial composition [16, 17], and crop rotational diversity and crop legacy affect crop vigor and associated microbes; consequently, crop yield and biomass increase with more diverse rotations [14]. Therefore, many studies have suggested the selection of crop types or genotypes that produce positive soil microbial legacy effects [18, 19]. Currently, research on the impact of crop type on soil legacy effects has focused on changes in the soil microbiome, and only a few studies have attempted to analyze the direct effects of crop rotation on soil ecosystem function and on subsequent crops. For example, the impact of introducing grassland during crop rotation on soil organic matter and microbial characteristics may lead to an increase in greenhouse gas emissions (such as N2O) from the soil during subsequent crop cultivation [20, 21]. However, there is currently no clear definition of which type of crop rotation can promote the required soil ecosystem services [22, 23]. Further research is needed on the specific effects of crops on soil microbial composition and the reactions of other crops to these changes. There is still a critical need to determine the optimal combinations for crop rotations. Moreover, whether changes in crop type and environmental conditions collectively determine the composition of soil microbial communities rather than relying solely on constantly changing crop types has not yet been confirmed.

In addition, bacteria and fungi, the most important components of the soil microbiome, often interact with each other because they share the same habitats [24]. Bacteria and fungi play important roles in the soil nutrient cycling. Bacteria are mainly involved in the transformation of soil organic carbon (SOC), whereas fungi mainly decompose mineral components in the soil [25]. The growth and reproductive conditions of bacteria and fungi in the soil environment and their sensitivities to environmental interference also differ [26, 27]. However, it is unclear how bacteria and fungi respond to crop rotation systems, particularly whether sensitivity is determined by changes in crop type.

In this study, six crop rotation systems cultivated for > 10 years were selected to test whether changing crop types under rotation diversification determines the composition of soil microbial communities. The spring crops mainly consisted of tobacco and gramineous crops (maize and rice), with the six systems including AT (asparagus lettuce and tobacco rotation), BT (broad bean and tobacco rotation), OT (oilseed rape and tobacco rotation), AM (asparagus lettuce and maize rotation), BM (broad bean and maize rotation), and OR (oilseed rape and rice rotation). In this study, the following hypotheses were verified: (1) Spring crops play a more dominant role in the crop rotation systems than autumn crops; (2) the factors driving changes in the soil microbial community vary depending on the type of crop rotation; and (3) the sensitivity of soil bacteria to environmental responses is greater than that of fungi. Through the verification of the above hypotheses, this research explored differences in soil microbial community composition caused by different crop rotation systems, determined optimal local rotation combinations, and compared the sensitivities of bacteria and fungi to different rotation combinations.

Materials and methods

Test site and sample collection

The soil samples were collected from Dali, Yunnan Province, China (25.81 N, 100.13 E), in the spring after the completion of each rotation combination (April 16, 2024). This site has a subtropical plateau monsoon climate, where the altitude is 1972 m. The annual average temperature is 15.7 °C, and the annual rainfall is 1078 mm, with the greastest amount of rainfall occurring from June to July. The 10-year climate data of the sampling site are shown in Fig. S1. The crop varieties information are shown in Table S1. The six crop rotation systems used were AT (asparagus lettuce and tobacco rotation), BT (broad bean and tobacco rotation), OT (oilseed rape and tobacco rotation), AM (asparagus lettuce and maize rotation), BM (broad bean and maize rotation), and OR (oilseed rape and rice rotation). Five fields were chosen for each crop rotation system, and five points were selected for sampling, with the samples subsequently mixed. Before soil collection, dead branches and leaves on the soil surface were removed. Then, a core of soil from 0 to 20 cm depth was collected using a soil drill (5 cm diameter). The soil samples were divided into two parts: one was used for soil physicochemical property determination and the other was used for 16 S and ITS high-throughput sequencing analysis.

Determination of soil physical and chemical properties

The basic physical and chemical properties of each soil sample were determined, including total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), available potassium (AK), organic carbon (SOC), and pH. The total nitrogen (TN) content was determined using the Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method [28]. The TP content was determined using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2700, Kyoto, Japan) and molybdenum–antimony colorimetry [29]. AK was digested with CH3COONH4 and its concentration was determined via flame atomic absorption spectrometry (Sherwood M410 Britain, Sherwood Scientific Ltd., Cambridge, UK) [30]. The soil pH was measured using a pH meter, and the soil to water ratio was 1:5 [28]. Soil organic carbon (SOC) was measured by oxidation with potassium dichromate [30].

16S and ITS high-throughput sequencing

A TGuide S96 magnetic soil DNA kit (Tiangen Biotechnology (Beijing) Co., Ltd.) was used to extract total genomic DNA from the soil samples, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) were used to amplify the 16S rRNA hypervariable region V3-V4. ITS 1F: 5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’ and ITS 2:5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’ were used to amplify the highly variable ITS1-2 region of the fungal ITS gene [31–33]. The PCR products were detected on agarose gels and purified using an Omega DNA Purification Kit (Omega, Inc., Norcross, GA, USA). Purified PCR products were collected for 2–250 bp pairings on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences among multiple groups, with p = 0.05 considered significant. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), a dimensionality reduction sequencing method, was employed to analyses differences in fungal and bacterial operational taxonomic units (OTUs) [34]. Analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) was used to analyze similarities between multidimensional data groups. The closer the R value obtained by ANOSIM was to 1, the greater the intergroup difference compared with the intragroup difference. LEfSe (LDA effect size) analysis was used to identify biomarkers with significant differences between groups [35]. The LEfSe parameter for the α value of the Kruskal‒Wallis test for intergroup factors was set to < 0.05, and the log-LDA score threshold for identifying features was > 4.0. To explore differences in the microbiome among the different soil environmental factors, redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed using the ‘vegan’ software package in R. The effects of soil environmental factors on the α diversity index and OTUs of bacteria and fungi were analyzed via network heatmaps. To study the interactions between bacteria and fungi, the dominant phyla of bacteria and fungi were visualized using correlation heatmaps.

Results

Soil physicochemical properties

The physical and chemical properties of soils in the different crop rotation systems differed significantly (Table 1). The soils in the AT, BT, and OT treatments were acidic, whereas those in the AM, BM, and OR treatments were neutral. AK in the AT, BT, and OT treatments was greater (> 450 mg/kg) than that in the AM, BM, and OR treatments (< 360 mg/kg). The soil TN concentration was highest in the OR treatment (4.7 g/kg) and lowest in the BM treatment (2.6 g/kg); the other treatments had values between 2.8 and 3.6 g/kg. The soil SOC content exhibited the same trend as the TN content, with the highest content occurring in the OR treatment (46.6 g/kg) and the lowest occurring in the BM treatment (22.6 g/kg). There were no significant differences in soil TP between the six crop rotation systems (approximately 4.5 g/kg).

Sequence data and microbial diversity

To investigate the structure and diversity of microorganisms in the six crop rotation systems, clean reads without primer sequences were obtained using the Trimmomatic v0.33 software to filter the raw reads and the cut adapt 1.9.1 software to identify and remove primer sequences. In total, 2,393,601 raw reads and 2,200,621 high-quality clean reads were obtained from the 16 S rRNA gene. ITSs yielded 2,350,451 raw reads and 1,978,072 high-quality clean reads. The percentage of bacteria and fungi in the DNA library was > 98%, indicating that the results were reliable. For bacteria, the Simpson index, Shannon index, and OTU number were also lowest in the OT treatment, but most of the differences among the other treatments were not significant. For fungi, the Simpson index, Shannon index, and OTU number were significantly higher in the BM than in the AT, BT and OT, and there were no significant differences between the other groups except for BM. (Fig. 1).

Microbial community structure and functions

At the OTU level, PCoA revealed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 15.24% and 7.3% of the total variation in the soil bacteria, respectively (Fig. 2a). Similarly, the contribution rates of soil fungi were 12.81% and 6.85%, respectively (Fig. 2b). In the same crop rotation system for spring crops, the soil microbial community structure had a high degree of similarity. The bacterial community structure was significantly different among different crop rotation systems for spring crops (Fig. 2a). The soil fungal community structure in the flue-cured tobacco rotation system (AT, BT, and OT) exhibits similarities (Fig. 2b). Overall, The bacterial communities exhibited significant differences under the six crop rotation systems, and there were significant differences along the PC1 axis among the AT, BT, and OT treatments and among the AM, BM, and OR treatments (Fig. 2a). However, differences in fungal communities were not significant, and the AT, BT, and OT treatments exhibited similarities (Fig. 2b).

Relative abundance of bacteria and fungi

The dominant phyla in the microbial communities of the six crop rotation systems were the same, but there were significant differences in relative abundance. Among the dominant bacterial phyla, Proteobacteria (∼ 27.6–42.8%) was the dominant phylum, followed by Acidobacteriota (∼ 12.1–18.3%), and Gemmatimonadota (∼ 8.4–11.3%) [Fig. 3a]. Among the fungal phyla, Ascomycota (∼ 40.3–68.3%) was significantly dominant, followed by Mortierellomycota (∼ 9.5–32.4%) [Fig. 3b]. The results confirm that crop rotation systems can lead to changes in soil microbial community composition.

Abundance of (a) bacterial and (b) fungal phyla under different crop rotation patterns

LEfSe analysis was used to identify indicator taxa of abundance changes in the crop rotation systems, based on LDA score > 4 and p < 0.05. A total of 65 taxa (from phylum to genus) were identified in bacteria and 71 in fungi. The numbers of bacterial biomarkers in the AT, BT, OT, AM, BM, and OR-treated groups were 14, 6, 24, 4, 8, and 9, respectively (Fig. S2a), and the numbers of fungi in the AT, BT, OT, AM, BM, and OR-treated groups were 0, 9, 14, 13, 11, and 24, respectively (Fig. S2b). The bacterial biomarkers were mainly distributed in Acidobacteria, Methylomirabilota, Nitrospirota, Gemmatimonadota, Proteobacteria, Patescibacteria, Desulfobacterota, Actinobacteria, and Chloroflexi. Fungal biomarkers were mainly distributed among Mortierellomycota, Chytridiomycota, Basidiomycota, Olpidiomycota, and Ascomycota species. We identified the indicator taxa for each crop rotation system at the genus level, with differences in biomarkers mainly dependent on the crop rotation patterns. AT, BM, and OR did not contain any specific bacterial genera. The endemic genera of BT were Sphingomonas, those of OT were Rhodanobacter and Chujaibacter, and those of AM were Lysobacter and Luteimonas (Fig. 4a and Fig. S2a). AT had no endemic genera at the fungal level. The endemic genus of BT was Solicoccozyma and that of BM was Pseudeurotium. The endemic genera of OT were Mortierella and Humicola; those of AM were Enterocarpus, Fusarium, Plectosphaerella, Cladorrhinum, and Botryotrichum; and those of OR were Cladosporium, Erysiphe, Olpidium, and Pyrenochaetopsis (Fig. 4b and Fig. S2b).

Effects of crop rotation diversity on microbial symbiotic network complexity

Symbiotic networks of soil bacterial and fungal communities were constructed to explore microbial symbiotic patterns under crop rotation diversification (Fig. 5). The soil microbial networks for each treatment exhibited different co-occurrence patterns. Network topology parameters (number of nodes and edges) were used to assess the complexity of soil microbial networks. The results showed that there were significant differences in the topology of the co-occurrence networks among the different crop rotation patterns and that the rotation effect interfered with the network complexity of microorganisms. Nodes and edges showed that the network complexity of microbial communities treated with OT in the tobacco system was significantly higher than that of AT and BT, whereas that of BM treatment was significantly higher than that of AM and OR in the gramineous system. Network density analysis showed that the microbial density in the gramineous system was significantly lower than that in the tobacco system except OT and OR. In addition, the percentages of positive and negative microbial communities showed that the proportions of AT-, BT-, and AM-positive bacteria were much greater than those of negative bacteria, whereas the percentages of OT-, BM-, and OR-positive bacteria were relatively consistent.

Soil drivers of microbial community change

The RDA results showed that soil factors played an important role in the microbial community. Changes in pH (r2 = 0.822, p = 0.001) and AK (r2 = 0.855, p = 0.001) significantly affected the changes in bacterial composition in the soils of the different crop rotation systems, whereas changes in SOC (r2 = 0.011, p = 0.822), TP (r2 = 0.023, p = 0.667), and TN (r2 = 0.144, p = 0.083) had no obvious effect on the bacterial communities (Fig. 6a). pH and AK were positively correlated with the dominant bacteria Sphingomoda, Rhodanobacter, and MND1, and negatively correlated with Haliangium. Changes in pH (r2 = 0.781, p = 0.001), TN (r2 = 0.834, p = 0.001), SOC (r2 = 0.615, p = 0.001), and AK (r2 = 0.713, p = 0.001) had significant effects on the fungal community. Changes in the TP (r2 = 0.106, p = 0.163) did not affect the fungal community structure (Fig. 6b). TN, SOC, and AK were positively correlated with Cladosporium, Mortierella, and Humicola, and negatively correlated with pH.

Discussion

Sustainability issues often arise in agricultural systems because single crops are cultivated during the growing season with extremely low biodiversity. Crop rotation is used to increase crop diversity and nutrient competition, with temporal changes in the chemistry and quality of the soil input by plants greater than the spatial changes [8]. Crop rotation usually results in yield gains, which are attributed to the enhancement of agricultural ecosystem functions, such as increases in soil fertility and the maintenance of the soil structure [36]. These processes are regulated mainly by the complex biochemical processes of soil microorganisms [9, 37]. In natural systems, the subsurface benefits of plant diversity are associated with changes in microbial communities [38, 39], as agroecosystem changes [40]. Although crop rotation can increase the sustainability of agroecosystems, little is known about how different crop rotation systems affect soil properties and microbial community composition, which are related to the sustainability of agricultural production. Six crop rotation systems were used in this study. With the replacement of spring crops, the soil properties and microbial community compositions of the different crop rotation systems changed significantly (Table 1; Fig. 2). The soil pH of the tobacco systems was acidic (pH 4–5), whereas that of the gramineous systems was neutral (approximately 7). The available potassium in the tobacco systems was maintained at > 460 mg/kg and that in the gramineous systems was maintained at < 360 mg/kg. Moreover, PCoA revealed a significant difference in the bacterial community between the tobacco and gramineous systems, whereas the fungal community compositions of the six crop rotation systems were similar, which might indicate that the response of fungi to the environment is weaker than that of bacteria [41]. Soil bacteria and fungi typically respond differently to soil environmental factors, including pH, the C: N ratio, and soil nutrients, all of which influence the community structure of soil bacteria and fungi [42, 43]. The disparities in the responses of bacteria and fungi to these factors can be used to assess the health of the soil and guide various agricultural measures, including fertilization, to evaluate their impact on soil sustainability. In this study, the tobacco and gramineous spring crops (maize and rice) played a dominant role in determining the soil properties and microbial community composition throughout the entire crop rotation systems. Crop rotation systems with the same spring crop had similar soil properties and microbial community compositions. The growing season of spring crops in the study region is the rainy and hot season, and environmental factors may control the metabolic activity of soil microorganisms. However, such changes might also be due to the impact of previous crop residues on the physical, chemical, and biological properties of the soil [44]. Although the microbial communities in the tobacco and gramineous systems were similar, soil microbial diversity and relative abundance were significantly different. Moreover, most functions of microorganisms are controlled by community composition, and the functions of microorganisms change [45]. Among the six crop rotation systems, the Simpson index, Shannon index, and OTU number of microorganisms under the BM treatment were greater than those under the other crop rotation systems, whereas the OT treatment had the lowest diversity index and OTU number (Fig. 1); the relative abundances of the dominant bacteria in the tobacco and gramineous systems were different. The relative abundance of Proteobacteria, the dominant bacteria, in the OT treatment was the highest (42.8%), whereas that in the AT treatment was the lowest (27.6%). The relative abundance of Ascomycota, the dominant fungus, was significantly higher in the AM treatment than it was in the other crop rotation systems. This demonstrates that variable crop rotation is conducive to increasing the diversity and relative abundance of microbial community [46, 47]. This process is also beneficial for increasing the decomposition of soil organic matter and improving soil nutrient supply, which ensures the sustainability of agroecosystem production. This study also showed that different crop combinations in crop rotation systems can have different effects on the composition and abundance of soil microorganisms. These influences also determine the efficacy of different crop rotation systems for soil improvement in this research. Therefore, to fully utilize the sustainable effects of crop rotation systems on production, it is necessary to analyze the soil properties and microbial diversity under different rotation combinations in the target area to determine the most sustainable crop rotation system for that area.

According to LEfSe analysis, Sphingomonas, Rhodanobacter, Chujaibacter, Solicoccozyma, Mortierella, and Humicola were indicator species in the BT and OT rotation systems in low-pH soil. This not only indicates that these bacteria are suitable for acidic environments, but also that they can maintain normal plant growth in low-pH environments. For example, Sphingomonas promotes plant growth under various stress conditions, mainly through the production of plant hormones such as gibberellin, salicylic acid (SA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), zeatin, and abscisic acid, to promote plant germination and growth [48, 49]. Rhodanobacter species are likely to undergo long-term denitrification in soil ecosystems at pH values as low as 3 or less [50]. Mortierella spp. produce antibiotics that inhibit many pathogens and are beneficial to fungi [51]. Lysobacter, Luteimonas, Enterocarpus, Fusarium, Plectosphaerella, Cladorrhinum, and Botryotrichum were found to be indicator species of the AM rotation system. Lysobacter species inhibit plant pathogens by releasing various antibacterial compounds and extracellular enzymes [52]. The indicator species Pseudeurotium, under BM rotation, has strong cellulose decomposability [53]. Even with the same spring crop, different rotations produced different indicator species. For example, compared with the BM system, the AM system produced more harmful fungi, and the diversity index of the microorganisms was lower than that of the BM system.

A symbiotic network is important to ensure the stability of microbial communities in response to external disturbances, and network analysis is widely used in microbial ecology [54]. Several studies have shown that the complexity of soil microbial networks is influenced by environmental changes such as crop rotation combinations, which increase the diversity of microbial communities, symbiotic networks, and ecosystems [55]. This study showed that crop rotation systems interfere with the complexity of soil microbial community symbiotic networks during crop rotation diversification. Even during the rotation of a similar system (tobacco or gramineous systems), the replacement of different spring crops results in changes in symbiotic network complexity and symbiotic relationships of the microbial composition. The symbiotic network density of the tobacco systems was lower than that of the gramineous systems, and network complexity was highest in the OT and BM treatment groups. The positive symbiosis effects of AT, BT, and AM were significantly greater than those of the other treatments. Previous studies have shown that environmental factors shape the structure and community of soil microbes [56]. pH and AK were positively correlated with the dominant bacteria Sphingomoda, Rhodanobacter, and MND1, and negatively correlated with Haliangium. Our results are similar to previous findings, which showed that the impacts of various soil factors on bacterial population composition were highly inconsistent, fluctuating among taxa [57]. In the fungal communities, TN, SOC, and AK were positively correlated with Cladosporium, Mortierella, and Humicola, respectively, and negatively correlated with pH, which is similar to previous results [58]. Mortierella is a slow-growing potassium strategy fungus that helps crops and mycorrhizal fungi acquire phosphorus [59]. Moreover, the Mantel test showed that the pH, AK, and TN significantly affected the diversity index and OTU number of the microbial communities.

Overall, different crop rotation systems play key roles in sustainable crop production and soil improvement. Nevertheless, as rotation has become an extremely common approach in current farmland production, soil samples from long-term continuous cropping systems were not collected for use as a control treatment to analyze the effects of different crop rotation systems on crop yield and soil improvement precisely. Consequently, the benefits of the different crop rotation systems in this study may have been overestimated. However, we should also recognize that crop rotation systems play an important role in the sustainability of agricultural production.

Conclusions

In this study, we showed that soil properties and soil microbial communities change under crop rotation diversification, and that crop types alter the abundance, diversity, and composition of soil microorganisms by altering soil properties. Although the microbial community composition types in the different crop rotation systems were similar, there were significant differences in microbial diversity and relative abundance, with pH and AK being the most important factors driving microbial community changes. Different crop rotation systems had different indicator taxa at the genus level. Network complexity and symbiotic relationships differed under different rotation treatments, and the network map density of the tobacco system was significantly lower than that of the gramineous system. In this study, the results indicate that soil properties and microbial communities are determined by crop type under rotational diversification, and the sustainability of crop production and soil health could be ensured by considering the crop combinations within the crop rotation systems. Moreover, understanding the driving factors behind changes in the soil microbial structure and adjusting agricultural practices such as fertilization can help maintain the stability of different crop rotations. Therefore, it is crucial to design optimal continuous crop combinations for the sustainable operation of agricultural ecosystems.
