Design and classify innovative antimicrobial and dual- and multi-functional peptides using generative artificial intelligence
Authors: Alexa Sowers, Jinge Wang, Gangqing Hu, Malcolm Xing, Bingyun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance is a growing global crisis, where antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have emerged as promising alternatives to conventional antibiotics due to their unique mechanisms of action. However, traditional peptide design approaches, especially when designing multi-functional peptides, are often costly and time-consuming. This study explores the potential of ChatGPT as a prompt-based tool used without additional fine-tuning, for the classification and design of peptides with varying degrees of functional capabilities. We optimized the AMP design prompts by incorporating relevant information and refining them to design multi-functional peptides. For peptide classification, representative examples of positive and negative sequences were sourced from databases specific to each functionality, and a small exploratory set (10 positive sequences and 10 negative sequences) from the respective databases were randomly selected. In this preliminary evaluation, ChatGPT designed single- and dual-functional peptides with a promising apparent performance (accuracy > 0.9) and a decreased performance for triple- and quadruple-functional peptides (accuracy of 0.8) based on in silico testing. Additionally, ChatGPT showed promising preliminary results in classifying peptides with antimicrobial, non-hemolytic, cell-penetrating, and anticancer properties. Incorporating in-context example sequences significantly improved its classification performance. This work provides a proof-of-concept study of using a generative artificial intelligence tool for classification and multi-functional peptide design. While experimental validation is required, these findings suggest that accessible, non-coding Large Language Model approaches may support early-stage in silico peptide discovery that may be used in combination with existing computational tools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49612-x.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR), often referred to as the “silent pandemic”1, is one of the most significant global challenges of the twenty-first century2–4. AMR is projected to cause 10 million deaths annually and a financial burden of $100 trillion dollars by 2050 if no effective solutions emerge5. However, the development of antibiotics has declined over the past 30 years, due to concerns about rapid resistance6,7. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have emerged as a promising alternative, as they naturally defend against pathogens and are less likely to induce resistance compared to conventional antibiotics8–10. They can suppress and disperse biofilms11,12, and have broad-spectrum activity13,14, and immunomodulatory properties15–18.

Synthetic AMPs have limited success in clinical use, specifically due to hemolytic properties. Hemolysis, the rupture of red blood cells19, is a major limitation with current AMPs, where nearly 70% of them have predicted hemolytic activity20. Modifications may be made to reduce hemolysis19. Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) have been used as delivery vehicles for transporting proteins and other drugs into intracellular targets21–25. Given that AMPs and CPPs share physicochemical properties, such as cationicity26, some peptides in nature have both features27,28, thereby increasing their therapeutic potential. To reduce hemolysis, designing AMPs with neutral or slightly charged peptide sequences may be more effective20. Designing peptides with both AMP and non-hemolytic peptide (NHP) features enhances antimicrobial activity while reducing hemolysis, thereby enhancing their potential for therapeutic applications. Immunocompromised cancer patients are vulnerable to drug-resistant strains of bacteria4,29, making sequences with anticancer and antimicrobial properties a valuable approach. Meanwhile, incorporating dual and multiple functionalities into a single peptide enhances marketability, is cost-effective, reduces side effects30, and minimizes the need for multiple treatments31. However, designing multi-functional peptides is challenging. Current approaches focus on fusing peptides31–33 or amino acid substitution34,35, requiring time-consuming screening processes and often resulting in larger sequences36. Artificial intelligence (AI) can streamline this process by strategically designing multi-functional peptides in a single sequence.

AI is a powerful tool for drug development37. Large language models (LLMs) are trained on articles and books, use machine learning models, such as neural networks, to understand and perform a variety of tasks38. ChatGPT, a transformer-based neural network39 generates natural language responses through supervised and unsupervised learning for pre-training and fine-tuning to optimize the performance40. LLMs have been applied in biomedical research41–46, including classification of bioactive peptides such as antimicrobial and anticancer peptides44,47. Although prior studies have used LLMs for peptide classification and design, most have focused on fine-tuning or approaches requiring coding expertise, whereas prompt-based classification and design using the general ChatGPT user interface remain less explored.

Building on this foundation, we explore the use of a pretrained LLM, ChatGPT, without additional fine-tuning, as a prompt-based tool accessed via ChatGPT’s user interface for peptide classification and design. In contrast to other LLM approaches, this work avoids fine-tuning and coding requirements, providing a proof-of-concept study for evaluating peptide sequence classification and generation while prioritizing accessibility for non-coding users. Furthermore, we investigate the design of single-, dual-, and multi-functional peptides, which present additional challenges due to the potential for conflicting physicochemical constraints within a single sequence.

This study evaluates ChatGPT’s ability to classify bioactive peptides, including AMPs, NHPs, CPPs, and anticancer peptides (ACPs), and assesses its feasibility for designing peptides with increasing functionality from single- to multi-functional sequences. The generated peptides were evaluated using well-established machine learning predictors. Overall, our work serves as an exploratory study that investigates how prompt-based LLMs may be used to support early-stage peptide design before experimental validation.

Materials and methods

ChatGPT was used to design and classify peptides with various functions including AMP, NHP, CPP, and ACP, as well as dual- and multi-functional peptides. The designed peptides were validated, and the experimental design is detailed in Fig. 1. The process involved optimizing prompts using prompt engineering and in-context training sequences. Most prompts were analyzed using ChatGPT 4.0, except for a few specified with versions 3.5 or 4o. During our experiments, ChatGPT 4o was released, prompting the evaluation of different versions. Sequences for in-context training in peptide design and classification were collected from well-known databases, selected randomly using a number generator to avoid bias. Designed AMPs, CPPs, and ACPs were validated using peptide classification tools48–51, where scores above 0.5 indicated antimicrobial, cell-penetrating, or anticancer properties. NHPs were similarly validated49, where the original classifier outputs a hemolytic probability in which scores above 0.5 indicate hemolytic activity. To repurpose this, NHP classification probability was determined by taking the inverse probability by subtracting the hemolytic score from 1. Using this approach, peptides with the adjusted score of > 0.5 were interpreted as having non-hemolytic properties. The selection of the prompt information (i.e., 2 positive and 2 negative training sequences, 30 amino acids, and 10 training sequences) for ACPs, CPPs, and NHPs was based on the results from Fig. 2A–L. Datasets used for peptide classification

Antimicrobial peptides

The peptide sequences used for training ChatGPT to classify AMPs were sourced from the APD352, which contains experimentally validated peptides. Since there are no known databases that report non-AMPs, a negative dataset from the sequences used to train the Antimicrobial Peptide Scanner vr.2 (AMP Scanner)48 was used. These negative sequences were compiled by downloading data from UniProt53 and removing sequences containing the keywords: antimicrobial, antibiotic, antiviral, antifungal, effector, or excreted48. We randomly selected 10 positive sequences from APD3 and 10 negative sequences from AMP Scanner for testing. Additional sequences from these sources were used to design the prompt, including 1–15 randomly selected sequences to test the role of the number of training examples.

Anticancer peptides

For ACP classification, positive training sequences were selected from CancerPPD54, a database of experimentally validated ACPs. For classification, 10 sequences that are 30 amino acids long were randomly selected from the CancerPPD database. Negative training sequences were selected from a negative dataset for AntiCP 2.0, a model trained on positive and negative ACPs55. This negative dataset was sourced from databases including ACP-DL56, ACPP57, ACPred-FL58, AntiCP55, and iACP59 which did not show any anticancer properties55. From this negative dataset, 10 sequences at 30 amino acids in length were randomly selected for testing. Additionally, 2 positive and 2 negative sequences were randomly selected for in-context training for ACP classification.

Cell-penetrating peptides

CPP classification was performed using 10 randomly selected positive and 10 negative sequences, each 30 amino acids long, from the AiCPP dataset60. This dataset was curated from known CPP sequences in sources, including CellPPD61, MLCPP62, CPPsite63, and other publications53,64,65, and refined by removing redundant sequences or non-amino acid sequences. Negative sequences were collected from the same sources to compile sequences that do not have CPP activity60. For in-context training, 2 positive and 2 negative sequences were randomly selected.

Non-hemolytic peptides

The dataset for positive and negative testing and training was selected from the dataset used to train HemoNet, a machine learning model that classifies hemolytic activity66. This dataset was curated from two databases: DBAASP67 and Hemolytik68. Peptides with a minimum hemolytic concentration (MHC) ≥ 100 µM were considered as non-hemolytic, whereas those with an MHC ≤ 50 µM were hemolytic66. From this positive and negative datasets, 10 sequences with a length of 30 amino acids were randomly selected for testing. For NHP classification with training, 2 positive and 2 negative sequences were randomly selected from these datasets.

Prompts

All peptide design and classification experiments using different prompts were performed using ChatGPT (version 4.0, accessed on January 2024) via the standard web interface. Prompts were optimized using common prompt engineering strategies, and performance was measured using appropriate metrics for peptide design or classification tasks. All prompt examples are available in the supplementary materials (Figs. S1-S12).

Design of AMPs

The prompts for AMP design were optimized by incorporating varied information about AMPs. Starting with a basic prompt that included general information, it was modified to include more specific AMP knowledge (specific prompt) and further divided into tasks in a designated section (task-driven prompt). The most detailed version incorporated both specific information about AMPs and a designated task section (informed task prompt). Examples of these prompts are explained in Fig. S1A-D. Additional modifications included assigning ChatGPT the role of an expert peptide designer (role prompt), adding positive and negative AMPs examples (few shot), and providing rationales for the training examples (few-shot with rationale), as illustrated in Fig. S2A-D. Using the basic prompt as a baseline, training sequences were incorporated, including positive, negative, and mixed examples (Fig. S3A-D). The prompts were further modified to incorporate a range of 2 to 20 training sequences to determine the optimal AMP design conditions (Fig. S4A-D), and sequences with lengths from 10 to 40 amino acids in length (Fig. S5A-D).

Classification of AMPs

The prompts for AMP classification were similar to those used for designing AMPs but modified. The baseline prompt for classifying AMPs included minimal information without training data (zero-shot prompt). This was modified by assigning ChatGPT the role of an expert in peptide design (role prompting). More information was added by incorporating positive and negative training sequences (few-shot prompt) which was further modified to include rationales for each sequence (few-shot with rationale prompt), providing more detail on why a sequence was positive or negative. These prompt details are in Fig. S6A-D. ChatGPT’s classification ability with and without AMP in the prompt and training sequences is illustrated in Fig. S7A-B. The role of incorporating only positive, only negative, or both types of training sequences is outlined in Fig. S8A-D. Given the high scores of only negative sequences, the number of training examples was evaluated for ChatGPT classification efficiency, including a range from no training, 1 training sequence, to 15 training sequences in the prompt, as detailed in Fig. S9A-D. The prompts were also modified by using 5 training sequences and varying AMP lengths (10–40 amino acids), as shown in Fig. S10A-D.

Benchmark classification of AMPs

The benchmark dataset consisted of 500 experimentally validated AMP sequences and 500 non-AMP sequences. Positive and negative examples of AMPs were selected using the same criteria described in Section "Antimicrobial Peptides". All sequences were selected between 15 and 25 amino acids in length. Classification was performed using the few-shot with rationale prompt incorporating both positive and negative examples using GPT-4o, as detailed in Fig. S11. This was selected based on its performance in development studies.

To quantify uncertainty, bootstrap resampling was performed by randomly sampling the dataset with 1000 iterations. For each resample, the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles were used to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the F1 score and Matthew correlation coefficient (MCC). To assess run-to-run variability from stochastic nature of LLMs, this benchmark set was repeated across five independent runs. Performance metrics including precision, recall, specificity, and accuracy were calculated for each run, and the mean and standard deviation across runs were reported.

Design and classification of peptides with a single function other than antimicrobial property

The prompt demonstrating the highest performance for designing AMPs (informed task prompt) was used as a baseline for designing peptides with other functions. The prompts for designing AMPs focused on incorporating information important for an AMP, such as cationic and amphipathic characteristics, which is shown in Fig. S12A. This prompt was modified to include specific information like a high proportion of basic residues and a moderate length (Fig. S12B) to design CPPs, avoiding excess hydrophobicity and high positive charges (Fig. S12C) to design NHPs, and having sequences that can induce apoptosis (Fig. S12D) to design ACPs.

For classifying CPPs, NHPs, or ACPs, given that the positive and negative training sequences had the best performance for the classification of ACPs, four training sequences (2 positive and 2 negative) were used for each prompt. Training and testing sequences for peptides with other functions were 30 amino acids in length, which was optimized based on AMP classification. The ability of ChatGPT to classify CPPs, NHPs, and ACPs with and without training sequences was evaluated (Fig. S13A-C).

Design of peptides with dual- and multi-functions

For dual-function peptide prompts, conditions evaluated included combinations of AMP and NHP, AMP and CPP, and AMP and ACP. The design prompt focused on incorporating specific information about each combination (Fig. S14A). The triple-functional prompt included three unique properties for each function. Conditions for triple-functional peptides involved combinations of functions: AMP, NHP, and CPP; AMP, NHP, and ACP; and AMP, CPP, and ACP, with an example shown in Fig. S14B. The prompt for quadruple functional peptide design prompt is explained in Fig. S14C.

Homology analysis of designed sequences

Generated peptide sequences were screened for similarity against sequences in both CAMPR472 database and Swiss-Prot database using BLASTp. For designed peptides with other functions, similarity was also screened against known NHPs using Hemolytik database68, CPPs using CPPsite 2.0 database63, and ACPs using CancerPPD 2.0 database54. Default parameters were used for the BLAST against each database. Due to the short length of the peptide sequences, identity and coverage percentages were also used as comparative indicators for homology rather than strict thresholds for novelty.

Results

Design of AMPs using ChatGPT

ChatGPT was explored in the design of AMPs. Peptide design was optimized via prompt engineering and in-context training, and the designed AMP sequences were evaluated computationally using multiple classification tools reported in literature. Overall, these results should be interpreted within the context of a computationally validated set of designed sequences that serve as an exploratory evaluation of ChatGPT’s performance in AMP design, which would further require experimental validation.

Prompt engineering

We evaluated the impact of different prompt engineering strategies on ChatGPT’s ability to design AMPs. Using a basic prompt as a baseline, the generated sequences demonstrated the broadest distribution of predicted AMP probability scores and the lowest accuracy (Fig. 2A, D). Sequences generated in one trial were 10 amino acids long, with 3 out of 5 predicted incorrectly by the computational prediction tool (Table 1). Incorporating specific knowledge (specific prompt) improved the distribution and accuracy of probability scores when compared to the basic prompt (Fig. 2A, D). The generated sequences remained only 10 amino acids in length with more repetition in the sequences, and 2 out of 5 were predicted incorrectly in the trial (Table 1). Separating tasks into a designated section (task-driven prompt) improved the predicted probability distribution, outperforming both basic and specific prompts (Fig. 2A) and exhibiting an improvement in accuracy (1.0), when compared to the basic prompt (Fig. 2D). The informed task prompt exhibited the most uniform probability distribution, with an average probability score of 0.99 and an accuracy of 1.0 (Fig. 2A, 1D), generating sequences of varying lengths, all correctly identified by the computational predictor tool in that trial (Table 1). Incorporating prompt engineering techniques, such as assigning ChatGPT the role as an expert peptide designer (role prompt), improved the predicted AMP probability score distribution, with slightly improved accuracy, trending toward significance (p = 0.50) (Fig. 2B, 2E), when compared to the basic prompt. Including a few in-context training sequences (few-shot) improved the distribution (average probability score of 0.72) compared to the basic prompt (Fig. 2B) but had a slightly reduced distribution and accuracy when compared to the role prompt (Fig. 2E). Adding rationales to the few-shot prompt (few-shot with rationale prompt) further improved the probability score distribution, achieving the highest average probability score of 0.99 (Fig. 2B) and an accuracy of 1.0 (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that the few-shot with rationale prompt exhibited the best distribution and highest average probability score, indicating the importance of rationales for a more robust design of AMPs.

In-context training

We also evaluated the impact of different training sequences compared to the basic prompt containing no training sequences. Including only positive training sequences slightly improved distribution and accuracy (Fig. 2C, F), but the differences were not statistically significant compared to the basic prompt. Including only negative training sequences improved the predicted probability distribution with an average probability score of 0.70 (Fig. 2C) compared to the basic prompt. This prompt also demonstrated a slightly improved average accuracy of 0.68 (Fig. 2F) compared to the basic prompt and positive training-only prompt. Incorporating both positive and negative training sequences resulted in the highest average probability score of 0.72 (Fig. 2C) and accuracy of 0.76 (Fig. 2F). These results suggest that including both positive and negative sequences is recommended and may improve the model’s ability to differentiate between positive and negative AMP sequences.

Since the prompt containing both positive and negative sequences had the strongest performance in the previous analyses, we evaluated the effect of the number of training sequences. Using the smallest training set of two sequences (1 positive and 1 negative) had a significantly improved average probability score of 0.96 (Fig. 2G) and accuracy of 0.96 (Fig. 2J) compared to the basic prompt. Increasing the number of training sequences (10 and 20) resulted in a slightly worse performance, with average probability scores of 0.84 and 0.79 (Fig. 2G), respectively and a slight decrease in accuracy (Fig. 2J).

Using two training sequences and a combination of positive and negative sequences, we assessed the role of training sequence length. Since AMPs are typically between 10 and 50 amino acids in length69, we selected sequences within this range for evaluation. In-context training sequences of 20 and 30 amino acids in length produced the highest average AMP probability scores of 0.96 and 0.95 (Fig. 2H), respectively, both achieving the highest accuracy of 0.96 (Fig. 2K). In contrast, incorporating shorter (10 amino acids) or longer (40 amino acids) sequences resulted in decreased performance with an average AMP probability score of 0.82 and 0.76, respectively (Fig. 2H). Specifically, sequences that are 40 amino acids in length demonstrated significantly worse performance in average probability scores than sequences with 20 and 30 amino acids. These findings suggest that using in-context training sequences of 20 or 30 amino acids in length may be more effective for designing AMPs for this approach.

To evaluate a different ChatGPT version, we used the informed task prompt from Fig. 2A, 2D, which demonstrated the strongest performance across all parameters in the previous analyses. The newer versions of ChatGPT (GPT-4.0 and GPT-4o) demonstrated improved distribution and accuracy compared to the older version (GPT-3.5) (Fig. 2I–L). GPT-4o had the best performance with an average AMP probability score of 1.0 (Fig. 2I) and an accuracy of 1.0 (Fig. 2L). GPT-4.0 also performed well, with an average AMP probability score of 0.99 (Fig. 2I) and an accuracy of 1.0 (Fig. 2L). While these findings are exploratory, they suggest that newer versions (GPT-4.0 and GPT-4o), offer advantages over older models, consistent with the model’s updated knowledge and training.

Novelty analysis

To assess the novelty and similarity of generated peptides under different prompt strategies, all sequences were compared against the CAMPR4 database using BLAST, and the best hit (lowest E-value) for each sequence was used for identity and coverage ranges (Table 2). The basic prompt had a higher percentage of sequences with no detectable hits in the CAMPR4 database (80%), indicating different sequences from known AMPs. In contrast, more structured prompts and newer model versions, such as informed task prompt and GPT-4o, showed lower no-hit rates. This trend was consistent across similarity metrics, with structured prompts generating sequences with lower minimum E-values and higher identity and coverage ranges, reflecting closer alignment with AMPs. Prompt optimization and selection for AMP classification

Our study evaluated multiple prompting strategies and various versions of ChatGPT to identify an effective approach for classifying AMPs using the ChatGPT interface without additional fine-tuning. These comparisons were conducted on a small classification set (10 positive and 10 negative sequences) and were used to guide prompt optimization and selection for further analysis. Due to the limited size of this development set, performance metrics and statistical comparisons were used to support prompt selection rather than providing classification performance. Final classification performance was evaluated on a larger independent benchmark dataset using the prompting strategy based on initial screening of prompt performance.

Prompt engineering

We assessed the impact of different prompt engineering techniques on ChatGPT’s ability to classify AMPs (Fig. 3A, B). The zero-shot prompt was used as a baseline, achieving one of the lowest F1 scores at 0.76. Assigning ChatGPT the role of an expert peptide designer (role prompting) slightly lowered the average F1 score to 0.74 (Fig. 3A). Across additional performance metrics, role prompting performed similarly to or slightly worse than the zero-shot prompt, with comparable scores in recall, with non-significant decreases in specificity, precision, and accuracy (Table S7). Incorporating training sequences, as with the few-shot and few-shot with rationale prompts, significantly improved F1 scores compared to the two prompts without in-context training (Fig. 3A). The few-shot prompt increased recall compared to both zero-shot and role prompts and significantly improved accuracy compared to the role prompt, with no significant differences in specificity and precision compared to both zero-shot and role prompts (Table S7). The few-shot with rationale prompt achieved the highest F1 score of 0.83 (Fig. 3A), significantly outperforming the zero-shot and role prompts while demonstrating comparable scores with the few-shot prompt. The role of incorporating the term AMP into the prompt is important for improving ChatGPT’s classification and accuracy. As shown in Fig. 3B, including no AMP with in-context training had higher F1 scores compared to no AMP in the prompt without in-context training, though the difference was not statistically significant. Including the term AMP without in-context training significantly increased F1 scores compared to the prompt with no AMP and no training. Incorporating the term AMP in the prompt with in-context training further improved F1 scores compared to both no training with or without AMP in the prompt and higher (not statistically different) F1 scores compared to no AMP in the prompt with in-context training. Across all parameters, the lowest performance was observed for the prompt with no AMP without in-context training (Table S7). Including the term AMP without in-context training significantly improved recall with slightly improved parameters compared to the prompt with no AMP without in-context training (Table S7). Incorporating the term AMP with in-context training sequences in the prompt performed the best across all parameters except for specificity, showing significantly improved recall when compared to the prompt with AMP without in-context training and no AMP without in-context training. There was also a significant improvement in accuracy when compared to the prompt with no AMP without in-context training (Table S7). Overall, these findings suggest that including the term AMP in the prompt with in-context training may enhance AMP classification in this study.

In-context training

The ability of ChatGPT in classify AMPs based on in-context training was analyzed by evaluating the impact of the type, number, and length of example sequences (Fig. 3C–F). The baseline prompt without training sequences showed the lowest F1 score of 0.76 (Fig. 3C). Including only positive training examples increased the F1 score to 0.80, but this difference was not statistically different from the prompt without training sequences (Fig. 3C). Including only positive training sequences performed slightly worse on the other parameters except recall and accuracy, with a significantly improved recall when compared to the prompt with no training sequences (Table S8). Including only negative examples significantly increased the F1 score to 0.81 and exhibited the best performance across the other parameters except for recall compared to the prompt with no training sequences (Fig. 3C). Moreover, this prompt demonstrated significantly improved specificity and precision with a significant decrease in recall compared to the prompt with only positive training sequences (Table S8). Including both positive and negative training examples resulted in an F1 score of 0.83, which was significantly improved compared to the prompt with no training sequences (Fig. 3C). Although the prompt containing both positive and negative training examples had the best overall performance, the specificity and precision were lower compared to the prompt containing only negative training examples. Additionally, incorporating both positive and negative training examples in the prompt significantly improved recall score compared to the prompt with no training (Table S8).

The baseline prompt without training sequences performed the worst with the lowest F1 score of 0.76 (Fig. 3D) and performed the worst across all parameters (Table S8). Including a single training sequence showed slight improves across all the parameters compared to the baseline, but these changes were not statistically significant (Fig. 3D, Table S8). Incorporating five training sequences significantly improved the F1 score to 0.81 when compared to the prompt without training (Fig. 3D), achieving the highest scores in specificity and precision (Table S8). Incorporating 10 training sequences exhibited the best performance with an F1 score of 0.83, which was significantly improved when compared to the prompts without training and one training sequence (Fig. 3D). This prompt also achieved the highest recall and accuracy scores, with significantly improved accuracy when compared to the prompt without training (Table S8). Including 15 training sequences slightly decreased performance with an F1 score of 0.80 (Fig. 3D). and slight improvements in specificity and precision, but lower recall and accuracy compared to the prompt with 10 training sequences.

The effect of AMP length (10 to 40 amino acids) was assessed without training sequences. AMPs with 10 amino acids in length had the lowest F1 score (0.71) (Fig. 3E) and performed the worst in recall and accuracy, while achieving the highest specificity and precision (Table S8). Increasing AMP length to 20 amino acids slightly improved F1 score, recall, and accuracy, but decreased specificity and precision compared to the prompt with AMPs 10 amino acids in length (Fig. 3E, Table S8). The prompt with AMPs with 30 amino acids in length had the best performance with an F1 score of 0.84, which was significantly improved when compared to the prompt containing AMPs with 10 amino acids (Fig. 3E). This also achieved the highest recall score but the lowest scores in specificity and precision, with significant decreases in specificity when compared to AMPs that are 10 amino acids in length. However, this same prompt achieved significantly higher recall scores when compared to prompts with AMPs that are 10 and 20 amino acids in length (Table S8). Sequences that were 40 amino acids in length performed similarly to AMPs with 30 amino acids when comparing F1 scores. (Fig. 3E). This prompt exhibited slightly improved scores in specificity, precision, and accuracy but had a lower score in recall, but none of these changes were statistically significant.

Different versions of ChatGPT were evaluated for the classification efficiency of AMPs. GPT3.5 performed the worst with an F1 score of 0.54 (Fig. 3F). and the lowest scores across all parameters (Table S8). GPT-4.0 significantly improved performance with an F1 score of 0.84 (Fig. 3F), outperforming GPT-3.5 in all parameters. Specifically, this prompt showed a significant increase in both recall and accuracy compared to GPT-3.5 (Table S8). The most recent version, GPT-4o, achieved the best performance with an F1 score of 0.94, which was significantly improved from GPT3.5 (Fig. 3F). GPT-4o not only scored the highest across all parameters, with each > 0.88, but also showed a significant improvement in recall and accuracy when compared to both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.0 versions (Table S8).

Discussion

Conventional peptide design focuses on rational methods like template-based, physiochemical approaches, and de novo techniques, requiring extensive knowledge and time-consuming screening70,71. AI integration offers a novel approach to reduce the labor-intensive tasks associated with conventional peptide design. Designing and classifying peptides requires an understanding of features important for therapeutic activity. Our evaluation showed ChatGPT was able to classify peptides (AMP, CPP, NHP, and ACP), achieving high F1 scores. Additionally, ChatGPT designed new sequences that were predicted to have biological activity based on computational validation tools.

Machine learning has advanced peptide development by recognizing sequence patterns and trends, but requires large datasets for classification models. Most of these machine learning models focus on using training sequences to design a classification software for AMPs48,67,72, CPPs50,61,64,73, NHPs49,74, and ACPs51,56–59. Although these classification software tools are effective, they may have limited input and output responses, and a large amount of coding expertise is required to develop these neural networks. LLMs, like ChatGPT, were pre-trained extensively on widely available information, where the model can solve a variety of tasks, exceeding the abilities of other machine learning models75. ChatGPT can also explain the rationale behind the classification through different prompting techniques, which provides researchers with a greater understanding of how the algorithm makes various classifications76. Although there have been a few articles focused on using ChatGPT to classify AMPs47,75, NHPs47, and ACPs75, these models required fine-tuning using a large amount of sequences for training. However, the majority of functions like wound-healing and anti-inflammatory properties do not have a large number of available databases like AMPs; therefore, it is important to discover new ways to overcome this lack of available sequences for classifying these niche properties. To the best of our knowledge, ChatGPT has not been utilized to design and classify peptides with dual or multiple biological functions.

ChatGPT generated peptide sequences with predicted dual- and multi-functional properties based on in silico models. The informed task prompt performed the best when designing AMPs (Fig. 2A, 2D), suggesting that organizing the tasks into numbered lists enhances ChatGPT’s understanding. Including a few positive and negative sequences showed further improvements in AMP design (Fig. 2C, 2F), likely due to providing ChatGPT with a distinction between positive and negative sequences. We discovered that only using a few training sequences was necessary, while too many sequences decreased performance, potentially due to conflicting patterns (Fig. 2G, J). For optimal peptide design, we recommend either the informed task or the few-shot with rationale prompt depending on the specific requirements and available information required. In situations where there are limited sequences available, using the informed task prompt is more feasible.

Short AMPs are generally characterized by cationic charge and an amphipathic structure. Due to these shared features and their short length, some sequence similarity is expected. Generating AMP sequences using prompt optimization, as well as sequences with other functions (NHP, CPP, and ACP) and multi-functional sequences, most sequences had E-values > 10–3 (Tables S1-6 and S10-12). Although the basic prompt generated peptides with the lowest similarity to known AMPs in the CAMPR4 database (Table 2), it exhibited the worst performance with broad probability scores and low accuracy (Fig. 2). This supports the observation that sequences lacking AMP features tend to exhibit broad identity and coverage ranges. In contrast, the informed task prompt and GPT-4o generated sequences with higher similarity and coverage relative to known AMPs (Table 2), consistent with its improved probability score distributions (Fig. 2). These results indicate that higher predicted activity is associated with features common to AMPs and prompt strategies that generate sequences that resemble established antimicrobial sequences. Although a small number of sequences exhibited 100% identity and coverage, the majority displayed broad identity and coverage ranges, suggesting that the model generates a diverse set of sequences rather than reproducing sequences from known AMP databases. Although these sequences have high prediction probability with most sequences showing moderate similarity, experimental validation is required to confirm functionality. Overall, novelty in this study is interpreted from short peptide sequences, where partial homology is expected due the length and characteristics of AMPs. BLAST-based similarity, including E-values, identity, and coverage are used for comparative indicators rather than strict novelty thresholds.

ChatGPT effectively classified peptides, with the few-shot with rationale and few-shot prompts achieving high AMP classification performance (Fig. 3A). The few-shot prompt is ideal when information is limited or simplicity is preferred. Including AMP in the prompt significantly improved the accuracy of classifying AMPs, especially with in-context training (Fig. 3B). While 10 training sequences performed the best, five sequences were also effective (Fig. 3D), making it a promising alternative when there are limited sequences available. AMPs of 30 and 40 amino acids had the best classification performance (Fig. 3E), suggesting that these lengths are optimal for classifying AMPs. Newer versions of ChatGPT (4.0 and 4o) showed improved performance (Fig. 3F), indicating that the newer versions can understand and classify AMPs with high accuracy. Expanding our research to include peptides with functions other than antimicrobial properties provides a greater understanding of ChatGPT’s versatility in classifying and designing unique peptides with important biological functions. ChatGPT was effective at designing and classifying peptides with individual functions, including AMPs, CPPs, NHPs, and ACPs. When classifying the properties of peptides with various functions, incorporating in-context training sequences in the prompt significantly improved the overall performance in NHPs and ACPs with higher F1 scores (Fig. 5).

The expanded benchmark provides a more reliable assessment of ChatGPT’s ability to classify AMPs than initial development experiments by evaluating the selected prompting strategy on a large dataset. Using 500 experimentally validated AMP sequences and 500 non-AMP sequences, ChatGPT achieved a high classification performance, as reflected by a strong F1 score and MCC when applied to a large and balanced dataset. Additionally, uncertainty estimates from bootstrap resampling had a low 95% CI for F1 and MCC, suggesting stable behavior across resampled datasets (Table 4). The relatively low run-to-run variability observed for precision, recall, and accuracy (Table 5) supports the reproducibility of this model despite the inherent variability associated with LLMs.

While multi-functional peptides offer advantages, rational design poses challenges, including increased sequence length, conflicting physiochemical requirements, and inactivity77. Designing peptides, especially short AMPs, with multiple functionalities requires simultaneously satisfying multiple and sometimes competing functional requirements, such as charge distribution, hydrophobicity, amphipathicity, and structural conformations associated with peptides with various functions. As a result, optimizing a sequence for one function may compromise another, which likely contributes to the reduced performance observed in dual- and multi-functional peptides. Our approach leverages ChatGPT’s interface to design AMPs with multiple functions, which was computationally validated using classification tools. Peptides with two or more functions showed slightly decreased accuracy compared to single-function peptides (Fig. 6F). This trend reflects the challenges associated with designing peptides with multiple functions in both rational design and LLM design. Future improvements may focus on optimizing prompts for multi-functionality and integrating LLMs with peptide-specific AI platforms to balance competing functional constraints. While the performance for dual- and multi-functional peptides was slightly lower, the results support the computational feasibility of using ChatGPT-generated sequences as candidates for future investigation. Future directions involve experimental validation of these sequences through in vitro and in vivo studies. For the classification capability of ChatGPT, using more diverse training datasets could improve the accuracy of utilizing ChatGPT to classify peptides with various biological functions.

Outputs from ChatGPT can vary between independent runs due to the inherent variability within the model. Our study focused on using ChatGPT’s user interface without additional fine-tuning, so reproducibility of individual sequences or results is not guaranteed. To address this concern, an expanded benchmark was evaluated to further support the reliability of the approach between various runs, which included an average of multiple sequences over five trials, focusing on trends in the model as opposed to single results. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates ChatGPT’s ability to design and classify peptide sequences, while addressing the expected variability observed with prompt-based LLMs.

Limitations to our applications of ChatGPT for peptide design and classification include relying on its interface rather than fine-tuning the model with extensive training data. While the interface is useful in cases when datasets are not available, fine-tuning the model could improve accuracy. As with other LLMs, ChatGPT may generate potential sequences without experimental validation, highlighting the risk of hallucinations. Additionally, our evaluation focused on computational techniques, relying on classification tools that do not fully capture the complexity and pharmacokinetics of how peptides behave in vivo. Experimental validation for determining biological properties is required in future studies. In addition, the accuracy of classifying peptides is dependent on the algorithms and types of sequences used to train ChatGPT, which may not fully represent rare peptide sequences that do not behave similarly.

Conclusions

This study explored ChatGPT’s feasibility in designing and classifying peptides with single, dual- and multi-functions. Incorporating multiple functions (AMP, NHP, CPP, ACP) into a single peptide sequence offers a promising, cost-effective alternative to traditional methods. Starting with AMPs and gradually adding functions could contribute to the development of therapeutic candidates for reducing infections, pending experimental validation. Based on in silico validation, this study provides early evidence supporting the feasibility of applying ChatGPT to generate peptide sequences with dual- and multi-functional peptides. Using one tool for peptide design and classification reduces the complexity associated with designing separate models.

For peptide design, our findings suggest using the informed task prompt and newer ChatGPT versions, including specific characteristics for the type of designed peptides. Use of training sequences is recommended in applications where there is limited knowledge of the peptide characteristics, and in such cases, a combination of positive and negative training sequences along with rationales should be incorporated. To classify peptides, using positive and negative sequences, the few-shot prompt, and a newer version of ChatGPT are recommended. The few-shot with rationale prompt is recommended in scenarios where there is sufficient information about a peptide’s characteristics. It is also recommended to include the term “AMP,” which enhances the contextual understanding, in combination with training sequences in the prompt when classifying AMPs.

These findings are exploratory with potential limitations including limited datasets, in vivo functionality of designed sequences, and risk of hallucinations associated with AI models, including LLMs. Experimental validation is required to confirm the properties and multi-functionality of the designed sequences.
