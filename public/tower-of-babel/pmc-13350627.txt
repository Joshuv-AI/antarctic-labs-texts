Infections in Burn Patients: Pathophysiology, Prevention, and Contemporary Therapeutic Strategies in the Era of Antimicrobial Resistance
Authors: Ana Cătălina Țânțu, Monica Marilena Țânțu, Corneliu Ovidiu Vrancianu, Andreea-Mihaela Sandu, Roxana-Elena Cristian, Marian Constantin, Alina Păunescu, Daniel Diaconescu, Elena Rodica Dragu, Mădălina Olivia Adameșteanu, Bogdan Chioaru, Sorin Cristian Hariga, Cristian Radu Jecan, Ionică Daniel Vîlcea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurn injuries are associated with significant morbidity and mortality, largely driven by infectious complications. Disruption of the skin barrier, systemic inflammation, and postburn immunosuppression promote microbial colonization and progression to local and systemic infections, a challenge further exacerbated by the increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDRO). Patients with extensive burns, inhalation injury, prolonged hospitalization, and invasive device use are particularly vulnerable. The microbiological profile of burn wound infections evolves dynamically, shifting from early Gram-positive predominance to Gram-negative and multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, including members of the ESKAPE group, in later stages. Effective management, therefore, requires an integrated approach combining early surgical intervention, strict infection control measures, continuous microbiological surveillance, and targeted antimicrobial therapy. This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on the pathophysiology, risk factors, and microbiological dynamics of burn-associated infections, alongside contemporary prevention and treatment strategies, including topical therapies, advanced biomaterials, systemic antibiotic approaches, and antimicrobial stewardship. Emerging strategies such as nanoparticle-based systems and artificial intelligence-driven predictive models highlight a shift toward precision medicine in burn care; however, their clinical translation remains limited by insufficient validation and standardization. In conclusion, optimizing outcomes in burn-associated infections will depend on integrating evidence-based clinical practices with innovative technologies, while reinforcing antimicrobial stewardship and developing validated predictive tools to address the growing burden of antimicrobial resistance.Keywords: Burns, Burn wound infection, Antimicrobial resistance, ESKAPE pathogens, Infection prevention, Burn patient care

Introduction

Burns represent a severe form of trauma associated with major physiological disruption, prolonged hospitalization, and substantial psychological burden. Despite advances in burn care, burn injuries remain a significant global public health problem, contributing considerably to morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Although age-standardized incidence and mortality rates have declined, the absolute burden of burns is projected to increase, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, driven by population growth and demographic changes [2, 3]. According to the World Health Organization, burns rank among the leading causes of traumatic injury, with mortality rates exceeding 18% in critically ill patients and prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays frequently required [4]. Burn incidence and outcomes are strongly influenced by age, sex, socioeconomic status, and environmental exposure, with most injuries occurring in domestic settings [5].

Burn wound infections remain one of the leading causes of complications and death in burn patients. Their development is influenced by multiple factors, including burn size and depth, prolonged hospitalization, mechanical ventilation, and exposure to specialized burn units or ICUs [6]. The microbiological profile of burn wounds evolves over time, with early colonization commonly dominated by Gram-positive organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus, followed by increasing prevalence of Gram-negative pathogens, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii, particularly during prolonged hospitalization [7, 8]. In parallel, multi-drug resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) microorganisms have become increasingly common in burn units, complicating antimicrobial therapy and worsening clinical outcomes [9, 10]. The high susceptibility of burn patients to infection is largely driven by disruption of the skin barrier together with profound immune dysregulation induced by thermal injury [11–13]. Loss of epithelial integrity facilitates microbial adherence and invasion, while alterations in innate and adaptive immune responses promote persistent inflammation, dysbiosis, and impaired pathogen clearance. In addition, biofilm formation by pathogens such as P. aeruginosa contributes to chronic wound colonization, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and therapeutic failure [14, 15]. Consequently, healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) occur more frequently in burn patients than in most other hospitalized populations.

Burn-associated infections encompass a broad clinical spectrum, including wound infections, pneumonia, urinary tract infections, bloodstream infections, cellulitis, and sepsis [4, 16]. Infection risk increases with burn severity, particularly in patients with total body surface area (TBSA) involvement exceeding 20%, and is further amplified by advanced age, comorbidities, immunosuppression, and prolonged intensive care exposure [17, 18]. The infectious profile also changes during hospitalization, progressing from early wound colonization to later systemic and device-associated infections, often accompanied by increasing AMR [19–23].

Despite major advances in critical care, the diagnosis and management of burn wound infections remain challenging. Clinical manifestations frequently overlap with the systemic inflammatory response induced by burn injury, while conventional biomarkers often lack specificity in this setting. At the same time, the growing prevalence of MDR pathogens further complicates therapeutic decision-making. Although many studies have addressed individual aspects of burn-associated infections, an updated and integrated overview of their pathophysiology, microbiology, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment remains necessary.

Therefore, this review provides a comprehensive overview of burn wound infections, focusing on microbiological and clinical diagnosis, prevention strategies, antimicrobial management, and emerging therapeutic approaches, including advanced biomaterials and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted technologies.

Literature Search and Selection Criteria

A structured literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies on burn wound infections. Major databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect, were searched for articles published between 2016 and 2026. The search strategy combined keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms such as “burn wound infection,” “burn sepsis,” “multidrug-resistant organisms,” “ESKAPE pathogens,” “antimicrobial therapy,” “biofilm,” and “microbiome,” using Boolean operators (“AND,” “OR”).

Studies were included if they were available in full text, published in English, and directly relevant to the epidemiology, pathophysiology, microbiology, prevention, or management of infections in burn patients. Priority was given to international guidelines, meta-analyses, and high-quality clinical studies. Articles lacking sufficient data or not directly related to burn-associated infections were excluded.

Relevant studies were screened and selected on the basis of title, abstract, and full-text evaluation, as needed, and the extracted data were synthesized to provide an integrated overview of current evidence.

Pathophysiology of Burn Wound Infection

Burn Wound as a Dynamic Microenvironment

Although burn injury primarily affects the skin, its systemic consequences are profound and involve a complex interaction between inflammation, immune dysfunction, endothelial injury, and hypermetabolism. The magnitude of this response depends on several factors, including burn extent and depth, patient age, comorbidities, associated trauma, and preexisting toxic exposures such as chronic alcohol or drug use.

Thermal injury triggers an immediate inflammatory response aimed at tissue repair. In severe burns; however, disruption of the skin barrier together with the release of proinflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interferon gamma (IFN-γ) amplifies systemic inflammation and promotes the development of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) [24]. Hypovolemic shock, increased vascular permeability, generalized edema, and hypercatabolism further contribute to immune dysfunction and organ injury [25]. Consequently, patients frequently present with tachycardia, tachypnea, leukocytosis, and hyperthermia even in the absence of infection [26].

Recent transcriptomic studies indicate that severe burns induce an early hyperinflammatory state followed by rapid suppression of immune-related pathways, particularly in patients with burns involving > 20% TBSA [27]. This transition from immune activation to immune exhaustion appears to be a central mechanism underlying susceptibility to secondary infections and sepsis. In parallel, large-scale analyses have identified distinct immune endotypes involving both myeloid and lymphoid dysfunction, which correlate with disease severity and mortality and share similarities with the host-response patterns observed in trauma and sepsis [28].

Persistent inflammation, immunosuppression, and catabolism syndrome (PICS) has also emerged as an important contributor to adverse outcomes after severe burns. Clinical studies suggest that PICS is strongly associated with sepsis development, prolonged critical illness, and lymphopenia-related immune dysfunction [29]. At the same time, systemic inflammation and hypermetabolism increase the risk of sepsis and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), which remain major causes of mortality in burn patients [30].

Extensive tissue injury promotes the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including extracellular deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and inflammatory cytokines, which further disrupt immune homeostasis and endothelial integrity. Among these mediators, HMGB1 appears to play a central role in vascular leakage and distant organ injury, particularly at the pulmonary level [31]. The resulting imbalance between inflammatory activation and immune suppression compromises microbial clearance and favors progression toward invasive infection and sepsis [32]. Beyond classical inflammatory pathways, burn injury involves a broader network of immune, neural, endothelial, and coagulation responses triggered by both DAMPs and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) [33]. Simultaneously, increased production of suppressive mediators such as prostaglandin E2 contributes to a state of immunological vulnerability [19, 34]. Recent bioinformatics studies have also identified inflammatory regulators such as S100A8 as potential markers of disease severity and mortality, highlighting possible future targets for precision-based therapeutic approaches [35].

Experimental and translational evidence supports the concept of a biphasic immune response after severe burns, characterized by early neutrophil and monocyte hyperactivation followed by sustained lymphocyte depletion and impaired antibacterial defense [36]. Superimposed on the loss of the cutaneous barrier, these alterations markedly increase susceptibility to local and systemic infections.

Severe burns are also associated with a prolonged hypermetabolic state driven by catecholamines and stress hormones, resulting in increased energy expenditure, insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, muscle wasting, and persistent catabolism [19]. In some patients, this response may persist for months or even years after wound closure.

The diagnosis of sepsis in burn patients remains particularly difficult because infection-related manifestations overlap extensively with the baseline inflammatory response induced by thermal injury. To address this challenge, the American Burn Association proposed burn-specific diagnostic criteria, particularly for patients with burns exceeding 20% TBSA, who frequently fulfill conventional SIRS criteria even in the absence of infection [6]. Effective management of burn-associated infections therefore requires integration of both microbial factors and host-response dynamics, which ultimately determine progression toward sepsis and MODS [19, 30].

Transition From Colonization to Infection and Sepsis

Burn wound infection develops through a dynamic sequence of events involving microbial colonization, biofilm formation, local tissue invasion, and progression toward systemic infection. Partial- and full-thickness burns generate an avascular necrotic environment rich in proteins and cellular debris, which promotes microbial proliferation while simultaneously impairing tissue repair through reduced fibroblast growth factor and vascular endothelial growth factor activity [20, 37].

Microorganisms rapidly adhere to the wound surface and initiate biofilm formation through sequential stages of adhesion, aggregation, maturation, and dispersion [38]. Biofilms consist of microbial communities embedded within an extracellular matrix that protects pathogens from host immune responses, biocides, and antibiotics, allowing survival under hypoxic, nutrient-deprived, and fluctuating pH conditions [38]. This architecture contributes substantially to persistent infection, antimicrobial tolerance, and therapeutic failure, particularly in infections caused by multidrug-resistant organisms (MDRO). Bacteria embedded within biofilms exhibit reduced metabolic activity and may become 10- to 1000-fold less susceptible to topical and systemic antibiotics compared with planktonic forms [39–41].

Burn wound colonization is generally defined as the presence of microorganisms at low concentrations, typically below 105 colony-forming units, without overt cellulitis or invasive infection [19]. However, positive cultures alone do not necessarily indicate clinically significant infection, as microbial colonization is almost universal in burn wounds. Instead, progression toward infection is suggested by clinical changes such as increased exudate, expansion of perilesional erythema, edema, local temperature changes, malodor, necrosis, or discoloration of the wound surface [6, 18].

Recent evidence indicates that colonization should not be regarded as a passive microbiological state but rather as a dynamic precursor to invasive infection. A retrospective cohort study including 634 burn patients reported colonization in 27.3% of cases, with progression risk strongly associated with advanced age, larger TBSA, full-thickness burns, inhalation injury, and diabetes mellitus [42]. In parallel, pathogen-specific characteristics further influence this transition. For example, P. aeruginosa demonstrates particularly high biofilm-forming capacity, which is closely associated with AMR, persistence, and chronic wound infection [43].

The progression from colonization to invasive infection is influenced by multiple host- and treatment-related factors, including burn size and depth, timing of debridement, delayed removal of necrotic tissue, immune status, local tissue perfusion, and microbial virulence. Importantly, microbial burden alone does not fully predict clinical progression, as severely immunocompromised patients may harbor extensive colonization with limited inflammatory manifestations.

Nevertheless, these criteria may have limitations in critically ill patients, particularly in the presence of biofilm-associated infections and severe immune dysregulation. Emerging technologies such as fluorescence imaging have demonstrated improved sensitivity for detecting clinically significant bacterial burden compared with clinical assessment alone [45]. This point-of-care technique uses violet light-induced autofluorescence to enable real-time visualization of bacterial loads directly at the bedside. Recent studies further suggest that fluorescence-guided assessment may improve detection of bacterial hotspots, support targeted sampling and debridement, and optimize antimicrobial stewardship strategies in burn wound management [46–48]. However, current evidence remains limited by relatively small cohorts and heterogeneous study designs, highlighting the need for larger burn-specific validation studies.

Current evidence increasingly supports the concept that burn wound infection exists along a continuum ranging from contamination and colonization to invasive infection, sepsis, and multiple organ dysfunction [49]. Large epidemiological studies confirm that infection remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in burn patients, particularly during the early postburn period and in patients with extensive injuries [50]. At the same time, recent research agendas continue to highlight the need for standardized surveillance systems, improved diagnostic tools, and better integration of microbiome and biofilm research into clinical practice [51].

Overall, progression from colonization to infection in burn wounds reflects a complex interaction between microbial persistence, host immune dysfunction, tissue injury, and biofilm-associated resistance mechanisms. Rather than representing a discrete event, burn wound infection should be understood as a dynamic and evolving process that may ultimately culminate in sepsis and multiple organ dysfunction.

Microbiology and Epidemiology of Burn Wound Infections

In burn patients, most complications are caused by polymicrobial infections, with both the site and microbiological profile varying depending on the duration of hospitalization. During the first week, burn wound infections are predominantly caused by Gram-positive bacteria, with S. aureus being the most frequently identified pathogen.

As hospitalization progresses, there is a shift toward infections such as pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and bloodstream infections of hematogenous origin, with a predominance of Gram-negative bacteria. Respiratory tract infections are most commonly diagnosed after the first week of hospitalization [20, 25].

Gram-Positive Bacteria

Gram-positive bacteria predominate during the early stages of burn wound colonization and infection, particularly within the first week after injury [52, 53]. The most frequently implicated pathogens include S. aureus, Enterococcus spp., and β-hemolytic streptococci, with methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) increasingly reported in severe burn cases [4].

Among these organisms, S. aureus remains the most commonly isolated pathogen from burn wounds. Although it is part of the normal skin microbiota, disruption of the cutaneous barrier facilitates opportunistic invasion and dissemination. Its pathogenicity is driven by multiple virulence factors, including proteases, collagenases, and hyaluronidases, which promote tissue destruction and systemic spread, potentially leading to pneumonia, bacteremia, and sepsis [19]. Recent longitudinal studies further show that MRSA remains highly prevalent in burn units and is associated with distinct clonal lineages and toxin profiles linked to transmission persistence and adverse clinical outcomes [54].

Infections caused by Streptococcus spp. have become less common following the widespread use of penicillin, although group A Streptococcus (S. pyogenes) remains associated with graft failure and invasive soft tissue infection when present [4].

Enterococcus spp. also represent clinically relevant opportunistic pathogens in critically ill burn patients, particularly with the emergence of VRE strains. These infections are associated with substantial morbidity and limited therapeutic options, including linezolid or combination regimens involving ampicillin and aminoglycosides [4]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated significantly higher mortality rates in bloodstream infections caused by VRE compared with vancomycin-susceptible strains, highlighting the growing clinical burden of enterococcal resistance in healthcare settings [55].

Gram-Negative Bacteria

Gram-negative pathogens predominate during the later stages of burn wound infection and are strongly associated with biofilm formation, multidrug resistance, sepsis, and poor clinical outcomes. Among these organisms, P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii are the most frequently isolated pathogens in burn units [4, 56].

P. aeruginosa is particularly adapted to the burn wound environment because of its affinity for moist surfaces, metabolic versatility, and extensive repertoire of virulence factors [4, 56]. It is commonly implicated in burn wound infections, ventilator-associated pneumonia, bacteremia, and sepsis, with infections originating from both environmental and endogenous reservoirs, including the gastrointestinal tract. Clinically, superficial infections may present with characteristic yellow-green discoloration and a fruity odor, whereas invasive disease can progress to tissue necrosis and ecthyma gangrenosum, often requiring urgent surgical debridement [4].

The persistence of P. aeruginosa is largely driven by its capacity for biofilm formation and immune evasion. Multiple virulence determinants, including adhesins, alginate, exotoxins, proteases, and hemolysins, contribute to tissue destruction, systemic dissemination, and intrinsic AMR [57]. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) P. aeruginosa infections also tend to emerge later during hospitalization, increasing from approximately 8% during the first week to more than 50% after 28 days of hospitalization [58].

Biofilm formation further amplifies the pathogenic potential of Gram-negative organisms in burn wounds. Biofilms consist of structured microbial communities embedded within an extracellular polymeric matrix that limits antibiotic penetration, protects bacteria from host immune responses, and promotes persistent infection [59]. Recent studies demonstrated that burn-related physiological conditions may enhance biofilm formation and increase antimicrobial tolerance in pathogens such as A. baumannii [60]. In P. aeruginosa, biofilm-associated persistence is closely linked to quorum sensing, virulence regulation, and multidrug resistance, while emerging antibiofilm approaches targeting matrix degradation or quorum-quenching pathways have shown promising experimental results [61].

A. baumannii represents another major opportunistic pathogen in burn patients and is associated with pneumonia, wound infections, urinary tract infections, and prolonged ICU outbreaks [19]. Its remarkable environmental persistence, high transmissibility, and rapid acquisition of multidrug resistance make it particularly difficult to eradicate in burn care settings.

Other clinically relevant Gram-negative pathogens include members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., Enterobacter spp., Serratia spp., and Proteus spp., which are commonly associated with urinary tract infections and pneumonia [19]. Increasing resistance to aminoglycosides, cephalosporins, quinolones, and carbapenems further complicates antimicrobial management.

Less common but clinically significant pathogens include Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, particularly in mechanically ventilated patients or those with flame burns, where infection has been associated with increased morbidity and mortality [57]. In contrast, anaerobic infections are more frequently encountered in electrical burns, crush injuries, and deep burns involving the oral or perianal regions, where tissue necrosis favors the proliferation of organisms such as Clostridium, Bacteroides, Peptococcus, and Fusobacterium spp. [19, 57].

Overall, Gram-negative pathogens represent a major driver of late burn-associated infections, particularly in critically ill patients, where biofilm persistence and AMR substantially increase the risk of invasive infection, sepsis, and mortality.

Fungal Infections

Patients with severe burns are at high risk of invasive fungal infections, which typically occur after the second week following injury in the setting of progressive barrier disruption, immune dysfunction, prolonged ICU stay, and extensive antimicrobial exposure [58, 62]. These infections are associated with markedly worse outcomes, including increased risk of candidemia, prolonged hospitalization, sepsis, and mortality, which may be up to tenfold higher compared with noninfected patients [58]. Importantly, fungal colonization itself represents a critical intermediary stage, substantially increasing the likelihood of progression to invasive disease [62].

Fungal infections in burn patients frequently mimic bacterial infections, often resulting in delayed recognition and treatment [4]. Major risk factors include advanced age, burns involving 30–60% TBSA, full-thickness injuries, inhalation trauma, prolonged mechanical ventilation, delayed excision of necrotic tissue, central venous catheters, and prolonged intensive care exposure [58, 62]. The use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and topical antimicrobials further promotes fungal overgrowth and dysbiosis.

Candida spp. remain the most commonly isolated fungal pathogens in burn units, accounting for approximately half of fungal infections [63, 64]. Although C. albicans remains predominant, recent epidemiological studies indicate an increasing prevalence of non-albicans Candida species, particularly C. parapsilosis, which are often associated with higher antifungal resistance and mortality [63]. Other species, including C. tropicalis and C. krusei, have also been linked to poorer clinical outcomes.

In parallel, opportunistic molds such as Aspergillus spp., Fusarium spp., and zygomycetes (Rhizopus and Mucor) are increasingly recognized in severely burned patients [64, 65]. These infections are typically associated with deeper tissue invasion, delayed diagnosis, aggressive clinical progression, and high mortality rates [65].

Early diagnosis remains essential for improving survival. Identification of the etiological agent combined with tissue biopsy in selected cases remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, particularly for confirming invasive fungal disease [58]. Nevertheless, delayed culture growth and non-specific clinical manifestations continue to complicate timely antifungal therapy. Standardized surveillance protocols, including sequential wound sampling and semi-quantitative tissue culture analysis at defined postburn intervals, are therefore frequently employed to support early detection and therapeutic decision-making [4].

Given the high mortality associated with invasive fungal disease, prevention remains a central strategy in burn care and relies primarily on early excision of necrotic tissue, prompt wound coverage, strict infection control measures, and careful antimicrobial stewardship [4, 19].

Viral Infections

Viral infections are less common in burn patients than bacterial or fungal infections and most often reflect reactivation of latent viruses in the setting of burn-induced immune dysfunction [19]. Herpesviruses, particularly herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella-zoster virus (VZV), and cytomegalovirus (CMV), represent the most frequently implicated pathogens, with antiviral therapy based primarily on acyclovir administration [19].

HSV reactivation is the most commonly reported viral complication after severe burns and typically affects healing wounds, donor sites, or mucocutaneous regions such as the face and genital area, where characteristic vesicular lesions may contribute to wound progression [19, 66]. In critically ill patients, viral reactivation may also lead to severe systemic complications, including tracheobronchitis, pneumonia, respiratory failure, hepatitis, and encephalitis [19, 66]. A systematic review by Wurzer et al. [67] showed that HSV and CMV reactivation are relatively frequent after major burns, although their independent impact on mortality remains uncertain. More recent clinical data reported pulmonary HSV reactivation in up to 59% of critically ill burn patients, particularly in those with inhalation injury and higher disease severity scores [68]. These findings suggest that HSV reactivation may serve more as a marker of critical illness and immune dysfunction than as an isolated determinant of mortality.

CMV infection may occur either through primary infection or reactivation of latent virus and is most commonly observed in patients with severe burns, prolonged ICU stays, and concomitant bacterial or fungal infections. CMV reactivation has been associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, increased transfusion requirements, extensive grafting procedures, and overall more severe clinical courses [69].

In contrast, VZV infection remains rare in burn patients, with only isolated cases reported in the literature. However, when present, it may be associated with severe complications and increased mortality, particularly in pediatric patients and those with extensive burns or multiple organ dysfunction [4, 70].

Recent evidence indicates that viral reactivation in burn patients should be interpreted within the broader context of burn-induced immunoparalysis and critical illness [71]. Severe burns profoundly alter both innate and adaptive immune responses through dysregulated cytokine signaling, impaired antigen presentation, and lymphocyte dysfunction, thereby facilitating viral reactivation. Consequently, viral infections in burn patients appear to reflect host immune status more closely than exogenous viral exposure.

Taken together, infections in burn patients follow a dynamic and time-dependent progression shaped by immune dysfunction, microbial colonization, and healthcare-associated exposures. Early stages are typically dominated by Gram-positive bacteria, followed by Gram-negative and MDR pathogens, while fungal infections and viral reactivation become more prevalent during prolonged hospitalization and critical illness. AMR in Burn Patients

AMR has emerged as a major global public health threat, contributing substantially to morbidity and mortality worldwide. In 2019 alone, nearly 5 million deaths were associated with antibiotic-resistant infections, with approximately one-quarter directly attributable to AMR [75]. Among the most commonly implicated pathogens are Gram-positive organisms such as S. aureus and Gram-negative bacteria including E. coli, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, and P. aeruginosa.

Burn patients are particularly vulnerable to the emergence and dissemination of MDR organisms because of extensive antibiotic exposure, prolonged hospitalization, invasive procedures, disruption of the skin barrier, and profound immune dysfunction. Importantly, AMR in burn units develops as a dynamic and time-dependent process that parallels the microbiological evolution of burn wound infections. Early Gram-positive colonization is progressively replaced by Gram-negative and opportunistic pathogens under increasing selective pressure from broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy and healthcare-associated exposure [72, 74, 76].

The prevalence of MDR pathogens rises substantially during hospitalization, increasing from approximately 6% during the first week to nearly 44% after 28 days [68]. Recent epidemiological studies further highlight the magnitude of this problem, with MDR prevalence rates exceeding 50% in some burn units [77]. A recent ICU-based cohort reported MDR rates above 70%, predominantly involving A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae, emphasizing the strong relationship between resistance development, intensive care exposure, and invasive device use [78].

Biofilm formation plays a central role in resistance persistence and therapeutic failure in burn wound infections. Biofilm-producing P. aeruginosa strains demonstrate markedly increased tolerance to antibiotics and host immune responses, with resistance closely associated with biofilm-related genes such as pslA [79]. In addition, genomic studies of MDR and XDR isolates have identified the coexistence of multiple β-lactamase genes, including blaNDM-1 and blaVIM-2, together with virulence and biofilm-associated determinants [80]. These findings illustrate the close interaction between AMR, virulence, and microbial persistence.

The most frequently encountered MDR pathogens in burn units include A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, S. maltophilia, and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae such as K. pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, and E. coli [73]. These organisms are particularly difficult to eradicate because of their environmental persistence, intrinsic resistance mechanisms, and capacity for rapid genetic adaptation.

At the molecular level, horizontal gene transfer mediated by plasmids, transposons, and integrons contributes substantially to resistance dissemination. Studies investigating A. baumannii isolates from burn patients reported universal MDR profiles together with a high prevalence of class 1 and class 3 integrons, highlighting the rapid spread of resistance determinants under sustained antibiotic pressure [80, 81]. Large molecular epidemiology studies have additionally documented widespread dissemination of MDR and XDR clonal lineages carrying resistance genes, efflux pump systems, and biofilm-associated determinants [82].

A major contributor to AMR in burn units is the emergence of ESKAPE pathogens, including Enterococcus faecalis, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp. [83]. These organisms account for more than half of burn-associated infections and employ multiple resistance strategies, including enzymatic antibiotic degradation, efflux pump overexpression, chromosomal mutations, horizontal gene transfer, and biofilm formation [83, 84]. Their ability to evade conventional antimicrobial therapy severely limits treatment options and contributes to increased mortality.

Overall, AMR in burn patients arises from a complex interaction between host immune dysfunction, microbial adaptation, biofilm persistence, antibiotic selective pressure, and healthcare-associated transmission. The convergence of these mechanisms transforms burn units into critical reservoirs for the emergence and dissemination of MDR and XDR pathogens. Risk Factors for Infection in Burn Patients

Burn patients are highly susceptible to HAIs because of disruption of the skin barrier, prolonged hospitalization, immune dysfunction, and frequent exposure to invasive medical devices. Repeated wound manipulation and intensive nursing care further facilitate microbial contamination and colonization by opportunistic pathogens.

The main risk factors associated with infection development include extensive TBSA involvement, full-thickness burns, inhalation injury, prolonged ICU stay, mechanical ventilation, invasive devices, and prior antibiotic exposure [72, 85]. The risk of MDR infections increases progressively during hospitalization, with detection rates rising from approximately 6% during the first week to 44% after 28 days [72]. Additional host-related factors such as advanced age, sex, immune status, burn mechanism, diabetes mellitus, smoking, alcohol abuse, and psychiatric comorbidities further contribute to infection susceptibility [85, 86].

Several studies have identified TBSA > 20%, age over 65 years, inhalation injury, flame burns, central venous catheterization, and full-thickness injury as independent predictors of infection and sepsis [85]. In high-risk patients, infection rates may exceed 50% [86]. Female sex has also been associated with worse outcomes in adult burn patients, potentially through estrogen-mediated modulation of inflammatory pathways involving IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α [86, 87]. Although attenuation of excessive inflammation may be beneficial in other septic conditions, impaired activation of proinflammatory and hypermetabolic pathways after severe burns may adversely affect tissue repair and immune defense.

Sepsis remains the leading cause of mortality in burn patients and reflects a complex interaction between host immune dysfunction and infection severity. The risk of sepsis increases with age, burn size, inhalation injury, and full-thickness burns, while extensive burns may increase sepsis risk several-fold [86]. Elderly patients are particularly vulnerable because of impaired immune responses, delayed wound healing, altered hypermetabolism, and increased rates of organ dysfunction. In patients with burns involving > 20% TBSA, reported sepsis incidence ranges from 3% to nearly 40% [86].

Burn-associated sepsis is strongly linked to adverse clinical outcomes, including acute kidney injury, pneumonia, graft failure, prolonged ICU stay, and increased short-term mortality [86]. Importantly, early-onset sepsis is associated with worse prognosis, likely reflecting severe immune dysregulation and hemodynamic instability during the acute postburn phase. In contrast, delayed sepsis onset may indicate partial immune adaptation and improved host tolerance to systemic infection [86, 88].

Fungal infections represent an additional high-risk complication in severely burned patients. Major risk factors include prolonged ICU admission, broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, invasive devices, hyperglycemia, inhalation injury, delayed wound closure, and extensive skin barrier disruption [89]. These factors promote fungal colonization and progression toward invasive disease, particularly in critically ill patients.

Recent research has also focused on biomarker-based approaches for predicting infection severity and mortality in burn patients. Dynamic changes in lactate, pH, platelet count, and procalcitonin (PCT) have shown prognostic value in burn-associated sepsis, supporting a shift toward time-dependent risk stratification rather than isolated static measurements [90]. Similarly, elevated inflammatory biomarkers such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and PCT have been consistently associated with increased mortality risk [91]. Emerging diagnostic tools such as the Infection Clinical Inflammatory Score may further improve differentiation between infectious and non-infectious inflammatory states in critically ill burn patients [92], although not all composite indices (e.g., systemic immune–inflammatory response) demonstrate consistent predictive performance across patient populations [93].

Large epidemiological studies further emphasize the dynamic relationship between infection risk, microbial patterns, and clinical outcomes. Extensive burns, ICU admission, advanced age, and lower extremity involvement have been consistently associated with increased infection risk and predominance of MDR Gram-negative pathogens, particularly P. aeruginosa [94, 95]. Moreover, Gram-negative infections themselves have emerged as independent predictors of mortality in large burn cohorts [96].

In conclusion, infection risk in burn patients results from a multifactorial interaction between host vulnerability, burn severity, immune dysregulation, microbial evolution, and healthcare-associated exposure. These interconnected determinants are summarized in Table 6.

Diagnosis of Burn Wound Infection

Clinical Diagnosis

Clinical evaluation remains the cornerstone of burn wound infection diagnosis, with cellulitis representing one of the most important diagnostic indicators. Typical manifestations include erythema extending beyond wound margins, increased local temperature, edema, pain, induration of adjacent tissues, and occasionally lymphangitic streaking, often accompanied by deepening of partial-thickness lesions.

Invasive infection is usually associated with both local wound changes and systemic manifestations of sepsis. Burn eschar may develop abnormal discoloration, including black, blue, or brown areas, together with purulent or malodorous secretions, increased exudate, and progressive tissue necrosis [19].

The clinical appearance may also provide clues regarding the etiological agent. Gram-positive infections frequently present with maceration and abundant exudate, whereas Staphylococcus spp. infections are commonly associated with localized necrosis and creamy yellow purulence. Gram-negative infections often exhibit a gangrenous appearance. Infections caused by P. aeruginosa may produce characteristic blue-green secretions with a sweet odor, while Proteus spp. are classically associated with a foul fish-like smell. Fungal infections may present with yellow-orange discoloration, atrophic granular lesions, rapid tissue destruction, and progressive reduction or disappearance of wound exudate.

Early systemic manifestations suggestive of sepsis include increased fluid requirements, reduced urine output, altered mental status, feeding intolerance, gastrointestinal dysfunction, hyperglycemia with increased insulin demand, and marked hyperthermia, frequently exceeding 39 °C [19].

A major challenge in burn care is the substantial overlap between infection-related sepsis and the sterile systemic inflammatory response induced by thermal injury. As a result, many clinical signs lack specificity, frequently delaying accurate diagnosis and timely antimicrobial intervention.

Laboratory and Microbiological Assessment

The diagnosis of infection and sepsis in burn patients remains challenging because severe burns themselves induce a persistent hypermetabolic and systemic inflammatory state characterized by fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, and leukocyte abnormalities, thereby limiting the specificity of conventional clinical and laboratory markers [97].

An ideal biomarker in burn-associated sepsis should support early diagnosis, guide antimicrobial therapy, provide prognostic information, and remain easily reproducible in routine clinical practice. Current biomarkers include acute-phase reactants, inflammatory cytokines, markers of tissue injury and organ dysfunction, hematological parameters, and molecular pathogen detection methods [97].

Among acute-phase reactants, PCT remains one of the most extensively studied biomarkers in burn-related sepsis. During bacterial infection, transcription of the CALC-1 gene markedly increases, resulting in elevated PCT levels within hours of infection onset [98]. Because of its relatively short half-life, serial PCT measurements are frequently used to monitor infection dynamics and response to antimicrobial therapy. However, PCT specificity remains limited in burn patients, as severe sterile inflammation may also induce elevated levels, particularly in extensive burns [97, 99].

CRP is another widely used inflammatory marker, although its diagnostic utility is similarly restricted by persistent elevation in both infectious and non-infectious inflammatory states. Consequently, CRP is generally more informative when interpreted together with other biomarkers rather than as an isolated indicator [97].

The N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) has also emerged as a potential biomarker in burn-associated sepsis, particularly in the context of cardiovascular dysfunction. Elevated levels appear to reflect myocardial stress and ventricular dilation associated with septic cardiomyopathy and systemic volume overload [100].

Proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8, together with the antiinflammatory cytokine IL-10, play central roles in the pathophysiology of burn-associated sepsis [101]. Among these, IL-6 appears particularly promising because of its early elevation and strong association with disease severity and bloodstream infection. IL-8 has been linked to organ dysfunction and mortality, whereas IL-10 may reflect progression toward immunosuppression. Nevertheless, routine clinical application of cytokine profiling remains limited by short half-life, high biological variability, and lack of standardized diagnostic thresholds [97].

Recent studies increasingly support a transition from isolated static biomarkers toward dynamic, multiparametric, and data-driven diagnostic models. Longitudinal analyses demonstrated that temporal trajectories of biomarkers such as IL-6, albumin, immunoglobulins, and mitochondrial DNA may improve prognostic stratification and allow identification of distinct clinical phenotypes in burn-associated sepsis [102]. Emerging biomarkers such as presepsin have also shown promising diagnostic performance, particularly in culture-negative sepsis [103].

In parallel, machine learning-based predictive models are gaining increasing attention in burn medicine. Algorithms such as random forest and gradient boosting have demonstrated improved performance compared with conventional scoring systems for predicting sepsis, mortality, and resistant infections in burn patients [104, 105]. These approaches integrate clinical, laboratory, and demographic variables, supporting more individualized and data-driven risk assessment.

Overall, no single biomarker currently provides sufficient diagnostic accuracy for burn-associated sepsis. Current evidence instead supports an integrated approach combining clinical evaluation, serial biomarker monitoring, microbiological assessment, and emerging predictive models. Prevention and Treatment Strategies

Prevention represents a cornerstone in the management of infections in burn patients, given the complexity and difficulty of treatment once infections are established. Antibiotic and surgical strategies are complemented by non-pharmacological measures, particularly targeting the hospital environment and infection control practices, with the ultimate goal of ensuring patient safety.

These measures aim to prevent the transmission of infectious agents between patients, reduce infection risk, and maintain a safe hospital environment. Additionally, they seek to limit contamination with MDRO originating from other hospital sectors or external healthcare facilities, including through patient transfers or access to services not specialized in burn care [106]. Nonantibiotic Preventive Interventions

Nonantibiotic preventive strategies are fundamental for reducing infection burden and limiting the emergence and transmission of MDRO in burn units. Because burn patients present with extensive barrier disruption, immune dysfunction, prolonged hospitalization, and frequent exposure to invasive procedures, strict infection control measures are essential to prevent both endogenous and healthcare-associated microbial colonization [106].

Current evidence supports a multimodal prevention approach integrating early screening, environmental control, isolation measures, surveillance cultures, and strict adherence to infection control protocols. A recent interventional study conducted in a multidisciplinary ICU demonstrated that implementation of bundled infection prevention measures, including contact isolation, environmental disinfection, water contamination control, and active microbiological surveillance, reduced HA-MDRO acquisition by nearly 50% [107]. These findings highlight the importance of coordinated, system-level interventions in burn care settings.

Preventive measures begin at hospital admission through structured assessment of infection risk factors such as previous hospitalization, prior antibiotic exposure, history of MDR colonization, and clinical signs suggestive of active infection. Until infection status is clarified, patients are frequently managed using enhanced precautionary measures, including isolation when necessary.

Early microbiological screening and surveillance cultures remain important for identifying patients colonized with MDR organisms and for guiding infection control strategies [108, 109]. Sampling from multiple anatomical sites may improve early detection, although the optimal screening method remains debated. Surface swabs are widely used because they are non-invasive and easily repeatable, whereas tissue biopsy provides quantitative information and may better predict progression toward invasive infection and sepsis [109].

Environmental control is another major component of burn infection prevention, as hospital water systems, air circulation, surfaces, and medical equipment may serve as reservoirs for opportunistic pathogens such as P. aeruginosa [109]. Consequently, specialized burn units require dedicated infrastructure, controlled patient flow, strict room isolation, negative-pressure ventilation systems, and continuous environmental monitoring [106, 110, 111].

Additional preventive measures include rigorous hand hygiene, appropriate use of personal protective equipment (PPE), safe handling of medical devices, regular environmental disinfection, microbiologically controlled water systems, proper waste management, and strict visitor policies [106, 109]. Adequate staffing and continuous personnel training are equally important, as adherence to infection prevention protocols remains highly dependent on healthcare worker compliance and institutional organization.

To conclude, prevention of burn-associated infections relies on coordinated implementation of environmental, microbiological, organizational, and behavioral measures rather than on isolated interventions. The main nonantibiotic preventive strategies used in burn care are summarized in Table 8.

Wound Care and Surgical Practices

Early wound management plays a central role in preventing burn wound colonization, invasive infection, and sepsis, particularly in the context of MDRO [112]. Continuous monitoring for local and systemic signs of infection is therefore essential from the time of admission and remains a core component of the broader preventive framework summarized in Table 8.

Timely wound cleaning and debridement reduce microbial burden, limit eschar formation, and promote re-epithelialization, thereby improving healing and shortening hospitalization. Hydrotherapy may facilitate mechanical debridement and removal of wound secretions; however, its routine use remains limited because contaminated water systems and equipment may act as reservoirs for nosocomial pathogens, particularly Gram-negative bacteria [109]. Consequently, strict environmental control, microbiological monitoring of water systems, and appropriate disinfection protocols are essential to minimize infection risk associated with hydrotherapy practices. Preventive measures include maintenance of sterile conditions, use of disinfectants such as chloramine or sodium hypochlorite, and careful limitation of exposure frequency [109].

Debridement and Local Wound Management

Effective burn wound management relies on thorough cleaning, early debridement, appropriate dressings, and topical antimicrobial therapy to reduce microbial burden and prevent progression toward invasive infection and sepsis. Among these measures, early excision of necrotic tissue remains a cornerstone of burn care because eschar provides a favorable environment for microbial proliferation and biofilm formation [109, 112].

Delayed removal of devitalized tissue is associated with increased bacterial load, higher risk of invasive infection, and poorer clinical outcomes [109, 112]. Large cohort studies further support the benefit of early surgical intervention. Wound excision performed within the first 3 days after injury has been associated with lower mortality and reduced infection rates compared with delayed intervention [113]. Similarly, early excisional debridement in elderly burn patients was linked to lower sepsis rates, fewer complications, and shorter ICU and hospital stays [114]. These findings reinforce the role of early debridement as a central component of infection prevention strategies summarized in Table 8.

Beyond reducing microbial burden, debridement improves local tissue perfusion, restores immune competence, enhances penetration of antimicrobial agents, and disrupts biofilm architecture, thereby limiting persistent infection and AMR. However, burn-induced physiological alterations and abundant wound exudate may reduce the effectiveness of conventional topical antiseptics, supporting the development of more advanced local therapeutic approaches.

At the systems level, delays in surgical intervention, often related to late referral or delayed recognition of burn severity, have been associated with prolonged time to first surgery and worse clinical outcomes [115]. Consequently, early excision followed by grafting remains the standard approach in severe burns, although real-world practice continues to vary between centers depending on institutional protocols and patient characteristics [116].

Dressings and Advanced Biomaterials

Traditional burn dressings were primarily based on compresses or sponges impregnated with topical antimicrobial agents such as silver sulfadiazine, framycetin, fusidic acid, povidone-iodine, or chlorhexidine. Although widely used, these materials are limited by poor exudate absorption, frequent replacement requirements, and reduced capacity to actively modulate the wound microenvironment.

Recent advances in biomaterials have led to the development of multifunctional dressings based on natural or synthetic polymers combined with antimicrobial compounds [117]. These systems aim not only to prevent infection but also to maintain moisture balance, reduce pain, promote tissue regeneration, and minimize scar formation. Natural polymers, including cellulose, collagen, alginate, gelatin, carboxymethylcellulose, and silk fibroin, are generally favored because of their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and structural similarity to the extracellular matrix [118].

Hydrogel-based dressings have shown promising regenerative and immunomodulatory properties by supporting autolytic debridement, maintaining optimal hydration, and modulating local inflammatory responses. Experimental studies demonstrated that multifunctional hydrogels incorporating graphene oxide and magnesium ions can enhance angiogenesis, promote macrophage polarization toward regenerative phenotypes, and accelerate wound healing [119].

Nanofiber-based dressings represent another major advance because of their high surface area, extracellular matrix-like architecture, and efficient drug-loading capacity. Electrospun nanofiber scaffolds loaded with bioactive compounds have been associated with accelerated wound closure, reduced inflammation, and improved collagen organization in experimental burn models [120, 121]. Incorporation of nanoparticles, particularly silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), further enhances antimicrobial activity against MDROs while promoting re-epithelialization and angiogenesis [120, 122]. These multifunctional biomaterials are summarized in Table 8.

Clinical translation of these technologies is gradually emerging. Recent observational studies evaluating electrospun nanofiber dressings reported faster epithelialization, lower pain scores, and improved quality of life compared with conventional wound care [123]. Nevertheless, despite their considerable therapeutic potential, widespread clinical implementation remains limited by cost, heterogeneity of study designs, and the predominance of preclinical evidence. Consequently, larger randomized controlled trials are still needed to define their long-term efficacy and optimal clinical applications.

Nonantibiotic Antimicrobial Dressings

The growing prevalence of AMR has accelerated the development of nonantibiotic antimicrobial dressings based on agents such as silver, honey, iodine, and chlorhexidine [117]. These approaches aim to reduce microbial burden while limiting the selective pressure associated with prolonged topical antibiotic exposure.

Silver-containing dressings remain among the most widely used nonantibiotic strategies in burn care because of their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, including MDROs [117]. In clinical practice, these materials are available as creams, solutions, and silver-impregnated dressings and are primarily used for local infection control and reduction of wound bioburden.

Honey-based dressings have also emerged as a promising alternative supported by growing clinical evidence. Meta-analyses comparing honey dressings with silver sulfadiazine reported higher rates of early wound sterilization with honey, although differences in overall healing time remain inconsistent [124]. In addition to antimicrobial activity, medical-grade honey may promote re-epithelialization and wound healing while reducing bacterial burden, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa [125]. However, therapeutic effects appear formulation-dependent, as some products may adversely affect keratinocyte proliferation under specific conditions [125].

Antiseptic-based dressings, particularly iodine-releasing materials, have demonstrated promising activity against biofilm-associated pathogens. Experimental studies using advanced biofilm models showed that controlled-release iodine foam dressings effectively reduced bacterial burden and limited dissemination of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus [126]. These findings support their potential role in managing chronic or biofilm-dominated burn wound infections.

Despite these advances, no single nonantibiotic dressing has demonstrated clear superiority across all clinical scenarios. Treatment outcomes remain strongly influenced by burn depth, exudate level, microbial burden, and patient-specific characteristics. Current evidence therefore supports an individualized approach integrating multiple nonantibiotic strategies to optimize antimicrobial efficacy while minimizing toxicity and resistance selection, as summarized in Table 8.

Metal-based Topical Agents

Metal-based topical therapies remain widely used in burn care because of their broad antimicrobial activity and local effects on wound healing. Beyond their incorporation into antimicrobial dressings, metal-derived compounds are increasingly explored as multifunctional regenerative and drug-delivery platforms.

Silver-based agents, particularly silver sulfadiazine, remain among the most commonly used topical compounds in burn management because of their activity against S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, enteric Gram-negative bacteria, and Candida albicans [127]. However, conventional silver sulfadiazine presents important limitations in severe burns. Its penetration through eschar is restricted, silver ions may be rapidly inactivated through interaction with proteins and cationic surfaces, and prolonged application can promote pseudoeschar formation, potentially delaying wound healing and favoring bacterial persistence [109].

Recent evidence suggests that nanocrystalline silver formulations may offer advantages over conventional silver sulfadiazine by reducing wound healing time and dressing change frequency, although current evidence remains limited and heterogeneous [128]. At the same time, concerns have emerged regarding the cytotoxicity of some silver-containing formulations. Experimental studies reported oxidative stress, DNA damage, and reduced viability of skin and immune cells following exposure to specific silver dressings, highlighting the importance of formulation-dependent safety profiles [129].

Zinc-based materials have also gained increasing attention because of their antimicrobial, antiinflammatory, and regenerative properties. Zinc is incorporated into creams, hydrogels, and advanced dressings, particularly for highly exudative wounds. Recent systematic reviews support the beneficial role of zinc supplementation and topical zinc application in improving wound healing outcomes [130]. In particular, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) demonstrated enhanced antimicrobial activity, stimulation of tissue regeneration, and modulation of inflammatory responses in experimental burn models [131].

ZnO nanoparticles also showed activity against MDR pathogens such as A. baumannii, while simultaneously promoting re-epithelialization, collagen organization, and restoration of normal skin architecture [132]. Their antimicrobial activity is mediated through disruption of membrane integrity, oxidative stress induction, and interference with enzymatic and DNA functions [133, 134]. Beyond antimicrobial effects, zinc additionally supports angiogenesis, collagen synthesis, keratinocyte migration, and granulation tissue formation [135]. Nevertheless, prolonged exposure to high zinc concentrations may contribute to bacterial tolerance and coselection of resistance determinants linked to antibiotic resistance genes [136].

Copper-based materials represent another promising class of topical antimicrobial agents. Copper exerts broad-spectrum antibacterial activity through reactive oxygen species generation, membrane disruption, enzyme inactivation, and DNA damage [137]. Recent animal studies demonstrated that nano-chelated copper formulations accelerate burn wound healing, improve survival, and reduce inflammatory signaling compared with conventional silver sulfadiazine treatment [138].

Advanced copper-containing hydrogels and nanoparticle systems further demonstrated enhanced angiogenesis, macrophage polarization toward regenerative M2 phenotypes, collagen deposition, and improved re-epithelialization [139]. In addition to antimicrobial effects, copper contributes to tissue repair by stimulating collagen production, glycosaminoglycan synthesis, proteoglycan formation, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-mediated angiogenesis [140].

Recent advances in nanotechnology have expanded the therapeutic potential of metal-based systems by enabling controlled drug release, modulation of the wound microenvironment, and development of multifunctional antimicrobial-regenerative platforms [141]. However, increasing evidence also suggests that excessive or prolonged use of heavy metal-based therapies may contribute to resistance development. Genetic determinants such as the “copper homeostasis and silver resistance island” have been identified in organisms including S. aureus and Listeria monocytogenes, potentially contributing to reduced susceptibility to both metals and last-line antibiotics such as colistin [142, 143].

Overall, metal-based topical agents remain valuable components of burn wound management because of their combined antimicrobial and regenerative properties. However, their use requires careful balancing between therapeutic efficacy, cytotoxicity, and the potential for resistance development within the broader preventive framework summarized in Table 8.

Topical Antibiotic Therapy

Topical antimicrobial therapy remains a cornerstone of burn wound management because it enables high local antimicrobial concentrations while minimizing systemic toxicity. In contrast, prophylactic systemic antibiotics have not consistently demonstrated benefit in preventing burn wound infections in patients with moderate burns (20–40% TBSA), with some studies reporting comparable or even higher infection rates in treated groups [144]. Consequently, current guidelines continue to favor topical antimicrobial strategies as first-line approaches in most clinical scenarios [145].

Silver-based dressings remain the most widely recommended topical agents because of their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and versatility across different burn wound settings, although no single formulation has demonstrated universal superiority [145].

Among conventional topical antibiotics, mafenide acetate retains particular value against Gram-negative pathogens, especially P. aeruginosa. Recent advances in delivery systems, including electrospun dressings and sustained-release formulations, have improved drug stability and prolonged antimicrobial activity compared with traditional preparations [146].

Newer topical formulations are increasingly being developed to improve targeted delivery, antimicrobial efficacy, and local tolerability. Fusidic acid-loaded microemulsion gels demonstrated antibacterial efficacy comparable to standard formulations while additionally promoting antiinflammatory effects and accelerated wound contraction [147]. Similarly, usnic acid-based bioadhesive films showed promising activity against Gram-positive and biofilm-associated pathogens while maintaining low cytotoxicity, suggesting potential utility as adjunctive therapies in burn wound care [148].

Beyond antibiotics, antiseptic agents continue to play an important complementary role. Sodium hypochlorite solutions, for example, may contribute not only to microbial reduction but also to modulation of the wound microenvironment through downregulation of matrix metalloproteinases such as MMP-9, thereby supporting tissue remodeling and wound healing [149].

In conclusion, current evidence supports a multimodal topical approach integrating antimicrobial efficacy, biofilm control, antiinflammatory activity, and tissue regeneration. The principal topical antimicrobial strategies and their mechanisms of action are summarized in Table 8.

Overall, the data summarized in Table 8 highlight that antimicrobial strategies in burn care are inherently complementary rather than hierarchical. While early interventions such as infection control measures and surgical debridement are strongly supported by clinical evidence and remain essential for reducing microbial burden, advanced approaches, including biomaterial-based dressings, metal-based nanoparticles, and novel topical agents, offer additional benefits through combined antimicrobial and regenerative effects. However, these emerging strategies are still predominantly supported by preclinical or early-phase clinical data and are associated with significant limitations, including cytotoxicity, formulation variability, and a lack of standardization. Furthermore, the persistence of biofilm formation and the emergence of AMR continue to challenge the long-term effectiveness of both conventional and advanced therapies. These findings emphasize the need for integrated, multimodal treatment strategies and for high-quality randomized clinical trials to validate innovative approaches and facilitate their translation into routine clinical practice.

Systemic prophylaxis and treatment

The use of systemic antibiotics in burn patients remains controversial because potential therapeutic benefits must be balanced against the risk of promoting AMR. While systemic antimicrobial therapy is essential for confirmed invasive infection and sepsis, routine prophylactic administration in patients without clear evidence of infection is generally not supported by current evidence [150].

Recent studies indicate that prophylactic systemic antibiotics do not consistently reduce mortality or overall infection rates and may contribute to the emergence of MDROs, particularly in non-ventilated patients [106, 150]. However, selected high-risk populations, especially mechanically ventilated patients, may derive some benefit from early targeted prophylaxis, although available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous [150].

Empirical systemic therapy should also reflect the dynamic microbiological evolution of burn wound infections. Gram-positive organisms, particularly S. aureus, predominate during the early phase after injury, whereas Gram-negative pathogens such as P. aeruginosa and Acinetobacter spp. become increasingly prevalent after 48–72 h of hospitalization, often necessitating broader antimicrobial coverage [109].

A major challenge in burn care is the profound alteration of antibiotic pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) caused by capillary leak, hypermetabolism, fluid shifts, and augmented renal clearance [112]. These physiological changes may substantially affect antibiotic distribution and elimination, increasing the risk of subtherapeutic exposure or toxicity. Consequently, individualized dosing strategies and therapeutic drug monitoring are increasingly recommended in critically ill burn patients.

In parallel, antimicrobial stewardship has become a central component of modern burn management. Current strategies emphasize rapid de-escalation from broad-spectrum empirical therapy toward targeted treatment based on microbiological results in order to minimize toxicity, unnecessary antibiotic exposure, and resistance selection [151, 152]. Emerging stewardship approaches additionally integrate rapid molecular diagnostics, biomarker-guided therapy, and AI-assisted decision systems to optimize antimicrobial selection and treatment duration [153].

Despite these advances, important evidence gaps remain. Most available studies are observational, heterogeneous, or limited in scale, while high-quality randomized controlled trials remain scarce. Current evidence therefore supports a personalized and risk-adapted approach in which systemic antibiotics are reserved for confirmed or strongly suspected infections, whereas prophylactic use should remain restricted to carefully selected high-risk scenarios [154].

Discussion

The management of infections in burn patients remains a major clinical challenge because of the complex interaction between immune dysregulation, disruption of the skin barrier, prolonged hospitalization, and the emergence of MDROs. Despite substantial advances in burn care, important diagnostic, microbiological, and therapeutic limitations persist.

Early identification of infection and sepsis remains particularly difficult in burn patients because the systemic inflammatory and hypermetabolic response induced by thermal injury frequently overlaps with the clinical manifestations of infection. Conventional biomarkers such as CRP and PCT, although widely used, demonstrate inconsistent specificity and sensitivity in this setting, limiting their value as standalone diagnostic tools [57]. Increasing evidence suggests that integrating biomarkers with clinical and computational models may improve diagnostic accuracy, although robust validation in burn-specific populations remains limited.

Burn wound microbiology also evolves dynamically over time, further complicating treatment strategies. Early colonization is generally dominated by Gram-positive organisms, whereas prolonged hospitalization favors Gram-negative and MDR pathogens under sustained antibiotic selective pressure [112]. This temporal shift reinforces the importance of continuous microbiological surveillance, targeted antimicrobial therapy, and regular reassessment of empirical treatment protocols.

Balancing adequate antimicrobial coverage with antimicrobial stewardship remains another major challenge. Although systemic antibiotics are indispensable for invasive infection and sepsis, routine prophylactic administration is generally discouraged because it does not consistently improve outcomes and contributes to MDR emergence [112, 150, 154]. Limited evidence suggests potential benefit in selected high-risk populations, particularly mechanically ventilated patients, but current data remain heterogeneous and insufficient to support routine use [150]. Consequently, de-escalation strategies based on microbiological findings have become increasingly important in burn care, aiming to minimize unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure while preserving therapeutic efficacy [151, 152].

Local wound management remains equally critical for infection control. Early debridement and surgical excision of necrotic tissue continue to represent the cornerstone of therapy by reducing microbial burden, disrupting biofilms, and limiting systemic dissemination [109, 113, 114]. In parallel, modern dressings and advanced biomaterials have expanded therapeutic possibilities by combining antimicrobial, regenerative, and immunomodulatory functions. Nevertheless, their effectiveness may still be influenced by factors such as wound exudate, tissue penetration, local microenvironment conditions, and variability between formulations.

Metal-based antimicrobial strategies, including silver-, zinc-, and copper-containing materials, have shown promising antimicrobial and regenerative properties. However, increasing evidence regarding cytotoxicity, long-term safety, and resistance development highlights the need for cautious and individualized application [142, 143]. The identification of genetic resistance determinants such as the “copper homeostasis and silver resistance island” further illustrates the remarkable adaptive capacity of burn wound pathogens and raises concerns regarding the long-term sustainability of heavy metal-based therapies.

Another important limitation is the absence of validated tools specifically designed for infection risk stratification in burn patients. Common burn severity scores such as Baux and ABSI primarily estimate mortality risk rather than susceptibility to infection [155, 156], while general ICU scoring systems fail to adequately capture burn-specific immune and microbiological dynamics. Existing predictive models for burn-associated sepsis also remain limited by retrospective design, heterogeneity in sepsis definitions, and insufficient external validation.

In this context, AI and machine learning approaches have emerged as promising tools for improving early detection and risk prediction. Several AI-based models developed in critical care settings demonstrated the ability to predict sepsis hours before clinical onset [157–159]. Burn-specific models such as MILO achieved encouraging predictive accuracy using only a limited number of clinical variables [160, 161], suggesting potential feasibility for clinical implementation. Notably, recent findings indicate that simplified models may perform comparably to more complex systems, which may facilitate integration into routine practice.

However, important limitations remain before widespread implementation of AI-assisted systems can be achieved. Current models are often dependent on retrospective datasets, vulnerable to data quality bias, and insufficiently validated across diverse patient populations. Moreover, AI should be viewed as a complementary tool rather than a replacement for clinical judgment, particularly in critically ill burn patients where rapid physiological changes frequently require individualized decision-making.

Future directions should therefore focus on integrating antimicrobial stewardship, individualized PK/PD-guided therapy, biomarker-based monitoring, microbiological surveillance, and AI-assisted prediction into unified clinical frameworks. Such approaches may improve diagnostic precision, optimize antimicrobial use, reduce resistance development, and ultimately improve outcomes in burn patients.

Overall, infection management in burn patients requires a multidisciplinary and dynamic approach that integrates clinical assessment, microbiological surveillance, optimized local and systemic therapies, and emerging predictive technologies. Continued refinement of diagnostic tools, standardized protocols, and validated predictive models remains essential for improving both infection control and long-term patient outcomes.

Future Perspectives

Future research in burn wound infection management is increasingly focused on the development of advanced antimicrobial and regenerative strategies capable of overcoming multidrug resistance, biofilm persistence, and limitations of conventional therapies. Among the most promising approaches are nanoparticle-based systems, including silver (AgNPs), gold (AuNPs), copper (CuNPs), zinc oxide (ZnONPs), and copper oxide nanoparticles (CuONPs).

Nanoparticles exhibit antimicrobial activity through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Direct effects include disruption of bacterial membranes, intracellular enzyme inhibition, oxidative stress induction, and interference with DNA replication. Their small size, high surface-to-volume ratio, surface charge, and tunable morphology facilitate close interaction with bacterial cells and improve penetration into biofilms, where conventional antibiotics are often ineffective [119, 162–169].

The antimicrobial activity of nanoparticles may be intrinsic or externally activated. Endogenous activity depends on the structural and catalytic properties of the nanoparticle core, whereas exogenous activity involves external stimuli such as light or infrared irradiation to induce reactive oxygen species generation or photothermal effects [170, 171]. In addition, modern nanoparticle platforms increasingly integrate hydrogels, nanofibers, and controlled-release carriers to improve stability, biocompatibility, and targeted delivery [172].

Composite nanoparticles combining metallic cores with bioactive ligands or antimicrobial compounds appear particularly promising because they enable synergistic antimicrobial activity and controlled release of active agents [119]. Several studies demonstrated enhanced antibacterial effects when nanoparticles were combined with antibiotics. For example, AgNPs combined with ampicillin showed improved activity against E. coli, while AuNP-doxycycline combinations demonstrated enhanced efficacy against P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and other clinically relevant pathogens [173–176].

Beyond nanoparticles, alternative antimicrobial approaches are also gaining increasing attention. Nucleotide-derived compounds known as nubiotics demonstrated activity against MDROs, particularly when incorporated into liposomal or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose-based delivery systems [109].

Topical probiotics represent another emerging strategy aimed at restoring microbial balance rather than directly eliminating all microorganisms. Strains such as Lactobacillus plantarum, L. acidophilus, and L. reuteri demonstrated inhibitory activity against S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii, with effects comparable to some silver-based dressings in experimental models [177]. Unlike conventional antimicrobials, probiotics may preserve commensal microbiota while limiting pathogen colonization through competitive exclusion and release of antimicrobial metabolites.

Bacteriophage therapy also represents a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics, particularly for MDR and biofilm-associated infections. Experimental studies demonstrated that bacteriophage-loaded hydrogels effectively reduced bacterial burden and promoted tissue regeneration in burn wounds infected with P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae, including polymicrobial infections [178].

Alongside these therapeutic advances, antimicrobial stewardship and PK/PD-guided antimicrobial optimization are increasingly recognized as essential components of burn infection management. Major burns induce profound physiological alterations, including hypermetabolism, increased volume of distribution, hypoalbuminemia, and augmented renal clearance, which may significantly affect antibiotic exposure and contribute to subtherapeutic concentrations or toxicity [179, 180]. Consequently, recent studies support the integration of therapeutic drug monitoring and PK/PD-guided dosing strategies, particularly for beta-lactams, vancomycin, and aminoglycosides, to improve antimicrobial target attainment, reduce underdosing, and limit resistance selection and adverse effects [181–183]. Epidemiological investigations from burn ICUs additionally continue to highlight the increasing prevalence of MDR and XDR Gram-negative pathogens, emphasizing the importance of cumulative antibiograms, real-time microbiological surveillance, and structured AMS programs to optimize empirical and targeted therapy [184, 185]. Future burn care frameworks will likely integrate AMS strategies, biomarker-guided therapy, PK/PD-informed dosing, and AI-assisted predictive models to support individualized antimicrobial decision-making in critically ill burn patients.

In parallel, AI and machine learning approaches are increasingly being explored to improve early infection detection, sepsis prediction, and individualized treatment strategies in burn patients. AI-based systems may integrate large volumes of clinical, microbiological, and biomarker data to identify subtle patterns associated with infection before clinical deterioration becomes evident. Early studies demonstrated promising predictive performance in critically ill and burn populations, suggesting potential applications in risk stratification, antimicrobial stewardship, and personalized decision-making.

Despite these advances, most emerging therapies remain in preclinical or early translational stages, and robust clinical validation is still limited. Future progress will therefore depend not only on technological innovation but also on standardized clinical trials, improved translational frameworks, and integration of antimicrobial, regenerative, and AI-assisted approaches into comprehensive burn care strategies.

Conclusions

Burn wound infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality because of the combined effects of immune dysfunction, skin barrier disruption, prolonged hospitalization, and the increasing prevalence of MDR pathogens. Effective management requires an integrated approach combining early diagnosis, microbiological surveillance, optimized wound care, and targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Although advances in wound dressings, topical therapies, surgical management, and antimicrobial stewardship have improved infection control, important challenges persist, particularly regarding AMR, delayed diagnosis, and variability in clinical presentation. Emerging strategies such as nanoparticle-based therapies, bacteriophages, probiotics, and AI-assisted predictive models offer promising perspectives for improving prevention, early detection, and personalized treatment.

Future progress will depend on the integration of antimicrobial stewardship, individualized PK/PD-guided therapy, biomarker-based monitoring, and data-driven decision-support systems into standardized multidisciplinary care pathways. Further clinical validation and translational research remain essential to improve outcomes and limit the growing impact of AMR in burn patients.
