Abstract
. Internet of Things (IoT) devices are becoming increasingly commonplace in numerous public and semi-private settings. Currently, most such devices lack mechanisms to facilitate their discovery by casual (nearby) users who are not owners or operators. However, these users are potentially being sensed, and/or actuated upon, by these devices, without their knowledge or consent. This naturally triggers privacy, security, and safety issues. To address this problem, some recent work explored device transparency in the IoT ecosystem. The intuitive approach is for each device to periodically and securely broadcast (announce) its presence and capabilities to all nearby users. While effective, when no new users are present, this P ​ u ​ s ​ h Push -based approach generates a substantial amount of unnecessary network traffic and needlessly interferes with normal device operation. In this work, we construct DB-PAISA which addresses these issues via a P ​ u ​ l ​ l Pull -based method, whereby devices reveal their presence and capabilities only upon explicit user request. Each device guarantees a secure timely response (even if fully compromised by malware) based on a small active Root-of-Trust (RoT). DB-PAISA requires no hardware modifications and is suitable for a range of current IoT devices. To demonstrate its feasibility and practicality, we built a fully functional and publicly available prototype. It is implemented atop a commodity MCU (NXP LCP55S69) and operates in tandem with a smartphone-based app. Using this prototype, we evaluate energy consumption and other performance factors.



1.
Introduction

In recent years, Internet of Things (IoT), embedded, and smart
devices have become commonplace in many aspects of everyday life.
We are often surrounded by cameras, sensors, displays, and robotic appliances
in our homes, offices, public spaces, and industrial settings.
With rapid advances in AI/ML, 5G, robotics, and automation,
the use of IoT devices is becoming more and more prevalent.

IoT devices feature various sensors and/or actuators.
Sensors collect information about the environment, while actuators control the environment.
Some IoT devices use sensors to collect sensitive information, e.g., cameras, voice assistants, and
motion detectors. Whereas, actuators on some IoT devices perform safety-critical tasks, e.g., operate door locks,
set off alarms, and control smart appliances. This is not
generally viewed as a problem for owners who install, deploy, and operate these devices.
After all, owners should be aware of their devices’ locations and functionalities.
However, the same does not hold for casual users who come within sensing and/or
actuation range of IoT devices owned and operated by others.
Such users remain unaware unless nearby devices are human-perceivable, e.g., visible or audible.

This issue is partly due to lack, or inadequacy, of security features on commodity IoT devices,
which manufacturers often justify with size, energy, and monetary constraints of the devices.
Moreover, consumers gravitate towards monocultures, as witnessed by the great popularity
of certain devices, such as Ring doorbells, Roomba vacuum cleaners, and Echo voice assistants.
This leads to such massively popular IoT devices becoming highly attractive attack targets.
Attacks can exploit devices’ software vulnerabilities to exfiltrate
sensed data, report fake sensed data, perform malicious actuation, or simply zombify devices

Privacy and safety risks are not only apparent in public spaces, e.g., city streets,

1.1.
Current State of Device Transparency

To address the issues presented above, some recent research explored a privacy-agile IoT
ecosystem based on manufacturer compliance. In particular,
PAISA

(
Jakkamsetti et al. 2023
)
allows
users to learn about the presence and capabilities of nearby devices by listening to periodic secure
announcements by each device using WiFi broadcast. Announcements are guaranteed
to be sent in time even if the device is fully malware-compromised. Also, each announcement includes
a recent attestation token, allowing a recipient user to check whether the device is trustworthy.

PAISA
works on commodity devices that have a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) (e.g., ARM
TrustZone
(
Arm Ltd. 2018
)
). It is unsuitable for low-end IoT devices without TEE support.

Another recent work, DIAL
(
Kaplan et al. 2023
)
, requires each device to have a physically attached NFC tag.
The device helps users locate it by either sounding a buzzer or using
an ultra-wideband (UWB) interface. Upon physically locating a device,
the user simply taps a smartphone on the NFC tag to get device information. Although DIAL does not
require a TEE on each device, it imposes other requirements on the manufacturer and user,
such as mounting an NFC tag and a buzzer or a UWB interface (rare on commodity IoT devices)
for localization. It also requires physical access to a device to tap the NFC tag manually.

Both
PAISA
and DIAL follow the
Push
model: IoT devices announce their presence at
fixed time intervals. This raises two concerns: (1)
unnecessary network traffic
,
the volume of which can be high when numerous compliant devices are deployed in a given
space, and (2)
interference with normal device operation
(in
PAISA
only), which is
detrimental for devices that perform safety- or time-critical tasks.
Both (1) and (2) are especially problematic when no new users are around to scan for
these announcements. Also, as discussed above, DIAL’s requirements for NFC-s and buzzers or
UWB interfaces are currently unrealistic for most settings.

Another common application domain is large IoT deployments in
industrial settings. Industrial IoT (
IIoT
) devices play an increasingly important role in
automation across various industry sectors. Their numbers surpassed
3.5
billion in 2023,
accounting for
25
% of total IoT deployments
(
Statista 2024
;
Statista 2022
)
.
According to a recent Ericsson mobility report
(
Ericsson 2024
)
, the estimated
number of connected devices in a typical smart factory is between 0.5 and 1 per square meter.
This implies potentially millions of devices in a large industrial installation,
e.g., a warehouse, port, or factory
(
Tim Newcomb 2017
)
.

In such large
IIoT
systems, the owner/operator needs to maintain and keep track of all deployed devices.
Unlike public and semi-public settings with casual users, the owner knows the identities and types of
deployed
IIoT
devices. However, they might not know which devices are reachable, operational, or
malware-compromised. They might also not know device locations since many industrial settings involve
moving components. An intuitive approach is to use existing infrastructure to probe devices.
For example, specialized software for managing merchandise or IoT devices associated with
tagging information (e.g., barcode or NFC) can be used for inventory management

(
Tejesh and Neeraja 2018
;
Kobbacy and Liang 1999
;
Atieh et al. 2016

Zahran et al. 2021

do not provide either reliable

or

of moving devices.

Overview and Contributions

In this paper, we construct (1)

2.1.
Targeted IoT Devices

In general, we consider constrained IoT devices geared for executing simple tasks and
deployed on a large scale, e.g., smart bulbs, locks, speakers, plugs, and alarms.
Given tight resource budgets, they typically rely on microcontroller units (MCUs) with
no memory virtualization.
Many of such MCUs have ARM Cortex-M
(
Arm Ltd. 2017
)
or RISC-V cores
(
RISC-V International 2015
)
.
Our focus is on devices equipped with relatively simple TEEs, such as TrustZone-M.
Note that we explicitly rule out low-end MCUs with no hardware security features.

As shown in Figure
1
, a typical IoT device contains an MCU and multiple
peripherals. The MCU includes a core processor(s), main memory, and memory bus, forming a
System-on-a-Chip (SoC).
Its primary memory is typically partitioned into: (1) program memory (e.g., flash),
where software is installed, (2) data memory (RAM), which the software uses for its stack
and heap, and (3) read-only memory (ROM), where the bootloader and tiny immutable
software are placed during provisioning.
Such an MCU also hosts peripherals, including a timer, General-Purpose Input/Output (GPIO), and
Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter (UART).

The MCU interfaces with various special-purpose sensors and actuators through such peripherals.
Common sensors are exemplified by microphones, GPS units, cameras, gyroscopes, as well as
touch and motion detectors. Whereas, speakers, door locks, buzzers, sprinklers,
as well as light and power switches, are examples of actuators.

Scope of Targeted IoT Device Types:
For certain personal IoT devices,
there is no need to inform nearby users of their presence since doing so can
compromise their owners’ privacy. This involves medical devices, e.g., blood pressure monitors,
insulin pumps, catheters, or pacemakers. Clearly, neither
PAISA
nor
DB-PAISA
is
applicable to such devices. Delineating the exact boundaries between devices’
presence of which should (or should not) be released (or be discoverable) is
beyond the scope of this paper.

2.2.
Network

IoT devices are connected to the Internet and/or other devices either directly or through
intermediate entities, e.g., hubs or routers. To support this, a typical IoT device features
at least one network interface (e.g., WiFi, Bluetooth, Cellular, Ethernet, or Zigbee).
WiFi and Cellular provide wireless Internet connectivity at relatively high speeds,
while Bluetooth and Zigbee are geared towards lower-speed, shorter-range communication
with neighboring devices. In this work, we focus on WiFi and Bluetooth since they
are commonplace on both smartphones and IoT devices

Arellano 2019
)
.

WiFi

m

The latest WiFi standards (e.g., WiFi 6) achieve multi-gigabit speeds, making it ideal for

Bluetooth

\leq\penalty\ 40

Since Bluetooth 4.0, Bluetooth devices have started supporting Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE).
BLE is optimized for low power consumption, making it well-suited for resource-constrained IoT devices.
Bluetooth Classic is utilized for connection-oriented data transfer, such as audio streaming.
Whereas, BLE is widely used in beaconing applications (e.g., asset tracking, proximity marketing, and indoor navigation)
for its power efficiency.

2.3.
Trusted Execution Environment (TEE)

A TEE is a secure and isolated environment within a computer system,
typically implemented as a hardware-based component. It provides a secure area for the
execution of sensitive operations (e.g., cryptographic processing or handling of sensitive data)
without interference or compromise from other software, operating systems, and hypervisors.

ARM TrustZone, one of the most prominent commercial TEE-s, divides the system into two separate
execution environments: Secure world and Normal (non-secure) world. Non-secure applications and interrupts
cannot access computing resources, such as memory and peripherals, that are configured as secure.
Within the ARM TrustZone framework, TrustZone-A (TZ-A) and TrustZone-M (TZ-M) refer to two specific
implementations tailored for different types of processors; TZ-A is designed for high-performance
application processors (e.g., smartphones), while TZ-M is tailored for low-power, resource-constrained
MCUs commonly used in IoT devices. Although core security goals are the same for both TZ-A and TZ-M,
techniques used to achieve these goals differ.

Unlike TZ-A, TZ-M is memory map-based.
Memory areas and other critical resources marked as secure can only be accessed when the core is
running in a secure state. Peripherals can also be designated as
secure
,
which makes them exclusively accessible through and controlled by secure code.
Besides two security regions (Secure world/Normal world), TZ-M introduces a non-secure callable (NSC) region.
The NSC region allows secure functions to be safely called from non-secure code,
exposing certain secure functions to Normal world without compromising overall system security.

\sf{\mathcal{R}A}

As shown in

\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}

(2)

\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}

\sf{\mathcal{C}hal}
, and returns it to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
.

(3)

\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
verifies the attestation report and determines
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
’s current state.

Figure
2(b)
shows a variation of the above protocol, referred to as non-interactive

(

)
.
In the variant,
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}

This way,
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
no longer needs to know the expected software configuration for each
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
.

3.2.
Adversary Model

An adversary (
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
) can access all memory regions (e.g., flash and RAM), except for the
TCB (defined in Section
4.1
) and its data within the TEE.
Outside the TCB, any
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
components and peripherals, including timers,
network interfaces, sensors, and actuators, are subject to compromise.
Communication between
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s and
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s is subject to eavesdropping and manipulation by

\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
following the Dolev-Yao model
(
Dolev and Yao 1983
)
. Additionally,
\mathsf{\it Registration}
phase is
considered to be secure.
\mathsf{M}
fr is trusted to accurately provision IoT devices and safeguard their secrets.
Similarly,
DB-PAISA
app on
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
is considered trustworthy. Though it maintains no secrets, it is
assumed to correctly generate formatted requests as well as accurately process and
display information to the user.

Denial-of-Service (DoS):

\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
can exploit vulnerabilities in non-TCB software to introduce malware, and then
attempt to exhaust
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
’s computing resources, e.g., cores, memory, and peripherals.
Through such malware, it can also occupy (squat on)
non-TCB-controlled peripherals from inside the device.
Also, it can swamp peripherals from the outside, e.g., via excessive network traffic or fake
request flooding, which is possible since requests are not authenticated.
Sections
3.4
and
7.2
describe how

DB-PAISA
fully mitigates malware-based, and partially defends against network-based, attacks.

Replay Attacks:

\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
can reply with stale, yet valid, responses to
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
.
Replay attacks on
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
are not a serious concern due to lack of authentication for
\sf{Msg_{req}}
.
Replays of stale
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s are a special case of request flooding mentioned above.

DB-PAISA
does not defend against wormhole attacks
(
Hu et al. 2006
)
. In such attacks,

\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
tunnels both
\sf{Msg_{req}}
and its corresponding
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
to/from a remote

.

{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
, thus fooling a

Runtime Attacks:

Similar to
PAISA

detects software modifications via periodic
\sf{\mathcal{R}A}

However, runtime attacks (e.g., control-flow and non-control-data) are out of scope.
Prior research
(

;

Physical Attacks:

DB-PAISA
protects against non-invasive physical attacks, e.g., reprogramming
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
using a legal interface, such as JTAG.
However,
DB-PAISA
does not protect against physically invasive attacks, such as

For defenses against these attacks, we refer to

Non-compliant (Hidden) Devices:

We do not consider attacks whereby
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
gains physical access to an environment
and surreptitiously deploys non-compliant malicious devices. For this purpose,
we refer to some recent research on detecting and localizing hidden devices via

(
Li et al. 2018
;
REI 2015
;
Spygadgets 2015
;
Sengupta et al. 2020
)
and network traffic

(

;

)
.

Co-existence with Other Secure Applications:

In single-enclave TEEs (e.g., ARM TrustZone), all secure resources
are shared by all secure applications. Consequently, a compromised secure application
can access
DB-PAISA
secrets or mount a DoS attack by squatting on the network interface, which is part of

TCB.
As in

, we assume that all secure applications are free of vulnerabilities.
Although this issue can be addressed by TEEs that support multiple enclaves (e.g.,
Intel SGX
(
Intel 2015
)
, ARM CCA
(
ARM 2021
)

-s.
In terms of performance and functionality,
DB-PAISA
must satisfy the following properties:

•

Low latency:

\sf{Msg_{req}}
reception and
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation time on
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
must be minimal.

DB-PAISA
implementation should have a minimal impact on
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
’s normal operation.

•

Low bandwidth:

DB-PAISA
messages (
\sf{Msg_{req}}
and
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
) must consume minimal bandwidth.

•

Scalability:

{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
should be able to handle multiple
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s and respond in time.

•

Casualness:

As in
PAISA
, no prior security association between
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s and
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s should be assumed, and
secure sessions/handshakes between them must not be required.

To mitigate attacks defined in Section
3.2
,
DB-PAISA
must provide:

•

Unforgeability:

{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
must verify that
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
comes from a
genuine
DB-PAISA
-enabled
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
, i.e.,
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
cannot forge
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
.

•

Timeliness:

{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
must receive
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
containing
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
information in a timely fashion.

•

Freshness:

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
must be fresh and reflect
the recent software state of
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
.

3.4.
DoS Attacks

We now consider DoS attacks on
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
and
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
.

A network-based
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
can mount DoS attacks by flooding

{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
with fake
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
-s. This forces
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
to verify numerous (invalid) signatures, clearly wasting resources.

DB-PAISA
offers no defense against such attacks, due to the casual nature of
the
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
relationship.
Also, this may not be a critical issue on higher-end
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
.

As discussed in Section
3.2
, there are two types of
DoS attacks on
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
: (1) an internal malware-based
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
who keeps the
CPU and/or network peripherals busy, and (2) a network-based
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
who floods
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
with frivolous
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s and thereby depletes computing resources with expensive
cryptographic operations needed for
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation.
Similar to
PAISA
, (1) is mitigated by configuring the TEE such that a network
peripheral is placed under the exclusive control of the TCB.
Consequently,
\sf{Msg_{req}}
reception and
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation tasks have the highest priority.

Dealing with (2) is more challenging. Since
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s are not authenticated,
a flood of fake
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s can simply exhaust

{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
resources. The same can occur in a non-hostile scenario when a flurry
of valid requests from multiple benign users (e.g., in a suddenly crowded space) overwhelms
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
.

To mitigate
\sf{Msg_{req}}
flooding (whether hostile or not),
DB-PAISA
uses a
lazy-response technique whereby
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
collects
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s for
a certain fixed time and responds to all of them with a single
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
.
Specifically,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
maintains a pool of nonces (up to a certain maximum number)
from
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s. At the end of the period or when the nonce pool reaches its capacity,

{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
signs a collective

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
containing all pooled nonces. Each
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
with an outstanding

\sf{Msg_{req}}
checks whether the received
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
contains its nonce.
This approach allows
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
to pace its compute-intensive cryptographic operations.
Details are in Section
4
.

We acknowledge that pooling nonces from
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s does not fully
address
\sf{Msg_{req}}
flooding:
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
can simply flood
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
at a high
enough speed, causing
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
to generate signed
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
-s at an
unsustainable rate. One remedial measure is to adopt random deletion – a relatively
effective countermeasure to the well-known TCP SYN-flooding attack
(
Schuba et al. 1997
)
.
Furthermore, nonce pooling delays
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation, making
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s wait longer.
To address this issue, an alternative approach (albeit with a slightly different setting) is discussed in Section
7.2
.

from

2

2

Metadata:

The following parameters are stored in the secure memory region of

, (2)

4.2.1.
DB-PAISA
Trusted Software (
SW_{{{\sf DB-PAISA}}}
) on
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}

As shown in Figure
4
,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
has four states in
\mathsf{\sf Response}
step:

\sf{\mathcal{W}ait}
,
\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}
,
\sf{\mathcal{R}cv}
, and
\sf{\mathcal{G}en}
.
\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}
is a periodic state independent
of others: its periodicity is governed by
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}
.

(a)
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{W}ait}}}
:

After its boot sequence completes,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
runs
SW_{dev}
while listening
for
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s on the network interface. Depending on the condition below,
it transitions to other states:

•

Every
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}
:
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
periodically attests
SW_{dev}
via a
secure timer set off
every
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}
. When the timer
expires,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
proceeds to
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}
.

•

\sf{Msg_{req}}
received
: Once
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
identifies an incoming packet as a

\sf{Msg_{req}}
(via
DB-PAISA
protocol identifier –
ID_{{{\sf DB-PAISA}}}
, in a header field), it transitions to

\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{R}cv}}}
.

•

After
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
: It proceeds to
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}
.

(b)
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}
:

{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
computes a hash over
SW_{dev}
and compares it to
H_{SW_{dev}}
.
If they do not match, the 1-bit
\mathsf{Att_{result}}
flag is set to
Fail
and
to
Success
otherwise. Next,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
securely records its current timer
value (
\mathsf{time_{L_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}}
) and returns to
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{W}ait}}}
.

An intuitive alternative to timer-based attestation is to perform attestation
upon each
\sf{Msg_{req}}
. The attestation itself consumes far less time/energy than
signing
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
Ḣowever, for a low-end
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
that performs safety-critical tasks,
decoupling attestation from discovery can be more appealing.

(c)
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{R}cv}}}
:

As discussed in Section
3.4
,
DB-PAISA
uses a flexible lazy-response strategy
to amortize signature costs and mitigate potential DoS attacks via
\sf{Msg_{req}}
flooding.
In this state,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
maintains
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
, composed of nonces extracted from
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s.

•

{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
initially transitions to this state when it receives the first
\sf{Msg_{req}}
.
It extracts the nonce from
\sf{Msg_{req}}
,
\mathsf{N_{usr}}
, and places it
into a (initially empty) nonce pool
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
.
It then sets a secure timer to
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
and returns to
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{W}ait}}}
. Note that the timer for
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
is only set when the first
\sf{Msg_{req}}
comes in.

•

If
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
is not empty,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
extracts the nonce and adds it to
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
.
Once
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
reaches
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
transitions to
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}
.
Otherwise, it returns to
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{W}ait}}}
.

•

If it has been transitioned from
\sf{\mathcal{G}en}
(i.e.,
\sf{Msg_{req}}
arrives while generating
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
),
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
extracts the nonce and adds it to a temporary list –
\mathsf{Pool_{{N}_{Tmp}}}
, and returns to
\sf{\mathcal{G}en}
.

Although
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
is a configurable parameter, it is physically upper-bounded by
the amount of space available in
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
, which depends on the network interface and
the underlying wireless medium. This is further discussed in Section
5.4
.

Note that if
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
is set to
1
, a separate

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
is generated for each
\sf{Msg_{req}}
.
Also,
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
should not be set over the physical upper bound because it leads to all
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s ignored until the current
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
is completely handled.
This can result in a false sense of privacy, frustrating users to erroneously think there are no
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s around.

(d)
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}
:

{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
computes attestation time (
\mathsf{time_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}
) as:

\mathsf{time_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}=\mathsf{time_{IoT}}-\mathsf{time_{L_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}}

where
\mathsf{time_{IoT}}
is the current timer value.
Then, it generates
\mathsf{Att_{report}}:=[\mathsf{Att_{result}},\mathsf{time_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}]
, signs the message

\mathsf{Sig_{resp}}:={\sf{\mathcal{S}IG}}(\mathsf{N_{dev}}||\mathsf{Pool_{N}}\\
||\mathsf{URL_{Man}}||\mathsf{Att_{report}}
),
and creates
{\sf{Msg_{resp}}}:=[\mathsf{N_{dev}},\mathsf{Pool_{N}},\mathsf{URL_{Man}},\\
\mathsf{Att_{report}},\mathsf{Sig_{resp}}]
.

{\sf{\mathcal{S}IG}}
is a signing operation and

\mathsf{N_{dev}}
, generated by
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
, is added to randomize
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
.
Next,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
assigns
\mathsf{Pool_{{N}_{Tmp}}}
to
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
, resets
\mathsf{Pool_{{N}_{Tmp}}}
to be empty, and broadcasts
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
.
Note that if an influx of
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s (i.e., # of
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s
>2*
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
) is received within
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
,
\mathsf{|Pool_{{N}_{Tmp}}|}
exceeds
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
.
Then, the first block (of length
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
) of
\mathsf{N_{usr}}
-s from
\mathsf{Pool_{{N}_{Tmp}}}
is moved to
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
after the current
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
is sent out.
The remaining
\mathsf{N_{usr}}
-s in
\mathsf{Pool_{{N}_{Tmp}}}
are in turn handled similarly when
\sf{\mathcal{G}en}
is triggered.
Finally, it goes to the next state with the below conditions:

•

If
\mathsf{|Pool_{{N}_{Tmp}}|}
<
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
, it transitions to
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{W}ait}}}
.

•

Otherwise, it re-enters
\boldsymbol{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}
to process a new
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
with next
\mathsf{N_{usr}}
-s.

If any
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s are received in this state, it goes to
\sf{\mathcal{R}cv}
to handle them.

DB-PAISA
App on
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}

There are two steps:

&

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\sf Request}}

{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}

5.2.
Network

Device Discovery:

We focus on popular wireless media types: WiFi and Bluetooth.
Each has a network discovery procedure. To initiate discovery, one typically broadcasts a
short (specially formatted) network discovery message.
Most fields in this message are reserved and of fixed size.
Thus, using network discovery messages as

\boldsymbol{{\sf{Msg_{req}}}}
&
\boldsymbol{{\sf{Msg_{resp}}}}
Length:

As shown in Figure
6

\sf{Msg_{req}}

18

The minimum size of a

114

We use one extra leading byte for this purpose.
In general, a network discovery packet must allow for at least

Bluetooth
vs

Both WiFi and Bluetooth are common on many types of IoT devices as well as smartphones,

To support lightweight connection-less communication between

\boldsymbol{SW_{{{\sf DB-PAISA}}}}

-s by

is empty, it places

\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
.

As mentioned earlier, two conditions can trigger timer ISR:
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}
and
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
.
This timer ISR checks which timer expired. If it is
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}
, ISR
computes
SHA256
over non-secure memory (including
SW_{dev}
) and generates
\mathsf{Att_{result}}
.
Otherwise, if
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
expired, timer ISR initiates
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation process, which
(1) computes
\mathsf{time_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}
, (2) signs

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
contents (
\mathsf{N_{dev}}
,
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
,
\mathsf{URL_{Man}}
, and
\mathsf{Att_{report}}
) using

ECDSA
(
Pri-

me256v1
curve), (3) composes
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
, and (4)
hands it over to the network module via
UART_WriteBlocking()
.
Note that, in order to prioritize
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation, the timer interrupt from
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}
is
inactivated when
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
is reached.
Also, when
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
is empty, the timer interrupt from
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
is
not
in use, i.e., not set.

When
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
reaches its capacity (
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
),
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation is triggered.

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation can also be triggered by
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
expiring.
UART ISR has the highest priority to receive
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s
and process
\mathsf{N_{usr}}
-s, even during
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation.
If UART ISR receives a new
\sf{Msg_{req}}
while
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
is being generated,

\mathsf{N_{usr}}
from this new
\sf{Msg_{req}}
is stored in a separate temporary list,
\mathsf{Pool_{{N}_{Tmp}}}
.
It is fed into
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
after handling the current
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
.

The current minimum nonce length recommended by NIST
for lightweight cryptography
(
Cybersecurity 2019
)
is
12
bytes.
In Bluetooth 5, a single
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
can contain
\leq\penalty\ 129

nonces, meaning that it can collectively respond to as many
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s. This upper bound is
based on the maximum capacity of Bluetooth extended advertisements (
1,650
bytes)
and other
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
fields, e.g.,
64
-byte signature, and
5
-byte
\mathsf{Att_{report}}
.

Network Module:
ESP32-C3-DevKitC-02 board features Bluetooth
5, which supports Bluetooth extended advertisements. To send/receive such broadcast
messages (
\sf{Msg_{req}}
,
\sf{Msg_{resp}}

6.1.
Security Analysis

We now consider security guarantees of
DB-PAISA
against
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
defined in Section
3.2
.

\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}}
Compromise:
TZ-M guarantees that the
TCB cannot be manipulated by
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
.
Also, due to the highest priority of UART and timer interrupts,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
is guaranteed
to perform
DB-PAISA
tasks (i.e., receiving, processing, and replying to
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s) even
with malware presence in TZ-M Normal world.
Furthermore,
\mathsf{Att_{report}}
contained in
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
reflects the recent
state of
SW_{dev}
which allows
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s to detect compromised
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s.

Forged
\boldsymbol{{\sf{Msg_{resp}}}}
:

\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
cannot generate a valid
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
unless
sk_{\mathsf{IoT}}
is leaked or the public key algorithm used for digital signature computation is broken.

DoS Attacks on
\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}}

can be launched by (1) malware on
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
or (2) other malicious devices over the network.
Since timer ISR and UART ISR are configured as secure, the former can be addressed with

SW_{{{\sf DB-PAISA}}}
’s exclusive control over the network interface. The latter requires the physical presence of

\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
within Bluetooth range of
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
, making this type of attack harder.
This is partially addressed with the lazy-response approach
as discussed in Sections
3.4
and
4.2
.
Other mitigation techniques are outlined in Section
7.2
.

Replay Attacks on
\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}}
:

{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
can readily detect replay attacks by checking whether
\mathsf{N_{usr}}
from an outstanding

\sf{Msg_{req}}
is included in corresponding
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
. Such
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
-s can be simply discarded.

Physical Attacks on
\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}}
:

TZ-M offers secure storage to store secrets and secure boot to thwart non-invasive physical attacks.

6.2.
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
Runtime Overhead

To measure runtime overhead on
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}

\mathsf{U_{Busy}}
to denote CPU usage for
DB-PAISA
trusted
software (
SW_{{{\sf DB-PAISA}}}
), computed as:
\mathsf{U_{Busy}}:=\frac{\mathsf{time_{{{\sf DB-PAISA}}}}}{\mathsf{time_{Normal}}+\mathsf{time_{{{\sf DB-PAISA}}}}}
,
where
\mathsf{time_{Normal}}
is time used for normal device operation (
SW_{dev}
) and
\mathsf{time_{{{\sf DB-PAISA}}}}

\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation overhead is considered

\approx 1
ms) compared to

\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}tt}}}}

1

Also, the signing operation (
231
ms out of

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation.

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
generation takes (almost) constant time even with a barrage of

\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s within one

is inter-announcement

\mathsf{time_{Ann}}
is constant (
\approx 232

\mathsf{U_{Busy}}
is a function of
configurable
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{A}nn}}}}
.

NOTE1:

On single-core IoT devices (with TZ-M), only Secure world or Normal world can run at any given time.
Therefore,
DB-PAISA
execution blocks normal functionality of
SW_{dev}
running in Normal world.
While this is not an issue on multi-core devices,
DB-PAISA
execution still incurs certain
runtime overhead, including the relatively expensive signing operation.

NOTE2:

Listening for
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s does not interfere with normal device functionality, since the network modem
is typically separate from the primary CPU core(s). However, if
SW_{dev}
attempts to send any packets while
DB-PAISA
is running,
SW_{dev}
will hang until
DB-PAISA
execution completes. Similarly, if an external entity
(e.g., a server or a digital twin) attempts to communicate with
SW_{dev}
while
DB-PAISA
is running,
its packets might be dropped and would need to be retransmitted.

7.
Discussion & Limitations

7.1.
Push

vs

Pull
Tradeoffs & Blending

Push
and
Pull
models have trade-offs in terms of energy consumption as well as performance overheads.
Also, network bandwidth utilization in both models depends on the number of
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s and
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s in an area.
The better-suited model is contingent on the deployment use case.
However, it is quite straightforward to blend them and support on-the-fly switching between them.

For example, suppose that
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
is using
Pull
mode. Once it starts receiving an influx of
\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s (say, over a certain threshold) in rapid succession, it switches to
Push
mode.

{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
stays in
Push
mode for a certain period (configurable by
\mathsf{M}
fr) and then switches back to
Pull
mode.
In other words, when the setting experiences an
influx of new users,
Push
mode works better, while
Pull
is better when there are fewer and/or infrequent new users.
Note that
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
need not be aware of whether
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
is running in
Push
model or
Pull
model.
It can collect both announcement messages (from
Push
devices) and
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
-s (from
Pull
devices) once it sends a single
\sf{Msg_{req}}
.
This heuristic is quite easy to implement, in part because announcement messages in
Push
and

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
-s in
Pull
are almost identical format-wise.

7.2.
\sf{Msg_{req}}
DoS Mitigation

As discussed in Section
3.4
, the lazy-response approach does not fully mitigate
DoS attacks: once
\mathsf{Pool_{N}}
size reaches
\mathsf{|Pool_{N}|_{Max}}
,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
composes and signs
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
without waiting for
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
to elapse. Thus, if it is subject to a high volume of incoming

\sf{Msg_{req}}
-s,
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
can be essentially choked.

Intuitively, public key-based signing can be replaced with highly efficient symmetric MACs.
However, this would require the existence and involvement of a trusted third party (e.g.,
\mathsf{M}
fr)
to verify such MACs for
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
. (Recall that
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s and
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s have no prior security context.)
When
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
sends
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
with an HMAC,
{\mathsf{U}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
can ask
\mathsf{M}
fr, via a secure channel, to verify the HMAC.
We consider this approach undesirable as it involves an additional (and trusted) third party, which
can become a bottleneck and would represent a highly attractive attack target.

7.3.
Localization

Localizing
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
-s can help considerably improve user awareness and IoT device transparency.
Although
PAISA
and
DB-PAISA
discover nearby devices, none of them truly and reliably localize them.

Indoor localization using WiFi or Bluetooth poses a challenge.
Localization typically relies on signal strength (e.g., RSSI), timing information (e.g., time-of-flight,
time-of-arrival, and round-trip time), or directional information (e.g., angle-of-arrival).
Signal strength-based localization is relatively imprecise and vulnerable to spoofing attacks

(
Tippenhauer et al. 2009
;
Bauer et al. 2009
;
Humphreys et al. 2008
)
. Meanwhile, timing-based localization is vulnerable to distance
enlargement attacks, whereby the device falsely claims to be farther than it really is
(
Singh et al. 2019
)
.

High-accuracy localization relies on external infrastructure or multiple antennas to triangulate location.
Recently, Bluetooth 5.1
(
Bluetooth Special Interest Group 2019
)
introduced a new feature called Direction Finding, which
supports highly accurate localization.
This requires devices to have multiple antennas to triangulate the locations of other devices.
The emerging Ultra-Wideband (UWB) technology
(
Aiello and Rogerson 2003
)
is promising for indoor localization
due to its high precision and resistance to interference. Nonetheless, these advanced
localization technologies are not yet widely available on commodity IoT devices.

7.4.
TZ-M Alternatives

DB-PAISA
relies on TZ-M to provide guaranteed execution.
It can also be deployed on devices with a Root-of-Trust (RoT) that supports a secure timer, secure network peripherals, secure storage, and task prioritization.
As one of the examples of low-end, GAROTA
(
Aliaj et al. 2022
)
, an active RoT solution for small embedded devices, supports a secure timer, UART, and GPIO.
It customizes hardware to offer these security features.
Alternatively, RISC-V devices can execute
DB-PAISA
using MultiZone
(
HexFive 2019
)
.
It supports Physical Memory Protection (PMP) to isolate secure execution from non-secure tasks.
In addition, I/O PMP (IOPMP) allows configuring peripherals as secure.

7.5.
False Sense of Privacy with Large
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}

Fast response time of
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
through small
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
is relevant to users who linger long enough to receive
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
-s.
Users who run, walk, or cycle by
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
’s location (i.e., move out of
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}
’s WiFi/BT broadcast range too quickly) may miss
{\mathsf{I}_{\mathsf{dev}}}

\sf{Msg_{resp}}
-s.
We expect privacy-concerned users to remain in a given location long enough to receive
\sf{Msg_{resp}}
-s;
\sf{T_{{\sf{\mathcal{G}en}}}}
should be configured by manufacturers to be at most a few seconds to retain users’ attention.

8.
Related Work

Device discovery:

In addition to recent work described in Section
1.1
, some research
(
Das et al. 2018
;
Miao et al. 2015
;
Pappachan et al. 2017
)

introduces a registration-based approach to enhance the transparency of device presence and capabilities.
The technique provides a scalable registry-based privacy infrastructure, where IoT device owners publish information
about their IoT devices and their capabilities in online registries accessible to other users.
This setup assists users in identifying nearby IoT resources and selectively informs users
about the data privacy practices of these resources. Furthermore, IoT-PPA
(
Das et al. 2018
)
facilitates the discovery of user-configurable
settings for IoT resources (e.g., opt-in, opt-out, data erasure), enabling privacy assistants to help
users align their IoT experience with their privacy preferences.

Hidden IoT device detection:

There is also a large body of research focused on discovering hidden devices through either
emitting/measuring signals with specialized hardware or analyzing network traffic.

(
REI 2015
;
Spygadgets 2015
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Gvozdenovic et al. 2022
;
Sami et al. 2021
)
employ specialized hardware to detect hidden
IoT devices using various technologies, including NLJD sensors
(
REI 2015
)
, millimeter waves

(
Li et al. 2018
)
, software-defined radio (SDR) signals
(
Gvozdenovic et al. 2022
)
, and time-of-flight
sensor data
(
Sami et al. 2021
)
. While these methods are effective in detecting the presence of
IoT devices, they are incapable of identifying them.
Moreover, this technique requires users to possess specialized tools to discover IoT devices.

Network traffic analysis involves examining patterns and packets to deduce the presence of devices.
Prior research used this approach to identify devices
(
Ortiz et al. 2019
;
Sharma et al. 2022
)
, detect
active sharing of sensed information
(
Heo et al. 2022
)
, and localize devices
(
Sharma et al. 2022
)
. In particular,

(
Singh et al. 2021
)
achieves these objectives by establishing causality between patterns in
observable wireless traffic. However, network analysis is less effective when IoT devices
communicate infrequently. Also, malicious
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
can evade detection by not communicating
when users are detected nearby or manipulating its network traffic
(
Hou et al. 2021
)
.

IoT privacy:

Another line of IoT privacy research involves suggesting privacy labels designed for IoT devices that inform users about security and privacy concerns.
This entails understanding user concerns, incorporating expert recommendations, and offering the privacy
labels on devices to aid purchasing decisions
(
Feng et al. 2021
;
Emami-Naeini et al. 2020
)
.
Another related research direction explored users’ perceptions of risk and their willingness
to pay for security/privacy features
(
Emami-Naeini et al. 2021
;
Emami-Naeini et al. 2023
)
.

Automated privacy assistants and consent platforms
(
Colnago et al. 2020
;
Hong and Landay 2004
;
Jin et al. 2013
;
Meng et al. 2019
;
Utz et al. 2023
)

have been proposed to assist users in managing privacy settings for IoT devices that
they encounter, especially, in scenarios where the volume of notifications might overwhelm users.

9.
Conclusion

This work presents
DB-PAISA
, a novel approach to enhance privacy and security in IoT ecosystems through a
Pull
-based discovery mechanism.
Unlike previous
Push
-based models,
DB-PAISA
minimizes unnecessary network traffic and interference with device operations by enabling IoT devices to respond to explicit user requests.
Our implementation and evaluation demonstrate
DB-PAISA
’s practicality and efficiency, comparing runtime overheads and energy consumption with the
Push
model.
