UBE3A stabilization of β-catenin preserves synaptic proteins essential for motor and cognitive functions in Angelman Syndrome
Authors: Xin Yang, Yuwen-Alvin Huang, John Marshall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUbiquitin protein ligase E3A (UBE3A) regulates signaling pathways associated with autism spectrum disorders (ASDs). As an E3 ligase, UBE3A ubiquitinates and promotes the degradation of proteins crucial for regulating synaptic plasticity and learning. Loss-of-function mutations in UBE3A result in Angelman Syndrome, while gain-of-function or duplications contribute to ASDs and Dup15q Syndrome.MethodsIn this study, we investigated the effects of UBE3A loss on β-catenin stabilization in iPSC-derived neurons. Background

UBE3A [also known as E6AP ubiquitin-protein ligase (E6AP)] is a HECT-family E3 ligase that regulates signaling pathways related to neurodevelopmental disorders and cervical cancer [1]. The importance of investigating the normal function of UBE3A came to the forefront with the loss of neuronal UBE3A expression as the cause of Angelman syndrome (AS) [2–4], a severe neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability (ID), epilepsy, absence of speech, ataxia, and sleep disturbance [5]. Excess expression of UBE3A due to chromosome 15q11.2–q13.3 duplication (Dup15q) [6, 7] or gain-of-function of UBE3A mutations increases the risk for autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) [8–10], providing an additional impetus to investigate UBE3A function and signaling pathways. While emerging preclinical and clinical treatment strategies hold great promise [11–24], there is no current treatment for AS.

In this study, we provide evidence that developing therapeutics that stabilize β-catenin may offer a new treatment approach for AS and other neurodevelopmental disorders. Maternal UBE3A deletion results in dysregulation of Wnt signaling and other developmental pathways [25]. Abnormal Wnt/β-catenin signaling has been demonstrated in the pathogenesis of autism [9, 26] and neurodegenerative disease [27–30]. Furthermore, β-catenin is part of the cadherin/catenin adhesion complex to regulate synapse formation and memory consolidation [31]. Mutations in CTNNB1 (β-catenin) result in severe ID in humans [32], and depletion of β-catenin in mice results in cognitive impairment and anxiety [33]. Under resting conditions, β-catenin is targeted for proteasomal degradation by a “destruction complex” composed of adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), AXIN, casein kinase 1 (CK1), and glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β), which phosphorylate β-catenin, leading to its ubiquitination by the E3 ubiquitin ligase β-Trcp [34, 35]. Wnt growth factors stimulate the co-receptor complex Frizzled and LRP5/6 [34] leading to the stabilization of β-catenin, which translocates to the nucleus where it interacts with the co-activator TCF/LEF to activate the transcription of target genes [35] involved in neurogenesis [36] and synaptic remodeling [37]. β-catenin is required for the consolidation of fear memory in mice, and inhibition of GSK-3β with lithium chloride (LiCl) stabilizes β-catenin to enhance memory formation [38].

UBE3A gain-of-function leads to the ubiquitination of multiple proteasome subunits that inhibit proteasomal activity [9, 39–44] to decrease β-catenin turnover [9], whereas UBE3A mutations that prevent binding to the proteasome fail to stimulate β-catenin signaling [41]. Here, using human iPSC-induced neuronal cultures and a well-established AS mouse model, we show that the loss of UBE3A results in the depletion of β-catenin. GSK3β inhibition with lithium stabilized β-catenin to ameliorate impaired motor coordination and contextual fear memory in adolescent AS mice. Our study demonstrates that the rapid turnover of synaptic proteins can be prevented by normalizing β-catenin levels in neurons depleted of UBE3A and in AS mice. Additionally, we provide evidence that UBE3A binds to and stabilizes β-catenin. These findings demonstrate that inhibiting GSK3β can alleviate impairments in learning and memory by stabilizing β-catenin, which may provide a new treatment approach for AS and other neurodevelopmental disorders.

Methods

Animals

Mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (UBE3Amat–/pat+, 016590). WT C57BL/6 mice were crossed with female heterozygous B6.129S7–UBE3Atm1Alb/J (with paternal deletion) to obtain heterozygous mice lacking neuronal UBE3A gene expression (AS mice). The genotyping of animals was performed according to the Jackson Laboratory website. All mice were weaned at 3 weeks and housed 2–3 in a cage on a 12 h light/ dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. LiCl groups received lithium-containing Rodent Diet (4 gm/kg LiCl + Grain-Based Rodent Diet in Pellets, Bioserv.F5686) from weaning. Behavior was investigated at 7–8 weeks. The animal numbers used in this study were based on previous reports [16, 18, 45]. All experiments followed the Brown University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Behavior

Rotarod and hindlimb clasping score

Animals were gently placed on the accelerating rotarod (# ENV-575 M; 4–40 rpm in 5 min), and the time to fall was recorded [18]. Two trials, with at least 20 min. intervals were performed per day over 3 consecutive days. The animal was retested if it fell in the first 30 s. The Hindlimb Clasping Score [46] was measured at the end of the Rotarod test for 3 days and averaged.

Fear conditioning

Mice were placed in an electrified, sound-attenuated chamber (Coulbourn Instruments, Allentown, PA) and exposed to a scent (0.1% coconut) diluted in ethanol (70%). Mice were acclimatized to the chamber for 3 min before undergoing three conditioning trials. Each trial involved a tone (5 kHz, 70 dB, 30 s) coinciding with a foot shock (0.7 mA, 1 s) [18]. Conditioning trials were interspersed with a 30-second interval. After conditioning, mice rested in the chamber for 2 min before returning to their home cage. A camera connected to ANY-maze video tracking was used to measure how long mice remained immobile (“freezing”). Context recall was tested by reintroducing the mouse to the same chamber (same scent, no shock) 24 h after conditioning and measuring freezing for 10 min. Cued recall was tested by introducing the mouse to a novel context (different scent, different chamber walls and floor) 48 h after conditioning and measuring freezing in response to the same auditory stimulus used during conditioning. 70% (70%) ethanol was used to clean the chamber between trials.

HEK293T cells

HEK293T cells (ATCC) were grown in DMEM, high glucose (Thermo Fisher Scientific #11965092) with 10% FBS (Millipore Sigma #F4135-500ML) supplemented with antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific #15240062). Cells were expanded into 10 cm petri dishes and seeded into 24-well plates containing coverslips for image experiments. For gene knockdown, the same concentration of siRNAs for UBE3A (siUBE3A, Thermo Fisher Scientific #4390826) and negative control (siNC, Thermo Fisher Scientific #AM4615) were used. LiCl (final concentration 20mM, Sigma-Andrich 203637-10G), GHIR-99021 (1 μm final, MCE #HY-10182), and/or KY19382 (10 μm final, Cayman Chemical Company #37579) were incubated for 24 h. For overexpression, the plasmid (HA-E6 AP Addgene#8648) was transfected with PEI reagent and refreshed medium 4 h later. Cell lysates were harvested after 48 h.

CRISPR-sgRNAs and gene depletion via lentiviruses

The sgRNAs targeting negative control and UBE3A were obtained from Applied Biological Materials Inc. Targeting sgRNA sequences against UBE3A are: sgRNA1, GCTTCAATGTCGTCAGACTG; sgRNA2, CTACTACCACCAGTTAACTG. These sgRNAs were integrated into the backbone of the negative control pLenti-U6-sgRNA-SFFV-Cas9-2 A-Puro. All constructs were packaged into lentiviruses and concentrated by Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara Bio #631231). In each experiment, 5–10 µL of concentrated virus was administered into HEK293T cells and selected by 5ug/ml Puromycin for 2 days. The surviving cells were expanded to validate the efficacy of LiCl and CHIR99021 in a concentration-dependent manner.

Pluripotent stem cell-induced neuron (iNs) generation and treatment

iNs were generated following our previous protocol by forced expression of Neurogenin-2 (NGN2) [47] with the kolf2.J1 stem cell line, a well-characterized control line acquired from the NIH-funded iPSC Neurodegenerative Disease Initiative (iNDI) [48], distributed by the Jackson Laboratory. 2-5ug/ml Puromycin was added to the culture for iNs selection. The selected iNs were cultured in neural basal medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific #10888022) supplemented with N2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #17502001) and B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #17504044). siRNA targeting UBE3A (siUBE3A), and negative control (siNC) were diluted to a 100nM final concentration on ~ DIV8 and incubated for 2–3 days before drug treatments. LiCl (final 20mM) was applied for 24 h before harvesting samples for western blot analysis. For β-catenin inhibition and synaptic protein turnover, DIV20-24 iNs were used. 1 μm β-catenin inhibitors BC2059 or LF3 were added to siUBE3A iNs 1 h before 20mM LiCl or 1 μm CHIR99021 for the indicated time. For NMDA stimulation: DIV21-24 iNs (siNC and siUBE3A) with or without CHIR99021 were incubated with 50 μm NMDA for 5 min before being replaced with normal culture medium for the indicated time points (0, 30 min, 60 min, and 90 min). Whole-cell lysates were harvested for Western blot.

Nuclei and cytosol separation

Nuclei and cytosol samples from HEK293T cells and iNs were separated with a commercial kit following the manual (Thermo Fisher Scientific #78833).

Pull-down assay

Cell lysis was performed with Pierce™ IP Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific # 87787) complemented with the Roche proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Millipore Sigma # 11697498001). One-fifth of the sample was left as a whole cell lysate, mixed with loading buffer, and boiled for a western blot assay, and the remaining sample was incubated with the indicated antibodies overnight at 4℃, followed by resuspension in Protein A/G Magnetic beads for 2 h at room temperature. The beads were collected and resuspended in 2X loading buffer and boiled for 5 min before the western blot for the primary antibodies indicated.

Western blot

All samples were harvested in cell lysis buffer (1% SDS, 0.5% Deoxycholate, 50 mM Sodium phosphate diabasic heptahydrate, 150 mM Sodium chloride, 2mM EDTA, 50 mM Sodium fluoride, 10mM Sodium pyrophosphate decahydrate, 1mM Sodium orthovanadate, 1mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 1% IGEPAL) containing protease inhibitor cocktail on ice and centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was measured using the BCA protein assay before being mixed with 4x Laemmli sample buffer. After 10 min of boiling, the samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred using the Bio-Rad Trans-Blot system. For PFA fixed samples, we referred to the well-established protocol [49]. Briefly, the dissected hippocampus was cut into pieces, and repeatedly pipetting with Insulin Syringes in 500 µl of modified fixed tissue lysis buffer (500 mM TRIS − HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM EDTA, 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1% (v/v) IGEPAL CA-630 (NP40), 2% (w/v) SDS and protease inhibitor cocktail until full dissolved. The homogenates were further incubated at 90 °C for 120 min with agitation at 300 rpm. Supernatants were collected by centrifugation (1000xg) at 4 °C for SDS-PAGE separation. GAPDH, β1-tubulin, or β-actin expression was used as a loading control to ensure that equal amounts of protein were applied to each lane. Primary Antibodies (Ms-E6AP and Ms-Tubulin: Santa Cruz technology sc-166689 and sc-166729; Ms-GAPDH Proteintech #60004-1-IG; Rb-β-catenin, Rb-GSK3pSer9, and Ms-GSK3β Cell Signaling Technology #8480s, #9336s, and #9832S; Rb-PSD95, Rb-GluA4 Proteintech #20665-1-AP and #23350-1-AP; Ms-GluA1 Antibodies Incorporated #75–327, Rabbit-anti Ubiquitin Cell Signaling Technology #43124) were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ after blocking in TBST buffers. Membranes were developed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology 7074 S and 7076 S) and filmed under iBright Imaging Systems. Band intensities were measured by the ImageJ system (https://imagej.net/ij/).

Immunofluorescent staining

Perfusion was performed after anesthesia (Ketamine/Xylazine (100/10 mg/kg)) with iced cold PBS and 4% Paraformaldehyde (PFA, Millipore Sigma P6148-1KG) in PBS. Mouse brains were dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 ℃. After sequential dehydration in 15% and 30% sucrose, mouse brains were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT, VWR #25608-930) and coronally cut into 25 μm frozen sections. Sequential sections were collected every 5 sections on charged microscope slides (Globe Scientific 1354 W). After at least 2 h air dry, slides were washed with PBS to remove OCT and blocked for 30 min in 5% normal goat serum (Fisher Scientific 16-210-064). Primary antibodies (Ms-E6AP: Santa Cruz technology sc-166689 and Sigma SAB1404508; Rb-β-catenin Cell Signaling Technology 8480s; Rb-GSK3β-pSer9 Cell Signaling Technology #9336s; Ms-GluA1 Antibodies Incorporated #75–327, and Gp-MAP2 Synaptic Systems 188004, Chicken Synaptotagmin-1 Synaptic system #105011) were prepared in 5% normal goat serum with 0.1% Triton-X100 (Fisher Scientific PI85111) and incubated for 4 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 ℃. Secondary Antibodies (Goat-anti Mouse 568, Goat anti-Rabbit 488, Goat-anti-Guinea Pig 647: Thermo Fisher Scientific A11004, A32731 and A21450) prepared in 5% normal goat serum with 0.1% Triton-X100 were incubated for 3 h at room temperature after 3 times wash by PBS. Slides were mounted with Fluoromount-G™ Mounting Medium with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific 00-4959-52). Coverslip staining was performed after 4% PFA fixation for 10 min at room temperature. Images were acquired by BioTek Gen5 Microscopy or Zeiss LSM 880 Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope. We used the ImageJ system to measure the intensities of the targeted protein (https://imagej.net/ij/). 3–4 images from the same animal were averaged for comparison.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

mRNA samples were obtained with QiagenTM RNeasy Mini Kit (74104). cDNA was synthesized by the iScript™ gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad #1725035) after potential DNA contamination was cleaned. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the CFX96 Touch Deep Well Real-Time PCR Detection System. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method and are presented as fold changes relative to a control sample after normalization against the expression of the housekeeping gene GAPDH. FAM probes for UBE3A, syt1, PSD-95, glua1, glua4, and GAPDH were purchased from (iDT) Integrated DNA Technologies.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 was used to create graphs and perform statistical quantification. The unpaired t-test was used for quantification in experiments with only two groups. One-way/Two-way ANOVA with post-hoc analysis was performed to calculate statistical significance unless otherwise indicated. The normality of the normalized measurements used in each comparison was tested with a Shapiro–Wilk test; P > 0.05 was considered as a normal distribution. P values of our statistics are included in Table S1. A coefficient of variation for the internal control samples on each immunoblot was calculated, and values for the experimental samples were accepted as valid when the percent coefficient of variation (CV) for the internal control samples was less than 10%. All results are presented as mean ± S.E.M., n means independent experiments or animal numbers, and the significance is indicated by * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, or ****P < 0.0001.

Results

UBE3A regulates β-catenin stability in human iPSC-induced neurons

In the canonical Wnt-β-catenin pathway, in the absence of Wnt, GSK3β phosphorylates β-catenin in the destruction complex, targeting β-catenin for proteasomal degradation [34]. Previous studies have reported that UBE3A ubiquitinates proteasome-related proteins [42]. Excessive UBE3A activity inhibits proteasome function to stabilize β-catenin indirectly [9]. Here, we investigated the possibility that UBE3A may directly modulate the Wnt-β-catenin destruction complex. We evaluated GSK3β activity and the stability of β-catenin in human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)-induced neurons (iNs) [50–53], which we previously demonstrated to recapitulate dysregulated signaling upon UBE3A depletion [16, 18]. The N-terminal phosphorylation of GSK3β at Ser9 (GSK3β-pSer9) by Akt activity or lithium treatment creates a pseudo-substrate that inhibits GSK3 activity, and detection of GSK3β-pSer9 can be used as a surrogate marker for GSK3β activity [54]. Following UBE3A knockdown in cultured iNs (days in vitro 11, DIV11), we observed decreased GSK3β-Ser9 phosphorylation, indicating augmentation of GSK3β activity, and consequently, β-catenin expression was reduced (Fig. 1A and B). Similar results were observed in HEK293T cells, where overexpression of HA-tagged UBE3A increased β-catenin levels (Fig. S1A), in agreement with a previous study showing HPV16 E6 and E6AP/UBE3A differentially cooperate to stimulate or augment Wnt signaling [55]. Conversely, the depletion of UBE3A resulted in a significant decrease in the level of GSK3β-pSer9 (Fig. S1B). Consistent with the increased activity of GSK3β, we observed a decrease in β-catenin levels in whole cell lysates by immunoblot analysis (Fig. S1B).

To explore whether UBE3A associates with β-catenin, either directly or indirectly, to regulate stability, a co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay was performed from iNs. β-catenin was detected in cell lysates from siNC but not siUBE3A iNs pulled down by UBE3A antibody, indicating that β-catenin associates with UBE3A (Fig. 1C). These results agree with a previous study showing that UBE3A physically binds β-catenin to mediate nonproteolytic polyubiquitylation [56].

In our model, the presence of UBE3A is predicted to decrease the activity of the destruction complex to slow the degradation of β-Catenin. To explore this possibility, we inhibited protein translation in DIV11 iNs using Anisomycin. In siNC iNs, we observed a gradual degradation of β-catenin, whereas the degradation rate of β-catenin in siUBE3A iNs was significantly enhanced (Fig. 1D and E). These results further demonstrate that UBE3A acts to slow down the turnover of β-catenin.

To investigate the translational potential of GSK3β inhibitors in Angelman syndrome (AS), we evaluated lithium regulation of β-catenin. Lithium is a well-documented inhibitor of GSK3 used in the treatment of bipolar disorder and tested for beneficial effects in Fragile X syndrome (FXS), where elevated GSK3 activity is also observed [57, 58]. Following UBE3A knockdown in cultured DIV11 iNs, treatment with 20 mM lithium chloride (LiCl) to inhibit GSK3β activity [59] led to increased GSK3β-pSer9 and normalized β-catenin expression, as shown by immunoblot assays (Fig. 1F and G) and immunofluorescence staining of iNs (Fig. 1H and I). Similar results were observed in HEK293T cells, where treatment of UBE3A knockdown cells with lithium normalized β-catenin levels (Fig. S1B). To confirm that lithium, a non-specific GSK3β inhibitor, acts via GSK3β inhibition, we also evaluated the effect of KY19382, a specific small molecule inhibitor of GSK3β [60]. In control HEK293T cells, immunofluorescent staining of β-catenin showed that KY19382 and lithium increased β-catenin fluorescence intensity (Fig. S1C). Notably, UBE3A knockdown robustly reduced the immunofluorescence intensity of β-catenin expression, whereas KY19382 restored β-catenin intensity to control levels, while lithium raised β-catenin intensity above control levels (Fig. S1C).

Lithium ameliorates motor coordination and memory deficits in an Angelman Syndrome mouse model

The in vitro results demonstrate that lithium inhibition of GSK3β can reverse the decreased levels of β-catenin caused by UBE3A depletion. To test the efficacy of lithium for treating AS, we used the UBE3A exon 2 mouse model (UBE3Amat−/pat+, or E6-AP UBE3Atm1Alb), which has been proven to be a reliable in vivo model that recapitulates relevant behaviors, including deficits in contextual fear memory and motor abilities [2, 11, 61–65]. To examine the effects of lithium, juvenile AS animals (UBE3Amat−/pat+, both genders, postnatal 21 days, P21) and littermate controls (UBE3Amat+/pat+) were randomly housed with or without a lithium chloride-containing food pellet preparation (lithium-Diet and normal-Diet respectively) until young adult (P49), at which point behavioral assays and histological analysis were performed (Fig. 2A). The hindlimb clasping test was used to monitor the progression of CNS pathology [46], and was effective in distinguishing AS (UBE3Amat−/pat+) mice from the littermate wild type (WT) control (UBE3Amat+/pat+) mice (Fig. 2C). The lithium diet significantly mitigated motor and contextual-fear learning deficits of AS mice (Fig. 2B and D). Utilizing the three-day accelerating rotarod protocol [16], we observed a pronounced impairment in the motor learning abilities of AS mice at baseline as well as during the behavioral assessment (Fig. 2B). Strikingly, AS mice raised on the lithium diet exhibited a significant improvement in motor learning relative to AS mice on the normal diet (Fig. 2B). In the contextual fear conditioning test, all the animals learned the contextual environment by foot shock (Fig. 2E). However, AS mice fed on the normal diet lacked freezing behavior during the context recall session after 24 h (Fig. 2D), demonstrating deficits in contextual fear memory [2]. AS animals fed on the lithium diet performed similarly to WT mice, indicating that lithium had a significant beneficial effect on memory (Fig. 2D). In agreement with previous reports [66], WT mice fed the lithium diet did not exhibit significant motor deficits. However, WT mice on the lithium diet performed more poorly on contextual fear memory (Fig. 2D), suggesting that lithium may be eliciting cognitive impairment in WT mice. As previously reported [2, 18], we did not observe behavioral differences during cued fear recall between groups (Fig. 2E). We also did not observe any discernible differences between the male and female mice in the present study.

Lithium chloride stabilizes β-catenin and upregulates synaptic proteins in the Angelman Syndrome mouse

Following the behavioral assessment, we conducted a histopathological examination to assess β-catenin levels in the hippocampal CA1 and CA3 areas and layers 4–6 of the motor cortex (Fig. 2F and S2A). Immunostaining revealed strong expression of UBE3A in neurons of WT mice, which was absent in AS mice (Fig. 2G). No detectable differences were observed in the neuronal population as revealed by DAPI staining of the gross brain structure (Fig. 2G and S2C). In WT mice, we observed strong cytosolic neuronal β-catenin staining (Fig. 2H-I and S2A). In the CA3 region of normal-diet AS mice, β-catenin was significantly reduced compared to WT mice, which was rescued by the lithium diet (Fig. 2H-I and S2A). Interestingly, we observed a region-dependent effect on β-catenin levels in the CA1 region and layers 4–6 of the motor cortex in AS mice. In layers 4–6 of the motor cortex of AS mice, the reduced β-catenin levels were normalized by the lithium diet (Fig. 2I). In the CA1 region, the lithium diet trended to increased β-catenin in the AS mice but did not reach significance (Fig. 2H-I and S2A), which could be due to the generally weaker β-catenin expression in the rodent CA1 region [38].

Wnt-β-catenin signaling induces BDNF expression [67] and disruption of Wnt-β-catenin signaling could account for decreased BDNF in the AS mouse brain [68]. In support, we observed reduced expression of BDNF in AS mice (Fig. 3A, B, and S2B), whereas mice on the lithium diet had a significantly increased expression of BDNF in CA3 neurons (Fig. 3A, B, and S2B). Notably, immunostaining revealed a strong colocalization of BDNF signals with MAP2 signals, suggesting autocrine BDNF expression from neurons during GSK3β inhibition. As BDNF signaling plays a key role in regulating synaptic strength, we also stained for synaptic proteins, finding that the protein levels of Synaptotagmin-1 (SYT1) and the GluA1 subunit of the α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor subunit in AS mice were significantly reduced compared to WT mice, which was normalized by the lithium diet (Fig. 3C, D, and S2C). Immunoblotting of hippocampal lysates from the normal diet AS group confirmed the reduced expression of β-catenin, BDNF, and GluA1 (Fig. 3E, F). The reduced expression of β-catenin and BDNF was significantly upregulated in the AS animals by the Li-diet to WT levels (Fig. 3E, F), with a trend towards increased GluA1 (Fig. 3E, F). To determine whether lithium rescue of synaptic expression was dependent on β-catenin activity, we examined the effects of the β-catenin inhibitors, BC2059 and LF3, on iNs following the knockdown of UBE3A. As shown in Fig. 3G, H, the protein expression of BDNF, GluA1, and GluA4 in siUBE3A iNs was decreased compared to siNC iNs but normalized by treatment with LiCl and the highly selective GSK3 inhibitor CHIR99021 [69]. In control iNs (Fig. S3A), LiCl and CHIR99021 increased the protein expression of β-catenin, BDNF, and GluA1 (Fig. S3B, C). Notably, the ability of LiCl and CHIR99021 to restore the protein levels of these synaptic proteins was blocked by the β-catenin inhibitors BC2059 and LF3 (Fig. 3G, H). The ability of the β-catenin inhibitors to impede the effects of lithium strongly suggests that GSK3β inhibition leading to the stabilization of β-catenin is required to upregulate these synaptic proteins.

Lithium elevates β-catenin in both the cytoplasm and nucleus

In the nucleus, β-catenin acts as a cofactor to regulate gene expression through the transactivation of the LEF1/TCF transcription factors. We hypothesized that lithium rescue of the protein levels of SYT1 and GluA1 (Fig. 3C, D) could be accounted for by increased nuclear β-catenin levels and enhanced transcription. To examine whether GSK3β regulates the nuclear pool of β-catenin, we examined the subcellular localization of β-catenin in established stable HEK293T cell lines in which UBE3A was downregulated by introducing CRISPR constructs with sgRNA targeting UBE3A. Immunoblotting confirmed the downregulation of UBE3A in the two lines of sgRNA (Fig. 4A). In the Lenti-sgUBE3A lines, β-catenin was significantly reduced, and LiCl and CHIR99021 dose-dependently stabilized β-catenin (Fig. 4B). As shown by immunoblotting, UBE3A loss resulted in decreased β-catenin in both the cytosolic and nuclear fractions, which was restored by both LiCl and CHIR99021 incubation (Fig. 4C). The successful fractionation was confirmed by the cytosolic protein marker GAPDH and the nuclear marker Lamin B1 (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, a similar rescue was observed in iNs samples, where LiCl and CHIR99021 comparably normalized β-catenin in both cytosol and nuclei (Fig. S4A, B). These results support that lithium promotes nuclear β-catenin levels and potentially regulates the transcription of synaptic proteins.

Lithium blocks activity-induced loss of β-catenin and synaptic proteins

A bioinformatic study found several synaptic genes, including SYT1, as potential novel Wnt-β-catenin target genes [70]. The histological staining of AS mouse brain (Fig. 3C, D, and S2C) and western blotting of siUBE3A iNs (Fig. 3G, H) demonstrate a reduction in the protein levels of SYT1 and GluA1. Our results are also supported by analyzing the Angelman Syndrome Proteome (ASP, https://www.angelman-proteome-project.org/), in which the downregulation of SYT1, GluA1, and GluA2 was identified in AS-patient (chromosome deletion including UBE3A) -derived neurons (Fig. S4C). To explore β-catenin-dependent transcriptional regulation, we performed q-PCR to quantify the mRNA expression of SYT1 and the α-AMPA receptor subunit GluA1, as well as GluA3 and PSD-95 in siUBE3A iNs. We observed a significant reduction of SYT1 mRNA levels in siUBE3A iNs compared to control iNs, which was rescued following 3 h of incubation with 1 μm CHIR99021 or 20mM LiCl (Fig. 5A). In siUBE3A iNs, there were no significant changes in the mRNA levels of GluA1, GluA3, or PSD-95 compared to control cells under basal conditions (Fig. 5B). However, CHIR99021 incubation significantly increased GluA3 and PSD-95 mRNA, with a trend toward increased GluA1 mRNA (Fig. 5B). Lithium treatment trended toward increased GluA1, GluA3, and PSD-95 mRNA (Fig. 5B). These results suggest that GSK3β inhibition leads to β-catenin-dependent transcription of synaptic proteins.

In addition to the role of β-catenin in gene expression, β-catenin acts pre- and post-synaptically to regulate synapse formation and function [31]. In AS mice, synaptic plasticity is disrupted with impaired hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP) [2, 18, 71] and enhanced long-term depression (LTD) [72]. To examine whether NMDAR-dependent LTD could affect β-catenin stability, DIV21-24 iNs were stimulated with NMDA, a chemical protocol (chem-LTD) well-established to trigger a long-lasting synaptic depression [73]. In UBE3A-depleted iNs, NMDA stimulation led to the rapid degradation of β-catenin compared to control iNs, and inhibition of GSK3β with CHIR99021 blocked the loss of β-catenin (Fig. 5C, D). NMDA-dependent LTD is reported to lead to the degradation of the GluA1 AMPAR subunit and PSD-95 [74, 75]. In agreement, we found that NMDA stimulation decreased the protein levels of both PSD-95 and GluA1 (Fig. 5C, D). However, UBE3A knockdown significantly enhanced the rate of degradation (Fig. 5C, D), and pre-incubation with CHIR99021 prevented PSD-95 and GluA1 degradation (Fig. 5C, D). These results show that in addition to regulating transcription, loss of UBE3A impairs β-catenin signaling pathways that control the homeostasis of synaptic proteins (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

The mechanisms by which UBE3A deficiency results in severe cognitive deficits and motor dysfunction in neurodevelopmental disorders remain poorly understood. We show that UBE3A controls β-catenin levels to influence synaptic function through both transcription and protein homeostasis (Fig. 5E; simplified diagram of UBE3A regulation). Excessive UBE3A protein expression inhibits proteasomal activity to stabilize β-catenin [9, 41]. We report that UBE3A can also promote β-catenin stability through the inhibition of GSK3β activity, which points to UBE3A inhibition of the destruction complex-mediated degradation of β-catenin in the canonical Wnt pathway. In AS, the absence of UBE3A elicited an increase in β-catenin degradation, suggesting upregulation of the destruction complex. Our results are consistent with a previous human papillomavirus (HPV) study showing that E6AP/UBE3A has a regulatory function in the Wnt signaling pathway [55]. Sominsky et al. (2014) demonstrated that E6AP/UBE3A stabilization of β-catenin depends on the activity of the β-catenin destruction complex [55]. The same group further reported that UBE3A directly ubiquitinates β-catenin [56] to mediate nonproteolytic polyubiquitylation of β-catenin, which could potentially stabilize β-catenin [56]. Extending these findings, we show that UBE3A restricts GSK3β activity to stabilize β-catenin in neurons. We found that the GSK3β inhibitors, lithium, KY19832, and CHIR99021, enhanced inhibitory GSK3β-Ser9 phosphorylation to increase β-catenin levels in UBE3A-depleted HEK293T cells and human pluripotent stem cell-induced neurons (iNs).

Lithium induces inhibitory Ser9-GSK3β phosphorylation through direct inhibition of GSK3β itself [54] or phosphorylation of Ser9-GSK3β mediated by several different kinases, including Akt, protein kinase A, protein kinase C, and p70 S6 kinase [76]. BDNF upregulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling [77, 78], and impaired crosstalk may contribute to the dysregulation of Wnt-GSK3β-β-catenin signaling. We previously reported deficits in BDNF-Akt-mTOR and PLC-CaMKII signaling in AS mice [79]. As BDNF signaling inhibits GSK3β activity through Akt phosphorylation of GSK3β-Ser9 [80], the deficits in BDNF-Akt signaling could contribute to overactive GSK3β, leading to the downregulation of β-catenin [81]. In support, we detected lower BDNF expression in AS mice that was upregulated by GSK3β inhibition, and BDNF upregulation was β-catenin-dependent in iNs. Thus, the Wnt/β-catenin and BDNF signaling pathways act cooperatively to regulate dendritic spine formation and synaptic plasticity [67, 82]. Similar to β-catenin signaling [37, 38, 83], BDNF promotes synaptic plasticity [84–87] and spine formation [88], the dysfunction of which could account for the abnormalities observed in AS [89–92], including deficits in experience-dependent spine maturation [92] and maintenance [90].

The role of β-catenin in controlling the transcription and degradation of synaptic proteins may explain the synaptic defects observed in AS [25, 93]. Loss-of-function mutations in the CTNNB1 gene encoding β-catenin in ASD probands result in intellectual disability and microcephaly, similar to AS [94]. β-catenin regulates the transcription of pre- and postsynaptic proteins, including the scaffold protein PSD-95 [95]. In siUBE3A iNs, we observed a significant reduction in PSD-95 and SYT1, both at the mRNA and protein level, which was mitigated by GSK3β inhibition. These results support β-catenin-dependent transcriptional regulation.

In addition to transcriptional regulation, β-catenin is localized at pre- and post-synaptic compartments, where it regulates synaptic vesicle clustering and synaptic strength [31]. The activity of GSK3β is enhanced during LTD [96]. Our results show that loss of UBE3A promotes rapid chem-LTD degradation of β-catenin, in addition to PSD-95 and GluA1, which was prevented by GSK3β inhibition. UBE3A deficiency enhances autophagic activity [18, 97], and increased GSK-3β activity is known to upregulate autophagy [98]. NMDA-dependent LTD is associated with AMPAR subunit (GluA1 and GluA2) and PSD-95 autophagosome-dependent degradation [75, 99]. β-catenin has been reported to suppress autophagy [100], and the stabilization of β‐catenin by GSK-3β inhibition could mitigate LTD-inducing degradative autophagy. Therefore, UBE3A may maintain synaptic homeostasis through the linking of two pivotal systems: the ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagy [101].

In the AS mouse model, a lithium diet restored β-catenin levels to WT levels to ameliorate behavioral deficits. Using the contextual fear conditioning assay to measure learning and memory, lithium-treated AS mice showed improvements comparable to WT. In these 7–8 weeks of age adolescent mice, we observed a partial rescue of motor coordination in rotarod performance across three days. These results are consistent with our previous studies reporting that restoration of BDNF-TrkB signaling similarly showed partial rescue in motor learning in juvenile mice [16, 18]. Accumulating work with conditional UBE3A reinstatement demonstrates that the “critical period” in AS mice is phenotype-dependent: early restoration (postnatal day 0–21) rescues motor coordination and other sensorimotor deficits [102], whereas adult reinstatement cannot. In contrast, later re-expression can still normalize prefrontal-cortical excitatory/inhibitory balance [103] and restore hippocampal long-term potentiation and associated learning tasks in adolescent or adult animals [16, 18, 104, 105]. Whether a comparable window exists in humans is unknown, but our previous study [18], along with reports from other neurodevelopmental disorders [57], suggest that initiating therapy as early as feasible is likely to yield the greatest benefit.

Although there are numerous ongoing therapeutic approaches to treat AS, including the reactivation of the silenced paternal allele [12, 15, 21, 106, 107], there are currently no FDA-approved therapeutic interventions to treat the major symptoms of this devastating disorder [108]. This study highlights the potential of β-catenin stabilization through GSK3β inhibition for treating AS. Understanding the role of UBE3A regulation of GSK3β and β-catenin in neuronal function may provide insight into other neurological and neurodevelopmental disorders [58, 93]. Lithium is the mainstay for the treatment of bipolar disorder [109], and in Angelman syndrome, lithium can be used for behavior management [110]. In a pilot fragile X syndrome trial, lithium treatment of individuals ages 6–23 was reported to be well-tolerated and appeared to improve verbal communication in the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status List Learning [57]. In Phelan-McDermid syndrome, lithium therapy reversed clinical regression and stabilized behavioral symptoms [111]. Rigorous pediatric data remain sparse: long-term safety in children has not been established, and even therapeutic serum lithium concentrations can trigger nausea, tremors, and renal or thyroid dysfunction [112]. Short-term studies nevertheless suggest that low-dose lithium is well tolerated in children with intellectual disability and may confer modest cognitive benefits [57, 113, 114]. Given the risk of adverse effects, any dietary or pharmacological use of lithium should proceed only under direct medical supervision [112]. To avoid such limitations, next-generation therapeutics—potent, highly selective GSK3β inhibitors [58] or direct β-catenin stabilizers—are being pursued as potentially safer alternatives [115].
