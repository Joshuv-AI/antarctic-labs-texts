Prediction of compressive strength of recycled concrete cylinders reinforced with fiber-reinforced polymer using a combined genetic algorithm and neural network approach
Authors: Hossein Khosravi, Marzie Mohammadi Pour, Mohammad Bahram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Concrete is one of the most widely used construction materials and may experience cracking and erosion over time, leading to reduced strength and structural performance. Using recycled concrete as aggregate is an effective approach to minimize construction waste. This study employs a hybrid computational approach combining Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) to predict the mechanical behavior of recycled aggregate concrete cylinders reinforced with Fiber-Reinforced Polymer (FRP) tubes. Laboratory data from the research of Jin and Yan, involving two types of FRP—flax and polyester—were utilized. The developed model demonstrated high accuracy in predicting compressive strength (R2 = 0.94) and ultimate strain (R2 = 0.91). Sensitivity analysis revealed that the elastic modulus, tensile strength, and thickness of the FRP layers have the most significant impact on the stress–strain response. These results confirm the model’s reliability and its potential application in designing sustainable and efficient reinforced concrete structures.

Introduction

Concrete is the most widely used construction material in the world. It comprises 50–70% of all demolition and construction (D&C) waste1. Recycling and reusing concrete and old bricks from D&C waste in recycled dense concrete has been widely studied2. This research aims to predict the behavior of recycled concrete cylinders reinforced with FRP using a hybrid model combining a genetic algorithm and an artificial neural network (ANN(.

This research uses clay brick waste and recycled concrete (RCBA-RAC) as recycled coarse aggregates composed of Jinke resources, including 60% clay brick aggregates and 40% recycled concrete and mortar aggregates by mass. The size of recycled and natural coarse aggregates ranges from 5 to 15 mm.

In recent years, there has been growing attention to the structural and mechanical behavior of high-toughness concrete mixtures incorporating recycled aggregates and supplementary fibers. Several studies have investigated the effect of alkali content, silicate modulus, fiber distribution, and mesoscopic properties on performance characteristics of such materials3,4. These investigations demonstrate significant potential in enhancing ductility, toughness, and stiffness retention under loading conditions, especially when reinforced with microfibers or steel fibers. Incorporating these insights can lead to a better understanding of failure mechanisms and long-term behavior of eco-friendly concrete systems.

Soft computing is the basis for the growing field of computational intelligence. This branch of computation is a set of methods used to obtain control, high accuracy, and low-cost solutions by handling error ranges or uncertainties. Soft computing includes techniques such as fuzzy logic (FL), artificial neural networks (ANNs), and evolutionary algorithms (EAs), which are not derived from classical theoretical methods5,6.

Many studies have investigated concrete containing recycled aggregates, genetic algorithms, and neural networks. For example, Ma et al.7 proposed a modified stress–strain formula for damaged concrete reinforced with BFRP based on altered strength and strain. They concluded that BFRP is an effective strengthening material for damaged concrete column members after an earthquake.

Hsin Chu investigated the potential of artificial neural networks (ANNs) to predict the compressive strength of high-performance concrete and found that ANN models are more accurate than regression-based models. These models allow for numerical testing to assess the effect of each variable on concrete mixes.

Chithra et al. developed multiple regression analysis (MRA) and ANN models to predict the compressive strength of high-performance concrete containing nano-silica and copper slag. Their study modeled three datasets and concluded that ANN models offered higher accuracy and correlation.

Dias et al.8 used neural networks to predict the strength and slump of ready-mix and high-strength concrete mixes containing chemical and mineral additives. Oreta and Kawashima9 applied neural networks to predict the compressive strength and strain of reinforced circular columns, achieving greater accuracy than other methods.

Sadr al-Sadat et al.10 used gene expression programming to model the compressive strength and strain of reinforced concrete columns, while Ozbakkaloglu and Gholampour et al.11 applied this method to predict properties of concrete with recycled materials. Jin and Yan12 developed multivariate statistical models to estimate the mechanical behavior of recycled aggregate concrete confined with FRP.

Despite the increasing use of recycled aggregates and the promising capabilities of AI-based models, predicting the behavior of FRP-confined recycled concrete remains a complex task due to the nonlinear interactions of material properties and confinement mechanisms. Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of combining artificial neural networks with genetic algorithms to accurately predict the compressive strength of geopolymer concrete13. This hybrid approach enhances modeling capabilities by optimizing network parameters and capturing complex nonlinear relationships within recycled or sustainable concrete materials.

While various studies have applied artificial neural networks or genetic algorithms separately, few have combined these methods to predict the behavior of FRP-reinforced recycled concrete. Additionally, limited research compares the performance of hybrid models against conventional regression-based approaches in this context.

The enhancement of concrete with steel fibers (SFRC) has become prevalent in structures due to its improved crack resistance, but high temperatures can degrade its properties. Machine learning (ML) has been employed to estimate concrete properties and optimize processes. Ali et al.14 utilized ML methods such as Genetic Programming (GEP), Multipath Programming (MEP), XGBoost, and Bayesian Estimation (BES) to predict the compressive strength (CS) of steel fiber-reinforced concrete at elevated temperatures. Their study, using 307 experimental data points, indicated that GEP maintained stable accuracy in predicting CS under various temperature conditions, emphasizing factors like temperature and steel fiber content.

In the context of concrete durability, fly ash is recognized as a supplementary material for enhancing concrete properties. However, predicting the depth of wear in concrete modified with fly ash presents a challenge. Khan et al.15 applied two ML methods, MEP and GEP, to predict the wear depth of concrete modified with fly ash. This study, based on 216 experimental data points, considered various concrete mix parameters and demonstrated the efficacy of these models in estimating wear depth.

This study contributes to the advancement of sustainable construction by promoting the use of recycled materials and leveraging intelligent modeling techniques to enhance prediction accuracy. It supports the reduction of physical testing needs and encourages efficient design approaches in structural engineering.

The novelty of this research lies in developing a hybrid predictive model that integrates a genetic algorithm with an artificial neural network to forecast the behavior of recycled concrete cylinders confined with FRP. This integrated approach is expected to improve prediction accuracy and provide a more reliable tool for evaluating eco-friendly structural systems.

Despite the advancements in the field of recycled concrete and FRP confinement, significant gaps remain in the precise modeling of its mechanical behavior. Previous research has often faced challenges in accurately capturing the complex stress–strain relationships of FRP-confined recycled concrete. These challenges primarily include the inability to effectively account for non-linear interactions and the dynamic responses arising from variations in FRP confinement ratios and recycled concrete properties. Furthermore, many existing models have struggled to adequately address the geometrical non-linearities and material heterogeneity inherent in these composites.

To address this research gap, the present study introduces an enhanced prediction model: the GA–ANN framework. This innovative approach synergistically leverages the optimization capabilities of Genetic Algorithms (GA) with the predictive power of Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). It enables the analysis of intricate non-linear relationships between input parameters, thereby providing highly accurate predictions for the behavior of FRP-confined recycled concrete. This framework not only aids in key feature selection and reduces computational costs but also significantly improves prediction accuracy and enhances result interpretability. Consequently, the GA–ANN model represents a substantial advancement over previous prediction methodologies, offering more precise and reliable analyses of the mechanical performance of FRP-confined recycled concrete.

Research methodology

Genetic algorithm

As an optimization calculation algorithm, the genetic algorithm effectively searches different areas of the solution space by considering the sets of solution space points in each calculation iteration16. Briefly, the genetic algorithm consists of the following five operators:

Coding

This step is perhaps the most complicated step of solving the problem by the algorithm method. Instead of working on the parameters or variables of the problem, the genetic algorithm deals with their coded form. One of the coding methods is binary coding, which converts the answer to the problem into strings of binary numbers (in base 2).

Evaluation

The fitness function is obtained by applying the appropriate transformation on the objective function, the function to be optimized. This function evaluates each string with a numerical value that determines its quality. The higher the quality of the answer thread, the higher the fitness value of the answer, and the probability of participation for the next generation will increase.

Composition

The most important operator in the genetic algorithm is the combination operator. Combination is a process in which the old generation of chromosomes is mixed and combined to create a new generation. The pairs considered parents in the selection section exchange their genes and create new members. Mixing in the genetic algorithm eliminates the dispersion or genetic diversity of the population because it allows good genes to find each other.

Mutation

Mutation is another operator that produces other possible answers. In the genetic algorithm, after a member is created in the new population, each gene is mutated with the probability of mutation. In the mutation, a gene may be removed from the set of genes in the population, or a gene may be added to it that has never existed in the population. The mutation of a gene means the change of that gene, and depending on the type of coding, different mutation methods are used.

Decoding

After applying the combined genetic algorithm and neural network approach to optimize the parameters of recycled concrete cylinders reinforced with fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP), a decoding step is essential. This step translates the encoded solutions obtained by the algorithm into practical engineering values that can be used for further analysis.

In the genetic algorithm, the roulette wheel selection method is used to probabilistically select chromosomes based on their fitness values. Statistical analysis of data banks

In this study, experimental data collected by Jin and Yan have been utilized. The final dataset comprises 34 cylindrical samples of FRP-reinforced recycled concrete. To train and evaluate the proposed model, these data have been divided into two subsets:

Training Set: A total of 23 samples were randomly selected from the entire dataset and used for training the neural network. This set is employed to learn the patterns and relationships within the data.

Testing Set: The remaining 11 samples were randomly chosen for the final evaluation of the model’s performance. This set is used to assess the model’s generalization ability on unseen data.

This division allows for a precise evaluation of the model’s performance and ensures appropriate control over overfitting. Artificial neural network

In the optimization process, the artificial neural network (ANN) plays a key role by minimizing the difference between predicted and actual values through a cost function. This function helps guide the learning process and improve the model’s prediction accuracy over successive iterations.

The ANN processes input variables through layers of interconnected nodes (neurons), each with adjustable weights. These weights determine the strength of influence between neurons in different layers. As the input values move through the network, they are transformed by activation functions, which introduce non-linearity and enable the ANN to learn complex relationships. Through repeated training cycles, the network adjusts its internal parameters to optimize performance and make accurate predictions based on new data.

Weighing the input values and transferring them through the network is done through connections associated with adjustable weights. Each neuron receives multiple inputs, and these are aggregated and passed through an activation function. We used a feed-forward neural network to develop a dynamic model. The feed-forward neural network is the most basic and straightforward type of artificial neural network, where information flows in one direction only. Starting from the input nodes (neurons), it passes through any hidden layers (if present) and moves toward the output nodes.

In simpler terms, this type of network does not involve loops or recurrent connections. Our model employs a simple structure with only one hidden layer in the feed-forward network. When an input is introduced to the network, its output is compared to the desired target. According to the learning rules, necessary adjustments are made to the weights and biases to minimize the error and bring the output closer to the target. The perceptron learning rule follows a similar approach for such adjustments.

In unsupervised learning, the weights and biases are adjusted only based on the input data, without a specific target value. This method is commonly used for classification tasks, helping to build a dynamic model that categorizes data—such as concrete-related datasets—into distinct classes. The primary function of the network here is to group inputs into a limited number of categories.

On the other hand, reinforcement learning emphasizes learning through interactions with the environment, with the goal of maximizing the reward signal. This type of learning is applied in a wide range of fields due to its broad applicability and potential.

The feed-forward neural network, being the most basic type, is frequently used for correlation tasks, such as FRP detection in concrete. In the output layer, the predicted values are compared with the target labels. The process is repeated over multiple iterations or within a predefined time frame to optimize the network’s structure according to the user’s preferences.

Validation

One of the most effective validation approaches is the K-fold cross-validation method, which involves splitting the dataset into K equal parts (folds). In this method, the model is trained and evaluated K times, each time using K—1 folds for training and the remaining one for testing. This process ensures that each data point is used for both training and validation exactly once. After completing all K iterations, the prediction errors obtained in each run are averaged to provide a more reliable estimate of the model’s generalization performance.

Model selection

To identify the best-performing model, we employed the average–maximum accuracy strategy. In this approach, the algorithm is executed for n independent repetitions. From the resulting n sets of K-fold validation results, we first identify the K-fold group with the highest average accuracy. Then, within that group, we select the fold with the highest individual accuracy. The corresponding model structure of that fold is ultimately chosen as the optimal predictive model.

In the present study, this method was applied by calculating the average experimental accuracy for each K-fold group, and then selecting the model with the maximum experimental accuracy from the group with the highest overall performance.

Below the description of the architecture of the artificial neural network used in this study has been added for clarity. The developed model is a feed-forward neural network consisting of one hidden layer. The network receives ten input parameters, including Efrp, ɛfrp, ffrp, h/d, d, Ec, fco, nfrp, tfrp, and ρfrp, which represent the mechanical and geometrical properties of the FRP-confined concrete specimens. The hidden layer contains 12 neurons and employs the sigmoid activation function, which has been selected to introduce nonlinearity and enhance the model’s ability to capture complex relationships among input variables. The output layer provides four predicted variables: ɛct, fct, fcu, and ɛcu, corresponding to the initial strain, initial stress, final stress, and final strain of the confined concrete, respectively. This network architecture was optimized using the genetic algorithm to achieve the minimum prediction error.

The sensitivity analysis, conducted across Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4, systematically quantifies the influence of various input parameters on the output variables (stress and strain at transfer and final points). As evidenced in Fig. 1, the Efrp parameter, representing the elastic modulus of FRP sheets, demonstrated the most substantial positive effect on the stress output at the transfer point (Fct), accounting for approximately 60% of the influence. Following this, parameters nfrp, tfrp, Ec, ρfrp, fco, and ɛfrp exhibited minor positive effects, while parameters d (cylinder diameter) and d/h (slenderness ratio) had no discernible impact on this specific output. Conversely, the ffrp parameter (tensile strength of FRP sheets) yielded a significant negative effect of approximately 61%, thereby decreasing the output variable.

Similar trends were observed in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 regarding the stress output at the transfer point, with Efrp consistently showing a strong positive influence, and ffrp a notable negative one. The relative importance of other parameters varied slightly across these figures, but the dominant roles of Efrp and ffrp remained consistent.

In Fig. 4, the focus shifted to the strain output at the final point. Here, the tfrp parameter (thickness of FRP tube) emerged as the most influential, contributing positively to the strain output. Parameters such as ffrp and nfrp also showed some effectiveness, whereas Ec, ρfrp, fco, Efrp, d, and d/h had minimal or no significant effect on the final strain. Notably, Efrp in this context was associated with a negative value, indicating a decreasing effect on the strain output.

The comprehensive comparison derived from this sensitivity analysis, particularly the identification of Efrp, tfrp, and ffrp as the most critical input variables, informed the subsequent stages of the study. It was observed that the neural network’s response, when trained with all input variables, did not differ substantially from its response when trained using only these three highly influential parameters. This finding strongly suggests that the remaining input variables play a less critical role in the network’s training and predictive accuracy. Consequently, by leveraging the insights from this sensitivity analysis, the input variable space for the network can be effectively reduced, leading to potentially shorter training times while maintaining acceptable levels of accuracy in predicting stress and strain at critical points.

In the sensitivity analysis section, the influence of key parameters on the performance of FRP-confined concrete cylinders was investigated. The results indicated that parameters such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document}(Elastic Modulus of FRP), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{t}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document}(FRP Jacket Thickness), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{f}}_{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}(Compressive Strength of Concrete) have the most significant impact on the evaluated performance metrics (e.g., ultimate strength and ductility). The elastic modulus of FRP, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document}, represents the inherent stiffness of the material. In confined concrete cylinders, the FRP acts as an external jacket that, under increasing compressive stress in the concrete, undergoes lateral strain and consequently applies a confining pressure to the concrete. A higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document} implies that for a given lateral strain in the concrete, the FRP experiences lower stress. However, a higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document} leads to a significant increase in the overall stiffness of the composite system (concrete + FRP). This increased stiffness allows the FRP to sustain higher axial stresses, particularly in the early stages of loading, and provides more effective confinement pressure by more robustly resisting the lateral expansion of the concrete at higher stress levels. From the perspective of confinement theory, the lateral confining pressure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{l}}$$\end{document}) is directly proportional to the hoop stress (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\upsigma}_{\mathrm{h}}$$\end{document}) in the FRP jacket, which is a function of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{t}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document}, and the hoop strain (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\upvarepsilon}_{\mathrm{h}}$$\end{document}). According to basic mechanics of materials principles for cylindrical shells under internal pressure (here, the lateral confining pressure), the stress within the jacket is dependent on its thickness. For a given \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document} and lateral strain, a larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{t}}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document} results in higher hoop stress within the FRP and, consequently, a greater lateral confining pressure. This enhanced confining pressure significantly increases the ultimate compressive strength and substantially improves the ductility of the concrete, as it actively restrains the brittle failure mechanisms (such as cracking and dilation-induced fracture), thereby enabling greater energy absorption. Findings in this study align with previous research, highlighting the high sensitivity of confined concrete performance to the FRP jacket thickness.

Discussion about the findings of the sensitivity analysis of this research with the findings of the analysis of individual factors of other researchers

According to the sensitivity analysis of the input parameters affecting the four output variables—namely stress and strain at the transitional and final points—it was found that the Efrp parameter positively influences the stress and strain at the transitional point and the stress at the final point. Meanwhile, the tfrp parameter exhibits the highest percentage of positive sensitivity across stress and strain at both the transitional and final points. In contrast, ffrp shows the most negative impact, and Efrp also has the highest negative sensitivity in predicting the strain at the final point.

The influence of other parameters on stress and strain at both transitional and final points was found to be negligible or minimal.

A comparison between the output of the hybrid model (genetic algorithm and neural network) and the results from the sensitivity analysis confirms that the most influential parameters are Efrp, tfrp, and ffrp. When only these three parameters are used as inputs, the model’s predictions closely match those produced with the full set of input variables. This suggests that the remaining input features contribute marginally to the network’s learning and prediction process.

These findings align with the research of Jin and Yan, who used multivariate quantitative regression analysis to evaluate the effect of individual parameters on stress and strain in FRP-reinforced RAC concrete cylinder samples. Their results showed that the compressive strength of unreinforced RAC had the strongest effect on fct (stress at the transition point) in specimens reinforced with natural fibers. However, as the samples approached ultimate stress, the fiber reinforcement content became more dominant in influencing behavior.

In contrast, among various response variables (RRVs), the ultimate strain of FRP-strengthened RAC cylinders was less affected by this factor. According to the multivariate regression analyses by Huang et al. and Yan et al.18, parameters like specimen size and slenderness ratio also play a role. For instance, Huang et al.17 found that fct decreases as specimen size or slenderness ratio increases, while no significant changes were observed in ɛct, fcu, or ɛcu.

Their results also indicated that while fct has a negative relationship with slenderness ratio, this relationship is weaker than the effects of other variables such as FRP reinforcement configuration or the tensile strength of FRP sheets.

Further analyses using an abbreviated list of independent variables (IVs) produced more consistent results than using the full list. For example, the slenderness ratio had no significant effect on either fct or fcu when the reduced variable set was used. This approach helped reduce internal multicollinearity among IVs and improved model quality in terms of lower residual standard deviation, higher R2, higher F-value, and optimal Durbin-Watson statistic.
