The Dirac oscillator, generalised parastatistics and colour Lie superalgebras
Phillip S. Isaac, Mitchell Ryan

Abstract
. We study the Dirac oscillator in one, two and three spatial dimensions, showing that the corresponding ladder operators realise the ℤ 2 × ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2} -graded Lie superalgebras 𝔭 ​ 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 3 | 2 ) \mathfrak{pso}(3|2) , 𝔭 ​ 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 3 | 4 ) \mathfrak{pso}(3|4) and 𝔬 ​ 𝔰 ​ 𝔭 01 ​ ( 1 | 2 ) ⊕ 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 10 ​ ( 1 | 1 ) \mathfrak{osp}_{01}(1|2)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_{10}(1|1) . These algebraic structures are related to parastatistics and their Fock spaces. We demonstrate that these colour algebras and Fock spaces are useful for analysing the Dirac oscillator and its eigenspaces, particularly in ( 1 + 3 ) (1+3) -dimensions. Apart from this current work, to our knowledge, the recent article by Ito and Nago [ 1 ] is the only other such work that makes use of ℤ 2 × ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2} graded colour Lie superalgebras in a relativistic setting.

2.
Dirac Oscillator

Throughout this paper, we will work in natural units:
\hbar=c=1
.
The Dirac oscillator is obtained by introducing a linear potential
via coupling
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}\rightsquigarrow\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}-\beta im\omega\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}
in the free Dirac equation;
explicitly, the Hamiltonian for the Dirac oscillator is

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\alpha}}\dotproduct(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}-\beta im\omega\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}})+\beta m

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}=(-i\partialderivative{x_1},\ldots,-i\partialderivative{x_n})

(position representation);

m
is the mass;
\omega
is the oscillation frequency;
and the components of the vector operator
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and
\beta

form generators of the Clifford algebra
C\ell_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
, so that

\anticommutator{\alpha_i}{\alpha_j}=\delta_{ij},\qquad\anticommutator{\alpha_i}{\beta}=0,\qquad\anticommutator{\beta}{\beta}=0.

We use the convention that
(x,y,z)=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
,
and use the notation
\partialderivative{x}=\partial_{x}
, etc.
Squaring
H
and taking a non-relativistic limit yields the non-relativistic quantum harmonic oscillator with a strong spin-orbit coupling
[
14
]
.

In three spacial dimensions or less,
the Dirac representation gives a specific matrix representation for the Clifford algebra generators:

\displaystyle\beta=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&-I\end{pmatrix},\qquad\alpha_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma_{i}\\
\sigma_{i}&0\end{pmatrix}

where
\sigma_{i}
are the Pauli matrices.
The Dirac representation allows us to give a physical interpretation to the Clifford algebra elements.
For example,

S_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{i}&0\\
0&\sigma_{i}\end{pmatrix}\in C\ell_{3}(\mathbb{C})

are the spin matrices.

3.
Parastatistics

Recall that the creation and annihilation operators for fermions
f_{i}^{\pm}
(
i=1,\ldots,m
) and bosons
b_{i}^{\pm}
(
i=1,\ldots,n
) satisfy the following canonical (anti)commutation relations

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\anticommutator{f_i^+}{f_j^-}&=\delta_{ij},\\
\commutator{b_i^+}{b_j^-}&=\delta_{ij},\end{split}\begin{split}\anticommutator{f_i^\pm}{f_j^\pm}&=0,\\
\commutator{b_i^\pm}{b_j^\pm}&=0.\end{split}

The corresponding free Hamiltonians are

(2)

H_{f}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\commutator{f_j^+}{f_j^-}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\anticommutator{b_j^+}{b_j^-}

for fermions and bosons respectively,
where the constants
\omega_{j}
represent the energy of a free particle in quantum state
j
.
The fermion and boson operators are ladder operators for their respective free Hamiltonian:

(3)

\displaystyle\commutator{H_f}{f_k^\pm}

\displaystyle=\pm\omega_{k}f_{k}^{\pm},

\displaystyle\commutator{H_b}{b_k^\pm}

\displaystyle=\pm\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\pm}.

Parastatistics generalises the fermion and boson operators to

parafermion
operators
f_{i}^{\pm}
and
paraboson
operators
b_{i}^{\pm}
,
which need not satisfy the canonical (anti)commutation relations (
1
),
and instead satisfy
[
3
,
4
]
the trilinear relations

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\commutator{\comm{f_i^+}{f_j^-}}{f_k^-}&=-2\delta_{ik}f_{j}^{-},\\
\commutator{\acomm{b_i^+}{b_j^-}}{b_k^-}&=-2\delta_{ik}b_{j}^{-},\end{split}\begin{split}\commutator{\comm{f_i^-}{f_j^-}}{f_k^-}&=0,\\
\commutator{\acomm{b_i^+}{b_j^-}}{b_k^-}&=0.\end{split}

Note that ordinary bosons and fermions satisfy the above relations,
but there exist operators which satisfy (
4
) but not (
1
).
These trilinear relations (
4
) were chosen to ensure that the parafermion and paraboson operators remain ladder operators for their respective free Hamiltonians
[
3

, i.e. still satisfy relations (

By applying the (super) Jacobi identity and taking adjoints of the parastatistics relations (
4

and
(b_{i}^{\pm})^{\dagger}=b_{i}^{\mp}
),
we obtain relations for all possible triples of parafermions or parabosons.
We can express all such relations with the following two expressions

(5)

\displaystyle\commutator{\comm{f_i^{\zeta}}{f_j^{\eta}}}{f_k^{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\xi- \eta}\delta_{jk}f_{i}^{\zeta}-\absolutevalue{\xi- \zeta}\delta_{ik}f_{j}^{\eta},

(6)

\displaystyle\commutator{\acomm{b_i^{\zeta}}{b_j^{\eta}}}{b_k^{\xi}}

\displaystyle=(\xi-\eta)\delta_{jk}b_{i}^{\zeta}+(\xi-\zeta)\delta_{ik}b_{j}^{\eta}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\commutator{\comm{f_i^{\zeta}}{f_j^{\eta}}}{b_k^{\xi}}&=0,\\

\commutator{\acomm{f_i^{\zeta}}{b_j^{\eta}}}{b_k^{\xi}}&=(\xi-\eta)\delta_{jk}f_{i}^{\zeta}.\end{split}

4.
Dirac Oscillator in (1+1)-dimensions

The Dirac Oscillator Hamiltonian in
(1+1)
-dimensions is as follows

(11)

H=\alpha(-i\partial_{x}-\beta im\omega x)+\beta m

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are gamma matrices (elements of the Clifford Algebra
C\ell_{2}(\mathbb{C})
) satisfying

\anticommutator{\alpha}{\alpha}=\anticommutator{\beta}{\beta}=2I,\qquad\anticommutator{\alpha}{\beta}=0.

The square of this Hamiltonian is

H^{2}=-(\partial_{x})^{2}+m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}+m^{2}-\beta m\omega.

Note that the familiar Harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian for the Schrödinger equation

(12)

H_{\textup{Sch}}\coloneq\frac{1}{2m}(-i\partial_{x})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}x^{2}

appears as a term in
H^{2}

By direct computation, we can verify that
H^{2}
has the following ladder operators,

(13)

\displaystyle b^{-}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}x-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega}}\partial_{x},

\displaystyle\commutator{H^2}{\bann}

\displaystyle=-2m\omega\,b^{-},

\displaystyle b^{+}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}x+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega}}\partial_{x},

\displaystyle\commutator{H^2}{\bcreate}

\displaystyle=2m\omega\,b^{+},

\displaystyle s^{-}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}(\beta\alpha+\alpha),

\displaystyle\commutator{H^2}{\sann}

\displaystyle=-2m\omega\,s^{-},

\displaystyle s^{+}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}(\beta\alpha-\alpha),

\displaystyle\commutator{H^2}{\screate}

\displaystyle=2m\omega\,s^{+}.

These ladder operators for
H^{2}
have the following relations with the Hamiltonian
H
itself:

(14)

\displaystyle\anticommutator{H}{\spm}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2m\omega}\,b^{\pm},

\displaystyle\commutator{H}{\bpm}

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{2m\omega}\,s^{\pm}.

Notice that
b^{-}
and
b^{+}
are the familiar ladder operators for the non-relativistic Schrödinger harmonic oscillator.
As such,
b^{-},b^{+}
satisfy the boson commutation relation

\commutator{\bann}{\bcreate}=I.

Meanwhile, in the Dirac representation,
s^{-}
and
s^{+}

s^{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma_{1}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad s^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\

We can interpret
s^{-}
as shifting the small component to the large component (hence, decreasing the energy of the bispinsor) and interpret
s^{+}
as shifting the large component to the small component (hence, increasing the energy level of the bispinor).

Remark 4.1
.

In the Dirac representation,

s^{-}=\Sigma_{-}S_{1}
and
s^{+}=\Sigma_{+}S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2m\omega}(b^{+}s^{-}-b^{-}s^{+}),

Remark 4.2
.

Considering the shifted Hamiltonian is not the only way to resolve the non-homogeneity of

4.3.
Fock space and the spectrum

Here, we give details about the spectrum of
H
, not only for completeness, but also for insight into the higher dimensional cases presented in later sections. For this
(1+1)
-dimensional case, our approach here derives the spectrum without making much use of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded colour superalgebra, unlike the
(1+3)
-dimensional case studied later in the article.

Since
H^{2}
is the square of the self adjoint operator
H
, its spectrum must be non-negative.
Thus, there must exist a vacuum state
\ket{0}
such that
b^{-}\ket{0}=s^{-}\ket{0}=0

(otherwise we could continually apply
b^{-}
or
s^{-}
to obtain negative eigenvalues).

Lemma 4.3
.

\beta\ket{0}=\ket{0}

Proof.

\displaystyle\beta\ket{0}

\displaystyle=\commutator{\sann}{\screate}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=s^{-}s^{+}\ket{0}-s^{+}s^{-}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=-s^{+}s^{-}\ket{0}+\anticommutator{\sann}{\screate}\ket{0}-s^{+}s^{-}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}\qed

Lemma 4.4
.

H_{0}\ket{0}=0

Proof.

Observe that
H_{0}=\sqrt{m\omega/2}(\anticommutator{\bcreate}{\sann}+\anticommutator{\bann}{\screate})
.
Since
\commutator{\bcreate}{\sann}=\commutator{\bann}{\screate}=0
, we have that

H_{0}=\sqrt{2m\omega}(b^{+}s^{-}+s^{+}b^{-})
.
Therefore,
H_{0}\ket{0}=0
.
∎

Notice that the above proofs used both relations from
\mathfrak{bf}(1|1)
and
\mathfrak{pso}(3|2)
.

Combining the above two lemmas, we find that

H\ket{0}=H_{0}\ket{0}+m\beta\ket{0}=m\ket{0}.

Now, consider an eigenstate
\ket{\psi}
with eigenvalue
E
.
Then

(16)

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{\pm}}

\displaystyle=(E\pm\sqrt{E^{2}+2m\omega})b^{+}\ket{\psi}+\sqrt{2m\omega}\,s^{+}\ket{\psi}

is a (normalisable) eigenvector of
H
with eigenvalue
\pm\sqrt{E^{2}+2m\omega}
.
This is easily verified using the relations of
H
with
b^{+}
and
s^{+}
.
We conclude that the spectrum of
H
is given by

\{m\}\cup\{\pm\sqrt{m^{2}+2nm\omega}\mid n~\text{a positive integer}\}

(compare with the spectrum in
[
34
]
for example).

5.
Dirac Oscillator in (1+2)-dimensions

The Dirac Oscillator Hamiltonian in
(1+2)
-dimensions is as follows

(17)

H=\alpha_{1}(-i\partial_{x}-\beta im\omega x)+\alpha_{2}(-i\partial_{y}-\beta im\omega y)+\beta m

where
\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2}
and
\beta
are gamma matrices (elements of the Clifford Algebra
C\ell_{3}(\mathbb{C})
) satisfying

\anticommutator{\gamma_j}{\gamma_k}=\delta_{jk}\qquad\text{for}~(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta).

The square of this Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H^{2}

\displaystyle=-\Delta+m^{2}\omega^{2}r^{2}+m^{2}-2m\omega\beta+(2m\omega x\partial_{y}-2m\omega y\partial_{x})\beta\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}

where
\Delta=(\partial_{x})^{2}+(\partial_{y})^{2}
and
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
.

In
(1+2)
-dimensions, angular momentum appears (both orbital and intrinsic).
We shall treat
S_{0}\coloneq-(1/2)i\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
as the total spin.
Indeed, in the Dirac representation,
S_{0}
becomes

\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{z}&0\\
0&\sigma_{z}\end{pmatrix}.

(Recall that, in
(1+2)
-dimensions, only the
z
-direction contributes to the spin.)

The orbital angular momentum
L_{0}=x(-i\partial_{y})-y(-i\partial_{x})
and spin
S_{0}=(-1/2)i\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
both commute with
H^{2}
.
Neither
L_{0}
nor
S_{0}
commute with
H
,
but the total angular momentum
J=L_{0}+S_{0}
does:
\commutator{H}{J}=0
.
In addition, the operator
\beta S_{0}
commutes with
H
.

One of the main distinctions between the Dirac oscillator in
(1+2)
-dimensions, compared to
(1+1)
-dimensions, is the appearance of a spin-orbit coupling term in
H^{2}

C_{LS}=-2\beta L_{0}S_{0}-(1/2)\beta=-\frac{1}{2}\beta+(x\partial_{y}-y\partial_{x})\beta\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}.

This specific spin-orbit coupling
C_{LS}
was chosen for the fact that
\commutator{H}{\CLS}=0
.
Hence
H
and
C_{LS}
are simultaneously diagonalisable.

The Hamiltonian squares to

\displaystyle H^{2}=-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}+m^{2}\omega^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}-2m\omega\beta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}-3m\omega\beta+m^{2}I.

- and

\displaystyle s^{-}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}(\beta\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j}),

\displaystyle s^{+}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}(\beta\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j}),

\displaystyle b^{-}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}x_{j}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m\omega}}\partial_{x_{j}},

\displaystyle b^{+}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}x_{j}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m\omega}}\partial_{x_{j}}

for

(recalling that
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(x,y,z)

6.1.
The colour Lie superalgebra

Consider the operators

\displaystyle b^{-}_{\sigma}=2\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{-}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}},\qquad b^{+}_{\sigma}=2\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{+}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}},\qquad s^{-}_{\sigma}=\frac{2}{3}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}^{-}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}},\qquad s^{+}_{\sigma}=\frac{2}{3}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}^{+}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}.

Observe that they satisfy the following commutation relations:

\displaystyle\commutator{N}{\bannspin}

\displaystyle=-b^{-}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle\commutator{N}{\bcreatespin}

\displaystyle=b^{+}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle\commutator{N}{\sannspin}

\displaystyle=-s^{-}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle\commutator{N}{\screatespin}

\displaystyle=s^{+}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle\commutator{\beta}{\bannspin}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\commutator{\beta}{\bcreatespin}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\anticommutator{\beta}{\sannspin}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\anticommutator{\beta}{\screatespin}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\commutator{\vec{L}^2}{\sannspin}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\commutator{\vec{L}^2}{\screatespin}

\displaystyle=0

and all commute with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}^{2}
and all anticommute with
C_{LS}
.
Moreover,
we have
H=\sqrt{2m\omega}(b^{+}_{\sigma}s^{-}_{\sigma}+b^{-}_{\sigma}s^{+}_{\sigma})-2m{s_{0}}
.

Using the above relations, these ladder operators send a simultaneous eigenstate of
N
and
C_{LS}
to another simultaneous eigenstate of
N
and
C_{LS}
.
Therefore,
b^{\pm}_{\sigma}
and
s^{\pm}_{\sigma}
send
H^{2}
-eigenspaces to
H^{2}
-eigenspaces.

The ladder operators
s^{\pm}_{\sigma}
satisfy the fermion relations
but the ladder operators
b^{\pm}_{\sigma}
do
not
satisfy the boson relations.
Instead,
b^{\pm}_{\sigma}
are paraboson operators, satisfying (
6
).
The two series of ladder operators
s^{\pm}_{\sigma}
and
b^{\pm}_{\sigma}
commute,
but do not satisfy the relative parafermion (
7
), nor the relative paraboson (
8
) relations.

Set
{s_{0}}=-(1/2)\beta
.
To realise both the parafermion relations (
5
) for
s^{\pm}_{\sigma}
(recalling that fermion operators satisfy the parafermion relations) and the paraboson relations (
6
) for
b^{\pm}_{\sigma}
,
we can use the Lie superalgebra with the following basis:

\displaystyle 0\text{-sector: }

\displaystyle N+I,{s_{0}},s^{-}_{\sigma},s^{+}_{\sigma},(b^{-}_{\sigma})^{2},(b^{+}_{\sigma})^{2},

\displaystyle 1\text{-sector: }

\displaystyle b^{-}_{\sigma},b^{+}_{\sigma}.

This Lie superalgebra is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{osp}(1|2)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2)
.

To instead realise the fermion relations (
1
) for
s^{\pm}_{\sigma}
and the paraboson relations (
6
) for
b^{\pm}_{\sigma}

requires a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded colour Lie superalgebra spanned by the following basis:

\displaystyle 00\text{-sector:}

\displaystyle\,N-{s_{0}},I,(b^{-}_{\sigma})^{2},(b^{+}_{\sigma})^{2},

\displaystyle 01\text{-sector:}

\displaystyle\,b^{-}_{\sigma},\,b^{+}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle 10\text{-sector:}

\displaystyle\,s^{-}_{\sigma},\,s^{+}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle 11\text{-sector:}

\displaystyle\,\text{zero}.

Note that any Lie superalgebra can be given a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading
by choosing an embedding of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
into
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}

(doing so will make two of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded sectors empty).
With this in mind,
the above colour Lie superalgebra is isomorphic to

\mathfrak{osp}_{01}(1|2)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_{10}(1|1)

where
\mathfrak{osp}_{01}
is the orthosymplectic Lie superalgebra with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{00,01\}
-grading and

\mathfrak{sl}_{10}
is the special linear Lie superalgebra with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{00,10\}
-grading.
Note that, despite the colour Lie superalgebra being a direct sum of Lie superalgebras,
it is not a Lie superalgebra itself due to gradings of the different direct summands having different embeddings into
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}

The advantage of considering the colour Lie superalgebra is that we can include

as an element.
To do so, we enlarge the algebra by adding
{s_{0}}
to the

-sector and

to the

-sector,
forming a colour Lie superalgebra isomorphic to

\mathfrak{osp}_{01}(1|2)\oplus\mathfrak{gl}_{10}(1|1)\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{11}

(where

Then,

is an element of this enlarged algebra.
This element

is not homogeneous,
but satisfies the following relations

\displaystyle\braces{\llbracket}{\rrbracket}{H^{2},b^{-}_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=-2m\,b^{-}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle\braces{\llbracket}{\rrbracket}{H^{2},b^{+}_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=2m\,b^{+}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle\braces{\llbracket}{\rrbracket}{H^{2},s^{-}_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=-2m\,s^{-}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle\braces{\llbracket}{\rrbracket}{H^{2},s^{+}_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=2m\,s^{+}_{\sigma}

where
\braces{\llbracket}{\rrbracket}{\cdot,\cdot}
is the colour bracket (realised as either a commutator or anticommutator on homogeneous elements).
Compare this with the corresponding relations for the
(1+1)
-dimensional equation (

In fact,
setting

6.2.
Fock space and the spectrum

In this section, we demonstrate the utility of the paraboson operators and the colour Lie superalgebra by solving the Dirac oscillator within a Fock space.
Solutions to the Dirac oscillator have been computed elsewhere (e.g.
[
16
]
),
so we omit many of the details and instead draw the reader’s attention to the steps in our solution which make use of the colour Lie superalgebra relations.

Proposition 6.3
.

There exists a vacuum state
\ket{0}
such that
b^{-}_{k}\ket{0}=s^{-}_{k}\ket{0}=S_{-}\ket{0}=0
for
k=1,2,3
.

Proof.

The standard argument shows that there exists a state
\ket*{\widetilde{0}}
with
b^{-}_{k}\ket*{\widetilde{0}}=s^{-}_{k}\ket*{\widetilde{0}}=0
.
If
S_{-}\ket*{\widetilde{0}}=0
, then we can set
\ket{0}=\ket*{\widetilde{0}}
and we are done. Otherwise, we set
\ket{0}=S_{-}\ket*{\widetilde{0}}
.
Since the commutator of the annihilation operators with
S_{-}
is a linear combination of annihilation operators
and since
(S_{-})^{2}=0
, we have that
\ket{0}
is a such a vacuum state.
∎

We now consider the
(\mathfrak{osp}_{01}(1|2)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_{10}(1|1))
-module generated by
\{\ket{0},S_{+}\ket{0}\}
, which we interpret as the Fock space.
(We could have instead considered the
(\mathfrak{osp}(1|2)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2))
-module,
which would yield the same vector subspace. However, as we will see, the colour algebra module is more useful.)

We will first search for
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}
- and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}
-modules within the Fock space.
Let
L_{\pm}=L_{1}\pm iL_{2}
,
S_{\pm}=S_{1}\pm iS_{2}
and
J_{\pm}=L_{\pm}+S_{\pm}
be raising/lowering operators for orbital angular momentum, spin and total angular momentum, respectfully.

The elements

(b^{+}_{1}+ib^{+}_{2})^{\ell}\ket{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(b^{+}_{1}+ib^{+}_{2})^{\ell}S_{+}\ket{0}

are both
L_{3}
-highest weight vectors with
L_{3}
-weight
\ell
.
Thus, the Fock space contains the
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}
-module
V_{\ell}\oplus V_{\ell}
for each
\ell=0,1,2,\ldots
.
As a
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}
-module, this
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}
-module becomes
V_{\ell}\otimes V_{1/2}
.
Applying the Clebsch–Gordon decomposition, we find a
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}
-module

V_{\ell+1/2}\oplus V_{\ell-1/2}

as a submodule of the Fock space
(if
\ell=0
, then the
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}
-module is
V_{1/2}
).
The highest weight vectors of
V_{\ell+1/2}
and
V_{\ell-1/2}
are

(b^{+}_{1}+ib^{+}_{2})^{\ell}S_{+}\ket{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(b^{+}_{1}+ib^{+}_{2})^{\ell}\ket{0}+(b^{+}_{1}+ib^{+}_{2})^{\ell-1}b^{+}_{3}S_{+}\ket{0}

respectively.
Notice that the eigenvalue of
N
on these subspaces is
n=\ell

(recall that
\commutator{N}{\vec{J}^2}=0
).

We wish to raise the eigenvalue of
N
,
without changing the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2},\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}^{2}
and
J_{3}
.
However, we cannot achieve this with either
b^{+}_{\sigma}
(which does not preserve the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
) nor with
s^{+}_{\sigma}
(which could only raise the eigenvalue of
N
at most once, since
(s^{+}_{\sigma})^{2}=0
).
Instead, we define new operators

t^{\pm}=(b^{\pm}_{\sigma})^{2}s^{\mp}_{\sigma}+s^{\pm}_{\sigma}

(note that
t^{\pm}
is a spectral rescaling of
(1/2\sqrt{2m\omega})\anticommutator{H_0}{\bpmspin}
).
Observe that
(t^{\pm})^{2}=(b^{\pm})^{2}
:

\displaystyle(t^{\pm})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{\tpm}{\tpm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\anticommutator{(\bpmspin)^2\spmspin[\mp]}{(\bpmspin)^2\spmspin[\mp]}+2\anticommutator{(\bpmspin)^2\spmspin[\mp]}{\spmspin}+\anticommutator{\spmspin}{\spmspin})

\displaystyle=\anticommutator{(\bpmspin)^2\spmspin[\mp]}{\spmspin}

\displaystyle=(b^{\pm}_{\sigma})^{2}\anticommutator{\spmspin[\mp]}{\spmspin}+\commutator{(\bpmspin)^2}{\spmspin}s^{\pm}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=(b^{\pm}_{\sigma})^{2}.

Note that the above calculation can be performed within the universal enveloping algebra for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded colour Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{osp}_{01}(1|2)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_{10}(1|1)
(where
t^{\pm}
is
10
-graded)
but
cannot
be performed in the universal enveloping algebra for the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{osp}(1|2)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2)
(where
t^{\pm}
is
0
-graded).

Proposition 6.4
.

The operator
t^{+}
is injective.
Moreover,
t^{+}
preserves the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J^{2}}}
and
J_{3}
.

Proof.

First, we claim that
N-{s_{0}}+I
is injective.
Indeed, take a non-zero state
\ket{\psi}
and consider

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}(N-{s_{0}}+I)\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{+}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{-}+\frac{3}{2}\right)\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{+}\dotproduct\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{-}\ket{\psi}+\frac{3}{2}\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}

\displaystyle>0.

Thus
N-{s_{0}}+I
sends non-zero states to non-zero states (trivial kernel).

Similarly,
we claim that
(b^{+}_{\sigma})^{2}
is injective.
Indeed, take a non-zero state
\ket{\psi}
and observe that

\bra{\psi}(b^{-}_{\sigma})^{2}(b^{+}_{\sigma})^{2}\ket{\psi}=\bra{\psi}(b^{+}_{\sigma})^{2}(b^{-}_{\sigma})^{2}\ket{\psi}+4\bra{\psi}(N-{s_{0}}+I)\ket{\psi}>0.

This last inequality follows because
\bra{\psi}(b^{+}_{\sigma})^{2}(b^{-}_{\sigma})^{2}\ket{\psi}
is the norm squared of
(b^{-}_{\sigma})^{2}\ket{\psi}
(which is non-negative);
and we have already shown above that
\bra{\psi}(N-{s_{0}}+I)\ket{\psi}>0
.
Thus,
(b^{+}_{\sigma})^{2}=(t^{+})^{2}
is injective.
Therefore,
t^{+}
is injective.

By direct computation,
we find that
\commutator*{\vec{L}^2}{(\bcreatespin)^2}=0

and that
\commutator*{\vec{L}^2}{\spmspin}=0
.
That is,
t^{+}
preserves the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
.
Since
\commutator{J_i}{\bcreatespin}=\commutator{J_i}{\screatespin}=0
,
the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J^{2}}}
and
J_{3}
are also preserved by
t^{+}
.
∎

By repeatedly applying
t^{+}
to a basis for
V_{\ell+1/2}\oplus V_{\ell-1/2}
,
we can form a basis for the Fock space.
Explicitly, such a basis is

\ket{n,\ell,j,j_3}=\begin{cases}(t^{+})^{n-\ell}(J_{-})^{j-j_{3}}(b^{+}_{1}+ib^{+}_{2})^{\ell}S_{+}\ket{0}&\text{if}~j=\ell+(1/2)\\
(t^{+})^{n-\ell}(J_{-})^{j-j_{3}}((b^{+}_{1}+ib^{+}_{2})^{\ell}+(b^{+}_{1}+ib^{+}_{2})^{\ell-1}b^{+}_{3}S_{+})\ket{0}&\text{if}~j=\ell-(1/2).\end{cases}

N
.
Note that
n,\ell(\ell+1),j(j+1),j_{3}
are the eigenvalues of
N,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}^{2},J_{3}
respectively.
Hence,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=0,1,2,\ldots

\displaystyle\ell

\displaystyle=0,1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle=\ell\pm(1/2),

\displaystyle j_{3}

\displaystyle=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j-1,j.

Note also that the basis vectors have not been normalised.
