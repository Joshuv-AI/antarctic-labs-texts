The Dichotomy in the Nuclear and Host Galaxy Properties of High-redshift Quasars SExtractor ( Bertin & Arnouts 1996 ) , photutils ( Bradley et al. 2024 ) , GalfitS ( Li & Ho 2025 )
Ruancun Li, Luis C. Ho, Chang-Hao Chen

Abstract
The early growth of high‑redshift quasars and their host galaxies raises critical questions about their cosmic evolution. We exploit the angular resolution and sensitivity of NIRCam to investigate the host galaxies of 31 quasars at 4 ≲ z ≲ 7 4\lesssim z\lesssim 7 drawn from multiple JWST surveys. Using a new multi-band forward-modeling code ( GalfitS ) that incorporates physically motivated priors, we securely detect and quantify the host emission in 30 objects, while simultaneously characterizing the nuclear spectral energy distribution. The host galaxies of high-redshift quasars are ∼ 0.3 \sim 0.3 dex more compact than star-forming galaxies of comparable mass. A striking dichotomy emerges: luminous “blue” quasars ( L 5100 ≳ 10 45 ​ erg ​ s − 1 L_{5100}\gtrsim 10^{45}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}} ) reside in bulge-dominated galaxies ( n ≈ 5 n\approx 5 ) and exhibit a narrow range of ultraviolet nuclear slopes (median β UV ≈ − 1.4 \beta_{\rm UV}\approx-1.4 ), while fainter “red” quasars inhabit disk-like hosts ( n ≈ 1 n\approx 1 ) and display a broad range of slopes ( β UV ≈ − 2 \beta_{\rm UV}\approx-2 to 4). These two populations differ markedly in their black hole-to-stellar mass ratios, with high-luminosity quasars showing M BH / M ∗ = 1.2 % M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_{\ast}=1.2\% compared to 4.7 % 4.7\% for lower luminosity sources, placing them collectively ∼ \sim 0.6 dex above the local M BH − M ∗ M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\ast} relation. This offset likely reflects rapid black hole growth in early gas-rich environments, where feedback from the active galactic nucleus becomes effective only after substantial gas depletion. Our findings suggest that the observed dichotomy, whether due to intrinsic spectral differences or dust extinction, fundamentally shapes the coevolution of supermassive black holes and their host galaxies in the early Universe.



I
Introduction

One of the most striking early discoveries from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is the identification of numerous active galactic nuclei (AGNs) in the high-redshift (
z>4
) Universe. Spectroscopic observations with JWST have revealed a population of broad-line AGNs with bolometric luminosities ranging from
\sim 10^{44}
to
10^{46}\,\rm erg\,s^{-1}
, approximately 2 to 3 orders of magnitude lower than those of the bright quasars detected by ground-based surveys
(
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Kocevski et al. 2023a
;
Maiolino et al. 2024
;
Matthee et al. 2024
;
Scholtz et al. 2025
)
. Powered by black holes (BHs) with masses of only
M_{\rm BH}\approx 10^{6}-10^{8}\,M_{\odot}
, these objects represent some of the least massive BHs known in the early Universe. Among them, the “little red dots” (LRDs) stand out as particularly fascinating. These largely unresolved sources, which morphologically resemble distant analogs of local quasars
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, display an unusual “V-shaped” spectral energy distribution (SED) featuring a steep red continuum in the rest-frame optical yet relatively blue ultraviolet (UV) colors
(
Kokorev et al. 2024
;
Akins et al. 2025b
;
Kocevski et al. 2025
;
Labbé et al. 2025
)
. Their SEDs are often interpreted as the combined emission from a compact star-forming galaxy contributing to the UV and a dust-obscured nucleus that dominates in the optical
(
Greene et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
. Follow-up observations confirm broad emission lines in most photometrically selected LRDs
(
Greene et al. 2024
)
, alongside detected variability
(
Furtak et al. 2025
)
, reinforcing their classification as high-redshift AGNs with atypically red SEDs.

Theoretical works hypothesize that “red quasars” mark an early growth phase of supermassive BHs–—a dust-enshrouded stage that may eventually evolve into the traditional blue quasar phase
(
Kim & Im 2025
)
. In merger-driven coevolution models, gas-rich galaxy mergers channel substantial amounts of gas and dust into the nucleus, igniting a buried quasar that accretes rapidly amid a vigorous starburst
(
Hopkins et al. 2008
)
. During this obscured phase, the supermassive BH grows swiftly behind a thick veil of dust, producing a compact, reddened object
(
Li et al. 2025
)
. The red, point-like AGNs observed by JWST could be nascent quasars captured during this transition phase—early BH growth spurts destined to mature into the familiar blue quasars or massive spheroidal host galaxies observed in the local Universe
(
Noboriguchi et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. Observational evidence bolsters this red-to-blue evolutionary scenario. Recent studies reveal that the nuclei of these objects are often surrounded by star-forming clumps
(
Tanaka et al. 2024
;
Meyer et al. 2025
)
, while at lower redshifts dust-obscured (red) quasars frequently show merger signatures in their host galaxies
(
Urrutia et al. 2008
;
Fan et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
, consistent with a merger-driven trigger for their activity. Additionally, detailed JWST/NIRSpec spectroscopy of LRDs has uncovered a broad-line AGN component with large Balmer decrement that has been interpreted as significant extinction (
A_{V}\approx 4–6
mag) dominating the rest-frame optical emission, accompanied by star-forming regions of low extinction (
A_{V}\approx 0.4
mag) that account for the UV light
(
Killi et al. 2024
;
Akins et al. 2025a
)
. These findings suggest that a heavily reddened quasar can drive the distinctive red SEDs of these sources.

However, this evolutionary picture confronts two significant unresolved challenges. First, red AGNs appear ubiquitous in JWST deep fields, accounting for a few percent of galaxies at
z\approx 5

(
Kokorev et al. 2024
;
Akins et al. 2025b
;
Kocevski et al. 2025
)
. If all these objects are undergoing a prolonged, rapid growth phase (e.g.,
Madau 2025
), this could overestimate the BH mass function at the high-mass end
(
Cavaliere & Padovani 1989
)
. Second, it remains unclear how the host galaxy evolves to bring the entire system into alignment with lower redshift observations. High-redshift quasars often deviate from the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
scaling relation, hosting BHs that are overmassive relative to their galaxies, suggesting that central BHs grow rapidly before their hosts catch up. JWST and ALMA studies indicate that many quasars at
z\gtrsim 5
harbor BHs that are
\sim 2–10
times more massive relative to their galaxy’s stellar mass than predicted by local scaling relations
(
Izumi et al. 2019
;
Pensabene et al. 2020
;
Maiolino et al. 2024
;
Yue et al. 2024
)
. In extreme cases, the BH mass rivals the stellar mass of the entire galaxy
(
Goulding et al. 2023
;
Stone et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
. Although the redshift evolution of the
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
correlation remains uncertain
(
Shen et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Zhuang et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Tanaka et al. 2025
)
, and some offsets may stem from selection effects
(
Venemans et al. 2016
;
Li et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, the discrepancy seems to persist even after accounting for such biases
(
Ding et al. 2020
)
.

A detailed study of the properties of both the AGN and its host galaxy is essential to disentangle fully the two observational challenges outlined above, namely understanding the evolutionary pathways of high-redshift quasars and explaining their offset from the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
scaling relation. However, the observed SED comprises contributions from both components, complicating their separation. At lower redshifts, broad-band SEDs, including X-ray data, can decompose effectively the signal contributions from the AGN and the host galaxy
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
. In contrast, the SED of high-redshift quasars is typically dominated by the AGN, rendering the host galaxy’s spectral features nearly undetectable
(
Marshall et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. Pure SED decomposition becomes challenging for fainter high-redshift AGNs because their broad-band spectrum often appears significantly redder, sometimes exhibiting a V-shaped distribution
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Labbé et al. 2025
)
.

Previous studies often relied on gas kinematics to measure the dynamical mass of the host galaxy to provide an indirect estimate of the stellar mass
M_{\ast}

(
Izumi et al. 2019
;
Nguyen et al. 2020
;
Pensabene et al. 2020
)
. With JWST, we can now observe quasar hosts in the rest-frame optical, which better traces stellar light. The high angular resolution and sensitivity of JWST deliver exceptionally deep images of quasar host galaxies previously accessible only for nearby AGNs using the Hubble Space Telescope
(
Kim et al. 2008
;
Kim et al. 2017
;
Kim et al. 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. Nevertheless, challenges persist: the angular sizes of high-redshift hosts are smaller on account of the larger angular diameter distances and their intrinsically compact nature
(
van der Wel et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
, rendering them to blend morphologically with the AGN point source. Nothwithstanding these difficulties, image decomposition techniques have detected successfully numerous quasar hosts at
z\gtrsim 6
and measured their
M_{\ast}

(
Ding et al. 2022
;
Yue et al. 2024
)
, often complemented by point-spread function (PSF) subtraction methods
(
Stone et al. 2024
)
. However, these approaches typically rely on a single filter—usually the reddest feasible band—leading to potential inconsistencies in the SED derived across different filters. Without comprehensive multi-band imaging, a significant fraction of AGN hosts remains undetected
(
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Yue et al. 2024
)
. Moreover, these studies poorly constrain the morphology or internal structure of the host galaxy, often assuming a specific functional form, such as a
Sérsic 1968
function with index
n=1

(
Stone et al. 2024
;
Yue et al. 2024
)
. In summary: to accurately disentangle the SEDs of the AGN and its host, investigate their physical properties, and elucidate the early evolutionary pathways of high-redshift quasars and their location relative to the
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
scaling relation, a self-consistent analysis of high-redshift quasars is imperative. This requires a multi-band imaging decomposition technique that ensures consistency across wavelengths.

This paper is structured as follows. Section
II
defines our sample and data reduction procedure for the JWST NIRCam images. We discuss our method to analyze the multi-band data and conduct extensive tests to assess the reliability of the AGN host galaxy measurements (Section
III
). Section
IV
presents our main results, including the mass and morphology of the host galaxy, as well as the properties of the AGN. Section
V
discusses the potential implications for the growth path of high-redshift supermassive BHs. The main conclusions are summarized in Section
VI
. For the adopted
\Lambda
CDM cosmology,
\Omega_{m}=0.308
,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.692
, and
H_{0}=67.8\,\rm\;km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016
)
.

II
Sample and Data Reduction

To investigate the early growth phases of high-redshift BHs and their host galaxies, we construct a comprehensive AGN sample by combining observations from several JWST surveys. Our final sample consists of 31 AGNs, comprising 15 powerful quasars and 16 lower luminosity counterparts at
z\gtrsim 4
.

II.1
Data

The high-luminosity quasars are drawn from three JWST programs.

•

We use data from the Subaru High-redshift Exploration of Low-luminosity Quasars (SHELLQs) survey, which focuses on quasars at redshifts
6.0<z<6.4
discovered by the Hyper Suprime-Cam Subaru Strategic Program
(
Izumi et al. 2019
)
. From the 12 SHELLQs quasars observed with JWST/NIRCam in the F150W and F356W filters (PID 1967, PI: M. Onoue), we select two (J2236+0032 and J2255+0251) that have publicly available NIRSpec spectroscopy analyzed by
Ding et al. 2023
. We adopt directly from
Ding et al. 2023
the luminosities at 5100 Å (
L_{5100}
; Table
1
) prior to any host-galaxy correction to ensure consistency across our sample.

•

We include data from the Emission-line Galaxies and Intergalactic Gas in the Epoch of Reionization (EIGER) survey (PID 1243; PI: S. Lilly), a Guaranteed Time Observation program targetting quasars at redshifts
6\lesssim z\lesssim 7
that provides deep NIRCam imaging in the F115W, F200W, and F356W filters and spectra from Wide-Field Slitless Spectroscopy (WFSS).
Yue et al. 2024
analyzed the WFSS data to derive spectral properties and physical parameters, including
L_{5100}
and
M_{\mathrm{BH}}
. EIGER provides five quasars, after excluding J0100+2802, which is saturated in all NIRCam bands.

•

The JWST program SPectroscopic Survey of Biased Halos In the Reionization Era (ASPIRE; PID 2078, PI: F. Wang) targets fields surrounding
z\approx 6-7
quasars with NIRCam imaging (F115W, F200W, and F356W) and WFSS spectroscopy. We select eight quasars with redshifts
6.5<z<6.8
whose WFSS F356W spectra have been analyzed by
Yang et al. 2023
.

The low-luminosity quasars comprise two subsets.

•

The first includes AGNs identified by
Harikane et al. 2023
through JWST/NIRSpec spectroscopy that show significant broad
H
\alpha
emission with signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
>5
. Among the 12 type 1 AGNs identified, four have publicly available JWST imaging from the Cosmic Evolution Early Release Science (CEERS;
Finkelstein et al. 2025
) survey covering all seven NIRCam bands (F115W, F150W, F200W, F277W, F356W, F410M, and F444W). For consistency with the rest of our sample, we recalculate
L_{5100}
from the broad
H
\alpha
luminosity (without intrinsic reddening correction) and convert the observed full width at half maximum (FWHM) of broad
H
\alpha
to that of broad
H
\beta
following
Greene & Ho 2005
. These CEERS sources span
5<z<6
with
L_{5100}\gtrsim 10^{43.5}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
.

•

The second subset consists of broad
H
\alpha
emitters (BHAEs) identified in WFSS F356W spectra within the ASPIRE fields
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
. From their initial sample of 16 sources, we exclude four that have incomplete imaging coverage or suffer from contamination by nearby stars, to yield 14 sources with redshifts (
4<z<5
) slightly lower compared to the CEERS subsample but that have comparable luminosities (
L_{5100}\gtrsim 10^{43.5}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
). We similarly convert their observed broad
H
\alpha
luminosities to
L_{5100}
and
\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mbox{H$\alpha$}}
to
\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mbox{H$\beta$}}
based on
Greene & Ho 2005
.

We uniformly calculate the BH masses (
M_{\mathrm{BH}}
; Table
1
) following the virial method described by
Ho & Kim 2015
. In the absence of detailed bulge classification at high redshift, we adopt the virial factor suitable for both classical and pseudo bulges.

II.2
Data Reduction

The NIRCam images were retrieved from the JWST Science Archive. For the CEERS fields, we downloaded complete imaging data for pointings 3 and 6, which cover all the CEERS objects under consideration in this study. For other targets, we selected all relevant JWST exposures within
6^{\prime}
of each object to ensure coverage by both NIRCam modules A and B. The level 2 calibrated images (
*cal.fits*
) of each filter were combined to maximize SNR.

Following JWST pipeline version 1.8.4, we first corrected the short-wavelength images (F115W, F150W, F200W) for “wisp” features and
1/f
noise. Subsequently, large-scale background subtraction was performed using iterative masking and the
Background2D
routine in
photutils
according to the procedures outlined in
Bagley et al. 2023
. Astrometric calibration involved aligning individual images using
Tweakwcs
, followed by absolute calibration against the Gaia DR3 catalog
(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023
)
. Individual images were inspected to remove those with severe pixel saturation or artifacts near target sources, which predominantly affect the EIGER and ASPIRE data. Cleaned images were then drizzled using the Image3Pipeline. To optimally sample the PSF, we adopt a pixel scale of
0\farcs 015
for shorter wavelengths (FWHM
=

0\farcs 060-0\farcs 075
) and
0\farcs 03
for longer wavelengths (FWHM
=

0\farcs 119-0\farcs 160
), based on the JWST PSF characterization by
Zhuang & Shen 2024
. This strategy ensures a Nyquist sampling rate approximately twice the instrumental PSF, which facilitates accurate morphological analysis.

III
Method

To characterize robustly the physical properties of both AGNs and their host galaxies using multi-band imaging, we perform a self-consistent image decomposition coupled with SED modeling. For this purpose, we utilize the newly developed code
GalfitS

(
Li & Ho 2025
)
, a forward-modeling decomposition tool designed explicitly for analyzing multi-band imaging data. Unlike traditional single-band imaging decomposition tools such as
Galfit

(
Peng et al. 2002
;
Peng et al. 2010
)
or
GaLight

(
Ding et al. 2020
)
,
GalfitS
simultaneously fits images taken in multiple filters by physically linking morphological parameters across different bands, thereby maintaining model consistency with well-motivated astrophysical priors. Furthermore,
GalfitS
does not require uniform world coordinate system transformations or identical pixel sampling, as mandated by tools like
GalfitM

(
Häußler et al. 2013
)
and
ProFuse

(
Robotham et al. 2022
)
. Crucially,
GalfitS
incorporates physically motivated SED models into the multi-band fitting process, enabling simultaneous extraction of the physical properties of the AGN and its host galaxy across all available imaging data at their optimal pixel scales.

Below we describe our method to construct empirical PSFs tailored to each individual source, our procedure for AGN-host decomposition, and extensive input-output tests conducted to evaluate the reliability of the image decomposition and host galaxy detections. We then present the fitting results and discuss our approach to interpreting physically the derived AGN SEDs.

III.1
Point-spread Function

Accurate PSF modeling is critical for robust AGN-host decomposition. For each target, we construct empirical PSFs from unsaturated, bright stars within the same NIRCam field. This approach is known to produce superior PSF models compared to purely theoretical methods such as
WebbPSF
(e.g.,
Zhuang & Shen 2024
). We select suitable stars in each band using
SExtractor

(
Bertin & Arnouts 1996
)
, following criteria recommended by
Zhuang & Shen 2024
. Candidate stars are identified as sources that have high SNR (
SNR_WIN

>
100) and unblended (
FLAGS

<
2), star-like (
CLASS_STAR

>
0.8) morphologies with elliptical elongations less than 1.5 and no problematic pixels (
IMAFLAGS_ISO

=
0). We then apply
3\,\sigma
-clipping around the median FWHM of the selected sources to exclude saturated stars. Importantly, we do not impose brightness limits relative to our quasars, for fainter stars naturally carry less weight in subsequent fitting procedures. Owing to variations in image size and field-of-view across different bands, the selection of PSF stars is performed independently in each filter. Consequently, the number of PSF stars differs from band to band.

There are several commonly used methods to create empirical PSFs, such as those implemented in
PSFEx
and
photutils
, and hybrid approaches that combine the empirical core of an observed star with the theoretical wings from
WebbPSF

(
Yue et al. 2024
;
Zhuang & Shen 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
. Appendix
A
uses CEERS data to compare these approaches against a new direct-fitting method implemented within
GalfitS
. Our direct-fitting method consistently yields the lowest reduced chi-square values among the tested stars, and we adopt it for the subsequent analysis. Example PSF models fitted to seven NIRCam bands from CEERS pointing 1 are shown in the top panel of Figure
1
.

As a preliminary assessment of host galaxy detection, Figure
1
(bottom panel) compares the median one-dimensional surface brightness profile of the AGNs with that of their respective PSF, derived using concentric circular apertures. While the powerful quasars exhibit profiles nearly identical to those of the PSFs, consistent with their high luminosities (
L_{5100}\gtrsim 10^{45}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
), the radial profile of lower luminosity AGNs (
L_{5100}\lesssim 10^{45}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
) depart significantly from that of the PSF at shorter wavelengths (F115W to F200W). This deviation diminishes progressively toward longer wavelengths (F277W and beyond), indicating higher host-to-AGN flux ratios in the UV and relative to rest-frame optical wavelengths. It is interesting to note that this trend is the opposite of that traditionally observed
(
Zhao et al. 2021
)
or assumed
(
Ding et al. 2022
)
in most prior AGN host galaxy studies.

III.2.1
GalfitS
Model Setup

Using
GalfitS
to decompose multi-band images of a galaxy into physically distinct subcomponents requires the specification of both morphological and SED models. Considering the spatial resolution limit of NIRCam, which is
\sim 0\farcs 06
for the F115W filter
(
Zhuang & Shen 2024
)
, we represent the underlying stellar mass distribution of the host galaxy with a simplified model of a single Sérsic component, whose parameters are the galaxy centroid position, Sérsic index (
n
), effective radius (
R_{e}
), axis ratio (
q
), and position angle (
\Theta
). While
GalfitS
permits wavelength-dependent variations in galaxy morphology, which would be a manifestation of, for example, radial gradient in stellar population or dust extinction (e.g.,
Jin et al. 2025
), for simplicity we assume that the SED is constant with radius. We model the SED of the host galaxy using stellar population synthesis with a
Kroupa 2001
stellar initial mass function and a non-parametric star formation history (SFH) motivated by the complex SFHs of high-redshift galaxies revealed by recent JWST observations (e.g.,
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Wright et al. 2024
. The stellar population parameters include stellar mass (
M_{\ast}
), star formation rate (SFR) within discrete time bins, metallicity (
Z
), and
V
-band dust extinction (
A_{V}
) assuming a
Calzetti et al. 2000
extinction curve.

Figure
2
gives two examples of fitted host galaxy models to illustrate the simultaneous derivation of morphological and SED fits. The AGN overwhelmingly dominates the host galaxy in J0305
-
3150, whereas in J0224
-
4711-BHAE-1 the nucleus and galaxy are of comparable brightness. In both cases we only have three filters. In situations when nearby companions potentially contaminate the target (e.g., CEERS 027825; left panel of Figure
3
), we introduce an additional Sérsic component to model the neighboring galaxy. In the absence of redshift information for the neighbor, we treat its redshift as a free parameter during the fit. Notes for individual objects are provided in Appendix
B
.

We represent the AGN with a pure point source described by the empirical PSF.
GalfitS
offers both parametric and non-parametric options for modeling the nuclear SED. The parametric approach relies on predefined emission templates commonly employed in AGN spectroscopic analyses
(
Ho & Kim 2009
;
Li et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. However, given the limited number of filters (typically three per source in our dataset), we opt for a non-parametric model, independently fitting the luminosities in each band. Even for the four CEERS AGNs with more extensive photometric coverage, our input-output tests (Appendix
C
) demonstrate that parametric models do not give significant improvements. Examples of the best-fit non-parametric nuclear SEDs are shown as green circles in Figures
2
and
3
. Table
2
summarizes the specific
GalfitS
modeling configurations for each dataset of our sample.

III.2.2
GalfitS
Prior Setup

GalfitS
allows the user the option to set up priors during the posterior sampling process to avoid unphysical solutions. Previous studies have encountered major major challenges when decomposing AGNs to derive the physical properties of their host galaxies. When the AGN light—described by the PSF—overshines the host galaxy, it can bias the solution toward an unphysically small effective radius (
R_{e}
) for the Sérsic profile
(
Stone et al. 2024
)
of the host. Moreover, the limited number of available filters (e.g., only two for the SHELLQs quasars and three for the EIGER and ASPIRE AGNs) hampers adequate sampling of the host galaxy SED. This, in turn, results in significant degeneracy among the SED parameters, particularly the SFH, metallicity, and dust extinction
(
Conroy 2013
)
.
GalfitS
addresses these challenges by incorporating astrophysical priors during the fitting process, as follows.

First, to prevent the galaxy model from becoming arbitrarily compact, we assume that AGN host galaxies occupy a similar region of parameter space in the stellar mass–size plane as inactive galaxies at comparable redshifts. During posterior sampling, we impose the stellar mass-size relation (MSR) as a prior to constrain the effective radius of the Sérsic component according to

\log\,\left(\frac{R_{e}}{\mathrm{kpc}}\right)=\log\,A+\alpha\left[\log\,\left(\frac{M_{\ast}}{M_{\odot}}\right)-10.7\right]+\sigma.

(1)

Here, we adopt a slope of
\alpha=0.22
derived from JWST imaging analyses of high-redshift star-forming galaxies
(
Allen et al. 2025
)
, which remains approximately constant for
3\lesssim z\lesssim 7
. Consistent with previous results
(
van der Wel et al. 2014
)
, the zero point varies with redshift as
\log\,A=-0.81\log\,(1+z)+0.95

(
Allen et al. 2025
)
. For the luminous quasar sample at
6\lesssim z\lesssim 7
, we adopt
\log\,A=0.23
based on their median redshift; for the less luminosity A-BHAE AGNs, we use
\log\,A=0.35
to reflect their lower redshifts. An intrinsic scatter of
\sigma=0.2
dex is assumed for both. In practice, as illustrated in Figure
4
, this implies that during sampling
R_{e}
is drawn from a normal distribution with a median value of
\log\,A+\alpha\left[\log\,\left(\frac{M_{\ast}}{M_{\odot}}\right)-10.7\right]
and a width given by
\sigma
.

Second, dust extinction in the host galaxy cannot be measured independently via the Balmer decrement (e.g.,
Domínguez et al. 2013
) because our data do not cover both
H
\alpha
and
H
\beta
in the observed wavelength range, not to mention of the difficulty of measuring the narrow lines in the presence of the much stronger broad lines from the AGN. As a result,
A_{V}
becomes degenerate with other SED parameters that affect the colors, such as
Z
and SFH. To mitigate this degeneracy, we adopt an a scaling relation between dust extinction and stellar mass derived from the
H
\alpha
/
H
\beta
decrement for nearly one million galaxies at
z\lesssim 3

(
Maheson et al. 2024
)
. Assuming the extinction curve of
Calzetti et al. 2000
, which is adopted in
GalfitS
(Section
III.2.1
), we convert
H
\alpha
/
H
\beta
to
A_{V}
and transform Equation 8 of
Maheson et al. 2024
to the stellar mass–dust extinction relation (MER)

\displaystyle A_{V}

\displaystyle=a_{0}+a_{1}\log\,M_{\ast}+a_{2}\bigl(\log\,M_{\ast}\bigr)^{2}

(2)

\displaystyle+a_{3}\bigl(\log\,M_{\ast}\bigr)^{3}+a_{4}\bigl(\log\,M_{\ast}\bigr)^{4}+\sigma,

with
a_{0}=116.282
,
a_{1}=-32.7843
,
a_{2}=2.98265
,
a_{3}=-0.079877
, and
a_{4}=-0.00059
.
1
1

1

The original
H
\alpha
/
H
\beta
–
\log\,M_{\ast}
relation was a third‐order polynomial, but converting
H
\alpha
/
H
\beta
to
A_{V}
introduces additional nonlinearity. We find that a fourth‐order function reproduces well the results of
Maheson et al. 2024
.
The MER should evolve with redshift on account of changes in metallicity with cosmic epoch. Moreover, Equation
2
pertains to galaxies at redshifts (
z\lesssim 3
) lower than those of our quasar sample. However, overplotting recent JWST measurements of the Balmer decrement in galaxies at
4\lesssim z\lesssim 6.5

(
Shapley et al. 2023
)
reveals that they track our MER prior (Figure
4
). Instead of adopting the intrinsic scatter of
\sigma=0.2
dex from
Maheson et al. 2024
, we increase it to
\sigma=0.4
dex to better match the scatter observed in the JWST data.

Third, to mitigate degeneracies among metallicity, age, and SFH, we impose a stellar mass–metallicity relation (MZR) prior based on
(
Nakajima et al. 2023
)

\log\,(\mathrm{O/H})+12=5.74+0.25\,\log\,M_{\ast}+\sigma,

(3)

assuming an intrinsic scatter of
\sigma=0.2
dex. This relation is derived from JWST/NIRSpec data at redshifts
z=4-10
, although it only covers the limited stellar mass range of
\log\,(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})=7.5-9.5
. The flattening of the MZR for
Z\gtrsim Z_{\odot}
observed at
z=0

(
Kewley & Ellison 2008
)
does not affect our fitting results because the metallicities of our sample are generallu sub-solar (Table
3
). Incorporating the MZR as a prior thus effectively constrains the metallicity based on stellar mass, reducing degeneracies with age and SFH
(
Maiolino et al. 2008
)
.

Lastly, as outlined in Section
III.2
, we adopt a non‐parametric SFH
(
Carnall et al. 2019
)
to avoid unphysical constraints that can arise from parametric models. In practice, however, most objects in our sample (except those in the CEERS subsample) have fewer than three photometric filters, which are insufficient to constrain robustly a non‐parametric SFH
(
Leja et al. 2019
)
. Therefore, for all fits apart from those for the CEERS subsample, we impose a SFH prior derived from JWST galaxies observed at comparable redshifts. We define a uniform set of bins in look‐back time logarithmically spaced as [0.0, 0.02, 0.0477, 0.0689, 0.1, 0.144, 0.208, 0.301, 0.434, 0.628, 0.91] Gyr, each truncated at a cosmic time of 0.2 Gyr to represent the approximate onset of galaxy formation
(
Bouwens et al. 2015
)
. These SFH bins are applied to all objects, including those in the CEERS sample. For objects not in CEERS, we further refine the SFH by computing the median observed SFR in each bin, along with the
1\,\sigma
scatter, using a sample of approximately 2500 JWST galaxies at
5.5\lesssim z\lesssim 7.0

(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
2
2

2

For the A-BHAE subsample, which occupies a lower redshift range, we use a slightly different set of time bins, namely [0., 0.02, 0.04, 0.062, 0.096, 0.149, 0.232, 0.359, 0.558 , 0.865, 1.342] Gyr, and we base the SFR priors on
\sim
4300 JWST galaxies at
4.0\lesssim z\lesssim 5.0

(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
.
. During the fitting process, an overall normalization offset from these median SFRs is permitted, while the relative shape across the bins is constrained to match the observed distribution. An example of this procedure is illustrated in Figure
5
.

We applied the aforementioned priors to the SHELLQs, EIGER, ASPIRE, and A-BHAR datasets (Table
2
), but not to the CEERS dataset because our input–output tests (Appendix
C
) indicate that the available filters are sufficient to constrain well the host galaxy parameters.

III.2.3
Parameter Posterior Estimation

We use a nested‐sampling algorithm (e.g.,
Skilling 2004
) to estimate the posterior distributions because it explores high‐dimensional parameter spaces more efficiently than many traditional Markov chain Monte Carlo approaches (e.g.,
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
). For the galaxy stellar mass, we adopt a log‐uniform prior from
M_{\ast}=10^{7.5}
to
10^{12.5}\,M_{\odot}
. For
A_{V}
and the SFR in different time bins, we use Gaussian priors (Section
III.2.2
) derived from JWST observations of real galaxies at similar redshifts. Regarding geometric parameters, we allow the galaxy or PSF centroid to vary within
\pm\,0\farcs 1
of the image peak position, fitting the positions of the galaxy and PSF independently. The axis ratio has a uniform prior in the range
q=[0.2,1.0]
, motivated by the intrinsic thickness for typical galaxy morphologies
(
Padilla & Strauss 2008
)
, while the position angle is uniformly sampled over all possible orientations. The Sérsic index has a uniform prior from
n=0.5
to
6
. For blended systems, we also allow the redshift of the nearby galaxy to vary from
0.5
to
8.0
, as we generally lack information on whether the nearby source is projected or is physically associated. Detailed notes on the properties of the nearby sources appear in Appendix
B
.

We use the Python package
dynesty
to carry out the nested‐sampling process, employing
500
live points (roughly 20 times the dimensionality of the parameter space) to ensure a well‐sampled posterior. Figure
4
shows a corner plot of an example of the sampling for a subset of parameters. We take the weighted median for each parameter as our best‐fit value, with uncertainties derived from the diagonal elements of the weighted covariance matrix. Instead of giving the full parameters for the SFH modeling, we calculate the SFR during the recent
20\,
Myr. We also compute the mass-weighted look-back age,

\bar{t}=\frac{1}{M_{\ast}}\int_{0}^{t_{b}}\tau\,\mathrm{SFR}(\tau)\,d\tau,

(4)

where
t_{b}
represents the look-back time of the object when the cosmic age is 0.2 Gyr, and
\tau
is the look-back time. An example of the calculated SFR and
\bar{t}
is shown in Figure
5
. A summary of the properties of the host galaxies and the AGN is given in Tables
3
and

, respectively.

III.3
Host Galaxy Detectability and Uncertainty

We perform a series of input–output tests (Appendix
C
) using mock AGN images to assess the detectability of host galaxies with our method and to estimate upper limits on host galaxy properties (Section
III.3.1
). These empirical experiments also allow us to test the reliability of the key host galaxy parameters, as well as to quantify possible systematics in our fitting approach and the applied priors (Section
III.3.2
). While the uncertainties derived from nested sampling reflect only the formal errors based on our chosen parametric model, empirical PSF, and astrophysical priors, additional systematic uncertainties arising from our choice of empirical PSF must also be considered (Section
III.3.3
). We generate the mock images by artificially inserting a nucleus, represented by the PSF model, of varying strength onto real galaxy images at similar redshifts.

III.3.1
Host Galaxy Detection

We define a host galaxy as detected if the addition of a galaxy component results in an improved Bayesian information criterion (BIC) relative to a pure nucleus model, where

\mathrm{BIC}=\chi^{2}_{r}+k\ln N,

(5)

with
k
the number of model parameters and
N
the total number of unmasked pixels across all images. To compare the BIC of the nucleus plus galaxy model (i.e., the
GalfitS
model from Section
III.2
) with that of a pure nucleus model, we remove the galaxy component and refit the data, allowing the PSF luminosity and position to vary independently in each band, and then compute

\delta_{\mathrm{BIC}}=\frac{\mathrm{BIC}_{\rm nuc+gal}-\mathrm{BIC}_{\rm nuc}}{\mathrm{BIC}_{\rm nuc}}.

(6)

The values of
\delta_{\mathrm{BIC}}
for our sample are listed in Table
3
. Adopting
\delta_{\mathrm{BIC}}<0
as the criterion for detection, we find evidence for a host galaxy in 30 out of the 31 AGNs in our sample. The only non-detection is J1526
-
2050-BHAE-2, which has
\delta_{\mathrm{BIC}}=0.002
and the lowest stellar mass [
\log\,(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})=8.35
] in the PSF+galaxy fit. We detect galaxy hosts in several objects that previously had been unsuccessful
(
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Yue et al. 2024
)
. This improvement arises from several advantages of our approach (Section
III.2
) compared to earlier single-band image decomposition methods. First,
GalfitS
performs a simultaneous fit across multiple bands, which effectively boosts the host galaxy signal. Second, previous decomposition techniques
(
Ding et al. 2023
;
Yue et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
relied solely on the F356W band, which, despite offering a favorable AGN/host flux ratio (see Figure
2
), has a spatial resolution that is about 2.4 times worse than F115W and 1.5 times worse than F200W. Our multi-band fit yields a higher effective spatial resolution than fitting only the longest wavelength filter. Third,
GalfitS
incorporates observational priors (Section
III.2.2
) to exclude unphysical parameter spaces, thereby promoting convergence toward a more globally optimal solution.

To confirm that the higher detection rate indeed arises from our improved decomposition method, we carry out three types of
GalfitS
runs on the mock AGNs: (1)
run1
: a pure‐galaxy, single‐Sérsic fit on ia carefully matched sample of inactive galaxies before manually adding the nucleus; (2)
run2
: a point source‐only fit on the mock AGN images; and (3)
run3
: a point source
+
galaxy fit on the mock AGN images, applying the astrophysical priors from Section
III.2.2
. We repeat these runs for three data configurations, using all NIRCam filters (7-band), only F115W+F200W+F356W (3‐band), and only F356W (1‐band). To mimick our treatment of the CEERS data, no astrophysical priors are applied for the 1‐band and 7-band fits in
run3
, while for the 3-band fits we adopt the priors in Section
III.2.2
to be consistent with our analysis of the EIGER, ASPIRE, and A-BHAE data. We then compare detection rates under these different setups as a function of
f_{\mathrm{F356W}}
, defined as the ratio between the injected AGN luminosity and the galaxy luminosity at F356W, from the single‐Sérsic, pure‐galaxy fit (Figure
6
). The mock AGNs span
\log\,f_{\mathrm{F356W}}=-3
to 3, covering both the high-luminosity (
\log\,f_{\mathrm{F356W}}=1.33^{+0.14}_{-0.41}
) and low-luminosity (
\log\,f_{\mathrm{F356W}}=-1.03^{+0.51}_{-0.12}
) samples. For the 1‐band fits, the detection rate exceeds 60% only for
0.1\lesssim f_{\mathrm{F356W}}\lesssim 1
, whereas for the 3‐band or 7‐band fits the detection rate exceeds
80\%
for
f_{\mathrm{F356W}}\lesssim 100
. These results demonstrate the strong capability of our multi‐band approach for detecting AGN host galaxies across a wide dynamic range in AGN‐to‐host luminosity ratios.

We designed a dedicated set of mock AGNs for J1526
-
2050-BHAE-2 (hereafter “mock J1526
-
2050-BHAE-2;” Table
2
), whose host galaxy was undetected (
\delta_{\mathrm{BIC}}>0
), in order estimate an upper limit on the stellar mass for this source, recognizing that its SED may differ from that of the general mock AGNs. Specifically, we select 190 additional CEERS galaxies from
Sun et al. 2024
with
4\lesssim z\lesssim 5
to match the redshift range of the A-BHAE AGNs. We then inject a nucleus into these galaxies using the same empirical PSF from the CEERS field, scaling the point source fluxes to the observed F115W, F200W, and F356W fluxes of J1526
-
2050-BHAE-2 obtained from a point source–only fit. We use the “uplim” run (Table
2
) to estimate the detection rate as a function of stellar mass derived from the pure-galaxy fit. The detection rate exceeds 60% in the bin
8.4\leq\log\,(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})\leq 8.6
(Figure
7
), and thus we adopt the upper bound of this bin and set the stellar mass upper limit for J1526
-
2050-BHAE-2 at
\log\,(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})=8.59
.

III.3.2
Reliability of the Fitting Results

To evaluate the ability of
GalfitS
to recover host galaxy properties, we compare the results of
run1
and
run3
to study the systematic offsets in stellar mass (
\Delta\log\,M_{\ast}
), effective radius (
\Delta\log\,R_{e}
), and Sérsic index (
\Delta n
) as a function of the AGN‐to‐host flux ratio (Figure
8
). Across a wide range of
f_{\mathrm{F356W}}
, the median offsets remain small, which indicates that even in the presence of a luminous AGN component the multiband decomposition reliably recovers the underlying host parameters. It is worth noting that for the 7‐band run, we adopt only the MZR prior, leaving
R_{e}
entirely free, following our treatment for the CEERS AGNs. In contrast, the 3‐band run includes the MSR, MER, and SFH priors (Section
III.2.2
) to match our treatment of the other quasars. Nevertheless, Figure
8
shows that the recovered values
M_{\ast}
,
R_{e}
, and
n
are consistent regardless of whether the MSR, MER, and SFH priors are imposed, reinforcing the general reliability of our decomposition method (Section
III.2
).

III.3.3
Uncertainty from the PSF

As in many previous studies, our AGN–host decomposition method adopts a PSF model derived from field stars to represent the active nucleus. Care must be taken, however, to understand the potential impact of PSF variations that might arise from, for instance, differences in color and detector position
(
Zhuang & Shen 2024
)
. We employ
run3
of our mock AGN analysis to assess the sensitivity of
M_{\ast}
to the choice of PSF. For each galaxy and for mock AGN images with nuclear luminosities
L_{5100}=10^{43}-10^{46}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
, we generate independent realizations by adopting the PSF from five distinct field stars as the point source profile. We then compute
\sigma_{M_{\ast}\,\rm PSF}
, the standard deviation of
\log\,M_{\ast}
obtained from fitting these images. Notably,
\sigma_{M_{\ast}\,\rm PSF}
shows little dependence on the true galaxy mass but increases significantly with the input AGN luminosity (Figure
9
), and over the range
\sim 0.05-0.4
dex it can be approximated by
\sigma_{\log\,{M_{\ast}}\,\rm PSF}=0.12\,\log\,(L_{5100}/\rm erg\,s^{-1})-5.11
. We estimate the final uncertainty of the stellar mass measurements (
\sigma_{\log M_{\ast}}
) as the quadrature sum of the PSF-induced uncertainty and the nested-sampling error derived from the posterior distribution (Section
III.2.3
). For all other parameters, we estimate uncertainties by propagating the error in
\log M_{\ast}
. If we let
\widehat{\log M_{\ast}}
be the best‐fit value, we first select from the nested‐sampling “dead” points those satisfying
\widehat{\log M_{\ast}}-\sigma_{\log M_{\ast}}\;\leq\;\log M_{\ast}\;\leq\;\widehat{\log M_{\ast}}+\sigma_{\log M_{\ast}}
. This filtering effectively enlarges our posterior sample to encompass the
\pm\,1\,\sigma
range in stellar mass. We then define the uncertainty of each remaining parameter as the standard deviation of its values over this filtered posterior set. The resulting parameter uncertainties are presented in Figure
4
and summarized in Table
3
as the final parameter uncertainties.

III.4
AGN SED Analysis

The
GalfitS
AGN-host decomposition (Section
III.2
) treats the observed AGN luminosity in each band as an independent parameter. Unlike the rest‐frame luminosity, the observed AGN luminosity is defined as
L_{\mathrm{AGN}}\equiv 4\pi f_{\mathrm{AGN}}D_{L}^{2}
, where
f_{\mathrm{AGN}}
is the observed (fitted) flux in a given filter and
D_{L}
is the luminosity distance (Table
4
).
GalfitS
adopts a minimal‐parameter model to further characterize the broad‐band SED of the AGN. Motivated by the composite UV-optical spectrum of quasars of
Vanden Berk et al. 2001
, we employ a single power law (
f_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{\beta}
) for the high-luminosity quasars and for the less luminous subsample a broken power law that breaks at
5100
Å. This parameterization suffices because our longest observed filter only reaches
\sim 4700
Å in the rest-frame for the luminous sources at higher redshifts. The free parameters of this SED are the rest‐frame luminosity at
5100
Å (
L_{\mathrm{5100}}
), the UV spectral index
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
for
\lambda\leq 5100
Å, and the optical spectral index
\beta_{\mathrm{opt}}
for
\lambda\geq 5100
Å. We include broad (O VI
\lambda 1038
, Ly
\alpha
, N V
\lambda 1242
, Si IV
\lambda 1400
, C IV
\lambda 1549
, He II
\lambda 1640
, C III]
\lambda 1909
, Mg II
\lambda 2800
, H
\gamma
, H
\beta
, H
\alpha
) as well as narrow (H
\beta
, [O III]
\lambda\lambda 4959,\,5007
, [O I]
\lambda\lambda 6300,\,6364
, [N II]
\lambda\lambda 6548,\,6584
, H
\alpha
, [S II]
\lambda\lambda 6716,\,6731
) emission lines, whose relative intensities are linked via empirically established scaling relations. In particular, the broad H
\alpha
and H
\beta
luminosities are tied to
L_{\mathrm{5100}}
following
Greene & Ho 2005
, and other lines are scaled according to the composite spectrum of
Vanden Berk et al. 2001
. The [O III]
\lambda 5007
luminosity is further linked to broad H
\alpha
based on
Stern & Laor 2012
, while other narrow lines adhere to the ratios given by
Stern & Laor 2013
for type 1 AGNs. The line widths are also constrained: for simplicity, the broad lines follow the virial relation from
Ho & Kim 2015
, whereas narrow lines have a fixed width of
300
km s
-1

(
Nelson & Whittle 1996
)
. We do not include a pseudo‐continuum (e.g., broad
F
e
ii
emission and Balmer continuum), given that our coverage is primarily in the rest‐frame UV and their strengths are not well constrained by the available data. In practice, the broken power law effectively accounts for both the accretion disk continuum and any unmodeled
F
e
ii
emission or Balmer continuum. An example of our AGN SED model is illustrated in the bottom panels of Figure
2
, and the best‐fit parameters appear in Table
4
.

Figure
10
compares our
L_{\mathrm{5100}}
measurements with independent spectroscopic estimates. For the low-luminosity quasars,
L_{\mathrm{5100}}
is derived from the broad H
\alpha
luminosities reported by
Harikane et al. 2023
and
Lin et al. 2024
, converted using the empirical scaling relation derived for nearby AGNs by
Greene & Ho 2005
. Our SED‐based measurements are systematically lower by
\bar{\Delta}\log\,L_{\mathrm{5100}}=-0.62
dex, implying that the observed ratio of broad H
\alpha
to continuum strength is larger than in local AGNs. This trend is consistent with the overall tendency for JWST‐selected AGNs at
z\geq 4
to exhibit broad H
\alpha
equivalent widths roughly 3 times those in nearby AGNs
(
Maiolino et al. 2025
)
. For the luminous quasar subsample, we obtain slightly higher luminosities (
\bar{\Delta}\log\,L_{\mathrm{5100}}=0.08
dex) compared to spectral‐decomposition measurements
(
Ding et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Yue et al. 2024
)
, an affect that can be plausibly attributed to our power‐law model, which includes as part of the continuum
F
e
ii
emission that is known to be strong in these sources
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
. We convert
L_{\mathrm{5100}}
into a rough estimate of the mass accretion rate of the BH (
\dot{M}_{\rm BH}
; Table
4
), assuming that the AGN bolometric luminosity
L_{\mathrm{bol}}=10.6\,L_{\mathrm{5100}}

(
Richards et al. 2006
)
and a fiducial radiative efficiency of
\eta=0.1

(
Sołtan 1982
;
Yu & Tremaine 2002
)
.

We keep the AGN SED model intentionally simple, with only 2 to 3 free parameters designed to capture both the overall strength and spectral shape of the continuum. Despite its minimalism, it performs well for our sample of 31 AGNs. In principle,
GalfitS
can also be configured to fit the AGN SED simultaneously during the image decomposition. Instead of treating AGN luminosity in each band as an independent parameter, one can solve directly for
L_{\mathrm{5100}}
,
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
, and
\beta_{\mathrm{opt}}
. We tested this more constrained, parametric approach but found that, because of the fewer degrees of freedom and the assumption that all emission lines are fixed to a fraction of the continuum luminosity, the resulting
\chi^{2}_{r}
of the fits often deteriorate by an average of
\sim 30\%
. To evaluate systematically which strategy recovers host galaxy properties more accurately, we performed an additional series of tests using on our mock AGNs (
run4
) by directly fitting the AGN+galaxy system with the above parametric SED model. Appendix
C
compares the derived 7‐band stellar masses from our baseline approach (
run3
) and
run4
. For systems with AGN‐to‐host flux ratios
f_{\mathrm{F356W}}\lesssim 2
, the free‐SED method (Section
III.2
) exhibits less systematic bias, whereas the parametri-SED approach underestimates the stellar mass and shows larger scatter. Interestingly, at very high flux ratios (
f_{\mathrm{F356W}}\gtrsim 2
)—wherein the AGN strongly dominates—the parametric approach achieves near‐zero systematic bias. However, across the range of
f_{\mathrm{F356W}}
relevant for our sample
run3
provides much better consistency. This outcome further supports the robustness of our chosen AGN–host decomposition strategy (Section
III.2
).

IV.1
Host Galaxy Stellar Mass

Stellar masses (or upper limits) have already been published for 11 of the 31 AGNs in our sample, including two from SHELLQs
(
Ding et al. 2023
)
, five from EIGER
(
Yue et al. 2024
)
, and four from CEERS
(
Harikane et al. 2023
)
. These studies employed a two-step method to derive
M_{\ast}
: imaging decomposition to detect possible host galaxy emission, followed by SED fitting. Comparison of our stellar masses with those from the literature reveals that the two sets of meaqsurements are generally consistent, with a slight tendency for our values to be higher (
\bar{\Delta}\log\,M_{\ast}=0.22
dex; Figure
11
). However, we emphasize that our stellar mass measurements are likely to be more robust. We use a multi-band decomposition approach that ensures that the imaging model of the host galaxy is consistent across multiple bands and aligns with the SED model of the AGN. Extensive mock simulations (Section
III.3.2
) validate our methodology, confirming that we can recover accurate stellar masses even in the presence of a luminous quasar.

Despite these improvements, stellar mass derivation remains sensitive to assumptions about the galaxy’s SED.
Yue et al. 2024
show that SED parameters are poorly constrained using
Prospector

(
Johnson et al. 2021
)
when only three photometric points are available. For instance, although our measurement of the host galaxy flux of J0148+0600,
\log\,f_{\rm 356W}=1.47\pm 0.07
, agrees with the value of 1.4 reported by
Yue et al. 2024
, our derived stellar mass is 0.44 dex higher than their value of
\log\,(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})=10.64
, likely as a consequence of differences in assumed dust extinction and SFH. In the case of J1030+0524 and J1148+5251, our stellar masses exceed the upper limits from
Yue et al. 2024
, even though, by our estimation, their detection limit is comparable to ours.
Yue et al. 2024
calculated their
M_{\ast}
upper limits by scaling the SED of J0148+0600, which, as mentioned above, yields a lower stellar mass than our measurement.

Figure
12
examines the relationship between BH mass and total stellar mass for our sample of high-redshift quasars. For comparison, we overlay the
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\ast}
relation for over 11,000 low-redshift type 1 AGNs, after applying a mild evolutionary correction to
z=0
(blue dashed line;
Zhuang & Ho 2023
), the equivalent relation for inactive local galaxies (black dashed line;
Greene et al. 2020
), as well as the relation that pertains only to nearby inactive classical bulges and ellipticals (red dashed line;
Kormendy & Ho 2013
). A linear fit to the 30 high-redshift quasars with detected host galaxies yields
\log\,M_{\mathrm{BH}}=(0.70\pm 0.07)\,\log\,M_{\ast}\,+\,(1.29\pm 0.67)
. Notably, the slope of this relation is roughly only half of that of
z=0
galaxies, whether active (slope 1.46;
Zhuang & Ho 2023
) or not (slope 1.26;
Greene et al. 2020
).

Among inactive classical bulges and elliptical galaxies in the local Universe, the central BH occupies
\sim 0.5\%
(intrinsic scatter 0.28 dex) of the stellar mass budget for
M_{\mathrm{\ast,bulge}}\approx 10^{10}\,M_{\odot}

(
Kormendy & Ho 2013
)
. The 15 high-z quasars analyzed in this study (blue points) exhibit a systematically somewhat higher relative BH mass fraction (median
M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_{\ast}=1.2\%
; scatter 0.51 dex), but substantially lower than previous estimates that approach 10% based on stellar masses inferred from ALMA-derived dynamical masses
(
Venemans et al. 2017
;
Pensabene et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2024c
, e.g.,)
. For instance, two quasars in our sample, J0224
-
4711 and J2002
-
3013, have published dynamical masses of
M_{\mathrm{dyn}}=10^{11.32}\,M_{\odot}
and
10^{10.76}\,M_{\odot}

(
Wang et al. 2024c
)
, which are lower than our measurements of
M_{\ast}=10^{11.92}\,M_{\odot}
and
10^{11.48}\,M_{\odot}
, respectively. What underlies this discrepancy? A likely culprit for the underestimated dynamical masses is that the [C II]
158\,\mu
m emission detected by ALMA does not reach yet the flat part of the rotation curve, which typically lies at
2-3
times
R_{e}

(
Sofue & Rubin 2001
;
Walter et al. 2008
)
. According to our
GalfitS
analysis, J0224
-
4711 has
R_{e}=1.09\pm 0.48
kpc and J2002
-
3013
R_{e}=1.41\pm 0.34
kpc (Table
3
), which are comparable to the [C II] disk sizes of
R_{\mathrm{[C\,II]}}=1.42\pm 0.08
and
1.21\pm 0.19
kpc, respectively
(
Wang et al. 2024c
)
.

More extreme BH-to-host mass ratios characterize the less luminous quasars (red points; Figure
12
), for which
M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_{\ast}=4.7\%
with a scatter of 0.50 dex,
\sim 4
times higher than the value for the most luminous quasars. While these mass ratios are less extreme than the values reported by
Chen et al. 2025
for LRDs (orange points), our results contribute to the growing body of evidence that
z\gtrsim 4
AGNs often host overmassive BHs
(
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Kocevski et al. 2023b
;
Maiolino et al. 2024
)
. Collectively, these findings support the notion that BH growth outpaces stellar mass assembly in high-redshift systems.

IV.2
Host Galaxy Morphology

Among the many empirical correlations that characterize the galaxy population, of the most widespread is the relation between stellar mass and size (e.g.,
Shen et al. 2003
;
van der Wel et al. 2014
). Our sample of high-redshift AGN host galaxies lies systematically below the MSR for galaxies at similar redshifts (Figure
13
). Using the fiducial MSR at
z=6.5
as reference
(
Allen et al. 2025
)
, the median size deviations of low-luminosity and high-luminosity quasars is
\bar{\Delta}\log\,{R_{e}}=-0.34
and
-0.35
dex, respectively.

Apart from size, we also examine the distribution of Sérsic indices of our quasar hosts with matched galaxy populations from
Sun et al. 2024
, who used two-dimensional parametric fits to analyzed a large sample of redshift
4<z<9:5
with
M_{\ast}\approx 10^{7}-10^{11}\,M_{\odot}
using seven-band NIRCam observations from the CEERS program. To enable a direct comparison, we divide the galaxy sample into two redshift bins,
4\leq z\leq 6
and
6\leq z\leq 7
, matching their stellar masses to those of the high-luminosity and low-luminosity quasars, respectively (upper panel in Figure
14
). Relative to the control sample of inactive galaxies, the host galaxies of high-luminosity quasars possess significantly larger median Sérsic indices (
n\approx 5
), consistent with early-type morphologies. By contrast, the host galaxies of the less powerful quasars exhibit Sérsic indices
n\approx 1
, similar to their matched counterparts and indicative of exponential, disk-like structures. These results strongly indicate that luminous quasars reside in more centrally concentrated, evidently bulge-dominated host galaxies distinctly different from both typical star-forming galaxies and lower luminosity AGNs at similar redshifts and comparable stellar mass. Like the most powerful quasars, their less luminous counterparts are also more compact at a given stellar mass than typical galaxies, but their stellar mass distribution or internal structure (concentration, Sérsic
n
, bulge-to-disk ratio) is manifestedly different. Our results echo
Hubble Space Telescope
studies that find a higher incidence of early-type hosts among more low-redshift quasars of higher luminosity and larger BH mass (e.g.,
Dunlop et al. 2003
;
Kim et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2021
. Meanwhile, our observations are broadly consistent with theoretical expectations that luminous quasars at high redshift form in dense environments conducive to rapid gas inflows and gas-rich mergers. Such dissipative processes efficiently funnel gas toward galaxy centers, rapidly building dense stellar cores that host luminous accreting supermassive BHs (e.g.,
Dekel & Burkert 2014
;
Zolotov et al. 2015
).

While the comparison between the AGNs and the control sample of inactive galaxies points to plausible intrinsic differences the two populations, some caution needs to be exercised because there are methodological differences between our decomposition technique and the approach employed to study the control sample. With the aid of
GalfitM

(
Häußler et al. 2013
)
,
Allen et al. 2025
performed single-compoment Sérsic profile fits and showed that galaxy size increases toward longer rest-frame wavelengths (but see
Jin et al. 2025
for discussion about departures from this trend). In contrast, our
GalfitS
decomposition assumes a uniform spectral shape for the host galaxy without accounting for wavelength-dependent structural structural variations. Given the younger ages typical at high redshift, as reflected in our stellar population modeling results (see Table
3
), the sizes we measure are likely biased toward UV wavelengths. Although this effect potentially can explain part of the observed compactness of quasar host galaxies, we believe that it is unlikely to be the whole story. The effective radii of high-redshift galaxies is typically only
0.15
dex smaller in the UV than in the optical
(
Allen et al. 2025
)
, much less than the observed size discrepancy between quasar hosts and normal galaxies. Instead, our findings strongly imply that high-redshift quasar host galaxies are intrinsically more compact (smaller
R_{e}
) than inactive galaxies of similar stellar mass at comparable redshift. This conclusion aligns with previous observational studies at intermediate redshifts (
1.6\lesssim z\lesssim 3.5
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2022
). A similar caveat applies to the Sérsic indices drawn from
Sun et al. 2024
, which were also derived using
GalfitM
. Radial color gradients produce wavelength-dependent variations in Sérsic
n

(
Jin et al. 2025
)
, but not at a level that can account for the gross differences in concentration found between the high-luminosity quasars and control galaxies (lower panel in Figure
14
).

IV.3
A Bimodality in AGN Colors?

We fit the nuclear point source with a simple power‐law model (Section
III.4
) to derive two essential quantities,
L_{\mathrm{5100}}
and the UV spectral index (
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
). The “color-magnitude diagram” of
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
versus Eddington ratio,
L_{\mathrm{bol}}/L_{\mathrm{Edd}}\equiv 10.6\,L_{\mathrm{5100}}/L_{\mathrm{Edd}}
, reveals distinctly different distributions for our two subsamples (Figure
15
). Whereas the most luminous quasars, characterized by
L_{\mathrm{bol}}/L_{\mathrm{Edd}}\gtrsim 0.3
, have a median spectral slope of
\overline{\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}}=-1.39\pm 0.36
and cluster closely between the spectral slope of the composite spectrum of quasars (
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}=-1.56
;
Vanden Berk et al. 2001
) and a slim accretion disk (
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}=-1
;
Abramowicz et al. 1988
;
Kato et al. 2008
), the lower luminosity population, with
L_{\mathrm{bol}}/L_{\mathrm{Edd}}\approx 0.01-0.1
, occupies a surprisingly wide range of spectral slopes from
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}\approx-1.7
to
+3.8
. A typical luminous quasar such as J0305
-
3150 (Figure
2
) has a blue spectrum that differs markedly from the red continuum of the less powerful nuclei in J0224
-
4711-BHAE-1 (Figure
2
) and CEERS 006725 (Figure
3
).

Our decomposed AGN+host SED bears an intriguing resemblance to the popular interpretation of LRDs that attributes the blue portion of the SED to host galaxy emission and the red part to a heavily dust-reddened AGN (e.g.,
Greene et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024a
). If such a scenario applies to the low-luminosity quasars under consideration, we can estimate roughly the required level of extinction
A_{V}
by assuming that the intrinsic SED follows the composite quasar spectrum of
Vanden Berk et al. 2001
, for which
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}=-1.56
, and adopting the dust extinction curve from
Calzetti et al. 2000
. Explaining the red slopes of the lower luminosity quasars would require dust extinctions of
A_{V}\approx 2–3.5
mag (Figure
15
).

We hasten to add that there is no shortage of alternative explanations for the V-shaped SED of LRDs, ones that place less reliance, if any, on dust reddening. Physical mechanisms that can produce intrinsically red optical spectra include a starburst accretion disk with an inner truncation radius
(
Chen & Lin 2024
)
, the gravitationally unstable outer region of an otherwise standard accretion disk
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
, blackbody radiation from a massive, optically thick gaseous envelope
(
Kido et al. 2025
)
, and the circumbinary disk around a binary massive BH
(
Inayoshi et al. 2025
)
. Additionally, the moderately low Eddington ratios (
L_{\mathrm{bol}}/L_{\mathrm{Edd}}\approx 0.03
) of the low-luminosity population may implicate a radiatively inefficient accretion flow
(
Narayan & Yi 1994
)
, which, by analogy with nearby low-luminosity AGNs
(
Ho 2008
)
, would lack of a strong “big blue bump” and naturally produce a redder continuum
(
Ho 1999
)
.

V.1
The Growth Path of High-redshift Supermassive BHs

We have shown that high-redshift low-luminosity quasars lie significantly above the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
relation (Figure
12
). They are characterized by BH-to-stellar mass ratios of
M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_{\ast}\approx 1\%-10\%
, values rarely found in the local Universe
(
Zhuang & Ho 2023
)
or even at cosmic noon (e.g.,
Bongiorno et al. 2014
), highlighting the importance of clarifying their growth pathways. Theoretical considerations predict that the first supermassive BHs form via heavy seeds (
M_{\rm BH}\approx 10^{4}-10^{6}\,M_{\odot}
) from “direct collapse” of metal-free gas
(
Latif & Ferrara 2016
;
Regan et al. 2020
)
or light seeds (
M_{\rm BH}\approx 10^{2}\,M_{\odot}
) of stellar origin that undergo super-Eddington accretion
(
Natarayan 2014
;
Pacucci et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. With
M_{\rm BH}\approx 10^{7}-10^{8}\,M_{\odot}
, however, the BHs in low-luminosity quasars are already quite massive and cannot be regarded as “seeds” in the conventional sense. Analogous to the LRDs (orange points in Figure
12
), which are even more extreme in their near complete absence of any detectable host galaxy
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, the under-developed hosts of high-redshift low-luminosity quasars underscore that this population is caught during an early epoch of rapid evolution during which the growth of the BH and its host are not tightly synchronised.

Leveraging our
GalfitS
analysis, which yields robust measurements of the properly separated host galaxy and AGN components, we estimate the SFR and the BH accretion rate (
\dot{M}_{\rm BH}
) of each source. This enables a first-order forecast of the future evolution of
M_{\ast}
and
M_{\rm BH}
. Figure
16
a illustrates the likely trajectory of each low-luminosity quasar over the next 10 Myr, roughly one
Salpeter 1964
e-folding time, assuming constant SFR and
\dot{M}_{\rm BH}
. Under steady conditions the sources will migrate toward the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
relation (red arrow), consistent with the findings in
Zhuang & Ho 2023
that objects offset from the local relation tend to move back onto it. A major complication arises from the uncertain interpretation of the systematically redder SEDs of the low-luminosity quasars (Figure
15
). Are the unusual SEDs intrinsically red or reddened? If the colors implicate dust reddening, then extinction correction would dramatically alter the evolutionary vector of the sources. Under this alternative scenario (Figure
16
b), wherein we assume that the intrinsic SED has a slope of
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}=-1.56

(
Vanden Berk et al. 2001
)
and deredden the observed spectral indices and luminosities using the
Calzetti et al. 2000
extinction law, the low-luminosity quasars would grow along a path roughly consistent with
M_{\mathrm{BH}}\approx 0.1\,M_{\ast}
(blue arrow), ultimately overlapping with the locus of luminous quasars at later epochs.

Figure
17
gives a schematic overview of how low-luminosity quasars might evolve, depending on whether the accretion disk is truly red or simply reddened by dust. We envisage two possible scenarios:

•

Upper pathway:
The accretion disk is intrinsically red, with no significant dust extinction, because it
has entered a starburst phase that reduces its gas inflow. After an early period of rapid BH growth, via a massive “heavy seed” (e.g.,
Volonteri 2010
;
Natarayan 2014
), a light seed undergoing super-Eddington accretion (e.g.,
Inayoshi et al. 2022
), or even ab initio from the core collapse of a self-interacting dark matter halo (e.g.,
Jiang et al. 2025
), the BH attains a mass substantially above the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
relation. At this point, the disk may become self-gravitating
(
Chen & Lin 2024
)
, with newly formed stars and their remnants depleting the inward gas supply. This process effectively truncates the disk at a radius of
\sim 100
gravitational radii, producing a red SED similar to that seen in the low-luminosity quasars. With gas accretion onto the BH severely diminished, the BH barely grows further, while the host galaxy continues to form stars without strong AGN feedback. These systems evolve along the red arrow in Figure
16
a and gradually converge with the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
relation. The central star-forming phase can culminate in the formation of a compact nuclear star cluster
(
Neumayer et al. 2020
)
, and over time the galaxy may settle into a Seyfert-like system experiencing episodic low-level accretion
(
Ho 2008
)
. This scenario is reminiscent of nearby AGNs with evidence of nuclear star formation (e.g.,
Davies et al. 2007
), and may be related to the relic stellar populations detected in the Milky Way’s center (e.g.,
Pfuhl et al. 2011
).

•

Lower pathway:
If the nucleus is reddened by a dust-enshrouded environment, then after correcting for extinction the BHs in low-luminosity quasars would lie even farther above the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}–M_{\ast}
relation (blue arrow in Figure
16
b), in some cases reaching
M_{\mathrm{BH}}\approx M_{\ast}

(
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Maiolino et al. 2024
, see also)
. Once the BH becomes sufficiently massive, energy-driven winds or radiative feedback (e.g.,
Hopkins et al. 2008
;
King & Pounds 2015
) expel the surrounding gas, revealing a luminous quasar. Although the BH and the galaxy may continue to grow for some time, prolonged or repeated episodes of strong feedback can eventually quench star formation, leaving the BH either on or above the local scaling relation of massive, early-type galaxies.

Although both evolutionary pathways remain plausible, the obscured-nucleus scenario may overproduce massive BHs relative to the observed number densities at low redshift. The local number density of BHs with masses
M_{\rm BH}\geq 10^{9}\,M_{\odot}
is
1.5\times 10^{-5}\,\rm Mpc^{-3}

(
Shankar et al. 2009
;
Kormendy & Ho 2013
)
. In contrast, at high redshift the low-luminosity quasars are significantly more common, with a number density of
6.3\times 10^{-5}\,\rm cMpc^{-3}
for
M_{\rm BH}=10^{6}-10^{8}\,M_{\odot}

(
Kokorev et al. 2024
)
. If all the high-redshift systems were to evolve into massive systems, it would result in an overabundance of local
10^{9}\,M_{\odot}
BHs. This apparent discrepancy suggests that the obscured-nucleus pathway, if applied uniformly to all high-redshift low-luminosity quasars, may not be viable without invoking additional mechanisms to reduce the final BH masses or alter the host galaxy properties during subsequent evolution.

V.2
UV SED of CEERS 006725 and CEERS 007465

CEERS 006725 and CEERS 007465 have strong rest-frame UV emission lines despite their relatively red continua (Figure
18
). To quantify the line strengths, we anchored the broad
H
\alpha
flux to the values reported by
Harikane et al. 2023
and scaled the UV emission lines using typical quasar line ratios from
Vanden Berk et al. 2001
. This approach reproduces the observed UV photometry well. CEERS 006725 requires twice the fiducial quasar line fluxes, whereas CEERS 007465 only needs slightly lower line fluxes (0.9 times) compared to the quasar composite. Nonetheless, both sources have very low continuum luminosities, resulting in extraordinarily high equivalent widths for lines such as C IV (2861 Å for CEERS 006725 and 4560 Å for CEERS 007465), far in excess of the equivalent width of
\sim 25
Å in typical unobscured quasars
(
Vanden Berk et al. 2001
)
.

In the dust extinction scenario, the red UV spectral indices of CEERS 006725 and CEERS 007465 imply high extinction values of
A_{V}=1.93
and 2.62 mag, respectively.
Harikane et al. 2023
estimate even higher dust extinctions of
A_{V}=2.48
and 4.28 mag for these sources based on the steep Balmer decrements from
Nakajima et al. 2023
. A significant challenge emerges: if the broad-line region (BLR) is embedded within a dusty region, the dust should also extinct the broad emission lines and lead to lower observed line fluxes. Reconciling the extremely high C IV equivalent widths under this scenario would require invoking a configuration in which the BLR lies outside the dusty region, a possibility that contradicts reverberation mapping studies that indicate that the BLR is confined within the dust sublimation radius
(
Cackett et al. 2021
)
.

Alternatively, the intrinsically red SED scenario posits that the red continuum is an inherent property of the AGN and not a consequence of dust reddening. Although the steep Balmer decrement might be interpreted as evidence for dust extinction, the Balmer decrement in the BLR is highly sensitive to its physical conditions
(
Li et al. 2022
)
. The theoretical models of
Korista & Goad 2004
show that the H
\alpha
/H
\beta
ratio generally increases as the BLR cloud density decreases. A cloud density that drops slightly below the critical density for the C IV transition (
n_{e}\approx 10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}
) would yield both an elevated H
\alpha
/H
\beta
ratio and a higher
\mathrm{C\,IV}/\mathrm{H\alpha}
ratio, as observed in CEERS 006725. Of course, this scenario faces the quandary of how an intrinsically red accretion disk can produce a sufficiently hard ionizing spectrum to power the strong UV emission lines.

In summary, we favor the intrinsically red SED scenario. The central regions of active galaxies in general (e.g.,
Xie et al. 2021
;
Zhuang et al. 2021
) and those at high redshifts in particular often experience vigorous star formation, which yields a generally blue host continuum (see Figure
3
) and supplies ample ionizing photons. The frequent failure by JWST/MIRI and ALMA observations of red high‑
z
AGNs to detect the levels of dust emission predicted by energy balance considerations (e.g.,
Setton et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
) may be regarded as further support for the idea that the red continuum is an intrinsic property of the AGN instead of the result of dust extinction.

V.3
Trigger of Quasar Activity

The mass-weighted effective radii of our quasar host galaxies are systematically smaller by
\sim 0.3
dex than typical galaxies observed at similar redshift in rest-frame optical images (Section
IV.2
). This discrepancy significantly exceeds the expected wavelength-dependent size variation of
0.15
dex
(
Allen et al. 2025
)
. Luminous quasars also exhibit high Sérsic indices (
n\approx 5
) that further implicate morphologies akin to those of bulge-dominated, early-type galaxies. Despite having already built substantial mass in stars (
\bar{M}_{\ast}\approx 10^{11}\,M_{\odot}
) and in the central BH (
\bar{M}_{\rm BH}=10^{9.15}\,M_{\odot}
), these systems harbor active star formation and ongoing BH accretion, consistent with quasars at high
(
Salvestrini et al. 2025
)
and low
(
Shangguan et al. 2018
;
Xie et al. 2021
;
Molina et al. 2023
)
redshifts.

To investigate how these massive systems formed at high redshifts, we compare the host galaxy stellar mass with estimates of the halo-based maximum mass (Figure
19
). Considering the total surveyed cosmic volume derived from an area of
0.088\,\mathrm{deg}^{2}
across the redshift range
4\leq z\leq 7
, we estimate the maximum halo mass (
M_{\mathrm{halo}}^{\mathrm{max}}
) using the halo mass function from
Tinker et al. 2008
. Most quasars lie near the theoretical upper bound (black dashed line) that corresponds to an efficiency of 100% in converting baryons into stars. The median stellar mass fraction of our luminous quasars is
\sim 20\%
of the baryonic limit (green dashed line), closely resembling high-redshift massive galaxies
(
Xiao et al. 2024
)
. Notably, J0224
-
4711 from the ASPIRE survey, with
\log\,(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})=11.92\pm 0.47
, surpasses the theoretical 100% baryonic conversion line. Considering the cosmic variance of
\sim 0.3
dex from our relatively small survey area, similar to
Xiao et al. 2024
, this object is only
\sim 1\,\sigma
above the nominal limit and therefore plausible. Furthermore, the detection of filamentary structures surrounding the ASPIRE quasars indicates that they inhabit some of the densest known regions at these epochs
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, and thus the actual halo masses of these luminous quasars might exceed our current estimates. Nevertheless, our results clearly indicate extremely high star formation efficiencies among luminous quasars, far in excess of the typical stellar-to-halo mass ratios of
\sim 2\%
of local massive galaxies
(
van Uitert et al. 2016
)
.

Collectively, our luminous quasars exhibit more highly concentrated morphologies (
n\approx 5
) and significantly enhanced star formation efficiencies compared to less luminous quasars and the general galaxy population. The observed compactness strongly suggests rapid central stellar mass assembly, possibly via intense, gas-rich starburst episodes driven by strong central gas inflows concurrently fueling supermassive BH growth
(
Dekel et al. 2009
;
Zolotov et al. 2015
)
. Such a scenario is reminiscent of the “blue nugget” phase, wherein galaxies rapidly accumulate dense stellar cores through starburst events, achieving star formation efficiencies that can exceed
30\%-50\%
of the available baryonic matter, analogous to efficiencies inferred in local starburst regions and sites of globular cluster formation
(
Kruijssen et al. 2015
;
Elmegreen 2018
)
.

Given the overdense environments surrounding the luminous quasars
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, it is plausible that major mergers play a complementary role in triggering quasar activity, instead of acting as a competing scenario. The merger-driven scenario traditionally predicts heightened activity at intermediate redshifts (
z\approx 2-3
;
Hopkins et al. 2006
), but evidence for substantial merger activity at earlier epochs (e.g.,
z\gtrsim 6
) increasingly has been reported
(
Neeleman et al. 2019
;
Meyer et al. 2025
)
. Luminous quasars at
z\approx 6-7
could experience enhanced gas inflows induced by both merger-driven gravitational torques and cosmological cold streams. Under this dual-trigger hypothesis, the intense SFRs and compactness observed in our luminous quasars would naturally arise from a combination of merger-driven dynamical disturbances and continuous inflow-driven gas accretion. Our results reinforce a picture in which both mergers and secular inflows synergistically facilitate early quasar growth and galaxy evolution.

V.4
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\ast}
Relation and Early AGN Feedback

The
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-\sigma_{\ast}
and
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\mathrm{bulge}}
relations have often been interpreted as evidence for coevolution between supermassive BHs and their host galaxies
(
Ferrarese & Merritt 2000
;
Gebhardt et al. 2000
;
Kormendy & Ho 2013
)
. However, the physical processes driving this coevolution remain elusive. AGN feedback mechanisms, particularly radiation pressure-driven outflows, may expel gas, suppress star formation, and limit the growth of galaxy size
(
King & Pounds 2015
;
Yuan et al. 2018
)
. Cosmological simulations (e.g., IllustrisTNG,
Weinberger et al. 2017
; EAGLE,
Schaye et al. 2015
; SIMBA,
Davé et al. 2019
) adopt different implementations of AGN feedback to reproduce these scaling relations, attributing them to the interactions between supermassive BHs and their host galaxies. The deviation of high-redshift quasars from the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\ast}
relation may reflect indirectly the strength of AGN feedback.

High-redshift low-luminosity quasars have systematically higher equivalent widths for broad H
\alpha
relative to their more luminous counterparts, or compared to nearby AGNs
(
Maiolino et al. 2024
)
. An immediate, practical ramification is that H
\alpha
-based BH masses
(
Greene & Ho 2005
)
might be overestimated if the BLR radius is inferred from broad H
\alpha
luminosity. Using BLR radii estimated from
L_{5100}
instead of
L_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}
systematically lowers
M_{\mathrm{BH}}
and reduces the offset relative to the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\ast,\mathrm{bulge}}
relation
(
Kormendy & Ho 2013
)
from 1.11 dex to 0.76 dex (Figure
20
). This offset remains larger than the 0.45 dex deviation of the luminous quasar subsample. Fitting a linear relation to the 30 quasars in our sample with detectable host galaxies, with the slope fixed to 1.16
(
Kormendy & Ho 2013
)
, yields
\log\,M_{\mathrm{BH}}=1.16\,\log\,M_{\star}-3.46
. The zero point is
\sim 0.6
dex higher relative to the zero point of the
Kormendy & Ho 2013
relation.

Ruling out the possibility that the observed offset reflects selection bias is beyond the scope of this work. Instead, we suggest that AGN feedback in the early Universe may contribute to the observed offset. According to
King 2005
, the gas that fuels BH growth can be impacted by momentum-driven or energy–driven outflows. The overall gravitational force experienced within the central bulge region can be approximated as

F_{\rm grav}\sim\frac{M_{\mathrm{dyn}}M_{g}}{R^{2}}=\frac{f_{g}M_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{2}}{R^{2}},

(7)

where
M_{g}
is the gas mass and
f_{g}\equiv M_{g}/M_{\mathrm{dyn}}
is the gas-to-dynamical mass fraction. For an isothermal galaxy profile with velocity dispersion
\sigma
,
M_{\mathrm{dyn}}=\frac{2\,\sigma^{2}R}{G}

(
Binney & Tremaine 1987
)
, with
G
the gravitational constant. Substituting this expression into Equation
7
yields

F_{\rm grav}\sim f_{g}\sigma^{4}.

(8)

In an energy-driven outflow, the overall feedback force can be approximated by

F_{\rm feed}\sim\frac{L_{\mathrm{Edd}}}{c}\propto M_{\mathrm{BH}}.

(9)

Balancing
F_{\rm grav}
with
F_{\rm feed}
gives
M_{\mathrm{BH}}\sim f_{g}\sigma^{4}
, which, in combination with the
Faber & Jackson 1976
relation
M_{\ast,\mathrm{bulge}}\sim\sigma^{4}
then implies a critical mass

M_{c}\propto f_{g}\,M_{\ast,\mathrm{bulge}}.

(10)

Strong feedback halts BH growth when
M_{\mathrm{BH}}
reaches the critical value
M_{c}

(
Ding et al. 2022
)
, and the final BH mass follows a relation roughly consistent with that of
Kormendy & Ho 2013
.

As elaborated in Section
V.3
, quasar activity is likely triggered during a starburst phase, during which a compact (
n\gtrsim 4
), massive host galaxy is assembled. Consequently, the bulge or spheroidal component dominates the host galaxy, such that
M_{\ast,\mathrm{bulge}}\approx M_{\ast}
. This may explain why our objects align more closely with the
M_{\rm BH}-M_{\ast,\mathrm{bulge}}
relation of
Kormendy & Ho 2013
than with the
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\ast}
relation of
Zhuang & Ho 2023
derived for a broader AGN population (Figure
12
).

It is noteworthy from Equation
10
that the zero point of the correlation is related to the gas fraction. At
z\approx 0
, the gas fraction is approximately equal to the cosmic baryon fraction on large scales,
f_{b}=15.8\%
. Within this context, the 0.6 dex offset observed for high–redshift AGNs (Figure
20
) suggests that in these systems the gas fraction can be as high as
f_{g}\approx 60\%
. At early epochs, it is easy to imagine that the small–scale bulge region can attain such high gas fractions because galaxies are inherently gas–rich, as observed (e.g.,
Decarli et al. 2018
;
Herrera-Camus et al. 2025
), and dynamical processes efficiently drive gas inward
(
Hopkins et al. 2008
, e.g.,)
. This implies that although the BH in high–redshift AGNs may be overly massive on large scales—thereby effectively influencing the surrounding gas—the gas density in the immediate vicinity of the BH remains high enough so that local feedback cannot completely quench accretion, prolonging the quasar phase until the small–scale gas reservoir is consumed.

VI
Summary

We assemble a representative sample of 31 high‐redshift (
z\gtrsim 4
) AGNs comprising JWST NIRCam observations from multiple surveys, including ASPIRE, CEERS, EIGER, and SHELLQs, to probe the early growth of supermassive BHs and their host galaxies. The sample consist of 15 high-luminosity quasars (
L_{\mathrm{5100}}\gtrsim 10^{45}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
) and 16 less luminous counterparts recently identified by JWST. We uniformly reduce and process the images using state-of-the-art corrections, background subtraction, and astrometric calibration to ensure optimal spatial resolution across the different filters. We perform multi‐band AGN-host decomposition using a newly developed forward-modeling code (
GalfitS
) to disentangle the nuclear AGN emission from the underlying host light. Simultaneously combining imaging and spectral fitting, this approach incorporates physically motivated priors to facilitate extraction of accurate host galaxy and AGN properties. Extensive input-output tests with mock images validate our methodology, quantify uncertainties related to PSF variations and parameter degeneracies, and place rigorous upper limits on non-detections.

Our key findings are summarized as follows:

1.

We successfully detect the host galaxies of 30 out of the 31 quasars, among them 20 new measurements and five that previously only had published upper limits on stellar mass.

2.

Quasar host galaxies are intrinsically more compact than star-forming galaxies of comparable stellar mass, lying
\sim 0.3
dex below the fiducial stellar mass–size relation at
z=6.5
.

3.

The internal structure of the host galaxies of high-redshift quasars comes in two flavors. Whereas low-luminosity quasars structurally resemble matched star-forming galaxies of similar stellar mass and redshift, both characterized by disk-like profiles with Sérsic indices
n\approx 1
, the more power quasars predominantly reside in highly concentrated (
n\approx 5
), presumably bulge-dominated, early-type host galaxies.

4.

The spectral slopes of the isolated active nuclei exhibit a remarkable dichotomy. Parameterizing the nuclear SEDs with a simple power law reveals that while luminous quasars have a relatively narrow range of blue UV spectral slopes (
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}=-1.39\pm 0.36
) similar to that of typical lower redshift quasars, the less luminous quasars span a broad range of UV spectral slopes, from
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}\approx-2
to extremely red values of
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}\approx 4
.

5.

High-redshift quasars deviate systematically above the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\ast}
relation, by different relative amounts depending on luminosity (or BH mass): high-luminosity sources (
L_{\mathrm{5100}}\gtrsim 10^{45}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
;
M_{\rm BH}\gtrsim 10^{9}\,M_{\odot}
) exhibit a median
M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_{\ast}=1.2\%
, to be compared with
M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_{\ast}=4.7\%
for lower luminosity sources (
L_{\mathrm{5100}}\lesssim 10^{45}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
;
M_{\rm BH}\approx 10^{7}-10^{8}\,M_{\odot}
). After accounting for possible biases in the BH mass estimates of the low-luminosity quasars arising from their higher H
\alpha
equivalent widths, we estimate an ensemble offset of 0.6 dex in
M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_{\ast}
for our combined sample of high-redshift quasars.

The physical implications of the above empirical findings depend critically on the interpretation of the observed bimodality in nuclear spectral SEDs. While we cannot rule out the possibility that the red spectral slopes arise from dust extinction, we suggest that the red continuum is an intrinsic property of the AGN connected with physical conditions in the BLR or accretion disk. We argue that the offset of high-redshift quasars above the local
M_{\mathrm{BH}}-M_{\ast}
relation reflects the influence of AGN feedback in gas-rich environments. With gas fractions as high as
\sim 60\%
, inferred from the offset and theoretical considerations, BHs can grow rapidly in the early Universe. Feedback from energy-driven outflows may be insufficient to quench accretion until the central gas reservoir is depleted. This delayed regulation can explain the overmassive BHs relative to their hosts, suggesting that feedback becomes more effective at later stages as the gas supply diminishes, guiding these systems toward the local scaling relation.

PSFEx

Appendix C
Input-output Tests

We perform input–output tests to address several key objectives: (1) assess the detectability of host galaxies using our method (Section
III.3.1
); (2) evaluate the reliability of the derived key host–galaxy parameters (Section
III.3.2
); (3) quantify the uncertainties arising from our choice of empirical PSF (Section
III.3.3
); and (4) validate the use of a non-parametric AGN SED compared to a parametric AGN method (Section
III.4
). Here we provide details on the generation of our mock AGNs.

We generate mock AGNs by manually adding a nucleus to the multi-band images of real galaxies. We use galaxy images from the CEERS field, where imaging in all seven filters is available, to avoid potential systematics associated with a parametric multi-band galaxy model, especially given the limited prior constraints at high redshift. We select two sets of galaxies samples from the parent sample of
Sun et al. 2024
: an subsample of 82 galaxies at
5.5\leq z\leq 7.0
, which primarily matches our high-luminosity quasars,
and a subsample of 190 galaxies at
4.0\leq z\leq 5.0
, which mainly corresponds to the lower luminosity quasars. We use all available galaxies in the given redshift range to maximize the accuracy of our detection rate determination. The division into two groups is motivated by the different nuclear SEDs of the two subsamples of quasars
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
(see also Section
IV.3
). We first perform a single-Sérsic
GalfitS
fit on all 272 galaxies (denoted as
run1
in Table
2
); the derived stellar mass distribution is shown in Figure
C1
.

For each galaxy, we manually add a point source at the best-fit galaxy center obtained from
run1
. For the 82 galaxies in the luminous quasar subsample, the point source SED is derived from the quasar SED template of
Temple et al. 2021
, which accounts for both redshift evolution and host galaxy subtraction. We scale this template to four luminosity levels,
\log\,L_{5100}=43,44,45
, and
46~\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
, covering the full luminosity range of our sample. Mock images are generated in seven NIRCam bands (F115W, F150W, F200W, F277W, F356W, F410M, F444W). Instead of using the fitted PSF model constructed from the CEERS field, for each AGN luminosity we select five foreground stars whose fluxes are similar to those expected for an AGN at
z=6.5
in the F356W filter, and create five distinct mock AGN images based on these stars. Finally, we update the error maps with appropriate Poisson noise. In total, this procedure produces
82\times 4\times 5=1640
mock AGN images with seven-band coverage.

We perform three sets of fits on the mock AGNs. In
run2
, we perform a point source–only fit. In
run3
, we perform a point source plus galaxy fit following our optimal strategy described in Section
III.2.1
. In
run4
, we perform a seven-band fit using the AGN SED model from Section
III.4
instead of the non-parametric AGN SED model used in
run3
. Analysis of the results from
run1
,
run2
, and
run3
shows that:

1.

The
GalfitS
method, when applied to multi-band images, achieves a higher detection rate than single-band AGN–host decomposition (Figure
6
).

2.

The key host galaxy parameters are reliably recovered using our optimal fitting strategy (Figure
8
).

3.

The uncertainty arising from the PSF can be quantified following
\sigma_{\log\,{M_{\ast}}\,\rm PSF}=0.12\,\log\,(L_{5100}/\rm erg\,s^{-1})-5.11
(Figure
9
).

4.

Adopting a non-parametric AGN SED for the nucleus is consistent with the parametric approach for
\log\,f_{\mathrm{F356W}}\lesssim 0
, and results in a smaller systematic bias when the AGN is brighter (Figure
C2
).

For the 190 galaxies designed for the low-luminosity quasars, our primary goal is to determine the stellar mass upper limit for the non-detection case of J1526
-
2050-BHAE-2. To this end, we directly use the SED of J1526
-
2050-BHAE-2 to generate mock AGNs in the F115W, F200W, and F356W bands. We employ the fitted PSF model as the point source, resulting in a total of 190 mock AGN images. We then perform both a point source–only run and a point source plus galaxy run (denoted as
uplim
in Table
2
) to calculate the detection rate as a function of the stellar mass
M_{\ast}
. The resulting detection rate, shown in Figure
7
, is used to estimate the stellar mass upper limit for J1526
-
2050-BHAE-2.
