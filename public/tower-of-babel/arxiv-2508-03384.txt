Hopf–Galois structures on separable field extensions of degree related to Cunningham chains
Andrew Darlington

Abstract
. The past few years have seen Hopf–Galois structures on extensions of squarefree degree studied in various contexts. The Galois case was fully explored by Alabdali and Byott in 2020, followed by a first attempt at generalising these results to include non-normal extensions by Byott and Martin-Lyons; their work looks at separable extensions of degree p ​ q pq with p , q p,q distinct odd primes, and p = 2 ​ q + 1 p=2q+1 . This paper extends the latter work further by considering separable extensions of squarefree degree n = p 1 ​ … ​ p m n=p_{1}...p_{m} where each pair of consecutive primes p i , p i + 1 p_{i},p_{i+1} are related by p i = 2 ​ p i + 1 + 1 p_{i}=2p_{i+1}+1 .



1.
Introduction

Hopf–Galois structures arose in a paper by Chase and Sweedler in 1969
[
CS69
]
. Building on the notion of a Galois extension of commutative rings in
[
CHR65
]
, they stated what it meant for a commutative ring
S
to be an
H
-Galois extension of a commutative ring
R
for some
R
-Hopf algebra
H
. Almost twenty years later, Greither and Pareigis
[
GP87
]
showed that, in the case that
L/K
is a finite separable field extension, the problem of finding Hopf–Galois structures on
L/K
can be tackled using a group-theoretic approach. One may view such a structure as essentially what arises when one replaces the Galois group of an extension with some
K
-Hopf algebra
H
, requiring it to fulfil a somewhat similar role - that is
H
should act on
L/K
in some ‘nice’ way.

Let
E
be the Galois closure of
L/K
, with
G=\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)
,
G^{\prime}=\mathrm{Gal}(E/L)
, and
X=G/G^{\prime}
. Then the Greither-Pareigis approach gives a bijective correspondence between Hopf algebras giving a Hopf–Galois structure on
L/K
and regular subgroups
N
of
\text{Perm}(X)
normalised by the image of the left-translation map
\lambda:G\rightarrow\text{Perm}(X)
given by
\lambda(\sigma)(\overline{\tau})=\overline{\sigma\tau}
(where the bar represents elements in the coset space
X
).

The isomorphism class of
N
is known as the
type
of the Hopf–Galois structure. In particular, we have
H=E[N]^{G}
, where
G
acts on
E
and
N
by automorphisms and by conjugation (via
\lambda
) respectively, and
H
acts on
L/K
as follows:

\left(\sum_{\eta\in N}a_{\eta}\eta\right)\cdot x=\sum_{\eta\in N}a_{\eta}(\eta^{-1}(1_{X}))(x).

Examples 1.1
.

Let all notation be as above, but with
L/K
now Galois.

(1)

Consider the right translation map
\rho:G\rightarrow\text{Perm}(G)
given by
\rho(\sigma)(\tau)=\tau\sigma^{-1}
. Then
H=L[\rho(G)]^{G}=K[\rho(G)]
gives the canonical classical Hopf–Galois structure on
L/K
.

(2)

Now consider the left translation map
\lambda:G\rightarrow\text{Perm}(G)
. Then
H=L[\lambda(G)]^{G}
gives the canonical non-classical Hopf–Galois structure on
L/K
.

Note that if
G
is abelian, then
\rho(G)=\lambda(G)
, and so
L[\lambda(G)]^{G}=L[\rho(G)]^{G}
.

Aside from these well-known examples, it is possible for many other Hopf algebras to act on an extension in the way described above. It is even possible for the same Hopf algebra to act on
L/K
in two or more ways such that the resulting Hopf–Galois structures are distinct, even in the case that
L/K
is Galois. This then, for example, greatly enlarges the study of Galois module theory as each Hopf–Galois structure essentially offers a different lens through which to view the extension. Additionally, there exists a ‘Hopf–Galois correspondence’ between the Hopf subalgebras of
H
and the intermediate extensions of
L/K
. Unlike the classical Galois correspondence, although it is always injective, it is not so often surjective. The growing interest in Hopf–Galois theory has led to the classification of Hopf–Galois structures in many different cases; typically with restrictions on the degree of the field extension, as in
[
CS20
]
,
[
CS21
]
and
[
Byo04
]
(the latter in the case of Galois extensions), or specifying Hopf–Galois structures of certain types, as in
[
Koh98
]
and
[
JA23
]
. In 2019, Byott and Alabdali
[
AB20
]
gave a count and classification of the number of Hopf–Galois structures on Galois extensions of squarefree degree. In a first attempt at generalising this work to separable (but not necessarily normal) extensions, Byott and Martin-Lyons in
[
BML22
]
looked at Hopf–Galois structures on extensions of degree
pq
, where
p=2q+1
with
(p,q)
a safe prime - Sophie Germain prime pair.

In a further generalisation, a previous work of the author’s
[
Dar24
]
extended this to the situation where
p,q
are arbitrary distinct odd primes. Initial further work suggested that the problem of finding Hopf–Galois structures on separable squarefree degree
n
extensions becomes significantly more involved each time another prime is added to the factorisation of
n
, and so it is of interest to find special cases or trends which could give an insight into the more general case. In this paper, therefore, we explore the case where
n
can be written as a product of distinct odd primes
p_{1}>\cdots>p_{l}
, such that each consecutive prime pair
(p_{i},p_{i+1})
is related by
p_{i}=2p_{i+1}+1
, thus the primes
p_{2},\cdots,p_{l}
are Sophie-Germain primes, and the primes
p_{1},\cdots,p_{l-1}
are safe primes. The
l
-tuple
(p_{1},\cdots,p_{l})
is called a
Cunningham chain
of length
l
. In 1904, Dickson formulated the following conjecture which extended Dirichlet’s theorem on arithmetic progressions:

Conjecture 1.2

(
[
Dic04
]
, this formulation due to Ribenboim in Chapter 6 of
[
Rib96
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer,
a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}
a sequence of integers and
b_{1},\cdots,b_{k}
be a sequence of integers with
b_{i}\geq 1
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Further, suppose that there does not exist an integer
m>1
such that
m
divides the product

\prod_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}+nb_{i})

for all positive integers
n
. Then there are infinitely many positive integers
n
such that the linear forms

a_{1}+b_{1}n,\;a_{2}+b_{2}n,\;\cdots\;,a_{k}+b_{k}n

are all prime.

One notes that the case
k=1
corresponds to Dirichlet’s Theorem. We also get the following two notable corollaries:

Conjecture 1.3

(Twin Prime Conjecture)
.

There are infinitely many pairs of primes of the form
n,n+2
.

Conjecture 1.4
.

For any
l
, there are infinitely many Cunningham chains of length
l
.

Proposition 1.5
.

Conjectures
1.3
and
1.4
are both corollaries of Conjecture
1.2
.

Proof.

For the twin prime conjecture, we take
k=2
with
a_{1}=0,a_{2}=2
, and
b_{1}=b_{2}=1
. For Conjecture
1.4
, we take
k=l
,
a_{i}=2^{i-1}
and
b_{i}=2^{i-1}-1
.
∎

We note that taking
l=2
gives the conjecture for Sophie-Germain primes. We now give some preliminary results and definitions.

2.
Preliminaries

Definition 2.1
.

A positive integer
n
will be called a
Cunningham product
if it can be expressed as a product of primes which form a Cunningham chain of some length.

Let
L/K
be a separable field extension of degree
n
with
E,G,G^{\prime}
and
X
as above.

Definition 2.2
.

For an abstract group
N
of order
n
, define the holomorph,
\mathrm{Hol}(N)
, of
N
as

\mathrm{Hol}(N)=N\rtimes\mathrm{Aut}(N).

For
\eta,\mu\in N
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathrm{Aut}(N)
, we have

[\eta,\alpha][\mu,\beta]=[\eta\alpha(\mu),\alpha\beta].

As is convention, we will write
\eta
for
[\eta,\text{id}_{N}]
and
\alpha
for
[1_{N},\alpha]
.

Theorem 2.3

(Byott’s Translation,
[
Chi00
]
)
.

There is a bijection between the following two sets of homomorphisms:

\mathcal{N}:=\{\varphi:N\rightarrow\mathrm{Perm}(X)\mid\varphi(N)\text{ regular}\}

and

\mathcal{G}:=\{\vartheta:G\rightarrow\mathrm{Perm}(N)\mid\vartheta(G^{\prime})=\mathrm{Stab}(1_{N})\}.

Furthermore, if
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
correspond to
\vartheta,\vartheta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
, then
\varphi(N)=\varphi^{\prime}(N)
iff
\vartheta(G)
and
\vartheta^{\prime}(G)
are conjugate and element of
\mathrm{Aut}(N)
; and
\varphi(N)
is normalised by
\lambda(G)
iff
\vartheta(G)
is contained in
\mathrm{Hol}(N)
.

This leads to the following counting formula:

Lemma 2.4

(
[
Byo96
]
)
.

Let
G,G^{\prime},N
be as above, let
e(G,N)
be the number of Hopf–Galois structures of type
N
which realise
G
, and
e^{\prime}(G,N)
the number of subgroups
M
of
\emph{Hol}(N)
which are transitive on
N
and isomorphic to
G
via an isomorphism taking the stabiliser
M^{\prime}
of
1_{N}
in
M
to
G^{\prime}
. Then

e(G,N)=\frac{|\emph{Aut}(G,G^{\prime})|}{|\emph{Aut}(N)|}e^{\prime}(G,N),

where

\mathrm{Aut}(G,G^{\prime})=\left\{\theta\in\mathrm{Aut}(G)\mid\theta(G^{\prime})=G^{\prime}\right\},

the group of automorphisms
\theta
of
G
such that
\theta
fixes the identity coset
1_{G}G^{\prime}
of
X=G/G^{\prime}
.

In this paper, therefore, in order to find and count Hopf–Galois structures on separable extensions whose degree
n
is a Cunningham product, we compute the transitive subgroups of
\mathrm{Hol}(N)
for each group
N
of order
n
. The following proposition aids the counting of these:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
l
be a positive integer and
1\leq k\leq l
. Then there are

(1)

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-(i-1)}-1}{2^{i}-1}

subgroups of
(C_{2})^{l}:=\underset{l-\text{times}}{C_{2}\times\cdots\times C_{2}}
of order
2^{k}
.

Proof.

We note that
(C_{2})^{l}
can be identified with the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{l}
, and that a subgroup of
(C_{2})^{l}
of rank
k
corresponds to a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{l}
.

Now, a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{l}
is generated by
k
-tuples
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
of linearly independent vectors. We claim that there are
\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2^{l}-2^{k-1})
such tuples in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{l}
. To see this, note first that we have a choice of
2^{l}-1
vectors for
v_{1}
. Given a choice
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-1})
of linearly independent vectors, there are
2^{l}-2^{j-1}
choices for
v_{j}
such that the set
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{j}\}
is linearly independent, as clearly
|\mathrm{Span}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-1})|=2^{j-1}
. The total number of
k
-tuples
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
of linearly independent vectors is therefore
(2^{l}-1)(2^{l}-2)\cdots(2^{l}-2^{k-1})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2^{l}-2^{i-1})
.

We now need to take into account of the fact that several different
k
-tuples of linearly independent vectors will generate the same subspace. To this end, given such a subspace
V
, we note that this is precisely the same as asking how many different bases
V
has. As
V
is a
k
-dimensional vector space, this is the same as asking how many
k
-tuples of linearly independent vectors there are in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
. By the above argument, there are
\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2^{k}-2^{i-1})
such
k
-tuples.

The total number of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{l}
, and hence the number of subgroups of
(C_{2})^{l}
of order
2^{k}
, is therefore

\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2^{l}-2^{i-1})}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2^{k}-2^{i-1})}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l}-2^{i-1}}{2^{k}-2^{i-1}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-(i-1)}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

∎

To obtain the total number of subgroups of
(C_{2})^{l}
, we therefore must sum (
1
) over each order
2^{k}
for
1\leq k\leq l
, and add 1, which represents the trivial subgroup. For conciseness, we will extend the sum and take the value of the empty product to be 1. That is, for example,
\prod_{i=1}^{0}f(i):=1
where
f(i)
is any summand.

Corollary 2.6
.

The number of subgroups of
(C_{2})^{l}
is

\sum_{k=0}^{l}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-(i-1)}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

We now state two useful propositions from
[
BML22
]
.

Proposition 2.7

(Proposition 2.2 of
[
BML22
]
)
.

Let
N
be an abelian group such that
\mathrm{Aut}(N)
is also abelian, and let

,
A^{\prime}
be subgroups of
\mathrm{Aut}(N)
. Consider the subgroups
M=N\rtimes A
and
M^{\prime}=N\rtimes A^{\prime}
of

. If there is an isomorphism
\phi:M\to M^{\prime}
with
\phi(N)=N
, then

.

Proposition 2.8

(Proposition 2.3 of
[
BML22
]
)
.

Let
N
be any group and
A
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(N)
. Let
M
be the
subgroup
N\rtimes A
of

, and suppose that

is a characteristic subgroup of

. Then the group

\mathrm{Aut}(M,A):=\{\theta\in\mathrm{Aut}(M):\theta(A)=A\}

is isomorphic to the normaliser of

in

. In particular, if

is abelian then

.

We finally recall the definition of Hall

-subgroups, as well as a related theorem. Let

be a group with order divisible by

, we will denote by

the set of primes dividing

, and by

the set of primes dividing

which do not divide

. If

is any set of primes dividing

, we recall that a

-subgroup
of

, whose order is a product of primes in

and a

of

\pi
-subgroup of

(Hall, 1928. See 9.1.7 of
[

)

Let

. Then every

3.
Extensions of degree
n
for
n
a Cunningham product

Let
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{l}
be a Cunningham product. For each
1\leq i\leq l-1
, we have
p_{i}-1=2p_{i+1}
. Let
p_{l}-1=2^{x}s
for some odd
s
. Further, let
F(l)
denote the
l
th
Fibonacci number; we will use the convention that
F(2)=2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
n
be as above. There are
F(l)
groups of order
n
. These groups consist of a chain of ordered cyclic factors
...,C_{p_{i-1}},C_{p_{i}},C_{p_{i+1}},..
such that each pair of consecutive factors is joined together via either a direct or semidirect product. Within each set of three consecutive factors, there is at most one semidirect product.

Example 3.2
.

Let
n=p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}
, then we get the following
F(3)=3
groups

\displaystyle C_{p_{1}}\times C_{p_{2}}\times C_{p_{3}},

\displaystyle(C_{p_{1}}\rtimes C_{p_{2}})\times C_{p_{3}},

\displaystyle C_{p_{1}}\times(C_{p_{2}}\rtimes C_{p_{3}}).

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Firstly, as
n
is squarefree, by
[
MM84
]
we know that any group of order
n
is metacyclic of the form

C_{e}\rtimes C_{d},

where
n=ed
is some factorisation of
n
and
C_{d}
acts faithfully on
C_{e}
. In particular,
C_{e}
is normal. The elements of
C_{d}
may therefore be identified with a subset of automorphisms of
C_{e}
, so
d
must divide
\varphi(e)
. Thus if, say,
p_{i}\mid d
, then
p_{i}\mid\varphi(e)
, which is only possible if
p_{i-1}\mid e
, as we recall that
\varphi(p_{i-1})=2p_{i}
. Further, if
p_{i+1}\mid d
as well, then by the same logic, we have
p_{i}\mid e
, which gives a contradiction, and thus we must have
p_{i-1}p_{i+1}\mid e
. We then obtain a
C_{p_{i}}
factor of
C_{d}
acting non-trivially on the
C_{p_{i-1}}
factor of
C_{e}
. We may continue to choose primes which divide
d
in this manner, which leads to the presentation in the statement of the proposition (indeed each prime dividing
d
introduces a semidirect product between cyclic groups of ‘consecutive’ prime order). It is clear to see that this does give us a group as it is the direct product of a cyclic group and several metacyclic groups, where each metacyclic group has the form
C_{p_{i}}\rtimes C_{p_{i+1}}
.

The number of such groups can be obtained by using induction. Indeed, we note that there is just
F(1)=1
group,
C_{p_{1}}
of size
n=p_{1}
. For
n=p_{1}p_{2}
, we obtain the cyclic group
C_{p_{1}}\times C_{p_{2}}
and the metacyclic group
C_{p_{1}}\rtimes C_{p_{2}}
, giving us
F(2)=2
groups in total. Now, for some
m
, assume for all
k\leq m
, that there are
F(k)
groups of order
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
. Consider
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}p_{k+1}
. We may introduce a semidirect product between the last two cyclic factors; there are
k-1
cyclic factors remaining, for which we know there are
F(k-1)
such groups. If we choose instead to have a direct product between the last two cyclic factors, there are
F(k)
such groups of this form. In total, we have
F(k)+F(k-1)=F(k+1)
groups. Therefore, for
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{l}
, there are
F(l)
groups of order
n
.
∎

3.1.
The cyclic group

Let
N
be the cyclic group of order
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{l}
. This corresponds to the case in Proposition
3.1
where all actions are trivial. Suppose then that
N=\langle\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{l}\rangle\cong C_{n}
, where
\sigma_{i}
has order
p_{i}
. For
1\leq i\leq l-1
, let
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle)
have orders
p_{i+1},2
respectively; further, let
\gamma,\delta\in\mathrm{Aut}(\langle\sigma_{l}\rangle)
have orders
2^{x},s
respectively, where
p_{l}-1=2^{x}s
with

odd. Then the set of these elements generates
\mathrm{Aut}(N)
. We will write

\text{Hol}(N)=\langle\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{l}\rangle\rtimes\left(\langle\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l-1}\rangle\times\langle\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l-1},\gamma\rangle\times\langle\delta\rangle\right),

which has order
2^{x+l-1}p_{1}p_{2}^{2}\cdots p_{l}^{2}s
. For ease of notation, we will sometimes denote
\delta
by
\alpha_{l}
and
\gamma
by
\beta_{l}
. We now move towards finding and counting the subgroups of

.

It is clear that the subgroups of
\langle\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l-1}\rangle
are of the form
\langle\alpha_{1}^{a_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{l-1}^{a_{l-1}}\rangle
where

, as
\alpha_{j}
and

have coprime orders for

; there are
2^{l-1}
such subgroups.

Recall that
\sigma_{0}(s)
denotes the number of divisors of

. The subgroups of
\langle\delta\rangle
are of the form
\langle\delta^{s/d}\rangle
, where

is some divisor of

Proposition 3.3
.

The number of subgroups of
\langle\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l-1},\gamma\rangle
is

\Sigma_{l}:=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}(2^{l-(k+1)}x+1)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

Proof.

Let
B:=\langle\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l-1},\gamma\rangle
and
B^{\prime}:=\langle\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l-1}\rangle
. We first determine the form of an arbitrary subgroup
\langle z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}\rangle
of
B
. Suppose then that
z_{i}=\beta_{1}^{a_{i1}}\beta_{2}^{a_{i2}}\cdots\beta_{l-1}^{a_{il-1}}\gamma^{a_{il}}
. We can consider the following matrix

\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&\cdots&a_{1l}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}&\cdots&a_{2l}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{r1}&a_{r2}&\cdots&a_{rl}\end{pmatrix}

where each row represents a generator of the subgroup. The entries can be seen to be in
\mathbb{Z}/2^{x}\mathbb{Z}
, but we have
a_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
for

. We note that applying elementary row operations does not change the subgroup, and so we may assume without loss of generality that

and then that
a_{ij}=0

\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&\cdots&a_{1l}\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

where we delete rows

(2)

\langle\beta^{\prime}_{1}\gamma^{2^{x-c_{1}}},\ldots,\beta^{\prime}_{l-1}\gamma^{2^{x-c_{l-1}}},\gamma^{2^{x-c_{l}}}\rangle

for some fixed

,

, and so we may assume that

. More generally therefore, we may assume that

We now count the subgroups

(i

) If

and the final column has all zeros apart from

in the last entry.

(ii

) If
\gamma^{2^{x-c}}
does not occur as a generator of

, then

corresponds to a matrix of
l
columns in RREF such that the first

columns have entries in
\{0,1\}
and entries in the final column are either

, with
0
in the last entry.

We note that the cases (i

) are disjoint from (i
{}_{c^{\prime}}
) for

and from (ii
{}_{c^{\prime\prime}}
) for any

. Further, we note that the matrix determines the value of
c
except when the last column consists entirely of zeros; in this case the matrix corresponds to a subgroup of

. Thus, given two values of
c
, say
c
and
c^{\prime}
, the cases (ii
c
) and (ii
{}_{c^{\prime}}
) are not disjoint, but intersect precisely when every entry of the final column of the matrix is equal to 0.

Now, for each
c>0
, we have a bijection between the matrices in (i
c
) and the matrices in (ii
c
), and the matrices with
l
columns over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
in RREF by replacing each occurrence of

by
1
, whenever it occurs. The number of these matrices is the same as the number of subgroups of
(C_{2})^{l}
. That is, by Corollary
2.6
:

(3)

\sum_{k=0}^{l}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l+1-1}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

To obtain the total number of subgroups of
B
, we note that we can just sum (
3
) over
1\leq c\leq x
, with the caveat that we over-count by

(x-1)\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

This is precisely due to the intersection between the two cases (ii
c
) and (ii
{}_{c^{\prime}}
) described above. In theory, the subgroups of
B^{\prime}
correspond to the case
c=0
, but this is already covered as discussed. So we need to take away
(x-1)
times the number of subgroups of
B^{\prime}\cong(C_{2})^{l-1}
from our initial count, as we need to count it exactly once. By Corollary
2.6
, the number of subgroups of
B^{\prime}
is precisely

\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

Therefore, the number of subgroups of
B
is:

(4)

x\sum_{k=0}^{l}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l+1-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}-(x-1)\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

We claim that this simplifies to

\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}(2^{l-(k+1)}x+1)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

To see this, we may first split the sum (
4
) as follows:

(5)

x\left[\sum_{k=0}^{l}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l+1-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}-\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}\right]+\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

Noting that the product

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l+1-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}

evaluates to
1
when
k=l
, we have that the ‘
x

) can be written as

1+\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l+1-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}-\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}\right].

We further note that the summand for

evaluates to
1-1=0
, and therefore we may start the sum from

. Considering, then, the difference between the two products in the summand, this evaluates to

1+\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\frac{2^{l}-2^{l-k}}{2^{k}-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

Noting now that
(2^{l}-2^{l-k})/(2^{k}-1)=2^{l-k}
and making the change of variables

, we see that this is equal to

1+\sum_{k=0}^{l-2}2^{l-(k+1)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

Finally, noting that when

, the summand evaluates to
1
, and hence the ‘
x
’ part of the sum can be written as

\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}2^{l-(k+1)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{l-i}-1}{2^{i}-1}.

The rest is clear.

\varphi_{1}:M_{1}\to M_{2}
by

\varphi_{1}((\alpha_{i-2}^{\nu},\textbf{e}_{j}))=(\alpha_{i-2},\textbf{e}_{j})^{\nu}

and acting as identity on the other generators. Then
\varphi_{1}
gives an isomorphism of permutation groups.

Let
M_{3},M_{4}\leq\mathrm{Hol}(N)
be transitive subgroups differing only by the fact that
M_{3}
contains the generators
(\alpha_{i-2}^{\nu},\textbf{e}_{1})
,
[\mu\textbf{e}_{2},S_{i}A_{i}^{-1}]
and
\textbf{e}_{2}
, and
M_{4}
instead contains the generators
(\alpha_{i-2},\textbf{e}_{2})^{\nu}
,
[\mu\textbf{e}_{1},S_{i}]
and
\textbf{e}_{1}
, for some
1\leq\nu\leq p_{i-1}-1
and
0\leq\mu\leq p_{i-1}-1
. Define the map
\varphi_{2}:M_{3}\to M_{4}
by

\varphi_{2}((\alpha_{i-2}^{\nu},\textbf{e}_{1}))=(\alpha_{i-2},\textbf{e}_{2})^{\nu},\;\;\varphi_{2}([\mu\textbf{e}_{2},S_{i}A_{i}^{-1}])=[\mu\textbf{e}_{1},S_{i}],\;\;\varphi_{2}(\textbf{e}_{2})=\textbf{e}_{1},

and acting as identity on the other generators. Then
\varphi_{2}
(and hence also
\varphi_{2}^{-1}
) gives an isomorphism of permutation groups.

Other than the isomorphisms already described, there are no further isomorphisms between the transitive subgroups of
\mathrm{Hol}(N)
, even as abstract groups.

Proof.

It is clear that the maps
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
give isomorphisms of permutation groups which restrict to isomorphisms of any two subgroups of the form
\langle(\alpha_{i-2}^{\nu},\textbf{e}_{j})\rangle
. By Corollary
3.19
, without loss of generality, we now only need to show that if
M,M^{\prime}\leq\mathrm{Hol}(N)
are transitive subgroups such that
M
and
M^{\prime}
contain
2
-subgroups of
B
, as given by (
6
), of the same order, but there is a
2
-subgroup
Y\leq M\cap H
such that
Y\not\subseteq M^{\prime}
, then
M
and
M^{\prime}
are not isomorphic as permutation groups. To this end, assuming
M\cong M^{\prime}
, we observe that
Y\leq\mathrm{Aut}(N)
with
Y
abelian and both
M
and
M^{\prime}
must contain a characteristic abelian subgroup
N_{I}\leq C_{m}
. We may then apply the method used in the proof of Proposition
3.12
to show that
M^{\prime}
must contain
Y
, which contradicts our assumption and finishes the proof.
∎

The next proposition gives us a way of computing
|\mathrm{Aut}(M,M^{\prime})|
for each transitive subgroup
M\leq\mathrm{Hol}(N)
.

Proposition 3.25
.

Let
M\leq Hol(N)
be a transitive subgroup, and let
M_{X}
denote the projection of
M
to the factor
X
of
\mathrm{Hol}(N)
, where
X
is one of
\mathrm{Hol}(C_{m_{j}})
(with each
m_{j}
a Cunningham product such that
\prod m_{j}=m
) or
\mathrm{Hol}(C_{p_{i-1}}\rtimes C_{p_{i}})
for some
i\in I
. Let
\mathcal{X}:=\{X\mid X\text{ is a factor of }\mathrm{Hol}(N)\}
. Then

|\mathrm{Aut}(M,M^{\prime})|=\prod_{X\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathrm{Aut}(M_{X},M_{X}^{\prime})|.

Proof.

Let
\phi:M\to M
be an isomorphism of permutation groups. Then
\phi
is a composition of isomorphisms of each factor
X\in\mathcal{X}
and isomorphisms of the form
\varphi_{1}
or
\varphi_{2}
described in Proposition
3.24
. However, we note that
\varphi_{1}(M),\varphi_{2}(M)\neq M
, so
\phi
must just be composed of isomorphisms on each factor.
∎

\bullet

Section 4.2 of

\bullet

\bullet
