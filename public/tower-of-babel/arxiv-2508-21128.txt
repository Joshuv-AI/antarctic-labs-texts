Detection of collective and point anomalies at the presence of trend and seasonality
Yiyin Zhang, Florian Pein, Idris Eckley

Abstract
Detecting anomalies in time series data is a challenging task with broad relevance in many applications. Existing methods work effectively only under idealized conditions, typically focusing on point anomalies or assuming a constant baseline. Our approach overcomes these limitations by detecting both collective and point anomalies, while allowing for polynomial trends and seasonal patterns. We establish statistical theory demonstrating that our method accurately decomposes the time series into anomaly, trend, seasonality, and a remainder component. We further show that it estimates the number of anomalies consistently and their locations with minimal error. Simulation studies confirm its strong detection performance with finite samples, and an application to energy price data illustrates its practical utility. An R package is available on request.

Introduction

The ability to accurately and efficiently estimate the location of anomalies in time series data has become increasingly important in various domains, including energy
(
Serrano-Guerrero et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
, network monitoring
(
Wu et al. 2021
)
and economics
(
Zamanzadeh Darban et al. 2024
)
.

Historically, there has been extensive research on anomalies within both the statistical and machine learning literature. For example, much work within statistics has been on developing inference methods that can cope with the effect of outlying observations.

Notable contributions include the foundational work of
Huber 1981
and
Rousseeuw and Yohai 1984
who introduced M- and S-estimators to provide robust estimates of the mean and variance, effectively mitigating the impact of outliers. In addition, several robust time series models have been developed to provide modelling approaches which are robust to anomalous observations. See, for example, work developing robust modelling for ARMA
(
Muler and Yohai 2009
)
, ARCH
(
Muler and Yohai 2002
)
, and GARCH
(
Muler and Yohai 2008
)
settings. A recent overview was given by
Maronna et al. 2019
.

In parallel, the machine learning community has contributed a diverse set of methods tailored for anomaly detection in time series data. These methods range from tree-based approaches such as Isolation Forests
(
Liu et al. 2008
)
to deep learning-based techniques. For instance,
Sakurada and Yairi 2014
apply auto-encoders to detect anomalies by reconstructing input data and measuring the reconstruction error. We refer the interested reader to reviews given by
Chandola et al. 2009
;
Chalapathy and Chawla 2019
;
Zhao et al. 2023
and the references therein.

Traditionally, anomaly detection focuses on identifying individual data points or entire sequences that deviate from expected patterns. More recent research, however, has shifted toward integrating changepoint detection with anomaly detection to capture collective anomalies – groups of consecutive data points that appear anomalous only when considered together. A prominent example is the work of
Fisch et al. 2020
, who introduced the CAPA (Collective And Point Anomalies) algorithm, capable of detecting both collective and point anomalies effectively. It operates under the assumption (
1
) that the data sequence follows a constant baseline with a normally distributed noise, treating any deviations as anomalies:

Y=A+B+\varepsilon,

(1)

where
Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is the observed time series,
A=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
represents point and collective anomalies,
B
is the constant baseline, and
\varepsilon
is the normally distributed noise.
By employing a penalized cost method, CAPA efficiently identifies both the number and locations of point and collective anomalies, balancing model complexity with accuracy. However, real-world time series data often exhibit complex structures, including trends, seasonal patterns, and noise. Recognizing these patterns is vital for accurate analysis across various domains such as finance
Liu et al. 2008
;
Kumar and Thenmozhi 2006
, energy
Hong and Fan 2016
;
Amjady and Keynia 2010
, and healthcare
Clifford et al. 2006
;
Liu et al. 2015
. An example of such data is displayed in Figure
1
, consisting of a seasonal component and a quadratic trend:

Y=A+S+T+\varepsilon.

(2)

Here the anomaly component
A
represents unexpected deviations from normal behavior, the long-term deterministic trend
T
captures the overall systematic direction of the data over time, seasonality
S
models recurring fluctuations, and noise
\varepsilon
accounts for random, unexplained variability. A full description of the model is given in Section
2.1
.

Distinguishing between genuine anomalies and normal variations within such data presents a significant challenge, particularly when trends and seasonal effects are pronounced. In such situations, CAPA and other variants face limitations. The presence of strong trend and seasonal components can obscure or mask anomalies, leading to false positives, as seen in Figure
2
.

Figure 2
:
As an illustration of its anomaly detection capabilities, CAPA is applied to the example shown in Figure
1
, with the yellow areas highlighting the collective anomalies estimated by CAPA. The significant number of false positives indicates that CAPA’s performance is inadequate when dealing with data that exhibits strong trend and seasonal components.

Several decomposition methods have been introduced to separate time series data into trend, seasonal, and residual components. Examples include Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess (STL)
(
Cleveland et al. 1990
)
, its version robust to outliers RobustSTL
(
Wen et al. 2019
)
, and Prophet
(
Taylor and Letham 2018
)
. They are designed for a model without (collective) anomalies, i.e. model (
2
) without the anomaly component
A
or
A
limited to point anomalies, but allow for a more flexible trend. Decomposition may improve anomaly detection by isolating trend and seasonal components, allowing for a more focused analysis of residuals where anomalies may be more easily identified.
Wen et al. 2019
demonstrate that such decomposition enhances the precision of anomaly detection by revealing hidden irregularities in the residuals. Despite the utility of decomposition methods, they struggle with time series that contain collective anomalies, which can be inadvertently incorporated into the estimated trend or seasonal components, complicating their identification and separation from regular patterns. This issue can misrepresent the anomalies and diminish the effectiveness of the decomposition approach, as shown in Figure
3
. We remark that RobustSTL was too slow to be applied to this data example with
5\,000
observations, but examples with less observations demonstrated that its decomposition has similar issues.

Figure 3
:
Decomposition of the simulated example illustrated in Figure
1
using STL. It consists of four subplots: the original data; the decomposed seasonal component; the decomposed trend; and the remainder. The collective anomalies have affected the decomposition, showcasing noticeable variations in both the seasonal and trend components.

Consequently, current methods are not well-suited for handling such complex anomaly structures, highlighting the need for more robust approaches. To overcome the limitations of existing methods, we propose
S
easonal
T
rend
A
nomaly
D
etection, (
STAD
), a novel approach extending existing anomaly detection methods by allowing a polynomial trend and seasonality as part of the typical pattern. At the same time, STAD decomposes the data into four components: anomaly, trend, seasonality, and residuals. By convention the baseline is included in the trend component. As discussed before none of the components can be estimated reliably by existing robust estimators when the other three components are present.

The method we propose is built upon a lag-differenced data, where the lag is a multiplicative of the seasonal length to remove the seasonal component. Standard robust methods can effectively estimate the polynomial trend at presence of point anomalies but struggle with collective anomalies, since they are non-symmetric and contain potentially a large number of anomalous points. We aim to overcome this issue by finding a subsample of the data that is free of collective anomalies and large enough to estimate the trend reliably. To this end, we propose a systematic sampling strategy that gives us a collection of subsamples. Under weak assumptions at least one of them will be free of collective anomalies and allow us to estimate the trend reliably. Once the trend has been removed, the seasonality can be estimated using robust estimators. Once the seasonality is estimated, the trend can be estimated without that differencing is required. Therefore, to improve accuracy we re-estimate the trend and seasonal component once before estimating the anomalies. Section
2
gives full details of the proposed methodology.

In Section
3
we use statistical theory to confirm the excellent detection properties of STAD. We show that it successfully decomposes the time series by providing asymptotic L2 bounds for the error of its estimation of the anomaly, trend and seasonal component. Furthermore, we show consistent estimation of the number of anomalies and that estimation of start and end points have a small asymptotic error. These results substantially improve on the results in
(
Fisch et al. 2020
)
for CAPA. Not only that we allow for the presence of a polynomial trend and seasonality with minor assumptions on them, but we also overcame the limitation that anomalies are at most of order
\sqrt{n}
while we mirror the condition on the size of the anomalies despite allowing for a much more complex setting.

The finite sample performance is studied via simulation studies in Section
4
. STAD estimates anomalies almost as good as its oracle version that knows the trend and seasonal component unless anomalies are extremely long (more than a quarter of the total time series) or the seasonal component has very few repetitions (less than five). In Section
5
we use STAD to detect anomalies in the price of energy data. A brief illustration of the effectiveness of our method is given in Figures
4
and
5
, in which we have applied STAD to the data shown Figure
1
. Different to CAPA and STL, confer Figures
2
and
3
, it does not include false positives and decomposes the data successfully into anomaly, trend, seasonal, and residual components.

Methodology

We first detail our model, see Section
2.1
, and then STAD. In Section
2.2
we explain how we estimate the anomaly component once trend and seasonality have been removed. In Section
2.3
we propose a robust method to estimate the trend at presence of collective anomalies, but under the assumption that the seasonality has been removed, either by estimation or as detailed in Section
2.4
by differencing the data. Similarly, Section
2.5
details the estimation of the seasonal component at presence of collective anomalies, but when the trend has been removed. Finally, STAD, a combination of the previous steps, is proposed in Section
2.6
. All estimators are summarized in Algorithms
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
in Appendix
A
.

2.1
Model

As motivated in the introduction, we assume that the observation vector
Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is given by the composition

Y=A+T+S+\varepsilon,

(3)

with anomalies
A=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, trend
T=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
, seasonality
S=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
, and noise
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
. The four components are defined as follows:

The
anomalies
split further into collective and point anomalies

a_{i}=a_{i}^{\operatorname{coll}}+a_{i}^{\operatorname{point}}.

The collective anomalies are defined as

a_{i}^{\operatorname{coll}}=\begin{cases}\mu_{k},&\text{if }b_{k}<i\leq e_{k},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

with
K\in\mathbb{N}
being the number,
0\leq b_{1}<e_{1}\leq\cdots\leq b_{K}<e_{K}\leq n
being the start and end points, and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{K}
being the magnitudes of the collective anomalies. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{A}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}(b_{k},e_{k}]
be the union of all anomalous intervals. Let
O=\{o_{1},\ldots,o_{m}\}
be the index set of the point anomalies, i.e.
a_{i}^{\operatorname{point}}\neq 0
if and only if
i\in O
.

The
trend
is given by a polynomial of degree
Q
, i.e.

T=X\beta,

(4)

with regression matrix
X=(x_{i,j})_{i=1,\ldots,n;\,j=1,\ldots,Q+1}
having entries

x_{i,j}=\left(\frac{i}{n}\right)^{j-1},

(5)

and coefficients
\beta=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{Q})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q+1}
.

We assume that the
seasonality
has a known period
P
, and is given by

s_{i}=\vartheta_{(i\bmod P)},

(6)

with
\vartheta_{0},\ldots,\vartheta_{P-1}\in\mathbb{R}
. For identifiability, we further assume

\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}=0,

(7)

i.e. the global constant is assumed to be specified by the trend, without a baseline in the anomaly component or a contribution by the seasonality.

Finally, we assume the
noise

\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
to be i.i.d. centered Gaussian distributed with variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
, i.e.

\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}).

(8)

The global standard deviation
\sigma_{0}
can be pre-estimated using robust difference based estimators
(
Frick et al. 2014
;
Dette et al. 1998
)
. More precisely, we use the IQR-based estimator from
Pein et al. 2017
:

\hat{\sigma}_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{\text{IQR}(\Delta y)}{\text{IQR}_{\Phi}},

(9)

where
\Delta y=(y_{i+1}-y_{i})_{i=1}^{n-1}
are the first order differences,
\text{IQR}(\Delta y)
is the interquartile range of
\Delta y
, and
\text{IQR}_{\Phi}
is the interquartile range of a standard normal distribution. The factor
1/\sqrt{2}
corrects for the variance inflation introduced by differencing.

2.2
Anomaly Detection

We now present our methodology for estimating the collective and point anomalies. To this end, we assume that trend and seasonality have been removed, up to small estimation errors
R,W\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e. our method is based on the model

Y=A+R+W+\varepsilon.

(10)

To infer the number and locations of anomalous segments, we use a penalized cost approach analogous to CAPA
(
Fisch et al. 2022
)
. We employ a change-in-mean version and assume that the trend estimation has removed any baseline, i.e. we apply CAPA with baseline
\mu_{0}=0
. More precisely, for any vector of observations
Z\coloneq(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})
we define the costs for a constant segment from
i
to
j
as

C_{i,j}(Z)\coloneq\sum_{l=i}^{j}z_{l}^{2},\quad 1\leq i\leq j\leq n.

(11)

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\coloneq\bigcup_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}}(\tilde{b}_{k},\tilde{e}_{k}]
be a candidate set of anomalies. Here,
\tilde{b}_{k}
and
\tilde{e}_{k}
denote the start and end of the
k
-th anomalous segment, respectively. Let further
\tilde{\mu}=(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mu}_{\tilde{K}})
be the candidate vector for the anomaly magnitudes. We then define their penalised costs as

C(Z,\tilde{\mathcal{A}},\tilde{\mu})\coloneq\sum_{k=0}^{\tilde{K}}C_{\tilde{e}_{k}+1,\tilde{b}_{k+1}}(Z)+\sum_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}}C_{\tilde{b}_{k}+1,\tilde{e}_{k}}(Z-\tilde{\mu}_{k})+\tilde{K}\lambda_{\operatorname{coll}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}\log(n),

(12)

By convention, we set
\tilde{e}_{0}=0
and
\tilde{b}_{\tilde{K}+1}=n
and for any constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
we write
Z-a
for the vector
(z_{1}-a,\ldots,z_{n}-a)
. The first two terms in (
12
) quantify the deviation of observations from the baseline
0
and the anomaly magnitude respectively. The final term,
\tilde{K}\lambda_{\operatorname{coll}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}\log(n)
is a SIC-type penalty. The cost function can readily be extended to allow for point anomalies. Specifically, let
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
be a candidate set of point anomalies. Then, (
12
) is replaced by

\begin{split}C_{O}(Z,\tilde{\mathcal{A}},\tilde{\mu})\coloneq&\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i\notin\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\cup\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}z_{i}^{2}+\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle k=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\tilde{K}}\Big[C_{\tilde{b}_{k}+1,\tilde{e}_{k}}(Z-\tilde{\mu}_{k})+\lambda_{\operatorname{coll}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}\log(n)\Big]+\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i\in\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\big[\lambda_{\operatorname{point}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}\log(n)\big],\end{split}

(13)

The reformulation of the first term in (
12
) follows directly from (
11
). The second term is unchanged and for the final term note that the unpenalised cost term is equal to
0
for a point anomaly, since the estimated function value is equal to the observation value. Similar to the collective anomaly penalty in (
12
), we add a SIC-type penalty
\lambda_{\operatorname{point}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}\log(n)
. By default, we recommend
\lambda_{\operatorname{coll}}=4
for collective anomalies and
\lambda_{\operatorname{point}}=3
for point anomalies. The reasoning behind this choice is discussed in Section
4.4
.

The final estimate of the anomalies is obtained by minimizing the penalized cost

(\hat{\mathcal{A}},\hat{\mu})\coloneq\operatorname*{argmin}_{(\tilde{\mathcal{A}},\tilde{\mu})}C(Z,\tilde{\mathcal{A}},\tilde{\mu}),

(14)

and the final estimate of the anomaly component is
\hat{A}=(\hat{a}_{1},\ldots,\hat{a}_{n})
with

\hat{a}_{i}=\begin{cases}\hat{\mu}_{k}&\text{if }\exists\ k\,:\,\hat{b}_{k}<i\leq\hat{e}_{k},\\
y_{i}&\text{if }i\in\hat{\mathcal{O}},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(15)

The penalized cost is additive over segments and can therefore be minimized via dynamic programming, leveraging the shared zero mean across non-anomalous segments
(
Fisch et al. 2022
)
. To calculate (
14
), we employ the Generalized Functional Pruning Optimal Partitioning (GFPOP) software package
(
Hocking et al. 2022
)
.

2.3
Trend Estimation

We now present our approach for polynomial trend estimation in the presence of anomalies, in particular collective anomalies. In other words, we assume that the seasonality has been removed up to a small estimation error
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is, we model our data by

Y=T+A+W+\varepsilon.

(16)

Our procedure is based on finding an index set
U\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
, such that
U\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
, and
U
is large enough and well structured to permit a reliable estimation of the trend. Specifically, we propose in Section
2.3.2
a sampling procedure that obtains a collection of index sets
\mathcal{U}
. For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, the trend is then estimated by a robust estimator using only the observations in
U
, see Section
2.3.1
. To select our final estimate we apply our anomaly detection method to the residuals of the trend estimate and choose the one that minimises the costs (
12
), full details are given in Section
2.3.3
.

2.3.1
M-estimator

Given an index set
U\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
, which may include both anomalous and non-anomalous observations, we estimate
\beta
by an M-estimator using solely the data in
U
. We aim to obtain a good trend estimate if
U\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
. We use the following wording in the following.

Definition 1
.

We call an index set
U\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}

anomalous
if and only if at least one of its points is part of a collective anomaly, i.e.
U\cap\mathcal{A}\neq\emptyset
.

If
U
is free of any anomalies, then least squares regression is best; however, since point anomalies may be present, a robust trend estimator is preferable. Following extensive experimentation, we adopt the M-estimator
(
Huber 1964
)
with Tukey’s biweight loss. For any observation vector
Z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
and any index set
U
, we define an M-estimator for the polynomial trend
T
in (
16
) by

\hat{\beta}(U)\coloneq\operatorname*{argmin}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{Q+1}}\sum_{i\in U}\rho\left[\frac{z_{i}-\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\beta_{q}x_{i,q}}{\hat{\sigma_{0}}}\right],

(17)

where
\rho(\cdot)
is the robust loss function,
x_{i,q}
are the polynomial regressors (
5
) and
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
is the estimated scale parameter (
9
). For the loss function
\rho
, we choose Tukey’s biweight loss function
(
Beaton and Tukey 1974
)
,

\rho_{c}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{c^{2}}{6}\left[1-\left(1-\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)^{2}\right)^{3}\right],&\text{if }|x|\leq c,\\
\frac{c^{2}}{6},&\text{if }|x|>c.\end{cases}

(18)

Unlike for instance Huber’s loss function which only reduces the contribution of outliers to the loss, the biweight function does not increase for outliers beyond the threshold
c
; set to 4.685 by default. M-estimator
(
Huber 1964
)
with Tukey’s biweight loss is robust against substantial outlier contamination while maintaining statistical efficiency
(
Özlem Gürünlü Alma 2011
)
.

The non-convex optimization challenge coming from using Tukey’s biweight loss is alleviated by our sampling approach as we rerun the algorithm multiple times for different observations. We employ the widely used iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) algorithm
(
Beaton and Tukey 1974
)
to compute (
17
), details are provided in Appendix
C
.

2.3.2
Sampling Procedure

We aim at sampling a collection of index sets
\mathcal{U}
that contains at least one index set
U
that is non-anomalous, i.e.
U\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
, and secondly that is large enough and well structured to allow a good estimate of
\beta
.

We use the following structured partitioning approach to sample
U
. First, we divide the index set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
into
2(Q+1)
disjoint segments
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2(Q+1)}
of roughly equal length (be made more precise later).
We will sample a sub-segment from either each odd or from each even indexed segment. More precisely, let
l\sim\operatorname{Bern}(\frac{1}{2})
be a Bernoulli-distributed random variable. We then sample sub-segments from
u_{l},u_{l+2},\ldots,u_{l+2Q}
(see below). This constructions ensures that the sampled sub-segments are well separated from each other, which is a key requirement to estimate the
Q+1
parameters in
\beta
well.

We now detail how we sample from
u_{k}
. We further partition each segment
u_{k}
into
B
sub-segments of roughly equal length. In total, we obtain

V=B\times 2(Q+1)

(19)

sub-segments

\tilde{u}_{k}\coloneq\left\{\Big\lfloor(k-1)\frac{n}{V}\Big\rceil+1,\ldots,\Big\lfloor k\frac{n}{V}\Big\rceil\right\},\quad k=1,\ldots,V.

(20)

We then sample one sub-segment from each selected segment. More precisely, we obtain the index set

U\coloneq\bigcup_{k=0}^{Q}\tilde{u}_{(2k+l)B+m_{k}},

(21)

where
l\sim\operatorname{Bern}\big(\frac{1}{2}\big)
is a Bernoulli-distributed random variable and
m_{k}\sim\text{Uniform}\big(\{1,\ldots,B\}\big)
. All random variables are independent of each other.

In summary, suppose appropriately chosen tuning parameters (see Section
2.3.3
), the proposed sampling procedure obtains a collection of index sets
\mathcal{U}
that ensures the following: With high probability at least one of the
U\in\mathcal{U}
is non-anomalous, see Lemma
3
. At the same time each
U\in\mathcal{U}
is constructed such that if it is non-anomalous, then the trend is estimated well (with high probability), see Lemma
9
. A key step was Lemma
4
in which we have shown that the determinant of
X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}
is lower bounded. This lemma relies heavily on the fact that the
\tilde{u}_{(2k+l)B+m_{k}}
’s are well separated from each other.

2.3.3
Trend estimate selection

To obtain a good estimation of the trend
T
, we repeat the sampling procedure multiple times. We also have to select the tuning parameter
B
and polynomial degree
Q
.

To this end, let
\mathbf{B}=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{b}\}
and
\mathbf{Q}=\{Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{q}\}
be the candidate sets for
B
and
Q
, respectively. For each combination
\tilde{B}\in\mathbf{B}
and
\tilde{Q}\in\mathbf{Q}
we repeat the sampling procedure in Section
2.3.2

J
times. We obtain the collection of index sets

\mathcal{U}\coloneq\big\{U_{\tilde{B},\tilde{Q},j}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}:\tilde{B}\in\mathbf{B},\,\tilde{Q}\in\mathbf{Q},\,j=1,\ldots,J\big\},

and trend estimates

\hat{\mathcal{T}}=\big\{\hat{T}_{U}:U\in\mathcal{U}\big\},

(22)

with
\hat{T}_{U}\coloneq X\hat{\beta}(U)
and
\hat{\beta}(\cdot)
as defined in (
17
).

We then select the final trend estimate
\hat{T}
by selecting the one that minimizes the penalized cost of the anomaly estimator in (
12
) applied to
Y-\hat{T}_{U}
plus a penalty for the degree of the trend, i.e.

\hat{T}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{T\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\min_{(\tilde{\mathcal{A}},\tilde{\mu})}C(Y-T,\tilde{\mathcal{A}},\tilde{\mu})+\tilde{Q}\,\hat{\sigma}_{0}\log(n).

(23)

The tuning parameter
J
is chosen to be sufficiently large such that with high probability at least one of the
J
sampled segments is non-anomalous, confer Lemmas
2
and
3
. By default we suggest
J=20
based on the simulations in Section
4.3
. The optimal choice of the tuning parameter
B
is contingent on anomaly characteristics, e.g. number and length of collective anomalies. If
B
is too small and collective anomalies are long, then all samples in
\mathcal{U}
may intersect with collective anomalies. Conversely, if
B
is too large, then the sample
U_{j,B,Q}
is too small to allow a good estimation of the trend. These points are further illustrated in Appendix
B
. Finally, the correct polynomial degree
Q
should be contained in
\mathbf{Q}
, though a too large degree may still allow good estimates. Based on empirical studies, in the absence of further information we recommend
\mathbf{B}=\{1,3,5\}
and
\mathbf{Q}=\{0,1,2,3\}
as they provide a good trade-off between statistical efficiency and computational demands.

2.4
Initial trend estimation

To estimate the trend without interference from the seasonality, we apply differencing to
Y
with lag
D=C\cdot P
, where
C\in\mathbb{N}
. More precisely, we use the differenced series
Y_{D}
, with entries

y_{D,i}=y_{i+D}-y_{i},\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,n-D,

which has no seasonal component, confer Lemma
1
. The differenced sequence has trend

T_{D}=X_{D}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{D},

where the regression matrix
X_{D}
has entries

(X_{D})_{i,q}\coloneq X_{i+D,q+1}-X_{i,q+1}=\left(\frac{i+D}{n}\right)^{q}-\left(\frac{i}{n}\right)^{q},

for
i=1,\ldots,n-D,\ q=1,\ldots,Q
, and parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{D}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{Q})
.
Lemma
1
confirms this trend and details the effects of differencing on the noise and anomaly components, respectively. We then estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{D}
and hence all entries of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
except
\beta_{0}
by applying the trend estimation method from Section
2.3
to
Y_{D}
. We denote the resulting parameter estimate by

\hat{\beta}_{D}=(\hat{\beta}_{D,1},\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{D,Q})

and we obtain the initial trend
\hat{T}_{0}
as

\hat{T}_{0}\coloneq X\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\hat{\beta}_{D}\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

The selection of an appropriate multiplier
C
is crucial, as an inappropriate choice can lead to non-detectable anomalies. Theoretical justification is provided in Lemma
1
. By default,
C
is chosen to be an integer such that
D=C\cdot P\approx 0.1n
and if
P\geq n/10
, then
C=1
.

2.5
Seasonal Component Estimation

We now introduce a robust estimator for the seasonal component that allows for the presence of anomalies. To this end, we assume that the trend has been removed, up to a small estimation error
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Under this assumption, the data follows the model:

Y=S+A+R+\varepsilon.

(25)

We also allow the estimator to take into account an estimation of the anomalies
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
, obtained by (
23
). Let
\hat{\mathcal{A}}=\emptyset
for the initial seasonality estimation
\hat{S}_{0}
.

For
p=0,\ldots,P-1
, to estimate
\vartheta_{p}
we use the index set

V_{p}=\big\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\hat{\mathcal{A}}:(i\bmod P)=p\big\},

(26)

and Tukey’s M-estimator

\hat{\vartheta}_{p}=\hat{\beta}(V_{p}),

(27)

with
\hat{\beta}
being the M-estimator from (
17
) and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. For robustness, we initialize the estimator with the median of
Y_{V_{p}}
rather than using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). The final seasonal estimate
\hat{S}=(\hat{s}_{1},\ldots,\hat{s}_{n})
is then defined as

\tilde{s}_{i}\coloneq\hat{\vartheta}_{i\bmod P}\text{ and }\hat{s}_{i}\coloneq\tilde{s}_{i}-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{s}_{i}.

(28)

This ensures that
\hat{S}
satisfies (
7
).

2.5.1
Smoothing

If the underlying seasonality is assumed to be smooth, we further improve the estimate
\hat{S}
by applying spline smoothing. This enhances accuracy and interpretability. We choose spline smoothing due to its statistical robustness, computational efficiency, and flexibility in capturing complex seasonal patterns
(
De Boor 1978
)
, but other smoothing approaches would be suitable as well. More precisely, we apply cubic smoothing splines to
(\hat{\vartheta}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\vartheta}_{P-1})
with the smoothing parameter selected by Generalized Cross-Validation. To this end, we use the R function
smooth.spline
.

2.6
STAD

We are now ready to define our main method STAD. It is the following combination of the previous estimators.

Step 0: Scale parameter estimation

We estimate
\sigma_{0}
by
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
as defined in (
9
).

Step 1: Initial Estimation

Obtain an initial trend estimation
\hat{T}_{0}
by applying the proposed robust trend estimator in (
23
) to a differenced sequence of
Y
, which has removed the seasonality. Subsequently, obtain an initial estimate of the seasonality
\hat{S}_{0}
by applying the robust seasonality estimator in (
27
) to the residuals
Y-\hat{T}_{0}
.

Step 2: Trend Estimation

Obtain a refined trend estimation
\hat{T}
by applying the robust trend estimator in (
23
) to the residuals
Y-\hat{S}_{0}
. This process simultaneously estimates anomalous intervals, denoted by
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
.

Step 3: Seasonality Estimation

Obtain a refined seasonality estimation
\hat{S}
by applying the robust seasonality estimator in (
27
) to the residuals
Y-\hat{T}
, but excluding the estimated anomalous intervals
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
.

Step 4: Anomaly Detection

Finally, we estimate the anomalous intervals
\mathcal{A}
and the anomaly component
A
by our modified version of the CAPA algorithm, detailed in Section
2.2
, applied to the residuals
Y-\hat{S}-\hat{T}
.

3
Theory

With our methodology now established, we discuss theoretical properties of the STAD approach in detecting anomalies and decomposing the time series into anomalies, trend, seasonality, plus a remainder. Specifically in Theorem
1
we establish asymptotic L2-bounds on the error of each component’s estimate individually. Theorem
1
also shows that STAD estimates the number of anomalies consistently and their start and end points are close to the true ones. More precisely, we show that
\hat{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathcal{B}
, where

\mathcal{B}\coloneq\{\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\text{ s.t. }\tilde{K}=K,|\tilde{b}_{k}-b_{k}|<\kappa_{k},|\tilde{e}_{k}-e_{k}|<\kappa_{k},\ \forall\ k=1,\ldots,K\}.

Here, the errors
\kappa_{k}
are defined as

\kappa_{k}\coloneq\left\{\bigg\lceil C_{1}\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{k}^{2}}\log(n)^{1+\delta}\bigg\rceil,\ 4^{Q+1}\right\},\quad k=1,\ldots,K,

(29)

with
C_{1}>0
being a large enough constant such that the last step in the proof of Lemma
15
holds. The maximum with
4^{Q+1}
ensures that we have locally enough observations. For Theorem
1
to hold we require the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

There exists a
\tilde{\delta}>0
such that

e_{k}-b_{k}\geq\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{k}}\log(n)^{1+\delta+\tilde{\delta}},\ \forall\ k=1,\ldots,K.

(30)

Furthermore, we require that

\min_{k=0,\ldots,K}\{b_{k+1}-e_{k}\}\geq 3\max_{k=1,\ldots,K}\{e_{k}-b_{k}\}+1,

(31)

and
D=CP
is such that

2\max_{k=1,\ldots,K}\{e_{k}-b_{k}\}\leq D=CP\leq\min_{k=0,\ldots,K}\{b_{k+1}-e_{k}\}-\max_{k=1,\ldots,K}\{e_{k}-b_{k}\}-1.

(32)

Equation (
30
) in Assumption
1
is a standard assumption on the length and size of an collective anomaly to be detectable. It mirrors Assumption 2 in
(
Fisch et al. 2020
)
for the CAPA algorithm in the absence of trend and seasonality. Note that the result
\hat{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathcal{B}
is identical, up to the definition of
\kappa_{k}
as we consider a change-in-mean instead of a change-in-mean-and-variance problem. The second requirement (
31
) ensures that anomalies are separated and hence individually detectable after differencing and is not required in the absence of either trend or seasonality.

Assumption 2
.

The total length of collective anomalies is bounded. More precisely, there exists a
\gamma<1
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{K}e_{k}-b_{k}<\gamma\frac{1}{\max(4(Q+1),8)}.

Assumption
2
ensures that we have locally enough non-anomalous observations to estimate trend and seasonality well. Remarkably, the total length of anomalies can be of order
n
. This shows that we have overcome the substantial limitation of CAPA given by Assumption 2(b) in
(
Fisch et al. 2020
)
that anomalies can be at most of order
\sqrt{n}
to allow a good estimate of the baseline. The
4(Q+1)
-term is required for a good trend estimation, while the
8
is required for a good seasonality estimation.

Assumption 3
.

(i)

There exists a large enough
B\in\mathbf{B}
such that (
39
), (
48
), and (
50
) are satisfied.

(ii)

We have that
Q\in\mathbf{Q}
.

Assumption
3
is on the tuning parameters
Q
and
B
to ensure that we can estimate the trend well. It requires the true order of the polynomial
Q
to be in the set
\mathbf{Q}
. This could be weakened to only a
\tilde{Q}\geq Q
being required. Secondly, the tuning parameter
B
has to be large enough. We remark that the precise conditions work well for the asymptotic theory, but are not of practical relevance. We refer to Section
2.3.3
and Appendix
B
for a discussion of its choice.

Assumption 4
.

There exits a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\max_{i=2,\ldots,n}|t_{i}-t_{i-1}|\leq C_{2}\sigma_{0},

(33)

and

\max_{i=2,\ldots,n}|s_{i}-s_{i-1}|\leq C_{2}\sigma_{0}.

(34)

Assumption
4
is only used to bound the variance estimate
\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}
, see Proposition
1
. The assumption could be relaxed by allowing a small proportion of points to fail conditions (
33
) and (
34
).

Under these assumptions, Theorem
1
shows that we have successfully solved the structural challenge to decompose a time series into anomalies, trend, and seasonality and to estimate collective anomalies when the typical distribution is not stationary. Our theory even accounts for the initial variance estimate. However, as, to the best of our knowledge, finite sample bounds for robust estimators are not available, we assume that we have no point anomalies and use the least squares estimator instead of Tukey’s estimator, i.e. the loss function
\rho
is given by the
L_{2}
-norm. To estimate the seasonality we stop after the initial median, i.e. (
27
) is replaced by the median of the observations indexed by
V_{p}
. Using robust estimators instead will have slightly worse statistical efficiency, but better robustness to potential anomalies in
U
, either small parts of collective anomalies or point anomalies.

4
Simulation study

We now assess STAD’s finite sample performance by simulation studies. We compare STAD’s performance against oracle versions of itself that know the trend (Oracle-Trend), the seasonality (Oracle-Seasonality), and both components (Full Oracle). For all methods we use the default choices for tuning parameters as detailed in Section
2
. A comparison with other methods is omitted, as most existing anomaly methods focus on point anomalies only and to the best of our knowledge no existing methods achieves acceptable results for detecting collective anomalies at the presence of significant trends and seasonality such that a systematic comparison is not meaningful, c.f. the illustrations in Section
1
.

Our simulation studies are based on synthetic time series of length
n=5000
, generated from the model in Section
2.1
. We chose the trend component to be
t_{i}=2(i/n)^{2}-2(i/n)
, a sine-wave seasonality
s_{i}=2\sin(2\pi i/P)
, where
P
is the period length, and the noise component is
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Simulations (not displayed) showed that results are stable in the choice of the concrete form of the trend and seasonality, so for brevity we do not vary their form in the following. Instead we consider various settings for the anomalies and also vary in one study the period length
P
, which is otherwise chosen as
P=250
.

We found that the anomaly length, the number of seasonal repetitions, and of course the signal to noise ratio have the most dominant effect on the performance of all contrasted methods. We demonstrate the influence of the first two points in the most simplest setting with a single anomaly. In Sections
4.1
and
4.2
we vary the length of the single anomaly and the period length, respectively. Finally, in Section
4.5
we consider a more evolved setting with multiple anomalies which considers different signal to noise ratios but also different anomaly lengths. In between we discuss tuning parameter choices. Most tuning parameters have been theoretically discussed in Section
2
. So, in Sections
4.3
and
4.4
we focus on the number of repetitions
J
and the penalty, respectively.

All simulations are repeated
1\;000
times. We then evaluate the methods based on their average accuracy in detecting the collective anomalies. We define an estimation as positive if the estimated number of anomalies is equal to the true number of anomalies and if start and end points differ by at most
\lfloor\log(n)\rfloor
. We omit other metrics for brevity and since we found for instance that the average mean square error for the anomaly, trend, and seasonal component estimation shows qualitatively the same result as the accuracy. Also changing the
\lfloor\log(n)\rfloor
tolerance level for the locations of anomalies allows for the same comparisons.

Appendix C
The Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares

For completeness, we detail the Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) algorithm that we use to compute the
M
-estimator.

1.

Start at
t=0
with an initial estimate
\hat{\beta}^{(0)}
. We use ordinary least squares for the tend estimator and the median for the seasonal estimate.

2.

Compute the residuals

e_{i}^{(t)}=y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\hat{\beta}^{(t)},

and the corresponding weights

w_{i}^{(t)}=\frac{\psi(e_{i}^{(t)})}{e_{i}^{(t)}},

where
\psi
denotes the influence function, i.e. the derivative of the loss function
\rho
.

3.

Update the estimate
\hat{\beta}
using weighted least squares regression

\hat{\beta}^{(t+1)}=\left(X^{\top}W^{(t)}X\right)^{-1}X^{\top}W^{(t)}y,

where
W^{(t)}=\text{diag}(w_{1}^{(t)},w_{2}^{(t)},\dots,w_{n}^{(t)})
.

4.

Increase
t
by
1
and repeat steps 2 and 3 until the estimated coefficients converge, i.e.

\sum_{j}\left|\hat{\beta}^{(t+1)}_{j}-\hat{\beta}^{(t)}_{j}\right|<10^{-5},

or a maximum of
500
iterations is reached.

D.2
Bound on the variance estimate

The following proposition bounds the variance estimate.

Proposition 1
.

We assume the model from Section
2.1
. Let
\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}
be the variance estimator as defined in (
9
). Suppose Assumption
4
holds. Then, there exists constants
0<C_{4}\leq 1
and
C_{5}\geq 1
such that

\mathbb{P}\big(C_{4}\sigma_{0}\leq\hat{\sigma}_{0}\leq C_{5}\sigma_{0}\big)\to 1,\text{ as }n\to\infty.

(37)

Proof.

Let
\Delta y
be the first order difference of the observations as defined before (
9
), let
\operatorname{IQR}
be the interquartile range, let
\operatorname{IQR}_{\Phi}
be the interquartile range of a standard Gaussian distribution, let
q_{0.75}
and
q_{0.25}
be the 75% and 25% quantile, respectively. Then, for a constant
\tilde{C}_{1}>0
to be chosen later, the definition of the interquartile range and a union bound yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{IQR}\big(\Delta y\big)>2\tilde{C}_{1}\sigma_{0}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(q_{0.75}\big(\Delta y\big)>\tilde{C}_{1}\sigma_{0}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(q_{0.25}\big(\Delta y\big)<-\tilde{C}_{1}\sigma_{0}\right).

In the following we focus on bounding the first probability, but the bounding the second probability follows similar steps. It follows from the definition of a quantile for the first inequality, from Assumption
4
for the second inequality, and from the fact that there are at most
2K
different
i
’s for which
a_{i}-a_{i-1}\neq 0
, since this can only occur at the beginning or end of an anomaly, for the third inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(q_{0.75}\big(\Delta y\big)>\tilde{C}_{1}\sigma_{0}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{y_{i}-y_{i-1}>\tilde{C}_{1}\sigma_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{4}(n-1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i-1}+a_{i}-a_{i-1}>\tilde{C}_{1}\sigma_{0}-2C_{2}\sigma_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{4}(n-1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i-1}>\tilde{C}_{1}\sigma_{0}-2C_{2}\sigma_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{4}(n-1)-2K\right).

We choose
\tilde{C}_{1}
such that
\frac{\tilde{C}_{1}-2C_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}
is the
80\%
quantile of a standard Gaussian distribution. Then, the left hand side in the probability above is binomial distributed with size
n-1
and success probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{0}}>\frac{\tilde{C}_{1}-2C_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{5}.

We use the following bound from
(
Feller and Morse 1958
, (3.5))
,

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq r)\leq\frac{r(1-p)}{(r-np)^{2}},

if
r\geq np
, where
S_{n}
is Binomial distributed with size
n
and success probability
p
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(q_{0.75}\big(\Delta y\big)>\tilde{C}_{1}\sigma_{0}\right)\leq\frac{n}{\big(\frac{1}{4}(n-1)-2K-\frac{1}{5}(n-1)\big)^{2}}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
. This completes the proof with
C_{5}=2\tilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{2}\operatorname{IQR}_{\Phi}
, as the second probability can be bounded following similar arguments. Also the proof of
C_{4}\sigma_{0}\leq\hat{\sigma}_{0}
follows similar arguments.
∎

.

We assume the model from Section

. Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
, and

, and

Proof of Lemma
1
.

We obtain

y_{D,i}=y_{i+D}-y_{i}=(a_{i+D}-a_{i})+(t_{i+D}-t_{i})+(s_{i+D}-s_{i})+(\varepsilon_{i+D}-\varepsilon_{i}).

In the following we look at each component separately. We start with the seasonal component. Since

This shows that

has no seasonal component.

Next, we look at the trend component. We obtain

\displaystyle t_{D,i}\coloneq t_{i+D}-t_{i}=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\beta_{q}X_{i+D,q}-\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\beta_{q}X_{i,q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\beta_{q}\big(X_{i+D,q}-X_{i,q}\big)\eqcolon\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\beta_{q}\big((X_{D})_{i,q}\big).

It remains to show that

disappears and that it is a polynomial of degree

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\beta_{q}\big(X_{i+D,q}-X_{i,q}\big)=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\beta_{q}\left[\left(\frac{i+D}{n}\right)^{q}-\left(\frac{i}{n}\right)^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\beta_{q}\left[\left(\frac{i+D}{n}\right)^{q}-\left(\frac{i}{n}\right)^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\beta_{q}\left[\frac{D}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\left(\frac{i}{n}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{i+D}{n}\right)^{q-1-r}\right].

We conclude that

is a polynomial of degree

, with regression matrix

\beta_{D}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{Q})

Next we consider the anomaly component. We focus for now on the contribution of the

yields

and hence
e_{k}-D<b_{k}
. We distinguish a few cases.

a_{D,i}\coloneq a_{i+D}-a_{i}=\begin{cases}0-0=0,&\text{if }i\leq b_{k}-D,\\
\theta_{k}-0=\theta_{k},&\text{if }b_{k}-D<i\leq e_{k}-D,\\
0-0=0,&\text{if }e_{k}-D<i\leq b_{k},\\
0-\theta_{k}=-\theta_{k},&\text{if }b_{k}<i\leq e_{k},\\

We obtain two anomalies of magnitude
\theta_{k}

. Hence, Assumption

b_{k}-(e_{k}-D)=D-(e_{k}-b_{k})\geq e_{k}-b_{k},

(b_{k+1}-D)-(e_{k}+1)=b_{k+1}-e_{k}-D-1\geq\min\{e_{k}-b_{k},e_{k+1}-b_{k+1}\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\left[\varepsilon_{i+D},\varepsilon_{j+D}\right]+\operatorname{Cov}\left[\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{j}\right]-\operatorname{Cov}\left[\varepsilon_{i+D},\varepsilon_{j}\right]-\operatorname{Cov}\left[\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{j+D}\right]

This completes the induction to show (

Let

now be given by Setting

(

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Lemma
4
hold. Then there exists a constant
C_{11}>0
such that

\operatorname{tr}\left[X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\right]\leq C_{11}.

Proof of Lemma
8
.

For any matrix
A
and any symmetric, positive definite matrix
B
, with dimensions such that the products exist, we have that
\operatorname{tr}(ABA^{\top})=\operatorname{tr}(BA^{\top}A)
. We obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left[(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)\right].

Let
X
now be given by Setting
1
(
a
). Then, we have that
0\leq X_{i,j}\leq 1
for all
i=1,\ldots,n;\ j=1,\ldots,Q+1
. Consequently,
(X^{\top}X)_{i,j}\leq n
for all
i=1,\ldots,Q+1;\ j=1,\ldots,Q+1
.

Next, we focus on the entries in the inverse matrix
(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}
. Since
X_{U}
is a sub-matrix of
X
, we have once again that
(X_{U})_{i,j}\leq 1
and
(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})_{i,j}\leq n
. The inverse
(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}
is given by the cofactor matrix of
X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}
divided by the determinant of
X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}
. Each entry in the cofactor matrix is given by the determinant of a
Q\times Q
sub-matrix of
X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}
. Thus, each entry is bounded by
C_{12}n^{Q}
, where
C_{12}>0
is a constant only depending on
Q
. Together with Lemma
4
, which says
\operatorname{det}\left(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}\right)\geq C_{9}n^{Q+1}
, we obtain

\left[(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}\right]_{i,j}\leq\frac{C_{12}n^{Q}}{C_{9}n^{Q+1}}=\frac{C_{12}}{C_{9}}n^{-1}\text{ for all }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,Q+1\}.

Consequently,

\left[(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)\right]_{i,j}\leq(Q+1)\frac{C_{12}}{C_{9}}n^{-1}n=(Q+1)\frac{C_{12}}{C_{9}},

for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,Q+1\}
. Thus,

\operatorname{tr}\left[(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)\right]\leq(Q+1)^{2}\frac{C_{12}}{C_{9}}\eqcolon C_{11}.

Note that
C_{11}>0
only depends on
Q
. This completes the proof if
X
is given by Setting
1
(
a
).

Let
X
now be given by Setting
1
(
b
). Then, we have that
0\leq X_{i,j}\leq Q\frac{D}{n}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n-D;\ j=1,\ldots,Q
. Consequently, there exists a constant
\tilde{C}_{1}>0
such that
(X^{\top}X)_{i,j}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}\frac{D^{2}}{n}
for all
i=1,\ldots,Q;\ j=1,\ldots,Q
.

Next, we focus on the entries in the inverse matrix
(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}
. Since
X_{U}
is a sub-matrix of
X
, we have once again that
(X_{U})_{i,j}\leq Q\frac{D}{n}
and
(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})_{i,j}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}\frac{D^{2}}{n}
. The inverse
(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}
is given by the cofactor matrix of
X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}
divided by the determinant of
X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}
. Each entry in the cofactor matrix is given by the determinant of a
(Q-1)\times(Q-1)
sub-matrix of
X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}
. Thus, each entry is bounded by
C_{12}\left(\frac{D^{2}}{n}\right)^{Q-1}
, where
C_{12}>0
is a constant only depending on
Q
. Together with Lemma
4
, which says
\operatorname{det}\left(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U}\right)\geq C_{9}\left(\frac{D^{2}}{n}\right)^{Q}
, we obtain

\left[(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}\right]_{i,j}\leq\frac{C_{12}\left(\frac{D^{2}}{n}\right)^{Q-1}}{C_{9}\left(\frac{D^{2}}{n}\right)^{Q}}=\frac{C_{12}}{C_{9}}\left(\frac{D^{2}}{n}\right)^{-1}\text{ for all }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,Q\}.

Consequently,

\left[(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)\right]_{i,j}\leq Q\frac{C_{12}}{C_{9}}\left(\frac{D^{2}}{n}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{D^{2}}{n}\right)=Q\frac{C_{12}}{C_{9}},

for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,Q\}
. Thus,

\operatorname{tr}\left[(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)\right]\leq Q^{2}\frac{C_{12}}{C_{9}}\eqcolon C_{11}.

Note that
C_{11}>0
only depends on
Q
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now bound the error
\hat{T}_{U}-T
of the trend estimate given by
T_{U}
in (
41
).

Lemma 9
.

Let
U\coloneq U_{B,Q,j}\subset\{1,\ldots,{m_{n}}\}
be a sample from the sampling procedure in Section
2.3.2
and suppose that
U\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
. Suppose that our data follows Setting
1
. Then there exists a constant
C_{8}>0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left((\hat{T}_{U}-T)^{\top}(\hat{T}_{U}-T)>C_{8}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)\right)\to 0.

(51)

Proof of Lemma
9
.

The least squares regression estimator is given by
\hat{T}_{U}=X(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}Y_{U}
.

Since
U\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
, we have that
Y_{U}=X_{U}\beta+W+\varepsilon_{U}
. Hence,
\hat{T}_{U}-T=X(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}(W+\varepsilon_{U})
. Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle(\hat{T}_{U}-T)^{\top}(\hat{T}_{U}-T)=

\displaystyle(W+\varepsilon_{U})^{\top}X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}(W+\varepsilon_{U})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2W^{\top}X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}W

\displaystyle+2\varepsilon_{U}^{\top}X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\varepsilon_{U}.

For the first term, we obtain

\displaystyle W^{\top}X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}W

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle W^{\top}W\operatorname{tr}\left[X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{6}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)C_{11},

where the final inequality follows from (
38
) and Lemma
8
.

We now focus on the second term. For any symmetric positive definite matrix
A
and any random variable
X
with
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
, it follows from
Kendrick 2002
that
\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\top}AX\right]=\operatorname{tr}\big(A\operatorname{Cov}[X]\big)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{U}^{\top}X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\varepsilon_{U}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\operatorname{Cov}\left[\varepsilon_{U}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{r_{Q}}\operatorname{colSum}\left(|\operatorname{Cov}\left[\varepsilon_{U}\right]|\right)\operatorname{tr}\left[X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4{r_{Q}}\sigma_{0}^{2}C_{11},

where
\operatorname{colSum}\left(|\operatorname{Cov}\left[\varepsilon_{U}\right]|\right)
is the column sum of the element-wise absolute values of the covariance matrix. The final inequality follows from Lemmas
1
and
8
.

Next, since
\varepsilon_{U}^{\top}X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\varepsilon_{U}\geq 0
, Markov’s inequality gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\varepsilon_{U}^{\top}X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\varepsilon_{U}>\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{U}^{\top}X_{U}(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}(X^{\top}X)(X_{U}^{\top}X_{U})^{-1}X_{U}^{\top}\varepsilon_{U}\right]}{\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)}\to 0.

It follows that (

.
∎

D.4.3
Uniform Bounds on the Noise

In this section we bound relevant noise terms.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose that our data follows Setting
1
. Then, there exists a constant
C_{13}<\infty
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq j\leq{m_{n}}}\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}\leq C_{13}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)\right)\to 1,

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

We first consider the Setting
1
(
a
). Note that
H_{i:j}
is a projection matrix on a
Q+1
dimensional column space. Hence,
H_{i:j}
is symmetric, idempotent, and has rank
\min\{Q+1,j-i+1\}
. Consequently,
(
Mathai and Provost 1992
, Theorem 5.1.1)
implies that

\frac{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}

is chi-squared distributed with
\min\{Q+1,j-i+1\}
degrees of freedom. Thus,
(
Laurent and Massart 2000
, Lemma 1)
yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}>\min\{Q+1,j-i+1\}+2\sqrt{\min\{Q+1,j-i+1\}t}+2t\right)\leq\exp\left(-t\right),

for any
t>0
. Let
t=C_{13}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)
for a constant
C_{13}>0
. If
n
is large enough, then

\displaystyle\min\{Q+1,j-i+1\}+2\sqrt{\min\{Q+1,j-i+1\}t}+2t\leq\left(\sqrt{Q+1}+\sqrt{2t}\right)^{2}\leq 3t

Thus, if
n
is large enough, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}>C_{13}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{C_{13}}{3}\log(n)\right)=n^{-\frac{C_{13}}{3}}.

If
C_{13}>6
, combining this bound with a union bound gives us

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}\leq C_{13}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{P}\left(\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}>C_{13}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}n^{-\frac{C_{13}}{3}}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
.

We now consider the Setting
1
(
b
). Lemma
1
implies that
\varepsilon_{D}=\varepsilon_{D+1:n}-\varepsilon_{1:{n-D}}
. Consequently,
(\varepsilon_{D})_{i:j}=\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}-\varepsilon_{i:j}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{D})_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}(\varepsilon_{D})_{i:j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}+\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}-2\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}+\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}+2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i+D:j+D}+2\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}.\varepsilon_{i:j}.

Following the same steps as in Setting
1
(
a
) completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
11
bounds the mixed term between noise and a polynomial fit.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\tilde{T}=\big(\tilde{t}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{t}_{{m_{n}}}\big)
be a polynomial of degree
{r_{Q}}
, then for any
1\leq i\leq j\leq{m_{n}}
, we have that

\left|\sum_{l=i}^{j}\varepsilon_{l}\tilde{t}_{l}\right|\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}\sqrt{\sum_{l=i}^{j}\tilde{t}_{l}^{2}}.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{T}
is a polynomial of degree
{r_{Q}}
, there exists a
\tilde{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{{r_{Q}}+1}
such that
\tilde{T}=X\tilde{\beta}
. Let
C^{2}\coloneq\tilde{\beta}^{\top}X_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\tilde{\beta}
and
\breve{\beta}=\frac{1}{C}\tilde{\beta}
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=i}^{j}\varepsilon_{l}\tilde{t}_{l}=\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\tilde{\beta}=C\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\breve{\beta}\leq C\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{{r_{Q}}+1}:\ \beta^{\top}X_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta=1}\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta.

It remains to solve the optimisation problem on the right. We use Lagrangian multipliers. We have to maximise

\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta+\lambda\left(\beta^{\top}X_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta-1\right).

We obtain

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(X_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\right)^{-1}X_{i:j}^{\top}\varepsilon_{i:j},

\displaystyle\beta^{\top}X_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta=1.

Hence,
\lambda=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=i}^{j}\varepsilon_{l}\tilde{t}_{l}\leq

\displaystyle C\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{{r_{Q}}+1}:\ \beta^{\top}X_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta=1}\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\frac{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}\sqrt{\sum_{l=i}^{j}\tilde{t}_{l}^{2}}.

Similarly, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{l=i}^{j}\varepsilon_{l}\tilde{t}_{l}\geq-\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{{r_{Q}}+1}:\ \beta^{\top}X_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta=1}\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}X_{i:j}\beta=-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}}\sqrt{\sum_{l=i}^{j}\tilde{t}_{l}^{2}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

D.4.4
Bounding the cost difference

To show that we select the right trend estimate using the cost of CAPA, we contrast in the following the costs of a good trend estimate with the one of a bad trend estimate. We require multiple lemmas to bound these costs.

We start by rewriting the anomaly model as a changepoint model. Let
\tau_{2k}\coloneq b_{k}
and
\tau_{2k+1}\coloneq e_{k}
, for
k=0,\ldots,K
. Furthermore, we define the function values
\theta_{2k-1}\coloneq\mu_{k}
, for
k=1,\ldots,K
, and
\theta_{2k}\coloneq 0
. We then define the jump sizes
\Delta_{2k-1}\coloneq|\mu_{k}|
and
\Delta_{2k}\coloneq|\mu_{k}|
for
k=1,\ldots,K
. We also use the notation
K_{\tau}\coloneq 2K
.

In several of the following lemmas we make use of the following inequalities. Let
R=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{{m_{n}}})
be a polynomial of degree
{r_{Q}}
for which

\sum_{l=1}^{{m_{n}}}r_{l}^{2}\leq C_{14}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)

(52)

holds. Suppose that

\max_{1\leq i\leq j\leq{m_{n}}}\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}\leq C_{13}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n).

(53)

Lemma
12
bounds the reduction in costs by having an estimated polynomial trend instead of the true parameter.

Lemma 12
.

Suppose that our data follows Setting
1
. Suppose that (
52
) and (
53
) hold. Then, there exists a constant
C_{15}>0
such that for any
k\in\{0,\ldots,{K_{\tau}}\}
and any
i,j
such that
\tau_{k}<i\leq j\leq\tau_{k+1}

C_{i,j}(\varepsilon+R+W)-C_{i,j}(Y-\hat{\theta}_{i:j})\leq C_{15}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n).

Proof.

Note that
y_{l}=\theta_{k}+t_{l}+w_{l}+\varepsilon_{l},\ l=i,\ldots,j
. We further remark that
\theta_{k}+T
is a polynomial of degree
{r_{Q}}
, so
\hat{\theta}_{i:j}=\theta_{k}+T_{i:j}+H_{i:j}W_{i:j}+H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}
. For the last equality before the inequalities note that
H_{i:j}
is a projection matrix, i.e.
H_{i:j}^{\top}=H_{i:j}
,
H_{i:j}^{2}=H_{i:j}
. We obtain the first inequality from Lemma
11
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and
x^{\top}H_{i:j}x\leq x^{\top}x
for any vector
x
since the largest eigenvalue of
H_{i:j}
is
1
as
H_{i:j}
is a projection matrix. The second inequality follows from
\sum_{l=i}^{j}r_{l}^{2}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{{m_{n}}}r_{l}^{2}
and
\sum_{l=i}^{j}w_{l}^{2}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{{m_{n}}}w_{l}^{2}
. The final inequality is a direct consequence of (
52
), (
53
),
and (
38
). In total, we obtain

\displaystyle C_{i,j}(\varepsilon+R+W)-C_{i,j}(Y-\hat{\theta}_{i:j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=i}^{j}\Big(\varepsilon_{l}+r_{l}+w_{l})^{2}-\sum_{l=i}^{j}(\varepsilon_{l}+w_{l}-\big(H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}\big)_{l}-\big(H_{i:j}W_{i:j}\big)_{l}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{l=i}^{j}\varepsilon_{l}r_{l}+2\sum_{l=i}^{j}w_{l}r_{l}+\sum_{l=i}^{j}r_{l}^{2}+\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}\varepsilon_{i:j}+2\varepsilon_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}W_{i:j}+W_{i:j}^{\top}H_{i:j}W_{i:j}

and
\theta_{1}

\kappa_{0}
and
\kappa_{1}

Without loss of generality we assume that
\theta_{0}=0
and
\theta_{1}>0
, as subtracting a constant or multiplying everything inside the squares with
-1
do not change the result. Furthermore, without loss of generality assume that
\kappa_{0}
and
\kappa_{1}
are as given in the lemma. Otherwise consider the observations in reverse order which is not changing the costs.

We now distinguish the cases
\kappa_{1}<4^{Q+1}
and
\kappa_{1}\geq 4^{Q+1}
. If
\kappa_{1}<4^{Q+1}
, then we distinguish the cases whether

f\left(\frac{\tau+1}{n}\right)-\theta_{0}\geq\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}{2}

(54)

holds or not. If it does not hold, then

\displaystyle\sum_{l=\tau+1}^{\tau+\kappa_{1}}\left(f\left(\frac{l}{n}\right)-\theta_{1}\right)^{2}\geq\left(f\left(\frac{\tau+1}{n}\right)-\theta_{1}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})^{2}.

(55)

So the statement holds with
C_{17}=4^{-Q-1}

If
\kappa_{1}\geq 4^{Q+1}
, then we distinguish the cases whether

f\left(\frac{\tau+0.5}{n}\right)-\theta_{0}\geq\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}{2}

(56)

\tau

The remaining proof uses the following idea. We consider a large number of small boxes around the step function. If
f
is fully outside of any of these boxes, then the statement holds with

resembling the size of the box. If
f
is inside all boxes for at least one time point, then by using repeatedly the mean value theorem we will show that the
(Q+1)
-th derivative of

cannot be a polynomial of degree

We start by constructing the boxes. For any
\iota=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\{0,1\}^{m}

I_{\emptyset}\coloneq(\tau-\kappa_{0},\tau]

\kappa_{0}4^{-m}

I_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-1})}

,
I_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-1},1)}
, and the interval in between, which is of length
2\kappa_{0}4^{-m}
.

Let

b\coloneq\sum_{l=2}^{Q+2}2^{l}.

If there exists an
\iota\in\{0,1\}^{Q}
such that for all
x\in I_{\iota}
we have that

f\not\in\left[\theta_{0}-\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}{b},\theta_{0}+\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}{b}\right],

\sum_{l=\lceil L_{\iota}\rceil}^{\lfloor R_{\iota}\rfloor}\left(f\left(\frac{l}{n}\right)-\theta_{0}\right)^{2}\geq\big(\lfloor R_{\iota}\rfloor-\lceil L_{\iota}\rceil\big)\frac{(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})^{2}}{b^{2}}=C_{17}\kappa_{0}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})^{2},

being a constant only depending on
Q
, since
\lfloor R_{\iota}\rfloor-\lceil L_{\iota}\rceil\geq\frac{\kappa_{0}}{4^{Q}}-1
and

contains at least one observation.

for which

Furthermore, for every
m\in\{Q,\ldots,-1\}

\mathbf{1}_{m}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\{0,1\}^{m}

) or (
56
) holds, for which

-th derivative is non-zero and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l}-\kappa_{0}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}+a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l}+\kappa_{1}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}+a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l}-\kappa_{0}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l}+\kappa_{0}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}-\hat{\mu})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C_{17}\kappa_{1}\hat{\mu}^{2}=C_{17}\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}}(a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}>C_{17}\tilde{C}_{6}\gamma_{n}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n).

This is a contradiction to (
63
).

3.

Suppose
[\tilde{\tau}_{l}+1,\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}]
is anomalous and to be estimated anomalous, i.e. there exists a
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
such that
\tilde{\tau}_{l}=\max\{b_{k},\hat{b}_{k}\}
and
\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}=\min\{e_{k},\hat{e}_{k}\}
.

Let
\kappa_{0}=\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}-\tilde{\tau}_{l}
and
\kappa_{1}=\tilde{\tau}_{l+2}-\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}
. Because of the definition of
\kappa_{k}
and Assumption
5
we have that
\kappa_{0}>\max\{\kappa_{1},4^{Q+1}\}
for
n
large enough. If
\kappa_{1}=0
, then consider the next interval and we may have to switch the roles of
\kappa_{0}
and
\kappa_{1}
. If there exists a sequence
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\to\infty
such that
\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+2}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i})^{2}>\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)

\tilde{C}_{7}>0
such that
\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+2}}(a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}>\tilde{C}_{7}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)

l-1
and case 2. leads to a contradiction. Otherwise, there exists a constant
\tilde{C}_{8}
such that

\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+2}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i})^{2}\leq\tilde{C}_{8}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n).

Then, from the same arguments as before, there exists constants
\tilde{C}_{9},\tilde{C}_{10}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}+a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+2}+\kappa}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}+a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}+a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+2}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+2}}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=\tilde{\tau}_{l+1}+1}^{\tilde{\tau}_{l+2}+\kappa}(t_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}+a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}

This is a contradiction to (

In summary all cases lead to a contradiction. This completes the proof.

We have two technical lemmas on how to bound the density and cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian random variable.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\phi
be the density of a standard Gaussian random variable. If
x\leq 1
, then

\phi(x)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(1-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right).

Proof.

We obtain the Taylor series

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{1}{k!}2^{-k}x^{2k}.

The statement follows from the fact that the terms have alternating signs and decay in absolute values, since
x\leq 1
.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
\Phi
be the cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian random variable. If
x\leq 1
, then

\Phi(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{5}{6\sqrt{2\pi}}x.

Proof.

Taylor’s theorem gives us

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi}}-\frac{\tilde{x}^{3}}{6\sqrt{2\pi}},

for an
\tilde{x}\in[0,x]
. The statement follows from
\frac{\tilde{x}^{3}}{6\sqrt{2\pi}}\leq\frac{x^{3}}{6\sqrt{2\pi}}\leq\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2\pi}}x
, since
\tilde{x}\leq x\leq 1
.
∎

The following lemma bounds
\tilde{q}
.

Lemma 23
.

Under the setting of Proposition
4
, if
n
is large enough, then there exists a constant
0<C_{22}<0.752
and a constant

such that

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{q}>C_{22}\,\sigma_{0})\leq C_{23}\frac{1}{n}.

Proof.

Let
n_{0}
be the size of
V_{0}

\sum_{j\in V_{0}}\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}\geq C_{22}\,\sigma_{0}\}}>\frac{1}{2}n_{0}.

We obtain from Lemma
20
and

that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j\in V_{0}}\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}\geq C_{22}\,\sigma_{0}\}}>\frac{1}{2}n_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{5}{6}\frac{n}{P}-C_{21}}\mathbb{1}_{\{\varepsilon_{j}\geq C_{22}\,\sigma_{0}\}}>\frac{1}{2}\frac{n}{P}-1-\frac{1}{6}\frac{n}{P}-C_{21}\right).

The random variable on the left hand side in the probability is Binomial distributed with size
\frac{5}{6}\frac{n}{P}-C_{21}
and success probability
\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{1}\geq C_{22}\,\sigma_{0})
. We use the following bound from
(
Feller and Morse 1958

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq r)\leq\frac{r(1-p)}{(r-np)^{2}},

if

n

p
. It follows from Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{1}\geq C_{22}\,\sigma_{0})=1-\Phi\left(C_{22}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{5}{6}\frac{C_{22}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

The following lemma bounds the probability that the noise is between
\tilde{q}

Lemma 24
.

Under the setting of Proposition
4
, if
n
is large enough, then there exists a constant

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{q}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\leq\tilde{q}+\gamma)\geq C_{24}\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}.

Proof.

Let

. Then, if
n
is large enough, there exists a constant

such that

\frac{q+\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}.

(69)

In the first inequality we lower bound the integral by minimal value times the length of the interval of integration. We use Lemma
21
for the second and (
69
) for the third inequality. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(q\leq\varepsilon_{1}\leq q+\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{q}{\sigma_{0}}}^{\frac{q+\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}}\phi(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\phi\left(\frac{q+\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}\right)\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{q+\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}\right)^{2}\right]\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{C}_{1}^{2}\right)\right]\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}.

It follows from Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{q}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\leq\tilde{q}+\gamma)\geq

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{C}_{1}^{2}\right)\right]\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}-C_{23}\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}\geq C_{24}\frac{\gamma}{\sigma},

With these lemmas we are finally able to prove Lemma

Proof of Lemma
19
.

For any

, let
n_{p}
be the number of observations in

. As

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma_{L,p}\leq\tilde{\vartheta}_{p}\leq\gamma_{U,p}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j\in V_{p}}\mathbb{1}_{Y_{j}>\gamma_{U,p}}\geq\frac{1}{2}n_{p}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j\in V_{p}}\mathbb{1}_{Y_{j}<\gamma_{L,p}}\geq\frac{1}{2}n_{p}\right).

We focus on the first probability, but bounding the second probability follows similar arguments. The following relies on the fact that
V_{0}
and
V_{p}
are similar for most observations. For
p\neq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in V_{p}}\mathbb{1}_{\{Y_{j}>\gamma_{U,p}\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\tilde{n}_{p}-1}\mathbb{1}_{\{Y_{p+jP}>\gamma_{U,p},\ p+jP\in V_{p}\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\tilde{n}_{p}-1}\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{p+jP}+\varepsilon_{p+jP}>\tilde{q}+\gamma,\ p+jP\in V_{p}\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\tilde{n}_{0}-1}\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{(j+1)P}+\varepsilon_{p+jP}>\tilde{q}+\gamma,\ (j+1)P\in V_{0}\}}+\sum_{j=0}^{\tilde{n}_{p}-1}\mathbb{1}_{a_{p+jP}\neq a_{(j+1)P}}+\sum_{j=0}^{\tilde{n}_{0}-1}\mathbb{1}_{p+jP\in V_{p},\ (j+1)P\not\in V_{0}}

The last inequality holds, since there are at most

many

for which either

or

, but

, since this can only happen at the beginning or end of a collective anomaly or an estimated collective anomaly or for the last observation in

. The last line has the same distribution as

\sum_{j\in V_{0}}\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}>\tilde{q}+\gamma\}}+4K+1.

For similar reasons we have that
n_{p}\geq n_{0}-2K
. Hence, there exists a constant
\tilde{C}_{1}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j\in V_{p}}\mathbb{1}_{Y_{j}>\gamma_{U,p}}\geq\frac{1}{2}n_{p}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j\in V_{0}}\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}>\tilde{q}+\gamma\}}\geq\frac{1}{2}n_{0}-\tilde{C}_{1}\right).

If
p=0

\sum_{j\in V_{0}}\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}\geq\tilde{q}\}}=\frac{1}{2}n_{0}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j\in V_{0}}\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}>\tilde{q}+\gamma\}}\geq\frac{1}{2}n_{0}-\tilde{C}_{1}\right)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j\in V_{0}}\mathbb{1}_{\{\tilde{q}\leq a_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}\leq\tilde{q}+\gamma\}}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{5}{6}\frac{n}{P}-C_{21}}\mathbb{1}_{\{\tilde{q}\leq\varepsilon_{j}\leq\tilde{q}+\gamma\}}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}\right),

where the final inequality follows from Lemma

.

The random variable on the left hand side in the probability is Binomial distributed with size
\frac{5}{6}\frac{n}{P}-C_{21}
and success probability
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{q}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\leq\tilde{q}+\gamma)
. We use the following bound from
(
Feller and Morse 1958
, (3.6))
,

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\leq r)\leq\frac{(n-r)p}{(np-r)^{2}},

if
r\leq np
, where

is Binomial distributed with size

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{5}{6}\frac{n}{P}-C_{21}}\mathbb{1}_{\{\tilde{q}\leq\varepsilon_{j}\leq\tilde{q}+\gamma\}}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}\right)\leq\frac{\frac{n}{P}}{\big((\frac{5}{6}\frac{n}{P}-C_{21})C_{24}\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}-\tilde{C}_{1}\big)^{2}}\to 0,

\displaystyle-2\left(\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{\tilde{K}}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}+1:\tilde{\tau}_{k+1}}^{\top}H_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}+1:\tilde{\tau}_{k+1}}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}+1:\tilde{\tau}_{k+1}}}+\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{n}}}r_{i}^{2}}+\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{n}}}w_{i}^{2}}\right)\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{n}}}(a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{n}}}(\varepsilon_{i}+r_{i}+w_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{n}}}(a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle-2\left(\sqrt{4KC_{13}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)}+\sqrt{C_{19}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)}+\sqrt{C_{6}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)}\right)\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{n}}}(a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}}.

This and

for any

yield

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{n}}}(a_{i}-\hat{a}_{i})^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 4\left(\sqrt{4KC_{13}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)}+\sqrt{C_{19}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)}+\sqrt{C_{6}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{25}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log(n),

with

. This completes the proof.
