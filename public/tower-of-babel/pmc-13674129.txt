Identification of predictive factors for reversal of cerebral vasculopathy in an original longitudinal cohort study in newborns with sickle cell anaemia
Authors: Julie Sommet, Priscilla Boizeau, Zinedine Haouari, Berengère Koehl, Monique Elmaleh, Emmanuelle Lesprit, Florence Missud, Ghislaine Ithier, Laurent Holvoet, Valentine Brousse, Christophe Delclaux, André Denjean, Suzanne Verlhac, Corinne Alberti, André Baruchel, Nathalie Couque, Malika Benkerrou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCerebral macrovasculopathy (CV) is a major complication in children with sickle cell anaemia (SCA) and usually requires a long‐term transfusion programme (TP) to prevent stroke. This study aimed to identify factors predicting reversal of CV on TP in a single‐centre newborn cohort. Among 375 patients, 50 presented CV and received TP. Median age at diagnosis was 4.9 years [3.6–6.1], with median follow‐up of 13.3 years (915 patient‐years). Transcranial Doppler (TCD) velocities and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) normalised in 35 patients, that is, they had reversal of CV (RCV group). Fifteen patients had no reversal of CV (No‐RCV group). Kaplan–Meier analysis estimated that 48% (95% confidence interval [CI] [33–62]) still had abnormal TCD and/or MRA 5 years after diagnosis. In a multivariate Cox model, three baseline factors independently predicted CV reversal: age at CV onset, initial severity of stenosis and bronchial obstruction. Older age at diagnosis increased the likelihood of CV reversal (hazard ratio [HR] 1.31 per year, 95% CI [1.08–1.59], p = 0.01). In contrast, severe stenosis (HR 0.72 per 10% increase, 95% CI [0.62–0.85], p < 0.0001) and bronchial obstruction (HR 0.14, 95% CI [0.03–0.63], p = 0.001) reduced the likelihood of CV reversal. These results suggest that, beyond TP, managing bronchial obstruction may improve vascular outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral sickle cell vasculopathy is a very severe and frequent complication in patients with sickle cell anaemia (SCA), leading to ischaemic stroke. Cerebral macrovasculopathy (CV) in SCA patients can be detected by transcranial Doppler (TCD). 1 Abnormally high blood‐flow velocities on TCD are predictive of a high risk of primary ischaemic stroke. TCD is now used for routine yearly screening in children with sickle cell anaemia (SS) or sickle cell beta‐0 thalassaemia (Sβ0) between 2 and 16 years of age. Since 2010, very early TCD between 12 and 18 months has been recommended to identify patients at high risk of stroke. 2 , 3 , 4 In 1998, the Stroke Prevention (STOP) trial demonstrated that monthly blood transfusions could reduce the risk of ischaemic stroke by 90% compared to standard care in SCA patients with abnormal TCD velocities. 5

Confirmed abnormal velocities on TCD require a transfusion programme (TP) to prevent stroke, along with systematic annual magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) in high‐income countries. Very early treatment intensification with early implementation of TCD screening could reduce the risk of stroke to as low as 1.9%. 3 Research is being conducted to identify predictive factors for safe discontinuation of TP.

A randomised study in patients on TP for abnormal TCD velocities with no severe CV (one stenosis ≤50%, no clinical stroke, no transient ischaemic attack) showed, in a non‐inferiority analysis, no difference between patients who remained on TP and those progressively switched to hydroxyurea (HU; hydroxycarbamide). 6 All patients had at least 1 year of TP (mean 3.8 years and 4.5 years respectively). Mean follow‐up was 1 year.

A single‐centre French study showed that recurrence of abnormal TCD velocities on HU was predicted by a high baseline reticulocyte count >400 × 109/L during TP. 7 Abnormal TCD velocities and the presence of mild to severe stenosis were risk factors for persistence or relapse of CV. In the multivariate analysis, only mild to severe MRA stenosis was significantly associated with persistence (p = 0.001).

We aimed to identify factors predictive of CV reversal in a newborn cohort at the Robert‐Debre University Hospital, National Paediatric Sickle Cell Disease Reference Centre (tertiary centre). Identification of such factors could lead to greater awareness and eventually early tailored therapy. This work followed our previous original study on 375 newborns born between 1991 and 2010, combining prospectively collected longitudinal laboratory and clinical data, including upper and lower respiratory tract abnormalities. 4

The very long follow‐up of our cohort in a single‐centre longitudinal clinical, laboratory and radiological study allowed us to investigate factors associated with CV reversal in a real‐world setting.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patients

The present study analysed 50 SS patients with identified high risk of stroke (CV) whose clinical, laboratory and radiological data were prospectively entered into our database (Base Syndrome Drépanocytaire Majeur [SDM]/Epiconcept‐Voozanoo, version 2.0, Epiconcept™) as part of standard patient management, as previously described. 4 The database was approved by the French National Committee for Computerised Databases (no. 1299665) and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Groupe Hospitalo‐Universitaire Nord (IRB# 00006477). Written informed consent was obtained for all genetic studies.

SS and Sβ0 patients were systematically assessed by TCD performed by an expert operator, annually from the age 12 months. Based on the STOP classification, 5 , 8 the highest time‐averaged maximum mean velocities (TAMMV) (cm/s) recorded on the middle cerebral, internal carotid and anterior cerebral arteries were used to classify TCD. Patients with a conditional or abnormal TCD were then evaluated within 2–3 months for confirmation.

The latest update was obtained on 31 January 2021.

A high risk of stroke (CV diagnosis) was defined as follows, based on TAMMV on TCD using ultrasound colour imaging: TAMMV ≥200 cm/s on two occurrences 2 months apart,

Nine patients from our previous study were excluded because their condition precluded CV reversal on TP: one with haemorrhagic stroke and no CV, five with overt ischaemic stroke prior to any abnormal TCD in the previous year, three on HU due to parents' refusal of TP or delayed haemolytic reaction at first transfusion for CV.

Patient management

Non‐contrasted cerebral magnetic resonance imaging, including a 3D time of flight angiography (MRI/MRA), was performed for abnormal, high conditional or inadequate TCD. An additional reading of MRI/MRAs by two expert neuroradiologists was performed systematically and then annually. Stenosis was graded according to accepted nomenclature. 10 All patients were transfused prior to MRA, and sequences were adapted to high blood velocities in order to avoid flow artefacts.

At diagnosis of CV, all patients were put on TP, either manual or erythrocytapheresis, within 3 weeks after diagnosis in order to maintain pre‐transfusion HbS below 30%.

Silent cerebral infarct was defined as an MRA signal abnormality at least 3 mm in one dimension and visible in two planes on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery in T2‐weighted images, with a normal neurological examination. 11

HU was provided at the initial dose of 15 mg/kg/day after TCD returned to normal, which was checked every 3 months for 1 year and, if abnormal, TP was restarted. The dose was adapted to the neutrophil count for patients receiving TP, targeting neutrophils <5 × 109/L and to an increase in mean corpuscular volume (MCV) >10 fL from baseline for patients on HU alone. HU dose escalation was performed in steps of 2.5 mg/kg/day every 2 months to achieve these criteria.

Data collection and clinical outcomes

Prior to CV onset and during follow‐up, longitudinal baseline laboratory parameters were recorded yearly, that is, leucocytes, neutrophils, steady‐state haemoglobin (Hb), fetal haemoglobin percentage (HbF), MCV, reticulocytes and plasma ferritin, as well as radiological findings including silent infarcts and cervical carotid artery velocities.

Upper airway and bronchial obstruction were also investigated prior to diagnosis and during follow‐up, as previously described, 4 , 12 as were specific therapies. Bronchial obstruction was defined based on internationally accepted standards and using both clinical findings and, when possible, lung function testing, adjusted for age, gender, height and corrected for those of African origin. Results were expressed as percentages of predicted values. 12

Statistical analysis

The results of the descriptive analysis were expressed as numbers and percentages for qualitative variables, as medians with minimum, maximum and quartiles (Q1, Q3) for quantitative variables.

Time to CV reversal was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of normalisation of TCD and MRA. On 31 January 2021, two groups of patients were identified, one with CV reversal (RCV) and the other with no CV reversal (No‐RCV) with abnormalities on either TCD or MRA at their latest follow‐up.

For between‐group comparisons, Student t‐test and Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney tests were used for continuous variables according to a Gaussian distribution and chi‐squared test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to describe the probability of RCV.

Factors for RCV were assessed by a Cox survival model. A 20% level in bivariate analyses was used to select variables entered into the multivariable model. A stepwise selection process was used. The results were presented as the hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (95% confidence interval [CI]).

Statistical analysis was performed with SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA). All tests were bilateral, and p‐values of 0.05 or less denoted statistical significance.

RCV group

In 35 patients, TCD and MRA normalised at a median of 3.0 years after diagnosis (1.5; 5.8). HU was therefore introduced for 34 patients after a median period of 3.3 years of TP. Median overlap period with the two treatments was 7.8 months. One patient declined HU and remained normal at the latest update. Among the 34 patients, one relapsed on HU. He was treated again with TP and subsequently normalised with ongoing HU.

No‐RCV group

CV persisted in 15 patients at least until the end of TP.

Two patients (13%) had progressive vasculopathy leading to occlusion and consecutive stroke despite well‐conducted TP. One with abnormal TCD moved to sub‐Saharan Africa on HU for 9 years and returned with a stroke.

The seven patients who underwent geno‐identical sibling HSCT had successful engraftment and are currently alive.

At the latest update, eight patients were still on TP with CV and no HLA‐identical siblings. For seven of them, HU had been added after a median of 4 years on TP to potentially slow ongoing cerebral occlusion.

Therapy

During TP, in the No‐RCV group, median pre‐transfusional haemoglobin was 9 g/dL (min: 7.4, max: 10.0) and median HbS percentage was 32% (min: 20, max: 34). In the RCV group, median haemoglobin was 9.1 g/dL (min: 8.2, max: 9.5) and median Hb S was 37% (min: 28, max: 40).

No patients had iron overload (Data S1).

At the latest update, 34/50 patients (68%) were on HU alone at a median dose of 20 mg/kg/day (min: 18, max: 28) with median MCV at 97 fL (min: 83, max: 106), which is higher than the initial value at CV diagnosis.

No significant differences were observed for demographic data.

No differences were found between groups in upper airway obstruction or in pulse oximetry values (median 98%). Bronchial obstruction was present in both groups, 94% of the 50 patients having such a diagnosis. No significant difference was observed in the median age of onset of bronchial obstruction (p = 0.72); however, there was a trend towards a higher rate of treatment for the condition in the RCV group (p = 0.09).

Concerning laboratory data, median reticulocyte counts at diagnosis were significantly lower in the RCV group (p = 0.04).

Significant differences in radiological findings were also identified. Indeed, median TCD velocities were statistically higher in the No‐RCV group (215 cm/s) than in the RCV group (201 cm/s) (p = 0.002). Moreover, severity of stenosis was significantly higher in the No‐RCV group (71%) than in the RCV group (16%) (p = 0.0002). No statistical difference was observed in the median number of abnormal vessels as assessed by TCD or MRA. Only one ischaemic stroke, which occurred during acute splenic sequestration, was observed in the RCV group compared with 20% of patients in the No‐RCV group (3/15). No difference between the two groups was noted in the frequency of silent infarcts at diagnosis or the latest update.

TCD follow‐up was similar in the two groups. No abnormal velocities on cervical internal carotid arteries were observed.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study with long‐term analysis of predictive factors for CV reversal as well as its kinetics in a cohort of previously described SCA neonates. 4

Outcomes

In our cohort, with median follow‐up of 13.3 years, TCD and MRA normalised in 35 patients (70%) on TP at a median of 3 years after diagnosis. A similar rate, that is, 66%, was observed in the follow‐up to the STOP‐2 study 13 and in another newborn cohort, 7 that is, 64% at a median of 2.5 years after CV diagnosis. This is surprising because the enrolment criteria in the two studies differed. In the follow‐up to the STOP‐2 study, enrolment came from different cohorts with an older median age at CV diagnosis, that is, 8.5 years. 13 Inclusion in Bernaudin's newborn cohort was decided by the first abnormal TCD, which could potentially occur during usual intercurrent events. 7

We confirmed that, after normalisation of TCD and MRA, switching to HU was safe with only one relapse, which was corrected after short‐term TP and remained normal on HU at the latest update. 6 Long‐term follow‐up of RCV patients showed the durability of normalisation on relatively low doses of HU despite more severe vasculopathy as compared to the inclusion criteria in the TCD With Transfusions Changing to Hydroxyurea (TWiTCH) study. Indeed, in the TWiTCH study only one moderate stenosis was permitted, 6 Moreover, as previously described, 14 our TP method with partial‐exchange transfusion drastically reduced the probability of iron overload.

It is interesting to note that CV reversed in 50% of patients at a median of 3 years after TP initiation whereas, at a median of 10 years, CV reversed in only 25% more patients. In order to address the fact that CV reversal slows after 3 years of TP and is almost absent after 10 years, specific trials are needed, investigating alternatives such as HSCT, as well as other innovative therapies.

Our excellent results in RCV patients may be related to the end of the high‐risk period. Indeed, early childhood, between 2 and 5 years, is the period with the highest conversion rate to abnormal TCD as well as the highest incidence of stroke. 15 , 16 , 17 Moreover, all RCV patients were switched to HU, which is known to lower TCD velocities by a median of 30 cm/s. 18 To sustain HU adherence, specific educational programmes were initiated, including e‐programmes 19 , 20 taking into account cultural specificities. Indeed, adherence to long‐term oral medication requires support and is key for efficacy.

Stenosis

Rates of stenosis are difficult to compare between studies. Indeed, in our newborn cohort, 78% of patients had moderate to severe intracranial stenosis. In patients who underwent MRA in the STOP‐1 study, only 25% had stenosis. 21 However, as in the STOP‐2 study, median age was older and therefore excluded younger patients who had prior stroke during the high‐risk period. In Bernaudin's study, 7 20% of patients were found with mild to severe intracranial stenosis following the first abnormal TCD. Our inclusion criteria were consistent with STOP criteria, using the recommended practice of TCD verification when abnormal before starting TP. In these young patients, many with significant socio‐economic difficulties, families sometimes had difficulty keeping medical appointments, 22 potentially leading to late identification of CV, which increases the risk of conversion to abnormal TCD and/or stroke.

Predictive factors for CV reversal

The study found that TCD velocities, severity of stenosis, bronchial obstruction and baseline reticulocyte count were significantly different between the RCV and No‐RCV groups, being higher and/or more frequent in the No‐RCV group. Contrary to earlier reports, 11 we did not observe any differences in microvascular disease (i.e. number of silent cerebral infarcts) between groups or at the end of follow‐up. This could be due to the protective effects of TP and, in RCV patients, to implementation of HU.

Among the three major independent factors found to be significantly associated with CV reversal in the Cox multivariate analysis, that is, age, bronchial obstruction and severity of stenosis, only severity of stenosis has previously been described as an independent risk factor. 7 Indeed, this was the first study where young age and bronchial obstruction were identified as independent risk factors for CV persistence. Young age is known to be the highest risk period for developing CV 15 , 16 and, in our study, was identified as an independent risk factor for CV persistence despite TP. Bronchial obstruction has also been identified as a risk factor for developing stroke and CV. 4 , 16 Indeed, patients with SCA are prone to an asthma‐like condition due to inflammation and haemolysis, as well as greater sensitivity to atopic and atmospheric pollutants with bronchial inflammation. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 This is associated with acute pain crisis, acute chest syndrome and more frequent visits to emergency room. TP alone is not sufficient to reduce inflammation in SCA, 23 , 27 and therefore inflammation of bronchial smooth muscle and epithelium potentially persists during TP. Upper airway obstruction was not found to be a risk factor for CV persistence, possibly because treatment was primarily surgical with no need for long‐term treatment, unlike bronchial obstruction.

HU is known to lower velocities by a median 30 cm/s, 18 , 28 , 29 possibly by reducing haemolysis and inflammation in SCA patients. 27 Indeed, it has been reported that replacing sickle red blood cells with healthy red blood cells is not sufficient to treat SCA‐associated inflammation. Significant reduction of inflammatory parameters is observed in children on TP for CV with at least 6 months of HU as seen in patients treated with HU without CV. 27 , 30 To date, in several studies, HU has occasionally been added to control progressive vasculopathy despite well‐conducted TP. 27 , 31 In our study, 7 of the 8 NO‐RCV patients received HU at 20 mg/kg/day after a median of 4 years of TP, and was still ongoing.

National guidelines recommending initiation of HU for children at their first conditional TCD with close follow‐up have recently been published. 22 It is also recommended for children whose Hb level is below 80 g/L, 2 which was the case for almost all patients with CV in this cohort. Given these recent recommendations, all of the patients in our cohort should have initiated HU much earlier, prior to CV onset. Barriers for HU prescription and adherence to therapy have been identified for caregivers, as well as for both patients and their families. 32 , 33 , 34 Several studies have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of HU on organ damage, with only transient toxicity even for male fertility. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38

Our study has certain strengths including very long follow‐up and homogenous treatment guidelines, as well as some limitations due to the small sample size. As a result, baseline reticulocyte count, for example, was significant for the No‐RCV group (p = 0.03) in the bivariate analysis but not in the Cox multivariate analysis. Furthermore, laboratory data could not be modelled due to the small sample size in the two groups. Lastly, cumulative incidence of clinical events prior to CV was not included in the analysis conducted to identify predictive factors of CV reversibility.

In conclusion, to our knowledge, this is the first study reporting age and bronchial obstruction as new predictive factors for RCV. Our results highlight bronchial obstruction as a previously unrecognised predictive factor for CV reversal, as well as the potential benefit of early initiation of HU. Therefore, asthma‐like conditions should be routinely explored and specific therapy for bronchial obstruction, also a risk factor for onset of CV, 4 is recommended when appropriate. Early initiation of HU should be proposed to prevent organ damage such as CV with the need for specific support adapted to the sociocultural context and known medical barriers. These factors open new therapeutic perspectives with potential to lower the incidence of CV as well as to reduce the length of (TPs) and may lead to a decrease in the number of patients with uncorrected CV. We believe our data will be very useful in lowering the burden of CV in SCA patients. Larger prospective studies may be warranted to corroborate this therapeutic approach.
