Molecular mechanisms for subtype selectivity of kinin receptors’ antagonists
Authors: Meng Xia, Aleksey Raskovalov, My V. T. Nguyen, Shiliu Mei, Donggyun Kim, Jayden Salve, Vsevolod Katritch, Vadim Cherezov, Haitao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The kinin receptors B1R and B2R act as key regulators within the kallikrein–kinin system, mediating vasodilation, inflammation, and pain. Antagonists targeting these receptors have shown therapeutic potential in the treatment of angioedema, pancreatitis, and asthma. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying subtype selectivity of kinin receptors’ antagonists remain unclear. Here we report three cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of B1R in complex with the peptide antagonist R715 and small-molecule antagonist ELN441958, as well as B2R in complex with small-molecule antagonist Win64338. Together with functional assays and molecular docking, these structures unveil the molecular basis of antagonist binding modes in kinin receptors. Three non-conserved residues at the bottom of the ligand binding pocket are identified to confer the antagonist selectivity to B1R and B2R. Furthermore, our results reveal a shared mechanism for antagonism in both B1R and B2R, wherein the antagonist binding restricts the conformational changes of the toggle switch tryptophan residue. Collectively, these insights lay the foundation for the structure-based design of selective antagonists targeting B1R and B2R.

Introduction

Kinins, a group of peptides derived from kininogens, are essential components of the kallikrein–kinin system (KKS), which regulates various physiological functions, including vasodilation, pain control, proliferation, and inflammation1–4. Generally, kinins refer to the nonapeptide bradykinin, the decapeptide kallidin (Lys-bradykinin), and their carboxy-terminal des-Arg metabolites5. The actions of these peptides are mediated by two G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), known as the bradykinin type 1 receptor (B1R) and the bradykinin type 2 receptor (B2R)6.

The human B1 and B2 receptors share 32% sequence identity, and belong to the same branch of class A GPCRs, but show substantial differences in expression levels and ligand selectivity7. B2R is ubiquitously expressed in most tissues, whereas B1R expression is generally low but could be upregulated in response to inflammation, infection, or pain8. The endogenous bradykinin (BK) and kallidin (KD) peptides exhibit higher affinity to B2R, while the peptides lacking C-terminal arginine, des-Arg9-bradykinin (des-Arg9-BK) and des-Arg10-kallidin (des-Arg10-KD), bind preferably to B1R9,10. Binding of endogenous kinin peptides to B1R triggers prolonged activation, regulating inflammatory responses, strongly associated with the pathogenesis of chronic inflammatory disorders such as neuropathic pain and arthritis. Given their critical role in regulating blood pressure, mediating pain, and modulating inflammation, B1R and B2R have emerged as therapeutic targets for treating hypertension, asthma, diabetes, and cancer11–13. The search for kinin receptor antagonists has drawn substantial interest over the years. Since the development of the first competitive antagonist for kinins, [Leu8]des-Arg9-bradykinin, which specifically binds to B1R, in 1977, considerable efforts have been dedicated to creating compounds with improved efficacy and bioavailability14–16. Despite extensive research, only one drug targeting kinin receptors, icatibant17, a B2R peptide antagonist for the treatment of hereditary angioedema, has been approved by the FDA. No approved antagonists specifically targeting B1R are available18,19, and although several small molecule antagonists for both B1R and B2R have shown promise in preclinical studies20, none of them have been approved yet by the FDA.

Although recent cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of B1R and B2R in complex with G proteins have provided insights into their specific activation mechanisms and ligand selectivity for endogenous agonists21,22, the structural basis of kinin receptors antagonism remains poorly understood. This knowledge gap limits the design of selective antagonists and hinders the progression of structure-based drug development for kinin receptors.

In this study, we present three cryo-EM structures, including human B1R in complex with its selective antagonists ELN44195823 and R71524, and human B2R in complex with its selective antagonist Win6433825. In combination with molecular docking and functional assays, these structures provide molecular insights into ligand recognition and receptor antagonism, paving the way for further development of subtype-selective antagonists for kinin receptors.

Results

Overall structures of antagonist-bound B1R and B2R

To stabilize the inactive state of kinin receptors in complex with their antagonists and increase their size for cryo-EM structure determination, we swapped the intracellular loop 3 (ICL3) of kinin receptors with that of the κ-opioid receptor (KOR) to enable the binding of the nanobody Nb626. Specifically, the 24 residues of B1R ICL3 ranging from R2325.68 (superscripts refer to Ballesteros–Weinstein numbering for class A GPCRs27) to L2556.40 were replaced by residues from V2565.68 to V2796.40 of KOR, while the 28 residues of B2R ICL3 ranging from N2535.67 to I2806.45 were replaced by residues from S2555.67 to V2846.45 of KOR. The flexible regions of C-terminal residues 342–353 in B1R and N-terminal residues 1–28 in B2R were truncated to increase expression. In addition, two thermostabilizing mutations (S1243.39K and S1323.47C in B1R, S1443.39K and C1463.41W in B2R) were introduced to improve receptor stability28–30. A comprehensive pharmacological characterization confirmed that the key receptor modifications employed for structural studies preserve the native ligand-binding properties (Supplementary Figs. 6–7, Supplementary Tables 2–4). The cryo-EM structures of ELN441958-bound B1R, R715-bound B1R, and Win64338-bound B2R complexes were determined to global resolutions of 3.48 Å, 3.57 Å, and 3.58 Å, respectively. The cryo-EM maps allowed unambiguous modeling of all three antagonists and most receptor residues (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Figs. 1–3, Supplementary Table 1).

Binding mode of small-molecule antagonist ELN441958 in B1R

ELN441958 is a non-peptide antagonist selectively inhibiting B1R with high oral bioavailability and potent systemic efficacy in rhesus monkey inflammatory pain23. It is characterized by a benzamide core with a 7-chloro-isoindolinone group attached to its benzene ring and a 3-(pyridin-4-yl)−3,9-diazaspiro[5.5]undecane group attached to its amide. ELN441958 extends from TM1 at the entrance to the pocket to a hydrophobic cavity deep in the pocket comprising TM2, TM3, and TM7 of B1R. The 7-chloro-isoindolinone group of ELN441958 makes hydrophobic interactions with F922.59, W932.60, W103ECL1, I1133.28, I1173.32, and F3027.43, and a hydrogen bond with N2987.39 (Fig. 2a, b). The core benzene ring mainly interacts with Q2957.36. Our mutagenesis analysis showed that W103ECL1A, N2987.39A, and Q2957.36A decreased the potency of ELN441958 (Fig. 2c). Additionally, the pyridine and diazaspiro[5.5]undecane groups are stabilized by hydrophobic interactions with W331.28 and F101ECL1 (Fig. 2b). Molecular docking was performed based on our B1R-ELN441958 structure to explore the binding modes of various B1R antagonists SSR24061231, safotibant32, and MK-068633 (Fig. 2d–f). SSR240612 and safotibant are arylsulfonamide derivatives, while MK-0686 is identified with carboxamide chemotype. In our docking results, all three antagonists share a binding pose and interactions similar to those found in the ELN441958-B1R complex structure. The sulfonylamide groups of SSR240612 and safotibant, along with the amide group of MK-0686, form polar contacts with N2987.39, firmly anchoring the ligand in the binding pocket (Fig. 2d-g). Deep in the pocket, the hydrophobic moieties of all three antagonists make hydrophobic contacts with W932.60, I1133.28, N1143.29, I1173.32, and F3027.43. The mutation W932.60A compromised the potency of all three antagonists, suggesting a critical role for W932.60 in the recognition of these compounds (Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, all three compounds form stacking or hydrophobic contacts in the extracellular loop (ECL) region, especially with W331.28, W103ECL1, and F101ECL1, along with polar interactions with TM2/7 (Fig. 2h). Notably, alanine mutations of W331.28 and F101ECL1 significantly impaired the inhibitory potency of all four compounds (ELN441958, SSR240612, safotibant, and MK-0686), confirming the essential role of this hydrophobic interaction in antagonist binding (Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 3). To summarize, a combination of polar interactions with N2987.39 and hydrophobic interactions in the binding pocket, along with polar contacts with N962.63, D2917.32, and Q2957.36, delineate critical aspects for B1R small-molecule antagonist binding.

Binding mode of peptide antagonist R715 in B1R

The specific B1R peptide antagonist R715 was designed based on [Leu8]des-Arg9-bradykinin24,34. The N terminus of R715 is acetylated (N-α-acetyl-L-lysine), and an unnatural aromatic amino acid, D-βNal, is introduced to position 8 for enzymatic resistance35 (Fig. 3a). In our B1R-R715 complex structure, R715 is positioned within the orthosteric binding pocket (OBP) composed of most transmembrane helices and extracellular loops, except TM1 and ECL3. R715 adopts a snake-shaped conformation, with its N-terminus extending towards the ECL region and its C-terminus inserting deeply into the transmembrane domain (TMD) bundle (Fig. 3a). In the ligand-binding pocket, the N-α-acetyl-Lys1 charged amino group forms a salt bridge with E2736.58 and its backbone nitrogen makes a hydrogen bond with E2877.28, while the C-terminal carboxylic group of R715 makes a salt bridge with R2025.38 and hydrogen bonds with Y2666.51 and N2987.39 (Fig. 3b, c). The alanine mutations of E2737.28 and N2987.39 decreased the potency of R715, confirming the essential roles of these residues in R715 binding (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table 3). Besides, the main chain carbonyl group of Gly5 forms a hydrogen bond with R1764.64, and the side chain of Ser7 makes a hydrogen bond with Q2957.36 (Fig. 3b, c). However, the E2877.28A and Q2957.36A mutants did not significantly affect the potency of R715 (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table 3), possibly because the loss of corresponding interactions could be compensated by similar interactions with other residues. For example, in the Q2957.36A mutant, Ser7 may form a hydrogen bond with W932.60. Additionally, Pro3, Pro4, Phe6, and D-βNal8 of R715 exhibit extensive hydrophobic interactions with B1R residues. Pro3 and Pro4 interact with V181ECL2, I190ECL2, and L191ECL2, while Phe6 interacts with the aromatic rings of F101ECL1. D-βNal8 inserts deeply into the hydrophobic pocket surrounded by W932.60, I1173.32, and F3027.43 (Fig. 3c). Binding mode of small-molecule antagonist Win64338 in B2R

Win64338 is the first non-peptide B2R antagonist36, which was designed by engineering a pharmacophore with a 10 Å separation between the phosphonium cation and guanidyl moieties to mimic the distance between two terminal arginine residues of bradykinin25. In our B2R-Win64338 complex structure, Win64338 adopts a tripod-like conformation, fitting snugly at the bottom of the B2R pocket, with two bulky cyclohexyl and one naphthyl groups establishing extensive hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 4a). For instance, the dicyclohexylguanidinium moiety makes hydrophobic contacts with W1132.60, W123ECL1, V1333.28, I1373.32, and C211ECL2. The naphthyl group interacts with W1132.60, W2836.48, F2866.51, and Y3227.43. Moreover, the charged nitrogen of the guanidinium moiety forms a hydrogen bond with N1343.29, which functions as an anchor at the bottom of the binding pocket (Fig. 4b). Indeed, the N1343.29A mutation markedly decreased Win64338 potency (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 4). The positively charged phosphonium group with alkyl chains engages the top binding pocket through cation-π interaction with Y201ECL2, as well as hydrophobic interactions with D3117.32 and Q3157.36. Molecular docking of B2R antagonists anatibant37, fasitibant38, and deucrictibant39 was further performed using our B2R-Win64338 structure (Fig. 4d-f). Anatibant and fasitibant share a central sulfonamide group with the attached dichlorobenzyloxy-dimethylquinoline moiety. On the opposite end of the molecule, anatibant contains a benzamidine moiety, whereas fasitibant possesses a quaternary amine group. Deucrictibant is an acetamide derivative with a deuterium modification. The docking analysis suggests that all these antagonists share common interaction patterns. These include hydrophobic interactions with W1132.60 in the middle of the pocket and engagement with a hydrophobic cavity at the bottom of the pocket comprised of I1373.32, W2836.48, F2866.51, and Y3227.43. This cavity accommodates the naphthyl group of Win64338, the pyrrolidine moiety of anatibant, quinoline moiety of fasitibant, and triazole group of deucrictibant, respectively (Fig. 4g, h). Previous mutagenesis studies supported the role of W1132.60, W2836.48, and Y3227.43 in both fasitibant and anatibant binding40–42. At the top of the pocket, the hydrophobic interactions formed by F121ECL1 and C211ECL2 further stabilize all four antagonists’ binding (Fig. 4h), while Win64338, anatibant, and fasitibant share π–stacking interactions with the phenyl ring of Y201ECL2, formed by their phosphonium, benzamidine, and tertiary amine groups, respectively.

Subtype selectivity of B1R and B2R antagonists

Comparison of our structures of kinin receptors in complex with various antagonists provides a framework to explore subtype selectivity between B1R and B2R. Although the general antagonist binding mode in both receptors involves extensive hydrophobic and polar interactions with TM2/3/7 and ECLs, there are key differences in the pocket enabling B1R/B2R selectivity.

Sequence alignment of B1R and B2R in the OBP region shows that 7 out of 16 residues differ between the two subtypes (Fig. 5a). For the small molecule antagonist binding pocket, the ligand selectivity mainly results from the non-conserved residues Y2666.51, N2987.39, and F3027.43 in B1R, with the corresponding residues F2866.51, S3187.39, and Y3227.43 in B2R. The bottom part of the pocket formed by residues at the positions of 2.60, 3.29, 3.32, 6.51, 7.39, and 7.43 of B1R is much narrower than that of B2R. The bulky side chains of Y2666.51 and N2987.39 along with the indole ring of W932.60 shrink the bottom cavity of B1R, limiting the ligand position in proximity to TM2/3 (Fig. 5b). For example, ELN441958 forms a hydrogen bond with N2987.39 and makes a hydrophobic contact with F3027.43 in B1R, whereas the corresponding residue S3187.39 with a smaller side chain in B2R might not have the optimal distance for such interaction, and there is a spatial hindrance between Y3227.43 and the isoindolinone moiety of ELN441958 (Figs. 2b, 5d). This hypothesis is supported by our swapping functional analysis. Substitutions of Y2666.51, N2987.39, and F3027.43 in B1R with their cognate residues from B2R (Phe, Ser, and Tyr) impaired the binding of ELN441958, implicating the critical role of these residues in the selectivity of ELN441958 for B1R over B2R (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Table 3).

The naphthyl group of Win64338 engages the hydrophobic cavity formed by F2866.51 and Y3227.43 in B2R, while the bulkier side chains of Y2666.51 and N2987.39 in B1R restrict the space to accommodate the naphthyl ring of Win64338 (Figs. 4b, 5e). Swapping of S3187.39 in B2R with the corresponding asparagine residue from B1R markedly reduced the potency of Win64338, whereas the reciprocal mutation N2987.39S in B1R enhanced Win64338 potency, switching its selectivity from B2R to B1R (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Fig. 8a, Supplementary Tables 3-4). These findings suggest that the smaller side chain of serine at position 7.39 in B2R helps maintain a more spacious binding pocket, thereby contributing to Win64338 selectivity for B2R over B1R. Other substitutions did not produce significant changes in Win64338 potency at either B1R or B2R (Supplementary Fig. 8a, Supplementary Tables 3, 4). Notably, ELN441958 exhibited high selectivity for B1R, as no measurable inhibitory activity against B2R was detected in our assays (Supplementary Fig. 8b, Supplementary Table 4). Overall, our results indicate that exchanging a single key residue between B1R and B2R is generally insufficient to switch antagonist selectivity between these receptors, with the exception of residue 7.49 in the case of Win64338.

Additionally, differences in ECL2 may contribute to conformational variations between the two receptor subtypes (Fig. 5a, d-e, Supplementary Fig. 4b). In B2R, Y201ECL2 and E204ECL2 are positioned to engage in π–stacking or polar interactions, whereas the corresponding positions in B1R are occupied by V181ECL2 and L184ECL2, which lack such interaction capabilities. Taken together, we conclude that although key residues in these two pockets play an important role in ligand recognition, they may not fully account for subtype selectivity, which likely arises from cooperative interactions across multiple regions of the receptor.

Antagonistic mechanisms of kinin receptors

To elucidate the potential antagonistic mechanisms of kinin receptors, we compared our antagonist-bound inactive B1R and B2R structures with cryo-EM structures of active B1R and B2R bound to peptide agonists des-Arg10-kallidin and bradykinin21,22. Compared to the active state, the cytoplasmic parts of TM2 and TM6 in the inactive B1R show significant inward movements of 1.6 Å and 9.2 Å, respectively (measured by Cα at 2.39 and 6.31), while TM4 and TM7 exhibit outward shifts by 1.5 Å and 5.0 Å, respectively (measured by Cα at 4.41 and 7.53, Fig. 6a). The Pro8-Phe9 residues of des-Arg10-kallidin insert deeply into OBP, triggering the re-packing of F862.53, W932.60, W2636.48, Y2666.51, F2997.40, and F3027.43, which further induce conformational rearrangements of PIF, DRY, and NPxxY motifs (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Structure superimposition with des-Arg10-kallidin-bound B1R indicates that ELN441958 and R715 occupy similar binding pockets to des-Arg10-kallidin in B1R with different binding modes (Fig. 6b). Structural comparison of kinin and angiotensin receptors

The kinin and angiotensin receptors are closely related class A GPCR members and share functional relevance in regulating the blood pressure43–46. From structural comparison of kinin receptors B1R and B2R with angiotensin II receptors AT1R and AT2R, all endogenous peptide agonists adopt a similar snake-shaped pose with their N-terminus extending outward and the C-terminus penetrating deeply into the helical bundle (Fig. 8a). The Arg2-Pro7-Phe8 of AngII overlays with Arg2-Pro8-Phe9 of des-Arg10-KD and Arg1-Pro7-Phe8 of BK, respectively. Structure-based sequence alignment reveals several conserved residues between kinin and angiotensin receptors in the agonist-binding pocket, including W2.60, R4.64, W6.48, and D7.32, resulting in a similar agonist-binding mode in these receptors (Fig. 8b). Specifically, the negatively charged D7.32 forms a salt bridge with the N-terminal arginine of peptide agonists. In the middle of the pocket, the side chain of R4.64 of B1R and B2R makes hydrogen bonds with the backbone of Gly5 in des-Arg10-KD and Gly4 in BK, respectively, which is akin to AT1R and AT2R. The R4.64 of angiotensin receptors form hydrogen bonds with the backbone of His6 and Pro7 of AngII. At the bottom cavity, the conserved W2.60 forms hydrophobic contacts with the conserved proline of all peptide agonists, which plays a critical role in the receptor activation. Furthermore, a similar hydrophobic core surrounding phenylalanine at the C-terminus of the peptide agonists is mediated by residues in TM2, TM3, TM6, and TM7. To elucidate the antagonist binding modes in the kinin and angiotensin receptors, we aligned our inactive B1R and B2R structures with the previously solved antagonist-bound structures of AT1R-ZD7155 and AT2R-compound243,44. Both ZD7155 and compound2 occupy a hydrophobic cavity comprised of W2.60, V/L3.32, and Y/F7.43 in AT1R and AT2R, which is similar to the kinin receptors antagonist binding modes. The antagonist of B1R, however, sits in a shallower cleft, whereas the compound2 exhibits deeper penetration into the TM core (Fig. 8c). The observed differences in binding pocket depth between B1R and AT2R are likely influenced by non-conserved residues surrounding the pocket, including those at positions 2.63, 3.32, 3.36, 5.42, 6.51, and 7.39 (Fig. 8b). Besides, the tetrazole moieties of ZD7155 and compound2 form hydrogen bonds with R4.64 in AT1R and AT2R, which is crucial for antagonist binding. However, R4.64 is not engaged in hydrogen bonding with ELN441958 and Win64338 in B1R and B2R, respectively. Therefore, these findings reveal the common and distinct features of agonist and antagonist binding pockets of kinin and angiotensin receptors.

Discussion

Kinin receptors have garnered a great deal of interest as potential therapeutic targets due to their critical roles in mediating the vascular aspect of inflammation. B1R and B2R antagonists with analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties hold potential for the treatment of angioedema, allergic rhinitis, cirrhosis, and other diseases48,49. To facilitate the development of drugs with high affinity and bioavailability, a better understanding of the structural basis underlying ligand selectivity and antagonism of B1R and B2R is essential. In this study, we reported three cryo-EM structures of B1R and B2R bound to different small molecule and peptide antagonists. Combined with molecular docking and functional assays, these structures revealed common features of kinin receptor antagonist binding. In B1R, ELN441958 and R715 are anchored by a hydrogen bond with N2987.39 and engage in hydrophobic contacts with residues on TM2, TM3, TM7, ECL1, and ECL2. Our structural comparison revealed that the B2R antagonist binding induces an expanded pocket relative to B1R, characterized by the outward movements of TM1, TM6, and TM7. In B2R, Win64338 occupies a hydrophobic cleft formed by TM2, TM3, TM6, and TM7, extending deeper into the transmembrane bundle. A hydrogen bond between the guanidinium moiety of Win64338 and N1343.29 further stabilizes the antagonist binding.

Our findings establish a framework for understanding the molecular basis of antagonist selectivity between B1R and B2R. It was found that non-conserved residues at the bottom of OBP, including those at positions 6.51, 7.39, and 7.43 of B1R and B2R, serve as critical determinants of small-molecule antagonist selectivity for kinin receptors. The side chains of Y2666.51, N2987.39, and F3027.43 in B1R, accompanied by the rotation of W932.60 in the antagonist-bound state, create a compact bottom cavity that favors smaller hydrophobic groups, whereas the corresponding residues in B2R can accommodate larger groups. Additionally, polar interactions of N2987.39 in B1R compared to S3187.39 in B2R, as well as variations clustered in ECL2 may further contribute to subtype selectivity.

Further comparison of the inactive and active B1R and B2R structures uncovers the conserved mechanisms of receptor activation and inhibition between kinin receptors. The C-terminal phenylalanine of the endogenous peptide agonist inserts into OBP and promotes the displacement of the toggle switch residue W6.48. In contrast, the antagonist occupation restricts the conformation of this residue. Specifically, the isoindolinone group of ELN441958 and the D-βNal8-Ile9 residues of R715 stabilize the hydrophobic pocket formed by F862.53, L1213.36, and Y2666.51 in B1R through interactions with W932.60, I1173.32, Y2666.51, N2987.39, and F3027.43, while the naphthyl group of Win64338 makes a direct contact with the toggle switch residue W6.48 in B2R, preventing the conformational changes of W6.48 and surrounding residues, thus blocking receptor activation.

Overall, our work enhances the molecular understanding of antagonist selectivity and antagonism for kinin receptors, paving the way for the discovery of structure-based compounds targeting B1R and B2R and accelerating the development of next-generation therapeutics for related diseases.

Methods

Constructs cloning

Both human B1R and B2R genes were codon optimized for insect cell expression and cloned into a modified pFastbac1 vector (Invitrogen). For B1R (UniProt ID: P46663), a haemagglutinin (HA) signal peptide, a FLAG tag, a 10 × His tag, a maltose-binding protein (MBP), and a tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage site were fused to the N-terminus to facilitate expression and purification. The C-terminal residues 342-353 were truncated. Residues from R2325.68 to L2556.40 were swapped with residues from V2565.68 to V2796.40 of the human kappa opioid receptor (UniProt ID: P41145)50. In addition, two thermostabilizing point mutations S1243.39K and S1323.47C were introduced to improve receptor stability28,29.

For human B2R (UniProt ID: P30411-1), the N-terminal residues 1–28 were replaced by an HA tag, a FLAG tag, a 10×His tag, an MBP, a 6-residue GSGGSG linker, and a TEV cleavage site. The B2R residues between N2535.67 and I2806.45 were replaced by those of the kappa opioid receptor (UniProt ID: P41145)50. Two mutations, S1443.39K and C1463.41W, were incorporated to enhance the thermal stability29,30.

Expression and purification of B1R and B2R complexes

The Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus Expression System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to generate recombinant baculovirus for protein expression. The Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) insect cells (Invitrogen) were grown to a density of 2–3 × 106 cells/mL and infected with amplified baculovirus encoding the receptor (B1R or B2R) and the nanobody Nb651 at the ratio of 1:2. Cells were harvested 48 h after infection by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C for future purification.

Frozen cell pellets were thawed at room temperature and lysed in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and a protease inhibitor cocktail by dounce homogenization. The cell lysate was incubated for 30 min at 4 °C, supplemented with 10 μM antagonist and 2 mg/mL iodoacetamide. Then the membrane was solubilized by 0.5% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG, Anatrace) and 0.05% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate tris salt (CHS, Anatrace) for 3 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected by ultracentrifugation at 58,000 g for 1 h and then incubated with Talon IMAC resin (Takara) overnight at 4 °C. The resin was then washed with 20 column volume (CV) of wash buffer I (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.05% (w/v) LMNG, 0.005% (w/v) CHS, 20 mM imidazole) and 20 CV of wash buffer II (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.002% (w/v) LMNG, 0.0002% (w/v) CHS, 40 mM imidazole). The protein was eluted with six CV of elute buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.002% (w/v) LMNG, 0.0002% (w/v) CHS, 300 mM imidazole, 10 μM antagonist). The purified sample was treated with TEV protease on ice overnight, followed by incubation with amylose resin (NEB) for 3 h at 4 °C. The flow-through protein was collected and concentrated using a 50 kDa cut-off concentrator (Sartorius). The concentrated protein was loaded onto a Superdex 200 increase 10/300 column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.002% (w/v) LMNG, 0.0002% (w/v) CHS, and 10 μM antagonist. Fractions containing monomeric complex were pooled and concentrated for electron microscopy experiments.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

For the preparation of cryo-EM grids, 3 μL of purified B1R-ELN441958-Nb6 (5 mg/mL), B1R-R715-Nb6 (5 mg/mL), or B2R-Win64338-Nb6 (10 mg/mL), was applied onto a glow-discharged holey carbon grid (Quantifoil, R1.2/1.3, 300 mesh) and flash frozen in liquid ethane using Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a blot time of 3 s and a blot force of 10 at 4 °C and 100% humidity.

For B1R-R715-Nb6 and B1R-ELN441958-Nb6, data collection was performed on a Titan Krios (Thermo Fisher Scientific) electron microscope operated at 300 kV equipped with a Selectris energy filter and a Falcon 4i direct electron detector in the Center of Cryo-Electron Microscopy at Zhejiang University. The movies were captured with a magnification of ×130,000 using EPU software for automated data collection, corresponding to a pixel size of 0.93 Å. The defocus range was set from −0.7 to −1.5 μm. Each micrograph was dose-fractionated to 40 frames under a dose rate of 12.11 e− per pixel per s, with a total exposure time of 3.57 s, resulting in a total dose of about 50 e− Å−2.

For B2R-Win64338-Nb6, data collection was performed on a Titan Krios (Thermo Fisher Scientific) electron microscope operated at 300 kV equipped with a spherical aberration (Cs) image corrector, a GIF-Quantum energy filter, and a Gatan K3 Summit direct electron detector at the National Facility for Protein Science in Shanghai. The movies were captured with a magnification of ×81,000 using EPU software for automated data collection, corresponding to a pixel size of 0.89 Å. The defocus range was set from −0.9 to −1.7 μm. Each micrograph was dose-fractionated to 40 frames under a dose rate of 17.52 e− per pixel per s, with a total exposure time of 2.26 s, resulting in a total dose of about 50 e− Å−2.

Cryo-EM data processing

The depiction of each cryoEM processing workflow could be found in Supplementary Figs. 1–3. Briefly, the motion correction was performed using the MotionCorr2 program52, and the CTF parameters of each micrograph were estimated using CTFFIND4 in RELION-4.053 or patch CTF54 in cryoSPARC v3.3.255.

For B1R-R715-Nb6 and B1R-ELN441958-Nb6, the extracted particle stack from Laplacian-of-Gaussian-based autopicking in RELION was imported into cryoSPARC for multiple rounds of 2D classification. The best projections in 2D classification served as a 2D template to perform template-based particle picking. Particles picked from auto picking and template picker were subject to several rounds of 2D classification, followed by iterative rounds of ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement. Good particles were merged and duplicated particles were removed. A total of 746,530 and 420,639 particles for B1R-R715-Nb6 and B1R-ELN441958-Nb6, respectively, were selected for further rounds of heterogeneous refinement. After non-uniform refinement, the final reconstruction maps were obtained at 3.57 Å with 198,063 particles for B1R-R715-Nb6 and 3.48 Å with 232,403 particles for B1R-ELN441958-Nb6, respectively. Local resolution estimations for the density map were performed in cryoSPARC.

For B2R-Win64338-Nb6, the extracted particle stack from Laplacian-of-Gaussian-based autopicking was imported into cryoSPARC for multiple rounds of 2D classification. The best projections in 2D classification served as a 2D template to perform template-based particle picking. Particles picked from auto picking and template picker were subject to several rounds of 2D classification, followed by iterative rounds of ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement. Good particles were merged, and duplicated particles were removed, yielding 523,855 particles. These particles were then subject to several rounds of 3D classification to further remove suboptimal particles and 3D refinement followed by Bayesian polishing in RELION. The polished particles were re-transferred into cryoSPARC for additional ab-initio reconstruction and non-uniform refinement. The final reconstruction maps were refined to 3.58 Å for B2R-Win64338-Nb6 from 252,676 particles. Local resolution estimations for the density map were performed in cryoSPARC.

Model building and refinement

The initial template models for B1R and B2R were derived from the cryo-EM structures of B1R-Gq complex (PDB: 7EIB)22 and B2R-Gq complex (7F6H)21, respectively. KOR-Nb6 complex (PDB: 6VI4)50 was aligned to the receptor to produce the initial model for Nb6. All models were docked into the density maps using ChimeraX56 followed by a manual adjustment in Coot57 and iteratively refined by real-space refinement in Phenix58. The final models were validated using MolProbity59. All the figures were prepared using ChimeraX (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimerax) and Pymol (https://pymol.org). The refinement statistics are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Constructs design and site-directed mutagenesis for functional assays

For both the wild-type B1R and B2R constructs used in ONE-GO Gq BRET assay, a full-length human receptor gene (residues 1–353 for B1R or residues 1–391 for B2R) was cloned into a pcDNA3.1(+) vector with an N-terminal Flag tag after an HA signal peptide. Mutations tested in assays were introduced to WT B1R and B2R genes in pcDNA3.1(+) vector using the Agilent QuickChange site-directed mutagenesis with synthetic primers (Integrated DNA Technologies), AccuPrime™ Pfx SuperMix (Invitrogen) and DpnI restriction enzyme (New England Biolabs) in rCutsmart buffer (New England Biolabs). The sequences for all plasmids were verified using the Genewiz (Azenta) whole plasmid sequencing Plasmid-EZ service.

Cell culture for functional assays

Human Embryonic Kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were maintained in culture flasks using Complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) Media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% heated inactivated Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM L-Glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 U/mL penicillin-100 µg/mL streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and grown in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C and 10% CO2. Cells were passed every 3 days and seeded at a density of 20,000 cells/cm2 with respect to the size of the culture flask using 0.1% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Surface expression measurement by flow cytometry

Solution Preparation

Surface expression levels of each B1R and B2R construct were measured relative to their respective WT receptor using flow cytometry by detecting the N-terminal FLAG tag. A FLAG tag labeling solution was prepared by performing a 1:100 dilution on an ANTI-FLAG® M2-FITC antibody commercial stock (~1 mg/mL) (Sigma Aldrich; catalog# F4049, Lot: 0000522295) using 1× TBS (Corning) supplemented with 4% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) (Sigma Aldrich). An M2-FITC + 7-AAD working stock was then prepared by performing a 1:6 dilution on a commercial 7-AAD stock (50 µg/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog# 00-6993-59) using the previously prepared Anti-FLAG M2-FITC solution to achieve final concentrations of 8.3 µg/mL for both ANTI-FLAG® M2-FITC and 7-AAD.

Transfection

HEK293T cells were seeded in a 24-chamber culture dish (Corning) at a density of 3.5 × 104 cells per chamber in 400 µL of Complete DMEM Media and incubated for 24 h. Following an overnight incubation, each chamber was transfected with 200 ng of a given receptor construct (including empty vector as a negative control) using PEI STAR™ transfection reagent (Tocris) at a ratio of 1:3 DNA(μg):PEI(μg). After transfection, cells were allowed to incubate for 48 hours to facilitate maximal expression.

Flow Cytometry

After a 48-h incubation period, cells were removed from their incubator and washed with Assay Buffer (140 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.37 mM NaH2PO4, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 0.1% Glucose), then unadhered via trituration using 1 mL DMEM, high glucose, HEPES, no phenol red media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. The tubes were centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min, after which the supernatant was removed, and cell pellets were resuspended in 30 µL of Assay Buffer. 10 µL of resuspended cells were transferred to corresponding wells of a 96-well plate (Corning) and then mixed with 10 µL of M2-FITC + 7-AAD working stock solutions prepared above. The plate was incubated for 20 min, after which the cells were diluted with 180 µL of 1× TBS buffer (Corning). The plate was then run through a Millipore Guava EasyCyte™ 6HT flow cytometer controlled by the Millipore guavaSoft - ExpressPlus (v.3.6) software, with the instrument being set to count 10,000 particles and simultaneously measure respective forward scattering, red fluorescence to gate around living cells (640 nm laser and a Red-B 680/30 nm fluorescent filter) and green fluorescence to measure receptor expression (488 nm laser and a Green-B 525/30 nm fluorescence filter). Using WT B1R and B2R and empty vector transfected cells, suitable gain and threshold settings were determined to ensure adequate spread and separation of the data points (Supplementary Fig. 10). To determine the relative surface expression of each construct, the median green fluorescence signal of each construct was normalized by the median green fluorescence signal of the WT receptor (100%) and the median green fluorescence signal from the empty vector transfected cells (0%). Surface expression measurements were performed in three independent experiments.

ONE-GO BRET Gq protein recruitment assays

Transfection

HEK293T cells were seeded in a 6-chamber culture dish (Corning) at a density of 3.5 × 105 cells per well in 2 mL of Complete DMEM Media and incubated for 24 h. The following day, cells were transfected with 50 ng of the respective receptor DNA and 50 ng of the ONE-GO Gαq BRET sensor DNA using the TransIT®-2020 transfection reagent (1:3 ratio of DNA(µg):TransIT(µL), Mirus). The cells were then gently rocked to distribute the complex and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 10% CO2, prior to ONE-GO Assay60–62 the next day.

Agonist mode assay

Prior to screening the mutants in antagonist mode, each mutant was screened in concentration-response agonist mode to determine the EC80 values for its respective agonist. The B1R agonist, des-Arg10-kallidin (Sangon Biotech), was dissolved at 20 mM in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich), while the B2R agonist, bradykinin (Sangon Biotech), was dissolved at 20 mM in water. These stocks were then further diluted to 3 mM 300× stocks in DMSO (for des-Arg10-kallidin) or water (for bradykinin) and then serially diluted using a 1:10 serial dilution across 7 points to ensure equal amounts of DMSO or water over all concentrations while also leaving room for a no drug control (last point). Using Assay buffer (140 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.37 mM NaH2PO4, 20 mM HEPES, 0.1% Glucose), these 300× stocks were then diluted to 3× stocks with a concentration range from 30 µM to 30 pM. Once prepared, the 6-chamber culture dish with the transfected cells was removed from the incubator, media was aspirated from each chamber, and the cells were washed with 1 mL of 1× Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS, Quality Biological). Cells were then detached via trituration using 1 mL of DMEM, high glucose, HEPES, no phenol red media (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Unadhered cells were transferred to Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was aspirated, and the pelleted cells were resuspended in 300–600 µL of Assay buffer to produce a cell density of ~0.75–1.5 × 106 cells/mL. 10 µL of resuspended cells were then transferred to each well (7,500–15,000 cells/well) of a white bottom 384 well OptiPlate (Revvity), and centrifuged at 200 × g for 2 min. 10 µL of 3× serially diluted agonist was then added to respective wells, after which the cells were centrifuged once more at 200 × g for 2 min and incubated for 5 min to allow for agonist binding. During incubation, a 30 µM (3×) Coelenterazine 400a (CTZ400a) (AmBeed) stock was prepared by diluting 2 mM CTZ400a in NanoFuel-400a solvent (Nanolight Technology) using Assay buffer. After 5 min incubation, 10 µL of the 3× CTZ400a solution was then distributed across all wells, and the plate was centrifuged at 200 × g for 2 min. The plate was then inserted into a PHERAstar FSX plate reader (BMG Labtech), and a gain adjustment was performed using the well with the highest expected luminescence. Using the adjusted gain, a kinetic reading was recorded over 30 min at 28 °C using a top optic BRET 1 plus optic module (535/30 nm and 475/30 nm filters) with simultaneous dual emission detection and 0.32 s measurement intervals. The protocol was repeated across three biologically independent trials (N = 3) with each independent trial consisting of three technical replicas per condition.

Agonist mode data processing

The BRET signal was observed to stabilize after 20 min post agonist addition, and these points were used for further data processing. BRET ratios were calculated by dividing the 535/30 nm emission by the 475/30 nm emission using the PHERAstar FS MARS (v. 5.02 R3) software and exported to Microsoft Excel for further processing. The basal activity of each mutant was estimated by averaging the raw BRET signals from the wells containing no agonist for that mutant. BRET data at different agonist concentrations were presented as the difference between the raw BRET signal and the corresponding basal signal, resulting in ΔBRET values. The ΔBRET values for different mutants were normalized by the maximal ΔBRET value for the corresponding WT receptor and expressed in percents. The resulting normalized ΔBRET % data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism (v. 10.0.2) using a nonlinear regression with the log(agonist) vs. response (three parameters) curve. The EC80 values of each mutant were estimated using GraphPad Prism’s ECanything analysis.

Antagonist mode assay

After determining the EC80 concentrations, the ONE-GO assays were conducted in antagonist mode in the presence of the corresponding agonist at its EC80 concentration. For quantitative antagonist potency, a near-saturating agonist concentration (EC80) is used to yield a stable IC50 under high-occupancy conditions, enabling reliable analysis. Agonist solutions for each mutant were prepared by diluting their stocks to 4× EC80 agonist concentrations in Assay buffer. Stock solutions of B1R antagonists, ELN441958 (GlpBio Techonology), R715 (Sangon Biotech), Safotibant (TargetMol), MK-0686 (TargetMol), SSR240612 (Cayman Chemical), and B2R antagonist Win64338 (Tocris Biosciences) were all prepared at 10 mM in DMSO with the exception of R715, which was prepared in water. These stocks were then further diluted to 4 mM (400×) solutions in DMSO or water for R715, and then serially diluted in DMSO or water using a 1:10 serial dilution across 6 points in order to ensure equal amounts of DMSO or water over all concentrations while also leaving room for a no ligand control (first point) and an EC80 agonist control (last point). Using Assay buffer these 400× stocks were then diluted to 4× stocks, creating a concentration range from 40 µM to 400 pM for ELN441958, R715, Safotibant, MK-0686, SSR240612, and from 400 µM to 4 nM for Win64338 due to its lower potency. Transfected HEK293T cells were harvested as described in the agonist mode assay, and 10 µL of resuspended cells were then transferred per well (7,500–15,000 cells/well) of a white bottom 384 well plate (OptiPlate, Revvity), and the plate was centrifuged at 200 × g for 2 min. 10 µL of serially diluted antagonist solutions were then added to the wells containing the respective receptor after which the cells were centrifuged once more at 200 × g for 2 min and incubated for 5 min to allow for antagonist binding. Following the 5-min incubation, 10 µL of a given mutant’s 4× EC80 agonist solutions were added to all sets of wells containing the respective mutants to induce ligand competition, except for the first set of wells where 10 µL of Assay buffer was added instead to measure the basal activity. The plate was centrifuged once more at 200 × g for 2 min and incubated for an additional 5 min. During the incubation, a 40 µM (4×) CTZ400a stock was prepared by diluting 2 mM CTZ400a in NanoFuel-400a solvent (Nanolight Technology) using Assay buffer. Following the 5-min incubation, 10 µL of the 4× CTZ400a solution was distributed across all wells, and the plate was centrifuged at 200 × g for 2 min. The plate was then inserted into a PHERAstar FSX plate reader (BMG Labtech), and a gain adjustment was performed using the well with the highest expected luminescence. Using the adjusted gain, a kinetic reading was recorded over 30 min at 28 °C using a top optic BRET 1 plus optic module (535/30 nm and 475/30 nm filters) with simultaneous dual emission detection and 0.32 s measurement intervals. The protocol was repeated across three biologically independent trials (N = 3) with each independent trial consisting of three technical replicas per condition.

Antagonist mode data processing

Data acquired 30 min post agonist addition were used for processing. BRET ratios were calculated by dividing the 535/30 nm emission by the 475/30 nm emission in the PHERAstar FS MARS (v. 5.02 R3) software and exported to Microsoft Excel for further processing. The basal activity of each mutant was then estimated as described above by averaging the raw BRET signals of the wells containing no ligands for that mutant. BRET data at different antagonist concentrations were presented as the difference between the raw BRET signal and the corresponding basal signal (ΔBRET). The ΔBRET values were normalized by the average maximal ΔBRET for each mutant using the wells containing EC80 agonist but no antagonist and set at 80%. The resulting normalized ΔBRET % data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism (v. 10.0.2) using a nonlinear regression with the log(agonist) vs. response (three parameters) curve.

Statistical Analysis

For surface expression data, normalized median expression values were compared with WT receptor expression using a one-way ANOVA assuming a Log-Normal Distribution followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test in GraphPad Prism (v. 10.0.2).

For Dose Response results, Log(EC50/IC50) values were compared with values for WT receptor using a one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test in GraphPad Prism (v. 10.0.2).

Molecular docking

We generated receptor models using the ICM-Pro-3.9-4 molecular modeling package63, using the cryo-EM structures of B1R and B2R in their inactive states bound to ELN441958 and Win64338, respectively. The PDB coordinates were converted into ICM objects, a process that included hydrogen addition, secondary structure assignment, and protonation state assignment for His, Asn, and Gln side chains. To optimize the orthosteric ligand-binding pockets, we kept the ligand and pocket side chains within 6 Å of the ligand flexible. First, we performed a local minimization on these side chains and the ligand while applying a quadratic restraint to the ligand to prevent large deviations in its pose. This restraint was then removed in a second step, where we conducted a Monte Carlo optimization on the ligand and the pocket side chains. The resulting lowest-energy conformation was used for docking Bradykinin antagonists.
