A Surface Integrand for the Inverse KLT Kernel
Christoph Bartsch 1, Karol Kampf 1, Jiří Novotný 1, Jaroslav Trnka 2

Abstract
We propose a loop-level generalization of the inverse string theory Kawai-Lewellen-Tye (KLT) kernel: the planar inverse KLT integrand . The integrand is defined constructively via a novel Berends-Giele-like recursion that exposes the inverse KLT kernel as the simplest toy model of a “stringy amplitude”. We show that, to all loop orders, the inverse KLT integrand is structurally equivalent to integrands in the cubic scalar tr ϕ 3 \phi^{3} theory. This simplicity is obscured in the conventional Feynman diagram approach, where the inverse KLT integrand receives contributions from an infinity of infinite towers of contact interactions. The inverse KLT integrand is a rational function of stringified kinematic variables and is naturally defined on the kinematic surface proposed by Arkani-Hamed et al. 2 ; 3 . It provides an elementary analogue of the surfacehedron integrand for the tr ϕ 3 \phi^{3} theory involving only scalar resonances and unifies the scattering of cubic scalars and pions in the non-linear sigma model (NLSM) to all loop orders via kinematic α ′ \alpha^{\prime} -shifts.



1
Introduction

One of the challenges of theoretical physics is to identify toy models that capture some aspect of a physical system while stripping away much of its complexity. In a more realistic setting, certain properties of a given model may be obscured by the intricacy of the problem or by the prohibitive effort required to compute its consequences. Toy models allow to focus on the essence of the problem while keeping just enough structure to make progress. Time and again this approach has led to new insights and informed our understanding of problems that were previously thought intractable.

The aim of this article is to bring attention to one such toy model for the study of string-like scattering amplitudes, the
inverse string theory Kawai-Lewellen-Tye (KLT) kernel
,

\displaystyle m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{n}\!\equiv(S_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}})^{-1}.

First studied thoroughly in
47
;
46
, the stringy matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
of the inverse KLT kernel were recognized to be simple functions of the external kinematics at finite
\alpha^{\prime}
. As such, they retain many of the properties of string amplitudes such as an infinite resonance structure or the monodromy relations, a characteristic feature of the worldsheet formulation of string theory.
They provide an interesting trade-off between the field theory limit where string-like features of amplitudes are often lost or incomplete and, on the other side of the complexity spectrum, bosonic string amplitudes, or
Z
-theory functions
20
;
43
;
25
;
24
, which are guaranteed to exhibit stringy behavior but often implement it in an intransparent way, since closed expressions for these amplitudes are not known beyond five points
8
.

In fact, we will argue that the inverse KLT kernel might possibly be the
simplest
toy model of stringy amplitudes, not just at tree-level but to all loop orders. We support this claim by formulating a new kind of recursion relation, called the
cubic Berends-Giele recursion
, for the tree-level matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
that exposes an underlying equivalence of the inverse KLT kernel and amplitudes in the cubic bi-adjoint scalar (BAS) or tr
\phi^{3}
field theory
44
.
This structural similarity reveals a previously unknown simplicity of the inverse KLT kernel which, in its conventional formulation in terms of Feynman diagrams, is obscured by an infinite tower of interaction terms
47
.

More importantly, by generalizing the cubic Berends-Giele recursion to loop integrands, we reveal the existence of a loop-level analogue of the inverse KLT kernel which we call the
inverse KLT integrand
. This integrand represents a simple stringy analogue of the surface integrand for the
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
theory recently studied in
2
;
3
involving only scalar resonances. We will show how the inverse KLT integrand naturally implements the recently observed close connection between amplitudes in the
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
theory and pions in the non-linear sigma model (NLSM)
39
via kinematic
\alpha^{\prime}
-shifts similar to those described in
14
;
15
for the tree-level inverse KLT kernel.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we provide a short review of the cubic bi-adjoint scalar theory and how its amplitudes can be efficiently computed using the well-known Berends-Giele recursion relations. We then introduce the inverse string theory KLT kernel and discuss some of its relevant features as well as its connection to stringy pion amplitudes via the kinematic
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift. Section
3
is then concerned with formulating the novel Berends-Giele recursion relations for the matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
, exposing the cubic simplicity of the inverse string theory KLT kernel. In Section
4
we use the cubic Berends-Giele recursion relation to define the
inverse KLT integrand

m_{L,n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
at
L
loops and
n
points. We first focus on the one-loop case where we touch on the connection to the surface integrand
2
;
3
defined for the
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
field theory. The recursion is then extended to the general
L
loop case. Finally, in Section
5
we briefly outline how the
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift connects the inverse KLT integrand to a suitably stringified version of the “perfect” NLSM integrand for pions constructed in
6

2
Review

In this section we introduce some notational conventions used in this article. We also review the general structure of Berends-Giele recursion relations
17
and their application to the tr
\phi^{3}
theory
44
. Further, we provide a short introduction to the inverse string theory KLT kernel
47

Notation

Throughout this article we will only consider ordered amplitudes
A_{n}(p_{1}\,p_{\sigma(2)}p_{\sigma(3)}\dots p_{\sigma(n)})
where
p_{i}
are on-shell, external momenta
p_{i}^{2}\,{=}\,0
and
\sigma\,{\in}\,S_{n-1}
denotes some ordering of particle labels
\{2\dots n\}
. In particular, we will be studying amplitudes of scalar particles exclusively, which are naturally functions just of momentum invariants
p_{i}\cdotp p_{j}
. A convenient basis for these invariants at
n
points (disregarding Gram determinant relations) is given by the planar Mandelstam variables

\displaystyle X_{ij}=(p_{i}+p_{i+1}+\dots+p_{j-1})^{2},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for }1\leq i<j\leq n,

(1)

satisfying
X_{ij}=X_{ji}
and on-shell conditions
X_{i,i+1}=0
. All indices are treated as cyclic, identifying labels
n+1\equiv 1
.
The advantage of using a Mandelstam basis is that a given scalar amplitude
A_{n}
has a unique representation in terms of the variables (
1
),

\displaystyle A_{n}\equiv A_{n}(X_{ij})\equiv A_{n}(X).

(2)

Given this unique
X
-
representation
, it will be convenient for us to adopt a notation where we consider the amplitude as a formal function of the
X
-labels
appearing as subscripts in the
X_{ij}
variables. That is, for some abstract set of labels
\{a_{1}\dots a_{n}\}
we define the
X
-
label representation
of an amplitude by appropriately plugging the labels
a_{i}
into the
X
-representation (
2
) of the amplitude,

\displaystyle A_{n}(a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{n})\equiv A_{n}(X_{a_{i}\,a_{j}}).

(3)

To give an example, we can define a function of four labels

\displaystyle m_{4}(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4})=\frac{1}{X_{a_{1}a_{3}}}+\frac{1}{X_{a_{2}a_{4}}}.

(4)

Evaluated on the specific set of labels
\{a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}\}\,{=}\,\{1234\}
this function yields the four-point bi-adjoint scalar amplitude (cf. (
10
)),

\displaystyle m_{4}(1234)=\frac{1}{X_{13}}+\frac{1}{X_{24}}\equiv\frac{1}{(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}}+\frac{1}{(p_{2}+p_{3})^{2}}\,,

(5)

once the abstract
X
-variables are identified with on-shell momenta according to (
1
).
Note that in principle we allow
X
-labels to take any values such that e.g.
m_{4}(*19\beta)=X_{*,9}^{-1}+X_{1,\beta}^{-1}
makes sense as a formal function of
X
-labels but not necessarily as an amplitude.

The main reason to use the
X
-label representations (
2
) and (
3
) rather than the usual on-shell momentum representation is that it allows to canonically “off-shellize” an amplitude. Starting with an amplitude
A(X_{ij})
as a function of
X
-variables (on-shell amplitudes depend only on
X_{ij}
with
j\geq i+2
) we may parameterize an off-shell variant of it by adding terms proportional to
X_{i,i+1}\equiv p_{i}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{A}(X_{ij},X_{i,i+1})=A(X_{ij})+\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k,k+1}\,F_{k}(X_{ij},X_{i,i+1}),

(6)

Assuming that the functions
F_{k}
do not blow up as any
X_{i,i+1}\to 0
, this allows to canonically lift an on-shell amplitude to an off-shell function. Concretely, we demand that the off-shell function, like the on-shell amplitude, should remain independent of the off-shellness variables
X_{i,i+1}
. This can be achieved trivially by setting all
F_{k}\equiv 0
in (
6
) and defining

\displaystyle\tilde{A}(X_{ij},X_{i,i+1})=\tilde{A}(X_{ij})\equiv A(X_{ij}).

(7)

In other words, we identify the on-shell amplitude and its canonical “off-shellization” as functions of
X_{ij}
. While this construction is certainly not unique, it will turn out to be the right choice for the purposes of defining recursion relations for the bi-adjoint scalar theory as well as the inverse KLT kernel discussed subsequently. Because of (
7
) we will usually not distinguish between the amplitude and its canonical off-shell function and denote both as
A(X_{ij})
or just
A(X)
.

2.1
Bi-Adjoint Scalar Theory (BAS)

Since we will refer to it throughout this article, we give a very brief introduction to the so-called bi-adjoint scalar theory (BAS). Conventionally, it can be defined in terms of the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{BAS}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial\phi^{a\bar{a}}\cdot\partial\phi^{a\bar{a}}-\frac{1}{3!}f_{abc}f_{\bar{a}\bar{b}\bar{c}}\phi^{a\bar{a}}\phi^{b\bar{b}}\phi^{c\bar{c}},

(8)

where
\phi^{a\bar{a}}
is a doubly-charged scalar under two non-abelian groups, which, for concreteness, we will take to be
U(N)\times U(\bar{N})
. Amplitudes in this theory can be color-ordered with respect to both groups,

\displaystyle m_{n}^{a_{1}\bar{a}_{1}\dots a_{n}\bar{a}_{n}}=\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\sigma,\rho\in S_{n-1}}\!\!\!\!\text{tr}(t^{a_{1}}t^{a_{\sigma(2)}}\dots t^{a_{\sigma(n)}})\text{tr}(t^{\bar{a}_{1}}t^{\bar{a}_{\rho(2)}}\dots t^{\bar{a}_{\rho(n)}})\,m_{n}(1\sigma|1\rho),

(9)

where
t^{a},t^{\bar{a}}
denote the generators of
U(N)
and
U(\bar{N})
respectively. The doubly stripped amplitudes
m_{n}(1\sigma|1\rho)
can be computed from the Lagrangian (
8
) by summing over all trivalent Feynman graphs which are compatible with both orderings
\{{1\sigma\}}
and
\{1\rho\}
as described in
22
. For example, at
n=3
we have
m_{3}(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})=-m_{3}(\mathds{1}|132)=1
while for
n=4,5
we can compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{4}(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})&=\frac{1}{X_{13}}\!+\!\frac{1}{X_{24}},\hskip 8.5359ptm_{4}(\mathds{1}|1243)=-\frac{1}{X_{13}},\hskip 8.5359ptm_{4}(\mathds{1}|1324)=-\frac{1}{X_{24}},\\
m_{5}(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})&=\frac{1}{X_{13}X_{14}}+\text{cyc.},\hskip 8.5359ptm_{5}(\mathds{1}|13245)=\frac{1}{X_{14}}\left(\!\frac{1}{X_{13}}\!+\!\frac{1}{X_{24}}\!\right),\end{split}

(10)

where
\mathds{1}=\{1\dots n\}
always denotes the identity permutation of
n
labels and the momentum invariants
X_{ij}
are as defined in (
1
). In the following we will refer to matrix elements
m_{n}\!\left(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1}\right)
as
diagonal
whereas
off-diagonal
matrix elements are those
m_{n}\!\left(\mathds{1}|\rho\right)
with
\rho\neq\mathds{1}
.

However, throughout this article we will exclusively study the properties of diagonal matrix elements
m_{n}\,{\equiv}\,m_{n}(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})
. Since both orderings
\sigma\,{=}\,\rho
agree for diagonal matrix elements, they can be equivalently computed as amplitudes of a singly charged scalar in the so-called
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
theory,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{tr}\phi^{3}}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\partial\phi\cdot\partial\phi\rangle-\frac{1}{3!}\langle\phi^{3}\rangle,

(11)

where
\langle\dots\rangle\equiv\text{tr}(\dots)
denotes the fundamental trace over the fields
\phi=\phi^{a}t^{a}
. For this reason we will refer to the matrix elements
m_{n}
as diagonal BAS or
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
amplitudes interchangeably.

2.2
Berends-Giele Recursion for diagonal BAS amplitudes

While diagonal BAS amplitudes
m_{n}
can be calculated directly from the Lagrangian (
8
) as sums over cubic Feynman graphs, more efficient methods have been devised to facilitate their computation. Of particular relevance for the present article are the Berends-Giele recursion relations derived in
44
. In this section we will recast some of the results obtained there into the
X
-label representation (
3
) in terms of which the recursion for the matrix elements
m_{n}
takes a particularly simple form. This will also allow us to introduce
Berends-Giele diagrams
as useful notation to facilitate later generalizations to stringy matrix elements and loop integrands.

The Basics

Before getting into the specifics of amplitudes in the BAS theory, let us briefly remark on the general idea behind the Berends-Giele approach
17
. At the most basic level, the Berends-Giele recursion can be understood as an efficient regrouping, or partitioning, of Feynman diagrams into recursively definable building blocks, called
Berends-Giele currents
.

This partitioning is accomplished as follows. First, a particular (say the
n
-th) external line is fixed in each Feynman diagram, designating the so-called
root leg
. We then follow this line into the diagram until it first meets a vertex, which we will correspondingly call the
root vertex

V
. Supposing the vertex has valency
v
then the remaining external lines can be partitioned into
v{-}1
non-overlapping sets
\{1\dots n{-}1\}=\{P_{V,1}|P_{V,2}|\dots|P_{V,v-1}\}
depending on where they connect to the root vertex. Generically there will be multiple partitions
P_{V}
compatible with a given root vertex
V
. The sum of all Feynman diagrams with a particular root vertex
V
and a fixed partition
P_{V}
of external momenta can then be associated with a
Berends-Giele diagram
as shown on the right-hand side of Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
A generic Berends-Giele diagram is specified by a root vertex
V
(valency
v
) and a compatible partition
P_{V}
of external legs. The grey blobs can be identified with lower-point semi-on-shell amplitudes or Berends-Giele currents. The
n
-point amplitude is obtained recursively by summing over all Berends-Giele diagrams.

Since a given Berends-Giele diagram represents the sum over all Feynman diagrams compatible with a partition
P_{V}
, each gray blob in Figure
1
can essentially be identified with a
(|P_{V,i}|{+}1)
-point amplitude where the line connecting to the root vertex is off-shell. These are the Berends-Giele currents in
44
for which the recursion is conventionally defined.

The full amplitude is obtained by summing over all vertices
V
in the theory (as, for example, specified by a given Lagrangian) and compatible partitions
P_{V}
of the external lines. This amounts to a sum over all Berends-Giele diagrams (again, see Figure
1
).

Since, throughout this article, we are only considering scalar functions, we will always identify Berends-Giele currents (i.e. amplitudes with one or more legs off-shell) with the on-shell amplitudes themselves via the canonical
off-shellization
(
7
) of their
X
-label representation. Omitting this (in our case trivial) distinction allows us to talk about recursion relations for amplitudes and matrix elements directly without further complicating the presentation with jargon.

Berends-Giele for
m_{n}(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})

Let us now specify the discussion to diagonal bi-adjoint scalar (or tr
\phi^{3}
) amplitudes
m_{n}\equiv m_{n}(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})
with canonical ordering
\mathds{1}{=}\{1\dots n\}
and show how this dramatically simplifies the generic form of the Berends-Giele recursion in Figure
1
.

Since the BAS theory is cubic, there is only a single trivalent root vertex from the Lagrangian (
8
) entering the recursion which is given by

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BASrootVert.pdf}\end{matrix}\,=\,1.

(12)

Consequently, any partition
P
of external lines compatible with the root vertex (
12
) must be into two sets
\{1\dots n{-}1\}=\{P_{L}|P_{R}\}
. Moreover, since we are considering an ordered amplitude, each set
P_{L},P_{R}
must itself be ordered. This only leaves a total of
n{-}2
possible partitions of the form
\{P_{j,L}|P_{j,R}\}=\{1\dots j{-}1|j\dots n{-}1\}
where
j=2,\dots,n{-}1
. Summing over the corresponding Berends-Giele diagrams we obtain the
n
-point diagonal matrix element,

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-4.26773pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagBAS1.pdf}\end{matrix}+\hskip-5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-4.26773pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagBAS2.pdf}\end{matrix}\\
&\hskip 12.80365pt+\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\hskip 2.84544pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-4.26773pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagBAS.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\,.\end{split}

(13)

Since the theory is cubic, there are only up to two sub-amplitudes
m_{L/R}
appearing in each Berends-Giele diagram. This fact makes the Berends-Giele recursion highly efficient, as in each recursive step only
n{-}2
terms need to be summed to obtain
m_{n}
.

We can easily read off the algebraic expression for the recursion in
X
-label representation from the Berends-Giele diagrams (
13
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{n}(1\dots n)&=\frac{1}{X_{2n}}\,m_{n-1}(2\dots n)+\frac{1}{X_{1n-1}}\,m_{n-1}(1\dots n{-}1)\\
&+\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\frac{1}{X_{1j}X_{jn}}\,m_{j}(1\dots j)m_{n-j+1}(j\dots n).\end{split}

(14)

Note that for the first two contributions there is only one explicit propagator as either the left- or right-hand momentum corresponds to an on-shell external line
X_{L/R}\,{=}\,0
. Furthermore, the recursion generates matrix elements
m_{n}
in a form that is manifestly independent of the off-shellness variables
X_{i,i+1}
, meaning they are already canonically off-shellized according to (
7
). We can therefore directly use matrix elements obtained through (
14
) as input for the next iteration of the recursion without having to distinguish between on-shell amplitudes and (semi-on-shell) Berends-Giele currents.

The Berends-Giele recursion (
14
) computes amplitudes simply as sums of products involving lower point amplitudes. This makes the recursion particularly amenable for implementation on a computer since there are no non-trivial kinematic shifts to keep track of as is the case for a BCFW recursion. Adding to its efficiency, the Berends-Giele recursion generates amplitudes without introducing spurious poles. In fact, no cancellations between different Berends-Giele diagrams occur.

n\,{=}\,5
)

To get comfortable with the notation, let us illustrate the recursion (
13
) and compute the diagonal matrix element
m_{5}
. At
n=5
only three Berends-Giele diagrams contribute,

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07182ptm_{5}=\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-8.5359pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiag5pt1.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\,+\,\,\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-11.9501pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiag5pt3.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\,+\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-8.5359pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiag5pt2.pdf}\end{matrix}\,,

(15)

corresponding to partitions
P=\{1|234\},\{12|34\},\{123|4\}
. From the diagrams it is straightforward to read off the expressions,

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{5}(\mathds{1})&=\frac{1}{X_{25}}m_{4}(2345)+\frac{1}{X_{14}}m_{4}(1234)+\frac{1}{X_{13}X_{35}}m_{3}(123)m_{3}(345)\\

(16)

and correctly recover the five-point matrix element in (

).

2.3
The inverse String Theory KLT Kernel

In this section we briefly review some of the properties of the inverse string theory Kawai-Lewellen-Tye (KLT) kernel
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(\sigma|\rho)
first studied in
47
;
46
. Our discussion here will largely follow the initial work, and is mainly intended to introduce some of the notations and conventions used throughout this article.

The string theory KLT kernel
S_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
was first introduced in a seminal paper by Kawai, Lewellen, and Tye
40
who observed that closed string amplitudes
M^{\text{cl}}
could be written as a “square” of ordered open string amplitudes
A^{\text{o}}(\sigma)
when appropriately sewn together by a scalar function
S^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{n}\!(\sigma|\rho)
,

\displaystyle M_{n}^{\text{cl}}=\!\!\!\sum_{\sigma,\rho\in S_{n-3}}\!\!\!A_{n}^{\text{o}}(1\,\sigma\,n{-}1\,n)\,S^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{n}\!(\sigma|\rho)\,A_{n}^{\text{o}}(1\,\rho\,n\,n{-}1),

(17)

where the sum runs over all orderings
\sigma,\rho
of
n{-}3
particle labels
\{2\dots n{-}2\}
. Importantly, the KLT kernel
S^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{n}(\sigma|\rho)
admits a simple closed-form expression
18
involving trigonometric functions of the external kinematics.

In
47
;
46
Mizera initiated the study of the matrix
inverse
of the KLT kernel,

\displaystyle m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{n}\!\equiv(S_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}})^{-1},

(18)

and showed that its matrix elements
m^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!\left(\sigma|\rho\right)
can similarly be written as simple rational functions of the stringy planar Mandelstam variables,

\displaystyle t_{ij}=\tan(\pi\alpha^{\prime}X_{ij}),\hskip 14.22636pts_{ij}=\sin(\pi\alpha^{\prime}X_{ij}).

(19)

These variables inherit all the properties of the field theory
X
-variables (
1
), i.e.
t_{ij}=t_{ji}
,
t_{i,i+1}=0
and similar for
s_{ij}
. Expanding for small
\alpha^{\prime}
we have
t_{ij}\simeq s_{ij}=\pi\alpha^{\prime}X_{ij}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{\prime 3})
.

Some of the simplest examples for the matrix elements of the inverse string theory KLT kernel include the three-point functions
m_{3}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1}\right)=-m_{3}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\mathds{1}|132\right)=1
as well as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&m_{4}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!\left(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1}\right)=\frac{1}{t_{13}}+\frac{1}{t_{24}},\hskip 14.22636ptm_{4}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!\left(\mathds{1}|1243\right)=-\frac{1}{s_{13}},\hskip 14.22636ptm_{4}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!\left(\mathds{1}|1324\right)=-\frac{1}{s_{24}},\\
&m_{5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!\left(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1}\right)=\left(\!\frac{1}{t_{13}t_{14}}\!+\text{cyc.}\!\right)\!+\!1,\hskip 31.2982ptm_{5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!\left(\mathds{1}|13245\right)=\frac{1}{s_{14}}\!\left(\!\frac{1}{t_{13}}\!+\!\frac{1}{t_{24}}\!\right)\!,\\
&m_{6}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!\left(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1}\right)=\frac{1}{t_{13}t_{14}t_{15}}\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{t_{13}t_{14}t_{46}}\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{t_{13}t_{36}t_{46}}\!+\!\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{t_{13}t_{15}t_{35}}\!+\!\frac{1}{t_{13}}\!+\text{cyc.},\end{split}

(20)

at four, five and six points. From (
20
) we see that diagonal matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\left(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1}\right)
depend on
X_{ij}
only through tangents
t_{ij}
, whereas off-diagonal elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!\left(\mathds{1}|\rho\right)
,
\rho\neq\mathds{1}
generically involve
t_{ij}
and
s_{ij}
. Again, only diagonal matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\equiv m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})
will be considered subsequently.

Diagonal matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
can be systematically computed using diagrammatic rules
47
. In particular, they can be obtained as sums over all tree-level Feynman diagrams built from stringy propagators and vertices

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-12.80365pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/KLTprop.pdf}\end{matrix}\,=\,\frac{1}{\tan(\pi\alpha^{\prime}p^{2})},\hskip 42.67912pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/KLToddVert.pdf}\end{matrix}\hskip-19.91684pt\equiv\kappa_{n}=\begin{cases}C_{\frac{n-3}{2}}\hskip 22.76228pt\text{ if }\,n\geq 3\text{ odd},\\
0\hskip 42.67912pt\text{ if }\,n\text{ even},\end{cases}

(21)

with
C_{n}
denoting the
n
-th Catalan number. It can be shown that these Feynman rules follow directly from the inverse KLT interaction Lagrangian
46

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{KLT}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big\langle\!\phi-\frac{1}{4}\arcsin(2\phi)-\frac{1}{2}\phi\sqrt{1-4\phi^{2}}\Big\rangle,

(22)

when expanded into an infinite series in powers of
\phi
. As in the case of the Lagrangian (
11
) for the
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
theory,
\langle\dots\rangle=\text{tr}(\dots)
denotes the fundamental trace of the fields
\phi^{i}_{\,j}=\phi^{a}(t^{a})^{i}_{\,j}
.

Some properties of
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}

Using the Feynman rules (
21
), the lowest order diagonal matrix elements (up to

) in (

A distinctive feature of the stringy inverse KLT kernel, when compared to amplitudes in the BAS theory (
8

\sim C_{0}\,{=}\,1

1/X_{ij}\to 1/t_{ij}
according to (

\alpha^{\prime}\,{\to}\,0
each such propagator contributes a factor
1/\alpha^{\prime}
to the overall scaling of the diagram.

It is now easy to see that the additional diagrams in (
23

\geq 5
, only contribute at sub-leading orders in
\alpha^{\prime}
when compared to purely cubic diagrams. This is because at fixed
n
trivalent graphs allow for the largest number of propagators (
n\,{-}\,3
). This ensures that at the leading order in the limit
\alpha^{\prime}\,{\to}\,0
only cubic graphs contribute and we recover amplitudes of the tr
\phi^{3}
theory,

\displaystyle m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=(\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{3-n}\big(m_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{\prime})\big).

(24)

Conversely, taking a bottom-up perspective, we can consider the inverse KLT kernel as a
stringification
of BAS amplitudes. Due to propagators being of the form
t_{ij}^{-1}
, diagonal matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})
exhibit a periodic pole structure with simple poles when some

\displaystyle X_{ij}=k/\alpha^{\prime},\hskip 14.22636ptk\in\mathds{Z},

(25)

on all of which they consistently factorize into a product of two lower-point matrix elements,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Res}}_{X_{ij}=k/\alpha^{\prime}}m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(t_{ij})=m_{L}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(t_{L})\,m_{R}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(t_{R}).

(26)

From (
25
) and (
26
) we gather that the inverse KLT kernel exhibits an infinite spectrum of resonances corresponding to states of positive
and negative
mass squared
m^{2}=0,\pm\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}},\pm\frac{2}{\alpha^{\prime}},...
, all of which correspond to scalar excitations. This represents quite an unconventional stringification of BAS amplitudes, especially when compared to
Z
-theory

;
43
;
25
;
24
which possesses a more physical spectrum. Nevertheless the inverse KLT matrix elements exhibit many stringy properties, such as the monodromy relations,

\displaystyle m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(\mathds{1}|\mathds{1})+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}e^{ix_{k}}\,m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(2\dots k\,1\,k{+}1\dots n|\mathds{1})=0,

(27)

with phases given by
x_{k}\,{=}\,\pi\alpha^{\prime}2p_{1}\!\cdot\!(p_{2}+\dots+p_{k})
, and the associated (stringy)
hidden zeros
studied extensively in the recent literature

;
5
;
41
;
12
;
23
.

Another distinct advantage of the inverse KLT kernel is that it can be efficiently computed in closed form. In fact, we will show that there is a concrete sense in which the matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
can be considered the simplest stringification of the tr
\phi^{3}
theory. The situation is more complicated for other known stringifications of bi-adjoint scalars such as the (non-)abelian
Z
-functions for which explicit expressions are not known beyond
n\,{=}\,5
. The inverse KLT kernel can therefore serve as a particularly simple toy model for the study of stringy amplitudes, not just for bi-adjoint scalars but also for NLSM pions as we will now show.

2.4
Pions and the
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift

Recently it was shown
14
that the inverse KLT kernel also directly encodes stringified pion amplitudes in the non-linear sigma model (NLSM) as well as stringy mixed amplitudes involving both bi-adjoint scalars
\phi
and pions
\pi
.

Many amplitudes involving pions were shown to be
equivalent
to the diagonal inverse KLT matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
via so-called
\alpha^{\prime}
-shifts. The latter amount to simple
\alpha^{\prime}
-dependent deformations of the kinematic
X
-variables. In the following, we will quickly review the
\alpha^{\prime}
-shifts for the specific case of pure pion amplitudes.

To define the
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift for pure pion amplitudes it will be necessary to make a distinction between even- and odd-particle channels. Concretely, for the channel corresponding to
X_{ij}
we define its
parity
as

\displaystyle P(X_{ij})=\begin{cases}\text{even,}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ if }\hskip 5.69046ptp(i)=p(j),\\
\text{odd,}\hskip 17.07182pt\text{ if }\hskip 5.69046ptp(i)\neq p(j),\end{cases}

(28)

where
p(n)=\text{even/odd}
refers to the even- or oddness (i.e. parity) of the integer
n
. The definition (
28
) is such that it assigns even/odd parity to even/odd-particle channels. We now split up the set of
X
-variables according to their parity, defining

\displaystyle X_{\text{even/odd}}=\{X_{ij},\,P(X_{ij})=\text{even/odd}\}.

(29)

Of course the set of all
X\,{=}\,X_{\text{even}}{\cup}X_{\text{odd}}
. The
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift consists of two parts. First, we rescale

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}\to\alpha^{\prime}/2,

(30)

and then define
\alpha^{\prime}
-shifted
X
-variables via

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{\text{even}}=X_{\text{even}}\pm 1/\alpha^{\prime},\hskip 14.22636pt\hat{X}_{\text{odd}}=X_{\text{odd}}.

(31)

Let us now see how this shift acts on the stringy
t_{ij}
variables (
19
) of the inverse KLT kernel. Thanks to simple trigonometric identities the
t
-variables transform under (
30
) and (
31
) as,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t_{\text{even}}}\to-\tau_{\text{even}},\hskip 28.45274pt\frac{1}{t_{\text{odd}}}\to\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{odd}}}.

(32)

Here we use
\tau_{ij}=\tan(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha^{\prime}X_{ij})
to denote the
\alpha^{\prime}
-rescaled stringy variables
t_{ij}
.

Stringy Pions

For
n\,{=}\,2k
even multiplicity, we now consider the inverse KLT matrix elements
m_{2k}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(X)
directly as functions of
X
-variables. We can then define
stringy pion functions

A_{2k}^{\pi,\alpha^{\prime}}
by first applying the rescaling (
30
) to obtain
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}\!/2}
and then evaluating the
\alpha^{\prime}
-rescaled matrix element on
\alpha^{\prime}
-shifted kinematics (
31
), i.e.

\displaystyle A_{2k}^{\pi,\alpha^{\prime}}(X)=m_{2k}^{\alpha^{\prime}\!/2}(\hat{X}).

(33)

The rescaling
\alpha^{\prime}\to\alpha^{\prime}/2
is not essential but ensures that the stringy pion functions (
33

27
) as the inverse KLT matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
in their original tr
\phi^{3}
form.
For reference, we reproduce here the simplest stringy pion functions at four and six points,

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{4}^{\pi,\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!&=-\tau_{13}-\tau_{24},\\
A_{6}^{\pi,\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!&=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\tau_{13}+\tau_{24})(\tau_{46}+\tau_{15})}{\tau_{14}}-\tau_{13}-\frac{1}{3}\tau_{13}\tau_{35}\tau_{15}+\text{cyc.}\end{split}

(34)

Using the transformation rules (
32
) and the explicit expressions (
20
) for the inverse KLT matrix elements the reader can easily convince themselves of the correctness of (
34
).
The stringy pion functions reduce to ordinary
SU(N)
non-linear sigma model (NLSM) amplitudes at leading order in
\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle A_{2k}^{\pi,\alpha^{\prime}}\!=\frac{\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\Big(\!A_{2k}^{\text{NLSM}}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{\prime 2})\!\Big).

(35)

Moreover, they obey the Adler zero
1
at finite
\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle\lim_{p_{i}\to 0}A_{2k}^{\pi,\alpha^{\prime}}=0,

(36)

retaining another highly characteristic feature of pion amplitudes.

It should be mentioned that the structure of the
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift (
31

\delta
-shift

10

\phi^{3}
theory amplitudes. The latter allows to extract NLSM field theory amplitudes directly from those of the tr
\phi^{3}

\delta\,{\equiv}\,1/\alpha^{\prime}
, and expanding the amplitude around
\delta\,{=}\,\infty

\displaystyle m_{2k}(\hat{X}(\delta))=\frac{1}{\delta^{2k-2}}\left(A_{2k}^{\text{NLSM}}(X)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{-1})\right).

(37)

We can obtain a very similar looking relation involving the stringy inverse KLT kernel by combining (
33

35

\displaystyle m^{\alpha^{\prime}\!/2}_{2k}(\hat{X}(\alpha^{\prime}))=\frac{\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\left(A_{2k}^{\text{NLSM}}(X)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{\prime 2})\right).

(38)

The equivalence of the leading order contributions to the
\delta

\phi^{3}

\alpha^{\prime}
-shifted inverse KLT kernel (

\delta

\alpha^{\prime}
-shift can be given a geometric interpretation via the so-called associahedral grid
15

However, beyond the leading order the
\delta
-shifted tr
\phi^{3}

\alpha^{\prime}
-shifted inverse KLT kernel generate different higher-order corrections to pion amplitudes. For instance, comparing (
37

38

\displaystyle m_{4}(\hat{X}(\delta))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\left(\!-(X_{13}+X_{24})+\frac{1}{\delta}(X_{13}^{2}-X_{24}^{2})-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}(X_{13}^{3}+X_{24}^{3})+\dots\right)\!,

(39)

\displaystyle m^{\alpha^{\prime}\!/2}_{4}(\hat{X}(\alpha^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\left(\!-(X_{13}+X_{24})-\frac{(\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}{12}(X_{13}^{3}+X_{24}^{3})-\frac{(\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{4}}{120}(X_{13}^{5}+X_{24}^{5})+\dots\right)\!.

-shift is carefully chosen such that the
\alpha^{\prime}
-complete four-point function (

While both (

3
Cubic Recursion for the Stringy Inverse KLT Kernel (Trees)

Let us now turn to the first main result of this article. It lies in the realization that the infinite tower of interactions (
21
) for the inverse KLT kernel is spurious and can be replaced by a single, suitably defined
cubic
interaction vertex. Notably, this simplification does not occur in the conventional Feynman diagram representation of the inverse KLT kernel but only manifests itself when the matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
are recast in the form of a
new kind of
Berends-Giele recursion, which we will simply call the
cubic Berends-Giele recursion
. The latter allows for efficient computation of the stringy matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
and exposes their essential equivalence to amplitudes in the tr
\phi^{3}
field theory.

Importantly, the cubic Berends-Giele recursion derived in this section for the tree-level inverse KLT kernel will serve as a natural foundation for the later definition of the inverse KLT loop integrand in Section
4
.

3.1
Diagonal Matrix Elements

To start, let us briefly consider the structure of what we will call the
naive
Berends-Giele recursion for the stringy matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
based on the inverse KLT Lagrangian (
22
).
Considering the generic form of the Berends-Giele recursion in Figure
1
, and applying it to the stringy inverse KLT kernel, we expect that at
n
points all root vertices with multiplicity
{\leq}\,n
have to enter the recursion. Schematically, the recursion of
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
therefore involves Berends-Giele diagrams of the form

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptm_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\simeq\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-8.5359pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagV3Smpl.pdf}\end{matrix}\!\!\!\!+\,\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\geq 5\\
\text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{n}\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip 36.98866pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagVSmpl.pdf}\end{matrix},

(45)

The first term above is analogous to the cubic Berends-Giele diagrams (
13
) for the tr
\phi^{3}
theory, except that internal lines now correspond to stringy propagators as defined in (
21
).
The remaining terms in (
45
) combine contributions from Feynman diagrams where the root leg is connected to
5-,7-
and higher-point root vertices of valency
v
. Indeed, the naive recursion (
45
) is valid in principle and can be used to compute
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. However, its structure is not particularly compelling as it canonically follows from the Lagrangian (
22
).

A cubic simplification

We will now show that the naive recursion (
45
) based on the infinite tower of interactions (
21
) fails to take advantage of a remarkable hidden simplicity of the inverse KLT kernel. In fact, the recursive structure of the stringy matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
turns out to be
just as simple
as that of their field theory counterparts
m_{n}
(
13
) in the tr
\phi^{3}
theory. In particular,
all
higher valency diagrams (
v\,{\geq}\,5
) in (
45
) can be absorbed into a simple
\alpha^{\prime}
-dependent redefinition of the cubic root vertex

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-8.5359pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/EFFrootVert.pdf}\end{matrix}=1+\tan(\pi\alpha^{\prime}X_{L})\tan(\pi\alpha^{\prime}X_{R}),

(46)

where, as before,
X_{L/R}
are the invariants associated to the momenta flowing into the vertex from the left/right. In particular, if either of the left/right-hand momenta is on-shell, i.e. if
X_{L/R}\,{=}\,0
, the stringy root vertex becomes independent of
\alpha^{\prime}
and reduces to the tr
\phi^{3}
field theory vertex (
12
). We encourage the reader to pause for a moment and familiarize themselves with the remarkably simple structure of the
effective root vertex
(
46
). In a sense, the remainder of this article will be concerned just with working out the consequences of this vertex for the inverse KLT kernel at tree- and loop-level.

The first surprising consequence of the structure of the effective root vertex (
46
) is that it allows us to formulate a
purely cubic
Berends-Giele recursion for the matrix elements of the inverse KLT kernel,

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-4.26773pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagKLT1.pdf}\end{matrix}+\hskip-5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-4.26773pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagKLT2.pdf}\end{matrix}\\
&\hskip 15.6491pt+\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\!\!\begin{matrix}\hskip 5.69046pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagKLT3.pdf}\end{matrix}.\end{split}

(47)

Comparing the Berends-Giele diagrams above to the ones for the tr
\phi^{3}

46
) for the inverse KLT kernel. Notably, the first two terms in (

\phi^{3}
root vertex, as either the left- or right-hand momentum entering the effective vertex is on-shell.

Evaluating the Berends-Giele diagrams in (

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(1\dots n)&=\frac{1}{t_{2n}}m_{n-1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(2\dots n)+\frac{1}{t_{1,n-1}}m_{n-1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n{-}1)\\
&+\!\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\frac{1+t_{1j}t_{jn}}{t_{1j}t_{jn}}\,m_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(1\dots j)m_{n-j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(j\dots n).\end{split}

(48)

where all matrix elements are considered to be in
X

Of course, the validity of the cubic Berends-Giele recursion (
48
) is not at all obvious from the way we have stated the result, and we will prove its consistency shortly. First, however, we want to show how the recursion works for a simple, but non-trivial example and discuss some of its properties.

A simple example (
n\,{=}\,5
)

Let us demonstrate the cubic recursion (

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07182ptm_{5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-8.5359pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiag5pt1alpha.pdf}\end{matrix}\,+\,\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-13.08846pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiag5pt3alpha.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\,+\!\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-8.5359pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiag5pt2alpha.pdf}\end{matrix}.

(49)

Noting the similarity to the tr
\phi^{3}
recursion (

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\frac{1}{t_{25}}m_{4}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(2345)+\frac{1}{t_{14}}m_{4}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(1234)+\frac{1+t_{13}t_{35}}{t_{13}t_{35}}m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{3}\!(123)m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{3}\!(345)\\

which agrees with the matrix element shown in (

We will study how the recursion generates these contact interactions more generally once we prove the validity of the cubic Berends-Giele recursion at arbitrary multiplicity in Section

.

Discussion

By collapsing the infinite tower of interactions (
21
) to just a single, stringy cubic vertex (
46
), the recursion (
48
) achieves a drastic reduction in the number of Berends-Giele diagrams required to compute a given matrix element
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. When compared to the naive Berends-Giele recursion (
45
), for which the number of diagrams grows exponentially as
2^{n-3}
, the cubic recursion (
47
) requires only
n{-}2
contributions. This shows that from a computational point of view the string theory inverse KLT kernel is just as simple as amplitudes in the tr
\phi^{3}
theory. The apparent complexity of the stringy matrix elements associated with the infinite tower of odd-point interactions (
21
) is spurious.

It is no surprise then, that owing to the structure of the effective root vertex, the field theory limit (
24
) of the stringy matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
is manifest when expressed in terms of the cubic Berends-Giele recursion. Since

\displaystyle\frac{1+t_{1j}t_{jn}}{t_{1j}t_{jn}}\xrightarrow{\,\alpha^{\prime}\to 0\,}\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime 2}X_{1j}X_{jn}}+\mathcal{O}(1),

(51)

each Berends-Giele diagram in (
48
) precisely reduces to the corresponding diagram in the recursion (
13
) for bi-adjoint scalars.

Its similarity to the recursion for the tr
\phi^{3}
theory notwithstanding, it should be emphasized that the Berends-Giele recursion (
48
) is highly non-trivial and exposes a previously unknown simplicity of the stringy matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. In particular, the cubic recursion
cannot
be understood as a naive regrouping of appropriately redefined cubic Feynman diagrams.
To illustrate this, let us suppose that the recursion (
50
)
is
indeed just a regrouping of properly defined cubic Feynman diagrams. The structure of the root vertex (
46
) suggests that there should be a Lagrangian that generates a single cubic off-shell vertex

\displaystyle V_{3}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/3vertOFF.pdf}\end{matrix}=1+t_{1}t_{2}+t_{2}t_{3}+t_{3}t_{1},

(52)

where we use the notation
t_{i}\,{=}\,\tan(\pi\alpha^{\prime}p_{i}^{2})
. Note that if any two momenta entering the vertex are on-shell,
p_{i}^{2}\,{=}\,p_{j}^{2}\,{=}\,0
, it reduces to that of the BAS theory,
V_{3}(p_{i},p_{j},P)=1
. If instead only one momentum is on-shell,
p^{2}\,{=}\,0
, and two momenta
p_{L},p_{R}
are off-shell we recover the effective root vertex (
46
), i.e.
V_{3}(p,p_{L},p_{R})=1+t_{L}t_{R}
.
To compute matrix elements we just sum over all cubic Feynman diagrams involving the vertex (
52
).

Returning to the five-point example, only a single cubic diagram topology contributes,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{m}_{5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-14.22636pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/3vertOff5pt.pdf}\end{matrix}+\text{cyc.}=\frac{1+t_{13}t_{35}}{t_{13}t_{35}}+\text{cyc.}\,\overset{!}{\neq}\,m_{5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}.\end{split}

(53)

Comparing this to the inverse KLT matrix element in (
50
) we see that the above prescription fails to give the correct contact term as
\tilde{m}_{5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\,{-}\,m_{5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=4
.

This suggests that the cubic recursion (
48
) represents a
new kind of
Berends-Giele recursion, one where the root vertex does not follow from a Lagrangian. In other words, the inverse string theory KLT kernel is
not
cubic when expressed in terms of Feynman diagrams, but
is
cubic only once it is expressed in terms of Berends-Giele diagrams involving the effective root vertex (
46
).

3.2
Proof

We prove that the cubic Berends-Giele recursion proposed in (
48
) indeed reproduces the diagonal matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
of the inverse string theory KLT kernel. This will be accomplished in two steps. First, we will show that the functions generated via (
48
) have contact terms corresponding to those expected from the Feynman rules (
21
). Then we show that the functions obtained through the recursion consistently factorize according to (
26
). Since we are dealing with rational functions of
t_{ij}
, satisfying both these criteria is sufficient to establish equality with the matrix elements of the inverse KLT kernel (
18
). Readers not interested in the technical details of the proof are free to skip this section and move on to the introduction of the inverse KLT integrand in Section
4
.

An alternative proof of the recursion at the level of the generating functional of Berends-Giele currents can be found in Appendix
A
.

Contact Terms

We start by showing how the contact interactions (
21
) of the inverse KLT kernel arise from the cubic recursion (
48
). This can be understood using a two-step inductive argument. First, we show that there are no contact interactions at even points
n=2K
, for some fixed
K\geq 2
. For the induction we assume that for all
k<K
contact terms are in fact absent,

\displaystyle m_{2k}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots 2k)\Big\rvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!\!\!\!=0,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for }k<K.

(54)

The notation
\dots\rvert_{\text{ct.}}
indicates the restriction to contact terms of the matrix element, i.e. to those contributions that cannot be fixed from factorization (
26
) on poles. Since the matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
have a unique representation in terms of
t_{ij}
-variables we can find their contact terms simply by discarding all terms that involve at least one propagator
t_{ij}^{-1}
. In the following discussion, the same logic is applied also to products of matrix elements.
With this in mind we can use the explicit form of the recursion (
48
) to compute

\displaystyle m_{2K}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\rvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t_{1,2K-1}}m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{2K-1}\Big\rvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!+\sum_{j=3}^{2K-2}\!\left(\!1+\frac{1}{t_{1,j}t_{j,2K}}\!\right)m_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}m_{2K-j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\lvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!+\frac{1}{t_{2,2K}}m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{2K-1}\Big\rvert_{\text{ct.}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=3}^{2K-2}m_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}m_{2K-j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\lvert_{\text{ct.}},

(55)

where for visual clarity we have suppressed the
X
-label dependence of the individual matrix elements. To arrive at the second line we discard all terms in the recursion that explicitly involve a propagator
t_{ij}^{-1}
. Since there are no cancellations between numerators and denominators in the Berends-Giele recursion, these terms cannot possibly contribute to the contact term of
m_{2K}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. Now we observe that the remaining terms in (
3.2
) for any
j=3,\dots,{K{-}2}
involve a product of an odd- and an even-point matrix element. Therefore, assumption (
54
) implies that one factor always vanishes. This concludes the inductive step and shows that, as desired,

\displaystyle\kappa_{2K}\equiv m_{2K}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\rvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!=0,

(56)

where, for later reference we have introduced the notation
\kappa_{2k}
to denote the contact term.

Next we want to show that at
n=2K{+}1
odd, the recursion generates matrix elements with non-zero contact terms as given by (
21
). Again, we start the induction by assuming that for
k<K
the matrix elements already satisfy

\displaystyle m_{2k+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\rvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!=C_{k-1}.

(57)

In analogy to (
3.2
) we use the recursion (
48
) to compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{2K+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\rvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!&=\!\sum_{j=3}^{2K-1}m_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}m_{2K-j+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\lvert_{\text{ct.}}\\
&=\sum_{j=2}^{K-1}m_{2j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}m_{2(K-j+1)}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\lvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}m_{2j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}m_{2(K-j)+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\lvert_{\text{ct.}},\end{split}

(58)

where we split the sum into terms involving products of odd- and even-point matrix elements. The first term on the second line vanishes due to the previous result (
56
). What remains is to use the assumption (
57
) and evaluate

\displaystyle\kappa_{2K+1}\equiv m_{2K+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\rvert_{\text{ct.}}\!\!=\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}C_{j-1}C_{K-j-1}=C_{K-1},

(59)

which, owing to the recursive nature of Catalan numbers, yields the correct contact term.

This further illustrates that the Berends-Giele recursion (
48
) does not correspond to a straightforward regrouping of Feynman diagrams generated from the Feynman rules (
21
). In fact, reading (
59
) from right to left suggests that the contact term
C_{K-1}
is split up into a sum of
K{-}1
terms which are distributed among a set of Berends-Giele diagrams,

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/KLToddVertK.pdf}\end{matrix}\!\!\!\!=\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}C_{j-1}C_{K-j-1}\,\,\,\xhookrightarrow{\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,}\,\,\,\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagContK.pdf}\end{matrix}\,.

(60)

Therefore, unlike for the bi-adjoint scalar amplitudes, it is in general not possible to uniquely assign a given Feynman diagram to a single Berends-Giele diagram when using the cubic recursion for the inverse string theory KLT kernel.

Factorization

To complete the proof we still need to show that the functions obtained from the recursion (
48
) correctly factorize on poles. To facilitate this, we will first adapt the factorization condition (
26
) to the
X
-label representation of the matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. Since we are dealing with rational functions of the variables
t_{ij}\,{=}\,\tan(\pi\alpha^{\prime}X_{ij})
, instead of treating the periodic poles at
X_{ij}\,{=}\,k/\alpha^{\prime}
with
k\,{\in}\,\mathds{Z}
separately (cf. (
25
)), we will equivalently just consider poles when
t_{ij}\,{=}\,0
. This allows us to write the factorization condition (
26
) in
X
-label representation as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1i}}\,\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/DiagBlobKLT.pdf}\end{matrix}&=\begin{matrix}\hskip-2.84544pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BlobFactKLT.pdf}\end{matrix}\\
\Leftrightarrow\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1i}}\,m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{n}\!(1\dots i\dots n)&=m_{i}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(1\dots i)m_{n-i+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(i\dots n\,1),\end{split}

(61)

for
i\,{=}\,3,\dots,n{-}1
, where we formally replace taking a residue at
t_{ij}\,{=}\,0
by a derivative with respect to the corresponding propagator
t_{ij}^{-1}
. Due to the cyclicity of the matrix elements we can restrict, without loss of generality, to studying poles of the form
t_{1i}^{-1}
.

As we did for the contact terms before, we will prove consistent factorization of the cubic Berends-Giele recursion (
48
) inductively for a matrix element
m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{K}
at fixed multiplicity
K
, assuming that the factorization property (
61
) holds for matrix elements
m_{k}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
with
k\,{<}\,K
. We start with the special case of factorization on the pole at
t_{1,K-1}\,{=}\,0
as it is particularly simple. Indeed, from the explicit form of the recursion (
48
) we find that only a single term contributes, giving

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1K-1}}\,m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{K}(1\dots K)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1K-1}}\frac{1}{t_{1,K-1}}m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{K-1}(1\dots K-1)=m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{K-1}(1\dots K-1).

(62)

Comparing this to (
61
) for the case
i\,{=}\,n\,{-}\,1
, we see that this is already the desired result, as the other matrix element expected to appear on the pole is
m_{3}(K{-1}\,K\,1)=1
.

Moving on to the generic case for poles of the form
t_{1i}\,{=}\,0
with
i=3,\dots,K{-}2
the derivation is slightly more involved. Using the recursion we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1i}}\,m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{K}(1\dots K)&=\frac{1}{t_{1,K-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1i}}m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{K-1}(1\dots K-1)+\frac{1}{t_{2,K}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1i}}m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{K-1}(2\dots K)\\
&+\sum_{j=3}^{K-2}\!\bigg\{\!\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1i}}\left(\!1+\frac{1}{t_{1j}t_{jK}}\!\right)\!\!\bigg\}m_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots j)m_{K-j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(j\dots K)\\
&+\sum_{j=3}^{K-2}\!\left(\!1+\frac{1}{t_{1j}t_{jK}}\!\right)\!\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1i}}m_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots j)m_{K-j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(j\dots K).\end{split}

(63)

Using consistent factorization (
61
) of the lower point matrix elements
m_{k}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
with
k\,{<}\,K
the above reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{-1}_{1i}}\,m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{K}(1\dots K)

\displaystyle=m_{i}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(1\dots i)\bigg\{\!\frac{1}{t_{1,K-1}}m_{K-i+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(i\dots K{-}1\,1)+\frac{1}{t_{i,K}}m_{K-i+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(i\dots K)

\displaystyle\hskip 73.97733pt+\!\!\sum_{j=i+1}^{K-2}\!\left(\!1+\frac{1}{t_{1j}t_{jK}}\!\right)m_{j-i+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(i\dots j\,1)m_{K-j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(j\dots K)\!\bigg\}

\displaystyle\equiv m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{i}\!(1\dots i)m_{K-i+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(i\dots K\,1),

(64)

where we recognize the terms in curly brackets as the Berends-Giele recursion for the matrix element
m_{K-i+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(i\dots K\,1)
appearing on the factorization channel.

This concludes the proof of consistent factorization for the matrix element
m_{K}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. Together with the correct structure of contact terms shown in (
56
) and (
59
) this proves that the cubic Berends-Giele recursion (
48
) correctly reproduces diagonal matrix elements of the stringy inverse KLT kernel.

4
Cubic Recursion for the Stringy Inverse KLT Kernel (Loops)

We will now take advantage of the previously defined cubic recursion relation (
48
) for the inverse KLT kernel
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
at tree-level, and generalize it in order to
define the inverse KLT loop integrand
. This will be accomplished by taking seriously the cubic nature of inverse KLT matrix elements when expressed in terms of the effective root vertex (
46
) and appropriately adapting the structure of the recursion to loop integrands. This will allow us to constructively define planar loop integrands
m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}
for any multiplicity
n
and number of loops
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L}
, while also providing a highly efficient means of calculating them.

In lifting the cubic Berends-Giele recursion to the loop-level, we will make the astounding observation that the recursion does not just encode the infinite tower of tree-level contact interactions (
21
) but in fact encapsulates an
infinite number of infinite towers
of contact terms. These additional interactions contribute at higher loop orders and only modify the loop
integrand
, not the integrated matrix element. Nevertheless, the existence of these contact terms for the inverse KLT integrand will turn out to be crucial when we want to make the connection to pion integrands via
\alpha^{\prime}
-shifts, ensuring key properties such as an integrand-level Adler zero. We will also show that this infinity of infinite towers of interactions can be neatly summed into a remarkably simple all-loop Lagrangian for the inverse KLT integrand that generalizes the tree-level Lagrangian (
22
).

4.1
Surface kinematics for the inverse KLT integrand

Before delving into the definition of the inverse KLT integrand, we first have to discuss the appropriate choice of kinematic variables for loop integrands. As we saw earlier, the stringy
t
-variables (
19
) were adequate to capture the dependence of inverse KLT matrix elements on external kinematics at tree-level. For an
L
-loop integrand we have to additionally take into account its dependence on the loop momenta
\ell_{i}
where
i\,{=}\,1,\dots,L
.

Since we will be studying only planar loop integrands in this work, there is a natural choice of invariants corresponding to inverse propagators of planar Feynman diagrams
4
,

\displaystyle X_{i,z_{j}}=(\ell_{j}+p_{1}+\dots+p_{i})^{2},\hskip 28.45274ptX_{z_{i},z_{j}}=(\ell_{i}-\ell_{j})^{2},

(65)

where at
n
points and
L
loops the labels run over
i\,{=}\,1,\dots,n
and
z_{i}\,{=}\,z_{1},\dots z_{L}
. To give an example, we can canonically label the propagators of a two-loop, four-point Feynman diagram in the
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
theory using the variables (
65
) as shown in Figure
2
.

) we chose to denote by

the

-label corresponding to loop momentum
\ell_{i}
, adopting the notational convention established in the context of the “surfaceology” framework
2
;
3
;
7
. There, loop variables
z_{i}
are identified with punctures on the interior of a disk, the
kinematic surface
, whereas external momenta are associated with punctures on its boundary. Variables
X_{i,j}
can then be interpreted as curves between punctures
i
on the boundary and/or interior punctures
z_{i}
of the kinematic surface as shown in Figure

.

The surfaceology framework offers a natural extension of kinematic variables beyond those introduced so far which will be indispensable to define the inverse KLT loop integrand. These additional

To define the inverse KLT integrand, we need to highlight one further peculiarity of the surface picture at the loop level. Since, by definition, kinematic variables correspond to
homologically inequivalent
curves on the surface
6
, there is a proliferation of
X
-variables associated to a given physical channel. For instance, at one loop, the tree-level variables
X_{ij}
can be distinguished into
X_{ij}^{+}
and
X_{ij}^{-}
according to whether they correspond to curves that pass the loop puncture
z
on the left/right as shown in Figure

In the construction of the KLT integrand provided subsequently, we will (mostly) choose not to make this distinction, mainly for the sake of readability. Instead, we identify all

Surface structure of the effective root vertex

Before we get into the explicit construction of the inverse KLT integrand, we first need to understand how the effective root vertex (
46
) of the cubic Berends-Giele recursion is sensitive to particular properties of the kinematic surface.

We start by noting the simple fact that a given Berends-Giele diagram can be canonically associated with a tiling of the kinematic surface into regions. For instance, taking an
n
-point Berends-Giele diagram from the tree-level recursion (
47
), the corresponding tiling of the kinematic surface can be obtained simply by drawing the curves associated to the left- and right-hand propagators
X_{L/R}
of the diagram as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagKLT3NoLabel.pdf}\end{matrix}&\simeq\hskip 5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/surfTilingTree.pdf}\end{matrix}\\
&\simeq\frac{1+t_{1j}t_{jn}}{t_{1j}t_{jn}}m_{L}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(1\dots j)m_{R}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(j\dots n).\end{split}

(66)

In this way we construct what is called the
dual
of the diagram on the surface, which, in principle, contains the same information as the original Berends-Giele diagram. The three regions of the surface dual canonically correspond to the effective root vertex as well as the left/right-hand sub-amplitudes of the original diagram.
For the generic example in (
66
) we have chosen the case where there are at least two external lines connecting to each left/right sub-amplitude (i.e.
j\,{=}\,3,\dots,n\,{-}\,2
) such that the momentum invariants
X_{L/R}
appearing in the propagators of the Berends-Giele diagram can be considered off-shell.

Now, a consistent definition of Berends-Giele diagrams involving “on-shell” momenta, i.e. diagrams where only one external leg connects to a given side of the effective vertex, requires the particular structure of the kinematic surface. For concreteness, let us consider the tree-level diagram in (
66
) where the right-hand propagator is on-shell (i.e.
j\,{=}\,n\,{-}\,1
). Drawing the dual diagram on the kinematic surface, we observe that the curve corresponding to the right-hand propagator can be continuously deformed to the boundary segment between punctures
n
and
n\,{-}\,1
, making the two curves homologically equivalent,

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagOS1.pdf}\end{matrix}\hskip 5.69046pt\simeq\hskip 5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/surfTilingTreeOS1.pdf}\end{matrix}\hskip 5.69046pt\simeq\hskip 5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/surfTilingTreeOS2.pdf}\end{matrix}.

(67)

Identifying the homologous curves as shown above can be interpreted effectively as collapsing the surface region of the right-hand sub-amplitude into a curve by deforming the curve corresponding to the right-hand propagator to the boundary curve (
n\,{-}\,1\,\!,\!n
). We will now instate the rule that,
whenever a region on the surface can be collapsed in such a way, the effective root vertex similarly collapses
into that of the tr
\phi^{3}
theory,

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/surfTilingTreeOS2.pdf}\end{matrix}\hskip 5.69046pt\simeq\hskip 5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagOS2.pdf}\end{matrix}\,=\frac{1}{t_{1,n-1}}m_{L}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(1\dots n\,{-}\,1).

(68)

This provides a surface interpretation for the appearance of the tr
\phi^{3}
vertex in the Berends-Giele diagrams involving an on-shell leg in the tree-level recursion (
47
).

Let us now anticipate what the homology-sensitive definition of the effective root vertex implies for loop integrands. The loop-level recursion will include Berends-Giele diagram topologies of the type (
66
) which, however, now involve lower-point sub-integrands on either side of the root vertex. In particular, let us focus on the one-loop analogue of the diagram (
67
), except that the right-hand sub-integrand is now itself of one loop order as shown in (
69
). In the dual picture this situation corresponds to a tiling of the kinematic surface into regions identical to (
67
) except that the right-hand region now contains a puncture representing the loop-dependence of the sub-integrand,

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagLoopOS.pdf}\end{matrix}\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\simeq\hskip 5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/surfTilingLoopOS1.pdf}\end{matrix}

(69)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1+t_{1,n-1}t_{n-1,n}}{t_{1,n-1}t_{n-1,n}}m_{L}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n\,{-}\,1)m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},R}(n\,{-}\,1\,n).

The appearance of the puncture in the dual picture is crucial as the curve corresponding to the right-hand propagator is
no longer homologous
to the boundary. As such, we are not entitled to collapse the right-hand region the same way we did in (
67
) and the full,
\alpha^{\prime}
-dependent structure of the effective root vertex (
46
) remains intact.

Having laid out the subtleties related to the sensitivity of the effective root vertex to homology on the kinematic surface, we are now fully equipped to define the inverse KLT integrand at one loop and beyond.

4.2
One-Loop Recursion

We will first consider the case of the one-loop (
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L}\,{=}\,1
) integrands
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
for the inverse string theory KLT kernel. This gives us an opportunity to discuss the basic idea behind generalizing the cubic Berends-Giele recursion to the loop-level, and to understand some of the new features encountered beyond tree-level in as simple a setting as possible.

The generalization to loop integrands is most easily understood at the level of Berends-Giele diagrams. Just as for the tree-level recursion (
47
) before, there will be certain contributions to the loop-level recursion where the effective root vertex (
46
) is in a tree-level configuration, for example,

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\,\supset\,\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BG1loopTreeTop1.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\,\,+\,\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BG1loopTreeTop2.pdf}\end{matrix}

(70)

This is a straightforward generalization of the tree-level diagram topology except that now one of the left- or right-hand sub-amplitudes is a lower-point one-loop integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},L/R}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. Going forward, we will also use the notation
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\,{\equiv}\,m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
for the tree-level inverse KLT matrix elements.

However, there is also an entirely new Berends-Giele diagram topology where the effective root vertex itself is fused into a loop,

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\,\supset\!\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BG1loopLoopTop.pdf}\end{matrix}.

(71)

At
n
points, this topology involves a tree-level matrix element
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n{+}1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
at
higher
multiplicity (
n\,{+}\,1
) but at
lower
loop-order (
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L}\,{=}\,0
) which therefore does not spoil the recursion.

In setting up the recursion for the matrix elements
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
we will discuss the special cases of integrands with
n\,{=}\,1,2,3
separately as they are slightly degenerate owing to their low multiplicity. Only then will we proceed to the general case where
n\,{\geq}\,4
and a discussion of the novel structures encountered for the inverse KLT integrand at one loop.

Tadpole (n=1)

Due to the existence of the loop topology (
71
) we encounter the first non-zero inverse KLT integrand already for

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiag1loop1ptOS1.pdf}\end{matrix}\hskip 5.69046pt\simeq\hskip 5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/surfTilingTadp.pdf}\end{matrix}\hskip 5.69046pt\simeq\hskip 5.69046pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-7.11317pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/surfTilingTadpOS.pdf}\end{matrix}\,.

Having shown how to analyze the behavior of the effective root vertex under homology for various examples, we will not continue belaboring this point in detail for each Berends-Giele diagram. Instead, in the remainder of this article, we simply state the Berends-Giele diagrams contributing to a given integrand with the homology rules for the effective vertex already implicitly implemented.

Single Cuts

The one-loop inverse KLT integrand (
80
) is a novel kinematic function
defined
via the cubic Berends-Giele recursion relation. As such, we cannot prove its validity by comparing to any previously known results in the literature. However, we can check certain consistency conditions that the integrand must satisfy to ensure that our definitions are meaningful.

One such crucial property are the single cuts of the integrand which are known to fix the integrand up to contact terms. The explicit form of the cubic Berends-Giele recursion relations allows us to study the behavior of the inverse KLT integrand on single-cuts in a straightforward fashion.

Let us start by investigating single-cuts involving propagator with loop-dependence. That is, we want to study the residue of the
n
-point integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
when e.g.
t_{1z}\,{=}\,0
. The corresponding pole is expected to involve the forward limit of the
(n\,{+}\,2)
-point tree-level function
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
.
As we are working with the
X
-label representation of the integrand, we can replace the residue by a derivative with respect to the corresponding propagator
t_{1z}^{-1}
, as we did in the proof of consistent factorization in (
61
). In this case we expect the integrand to satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1z}^{-1}}\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/DiagBlobKLTloop.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\,\,&=\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/DiagBlobKLTloopFwd.pdf}\end{matrix}\\
\Leftrightarrow\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1z}^{-1}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n)&=m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1z12\dots n),\end{split}

(88)

where both sides should be interpreted as functions of
X
-labels. The above coincides with the expected behavior of integrands defined on the kinematic surface
6
. We can easily verify (
88
) for the inverse KLT integrands at
n=1,2,3
by inspection,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1z}^{-1}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1)&=1=m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},3}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1z1),\hskip 19.91684pt\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1z}^{-1}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(12)=\frac{1}{t_{11}}+\frac{1}{t_{2z}}=m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},4}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1z12),\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1z}^{-1}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},3}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(123)&=\frac{1}{t_{11}t_{12}}+\frac{1}{t_{11}t_{13}}+\frac{1}{t_{12}t_{2z}}+\frac{1}{t_{13}t_{3z}}+\frac{1}{t_{2z}t_{3z}}+1=m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},5}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1z123).\end{split}

(89)

Note here the importance of the tadpole variables
t_{i,i}
and off-shellness variables
t_{i,i+1}
in regularizing the divergent behavior of certain propagators in the forward limit.
With the results from (
89
) we can move on to inductively prove the general case where
n\,{\geq}\,4
. Computing the single-cut for each sub-integrand in the recursion (
80
) according to (
88
) (i.e. using the inductive assumption) we find after some rearranging

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1z}^{-1}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n)&=\frac{1}{t_{nz}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n+1}^{\alpha}(z12\dots n)+\frac{1}{t_{1n-1}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n+1}^{\alpha}(1z1\dots n\,{-}\,1)\\
&+\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\frac{1+t_{1j}t_{jn}}{t_{1j}t_{jn}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0}j+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1z1\dots j)m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n-j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(j\dots n)\\
&\hskip-4.26773pt\overset{\eqref{BGKLT}}{\equiv}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n+2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1z1\dots n),\end{split}

(90)

which, upon comparing to the tree-level recursion (
48

88
).

At one loop, there is another type of single-cut involving the tree-level propagators
t_{1i}\,{=}\,0
. These single-cuts require a bit more care in their definition. Considering, for instance, the four-point integrand in (
86
) we see that it has single- and double-poles in
t_{13}
. For the corresponding single-cut to be well-behaved these higher-order poles have to be resolved into simple poles. This can be achieved by taking into account the homology of curves on the kinematic surface as shown in Figure

which has only simple poles in
t_{1i}^{\pm}
. Once this distinction is made, the single cuts are well defined and the inverse KLT integrands satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial(t_{1i}^{+})^{-1}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n)&=m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},i}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots i)m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n-i+2}(i\dots n1),\\

(91)

which corresponds to the canonical behavior of an integrand defined on the kinematic surface.
With the single-cuts given by (

m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
is fixed up to contact terms which we will study now.

Contact terms

The efficiency of the cubic Berends-Giele recursion is all the more remarkable as the KLT integrand turns out to receive contributions from an additional infinite tower of inherently
one-loop contact terms
which, in analogy to (
56
) and (
59
), we denote by

\displaystyle\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}\equiv m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\lvert_{\text{ct.}}.

(92)

We already encountered two examples of these contact terms at two and four points, (
74
) and (
86
) respectively, where we found
\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2}\,{=}\,1
and
\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},4}\,{=}\,3
.
In contrast, for the odd-point integrands at
n\,{=}\,1,3
in (
72
) and (
78
) contact terms were apparently absent,
\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},1}\,{=}\,\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},3}\,{=}\,0
. It can be shown that this holds more generally and contact terms vanish at odd multiplicity, i.e.
\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2k+1}\,{=}\,0
.

Thanks to the recursive nature of the inverse KLT integrand, the non-vanishing contact terms (
92
) at even multiplicity (
n\,{=}\,2k
) can be determined recursively in terms of the tree-level interactions
\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},n}
in (
21
),

\displaystyle\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},2j+1}\,\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2(k-j)}+\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2j}\,\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},2(k-j)+1}\right)+\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},2k+1}.

(93)

This relation can be obtained directly from the recursion (
80
) for loop integrands
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2k}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
by an analogous procedure to the one used to find the explicit form of the tree-level contact terms in (
3.2
) and (
58
).
Since the tree-level contact terms are given by
\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},2j+1}=C_{j-1}
, this recursion can be solved for
\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2k}
as a (rather complicated) polynomial in the Catalan numbers
C_{i}
. However, explicitly calculating the first few non-vanishing contact terms up to
n\,{=}\,10
we observe that

\displaystyle\hskip-9.95863pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/KLTVert1loop.pdf}\end{matrix}=\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}=\begin{cases}1,\,3\,,\,10\,,\,35\,,\,126\,,\dots=(n-1)\,C_{\frac{n-2}{2}},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{if }\,n\,{\geq}\,2\,\text{ is even,}\\
0\hskip 190.0642pt\text{if }\,n\,\text{ is odd,}\\
\end{cases}

(94)

and therefore the non-vanishing one-loop contact terms turn out to be proportional to Catalan numbers, just as at tree-level. In fact, the structure of the contact terms (
94
) as a polynomial in
n
times Catalan numbers allows to derive a simple form for a corresponding one-loop Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},\text{KLT}}=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg\langle\!\log\!\bigg(\!\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4\phi^{2}}}{2}\!\bigg)\!\bigg\rangle.

(95)

The interactions generated by the above Lagrangian should be treated formally as being of one-loop order, similarly to a counter-term Lagrangian, and serve to supplement the tree-level KLT Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},\text{KLT}}
in (
22
).
The inverse KLT integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
can then be computed from the
effective one-loop Lagrangian
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}(1)}}_{\text{KLT}}=\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},\text{KLT}}+\hbar\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},\text{KLT}},

(96)

using Feynman diagrams by considering all one-loop diagram topologies with vertices coming from
\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},\text{KLT}}
and tree-level topologies with a single vertex insertion from
\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},\text{KLT}}
. For example, the one-loop four-point integrand (
86
) can be computed from contributions of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}(1)}}_{\text{KLT}}&\bigg\lvert_{1-\text{loop}}\subset\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/1loop4ptFeynDiagEx1.pdf}\end{matrix}+\dots+\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/1loop4ptFeynDiagEx2.pdf}\end{matrix}+\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/1loop4ptFeynDiagEx3.pdf}\end{matrix}+\dots+\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/1loop4ptFeynDiagEx4.pdf}\end{matrix},\end{split}

(97)

where black vertices derive from the tree-level Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},\text{KLT}}
and red vertices correspond to insertions of
\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},\text{KLT}}
in (
95
). The diagrams in (
97
) involving one-loop vertices are of course exactly the contributions in (
79
) which where missing when comparing the Feynman integrand computed just with
\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},\text{KLT}}
to the inverse KLT integrand computed from the cubic Berends-Giele recursion (
80
).

Let us briefly remark on the significance of the additional one-loop interactions for the inverse KLT integrand, whose existence essentially follows from the requirement that the cubic simplicity observed for the tree-level matrix elements
m_{n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
when expressed in terms of a Berends-Giele recursion is preserved at loop level.

Since tree-level diagrams with insertions of (
95
) always involve fewer propagators than the maximum number possible at the given order, they do not contribute to the leading order in the limit
\alpha^{\prime}\to 0
so that the connection (
85
) to the
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
surfacehedron integrand is not spoiled. However, even at finite values of
\alpha^{\prime}
, the nature of these contact terms might seem perplexing. This is because each diagram topology involving some one-loop interaction (
94
) does not depend on the loop momentum
\ell
. As such, the given diagram is “scaleless” with respect to the loop integration over
\ell
and does not contribute to the integrated inverse KLT matrix element since

\displaystyle\int\!\text{d}^{d}\ell\,\,\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/1loopCntDiagTop.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\,=\,\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-2.84544pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/1loopCntDiagTop.pdf}\end{matrix}\times\left(\int\!\text{d}^{d}\ell\,\,1\right)\simeq 0.

(98)

Therefore, as far as the physical, integrated, matrix element is concerned, the effective one-loop Lagrangian (
96
) is fully equivalent to the tree-level Lagrangian (
22
). Indeed, the one-loop Lagrangian (
95
) contributes non-trivially only to the inverse KLT
integrand
.

However, once we connect the inverse KLT integrand to integrands for NLSM pions in Section
5
, we will see that the loop-level contact interactions are vital to make sure that certain desirable properties of pion integrands, such as the Adler zero, are satisfied. Furthermore, given their emergence from the effective root vertex, it is not unreasonable to suspect that these contact terms might reflect certain combinatorial properties of the kinematic surface.

4.3
All-Loop Recursion

Before we can define the recursion for the general
L
-loop integrand, a short remark on conventions and notation is in order.
At
L
loops an integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
will depend on
L
loop variables
Z\,{=}\,\{z_{1},\dots,z_{L}\}
. By convention, we will construct integrands using the Berends-Giele recursion that are completely symmetric with respect to their dependence on these loop variables, that is

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{L})=m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(z_{\sigma(1)},z_{\sigma(2)},\dots,z_{\sigma(L)}),

(99)

for any permutation
\sigma\,{\in}\,S_{L}
. In the above and for the remainder of this discussion we suppress the dependence of integrands on a fixed set of external labels in the notation.

To generate symmetric integrands like (
99
) using the recursion, it will be useful to introduce the symmetric product of integrands. Suppose we have a left- and a right-hand integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L_{L}},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
and
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L_{R}},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. Taking
L\,{=}\,L_{L}\,{+}\,L_{R}
we define the symmetric product of these integrands with respect to the punctures
Z\,{=}\,\{z_{1},\dots,z_{L}\}
as

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L_{L}},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L_{R}},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\equiv\frac{L_{L}!L_{R}!}{L!}\!\sum_{Z_{L}\subset Z}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L_{L}},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(Z_{L})\,m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L_{R}},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(Z_{R}),

(100)

where the sum runs over all subsets
Z_{L}\subset Z
of size
\lvert Z_{L}\rvert\,{=}\,L_{L}
. Consequently the complement has size
\lvert Z_{R}\rvert\,{=}\,\lvert Z\setminus\!Z_{L}\rvert\,{=}\,L_{R}
. Since each term on the right-hand side of (
100
) is expected to produce the same contribution once the loop integrations over all
z_{i}
are carried out, we normalize by a binomial factor
\frac{L_{L}!L_{R}!}{L!}
to avoid over-counting.

To give an example, let us consider the symmetric product of a one-loop integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
and a two-loop integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}2},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
as it would naturally appear in the recursion of an
L\,{=}\,1\,{+}\,2\,{=}\,3
loop integrand. In this case the symmetric product has three terms,

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}2},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{3}\!\left(\!m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(z_{1})m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}2},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(z_{2}z_{3})+m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(z_{2})m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}2},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(z_{1}z_{3})+m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(z_{3})m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}2},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\!(z_{1}z_{2})\!\right).

(101)

Applying this procedure to each Berends-Giele diagram, we make sure that the full integrand is constructed in a
z
-symmetric fashion term by term in the recursion.

Recursion

With notational conventions fixed, let us now define the
L
-loop inverse KLT integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
via the cubic Berends-Giele recursion. The generalization from the one-loop recursion is straightforward, as there are no new diagram topologies to include in the recursion beyond those already considered in (
70
) and (
71
). It is worth pointing out that this is a highly non-trivial feature of the inverse KLT integrand and the cubic Berends-Giele recursion based on the effective root vertex (
46
). In fact, at loop orders
L\,{\geq}\,2
, the analogue of the naive Berends-Giele recursion (
22
) following from tree-level inverse KLT Lagrangian (
22
) includes contributions from increasingly intricate Berends-Giele diagram topologies. Some representative diagrams contributing to the naive recursion at
L=3
are shown in Figure
7
. However, these diagrams can in fact be subsumed into the cubic Berends-Giele diagram topologies in (
102
) and (
103
), involving just the effective root vertex, illustrating the inherently cubic structure of the inverse KLT integrand.

Figure 7:
Non-trivial contributions to the three-loop matrix element
m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}3},n}
when computed using the naive Berends-Giele recursion based on the Lagrangian (
22
). The cubic structure of the inverse KLT integrand allows to capture these diagrams using simpler diagram topologies of the form (
102
) and (
103
) involving the effective root vertex.

A novel feature of the recursion at higher loop orders is that each Berends-Giele diagram needs to be summed over
all
ways of distributing the
L
loops between the left- and right-hand integrands. For Berends-Giele diagrams where the root vertex is in a tree-level configuration as in (
70
) this will require a sum over the
number of loops

L_{L/R}
of the right- and left-hand integrands such that
L\,{=}\,L_{R}\,{+}\,L_{L}
,

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\,\supset\,\hskip-8.5359pt\sum_{L=L_{R}+L_{L}}\hskip-2.84544pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGLloopTreeTop.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\simeq\,\frac{1+t_{L}t_{R}}{t_{L}t_{R}}\hskip-8.5359pt\sum_{L=L_{R}+L_{L}}\hskip-8.5359ptm_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L_{L}},n_{L}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L_{R}},n_{R}}^{\alpha^{\prime}},

(102)

as well as a symmetrization over loop variables
z_{i}
in each sub-integrand as in (
100
).

For loop topology Berends-Giele diagrams of the type (
71
), one loop variable always appears explicitly in the root vertex while the remaining
L\,{-}\,1
loops are contained in the (
n{+}1
)-point sub-integrand. At
L
loops we therefore only have to include a symmetrization over
z
-variables for this diagram topology, which we will denote

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\,\supset\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-11.38092pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGLloopLoopTop.pdf}\end{matrix}&=\frac{1+t_{1Z}t_{nZ}}{t_{1Z}t_{nZ}}\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-1},n+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots nZ)\\[-14.22636pt]
&\equiv\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{1+t_{1z_{i}}t_{nz_{i}}}{t_{1z_{i}}t_{nz_{i}}}\,m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-1},n+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots nz_{i}).\end{split}

(103)

With these conventions in mind, we can now proceed with the explicit construction of the all-loop inverse KLT integrand. Like before, we discuss the special cases
n\,{=}\,1,2,3
first, and then move on to the general case for
n\,{\geq}\,4
. We provide the full, detailed expressions for all integrands to make it as easy as possible for the interested reader to implement the recursion in a computer program.

Special cases (n=1,2,3)

For
L{\geq}2
the tadpole integrand receives some additional contributions compared to its one-loop counterpart (
72
). In particular, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\frac{1+t_{11}^{2}}{t_{11}^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1)+\frac{1+t_{1Z}^{2}}{t_{1Z}^{2}}\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-1},2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1Z).\end{split}

(104)

The first set of Berends-Giele diagrams, where two lower-loop tadpoles are joined into a tree topology,

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\,\supset\,\hskip-2.84544pt\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}\hskip 0.0pt\begin{matrix}\hskip 2.84544pt\vskip-1.42271pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BGdiagLloopTadp.pdf}\end{matrix}\,=\,\frac{1+t_{11}^{2}}{t_{11}^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1),

(105)

is entirely new for
L\,{\geq}\,2
, while the loop-topology diagram, i.e. the second term in (

), is a natural generalization of the original contribution to the one-loop tadpole (

).

The two-point inverse KLT integrand at
L
loops is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\frac{1+t_{11}t_{12}}{t_{11}t_{12}}\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(12)+\frac{1+t_{12}t_{22}}{t_{12}t_{22}}\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(12)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(2)\\
&+\frac{1}{t_{11}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1)+\frac{1}{t_{22}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(2)+\frac{1+t_{1Z}t_{2Z}}{t_{1Z}t_{2Z}}\cdot m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-1},3}(12Z).\end{split}

(106)

Finally, at three points, the general
L
-loop recursion reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},3}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\frac{1+t_{11}t_{13}}{t_{11}t_{13}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},3}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(123)+\frac{1+t_{13}t_{33}}{t_{13}t_{33}}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},3}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(123)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(3)\\
&+\frac{1}{t_{12}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2}(12)+\frac{1}{t_{23}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2}(23)+\frac{1+t_{12}t_{23}}{t_{12}t_{23}}\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(12)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(23)\\
&+\frac{1+t_{1Z}t_{3Z}}{t_{1Z}t_{3Z}}\cdot m^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-1},4}(123Z).\end{split}

(107)

Here, and in the following, we do not explicitly draw the Berends-Giele diagrams corresponding to individual contributions in the recursion. The diagram topologies are, with the exception of the tadpole diagram (
105
), identical to those encountered at one loop.

General case (n ​
\geq
​ 4)

Let us now finally turn to the most general case of the
L
-loop integrand for
n\,{\geq}\,4
. As in the case of the one-loop integrand (
80
) we split the integrand up into tadpole-, bubble- and higher-valency contributions as well as the contribution from the loop-topology diagram,

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n,(1)}^{\alpha^{\prime}}+m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n,(2)}^{\alpha^{\prime}}+m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n,(\geq 3)}^{\alpha^{\prime}}+m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n,(\text{loop})}^{\alpha^{\prime}}.

(108)

Firstly, the Berends-Giele diagrams involving tadpoles are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n,(1)}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\!\bigg(\!\frac{1+t_{11}t_{1n}}{t_{11}t_{1n}}\,m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n)+\frac{1+t_{1n}t_{nn}}{t_{1n}t_{nn}}\,m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(n)\!\bigg),\end{split}

(109)

which we recognize as a straightforward generalization of (

) to

loops.
Likewise, the general
L
-loop form of the bubble contributions (

) is simply

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n,(2)}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\frac{1}{t_{2n}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n-1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(2\dots n)+\frac{1}{t_{1n-1}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n-1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n{-}1)\\

&\hskip 22.76228pt+\frac{1+t_{1n-1}t_{n-1n}}{t_{1n-1}t_{n-1n}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},n-1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots n{-}1)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},2}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(n{-}1\,n)\!\bigg).\end{split}

(110)

Continuing with the higher-valency contributions we get

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n,(\geq 3)}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\frac{1+t_{1j}t_{jn}}{t_{1j}t_{jn}}\sum_{l=0}^{L}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}l},j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots j)\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-l},n-j+1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(j\dots n).

(111)

Finally we have the contribution from the loop-topology Berends-Giele diagram,

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n,(\text{loop})}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=\frac{1+t_{1Z}t_{nZ}}{t_{1Z}t_{nZ}}\cdot m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-1},n{+}1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(1\dots nZ),

(112)

where we employ the notation (

) for symmetrizing the

)-point integrand
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L-1},n{+}1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
.

-loop inverse KLT integrand. Counting up the number of Berends-Giele diagrams in each of the contributions to (

can be computed in less than a second on a standard desktop computer using the cubic Berends-Giele recursion.

A simple example at two loops

To illustrate the recursion at work, we compute the simplest two-loop example of the inverse KLT integrand at one point. There are only two Berends-Giele diagram topologies corresponding to the two contributions in (

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}2},1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}&=\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-22.76228pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BG2loop1ptBGDiag1.pdf}\end{matrix}\,\,\,+\,\,\,\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip-8.5359pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/BG2loop1ptBGDiag2.pdf}\end{matrix}\\

However, and more importantly, we observe a recurring phenomenon; that of the emergence of loop-level contact terms from the cubic Berends-Giele recursion. From the example above we can read off a one-point contact interaction,

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip 5.69046pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/KLTVert2loop1pt.pdf}\end{matrix}=\,1,

(114)

With this apparent proliferation of contact terms at hand, it is time we study their structure more generally.

General structure of contact terms

We can analyze the general
L
-loop Berends-Giele recursion (
108
) for contact terms in much the same way as we did at
L\,{=}\,1
in (
94
). Curiously, we find that there exists a separate infinite tower of interactions contributing to the inverse KLT integrand
at each loop order
, giving rise to an
infinity
of infinite towers of contact terms. The structure of the recursion implies that the contact terms for inverse KLT matrix elements
m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
satisfy

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\Big\lvert_{\text{ct.}}\equiv\kappa_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}\!=\begin{cases}\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ if $\,L\,{+}\,n\,$ is odd,}\\

(115)

5
Pion Integrands from
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift

Having discussed in detail how to efficiently construct the inverse KLT integrand for any number of loops and legs, let us briefly return to the connection between the inverse KLT kernel and stringy NLSM pions and generalize it to the loop level. At tree-level, the
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift allowed to deform matrix elements
m_{2k}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
at even multiplicity into stringy pions
A_{2k}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
through a simple shift of the kinematic variables
X_{ij}
(cf. (
31
)).
For loop integrands, we have to extend the shift prescription to
all
kinematic
X
-variables (
65
) as well as the tadpole variables defined formally on the surface as in Figure
4
. To do this, we take inspiration from the
\delta
-shift prescription that is known to connect
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
field theory integrands to those of the NLSM as well as mixed amplitudes
6
. For simplicity, we will focus here only on the shift prescription for pure pion integrands.

We recall that at tree-level the
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift required an assignment of even or odd parity to each channel
X_{ij}
according to (
28
). The most straightforward way to extend the notion of parity to channels involving loop labels
Z\,{=}\,\{z_{1},\dots,z_{L}\}
is to simply
prescribe
some parity for each loop label,

\displaystyle P_{Z}^{(i)}=\{p^{(i)}(z_{1}),\dots,p^{(i)}(z_{L})\},\hskip 28.45274ptp^{(i)}(z_{j})\equiv\text{even/odd}.

(124)

At
L
loops, the index
i\,{=}\,1,\dots,2^{L}
runs over all possible binary assignments of parities to loop labels
z_{i}
. Having defined the parity for loop labels
z_{i}
, we can immediately assign parities to channels such as
P(X_{iz_{j}})
or
P(X_{z_{i}z_{j}})
by employing the same definition (
28
) as at tree-level where the parities of
z_{i}
are determined according to (
124
).

Now to obtain the stringy pion integrand at
L
loops we have to perform
2^{L}
distinct
\alpha^{\prime}
-shifts. For each parity assignment of
z_{i}
in (
124
) we split the set of
X
-variables into channels of even and odd parity,

\displaystyle X=X^{(i)}_{\text{even}}\cup X^{(i)}_{\text{odd}},\hskip 42.67912pti=1,\dots,2^{L},

(125)

where
X^{(i)}_{\text{even/odd}}
are defined in analogy to (
29
) at tree-level. For fixed
i
we then define the corresponding
i
-th
\alpha^{\prime}
-shift via

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}\to\alpha^{\prime}/2,\hskip 28.45274pt\text{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\hat{X}^{(i)}_{\text{even}}=X^{(i)}_{\text{even}}\pm 1/\alpha^{\prime},\hskip 28.45274pt\hat{X}^{(i)}_{\text{odd}}=X^{(i)}_{\text{odd}},

(126)

again in complete analogy to the tree-level shift (
30
) and (
31
). Finally, the stringy
L
-loop pion integrand is then given by

\displaystyle A^{\pi,\alpha^{\prime}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2k}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2k}^{\alpha^{\prime}\!/2}\big(\hat{X}^{(i)}_{\text{even}},\hat{X}^{(i)}_{\text{odd}}\big).

(127)

As we mentioned before, the above shift procedure is highly reminiscent of the so-called
\delta
-shift for field theory amplitudes and integrands involving cubic scalars and pions. There, integrands in the
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
theory are deformed using a similar kinematic shift to (
126
) with a parameter
\delta\equiv 1/\alpha^{\prime}
. The field theory pion integrand can then be extracted from the shifted
\text{tr}\phi^{3}
(surface) integrand by expanding around
\delta\,{=}\,\infty
,

\displaystyle A^{\pi}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2k}=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}\lim_{\delta\to\infty}\delta^{2k+2L-2}m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2k}\big(\hat{X}^{(i)}_{\text{even}}(\delta),\hat{X}^{(i)}_{\text{odd}}(\delta)\big).

(128)

The specific pion integrands generated through this procedure have been studied in great detail
6
;
10
;
9
;
13
and exhibit many nice properties such as an exact Adler zero in appropriately defined “surface soft” limits and non-trivial split factorizations in multi-soft limits. They also match all single-cuts when defined in terms of surface variables. For this reason, the integrand (
128
) is sometimes dubbed the
perfect
NLSM pion integrand.

The stringy pion integrand (
127
) derived here from the inverse KLT integrand represents a simple stringification of precisely this perfect NLSM integrand in the sense that

\displaystyle A^{\pi,\alpha^{\prime}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2k}=\left(\!\frac{\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\!\right)^{\!\!1-L}\!\!\left(A^{\pi}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},2k}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{\prime 2})\right).

(129)

The above equality is exact, meaning that at leading order in
\alpha^{\prime}
, the integrands match as rational functions of kinematic surface variables, even including unphysical contributions involving tadpole and off-shellness variables that would vanish upon integration. We have checked this correspondence explicitly up to four loops and four points, as well as higher multiplicities at lower loop orders.

Example (n

To get a more concrete understanding of the general shift prescription discussed above, we consider the simplest example of the stringy pion integrand at loop.

We start with the simplest non-trivial one-loop integrand at

\displaystyle\begin{split}X^{(1)}_{\text{even}}&=\{X_{11},X_{22},X_{2z}\},\hskip 28.45274ptX^{(1)}_{\text{odd}}=\{X_{12},X_{1z}\},\\

Evaluating the two-point KLT integrand

6
Conclusion and Outlook

In this article we have introduced the
inverse KLT integrand

m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
, a generalization of the inverse string theory KLT kernel
47
to all loop orders. It is a simple, stringy function defined on the kinematic surface
2
;
3
and encodes the scattering of cubic scalars and pions.
The cubic Berends-Giele recursion relations formulated in this work, in particular their striking similarity to the known recursion for the tr
\phi^{3}
field theory, reveal an underlying simplicity of the stringy matrix elements and suggest that the inverse KLT integrand can be considered as a toy model for the “simplest stringy amplitude”.

The primary focus of this work was on the construction of diagonal matrix elements for the inverse KLT integrand. An immediate and interesting opportunity for generalization lies in finding an appropriate definition for
off-diagonal
matrix elements at loop-level. At tree-level, Mizera showed that off-diagonal inverse KLT matrix elements can be given an interpretation as intersection numbers of twisted cycles tiling the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0,n}

46
. It is reasonable to suspect that the integrands presented here allow for a similar interpretation as intersection numbers on appropriately defined loop-level moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L},n}
.

Presumably, this conjecture is intimately connected with another extension of the inverse KLT integrand not touched upon in this work. It concerns the consistent treatment of
closed curves
that exist on the kinematic surface at loop-level. At one loop, this problem was solved by the so-called halohedron integrand
49
for the tr
\phi^{3}
theory which includes an additional kinematic variable
X_{0}
,

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\hskip 0.0pt\vskip 0.0pt\includegraphics[width]{figures/CurveXClosed.pdf}\end{matrix}\,,

(133)

corresponding to the (only) closed curve on the kinematic surface with a single puncture.
We can similarly define stringy versions of the halohedron integrand that correspond to simple extensions of the inverse KLT integrand including the dependence on
X_{0}
. For instance, for the one-loop tadpole and bubble we can write

\displaystyle m_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},1}^{(X_{0})}=\frac{1}{t_{1z}}+\frac{1}{t_{0}},\hskip 22.76228ptm_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2}^{(X_{0})}=\frac{1}{t_{11}t_{1z}}+\frac{1}{t_{11}t_{0}}+\frac{1}{t_{22}t_{2z}}+\frac{1}{t_{22}t_{0}}+\frac{1}{t_{1z}t_{2z}}+1,

(134)

where
t_{0}\,{=}\,\tan(\pi\alpha^{\prime}X_{0})
as usual. It is no coincidence that these integrands are functionally
identical
to the four- and five-point tree-level matrix elements (
20
). It merely reflects the fact that the moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},1}\,{\simeq}\,\mathcal{M}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},4}
and
\mathcal{M}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}1},2}\,{\simeq}\,\mathcal{M}_{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}0},5}
are known to be combinatorially identical
32
. This strongly suggests that the one-loop inverse KLT integrand, even at higher multiplicities, correctly captures the underlying boundary structure of moduli spaces of the string worldsheet
directly in kinematic space
.

Of course, a particularly compelling question is whether this correspondence extends to the two-loop level and beyond. There, the structure of the corresponding moduli spaces of Riemann surfaces with more than two boundaries (
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L}\geq 2
) is known to be non-polytopal
32
. This will presumably require much more non-trivial closed-curve extensions of the inverse KLT integrand compared the one shown at one loop above. Still, the stringy nature of the inverse KLT integrand allows for a much richer set of functions to accommodate the boundary structure of the moduli space directly in kinematic space, especially when contrasted with its field theory analogue, the tr
\phi^{3}
surface integrand, which is constrained to be a rational function of
X
-variables.

In a similar vein, a further open question regards the existence of (positive) geometries for the inverse KLT integrand. At tree-level, the inverse KLT kernel was recently found to be the canonical function of the so-called associahedral grid
15
. It would be interesting to see if a similar geometric picture extends to the integrands studied in this work.

On a less technical note, there remains a more fundamental question related to the inverse KLT integrand: what is its purpose in life?
At tree-level, the
inverse
KLT kernel is exactly what it proclaims to be. It is the matrix inverse of the KLT kernel appearing in the double copy of tree-level open and closed string amplitudes.
This raises the question of whether the inverse KLT integrand presented here, once inverted, can be utilized in a similar way to facilitate a KLT-type double copy of stringy loop integrands directly in kinematic space, potentially providing another perspective on the one-loop double copy relations
50
;
45
recently observed for full string integrands.

Finally, it would be interesting to explore how far the simple stringification provided by the inverse KLT kernel for the BAS theory and NLSM pions can be extended to other known effective field theories with special soft limit behavior and those that satisfy double copy relations
30
;
28
;
29
;
27
;
34
;
42
;
36
;
48
;
35
;
31
;
11
;
26
;
16
;
38
;
21
;
19
;
37
. This includes unordered theories, raising the question of a canonical definition of the corresponding integrands due to the lack of (global) planar variables. The inverse KLT integrand might aid in finding such a canonical description.

A.1

The diagonal amplitudes
m_{diag}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\sigma\right)\equiv m^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\sigma|\sigma)

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha^{\prime}}=-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\phi\Delta^{-1}\phi\right\rangle+V\left(\phi\right)

(135)

\Delta^{-1}=\tan\left(\pi\alpha^{\prime}\square\right)

V\left(\phi\right)=\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\phi-\frac{1}{8}\arcsin 2\phi-\frac{1}{4}\phi\sqrt{1-4\phi^{2}}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{C_{k-1}}{2k+1}\left\langle\phi^{2k+1}\right\rangle,

(136)

Here

C_{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2k\\
k\end{array}\right)

are the Catalan numbers and we use the notation
\phi=\phi^{a}T^{a}
and
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\left(\cdot\right)
.

Let us define the generator
\mathcal{T}[\phi]
of the connected amplitudes
associated with the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
as

\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{T}[\phi]\right)\equiv\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\hbar}{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\cdot\Delta\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}S_{int}\left[\phi\right]\right),

where we abbreviated

\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\cdot\Delta\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\equiv\int\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi^{a}\left(x\right)}\Delta\left(x,y\right)\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi^{a}\left(y\right)},

The above formula is a compact expression of the Wick expansion of the
S-
matrix, which can be obtained formally as

S=\colon\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{T}[\widehat{\phi}]\right)\colon

(137)

where
\widehat{\phi}
is the operator of the free field in the interaction

\square\widehat{\phi}=0

and
\colon\cdot\colon
means normal ordering. The amplitudes are then

\mathcal{T}[\phi]
according to the prescription (all the

\displaystyle A\left(1^{a_{1}},2^{a_{2}},\ldots,n^{a_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}/\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\left\langle T^{a_{\sigma(1)}}T^{a_{\sigma(2)}}\ldots T^{a_{\sigma(n)}}\right\rangle m_{diag}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\sigma\right)

(138)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\int\mathrm{d}x_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}p_{j}\cdot x_{j}}\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi^{a_{j}}\left(x_{j}\right)}\right)\mathcal{T}[\phi]|_{\phi\rightarrow 0}.

(139)

This formula corresponds algebraically to the contraction of the

(

Using the above definition of

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{T}\left[\phi\right]}{\delta\phi_{a}\left(x\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{T}\left[\phi\right]}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\hbar}{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\cdot\Delta\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\right)\frac{\delta S_{int}\left[\phi\right]}{\delta\phi_{a}\left(x\right)}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}S_{int}\left[\phi\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{T}\left[\phi\right]}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\hbar}{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\cdot\Delta\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\hbar}{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta\chi}\cdot\Delta\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta\chi}-\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\delta}{\delta\chi}\cdot\Delta\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\right)
