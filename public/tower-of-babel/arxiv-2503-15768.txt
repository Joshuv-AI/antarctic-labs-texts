Can one size fit all?: Measuring Failure in Multi-Document Summarization Domain Transfer
Alexandra DeLucia, Mark Dredze

Abstract
Abstractive multi-document summarization (MDS) is the task of automatically summarizing information in multiple documents, from news articles to conversations with multiple speakers. The training approaches for current MDS models can be grouped into four approaches: end-to-end with special pre-training (“direct”), chunk-then-summarize, extract-then-summarize, and inference with GPT-style models. In this work, we evaluate MDS models across training approaches, domains, and dimensions (reference similarity, quality, and factuality), to analyze how and why models trained on one domain can fail to summarize documents from another (News, Science, and Conversation) in the zero-shot domain transfer setting. We define domain-transfer “failure” as a decrease in factuality, higher deviation from the target, and a general decrease in summary quality. In addition to exploring domain transfer for MDS models, we examine potential issues with applying popular summarization metrics out-of-the-box.

Multi-document summarization (MDS) is an extended task of summarization, where the goal is to represent the key ideas from multiple documents in a single new document or paragraph.
Current MDS systems (models) are trained and evaluated on data from a single domain, such as news, but there have been no robust studies on how models trained on these domains transfer, or perform, on different domains.
While the overarching goal of MDS models is to condense information, models trained on one type of summary (e.g., news) often fail to transfer successfully to other domains (e.g., science) due to the unique nature of how summaries across different domains are written.

Recent MDS system surveys compare different models either on a single domain (e.g., conversation/dialogue
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;

)
) or focus on MDS corpora biases
(

;

)

found that retrieve-then-summarize models are best according to ROUGE,
Mullick et al. 2024
did not find a clear pattern in model performance across a variety of metrics.
Few works explore the zero-shot domain transfer ability of MDS systems, but
Zhang et al. 2021
did find that pre-training on news datasets can transfer well to conversation. However,
Fabbri et al. 2021a
a zero-shot model from news to conversation had lower performance than on conversation alone.

All types would “condense” the same conversation but at varying levels of granularity, style, and word choice.
Similarly, summaries for other domains also vary in incorporated information and style.

2.2
Multi-Document Summarization Model Types

Current high-performing fine-tuned abstractive summarization methods can be grouped into four approaches: summary-specific pre-training
(
Zhang et al. 2020a
;
Xiao et al. 2022a
;
Peper et al. 2024
)
, incremental summarization (“chunk-then-summarize”)
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, extracting claims or viewpoints (“extract-then-summarize”)
(
Fabbri et al. 2021a
;
Ernst et al. 2022
;
Ouyang et al. 2023
)
, and fine-tuning or few-shot summarization with large pre-trained models
(
Mullick et al. 2024
)
.
These groupings have been used in prior work
(
Mullick et al. 2024
)
.

Modified Pretraining.

Prior work has shown that fine-tuning a language model specifically pre-trained with summary-focused objectives improves downstream performance over non-specialized models
(
Xiao et al. 2022b
;
Zhang et al. 2020a
;
Peper et al. 2024
)
.

Zhang et al. 2020a
first introduced this concept of summary-specific pre-training, and it was later augmented to the multi-document case
(
Xiao et al. 2022b
;
Peper et al. 2024
)
.
We select
PRIMERA
to represent this group. PRIMERA is Longformer-Encoder-Decoder model initialized from BART-Large
(
Beltagy et al. 2020
)
and pre-trained with an Entity Pyramid objective on a large news corpora
(
Xiao et al. 2022b
)
.
While PELMS
(
Peper et al. 2024
)
adjusts the pre-training strategy to improve upon PRIMERA, we omit comparison to this model because it also uses a data filtering step, which overlaps with the extract-then-summarize approach.

Chunk-then-summarize.

Another approach to MDS is incremental summarization of documents, as done with
Summ
N

(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.
Summ
N
first segments the source documents and target summary and creates mappings between the source and target segments (via ROUGE max similarity), which and trains a separate BART-Large-CNN model
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
each “coarse” stages before the final “fine-grained” stage which produces the end summary.

Extract-then-summarize.

A different approach to the long-context problem of MDS systems is to first
extract
the salient portions of the documents before summarizing them.
The main models in this category are COMPO
(
Ouyang et al. 2023
)
, ProCluster
(
Ernst et al. 2022
)
, and ConvoSumm
(
Fabbri et al. 2021a
)
,.
COMPO is specifically tailored toward conversational summarization and provides summaries based on extracted sub-components (topics and actions) within the dialogue.
ProCluster extracts “propositions” in documents, clusters them, and then summarizes the clusters.
ConvoSumm first filters sentences from documents to only the “claims” and then summarizes the claims.
A variation of the extract-then-summarize pipeline is attribute-then-generate
(
Slobodkin et al. 2024
)
which requires multiple steps of content selection, sentence planning, and sentence-by-sentence generation.
We chose
ConvoSumm
over other extract-then-summarize models due to its applicability to many domains and non-overlap with different approaches with clustering or sentence-planning steps.

Pre-trained LLM.

While the above models are strong MDS systems, they are all the size of BART-Large (336M params.).
A growing trend across natural language processing research has been to fine-tune or adapt large, pre-trained decoder language models (LLMs) through in-context learning.
We select
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
to represent this approach due to its strong performance on a variety of tasks
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
.

2.3
Summarization Evaluation

Most MDS papers and their surveys use off-the-shelf automatic evaluation metrics without discussing drawbacks.
Summarization metrics usually measure one or more of the following attributes: similarity to a ground-truth summary, overall quality, and factuality.
While many MDS systems are evaluated with manual annotations, we focus on automatic evaluation in this work.

Similarity to Ground Truth.

Most text evaluation metrics compare the model output to a known, quality summary or
ground truth
(i.e., reference).
The most common metric for evaluating summary quality is ROUGE
(
Lin 2004
)
but is better viewed as a
similarity
metric since it is the token recall of the generated summary based on the reference (i.e., ground-truth).
The complement of ROUGE is BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, which measures precision instead of recall of generated summaries.

Other metrics in the spirit of ROUGE/BLEU are semantic-based instead of lexical, which do not penalize generations with similar meanings but different word choices.
The most popular version is BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020b
)
, which maps tokens from the generation to the reference before calculating overlap.
An alternative is MoverScore, which “optimized” version of BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020b
)
which uses soft token alignments from the hypothesis to the reference instead of a hard 1:1 mapping, allowing for a greater reward for semantically similar generated text.

Other similarity metrics are sub-“dimensions” of a trained multi-dimensional evaluator, like UniEval.
UniEval is a T5-Large model fine-tuned on synthetically augmented datasets for positive and negative examples of different “dimensions” of summary attributes (fluency, relevance, coherence, consistency)
(
Zhong et al. 2022
)
.
The UniEval-Relevance dimension is a similarity metric that compares the reference and generation as a whole instead of per token.

Overall Quality.

Not all desired qualities of a summary depend on a reference, some are intrinsic or rely on the source documents (e.g., fluency).
UniEval measures both fluency and coherence.
UniEval-Fluency is based solely on the generation, and UniEval-Coherence measures the coherence of the generation with respect to the source.

Factuality.

A growing concern with generated text is the possibility of hallucinations and non-faithfulness to the source material
(
Min et al. 2023
)
.
While objective factuality is important for the safety of LLMs (e.g., knowing that boiling water is hot), for MDS we typically assume that the source documents are factually correct and that a summary is only factual if its claims match the claims from the source.
We measure summary factuality with
UniEval-Consistency
, a single-dimensional evaluator trained to measure the factual consistency of generated text to the source (as opposed to continuously trained as with UniEval-Fluency and UniEval-Coherence).
A popular framework for factuality analysis is decompose-then-verify
(
Min et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2024
)
, and we leave this analysis to future work.

We include
ROUGE
,
MoverScore
, and multiple dimensions of
UniEval
in our analysis as representations for similarity to ground truth, factuality, and overall quality metrics.
While BARTScore
(
Yuan et al. 2021
)
measures many of the same dimensions as UniEval, we chose UniEval because it is better correlated with human evaluation
(
Zhong et al. 2022
)
and the potential bias of BARTScore to model generations, since most of the models are BART-based (“self bias”)
(
He et al. 2023
)
.
Another evaluation for summaries is source coverage, or how much of the source documents are represented in the summaries
(
Wolhandler et al. 2022
)
.
We leave this evaluation to future work.

Model Training

PRIMERA.

To handle examples with source documents that are too long for the context window, we follow the author’s code and evenly truncate the documents to fit.

4.2
MDS Model Transfer

Grouping the domain transfer results by model type across all dataset domains reveals even more performance trends (
Table

2
).

Pre-trained LLM outperforms all fine-tuned smaller models.

For all metrics besides similarity, Llama 3.1 outperforms the other models concerning factuality and quality.
This trend holds during domain transfer, with a performance difference ranging from
-1
to
+25\%
across all metrics.
The second-best performing model across all metrics, including accuracy, is PRIMERA, indicating an advantage of directly fine-tuning a long-context model (4K context window).

Zero-shot performance is the same as in-context learning with Llama 3.1.

Surprisingly, Llama has a similar performance at generating summaries with (in-context learning, ICL) or without being shown an example (zero-shot).
This indicates a strong bias to its pre-training versus “learning” from the example.
These results could differ with the addition of more examples (at the expense of a smaller context window available for the source documents) or a larger model.

ConvoSumm claim extraction is ineffective on Science.

The first step in the extract-then-summarize model ConvoSumm is to filter out sentences that are not identified as claims by a small, fine-tuned model.
This step removes
35\%
of sentences on average from each ConvoSumm-Reddit example,
51\%
from Multi-News, but less than
1\%
from Multi-XScience.
This is intuitive since there would be more filler sentences in conversations and news articles, but less so in scientific publications.

Chunk-then-summarize is the only approach guaranteed to fit all source documents.

With all models, including Llama 3.1 with a 131K context window, there were issues fitting all source documents for every example comfortably in the context windows.
As mentioned in
Section

3
, PRIMERA “handles” this issue by evenly dividing the context window among the source documents and truncating the ends.
The assumption that the beginning of documents is more important than the end does not hold for all domains and has only been identified in News
(
Dey et al. 2020
)
.
While ConvoSumm was able to fit all source documents from ConvoSumm-Reddit, it did have to truncate documents from 25 samples in Multi-XScience and 699 samples from MultiNews.
PRIMERA, with a context window double the size of ConvoSumm (2K vs 4K), was able to fit all Conversation and Science samples, but truncated 835 Multi-News examples.
Llama only had issues with 3 samples from Multi-News.

4.3
Evaluation of Evaluation

Word-based similarity scores are low across all models.

For all models, the generated summaries are not very similar to the ground-truth reference according to the lexical and semantic similarity metrics ROUGE and MoverScore.
The model with the highest accuracy (
0.20
ROUGE_L and
0.12
MoverScore) was PRIMERA, higher than the much larger LM, Llama-8B.
For lexical-based metrics like ROUGE, the low similarity can be explained through the model’s use of synonyms, which would not be
captured through token-based recall.
However, the low MoverScores can be explained by (1) the penalization of generations longer or more detailed than the reference and (2) the lack of a normalizing constant as provided by BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020b
)
.
5
5

5

Zhang et al. 2020b
use a learned constant to normalize the scores to
[0,1]
for easier readability.

Still, low scores across both accuracy metrics indicate that the generated summaries differ from their human-written counterparts.
Despite overall low accuracy scores, all models have the highest ROUGE/MoverScore performance on Multi-News (News), indicating the summaries might not be as abstract as the other datasets.

Surprisingly, the Relevance score which compares the reference and generation as a whole instead of token mappings, shows higher similarity, especially for Llama (
0.91
average).

Accuracy is affected the most.

The reference-based metric, accuracy, shows the most extreme gains and losses compared to the other metrics.
This is intuitive because a model’s summary can still be good but not be written in the same manner, or
style
, as the reference, which can happen if a model is biased towards the writing style of its training data.
The zero-shot baseline, which is only prompted and not given any styles, shows the least variance in accuracy compared to the other models.

All models are fluent.

Unsurprisingly, all models have high fluency scores (
0.80+
) since all models are of size BART-Large and above.
While fluency is still an important attribute to check, it might not be as important as it once was.

Non-reference metrics do not scale well to the multi-document setting.

Evaluating for attributes that depend on the source documents (Consistency and Coherence) is most affected by the long-document nature of multi-document summarization.
The options are either to 1) truncate the documents, which can greatly alter the scores depending on which documents are included, or 2) calculate the metric for
every
source document and aggregate the scores.
More work is needed to determine whether truncating the source documents for UniEval over- or under-estimates model performance.
Another option is to turn to large-context models like GPT-4 to be a “judge” for specific criteria
(
Kim et al. 2024
;
Vu et al. 2024
)
.
This use of LLMs, which typically can fit 10K+ tokens in their context windows, is a potential solution to this scaling issue.
The reference-based metrics, i.e., similarity metrics that only compare the ground-truth and generated summaries, are not affected by this issue.

Trained metrics can penalize out-of-training data.

Just as summarization models can have lower performance on out-of-domain data, so can trained evaluation models like UniEval.
As noted before, the fluency and coherence scores on Science are
0.10
points lower than on News and Conversation.
This could be an issue with the summarization model training, but the fluency and coherence scores are also lower on the ground-truth summaries,
0.30+
points lower (“Ref.” in
Table

1
), which should not be the case as all summaries are human-written and should have high scores.
An explanation is that UniEval was trained on a synthetically augmented version of news data, CNN/DailyMail, which is very different from Scientific text.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

In most work that surveys or proposes multi-document summarization (MDS) systems, there is little overlap between the analysis of model performance and the shortcomings of popularly used summarization evaluation metrics.
We bridged this gap through experiments and analysis of not only the zero-shot domain transfer ability of MDS systems but also their evaluation.
We analyzed three components of a multi-document summarization (MDS) pipeline for domain transfer: 1) the domain of the training data, 2) the model type, and 3) the common evaluation metrics.

We found important facets to consider in each component.
For domain transfer, the training and test domains are important for model performance, regardless of the model type.
While we did not find a clear trend between Conversation, News, and Science, it was apparent that the transfer was highly affected by the
style
of the summaries, albeit more than the domain itself.

Summaries from different domains but written in the same style (e.g., high-level vs low-level) could transfer better with higher similarity to the reference.

For different approaches to MDS, a model that didn’t hold a strong bias towards the style of its training (or example) data is ideal, as is the case with a large pre-trained decoder model, like Llama 3.1.
If the domain styles differ greatly,
a zero- or few-shot approach with less bias is ideal.

Notable issues we uncovered with evaluation metrics that prevented the fair evaluation of summaries were domain-specific penalizations, mostly for Science, questions with scaling to the MDS case, and the inability of ground-truth similarity metrics to adjust for style change (ROUGE and MoverScore).
When evaluating an MDS model on a new domain,
word-similarity metrics judge style more than content similarity
and
trained evaluators could over- or under-judge model performance due to limited information in the context window.

Limitations

Despite the rise in popularity of large, pre-trained decoder models, most fine-tuned multi-document summarization models are on the scale of a few hundred million parameters, e.g., BART-Large.
We did not analyze the effect of model size or scale concerning summarization performance or domain transfer abilities.

Another limitation is our focus on English, as all of the datasets and learned metrics (i.e., UniEval), were in English or trained on English data.

Ethical Considerations

We did not evaluate the model output and training datasets for
absolute
factuality (i.e., correctness with respect to the world) or harmful language.
We leave these tasks to future work or the incorporation of an additional step for model safety.
