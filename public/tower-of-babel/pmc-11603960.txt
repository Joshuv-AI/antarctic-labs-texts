Head-to-head comparison of tau PET tracers [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 in non-demented individuals with brain amyloid deposition: the TAU-PET FACEHBI cohort

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSecond-generation tau tracers for positron emission tomography (PET) show high affinity for paired helical filaments tau deposits characteristic of Alzheimer´s disease and low off-target binding. Differences in their chemical structure though may lead to variations in their regional tau uptake and off-target signal. In this work, we aimed to compare the in-vivo uptake of tau tracers [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 in the early stages of the AD continuum.MethodsData from the TAU-PET FACEHBI clinical trial (EUDRA-CT 2021–000473-83) were analyzed. All participants were non-demented and underwent tau imaging with [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 PET within 3 months, amyloid imaging with [18F]Florbetaben and brain magnetic resonance imaging. Tau PET standardized uptake values ratios (SUVR) were calculated in Braak and typical off-target regions using the inferior cerebellar cortex as a reference region.ResultsThe cohort consisted of 18 individuals with subjective cognitive decline (n = 13) and mild cognitive impairment (n = 5), with centiloid values ranging from 17 to 159. Both tau tracers showed similar tau pathology distribution but presented a distinct off-target signal pattern on visual read. SUVR measurements for [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 were highly correlated in all Braak regions (R2 range [0.65–0.80]). Regarding off-target signal, [18F]PI-2620 had higher SUVRs in vascular structures, and [18F]RO948 had higher SUVRs in the skull/meninges.ConclusionsIn a cohort of individuals at early stages of the AD continuum, tau PET tracers [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 showed similar in-vivo uptake in all Braak regions and distinct off-target signal. These preliminary results support the development of standardized quantification scales for tau deposition that are tracer-independent.Trial registrationAEMPS EudraCT 2021–000473-83. Introduction

Beta-amyloid (Aβ) plaques in the extracellular space and neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein are the defining neuropathological hallmarks of Alzheimer´s disease (AD) [1, 2]. It is crucial to characterize and monitor these neuropathological alterations in-vivo across different cognitive stages for a more comprehensive understanding of AD pathophysiology. Presently, a variety of AD biomarkers are being utilized in both clinical evaluation of patients with cognitive symptoms and research studies or clinical trials. These biomarkers are measured in body fluids such as cerebrospinal fluid and plasma [3], and through brain neuroimaging techniques like magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [4] and positron emission tomography (PET) [5].

Several radiopharmaceutical tracers binding to tau have become available in the last decade [6]. The development of tau PET tracers has been challenging for multiple reasons, including the intracellular location of tau, the presence of different isoforms (three repeat and four repeat) and morphologies (paired helical vs straight filaments) of the tau protein, its multiple post-translational modifications, and the lower concentrations in the brain compared to Aβ plaques [7, 8]. Research using tau PET tracers has provided valuable information about the distribution of tau aggregates in the brain [9], their longitudinal changes [10, 11], association with cognitive decline [12, 13], and correlation with other AD biomarkers [14, 15]. [18F]flortaucipir (Tauvid, Eli Lilly and Co) was one of the first tau tracers developed and has recently received approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for clinical use [16, 17]. The main challenge of first-generation tau tracers was their significant off-target binding to non-tau structures (including dense core Aβ plaques, melanin, lipofuscin, MAO-A and MAO-B) [18–20] which complicated the correct interpretation of in-vivo PET images. Thus, second generation tau tracers, such as [18F]RO948 [21] and [18F]PI-2620 [22] among others were developed with the aim of overcoming these limitations.

Differences in chemical structure of these second-generation tau tracers may lead to variations in their in-vivo kinetics, binding specificity, in-vivo regional uptake and off-target signal. For instance, [18F]PI-2620 has a high binding affinity for aggregated tau filaments in both three repeat (3R) and four repeat (4R) isoforms [23], and autoradiography studies show that it binds to both PHF-tau in AD and also tau filaments in non-AD tauopathies. [18F]PI-2620 shows no off-target binding to Aβ, a-synuclein or MAO proteins [24]. Similarly, [18F]RO948 binds with high affinity and specificity to tau aggregates and lacks affinity for Aβ plaques and for MAO proteins [25]. [18F]RO948 though, shows specific binding to PHF-tau in AD, with absence of significant uptake in non-AD tauopathy cases [21, 26].

These discrepancies highlight the need to perform direct comparisons of in-vivo uptake across tracers and assess the feasibility of defining standardized quantitative tau metrics similar to the Centiloid (CL) scale created for Aβ imaging [27].

In this study we imaged 18 individuals with subjective cognitive decline (SCD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) showing early Aβ brain deposition with [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948. Part of this data has been used to develop CenTauR, a standard scale for tau PET imaging proposed by the Critical Path Institute (www.c-path.org/cpad) in order to facilitate the establishment of universal cut-off values, the pooling of large cohorts and the comparison of longitudinal data [28]. Here we provide further details on the in-vivo regional uptake and off-target signal profile of these two tau PET tracers.

Methods

Study participants

Data from participants of the TAU-PET FACEHBI study, a clinical trial (EUDRA-CT 2021–000473-83) carried out at Ace Alzheimer Center Barcelona, were analyzed. This study was embedded in the Fundació ACE Healthy Brain Initiative (FACEHBI) cohort [29], a longitudinal study of aging, life-style, cognition and biomarkers in 200 individuals with SCD ongoing since 2014 at Ace Alzheimer Center Barcelona. FACEHBI participants underwent neurological assessment, cognitive evaluation, brain MRI and amyloid PET imaging with [18F]Florbetaben at baseline visit and at 2- and 5-years follow-up visits (v2 and v5). A neurological assessment and cognitive evaluation were performed in intermediate 1, 3-, 4-, 6- and 7-years follow-up visits (v1, v3, v4, v6 and v7). More detailed information about the FACEHBI study can be found elsewhere [29–35].

Inclusion criteria for the TAU-PET FACEHBI study were as follows: being a participant of the FACEHBI study who completed follow-up v5, having a clinical diagnosis of SCD or MCI, and evidence of early brain amyloid accumulation (Centiloid (CL) > 13.5 at follow-up v5).

Participants underwent neurological assessment, cognitive testing, amyloid imaging with [18F]Florbetaben, brain MRI (all the former as part of FACEHBI v5) and tau PET imaging with [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 within 3 months.

Cognitive evaluation and clinical diagnosis

Participants were administered the Neuropsychological battery of Fundació ACE (NBACE) [36] along with additional cognitive tests and also completed self-administered questionnaires [29]. All FACEHBI participants had a diagnosis of SCD at baseline according to the study inclusion criteria. Their clinical diagnosis was reassessed at every follow-up visit. A diagnosis of MCI was established when any of the NBACE test scores was impaired according to the published cut-offs [37].

Amyloid PET image acquisition and processing

[18F]Florbetaben imaging was performed as part of FACEHBI follow-up v5. Images were acquired at the Nuclear Medicine Department from Hospital Clínic i Provincial de Barcelona in a Siemens© Biograph molecular PET-CT machine. Twenty-minutes PET images (4 × 5 min frames) were acquired from 90 min after intravenous administration of 300 MBq of [18F]Florbetaben radio tracer (NeuraCeq©), administered as a single slow intravenous bolus (6 s/ml) in a total volume of up to 10 ml. PET images were reconstructed using the Ultra-HD (PSF + TOF) algorithm (8 iterations and 21 subsets), with a matrix size of 400 × 400. MRI cortical and subcortical segmentation of the T1-weighted images was carried out with Freesurfer (v5.3, https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). [18F]Florbetaben images were processed with the FSL package (v5.0, https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki). [18F]Florbetaben images were aligned to the structural images, and the standard uptake value ratio (SUVR) was determined as the mean value of the cortical regions segmented on the MRI, using the whole cerebellum as the reference region. CL values were calculated as previously described [38]. [18F]Florbetaben positivity was established at CL > 13.5, corresponding to early amyloid deposition [39].

Brain MRI acquisition and processing

Brain MRIs were performed as part of FACEHBI follow-up v5 within 30 days of the [18F]Florbetaben PET scans. Scans were acquired at Clinica Corachan in Barcelona using a 1.5-T Siemens Magneton Aera (Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel head coil. Anatomical T1-weighted images were acquired using a rapid acquisition gradient-echo three-dimensional (3D) magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) 2.200 ms, echo time (TE) 2.66 ms, inversion time (TI) 900 ms, flip angle 8°, field of view (FOV) 250 mm, and isotropic voxel size of 1 mm3. Subjects also received axial T2-weighted, 3D isotropic fast fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and axial T2*-weighted sequences to detect significant vascular pathology or microbleeds.

3D T1-weighted images were processed using FastSurfer (v2.1.2, https://github.com/Deep-MI/FastSurfer) [40] to obtain cortical and subcortical parcellations. Six Braak regions of interest (ROIs) were composed from the FastSurfer parcellation as described by Cho et colleagues [41]. These Braak ROIs were used to represent areas commonly affected by tau pathology. Five additional ROIs typically spared by tau pathology were considered to evaluate off-target signal: thalamus, striatum (composed of caudate, putamen, and pallidum), white matter, choroid plexus, and skull/meninges. Thalamus, striatum, and white matter ROIs were obtained from FastSurfer parcellation. The choroid plexus ROI was obtained using a deep learning segmentation model [42]. Finally, the skull/meninges ROI was generated from the CT image obtained for the PET attenuation correction using the following steps: first, skull probability maps were obtained with CTseg (https://github.com/WCHN/CTseg) [43] and aligned to the 3D T1-weighted image using the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs v2.4.3, https://github.com/ANTsX/ANTs). Since two CT images were available for each subject, the skull probability maps in T1 space were multiplied with each other and thresholded at 0.9.

To get rid of bone structures far from the brain (teeth, vertebras, …), all voxels in the skull masks located at a distance greater than 10 mm from the FastSurfer brain mask were removed (see Supplementary Materials for a visual representation of these steps).

Finally, the 3D-T1-weighted images of each participant were non-linearly normalized to the MNI space using ANTs. The calculated transformations were used to bring the PET images to the MNI space, and to bring the SUIT cerebellum atlas [44] from the MNI to the 3D-T1 space. A ROI representing the inferior cerebellar cortex was generated from the SUIT cerebellum atlas and masked using the grey matter mask obtained from SPM (v12; https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/).

Tau PET image acquisition

Tau-PET images were performed at SIMM Imagen Médica in Barcelona, using a Philips Gemini TF16 Select PET/CT scanner with the head reclined and immobilized in an anatomical headrest. Each participant underwent PET imaging with [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 within 3 months.

For [18F]PI-2620 imaging, 185 MBq (5 mCi) of the radiotracer were injected via an intravenous cannula in a slow bolus (1 ml/6 s) with subsequent washing of 10 ml of saline solution. Image acquisition started 45 min post-injection and lasted for 30 min (6 × 5 min frames).

For [18F]RO948 imaging, 370 MBq (10 mCi) of the radiotracer were injected via an intravenous cannula in a slow bolus (1 ml/6 s) with subsequent washing of 10 ml of saline solution. Image acquisition started 60 min post-injection and lasted for 30 min (6 × 5 min frames).

PET images were reconstructed using the Philips iterative algorithm LOR-RAMLA (2 iterations and 33 subsets), with a matrix size of 128 × 128.

Tau PET quantitative analysis

Tau PET images were corrected for interframe subject motion using SPM. The motion-corrected time-averaged [18F]RO948 PET image was then rigidly aligned to the subject specific 3D T1-weighted image using ANTs. Subsequently, the motion-corrected time-averaged [18F]PI-2620 PET image was rigidly aligned to the motion-corrected time-averaged [18F]RO948 PET image in the T1 space. This pipeline ensured the best alignment between the two tau PET images. SUVR images were calculated using the previously derived inferior cerebellar cortex ROI as reference region. Mean SUVR values were extracted for all the ROIs (six Braak ROIs, thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, choroid plexus, and skull/meninges). SUVR images were then normalized to the MNI space using the previously calculated transformation, and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel with FWHM = 5 mm. To note, smoothing was applied only in the MNI space for the voxelwise analysis, and not for the regional analysis.

Statistical analysis

Pairwise t-test, Pearson correlation coefficient and linear regression analyses were used to compare [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 SUVR values in the different ROIs considered. Linear regression coefficients (slope and intercept) were computed using a total least square approach and expressed with [18F]RO948 as the independent variable (i.e., SUVR_PI2620 = slope * SUVR_RO948 + intercept). The total least square approach was used as it accounts for errors in both the dependent (SUVR of [18F]PI-2620) and independent (SUVR of [18F]RO948) variables.

Confidence interval for Pearson correlation coefficient and linear regression parameters were obtained by bootstrap analysis (10,000 resampling).

Voxelwise paired t-tests were computed using the Statistical NonParametric Mapping Toolbox (SnPM13.1.05, http://nisox.org/Software/SnPM13/) on the smoothed SUVR images. This analysis was run using the variance smoothing option (FWHM = 5 mm) and 5000 permutations. The variance smoothing option increases the statistical power of the test by generating smoothed pseudo-t statistical maps [45]. Significance level was set at 0.05 after false discovery rate correction. Since visual tau deposition was sparse and present in a limited number of participants, the voxelwise analysis was restricted only to participants not showing any elevated tracer uptake in regions typical of tau pathology to better understand the off-target profiles of these two tau PET tracers. The following areas of potential off-target signal were evaluated in the final pseudo-t statistical maps: scalp, base of the skull, eyes/retina, pineal gland, venous sinus, substantia nigra, nasal mucosa, vermis, choroid plexus, dentate nucleus, basal ganglia, and skull/meninges.

Three participants showed elevated tracer uptake in cortical regions, one had increased uptake only in the mesial temporal area, and the other 14 did not show any tau deposition, with both tracers. The cortical regions showing elevated tracer uptake were isolated, visible with both radioligands, and did not follow the typical AD pattern. One participant showed elevated signal in the basal ganglia with both tracers. It was also noted that high uptake in the skull/meninges was more frequently found with [18F]RO948, while [18F]PI-2620 had a higher uptake in areas further away from the brain, such as the scalp, the eyes/retina, and the nasal mucosa. Elevated uptake in vascular structures was more frequently observed with [18F]PI-2620.

[18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 SUVR quantification in Braak regions

The range of SUVR in the different Braak ROIs was relatively similar between [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 (Fig. 3, Table 2). More in detail, [18F]PI-2620 had significantly higher SUVR values in Braak I (mean difference: −0.08 ± 0.14 SUVR; p = 0.03); no significant differences were observed in Braak II, and [18F]RO948 had higher SUVR values in Braak III (mean difference: 0.06 ± 0.09 SUVR; p = 0.01), Braak IV (mean difference: 0.12 ± 0.10 SUVR; p = 0.0001), Braak V (mean difference: 0.13 ± 0.10 SUVR; p < 0.0001), and Braak VI (mean difference: 0.13 ± 0.09 SUVR; p < 0.0001) compared to [18F]PI-2620. [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 PET off-target signal

Discussion

In this study we performed a head-to-head comparison of second-generation tau PET tracers [18F]PI-2620 and [18F]RO948 in a cohort of 18 individuals in the early stages of the AD continuum (SCD and MCI with early Aβ accumulation). Our data showed a high correlation of SUVR values in all Braak regions (R2 range [0.65–0.80]) and a distinct off-target pattern between the two tracers. The visual inspection of the images showed similar uptake patterns for the two ligands, both in cortical and subcortical brain regions.

[18F]RO948 had higher SUVR values across the brain compared to [18F]PI-2620, even when excluding participants showing cortical tau deposition (mean SUVR values in the whole cortex were 1.04 ± 0.10 SUVR for [18F]RO948 and 0.90 ± 0.09 SUVR for [18F]PI-2620). One exception was Braak I, where [18F]PI-2620 had higher SUVR values compared to [18F]RO948, probably due to the proximity of this region to the cavernous sinus, an area of off-target binding for [18F]PI-2620.

Nonetheless, the regression slopes between the SUVR values of [18F]RO948 and [18F]PI-2620 ranged from 0.9 in Braak I to 1.22 in Braak V, with 95% confidence intervals always including the value 1. This suggests that while [18F]PI-2620 starts from a lower baseline SUVR value, [18F]RO948 and [18F]PI-2620 have similar dynamic range and that a unit increase of SUVR value with one tracer will roughly correspond to a unit increase of the SUVR value with the other tracer.

Regarding the off-target signal, the main area of concern for [18F]RO948 was the skull/meningeal uptake, as this area is close to the cortical surface, and it might affect tau quantification through spill-over effect. The origin of this signal is currently unknown but it has been reported to be patient specific, to decrease with age, and to be more frequent in females than in males [46], possibly due to lower bone density in females [47]. For [18F]PI-2620, the off-target areas of concerns were the choroid plexus, due to the proximity to the hippocampus (Braak II), the venous sinuses, due to the proximity to the reference region, and the cavernous sinus, due to the proximity to the entorhinal cortex (Braak I). It has been suggested that this off-target signal may be related to binding to neuromelanin, melanin and blood components [48]. Factors as scanner resolution, post reconstruction smoothing, and participant motion might influence the impact of these off-target signals on tau quantification, and ad-hoc techniques are being developed to deal with these issues [49, 50].

Moderate to strong correlation coefficients were measured between the SUVR of [18F]RO948 and [18F]PI-2620 in off-target regions (ranging from R2 = 0.29 in thalamus, to R2 = 0.67 in skull/meninges), suggesting a common substrate to the signal arising from these regions. Tracer differences in intensity in these regions could thus be a reflection of different tracer kinetic and imaging windows.

A limitation of this study is that our participants, being in the very early stages of the AD continuum, exhibited a relatively low tau burden. This, along with the use of a low CL cut-off (> 13.5) to determine early Aβ accumulation, may influence the SUVR correlation results in this study, which are numerically lower compared to those reported between first and second generation tau tracers [51, 52]. Nonetheless, the high correlations observed in particular in the earlier Braak ROIs, suggest that SUVR values obtained from these tracers can quantify low tau deposition before this is evident from visual inspection of the images.

Another limitation of this study was that we did not harmonize the difference in image count statistics resulting from differences in the injected doses, time windows, and kinetic of the tracers. The choice of the injected doses, 5 mCi for [18F]PI-2620 and 10 mCi for [18F]RO948 was based on the recommended use provided by the respective tracer manufacturers. However, previous research has demonstrated that the [18F]RO948 injected dose can be considerably reduced without substantial effects on SUVR values in the Braak ROI [53]. We thus believe that the higher injected dose of [18F]RO948 did not have an impact on the correlations reported in this study.

In conclusion, the results from this head-to-head tau PET comparison study indicate that [18F]RO948 and [18F]PI-2620 have a similar in-vivo uptake in all cortical brain regions considered and both tracers are able to detect tau binding in very early stages of the AD continuum. The off-target signal pattern was distinct, with higher signal in the skull/meninges for [18F]RO948, and in vascular structures for [18F]PI-2620. Altogether, these preliminary data support the feasibility of developing standardized quantification scales for tau-PET imaging that are tracer independent.
