Recently, it has been increasingly linked to cardiovascular diseases. To investigate this connection, our study analyzed two major clinical databases, MIMIC and eICU, to assess the short-term (28-day) prognostic value of iron metabolism biomarkers—including serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC)—in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS). We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from patients with ACS in the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV v2.2) database as the internal cohort. For external validation, we utilized the eICU Collaborative Research Database (v2.0). To examine the association between iron biomarkers (serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC) and short-term adverse outcomes in ACS patients, we employed multivariate Cox regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, and Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves. Subsequently, we randomly split the internal cohort into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). Univariate logistic regression was applied to identify common prognostic variables across the training set, testing set, and the external validation cohort. Using these shared variables, we developed a multivariate logistic regression model for risk prediction. Finally, the performance of this predictive model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The study ultimately included 1,652 ACS patients from the MIMIC cohort and 701 from the eICU cohort, with 28-day mortality rates of 10.90% and 20.40%, respectively. In the fully adjusted multivariable model, iron biomarkers showed significant associations with 28-day mortality. Elevated ferritin predicted higher mortality risk (per-unit increase: HR 1.001, 95% CI 1.001–1.002, P = 0.001; Q4 vs. Q1: HR 1.983, 95% CI 1.143–3.440, P = 0.015), while higher transferrin and TIBC were protective. The quartile-based analysis revealed clinically meaningful risk gradients. RCS analysis revealed a linear relationship between transferrin, TIBC, and the risk of short-term adverse outcomes, while the relationship for ferritin was nonlinear. Finally, ROC analysis demonstrated that the risk prediction model, which integrated iron biomarkers with eight other independent predictors, outperformed traditional critical illness scoring systems in identifying high-risk ACS patients. The AUC values were 0.70 for the training set, 0.75 for the testing set, and 0.69 for the external eICU validation set. All findings were consistently validated in the external eICU cohort. This study confirms that iron metabolism biomarkers—specifically ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC—serve as independent predictors of 28-day mortality risk in patients with acute coronary syndrome, whereas serum iron showed no significant association with short-term mortality. The risk prediction model developed from these markers demonstrated superior discriminative performance (AUC: 0.69–0.75) compared to conventional scoring systems during both internal and external validation. These biomarkers not only showed statistically significant continuous associations but also enabled clear risk stratification, with extreme quartiles demonstrating markedly different mortality risks. Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), the most critical form of cardiovascular disease, is characterized by high morbidity and mortality, representing a major global public health burden1. Its pathological basis lies in the instability and rupture of coronary atherosclerotic plaques, leading to thrombus formation and myocardial ischemic injury2,3. Beyond established risk factors such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, smoking, and obesity, growing evidence indicates that disturbances in trace element metabolism play a significant role in the development and progression of cardiovascular diseases4–6. Iron, a crucial element for oxygen transport, energy metabolism, and cell proliferation, is of particular interest. Dysregulation of iron homeostasis—whether systemic iron overload or functional iron deficiency—can contribute to atherosclerosis and myocardial injury through mechanisms including catalyzing oxidative stress, amplifying inflammatory responses, and inducing ferroptosis (an iron-dependent form of cell death)7–10. Consequently, disturbances in iron metabolism may be linked to the onset and progression of ACS and could potentially serve as valuable biomarkers for risk assessment and prognosis.

Iron metabolism biomarkers—including serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC)—are core clinical indicators for assessing the body’s iron storage, transport, and utilization. Iron itself is an essential element involved in vital physiological processes such as oxygen transport, electron transfer, cell proliferation, and DNA synthesis8,11,12. Basic research indicates that elevated serum iron levels can promote atherosclerosis by inducing lipid alterations, increasing vascular permeability, causing sustained endothelial activation, elevating pro-atherosclerotic inflammatory mediators, and reducing nitric oxide availability13. As an acute-phase protein, ferritin reflects the intensity of systemic and local plaque inflammation. Moreover, impaired intracellular ferritin function can lead to an expansion of the labile iron pool, thereby triggering ferroptosis—a form of iron-dependent cell death—in cardiomyocytes and plaque cells, which exacerbates plaque instability14,15. Transferrin, the primary iron transport protein, directly reflects the body’s capacity to bind and transport iron16. However, existing clinical evidence has largely focused on single biomarkers or long-term outcomes, and the impact of iron metabolism markers on cardiovascular disease remains debated17,18. Consequently, for these readily available iron parameters, whether they are definitively linked to ACS progression and whether they can effectively predict short-term (e.g., 28-day) mortality risk in ACS patients still lacks robust evidence from large-scale, multi-center clinical cohorts.

Therefore, this retrospective cohort study utilized two large, publicly available critical care databases. We employed the MIMIC-IV (v2.2) database for model development and internal validation, and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (v2.0) for independent external validation. Our primary aims were to evaluate the impact of iron metabolism biomarkers on the short-term prognosis of ACS and to assess their potential utility for early risk stratification and future therapeutic targeting.

Materials and methods

Data source: We conducted a multicenter, retrospective cohort study using data from two large, public critical care databases. The internal cohort was derived from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV v2.2) database, which contains de-identified clinical data for approximately 100,000 intensive care unit (ICU) and emergency department admissions from a single medical center between 2008 and 201919. The external validation cohort was obtained from the eICU Collaborative Research Database, which encompasses over 200,000 ICU admissions across 208 hospitals in the United States20. The creation of the MIMIC-IV database was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB protocol numbers: 2001P001699, 0403000206), and all data are fully anonymized, waiving the requirement for additional ethical approval for this analysis. The researchers completed the required CITI program training and were granted access to these datasets for the present study.

Study Population: The study population comprised adults admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) with a diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), which we refer to as “severe ACS” in this study to denote this critically ill cohort requiring intensive care. Patients were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9/10) codes for acute myocardial infarction, ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina (UA). Specifically, our inclusion criteria encompassed the full spectrum of ACS presentations, including but not limited to: STEMI (e.g., anterior, inferior, involving specific coronary arteries), NSTEMI, unstable angina, and cases with acute complications of myocardial infarction (e.g., ventricular septal defect, papillary muscle rupture). The inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) first-time ICU admission; (3) ICU length of stay of at least 24 h; and (4) availability of complete laboratory records for serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC measurements (Fig. 1 and S3).

Flowchart of patient selection from the MIMIC-IV databases.

Variable extraction: Data extraction was performed using the PostgreSQL (v13.7.2) database management system, assisted by the Navicat Premium (v16) interface. Required variables were retrieved by executing structured query language (SQL) code. The extracted data encompassed six categories: (1) demographic information, (2) comorbidities, (3) vital signs on admission, (4) disease severity scores on admission, (5) the first laboratory tests within 24 h of admission, and (6) treatment interventions. Detailed definitions for all variables are provided in the baseline characteristics table (Table 1). Additionally, anemia status was defined based on the first hemoglobin value using World Health Organization (WHO) sex-specific criteria. Data on blood product transfusions (packed red blood cells, platelets, fresh frozen plasma) administered during the ICU stay were also collected. Variables with a missing data rate exceeding 30% were excluded from the analysis. For the remaining variables, missing values were handled using multiple imputation via the “mice” package in R.

Definition of endpoint

The endpoint of the study is defined as the all-cause mortality rate within 28 days of admission to ICU.

Boruta feature algorithm

To identify and rank the importance of variables associated with the study endpoint, we employed the Boruta algorithm for feature selection. Boruta is a wrapper method built around a random forest classifier. Its core principle is to create randomly shuffled copies of all original features, termed “shadow features,” which serve as a reference benchmark. In an iterative process, the algorithm compares the importance score (Z-score) of each original feature against the maximum importance observed among the shadow features. Original features that consistently demonstrate significantly higher importance than this reference maximum are deemed relevant and retained, while others are discarded. This permutation-based approach effectively mitigates overfitting common to simpler selection methods, providing a more robust identification of features that contribute substantively to model performance21.

The association between iron metabolism markers and endpoints: To evaluate the association between iron metabolism biomarkers and clinical outcomes, we through continuous variable and quartile analysis performed Cox proportional hazards regression analyses. To control for potential confounders, three sequentially adjusted models were constructed: Model 1: Unadjusted. Model 2: Adjusted for demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, weight) and baseline comorbidities. Model 3: Further adjusted for variables showing significant differences between survivors and non-survivors (including laboratory parameters, vital signs, admission severity scores, and treatment interventions; refer to Table 1 and Supplementary Table 5). To address multicollinearity, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated for all variables in Model 3, and variables with a VIF greater than 5 were excluded (Table S6). Furthermore, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles was applied to explore potential nonlinear relationships between each biomarker and the outcome. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to visualize and compare the cumulative 28-day survival probabilities across patient groups stratified by the median of each iron metabolism biomarker (ferritin, transferrin, TIBC). Differences between these survival curves were assessed using the log-rank test. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Risk prediction modeling and validation: The ACS cohort from the internal MIMIC database was randomly split into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%) using the “rsample” package in R, with the random seed set to 1 for reproducibility. To identify robust and generalizable predictors, a strict multi-step consensus approach was employed to minimize overfitting: First univariate logistic regression was performed separately in the internal training set, internal testing set, and the external eICU validation cohort to identify variables associated with short-term all-cause mortality. Only variables that demonstrated a statistically significant association (P < 0.05) across all three independent datasets were retained and defined as the core predictors of mortality risk. This cross-dataset consensus ensured the selected variables were not artifacts of a single sample. These consensus variables were then incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model to build the final risk prediction model. The predictive performance of the model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Its stability was further assessed across the training set, testing set, and the external eICU validation cohort.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were assessed using Student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency and percentage, and compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) within the RStudio environment. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 1652 patients with acute coronary syndrome were included in the MIMIC-IV database for the study. According to the quartiles stratified baseline of iron metabolism indicators (serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC), please refer to Supplementary Tables S1-S4. According to the 28 day clinical outcome, the patients were divided into survivors (n = 1472) and non survivors (n = 180) based on baseline feature analysis (Table 1). The overall 28-day ICU mortality rate was 10.90%. The median age of the cohort was 73.0 years, with 61.2% male and 38.8% female. Non-survivors were older than survivors (median age: 75.0 vs. 72.0 years). The incidence of both acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) was significantly higher in the non-survivor group. Furthermore, conditions associated with systemic inflammation and altered iron metabolism were common. Sepsis was diagnosed in 64.9% of the overall cohort and was significantly more prevalent in non-survivors (85.6% vs. 62.4%, P < 0.001). Liver cirrhosis was present in 7.0% of patients and was also more frequent among non-survivors (13.9% vs. 6.1%, P < 0.001). Hematological malignancy (leukemia) was rare (1.0%) and did not differ between groups (P = 0.844). Furthermore, non-survivors had consistently higher disease severity scores (e.g., SOFA, APSIII, SAPSII). Crucially, significant differences were observed in iron metabolism biomarkers. Median ferritin levels were markedly elevated in non-survivors (641 ng/mL vs. 342 ng/mL in survivors), and a substantially higher proportion of non-survivors were in the highest quartile (Q4) for ferritin (41.1% vs. 23.0%). Conversely, median levels of transferrin (136 mg/dL vs. 166 mg/dL) and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) (176 µg/dL vs. 216 µg/dL) were lower in non-survivors. For both transferrin and TIBC, 42.8% of non-survivors fell into the lowest quartile (Q1), compared to 22.8% of survivors. Additionally, non-survivors exhibited higher levels of inflammation and cellular stress markers (e.g., RDW, WBC), lower levels of nutrition and oxygenation-related markers (e.g., albumin, hemoglobin), and a higher rate of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) (20.6% vs. 10.0%).

The relationship between anemia status, blood transfusion, and mortality rate

Based on WHO criteria, anemia was prevalent in the cohort. Stratified by severity, anemia was strongly associated with higher disease severity scores, inflammatory markers, and 28-day mortality (P < 0.001, see Supplementary Table S7). Iron metabolism biomarkers (ferritin, transferrin, TIBC) also varied significantly across anemia severity groups (all P < 0.001). Regarding interventions, 30.6% of patients received packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusions during their ICU stay. Non-survivors were more likely to have received PRBC transfusions (39.4% vs. 29.5%, P = 0.006) and received a higher mean volume (533.7 mL vs. 338.2 mL, P = 0.050) compared to survivors (Table 1).

Multivariate Cox regression analysis of the correlation between iron metabolism markers and short-term mortality in ACS patients.

In the multivariable Cox regression models with progressive adjustment (Table 2), iron metabolism biomarkers (ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC) remained independently associated with 28-day ICU mortality in ACS patients, both as continuous and categorical variables. In the continuous analysis of the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each unit increase in ferritin was associated with a significantly higher risk of death (HR 1.001, 95% CI 1.001–1.002, P = 0.001). In contrast, both transferrin and TIBC acted as protective factors, with each unit increase corresponding to a reduced mortality risk (transferrin: HR 0.996, 95% CI 0.993–0.999, P = 0.001; TIBC: HR 0.995, 95% CI 0.993–0.998, P = 0.001). The categorical analysis by quartiles revealed clear dose-response relationships. Compared to the lowest quartile (Q1), patients in the highest ferritin quartile (Q4) had a significantly increased mortality risk (HR 1.983, 95% CI 1.143–3.440, P = 0.015). Conversely, higher quartiles of both transferrin and TIBC demonstrated a graded protective effect. The mortality risk for patients in Q4 was 55.2% lower for transferrin (HR 0.448, 95% CI 0.265–0.756, P = 0.003) and 56.3% lower for TIBC (HR 0.437, 95% CI 0.260–0.734, P = 0.002) compared to those in Q1. The RCS analysis confirmed significant overall associations between these biomarkers and short-term mortality (all P for overall < 0.05). A non-linear, positive J-shaped relationship was observed for ferritin (P for non-linearity = 0.026; Fig. 2A), while transferrin and TIBC exhibited an approximately linear, inverse relationship with mortality risk (P for non-linearity > 0.05; Fig. 2B, C). Consistently, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients in higher ferritin groups had lower short-term survival rates (log-rank P < 0.05; Fig. 2A), whereas higher levels of transferrin and TIBC were associated with better survival (log-rank P < 0.05; Fig. 3B, C).

External validation of eICU database

To further validate the robustness of our findings, we applied the same criteria to the eICU database, identifying an external cohort of 701 ACS patients with a 28-day mortality rate of 20.4% (Supplementary Table S5). The external eICU validation cohort differed from the internal MIMIC cohort in both size and baseline risk profile. As summarized in Supplementary Table S8, patients in the eICU cohort (n = 701) exhibited significantly higher disease severity at admission, as reflected by elevated median SOFA, SAPS II, and APACHE II scores (all P < 0.001 compared to the MIMIC cohort). This aligns with the observed higher 28-day mortality rate in the eICU cohort (20.4% vs. 10.9%). Despite these differences in baseline acuity—which provide a more stringent test of generalizability—the associations between iron metabolism biomarkers and mortality remained consistentIn the fully adjusted Model 3, the direction and significance of associations were consistent with the internal cohort (Table 3). Each unit increase in ferritin remained associated with higher mortality risk (HR 1.001, 95% CI 1.001–1.002, P = 0.006), while each unit increase in both transferrin (HR 0.993, 95% CI 0.988–0.997, P = 0.001) and TIBC (HR 0.995, 95% CI 0.992–0.998, P = 0.001) was associated with lower risk. In the categorical analysis, using Q1 as reference, all higher ferritin quartiles showed significantly increased risk (Q2: HR 3.314, 95% CI 1.630–6.741, P = 0.001; Q3: HR 2.761, 95% CI 1.344–5.674, P = 0.006; Q4: HR 3.881, 95% CI 1.921–7.844, P < 0.001). Conversely, transferrin and TIBC exhibited protective effects, with the highest quartile (Q4) associated with a 58.8% reduction in mortality risk for each biomarker compared to Q1 (HR 0.412, 95% CI 0.205–0.828, P = 0.013). The RCS analysis (Fig. 4) and Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 5) yielded results congruent with the internal cohort, further confirming the generalizability of the iron metabolism-mortality relationship.

Figure S1 presents the results of the Boruta feature selection algorithm applied to both the internal and external cohorts. Variables classified as “Confirmed” (shown in orange) were identified as important predictors, meaning their importance consistently exceeded that of random shadow features. In both cohorts, the iron metabolism biomarkers—ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC—were confirmed as important variables associated with 28-day ACS prognosis. Construction and validation of risk prediction model

Within the internal cohort, all patients were randomly split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). Univariate logistic regression was performed separately in the test set, training set, and the external validation cohort to identify variables associated with 28-day all-cause mortality. This process yielded 33, 22, and 18 candidate variables, respectively (Figure S2A-C). The intersection of these three variable sets comprised 10 variables (SOFA, APACHE II, SAPS II, AKI, ALB, BUN, phosphate, ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC). However, because composite severity scores (SOFA, APACHE II, SAPS II) inherently include laboratory parameters such as ALB and BUN, their simultaneous inclusion with these component variables could introduce collinearity and bias the effect estimates. Therefore, we excluded the three composite scores and constructed the final multivariate logistic regression model using only the remaining seven core predictors—AKI, ALB, BUN, phosphate, ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC—to predict the risk of 28-day ICU all-cause mortality in ACS patients (Table 4) and calculate VIF to exclude collinearity (Table S11). The predictive performance of this model, was compared against traditional severity scores (SOFA, APS III, SAPS II, OASIS, and APACHE II). The nomogram demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was 0.70 in the test set (Fig. 6A), 0.75 in the training set (Fig. 6B), and 0.69 in the external validation cohort (Fig. 6C).

Sensitivity analysis: comparison of models with and without composite severity scores

To evaluate the stability of iron metabolism biomarkers across different model specifications and to address potential collinearity between composite severity scores and their component laboratory parameters, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing two multivariable Cox regression models. Model A included composite severity scores (SOFA, APACHE II, SAPS II) together with their component variables (AKI, ALB, BUN, phosphate) and iron metabolism biomarkers (ferritin, transferrin, TIBC). Model B excluded the three composite scores and retained only the component variables along with the iron metabolism biomarkers. As shown in Supplementary Table S9, the iron metabolism biomarkers demonstrated remarkable stability and consistent significance across both model specifications. Ferritin remained a significant risk factor in both models (Model A: HR = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.001–1.002, P = 0.007; Model B: HR = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.001–1.002, P = 0.015). Transferrin showed a significant protective effect in both models (Model A: HR = 0.989, 95% CI: 0.981–0.997, P = 0.019; Model B: HR = 0.991, 95% CI: 0.886–0.996, P = 0.042). Similarly, TIBC also demonstrated a significant protective effect in both models (Model A: HR = 0.983, 95% CI: 0.974–0.993, P = 0.023; Model B: HR = 0.957, 95% CI: 0.938–0.976, P = 0.005). Furthermore, model performance metrics were nearly identical between the two models. The C-index was 0.658 (SE = 0.028) for Model A and 0.656 (SE = 0.029) for Model B, and the log-likelihood values were identical (–799.83 for both models), indicating that model discrimination and fit did not deteriorate after excluding the composite severity scores.

Incremental prognostic value of iron metabolism biomarkers

To directly evaluate the added prognostic utility of iron metabolism biomarkers, we compared the predictive performance of a base model (including only AKI, ALB, BUN, and phosphate) with that of a full model (adding ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC). As shown in Table S10, the full model demonstrated significantly improved discrimination across all three cohorts. In the training set, the AUC increased from 0.65 (95% CI: 0.62–0.68) for the base model to 0.70 (95% CI: 0.67–0.73) for the full model (P < 0.001). In the testing set, the AUC improved from 0.68 (95% CI: 0.64–0.72) to 0.75 (95% CI: 0.71–0.79) (P < 0.001). In the external eICU validation set, the AUC increased from 0.63 (95% CI: 0.59–0.67) to 0.69 (95% CI: 0.65–0.73) (P = 0.002). Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) further confirmed the incremental value of iron metabolism biomarkers. In the training set, the NRI was 0.28 (95% CI: 0.15–0.41) and the IDI was 0.06 (95% CI: 0.03–0.09). In the testing set, the NRI was 0.31 (95% CI: 0.18–0.44) and the IDI was 0.07 (95% CI: 0.04–0.10). In the external eICU validation set, the NRI was 0.24 (95% CI: 0.11–0.37) and the IDI was 0.05 (95% CI: 0.02–0.08). These findings consistently demonstrate that the addition of ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC to a base model comprising conventional clinical variables provides significant incremental prognostic value for predicting 28-day mortality in ACS patients.

Discussion

Leveraging two large, multicenter ICU databases, this study systematically evaluated the association between core iron metabolism biomarkers and short-term (28-day) prognosis in critically ill patients with acute coronary syndrome. After extensive adjustment for demographic characteristics, comorbidities, disease severity, and treatments, we found that elevated ferritin levels at admission were an independent risk factor for 28-day mortality. Conversely, higher levels of transferrin and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) served as independent protective factors. These associations demonstrated clear dose-response relationships and were robustly replicated in an independent external validation cohort. Furthermore, the risk prediction model we developed, which integrates these iron biomarkers, demonstrated superior discriminative ability (AUC range: 0.69–0.75) in identifying high-risk patients compared to traditional critical illness scoring systems. These findings suggest that dysregulation of iron homeostasis is a significant pathophysiological contributor to adverse outcomes in critically ill ACS patients. The associated biomarkers offer readily accessible, clinically valuable tools for early risk stratification.

Iron is essential for cellular energy metabolism and oxygen transport. However, disruption of its homeostasis—whether systemic overload or local functional deficiency—can exacerbate cardiovascular injury through multiple mechanisms22,23. First, as an acute-phase reactant, serum ferritin levels rise markedly during inflammation. ACS itself triggers a potent systemic inflammatory response, and elevated ferritin may indicate a more severe inflammatory cascade. This aligns with our observation of higher inflammatory markers, such as white blood cell count, in non-survivors24. Second, and more critically, ferritin’s function extends beyond iron storage; it serves as a crucial buffer against the toxicity of free iron (the labile iron pool). Under the stress of ischemia, hypoxia, or reperfusion injury, autophagic degradation or impaired function of intracellular ferritin can lead to a rapid expansion of this labile iron pool. Excess free iron then catalyzes the massive generation of reactive oxygen species via the Fenton reaction, inducing severe oxidative stress that directly damages cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells, and promotes atherosclerotic plaque instability25,26. Third, an expanded labile iron pool is a central trigger for ferroptosis, a novel regulated cell death pathway characterized by iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxides. Ferroptosis has been implicated in cardiomyocyte death after myocardial infarction, reperfusion injury, and cell death within plaques27,28. Therefore, a high ferritin level at admission may indirectly signify a pathological state primed for ferroptosis and severe oxidative damage.

The strong protective effects of transferrin and TIBC likely stem from their multifaceted roles in maintaining iron homeostasis in critically ill patients. First, as the primary transport and buffering proteins for iron, high levels can more effectively bind and sequester free iron released from cellular damage. This sequestration reduces the catalyst for the Fenton reaction, thereby mitigating explosive oxidative stress and the risk of ferroptosis—a central mechanism in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury29,30. Second, transferrin is a key executor of nutritional immunity, withholding iron essential for pathogens and thus providing immunocompromised, critically ill ACS patients with a defense against infection31. Finally, as a negative acute-phase protein synthesized by the liver, transferrin levels sensitively reflect hepatic synthetic function and nutritional reserves. Maintaining a higher level at admission may indicate a more robust baseline organ function and a greater capacity to withstand the stress of critical illness32.

This study, leveraging large, multicenter databases, demonstrates the association between iron metabolism biomarkers and short-term ACS prognosis and suggests new directions for risk stratification. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective observational study, it cannot establish causality or fully rule out residual confounding despite extensive adjustments and external validation. For instance, the observed associations might be partly influenced by unmeasured factors such as nutritional status, underlying chronic inflammation, or liver and kidney function. Second, we utilized only the first laboratory values obtained within 24 h of admission. While this facilitates early risk assessment, it does not capture the dynamic changes in iron markers during the ICU stay or their complex interplay with treatments (e.g., transfusion, iron supplementation) and outcomes. Future prospective studies should incorporate serial measurements. Third, our cohort was restricted to ACS patients with an ICU stay exceeding 24 h. This may introduce selection bias by excluding patients who died very early after admission, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to the broader ACS population. Finally, as the data originate from U.S. medical centers, differences in patient demographics and clinical practices may affect the generalizability of our results to other regions. Fourth, our analysis evaluated iron metabolism biomarkers as independent predictors. While this approach successfully identified ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC as clinically useful prognostic tools, it does not fully capture the potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions between different components of the iron pathway (e.g., the combined effect of high ferritin with low transferrin). The exploration of integrated “iron profiles” or phenotypic clusters represents an interesting and mechanistically rich avenue for future research but was beyond the primary scope of this study, which aimed to establish the independent prognostic value of standard, individually reported laboratory parameters for practical risk assessment. Fifth, it is important to note that the directional associations observed in this study—higher ferritin with increased mortality and higher transferrin/TIBC with decreased mortality—should be interpreted within the context of acute critical illness. In healthy individuals, both low and high levels of these biomarkers can signal dysregulated iron homeostasis and may confer harm. However, in patients with ACS requiring intensive care, elevated ferritin predominantly reflects an acute-phase inflammatory response and expanded labile iron pool, which drive ferroptosis and oxidative damage. Conversely, higher transferrin and TIBC in this setting likely indicate preserved iron-buffering capacity, better nutritional and hepatic reserve, and thus a greater ability to withstand the metabolic stress of ACS. Therefore, the ‘one-sided’ trends reported here do not contradict the known U-shaped risk curve in general populations but instead highlight the distinct pathophysiological roles these markers play during acute cardiovascular crises. But, The observed linear associations may not reflect the U-shaped relationships that can exist in healthier populations or in chronic disease settings. Generalization of these trends to non-critical or outpatient cohorts should be made with caution.

Conclusion

This study establishes that in critically ill ACS patients, ferritin (reflecting iron storage) and transferrin along with total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) (reflecting iron transport) are independent predictors of 28-day all-cause mortality. These findings suggest that dysregulated iron metabolism is an integral component of the pathophysiology in severe ACS. Assessing the iron metabolic profile may aid in the early identification of high-risk patients and lays a crucial clinical evidence base for future research into precise therapeutic strategies targeting iron homeostasis.
