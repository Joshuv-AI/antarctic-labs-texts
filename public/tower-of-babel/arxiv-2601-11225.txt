Operators and POVMs generated by Parseval frames


Abstract
Let ℱ e {\cal F}_{e} be a Parseval frame in a Hilbert space ℋ {\cal H} and let 𝖤 {\sf E} be a set of real numbers. From these data, we construct an operator H 𝖤 , e H_{{\sf E},e} and a positive operator-valued measure (POVM) F 𝖤 , e F_{{\sf E},e} This paper investigates in detail the relationship between the operator H 𝖤 , e H_{{\sf E},e} and the POVM F 𝖤 , e F_{{\sf E},e} . Our results extend the classical correspondence between a self-adjoint operator generated by an orthonormal basis and its associated projection-valued (spectral) measure.

I
Introduction

As is well known, in quantum mechanics the dynamics of a closed system
{\cal S}
is often determined by a self-adjoint operator, the Hamiltonian
H
of the system
{\cal S}
. A crucial feature of
H
, which plays a significant role in analyzing

{\cal S}
, is its spectrum of eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors. If

H
has a purely discrete spectrum
\sigma(H)=\{E_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}

\mathbb{J}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is an index set, then the eigenvectors corresponding to the distinct eigenvalues are mutually orthogonal. In this case, there exists an orthonormal basis
{\cal F}_{e^{+}}=\{e_{j}^{+}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
of the Hilbert space
{\cal H}^{+}

consisting of eigenvectors of
H
. Therefore, any physical state of
{\cal S}
can be represented as a linear combination of basic elements
e_{j}^{+}
. With respect to this basis, the operator
H

admits the spectral representation

H{\equiv}H_{{\sf E},e^{+}}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}}{E}_{j}\langle\cdot,e_{j}^{+}\rangle{e}_{j}^{+},\qquad{\sf E}=\{E_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}

with domain

\mathcal{D}(H_{{\sf E},e^{+}})=\{f\in{\cal H}^{+}\ :\ \sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}}{E}_{j}^{2}|\langle f,e_{j}^{+}\rangle|^{2}<\infty\}.

Alternatively, the system
{\cal S}
can be described using the projection-valued measure

F_{{\sf E},e^{+}}(\Delta)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}_{\Delta}}\langle\cdot,e_{j}^{+}\rangle e_{j}^{+},\qquad\mathbb{J}_{\Delta}=\{j\in\mathbb{J}:E_{j}\in\Delta\},\quad\Delta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
is the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel set on
\mathbb{R}
.

The relationship between
H_{{\sf E},e^{+}}
and
F_{{\sf E},e^{+}}

is remarkably simple and transparent

H_{{\sf E},e^{+}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda{d}F_{{\sf E},e^{+}}(d\lambda),\qquad\mathcal{D}(H_{{\sf E},e^{+}})=\{f\in{\cal H}^{+}\ :\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda^{2}d\langle F_{{\sf E},e^{+}}(d\lambda)f,f\rangle<\infty\}.

(1.1)

II.1
Frames

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space with linear scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
in the first argument.

A frame
is a family of vectors

{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
in
\mathcal{H}
for which

A||f||^{2}\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}}{|\langle f,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}}\leq{B}||f||^{2},\qquad f\in\mathcal{H},\quad 0<A\leq{B}.

A frame

{\cal F}_{e}

is said to be
A
-tight
if
A=B
, and
normalized
if

\|e_{j}\|=1
for
j\in\mathbb{J}
. For each
A
-tight frame
{\cal F}_{e}
,

f=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}}\langle f,e_{j}\rangle{e_{j}},\qquad f\in{\cal H}.

(2.1)

The coefficients in (
2.1
) are not uniquely determined and there exist alternative representations
f=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}}{c_{j}}{e_{j}}
where the coefficients
c_{j}
differ from those of (
2.1
). The range of available coefficients
can be evaluated using the concept of frame excess. We recall that the
excess

{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}]
of
{\cal F}_{e}
is the highest integer
n
such that
n
elements can be deleted from
{\cal F}_{e}
and still leave a complete set, or
\infty
if there is no upper bound to the number of elements that can be removed. The zero excess of a
A
-tight frame
{\cal F}_{e}
means that
{\cal F}_{e}
is a Riesz basis and the coefficients in (
2.1
) are uniquely determined.

An
1
-tight frame
{\cal F}_{e}
is called
a Parseval frame
. Every
A
-tight
frame
{\cal F}_{e^{\prime}}=\{e_{j}^{\prime}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
can be rescaled to obtain a Parseval frame
{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
by setting
e_{j}^{\prime}\to e_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}e_{j}^{\prime}
.

The Naimark dilation theorem is stated for Parseval frames in the following form
[
17
]
.

Theorem 1

Let
{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
be a Parseval frame in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Then there exist a Hilbert space
{\cal M}
and a Parseval frame

{\cal F}_{m}=\{m_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
in
{\cal M}
such that the set of vectors

{\cal F}_{e^{+}}=\{e_{j}^{+}=e_{j}\oplus{m}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}

(2.2)

is an orthonormal basis of
{\cal H}^{+}=\mathcal{H}\oplus{\cal M}
. The excess
{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}]
of
{\cal F}_{e}
coincides with the dimension of the Hilbert space
{\cal M}
, while the excess
{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{m}]
of
{\cal F}_{m}
coincides with
\dim{\cal H}
.

It is also straightforward to prove an inverse statement: given an orhonormal basis

{\cal F}_{e^{+}}
of
{\cal H}^{+}
and an orthogonal projection
P^{+}
in
{\cal H}^{+}
, the set

P^{+}{\cal F}_{e^{+}}=\{e_{j}=P^{+}e_{j}^{+}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
forms a Parseval frame in
\mathcal{H}=P^{+}\mathcal{H}^{+}
.

A normalized frame
{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
acting in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
{\cal H}
is called
equiangular
if there exists a constant
\alpha
such that

\alpha=|\langle e_{j},e_{i}\rangle|,\ j\not=i\in\mathbb{J}.

The constant
\alpha
is called
the coherence constant
and measures how close an equiangular frame
{\cal F}_{e}
is to being orthogonal.

Every equiangular

A
-tight frame

{\cal F}_{e}
is referred to as
an optimal Grassmannian frame
. Optimal Grassmannian frames
{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
exist only for certain combinations of the parameters
|\mathbb{J}|
and
{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}]
.
More precisely, in complex (respectively, real) Hilbert spaces, the existence of such frames requires that
(|\mathbb{J}|-{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}])^{2}\geq|\mathbb{J}|+{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}]
(respectively,
(|\mathbb{J}|-{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}])^{2}\geq|\mathbb{J}|
). In both cases, the coherence constant
\alpha
coincides with

\sqrt{\frac{{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}]}{(|\mathbb{J}|-{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}])(|\mathbb{J}|-1)}}
, and
{\cal F}_{e}
is a

A
-tight frame with frame bound

A=\frac{|\mathbb{J}|}{|\mathbb{J}|-{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}]}
.

II.2
Positive operator valued measure

A normalized positive operator-valued measure
(for short, a POVM) is defined as a map
F:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}({\cal H})
such that
2
2

2

A POVM is typically defined as a
\sigma
-additive map from the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(X)
of a topological space
X
. In the special case where

X=\mathbb{R}
, the term real POVM is used. Throughout the paper, we consider only real POVMs.

F\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{F(\Delta_{n})},\qquad F(\mathbb{R})=I,

where
\{\Delta_{n}\}
is a countable family of disjoint sets in
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
and the series on the right-hand side is understood as the strong limit of the corresponding sequence of partial sums.

The spectrum
\sigma(F)
of a POVM
F
is the closed set

\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\ :\forall\Delta\ \mbox{open},\ \lambda\in\Delta\ \Rightarrow F(\Delta)\not=0\}.

(2.3)

A POVM
F
is said to be
commutative
if

[F(\Delta_{1}),F(\Delta_{2})]:=F(\Delta_{1})F(\Delta_{2})-F(\Delta_{2})F(\Delta_{1})=0,\qquad\Delta_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})

and
orthogonal
if
\Delta_{1}\cap\Delta_{2}=\emptyset
implies

F(\Delta_{1})F(\Delta_{2})=0
.

A projection valued measure
(for short, a PVM) is an orthogonal POVM or, equivalently, is a POVM
F
such that
F(\Delta)
is an orthogonal projection operator for each
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
.

III
POVMs generated by Parseval frames

III.1
Definition and elementary properties

Let
{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
be a Parseval frame in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathbb{J}
is a finite or countable subset of integers, and let
{\sf E}=\{E_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
be the set of real numbers.
Using
{\cal F}_{e}
and
{\sf E}
, one can define the POVM

F_{{\sf E},e}(\Delta)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}_{\Delta}}\langle\cdot,e_{j}\rangle e_{j},\qquad\mathbb{J}_{\Delta}=\{j\in\mathbb{J}:E_{j}\in\Delta\},\qquad\Delta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}).

(3.1)

It is easy to see that multiplying each frame element
e_{j}
by a unimodular constant (i.e., a complex number of modulus one) yields another Parseval frame. From the POVMs perspective, this transformation does not alter the measurement statistics. In other words, replacing the frame elements
\{e_{j}\}
with
\{e^{i\theta_{j}}e_{j}\}
, leaves

F_{{\sf E},e}
unchanged. Thus, POVM

F_{{\sf E},e}
is defined by the Parseval frame
{\cal F}_{e}
up to multiplication by unimodular constants.

In general, a Parseval frame

{\cal F}_{e}
may contain zero vectors

e_{j}=0
. However, in our setting, the presence of such vectors does not affect the definition of POVM (
3.1
), while it may introduce unnecessary ambiguity regarding the numbers
E_{j}
associated with them. For this reason, in what follows we assume that our Parseval frames
contain no zero vectors
, i.e.

{\cal F}_{e}={\cal F}_{e\not=0}=\{e_{j}\in{\cal F}_{e}:e_{j}\not=0\}.

The formula (
3.1
) provides a bridge to the rich theory of POVMs, offering new perspectives for the study of frames. The importance of the relationship between POVM and frame theory is highlighted by the following simple proof of the fundamental identity for Parseval frames, originally established in
[
4
]
through intrinsic methods of frame theory. Specifically, one obtains that for every subset
J\subset\mathbb{J}
and
f\in{\cal H}
,

\sum_{j\in{J}}|\langle f,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}-\|\sum_{j\in{J}}\langle{f},e_{j}\rangle{e_{j}}\|^{2}=\sum_{j\in{J^{c}}}|\langle f,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}-\|\sum_{j\in{J^{c}}}\langle{f},e_{j}\rangle{e_{j}}\|^{2},\qquad J^{c}=\mathbb{J}\setminus{J}.

(3.2)

Taking (
3.1
) into account, choosing
\Delta=J\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
and setting
E_{j}=j
(i.e.,
{\sf E}={J}
), we can rewrite (
3.2
) as follows

\langle{F_{{\sf E},e}(\Delta)}f,f\rangle-\langle{F_{{\sf E},e}}(\Delta)f,{F_{\sf E,e}(\Delta)}f\rangle=\langle{F_{{\sf E},e}}(\Delta^{c})f,f\rangle-\langle{F_{{\sf E},e}}(\Delta^{c})f,{F_{{\sf E},e}(\Delta^{c})}f\rangle,

or, that is equivalent,
\langle{F_{{\sf E},e}}(\Delta^{c}){F_{{\sf N},e}}(\Delta)f,f\rangle=\langle{F_{{\sf E},e}(\Delta)}{F_{{\sf E},e}}(\Delta^{c})f,f\rangle.
The last relation is obviously true since
{F_{{\sf E},e}}(\Delta^{c})=I-{F_{{\sf E},e}}(\Delta)
.
This means that the fundamental identity (
3.2
) is, in fact, a straightforward property of POVMs. Similarly, the inequality

\sum_{j\in{J}}|\langle f,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}+\|\sum_{j\in{J^{c}}}\langle{f},e_{j}\rangle{e_{j}}\|^{2}\geq\frac{3}{4}\|f\|^{2}

established in
[
4
]
, turns out to be obvious in POVMs language because it is equivalent to the obvious inequality
\|f-2{F_{{\sf E},e}(\Delta)}f\|\geq{0}
.

III.2
Commutative POVM

As is well known, the orthogonality of a POVM
F

implies that

F
is commutative and, moreover, that

F
is a PVM. However, the commutativity of

F
does not necessarily imply that
F
is a PVM. In this sense, commutative POVMs are a straightforward generalization of PVMs. Our objective is to characterize the commutative POVMs
F_{{\sf E},e}
defined by (
3.1
).

In view of (
2.1
) with
A=1
and (
3.1
), each POVM
F_{{\sf E},e}
can be associated with the following decomposition of the identity as a sum of positive bounded operators
L_{k}
:

I=\sum_{k}L_{k},\qquad L_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}_{\Delta_{k}}}\langle\cdot,e_{j}\rangle{e_{j}},\qquad{\Delta_{k}}=\{E_{k}\},

(3.3)

where different elements

E_{k}\in{\sf E}
determine different operators
L_{k}
.

It follows from (
3.3
) that the commutativity of

F_{{\sf E},e}

is equivalent to the mutual commutativity of the operators

L_{k}
. For this reason, it is easier to find commutative POVMs when the number of distinct elements in
{\sf E}
is small.
In the simplest case, where
{\sf E}
contains only one element
E
, the collection
\{L_{k}\}

reduces to the identity operator
I
. The associated POVM

F_{{\sf E},e}(\Delta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}0\quad\mbox{if}\quad E\not\in\Delta;\\
I\quad\mbox{if}\quad E\in\Delta\end{array}\right.

is necessarily commutative for every choice of
{\cal F}_{e}
.

Let us now consider the opposite case of POVM
F_{{\sf E},e}
, where all elements of

{\sf E}
are pairwise distinct, i.e.,
E_{i}\neq E_{j}
whenever
i\neq j\in\mathbb{J}
. Then
the set
\{L_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{J}}
consists of rank-one operators
L_{k}=\langle\cdot,e_{k}\rangle{e_{k}}
.
The commutativity of
F_{{\sf E},e}
is equivalent to the condition
[L_{i},L_{j}]=0
.
This, in turn, is equivalent to the commutativity of the associated rank-one orthogonal projection operators

[P_{i},P_{j}]=0
, where

P_{k}=\frac{1}{\|e_{k}\|^{2}}\langle\cdot,e_{k}\rangle{e_{k}},\qquad k\in\{i,j\}.

In view of the commutation relation, the operator

P_{i}P_{j}
is the orthogonal projection in
{\cal H}
into the one-dimensional subspace spanned by both

e_{i}
and
e_{j}
or
P_{i}P_{j}=0
. The first alternative corresponds to the case where the vectors

e_{i}
and
e_{j}
are collinear, while the second corresponds to the case where the vectors

e_{i}
and
e_{j}
are orthogonal. Based on this observation, one can characterize commutative POVM’s

F_{{\sf E},e}
.

Theorem 2

Let
F_{{\sf E},e}
be the POVM defined in (
3.1
), where
{\sf E}
is a set of distinct numbers. The following are equivalent:

(i)

F_{{\sf E},e}
is a commutative POVM;

(ii)

there exists an orthonormal basis
\{\gamma_{i}\}
of
{\cal H}
such that

{\cal F}_{e}=\{c_{1}^{k}\gamma_{1}\}_{k=1}^{r_{1}}\cup\{c_{2}^{k}\gamma_{2}\}_{k=1}^{r_{2}}\cup\{c_{3}^{k}\gamma_{3}\}_{k=1}^{r_{3}}\cup\ldots,

(3.4)

where

\sum_{k=1}^{r_{i}}|c_{i}^{k}|^{2}=1,\qquad c_{i}^{k}\not=0,\qquad 1\leq{i}\leq\dim{\cal H},\qquad r_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

(3.5)

Proof:

Set
\gamma_{1}=\frac{e_{1}}{\|e_{1}\|}
,
e_{1}\in{\cal F}_{e}

{\cal F}_{e}
can be partitioned into two mutually disjoint subsets, denoted
{\cal F}^{1}
and

{\cal F}^{2}
, where

{\cal F}^{1}

consists of all vectors

e_{j_{1}}^{1},e_{j_{2}}^{1},\ldots,e_{j_{r_{1}}}^{1}

that are collinear with

\gamma_{1}
, and

{\cal F}^{2}
consists of all vectors
e_{j}\in{\cal F}_{e}

that are orthogonal to

\gamma_{1}
. By construction
{\cal F}^{1}=\{e_{j_{k}}^{1}=c_{1}^{k}\gamma_{1}\}_{k=1}^{r_{1}}
, where
|c_{1}^{k}|=\|e_{j_{k}}^{1}\|
,
1\leq{k}\leq{r_{1}}
. The first relation in (
3.5
) for the coefficients
\{c_{1}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{r_{1}}

III.3
Jointly measurable POVMs

POVMs
F_{j}:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}({\cal H}),\ j=1,2
are called
jointly measurable
if they are the marginals of a joint POVM
F:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}({\cal H})
. Here, the symbol
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
denotes the product
\sigma
-algebra generated by the family of sets
\{\Delta_{1}\times\Delta_{2}:\Delta_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}
.

Two POVMs
F_{j}:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}({\cal H})

commute
if

[F_{1}(\Delta_{1}),F_{2}(\Delta_{2})]=0
for each
\Delta_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),\ j=1,2
.
In the following, the commutativity of two POVMs is indicated by the symbol
[F_{1},F_{2}]=0
.

If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are PVMs, they are jointly measurable if and only if
[F_{1},F_{2}]=0
. In the case of POVMs, commutativity implies joint measurability, but the converse is not true
[
22
]
. Our objective is to determine under what conditions POVMs defined by (
3.1
) are jointly measurable.

Let
F_{{\sf E},e}
and
F_{\widehat{\sf E},\widehat{e}}
be two POVMs where
{\cal F}_{e}
and
{\cal F}_{\widehat{e}}
are Parseval frames in
{\cal H}
with the same excess number
{\bf e}[{\cal F}_{e}]={\bf e}[{\cal F}_{\widehat{e}}]
and with the same index set
\mathbb{J}
. According to the Naimark theorem
1
, these Parseval frames can be extended to orthonormal bases
{\cal F}_{e^{+}}
and
{\cal F}_{\widehat{e}^{+}}
of the same Hilbert space
{\cal H}^{+}
(see (
2.2
)). This allows one to construct the PVM

F_{{\sf E},e^{+}}(\Delta)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}_{\Delta}}\langle\cdot,e_{j}^{+}\rangle e_{j}^{+},\qquad\mathbb{J}_{\Delta}=\{j\in\mathbb{J}:E_{j}\in\Delta\},\qquad\Delta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})

(3.10)

and, similarly, the PVM
F_{\widehat{\sf E},\widehat{e}^{+}}
with the use of
{\cal F}_{\widehat{e}^{+}}
and
\widehat{E}=\{E_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
.

By virtue of

[
6
, Theorem 6]
,
POVMs
F_{{\sf E},e}
and
F_{\widehat{\sf E},\widehat{e}}
are
jointly measurable if and only if
[F_{{\sf E},e^{+}},F_{\widehat{\sf E},\widehat{e}^{+}}]=0
.
If
{\cal F}_{e}={\cal F}_{\widehat{e}}
, then the orthonormal bases
{\cal F}_{e^{+}}
and
{\cal F}_{\widehat{e}^{+}}
coincide and the last commutation relation is evidently valid.
The following result is now proved

Proposition 5

POVMs
F_{{\sf E},e}
and
F_{\widehat{\sf E},e}
constructed using the same Parseval frame
{\cal F}_{e}
and arbitrary sets
{\sf E}
and
\widehat{\sf E}
are jointly measurable.

Consider another special case, assuming
{\cal F}_{e}\not={\cal F}_{\widehat{e}}
, and assuming that both
{\sf E}
and

\widehat{\sf E}
contain distinct numbers. Then the relation
[F_{{\sf E},e^{+}},F_{\widehat{\sf E},\widehat{e}^{+}}]=0
, which ensures the joint measurability of
F_{{\sf E},e}
and
F_{\widehat{\sf E},\widehat{e}}
(see
[
6
, Theorem 6]
), is equivalent to the relation
[L_{j},\widehat{L}_{i}]=0
for all possible pairs of indices
j,i\in\mathbb{J}
, where

L_{j}=\langle\cdot,e^{+}_{j}\rangle{e_{j}^{+}},\qquad\widehat{L}_{i}=\langle\cdot,\widehat{e}^{+}_{i}\rangle{\widehat{e}_{i}^{+}}.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2
and taking into account that the sets
\{e_{j}^{+}\}
and
\{\widehat{e}^{+}_{i}\}
are orthonormal bases of
{\cal H}^{+}
, we conclude that
[L_{j},\widehat{L}_{i}]=0
holds for all indexes
j,i\in\mathbb{J}
if and only if
there exists bijection
j\to i
on
\mathbb{J}
such that each vector
e_{j}^{+}
coincides with
\widehat{e}_{i}^{+}
up to multiplication by a unimodular constant. Therefore (see Sect.
III.1
), we can assume

e_{j}^{+}=\widehat{e}_{i}^{+}
without loss of generality. In this case, as follows from (
2.2
),
e_{j}=\widehat{e}_{i}
. Therefore, in the case under consideration,
joint measurability is possible only if both POVM’s

F_{{\sf E},e}
and
F_{\widehat{\sf E},\widehat{e}}

are constructed based on the same Parseval frame
{\cal F}_{e}={\cal F}_{\widehat{e}}
.

\sigma(H_{{\sf E},e})

If the excess

However, when

and

The variety of possible relationships is easily illustrated in the case of commutative POVMs. Consider a commutative POVM as described in Theorem 8. Then, the spectrum of the self-adjoint operator

with the domain (

Assume that

Then, among the vectors

Consider one of the simplest examples of Parseval frames with

Instead of assuming a constant repetition number

In the general case of non-commutative POVMs, as shown below, if a Parseval frame

possesses additional properties relevant to applications, one may expect predictable relations between

Denote by

Consider an operator of multiplication by the set

IV.3.1
Mercedes-type Parseval frames

A frame

is called a
dual frame
for a PF

if

f=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}}\langle f,e_{j}\rangle\psi_{j}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{J}}\langle f,\psi_{j}\rangle e_{j},\qquad f\in{\cal H}.

There are multiple techniques for building dual frames, as described in
[
10
,
19
,
20
]
.
One of the most frequently used expressions is the formula
[
10
, p.159]

{\cal F}_{\psi}=\left\{\psi_{j}=e_{j}+h_{j}-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{J}}\langle e_{j},e_{n}\rangle h_{n}\right\},

where
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{J}}
is a Bessel sequence in
{\cal H}
. Applying this formula to the Mercedes frame
{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}

considered in Example
6
shows that all dual frames of

{\cal F}_{e}
can be constructed in an extremely simple way: by adding an arbitrary vector

to each vector of
{\cal F}_{e}
. Specifically,

{\cal F}_{\phi}=\{\phi_{j}=e_{j}+h\}_{j=1}^{3}.

Taking this property as characteristic of the Mercedes frame, we can introduce a generalized version of the Mercedes frame. That is, we say that a Parseval frame
{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
in
{\cal H}
is of

if at least one of its dual frames has the form

{\cal F}_{\psi}=\{\psi_{j}=e_{j}+h\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
, where
h\not=0\in{\cal H}
.

A simple analysis shows that

{\cal F}_{e}
is a Mercedes-type frame if and only if
3\leq|\mathbb{J}|<\infty
, where
k=|\mathbb{J}|

and the Gram matrix of

is a

-matrix of the form

G_{e}=\frac{1}{k}\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}k-1&-1&\dots&-1\\
-1&k-1&\dots&-1\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
-1&\dots&-1&k-1\end{array}\right].

(4.13)

Proposition 12

Each Mercedes-type Parseval frame
{\cal F}_{e}=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
has excess

and can be rescaled to the optimal Grassmannian frame

{\cal F}_{e^{\prime}}=\left\{{e_{j}}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{k}{k-1}}e_{j}\right\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}},\qquad k=|\mathbb{J}|.

Proof:
It follows from (
4.13
) that
{\cal F}_{e^{\prime}}
is an optimal Grassmannian frame with frame bound

. By comparing this relation with formula

in Sect.

.

\Box

The proof of the statement below is given in the Appendix.

IV.3.2

It turns out that, given a conference matrix
