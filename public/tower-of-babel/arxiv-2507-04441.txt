The Joys of Categorical Conformal Prediction ∗ ∗ The title of the present work is inspired by the wonderful book by diestel2014joys .
Michele Caprio

Abstract
. Conformal prediction (CP) is an Uncertainty Representation technique that delivers finite-sample calibrated prediction regions for any underlying Machine Learning model. Its status as an Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) tool, though, has remained conceptually opaque: While Conformal Prediction Regions (CPRs) give an ordinal representation of uncertainty (larger regions typically indicate higher uncertainty), they lack the capability to cardinally quantify it (twice as large regions do not imply twice the uncertainty). We adopt a category-theoretic approach to CP—framing it as a morphism, embedded in a commuting diagram, of two newly-defined categories—that brings us three joys. First, we show that—under minimal assumptions—CP is intrinsically a UQ mechanism, that is, its cardinal UQ capabilities are a structural feature of the method. Second, we demonstrate that CP bridges (and perhaps subsumes) the Bayesian, frequentist, and imprecise probabilistic approaches to predictive statistical reasoning. Finally, we show that a CPR is the image of a covariant functor. This observation is relevant to AI privacy: It implies that privacy noise added locally does not break the global coverage guarantee.

The structure of the paper can be summarized as follows.

In Sections

4
, we introduce two categories,

and

In Section

This has e.g. the following

\mathcal{M}_{j}
propagates automatically through the functor. The resulting prediction regions, then, still satisfy the global coverage guarantee while only the (possibly privatized) summary objects
\mathcal{M}_{j}

Finally, Section

\mathbf{UHCont}

Background

In this section, we introduce (Full) Conformal Prediction (Section
2.1
), and some basic notions of Imprecise Probability Theory (Section

2.1.
Full Conformal Prediction

This section is an expanded reproduction of
Caprio et al., 2025a
. It is based on
Cella and Martin, 2022
, who summarize work by
Shafer and Vovk, 2008
and
Vovk et al., 2005
on Transductive (of Full) Conformal Prediction.

Suppose that there is an exchangeable process
\mathcal{Y}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{2},\ldots
with distribution
\mathfrak{P}
, where each
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
is a random element taking values in the measurable space
(Y,\Sigma_{Y})
.
Recall that a sequence is exchangeable if, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any permutation
\text{perm}(\cdot)
, the two random vectors
(\mathcal{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Y}_{k})
and
(\mathcal{Y}_{\text{perm}(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{Y}_{\text{perm}(k)})
have the same joint distribution. This implies that the marginal distributions of the
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
’s are the same.
4
4

4

Exchangeability does not imply independence, so the standard i.i.d. setup is in a sense more restrictive. Note also that we are not assuming any parametric form for the distribution
\mathfrak{P}
.

We want to solve the following statistical problem. Suppose we observe the first
n
terms of the process, that is,
\mathcal{Y}^{n}=(\mathcal{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Y}_{n})
. With
this data, and the assumption of exchangeability, the goal is to predict
\mathcal{Y}_{n+1}
using a method that is valid or reliable in a certain sense.

Let
\mathcal{Y}^{n+1}=(\mathcal{Y}^{n},\mathcal{Y}_{n+1})
be an
(n+1)
-dimensional vector consisting of the observable
\mathcal{Y}^{n}
and the yet-to-be-observed value
\mathcal{Y}_{n+1}
. Consider the transform

\mathcal{Y}^{n+1}\rightarrow T^{n+1}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n+1})

defined by the rule

T_{i}\coloneqq\psi_{i}\left(\mathcal{Y}^{n+1}\right)\equiv\psi\left(y^{n+1}_{-i},y_{i}\right)

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
, where
y^{n+1}_{-i}=y^{n+1}\setminus\{y_{i}\}
and
\psi:Y^{n}\times Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a fixed function that is invariant to permutations in its first vector argument. Function
\psi
, which is called a
non-conformity measure
, is constructed in such as way that
\psi_{i}(y^{n+1})
is small if and only if
y_{i}
agrees with—i.e. is “close to”—a prediction based on the data
y^{n+1}_{-i}
. Or, the other way around, large values
\psi_{i}(y^{n+1})
suggest that the observation
y_{i}
is “strange” and does not conform to the rest of the data
y^{n+1}_{-i}
.
The key idea is to define
\psi_{i}(y^{n+1})
in a way that allows one to compare
y_{i}
to a suitable summary of
y^{n+1}_{-i}
, e.g.
\psi_{i}(y^{n+1})=|\text{mean}(y^{n+1}_{-i})-y_{i}|
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
. Notice that transformation
\mathcal{Y}^{n+1}\rightarrow T^{n+1}
preserves exchangeability.

As the value
\mathcal{Y}_{n+1}
has not yet been observed, and is actually the prediction target, the above calculations cannot be carried out exactly. Nevertheless, the exchangeability-preserving properties of the transformations described above provide a procedure to rank candidate values
\tilde{y}
of
\mathcal{Y}_{n+1}
based on the observed
\mathcal{Y}^{n}=y^{n}
, as shown in Algorithm
1
, where
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
denotes the indicator function.

Algorithm 1

Full Conformal prediction (CP)

Initialize: data
y^{n}
, non-conformity measure
\psi
, grid of
\tilde{y}
values

for
each
\tilde{y}
value in the grid
do

set
y_{n+1}=\tilde{y}
and write
y^{n+1}=y^{n}\cup\{y_{n+1}\}
;

define
T_{i}=\psi_{i}(y^{n+1})
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
;

evaluate
\pi(\tilde{y},y^{n})=(n+1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathbbm{1}[T_{i}\geq T_{n+1}]
;

end

for

return
\pi(\tilde{y},y^{n})
for each
\tilde{y}
on the grid.

The output of Algorithm
1
is a data-dependent function
\tilde{y}\mapsto\pi(\tilde{y},y^{n})\in[0,1]
that can be interpreted as a measure of plausibility of the assertion that
\mathcal{Y}_{n+1}=\tilde{y}
, given data
y^{n}
.
Vovk et al., 2005
refer to the function
\pi
as
conformal transducer
.
Conformal transducer
\pi
plays a key role in the construction of
conformal prediction regions
(CPRs). For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the
\alpha
-level CPR is defined as
(
Vovk, 2013
, Equation (2))

(1)

\mathscr{R}_{\alpha}^{\psi}(y^{n})\coloneqq\{y_{n+1}\in Y:\pi(y_{n+1},y^{n})>\alpha\},

and it satisfies

(2)

P\left[\mathcal{Y}_{n+1}\in\mathscr{R}_{\alpha}^{\psi}(y^{n})\right]\geq 1-\alpha,

uniformly in
n
and in
P

(
Vovk et al., 2005
)
. That is, (
2
) is satisfied for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all exchangeable distributions
P
.
Note that (
2
) holds regardless of the choice of the non-conformity measure
\psi
. However, the choice of this function is crucial in terms of the
efficiency
of conformal prediction, that is, the
size
of the prediction regions. A simple visual representation of Full CP is given in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.
A visual representation of Full CP methodology
(
Caprio et al., 2025a
, Figure 1)
. The Conformal Prediction Region is obtained as in (

Caprio et al., 2025a
point out how Full CP can be written as a correspondence

(3)

\kappa:[0,1]\times Y^{n}\times\mathscr{F}\rightrightarrows Y,\quad(\alpha,y^{n},\psi)\mapsto\kappa(\alpha,y^{n},\psi),

where

(4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}\coloneqq\{\psi:Y^{n}\times Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{, }\psi\text{ is invariant to permutations in its first argument}\},

and the image of
\kappa
is defined as

(5)

\displaystyle\kappa(\alpha,y^{n},\psi)\coloneqq\Bigg\{y_{n+1}\in Y:\underbrace{\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathbbm{1}\left[\psi\left(y^{n+1}_{-i},y_{i}\right)\geq\psi\left(y^{n+1}_{-(n+1)},y_{n+1}\right)\right]}_{\eqqcolon\pi(y_{n+1},y^{n})}>\alpha\Bigg\}.

It is easy to see, then, that
\kappa(\alpha,y^{n},\psi)\equiv\mathscr{R}_{\alpha}^{\psi}(y^{n})
.

Full Conformal Prediction (FCP) is not the only way of carrying out conformal prediction. There exists another type called Inductive (or Split) Conformal Prediction (ICP). It assumes exchangeability and it is used to build the same region
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha}^{\psi}(y^{n})
in (
1
) having the same guarantee (
2
), while being computationally less expensive than FCP.
Since ICP produces the same region as FCP, which is what we need in light of the goal of our paper, we do not present ICP in detail. We refer the interested reader to
(
Papadopoulos, 2008
;
Angelopoulos et al., 2024
)
.

2.2.
Imprecise Probabilities

We now introduce a few imprecise probabilistic concepts that will be used when studying CP in the Category Theory framework.

Let
(Y,\Sigma_{Y})
be a measurable space, and call
\Delta_{Y}
the space of finitely additive probabilities on
Y
, endowed with the weak
⋆
topology. A
credal set

\mathcal{M}
is a weak
⋆
-closed and convex element of
\Delta_{Y}

(
Levi, 1980
)
. Throughout the paper, we will denote by
\mathscr{C}\subset 2^{\Delta_{Y}}
the space of nonempty weak
⋆
-closed and convex subsets of
\Delta_{Y}
.
For any non-empty credal set
\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{C}
, define the
upper probability
as the upper envelope of
\mathcal{M}
,

\overline{P}_{\mathcal{M}}(A)\equiv\overline{P}(A)\;=\;\sup_{P\in\mathcal{M}}P(A),\quad A\in\Sigma_{Y}.

The
conjugate lower probability

\underline{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\equiv\underline{P}
of
\overline{P}
satisfies

\underline{P}(A)=1-\overline{P}(A^{c})
, for all
A\in\Sigma_{Y}
,
thus knowing one immediately gives us the other. In a statistical and ML framework, these concepts allow us to crystallize model ambiguity.

Even in the presence of the latter, we are able to define (a version of) a confidence interval. Fix a credibility level
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then, the
\alpha
-level Imprecise Highest Density Region (IHDR) of credal set
\mathcal{M}
is the set
B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}\subset Y
such that
\underline{P}(B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha})=1-\alpha
, and its “size is minimal”
(
Coolen, 1992
)
. More formally
(
Caprio et al., 2025a
, Equation (11))
,

B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}=\bigcap\left\{{A\in\Sigma_{Y}:\underline{P}(A)\geq 1-\alpha}\right\}.

Notice that
B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}
can be written as the image of the following correspondence

(6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{IHDR}:[0,1]\times\mathscr{C}\rightrightarrows Y,\quad(\alpha,\mathcal{M})\mapsto\mathsf{IHDR}(\alpha,\mathcal{M})\coloneqq\bigcap\left\{{A\in\Sigma_{Y}:\underline{P}(A)\geq 1-\alpha}\right\}.

Conformal Prediction is associated with a special case of upper probability
\overline{P}
, called a
possibility function

(
Cuzzolin, 2020
)
. The latter is an upper probability for which there exists a function
\pi:Y\rightarrow[0,1]
such that

•

\sup_{y\in Y}\pi(y)=1
;

•

\overline{P}(A)=\sup_{y\in A}\pi(y)
,
A\in\Sigma_{Y}
.

Cella and Martin, 2022
show that, under the so-called
consonance assumption
, i.e. if
\sup_{y_{n+1}\in Y}\pi(y_{n+1},y^{n})=1
, then conformal transducer
\pi
induces a possibility function
\overline{\Pi}(A)\coloneqq\sup_{y_{n+1}\in A}\pi(y_{n+1},y^{n})
, for all
A\in\Sigma_{Y}
, and in turn a credal set
\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Pi})\coloneqq\{P:P(A)\leq\overline{\Pi}(A),\forall A\in\Sigma_{Y}\}
. Such a credal set can be seen as the image of the correspondence
\mathsf{CRED}:Y^{n}\times\mathscr{F}\rightrightarrows\Delta_{Y}
, where
\mathscr{F}
is the collection of functions in (
4
), and
(y^{n},\psi)\mapsto\mathsf{CRED}(y^{n},\psi)\coloneqq

(7)

\displaystyle\Bigg\{P\in\Delta_{Y}:P(A)\leq\underbrace{\sup_{y_{n+1}\in A}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathbbm{1}\left[\psi\left(y^{n+1}_{-i},y_{i}\right)\geq\psi\left(y^{n+1}_{-(n+1)},y_{n+1}\right)\right]\right)}_{\equiv\sup_{y_{n+1}\in A}\pi(y_{n+1},y^{n})\eqqcolon\overline{\Pi}(A)}\text{, }\forall A\in\Sigma_{Y}\Bigg\}.

The consonance assumption is needed to ensure that
\mathsf{CRED}(y^{n},\psi)\in\mathscr{C}
; The elements of
\mathsf{CRED}(y^{n},\psi)
are characterized in
Martin, 2025
. In the remainder of the paper, we sometimes write
\pi(y,y^{n},\psi)\equiv\pi_{\psi}(y,y^{n})
for notational convenience (we drop the
n+1
subscript for
y_{n+1}
), and to explicitly acknowledge that the conformal transducer
\pi
depends on the choice of the non-conformity measure
\psi
.

We also consider a slight modification of
\mathsf{CRED}
, that will significantly simplify our proving endeavors in Sections
3
,
4
, and
5
; We denote it by
\mathsf{CRED}_{\text{cl}}(y^{n},\psi)\coloneqq\{P\in\Delta_{Y}:P(A)\leq\overline{\Pi}(A)\text{, }\forall A\in\Sigma_{Y}\text{ closed}\}
. As we can see,
\mathsf{CRED}_{\text{cl}}(y^{n},\psi)\supseteq\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Pi})
, because it imposes less constraints on the
P
’s: They only have to be setwise dominated on closed sets. The upper probability
\overline{\Pi}
and its conjugate lower probability
\underline{\Pi}
(the only characteristics of the credal sets that we use) stay the same for both
\mathsf{CRED}
and
\mathsf{CRED}_{\text{cl}}
: This justifies our simplifying choice. To avoid notation complications, in the remainder of this work we do not distinguish between
\mathsf{CRED}(y^{n},\psi)
and
\mathsf{CRED}_{\text{cl}}(y^{n},\psi)
.

3.
Conformal Prediction as an Upper Hemicontinuous Morphism

This section introduces category
UHCont
, and shows that, under minimal assumptions, the Conformal Prediction correspondence
\kappa
is a morphism of
UHCont
, embedded in a commuting diagram. The following is inspired by
Perrone, 2024
.

Definition 1

(Structure
UHCont
)
.

Define a structure
\mathbf{UHCont}
as follows,

•

Objects:
Topological spaces
(X,\tau_{X})
.

•

Morphisms:
Upper hemicontinuous correspondences
\Phi:X\rightrightarrows Y
(see Definition
26
in Appendix
A
).

Theorem 2

(
UHCont
is a Category)
.

The structure
\mathbf{UHCont}
defines a category.

Proof.

We verify the axioms of a category.

(1) Identity morphisms.
For each object
X
, define
\mathrm{id}_{X}(x)\coloneqq\{x\}
. Let
V\subseteq X
be open and suppose
x_{0}\in X
satisfies
\mathrm{id}_{X}(x_{0})=\{x_{0}\}\subseteq V
. Then, since
x_{0}\in V
and
V
is open, there exists an open neighborhood
U=V
of
x_{0}
such that for all
x\in U
,
\mathrm{id}_{X}(x)=\{x\}\subseteq V
. Hence,
\mathrm{id}_{X}
is upper hemicontinuous.

(2) Associativity of composition.
Let
\Phi:X\rightrightarrows Y
,
\Psi:Y\rightrightarrows Z
, and
\Theta:Z\rightrightarrows W
. Then for all
x\in X
,

((\Theta\circ\Psi)\circ\Phi)(x)=\bigcup_{y\in\Phi(x)}\bigcup_{z\in\Psi(y)}\Theta(z)=\bigcup_{z\in(\Psi\circ\Phi)(x)}\Theta(z)=(\Theta\circ(\Psi\circ\Phi))(x)

by the associativity of union. Thus, composition is associative.

(3) Closure under composition.
Let
\Phi:X\rightrightarrows Y
and
\Psi:Y\rightrightarrows Z
be upper hemicontinuous correspondences. By
Aliprantis and Border, 2006
, the composition
\Psi\circ\Phi
defined by

(\Psi\circ\Phi)(x)\coloneqq\bigcup_{y\in\Phi(x)}\Psi(y)

is also upper hemicontinuous. Thus, the composition of morphisms is again a morphism.

(4) Identity laws.
For any morphism
\Phi:X\rightrightarrows Y
and any

-
(viii)

\bigl\|N_{P_{m}}-N_{P}\bigr\|_{\infty}\leq\int_{\Theta}\lvert p_{m}-p\rvert\,M\,\text{d}\lambda_{\Theta}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}0,

and similarly

\|D_{P_{m}}-D_{P}\|_{\infty}\to 0.

Because
D_{P}
is bounded below on the compact set
Y^{n}

(by Step 2 there is a constant

\underline{d}_{P}\coloneqq\min_{y^{n}}D_{P}(y^{n})>0
)
and
D_{P_{m}}
converges uniformly, we have

\inf_{y^{n}}D_{P_{m}}(y^{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\underline{d}_{P}\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }m.

Consequently

\bigl\|p_{P_{m}}(\,\cdot\mid y^{n})-p_{P}(\,\cdot\mid y^{n})\bigr\|_{\infty}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}0\quad\text{uniformly in }y^{n}\in Y^{n},

and division remains well behaved thanks to the common lower bound

\tfrac{1}{2}\,\underline{d}_{P}
.

Step 5. Continuity of
\mathsf{BCP}
.

Fix a point

(y^{n},P)

in the domain. If

y^{n}_{m}\to y^{n}\text{ and }P_{m}\to P

(as in Step 4), then

\|\psi_{(y^{n}_{m},P_{m})}-\psi_{(y^{n},P)}\|_{\infty}\leq\|p_{P_{m}}(\cdot\mid y^{n}_{m})-p_{P_{m}}(\cdot\mid y^{n})\|_{\infty}+\|p_{P_{m}}(\cdot\mid y^{n})-p_{P}(\cdot\mid y^{n})\|_{\infty}.

The first term vanishes by uniform continuity from Step 3, the second by Step 4. Hence,

\|\psi_{(y^{n}_{m},P_{m})}-\psi_{(y^{n},P)}\|_{\infty}\to 0
,
and the first component
y^{n}_{m}\to y^{n}
by assumption. Therefore,
\mathsf{BCP}
is continuous.
∎

Remark 3

(Minimality of
(v)
-
(ix)
)
.

The five conditions
(v)
-
(ix)
are truly minimal for Lemma

to hold.
If any single hypothesis is dropped, one can construct a Bayesian model for which

fails to be continuous (or even well-defined). To see this, consider the following.

•

Drop
(v)
(Measurability of

).
Without measurability in

•

Drop
(vi)
(
y

If

—and hence

—can jump as a function of

•

Drop

(Integrable envelope).
In the absence of any

.

•

Drop
(viii)
(Prior integrability).
If

or

•

Drop
(ix)
(Positive marginal likelihood).
If

for some

, the posterior density

is undefined there, so
\mathsf{BCP}
cannot be continuous.

In practice, conditions
(v)
-
(ix)
are almost

to any reasonable Bayesian model. Let us briefly expand on this. First, notice that almost every statistical model (Gaussian, exponential
family, mixture, etc.) has a likelihood density continuous and
measurable in
y
, so that
(v)
is met.

Condition
(vi)
guarantees that, as we vary our

, the integrand in

and

moves continuously, so

(Normal, Laplace, Student-

.

The uniform-in-

bound of

is the key to swapping limits and integrals.
If

is compact (as it is in our setting, by
(i)
) and

is continuous, we can often take

, which is finite and

-integrable under mild assumptions on

.

Since

involves

, the moment condition in
(viii)

ensures finiteness and continuity of the predictive density. Common priors

is automatic.

Condition

prevents division by zero in the posterior

Bayesian models, the prior’s support overlaps every parameter value

never vanishes.

It is safe to say, then, that hypotheses

-
(ix)
are the

needed to ensure the posterior predictive is well-defined and

and the prior

. In

Before the main result of this section, we need to introduce a further condition on the posterior predictive Cumulative Density Function (CDF).

(x)

Well-behaved CDF.

For each fixed
(y^{n},P)\in Y^{n}\times\Delta_{\Theta}^{\text{dens}}
, the CDF

F(c;\,y^{n},P)\coloneqq\int_{\{\,p_{P}(\cdot\mid y^{n})\leq c\}}p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})\text{d}y

is continuous and strictly increasing in a neighborhood of
its
(1-\alpha)
-quantile

c(\alpha,y^{n},P)=\inf\{\,c:F(c;y^{n},P)\geq 1-\alpha\}.

Call then
\mathsf{QUANT}:[0,1]\times Y^{n}\times\Delta_{\Theta}^{\text{dens}}\rightrightarrows Y
the set-valued map that extracts the
\alpha
-level set of the posterior predictive distribution. That is,

(\alpha,y^{n},P)\mapsto\mathsf{QUANT}(\alpha,y^{n},P)\coloneqq H\bigl(c(\alpha,y^{n},P),\,y^{n},P\bigr),

where

H:\mathbb{R}\times Y^{n}\times\Delta_{\Theta}^{\mathrm{dens}}\;\rightrightarrows\;Y,\qquad H\bigl(c,y^{n},P\bigr)\coloneqq\{\,y\in Y:p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})\geq c\}.

Lemma 10
.

Under assumptions
(i)
-
(x)
, the correspondence
\mathsf{QUANT}
is upper hemicontinuous.

Proof.

First, we show that under
(i)
-
(x)
, the correspondence
H
is upper hemicontinuous. Fix
(c_{0},y^{n}_{0},P_{0})
and let
V\supset H(c_{0},y^{n}_{0},P_{0})
be open in
Y
.
Since
p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})
is jointly continuous and
Y
is compact,

H(c_{0},y^{n}_{0},P_{0})=\{y:p_{P_{0}}(y\mid y^{n}_{0})\geq c_{0}\}

is closed and contained in
V
. Thus there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

\bigl\{y:p_{P_{0}}(y\mid y^{n}_{0})\geq c_{0}-\varepsilon\bigr\}\;\subset\;V.

By joint continuity of
(c,y^{n},P,y)\mapsto p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})
, there is a
neighborhood
U
of
(c_{0},y^{n}_{0},P_{0})
in

\mathbb{R}\times Y^{n}\times\Delta_{\Theta}^{\mathrm{dens}}
such that for all

(c,y^{n},P)\in U
and all
y\in Y
,

p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})\;\geq\;c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_{P_{0}}(y\mid y^{n}_{0})\;\geq\;c_{0}-\varepsilon.

Hence for every
(c,y^{n},P)\in U
,

H(c,y^{n},P)=\{y:p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})\geq c\}\;\subset\;\{y:p_{P_{0}}(y\mid y^{n}_{0})\geq c_{0}-\varepsilon\}\;\subset\;V,

proving upper hemicontinuity.

Now, we show that under
(i)
-
(x)
, function
c
is continuous. By
(x)
, for each fixed
(y^{n},P)
the function

c\mapsto F(c;y^{n},P)
is continuous and strictly increasing near the target
quantile. Standard results on the continuity of inverse functions then
imply that

(\alpha,y^{n},P)\;\longmapsto\;c(\alpha,y^{n},P)

is continuous at every point, since it solves

F\bigl(c(\alpha,y^{n},P);y^{n},P\bigr)=1-\alpha
uniquely in
c
, and both the
left side and the right side vary continuously in

(\alpha,y^{n},P)
.

Finally, we put everything together to show the upper hemicontinuity of
\mathsf{QUANT}
. View
\mathsf{QUANT}
as the composition

(\alpha,y^{n},P)\;\xmapsto{\;c\;}\;\bigl(c(\alpha,y^{n},P),\,y^{n},P\bigr)\;\xmapsto{\;H\;}\;H\bigl(c(\alpha,y^{n},P),\,y^{n},P\bigr).

As we showed before,
H
is upper hemicontinuous in its three arguments, and

c(\alpha,y^{n},P)
is continuous, hence the
singleton-valued correspondence associated with
c
(that we still denote by
c
for ease
of notation) is upper hemicontinuous. The composition of two upper hemicontinuous correspondences is again upper
hemicontinuous
(
Aliprantis and Border, 2006
, Theorem 17.23)
. Therefore,
\mathsf{QUANT}
is upper hemicontinuous.
∎

Remark 4

(On the Well-Behavior of the Predictive CDF)
.

In many continuous Bayesian models,

p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})
is a continuous, strictly positive function on
compact
Y
(for us, this holds by
(i)
). As
c
varies, the level-sets

\{y:p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})\leq c\}
move continuously, so
F(c)
has no jumps. Since
p_{P}
has no plateaus of positive volume, for any

c<c^{\prime}
, the set
\{c<p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})\leq c^{\prime}\}
has strictly positive mass,
forcing
F(c^{\prime})-F(c)>0
. Hence,
F(c)
is both continuous and strictly increasing at the
target quantile.

The condition may fail in two cases, rather negligible in practice. If
p_{P}(y\mid y^{n})\equiv c
on a subset of
Y
of positive measure,
then
F(c)
is constant across that plateau, violating strict increase. Also, if the predictive has atoms (point-mass components),
F(c)
jumps at
those mass points and fails to be continuous.

Theorem

is the central payoff of our categorical treatment, and perhaps the greatest joy (so far!) of looking at Conformal Prediction from a categorical angle. It confirms the intuition put forth in

and

thus showing a very profound connections between these three only apparently far apart prediction mechanisms.

Notice also that Theorem

allows us to tie CP to the concept of e-posterior
(

)
, thus further testifying the central role of CP in modern Machine Learning.

To see this, denote by
\Delta_{Y}^{\text{dens}}
the space of finitely additive probability measures on
Y
that admit a density with respect to a
\sigma
-finite dominating (atomless) measure
\lambda_{Y}
. Since we observe the realizations
y^{n}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
of the first
n

\mathcal{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Y}_{n}
of an exchangeable process (as pointed out in Section
2.1
), the likelihood density

can be written as
(

;
de Finetti, 1975
)

\ell(y^{n}\mid\theta)=\int\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}h(y_{i}\mid\varsigma)\right]G_{\theta}(\text{d}\varsigma),

where
h(\cdot\mid\varsigma)
is the density of an element
L_{\varsigma}\in\Delta_{Y}^{\text{dens}}
(given a latent parameter
\varsigma
), and

is a mixing distribution indexed by the parameter
\theta\in\Theta
.

Consider then a credal prior set
\mathcal{P}_{\text{prior}}

⋆

\Delta_{\Theta}^{\text{dens}}

be its upper probability, and
\overline{p}\coloneqq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{prior}}}\frac{\text{d}P}{\text{d}\lambda_{\Theta}}
be the upper prior density; The lower prior density

is defined similarly, with

.

Proposition 12

(The Upper Posterior is an e-Posterior)
.

Suppose that
\underline{p}
is integrable.
Then, the upper posterior density

(10)

\displaystyle\overline{p}(\theta\mid y^{n})=\frac{\ell(y^{n}\mid\theta)\overline{p}(\theta)}{\underline{\ell}(y^{n})},

where
\underline{\ell}(y^{n})\coloneqq\int_{\Theta}\ell(y^{n}\mid\theta)\underline{p}(\theta)\lambda_{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\theta)
, is an e-posterior à la
Grünwald, 2023
if and only if
\int_{\Theta}\underline{p}(\theta)\lambda_{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\leq\overline{p}(\theta)
, for all
\theta\in\Theta
.

We can interpret the necessary and sufficient condition in Proposition
12
as follows: The probability mass on which all priors agree is no larger than the minimum “peak plausibility” that any state enjoys under at least one prior.

Proof of Proposition
12
.

First, notice that the upper posterior density in (
10
) is well defined.
In particular, it is an upper bound for the generalized Bayes’ upper posterior density
(
Walley, 1991
, Theorem 6.4.6)
.

Then, recall that an e-posterior is a function
p^{\text{e}}(\theta\mid y^{n})=1/Z_{\theta}(y^{n})
, where
Z_{\theta}(y^{n})
is an e-variable, that is, a statistic for which
\mathbb{E}_{Y^{n}\sim L_{\theta}}[Z_{\theta}(Y^{n})]\leq 1
. Call
\lambda_{Y}^{\otimes n}
the
n
-fold product of
\lambda_{Y}
; We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y^{n}\sim L_{\theta}}\left[\frac{1}{\overline{p}(\theta\mid Y^{n})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y^{n}\sim L_{\theta}}\left[\frac{\underline{\ell}(Y^{n})}{\ell(Y^{n}\mid\theta)\overline{p}(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{Y^{n}}\frac{\underline{\ell}(y^{n})}{\ell(y^{n}\mid\theta)\overline{p}(\theta)}\ell(y^{n}\mid\theta)\lambda_{Y}^{\otimes n}(\text{d}y^{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\overline{p}(\theta)}\int_{Y^{n}}\underline{\ell}(y^{n})\lambda_{Y}^{\otimes n}(\text{d}y^{n})

\displaystyle\leq 1\iff\int_{Y^{n}}\underline{\ell}(y^{n})\lambda_{Y}^{\otimes n}(\text{d}y^{n})\leq\overline{p}(\theta),\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

Now, notice that

\displaystyle\int_{Y^{n}}\underline{\ell}(y^{n})\lambda_{Y}^{\otimes n}(\text{d}y^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{Y^{n}}\int_{\Theta}\ell(y^{n}\mid\theta)\underline{p}(\theta)\lambda_{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\lambda_{Y}^{\otimes n}(\text{d}y^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}\underline{p}(\theta)\int_{Y^{n}}\ell(y^{n}\mid\theta)\lambda_{Y}^{\otimes n}(\text{d}y^{n})\lambda_{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}\underline{p}(\theta)\lambda_{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\theta),

where the last-but-one equality comes from Fubini-Tonelli, and the last one comes from
\ell(y^{n}\mid\theta)
being a proper density. Hence, the upper posterior density
\overline{p}(\theta\mid y^{n})
is an e-posterior if and only if

(11)

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\underline{p}(\theta)\lambda_{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\leq\overline{p}(\theta),\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

This concludes the proof.
∎

6.
Conformal Prediction Regions as Images of the Imprecise Highest Density Region Functor

In this section, we see how the correspondence
\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}
can be cast as a functor between two well-known categories. As a consequence, the Conformal Prediction Region can be seen as a functor image.

Theorem 14

(Functoriality of the
\,\alpha
-IHDR)
.

Let
(Y,\Sigma_{Y}=2^{Y})
be a measurable space, and let
\Delta_{Y}
be the set of all
finitely additive probability measures on
\Sigma_{Y}
endowed with the weak
∗
topology.
Let

\mathscr{C}\;\coloneqq\;\bigl\{\,\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Delta_{Y}:\mathcal{M}\text{ is convex and weak${}^{*}$-closed}\bigr\},

and regard both
\mathscr{C}
and
\Sigma_{Y}
as (pre-)categories whose only
morphisms are inclusions “
\subseteq

Fix
\alpha\in[0,1]
. For
\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{C}
, define

B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}\;\coloneqq\;\bigcap\Bigl\{A\in\Sigma_{Y}:\underline{P}_{\mathcal{M}}(A)\,\geq 1-\alpha\Bigr\}.

Set

\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}:\mathscr{C}\rightarrow\Sigma_{Y},\quad\mathcal{M}\mapsto B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha},\qquad\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{2})\coloneqq B_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha}.

Then,
\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}
is a covariant functor.

Proof.

(i) Objects.

Every
A
in the defining intersection for
B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}
is measurable; Arbitrary
intersections of sets in
\Sigma_{Y}
remain in
\Sigma_{Y}
, hence

B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}\in\Sigma_{Y}
. Thus
\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}
maps objects of
\mathscr{C}
to objects of
\Sigma_{Y}
.

(ii) Morphisms (monotonicity).

Suppose
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}
.
Let
y\in B_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\alpha}
. If
y\notin B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha}
, then by definition of

B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha}
, there exists
A\in\Sigma_{Y}
such that

\underline{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}(A)\;\geq 1-\alpha,\qquad y\notin A.

Because
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}
, the same inequality holds with
\mathcal{M}_{2}

replaced by
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, so
A
belongs to the family of sets whose intersection
defines
B_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\alpha}
. Consequently
y\in A
, contradicting
y\notin A
.
Therefore
y\in B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha}
, and

B_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\alpha}\subseteq B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha}.

Hence
\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}
maps the arrow
\mathcal{M}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}
to the arrow

B_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha}
.

(iii) Identities.

For each
\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{C}
, the identity morphism is
\mathcal{M}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}
.
Since inclusion is reflexive,
B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}
is the identity
in
\Sigma_{Y}
, so
\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}(\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{M}})=\mathrm{id}_{B_{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha}}
.

(iv) Composition.

Given
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{3}
, the composite morphism in
\mathscr{C}

is
\mathcal{M}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{3}
. By monotonicity,

B_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\alpha}\subseteq B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha}\subseteq B_{\mathcal{M}_{3}}^{\alpha},

so

\displaystyle\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{3})=B_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow B_{\mathcal{M}_{3}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow B_{\mathcal{M}_{3}}^{\alpha})\circ(B_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow B_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{2}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{3})\circ\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}).

Since
\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}
respects objects, morphisms, identities and
composition, it is a covariant functor.
∎

Remark 5

(Contravariant Variants)
.

Replacing

by its complement

yields a contravariant functor.
Allowing
\alpha

that is antitone in the first argument and covariant in the second.

To the best of our knowledge,

.

We also note in passing that we will not delve into the examination of categories

and

to denote the category whose objects are the elements of

We have the following corollary, which
allows us to see the Conformal Prediction Region outputted by a Full Conformal Prediction procedure as an object image of functor

Corollary 14.1

(Conformal Prediction as a Functor Image)
.

Suppose that the conformal transducer

Immediate from Theorem

∎

Viewing the conformal-prediction map
\mathsf{IHDR}_{\alpha}
as a covariant functor immediately yields practical AI privacy benefits. Suppose that we have many sources producing credal sets
\mathcal{M}_{j}

propagates through the functor without violating the coverage guarantee, so statistical validity is preserved “for free”.

Second, collaboration can proceed in a strictly federated manner: Every agent needs to transmit only its (possibly privatized) summary object

—never the underlying data—thereby minimizing exposure while still enabling the consortium to construct a globally valid prediction region.

7.1.
Monoidal Categories

The first, natural question is whether they are monoidal.

Alas,
\mathbf{UHCont}
is not, as upper hemicontinuity is not preserved under product in general unless the correspondences are compact-valued. We can define a slight strengthening of
\mathbf{UHCont}

Definition 15

(Structure
UHCont
{}_{\text{c}}
)
.

Define a structure
\mathbf{UHCont}_{\text{c}}

•

Objects:
Topological spaces
(X,\tau_{X})

•

Compact-valued

Theorem 16

(
\mathbf{UHCont}_{\text{c}}
is a Monoidal Category)
.

The structure
\mathbf{UHCont}_{\text{c}}
is a monoidal category under the Cartesian product of topological spaces.

Proof.

We verify the monoidal category structure.

(1) Tensor Product of Objects and Morphisms.
For objects
X,Y
, define
X\otimes Y\coloneqq X\times Y
, the Cartesian product with the product topology.

Given morphisms
\Phi_{1}:X\rightrightarrows X^{\prime}
and
\Phi_{2}:Y\rightrightarrows Y^{\prime}
, define their tensor product as

(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})(x,y)\coloneqq\Phi_{1}(x)\times\Phi_{2}(y).

Since compact sets are preserved under finite products,
\Phi_{1}(x)\times\Phi_{2}(y)
is compact for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. Also, the product of upper hemicontinuous correspondences with compact values is upper hemicontinuous with compact values
(
Aliprantis and Border, 2006
, Theorem 17.28)
. Thus,
\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2}
is a morphism in
\mathbf{UHCont}_{\text{c}}
.

(2) Unit Object.
Let the unit object be the singleton space
I\coloneqq\{*\}
, with the trivial topology. For any object
X
, the canonical identifications

\lambda_{X}:I\times X\rightarrow X,\quad\rho_{X}:X\times I\rightarrow X

are homeomorphisms. They define morphisms in
\mathbf{UHCont}_{\text{c}}
via single-valued, continuous correspondences (e.g.,
(x,*)\mapsto x
) with compact singleton values.

(3) Associativity.
Define the associator as the canonical homeomorphism,

\alpha_{X,Y,Z}:(X\times Y)\times Z\rightarrow X\times(Y\times Z).

Its graph defines a single-valued, continuous correspondence with compact-valued singleton fibers. Hence, it is a morphism in the category.

(4) Coherence Conditions.
The coherence diagrams (pentagon and triangle) commute strictly, as they correspond to natural identifications of Cartesian products in the category of topological spaces. Thus, the associators and unitors satisfy the coherence conditions.
∎

Theorem 17

(
\mathbf{WMeas}_{\text{uc}}

Proof.

Let the unit object be the singleton space

(X,\Sigma_{X})\,\otimes\,(X^{\prime},\Sigma_{X^{\prime}})\;\coloneqq\;(X\times X^{\prime},\,\Sigma_{X}\!\otimes\!\Sigma_{X^{\prime}}),\qquad(F\otimes G)(x,x^{\prime})\;\coloneqq\;F(x)\times G(x^{\prime}).

1. Tensor is a bifunctor.

For morphisms

Uniformly compact.

Weakly measurable.

Conclusion

In this paper, we took a Category Theory route to show that
Full Conformal Prediction exhibits intrinsic UQ capabilities. This is a result of framing it as a morphism of two categories,
UHCont
and
\textbf{WMeas}_{\text{uc}}
, and of showing that it is part of a diagram that commutes. As byproduct, we were able to prove that CP methods bridge Bayesian, frequentist, and imprecise approaches to statistical prediction. We also studied the properties of such categories, and we pointed out how a Conformal Prediction Region can be seen as a functor image.

This work opens up the study of Categorical Conformal Prediction, and hints at many unsolved problems. For example, it is an open question how to leverage the structure of the functional-analysis-flavored categories
UHCont
and
\textbf{WMeas}_{\text{uc}}
to learn further aspects of the CP methodology. In particular, because the morphisms of these categories are correspondences, we are interested in what the Kleisli categories
(
Kleisli, 1962
;
Kleisli, 1965
)
,
(
Perrone, 2024
, Section 5.1)
arising from the subcategories of
\mathbf{UHCont}
and
\mathbf{WMeas}_{\text{uc}}
admitting a monad, can tell us about Conformal Prediction.

In addition, it is left to inspect whether studying CP as a morphism of different categories can allow us to remove the need for assumptions altogether, in particular compactness of the state space
Y
.

Finally, it is left for the future to show whether our results extend to Split Conformal Prediction, and to any other extension of classical Conformal Prediction that ML researchers introduced in recent years.

The following is the definition of how a correspondence can be considered measurable in the weakest possible sense.
