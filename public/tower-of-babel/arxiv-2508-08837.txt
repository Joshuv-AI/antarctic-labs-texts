The Roots of International Perceptions: Simulating US Attitude Changes Towards China with LLM Agents
Nicholas Sukiennik Yichuan Xu, Yuqing Kan, Jinghua Piao, Yuwei Yan, Chen Gao, Yong Li

Abstract
The rise of LLMs poses new possibilities in modeling opinion evolution, a long-standing task in simulation, by leveraging advanced reasoning abilities to recreate complex, large-scale human cognitive trends. While most prior works focus on opinion evolution surrounding specific isolated events or the views within a country, ours is the first to model the large-scale attitude evolution of a population representing an entire country towards another – US citizens’ perspectives towards China. To tackle the challenges of this broad scenario, we propose a framework that integrates media data collection, user profile creation, and cognitive architecture for opinion updates to successfully reproduce the real trend of US attitudes towards China over a 20-year period from 2005 to today. We also leverage LLMs’ capabilities to introduce debiased media exposure, extracting neutral events from typically subjective news contents, to uncover the roots of polarized opinion formation, as well as a devils advocate agent to help explain the rare reversal from negative to positive attitudes towards China, corresponding with changes in the way Americans obtain information about the country. The simulation results, beyond validating our framework architecture, also reveal the impact of biased framing and selection bias in shaping attitudes. Overall, our work contributes to a new paradigm for LLM-based modeling of cognitive behaviors in a large-scale, long-term, cross-border social context, providing insights into the formation of international biases and offering valuable implications for media consumers to better understand the factors shaping their perspectives, and ultimately contributing to the larger social need for bias reduction and cross-cultural tolerance.

Introduction

Opinion evolution prediction is a long-standing task in simulation and has implications across a vast array of fields, including cognitive science
(
Schwarz 2000
)
, psychology
(
Edwards 1954
)
, and behavioral science
(
Simon 1966
)
. The dawn of large language models (LLMs) allows us to model dynamic and complex human opinion trends at a scale never before possible
(
Piao et al. 2025b
;
Yang et al. 2025b
)
. LLMs, via their unfathomably large latent semantic embedding space, have been shown to exhibit strong cognitive reasoning abilities on a variety of tasks
(

, from mathematics
(

)
, to logic
(

;
Xu et al. 2025
)
, is insufficient to model long-term thought processes such as trends in opinion evolution, because of the reliance of such tasks on long-term memory, which is far too long to be able to be stored in a single prompt, despite increasing context token sizes
(
Chen et al. 2023
;
Ding et al. 2024
)
. Therefore, several works have designed explicit architectures to capture and simulate more complex, long-term processes adhering to real-world human behaviors
(
Wang et al. 2025a
)
. The first major breakthrough was the use of memory and reflection mechanisms
(
Park et al. 2023
)
, both of which have become nearly standard practice in many agent-based simulation scenarios.

LLM-powered agent-based simulation, for its part, has been employed to simulate a diverse array of large-scale or long-term (or both) cognitive-behavioral phenomena, such as polarization formation
(
Piao et al. 2025b
)
, emergence of trust behaviors
(
Xie et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
)
, collaboration
(
Zhang, Xu, and Deng 2023
;

;
Lan et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
, theory of mind
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Strachan et al. 2024
)
. Yet another line of works has aimed to simulate real-world, data-driven scenarios such as election outcomes
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, economic stimuli
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, and social media polarization
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
. However, among all these prior works, none have aimed at combining cognitive mechanisms with data-driven real-world macro-trends in international perceptions. While prior works have touched upon international dynamics from various perspectives, such as simulating war
(
Hua et al. 2024
)
and diplomacy
(
Guan et al. 2024
)
, these works do not consider the cognitive processes of agents representing real humans at the micro scale to form macro trends.

Our work aims to bridge this gap with a timely scenario: perceptions of US citizens towards the country of China. This subject has increasingly garnered attention in pop culture as well as academia
(
Li and Ang 2025
)
over the past few years, notably due to its influence on global economic and political dynamics. LLMs provide us with a promising opportunity to address this subject via simulation, as doing so can provide insights not only into the way LLMs can be used to model broad, international political perspectives, but also on the way such opinions are formed – what drives them, and how they are influenced, whether positively or negatively. In this work, we aim to reproduce the trend in US citizen attitudes toward China using a data and cognitive theory-driven LLM-agent-based simulation framework. To do so, we first generate a set of thousands of agent profiles, leveraging a large-scale social survey in combination with social media data from X/Twitter, to create a representative and feature-abundant agent pool as a representative sample of the US population. We then design a news exposure-reflection mechanism using real-world news data consisting of more than 100,000 articles across 56 publications. Agents are first exposed to a certain number of articles per year and are sent to reflect on what they read using a cognitive dissonance mechanism, allowing them to rationally update their views in light of new information. At the end of each simulated year, agents conduct a survey assessing their overall attitude towards China.

The results of our simulation show that our framework design successfully reproduces the overall trend in attitudes of US citizens towards China, and a set of ablation studies highlights the necessity of each component. Meanwhile, two intervention mechanisms are implemented to explore the possible ways in which the increasingly negative trend can be alleviated: article debiasing and a devil’s advocate agent.

Overall, our work makes the following core contributions:

•

We propose a simulation framework that uses extensive real-world data and cognitive mechanisms to reproduce a long-term trend of international attitudes.

•

We propose a workflow for agent profiles creation from real-world surveys and social media data as a representative sample of a country’s population.

•

We discover mechanisms that successfully alleviate the negative influence of news media on agents’ attitudes, namely news debiasing and a devil’s advocate agent.

Our criteria for collecting news is that it should contain the word ‘‘China” or ‘‘Chinese” in the headline or sub-header (if available). 4
Simulation Framework

Figure 2:
Framework for Macro-Scale Attitudes Evolution

Our simulation framework, seen in Figure
2
, consists of two major components: agent profiles initialization and long-term attitudes evolution, The first is using the US demographics distribution data collected and prepared to initialize our agents’ profiles. The features, data distribution, and processing methods, are discussed in detail in the previous section, Section
3
. Therefore, in this section we will focus on the second major component: Long-term Attitudes Evolution, which in turn is composed of two parts: news distribution and a reflection mechanism. The two components go through an iterative cycle for each year of the simulate, where, prior to each new iterate, all agents conduct an overall attitudes survey to collect their new perceptions of China after the year of news consumption and reflection.

4.1
News Distribution

The news distribution stage is managed by a distribution engine, which, for each year, gathers random samples of articles published in that year, corresponding to the number of agents in the simulation, and distributes them to each agent. The distributor at first only broadcasts the Headlines of each article in the sample to the agents. Each agent sends back a number of article headlines that they would like to read, selecting based on their individual profiles: demographic features, political views, specific interests, and media habits. The distributor then retrieves the full text for the selected articles and sends it back to the agents. The process of sampling is formalized using the two formula below:

Some considerations are involved in this design. First of all, the headlines broadcast to each agent do not contain any other information, such as publication etc. The reason for this is to prevent bias based on strong correlations between certain political affiliations and certain media outlets. In other words, the agent will leverage his/her profile to determine interest in the article solely based on the headline. We also opt not to use any form of targeted recommendation to match agents with articles. 4.2
Reflection Mechanism

Once the articles are selected and the full text is retrieved, the agents view them and undergo the reflection process. Reflection takes place on a specified batch of articles at a time (equal to or less than the number of articles selected.

Reflection on a batch articles involves three steps: a cognitive mechanism based on cognitive dissonance theory
(
Festinger 1954
)
, an opinion decomposition stage, where the information synthesized and new cognitions are discretized under a set of topics on which agents will update views.

Cognitive Mechanism.
The cognitive mechanism is designed a way for an agent to rationally handle new information according to whether it aligns with or contradicts existing beliefs. First the agent is prompted to decide whether or not there are any contradictions. If there are none, then the given cognition will not change. But if there are contradictions, then the agent can choose from among three option: revise, reinforce, or dismiss. Revise means that the agent will essentially accept that their prior view was incomplete or incorrect, and adopt a new belief consistent with the new information. Reinforce means that the agent refuses to let go of her existing opinion, and in order to do so she adds a new cognition to bridge the gap between the existing and the old one, essentially rationalizing the existing opinion in the face of contradictory information. Finally, the last option is dismiss, where the agent opts to reduce the importance of the belief entirely, bypassing the need to rationalize it at all. During the cognitive process, the agent will also consult her own profile to help determine which beliefs are strong/important, in essence providing an implicit values framework.

Opinion Decomposition.

Once the cognitive process has been undergone, the new cognitions are broken down into a set of categories which reflect the topics covered by the viewed news. The agent will consult his new cognitions and then update the opinions one by one, skipping those not covered by the news and on which no cognitions were formed. The agent is prompted to quantify a valence between -2 and 2 according to whether his perception of the topic has improved or declined (as it pertains to China), where the valences are all initialized to 0. Once the full reflection is complete, the agent will engage in an overall attitude survey with the aim of consolidating his/her evolving views on China over the past year. The survey emulates the format of the real Pew survey on which the simulated results are compared. There is one question in the survey and it is:

Now that you’ve read the above news articles, please answer the following question:
On a scale from 1–4, where: 1 = Very unfavorable, 2 = Somewhat unfavorable, 3 = Somewhat favorable, and 4 = Very favorable.
How would you rate your current opinion of China? Please respond with a number (1, 2, 3, or 4).

Although the original Pew survey contains a “Don’t Know” option, all of our agents are forced to have opinions on China and therefore must respond.

To respond to the survey, the agent first loads in their overall attitude from the previous year, as well as the newly updated domain opinions. The agent’s profile remains in memory to add a “human” element to the decision. 5
Experiments and Results

The simulations conducted were set with 100 agents, randomly initialized from the population of generated profiles. The distribution engine is configured to send a unique random sample of 50 headlines to each agent from each year’s news. Each agent is configured to read and reflect on 10 news articles of their choice per year. This means a total of 20,000 articles are processed across the agent population over the 20 year period. Qwen3-14b is used for its low cost and high performance
(
Yang et al. 2025a
)
, being prompted via an API interface.

5.1
Main Attitudes Simulation

The main simulation result is displayed in Figure
3
. The figure shows the percentage of agents who ranked their attitude towards China as favorable (i.e. positive) vs. those that ranked it as unfavorable (negative), along with the corresponding ground truth statistics. A survey response of 3 or 4 is grouped together as favorable, and 1 or 2 is classified as unfavorable. It is seen that the major trends are readily reproduced by the simulation, started with slightly more negative than positive, an inflection point around 2010, and then increasingly negative from there. There are two areas where the simulation does not fully capture the trend. One is during a sudden increase in positivity around 2017, and the other is the increase in positivity in 2024 and 2025. We suggest that the reasons for these “reversals”, so to speak, could have something to do with events or phenomena that have to do with factors beyond simply media exposure. Therefore, in effort to simulate the mechanisms behind these reversals, we design two intervention mechanisms with the aim of alleviating the negative trend that results from pure media exposure.

The main simulation result, showing the proportion of agents who ranked their attitudes as positive vs. negative over time, compared with the ground truth values.

5.2
Interventions

Figure 4:
Intervention mechanisms for news exposure.

The two interventions include 1) Debiased News Exposure, and 2) Devil’s Advocate.

Debiased News Exposure.
The debiased news mechanism is intended to simulate the effect of people’s opinions being informed by sources other than news media, for example government policies, stock market trends, or hearsay about events in general. These forms of opinion influence are simulated through debiased news media, because they come in the form of events towards which the user can select his/her own emotional response, whereas news often comes pre-appended with subjective context, also known as sensationalism
(
Brown et al. 2018
)
. The debias mechanism works as follows. First the selected news articles are sent to a debiasing agent, with a prompt explaining how articles should be re-written, removing subjective or provocative language or other bias indicators, focusing primarily on conveying neutral events. The rewritten news is then sent to the agent in place of the original article. The rest of the simulation process remains the same.

Devil’s Advocate Agent.
The devils advocate agent is intended to simulate a scenario where a person obtains information, again not from news media, but in this case, from second-hand exposure with the subjects in question. The way this is achieved is that the selected news is first-sent to a devil’s advocate (DA) agent. The DA agent is given the task of critiquing potential weak points in reporting, such as logical fallacies, omitted context, or unsubstantiated claims, with the end goal of providing an alternative perspective on the events covered in the news media.

The results of the interventions are presented in Figure
5
. It is observed that both intervention mechanisms successfully allay the plunge in opinion from positive to negative over time. Therefore, we suggest that is it through being exposed to similar types of information that the trend was reversed on two occasions in the real world data. The fact that the results of the debias mechanism succeed in alleviating negative opinions suggests that news media plays a heavy role in influencing people’s opinions, considering that the debiased news did not convey any optimistic perspective, but rather covered events neutrally and allowed the agents to come to their own conclusions.
We also note that the devil’s advocate was told to be critical of all narratives, even those that were favorable to China, and the fact that the devil’s advocate mechanism succeeded in alleviating negative opinions tells us that there are alternative ways to look at events. The success of these two mechanisms on agents’ opinions suggests that humans, too, might benefit from being more critical of the narratives they hear prior to adopting beliefs.

Figure 5:
The impact of the debiasing and devil’s advocate mechanisms on the attitudes evolution. The left figure shows the difference in the positive user proportions for each type, whereas the right figure shows the average scores over time.

5.3
Ablations

Figure 6:
The average MAE for the full design and ablations with respect to ground truth.

We also conduct several ablation studies to highlight the performance of various components of our simulation framework.
We conduct the following 4 ablations:
No Cognitive:
The cognitive dissonance mechanism is removed.
No profile:
The user profile is removed when updating opinions.
No Selection:
There is no option to select articles based on headlines. The random sample sent by the news distributor must be read.
Title Only:
No article full text, headlines only.

The overall performance, expressed as mean absolute error (MAE) of all results, including the full framework design, are compared in Figure
6
.
We observe that the full design achieves the best performance among all ablations, suggesting that the proposed framework design is optimal for accurately simulating the scenario of US citizens opinions towards China.

Figure 7:
A comparison of four ablations with respect to the ground truth.

Next, the full simulation results compared to the ground truth for each of the ablations are presented in Figure
7
, allowing us to delve deeper and understand the implications of each component. First of all, is seen that without the cognitive mechanism, agents are more susceptible to adopting negative views in response to news exposure, causing an overwhelming proportion of the agent population to adopt an unfavorable opinion after only about 5 years, and still increasing from there. This points to the need for a nuanced design rooted in cognitive theory in order to model broad attitudes’ evolution such as the one simulated here.

The absence of a profile also causes a similar negative trend, albeit slightly slower than the previous one. This tells us the having a detailed identity including demographics, political beliefs, etc. keeps people’s views slightly more firm, making them a bit less susceptible to the full extent of news media’s influence.

The results for the No Selection have the opposite effect, causing the descent into negativity to occur more slowly. This could point to the presence of a confirmation bias, where agents are prone to selecting articles to read that align with their existing views
(
Mao, Bolouki, and Akyol 2018
)
.

Finally, the Title Only ablation also shows a slower negative trend, keeping people’s views moderate for most of the simulation period. This implies that in order to properly model macro-trend in the international attitudes of a population, rich and abundant media text must be used.

5.4
Influencing Factors

Beyond overall results, we also aim to understand some of the correlating factors with attitude changes among the agent population. We conduct two downstream factor analyses based on the overall simulation from Section
5.1
: domain analysis and demographics analysis.

The domain analysis is conducted by computed the average updated scores given for each of the domains during the opinion update of the agents’ reflection stage. The results of the domain analysis are illustrated in Figure
8
, showing the influence of different domains over time as well as in terms of overall opinion valence. We observe that there are some very evident domains that lead to positive and negative views, respectively, Those that lead to the strongest positive views include technology, which is the only one whose impact improves over time, lifestyle, sports and entertainment. Those that leading most strongly to negative views are articles that cover economics, politics and health. These findings pose interesting implications on the way media choose to frame their news and shed light onto possible ways for people to offset a negative influence.

6
Conclusions

The current work aims to develop a simulation framework for modeling broad attitudes changes in a country’s population – in this case, US citizens’ attitudes towards China, motivated by prior works that use LLMs to model cognitive mechanisms to achieve close fidelity to real-world trends and behaviors. The selection of US perceptions towards China possesses special relevance given the changing global situation, while also providing a novel simulation scenario that can provide new insights into LLMs’ cognitive abilities.
We first undergo a phase of data collection and processing consisting of 1) demographic data,
2) news media data,
and 3) offline survey data.
The core framework consists of news distribution and reflection mechanisms, with the latter informed by cognitive dissonance theory. The results of the simulation show that our framework design is closely correlated with the real 20-year trend of US attitudes towards China, with an overall MAE of 8.3% with respect to the ground truth. We conduct two intervention studies, article debiasing and a devil’s advocate agent, both of which successfully counteract the negative effect of news media over time. We delve deeper to find that news articles about technology have a heavily positive effect on agents’ opinions towards China, whereas those covering economics, politics, etc., typically reflect poorly on the country. The findings of our work have strong social implications, allowing news consumers to understand better the ways in which their opinions are being formed and influenced over time. The intervention findings, in turn, can be useful for media publishers to be better aware of the implicit biases in their coverage, and encourage a push towards more objective and less sensationalistic reporting.

The limitations of this work are as follows. First, our work relies on an offline survey as ground truth, which itself may contain sampling bias. Secondly, the agents in our system are all forced to have opinions based on news exposure, whereas a real population may not consume news, nor may have an opinion at all. Future work should address sampling bias and presence of non-opinion agents.
