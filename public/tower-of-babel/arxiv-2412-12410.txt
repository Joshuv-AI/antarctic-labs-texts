Abstract
. Let f f be a newform of prime level p p with any central character χ ( mod p ) \chi(\bmod\,p) , and let g g be a fixed cusp form or Eisenstein series for SL 2 ​ ( ℤ ) \hbox{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}) . We prove the subconvexity bound: for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , L ⁡ ( 1 / 2 , f ⊗ g ) ≪ p 1 / 2 − 1 / 524 + ε , \displaystyle L(1/2,f\otimes g)\ll p^{1/2-1/524+\varepsilon}, where the implied constant depends on g g , ε \varepsilon , and the archimedean parameter of f f . This improves upon the previously best-known result by Harcos and Michel. Our method ultimately relies on non-trivial bounds for bilinear forms in Kloosterman fractions pioneered by Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec, with later innovations by Bettin and Chandee.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Discussion of results

There are a handful of methods to prove subconvexity bounds for
L
-functions. Among these, the moment method has been widely used for many decades. This method embeds a given
L
-function into a family and analyzes a suitable (e.g., amplified) moment averaged over the family; see
[
DFI01
,
DFI02
]
, for instance.
The moment method has been largely constrained to
L
-functions for
\hbox{GL}_{1}
and
\hbox{GL}_{2}
, though with some notable exceptions
such as
[
Li11
,
BB20
,
Nel23
]
.

Around 2010, Munshi pioneered the delta method for subconvexity, which dispenses with averaging over a family.
This approach was the first to address subconvexity for a wide range of
\hbox{GL}_{3}

L
-functions across various aspects, such as in
[
Mun15a
,
Mun15b
]
. Moreover, the delta method has yielded strong subconvexity bounds, exemplified by results in
[
Lin21
,
Agg20
]
.
These notable successes over the past decade have spurred further explorations and new applications of this method.

Nevertheless, some of the most difficult subconvexity problems have only been resolved with the moment method. A prominent example is the subconvexity for
\hbox{GL}_{2}\times\hbox{GL}_{2}
Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions in the
level aspect
, which has important applications to equidistribution problems of Heegner points on Shimura curves (e.g.,
[
Mic04
]
,
[
HM06
]
). One of the most difficult cases is when the level is a prime
p
, in which case the problem is stated as follows.
Let
f
be a cuspidal Hecke newform of prime level
p
with central character
\chi\,(\bmod\,p)
and its archimedean parameter (i.e., weight or Laplace eigenvalue) treated as fixed, and let
g
be a fixed Hecke newform of level
1
(say). The problem is to establish a bound of the form

(1.1)

L(1/2,f\otimes g)\ \ll\ p^{1/2-\delta+\varepsilon}

for some absolute constant
\delta>0
, and where the implied constant depends on
\varepsilon>0
. When
g
is an Eisenstein series, (
1.1
) becomes

L(1/2,f)\ \ll\ p^{1/4-\delta/2+\varepsilon/2},

which was first proved by Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec in
[
DFI94a
]
for
\chi
trivial, and
[
DFI01
]
for
\chi
primitive.
For the case where both of the forms
f
and
g
are cuspidal,
Kowalski, Michel, and VanderKam
[
KMV02
]
proved a subconvex bound for
L(1/2,f\otimes g)
when
\chi
is trivial
and
f
is holomorphic. Subsequently, Michel
[
Mic04
]
addressed the case where
\chi
is primitive and
g
is holomorphic, whereas Harcos and Michel
[
HM06
]
treated the case where
\chi
is primitive and
g
is a Maass form. We should also mention that Michel and Venkatesh
[
MV10
]
famously proved subconvexity for
\hbox{GL}_{2}\times\hbox{GL}_{2}

L
-functions with arbitrary fixed
g
and with
f
varying in any aspect. The numerical values of
\delta
’s obtained in these works are recorded in Table
1
in Section
1.2
.

For the subconvexity problem (
1.1
), the delta method has thus far been applied only to the case where both
f
and
g
are cuspidal and
\chi
is trivial by Raju
[
Raj18
]
. However, his method does not work when
g
is an Eisenstein series (
[
Raj18
, p. 5]
). In fact, as commented in Kumar, Munshi, and Singh
[
KMKS24
]
, “(the) effectivity and adaptability (of the delta method) in the more
arithmetic problem of level aspect remains a point of deliberation. In particular, it
seems that new inputs are required.”

In this paper, we employ the delta method to prove a
subconvexity bound for
L(1/2,f\otimes g)
that holds
uniformly
for
f
holomorphic or Maass,
g
holomorphic, Maass or Eisenstein, and
\chi
primitive or trivial, in contrast to previous works using the moment method.
Our approach also overcomes the existing limitations of the delta method.
Furthermore, when
f
and
g
are both cuspidal, our bound improves upon the previous best-known result by Harcos
[
Har11
]
, where (
1.1
) was obtained with
\delta=1/1413
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p
be prime, and let
f
be a Hecke newform of level
p
with central character
\chi\,(\bmod\,p)
. Let
g
be a Hecke eigenform of level
1
. Then we have

(1.2)

L(1/2,f\otimes g)\,\ll\,p^{1/2-1/524+\varepsilon},

where the implied constant depends on
g
,
\varepsilon
, and the archimedean parameter of
f
.

Remark 1.2
.

The proof easily generalizes to show the same bound (
1.2
) for
L(1/2+it,f\otimes g)
, with polynomial dependence on
t
and on the archimedean parameter of
f
.

Remark 1.3
.

The bound (
1.2
) has the pleasant feature that it is
independent
of
\theta
, i.e., the approximation towards the Ramanujan conjecture.

Remark 1.4
.

Under the assumption

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}(L)\,:=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L/2<\nu\leq L\\
\nu\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{f}(\nu)|^{2}\,\gg\,L^{1-\varepsilon},

the bound (
1.2
) in Theorem
1.1
can be improved to

(1.4)

\displaystyle L(1/2,f\otimes g)\,\ll\,p^{1/2-1/302+\varepsilon};

see the discussion following (
4.18
).
This observation is helpful for comparing the main argument in Section
4
with the sketch presented in Section

2
, where
(
1.3
) is assumed for ease of exposition.

Remark 1.5
.

In Theorem
1.1
, the assumption that
p
is prime is largely to simplify our exposition, and it is expected that the same method works for general levels and arbitrary central characters.

Remark 1.6
.

Preliminary calculations suggest that our method of proof can be adapted to other aspects, such as subconvexity for
L(1/2+it_{f},f\otimes g)
as
t_{f}\to\infty
, where
t_{f}
denotes the spectral parameter of
f
. We plan to address this question on another occasion.

Unlike the aforementioned works, our proof does not require solving any shifted convolution problems for Fourier coefficients or divisor functions, which is the main reason our bound is independent of
\theta
.
Instead, it makes crucial use of the following theorem by Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec
[
DFI97
, Thm. 2]
on
Kloosterman fractions
.

Theorem 1.7

(Duke–Friedlander–Iwaniec)
.

Let
\alpha_{m}
and
\beta_{n}
be arbitrary complex numbers
supported on
M<m\leq 2M
and
N<n\leq 2N
, respectively.
Let
a
be a non-zero integer.
Then

(1.5)

\sum_{(m,n)=1}\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}e\Big(\frac{a\overline{m}}{n}\Big)\ \ll\ \|\alpha\|\|\beta\|(|a|+MN)^{3/8}(M+N)^{11/48+\varepsilon},

where
||\alpha||:=(\,\sum_{m}|\alpha_{m}|^{2})^{1/2}
and
||\beta||:=(\,\sum_{n}|\beta_{n}|^{2})^{1/2}
.

The work
[
DFI97
]
does not involve the spectral theory of automorphic forms, and neither does our method. In other words, our argument relies solely on
\hbox{GL}_{1}
harmonic analysis. This is more than just a curiosity, because in particular it means that our method works equally well for holomorphic cusp forms of small weights, including weight
1
. The
L
-functions associated with cusp forms of weight
1
are of great arithmetic significance, since they include examples such as the class group
L
-functions of imaginary quadratic fields and Artin
L
-functions of degree-two Galois representations. The subconvexity of such
L
-functions has important consequences for the arithmetic statistics of class groups (see
[
DFI02
, Thm. 2.7–2.8]
), but this problem is widely recognized as technically challenging, as the previous approaches entail deep use of spectral machinery with various surprising phenomena; see, e.g.,
[
DFI02
, pp. 491–492]
. In fact, even the full Kuznetsov formula does not appear to have been explicitly written down for this setting.

Our work introduces a new usage of Theorem
1.7
for subconvexity problems. Previously, Theorem
1.7
has been applied in only a few instances of subconvexity
[
DFI01
,
DFI02
,
Mun11
]
, all of which also relied on the spectral toolkit for
\mathrm{GL}_{2}
. More recently, Theorem
1.7
has been refined further by Bettin and Chandee
[
BC18
]
.
They improved the amount of savings in the bilinear sum
(
1.5
), and also extracted additional savings when averaging over a third variable. More precisely, they showed:

Theorem 1.8

(Bettin–Chandee)
.

Let
\alpha_{m}
,
\beta_{n}
, and
\gamma_{k}
be arbitrary complex numbers
supported on
M<m\leq 2M
,
N<n\leq 2N
, and
K\leq k\leq 2K
, respectively.
Let
a
be a non-zero integer.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{(m,n)=1}\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}\gamma_{k}e\Big(\frac{ak\overline{m}}{n}\Big)\

\displaystyle\ll\ \|\alpha\|\|\beta\|\|\gamma\|\Big(1+\frac{|a|K}{MN}\Big)^{1/2}

(1.6)

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big((KMN)^{7/20+\varepsilon}(M+N)^{1/4}+(KMN)^{3/8+\varepsilon}(KM+KN)^{1/8}\Big),

where
||\alpha||:=(\,\sum_{m}|\alpha_{m}|^{2})^{1/2}
,
||\beta||:=(\,\sum_{n}|\beta_{n}|^{2})^{1/2}
, and
||\gamma||:=(\,\sum_{k}|\gamma_{k}|^{2})^{1/2}
.

We shall use Theorem
1.8
instead of Theorem
1.7
to obtain the exponent in (
1.1
).

1.4.
Notations

Throughout the article, we use the following standard conventions in analytic number theory. We use
\varepsilon
to denote an arbitrarily small positive constant that may differ from line to line.
We write
A\asymp B
if
A\ll B
and
B\ll A
. A quantity
A
is said to be “very small” if
A\ll_{M}p^{-M}
for any
M>0
. We suppress the dependence on
\varepsilon
,
g
and the weight/Laplace eigenvalue of
f
in the asymptotic notations.

2.
Sketch of proof

In this section, we sketch the proof of Theorem
1.1
. This sketch is not intended to be fully rigorous but rather to provide a conceptual road map for the proof. After applying the approximate functional equation, the task of proving a subconvex bound for
L(1/2,f\otimes g)
reduces to establishing the estimate

(2.1)

S(N):=\sum_{n\asymp N}\lambda_{f}(n)\lambda_{g}(n)\ll\sqrt{N}p^{1/2-\delta}

for some
\delta>0
,
uniformly for
N\ll p^{1+\varepsilon}
.

2.1.
Amplification

For our method, it is essential to apply an
amplification
to
S(N)
as follows. Let
L\geq 1
be a parameter at our disposal, and
\gamma_{\ell}
be
1
if
\ell
is prime with
\ell\asymp L
and
0
otherwise. Then

(2.2)

\displaystyle S(N)\approx\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}_{1}(L)}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\gamma_{\ell}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell)}\sum_{n\asymp N}\lambda_{f}(n\ell)\lambda_{g}(n),\hskip 15.0pt\text{with}\hskip 10.0pt\mathcal{A}_{1}(L):=\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\gamma_{\ell}|\lambda_{f}(\ell)|^{2}.

To simplify our sketch, we assume the lower bound (
1.3
) for
\mathcal{A}_{1}(L)
, which is expected to be true though currently unproven.
This is a pervasive, but well-known technical issue with amplifiers. A standard work-around, first presented in
[
DFI94a
]
, exploits the fact that
\lambda_{f}(\ell)
and
\lambda_{f}(\ell^{2})
cannot be simultaneously small.
See Section
4.1
for our precise choice of amplifier.

2.2.
Delta method

We use the DFI delta method (Lemma
3.1
)
with
C\asymp\sqrt{NL}
,
giving

(2.3)

\displaystyle S(N)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}_{1}(L)}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\gamma_{\ell}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell)}\sum_{m\asymp NL}\lambda_{f}(m)\sum_{n\asymp N}\lambda_{g}(n)\delta(m=n\ell)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\approx\ \frac{1}{C\mathcal{A}_{1}(L)}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\gamma_{\ell}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell)}\sum_{c\ll C}\frac{1}{c}\quad\sideset{}{{}^{*}}{\sum}_{h\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\pmod{c}}\sum_{m\asymp NL}\lambda_{f}(m)e_{c}(hm)\sum_{n\asymp N}\lambda_{g}(n)e_{c}(-hn\ell).

2.3.
Voronoi and Cauchy–Schwarz

Applying the Voronoi formula (Lemma
3.2
) to the
m
and
n
-sums in the last expression, we obtain, in the generic case when
N\asymp p
,
c\asymp C
, the following:

(2.5)

\displaystyle S(p)\asymp\frac{1}{L^{3/2}}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\gamma_{\ell}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell)}\sum_{c\asymp\sqrt{Lp}}\frac{\chi(c)}{c}\sum_{m\ll p}\overline{\lambda_{f}(m)}\sum_{n\ll L}\lambda_{g}(n)S(0,pn-{\ell}m;c),

where
S(0,pn-\ell m;c)
is the Ramanujan sum. A trivial estimate using
\lambda_{f},\lambda_{g},\gamma_{\ell}\ll 1
gives
S(p)\ll\sqrt{L}p
, which is away from the “boundary” by a factor of
\sqrt{L}
.

In this sketch, we consider only the terms with
(c,\ell)=1
; the contribution from
(c,\ell)>1
can be estimated trivially after applying the Voronoi formula; see Lemma
4.7
.
Also, the contribution from
p\mid m
in (
2.5
) can be treated easily; see Lemma
4.8
. The terms with
p\mid m
differ from those with
(p,m)=1
, since the former case allows for
pn-\ell m=0
, causing
S(0,pn-\ell m;c)
to be large for all
c
.

Moving the
m
-sum to the outermost position and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the sum in (
2.5
), we observe that

|S(p)|^{2}\ll L^{-4}T^{\prime}
,
where

\displaystyle T^{\prime}\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\asymp L}\gamma_{\ell_{1}}\gamma_{\ell_{2}}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell_{1})}\lambda_{f}(\ell_{2})\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\ll L}\lambda_{g}(n_{1})\lambda_{g}(n_{2})\sum_{c_{1},c_{2}\asymp\sqrt{Lp}}\chi(c_{1}\overline{c_{2}})

(2.6)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\ll p\\
(m,p)=1\end{subarray}}S(0,pn_{1}-\ell_{1}m;c_{1})S(0,pn_{2}-\ell_{2}m;c_{2}).

Write
T^{\prime}=T^{\prime\prime}+T^{(0)}
, where
T^{\prime\prime}
and
T^{(0)}
consists of the terms with
\ell_{2}n_{1}\neq\ell_{1}n_{2}
and
\ell_{2}n_{1}=\ell_{1}n_{2}
respectively. A trivial bound is sufficient to estimate
T^{(0)}
satisfactorily; see Lemma

4.9
.

2.4.
Poisson summation

This step, together with the next, represents the major innovation of this article. We exploit the cancellation in the correlation sum of Ramanujan sums in (
2.6
), an arithmetic structure not present in previous works on the subconvexity of
L(1/2,f\otimes g)
. Most importantly, this structure is
independent
of the choices
\hbox{GL}_{2}
automorphic forms
f
and
g
. This is the primary reason why we are able to achieve strong uniformity in our subconvex bound and successfully overcome the limitations of delta methods discussed earlier.

We apply Poisson summation to analyze
T^{\prime\prime}
. To do so, we first extend the
m
-sum in
T^{\prime\prime}
to include all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, then subtract back the terms with
p\mid m
, say
T^{\prime\prime}=T-T^{(00)}
accordingly. Again, a trivial estimation to
T^{(00)}
is adequate; see Lemma
4.10
. Now, Poisson summation for the sum over
m\asymp p
in
T
gives

\displaystyle T\approx

\displaystyle\ p\mathop{\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\asymp L}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\ll L}}_{\ell_{2}n_{1}\neq\ell_{1}n_{2}}\gamma_{\ell_{1}}\gamma_{\ell_{2}}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell_{1})}\lambda_{f}(\ell_{2})\lambda_{g}(n_{1})\lambda_{g}(n_{2})\sum_{c_{1},c_{2}\asymp\sqrt{Lp}}\chi(c_{1}\overline{c_{2}})

(2.7)

\displaystyle\ \times\sum_{|m|\ll L}\mathop{\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\mathrlap{\ast}}}_{h_{1}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ c_{1})}\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\mathrlap{\ast}}}_{h_{2}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ c_{2})}}_{c_{2}h_{1}\ell_{1}+c_{1}h_{2}\ell_{2}\equiv m\ (\mathrm{mod}\ c_{1}c_{2})}e_{c_{1}}(h_{1}n_{1}p+h_{2}n_{2}p).

We first analyze the contribution of the zero frequency (
m=0
) to
T
. Here, we essentially have
c_{1}=c_{2}
, and the constraint in the
h_{1},h_{2}
-sums eventually reduces to
\ell_{2}n_{1}\equiv\ell_{1}n_{2}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ c_{1})
. The sum over
c_{1}
is controlled by the number of divisors of
\ell_{2}n_{1}-\ell_{1}n_{2}
. So,
this contribution to
T
is
\ll L^{9/2}p^{3/2}
(see Lemma
4.16
), which is satisfactory when
L
is small enough.

For the non-zero frequencies (
m\neq 0
), the character sums over
h_{1},h_{2}
can be evaluated in closed forms; see Lemma
4.13
. In the generic case when
m\asymp L
and
(c_{1},c_{2})=(c_{1},\ell_{1})=(c_{2},\ell_{2})=1
,
we arrive at

\displaystyle T\approx\

\displaystyle p\mathop{\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\asymp L}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\ll L}}_{\ell_{2}n_{1}\neq\ell_{1}n_{2}}\gamma_{\ell_{1}}\gamma_{\ell_{2}}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell_{1})}\lambda_{f}(\ell_{2})\lambda_{g}(n_{1})\lambda_{g}(n_{2})\sum_{c_{1},c_{2}\asymp\sqrt{Lp}}\chi(c_{1}\overline{c_{2}})

(2.8)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{m\approx L}e_{c_{1}}\left(\overline{c_{2}\ell_{1}}mn_{1}p\right)e_{c_{2}}\left(\overline{c_{1}\ell_{2}}mn_{2}p\right).

By using the additive reciprocity to

e_{c_{1}}(\overline{c_{2}\ell_{1}}mn_{1}p)
and observing that
e_{c_{1}c_{2}\ell}(mn_{1}p)
can be absorbed into the weight function as
mn_{1}p\asymp c_{1}c_{2}\ell_{1}
, we have roughly

\displaystyle T\approx

\displaystyle\ p\mathop{\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\asymp L}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\ll L}}_{\ell_{2}n_{1}\neq\ell_{1}n_{2}}\gamma_{\ell_{1}}\gamma_{\ell_{2}}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell_{1})}\lambda_{f}(\ell_{2})\lambda_{g}(n_{1})\lambda_{g}(n_{2})\sum_{c_{1},c_{2}\asymp\sqrt{Lp}}\chi(c_{1}\overline{c_{2}})

(2.9)

\displaystyle\ \times\sum_{m\asymp L}e_{c_{2}\ell_{1}}(\overline{c_{1}\ell_{2}}mp(n_{2}\ell_{1}-n_{1}\ell_{2})).

2.5.
Cancellation in Kloosterman fractions

With
A:=c_{1}\ell_{2}
and
B:=c_{2}\ell_{1}
, we arrange
T
as follows:

(2.10)

\displaystyle T\approx

\displaystyle\ p\mathop{\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\asymp L}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\ll L}}_{\ell_{2}n_{1}\neq\ell_{1}n_{2}}\gamma_{\ell_{1}}\gamma_{\ell_{2}}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\ell_{1})}\lambda_{f}(\ell_{2})\lambda_{g}(n_{1})\lambda_{g}(n_{2})

(2.11)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{m\asymp L}\,\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A,B\asymp L^{3/2}p^{1/2}\\
\ell_{2}\mid A,\thinspace\ell_{1}\mid B\end{subarray}}\chi(A/\ell_{2})\overline{\chi}(B/\ell_{1})e_{B}(\overline{A}mp(n_{2}\ell_{1}-n_{1}\ell_{2}))\bigg).

If one applies Theorem
1.7
to the
bilinear form
over
A,B
, with the following choices of parameters:
|a|=mp\cdot|n_{2}\ell_{1}-n_{1}\ell_{2}|\ll L^{3}p
,
M=N=L^{3/2}p^{1/2}
, and
\alpha_{A}
supported on integers divisible by
\ell_{2}
(and likewise,
\beta_{B}
supported on multiples of
\ell_{1}
), then we have

(2.12)

\displaystyle T\ll L^{7}p^{2}(L^{3/2}\sqrt{p})^{-1/48}.

However, (
2.12
) can be improved by invoking Theorem
1.8
for the
trilinear
form over
m,A,B
:

3.3.
Archimedean analysis

First we recall the definition of a family of inert functions. Suppose
\mathcal{F}
is an index set and
X:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}
is a function. We denote
X(i)
as
X_{i}

Definition 3.3
.

A family of smooth functions
\{w_{i}:i\in\mathcal{F}\}
supported on a Cartesian product of dyadic intervals in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}
is called
X
-inert if for each

we have

(3.12)

\sup_{i\in\mathcal{F}}\sup_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}}\frac{x_{1}^{j_{1}}\dots x_{d}^{j_{d}}|w_{i}^{(j_{1},\dots,j_{d})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})|}{X_{i}^{j_{1}+\dots+j_{d}}}<\infty.

For brevity, but as an abuse of terminology, we may refer to a single function being inert when we should properly say that it is part of an inert family.
In practice, we will often have functions depending on a number of variables and it is unwieldy to list them all.
Our convention will be to write
w(x_{1},\dots,x_{k};\cdot)
where ‘
\cdot
’ represents some suppressed list of variables for which
w
is inert; we will use this notation even for
k=0
.

We now state some properties of inert families. Interested readers can find the proofs of these lemmas in
[
BKY13
]
and
[
KPY19
, Sec. 2 and 3]
.

4.1.
Amplification

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(L):=\mathcal{A}_{1}(L)+\mathcal{A}_{2}(L),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{j}(L):=\sum_{\nu\leq L}\gamma_{\nu}a_{j}(\nu)\lambda_{f}(\nu^{j}),\qquad\text{where}\qquad a_{j}(\nu)=\begin{cases}\overline{\lambda_{f}(\nu)}&j=1,\\

For

(4.3)

\mathcal{L}_{j}=\{\nu^{j}:\nu\text{ prime},\thinspace L/2<\nu\leq L\},

and alternatively write

) and the definition of

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{j}}|b_{j}(\ell)|^{2}\ll Lp^{\varepsilon}.

The amplifier

will serve as an unconditional replacement for the conjectural estimate in (

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(L)=

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{A}_{1}(L)+\mathcal{A}_{2}(L)=\sum_{\nu\leq L}\gamma_{\nu}(|\lambda_{f}(\nu)|^{2}-\overline{\chi(\nu)}\lambda_{f}(\nu^{2}))

(4.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{\nu\leq L}\gamma_{\nu}\overline{\chi(\nu)}(\lambda_{f}(\nu)^{2}-\lambda_{f}(\nu^{2}))=\sum_{\nu\leq L}\gamma_{\nu}\gg L(pL)^{-\varepsilon}.

For a positive integer
r\geq 1

(4.7)

S(N,r):=\sum_{n}\lambda_{f}(nr)\lambda_{g}(n)w_{N}(n).

Note that
S(N,1)=S(N)
as defined in (

).

Now consider
an amplified sum of the form

(4.8)

\displaystyle S(N,\mathcal{A})=S(N,\mathcal{A}_{1})+S(N,\mathcal{A}_{2}),

where

(4.9)

S(N,\mathcal{A}_{j}):=\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}a_{j}(\nu)S(N,\nu^{j})=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{j}}b_{j}(\ell)S(N,\ell)\hskip 20.0ptj=1,2.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have

(4.10)

S(N)=\frac{S(N,\mathcal{A})}{\mathcal{A}(L)}+O\Big((pLN)^{\varepsilon}\frac{N}{L}\Big).

Proof.

Using the Hecke relation (
3.8
), we see that for
j=1,2

(4.11)

S(N,\mathcal{A}_{j})=\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}a_{j}(\nu)\sum_{d|\nu^{j}}\lambda_{f}(\nu^{j}/d)\mu(d)\chi(d)\sum_{n\equiv 0\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\pmod{d}}\lambda_{f}(n/d)\lambda_{g}(n)w_{N}(n).

The contribution from the sub-sum with

is given by

. The sub-sum with

gives

(4.12)

-\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}a_{j}(\nu)\lambda_{f}(\nu^{j-1})\chi(\nu)\sum_{n}\lambda_{f}(n)\lambda_{g}(n\nu)w_{N}(\nu n).

Using (

(4.13)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,\nu\\

\nu n\asymp N\end{subarray}}\left(|\lambda_{f}(\nu)\lambda_{f}(n)|^{2}+|\lambda_{g}(n\nu)|^{2}\right)\ll Np^{\varepsilon}.

Hence

S(N,\mathcal{A})=S(N)\mathcal{A}(L)+O((pLN)^{\varepsilon}N).

The lemma follows from the lower bound (

).
∎

\displaystyle I(m,n,\cdot)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ w_{N\ell}(y\ell+u)w_{N}(y)U\left(\frac{u}{cdC}\right)J_{k-1}\left(\frac{4\pi}{c}\sqrt{\frac{m(y\ell+u)}{p}}\right)J_{k^{\prime}-1}\left(\frac{4\pi\sqrt{ny}}{c}\right)\ du\,dy.

By assumption (
4.32
),
J_{k^{\prime}-1}
is oscillatory on the region of integration. This then forces the other Bessel function
J_{k-1}
to have some oscillation as well, as otherwise the entire integral is very small.
This shows that
I(m,n,\cdot)
is very small unless (
4.33
) holds.

By Taylor expansions, we have

(4.37)

\frac{\sqrt{m(y\ell+u)}}{c\sqrt{p}}=\frac{\sqrt{my\ell}}{c\sqrt{p}}\Big(1+\frac{u}{2y\ell}+O\Big(\frac{u^{2}}{y^{2}\ell^{2}}\Big)\Big).

Using (
4.34
) and
cdC\ll C^{2}\asymp N\ell
, the size of the quadratic term above is

(4.38)

\ll\frac{\sqrt{my\ell}}{c\sqrt{p}}\frac{u^{2}}{y^{2}\ell^{2}}\ll\frac{N\ell p^{\varepsilon}}{cdC}\frac{(cdC)^{2}}{N^{2}\ell^{2}}=p^{\varepsilon}\frac{cdC}{N\ell}\ll p^{\varepsilon}.

Hence, by Proposition
3.6
,
we have

(4.39)

J_{k-1}\left(\frac{4\pi}{c}\sqrt{\frac{m(y\ell+u)}{p}}\right)=P^{-1/2}e\left(\frac{2\sqrt{my\ell}}{c\sqrt{p}}+\frac{\sqrt{m}u}{c\sqrt{y\ell p}}\right)w(\cdot)+P^{-1/2}e\left(-\frac{2\sqrt{my\ell}}{c\sqrt{p}}-\frac{\sqrt{m}u}{c\sqrt{y\ell p}}\right)\overline{w}(\cdot).

In total,
I(m,n,\cdot)
is a linear combination (over
\pm
signs) of integrals of the form

(4.40)

P^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ w_{N\ell}(y\ell+u)w_{N}(y)U\left(\frac{u}{cdC}\right)e\left(\pm\frac{2\sqrt{my\ell}}{c\sqrt{p}}\pm\frac{2\sqrt{ny}}{c}\right)e\left(\frac{\pm\sqrt{m}u}{c\sqrt{y\ell p}}\right)w(\cdot)dudy,

where
w(\cdot)
is
p^{\varepsilon}
-inert in all variables.
The
u
-integral simply evaluates as
QdC
times a bump function, which enforces the effective support condition (
4.34
). Hence, the integral simplifies further as

(4.41)

\frac{QdCN}{P}\int_{y\asymp 1}e\left(\pm\frac{2\sqrt{myN\ell}}{c\sqrt{p}}\pm\frac{2\sqrt{nNy}}{c}\right)w(y,\cdot)dy.

When the two
\pm
signs are equal, then the
y
-integral is very small by integration by parts (recalling (
4.32
)).
When the two
\pm
signs are opposites,
after changing variables
y\rightarrow y^{2}/4
and re-defining the inert function
w
, we get (for one of the two sign combinations)

(4.42)

\int e\Big(y\Big(\frac{\sqrt{mN\ell}}{c\sqrt{p}}-\frac{\sqrt{nN}}{c}\Big)\Big)w(y,\cdot)dy=\widehat{w}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{mN\ell}}{c\sqrt{p}}-\frac{\sqrt{nN}}{c},\cdot\Big)=\widehat{w}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{m\ell}-\sqrt{np}}{c\sqrt{p}/\sqrt{N}},\cdot\Big).

This function may be replaced by a function of the form
w(\frac{m\ell-np}{zp^{\varepsilon}},\cdot)
. This completes the proof.
∎
