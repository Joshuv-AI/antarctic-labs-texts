Beyond seizure freedom: Reframing recovery in epilepsy
Authors: Giancarlo Di Gennaro, Andrea Tomasini
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Seizure freedom is widely treated as the endpoint of epilepsy care, yet this assumption risks conflating biological stabilization with restoration of the person as a whole. Advances in pharmacological and surgical treatment have made seizure cessation increasingly achievable, 1 , 2 but remission does not necessarily resolve the existential, social, and identity‐related consequences of epilepsy. We propose that seizure freedom should be conceptualized not as the endpoint of recovery, but as a transitional phase within a broader process of human adaptation.

Our perspective integrates three complementary lines of work: the concept of healing as multidimensional reconstruction rather than mere biological resolution, 3 the “burden of normality” and postremission identity restructuring, 4 , 5 and capability and flourishing approaches to health 6 , 7 (Figure 1). Together, these frameworks suggest that epilepsy persists as lived experience even after seizures stop.

Multilayer framework for epilepsy care: From seizure freedom to flourishing. Epilepsy care is conceptualized as a dynamic, three‐level process within contextual and structural determinants. The first level, epilepsy resolution, involves neurobiological stabilization through ASMs, neuromodulation, and surgery. The second, identity restructuring, reflects the transitional phase after seizure cessation, marked by self‐narrative change and adaptation, including the burden of normality. The third, capability and flourishing, encompasses autonomy, social participation, meaning, and relational and material security. These levels are bidirectionally connected and shaped by social, economic, and cultural factors. Recovery is framed as reconstruction of personhood beyond seizure freedom.

The “burden of normality,” originally described in postsurgical epilepsy cohorts, captures the paradoxical destabilization that may follow remission. 4 This phenomenon is often interpreted as a psychological complication of adjustment. However, qualitative and patient‐centered studies have shown that long‐term adaptation after epilepsy surgery involves ongoing processes of meaning‐making, identity reconstruction, and negotiation of social roles. 8 , 9 In this view, the challenges experienced after seizure cessation reveal that recovery itself has been incompletely defined. Long‐standing epilepsy may shape identity, interpersonal roles, anticipatory behaviors, and future expectations. When seizures cease, individuals are not simply restored to a pre‐illness state; rather, they may face the difficult task of reconstructing continuity under radically altered conditions. 5 Importantly, these dynamics are not universal. Many patients experience remission as liberation and restoration rather than disruption. Studies examining social functioning after epilepsy surgery have also highlighted variability in how individuals perceive and integrate changes in autonomy, relationships, and social participation. 10 Yet such variability strengthens rather than weakens the framework. Transitional outcomes after seizure freedom are probabilistic rather than deterministic, and a comprehensive model must account for both integration and disorientation following major biological change.

This transitional phase can also be interpreted through the lens of transformative experience. 11 Related discussions in neurotechnology and embodied cognition have further emphasized how major alterations in bodily function may reshape self‐perception and the relationship between identity and the body. 12 Seizure freedom, particularly after years of drug‐resistant epilepsy, may generate forms of self‐understanding that cannot be acquired otherwise. The experience of regaining autonomy and future orientation may expand possibilities while simultaneously destabilizing previous identity structures. Healing, therefore, cannot be reduced to symptom elimination alone. 3

A further limitation of seizure‐centered models is that seizure control does not necessarily translate into capability restoration. Even after remission, many individuals continue to experience stigma, employment restrictions, educational disruption, financial insecurity, driving limitations, and altered self‐perception. Conversely, some people with persistent seizures may preserve meaningful agency and coherent social participation. This dissociation highlights the inadequacy of seizure frequency as a sufficient proxy for health status.

We are not suggesting that neurologists become arbiters of existential fulfillment. Rather, epilepsy care should recognize the broader ecology within which recovery occurs. Seizure control remains necessary, but insufficient as a standalone definition of recovery. Psychological support, anticipatory counseling, and attention to capability restriction may therefore represent important dimensions of post‐remission care.

In this framework, seizure freedom occupies a liminal position between neurobiological stabilization and existential reconstruction. Reframing it as a transitional phase rather than a definitive endpoint does not diminish therapeutic success; instead, it restores the person, rather than the seizure alone, to the center of epilepsy care.
