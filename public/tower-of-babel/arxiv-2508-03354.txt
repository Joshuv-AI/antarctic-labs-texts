Quenching time and probability estimates for a stochastic reaction-diffusion system with coupled inner singular absorption terms driven by mixed noises Thanks: Corresponding author: N.I. Kavallaris ( nikos.kavallaris@kau.se )
Nikos I. Kavallaris, Christos V. Nikolopoulos, Subramani Sankar

Abstract
. This paper investigates a stochastic parabolic system under Robin boundary conditions, for which the deterministic counterpart exhibits finite quenching. The stochastic system incorporates mixed noise, combining standard one-dimensional Brownian motion and fractional Brownian motion. Under appropriate assumptions, we derive explicit lower and upper bounds for the quenching time of the solution and establish the global existence of a weak solution. Leveraging Malliavin calculus, we further obtain a quantifiable lower and upper bound on the quenching probability. To complement the theoretical analysis, we design a numerical scheme tailored to the system and present results that validate the analytical predictions, offering insights into the interplay between noise and quenching behaviour.

1.
Introduction and state of the art

In the current work we investigate the following stochastic coupled reaction-diffusion system:

(1.1)

\displaystyle du_{1}(t,x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Delta u_{1}(t,x)+\frac{\lambda_{11}}{(1-u_{1}(t,x))^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{12}}{(1-u_{2}(t,x))^{2}}}}\right]dt+(1-u_{1}(t,x))dN_{1}(t),x\in D,\;t>0,\qquad

(1.2)

\displaystyle du_{2}(t,x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Delta u_{2}(t,x)+\frac{\lambda_{21}}{(1-u_{2}(t,x))^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{22}}{(1-u_{1}(t,x))^{2}}}}\right]dt+(1-u_{2}(t,x))dN_{2}(t),x\in D,\;t>0,\qquad

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{i}(t,x)}{\partial\nu}+\beta u_{i}(t,x)=\beta,\ \ t>0,\ \ x\in\partial D,\ \ i=1,2,

(1.4)

\displaystyle 0\leq u_{i}(0,x)=f_{i}(x)<1,\ \ x\in D,\ \ i=1,2,

where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 1
, and smooth boundary
\partial D
. The constants
\beta,\lambda_{i1},\lambda_{i2}
for
i=1,2
are assumed to be positive real numbers. For each
x\in\partial D
,
\nu=\nu(x)
denotes the outward unit normal vector to the boundary
\partial D
.

The noise terms
\{N_{i}(t):t\geq 0\}
appearing in the system are defined as

(1.5)

N_{i}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}k_{i1}\,dW(s)+\int_{0}^{t}k_{i2}\,dB^{H}(s),\quad i=1,2,

for some positive constants
k_{i1},k_{i2},i=1,2
. These processes are mixtures of a standard one-dimensional Wiener process
\{W(t):t\geq 0\}
and an one-dimensional fractional Brownian motion (fBm)
\{B^{H}(t):t\geq 0\}
, both defined on a filtered probability space
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F},(\mathscr{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}}}\right)
; see also (
1.5
).

The fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}(t)
is characterized by a Hurst index
H\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2},1}}\right)
, which governs the correlation of the increments and the pathwise regularity of the process (see, e.g.,
[
38
]
). Values of
H>\frac{1}{2}
correspond to positively correlated increments, resulting in smoother sample paths.

Furthermore, the initial conditions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are assumed to be non-negative functions in
C^{2}(D)
, not identically zero.

System (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) is motivated by the following prototypical model for electrostatically actuated micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS), which has been extensively studied in the literature (see, e.g.,
[

):

(1.6)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\Delta u+\frac{\lambda}{(1-u)^{2}},&&x\in D,\ t>0,\\
&\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+\beta u=\beta_{c},&&x\in\partial D,\ t>0,\\
&0\leq u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)<1,&&x\in D,\end{aligned}}}\right.

Here,
u=u(t,x)
denotes the deformation of an elastic membrane that constitutes part of the MEMS device. The parameters
\lambda
,
\beta
, and
\beta_{c}
are positive real constants representing, respectively, the strength of the applied electrostatic voltage, the elastic restoring force at the boundary, and an external forcing at the boundary of the elastic membrane (see
[

for further details).

For additional mathematical models describing the operation of MEMS devices, including both local and nonlocal effects, we refer the interested reader to

Modeling the interaction between multiple elastic membranes would naturally lead to a system of partial differential equations more complex than (

), typically involving source terms of the form

State of the art

To the best of our knowledge, the stochastic system (

Consequently, the qualitative properties of its solutions, particularly the impact of stochastic perturbations on the system’s evolution and quenching behaviour, remain uncharted in the existing literature. This work aims to fill this gap by systematically investigating how the presence of mixed noise, consisting of both Brownian and fractional Brownian components, modifies the dynamics of the system, with special focus on the mechanisms and characteristics of finite-time quenching.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the key mathematical concepts, formulas and function spaces that will be employed throughout the manuscript.

Fractional Brownian motion

To start with, a fractional Brownian motion of Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
is a centered Gaussian process
\{B^{H}(t):t\geq 0\}
with the covariance function (see
[
43
, Definition of 5.1, p.273]
)

\displaystyle R_{H}(t,s):=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[B^{H}(t)B^{H}(s)}}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{2H}+t^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}}}\right),

so that
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|B^{H}(t)|^{2}}}\right]=t^{2H}
. It is known that
\{B^{H}(t):t\geq 0\}
admits the so called Volterra representation (for more details, see
[
43
]
),

(2.1)

B^{H}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}K^{H}(t,s)dW(s),

where
\{W(t):t\geq 0\},
is a standard Brownian motion and the Volterra kernel
K^{H}(t,s)
is defined by

(2.2)

\displaystyle K^{H}(t,s)=C_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{t^{H-\frac{1}{2}}}{s^{H-\frac{1}{2}}}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H-\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\int_{s}^{t}\frac{u^{H-\frac{3}{2}}}{s^{H-\frac{1}{2}}}(u-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}du}}\right],\ \ s\leq t,

and
C_{H}
is a constant depending only on the Hurst index
H.
In that case
\{W(t):t\geq 0\}
and
\{B^{H}(t):t\geq 0\}
are dependent processes. We then express the auto-covariance function of the fBM in terms of

(2.3)

\displaystyle R^{H}(t,s)=\int_{0}^{\min(s,t)}K^{H}(t,r)K_{H}(s,r)dr.

By Itô isometry, we also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|B^{H}(t)|^{2}}}\right]=\int_{0}^{t}(K^{H}(t,s))^{2}ds.

The fractional Brownian motion
\{B^{H}(t):t\geq 0\}
is not a semimartingale for
H\neq 1/2
. It is interesting to note though, that the process
\{W(t)+B^{H}(t):t\geq 0\}
, when
H\in(3/4,1)
, is equivalent in law to Brownian motion, cf.
[
5
]
.

Throughout this work, we will use the Banach space
\mathscr{B}^{\gamma,2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,t}}\right],L^{2}(D)}}\right)
, which consists of all measurable functions
u:[0,t]\rightarrow L^{2}(D)
for which the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\gamma,2}
is defined, i.e.,

\|u\|_{\gamma,2}^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\esssup_{s\in[0,t]}\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{2}}}\right)^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\|u(\cdot,s)-u(\cdot,r)\|_{2}}{(s-r)^{\gamma+1}}dr}}\right)^{2}ds<+\infty,

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the usual norm in
L^{2}(D)
, cf.
[
37
,
38
,
54
]
. The requirement that
u\in\mathscr{B}^{\gamma,2}\bigl([0,t],L^{2}(D)\bigr)
for some
\gamma\in(1-H,1/2)

ensures that the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{t}u(s)dB^{H}(s)
exists as a generalized Stieltjes integral in the Young sense (see
[
54
]
and
[
42
, Proposition 1]
).

As far as the mixed processes
\{N_{i}(t):t\geq 0\},\;i=1,2
are concerned, the stochastic integral with respect to
\{B^{H}(t):t\geq 0\}
will be considered in the above pathwise sense, whereas the stochastic integral with respect to
\{W(t):t\geq 0\}
will be considered in the Itô sense, see also
[
32
]
.

Malliavin derivative

Let
\mathscr{S}
denote the space of step functions on the interval
[0,T]
, and define
\mathscr{H}
as the closure of
\mathscr{S}
with respect to the inner product

\langle\mathbf{1}_{[0,s]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}:=R^{H}(s,t),

recalling that
R^{H}(s,t)
denotes the auto-covariance function given by (
2.3
).

We begin by defining the map
\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}\mapsto B_{t}^{H}=B^{H}(t)
on
\mathscr{S}
and then extend it to an isometry from
\mathscr{H}
into the Gaussian space
\mathscr{H}_{1}(B_{t}^{H})
generated by the fractional Brownian motion. This isometry is denoted by
\phi\mapsto B_{t}^{H}(\phi)
.
We now proceed to define the Malliavin derivative
D
with respect to fBM. Let us consider a smooth cylindrical functional of the form
F=f(B_{t}^{H}(\phi))
, where
\phi\in\mathscr{H}
and
f\in C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. The Malliavin derivative
\mathscr{D}F
is an
\mathscr{H}
-valued random variable defined via the duality relation

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{D}F,h\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}:=f^{\prime}(B_{t}^{H}(\phi))\langle\phi,h\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}f\big(B_{t}^{H}(\phi)+\epsilon\langle\phi,h\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}\big)}}\right|_{\epsilon=0},

which can be understood as a generalization of the directional derivative along the paths of fBm.

The operator
\mathscr{D}
is closable as a mapping from
L^{p}(\Omega)
into
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathscr{H})
for any
p\geq 1
, and it allows for the construction of Sobolev-type spaces on the Wiener space. Among these, the space
{\mathbb{D}}^{1,2}
is of particular importance. It is defined as the closure of the set of smooth cylindrical random variables with respect to the norm

\displaystyle\|F\|_{{\mathbb{D}}^{1,2}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}[|F|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\mathscr{D}F\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}]}}\right)^{1/2}.

In our framework, the Malliavin derivative will be interpreted as a stochastic process
\{\mathscr{D}_{t}F:\,t\in[0,T]\}
(see
[
43
]
, Section 1.2.1 and Chapter 5).

Integration by parts, Itô formula and miscellaneous

Next we review the integration by parts formula relevant to stochastic processes. Specifically, if
X(t)
and
Y(t),\ t\in[0,T],\ T>0
are Itô stochastic processes defined by

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=X(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\Psi(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(s)dB^{H}(s),\ \mbox{and}

\displaystyle Y(t)

\displaystyle=Y(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\hat{\Psi}(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\hat{\Phi}(s)dB^{H}(s),

then the following integration by parts formula holds:

(2.4)

\displaystyle X(t)Y(t)=X(0)Y(0)+\int_{0}^{t}X(s)dY(s)+\int_{0}^{t}Y(s)dX(s)+[X(t),Y(t)],\ \ t\in[0,T],

where the last term in (
2.4
) is the quadratic variation of
X(t)
,
Y(t)
and is defined as

\displaystyle[X(t),Y(t)]:=\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(s)\hat{\Phi}(s)ds,

for more details see
[
34
, Page 114]
.

Note that if
H>\frac{1}{2}
in this case there is no quadratic covariation between the processes. One can refer to
[
4
]
for more about integration by parts formula for fBm. In this study, we are examining both mixed Brownian and fBm by using the Itô’s formula as follows:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
38
, Theorem 2.7.2]
)
.

Let the process
X(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}B^{H_{i}}(t),
where
H_{1}=1/2
and
H_{i}\in(1/2,1)
for
2\leq i\leq m,\ \sigma_{i}
are real numbers and the function
F\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}).
Then for any
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle F(X(t))

\displaystyle=F(X(0))+\sigma_{i}\int_{0}^{t}F^{\prime}(X(s))dW(s)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(X(s))dB^{H_{i}}(s)

(2.5)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}F^{\prime\prime}(X(s))ds.

Hence, only the second derivatives will contribute to the Itô formula for standard Brownian and fBm.

Let us take

\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(t):=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}f(s)dW(s),

where
f
is any continuous function. By applying Itô’s formula (
2.5
) to the process
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exp\{\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(t)\}}}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle\exp\{\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(t)\}=1+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\{\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(s)\}d\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(s)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\{\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(s)\}f^{2}(s)ds.

Taking expectation on both sides, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\exp\{\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(t)\})=1+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exp\{\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(s)\}}}\right)f^{2}(s)ds.

Therefore by taking
\bar{\mathscr{Y}}(t):=\mathbb{E}(\exp\{\bar{\mathscr{Z}}(t)\})
, and variation of constants formula yields

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\int_{0}^{t}f(s)dW(s)}}\right\}}}\right)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f^{2}(s)ds}}\right\}.

Robin eigenvalue problem

Let
\chi
be the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta
on
D
, which satisfies

(2.7)

\displaystyle-\Delta\psi(x)=\chi\psi(x),\ \ x\in D,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nu}(x)+\beta\psi(x)=0,\;x\in\partial D,

with
\psi
being the corresponding eigenfunction, normalized so that

\displaystyle\int_{D}\psi(x)dx=1.
Then
\chi>0
and
\psi
is strictly positive on
D
for
\beta>0,
cf.
[
2
, Theorem 4.3]
, and by virtue of Jentsch’s Theorem (see
[
51
, Theorem V.6.6]
), we obtain

(2.9)

S_{t}\psi=\exp\{{-\chi t}\}\psi,t\geq 0,

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S_{t}}}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
stands for the semigroup generated
by the operator
\mathscr{A}=-\Delta_{R},
i.e., the Laplace operator associated with homogeneous
Robin conditions (see e.g.,
[
45
, Chap. IV]
) and domain
D(\mathscr{A})=W^{2,2}(D)\cap W^{1,2}(D).

Local existence

If we set

\displaystyle du_{2}(t,x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Delta z_{2}(t,x)-\lambda_{21}z_{2}^{-2}(t,x)-\lambda_{22}z_{1}^{-2}(t,x)}}\right]dt-z_{2}(t,x)dN_{2}(t),x\in D,\;t>0,

(Weak solutions)

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{21}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}S_{t}g_{2}(x)\mathscr{G}_{2}(t)\leq\mathscr{R}_{2}[W_{1},W_{2}](t,x)\leq\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{11}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}S_{t}g_{2}(x).

Now we consider the iteration scheme

\displaystyle\vartheta^{(0)}_{1}(t,x):=\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{11}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}S_{t}g_{1}(x),\ \ \ \vartheta^{(0)}_{2}(t,x):=\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{12}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}S_{t}g_{2}(x),

\displaystyle\vartheta^{(n)}_{1}(t,x):=\mathscr{R}_{1}[\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{1},\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{2}](t,x),\ \ \vartheta^{(n)}_{2}(t,x):=\mathscr{R}_{2}[\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{1},\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{2}](t,x),\ \ n\geq 1,

for
x\in D,\ 0\leq t<\tau_{*}.
Our aim is to show that the sequences of functions
\{\vartheta^{(n)}_{1}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \mbox{and}\ \{\vartheta^{(n)}_{2}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are decreasing.

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\vartheta^{(0)}_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{11}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}-\lambda_{11}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{k_{11}^{2}}{2}(t-r)+3N_{1}(r)}S_{t-r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta^{(0)}_{1}(r,x)}}\right)^{-2}dr

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda_{12}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{k_{11}^{2}}{2}(t-r)+N_{1}(r)+2N_{2}(r)}S_{t-r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta^{(0)}_{2}(r,x)}}\right)^{-2}dr

\displaystyle=\mathscr{R}_{1}[\vartheta^{(0)}_{1},\vartheta^{(0)}_{2}](t,x)=\vartheta^{(1)}_{1}(t,x).

Similarly

\displaystyle\vartheta^{(0)}_{2}(t,x)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{12}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}-\lambda_{21}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{k_{12}^{2}}{2}(t-r)+3N_{2}(r)}S_{t-r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta^{(0)}_{2}(r,x)}}\right)^{-2}dr

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda_{22}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{k_{12}^{2}}{2}(t-r)+N_{2}(r)+2N_{1}(r)}S_{t-r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta^{(0)}_{1}(r,x)}}\right)^{-2}dr

\displaystyle=\mathscr{R}_{2}[\vartheta^{(0)}_{1},\vartheta^{(0)}_{2}](t,x)=\vartheta^{(1)}_{1}(t,x).

Assuming that
\vartheta^{(n)}_{i}\geq\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{i},\ i=1,2,
for some
n\geq 1,
the monotonicity of
\mathscr{R}_{1},\mathscr{R}_{2}
leads to the inequalities

\displaystyle\vartheta^{(n+1)}_{1}=\mathscr{R}_{1}[\vartheta^{(n)}_{1},\vartheta^{(n)}_{2}](t,x)\geq\mathscr{R}_{1}[\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{1},\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{2}](t,x)=\vartheta^{(n)}_{1},

\displaystyle\vartheta^{(n+1)}_{2}=\mathscr{R}_{2}[\vartheta^{(n)}_{1},\vartheta^{(n)}_{2}](t,x)\geq\mathscr{R}_{2}[\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{1},\vartheta^{(n-1)}_{2}](t,x)=\vartheta^{(n)}_{2},

which by induction implies the monotonicity of the sequences
\{\vartheta^{(n)}_{1}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \mbox{and}\ \{\vartheta^{(n)}_{2}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

Therefore the limits

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{1}(t,x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\vartheta^{(n)}_{1}(t,x),\ \ \tilde{v}_{2}(t,x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\vartheta^{(n)}_{2}(t,x),

exist for
x\in D
and
0\leq t<\tau_{*}.
Then by the monotone convergence theorem for decreasing functions, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{1}(t,x)=\mathscr{R}_{1}[\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2}](t,x),\ \tilde{v}_{2}(t,x)=\mathscr{R}_{2}[\tilde{v}_{2},\tilde{v}_{1}](t,x),\ \ x\in D,\ \ 0\leq t<\tau_{*}.

Since system (
3.6
)–(
3.8
) has a unique solution
v=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\top}
then
\tilde{v}=(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2})^{\top}
coincides with that solution, so we obtain the desired estimates (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), that is

\displaystyle 0<\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{11}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}S_{t}g_{1}(x)\mathscr{G}_{1}(t)\leq v_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{R}_{1}[v_{1},v_{2}](t,x)\leq\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{11}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}S_{t}g_{1}(x)\leq 1\ \text{ and }

\displaystyle 0<\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{12}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}S_{t}g_{2}(x)\mathscr{G}_{2}(t)\leq v_{2}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{R}_{2}[v_{2},v_{1}](t,x)\leq\exp\Big\{{-\frac{k_{12}^{2}}{2}t}\Big\}S_{t}g_{2}(x)\leq 1,

for all
x\in D
and

holds for

Next, we consider again the stochastic process

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma t+\rho_{1}W(t)+\rho_{2}B^{H}(t),

then
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
5.4
for
\gamma=0
and
\delta=1.

Indeed, due to (
5.6
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{Z_{s}}}}\right)ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho_{1}^{2}s-\sigma s-\rho_{2}^{2}s^{2H}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{\rho_{1}W(s)+\rho_{2}B^{H}(s)}}}\right)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sigma s}e^{-\rho_{1}^{2}s+\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}s}{2}}e^{{-\rho_{2}^{2}s^{2H}+\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}s^{2H}}{2}}}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sigma s-\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}s}{2}-\frac{\rho_{2}^{2}s^{2H}}{2}}ds<\infty,

using also (
2.6
). Also for
r\leq t,
we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{r}Z_{t}=\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\mathscr{D}_{r}B^{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\mathscr{D}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{t}K^{H}(t,s)dW(s)}}\right)=\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}K^{H}(t,r).

Furthermore by (
5.1
), we have

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\int_{0}^{s}|\mathscr{D}_{r}Z_{s}|^{2}\leq 2\rho_{1}^{2}t+2\rho_{2}^{2}t^{2H}<\infty.

Note also that
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the condition (iii) of Theorem
5.4
for
f(t)=t^{\alpha},\alpha>H
and
U>0.
In particular,

\displaystyle L_{U}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{2\rho_{1}^{2}t+2\rho_{2}^{2}t^{2H}}{(\ln(U+1)+t^{\alpha})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{2\rho_{1}^{2}t}{(\ln(U+1)+t^{\alpha})^{2}}+\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{2\rho_{2}^{2}t^{2H}}{(\ln(U+1)+t^{\alpha})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}(2\alpha-1)^{2-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\alpha^{2}}\ln(U+1)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-2}+2\rho_{2}^{2}\ln(x+1)^{\frac{2H}{\alpha}-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha-H}{\alpha}}}\right)^{2-\frac{2H}{\alpha}}.

Finally, Theorem
5.4
for
U=\frac{E^{3}(0)}{12\tilde{\lambda}}
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\{3(\chi+k^{2})s+\rho_{1}W(s)+\rho_{2}B^{H}(s)\}ds<\frac{E^{3}(0)}{12\tilde{\lambda}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{-\alpha^{2}(L_{1}(\alpha)-1)^{2}}{\rho_{1}^{2}(2\alpha-1)^{2-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\ln(U+1)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-2}+2\alpha^{2}\rho_{2}^{2}\ln(U+1)^{\frac{2H}{\alpha}-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha-H}{\alpha}}}\right)^{2-\frac{2H}{\alpha}}}}}\right\}:=\mathscr{N}(U,\alpha),

where
L_{1}(\alpha)
is given in (
5.5
).

Hence by (
4.2
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\tau_{q}<\infty}}\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\tau^{*}<\infty}}\right\}

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\{\tau^{*}=\infty\}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathscr{N}(U,\alpha),

thus the desired bound is provided by (
5.5
) is derived. This completes the proof.
∎

5.3.
An interesting special case

In this subsection, we consider the case where
\frac{3}{4}<H<1
, and the processes
\{W(t):t\geq 0\}
and
\{B^{H}(t):t\geq 0\}
are independent, with a common coupling coefficient
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\rho
. In this setting, finite-time quenching occurs almost surely for the weak solution
v=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\top}
of system (
3.6
)–(
3.8
). This result is obtained by adapting the approach in
[
32
, Subsection 5.2]
; see also
[
10
, Subsection 5.1]
and
[
11
, Section 3]
for related analyses.

According to
[
5
, Theorem 1.7]
, the process
M(t):=W(t)+B^{H}(t)
is equivalent in law to a standard Brownian motion
\widetilde{B}(t)
. Here, equivalence refers to the fact that the probability laws of
M
and
\widetilde{B}
coincide on the space
(C[0,T],\mathscr{B})
, where
C[0,T]
is the space of continuous functions on
[0,T]
, and
\mathscr{B}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the cylinder sets.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\frac{3}{4}<H<1
. Then the solution
v=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\top}
to problem (
3.6
)–(
3.8
), as well as the solution
z=(z_{1},z_{2})^{\top}
to (
3.1
)–(
3.4
), quenches in finite time
\tau_{q}<\infty
almost surely.

Proof.

From identity (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\tau^{\ast}=\infty\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{t}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\rho W(s)+\rho B^{H}(s)+\sigma s}}\right\}ds<\frac{E^{3}(0)}{12\tilde{\lambda}},\ \text{for all }t\geq 0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\rho W(s)+\rho B^{H}(s)+\sigma s}}\right\}ds\leq\frac{E^{3}(0)}{12\tilde{\lambda}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\rho\widetilde{B}(s)+\sigma s}}\right\}ds\leq\frac{E^{3}(0)}{12\tilde{\lambda}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\widetilde{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho^{2}s}{4}}}\right)+\sigma s}}\right\}ds\leq\frac{E^{3}(0)}{12\tilde{\lambda}}}}\right),

where the last step uses the scaling property of Brownian motion.

Now, setting the change of variables
t=\frac{\rho^{2}s}{4}
and defining
\nu:=\frac{2\sigma}{\rho^{2}}
, see also
[
10
,
11
,
32
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\tau^{\ast}=\infty\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{4}{\rho^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\widetilde{B}_{t}^{(\nu)}}}\right\}dt\leq\frac{E^{3}(0)}{12\tilde{\lambda}}}}\right)

(5.7)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\widetilde{B}_{t}^{(\nu)}}}\right\}dt\leq\frac{\rho^{2}E^{3}(0)}{48\tilde{\lambda}}}}\right),

where
\widetilde{B}_{t}^{(\nu)}:=\widetilde{B}(t)+\nu t
is a Brownian motion with linear drift.

Since
\nu>0
, the law of the iterated logarithm (cf.
[
3
, Theorem 2.3]
,
[
24
, Theorem 9.23]
) implies

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{B}(t)}{t^{1/2}\sqrt{2\log(\log t)}}

\displaystyle=-1,\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{B}(t)}{t^{1/2}\sqrt{2\log(\log t)}}

\displaystyle=+1,\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Thus, for any sequence
t_{n}\to\infty
, we have the asymptotic behaviour

\widetilde{B}_{t_{n}}\sim\alpha_{n}t_{n}^{1/2}\sqrt{2\log(\log t_{n})},\quad\text{with }\alpha_{n}\in[-1,1],

which implies that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\widetilde{B}_{t}^{(\nu)}}}\right\}dt=\infty.

From (
5.3
), we then conclude that

\mathbb{P}[\tau^{*}=\infty]=0,

and hence

\mathbb{P}[\tau^{*}<\infty]=1.

Since
\tau_{q}<\tau^{*}
, it follows that

\mathbb{P}[\tau_{q}<\infty]=1.

Therefore, the solution to problem (
3.6
)–(
3.8
), as well as the solution to (
3.1
)–(
3.4
), quenches in finite time almost surely.
∎

6.
Numerical solution

6.1.
Finite elements approximation

In this section, we present a numerical investigation of problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) in the one-dimensional setting. To this end, we employ a finite element semi-implicit Euler scheme for the time discretization; see, for example,
[
36
]
.

Our study primarily focuses on homogeneous and nonhomogeneous Robin boundary conditions imposed at the endpoints
x=0
and
x=1
. However, some of the numerical experiments also explore the case of homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, which was not addressed in the analytical treatment provided in the previous sections.

Recall that the noise term considered here is multiplicative and takes the form
\sigma(u_{i})\,dN_{i}(t)
, where
\sigma(u_{i})=1-u_{i}
for
i=1,2
. In particular, we assume that the noise is given by the expression

\sigma(u_{i})\,dN_{i}(t)=k_{i1}(1-u_{i})\,dW(t)+k_{i2}(1-u_{i})\,dB^{H}(t),\;i=1,2,

representing a combination of standard Brownian motion
W(t)
and fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}(t)
, with coefficients
k_{i1},k_{i2},i=1,2
determining the intensity of each component.

We apply
a discretization in
[0,T]\times[0,1]
,
0\leq t\leq T
,
0\leq x\leq 1
with

t_{n}=n\delta t
,
\delta t=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}/{N}}}\right]
for
N
the number of time steps and we also
introduce the grid points in
[0,1]
,
x_{j}=j\delta x
, for
\delta x=1/M
and
j=0,1,\ldots,M
.

Then we proceed with a finite element approximation for problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
).
In particular, let
\Phi_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,M-1,
denote the standard linear
B-
splines on the interval
[0,\,1]

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{y-y_{j-1}}{\delta y},\quad y_{j-1}\leq y\leq y_{j},\\
\frac{y_{j+1}\,-y}{\delta y},\quad y_{j}\leq y\leq y_{j+1},\\
0,\quad\mbox{elsewhere in}\quad[0,\,1],\end{array}}}\right.

for
j=1,2,\ldots,M-1
. We then set

u_{i}(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{a}_{{u_{i}}_{j}}(t)\Phi_{j}(x)
,

t\geq 0
,
0\leq x\leq 1
for
i=1,2.

Substituting the expansion for
u=(u_{1},u_{2})
into equation (
1.1
) and applying the standard Galerkin method, namely, multiplying by the test functions
\Phi_{\ell}
for
\ell=1,2,\ldots,M-1
and integrating over the interval
[0,1],
we obtain a system of equations for the coefficients
a_{{u_{i}}_{j}}
, given by:

(6.5)

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84544pt\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{\dot{a}}_{{u_{i}}_{j}}(t)<\Phi_{j}(x),\,\Phi_{\ell}(x)>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{a}_{{u_{i}}_{j}}(t)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\Phi^{\prime}_{j}(x),\,\Phi^{\prime}_{\ell}(y)}}\right>

\displaystyle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<F_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{a}_{{u_{1}}_{j}}(t)\Phi_{j}(x),\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{a}_{{u_{2}}_{j}}(t)\Phi_{j}(x)}}\right),\,\Phi_{\ell}(x)}}\right>,\quad\quad

\displaystyle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{a}_{{u_{i}}_{j}}(t)\Phi_{j}(x)}}\right)dN_{i}(t),\,\Phi_{\ell}(x)}}\right>,\quad\quad

where
<\cdot,\cdot>
stands for the inner producr in
L^{2}(0,1)
and in our case

F_{i}(s_{1},s_{2})=\frac{\lambda_{i1}}{(1-s_{1})^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{i2}}{(1-s_{2})^{2}}
,
\sigma(s_{i})=(1-s_{i})
,
i=1,2
.

Setting
a_{u_{i}}=[a_{{u_{i}}_{1}},\,a_{{u_{i}}_{2}},\ldots,a_{{u_{i}}_{M-1}}]^{T}

the system of equations for the coefficients column vector
{a_{u_{i}}}
takes the form

\displaystyle A_{i}{\dot{a}_{u_{i}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-B_{i}a_{u_{i}}(t)+b_{i}(t)+{b_{i}}_{s}(t),

for

(6.6)

\displaystyle b_{i}({t}):=b_{i}(u_{1},u_{2})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<F_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{a}_{{u_{i}}_{j}}(t)\Phi_{j}(x),\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{a}_{{u_{i}}_{j}}(t)\Phi_{j}(x)}}\right),\,\Phi_{\ell}(x),}}\right>}}\right\},

(6.7)

\displaystyle{b}_{is}(t):={b_{i}}({u_{i}},\,\Delta{N_{i}}(t))=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}{a}_{{u_{i}}_{j}}(t)\Phi_{j}(x)}}\right)\Delta N_{i}(t),\,\Phi_{\ell}(x)}}\right>}}\right\},

with the
{(M-1)\times(M-1)}
matrices
A_{i},B_{i}
having the standard form (see
[
10
]
).

The latter term comes from the corresponding form of the noise
N_{i},i=1,2
(see (
1.5
)) and
d{N_{i}}(t)\simeq\Delta{N_{i}}_{h}(t)={N_{i}}_{h}(t+\delta t)-{N_{i}}_{h}(t)
for
{N_{i}}_{h}(t)
the finite sum giving the discrete approximation of
{N_{i}}(t)
,
i=1,2
. Moreover, we approximate the white noise by the method followed in
[
10
]
(see also
[
11
,
32
,
36
]
,)

We then apply a semi-implicit Euler method in time by taking

A_{i}{\dot{a}_{u_{i}}(t_{n})}\simeq A_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({a_{u_{i}}^{n+1}-a_{u_{i}}^{n}}}}\right)/({\delta t})=-B_{i}a_{u_{i}}^{n+1}+b_{i}({u_{1}^{n},u_{2}^{n}})+{b_{is}}(u_{i}^{n},\,\Delta{N_{i}}_{h}^{n}),

or

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{i}+\delta tB_{i}}}\right)a_{u_{i}}^{n+1}=a_{u_{i}}^{n}+\delta t\,b_{i}({u_{1}^{n},u_{2}^{n}})+\delta t\,{b_{i}}_{s}({u_{i}}^{n},\,\Delta{N_{i}}_{h}^{n}).

Finally, the corresponding algebraic system for the
a_{u_{i}}^{n}
’s after some manipulation becomes

\displaystyle a_{u_{1}}^{n+1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{1}+\delta tB_{1}}}\right)^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a_{u_{1}}^{n}+\delta t\,b_{i}(u_{1}^{n},u_{2}^{n})+\delta t\,{b_{i}}_{s}(u_{1}^{n})}}\right],

\displaystyle a_{u_{2}}^{n+1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{2}+\delta tB_{2}}}\right)^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a_{u_{2}}^{n}+\delta t\,b_{i}(u_{1}^{n},u_{2}^{n})+\delta t\,{b_{i}}_{s}(u_{2}^{n})}}\right],

for
a_{u}^{1}=(a_{u_{1}}^{1},a_{u_{2}}^{1})
being determined by the initial conditions.
