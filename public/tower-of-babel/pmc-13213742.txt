Effects of different thermal processing methods on the flavor and precursor substances of the longissimus dorsi muscle in the “Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire” ternary hybrid pigs
Authors: Xinran Yuan, Jialing Tang, Yulin Zhang, Zhengfeng Fang, Yong Yang, Guoquan Han, Teng Hui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of boiling, frying, and roasting on flavor compounds and their precursors in the longissimus dorsi muscle of “Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire” pigs. HS-SPME-GC–MS, electronic nose analysis, and multivariate statistical methods (PCA, PLS-DA, and PLS-R) were used to characterize changes in free fatty acids (FFAs), free amino acids (FAAs), reducing sugars, and volatile compounds. Frying resulted in the highest total volatile content, whereas roasting was characterized by higher levels of several Maillard-derived compounds, including phenylacetaldehyde and pyrazines. Boiling showed relatively higher levels of oxidation-derived alcohols such as 1-octen-3-ol. Correlation analysis and PLS-R further revealed significant associations between specific precursor substances and key odor-active volatiles. These results provide deeper insight into the biochemical basis of processing-dependent flavor formation in ternary hybrid pork and may support the optimization of thermal processing for improved flavor quality.

Introduction

China is the largest producer and consumer of pork globally, with per capita pork consumption reaching 40.76 kg. The ternary hybrid pigs “Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire” are highly favored by pig farmers and consumers due to their rapid growth rate, robust disease resistance, high vitality, exceptional feed conversion efficiency, and a high lean meat ratio. At present, they are widely cultivated in China's commercial pork market.

Pork flavor is a key determinant of consumer preference and is strongly affected by thermal processing. During heating, meat flavor is mainly generated through lipid oxidation, Maillard reactions, and thiamine degradation (Wang et al., 2023). Free fatty acids (FFAs) released by lipolysis are susceptible to oxidation and can generate aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols with important contributions to aroma (Tan et al., 2024). In particular, the oxidation of abundant monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in meat can produce a wide range of volatile compounds (Wang, Hou, et al., 2025). In addition, free amino acids (FAAs) and reducing sugars are important precursors of Maillard and Strecker reactions, leading to the formation of heterocyclic and nitrogen-containing odorants such as pyrazines and pyrroles (Merlo et al., 2021, Shakoor, Zhang, Xie and Yang, 2022).

Boiling, frying, and roasting are among the most common cooking methods used for pork in China, and each method creates a distinct thermal environment that can markedly influence precursor transformation and aroma formation. Previous studies have shown that cooking conditions can alter lipid oxidation, protein degradation, Maillard reaction intensity, and the resulting volatile profile of meat (Lu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). However, systematic comparisons of these three common thermal processing methods with respect to both precursor substances and volatile compounds remain limited for the longissimus dorsi muscle of the widely used “Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire” ternary hybrid pigs.

Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effects of boiling, frying, and roasting on free fatty acids, free amino acids, reducing sugars, volatile flavor compounds, and odor characteristics in the longissimus dorsi muscle of “Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire” ternary hybrid pigs. In addition, multivariate statistical analysis and electronic nose profiling were used to explore the relationships between precursor substances and volatile compounds under different thermal processing conditions.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Six castrated male Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire pigs were jointly provided by the Animal Nutrition Research Institute of Sichuan Agricultural University and Chengdu Jiajia Mei Food Co., Ltd. The pigs were reared under standardized commercial feeding and management conditions until they reached market weight (140.75 ± 4.02 kg). They were subsequently transported to a slaughterhouse near the farm and rested for 12 h before slaughter. Slaughter was performed by trained slaughterhouse staff, using electrical stunning followed by exsanguination in accordance with standard commercial practices. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Animal Welfare Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University and the National Research Council Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (2011) (Approval No. 20230362, 2023.08.30). Following a 24-h aging period at a temperature of 4 °C and a relative humidity of 87%, the longissimus dorsi muscle from the 6th to 13th ribs on the right side of the carcass was collected. The visible surface fat and connective tissue were meticulously excised. The muscle was transported to the laboratory via cold chain logistics within 1 h and vacuum-packed for subsequent experiments.

Solvents and reagents used included hexane, anhydrous ethanol, methanol, diethyl ether, chloroform, glacial acetic acid and anhydrous sodium sulfate (analytical reagent grade; Chengdu Kolon Chemical Co., Ltd.). n-Alkanes (C9–C31, 98%) and undecanoic acid glycerol triacylglyceride (≥99%) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. 2-Methyl-3-heptanone (≥99%) and boron trifluoride-methanol (12.02 g/L) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Solid phase microextraction (SPME) fibers (65 μm PDMS/DVB) were from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai). All aqueous solutions were prepared with Milli-Q water.

Instruments and equipment

Volatile analyses were performed using a Clarus 690-SQ8T GC–MS system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA) and a QP2010 SE GC–MS system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), equipped with Elite-5 MS and DB-WAX capillary columns (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm), respectively. Liquid chromatography analyses (FAA, reducing sugars) were performed using an Agilent 1260 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) and a Shimadzu LC-16 system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Free amino acids were quantified using an L-8900 automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Odor profiling was performed using a CNose-18 portable electronic nose (Shanghai Baosheng Industrial Development Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Additional equipment included a freeze-dryer, rotary evaporator, vortex mixer, ultrasonic bath, and analytical balance (FA1204C).

Samples preparation

Longissimus dorsi samples were trimmed of visible fat and connective tissue and standardized for processing. For boiling (BO), muscle pieces (2 × 2 × 2 cm, ≈10 g each) were placed in water at a meat: water ratio of 1:10 and heated on an induction cooker; after the water reached a rolling boil and brief degassing, samples were cooked until their internal temperature exceeded 80 °C and then cooled to room temperature prior to analyses. For frying (FY), samples were cut to 2 × 2 × 0.5 cm and pan-fried; samples were flipped periodically during frying and heated until the internal temperature exceeded 80 °C. For roasting (RO), 2 × 2 × 2 cm pieces were roasted in a preheated oven with top-bottom heating at 200 °C for 25 min; internal temperature was confirmed to exceed 80 °C. All thermal treatments were performed on subsamples from the same carcasses to ensure comparability. After processing, samples were cooled, vacuum-packed and stored at −20 °C until analysis.

Determination of free fatty acids (FFA)

Lipids were extracted according to Sun et al. (Sun et al., 2024) with minor modifications. Briefly, processed meat samples were homogenized, freeze-dried, and 10 g of the dried sample was mixed with 150 mL of chloroform/methanol (2,1, v/v). After thorough mixing, the extract was filtered and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure to obtain the total lipid fraction. The extracted lipids were then subjected to GC–MS analysis.

An aliquot of 1 μL of the sample was injected into the GC–MS system. Helium was used as the carrier gas with a split ratio of 1:20. The transfer line and ion source temperatures were 250 °C and 230 °C, respectively. The solvent delay was set at 3 min, and the mass scan range was m/z 45–450. The oven temperature program started at 120 °C for 2 min, increased to 200 °C at 4 °C/min and held for 2 min, then increased to 240 °C at 2 °C/min and held for 2 min. Fatty acids were identified by comparison of retention indices and mass spectra with standards and databases, and quantified using the internal standard normalization method.

Determination of free amino acids (FAA)

Approximately 2.0 g of the sample was homogenized and deproteinized with 0.02 mol/L HCl. Samples were sonicated, centrifuged (6000 rpm, 5 min) and the supernatant was collected. Solid-phase extraction on C18 cartridges was applied for cleanup; eluates were dried under vacuum (60 °C) and derivatized prior to analysis. Derivatization reagent was freshly prepared (ethanol: benzenethiol: water: triethylamine, 7:1:1:1, v/v) and samples were derivatized at ambient temperature for 30 min. After dilution with mobile phase A and filtration through a 0.45 μm organic membrane, analytes were separated on an Agilent 1260 LC with a C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1 mol/L sodium acetate: acetonitrile (97,3, v/v) adjusted to pH 6.5; mobile phase B was acetonitrile: water (80,20, v/v). The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, column temperature 40 °C, injection volume 10 μL and detection at 254 nm, and the gradient elution program is detailed in Table S-1 of the supplementary information. Calibration curves were constructed using mixed amino acid standards (5–100 mg/L) and recovery tests were performed.

Determination of reducing sugars

A quantity of 5 g of the cooked sample was extracted with 20 mL water in a boiling water bath for 30 min and sonicated for 10 min. The extract was cleaned by hexane washing and precipitation with potassium ferrocyanide (106 g/L) and zinc acetate (220 g/L) as described. After centrifugation and filtration (0.45 μm), aliquots were derivatized with PMP (0.5 mol/L) under alkaline conditions (70 °C, 60 min), neutralized with HCl, dried under nitrogen and reconstituted in water. Chloroform washes were performed to remove nonpolar impurities. The aqueous phase was filtered (0.45 μm) and analyzed by HPLC (Shimadzu LC-16) on an Agilent C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). Mobile phase A was 20 mmol/L KH2PO4 (pH 7.8 ± 0.1, adjusted with triethylamine) and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, column temperature 35 °C, injection volume 10 μL and detection at 250 nm, and the gradient elution program is outlined in Table S-2. Standard curves (5–500 μg/mL) and recovery rates were determined.

Electronic nose measurement

The instrument under consideration is composed of 18 electronic sensing probes, which correspond to sensitive substances as demonstrated in the Supplementary Information, specifically in Table S-3. The e-Nose measurement method was adapted from the approach outlined by Hui et al. (Hui et al., 2025) with certain modifications. Approximately 2.0 g of processed sample was placed into a 20 mL sealed headspace vial and equilibrated at 25 °C for 1 h. Headspace gas (1 L/min) was sampled for 1 min and sensor responses (G/G0) were recorded at 1 Hz; G represented sensor conductivity exposed to sample headspace and G0 represented baseline conductivity in activated carbon-filtered air. After each run the instrument was purged with clean air for 3 min to recondition sensors. Based on preliminary tests, steady-state sensor responses were reached after ∼45 s; therefore, response values at 48–52 s were averaged and exported for multivariate analysis using XLSTAT 2019 (Addinsoft Company, USA).

Determination of volatile flavor compounds

Extraction of volatile flavor compounds

Volatile compounds were extracted by HS-SPME according to Hui et al. (Hui et al., 2023) with slight modifications. Ground samples (2.0 g) were placed into 20 mL headspace vials and 1.50 μL of 2-methyl-3-heptanone (1.68 μg/μL in methanol) was added as internal standard. Vials were equilibrated at 50 °C for 15 min, and volatiles were adsorbed onto a 65 μm PDMS/DVB fiber for 45 min. The fiber was desorbed in the GC injector at 250 °C for 3 min. GC–MS analysis was performed on a Shimadzu QP2010 SE with DB-WAX column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Injector temperature was 250 °C; carrier gas (He) flow was 1.0 mL/min; splitless injection was used. The oven program started at 40 °C (3 min), ramped at 2 °C/min to 70 °C, then at 3 °C/min to 130 °C, and finally at 10 °C/min to 230 °C (hold 10 min). Mass spectra were recorded at 70 eV in electron ionization mode, scan range 40–500 m/z; ion source temperature was 200 °C and transfer line 250 °C. Volatile compounds were identified by comparison with NIST libraries and literature LRIs; semi-quantification was performed using the internal standard method and results were reported as mean ± SD.

Calculation of linear retention indices

The linear retention indices (LRI) were determined using a mixture of C9 ∼ C31 n-alkanes as external standard substances. The samples were then subjected to GC–MS analysis, which involved the use of a temperature program to elute the desired compounds. The retention indices of volatile flavor compounds were subsequently calculated based on the peak retention times of the n-alkane mixture. The retention index formula is as follows: LRI is expressed as a function of n, the number of carbon atoms, and tx, tn, and tn+1, the peak retention times of the unknown volatile flavor compounds, Cn and Cn+1, respectively. The expression is given as 100n + 100(tx– tn) / (tn+1– tn). The three compounds exhibit the following peak retention times: tn < tx < tn+1 (Hui et al., 2025). Quantitative calculations of volatile compounds were performed using the internal standard method, with results reported as the mean ± standard error.

Calculation of Odor activity values

The odor activity value (OAV) of volatile compounds is indicative of their contribution to flavor. The calculation of the ratio of the concentration of volatile compounds to their respective odor thresholds (Ci/Ti) is a fundamental step in the analysis (Jiang et al., 2022). For the volatile compound “i”, the concentration (Ci) is expressed in micrograms per cubic meter (μg/m3), and the threshold (Ti) is defined as the concentration at which no observable effects are perceived (μg/m3). The threshold data sources can be accessed at https://www.vcf-online.nl/OFTVCompoundSearch.cfm. The OAV is a measure of the influence of volatile chemicals on overall flavor characteristics. A higher OAV indicates a more pronounced contribution of these compounds to the overall flavor profile. Compounds with OAV ≥ 1 were considered likely contributors to aroma.

Statistical analysis

The biological replicate was the individual animal (n = 6). For each pig, the longissimus dorsi muscle was subdivided and subjected to the three cooking treatments (boiling, frying, and roasting). For each treatment, technical subsamples were prepared and analytical measurements were performed in replicate, and the averaged value for each animal under each treatment was used for statistical analysis.

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences among thermal processing treatments were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). When significant effects were detected, multiple comparisons were conducted, and different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among groups at P < 0.05.

Electronic nose data were analyzed using discriminant analysis (DA) in XLSTAT 2019 (Addinsoft, USA), with BO, FY, and RO as treatment groups and sensor responses (S1–S18) as explanatory variables. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied separately to free fatty acid and free amino acid datasets to visualize group separation and identify the major contributors to variation. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was further used to identify variables responsible for discrimination among treatments, and compounds with variable importance in projection (VIP) values greater than 1 were considered important discriminatory markers. A PLS-DA model was also used to analyze volatile flavor compounds among the different thermal processing groups. Correlation analysis between precursor substances and volatile compounds was performed using bivariate correlation analysis in SPSS 23.0 and visualized as heatmaps in Origin 2021. Partial least squares regression (PLS-R) was subsequently used to further evaluate the associations between precursor substances (X variables) and volatile flavor compounds (Y variables).

Compared with the BO group, both FY and RO showed significantly higher levels of total SFA, MUFA, PUFA, ∑n-6, and ∑n-3 (P < 0.05), with C16:0 and C18:0 being the predominant SFAs. In contrast, the BO group exhibited the lowest total FFA levels, which might be attributed to the partial dissolution of hydrolyzed fatty acids into the cooking water or a lower degree of lipid mobilization compared to high-temperature frying and roasting(Yu et al., 2021). The higher SFA levels observed in FY and RO may reflect differences in lipid hydrolysis, oxidation, and thermal degradation under more intense heating conditions. In addition, changes in the relative proportion of SFAs may also be associated with the preferential oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids (UFA) during thermal processing. Consequently, variations in the SFA ratio may also indicate the degree of lipid oxidation (Yu et al., 2020). The varying thermal environments appeared to influence the n-6/n-3 ratio, with FY and RO showing lower ratios than BO, a finding that aligns with previous reports suggesting that cooking intensity modulates fatty acid profiles (Harris et al., 2021, Rao et al., 2022).

To further characterize these differences, PCA was performed (Fig. 1a, b) to reduce the dimensionality of the FFA data. PC1 and PC2 explained 57.01% and 17.59% of the total variance, respectively, indicating that most of the variation in the dataset could be captured in a two-dimensional space. The samples clustered distinctly by treatment, although the FY and RO groups exhibited closer proximity to each other than to the BO group. A PLS-DA model (R2X = 0.569, R2Y = 0.465, Q2 = 0.795) further identified the major FFA variables responsible for discrimination among treatments (Fig. 1c, d), including C18:0, C16:0, C18:1n9c, and C18:2n6c, as significant factors in treatment discrimination. Effect of cooking methods on the free amino acids

During thermal processing, proteins are broken down into various FAA under high-temperature conditions. The types and content of these amino acids play a crucial role in determining the nutritional value, flavor, and taste of meat products. The standard curve and spike recovery are shown in Table S-4. The FAA profiles of processed pork are detailed in Table 2, covering 7 essential (EAAs) and 9 non-essential amino acids (NEAAs). Similar trends were observed for EAA and NEAA contents. In general, the contents of nearly all FAAs were significantly higher in the FY and RO groups than in the BO group (P < 0.05), indicating that frying and roasting promoted a greater release of amino acids from muscle proteins. This phenomenon is likely linked to the higher temperatures employed in frying and roasting, which may more effectively disrupt the protein structure and promote the cleavage of peptide bonds. Furthermore, the comparatively low FAA content in the BO group may be related to the leaching of water-soluble amino acids into the cooking medium (Fan et al., 2021). Additionally, the differences in FAA content caused by different processing methods also provide potential for variations in the concentrations of different volatile flavor compounds. Effect of cooking methods on reducing sugars

The reducing sugars in pork are primarily glucose and ribose, with ribose being particularly significant. These sugars can influence the flavor and other edible qualities of meat and meat products through the Maillard reaction and amino acid dehydration condensation reaction. As evidenced by the standard curve and recovery data (Table S-5), the analytical method provided reliable quantification. The contents of reducing sugars are presented in Table 3. Glucose content differed significantly among the three treatments (P < 0.05), with the lowest level observed in BO (0.20 mg/g), followed by RO (0.53 mg/g), and the highest level in FY (0.76 mg/g). Ribose content was also significantly lower in BO than in FY and RO, whereas no significant difference was observed between FY and RO. The lower levels of reducing sugars in boiled pork may be attributed to the migration of water-soluble sugars into the cooking water during heating (Zuo et al., 2026). Effect of cooking methods on Odor

E-Nose sensors provide benefits, including elevated sensitivity and excellent selectivity, rendering them proficient in differentiating meat flavors. The E-nose DA model effectively reflected the global odor differences among the ternary hybrid pork samples (Fig. 3a). The first two discriminant factors, F1 (85.38%) and F2 (14.62%), together explained all of the variance in the discriminant space, indicating that the two-dimensional plot adequately summarized the differences among the sample groups. A clear separation was observed between the FY group and the others (P < 0.05), while the BO and RO groups also occupied distinct regions. This indicates that the intrinsic odor characteristics of ternary hybrid pigs vary significantly under different processing methods, which is consistent with the findings of Peñaranda et al. (Peñaranda et al., 2017).Fig. 3Effects of different thermal processing methods on the odor of ternary hybrid pork.Fig. 3

Effects of different thermal processing methods on the odor of ternary hybrid pork.

Sensor response analysis (Fig. 3b) indicated that the BO group was more closely associated with sensors responsive to alcohol-related volatiles, consistent with the relatively high levels of 1-octen-3-ol and related lipid oxidation products. Conversely, roasted samples were more closely associated with sensors responsive to nitrogen- and sulfur-containing compounds, in line with the increased formation of Maillard-derived compounds such as phenylacetaldehyde and pyrazines, aligning with the findings of Hui et al. (Hui et al., 2023). FY showed strong associations with several sensors related to aldehydes, alcohols, and hydrocarbon-related compounds, reflecting its high total volatile content (Wei et al., 2024). The e-Nose demonstrated notable variations in the scents of the “Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire” ternary hybrid pigs subjected to varied thermal treatments, together with the primary chemical types accountable for these discrepancies.

Aldehydes have relatively high concentrations of volatile compounds in pork and relatively low odor thresholds, which significantly influence the overall flavor of pork (Zhang et al., 2024). The main volatile compounds produced by lipid oxidation are aldehydes, such as hexanal, butanal, and so on (Lee et al., 2025). Their total content was significantly higher in FY than in BO and RO (P < 0.05). Hexanal, nonanal, and valeraldehyde were among the major aldehydes detected. Notably, hexanal was highest in BO, nonanal was highest in FY, and benzaldehyde and phenylacetaldehyde were especially abundant in RO. These differences suggest that although lipid oxidation occurred under all treatments, the specific aldehyde profile was strongly affected by the thermal environment (Mu et al., 2024).

Alcohols rank as the second most abundant volatile flavor compounds detected in this study, typically formed through lipid oxidation, the Maillard reaction, and the oxidation of SFA (Kong et al., 2024). Pentanol and 1-octen-3-ol are the most important alcohols in this study, accounting for over 60% of total alcohols across all three processing groups. 1-octen-3-ol is a common unsaturated alcohol in processed pork, characterized by a mushroom-like scent, primarily formed by the oxidation of C18:2n6c. Its odor threshold is relatively low, and it significantly influences the overall flavor of pork products (Wang, Li, et al., 2025). These results suggest that frying favored the overall formation of alcohols, whereas boiling retained relatively higher levels of some oxidation-related alcohol compounds. Alcohols represented the second major class of volatile compounds, with total alcohol content following the order FY > BO > RO (P < 0.05). Among these compounds, 1-octen-3-ol was one of the most abundant and was significantly higher in BO and FY than in RO. As a typical lipid oxidation-derived alcohol, 1-octen-3-ol may contribute to the characteristic mushroom-like aroma of boiled and fried pork (Kim & Kim, 2005).These results suggest that frying favored the overall formation of alcohols, whereas boiling retained relatively higher levels of some oxidation-related alcohol compounds.

Ketone compounds are produced through lipid oxidation, the Maillard reaction, and amino acid degradation and play a significant role in contributing to the unique aromatic characteristics of meat products. These compounds serve as important precursors for the formation of pyrazine compounds (Lin, Su, et al., 2024). 3-hydroxybutan-2-one was only detected in the fried and roasted meat products. 3-hydroxybutan-2-one is an important intermediate product and flavor compound of the Maillard reaction. The lower reaction temperature and high-moisture environment during boiling inhibited the Maillard reaction, greatly reducing the synthesis of 3-hydroxy-2-butanone in the samples (Xie et al., 2022). In the pork products, the content of 3-octen-2-one was markedly higher in the RO group than in the BO and FY groups, while the content of 2,5,5-trimethylhexan-3-one and 2,6-dimethyl-4-heptanone was the opposite. There was no significant difference in the total ketone content among the three groups, although individual ketones varied markedly, indicating that the formation of ketone compounds still varies under different thermal processing methods.

Esters have a low odor threshold, pleasant fruity or wine-like aromas, thereby enhancing the overall flavor quality of pork (Xu et al., 2022). They are the primary source of characteristic flavor in pork, and their formation is primarily associated with the Maillard reaction (Long et al., 2025). No substantial differences in total ester concentration were observed between the FY and RO pork groups. However, varying thermal processing conditions resulted in notable disparities in the concentrations of ethyl nitrite and octanoic acid ester among the three groups of meat products, further suggesting that the intensity of the Maillard reaction varied among the three raw meat groups during cooking.

Acidic compounds are predominantly generated via the hydrolysis of triglycerides and phospholipids during heating, and may additionally arise from the subsequent oxidation of alcohols and aldehydes (Lin et al., 2024). These substances are not only flavor compounds but also precursors to ketones and esters. Acids often produce unpleasant odors, such as sweat-like and pungent smells. In this study, eight acid compounds were detected, total acid content was markedly elevated in FY, mainly due to increased levels of butyric acid and hexanoic acid.

Alkanes primarily originate from the decomposition of fatty acids and have a relatively high odor threshold, thus contributing minimally to the overall aroma (Wang, Wu, et al., 2025). The OAV contribution diagram in Fig. 5 indicates that these chemicals do not substantially affect pork flavor. In thermal processing methods such as boiling and frying, the variety of alkanes is greater than that found in roasting, with overall concentrations ranked as FY > RO > BO. The concentrations of 2-methylundecane and pentadecane during the frying treatment were markedly elevated compared to the other two groups, leading to a total alkane content much greater than that of the other groups, corroborating the findings from the e-Nose analysis.

Pyrazines are heterocyclic compounds with a unique aroma, unique volatile compounds formed after the Maillard reaction, possessing nutty or baked aromas, with a low odor threshold, and contributing to the aroma of baked meat products (Chen et al., 2024). Most pyrazines are derived from the Strecker reaction between amino acids and α-carboxylic compounds, which significantly enhance the sensory properties of meat products (Deng et al., 2025). Furthermore, pyrazine compounds, such as 2,6-dimethylpyrazine, were detected only in the FY and RO groups. These heterocyclic compounds, formed through Strecker degradation and Maillard reactions, provide the nutty and baked notes essential to roasted meat flavor (Park & Cho, 2025). The absence of pyrazines in boiled pork is consistent with its lower processing temperature, which appears to inhibit the Maillard reaction pathway (Zhang, 2020).

In addition, several Maillard- and Strecker-related compounds were detected predominantly in FY and RO. Phenylacetaldehyde was absent in BO but increased substantially in RO, and pyrazines such as 2,6-dimethylpyrazine and 3-ethyl-2,5-dimethylpyrazine were detected only in FY and/or RO. These results indicate that boiling mainly favored the formation of lipid oxidation-related volatiles, whereas frying and especially roasting promoted the generation of Maillard-derived aroma compounds.

Significant distinctions in pork products resulting from various thermal processing methods

This study utilized the Variable Importance Projection (VIP) and loadings of the PLS-DA model to reveal the differences in volatile flavor compounds under three different thermal processing methods: boiling, frying, and roasting. VIP reflects the importance of each independent variable by calculating its contribution to the PLS-DA model; the higher the VIP value, the greater the contribution of the independent variable to the dependent variable. After identifying the key flavor compounds corresponding to the VIP> 1, the selected flavor compounds were marked again in the PLS-DA model to identify the specific differences between meat products in different groups.

To further identify the volatile compounds responsible for discrimination among cooking treatments, a PLS-DA model (R2X = 0.487, R2Y = 0.496, Q2 = 0.960) was established based on the volatile dataset (Fig. 4). According to the VIP results, 29 volatile compounds with VIP > 1 were identified as the major discriminatory markers among treatments. These compounds mainly included aldehydes, alcohols, acids, and Maillard-related heterocyclic compounds. BO was characterized mainly by lipid oxidation-related volatiles (Zhou et al., 2025), whereas FY and RO were more strongly associated with the combined effects of lipid oxidation and Maillard reactions (Chang et al., 2020). Effect of different thermal processing methods on OAV values of pork products

As shown in the OAV contribution diagram (Fig. 5), hexanal, (2E, 4E)-2,4-nonadienal, and 1-octen-3-ol were major flavor contributors in all groups. These odor-active compounds mainly included aldehydes and alcohols derived from lipid oxidation, as well as several Maillard-related compounds in the high-temperature treatment groups. In general, the OAV profiles differed markedly among BO, FY, and RO, indicating that the sensory impact of volatile compounds depended not only on their concentration but also on their odor thresholds. Boiled pork was characterized mainly by odor-active lipid oxidation products, whereas fried and roasted pork showed a greater contribution from thermally generated aroma compounds. In particular, roasting exhibited a stronger contribution from Maillard- and Strecker-related compounds, which likely contributed to its more roasted and complex aroma characteristics. Correlation analysis between flavor precursors and volatile flavor compounds

To further explore the relationships between precursor substances and volatile flavor compounds, correlation heatmaps and a PLS-R model were constructed (Fig. 6, Fig. 7). The PLS-R model showed an overall relationship between precursor variables and volatile compounds, with model parameters of R2X = 0.677, R2Y = 0.200, and Q2 = 0.456, suggesting an acceptable explanatory capacity for the association analysis. The correlation analysis indicated that several aldehydes and alcohols were closely associated with major fatty acid precursors, particularly C16:0, C18:0, C18:1n9c, and C18:2n6c, supporting the important role of lipid oxidation in flavor generation (Wei et al., 2023). In addition, several nitrogen-containing and aromatic compounds, including phenylacetaldehyde and pyrazine-related compounds, showed associations with amino acid precursors such as phenylalanine, leucine, isoleucine, and valine, suggesting the involvement of Strecker degradation and Maillard reactions in the formation of these odorants (Li et al., 2025). Reducing sugars, especially glucose, also contributed to the differentiation of thermally generated flavor compounds. Overall, these results indicate that thermal processing modifies both precursor availability and volatile formation pathways, thereby shaping the characteristic flavor profiles of boiled, fried, and roasted ternary hybrid pork.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that boiling, frying, and roasting exerted markedly different effects on precursor substances and volatile flavor compounds in the longissimus dorsi muscle of “Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire” pigs. Frying significantly increased the total contents of FFAs, FAAs, reducing sugars, and volatile compounds, indicating a strong promoting effect on flavor generation. Roasting was characterized by a greater accumulation of several Maillard- and Strecker-related compounds, including phenylacetaldehyde and pyrazines, whereas boiling retained relatively higher levels of lipid oxidation-derived alcohols such as 1-octen-3-ol. Multivariate analysis and correlation modeling further suggested close associations between specific fatty acids, amino acids, reducing sugars, and key volatile compounds. These findings provide useful insight into the biochemical basis of flavor formation in ternary hybrid pork under different thermal processing conditions and may support the optimization of cooking strategies for desirable flavor quality.
