Integrated multiomics reveals host-virus coadaptation in persistent foot-and-mouth disease virus infection
Authors: Shuang Wang, Zhihui Zhang, Sumin Wei, Guoliang Huang, Shuanghui Yin, Suyu Mu, Hu Dong, Shiqi Sun, Huichen Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A comprehensive understanding of host-virus interactions during persistent foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) infection is essential for elucidating the mechanisms that underpin disease causation by this highly contagious pathogen. This understanding necessitates the development of stable in vitro models. In this study, we established a model of persistent FMDV serotype O infection in Madin-Darby bovine kidney epithelial cells followed by integrated multiomics analyses. These analyses revealed that host cells adapt to persistent viral infection by reprogramming mitochondrial metabolism. This reprogramming is accompanied by alterations in mitochondrial structure and function, as well as the suppression of the apoptotic response in host cells. In particular, bystander cells, which are devoid of active viral replication, display enhanced proliferative capacity and possess a distinct microenvironmental signature that potentially increases viral susceptibility, facilitating sustained virus persistence within the cell population. Moreover, persistent viruses have evolved enhanced replicative fitness. Our findings elucidate the biological characteristics of FMDV-infected cell populations that persistently harbour the virus, reveal host-virus coadaptation, and highlight the critical role of bystander cells in sustaining persistent FMDV infection. These discoveries establish the foundation for further mechanistic studies of FMDV persistence maintenance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05997-y.

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease, caused by foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), is a highly contagious condition of cloven-hoofed animals that has profound economic impacts on the global livestock industry. FMDV is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus within the Picornaviridae family genus Aphthovirus and is associated with persistent infection in ruminants, including cattle and African buffalo [1, 2]. Following clinical recovery, these animals may carry FMDV long term and may shed the virus intermittently from specific sites, including the nasopharynx and oropharynx [3, 4]. This persistent infection represents a significant reservoir for FMDV transmission and poses a major challenge for prevention and control strategies. Although the host immune response to acute FMDV infection has been characterized extensively [5–7], the molecular mechanisms that underlie viral persistence, particularly how FMDV evades host immune clearance to persist long-term within specific cellular niches, remain incompletely understood.

The interaction between viruses and host cell metabolism is a key facet of pathogenesis research. Diverse viruses hijack host cell metabolic pathways, particularly mitochondrial functions to facilitate replication, immune evasion, and the establishment of persistent infections [8, 9]. Mitochondria serve as the primary site for cellular energy production (ATP) and are central to innate immune signaling, redox balance, apoptosis regulation, and biosynthetic pathways [10]. For example, hepatitis B virus (HBV) targets mitochondria to induce metabolic reprogramming in host cells, which enables the virus to sequester essential biomacromolecules and energy for replication while suppressing antiviral responses, thereby facilitating persistent infection [11–13]. Acute FMDV infection induces mitochondrial damage and dysfunction [14, 15]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which FMDV regulates mitochondrial function within persistently infected cells, as well as the specific role of this regulation in sustaining viral persistence, remain incompletely understood.

Accumulating evidence suggests that persistent infection foci in multiple viral models, e.g., HBV, human immunodeficiency virus, and lymphocytic choriomeningitis mammarenavirus, constitute complex ecosystems that include not only productively infected and latently infected cells but also uninfected bystander cells that exhibit functional alterations [16–18]. Bystander cells are neighboring cells that, although uninfected or harboring minimal or defective viral genomes, undergo significant phenotypic and functional changes, e.g., cytokines, viral proteins, and damage-associated molecular patterns, in response to the infection microenvironment. Bystander cells actively promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment that supports viral persistence through mechanisms that include the secretion of immunomodulatory factors, e.g., anti-inflammatory cytokines and immunosuppressive metabolites; the expression of immune checkpoint molecules; and the provision of survival signals [19–22]. However, whether bystander cells contribute directly to maintaining FMDV persistence, particularly as potential viral reservoirs, remains a critical and unexplored area in FMDV research.

In vitro models provide technically accessible platforms with reduced systemic complexity and serve as essential tools for investigating cellular responses to persistent FMDV infection [23, 24]. Although persistent infection models in baby hamster kidney-21 (BHK-21) cells have demonstrated virus-host coevolution [23, 24], the impaired retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) signaling pathway in these cells is physiologically irrelevant to bovine-specific immune responses [25]. Primary bovine pharyngeal epithelial cells offer superior pathological fidelity [26] but are constrained by ethical challenges and finite proliferative capacity. Madin-Darby bovine kidney (MDBK) epithelial cells address these limitations as a well-validated surrogate model for bovine pharyngeal epithelia [27] by combining stable proliferation with molecular tractability to enable rigorous dissection of virus-host interactions. Longitudinal passage models further facilitate studies of viral evolutionary dynamics. Consequently, the development of in vitro systems remains crucial for elucidating FMDV persistence mechanisms and designing effective countermeasures.

Whereas multiomics approaches have revolutionized the understanding of RNA virus pathogenesis by decoding dynamic virus-host networks, systematic analyses of FMDV persistence remain scarce. Therefore, we established an in vitro FMDV persistence model to dissect virus-host interplay during persistence, segregated virus-carrying and bystander cells via single-cell cloning, and integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiling. Key findings revealed that mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming occurs in host cells adapting to viral persistence. Second, FMDV infection causes mitochondria to undergo structural and functional perturbations with concomitant suppression of apoptosis, which sustains cell survival. Third, hyperproliferative bystander cells exhibit increased susceptibility to FMDV, which potentiates viral dissemination. Finally, enhanced replicative fitness occurs in evolved persistent viruses. Overall, we delineated the multiomics landscape of cell populations with persistent FMDV infection, revealed host-virus coadaptation dynamics, and identified bystander cells as crucial for viral persistence. These combined findings provide key mechanistic insights into the maintenance of FMDV persistence and suggest novel strategies for combatting this pernicious pathogen.

Results

Establishment and characterization of an in vitro model for persistent FMDV infection

Establishing a suitable in vitro model that minimizes confounding factors is essential for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of persistent FMDV infection. Preliminary experiments revealed distinct differences between MDBK and BHK-21 cells infected with FMDV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1. MDBK cells exhibited no significant cytopathic effect (CPE) and re-established confluent monolayers within 24–36 h post-infection (hpi) which suggests the potential production of factors that suppress viral replication while preserving proliferative capacity [28]. In contrast, BHK-21 cells developed CPE at 6 hpi, followed by detachment and cell death at 12–24 hpi (Supplementary Fig. S1A), which was likely attributable to RIG-I pathway deficiency [25]. Therefore, MDBK cells were selected for model establishment, as this cell line sustained the coexistence of low-level viral replication and host proliferation, i.e., a permissive state for persistent infection.

MDBK cells were infected with FMDV, and changes in FMDV genomic copies were monitored from the acute phase to 48 hpi. The FMDV genomic copies initially increased but then decreased, peaking between 8 and 12 hpi (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Following the approach of Huang et al. [24], ammonium chloride (NH4Cl), which is an inhibitor of viral replication that targets the early stages of infection [29], was employed to facilitate persistent infection. Cytotoxicity assays optimized the NH4Cl concentration and duration for maximum efficacy (Supplementary Fig. S1C). The final protocol involved 1 h infection of MDBK cells with FMDV followed by 36 h treatment with 30 mM NH4Cl. The cells were then passaged routinely every three days up to passage 50 (P50) (Fig. 1A). The cells maintained a normal morphology and growth without lethal CPEs throughout this period (Fig. 1B). FMDV genomic copies in serially passaged cells fluctuated and were lower than those observed during the acute infection stage (Supplementary Fig. S1B), frequently falling below detection limits in most passages (Fig. 1C). Immunofluorescence detected the FMDV antigen in 6.57%, 4.42%, 6.38%, 21.72% and 6.16% of the cells at P10, P20, P30, P40 and P50, respectively (Fig. 1D). Multiomics profiling reveals the microenvironmental features of persistently FMDV-infected cells

Integrated multiomics analysis offers a systems-level perspective on molecular adaptations during viral persistence, thereby elucidating maintenance mechanisms and virus-host interactions. We compared transcriptomes (7,552 genes; FPKM > 10), proteomes (6,390 proteins), and metabolomes (608 metabolites) across uninfected, acute infection (AI; MDBK cells infected with FMDV MOI = 1 for 8 h), and persistent infection cells (P36/P50) to define multidimensional features of the FMDV persistence microenvironment. The analysis details are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. High interreplicate correlations confirmed the robustness of the data (Supplementary Fig. S3A-C). Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) revealed distinct segregation of persistently infection cells from both AI and uninfected cells at all molecular tiers (Supplementary Fig. S3D-F), with hierarchical clustering highlighting group-specific expression signatures (Supplementary Fig. S3G-I).

Biomolecules were clustered into four expression patterns (Clusters 1–4) via the Mfuzz R package to resolve dynamic molecular trajectories across infection stages. Cluster 1 is the acute infection-dominant module, which comprises molecules that are markedly upregulated in acute infection but are downregulated or unchanged in persistence. The genes (Supplementary Fig. S4A) and proteins (Supplementary Fig. S4B) in cluster 1 were enriched in functions and pathways related to defense response to viruses, ribosome biogenesis, translation, and nucleolus activity. Integrated analysis revealed 283 overlapping transcriptome-proteome molecules enriched in the nucleolus, nucleic acid binding, translation, and RNA helicase/polymerase activities (Fig. 2A). These alterations align with established acute-phase mechanisms of FMDV: viral hijacking of host replication and translation for genomic amplification and protein synthesis and induction of the innate immune system to suppress viral replication [30–32]. Metabolites in cluster 1 (Fig. 2B) are linked to phosphoinositide signaling, phospholipase D pathway, and glycerophospholipid metabolism which provide structural and energetic support for immune activation [33]. These findings suggest that acute viral invasion triggers robust antiviral responses, which are accompanied by the reprogramming of lipid metabolism to support immune activation. Clusters 2 and 3 are persistence-dominant modules that include molecules that either are downregulated or unchanged during acute infection but are markedly upregulated during persistence. Integrated Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses of overlapping transcriptome-proteome molecules revealed functional associations with mitochondrial energetics (mitochondrial inner membrane organization, cytochrome c oxidase assembly, V-type ATPase complex assembly, oxidative phosphorylation, and thermogenesis), proteolysis (proteasome and proteasome complex, and protein catabolism), and lipid signaling (phosphatidylinositol dephosphorylation, inositol phosphate metabolism, and phosphatidylinositol signaling system) (Fig. 2A). Upregulated mitochondrial functions indicate heightened energy demands in persistently infected cells that potentially activate oxidative phosphorylation to counter metabolic and oxidative stresses [34, 35]. Enhanced cytochrome c oxidase and V-ATPase assembly may optimize ATP production under chronic energy requirements [36, 37]. Elevated proteasomal activity may facilitate immune evasion by degrading antiviral interferon-stimulated genes and concurrently clearing cytotoxic viral proteins [38, 39]. Metabolite profiling linked clusters 2 and 3 to arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, and sphingolipid metabolism, as well as unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis (Fig. 2B), which underscores the important roles of lipid metabolic reprogramming in FMDV persistence. For example, elevated arachidonic acid and linoleic acid metabolism may contribute to the maintenance of chronic inflammation [40]. Increased sphingolipid/unsaturated fatty acid contents may enhance membrane fluidity which facilitates viral processes, whereas sphingolipid-mediated regulation of apoptosis and autophagy modulates stress adaptation [41]. These results suggest that persistent FMDV antigen exposure induces chronic stress characterized by increased energy demands, which drive the remodeling of mitochondrial and lipid metabolism to sustain low-level viral replication while promoting host survival.

Cluster 4 is a functional decline-dominant module that comprises molecules whose expression is downregulated persistently post-infection. Integrated GO/KEGG analysis of overlapping transcriptome-proteome molecules revealed associations with lipid biosynthesis (steroids, sterols, and cholesterol), protein tyrosine kinase activity, and cell adhesion (focal adhesion, cell adhesion molecules, basement membrane, and extracellular matrix-receptor interaction) (Fig. 2A). The suppression of cholesterol or sterol biosynthesis may compromise cell barrier function and intercellular junctions which potentially facilitates viral dissemination [42, 43]. Sustained downregulation of protein tyrosine kinases likely impairs antiviral signaling, e.g., interferon responses, thereby promoting immune evasion [44]. The metabolites in cluster 4 are linked to cofactors, aminoacyl-tRNA, and amino acid biosynthesis (Fig. 2B). The suppressed biosynthesis of lipids, amino acids, and cofactors may limit substrates for host antiviral protein synthesis, thereby increasing viral reliance on the host translation machinery, e.g., internal ribosome entry sites, for the selective production of viral proteins. These findings suggest that prolonged FMDV persistence is correlated with the progressive suppression of host structural integrity and biosynthetic capacity, which leads to a state of functional deterioration.

Overall, viral remodeling of the host microenvironment during FMDV persistence involves mitochondrial dysfunction, reprogramming of lipid metabolism, and suppression of antiviral immunity, all of which sustain low-level replication. Host adaptations simultaneously enhance mitochondrial energetics and lipid metabolism, thereby maintaining survival and proliferation. This virus-host equilibrium enables the coexistence of viral persistence and normal cellular proliferation.

Isolation of bystander cells and the role of these cells in persistent infection

Only a subset of cells in cultures persistently infected with FMDV were antigen positive (Fig. 1D), whereas the remainder comprised uninfected or virus-negative bystander cells. Bystander cells play dual regulatory roles in viral persistence by mediating both antiviral protection and host cell damage [21, 45, 46]. Virus-carrying cells (designated FMDV-OP) and bystander cells (designated FMDV-VEC) were isolated from P36 cultures to define the specific functions of the latter cell type. Immunofluorescence confirmed FMDV antigen no expression in FMDV-VEC cells (Fig. 3A). Microscopy revealed distinct morphological and proliferative differences between the FMDV-VEC and FMDV-OP clones (Fig. 3B). Growth curve analysis revealed significantly higher proliferation rates in FMDV-VEC cells than in FMDV-OP cells (Fig. 3C). We integrated transcriptomic (6,897 genes; FPKM > 10), proteomic (4,083 proteins), and metabolomic (826 metabolites) profiling of uninfected, FMDV-OP, and FMDV-VEC cells to define bystander cells functions systematically. High interreplicate correlations confirmed the robustness of the data (Supplementary Fig. S5A-C). Principal component analysis revealed profound molecular divergence between FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells across all omics layers (Supplementary Fig. S5D-F), with hierarchical clustering highlighting distinct expression signatures (Supplementary Fig. S5G-I).

FMDV-VEC cells presented differential expression of 399 upregulated/339 downregulated genes, 636 upregulated/684 downregulated proteins, and 131 upregulated/119 downregulated metabolites compared with uninfected cells. GO/KEGG enrichment of these differentially expressed molecules revealed the unique functional landscape of FMDV-VEC cells (Supplementary Fig. S6). We observed activation of diverse processes in FMDV-VEC cells, including inflammatory signaling (cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor, IL-17 signaling pathway, chemokine signaling pathway, and TNF signaling pathway), lysosomal function, and ATP binding. These alterations may reflect a response to persistent infection that contributes to cellular injury. In parallel, significant suppression of the cell proliferation machinery, including cell division, cell cycle, kinetochore, and spindle microtubule, was evident. These opposing molecular signatures collectively explain the reduced growth viability of FMDV-VEC cells relative to that of uninfected cells.

FMDV-VEC cells presented differential expression of 45 upregulated/74 downregulated genes, 815 upregulated/679 downregulated proteins, and 166 upregulated/56 downregulated metabolites compared with FMDV-OP cells (Fig. 4). The upregulated molecules in FMDV-VEC cells were enriched in immune surveillance (leukocyte chemotaxis in inflammation and hypoxia-inducible factor-1 [HIF-1] signaling), homeostasis (cellular homeostasis and cell volume homeostasis), proliferation (cell cycle, nucleus, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling), and metabolic specialization (cofactor biosynthesis, ascorbate/aldarate metabolism, and nucleotide-sugar biosynthesis). In particular, upregulated HIF-1 signaling potentiated the production of proinflammatory cytokines and immune cell infiltration [47]. The increase in leukocyte chemotaxis suggested that FMDV-VEC-mediated antiviral defense occurred through the secretion of chemokines and the sustained induction of proinflammatory states [48]. Concomitant upregulation of homeostatic and proliferative pathways indicated that FMDV-VEC cells maintained genomic stability to replenish virus-damaged cells. Paradoxically, this heightened proliferative status may increase susceptibility to viral infection [49, 50]. Metabolic rewiring potentially fuels proliferation and stress responses [51]. The downregulated molecules in FMDV-VEC cells were enriched in core energy metabolism (mitochondrion, tricarboxylic acid cycle, 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, carbon/pyruvate metabolism, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis), immunomodulation (cytokine receptor interaction, IgA regulation, complement and coagulation cascades, chemokine/apoptosis regulation, and p53 signaling), and degradation capacity (lysosomal and proteasomal catabolism). Strikingly, suppressed energy metabolism in FMDV-VEC cells may reallocate resources to support structural integrity and proliferation. Downregulation of immunomodulatory pathways potentially establishes immune tolerance to prevent excessive self-damage [51]. FMDV-VEC cells collectively play dual, seemingly paradoxical roles within persistently infected populations. Antiviral containment through sustained defense mechanisms, maintenance of genomic stability, and preservation of cellular homeostasis may restrict viral dissemination. Pro-survival adaptation occurs through the suppression of core energy metabolism, increased proliferative capacity, and immunomodulation to replenish cell populations. Critically, this heightened proliferative state may establishe a stable reservoir of susceptible host cells. FMDV-VEC cells may serve as prime targets for viral exploitation, thereby perpetuating FMDV persistence.

Mitochondrial functional states in persistently infected and bystander cells

Integrated multiomics profiling revealed profound mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming in both FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells. In view of the interdependence of mitochondrial structure and function, we assessed the ultrastructural integrity of FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells. The mitochondria in FMDV-OP cells exhibited extensive vacuolization, crista disorganization or loss, and membrane rupture. The mitochondria in FMDV-VEC cells presented hyperplasia, swelling, vacuolization, and crista abnormalities (Fig. 5A). Mitochondrial dysfunction is a primary source of reactive oxygen species (ROS). During acute infection, ROS levels peaked coincident with maximal viral replication (8 hpi) and declined sharply by 24–48 hpi which paralleled the decline in viral genomic expression (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. S1B), indicating that an ROS burst was directly linked to rapid viral replication. In terms of persistence, both FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells presented significantly elevated ROS levels compared with those of controls, with FMDV-VEC accumulation exceeding that of FMDV-OP. This finding correlates with structural damage and demonstrates chronic oxidative stress in virus-free bystander cells under prolonged coadaptation. Disruption of the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP, ΔΨm) signifies core dysfunction. Transient ΔΨm loss occurred at 8 hpi, with partial recovery during acute infection, and profound ΔΨm collapse was evident in both FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells during persistence, which was paradoxically more severe in FMDV-VEC (Fig. 5C, D). Dysregulation of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) drives the collapse of ΔΨm and cell death. Transient mPTP opening was observable at 8 hpi during acute infection, but persistent hyperopening was detected in both FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells compared with negative controls (Fig. 5E). Overall, acute-phase mitochondrial stress (high ROS, low ΔΨm, and high mPTP) is transient and replication-coupled. In contrast, persistence features chronic mitochondrial decompensation in both cell types, with FMDV-VEC cells exhibiting unexpectedly amplified dysfunction (higher ROS and lower ΔΨm) that indicates intensified metabolic stress despite the absence of viruses.

We next assessed key proteins associated with mitochondrial function and integrity (Fig. 5F), including heat shock protein 60 (HSP60, a mitochondrial matrix chaperone), voltage-dependent anion channel protein 1 (VDAC1, a mPTP component), translocase of the outer membrane 20 (TOM20, a mitochondrial biogenesis activity), and cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV isoform 1 (COXIV, a subunit of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV). HSP60 expression increased in a time-dependent manner during acute FMDV infection which suggests exacerbated mitochondrial protein folding stress. In contrast, VDAC1 and COXIV expression decreased in a time-dependent manner which indicates impaired outer mitochondrial membrane integrity and compromised respiratory function. TOM20 expression remained unchanged. HSP60 and COXIV levels were elevated significantly in FMDV-OP cells, indicating robust protein folding stress and potential compensatory enhancement of respiratory function. Instead, VDAC1 expression was reduced markedly which may decrease the release of proapoptotic factors, e.g., cytochrome c, and block the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. TOM20 expression remained unchanged which indicates that mitochondrial biogenesis was not activated substantially. Thus, FMDV-OP cells appear to optimize existing mitochondrial functions, including enhanced protein folding capacity and respiratory activity, rather than increasing the number of mitochondria while potentially inhibiting apoptosis. The expression of HSP60, TOM20, and COXIV was upregulated significantly in FMDV-VEC cells. This pattern indicated severe mitochondrial dysfunction, compensatory respiratory enhancement, and activated mitochondrial biogenesis, and suggested that stress prompted FMDV-VEC cells to generate more mitochondria to meet heightened energy demands which was consistent with the observations of mitochondrial hyperplasia. VDAC1 expression decreased significantly which was similar to the potential suppression of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. Overall, FMDV-VEC cells presented more pronounced mitochondrial stress and compensatory responses compared to FMDV-OP.

In view of the established link between mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis suppression that facilitates viral persistence [52, 53], we assessed the degree of apoptosis in FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells (Fig. 5G, H). Acute FMDV infection significantly increased the number of early and late apoptotic cells compared with uninfected cells. In contrast, both FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells presented significantly lower proportions of early apoptotic cells and a decreasing trend in late apoptotic cells compared with the negative controls. In particular, the percentage of apoptotic cells in FMDV-VEC was even lower than in FMDV-OP. Combined with the marked downregulation of VDAC1 in FMDV-OP and FMDV-VEC cells, these data confirmed that suppression of mitochondrial apoptosis promoted cell survival and proliferation during FMDV persistence.

Discussion

Persistent RNA virus infections involve a dynamic equilibrium that is achieved through long-term coadaptation between virus and host [54, 55]. We established a stable, proliferative in vitro model of FMDV serotype O persistence in MDBK cells to decipher the complex virus-host interplay that underlies FMDV persistence. Prior studies utilized BHK-21 cells for FMDV persistence models [23, 24], but the compromised RIG-I pathway in this cell line [25] limits the physiological relevance to bovine hosts. The MDBK cells model overcomes this limitation because of the clinical relevance to ruminant pharyngeal epithelia [27] and also because of the capacity for long-term propagation, that contrasts with the finite proliferative capacity of primary bovine cells [26]. A number of defining features validated this model as a faithful in vitro surrogate for FMDV persistence. First, infected MDBK cells maintained their normal morphology and proliferated through 50 passages (Fig. 1B), which contrasts with the lytic infection in BHK-21 cells (Supplementary Fig. S1A). This finding aligns with in vivo observations which demonstrated that FMDV persists in regenerating pharyngeal epithelia without destroying the basal layers. In addition, viral genomic copies subsequently fluctuated dynamically, frequently falling below detection limits (Fig. 1C), which mirrors the intermittent shedding patterns in cattle carriers [3]. This oscillatory release dynamics parallels observations in lymphocytic choriomeningitis mammarenavirus persistence, in which viral RNA intermittently falls below detection limits [56]. These dynamics suggest that host defenses transiently suppress but fail to eliminate viral replication which aligns with the paradigm in which viruses balance immune evasion against host viability through attenuated replication [57].

Integrated multiomics analysis systematically elucidated dynamic adaptation mechanisms within persistently FMDV-infected cell populations. These analyses revealed upregulated mitochondrial energy metabolism which is indicative of persistent mitochondrial stress. Consistent with this observation, further analysis demonstrated that mitochondrial damage manifested as ultrastructural alterations, ROS accumulation, ΔΨm dissipation, and mPTP hyperopening. Adaptively, cells upregulate COXIV and HSP60 to restore mitochondrial function. Critically, VDAC1 downregulation demonstrated by flow cytometry inhibited host apoptosis, thereby enabling cell survival despite viral persistence and mitochondrial damage. This strategy aligns with mechanisms in other viruses, including HBV that exploit mitochondrial dysfunction to inhibit apoptosis and facilitate persistent infection [52, 53]. Extending these findings, we established that FMDV persistence actively induces sustained lipid metabolic remodeling which is known to be triggered by mitochondrial dysfunction [58]. In addition to structural and energetic roles, remodeled lipids participate in signaling cascades, modulate inflammation [59], and facilitate the assembly of viral replication complexes [60]. Collectively, host cells create a metabolic microenvironment through coordinated mitochondrial and lipid reprogramming that concurrently supports cell survival/proliferation and viral replication. This coadaptation underpins long-term viral coexistence [61], but the precise molecular mechanisms that govern mitochondrial-lipid crosstalk in FMDV persistence require further elucidation to inform targeted interventions.

The results reveal heterogeneous FMDV antigen distributions across the cell populations, with discrete cellular subpopulations functioning as viral reservoirs that sustain population-wide persistence. Bystander cells (FMDV-VEC) within persistently FMDV-infected cell populations exhibit dual functionality. FMDV-VEC cells exhibited significantly increased proliferation rates compared with FMDV-OP cells which may promote upregulated cell cycle and transcriptional regulation that enables the rapid replenishment of virus-damaged cells and the sustained viability of the population. Despite lacking active virus, FMDV-VEC cells displayed profound mitochondrial perturbations, including increased mitochondrial mass, elevated ROS accumulation, and reduced ΔΨm which indicates intense mitochondrial stress and compensatory responses. Functionally, this mitochondrial dysregulation creates a permissive microenvironment and suppresses core metabolism-liberated biosynthetic resources (Fig. 4), whereas concomitant immunomodulatory downregulation promotes immune tolerance. Critically, the adapted FMDV Op36 strain exploited this niche and achieved greater replication within FMDV-VEC than FMDV-OP cells (Fig. 6I) which confirms that bystander cells are evolutionarily selected targets. This susceptibility does not appear to result from enhanced entry but rather from post-entry permissiveness which may be driven by mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 5) that liberates resources for viral replication. Consequently, we propose that bystander cells replenish the population through hyperproliferation, whereas the altered state of this subpopulation renders these cells as prime targets for evolved variants, thereby establishing a cycle of viral reservoir renewal and maintenance. This mechanism underpins the sustained low-level persistence of the virus within the cell population which suggests that targeting bystander cells activation may represent a critical strategy for intervening in persistent viral infections [47]. A pivotal unanswered question concerns the nature of bystander cells: are these cells truly uninfected which implies resistance, or transiently cleared, which indicates cyclic reinfection? Future single-cell RNA sequencing will resolve this problem by tracking viral RNA trajectories.

The persistent infection state fosters a specialized microenvironment that enables prolonged viral maintenance and replication. Continuous selective pressures within this niche drive adaptive viral evolution, including host immune responses and intracellular constraints. FMDV persistence during bovine infection results in significant mutation accumulation [62, 63]. Similarly, our study identified the T135I substitution in the viral nonstructural protein 2 C as a key adaptation (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 7). The 2 C protein mediates host membrane remodeling for viral replication complex assembly, and the substitution of a polar threonine with a hydrophobic isoleucine potentiates membrane affinity through increased hydrophobicity. This modification facilitates efficient replication within FMDV-VEC cells by promoting viral RNA synthesis that may involve structural optimization of 2 C helicase activity and modulation of interactions with host factors [64].

In summary, we established an in vitro model of FMDV persistence and applied integrated multiomics to delineate alterations in the host microenvironment. Our analysis revealed that mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming represents a key host adaption to persistent viral infection. This adaptation, coupled with mitochondrial dysfunction and suppressed apoptosis, facilitates long-term viral coexistence. Furthermore, we identified a bystander cell subpopulation characterized by heightened proliferative capacity and a unique microenvironment that increases susceptibility to infection, thereby promoting viral dissemination and persistence. Concurrently, the viral population demonstrated enhanced replicative fitness, underscoring a dynamic coadaptation process. Collectively, this work proposes a model of host-virus coadaptation and highlights the functional specialization of cell subpopulations during persistence, significantly advancing our understanding of FMDV persistence mechanisms. These insights not only elucidate underlying biology but also reveal novel therapeutic vulnerabilities. Specifically, targeting mitochondrial metabolic in persistently infected cells or disrupting communication and infection processes in bystander cells presents promising strategies. However, the limitations of this study include the inability of in vitro models to fully recapitulate the complex immune microenvironment in vivo and precise mechanisms remain unresolved that govern intercellular metabolite transport. Future investigations will leverage spatial transcriptomics and metabolic flux analysis to elucidate intercellular communication and evaluate combinatorial therapeutic strategies targeting bystander cells or metabolic hubs. The ultimately aiming to develop a multi-pronged interventions that disrupts the co adapted host-virus equilibrium, preventing the establishment and enabling the clearance of persistent FMDV infections.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

MDBK (GDC0290) and BHK-21 (GDC010) cells were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection. The cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

The FMDV strain O/BY/CHA/2010 (GenBank accession no. JN998085) was maintained at the State Key Laboratory of Animal Disease Control and Prevention, Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, China. Viruses were propagated and titrated in BHK-21 cells. All live-virus experiments were conducted in a biosafety level 3 laboratory at the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute.

The persistent infection model was established as follows. MDBK cells were infected with FMDV at MOI of 1 and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and cultured in medium containing 30 mM NH4Cl for 36 h. The cells were then passaged every three days until P50 (Fig. 1A). The persistently infected virus strain (designated FMDV Op36/50) was isolated from persistently infected cells at P36/50.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed via the MTS-based CellTiter 96® Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega). The absorbance was measured at 490 nm via a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Biotek).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted with RNAiso Plus reagent (TaKaRa) and complementary DNA was synthesized with PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed on a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). FMDV genome copies quantification was performed as described previously [65]. The reactions contained 2× Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa), FMDV 3D-specific primers (forward: 5’-ACTGGGTTTTACAAACCTGTGA-3’; reverse: 5’-GCGAGTCCTGCCACGGA-3’), and a FAM-BHQ dual-labeled probe (5’-FAM-TCCTTTGCACGCCGTGGGAC-BHQ-3’). A recombinant pET28a-FMDV/3D plasmid was serially diluted (10-fold) to generate a standard curve. Genomic copy numbers were extrapolated from cycle threshold (Ct) values via linear regression analysis. Primers for relative gene expression (Supplementary Table S1; Tsingke Biotechnology) were used. The data were normalized to those of GAPDH, and the relative expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Indirect immunofluorescence

The cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%) for 30 min, permeabilized with Triton X-100 (0.1%) for 15 min, and blocked with bovine serum albumin (5%) for 1 h. The cells were incubated with a porcine anti-FMDV polyclonal antibody for 1 h, followed by incubation with a FITC-conjugated rabbit anti-porcine IgG for 1 h. The nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beyotime) for 15 min. Images were acquired via a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica SP8).

Viral titration

Viral titers were determined via an endpoint dilution assay on BHK-21 cells in 96-well plates and calculated as the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) via the Reed-Muench method.

Mitochondrial function assays

Intracellular ROS was assessed by incubating cells with DCFH-DA (10 µM) for 20 min, whereas MMP was determined by incubating cells with JC-1 (10 µM) for 20 min. mPTP opening was assessed with an mPTP detection kit (Beyotime) with Calcein-AM or Calcein-AM plus cobalt chloride for 30 min. The stained cells were washed and resuspended in PBS and the fluorescence intensity was analyzed via flow cytometry.

Apoptosis assay

The cells were stained with the fixable viability dye eFluor® 780 (FVD780; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min. Cells were subsequently washed with BSA (0.5%) and then stained with fluorescein-labeled Annexin V (BioLegend) for 15 min. The cells were subsequently washed, resuspended in Annexin V binding buffer, and immediately analyzed via flow cytometry.

Transcriptomics

Total RNA was isolated via TRIzol™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific). mRNA was enriched via oligo (dT) magnetic beads (Dynabeads™, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform (LC Bio). Sequencing reads were aligned to the Bos taurus genome via HISAT2. Transcripts were assembled and quantified via StringTie. Gene expression was quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) and statistically compared.

Proteomics

Proteomic analysis was performed by nano liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (nanoLC-MS/MS). Briefly, the cells lysates were digested with trypsin. Desalted peptides were labeled with TMT reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and were fractionated via an UltiMate™ 3000 Binary Rapid Separation system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MS analysis was performed on an EASY-nLC™ 1200 system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The raw data were processed with MaxQuant (v2.1.4.0) against the UniProt Bos taurus database for protein identification and quantification.

Metabolomics

Untargeted metabolomics was performed. Metabolites were extracted with 50% methanol. The samples were separated by reversed-phase chromatography via an UltiMate™ 3000 UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed via a TripleTOF® 6600 tandem mass spectrometer. The raw data were preprocessed with XCMS for peak detection, alignment, and annotation. Metabolites were identified by matching m/z values against the KEGG and HMDB databases.

Bioinformatics analysis

All analyses were performed in R (v4.3.0). Correlation analysis was conducted via the Pearson correlation coefficient by cor R package. PLS-DA was executed via metaX with variable importance in projection (VIP) scores. Clustering heatmaps were generated by pheatmap. Time-series analysis was conducted with Mfuzz. Differentially expressed genes were identified by DESeq2 and edgeR (|FC|≥2 and adjusted P < 0.05), differentially expressed proteins were screened by Wilcoxon rank-sum test (|FC|≥1.2 and P < 0.05), differentially expressed metabolites were selected by t test (|FC|≥1.2 and P < 0.05) combined with PLS-DA (VIP > 1). Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses with P < 0.05 according to the hypergeometric test were considered significantly enriched.

Limiting dilution for single-cell cloning

Persistently infected cells (P36) were serially diluted to 2.5 cells/mL and seeded into 96-well plates. After the cells attached, the wells containing single cells were identified. At 7–10 days post-seeding, when the cells reached 30–50% confluency, they were transferred to 24-well plates for expansion. FMDV-positive (FMDV-OP) and FMDV-negative (bystander cells, FMDV-VEC) clones were identified by RT-qPCR and subjected to three rounds of subcloning.

Western blotting

The primary antibodies used included mouse monoclonal anti-HSP60 (66041-1-Ig), anti-TOMM20 (66777-1-Ig), and anti-COXIV (66110-1-Ig) from Proteintech, rabbit monoclonal anti-VDAC1 (4661 S) from Cell Signaling Technology, and mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin from CWBIO. Total protein was extracted via high-efficiency RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor. Lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed with TBST, incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibodies for 1 h, washed, and visualized using chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Transmission electron microscopy

The cells were fixed in cacodylate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4) containing glutaraldehyde (3%) and paraformaldehyde (2%) for 2 h, fixed in osmium tetroxide (1%) for 1 h, and stained with uranyl acetate (4%) for 2 h. The samples were dehydrated through an ethanol series, infiltrated with ethanol-Epon (1:1) for 7 h, and embedded in pure Epon resin. Polymerization was performed at progressively increasing temperatures: 35 °C for 6 h, 45 °C for 6 h, and 60 °C for 24 h. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were stained with aqueous uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were acquired via a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope.

Viral genome sequencing

Viral RNA was extracted via an E.Z.N.A.® Viral RNA Kit (OMEGA Biotek). Libraries were constructed from fragmented RNA-derived double-stranded cDNA. Paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform (Shanghai Tanpu Biotechnology). Reference FMDV sequences were retrieved from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed via MEGA (v11.0.13). Sequence alignments were generated with DNASTAR Lasergene MegAlign (v7.1.0).

Viral reverse genetics

Using the previously established infectious cDNA clone pcDNA3.1(+)-T7 + lacO-FMDV (O/BY/CHA/2010) in our laboratory as template, we introduced the T135I substitution into the 2 C protein to generate the recombinant virus rFMDV MU-2 C-T135I, which was subsequently rescued in BHK-21 cells. To compare replication kinetics, BHK-21 cells were infected with either the wild-type (rFMDV WT) or mutant virus at an MOI of 1. The cells and supernatants were harvested at the indicated timepoints for further analysis. The intracellular FMDV 3D RNA levels were quantified by RT‑qPCR, and viral titers in the supernatants were determined by TCID50 assay.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, and P values were calculated via GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2). The data are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs).
