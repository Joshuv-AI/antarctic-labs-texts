Effects of teach-back method-based continuing nursing on self-management behavior and quality of life in patients after aortic dissection surgery
Authors: Qiuxia Huang, Liqing Huang, Jianying Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency with declining postoperative quality of life (QoL). The teach-back method has shown effectiveness in chronic disease management, but its application in postoperative AD patients remains unexplored.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study with ward-level cluster allocation enrolled 210 AD patients (105 per group) at a tertiary hospital in Nanchang, China, from January 2023 to June 2024. The experimental group received teach-back method-based continuing nursing, while the control group received routine care. Primary outcomes included self-management behavior assessed by the Self-Management Assessment Scale (SMAS) and QoL measured by the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) of the Short Form-36 Health Survey (SF-36) at baseline, discharge, 1-month, and 6-month follow-up. Secondary outcomes comprised health knowledge scores at baseline and 1-month post-discharge, and adverse clinical outcomes including 30-day unplanned readmission rates, 6-month AD recurrence rates, and total adverse events.ResultsThe experimental group demonstrated significantly higher SMAS across all dimensions with a significant time×group interaction (P < 0.001). Disease knowledge questionnaire score at 1-month was significantly higher in the experimental group (21.36 ± 2.89 vs. 15.82 ± 3.54, P < 0.001). Both PCS and MCS scores showed significant improvements over time (both P < 0.001), with the experimental group achieving higher scores than the control group at all follow-up time points (all P < 0.001). A significant time×group interaction effect was observed for MCS (P = 0.0052) but not for PCS (P = 0.599). The experimental group achieved significantly better blood pressure control at 6 months (SBP: 125.6 ± 10.8 vs. 133.4 ± 14.8 mmHg, P < 0.001). The experimental group had lower 30-day readmission rates (6.67% vs. 17.14%, P = 0.016), 6-month AD recurrence rates (4.76% vs. 14.29%, P = 0.014), and total adverse events (4.76% vs. 12.38%, P = 0.048). Background

Aortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency with an estimated annual incidence of 3–6 per 100,000 population globally, with rates showing an upward trend in recent years [1]. The condition carries extremely high mortality rates, with untreated acute Stanford Type A dissections experiencing mortality rates of 1–2% per hour within the first 48 h [2]. Despite advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management, long-term outcomes remain concerning, with 1-year, 5-year, and 10-year survival rates of 73.3%, 68.2% and 53.5%, respectively [3].

Surgery serves only as a palliative treatment, as the pathological progression of the aortic wall continues postoperatively, with residual false lumens facing lifelong risks of aneurysmal degeneration and rupture [4, 5]. Recent studies have revealed that patients experience significant deterioration in physical health-related quality of life (QoL) following surgical repair, with physical component scores declining substantially over time [6, 7]. Postoperative self-management challenges are prevalent among AD patients, including limited disease-related knowledge, inadequate disease management behaviors, insufficient communication with healthcare providers, and compromised psychological well-being [4, 8].

The teach-back method, an evidence-based health literacy strategy recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, has emerged as an effective approach to verify patients’ understanding of health information [9, 10]. This method involves patients explaining health information in their own words, allowing healthcare providers to assess comprehension and provide targeted re-education [11]. Studies have demonstrated that teach-back significantly improves disease knowledge, self-management behaviors, medication adherence, and patient satisfaction across various chronic conditions [12–14]. Continuing nursing care, which extends professional support beyond hospital discharge, has proven effective in improving treatment compliance and reducing complications in cardiovascular patients [15].

However, no studies have examined the effectiveness of teach-back method-based continuing nursing specifically for postoperative AD patients. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a teach-back method-based continuing nursing for AD patients following surgery, assessing its impact on self-management behaviors and QoL.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This study employed a quasi-experimental design to evaluate the effectiveness of teach-back method-based continuing nursing for postoperative AD patients. The study was classified as quasi-experimental because individual-level randomization was not feasible; the teach-back nursing intervention required consistent delivery to all patients within the same ward, and randomizing individual patients within a ward would inevitably lead to cross-contamination between groups. Therefore, a cluster allocation approach was adopted at the ward level (detailed below). The study was conducted at the Department of Cardiac and Vascular Surgery in a tertiary general hospital in Nanchang, China, between January 2023 and June 2024. The study protocol received approval from the institutional ethics committee prior to participant recruitment, and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment (Approval Number: IIT Ethics Review [2024] No. 185).

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling from patients admitted for surgical treatment of AD. Eligible patients were those who had received a confirmed diagnosis of AD through computed tomography angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). All participants were required to be at least 18 years of age with intact cognitive function and normal communication abilities. To facilitate the delivery of the continuing nursing intervention, participants or their primary caregivers needed to possess a smartphone and be capable of using basic WeChat functions after instruction.

Patients were excluded if they demonstrated cognitive dysfunction, were concurrently participating in other clinical trials, or had a documented history of psychiatric disorders. Those with severe complications such as heart failure or respiratory failure where clinical condition could deteriorate rapidly were not eligible for enrollment. Patients with coexisting malignant tumors were also excluded from the study.

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation was based on the formula for comparing means between two independent groups: n = 2[(tα/2 + tβ)σ/δ]². The calculation was based on the SF-36 Physical Component Summary (PCS) score as the primary outcome, referencing findings from St Pierre et al. [7], who reported PCS changes following AD surgery. The expected mean difference (δ) of 10.6 PCS points (scale range: 0–100) represented the anticipated clinically meaningful between-group difference at 6-month follow-up. The pooled standard deviation (σ) of 13.6 PCS points was derived from the variability observed in that reference population. Using a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 0.90, the corresponding critical values were tα/2 = 1.96 and tβ = 1.28. Substituting these values into the formula yielded an initial sample size of approximately 32 participants per group. Accounting for an anticipated 20% attrition rate and considering potential limitations in the reference data, the final sample size was increased to 40 participants per group. To further enhance statistical power and ensure adequate representation, the study ultimately enrolled 105 participants in each group, resulting in a total sample of 210 patients. Post-hoc power analysis confirmed that the achieved sample size provided > 99% power to detect the observed between-group PCS difference.

Randomization and grouping method

To minimize contamination between the control and experimental groups, participants were recruited from two geographically separate wards within the same hospital department. Random allocation of the two wards to either the control or experimental condition was performed using a coin toss method, which provides an unbiased 50:50 probability of allocation and is an accepted approach for two-cluster randomization designs. This ward-level allocation ensured that all patients within a given ward received the same intervention protocol, thereby eliminating within-ward contamination. Patients admitted to the ward assigned as the control group received routine continuing nursing care, while those admitted to the ward assigned as the experimental group received the teach-back method-based continuing nursing in addition to routine care.

Due to the nature of the nursing intervention, it was not feasible to blind the nurses delivering the care or the patients receiving the intervention. However, participants were not explicitly informed about the differences between the two intervention approaches to minimize expectancy bias. Outcome assessments at all time points were conducted by two trained research assistants recruited from a separate department who were not involved in delivering either intervention and were blinded to group allocation. During hospitalization, these research assistants administered questionnaires during scheduled data collection visits independent of nursing care activities. Post-discharge assessments at 1 and 6 months were conducted via standardized telephone interviews or self-administered WeChat-based questionnaires managed by the same blinded research team. Questionnaires were primarily self-administered by patients, with research assistants providing standardized instructions without reviewing or discussing responses. Statistical analysts were blinded to group assignment during data analysis, with groups coded as Group A and Group B. The group allocation code was only revealed after completion of all statistical analyses to prevent potential bias in data interpretation.

Intervention methods

Control group

Patients in the control group received routine continuing nursing care following standard hospital protocols. Upon enrollment, the primary nurse instructed patients and their family members on accessing and navigating the department’s WeChat (Tencent Holdings Ltd., China) official account for health education materials. During hospitalization, bedside health education was conducted during periods of reduced treatment activity, typically in the afternoon following surgical procedures. The primary nurse delivered individualized education based on each patient’s clinical condition, referencing the departmental health education manual for AD, supplementary WeChat articles, and educational videos. Prior to each session, patients were informed of the topics to be covered and encouraged to review corresponding materials on the platform beforehand. The health education content covered disease knowledge about AD pathophysiology and surgical procedures, daily life management including dietary choices and exercise recommendations, medication guidance regarding proper administration and potential side effects, emotional management strategies, and medical follow-up information emphasizing the importance of scheduled appointments and emergency procedures.

Following hospital discharge, patients received routine telephone follow-up at 1-, and 6-month post-intervention. During these calls, nurses provided guidance on disease-related health knowledge, reinforced key self-management behaviors, and emphasized the importance of timely outpatient follow-up visits to monitor recovery and detect potential complications.

Experimental group

Patients in the experimental group received teach-back method-based continuing nursing in addition to routine care. The intervention was delivered in four phases corresponding to different stages of recovery (Table 1). During the early postoperative period (2–3 days after returning to the ward), disease knowledge education was provided through WeChat articles, one-on-one instruction, and group education sessions, utilizing health education manuals, models, and illustrated materials. From postoperative days 4–6, the focus shifted to daily life management and medication adherence, incorporating WeChat content, patient exchange meetings, and hands-on demonstrations with teaching aids including educational videos, electronic blood pressure monitors, and medication management tools. During the pre-discharge phase (7–8 days post-surgery), emotional and symptom management was emphasized using similar educational formats and materials.

The teach-back component was implemented consistently throughout all phases. After each educational session, patients were asked to verbally demonstrate their understanding by explaining key concepts in their own words. The nurse assessed comprehension through targeted open-ended questions related to the educational content just delivered. When patients provided correct and complete responses, positive reinforcement was given. For incomplete or incorrect responses, the nurse clarified misconceptions and re-taught the material using alternative explanations or visual aids, repeating the teach-back cycle until adequate comprehension was achieved. Patients who struggled to master certain content despite repeated instruction were provided with educational videos for self-review, followed by reassessment and additional guidance as needed.

Following hospital discharge, continuing care was provided weekly for four weeks via WeChat and telephone communication. Each week, educational content covering the four main domains (daily life, medication, emotion and symptom management, and follow-up care) was systematically delivered through the WeChat platform. Patients were encouraged to practice self-monitoring and share their daily progress, including dietary intake, physical activity, and blood pressure measurements, through the WeChat group. The teach-back method was applied during each interaction to verify retention of previously taught information and reinforce self-management behaviors. Formal follow-up assessments were conducted at 1- and 6-month post-intervention through WeChat, telephone, or outpatient visits to evaluate knowledge retention, self-management practices, and address any concerns or challenges patients encountered.

Outcome measures

Outcome indicators were assessed at four timepoints: baseline (upon enrollment before intervention), immediately post-intervention (at hospital discharge), 1-month post-discharge, and 6 months post-discharge. General demographic and clinical information were collected only at baseline. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews during hospitalization and via WeChat, telephone, or outpatient visits during follow-up periods. The primary outcomes included QoL measured by 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) and self-management behavior assessed by Self-Management Assessment Scale for Postoperative Patients with AD. The secondary outcomes comprised disease-specific knowledge and adverse clinical outcomes.

General information

Demographic and clinical data were collected using a self-designed questionnaire upon enrollment. Demographic information included age, gender, place of residence, marital status, occupation, monthly household income per month, type of medical insurance, and educational level. Clinical information included AD classification (Stanford Type A or B), surgical procedure, comorbidities, smoking and alcohol consumption history.

Self-management behavior

Self-management behavior was measured using the Self-Management Assessment Scale (SMAS) for Postoperative Patients with AD, developed and validated by Zhang in 2020 [16]. The scale consists of 35 items across six dimensions: psychological management, symptom management, disease knowledge management, self-monitoring management, treatment management, and daily life management. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with total scores ranging from 35 to 175. Higher scores indicate better self-management capacity. The scale demonstrated good reliability with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.908.

QoL

Health-related QoL was evaluated using the SF-36 [17]. The instrument comprises eight dimensions and scores for each dimension range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better QoL. The SF-36 has been widely validated in cardiovascular populations and demonstrates satisfactory psychometric properties. The scale demonstrated Cronbach’s α coefficient of ranging from 0.72 to 0.88. The norm-based scoring method was used to calculate two aggregate summary scores: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS) [18].

Health knowledge

Health knowledge was assessed using a self-designed health knowledge questionnaire specifically developed for patients with AD. The questionnaire encompasses five domains: disease-related knowledge, dietary management, blood pressure management, exercise management, and daily life management, comprising a total of 25 items. Each item offers three response options: “correct,” “incorrect,” and “uncertain.” Correct responses are awarded 1 point, while incorrect responses or selection of the “uncertain” option are scored as 0 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 25, with higher scores indicating a greater level of health knowledge. The questionnaire was administered at baseline (before the intervention) and at 1-month post-discharge to evaluate changes in patients’ health knowledge between the two groups.

Adverse clinical outcomes

All adverse clinical events were adjudicated by an independent committee consisting of two senior cardiovascular surgeons and one cardiologist who were blinded to group allocation. Unplanned readmission was defined as any hospital admission not scheduled for routine follow-up within 30 days post-discharge, with the specific reason for each readmission recorded and categorized. AD recurrence was identified through CTA imaging confirmation during follow-up visits or emergency admissions within the 6-month observation period, and was further characterized as: (a) extension of the original dissection flap, (b) new dissection at a previously unaffected aortic segment, or (c) aneurysmal degeneration or expansion of residual false lumen. Other adverse events including cerebrovascular accident (CVA), acute myocardial infarction (MI), arrhythmia requiring treatment, acute renal insufficiency, wound complications, and deep vein thrombosis were documented using standardized cardiovascular endpoint definitions.

Quality control

All research team members, including nurses from the cardiac and vascular surgery department, received uniform training on the study protocol, intervention procedures, and data collection methods before study initiation. Training sessions covered the theoretical foundation of the teach-back method, proper administration of measurement instruments, and standardized communication techniques with patients. Competency was assessed by the head nurse, and only those who demonstrated proficiency were authorized to participate in the study.

The health education manual for postoperative AD patients was reviewed and refined by senior nursing specialists and cardiovascular surgeons with expertise in AD management. A pilot study involving 8 patients was conducted prior to full implementation to identify potential issues in the intervention protocol and data collection procedures. Based on feedback from the pilot phase, minor adjustments were made to optimize the intervention delivery and assessment processes. All intervention materials, including educational videos and WeChat content, were standardized to ensure consistency in delivery. During data collection, questionnaires were checked immediately upon completion for missing or invalid responses, and participants were asked to provide clarification when necessary. Incomplete questionnaires that could not be rectified were excluded from analysis. Data entry was performed independently by two research personnel using EpiData 3.0 software. After initial entry, both datasets were cross-checked to identify and resolve any discrepancies. All raw data were stored securely and accessible only to authorized research team members to maintain confidentiality and data integrity.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed following the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, where participants were analyzed according to their original group assignment regardless of protocol adherence or study completion. For participants with missing follow-up data, the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method was used for the primary ITT analysis. Sensitivity analyses using per-protocol analysis (restricted to participants completing all assessments) and multiple imputation (m = 20 iterations under the missing-at-random assumption) were also conducted.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.3.1 (lme4 and lmerTest packages). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median with interquartile range.

For primary outcomes measured at multiple time points (SMAS and SF-36 scores), linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used to examine the fixed effects of group (control vs. experimental), time (baseline, discharge, 1-month, 6-month), and the group × time interaction, with participant-specific random intercepts to account for the correlated nature of repeated measurements. The model was specified as: Yiⱼ = β₀ + β₁(Group) + β₂(Time) + β₃(Group × Time) + ui + εiⱼ. Time was modeled as a categorical variable to allow non-linear trajectories. Significance of the interaction term was assessed using likelihood ratio tests, and F-statistics with P-values were obtained using Satterthwaite’s approximation for degrees of freedom. LMMs were chosen over repeated measures ANOVA for their robustness to missing data, relaxed sphericity assumptions, and ability to model within-subject correlations. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means with Bonferroni adjustment.

For SMAS subscale scores showing significant departures from normality at specific time points, non-parametric sensitivity analyses were performed using the Friedman test (within-group changes) and the Mann-Whitney U test (between-group comparisons). For health knowledge scores assessed at two time points (baseline and 1-month), within-group changes were analyzed using paired t-tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed data.

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. For adverse clinical outcomes, we performed the following analyses: (1) cumulative incidence functions (CIF) using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model with death treated as a competing risk for non-repeating events (AD recurrence and non-fatal adverse events); (2) the mean cumulative function (MCF) using the Nelson-Aalen estimator for potentially repeating events (readmissions); and (3) multivariable logistic regression adjusting for AD classification, surgical procedure, age, and sex to address potential baseline imbalances. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Workflow and study population

The workflow of this study as shown in Fig. 1. A total of 231 patients were initially screened for eligibility. Twenty-one patients were excluded due to psychiatric disorders (n = 5), severe complications (n = 8), refusal to participate (n = 6), and malignant tumors (n = 2). The remaining 210 patients were allocated to either the control group (n = 105) or experimental group (n = 105) based on ward assignment. All 210 participants completed baseline and discharge assessments. At 1-month follow-up, 102 patients in the control group (97.1%) and 104 in the experimental group (99.0%) completed questionnaires; at 6-month follow-up, 98 patients in the control group (93.3%) and 101 in the experimental group (96.2%) completed assessments. Reasons for incomplete follow-up included relocation (control: n = 3; experimental: n = 2), withdrawal of consent (control: n = 2; experimental: n = 1), and death during follow-up (control: n = 2; experimental: n = 1). Dropout rates did not differ significantly between groups at any time point (all P > 0.05). Sensitivity analyses using per-protocol and multiple imputation approaches yielded results consistent with the primary ITT analysis.

Blood pressure control

Blood pressure measurements were performed at each assessment visit using calibrated electronic sphygmomanometers following standardized protocols (seated position, 5-minute rest, average of two readings). At baseline, blood pressure values were comparable between groups (Table 3). Both groups demonstrated improved blood pressure control during hospitalization. After discharge, the experimental group maintained significantly lower systolic blood pressure (SBP) at 1-month (126.2 ± 11.2 vs. 131.6 ± 13.5 mmHg, P = 0.002) and 6-month follow-up (125.6 ± 10.8 vs. 133.4 ± 14.8 mmHg, P < 0.001). Similar patterns were observed for diastolic blood pressure (DBP). All patients with elevated blood pressure received antihypertensive therapy according to institutional protocols targeting SBP < 130 mmHg and DBP < 80 mmHg. Antihypertensive regimens were managed by treating physicians and were not part of the study intervention protocol. However, the teach-back intervention included blood pressure self-monitoring education and medication adherence reinforcement. The proportion of patients achieving target blood pressure (< 130/80 mmHg) at 6 months was significantly higher in the experimental group (78.4% vs. 61.2%, χ²=7.257, P = 0.007).

Adverse clinical outcomes

The incidence of adverse clinical outcomes compared between groups is shown in Table 5. The experimental group demonstrated significantly lower rates of unplanned readmission within 30 days compared to the control group (6.67% vs. 17.14%, P = 0.016). In the control group (n = 18 readmissions), the primary reasons included uncontrolled hypertension requiring intravenous therapy (n = 6, 33.3%), chest or back pain requiring diagnostic workup (n = 5, 27.8%), suspected AD progression confirmed by imaging (n = 3, 16.7%), surgical wound complications (n = 2, 11.1%), and pleural effusion requiring drainage (n = 2, 11.1%). In the experimental group (n = 7 readmissions), reasons included chest pain evaluation (n = 3, 42.9%), uncontrolled hypertension (n = 2, 28.6%), and lower respiratory tract infection (n = 2, 28.6%). Nelson-Aalen mean cumulative function analysis for readmissions yielded 0.190 events per patient in the control group versus 0.067 in the experimental group (P = 0.012).

In the control group (n = 15 recurrences), patterns included extension of the original dissection flap (n = 7, 46.7%), new dissection at a previously unaffected aortic segment (n = 3, 20.0%), and aneurysmal degeneration of residual false lumen (n = 5, 33.3%). In the experimental group (n = 5 recurrences), all were dissection extension (n = 2) or false lumen progression (n = 3), managed with endovascular intervention (n = 1) and conservative treatment (n = 4). Cumulative incidence function analysis with death as a competing risk yielded a 6-month cumulative incidence of AD recurrence of 14.56% (95% CI: 8.34–21.87%) in the control group versus 4.85% (95% CI: 1.59–10.98%) in the experimental group (Gray’s test P = 0.018).

A detailed breakdown of other adverse events is presented in Table 5. While individual event types did not reach statistical significance due to low event rates, the overall composite adverse event rate was significantly lower in the experimental group (4.76% vs. 12.38%, P = 0.048). Multivariable logistic regression adjusting for AD classification, surgical procedure, age, and sex confirmed that the intervention remained independently associated with lower odds of 30-day readmission (adjusted OR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.13–0.87, P = 0.024), 6-month AD recurrence (adjusted OR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.10–0.85, P = 0.023), and total adverse events (adjusted OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.12–0.99, P = 0.047), in Supplementary Table S1.

Outpatient follow-up adherence

Per institutional protocol, all postoperative AD patients were scheduled for routine outpatient follow-up at 1, 3, and 6 months post-discharge with their treating cardiovascular surgeons, independent of study assessments. The experimental group demonstrated significantly higher adherence to all three scheduled appointments (91.2% vs. 77.6% complete attendance, χ²=7.425, P = 0.006). The mean number of total outpatient visits (scheduled plus unscheduled) during the 6-month period was 3.82 ± 1.18 in the experimental group versus 3.25 ± 1.46 in the control group (P = 0.003). Most unscheduled visits in the experimental group involved routine concerns (blood pressure fluctuations, medication inquiries), whereas unscheduled visits in the control group more frequently resulted in emergency evaluations or readmissions.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that teach-back method-based continuing nursing significantly improved self-management behaviors and QoL in patients following AD surgery. The intervention group exhibited superior scores across all self-management dimensions and total scores at discharge, 1-month, and 6-month follow-up compared to the control group, with QoL showing similar positive trends. Additionally, the intervention group demonstrated enhanced disease knowledge mastery, reduced 30-day unplanned readmission rates, and lower 6-month AD recurrence rates. These findings provide novel evidence-based insights for postoperative care of AD patients.

The effectiveness of the teach-back method in enhancing patient disease knowledge and self-management capacity aligns with recent cardiovascular research. Oh et al. [11] confirmed that discharge education utilizing the teach-back method significantly reduced readmission rates among heart failure patients. The present study obtained similar positive outcomes in AD patients, suggesting the teach-back method’s cross-disease applicability as an interactive educational strategy. The most pronounced improvement occurred in disease knowledge management, likely attributable to the teach-back method’s protocol of repeatedly confirming patient comprehension and promptly correcting misconceptions [19]. Zhang et al. [4] revealed that postoperative AD patients commonly experience deficient disease-related knowledge and inadequate disease management behaviors. The intervention specifically targeted these core challenges through structured educational content and feedback mechanisms to establish correct disease cognition. Beyond knowledge acquisition, the intervention enhanced self-efficacy across multiple dimensions including psychological management, symptom monitoring, and treatment adherence, reflecting a holistic approach to patient empowerment. In addition, health literacy plays a pivotal role in chronic disease self-management. Zou et al. [20] demonstrated that health literacy directly influences disease management self-efficacy in chronic disease patients through the mediating effect of social support. The teach-back method, as an effective tool for enhancing health literacy, not only transmits information but emphasizes assessing patients’ comprehension and application capabilities [21]. The observed self-efficacy enhancement in this study was likely achieved through this pathway. A recent cross-sectional study involving chronic disease patients further confirmed that developing critical health literacy abilities represents a more effective approach to enhancing self-management behaviors than relying solely on social support or self-confidence [22]. This study’s integration of the teach-back method with the WeChat platform provided patients with continuous educational support and feedback opportunities. This remote continuing care modality has demonstrated favorable feasibility and effectiveness in Chinese cardiovascular disease management [23]. The combination of face-to-face education during hospitalization with post-discharge remote support created a seamless care continuum that addressed the documented communication gaps between patients and healthcare providers. [4]

Previous research indicated that QoL in postoperative AD patients frequently deteriorates over time, particularly regarding physical functioning [6, 7]. Both groups in the present study demonstrated gradual improvements in PCS and MCS scores over the 6-month follow-up period, with the experimental group achieving significantly better outcomes than the control group at all post-intervention time points. Notably, a significant time×group interaction effect was observed for MCS but not for PCS, suggesting that the teach-back method-based intervention exerted a more pronounced time-dependent effect on mental health compared to physical health recovery [7]. A latent profile analysis of health-related QoL in postoperative AD patients indicated that reduced cognitive representations and fear may enhance QoL, with continuous care facilitating self-management behaviors being essential [24]. Through regular follow-up and personalized guidance, this study helped patients establish correct disease cognition, alleviated psychological burden, and improved QoL across both physical and psychological dimensions. The sustained improvement in MCS at 6-month follow-up demonstrates the long-term sustainability of teach-back interventions in maintaining mental well-being, even as physical recovery plateaus. The WeChat platform played an instrumental role as the intervention vehicle. As China’s most widely utilized social application, WeChat possesses easy operability and diverse functionality, having been proven effective in various chronic disease management contexts. [25] Duan et al. [25] recent study confirmed that WeChat mini program-based health behavior interventions effectively promoted multiple health behavior changes among cardiovascular disease patients during home-based rehabilitation. This study leveraged the WeChat platform to disseminate educational content, establish patient communication groups, and conduct remote follow-up, overcoming geographical limitations and achieving real-time physician-patient communication. This hybrid intervention model combining in-person education during hospitalization with post-discharge remote support provided patients with continuous nursing services [26]. The platform’s accessibility facilitated frequent yet non-intrusive contact, enabling timely identification and management of potential complications. Importantly, the experimental group demonstrated significantly better blood pressure control at 6 months, with a higher proportion achieving target levels. Given that uncontrolled hypertension is the most important modifiable risk factor for AD progression and recurrence, the improved blood pressure management observed in the experimental group likely contributed to the reduced AD recurrence and readmission rates. The teach-back method’s emphasis on self-monitoring skills and medication adherence may have enabled patients to maintain better long-term blood pressure control. Additionally, the significantly higher outpatient follow-up adherence in the experimental group suggests that the intervention enhanced patients’ health-seeking behaviors, facilitating timely detection and management of potential complications.

The study holds important implications for clinical practice. For AD as a high-risk condition, postoperative management priorities should shift from merely focusing on survival rates toward comprehensively enhancing patients’ long-term rehabilitation quality. Nursing personnel should incorporate the teach-back method into routine health education protocols, confirming comprehension through patient articulation of key information in their own words rather than simply asking “do you understand.” Content design should encompass disease knowledge, daily life management, medication guidance, emotional management, and symptom monitoring across multiple dimensions, implemented progressively in phases. Healthcare institutions can fully utilize mobile health technologies such as WeChat to establish patient education resource repositories providing video, graphic, and other educational material formats for convenient patient reference and review. Post-discharge continuing care should maintain adequate frequency and duration. Furthermore, medical teams should emphasize cultivating patients’ critical health literacy rather than merely transmitting information, helping patients establish awareness and capacity for active health management participation [27]. Healthcare providers should also receive training in teach-back methodology to ensure consistent and effective implementation across the care team.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the quasi-experimental design with ward-level cluster randomization rather than individual randomization may introduce selection bias. Although baseline characteristics demonstrated group comparability and sensitivity analyses adjusting for AD classification and surgical procedure yielded consistent results, we cannot entirely exclude residual confounding from unmeasured variables. The control group had numerically higher proportions of Stanford Type A dissections and open surgical procedures, which are associated with more complex clinical courses; however, adjusted analyses confirmed the robustness of the intervention effect. Second, although outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation and were independent from the intervention team, the self-report nature of questionnaires means that patients’ awareness of receiving enhanced education could have influenced their responses through social desirability bias. Future studies should consider incorporating objective measures, such as biochemical markers or physiological parameters, to complement self-reported outcomes. Third, conducting the study at a single center in Jiangxi Province with a relatively homogeneous sample may limit the generalizability of findings to other populations with different healthcare systems, cultural backgrounds, or health literacy levels. Fourth, the six-month follow-up period may be insufficient to evaluate long-term intervention effects, as AD patients require lifelong monitoring and the durability of behavioral changes beyond 6 months remains uncertain. Future research should extend observation periods and examine the intervention’s impact on major adverse events including mortality. Finally, this study did not assess intervention cost-effectiveness, which is essential for informing widespread implementation decisions. Future investigations should consider multi-center randomized controlled designs incorporating diverse patient populations, formal economic evaluations, and exploration of how patients of varying health literacy levels respond to teach-back methods to develop more personalized intervention protocols.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that teach-back method-based continuing nursing following AD surgery. The intervention successfully enhanced patients’ disease knowledge, self-care capabilities across multiple domains, and overall health outcomes while reducing adverse clinical events. The integration of the teach-back method with mobile phone application-based remote follow-up provided a feasible and sustainable approach to bridging the gap between hospital discharge and home-based recovery. These findings support the implementation of structured, theory-driven educational interventions as an essential component of postoperative care for AD patients.
