Older adults and online health misinformation: a systematic literature review
Authors: Hongchao Hu, Guangyao Jin, Xiaomeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs older adults increasingly rely on the internet for health-related information, concerns about their vulnerability to online health misinformation have intensified. This systematic literature review synthesizes findings from 26 empirical studies published between 2000 and 2024 to examine how older adults interact with health misinformation in digital contexts. The review identifies a complex interplay of factors contributing to their susceptibility, including cognitive decline, gaps in digital and health literacy, emotional and psychological influences, and the role of social relationships. However, most studies lack theoretical grounding, platform-specific analysis, and geographical diversity. Conceptual inconsistencies and a COVID-19 research bias further limit generalizability. The review calls for clearer definitions, interdisciplinary approaches, and targeted interventions to address this urgent public health issue and provide a foundation for designing more targeted and effective interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04714-z.Keywords: Older adults, Health misinformation, Systematic literature review, Health communication, Social media

Introduction

The rise of online health misinformation

Over the past two decades, the Internet has fundamentally transformed the production, dissemination, and consumption of health information. Unlike traditional media, which rely on professional editorial oversight, social media platforms enable user-generated content to circulate in a decentralized, rapid, and emotionally driven manner. While this open environment has expanded access to information, it has also accelerated the spread of health misinformation. Health misinformation refers to health-related content that lacks scientific verification and may be false or misleading [1]. A growing body of evidence indicates that exposure to such content is closely associated with increased vaccine hesitancy, disrupted chronic disease management, reduced willingness to seek medical care, and declining trust in public health institutions [2–6]. The absence of structured expert review mechanisms on social media platforms allows false claims, pseudoscientific narratives, and intentionally deceptive content to circulate alongside evidence-based medical information, posing serious risks to public health decision-making [7, 8].

Although “health misinformation” has become a commonly used term in academic discourse, its conceptual boundaries remain unclear. The information disorder framework proposed by Wardle and Derakhshan [9] conceptually distinguishes three phenomena: misinformation (inaccurate information shared without intent to deceive), disinformation (false information deliberately created and disseminated), and malinformation (genuine information used maliciously to cause harm). However, these terms are often used interchangeably or conflated with related concepts such as “fake news”, “rumors”, “conspiracy theories” and “pseudoscience” [10, 11].

In this review, we adopt an inclusive operational definition that encompasses all digital health content lacking scientific verification or contradicting the best available evidence. This approach is justified for two main reasons. First, users are often unable to distinguish between unintentional errors and deliberate deception when evaluating content in real time [12]. Second, from a public health perspective, misinformation can cause harm such as delayed medical treatment and poor adherence to medical advice, regardless of whether it is shared intentionally or unintentionally. Accordingly, this review examines how different studies define and measure misinformation and considers how conceptual heterogeneity affects research on older adults’ susceptibility to health misinformation.

The distinct susceptibility and heterogeneity of older adults

Although health misinformation poses risks to individuals across all age groups, older adults warrant particular scholarly attention. First, the potential health consequences are often more severe. Older adults are significantly more likely to manage multiple chronic conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, and polypharmacy is common in this population [13, 14]. As a result, health decisions based on misinformation may directly lead to serious complications, preventable hospitalizations, or even life-threatening outcomes [15, 16]. Among older adults, who are disproportionately susceptible to online health misinformation [17], misinformation-driven health choices are not merely associated with delays in preventive behaviors but may produce irreversible health damage, including significantly elevated mortality risk [18].

Second, older adults exhibit complex cognitive and information-processing characteristics. Normal aging is associated with declines in working memory, processing speed, and inhibitory control, which may reduce the cognitive resources available for effortful source evaluation and logical verification, and these processes are central to identifying misinformation [17, 19, 20]. At the same time, older adults often maintain or even strengthen abilities related to emotional regulation, experiential judgment, and the integration of contextual knowledge, and their accumulated health knowledge and life experience can serve as protective factors in certain situations [21, 22]. Accordingly, older adults’ susceptibility to misinformation reflects not cognitive decline alone, but the interplay between cognitive capacities and the characteristics of digital information environments [23].

Third, compared with other age groups, adolescents, for instance, are also susceptible to misinformation, yet they typically possess higher levels of digital fluency and faster information-processing speed, while lacking accumulated health experience and well-developed source evaluation skills [24, 25]. In contrast, older adults bring decades of health knowledge and experience navigating healthcare systems. However, many of them must adapt to unfamiliar digital environments, including algorithmically curated information feeds and platform norms that differ from those encountered during their earlier media socialization [26]. Moreover, institutional trust, which is shaped through lifelong interactions with social and healthcare systems, exhibits qualitative differences across generations.

Although this review focuses on older adults as an analytical group, substantial heterogeneity exists within this population. Older adults differ markedly in education, socioeconomic status, race and ethnicity, cultural background, geographic location, and patterns of digital use [27, 28]. Socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals, racial and ethnic minorities, rural residents, and those with lower educational attainment tend to face greater challenges in health literacy, functional capacity, and access to healthcare services, and these structural inequalities are often compounded by ageism, language barriers, and medical mistrust [29–31]. While some older adults demonstrate advanced digital skills and critical evaluation strategies [32], others encounter additional barriers due to limited broadband access, unaffordable devices, or social isolation [33, 34]. Recognizing this diversity is essential to avoid stereotyping older adults as a uniformly vulnerable group [35, 36].

Older adults’ digital health information environment

The global population structure is undergoing a profound transformation. According to the World Health Organization [37], the population aged 60 and above has surpassed children under 5, and is projected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050. Alongside this demographic shift, older adults’ levels of digital engagement are also rapidly increasing. Although healthcare professionals, particularly primary care physicians, remain the primary and most trusted sources of health information for most older adults [2, 38], and many older adults continue to rely on traditional offline channels, such as community health lectures, printed materials, pharmacist consultations, telephone hotlines, and advice from family and peers [39, 40], a growing number of older adults are turning to digital platforms as supplementary channels. These platforms are primarily used to seek second opinions, clarify medical instructions, manage daily health concerns, or fill information gaps when professional consultation is not readily accessible [41]. In other words, older adults are adopting a hybrid information strategy that integrates professional medical guidance, interpersonal networks, institutional resources, and online tools.

However, this hybrid strategy also introduces new risks. While digital platforms can facilitate inclusive communication and peer support, particularly for underserved or geographically isolated populations, they also expose older adults to fragmented, algorithmically curated, and often misleading information environments [42, 43]. In these environments, evidence-based medical content coexists with commercial promotions, personal anecdotes, pseudoscience, and deliberately false information, creating a high-risk ecosystem where the boundaries between credible and unreliable sources are blurred [7, 44]. It is precisely when older adults independently use digital platforms without professional supervision that the risks and potential harms of encountering misinformation may be most significant. Even older users with adequate digital access may lack the critical literacy skills necessary for discernment, making it an urgent research imperative to understand how older adults navigate this complex information environment and identify and respond to health misinformation.

Research gaps and the positioning of this review

Against the backdrop of population aging and the growing reliance on digital health information, scholars have increasingly turned their attention to the intersection between older adults and online health misinformation. However, despite the expanding body of empirical research, existing knowledge remains fragmented. Previous systematic reviews have addressed related but conceptually distinct topics. Some have focused on older adults’ general online health information-seeking behaviors and motivations [41, 45], the digital divide and patterns of technology adoption [26, 28], or cognitive and psychosocial factors shaping internet use in later life [46, 47]. Others have synthesized health misinformation research more broadly, concentrating on specific health domains such as vaccine hesitancy [48], cancer-related misinformation [49], or pandemic-related infodemics [50, 51]. Still others have examined the structural dynamics of misinformation diffusion at the platform level [52, 53].

Although Wang et al. [5] and Zhang et al. [54] systematically reviewed research on health misinformation on social media, they did not specifically focus on older adults as a distinct population group with unique characteristics. As a result, existing reviews have yet to comprehensively integrate empirical evidence on how older adults, as a population marked by distinctive health susceptibility, cognitive aging processes, generational media socialization, and digital literacy challenges, engage with online health misinformation across different contexts, platforms, and health topics. Meanwhile, the concept of “older adults” itself lacks a uniform definition. Studies vary considerably in the age thresholds applied, with lower bounds ranging from 50 to 65 years, and differ in whether they adopt chronological, functional, or self-reported criteria [13, 55]. Similarly, the conceptual boundaries of health misinformation remain unsettled. As noted above, terms such as misinformation, disinformation, fake news, rumors, and pseudoscience are frequently used interchangeably across studies, reflecting unresolved definitional boundaries that complicate cross-study comparison.

This systematic literature review addresses these gaps. First, we identify patterns of conceptual heterogeneity, specifically in how key constructs including older adults, health misinformation, susceptibility, health literacy, and digital literacy are defined and operationalized across studies, and examine how such variation shapes research findings. By documenting how key concepts are defined and measured across studies, we highlight sources of heterogeneity that may explain inconsistent results. Second, we assess the use of theoretical frameworks and identify existing applications and major gaps. We identify which perspectives have been applied, which remain underutilized, and which mediating and moderating mechanisms remain untested. Third, we examine the geographic concentration of research and its implications for generalizability. The existing literature appears to be heavily concentrated in a small number of national contexts, which carries important theoretical implications. Given fundamental differences in media systems, political environments, healthcare arrangements, and cultural norms, patterns observed in these settings cannot be uncritically generalized to other regions. This review addresses the following research questions:

RQ1: What systematic patterns, biases, and gaps characterize the current research landscape, and how do these structural features shape or distort existing understanding?

Methods

Design and search strategy

This review was designed and conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [56], and emphasized transparency, reproducibility, and methodological rigor rather than exploratory mapping. Its primary objective is to synthesize empirical findings on older adults and online health misinformation published since the beginning of the 21 st century.

A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple academic databases, including Web of Science (WoS), PubMed, Scopus, Medline, PsycINFO, PsycArticles, and the Psychology and Behavioral Sciences Collection, covering publications from January 1, 2000 to December 31, 2024. The selection of these seven databases was strategically designed to ensure a comprehensive, interdisciplinary synthesis of literature at the intersection of gerontology, psychology, and health communication. PubMed and Medline provided authoritative coverage of biomedical and life sciences research. Given the significant psychological components of misinformation susceptibility, PsycINFO, PsycArticles, and the Psychology and Behavioral Sciences Collection were included to capture deep psychological and behavioral insights. Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus served as broad multidisciplinary engines to capture social science and communication studies. The search strategy employed a combination of keywords and Boolean operators (AND, OR), targeting relevant terms in titles, abstracts, keywords, subject headings, and full texts (see Table 1 for detailed search terms). The search strategy was developed by the authors based on established guidelines and relevant literature. In addition, the strategy was reviewed by a researcher with a background in information and resource management to assess its clarity and coverage.

Data extraction

For each of the 26 included studies, we extracted and analyzed the following elements: (1) the study aims and research questions; (2) theoretical perspectives and frameworks (if any); (3) the methods used; (4) the country or region of the sample; (5) the specific social media platform used (if any); (6) the specific topic of health misinformation used (if any); (7) summary of outcomes. All extracted elements and variables were defined in advance to allow systematic comparison across studies.

Quality appraisal

To evaluate the methodological rigor of the included studies, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [57] was employed. The MMAT is specifically designed for the critical appraisal stage of systematic reviews and is applicable to qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies. Based on study design, the MMAT categorizes empirical research into five groups: (1) qualitative research, (2) randomized controlled trials, (3) non-randomized studies, (4) quantitative descriptive studies, and (5) mixed methods studies.

The checklist consists of two general screening questions and five methodological criteria tailored to each study type. Each criterion is rated as “Yes”, “No”, or “Cannot tell” (see Supplementary Table S1). In line with MMAT guidelines, we did not compute an overall numeric score; instead, we assessed the extent to which each study met the relevant criteria. Two reviewers independently assessed all eligible articles, and the analysis yielded a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.85, indicating almost agreement between the two reviewers. Any discrepancies in scoring were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. For the purposes of this review, studies were deemed to be of sufficient quality if they met both screening questions and satisfied at least three out of five specific criteria (i.e., five or more “Yes” responses). All 26 studies met the established quality thresholds and were included in the final analysis. A detailed summary of the appraisal results is provided in supplementary materials (see Supplementary Table S2).

Results

Description of selected studies

The temporal distribution of the selected studies reveals a marked surge in scholarly attention to older adults and health misinformation in the past five years, with a pronounced peak in 2021 (n = 10), as illustrated in Fig. 2. While this increase clearly coincides with the COVID-19 pandemic, it should also be understood in relation to longer-term transformations in the digital media environment. In the early 2000 s, social media platforms were still emerging and smartphones were not yet part of everyday information practices. By contrast, contemporary health communication increasingly unfolds within platform-based, mobile, and highly networked environments, where misinformation can spread rapidly and repeatedly. As older adults have become more active users of digital media and online health resources, their encounters with health misinformation have become both more common and more visible as a research concern. The lack of included studies before 2020 may therefore reflect not only the catalytic effect of COVID-19, but also the gradual maturation of a digital environment in which this problem became more salient and empirically tractable.

Studies included in systematic review

An analysis of the geographic distribution of the included studies reveals a strong concentration of evidence in a few countries. Specifically, China (n = 11), the United States (n = 7), and the United Kingdom (n = 3) together accounted for nearly 81% of all included studies, with China and the United States emerging as the most prolific contributors (see Fig. 3).

Countries of studies

In terms of methodology, the majority of studies (n = 21) employed a single-method approach, while five adopted mixed-method designs. Among the specific methods used, questionnaire surveys were the most common (n = 11), followed by experimental designs (n = 8), and interviews (n = 7). Content analysis, thematic analysis, and machine learning techniques were each used in a different study.

Only four studies explicitly adopted a platform-based perspective, focusing on social media platforms such as WhatsApp, WeChat, and Facebook. In terms of population focus, 14 studies were dedicated exclusively to older adults, while 12 employed cross-age comparisons. Considerable heterogeneity was observed in how “older adults” were defined. Five studies used 50 years as the lower threshold, six used 55, three used 60, and another three defined older adults as 65 and above (see Fig. 4). Notably, eight studies did not specify any age criteria. Among the 18 studies that did provide a specific age threshold, only three offered a theoretical or empirical rationale for their classification, while the remaining 15 lacked explicit justification.

Experimental studies constituted the second most frequently used method in the reviewed literature (n = 8). As summarized in Table 3, these studies covered a range of designs, including randomized online experiments, repeated-measures interventions, interface-based experiments, and structured credibility-evaluation tasks. Of the eight experimental studies, six employed age-comparative frameworks, whereas two focused exclusively on older adults. This pattern indicates that experimental methods have been used not only to examine older adults’ susceptibility to health misinformation, but also to compare age-related differences in credibility judgment, sharing intention, and responses to corrective interventions.

Conceptualizing health misinformation

Across the reviewed literature, a variety of terms were employed to describe the phenomenon of misleading or inaccurate health-related information disseminated on social media platforms. The most frequently used term was misinformation, adopted in the majority of studies (e.g., [58, 59, 61–64, 66–75]. One study explicitly employed multiple terms interchangeably, most commonly misinformation and fake news (e.g., [76]. Other terms such as rumors (e.g., [77–79], fake news (e.g., [60, 80], misleading information (e.g., [81], and unreliable or non-credible health information (e.g., [65, 82, 83] were also observed, though less commonly.

Despite the frequent usage of these terms, the reviewed studies vary in their conceptual depth. Out of the 26 reviewed papers, only 6 studies (23%) offered explicit conceptual definitions or theoretical distinctions, while the remaining 20 studies (77%) focused on operational definitions by using specific stimuli.

Among the 6 studies providing refined conceptual frameworks, authors explained the boundaries of misinformation or other adjacent concepts. For instance, Duplaga [76] offered a detailed distinction among misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation. Choudhury [68] and Vijaykumar et al. [58] conceptualized it as a “shades of truth” spectrum rather than a binary truth-falsehood. Sun et al. [78] grounded their definition in classic rumor theory, while Pehlivanoglu et al. [60] defined the phenomenon through a cognitive veracity detection framework. Finally, Peng et al. [73] offered a novel framework by defining health misinformation through its persuasive strategies, such as emotional manipulation and pseudo-scientific evidence.

The remaining 20 studies operationalized misinformation (or other adjacent concepts) through two primary dimensions. First, 14 studies defined it as erroneous or unsubstantiated claims specifically regarding COVID-19 (e.g., [59, 62, 66, 67, 69–72, 74, 77, 80, 83] or the persistence of these myths (e.g., [61, 79]. Second, another 6 studies operationalized the concept through structural or presentation cues, such as deceptive online botanical supplement advertisements (e.g., [81], source-based vs content-based credibility markers (e.g., [75], the use of eye-catching headlines or emotional imagery in fabricated articles (e.g., [63, 64, 84], and multi-modal credibility assessments (e.g., [82]).

Theoretical perspectives and frameworks

While the majority of studies (n = 19, 73%) in this review did not explicitly adopt theoretical frameworks, a small subset of research (n = 7, 27%) applied established theories to guide their investigation into health misinformation among older adults on social media. These studies drew from a range of perspectives in communication, psychology, and information science, reflecting the multidimensional nature of the phenomenon.

Some studies employed communication and persuasion models to explain how misinformation was received and transmitted. For instance, McGuire’s Communication-Persuasion Matrix and the two-step flow model were used to understand how media exposure and interpersonal influence affect older adults’ beliefs and vaccine intentions [67]. Similarly, the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) was applied to find people who were strongly motivated (e.g., need for cognition) and competent (e.g., knowledge) were able to think analytically, thereby successfully identifying misinformation, and engaged (i.e., read, like, comment, and share) less in misinformation [62].

Cognitive and decision-making theories were also present. Dual-process theory was used to differentiate between intuitive versus analytical processing of information, and the theory of planned behavior was applied to link attitudes and behavioral intentions regarding vaccination [80].

Other studies incorporated models of information evaluation and diffusion. The 3As model was used to conceptualize older adults’ internal and external strategies for assessing information credibility on platforms like WeChat [82]. Theories such as rumor propagation, information diffusion, and social network theory were employed to explore how misinformation spreads and what individual or structural factors influence sharing behavior [78].

In addition, age-specific models such as the CISDA framework (Changes in Integration for Social Decisions in Aging) were applied to explain how reasoning, affect, and life experience shape older adults’ ability to assess the veracity of information [60]. In the consumer decision-making domain, involvement theory was used to investigate how cognitive and emotional engagement affect older adults’ responses to misleading health product advertising [81].

Key factors influencing older adults’ engagement with health misinformation and its effects

Based on the selected literature, five key factors were identified as shaping how older adults encounter, interpret, and respond to health misinformation: (1) demographic characteristics, (2) cognitive and information-processing ability, (3) media exposure, (4) psychological state, and (5) social relationships. A limited number of studies also examined the downstream effects of health misinformation on older adults. To ensure clarity regarding the source of evidence, this section reports only descriptive findings drawn from the studies included in the review, while interpretive analysis of broader trends and inconsistencies is developed further in the Discussion section.

Demographic characteristics

Demographic factors such as age [58, 60, 61, 63, 64], education level [72, 75, 76], occupation [66, 76], employment status [72], marital status [76], place of residence [83], and income level [70] have also been shown to influence older adults’ engagement with health misinformation. However, these findings are often inconsistent across studies. Considerable variation was also observed in the classification of older adults. Age thresholds ranged from 50 to 65, and studies that were nominally concerned with the same population may in practice have examined different age cohorts. These studies were nevertheless retained because they explicitly framed their focus around older adults and constitute a substantial body of evidence in the health misinformation literature.

Cognitive and information-processing ability

The reviewed studies identified cognitive and information-processing capacity as an important factor in older adults’ responses to misinformation. On one hand, older adults often exhibit lower levels of deliberative, effortful processing when encountering online health content. For example, Sun et al. [78] found that the perceived credibility of rumors significantly predicted older adults’ willingness to share them. Many participants in their study placed excessive trust in health-related rumors and lacked either the motivation or the intent to critically evaluate message content. Similarly, studies have indicated that older adults tend to have a lower need for cognition, making them less inclined to engage in deep reading or fact-checking and more prone to relying on emotionally charged headlines [62, 64].

On the other hand, even when older adults are motivated to assess the credibility of information, the reviewed studies suggest that cognitive limitations may constrain their performance. Brashier and Schacter [17] emphasized that cognitive aging reduces the ability to discern misleading content, especially when persuasive tactics such as pseudoscientific language, politicized health topics, or emotional framing are used. Peng et al. [73] similarly reported that cognitive decline increases susceptibility to these rhetorical strategies. Matchanova et al. [63] found that older adults with lower cognitive capacity were significantly less accurate in judging the truthfulness of COVID-19 headlines, while Pehlivanoglu et al. [60] showed that weaker analytical reasoning predicted greater difficulty in detecting fake news.

In addition to cognitive capacity, both digital literacy and health literacy are widely regarded as important for navigating the digital health information environment [17, 84]. Empirical findings, however, suggest that their effects are more complex than often assumed. For example, Seo et al. [75] observed that older adults with formal computer training showed greater discernment when evaluating online health claims. By contrast, Duplaga [76] found that although higher health literacy was modestly associated with lower endorsement of conspiracy beliefs, the effect was limited. More counterintuitively, older adults with higher eHealth literacy scores were more likely to believe in health-related conspiracies. We will explore this interesting finding during our discussion. Overall, the reviewed studies suggest that the relationship between literacy-related variables and misinformation susceptibility is mixed rather than uniformly protective.

Media exposure

Our analysis suggests that increased exposure to health information can impair older adults’ ability to detect misinformation [60] and influence their attitudes toward scientific and health content, such as COVID-19 vaccines [67]. Although older adults are often considered more vulnerable to digital misinformation due to the digital divide, Choudrie et al. [69] found that they still primarily rely on traditional media (e.g., television, radio) rather than digital platforms (e.g., social media) for COVID-19-related updates. This media preference may reduce their exposure to online misinformation relative to younger users.

Psychological state

The reviewed studies identified psychological state as a factor influencing older adults’ engagement with health misinformation. Sun et al. [78] found that anxiety negatively impacted the ability to discern rumours about the COVID-19, whereas Pehlivanoglu et al. [60] reported that positive emotions did not improve older adults’ ability to detect misinformation. Taken together, these findings suggest that the relationship between emotional factors and misinformation processing varies across studies. This inconsistency is explored further in the Discussion section. Additionally, Wang et al. [79] found that low health self-efficacy may increase older adults’ attention to health-related content, thereby raising the risk of encountering and engaging with misinformation.

Social relationships

Close social ties may both reduce and reinforce older adults’ engagement with health misinformation. On one hand, strong ties such as family members and close friends can serve as protective filters, helping older adults identify and correct false or misleading information [68].These networks may provide emotional support and assistance in evaluating information. On the other hand, these same strong ties can also become vectors for misinformation. When trusted contacts themselves are misinformed, they may unintentionally pass inaccurate or harmful content to older adults, thereby reinforcing existing misconceptions [67, 79].

The effects of health misinformation on older adults

Beyond the identification of influential factors, only a limited number of studies have examined the specific effects of health misinformation on older adults. A synthesis of the extant literature has yielded three principal effects. Firstly, exposure to misinformation has been demonstrated to increase the propensity of older adults to disseminate or forward false information [62, 64, 78]. Secondly, misinformation exposure has been demonstrated to be associated with heightened negative emotional responses, including anxiety, confusion, fear, and even depressive symptoms [60, 71, 74]. Thirdly, such exposure may result in heightened intentions to purchase unverified or ineffective health products, contributing to economic loss and potential harm [81]. These findings emphasise the necessity of addressing health misinformation as both a cognitive and emotional threat to the well-being of older adults.

Nevertheless, in contrast to the substantial body of research that has examined susceptibility and its associated factors, there is a paucity of studies that have systematically investigated these downstream effects. This imbalance in the literature has the effect of constraining our understanding of the actual social, psychological, and economic consequences of health misinformation for older adults. Furthermore, it limits the ability of existing research to inform targeted interventions and policy responses.

Discussion

This section provides further discussion of the main findings of the review, identifies the key gaps and unresolved challenges in the existing literature, and outlines priorities for future research on older adults and online health misinformation.

Interpreting the main findings

This section further discusses the findings reported in "Key factors influencing older adults’ engagement with health misinformation and its effects" sections, particularly the mixed and sometimes counterintuitive findings concerning demographic characteristics, cognitive and information-processing ability, media exposure, psychological state, and social relationships.

First, the inconsistency in demographic findings may partly reflect contextual differences rather than methodological weakness alone. Much of the existing evidence comes from China and the United States, two settings that differ substantially in their education systems, media environments, and health care arrangements. Under such conditions, variables such as age, education, and income may be linked to misinformation engagement through different mechanisms, producing divergent empirical findings across studies. From this perspective, demographic effects should be interpreted cautiously and in relation to the social and institutional settings in which the studies were conducted.

Second, the mixed findings regarding digital, health, and eHealth literacy suggest that these factors do not function as uniformly protective resources against misinformation. One reason may be that these forms of literacy do not necessarily translate into more careful or more accurate evaluation of information. Even when older adults are motivated to assess credibility, they may still lack the cognitive resources or digital skills needed to do so effectively [85, 86]. This helps explain why literacy-related advantages do not always function as straightforward protection against misinformation and why earlier assumptions about the uniformly beneficial role of health or digital literacy may be overly simplistic (e.g., [87–90]. A related explanation may lie in the way long-term media exposure shapes evaluative habits. Munyaka et al. [91] suggest that sustained exposure to news and media can foster a skeptical or cynical orientation toward information sources without necessarily promoting more systematic verification. In such circumstances, greater digital or eHealth literacy may increase confidence in one’ s own judgment without a corresponding increase in critical scrutiny. As a result, some individuals may become more likely to rely on familiar cues or selectively attend to information that aligns with prior views. This is also consistent with motivated numeracy, which suggests that individuals may use their reasoning capacities to interpret information in ways that reinforce prior beliefs or identity-related commitments rather than to improve accuracy [92]. Although this framework was not developed specifically for health misinformation, it helps explain why greater health-related knowledge or confidence does not always protect against misleading content, especially in highly politicized contexts such as COVID-19. Taken together, these observations suggest that the relationship between literacy and misinformation susceptibility is context-dependent rather than uniformly protective.

Third, media exposure appears to play a mixed role in older adults’ engagement with health misinformation. One possible explanation is that the effects of exposure depend not only on the source or channel of information, but also on the cognitive consequences of repeated contact with similar claims. Repeated exposure to false or misleading information can increase its perceived accuracy through the illusory truth effect, whereby familiarity itself becomes a cue for truthfulness [93, 94]. This mechanism may be especially relevant for older adults, who may rely more on familiarity and processing fluency than on detailed episodic recall when evaluating information [17]. From this perspective, even if older adults rely less on digital platforms than younger users, repeated exposure to misleading claims across media environments may still increase perceived credibility and susceptibility to misinformation.

Fourth, emotional influences on misinformation processing cannot be understood in a uniform manner. Previous research has shown that psychological states may shape credibility judgments differently depending on emotional valence. For example, negative emotions may sometimes heighten vigilance, whereas positive emotions may reduce suspicion toward misleading messages [95–99]. In the context of health misinformation, emotional states may interact with message characteristics, such as uncertainty or emotional framing, as well as with older adults’ coping motivations [100, 101]. Emotional influences may also depend on the digital environments in which information is encountered. In opaque, algorithmically mediated settings, for instance, users may rely on emotional appraisal as an intuitive basis for judgment when clear rational cues are lacking [102]. At the same time, emotional engagement with digital systems may increase perceived trust in, and receptivity to, misleading content [103]. Emotional factors should therefore be understood as conditional and context-dependent influences rather than as stable predictors of older adults’ susceptibility to misinformation [104].

Finally, the dual role of close social relationships suggests that social ties should not be understood simply as either protective or risky. Rather, their effects depend on the informational quality and interpretive capacity of the network itself. Social relationships are not only channels of information transmission, but may also shape the cognitive and emotional framing through which information is received and interpreted [105, 106]. When family members or close friends are able to identify misleading claims, strong ties may help older adults verify and correct problematic information. However, when these trusted contacts are themselves misinformed, the same relational trust may facilitate the transmission and reinforcement of false beliefs. In this sense, strong ties may function both as filters and as amplifiers of health misinformation. This also helps explain the influence of opinion leaders in older adults’ information environments. Perceived as knowledgeable, trustworthy, and persuasive, they may strongly shape how health-related messages are interpreted and shared. Their endorsement can increase the perceived credibility of messages among peers, thereby accelerating the diffusion of misinformation, particularly when they encourage specific attitudes or behaviors [78, 107].

Research gaps and challenges

Conceptual challenges

Although health misinformation is not a novel phenomenon, its rapid proliferation in the digital era, particularly through social media, has attracted increasing scholarly and public concern. A review of the literature reveals that terms such as non-credible information, misinformation, fake news, and rumors have been variously employed to describe inaccurate or false health-related content [17, 67, 78, 79, 84, 108]. However, the inconsistent and interchangeable use of these terms across studies creates conceptual ambiguity, hindering efforts to synthesize findings and conduct cross-study comparisons [109].

Moreover, approximately 70% of the included studies focused on COVID-19-related misinformation. While the pandemic provided a valuable context for exploring the spread and impact of health misinformation, this thematic concentration may limit broader theoretical development. The overemphasis on COVID-19 potentially narrows the generalizability of findings to other public health scenarios, thereby constraining our understanding of older adults’ responses to misinformation related to chronic illnesses, mental health, nutrition, or everyday health behaviors.

There is also substantial inconsistency in defining the age threshold for “older adults”. Eight studies failed to specify any age criteria, while the remaining 18 used divergent cutoffs ranging from 50 to 65 years old. Only three studies provided explicit justification for their definitions, citing disciplinary conventions or national retirement age policies [61, 81]. This definitional heterogeneity complicates comparative analysis and limits the external validity of research findings.

Lack of platform-specific perspectives

Despite widespread recognition that social media platforms are major conduits for health misinformation [110], few studies on older adults explicitly adopt a platform-centered lens [82]. Among the 26 studies reviewed, only four examined platform-specific dynamics. This omission obscures the unique roles that algorithms, platform affordances, and network structures play in shaping how older adults encounter and engage with misinformation. For instance, mechanisms such as algorithmic curation and echo chambers remain understudied in the context of aging populations.

Furthermore, most studies focused on platforms like WhatsApp, WeChat, and Facebook, with limited attention to emerging platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and Snapchat, where older adult user bases are reportedly growing [77, 111]. The lack of platform diversity constrains our understanding of how different technological environments may influence exposure to and engagement with health misinformation.

Insufficient theoretical grounding

Despite the growing number of empirical studies in this domain, theoretical development remains limited. Only around 25% of the reviewed studies explicitly incorporated theoretical frameworks into their research design or analysis. More importantly, theory is rarely used to formulate theory-driven hypotheses or to specify testable mechanisms, which limits explanatory leverage and cumulative knowledge building. Several well-established frameworks that directly speak to misinformation judgment and persuasion remain underused in this literature, including dual-process theories such as the Elaboration Likelihood Model and the Heuristic–Systematic Model [86, 112], health behavior theories such as the Health Belief Model and Protection Motivation Theory [113, 114], and aging-relevant perspectives such as Socioemotional Selectivity Theory [21]. As a result, key pathways remain insufficiently theorized, including when older adults rely on heuristic versus systematic processing, how uncertainty and threat shape credibility judgments, and how motivation, trust, and social influence translate exposure into sharing or behavior change. Future research should therefore more explicitly align constructs and measures with a priori mechanisms and derive hypotheses that can be compared across studies.

Limited geographic diversity

A notable shortcoming in the current body of literature is its geographic concentration. More than 65% of the reviewed studies were conducted in China, the United States, or the United Kingdom. Only two studies (i.e., [58, 69] included diverse, multi-country samples. Regions such as Africa, South America, and Oceania are significantly underrepresented, limiting the cross-cultural applicability of findings.

This geographic skew is particularly problematic given the well-documented influence of sociocultural factors on health beliefs, digital behaviors, and susceptibility to misinformation. A large proportion of the existing evidence is drawn from studies conducted in China and the United States, two contexts that differ substantially in their media systems, political communication environments, and health care arrangements. Cultural values, family structures, social support networks, and levels of institutional trust operate differently across these settings and may fundamentally shape how older adults perceive, interpret, and respond to health misinformation. Consequently, findings derived primarily from these two countries should not be assumed to generalize beyond similar contexts. Future research should therefore adopt a more global and context-sensitive approach to capture cross-national variation and to inform culturally responsive strategies.

Methodological limitations

Most studies did not directly measure older adults’ actual exposure to health misinformation. Instead, they relied on self-reported perceptions of exposure, typically obtained through survey methods. Self-reports are vulnerable to recall bias [115], social desirability bias [116], and inaccuracies in estimating media use [117]. Furthermore, the majority of studies adopted cross-sectional designs, limiting the ability to infer causal relationships or assess long-term impacts of misinformation exposure [118].

While some experimental studies explored short-term cognitive or behavioral effects of misinformation [119], few examined long-term consequences or employed longitudinal designs. In addition, qualitative research remains underutilized. Although several studies employed interviews or thematic analysis, quantitative methods predominated. Greater use of qualitative approaches could yield richer insights into how older adults make sense of and emotionally respond to health misinformation within their everyday contexts.

Lastly, most studies employed non-probability or convenience sampling (e.g., [67, 75], raising concerns about sample representativeness and external validity. Without robust sampling techniques, findings may not generalize beyond specific subgroups or settings, limiting their utility for informing policy or intervention design.

Future research directions

In light of the gaps and challenges identified through this systematic literature review, several key directions are proposed to guide future inquiry and strengthen the scholarly foundation of research on older adults and online health misinformation. These directions aim to broaden thematic coverage, refine conceptual clarity, enhance methodological rigor, and advance theoretical development.

Refinement of concepts

Future research should strive to establish clear and consistent definitions of key terms such as misinformation, disinformation, rumors, and fake news in the context of health communication. Precise conceptual delineation is critical not only for academic clarity but also for enabling comparative and cumulative research. Moreover, there is a need to further classify types of health misinformation based on their form, source, and potential harm. A typological framework distinguishing benign inaccuracies, misleading claims, and deceptive content can help guide public health responses and targeted interventions for vulnerable groups such as older adults.

The current literature has been heavily concentrated on misinformation related to COVID-19 and vaccines, leaving other health topics underexplored. Future studies should diversify their focus to include non-communicable diseases (e.g., cancer, diabetes), mental health, health technologies, and wellness products (e.g., supplements and alternative therapies), where misinformation is also prevalent [54]. This broader thematic scope would enhance both external validity and the theoretical generalizability of findings.

Additionally, researchers should adopt more unified and transparent criteria for defining the “older adult” population. This includes citing established classifications from relevant fields and explaining the rationale and implications of specific age thresholds. Consistent age criteria will facilitate more meaningful comparisons across studies.

Expansion of research themes

The interdisciplinary nature of health misinformation research has attracted scholars from medicine, information science, public health, and communication studies. Yet, there remains a paucity of empirical work specifically addressing older adults, hindering a full understanding of how misinformation affects this demographic.

First, future research should identify and validate the psychological, individual, and environmental factors that shape how older adults process and respond to misinformation. Beyond negative emotions such as fear and anxiety, future studies could explore the role of technology acceptance, digital self-efficacy, and media-related anxiety [120, 121]. Personality traits, including dark triad dimensions (e.g., narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy), may also influence susceptibility to and dissemination of misinformation. Moreover, contextual factors such as access to digital infrastructure, availability of technical assistance, and local information ecosystems deserve greater attention, especially given the heterogeneity of aging populations. The consequences of misinformation, from psychological distress to risky health behaviors, also require further empirical examination.

Second, at the message level, future research should investigate the unique characteristics of misinformation targeting older adults. This includes analyzing its linguistic style, framing techniques, and topic relevance, particularly in relation to issues such as chronic disease management, self care practices, and health concerns related to aging. Such inquiries would facilitate the development of misinformation classification frameworks that are more tailored to older adults’ cognitive and affective profiles.

Third, given the central role of social media in health information access, future research should examine how platform dynamics, including algorithmic curation, echo chambers, and social endorsement, shape older adults’ exposure to and interpretation of health content [109]. Platform-specific affordances and constraints should be systematically examined to better understand how misinformation circulates in digital environments inhabited by older users.

Fourth, the role of opinion leaders, that is, individuals perceived as credible, knowledgeable, or influential, should be examined more explicitly. These figures often serve as information intermediaries and can either mitigate or exacerbate misinformation exposure among older adults [122, 123]. Understanding their mediating role in shaping beliefs and behaviors is essential for designing effective communication strategies.

Fifth, given the adverse effects of misinformation, more research should be devoted to designing and evaluating intervention strategies. Although fact checking and media literacy campaigns have been explored, alternative approaches such as emotion based interventions and indirect correction techniques remain underdeveloped and require empirical validation [54]. As video based misinformation becomes more complex on platforms such as TikTok, artificial intelligence tools, including machine learning models and large language models, may help detect, monitor, and correct false content.Future research should therefore promote interdisciplinary collaboration between communication scholars, public health experts, and computer scientists to build comprehensive and adaptive governance mechanisms.

Theoretical enrichment

Theory plays a vital role in guiding systematic data collection, structuring analysis, and advancing generalizable knowledge. Future studies should actively integrate theoretical frameworks from psychology, communication, sociology, information systems, and marketing. An interdisciplinary approach can help uncover the complex mechanisms that underlie misinformation engagement and resistance among older adults. At the same time, future theorizing should move beyond an exclusive focus on individual cognition and literacy to account for the broader social contexts in which digital health information is accessed and interpreted. Factors such as digital inequality, social support, institutional trust, and differential access to digital resources may shape older adults’ encounters with health misinformation in important ways.

Several theoretical perspectives merit further application and integration, including the continued influence effect [124], motivated reasoning [125], filter bubbles [126], the strength of weak ties [127], the sociotechnical systems perspective [128], the Health Belief Model [113], and Self-Determination Theory [129]. In addition, scholarship on digital inequality and the social context of digital media use offers an important lens for understanding age-related differences in digital experience [130, 131]. Incorporating this perspective would help explain older adults’ interactions with health misinformation not only in terms of cognitive processing, but also in relation to social inequalities and contextual conditions.

Methodological advancements

To capture the nuanced realities of older adults’ experiences with misinformation, future research should place greater emphasis on qualitative methods. Approaches such as in-depth interviews, focus groups, and ethnographic observations can reveal subjective interpretations, emotional responses, and contextual influences that are often overlooked in quantitative surveys. Qualitative data can also inform the development of new constructs and hypotheses for future testing.

Additionally, future studies could adapt methodological innovations from the broader misinformation literature to older adult populations. For example, simulated social media environments, such as The (mis)information Game [132], provide a promising tool for observing engagement with misleading content in controlled yet ecologically valid settings. Such designs could be tailored to older adults’ platform use and health information practices. Research on the role of psychological traits and motivated reasoning in misinformation propagation [133] also points to the value of incorporating dispositional variables into studies of older adults’ engagement with health misinformation.

Researchers should consider indirect or behavior based methods to enhance validity and reduce social desirability bias, particularly in sensitive contexts such as the intentional sharing of false health information.These may include simulated information environments, passive data tracking, and real-time behavioral observation. Such methods would allow for a more accurate and ecologically valid understanding of information behaviors.

Lastly, longitudinal designs are crucial for capturing the temporal dynamics of exposure, belief formation, and behavioral change. Most existing studies rely on cross-sectional snapshots, which are insufficient for assessing causality or long-term intervention effectiveness. Longitudinal research can track how misinformation-related beliefs and behaviors evolve over time, especially in response to public health events or technological changes.

Conclusions, implications and limitations

Summary of key conclusions

This systematic review synthesized a growing body of literature on older adults’ engagement with online health misinformation, revealing a complex interplay of cognitive, technological, and sociocultural factors that shape their susceptibility. As older adults increasingly turn to the internet for health information, often without professional medical guidance, exposure to misinformation can have significant consequences. The evidence suggests that older adults face distinctive challenges including difficulty discerning credible sources and experiencing psychological distress triggered by misleading content. While many older adults can perform basic digital tasks, the review highlights that susceptibility is not merely a result of individual deficits but often stems from a mismatch between the sophisticated nature of online misinformation and the varying digital experience of this demographic. Rather than being a uniformly vulnerable group, older adults exhibit significant heterogeneity; their ability to identify misinformation includes the ability to distinguish evidence-based health content from information that appears scientific due to its formatting, language, or emotional appeal. These diverse experiences are compounded by age-related cognitive shifts, lower levels of digital and health literacy, and in some cases, a preexisting mistrust of mainstream health institutions. Psychological and contextual factors, such as anxiety, loneliness, social isolation, or a desire for agency, also influence how older adults process and respond to online health information. These dynamics help explain why emotionally charged misinformation often resonates with this population, emphasizing that health misinformation is not only an information problem but a public health challenge.

Implications for practice and policy

The findings of this review have several implications for practitioners, policymakers, and platform designers, suggesting that effective responses to online health misinformation among older adults require multi-level interventions.

First, there is a clear need for targeted media literacy programs specifically tailored for older adults, and the reviewed studies indicate that interventions should be embedded within older adults’ existing social environments, rather than focusing exclusively on individual skills acquisition. Programs that emphasize concrete evaluative strategies, such as recognizing emotional manipulation, pseudoscientific cues, and misleading health claims, are likely to be more effective when delivered through trusted social settings, including community centers, public libraries, and senior universities. Importantly, leveraging family members, caregivers, and peer mentors as mediators may enhance the effectiveness of these interventions, as social ties can function as protective resources when appropriately supported.

Second, the findings point to a need for institutional and healthcare-based interventions that recognize health misinformation as a public health risk. Primary care providers and community health services are well positioned to play a preventative role by offering brief, evidence-based guidance on evaluating online health information during routine consultations. Integrating misinformation awareness into preventive care, chronic disease management, and health promotion programs may help address both the cognitive and emotional vulnerabilities identified in the literature, particularly among older adults with low health self-efficacy or heightened anxiety.

Third, digital platforms and social media companies must take responsibility to adopt age-inclusive governance strategies. Rather than relying solely on generic warnings or user-initiated fact-checking, platforms could implement “safety by design” measures tailored to older users, such as clearer and high-contrast credibility indicators, simplified explanations of content source, and algorithmic prioritization of authoritative health sources for older adults. Additionally, enabling trusted family members or peers to help older adults assess questionable content privately may reduce the risk of misinformation reinforcement through close social ties.

Taken together, these implications would address online health misinformation among older adults with coordinated actions across individual, social, institutional, and platform levels. By shifting policy attention from isolated skill-building toward socially embedded, institutionally supported, and platform-responsive interventions, future strategies may better reflect the complex pathways through which older adults encounter and respond to online health misinformation.

Limitations

Despite the comprehensiveness of this systematic review, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the review followed systematic procedures, the scope of the database search may have been constrained. The inclusion of only peer-reviewed journal articles indexed in a limited number of academic databases (e.g., Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed) may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies published in other sources, such as conference proceedings, government reports, or dissertations, and the limitation of the perspective breadth.

Second, only studies published in English were included in the review. As a result, potentially valuable research conducted and published in other languages, particularly those from non-English-speaking countries with significant aging populations, may have been overlooked. This language restriction may have affected the cultural diversity of the studies analyzed.

Third, while the review aimed to be comprehensive, the screening, coding, and thematic synthesis processes involved a degree of subjective interpretation. Despite efforts to ensure consistency through predefined inclusion criteria and a structured coding framework, the identification of key themes and categorization of theoretical approaches may have been influenced by researcher judgment.

Finally, the review period coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, during which scholarly output related to misinformation experienced an unprecedented surge. As a result, approximately 70% of the studies included focused on COVID-19-related misinformation. While this offered rich data, it may also limit the generalizability of findings to other types of health misinformation (e.g., concerning chronic illness, mental health, or health product consumption).

Final concluding remarks

In conclusion, as digital health communication becomes the norm, protecting the integrity of the information ecosystem for older adults is an urgent public health priority. Addressing this issue is not merely about fixing the perceived deficits of an aging population, but about building a resilient, age-friendly digital society. Thus, the field is to transition now from descriptive accounts of vulnerability to rigorous intervention-based research that tests concrete solutions.
