Liver enzyme and risk of vascular dementia: A univariable and multivariable Mendelian randomization of European descent
Authors: Jieyu Chen, Jianzhun Chen, Chunyu Liang, Chenxi Liu, Liu Jie, Bin Liu, Xinglong Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObservational studies have indicated a link between liver enzymes and dementia, but the causal relationship remains uncertain. We conducted a two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study to investigate potential causal links between liver function markers (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alkaline phosphatase [ALP], and γ‐glutamyltransferase [GGT]) and various forms of dementia (all‐cause dementia, Alzheimer's disease [AD], vascular dementia [VaD], and frontotemporal dementia [FTD]).MethodsGenome‐wide association study (GWAS) data of liver enzyme levels with 517 single nucleotide polymorphisms from 315,572 individuals of European descent were considered as exposures. Additional GWAS data on dementia from the FinnGen consortium and the UK Biobank were used as outcomes. The causal relationship was evaluated using univariable MR (UVMR) and multivariable MR (MVMR) methods. UVMR approaches such as inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR‐Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode were used, with IVW as primary. MVMR techniques, such as extended versions of IVW, MR‐Egger, and Q‐minimization methods, were used to assess causal effects. The robustness of the MR analysis findings was verified through heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, and leave‐one‐out analyses.ResultsMVMR analysis demonstrated that a genetically determined one standard deviation rise in blood GGT levels was associated with an increased risk of VaD (IVW: odds ratio = 1.007, 95% confidence interval = 1.002–1.011, p = 0.010). These findings remained consistent after adjusting for confounding variables in MVMR analysis. Sensitivity analyses further supported the causal relationship. However, no significant links were observed between ALT, AST, ALP, and all‐cause dementia, VaD, AD, or FTD.ConclusionsOur study suggests clinical implications, demonstrating that high blood GGT concentrations are potential causal risk factors for VaD in European populations. Further research is needed to uncover the underlying biological mechanisms between GGT and VaD and validate the clinical relevance of early prevention and intervention strategies.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, dementia, frontotemporal dementia, Mendelian randomization, vascular dementia

INTRODUCTION

Dementia is defined by a decline in cognitive abilities across multiple domains. The subtypes of dementia include Alzheimer's disease (AD), vascular dementia (VaD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and dementia with Lewy bodies. With the aging population, the global incidence of dementia is projected to reach 152 million by 2050, 1 leading to significant financial and healthcare challenges for individuals, families, and communities. Research indicates that dementia could be avoided by addressing and eliminating factors that can be modified. 1 , 2 Owing to the highly diverse nature of the underlying causes of dementia, it is essential to identify and prevent the potential modifiable risk factors early. The liver, an essential organ, plays a key role in regulating metabolism in peripheral tissues and is the primary site for peripheral amyloid‐beta (Aβ) metabolism. A recent study suggested that liver health abnormalities, including decreased Aβ clearance and abnormal peripheral circulation metabolism during the aging process, may be involved in the occurrence of AD. 3 Thus, targeting the liver–brain axis may be a promising therapeutic approach for preventing AD. 4

Liver function can be assessed by measuring the levels of specific enzymes in the blood reflecting liver‐related metabolic disturbances. Commonly used enzymes to indicate liver dysfunction include alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT). Raised levels of ALT and AST typically suggest liver cell damage, while increased ALP and GGT may indicate cholestatic diseases. 5 Observational research has proposed that variations in liver function indicators (ALT, AST, ALP, and GGT) may disrupt various metabolites that affect both liver and brain metabolism, thereby influencing cognitive function, and potentially leading to dementia. A prospective study showed that individuals with plasma ALT levels <10th percentile of the normal range in mid‐life had a significantly increased long‐term risk of developing dementia. 6 Similarly, Lu et al. 7 emphasized the significance of late‐life ALT as a potential biomarker, linking liver hypometabolism with dementia. Besides peripheral metabolism, liver enzymes could also directly contribute to dementia pathogenesis. Specifically, ALT and AST are crucial enzymes involved in liver gluconeogenesis and the synthesis of neurotransmitters essential for synaptic maintenance. 8 Specific liver enzymes (e.g., ALP, ALT, and AST/ALT) are associated with biomarkers of AD pathophysiological characteristics. 9 , 10 Owing to its role as a marker of oxidative stress and its pro‐atherogenic and pro‐inflammatory effects, GGT could serve as a valuable predictor for dementia. 11 , 12 Following AD, VaD ranks as the second most prevalent form of dementia, sharing overlapping pathogenesis involving oxidative stress with AD. 13 Praetorius Björk and Johansson 14 further support that the link between liver enzymes like GGT and dementia is more apparent in those diagnosed with VaD. These results advocate for attention that liver enzymes might be risk factors for dementia. These findings highlight some indications of the possible link between liver enzymes and different types of dementia. However, observational studies face challenges, including reverse causality and residual confounders, resulting in inconsistent conclusions about correlations. Thus, the genetic causality relationship of this association needs to be further explored.

By leveraging genetic information, Mendelian randomization (MR) serves as a method to explore potential causal links from an exposure to a specific outcome. 15 Unlike observational studies where causality is challenging to establish due to external confounders, MR uses genetic variation as a tool to mimic the random assignment of individuals to different levels of exposure to offer more dependable proof of causation. Given that aggregated data from genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) are readily accessible, two‐sample techniques have become popular for conducting MR analysis. MR is a reliable approach for identifying the risk factors associated with diseases. However, there is no two‐sample MR study that assesses the causal links between liver function and dementia or decreased cognitive function.

In this study, we used European ancestry GWAS to conduct a comprehensive MR analysis, including univariable MR and multivariable MR (MVMR), to explore the causal effects between the liver function (ALT, AST, ALP, and GGT) and multiple dementia types (including AD, all‐cause dementia, VaD, and FTD). Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of the results.

METHODS

The process design is depicted in Figure 1, where we commence with a two‐sample MR analysis of exposure and outcome. Subsequently, MVMR analysis was conducted on the main findings obtained. To investigate the causal effects of liver dysfunction on the risk of dementia subtypes, we utilized specific datasets: (1) Exposure data included four liver enzymes (ALT, AST, ALP, and GGT). The liver function‐related data used were GWAS results derived from a secondary analysis of the UK Biobank's raw data by authors such as Pazoki et al. 16 through the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) OpenGWAS Project. The data set included individuals of European ancestry with a substantial sample size of over 430,000. (2) Outcome data for dementia subtypes was sourced from a meta‐analysis GWAS conducted by the International Genomics of Alzheimer's Project, 17 which included 63,926 individuals of European descent (21,982 AD cases and 41,944 cognitively normal controls) from four consortia. Data for VaD, all‐cause dementia, and FTD were sourced from the R10 version of the FinnGen consortium, 18 which included VaD (2717 cases and 393,024 controls), all‐cause dementia (20,338 cases and 391,843 controls), and FTD (129 cases and 392,463 controls). Data extraction

For the MR analysis, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) strongly associated with the exposure were used as instrumental variables (IVs) to evaluate the causal effect of exposure on the outcome. For IVs to be considered eligible, three criteria must be met: (1) they must demonstrate a direct relationship with the exposure, (2) they must remain unaffected by potential confounding factors, and (3) they should exhibit no correlation with the outcome. 19 To fulfill criterion 1, we applied a genome‐wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10−8) and required F‐statistics to be ≥10, ensuring the incorporation of robust IVs. 20 Weak IVs pose a risk of bias in MR results. To ensure the independence of the SNPs, linkage disequilibrium (correlation coefficient r 2 < 0.001) was removed within a genetic distance range of 10,000 kb. 21 We excluded certain SNPs associated with confounding factors through queries on the GWAS Chatlog website.

Sensitivity analyses

Four MR methods (MR‐Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode) with differing pleiotropy assumptions were adopted for sensitivity analyses. 15 Cochran's Q statistic, calculated through both inverse variance weighting (IVW) and MR‐Egger regression methods, was used to assess heterogeneity, with p > 0.05 indicating no significant heterogeneity. The MR‐Egger intercept test was used to evaluate the pleiotropy, where a p > 0.05 indicated the absence of significant pleiotropic effects. All MR analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria, https://cran.rstudio.com/bin/windows/base/) using specific packages, including “TwoSampleMR,” “tidyverse,” “ggplot2,” “purrr,” “data.table,” “MendelR,” and “LDlinkR.”

RESULTS

Univariable MR analysis

For the AST data set, a total of 87 SNPs were included in the analysis. For the ALT data set, we excluded SNPs rs114165349 and rs2642438 due to associations with hyperlipidemia and alcohol consumption. Similarly, for the GGT data set, we excluded SNPs rs1260326, rs13108218, and rs1800961, which are alcohol‐related or hyperlipidemia‐related. For the ALP data set, we excluded SNPs rs1260326, rs2642438, rs2911971, and rs79624003 for similar reasons. In Figure 2, we present the p values of MR analysis for exposure and outcome derived using the IVW method. The univariate MR analysis indicates that GGT is associated with an increased risk of VaD (IVW: odds ratio [OR] = 1.007, p = 0.010) (Figure 3). Table S2 provides results from four additional MR analyses besides IVW, along with tests for pleiotropy and heterogeneity. We observed that the conclusion of GGT being a risk factor for VaD passed both heterogeneity and multiple‐testing corrections, affirming the reliability of this association. Although GGT is a risk factor for VaD, we cannot conclude that elevated levels of other transaminases lead to an increased risk of VaD, AD, or dementia overall. However, upon observing the figures, a trend emerged where all ORs are greater than 1, and some MR results show relatively small p values, although they do not reach the conventional threshold of 0.05. This trend might suggest a potential association between elevated transaminase levels and dementia.

MVMR analysis

To further validate our primary conclusion, we conducted MVMR tests on four transaminases and VaD. After adjusting for the other three transaminases, we investigated whether GGT exhibited a significant association as a risk factor for VaD. As shown in Figure 4, the results indicate that after adjusting for the other three transaminases, GGT remains a risk factor for VaD (IVW: OR = 1.009, p = 0.002). However, the other three transaminases did not exhibit any causal relationship with VaD. Based on these results, we conclude that GGT is a risk factor for VaD.

DISCUSSION

Rare MR analysis aims to determine the causal and independent effects of four liver enzymes on dementia, including AD, all‐cause dementia, VaD, and FTD. In this study, we suggest that genetically determined elevated GGT levels are linked to an increased risk of VaD, highlighting a potential risk factor. Conversely, there is insufficient strong evidence to support a causal relationship between genetically predicted AST, ALT, and ALP levels and dementia. After controlling for confounding variables, our analysis using MVMR showed that the causal estimates remained significant, indicating that the risk factor is independent and impactful. Furthermore, multiple sensitivity analyses were carried out, all of which confirmed the strong and consistent results even when considering the potential influence of horizontal pleiotropy.

Several observational studies support our findings, as they have reported the significance of GGT and the risk of dementia, especially in VaD. For instance, the study conducted by Kunutsor and Laukkanen 22 demonstrated that elevated GGT levels during midlife were associated with a higher likelihood of developing AD in Finland. Similarly, Yavuz et al. 23 also indicated that elevated serum GGT levels are a contributing hazard factor to AD in later life. Given that AD and VaD share a common pathology, further research revealed stronger evidence supporting the connection between GGT and dementia, particularly among participants who were diagnosed with VaD at the time of the study, indicating that the levels of GGT in the serum are connected to pervasive and affects cognitive performance and changes in general. 14 Nevertheless, due to the limited sample size and the accuracy of previous studies on dementia subtypes, these findings prevent us from drawing definitive deductions about causality. A large‐scale longitudinal study demonstrated that not merely higher GGT but increased GGT variability might be an independent predictive variable of incident dementia (including all‐cause dementia, AD, and VaD), suggesting that GGT could be utilized to assess the risk stratification for future dementia. 24 In particular, a national population‐based study found a linear association between serum GGT levels and dementia risk, with a more pronounced effect on VaD than AD, independent of glycemic status or other key confounding factors. 25 A recent UK Biobank cohort study proposed that increased levels of GGT levels were strongly linked to the likelihood of developing VaD. 26 Moreover, Kunutsor et al. 27 used summary data from large‐scale published GWAS and found no evidence supporting a causal association between genetically determined serum GGT levels and the risk of AD. The findings from our study align with this understanding.

GGT is a widely distributed cell‐surface enzyme, integral to glutathione metabolism, 28 and is involved in mediating intracellular protective antioxidant mechanisms, which are implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative conditions like dementia. 29 , 30 , 31 Furthermore, GGT is being redefined as both an oxidative stress marker and an indicator of atherosclerosis, 12 , 32 , 33 which might explain the link between GGT and VaD. It is suggested that serum GGT promotes oxidative and inflammatory processes, resulting in the production of reactive oxygen species in the vasculature, 13 which may be associated with VaD. Moreover, atherosclerosis, which is now recognized as an important feature of VaD, 34 can be partly attributed to the pro‐oxidant action triggered by GGT within the atherosclerotic plaque. 11 This, in turn, is a contributing factor to the progression of neurodegenerative processes and cognitive deterioration. 35 Considering the role of GGT in mediating oxidative and inflammatory responses, it is biologically plausible that GGT is associated with the likelihood of developing VaD.

GGT is a commonly utilized biomarker in clinical settings for assessing possible hepatic or biliary conditions. It is worth discussing that GGT has been suggested as a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases and has been identified as a predictor of cardiovascular fatality. Increased GGT levels were found to be linked to the occurrence of various cardiovascular risk factors, including high cholesterol, high blood pressure, diabetes, heart attack, and metabolic syndrome. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 These vascular risk factors are also recognized to elevate the likelihood of VaD. However, no evidence supports the longitudinal relationship between changes in cognitive function, GGT levels, as well as vascular disorders. Despite this, our findings did establish a link between GGT and VaD, indicating that GGT levels in the blood could potentially serve as an informative biomarker for this association.

Current findings do not provide strong evidence for a causal link between GGT concentrations and AD risk, suggesting that previous observational results may have been influenced by residual confounding or reverse causality. The stronger association of GGT with VaD compared to AD is still under investigation and may involve multiple factors, such as lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and cerebrovascular damage, which are more pronounced in VaD. Nevertheless, the observed linear and independent association between GGT levels and AD risk in past cohort studies hints at a potential causal relationship. Further investigation is needed in longitudinal research to enhance our understanding of the causal pathways involved in the liver–brain biochemical communication axis.

However, the current study has some limitations that impact our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the potential link between liver dysfunction and dementia. First, the data utilized in this study were predominantly from GWAS involving individuals of European descent, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Further research employing MR with diverse ancestral backgrounds is necessary to validate the observed relationship across different populations. Second, the relatively small sample size of VaD cases (2717 cases and 39,302 controls) increases the risk of false‐negative outcomes. Third, our conclusions were based solely on genetic data, highlighting the need for prospective multicenter studies to establish a causal connection between liver dysfunction and dementia. Therefore, additional research is warranted to confirm our findings. Finally, while IVs were derived from comprehensive genetic data, manual validation of these instruments was not performed. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess for horizontal pleiotropy, and our results remained consistent even when considering potential violations of this assumption.

In conclusion, our study indicates that genetic prediction of GGT concentrations is positively related to the risk of VaD in individuals of European descent. The findings have clinical implications that GGT could be a candidate biomarker to predict VaD, which could shed new light on early VaD intervention by tracking and reducing serum GGT concentrations. Reducing GGT levels through healthier habits may lower the likelihood of cognitive impairment. 40 To confirm the validity of our findings, it is essential to conduct additional MR studies with adequate sample sizes, extensive genetic datasets, and stronger MR techniques. In the future, we expect that more randomized controlled trials and prospective studies will be conducted to explore the association between serum GGT levels and the likelihood of developing VaD to determine the clinical importance of serum GGT levels and whether elevated levels can independently predict the development of VaD.
