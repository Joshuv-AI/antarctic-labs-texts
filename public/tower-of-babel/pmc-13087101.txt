Incidentally Detected Mediastinal Mass by Transesophageal Echocardiography in a Patient With Severe Mitral Stenosis
Authors: Najmeh‐Sadat Mousavi, Ali Hosseinsabet, Shapour Shirani, Hassan Aghjani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA mediastinal mass can be incidentally detected during transesophageal echocardiography, so clinicians performing this procedure should be aware of this possibility and able to interpret related echocardiographic findings. In evaluating a 69‐year‐old man with dyspnea, mitral stenosis was identified on transthoracic echocardiography. Subsequently, during transesophageal echocardiography, a mediastinal mass in the posterior mediastinum was observed. The computed tomography scan confirmed the transesophageal echocardiography findings and suggested that lymphoma and metastatic malignancy should be considered in the differential diagnosis.Keywords: computed tomography scan, mediastinal mass, mitral stenosis, transesophageal echocardiography

Introduction

Transesophageal echocardiography is a widely used imaging modality for evaluating the structure and function of cardiac chambers and valves [1]. Because the transesophageal echocardiography probe is close to mediastinal structures, it offers the possibility of detecting mediastinal masses in some cases, in addition to assessing the compression and hemodynamic effect on cardiac structures [2]. The initial detection of a mediastinal mass by transesophageal echocardiography has been reported [3]. However, these reports are few, and more reports are required to enrich our knowledge regarding the detection of mediastinal mass by transesophageal echocardiography. On the other hand, clinicians performing transesophageal echocardiography should be prepared not only to detect mediastinal masses but also to interpret their related transesophageal echocardiography findings that are encountered incidentally. Here, we present a patient with significant mitral stenosis who had an incidental mediastinal mass detected during transesophageal echocardiography.

Case History/Examination

A 69‐year‐old man presented with dyspnea on exertion, classified as New York Heart Association functional class II. He was referred to our echocardiography laboratory. His electrocardiogram showed a normal sinus rhythm with a first‐degree atrioventricular block (PR interval = 240 ms).

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal left ventricular size with mild systolic dysfunction (ejection fraction = 50%), normal right ventricular size and systolic function, severe left atrial enlargement, trivial mitral regurgitation, severe mitral stenosis (area = 1.2 cm2, mean gradient = 12 mmHg), mild aortic and tricuspid regurgitation with an estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure of 51 mmHg. The modified Wilkins score of the mitral valve was 8 (each of mobility, thickening, calcification, and subvalvular apparatus received a score of 2). A transesophageal echocardiography probe was easily inserted, confirming the absence of thrombi in the left atrium and left atrial appendage, and other findings were consistent with the transthoracic echocardiography findings. Additionally, a well‐defined heterogeneous mass measuring 53 × 36 mm was incidentally detected in the posterior mediastinum, without compressing cardiac structures and hemodynamic effects (Figure 1, Video S1).

Mediastinal mass in transesophageal echocardiography (arrow). (A) The transesophageal echocardiography probe was used at the upper esophageal level at zero degrees, and (B) modified the upper esophageal view of the ascending aorta to 48 degrees. * Ascending aorta and ** Right pulmonary artery.

Chest contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a 64 × 43 mm mass in the posterior and middle mediastinum (subcarinal space), accompanied by lymphadenopathy involving the middle and anterior mediastinum and bilateral hilar regions (Figure 2A,B). The subcarinal lymph nodes showed central hypodensity on contrast‐enhanced CT, suggesting necrosis. Review of a coronary CT angiography performed 10 months earlier revealed that the mediastinal mass was already present at that time, measuring 17 × 13 mm, although it had not been reported initially (Figure 2C,D). The marked interval growth of the lesion over a relatively short period, together with the presence of necrotic‐appearing lymphadenopathy in multiple parts of the mediastinum and hilar involvement, raised concern for malignant etiologies.

(A) The sagittal (B) coronal section of chest computed tomography with contrast indicates a mediastinal mass (arrow). (C) The sagittal (D) coronal section of the previous coronary computed tomography angiography demonstrated a smaller size of the mediastinal mass (arrow). * Right pulmonary artery and ** Left atrium.

In this context, lymphoma and metastatic malignancy were prioritized in the differential diagnosis, given their known association with rapidly enlarging mediastinal masses and necrotic lymphadenopathy. Sarcoidosis was also considered, particularly given the associated hilar adenopathy and first‐degree atrioventricular block. Esophageal carcinoma was considered less likely, as these tumors typically protrude into the esophageal lumen and may impede transesophageal echocardiography; however, solid mural esophageal tumors without significant luminal stenosis may occur and were therefore not completely excluded.

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient underwent percutaneous transvenous mitral commissurotomy, increasing the mitral valve area to 1.4 cm2. Unfortunately, he declined further evaluation for his mediastinal mass.

Discussion

The incidental detection of a mediastinal mass by transesophageal echocardiography has been reported [3]. A mediastinal mass may be related to cardiovascular structures or other mediastinal structures [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. A cardiovascular‐related mediastinal mass may be connected to cardiovascular structures such as an aortic pseudoaneurysm [8] or an atrial appendage aneurysm [9]. Confirming the hemodynamic connection of these masses to cardiovascular structures and the presence of blood flow within them is essential during transesophageal echocardiography. These masses can appear as echo‐free spaces or may be completely or partially filled with clot [6]. However, pericardial cysts that are echo‐free are not hemodynamically connected to cardiovascular structures [10]. Mediastinal masses unrelated to cardiovascular structures originate from various mediastinal components with different causes [7, 8] Some are echo‐free, like hydatid cysts [11], while others are solid [12]. Along with accurately determining the localization of these masses [13], it is important to assess for invasion into cardiovascular or nearby mediastinal structures [14]. Similar to cardiovascular mediastinal masses, these can exert compressive effects on cardiovascular structures and impact hemodynamics [7, 15]. Hemodynamic effects should be assessed during transesophageal echocardiography. In our patient, there was no compressive or hemodynamic impact on the cardiovascular structures. Unfortunately, the patient's final pathological diagnosis of the mediastinal mass remains unknown; however, what was more important than the final diagnosis was the detection made during transesophageal echocardiography.

When a mediastinal mass is detected on any imaging modality, a CT scan or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging may be used to identify cardiac involvement or its nature [16, 17]. It is noteworthy for clinicians to be involved in transesophageal echocardiography to know the possibility of mediastinal mass detection during transesophageal echocardiography. They should be ready to detect these masses and interpret echocardiography findings.
