The Molecular and Immunological Landscape in Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma (NPC) Differs by Somatostatin Receptor 2 (SSTR2) Expression
Authors: Dara Bracken‐Clarke, Elisabetta Xue, Harris Krause, Tolulope Adeyelu, Mark G. Evans, Dilara Akbulut, Martha Quezado, Nishant Gandhi, Alex Farrell, Heloisa P. Soares, Emil Lou, Minh Phan, Rusha Patel, Ari M. Vanderwalde, Andrew Elliott, Conor E. Steuer, Nabil F. Saba, Daniel J. Lubin, Nyall R. London, James L. Gulley, Charalampos S. Floudas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSomatostatin receptor 2 is expressed in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). We report genomic and transcriptomic analysis results of 163 NPC cases, demonstrating that somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) gene expression in EBV‐positive and in EBV‐negative NPC correlated with genomic alterations and an inflamed microenvironment. Median SSTR2 expression was 6.52 transcripts per million (TPM), ranging from 0.59 TPM (Quartile 1 [Q1], 18.2% EBV‐positive) to 35.79 TPM (Q4, 82.9% EBV‐positive). PIK3CA (20.51%) and CYLD (10.53%)/NFKBIA (12.82%) mutations were enriched in SSTR2‐Q1 versus ‐Q4. Tumor mutation burden was negatively correlated (R = −0.27, p < 0.001) with SSTR2, while PD‐L1 tumor proportion score (2% vs. 92.5% [SSTR2:Q1 vs. Q4], p < 0.001) and T‐cell inflamed score correlated with SSTR2 expression (R = 0.53, p < 0.001). B‐cells, M1 macrophages, CD8+ T‐cells, Treg cells, and dendritic cells were enriched in SSTR2‐Q4, while neutrophils were prominent in SSTR2‐Q1. These results demonstrate a significant positive correlation between SSTR2 expression and an inflamed tumor microenvironment in NPC. This suggests that SSTR2 expression in NPC may be a clinically useful biomarker, correlating with the tumor microenvironment (including, potentially, sensitivity to immunotherapy) and its genetic profile.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a squamous epithelial cancer, most commonly arising from the pharyngeal recess and classified by the World Health Organization into keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), non‐keratinizing SCC (with differentiated and undifferentiated subtypes), and basaloid SCC [1]. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, particularly the non‐keratinizing subtype, is more frequent in east and southeast Asia (endemic regions); the keratinizing subtype is rare in endemic areas, whereas basaloid NPC is the least common variant [1, 2, 3]. Non‐keratinizing NPC is predominantly associated with the Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), especially in endemic regions, but for keratinizing NPC this association is weaker, especially in non‐endemic regions [1, 2]. Localized and locally advanced NPC are treated with either radiotherapy alone or concurrent platinum‐based chemoradiotherapy with induction or consolidation chemotherapy considered for large or locally advanced tumors [4, 5]. Recent data also suggest a role for immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) for locally advanced disease treated with curative intent [6]. For recurrent or metastatic NPC, combination chemotherapy (cisplatin plus gemcitabine) along with ICB with the anti‐PD‐1 (programmed cell death protein‐1) agent toripalimab is the current recommendation; subsequent treatment options include alternative chemotherapy and ICB along with local therapy, if appropriate [7, 8, 9]. Notably, the benefit of ICB therapy (toripalimab) was seen across all PD‐L1 expression levels and PD‐L1 positivity [7, 8] is not required per the FDA approval [10].

The tumor mutation burden (TMB) of NPC is reported to be in the range of 1.8–7.07 mutations/Megabase [11]. Reported mutations vary according to subtype: alterations in the nuclear factor kappa‐B (NF‐κB) pathway (acting via its negative regulators TRAF3 [21.4%], CYLD [11.4%], and NFKBIA [10%]) are reported in EBV‐driven NPC (EBV‐NPC), and alterations in the ERBB‐PI3K [~15%] pathway are reported in non‐EBV‐driven NPC (non‐EBV‐NPC) [12]. Mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) was reported in approximately 2% of NPC cases in cohorts from southeastern Asia [13, 14]. Non‐EBV‐NPC is associated with alterations in PIK3CA signaling and higher overall mutation burden; NFκB alterations are not commonly observed in non‐EBV‐NPC.

Expression of programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) by immunohistochemistry (IHC) is high in EBV‐NPC: at least 69% of cases being PD‐L1+ by tumor proportion score (TPS; tumor cell PD‐L1 expression of > 1%), increasing to 71% PD‐L1+ by combined positive score (CPS, staining of both tumor cells and immune cells ≥ 5%) and is reportedly lower at 23.5% in non‐EBV‐NPC [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) are reported as almost ubiquitously present, with increased TIL density (high TILs defined as occupying > 40% of the tumor field or present in > 10% of tumor epithelial nests and/or > 70% of tumor stroma) and increased CD3+, CD8+, and CD20+ fractions being associated with improved survival and reduced local relapse risk [15, 20, 21, 22, 23].

Somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2), encoded by the SSTR2 gene, is widely expressed on neuroendocrine tissues with suppressive, antisecretory and antiproliferative effects [24]. Somatostatin receptor 2 is also expressed on well‐ and moderately‐differentiated neuroendocrine tumors and more variably on high‐grade neuroendocrine tumors and neuroendocrine carcinomas [25] including in the rare sinonasal tumors olfactory neuroblastoma and sinonasal neuroendocrine carcinoma [26]. It has been successfully targeted therapeutically with direct SSTR2‐agonists (e.g., lanreotide and octreotide) and with radioligands (e.g., the beta particle emitter 177Lu DOTATATE, Lutathera) [27, 28, 29, 30]. New SSTR2‐directed therapies in development include SSTR2‐directed antibody‐drug conjugates, novel radioligands, such as the alpha‐emitter [212Pb] VMT‐Alpha‐NET (NCT06479811) [31], and cellular therapies [32, 33, 34]. Expression of SSTR2 protein has been detected in NPC through both SSTR2 radioligands [35, 36] and IHC [37, 38]. In a large, primarily European and Asian cohort, SSTR2 protein expression by IHC was reported in EBV‐NPC due to direct induction of SSTR2 expression by EBV oncogenic transcripts, though rare EBV+/SSTR2‐low and EBV‐/SSTR2‐high NPC cases are also described [37, 38, 39].

In this study, we examine the gene expression of SSTR2 in a NPC cohort and correlate this expression with EBV status, tumor genomic alterations, established immunotherapy biomarkers, and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME).

Materials and Methods

Cohort

Formalin‐fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) NPC tumor specimens were submitted to a commercial CLIA‐certified laboratory for molecular profiling (Caris Life Sciences, Phoenix, Arizona, U.S.A.). Samples were analyzed by next‐generation sequencing (NGS), whole exome (WES), whole transcriptome sequencing, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and in situ hybridization. This study was conducted using only de‐identified data and met the criteria for non‐human subjects research under 45 CFR 46.101(b); therefore, informed consent was not required. The study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, the Belmont Report, and the U.S. Common Rule.

DNA Sequencing

DNA Next‐Generation Sequencing (NGS)

A targeted 592‐gene panel or WES was performed using genomic DNA isolated from FFPE tumor samples. The 592‐gene panel was sequenced using the NextSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA). A custom‐designed SureSelect XT assay was used to enrich 592 whole‐gene targets (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). WES was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina Inc.). A hybrid pull‐down panel of baits designed to enrich for 700 clinically relevant genes at high coverage and high read depth was used, along with another panel designed to enrich for an additional > 20,000 genes at lower depth. 592‐gene and WES assays were cross‐validated and showed highly concordant results. Matched normal tissue was not sequenced.

Identification of Genetic Variants

Genetic variants identified were interpreted by board‐certified molecular geneticists and categorized as “pathogenic,” “likely pathogenic,” “variant of unknown significance,” “likely benign,” or “benign,” according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) standards. When assessing mutation frequencies of individual genes, “pathogenic” and “likely pathogenic” were counted as mutations while “benign,” “likely benign” variants and “variants of unknown significance” were excluded.

RNA Sequencing

Formalin‐fixed paraffin embedded specimens underwent pathology review to determine percent tumor content and tumor size; a minimum of 10% of tumor content in the area for microdissection was required to enable enrichment and extraction of tumor‐specific RNA. Qiagen RNA FFPE kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) was used, and the RNA quality and quantity were determined using the Agilent TapeStation (Agilent Technologies). Biotinylated RNA baits were hybridized to the synthesized and purified cDNA targets and the bait‐target complexes were amplified in a post capture PCR reaction. The resultant libraries were quantified, normalized and the pooled libraries were denatured, diluted, and sequenced. For transcript counting, transcripts per million molecules (TPM) were generated using the Salmon expression pipeline. Tumors were stratified as SSTR2‐High (H) and ‐Low (L) per top (i.e., SSTR2‐Q4) and bottom (i.e., SSTR2‐Q1) quartile expression of SSTR2, respectively. Somatostatin receptor 2 expression across NPC cases was also compared to gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors (GI NETs), from the Caris dataset and per the OncoTree classification, which classically express SSTR2 and for which SSTR2‐directed imaging and radionuclide therapy is standard, with median SSTR2 expression for GI NETs of 5.54 TPM (n = 153) [40].

EBV Status

Epstein–Barr virus‐encoded small RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization (ISH) was performed on FFPE sections on glass slides. Slides were stained using automated staining techniques, per the manufacturer's instructions, and were optimized and validated per CLIA/CAP and ISO requirements and were called as EBER positive or negative by a trained pathologist. A total of 82 samples were evaluated for EBER staining on digitized slides. Samples were labeled positive when there was nuclear specific staining observed. Both positivity status and staining percentages were recorded. Epstein–Barr virus status was also defined using WES by the read counts of EBV DNA, using ≥ 25,000 read counts as threshold to call EBV positive. When both EBER ISH and EBV by WES were available, we deferred to the EBER ISH result. In situ hybridization slides were reviewed by pathologists (D.A. and M.Q.).

Immunotherapy Biomarkers and Immune Cell Infiltration

Immunohistochemistry was performed on full FFPE sections on glass slides. Slides were stained using automated staining techniques, per the manufacturer's instructions, and were optimized and validated per CLIA/CAP and ISO requirements. Programmed death ligand‐1 (PD‐L1) positive staining (22C3, PharmDX, Dako, Agilent Technologies) was defined as TPS (tumor proportion score, as previously described) ≥ 1 [41, 42].

Tumor mutational burden was measured by counting all non‐synonymous missense, nonsense, in‐frame insertion/deletion, and frameshift mutations found per tumor that had not been previously described as germline alterations in dbSNP151 and Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) databases or benign variants identified by Caris geneticists. High TMB (TMB‐H) was defined by a cut‐off of ≥ 10 mutation/Megabase (mut/MB), based on KEYNOTE‐158 trial [43]. A combination of multiple test platforms was used to determine MSI‐H/dMMR status of the tumors profiled, including: immunohistochemistry (IHC) for MLH1 (M1 antibody), MSH2 (G2191129 antibody), MSH6 (44 antibody), and PMS2 (EPR3947 antibody, Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Tucson, AZ, USA), and NGS (for tumors tested with NextSeq platform; 7000 target microsatellite loci were examined and compared to the reference genome hg19 from the University of California, Santa Cruz). These three platforms generated highly concordant results; in the rare cases of discordant results, the MSI‐H or dMMR status of the tumor was determined in the order of IHC followed by NGS.

Immune cell fraction was inferred via deconvolution of transcriptomic data by quanTIseq [44]. QuanTIseq is an immune deconvolution algorithm that utilizes RNA transcripts that are known to be expressed in specific immune cell types to deconvolute bulk RNA sequencing data and predict the different immune cell fractions that were present in the bulk RNA lysate. Transcriptomic data was also used to calculate a T cell‐inflamed score (TCI) as previously described [45].

Statistics

Continuous data were accessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical data were evaluated using Fisher's Exact and Chi‐squared tests where appropriate; Spearman correlation was used for correlation analysis. A Bonferroni correction was performed for multiple comparisons. For all tests, p < 0.05 (or corrected p < 0.05, where applicable) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

One hundred and sixty‐three NPC tumor specimens were analyzed with 73.6% (n = 120) from male patients. Median age of patients was 59.0 years (range: 14–87 years); 52.3% (69/132) samples were EBV+, of which 19/132 (14.4%) were assessed by in situ hybridization (ISH) alone, 4/132 (3.0%) by whole exome sequencing (WES) alone and 109/132 (82.6%) by both ISH and WES. 49.7% (81/163) of specimens were obtained from the primary, 17.2% (28/163) from a lymph node and 33.1% (54/163) from a metastatic site; 57.1% (93/163) were reported as having squamous histology and 42.9% (70/163) as having non‐squamous histology (Table 1). Scanned hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)‐stained pathology slides (n = 83), all EBV+ by ISH and/or WES, and corresponding EBER ISH‐stained slides were reviewed by two board certified pathologists (M.Q. and D.A.). All reviewed tumor samples were positive for EBV by EBER‐ISH with high staining percentages (mean = 93% of tumor cells, median = 100%, IQR [interquartile range]: 75%–100%). There was high concordance between EBER ISH and EBV WES results in tumor where both were available (Figure S1). Comparison with matched H&E‐stained sections revealed EBER positivity in tumor cells, whereas tumor infiltrating immune cells (lymphocytes, plasma cells, etc.) were EBER‐negative by ISH (Figure 1d–g).

SSTR2 Expression

The median expression of SSTR2 in NPC was 6.52 TPM (IQR: 1.37–22.25). Cases were subsequently stratified according to SSTR2 expression quartile and labeled SSTR2‐Q1 to SSTR2‐Q4 (Table 1). Somatostatin receptor 2 median expression level in each quartile was 0.59, 3.15, 11.43, and 35.79 TPM for SSTR2‐Q1 to SSTR2‐Q4, respectively. No statistically significant differences in age (p = 0.169) or sex (p = 0.932) were noted across the groups.

The expression of SSTR2 was significantly higher in EBV‐NPC compared to non‐EBV‐NPC (Median: 15.0 vs. 1.66 TPM, p < 0.001) (Figure 1b). When compared to SSTR2 expression found in gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors (GI NETs) derived from the Caris database (median of 5.54 TPM), EBV‐NPC was significantly higher while non‐EBV‐NPC was significantly lower (p < 0.01; Figure 1b). Somatostatin receptor 2‐Q4 had a significantly higher EBV‐NPC rate compared to SSTR2‐Q1 (82.9% vs. 18.2%, respectively, p < 0.05) (Figure 1c).

Genomic Alterations

Consistent with previously reported data, our cohort was enriched for mutations in: PIK3CA, CYLD, CDKN2A, FAT1, NFKBIA, and pTERT (TERT promoter) with differences according to EBV‐NPC versus non‐EBV‐NPC status. The mutational landscape was compared between the top and bottom quartile SSTR2 tumors, SSTR2‐Q1 and SSTR2‐Q4. Compared to SSTR2‐Q4, SSTR2‐Q1 tumors demonstrated significant enrichment of PIK3CA mutations (20.51% vs. 2.56%, p = 0.028), and non‐significant enrichment of NOTCH1 (11.11% vs. 2.56%, p = 0.119), CDKN2A (10.52% vs. 2.63%, p = 0.115) and pTERT (10.71% vs. 0.00%, p = 0.112) mutations. Conversely, SSTR2‐Q4 tumors were non‐significantly enriched for pathogenic mutations in CYLD (10.53% vs. 0%, p = 0.115) and NFKBIA (12.82% vs. 2.56%, p = 0.200). Examination of genomic alterations by EBV status showed mutations in PIK3CA (11.86% vs. 0.00%, p < 0.001), CDKN2A (10.17% vs. 0.00%, p = 0.011) and FAT1 (9.43% vs. 0.00%, p = 0.023) were significantly and exclusively observed in non‐EBV‐NPC, whereas CYLD mutation (0.00% vs. 9.38%, p = 0.029) was exclusively observed in EBV‐NPC samples (Figure 2a,b).

Immune Biomarkers and Tumor Microenvironment

Overall, 11.2% (17/152) of samples were TMB‐H (tumor mutation burden‐high) and the median TMB was 4.0 muts/MBase (IQR: 2.0–6.0). A comparison between high and low SSTR2‐expressing groups revealed lower TMB‐H rate in the high‐SSTR2‐expressing (SSTR2‐Q4) tumors versus the low‐SSTR2‐expressing (SSTR2‐Q1) tumors (2.6% vs. 17.9%, p = 0.056). Median TMB was 5 muts/MBase (IQR: 3.0–7.5) in SSTR2‐Q1 tumors and 3 muts/MBase (IQR: 2.0–4.0) in SSTR2‐Q4 tumors (Figure 3a). Tumor mutation burden was negatively correlated with SSTR2 expression (R = −0.27, p < 0.001) (Figure 3a,c,e). Mismatch repair deficiency was only observed in one sample (0.63%)—which was EBV‐ and in SSTR2‐Q2. The prevalence of PD‐L1+ IHC expression (22c3) was 96.25% across the entire NPC cohort. PD‐L1 positivity was numerically higher in SSTR2‐Q4 compared to SSTR2‐Q1 (82.5% vs. 97.4%, p = 0.0571). Median PD‐L1 TPS was 2% (IQR 1%–70%) in SSTR2‐Q1 versus 92.5% (IQR 30%–100%) in Q4 (p < 0.001) (Figure 3a).

Tumor microenvironment (TME) analysis revealed that B‐cells (R = 0.61, p < 0.001), M1 macrophages (R = 0.52, p < 0.001), CD8+ T‐cells (R = 0.39, p < 0.001), T‐regulatory cells (R = 0.52, p < 0.001), and dendritic cells (R = 0.57, p < 0.001) correlated positively with SSTR2 expression. Conversely, the levels of neutrophil negatively correlated with SSTR2 expression (R = −0.42, p < 0.001). Somatostatin receptor 2 expression level did not correlate with NK cells, monocytes, and M2 macrophages (Figure 3b,e).

We also compared the TME across the top and bottom quartiles and observed significantly higher fraction of B‐cells (13.44% vs. 5.08%, p < 0.001), M1 macrophages (8.11% vs. 3.39%, p < 0.001), CD8+ T‐cells (3.55% vs. 0.53%, p < 0.001), Treg cells (7.25% vs. 2.31%, p < 0.001), and dendritic cells (5.06% vs. 1.36%, p < 0.001) in the SSTR2‐Q4 versus ‐Q1 groups. On the other hand, neutrophils were significantly lower in SSTR2‐Q4 compared to Q1 (0.74% vs. 5.4%, p < 0.001) (Figure 3b; Table S1).

Furthermore, the T‐cell inflamed (TCI) transcriptomic score significantly positively correlated with SSTR2 expression (R = 0.53, p < 0.001). The prevalence of T‐cell inflamed tumors was significantly higher in SSTR2‐Q4 compared to SSTR2‐Q1 (75.61% vs. 12.20%, p < 0.001) (Figure 3d,e).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the gene expression of SSTR2 and its correlation with EBV status, genomic alterations, immune biomarkers and the NPC immune microenvironment in a real‐world NPC cohort from a multicenter database notably, relatively enriched for non‐EBV‐NPC cases (47.7%). We report the expression of SSTR2 across NPC, demonstrating significantly higher expression in EBV‐NPC than in non‐EBV‐NPC. Additionally, SSTR2 expression, along with EBV status, demonstrates statistically significant and, potentially, clinically relevant, correlations with tumor genetic profile, immune biomarkers and the TME.

The expression of SSTR2 in our cohort varied considerably (median 6.52, IQR 1.37–22.5 TPM) and was shown to be associated with EBV status, with SSTR2 expression of 15 versus 1.66 TPM in EBV‐NPC versus non‐EBV‐NPC, and a significantly higher proportion of EBV‐NPC samples among the higher SSTR2 expression quartiles. However, EBV negativity and SSTR2 expression were not mutually exclusive: 17.1% of tumors in SSTR2‐Q4 were non‐EBV‐NPCs, and conversely, 18.2% in SSTR2‐Q1 were EBV‐NPC. These results are consistent with previously reported data in NPC, where, despite the correlation of SSTR2 protein expression with EBV and reported SSTR2 gene transcription induction by the EBV protein LMP1 (latent membrane protein 1) through NF‐κB signaling, there were paradoxical cases of EBV‐/SSTR2+ (30/402 cases) and EBV+/SSTR2‐ NPC (37/402 cases) [37, 39]. To the best of our knowledge, mechanisms responsible for the paradoxical cases of EBV‐/SSTR2+ and EBV+/SSTR2‐ NPC have not been reported thus far. Some cases likely represent mischaracterization of EBV or SSTR2 status (including discordance between gene and protein expression) and/or cross reactivity from infiltrating immune cells, especially potentially EBV+ lymphocytes—though this was evaluated in these data and not seen in these data. Potential mechanisms for EBV‐/SSTR2+ cases include non‐EBV driven SSTR2 expression (e.g., through non‐EBV mediated NF‐κB signaling). Potential mechanisms for EBV+/SSTR2‐ cases include loss of EBV LMP‐1 signaling (which is the primary driver of NF‐κB expression); given the marked immunogenicity of EBV LMP‐1, immunoediting would be a potential driver of this process. Considering this uncertainty, further analysis of these paradoxical cases may be warranted.

Notably, SSTR2 expression in EBV‐NPC exceeded that of GI NETs (15 vs. 5.54 TPM) obtained from the same real‐world database and analysis pipeline. From a clinical perspective, GI NETS are established as being SSTR2‐positive and demonstrate avidity on SSTR2‐directed nuclear medicine techniques (e.g., DOTATATE positron emission tomography [PET]) while also demonstrating sensitivity to SSTR2‐directed peptide‐receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) (e.g., 177Lu DOTATATE) [29]. In NPC, SSTR2‐directed imaging using DOTATATE PET outperformed (FDG) PET in sensitivity for regional nodal metastases, supporting its use in NPC staging and demonstrated tracer avidity and therefore SSTR2‐expression, in the primary lesion, local and distant nodal metastases. DOTATATE imaging also proved effective in evaluating osseous and visceral metastases with largely similar performance to FDG‐PET, albeit with reduced resolution of liver metastases due to background hepatic DOTATATE avidity. Additionally, DOTATATE avidity correlated strongly with SSTR2 IHC (median SUVmax: 12.61 [positive] vs. 4.95 [negative], p = 0.013) [46].

In this dataset, tumors demonstrating high SSTR2 expression had numerically higher prevalence of pathogenic mutations in genes known to be involved in EBV oncogenic signaling. Additionally, when comparing cases by SSTR2 expression quartile, we demonstrated enrichment for pathogenic mutations in genes known to be involved in EBV‐driven oncogenic signaling (CYLD, NFKBIA) in SSTR2‐H (high) NPC. Conversely, SSTR2‐L (low) NPC demonstrated a different and mutually exclusive genomic profile with enrichment for mutations in PIK3CA signaling, consistent with the role of EBV as a direct driver of SSTR2 expression in EBV‐NPC. Notably, the NF‐κB (CYLD, NFKBIA) and PIK3CA pathways are, potentially, actionable with both small molecule inhibitors and inhibitory RNAs being explored for NF‐κB targeting and direct inhibitors of PIK3CA signaling (e.g., alpelisib) in trials, albeit limited by toxicity [47, 48]. Clinical experience with targeting these pathways, other than in estrogen receptor positive breast cancer, has been disappointing to date (including the withdrawal of approval by the FDA for copanlisib in follicular lymphoma) and it remains an experimental approach [49, 50]. Clinical data for targeting these pathways in NPC are, at present, limited, though preclinical evidence does suggest a potential role for targeting PIK3CA in NPC [51, 52].

Analysis of the immune infiltrate and immune biomarkers according to SSTR2‐expression status again demonstrated fundamental and, potentially, clinically relevant differences between these cohorts. Overall, and potentially consistent with an underlying viral etiology, SSTR2‐H tumors demonstrated higher PD‐L1 expression (and higher PD‐L1 TPS), higher TCI score and a higher prevalence of M1 polarized macrophages. Conversely, SSTR2‐L tumors demonstrated a higher neutrophilic infiltrate, lower TCI score and significantly increased TMB. Although in both SSTR2‐H and ‐L tumors, median TMB (and the TMB IQR) was < 10 muts/MBase (the cutoff for ICI therapy [Pembrolizumab] established by the KEYNOTE‐158 trial) [43]. The relatively lower TMB in SSTR2‐H tumors is understood to relate to the underlying viral etiology of EBV+ NPC, with virally‐driven tumors having an overall lower mutation rate compared to tumors driven by environmental factors and chronic inflammation (including EBV‐ NPC). Notably, while (any) PD‐L1 positivity was high in both SSTR2‐H and ‐L tumors (82.5% vs. 97.4%), significantly higher PD‐L1 TPS in SSTR2‐H and ‐L tumors (2% vs. 92.5%) suggests that PD‐L1 may play a greater role in the oncogenesis of SSTR2‐H tumors and that they may be more ICI‐responsive. These data suggest that SSTR2 status, determined by IHC, RNA‐Seq, or SSTR2‐directed PET tracer avidity criteria may merit evaluation as a predictive biomarker for the sensitivity of NPC to immunotherapy, particularly relevant considering the role of immune checkpoint blockade in the treatment of NPC. In addition, early on‐treatment patterns of change in metabolic imaging characteristics, such as metabolic flare, can be predictive of immunotherapy response [53].

This study is limited by the lack of clinical outcome data, which could enable the correlation of these transcriptomic data with patient outcomes—particularly response data to specific therapeutic strategies and survival data. Additionally, this study did not evaluate tumor origin (primary vs. metastasis), histologic subtype or disease stage as contributors to SSTR2 expression or the immune microenvironment. We also note that the SSTR2 data are derived from the RNA rather than protein level—albeit that SSTR RNA expression has been shown to correlate with SSTR2 protein expression and positive SSTR2‐directed imaging (e.g., DOTATATE PET) is also seen in EBV‐NPC [54]. Thus, these findings, while interesting and potentially clinically actionable, should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating and require clinical validation before being used to make treatment decisions.

Our study on the expression of SSTR2 gene in a large cohort confirms that SSTR2 is widely expressed in NPC and predominantly, but not exclusively, associated with EBV‐positive status of the tumor. We describe an association of SSTR2 expression with inflamed TME, suggesting potential utility for SSTR2 as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy in NPC. This study demonstrates the feasibility of using real‐world molecular datasets (the Caris dataset in this case) for the evaluation of SSTR2 expression, tumor molecular profile and the tumor microenvironment for rare tumors such as NPC along and the utility of these data for biomarker discovery. Repeating this analysis with clinical data may allow additional conclusions to be drawn—in particular, the correlation between SSTR2 expression and immunotherapy response. Additionally, as SSTR2 is targetable both diagnostically and therapeutically (so‐called “theranostics”), our results suggest a potential role for SSTR2‐directed imaging and treatment in SSTR2‐expressing NPC—notably, radiotherapy is the backbone of local therapy for NPC and SSTR2‐directed radioligands are in clinical use (e.g., 177Lu DOTATATE—Lutathera) for other tumor types with demonstrable activity. Targeting of both SSTR2 and EBV (potentially by ADCs and/or cellular therapies) is also highly attractive in NPC both due to the demonstrable activity of ADCs in other malignancies and the co‐expression of SSTR2 and EBV products, facilitating a multi‐targeted approach. Further research is warranted to explore the clinical relevance and utility of SSTR2 expression in NPC.
