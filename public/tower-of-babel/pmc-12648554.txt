Plasma exchange as an effective treatment for refractory pure red cell aplasia post-ABO-incompatible hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: a rare complication
Authors: Asaad A Khalaf, Mohammed Al Farttoosi, Sajjad Ghanim Al-Badri, Abbas Hamza Abbas, Riyam Mudheher Nabbat Al-Ajrash, Ahmed Shamil Hashim, Zaryab Bacha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPure red blood cell aplasia (PRCA) is a rare complication of major ABO-incompatible hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), characterized by severe anemia due to impaired erythropoiesis. We report the case of 52 year old man with chronic myeloid leukemia who developed refractory PRCA four months post transplant. The patient was unresponsive to multiple immunosuppressive agents but showed marked hematologic recovery following ten sessions of plasma exchange (PEX) over five weeks. Hemoglobine level rose from 6 g/Dl to 16 g/dL, eliminating the need for further transfusions. This report highlights a case of refractory PRCA successfully treated with PEX, underscoring its potential efficacy and the need for further research to establish treatment guidelines.Keywords: pure red cell aplasia, red cell engraftment, ABO-incompatible HSCT, Theraputic plasma exchange, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, refractory Anemia

Introduction

Pure red blood cell aplasia (PRCA) is a condition presents as significant clinical challenge it often leads to persistent anemia requiring red blood cell (RBC) transfusions, thereby increasing the risks of iron overload and infections, can be classified into primary (idiopathic) and secondary (acquired) forms. Acquired PRCA may result from various etiologies including viral infections (e.g. parvovirus B19), autoimmune diseases, thymomas, certain medications, and alloimmune reactions post-ABO-incompatible hematopoietic stem cell transplantationas [1, 2].

The PRCA after ABO-incompatible HSCT remains incompletely understood but is thought to involve the persistence of recipient-derived memory B lymphocytes that produce hemagglutinins against ABO antigens on donor-derived RBCs. These antibodies inhibit erythroid recovery and delay donor RBC engraftment [3].

Management of PRCA including erythropoiesis stimulating agents (ESAs), steroids, rapid tapering of immunosuppressive agents to decrease anti-donor isohemagglutinins, rituximab, bortezomib, donor lymphocyte infusion, and mesenchymal stem cells. However no standard protocol has been universally established [4].

Recent evidence suggests that pretransplant removal of anti-donor isoagglutinins through plasma exchange (PEX) or immunoabsorption may reduce the incidence of post-HSCT PRCA. Moreover, emerging data highlight the potential efficacy of PEX in treating established PRCA. Despite these findings, reports remain scarce, and the variability in patient responses complicates the development of standardized treatment guidelines [5, 6].

In this case, we report a challenging instance of refractory PRCA following major ABO-incompatible HSCT, highlighting the role of therapeutic plasma exchange in achieving hematologic recovery and its implications for clinical practice.

Case presentation

A 52 year old man with a history of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) diagnosed in 2012, initially treated with imatinib and later nilotinib. There was no family history of hematologic malignancies or solid tumors. He underwent a successful allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-BMT) in June 2019 for myeloid blastic transformation. The transplant process, performed on July 12, 2019, was uneventful, and engraftment was achieved. Four months post-transplant, the patient began experiencing easy fatigability and pallor.

On examination, the patient appeared remarkable pale without oral ulcers, ocular manifestations, or significant graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), except for limited skin involvement. Cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, and neurological examinations were unremarkable.

Initial investigations showed severe anemia with a hemoglobin level of 6 g/dL (normochromic, normocytic) and a corrected reticulocyte count of 1%. White blood cell and platelet counts were normal, with unremarkable blood film morphology. Serum ferritin was elevated at 3000 ng/mL, The elevated serum ferritin level was attributed to repeated red blood cell transfusions administered during the period of persistent anemia prior to initiating plasma exchange. While renal and liver function tests and serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were within normal limits. Direct and indirect antibody testing and cytomegalovirus (CMV) PCR were negative.

Further diagnostic workup, including a bone marrow biopsy. The biopsy revealed a hypocellular marrow with a significant reduction in erythropoiesis. The megakaryocytic lineage was preserved, with an adequate number of megakaryocytes exhibiting normal morphology. Myelopoiesis was also reduced, primarily consisting of early and intermediate precursors, with a paucity of late-stage forms. The M:E ratio was 4:1, indicating a relative erythroid hypoplasia. No excess blasts were identified, and there was no evidence of leukemic relapse, fibrosis, or dysplasia. Additionally, focal erythroid hyperplasia with mild dysplastic changes was observed. These findings were consistent with confirmed pure red cell aplasia (PRCA). The underlying etiology was identified as major ABO incompatibility, a rare but recognized complication of allo-BMT, resulting in delayed red cell engraftment and PRCA.

Initial management with multiple immunosuppressive therapies (prednisolone, mycophenolate, ciclosporin, bortezomib, rituximab, and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents) for one year was unsuccessful. However, in October 2020, patient underwent a total of 10 therapeutic (PEX) sessions over a period of five weeks, administered at a frequency of twice weekly. Each session utilized donor AB plasma as the replacement fluid to efficiently remove circulating anti-donor isoagglutinins. Within one month Serial hemoglobin measurements indicated a gradual increase within the first two weeks, with significant hematologic level recovery rose to 10 g/Dl by the fourth week of therapy and continued to normalize, reaching 16 g/dL by November 2020.

The patient remains disease-free, off immunosuppressive therapy, and has resumed nilotinib at a dose of 200 mg/day, achieving BCR-ABL gene negativity and enjoying a normal quality of life.

Discussion

The management of pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) in the context of major ABO-incompatible hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains a challenge due to the absence of standardized treatment protocols. It results from persistent recipient-derived isoagglutinins targeting donor red cells, impairing erythropoiesis and prolonging transfusion dependence, increasing the risk of complications like iron overload [5, 6].

In our case, PRCA was diagnosed as a complication of major ABO incompatibility. The patient received extensive immunosuppressive therapy including prednisolone, mycophenolate, ciclosporin, bortezomib, and rituximab without improvement.

A growing body of literature supports the role of PEX in post-HSCT PRCA. Handisurya et al. demonstrated that antigen-specific immunoadsorption, a targeted form of plasma exchange, led to rapid hematologic improvement in PRCA post-ABO-incompatible HSCT [2]. Similarly, Hirokawa et al. reported successful erythroid recovery following PEX in patients unresponsive to conventional immunosuppressive strategies [3]. These findings highlight the potential of PEX not only as a rescue treatment but also as an earlier therapeutic option to improve post-transplant outcomes.

The turning point was initiating plasma exchange (PEX), which promptly reduced anti-donor isoagglutinins. The patient underwent twice-weekly PEX and achieved hematologic recovery within one month, with hemoglobin stabilizing soon after. Immunosuppressive therapies like rituximab and bortezomib target the immune-mediated destruction of erythroid precursors. Rituximab, a monoclonal anti-CD20 antibody, depletes B lymphocytes that produce isoagglutinins, but has delayed effect and does not eliminate existing antibodies [5].

Bortezomib, a proteasome inhibitor targeting plasma cells, has shown variable responses in PRCA treatment [6].

In contrast, PEX removes circulating isoagglutinins directly, enabling engraftment without prolonged immunosuppression [3]. Reports show hematologic improvement within 20 to 42 days, reducing anemia duration compared to traditional regimens [7, 8]. It also decreases long-term immunosuppressive dependence and infection risks. Though multiple sessions and equipment needs limit use in low-resource settings, PEX offers strong efficacy in refractory cases such as this, supporting timely erythroid recovery [1, 3]. These findings align with other reports where TPE was effective in eliminating antibodies hindering erythroid recovery. Long-term prognosis post-PEX is favorable, with low recurrence if isoagglutinins are adequately cleared and immunosuppression is tapered [9, 10]. Continued monitoring is essential for late complications like low-grade hemolysis.

Accessibility remains a barrier in resource-limited regions due to the need for apheresis devices, trained staff, and AB plasma [11]. Alternatives such as rituximab, bortezomib, or antigen-specific immunoadsorption may be considered [10, 12]. In equipped centers, early use of PEX can reduce transfusion duration and associated risks.

The absence of significant GVHD in this patient likely aided isoagglutinin clearance and contributed to therapeutic success. The procedure was well-tolerated, with only mild hypotension. It also reduced transfusion needs and potential long-term effects of iron overload [13].

This case emphasizes individualized strategies in post-transplant PRCA management. Though spontaneous recovery may occur, this patient’s rapid response underscores PEX therapeutic value. Follow-up showed normalized hemoglobin, resumed nilotinib therapy, and restored quality of life [14, 15].

Conclusion

The successful resolution of PRCA in this case highlights the role of PEX in managing refractory PRCA post-ABO-incompatible HSCT, 10 sessions of PEX over 5 weeks demonstrating its ability to achieve rapid and sustained hematologic recovery.further research is needed to optimize the timing, duration, and patient selection criteria for PEX in post-transplant PRCA. Early use may reduce transfusion dependence and improve outcomes. Notably, our patient remained transfusion-independent, with stable hemoglobin levels and no recurrence of PRCA over a two-year follow-up, and return to targeted CML therapy with nilotinib and the restoration of a normal quality of life further emphasize the long-term efficacy of this approach.
