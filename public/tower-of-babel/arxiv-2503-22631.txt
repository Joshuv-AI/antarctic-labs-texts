Accelerating a restarted Krylov method for matrix functions with randomization
Nicolas L. Guidotti, Per-Gunnar Martinsson, Juan A. Acebrón, José Monteiro

Abstract
Many scientific applications require the evaluation of the action of a matrix function on a vector, and Krylov subspace methods are the most common ones for this task. Since the orthogonalization cost and the memory requirements can quickly become overwhelming as the basis grows, the Krylov method is often restarted after a few iterations. This paper proposes a new acceleration technique for restarted Krylov methods based on randomization. The numerical experiments show that the randomized method greatly outperforms the classical approach at the same level of accuracy. In fact, randomization can actually improve the convergence rate of restarted methods in some cases. The paper also compares the performance and stability of several existing randomized methods for solving very large, ill-conditioned problems, complementing the numerical analyses from previous studies. Introduction

Matrix functions arise naturally in many scientific applications, for instance, in the solution of partial differential equations
Börner et al. (2015)
;
Güttel (2013)
;
Hochbruck and Ostermann (2010)
;
Strutt (2011)
, in the analysis of complex networks
Benzi and Boito (2020)
;
Benzi and Simunec (2022)
;
Higham (2008)
, and in the simulation of lattice quantum chromodynamics
Bloch and Heybrock (2011)
;
van den Eshof et al. (2002)
. Given a square matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, the matrix function
f(\mathbf{A})
can be defined using the Cauchy integral representation
Higham (2008)

f(\mathbf{A})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}f(z)\;(z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\;dz,

(1)

for any function
f
that is analytic on and inside a closed contour
\Gamma
that encloses the spectrum
\Lambda(\mathbf{A})
. Some common examples include the matrix exponential
e^{\mathbf{A}}
, the matrix inverse
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
, and the matrix square root
\sqrt{\mathbf{A}}
.

Most applications are only interested in the action of
f(\mathbf{A})
on a vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Since explicitly forming the full matrix
f(\mathbf{A})
is infeasible for large matrices, they generally employ Krylov subspace methods
Güttel (2013)
;
Güttel et al. (2020)
to approximate
f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}
directly. The key ingredient for these methods is the Arnoldi process that constructs an orthonormal basis for the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
. However, the evaluation of
f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}
requires that the entire basis be stored in memory, limiting the size of the problems that can be solved. A two-pass Lanczos method
Frommer and Simoncini (2008)
can eliminate the basis storage requirements for Hermitian matrices; however, it roughly doubles the number of matrix-vector products required for constructing the basis. Furthermore, for non-Hermitian matrices, the cost of orthogonalization can quickly become overwhelming as it grows quadratically with the basis size
m
.

To overcome this challenge, restarted Krylov methods
Afanasjew et al. (2008b)
;
Eiermann and Ernst (2006)
;
Eiermann et al. (2011)
;
Frommer et al. (2014b)
;
Güttel et al. (2020)
employ a sequence of Krylov subspaces of fixed size, refining the approximation for
f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}
at each new “cycle”. In this manner, the program only needs to store and orthogonalize a fixed number of basis vectors at a time. However, restarted methods are often accompanied by a slower convergence rate and may even lead to stagnation or divergence
Schweitzer (2016)
;
Simoncini (2000)
.

Randomization is another popular tool for reducing the orthogonalization cost in Krylov methods
Bergermann (2026)
;
Burke et al. (2025)
;
Burke and Güttel (2024)
;
Cortinovis et al. (2024)
;
de Damas et al. (2025)
;
Grigori et al. (2026)
;
Güttel and Schweitzer (2023)
;
Güttel and Simunec (2024)
;
Jang et al. (2024)
;
Nakatsukasa and Tropp (2024)
;
Palitta et al. (2025)
;
Simoncini and Wang (2025)
. Our first contribution is to augment a restarted method with randomization. In essence, we replace the standard Arnoldi procedure with the randomized version
Timsit et al. (2023)
to quickly construct a non-orthogonal, but well-conditioned Krylov basis at each restart cycle. We show that this modification significantly improves the performance of the restarted methods while maintaining the same level of accuracy and stability. Randomized methods with restarts have already been proposed for exponential integrators
Krieger and Schweitzer (2026)
, linear systems
Burke et al. (2025)
;
Jang et al. (2024)
, and eigendecomposition
de Damas and Grigori (2024)
.

Although randomized methods perform rather well in moderately conditioned and small problems
Cortinovis et al. (2024)
;
Güttel and Schweitzer (2023)
, it is still unknown how they behave when faced with the badly conditioned matrices that are commonly found in many real-world applications. In particular, the discretization of PDEs via the finite element method (FEM) is well known to produce very large, sparse, and ill-conditioned matrices. Another example is the study of the dynamics of complex networks via the graph Laplacian. Therefore, our second contribution is an extensive study of the convergence and performance of several randomized methods through a set of large-scale numerical experiments, focusing exclusively on ill-conditioned problems.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews the background theory of Krylov subspace methods. It also describes the randomized Arnoldi iteration and how it can be integrated into Krylov methods. Section
3
presents other approaches for accelerating Krylov methods using randomization. Section
4
describes the restarting procedure for the randomized Krylov method. Section
5
illustrates the performance and convergence of the proposed method and compares it against the state of the art. In Section
6
, we conclude our paper.

2
Theory

In this section, we provide the background theory of random sketching and describe the main components of the Krylov subspace methods for evaluating
f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}
.

2.1
Random Sketching

For a distortion parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we say that the matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
is an
\ell_{2}
embedding of a subspace
\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
if it satisfies

(1-\varepsilon)\,\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\,\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2},\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F},

(2)

or, equivalently,

|\langle\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{S}\mathbf{y}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle|\leq\varepsilon\,\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|,\quad\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}.

(3)

In practice, we do not have
a priori
knowledge of
\mathbb{F}
, e.g., the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
is only available at the end of the algorithm. Therefore, we have to generate the matrix
\mathbf{S}
from some random distribution that satisfies the relation (
2
) with high probability. In this case, we refer to
\mathbf{S}
as an
oblivious subspace embedding
of
\mathbb{F}
. There are many ways to construct a subspace embedding (e.g., see
(
Martinsson and Tropp, 2020
, Sections 8 and 9)
). Here, we focus on
sparse sign matrices

Cohen (2016)
;
Tropp et al. (2019)
;
Woodruff (2014)
, which take the form

\mathbf{S}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\zeta}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{s}_{1}&\mathbf{s}_{2}&...&\mathbf{s}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},

(4)

where
\zeta
is a “sparsity parameter”. The columns
\mathbf{s}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are sparse random vectors with
\zeta
nonzero entries drawn from an i.i.d. Rademacher distribution (i.e., each entry takes
\pm 1
with equal probability). The coordinates of the nonzero entries are uniformly chosen at random. Storing
\mathbf{S}
requires
O(\zeta n)
memory, and applying it to a vector requires
O(\zeta n)
flops. However, it requires the use of sparse data structures and arithmetic. In terms of its quality as a random embedding, it often has similar performance to Gaussian embeddings
Martinsson and Tropp (2020)
given an appropriate choice of
\zeta
and
d
. In this paper, we use
\zeta=1
(also known as
CountSketch
) and
\zeta=4
, which were chosen to minimize the sketching cost and demonstrate the maximum attainable performance. Although we did not observe numerical issues in our experiments (Section
5
),
CountSketch
has worse theoretical guarantees than denser sparse sign matrices and can be unreliable for certain subspaces. We recommend setting the values of
\zeta
and
d
based on properties of the target problem
Cohen (2016)
;
Li and Liu (2021)
;
Martinsson and Tropp (2020)
;
Tropp et al. (2019)
;
Woodruff (2014)
.

2.2
Randomized Krylov

Recall that the Krylov subspace of order
m
associated with
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
is defined as

\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})=\text{span}\{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b},\,\ldots\,,\mathbf{A}^{m-1}\;\mathbf{b}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}.

(5)

In the classical setting, we use the Arnoldi iteration to construct an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{V}_{m}
of
\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
and an upper Hessenberg matrix
\mathbf{H}_{m}=\mathbf{V}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
with the projection of
\mathbf{A}
onto the subspace. We can then approximate
f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}
as
\mathbf{f}_{m}=\beta\mathbf{V}_{m}f(\mathbf{H}_{m})\mathbf{e}_{1}
with
\beta=\|\mathbf{b}\|

Afanasjew et al. (2008b)
;
Eiermann and Ernst (2006)
;
Eiermann et al. (2011)
.

A similar procedure can be defined for non-orthogonal bases. Let
\mathbf{R}_{m}
be an upper Hessenberg matrix and
\mathbf{W}_{m}=[\mathbf{w}_{1}\;\mathbf{w}_{2}\;...\;\mathbf{w}_{m}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
be a matrix whose columns determine an ascending (i.e.,
\mathbf{W}_{k}=[\mathbf{W}_{k-1}\;\mathbf{w}_{k}]
with
\mathbf{W}_{1}~=~[\mathbf{b}/\eta]
for a constant
\eta\in\mathbb{C}
), but not necessarily orthogonal, basis of
\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
. Then, we can define an
Arnoldi-like decomposition

Eiermann and Ernst (2006)
as

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{m}=\mathbf{W}_{m+1}\mathbf{\underline{R}}_{m}=\mathbf{W}_{m}\mathbf{R}_{m}+\mathbf{w}_{m+1}\,r_{m+1,m}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}_{m},

(6)

with

\mathbf{\underline{R}}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{m}\\
r_{m+1,m}\mathbf{e}_{m}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{(m+1)\times m}.

Lemma 1
(Corollary 2.2 from
Balabanov and Grigori (2022)
)

If
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
is an oblivious subspace embedding of
\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
, then the singular values of
\mathbf{W}_{m}
are bounded by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}}\,\sigma_{min}(\mathbf{SW}_{m})\leq\sigma_{min}(\mathbf{W}_{m})\leq\sigma_{max}(\mathbf{W}_{m})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\,\sigma_{max}(\mathbf{SW}_{m}).

(7)

Therefore, it is sufficient to orthogonalize the small sketched matrix
\mathbf{SW}_{m}
for
\mathbf{W}_{m}
to be well-conditioned.
This observation serves as the foundation for the Randomized Gram-Schmidt (RGS) process
Balabanov and Grigori (2022)
;
Timsit et al. (2023)
. For each column
\mathbf{w}_{k+1}
, RGS orthogonalizes the sketch
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_{k+1}
against the sketches of the previous
k
columns, updating the values of
\mathbf{w}_{k+1}
accordingly. This leads to a
sketched-orthogonal
matrix
\mathbf{W}_{m}
, where the sketches of the columns of
\mathbf{W}_{m}
are orthogonal among themselves. In their paper, Balabanov and Grigori
Balabanov and Grigori (2022)
analyzed the stability of the RGS process based on the distortion property of random sketching, the stability of the least-squares solver, and the finite precision of modern computers.
Algorithm
1
describes the modified Arnoldi iteration based on the RGS process
Timsit et al. (2023)
.

In terms of computational complexity, forming the new basis vector
\mathbf{w}_{k+1}
requires
O(N)
operations with
N=\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})
, while the cost of sketching
\mathbf{w}_{k+1}
(line 7) depends on the choice of
\mathbf{S}
. For a sparse sign matrix with
d=O(m)
and
\zeta n
nonzeros, the sketch
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_{k+1}
can be constructed in
O(\zeta n)
time. Orthogonalizing the sketch
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_{k+1}
(lines 8–11) requires
O(dm)=O(m^{2})
operations and updating the vector
\mathbf{w}_{k+1}
(line 12), an additional
O(nm)
operations. Overall, the time complexity of Algorithm
1
is
O(Nm~+~\zeta nm~+~m^{3}~+~nm^{2})
.

The key difference here is that at each iteration
k
, the classical Arnoldi iteration requires two passes over the basis
\mathbf{V}_{k}
, while Algorithm
1
only needs a single pass over the full basis
\mathbf{W}_{k}
after orthogonalizing the sketch
\mathbf{SW}_{k}
. Therefore, if
\mathbf{A}
is very large and sparse, Algorithm
1
is expected to be up to twice as fast as the standard Arnoldi procedure
Balabanov and Grigori (2022)
. Algorithm
1
also uses a single matrix-vector product (BLAS-2) for updating
\mathbf{W}_{k}
instead of a sequence of
dot
products and
axpy
updates (BLAS-1), which is more efficient hardware-wise.

Suppose that
\mathbf{W}_{m}

\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{m}

\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{m}=\alpha\mathbf{W}_{m}f(\mathbf{R}_{m})\mathbf{e}_{1}.

(8)

Moreover, there is a unique polynomial
q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}^{m-1}
that interpolates
f

\mathbf{R}_{m}
in the Hermite sense. Then, it holds that

\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{m}=\alpha\mathbf{W}_{m}q_{m}(\mathbf{R}_{m})\mathbf{e}_{1}=q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b},

Lemma 2

As both
\mathbf{V}_{m}
and
\mathbf{W}_{m}
are bases for the same subspace
\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
, there is a nonsingular, upper triangular matrix
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
such that
\alpha\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{e}_{1}=\beta\mathbf{e}_{1}
and
\mathbf{W}_{m}=\mathbf{V}_{m}\mathbf{Z}
. Then,

\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{R}_{m}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}=\mathbf{H}_{m}-\frac{r_{m+1,m}}{z_{mm}}\,\mathbf{c}\,\mathbf{e}_{m}^{T},\quad\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{V}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{w}_{m+1}.

(10)

Proof

Replacing
\mathbf{W}_{m}
with
\mathbf{V}_{m}\mathbf{Z}
in the Arnoldi-like decomposition (
6
), we have

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{m}\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{V}_{m}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{R}_{m}+\mathbf{w}_{m+1}\,r_{m+1,m}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}_{m}.

Multiplying by
\mathbf{V}_{m}^{*}
on the left and by
\mathbf{Z}^{-1}
on the right,

\mathbf{V}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{m}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{R}_{m}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}+(\mathbf{V}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{w}_{m+1})\,r_{m+1,m}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}_{m}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}.

By construction,
\mathbf{H}_{m}=\mathbf{V}_{m}^{*}\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{m}
and
\mathbf{e}_{m}^{T}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}=1/z_{mm}\mathbf{e}_{m}^{T}
since
\mathbf{Z}
is upper triangular. Setting
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{V}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{w}_{m+1}
and rearranging the terms completes the proof.

Theorem 2.2 from
Grigori et al. (2026)
is equivalent to Lemma
2
when
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
, i.e., when the last vector
\mathbf{w}_{m+1}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{V}_{m}
. With Lemma
2
, the randomized Arnoldi approximation
\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{m}
can be rewritten as

\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{m}=\alpha\mathbf{W}_{m}f(\mathbf{R}_{m})\mathbf{e}_{1}=\beta\mathbf{V}_{m}f\left(\mathbf{H}_{m}-\frac{r_{m+1,m}}{z_{mm}}\,\mathbf{c}\mathbf{e}_{m}^{T}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}.

(11)

This effectively links the approximation (
11
) with the theory in
Palitta et al. (2025)
that analyzes how rank-one modifications to the Hessenberg matrix
\mathbf{H}_{m}
affect matrix functions. In particular, the numerical range
W(\mathbf{R}_{m})
may be significantly larger than
W(\mathbf{H}_{m})
, and may not even be contained in
W(\mathbf{A})
. Yet, according to the numerical analysis from
Palitta et al. (2025)
, only the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{R}_{m}
that are near or within the spectral region of
\mathbf{A}
may have an influence on the convergence of the method.

3
Related Work

A similar idea was explored in
Cortinovis et al. (2024)
. As the first step, their method generates a non-orthogonal basis
\mathbf{W}_{m}
of
\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
using either the incomplete Arnoldi process
(
Saad, 2003
, Chapter 6.4.2)
or the RGS process described in Section
2.2
. Then, the projection of
\mathbf{A}
onto the Krylov subspace can be computed as

\mathbf{W}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{m}=\mathbf{R}_{m}+r_{m+1,m}\mathbf{W}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}_{m+1}\mathbf{e}^{T}_{m}.

(12)

For a well-conditioned basis
\mathbf{W}_{m}
(e.g., when using Algorithm
1
), they argue that a few iterations of LSQR
Paige and Saunders (1982)
are sufficient to get a good enough approximation
\mathbf{y}_{m}
for
\mathbf{W}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}_{m+1}
. If
\mathbf{W}_{m}
is badly conditioned, they suggest using a
sketch-and-precondition
approach
Avron et al. (2010)
;
Martinsson and Tropp (2020)
. It is worth mentioning that solving the least-squares problem can be quite expensive for large
n
and/or
m
. In both cases,
f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}
can be approximated as

\mathbf{f}_{m}^{ckn}=\eta\mathbf{W}_{m}f(\mathbf{Y}_{m})\,\mathbf{e}_{1},

(13)

with
\mathbf{Y}_{m}=\mathbf{W}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{m}=\mathbf{R}_{m}+r_{m+1,m}\,\mathbf{y}_{m}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}_{m}
and
\eta=\|\mathbf{b}\|
for the incomplete orthogonalization or
\eta=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}\|
for the randomized Arnoldi (Algorithm
1
). The expression (
8
) is a simplification of (
13
) since generally
\mathbf{W}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{m}\neq\mathbf{R}_{m}
(see Lemma
2
). In practice, the upper Hessenberg matrices
\mathbf{Y}_{m}
and
\mathbf{R}_{m}
only differ in the last column, such that this difference may have only a minor influence on the first column of
f(\mathbf{W}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{m})
. A similar approximation was proposed in
(
Cortinovis et al., 2024
, Section 3.2)
, but for the incomplete orthogonalization case. Likewise, restarted methods often use a similar approximation with a non-orthonormal basis
\mathbf{W}_{m}
; see
Eiermann and Ernst (2006)
for more details.

The authors of
Grigori et al. (2026)
modify Algorithm
1
to produce a Hessenberg matrix
\mathbf{R_{m}}
that is similar to the one generated by the classical Arnoldi iteration. This is achieved by first constructing a sketch-orthogonal basis
\mathbf{W}_{m}
and then solving a least-squares problem to orthogonalize the last basis vector against all previous vectors.

In
Güttel and Schweitzer (2023)
, the authors propose to first construct a non-orthogonal Krylov basis
\mathbf{W}_{m}
using an incomplete Arnoldi process. They then approximately orthogonalize
\mathbf{W}_{m}
, working only with the sketch of the basis via a thin QR decomposition (“
basis whitening
”
Güttel and Schweitzer (2023)
;
Nakatsukasa and Tropp (2024)
). They call this method
sFOM
, which was later refined in
Palitta et al. (2025)
. It is worth mentioning that in the same paper
Güttel and Schweitzer (2023)
, the authors also propose another randomized Krylov method —
sGMRES
— which requires numerical quadrature rules for evaluating matrix functions.

For solving ODEs via exponential integrators, the authors of
Krieger and Schweitzer (2026)
derived an
a posteriori
error estimation formula for
sFOM
based on the residual of the underlying differential equation. They then use this information to monitor the convergence and decide when to restart. The restart is done by adaptively changing the length of the time step taken during the integration (residual-time restart). The authors include a direct comparison against the restarted randomized method from this paper.

Finally, a recent survey
de Damas et al. (2025)
summarizes the different randomization techniques that are applicable to Krylov methods, focusing primarily on the basis construction methods.

4
Restarted Randomized Krylov

Instead of using a single, large Krylov subspace for computing
f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}
, restarted methods
Afanasjew et al. (2008b)
;
Eiermann and Ernst (2006)
;
Güttel et al. (2020)
employ a sequence of Krylov subspaces of fixed size, refining the approximation
\mathbf{f}_{m}
at each new “cycle”.

In this section, we propose a restarting procedure for the randomized Krylov method from Section
2.2
. Let us consider the first two restart cycles. Suppose that after
m
iterations of Algorithm
1
, we obtain the following decomposition

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{m}^{(1)}=\mathbf{W}_{m}^{(1)}\mathbf{R}_{m}^{(1)}+\mathbf{w}_{m+1}^{(1)}\,r_{m+1,m}^{(1)}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}_{m},

with
\mathbf{w}_{1}^{(1)}=\mathbf{b}/\alpha
and the approximation
\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{1}=\alpha\mathbf{W}_{m}^{(1)}\;f(\mathbf{R}_{m}^{(1)})\mathbf{e}_{1}
. We then “restart” the randomized Arnoldi process, now with
\mathbf{w}_{m+1}^{(1)}
as the starting vector, and obtain a second decomposition

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{m}^{(2)}=\mathbf{W}_{m}^{(2)}\mathbf{R}_{m}^{(2)}+\mathbf{w}_{m+1}^{(2)}\,r_{m+1,m}^{(2)}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}_{m}.

Combining both decompositions, we have

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{2m}=\mathbf{W}_{2m}\mathbf{R}_{2m}+\mathbf{w}_{m+1}^{(2)}\,r_{m+1,m}^{(2)}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}_{2m},

(14)

where

\mathbf{R}_{2m}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{m}^{(1)}&\mathbf{0}\\
r_{m+1,m}^{(1)}\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{m}^{T}&\mathbf{R}_{m}^{(2)}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{W}_{2m}=[\mathbf{W}_{m}^{(1)}\quad\mathbf{W}_{m}^{(2)}].

The Krylov approximation
\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{2}
associated with (
14
) is then defined as

\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{2}=\alpha\;\mathbf{W}_{2m}\;f(\mathbf{R}_{2m})\;\mathbf{e}_{1}.

(15)

Due to the block triangular structure of
\mathbf{R}_{2m}
,
f(\mathbf{R}_{2m})
has the following form

f(\mathbf{R}_{2m})=\begin{bmatrix}f(\mathbf{R}_{m}^{(1)})&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{F}_{2,1}&f(\mathbf{R}_{m}^{(2)})\end{bmatrix},

and thus, (
15
) can be rewritten as

\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{2}=\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{1}+\alpha\;\mathbf{W}_{m}^{(2)}\;\mathbf{F}_{2,1}\;\mathbf{e}_{1}.

(16)

Therefore, as long as we can compute
\mathbf{F}_{2,1}
, we can update the Krylov approximation
\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{2}
without needing to store the basis from the previous cycle. For the method to be numerically stable
Eiermann and Ernst (2006)
;
Güttel et al. (2020)
, the matrix
\mathbf{F}_{2,1}
is taken directly from the bottom-left block of
f(\mathbf{R}_{2m})
, which in turn entails the computation of the full matrix
f(\mathbf{R}_{2m})
. Algorithm
2
describes the complete restarting procedure for an arbitrary number of cycles. Note that the size of
\mathbf{R}_{km}
grows with each restart, such that evaluating
f(\mathbf{R}_{km})
may become too expensive to compute after a large number of restarts
Eiermann and Ernst (2006)
;
Güttel et al. (2020)
. In this scenario, additional acceleration techniques may be required, such as spectral deflation
Eiermann et al. (2011)
, thick restarting
Baker et al. (2005)
, and weighted inner products
Embree et al. (2017)
. Note that the Krylov method proposed by
Frommer et al. (2014b)
;
Güttel et al. (2020)
does not have this limitation, but they rely on quadrature rules for evaluating Cauchy-Stieltjes functions and the exponential; hence, they are outside the scope of this paper.

As with other iterative procedures, we need to estimate the error
\|f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{k}\|
in order to determine when the restarted algorithm should stop. A simple error estimate that is often used in Krylov methods is the difference between two successive restart cycles:

\|f(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{k}\|\approx\|\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{k-1}-\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{k}\|.

The convergence of (classical) restarted methods has been established for entire functions of order
1

(
Eiermann and Ernst, 2006
, Theorem 4.2)
and Stieltjes functions
Frommer et al. (2014a)
, while the general case remains an open problem. The convergence of restarted methods in the context of Laplace transforms for Hermitian matrices is discussed in
Tsolakis (2023)
(our focus is on the non-symmetric case). With randomization, a rigorous analysis becomes even more challenging. Although
Cortinovis et al. (2024)
;
Güttel and Schweitzer (2023)
;
Palitta et al. (2025)
provide some theoretical results, the convergence of unrestarted randomized methods is still not fully understood due to many obstacles in the analysis, such as the fact that the numerical range of the Hessenberg matrix
\mathbf{R}_{m}
is difficult to control and may not be contained in the numerical range of
\mathbf{A}

Grigori et al. (2026)
;
Palitta et al. (2025)
. Considering these limitations, we will analyze the convergence of the proposed algorithm in terms of numerical experiments.

5.1
Convection-Diffusion

The first example consists of solving a three-dimensional convection-diffusion equation over a domain
\Omega

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(\mathbf{x},t)=\nabla(\alpha\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t))+\beta\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t)+g(\mathbf{x},t),

(17)

for a time
t>0
, the space variable
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)\in\Omega
, a diffusion coefficient
\alpha
, and a convection coefficient
\beta
. In this example, we consider
\alpha
and
\beta
to be constant in both time and space. For the problem to be well defined, we set the initial condition as
u(\mathbf{x},0)=u_{0}(\mathbf{x})
and impose boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
. After applying the standard Galerkin finite element procedure
Larson and Bengzon (2013)
;
Zienkiewicz et al. (2013)
over the domain
\Omega
, we obtain the following system of equations:

\mathbf{M}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{\hat{u}}=-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\hat{u}}+\mathbf{M}\mathbf{g}(t),

(18)

where
\mathbf{M}
is the mass matrix,
\mathbf{A}=\alpha\mathbf{D}+\beta\mathbf{C}
is the stiffness matrix, and
\mathbf{g}(t)
is the load vector. Here,
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{C}
correspond to the matrices related to the discretization of the diffusion and convection parts in the equation, respectively. We can then write the solution of (
18
) as

\mathbf{\hat{u}}(t)=\exp(-\mathbf{L}\,t)\,\mathbf{\hat{u}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\exp(-\mathbf{L}\,s)\,\mathbf{g}(t-s)\,ds,

(19)

with
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
. To simplify the computation, we assume that the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}
is lumped
Larson and Bengzon (2013)
;
Zienkiewicz et al. (2013)
, such that all of its mass is concentrated on the diagonal. We also assume that the load vector
\mathbf{g}
remains constant throughout the entire simulation, such that the solution (
19
) can be simplified to

\mathbf{\hat{u}}(t)=\exp(-\mathbf{L}\,t)\,\mathbf{\hat{u}}_{0}-\mathbf{L}^{-1}\exp(-\mathbf{L}\,t)\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{g},

(20)

or, equivalently,

\mathbf{\hat{u}}(t)=\mathbf{\hat{u}}_{0}+t\varphi_{1}(-\mathbf{L}t)\,\mathbf{b},\quad\varphi_{1}(z)=\frac{e^{z}-1}{z},

(21)

with
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{\hat{u}}_{0}
. The function
\varphi_{1}(z)
is known as the “phi function” in the exponential integrator literature
Hochbruck and Ostermann (2010)
. It is worth mentioning that evaluating (
21
) is more stable and faster than (
20
) as it avoids solving a linear system with
\mathbf{L}
, which can be quite problematic when
\mathbf{L}
has an eigenvalue near the origin. This is not an issue when using (
21
) as
\varphi_{1}(z)
is an entire function.

The convergence curves for the restarted methods are shown in Fig.
4
. As expected, restarting the Arnoldi procedure slows down the convergence of the method, requiring more iterations to reach the same accuracy. Yet, surprisingly,
restart-rand
converges faster than the standard
restart
in test (a), and even beats the
deflated
method for
m_{r}=20
. This can be explained in terms of the (sketched) Ritz values, as they determine the nodes of the underlying interpolation process for the Krylov approximant
\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{m}
(
8
).

At restart cycle
k
, let
\Lambda(\mathbf{R}_{km})=\{\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{km}\}
and let
q\in\mathcal{P}^{km-1}
interpolate
f
at these nodes in the Hermite sense, so that
\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{k}=q(\mathbf{L})\mathbf{b}
by (
9
). Suppose that
D
is a closed, convex set containing
W(\mathbf{L})
and all nodes generated up to cycle
k
, and that
f
is analytic on a neighborhood of
D
. The Crouzeix–Palencia theorem
Crouzeix and Palencia (2017)
then gives

\|f(\mathbf{L})\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{k}\|\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\;\|\mathbf{b}\|\;\max_{z\in W(\mathbf{L})}|f(z)-q(z)|.

(23)

The interpolation error
|f(z)-q(z)|
is given by

|f(z)-q(z)|=|f[\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},...,\vartheta_{km};z]|\;\chi_{km}(z)

with
\chi_{km}(z)=\Pi_{i=1}^{km}|z-\vartheta_{i}|
and
f[\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{km},z]
as the divided difference of
f
of order
km
over the nodes
\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{km},z
. Using the Hermite-Genocchi representation of the divided difference
Palitta et al. (2025)
;
de Boor (2005)
yields

\bigl|f[\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{km},z]\bigr|\leq\frac{\max_{\zeta\in D}\bigl|f^{(km)}(\zeta)\bigr|}{(km)!}.

Then, substituting the terms in (
23
), we have

\|f(\mathbf{L})\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{\hat{f}}_{k}\|\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\;\|\mathbf{b}\|\;\frac{\max_{\zeta\in D}\bigl|f^{(km)}(\zeta)\bigr|}{(km)!}\;\max_{z\in W(\mathbf{L})}|\chi_{km}(z)|.

(24)

From the bound, we can heuristically infer that
\max_{z\in W(\mathbf{L})}|\chi_{km}(z)|
will be small when every point of
W(\mathbf{L})
is close to one or more eigenvalues of
\mathbf{R}_{km}
, and that its value is controlled by the most distant point of
W(\mathbf{L})
(usually an extreme point) since
\chi_{km}(z)
grows rapidly outside the convex hull of the nodes. It is worth mentioning that the main purpose of the bound (
24
) is to provide some insight into how the distribution of the interpolation nodes affects the overall convergence of the methods. See
Eiermann et al. (2000)
;
Eiermann and Ernst (2006)
;
Frommer et al. (2014a)
;
Palitta et al. (2025)
for a more in-depth analysis.

5.2
Circular Membrane

The second example consists of simulating the vibrations of a circular membrane
(
Strutt, 2011
, Chapter 9)
. Let us consider a membrane
\Omega
of radius
\mu
centered at the origin. At a time
t>0
, the height of the membrane at any point
(x,y)
is given by
u(x,y,t)
, measured from the rest position. The membrane is attached to a rigid frame, such that
u(x,y,t)=0
for all
(x,y)\in\partial\Omega
. The vibrations in the membrane can then be described as

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}u(x,y,t)=\nu^{2}\,\Delta u(x,y,t),

(25)

where
\nu>0
is the speed at which the transverse waves propagate in the membrane.
After using the finite element method
Zienkiewicz et al. (2013)
to describe (
25
) in terms of a discrete mesh over
\Omega
, we have

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\mathbf{\hat{u}}=-\nu^{2}\,\mathbf{L}\mathbf{\hat{u}},

(26)

where
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
for the stiffness matrix
\mathbf{A}
and the lumped mass matrix
\mathbf{M}
. Suppose that the initial conditions are set to

u(x,y,0)=J_{p}\left(\frac{\eta_{pk}\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}{\mu}\right),\qquad\frac{d}{dt}u(x,y,0)=0,

(27)

where
J_{p}(x)
is the Bessel function

J_{p}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!\,(k+p)!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2k+p}},

and
\eta_{pk}
is the
k
-th zero of
J_{p}(x)
. Then, the solution for (
26
) can be written as

\mathbf{\hat{u}}(t)=\cos(\nu t\,\sqrt{\mathbf{L}})\,\mathbf{b},

(28)

where
\sqrt{\mathbf{L}}
is any square root of
\mathbf{L}

(
Gantmakher, 1959
, p. 124)
and
\mathbf{b}
is a vector containing the value of
J_{p}(x)
for each node in the discrete mesh. For our experiments, we consider a circular membrane with radius
\mu=1
and set the initial conditions using a fourth-order Bessel function
J_{4}(x)
and its fourth zero. The matrix
\mathbf{L}
was generated with
gmsh

Geuzaine and Remacle (2009)
and
FreeFem++

Hecht (2012)
using P1 finite elements with a mesh size of
0.0025
. To impose the Dirichlet boundary conditions
u(x,y,t)=0
for all
(x,y)\in\partial\Omega
, we replace the corresponding rows of
\mathbf{L}
with the identity matrix
Larson and Bengzon (2013)
. The resulting matrix
\mathbf{L}
has
582,547
rows and
4,062,636
nonzeros. The maximum and minimum eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
are
4.502\times 10^{6}
and
1
, respectively. Its condition number
\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{L})
is
7.11\times 10^{7}
. We adopted as reference the solution obtained by
arnoldi
with
m=2000
. The matrix cosine was computed using the scaling-and-squaring method from
Al-Mohy et al. (2015)
.

Graph Laplacian

The third and last example consists of modeling the diffusion on a graph
G=(V,E)
as

\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x},\quad\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0},

(29)

where
\mathbf{L}
is the out-degree graph Laplacian (i.e.,
\mathbf{L}[i,i]=\sum_{i\neq j}|\mathbf{L}[i,j]|
and
\mathbf{L}[i,j]=-1
if there is an edge from node
i
to node
j
,
0
otherwise) and
\mathbf{x}
is the probability distribution over the nodes at a time
t\geq 0
. Note that the Laplacian is singular (
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
) and its entire spectrum is located in the closed right half-plane. The solution for (
29
) is then expressed as

\mathbf{x}=e^{-t\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{H}(t)\mathbf{x}_{0}.

(30)

The matrix-valued function
\mathbf{H}(t)
is called the
heat kernel
on
G
and models the diffusion over the network (see
(
Benzi and Boito, 2020
, Section 6.2)
). In this example, the initial probability distribution
\mathbf{x}_{0}
was set by first drawing
n
i.i.d. random numbers, collecting them into a vector
\mathbf{y}=\{y_{i}\sim\textsc{uniform}(0,1),\quad i=1,2,...,n\}
, and then taking
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{y}/\|\mathbf{y}\|_{1}
. A summary of the complex networks used in this section is given in Table
. The reference solution was obtained by running the
restart
method with a restart length
m_{r}=100
and a tolerance of
10^{-12}
. Note that most results from this section can be extended to the in-degree case with some minor modifications.

Figs.
and
show the convergence curves for all numerical methods. For both the
kronecker
and
orkut
networks, the error curves are dictated by the type of orthogonalization — (sketched) full orthogonalization (
i.e.
,
arnoldi
and
rand
/
rand-ls
), incomplete orthogonalization, and restarting — without a clear separation between the standard and randomized versions. For
uk-2002
, all the methods share the same convergence rate due to the narrow spectrum of
\mathbf{L}
.

Performance

The performance tests were carried out on a commodity desktop with an AMD Ryzen 7 5800X 8C/16T @4.6GHz and 32GB of RAM, running Arch Linux. Fig.

compares the execution time and accuracy of the numerical methods. For the
conv-diff
test (a–c), we set
m_{r}=k=20

d=3200
for

,

, and
sFOM
; and
d=320
for

membrane
test (d), we set
m_{r}=k=80
;
d=3600
for
rand
,
rand-ls
, and
sFOM
; and
d=960
for
restart-rand
. The number of restarts and the basis size
m
were adjusted in such a way that the accuracy of all methods is as close as possible.

In all tests,
arnoldi
has the highest execution time among all methods due to the full orthogonalization of the Krylov basis. Replacing the classical Arnoldi procedure with the randomized version (i.e., the
rand
method) leads to a speedup of
1.26-2.90\times
for
\zeta=4
and
1.28-3.03\times
for
\zeta=1
. Generally speaking, these methods are quite memory-bound, and thus, the performance gains from
rand
heavily depend on the memory subsystem. The
conv-diff
examples are quite large, i.e.,
\mathbf{L}
consumes
1.13
GB, while a single column of
\mathbf{V}_{m}
/
\mathbf{W}_{m}
uses
38.4
MB. This entails that the method needs to load these matrices from the main memory as the L3 cache has only
32
MB. The modified Gram-Schmidt process orthogonalizes the basis via a sequence of BLAS-1 routines (
axpy
and
dot
), while RGS updates the basis
\mathbf{W}_{m}
using a single BLAS-2 routine (
gemv
), which is significantly more memory efficient. The sketch
\mathbf{SW}_{m}
is sufficiently small to fit into the L3 cache, e.g., a
3200\times 600
matrix
\mathbf{SW}_{m}
consumes just
15.4
MB, and a few of its columns can be stored comfortably in L2. As a result,
rand
has a speedup of
2\times
or more for this test. In contrast,
membrane
is noticeably smaller with
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{v}_{k}
/
\mathbf{w}_{k}
using
65
MB and
4.66
MB, respectively. Hence, calling
axpy
after a
dot
product is relatively cheap since the working vectors are already loaded in the cache, and thus, the performance gains of
rand
over
arnoldi
are smaller (
26-28\%
) for this test. Solving the least-squares problem using LSMR for
rand-ls
imposes a
9
to
23\%
performance penalty depending on the size of the input matrix and Krylov basis.

With a restarted Krylov method, the program spends significantly less time on the orthogonalization of the basis, since it only needs to work with a small set of basis vectors at each restart cycle. As a result, the faster orthogonalization in Algorithm
1
has less impact on the overall performance of the program. For tests (b), (c), and (d), where all restarted methods have similar convergence rates,
restart-rand
with
\zeta=4
reduces the execution time by
13-25\%
relative to the standard
restart
. Reducing the sparsity parameter to
\zeta=1
, it now achieves a speedup of
1.18\times
to
1.40\times
. Spectral deflation actually hurts the performance in these tests by up to
20\%
since it requires computing and sorting the Ritz values, augmenting the subspace with Schur vectors, and orthogonalizing
\ell=5
additional vectors. It is worth mentioning that the relative error of the regular
restart
method did not fall below

in the
membrane
test. Other methods do not have this problem. In test (a),
restart-rand
shows a speedup of
1.61
and
1.77
over the standard
restart
method with
\zeta=4
and
\zeta=1
, respectively. Recall from Section

that the convergence rate of

is significantly higher than

in test (a), and thus requires fewer restarts to achieve the same accuracy. Despite having a fast convergence rate,

Both

and
sFOM
have a fixed orthogonalization cost per new basis vector, regardless of the number of vectors already generated, but require a larger number of iterations to achieve the target accuracy compared to
arnoldi
. As a result, their performance is similar to the restarted procedures.
sFOM
pays a small overhead for sketching and basis whitening, increasing the execution time by between

and

6
Conclusion

In this article, we combine the restart technique with the randomized Krylov method from
Timsit et al. (2023)
in the context of general matrix functions. Our numerical experiments show that our randomized algorithm significantly outperforms the classical method while producing a highly accurate solution for complex problems. In some cases, the randomization can even lead to better convergence rates. Although our method performs well in practice, many of its features are not completely understood. In particular, it is missing a formal explanation of how the Ritz value perturbations can improve the convergence rate of restarted methods under certain conditions. Likewise, a theoretical analysis of its convergence may lead to a better error estimation, allowing greater control over the number of restarts. Our randomized Krylov method can also be combined with other acceleration techniques, such as spectral deflation
Eiermann et al. (2011)
, weighted inner products
Embree et al. (2017)
, or “thick” restarting
Baker et al. (2005)
, to further improve its performance.

Appendix A
Implementation details

The test suite was written using the HighFM toolbox
1
1

1

https://gitlab.com/highfm/highfm
. This toolbox is written entirely in
C++20
, and its main goal is to provide a user-friendly interface for solving problems from linear algebra without sacrificing performance. More specifically:

Matrices and their operations:
Sparse matrices follow the compressed sparse row (CSR) format, while dense matrices are stored in column-major form. Matrix and vector operations are directly translated into routine calls to Intel MKL with some operations hand-optimized to achieve the highest performance on the target system.

Random sketching:
We use
PCG64DXSM

O’Neill (2014)
as the pseudorandom number generator. It is an improved version of the default generator in the popular NumPy
Harris et al. (2020)
module. The sparse sign matrix is generated at the start of each randomized method and stored in a compressed sparse row format. It is then applied to the target vector or matrix using
spmv
and
spmm
, respectively.

Matrix functions:

\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{A})
,
\exp(\mathbf{A})
, and
\cos(\sqrt{\mathbf{A}})
were all computed using the scaling-and-squaring method with the Padé approximant from
Higham (2005)
;
Al-Mohy et al. (2015)
. The degree of the approximant is automatically chosen through a careful analysis of the error bounds. The LAPACK routines used are
dsgesv
for LU solve and
dgees
for the Schur decomposition.

sFOM:
For
sFOM
, the program calls
dgeqrf
for the thin QR decomposition (
\mathbf{SW}_{m}=\mathbf{Q}_{m}\mathbf{T}_{m}
) and
dtrsm
for the triangular linear solve. Per LAPACK specification, the factors of the QR decomposition are stored in a single object. The original formula
Güttel and Schweitzer (2023)
for
sFOM
is
\mathbf{f}_{m}^{gs}=\mathbf{W}_{m}\mathbf{T}_{m}^{-1}\,f(\mathbf{Q}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{SAW}_{m}\mathbf{T}_{m}^{-1})\,\mathbf{Q}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}
. We use the alternative form proposed in
(
Palitta et al., 2025
, Eq. 18)
:

\mathbf{f}_{m}^{gs}=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}\|\mathbf{W}_{m}\mathbf{T}_{m}^{-1}\,f(\mathbf{X}_{m})\,\mathbf{e}_{1},\qquad\mathbf{X}_{m}=\mathbf{T}_{m}\mathbf{R}_{m}\mathbf{T}_{m}^{-1}+\frac{r_{m+1,m}}{t_{m}}\mathbf{t}\mathbf{e}_{m}^{T}.

(A.1)

with

\mathbf{SW}_{m+1}=\mathbf{Q}_{m+1}\mathbf{T}_{m+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{m}&\mathbf{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{T}_{m}&\mathbf{t}\\
\mathbf{0}&t_{m+1}\end{bmatrix}

Note that
\mathbf{Q}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}\|\mathbf{e}_{1}
is only valid for a positive
t_{11}
, which is not guaranteed by
dgeqrf
. Therefore, we compute
\text{sign}(t_{11})\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}\|\mathbf{e}_{1}
instead of just
\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}\|\mathbf{e}_{1}
as all matrices are real in our experiments.

Spectral deflation:
At each restart cycle, the algorithm first computes the Schur decomposition via
dgees
, reorders the Schur form so that the
\ell
smallest eigenvalues appear at the start, and then constructs the deflation subspace with a single call to
gemm
, as shown in
Eiermann et al. (2011)
. The new basis vectors are then orthogonalized against the augmented subspace.
