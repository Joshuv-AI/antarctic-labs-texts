The limits of segmental dichotomy: rethinking how we classify and compare minimally invasive liver resections
Authors: Víctor Lopez-Lopez, Cecilia Maina, Ricardo Robles-Campos
Published: 2025

Minimally invasive liver surgery (MILS) has significantly transformed hepatobiliary surgery, offering reproducible perioperative benefits in well-selected patients, including reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stays, lower complication rates, and faster recovery times (1). These advantages have contributed to the growing adoption of laparoscopic approaches, particularly for minor liver resections, and have redefined the standard of care in many high-volume centers worldwide. However, despite the clear overall benefits, important questions remain regarding how these outcomes differ depending on the anatomical location of the lesion. Specifically, the extent to which these benefits are preserved when comparing resections in anterolateral (AL) segments versus more technically demanding posterosuperior (PS) segments has yet to be fully elucidated. This distinction is not trivial, as the technical complexity, exposure, and vascular proximity vary greatly between segments, potentially influencing both surgical planning and patient outcomes. In their large, multicenter international study, Sijberden et al. (2) address this critical gap by providing compelling evidence on the differential benefit of laparoscopic over open liver resection depending on segmental location, using a sound propensity score-matched analysis encompassing over 7,000 patients from expert hepatobiliary centers. Their findings offer new insights into how surgical anatomy interacts with the benefits of minimally invasive techniques, and how this knowledge should inform both clinical practice and future research.

The present study adheres to the Brisbane 2000 terminology (3), dividing minor liver resections into AL segments (2, 3, 4b, 5, 6) and PS segments (1, 4a, 7, 8). While the classification of liver segments into AL and PS groups provides a pragmatic framework for large-scale analyses, it fails to account for the substantial heterogeneity in technical difficulty that exists within each category. For instance, grouping segments 7, 8, 4a, and 1 together as “PS” obscures important distinctions: segment 4a, located anteriorly and often more accessible, is typically less challenging than deeply situated segment 7 lesions, which are posterior, high, and adjacent to the diaphragm and major hepatic veins. Similarly, although both segments 7 and 8 are considered PS, resections in segment 7 are often more technically demanding due to poorer visualization and more complex mobilization compared to segment 8 (4). The same principle applies to AL segments. Segments 2 and 3 are relatively superficial and located in the left lateral section, segments 5 and 6, especially when located posteriorly or deeply within the right lobe, may pose greater technical challenges (5). These intra-group variations highlight the oversimplification of binary AL/PS stratification and reinforce the need for more refined, anatomy-based classification systems that incorporate lesion depth, vascular proximity, and access trajectory to better represent the true complexity of liver resections.

On the other hand, it is important to acknowledge that not all resections within a given segment share the same technical complexity. A superficial segment 8 lesion near the liver capsule is significantly less challenging than a deep lesion adjacent to the middle hepatic vein or segmental pedicle. This anatomical nuance carries implications for surgical planning and comparative studies. Lesions in the same nominal segment may have very different technical demands, influencing the choice of surgical approach. As a result, the assignment of a lesion to open versus laparoscopic resection may be confounded by case complexity within each segment, leading to inherent selection bias that must be acknowledged when interpreting the results. Even within a single Couinaud segment, lesion characteristics such as depth, vascular contact, and prior surgical field can substantially alter technical feasibility and associated risks. A deep lesion in segment 7 abutting the right hepatic vein is markedly different from a peripherally located lesion in the same segment (6). In future studies, a more granular difficulty score accounting for lesion depth, vascular proximity, and prior surgical history may provide a better basis for comparison than segment alone.

One of the contributions of this study is its time-dependent analysis, which highlights that the differential benefit of laparoscopy appears more pronounced in earlier periods but tends to equalize in more recent years. As surgeons and centers advance along the learning curve, not only does operative efficiency improve, but key outcome metrics such as complication rates, conversion rates, and intraoperative blood loss also become more favorable. The findings further reveal that high-volume centers consistently demonstrated magnified benefits from minimally invasive approaches, particularly for AL resections, but also increasingly for PS resections as experience grew (7). Importantly, these observations raise a crucial methodological consideration. Comparing early versus mature stages of laparoscopic programs without accounting for the temporal evolution of expertise may misrepresent the true benefit of MILS, especially in anatomically demanding PS locations where the margin for error is narrower. Without proper stratification by time period or institutional experience, pooled outcome analyses risk conflating results from novice and expert phases, thereby diluting the observed advantages of laparoscopy or exaggerating its risks. Therefore, future comparative studies should systematically stratify outcomes not only by anatomical segment but also by center volume, individual surgeon experience, and temporal cohort.

The study reaffirms that laparoscopic resections in PS segments remain more technically demanding than those in AL segments. The restricted operative field, limited working angles, and proximity to major vascular structures, such as the hepatic veins and inferior vena cava, create significant challenges for visualization, parenchymal transection, and vascular control. Adequate exposure often requires extensive mobilization of the right liver lobe, which can lead to hepatic rotation and potential venous outflow obstruction, thereby increasing the risk of intraoperative bleeding and hemodynamic instability. These factors collectively contribute to the relatively smaller gains observed in estimated blood loss reduction, operative time, and complication rates for PS segments compared to their AL counterparts.

However, the landscape is evolving rapidly. Technical refinements—such as the adoption of semi-lateral or left decubitus positioning to optimize gravity-assisted retraction, and the incorporation of high-resolution laparoscopic intraoperative ultrasound for precise tumor localization and vascular mapping—have improved the feasibility of PS resections. Additionally, modern energy devices, improved hemostatic tools, and enhanced camera systems have facilitated safer and more efficient parenchymal transection in these difficult-to-access areas. In this context, the anatomical complexity of the PS segments no longer constitutes a contraindication to a laparoscopic approach. These developments support the broader notion that MILS, when performed in experienced hands and with proper infrastructure, can be safely and effectively extended to technically complex liver resections traditionally reserved for the open approach.

In addition to the discussion of laparoscopic approaches, nowadays it is also important to highlight the role of robotics in this field. The advent of robotic-assisted surgery in the field of liver resection has introduced new possibilities for approaching anatomically challenging segments (8). The intrinsic capabilities of robotic platforms—such as enhanced three-dimensional visualization, articulated instruments with seven degrees of freedom, tremor filtration, and stable camera control—provide significant ergonomic and technical advantages over conventional laparoscopy. These features facilitate precise dissection and suturing in deep or posteriorly located segments, where exposure and angulation are limited in standard laparoscopy. Moreover, most surgeons adopting robotic liver surgery today have previously gained substantial experience with laparoscopic techniques, which allows for a seamless transition to the robotic platform and mitigates the learning curve typically associated with new technologies (9). This cumulative expertise, combined with the robotic system’s strengths, has translated into increased confidence and efficiency when performing resections, further expanding the indications for MILS in complex anatomical locations.

While the authors used rigorous statistical matching, the inherent retrospective nature of the study implies that unmeasured confounders which may influence surgical choice and outcomes. Furthermore, decision-making may be impacted by factors not captured in databases, including individual surgeon judgment, intraoperative findings, and institutional protocols. Despite these limitations, the real-world scope of this multicenter study reflects current practice in expert centers across Europe and North America. It supports the broader adoption of MILS, particularly for AL segments, while encouraging the extension of these benefits to PS resections in centers with appropriate expertise. It is also worth noting that some centers have adopted validated difficulty scoring systems, which may provide a more nuanced framework and help bridge the oversimplification inherent in the AL/PS dichotomy.

In conclusion, the study by Sijberden et al. (2) delivers valuable insight into the evolving landscape of laparoscopic liver surgery. It confirms that laparoscopic resection offers significant perioperative benefits over open surgery across both AL and PS segments, with particularly strong evidence in AL resections. However, these benefits are not intrinsic to anatomical location alone but are modulated by case complexity, surgical experience, and institutional expertise. As laparoscopic techniques and training continue to advance, the historical distinction between “easy” and “difficult” segments may become less relevant. Ultimately, the decision to pursue a laparoscopic approach should be individualized, balancing anatomical considerations, lesion characteristics, and the surgeon’s experience. Studies like this one bring us closer to that goal and provide a strong foundation for ongoing refinement in the practice of MILS.
