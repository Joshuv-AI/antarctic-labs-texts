Comprehensive profiling of potato responses to Alternaria alternata and its toxins (TeA and AOH): ultrastructural, biochemical and multivariate insights
Authors: Adhishree Nagda, Mukesh Meena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeaf spot caused by Alternaria alternata poses a major threat to potato production, yet the distinct impacts of its key toxins remain unresolved. This pathogen releases a range of secondary metabolites that interfere with normal leaf function and accelerate disease progression. Among them, alternariol (AOH) and tenuazonic acid (TeA) stand out as potent phytotoxins, known for triggering oxidative damage and weakening host tissues during infection. In the present work, we employed a multidisciplinary approach to investigate the physiological, biochemical, histochemical, ultrastructural, and gene-expression responses of potato leaves subjected to A. alternata and its host-specific toxins, AOH and TeA.ResultsHistochemical assays confirmed enhanced accumulation of ROS (H2O2, O2·−), callose deposition, and lipid peroxidation. Pathogen infection induced necrotic lesions and chlorosis, with SEM and TEM analyses revealing distinct cytological damage. A. alternata and TeA caused extensive tissue collapse, membrane disruption, and organelle disintegration, whereas AOH was less destructive, primarily inducing vesicle accumulation and chloroplast disorganization. This represents the first ultrastructural evidence of AOH induced cytotoxicity in plant tissue, highlighting its underestimated role in plant stress. Antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and glutathione reductase (GR), along with defense related enzymes including phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), peroxidase (POX), exhibited significant induction, which was supported by Native-PAGE revealing treatment-specific isoform expression of SOD and CAT. SDS-PAGE demonstrated altered host protein profiles, with distinct induction of mid-molecular weight (40–50 kDa) and high-molecular weight (~ 75 kDa) proteins under toxin and pathogen stress, indicating activation of defense associated proteins. Gene expression analysis further demonstrated a clear temporal increase in Cu/Zn-SOD, CAT1, and APX, with the strongest activation observed at 24–48 h, following the pattern: pathogen > TeA > AOH. Principal component analysis (PCA) accounted for 82.13% and 12.52% of the total variance across all measured parameters. Correlation and clustering analyses highlighted early pigment loss followed by delayed activation of oxidative stress-responsive enzymes, while exhibiting strong negative correlations between pigments and ROS, alongside positive correlations among stress-related enzymes.ConclusionThe severity of oxidative damage followed the trend: pathogen > TeA > AOH. These findings reveal stress signatures and defense shifts, providing mechanistic insights into Alternaria pathogenesis and toxin-driven oxidative damage in potato. Background

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) ranks as the world’s third most important food crop, with global production estimated at around 383 million tonnes in 2023 [1]. Nonetheless, its productivity is severely constrained by fungal infections, notably Alternaria alternata, the causative agent of brown spot disease. The necrotrophic fungus is a cosmopolitan pathogen that thrives in warm, humid weather, produces dark brown spots ranging from dots up to 10 mm in diameter that may enlarge and eventually cause defoliation and yield losses of over 50% under favourable weather conditions [2–4]. Understanding both pathogen behavior and the host’s coordinated physiological and molecular defense responses is therefore fundamental for developing sustainable disease management strategies.

The pathogenicity of A. alternata is closely associated to its ability to secrete a range of secondary metabolites and toxins such as alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), tentoxin (TEN), and altenuene (ALT). These metabolites exert cytotoxic, genotoxic, and photosynthesis inhibiting effects on host cells [5–8]. AOH breaks DNA strands and is implicated in the generation of ROS, while TeA strongly inhibits protein biosynthesis and compromises chloroplast integrity [9, 10]. The distinct and combined impact of these toxins on host physiology is lacking research focus despite their known bioactivities, especially for plants like potatoes that provide significant economic value.

Response to pathogen and toxin exposure lies within a plant’s multifaceted defense system consisting of oxidative bursts, antioxidant systems, hypersensitive response (HR)-like cell death mechanisms, enhanced immunity through cell wall thickening via callose and lignin deposition [11–13]. At the cellular level, these responses damage organelles, including mitochondria and chloroplasts, which can be observed using transmission and scanning electron microscopy (TEM and SEM) [14]. Concomitantly, robust biochemical adjustments occur, particularly the enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione reductase (GR), as well as phenylpropanoid-associated enzymes, including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), and peroxidases (POX) [15, 16]. However, the temporal dynamics of potato defense responses to A. alternata infection and its phytotoxins remain insufficiently characterized, and the relationship between antioxidant enzyme activity and the corresponding gene expression during these stresses is still poorly understood.

Emerging work on Pleosporaceae pathogens highlights their capacity to manipulate host redox-regulating gene networks during infection [17]. In Solanaceae crops, including tomato and potato, pathogen attack rapidly alters the expression of core antioxidant enzymes such as Cu/Zn-SOD, CAT1/2, and APX1/2, reflecting the plant’s need to rebalance ROS levels under oxidative stress [18]. These classical ROS-scavenging genes function alongside broader redox-associated components, such as GalUR, which regulates ascorbate biosynthesis, and the SOS1 − SOS13 family, which contributes to ion homeostasis and stress resilience [19–21]. Although potato transcriptomic resources remain less comprehensive than those available for tomato, conserved Solanaceae-wide patterns suggest that coordinated regulation of SOD, CAT, and APX plays a central role in defense against A. alternata and its phytotoxins. Given this gap, targeted RT-qPCR analysis of these key antioxidant genes, as presented in the present study, provides essential insight into the molecular basis of potato tolerance or susceptibility.

To bridge these gaps, the present study employs an integrated analytical framework encompassing physiological, biochemical, histological, ultrastructural, and gene-expression approaches to decipher the mechanistic basis of A. alternata pathogenesis and toxin-induced damage in potato. Pigment degradation, ROS accumulation, membrane integrity or damage, antioxidant enzymatic activity, including isoenzyme polymorphism, along with ultrastructural changes were assessed under both pathogen and toxin (AOH and TeA) treatments. In parallel, RT-qPCR was used to quantify transcriptional changes in key antioxidant defense genes (Cu/Zn-SOD, CAT1, and APX) to elucidate their temporal regulation in relation to biochemical and structural damage. Multivariate analyses such as principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), together with correlation analysis, were applied to distinguish major indicators related to various stress responses. Collectively, this work advances our understanding of host-pathogen-toxin interactions in potato and establishes a comprehensive set of physiological, biochemical, ultrastructural, and molecular indicators that can guide future resistance breeding and integrated disease management initiatives.

Materials and methods

Pathogen isolation and identification

Infected potato leaves were surface sterilized and cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) with streptomycin at 25 °C for 3 − 7 days. Under a compound microscope, morphological characterisation was done based on conidial features and colony appearance. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method was used to extract genomic DNA [22]. The amplification of internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions and 5.8 S rDNA was performed using universal primer pair ITS1 (5’TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG’3) and ITS4 (5’TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC’3) [23], purified, and sequenced. Identification was confirmed by BLAST analysis. ClustalW was used to align the isolate’s ITS sequence with those of closely related Alternaria spp. Using the Maximum likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates, a phylogenetic tree was created in MEGA11 software [24].

Pathogenicity assay

Pathogenicity was assessed by inoculating whole plants and detached leaves with a conidial suspension (1 × 106 conidia mL-1 in 0.05% Tween 20). The seed tubers of a susceptible potato cultivar (cv. Kufri Bahar) were kindly provided by the Rajasthan College of Agriculture, Maharana Pratap University of Agriculture and Technology (MPUAT), Udaipur, Rajasthan, India. The potato plants used for inoculation were grown from seed tubers under controlled growth chamber conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 16/8 h photoperiod, 60 − 70% relative humidity). Four-week-old plants bearing fully expanded leaves were used for infection. Disease development was recorded daily, and sampling for symptom assessment was performed up to 8 days after inoculation. Re-isolation was done to confirm Koch’s postulates. Disease severity was scored at 7 days after inoculation using a 0 − 5 scale (0 = no symptoms, 1 = 1 − 10%, 2 = 11 − 25%, 3 = 26 − 50%, 4 = 51 − 75%, and 5 = > 75% leaf area infected) based on the percentage of infected leaf area as described in Bhat et al. [25].

Toxin extraction, fractionation, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) based identification

Alternaria isolates were cultured in 200 mL potato dextrose broth (PDB) for 21 days at 25 °C while being shaken at 150 rpm. After vacuum filtering the culture filtrates, secondary metabolites were extracted thrice with ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v) in a separating funnel. Using a rotary evaporator, the organic layers were vacuum-evaporated at 40 °C. Crude extracts were dissolved in methanol via ultrasonification [26], and purified using silica gel column chromatography (hexane: ethyl acetate: methanol gradient) and analytical thin layer chromatography (TLC) [27]. HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity II) was used to confirm AOH and TeA using a C18 column and a 0.1% formic acid-water/acetonitrile gradient (1 mL min-1, 10 µL injection). TeA and AOH standards were used to match retention times to confirm peak identity.

Phytotoxicity assay

The phytotoxicity of purified toxins (AOH and TeA) was examined using detached potato leaves [28]. Purified AOH and TeA were dissolved in methanol and diluted in sterile distilled water to a final concentration of 1 mg mL-1, while a working concentration of 200 µg mL-1 was used for all treatments. Fully expanded leaves from four-week-old plants were infiltrated with 20 − 30 µL of each toxin solution using a needleless syringe. For comparison, leaves were also treated with a spore suspension of A. alternata (1 × 106 conidia mL-1). Control leaves were infiltrated with sterile water containing the same amount of methanol. Symptom development was monitored at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 days after treatment.

Treatment of potato plants with the pathogen and toxins

Potato plants were challenged with a highly pathogenic Alternaria species by uniformly spraying a spore suspension (1 × 106 conidia mL-1) onto the leaf surface to ensure effective infection. For toxin treatments, purified TeA and AOH (200 µg mL-1) were gently infiltrated into the leaves using a needleless syringe. Plants treated with deionized water under the same conditions served as negative controls.

Photosynthetic pigments content

Arnon’s method was used to estimate the amounts of chlorophyll a, b, and total chlorophyll in leaves treated with pathogen and toxins [29]. Briefly, 0.1 g of leaf tissue was homogenised in 5 ml of 80% acetone and centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 rpm. The absorbance of supernatant was measured at 645 and 663 nm, and standard equations were used to determine the pigment concentrations. The carotenoid content was determined using Lichtenthaler and Wellburn’s [30] method by measuring absorbance at 470 nm. The concentrations of pigment were expressed in mg g-1 fresh weight (FW). Histochemical staining for the visualisation of oxidative stress and callose deposition

Potato leaves treated with pathogens and toxins were subjected to histochemical detection of oxidative stress markers at 48 h post-treatment. H2O2 was localised by incubating leaves in 1 mg mL-1 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (pH 3.8) overnight in the dark [31]. After staining, the leaves were cleared using a freshly prepared bleaching solution consisting of 95% ethanol, acetic acid, and glycerol, followed by heating in a hot water bath for 15 min to remove chlorophyll. Superoxide anion (O2·−) was detected using 0.1% nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) with 10 mM sodium azide for 2 h [32]. The leaves were cleared using boiling ethanol and examined under a microscope. Cell death was measured using 0.25% Evans blue for 15 min, followed by rinsing and soaking overnight in water with leaves washed twice for 15 min each to remove unbound dye [33]. To visualize lipid peroxidation, Schiff’s reagent was used, decolorised it with 0.5% potassium metabisulfite prepared in 0.05 M HCl to reduce background staining before microscopic observation [34]. For callose deposition, ethanol-cleared leaves were stained with 0.1% aniline blue in 67 mM Na2HPO4 (pH 12.0) for 1 h in the dark and examined under a Leica DM 2000 LED fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) [35].

Ultrastructural assessment of leaf tissue using SEM and TEM

To examine alterations in cell wall morphology following A. alternata infection and toxin exposure, leaf samples were collected at 48 h post-inoculation. For SEM, pathogen and toxin-treated leaf segments (0.5–1.0 cm) 48 h post infection (hpi) were vacuum fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) for 2 h at room temperature and then left overnight at 4 °C to ensure complete penetration and stabilization. Following washing with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 95, and 100% v/v) and dried using liquid CO2. Dried tissues were mounted on aluminium stubs using conductive adhesive and sputter-coated with a thin metal layer to improve conductivity and imaging stability, and examined with a Apreo 2 S scanning electron microscope (Thermo Scientific, USA) [36].

For TEM, small leaf tissues (1 − 2 mm2) were fixed for 6 h at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), rinsed and post-fixed for 2 h in 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C to enhance membrane contrast and stabilization. Samples were dehydrated in acetone and graded ethanol (50, 70, 80, 95, and 100%, v/v) before being embedded in Spurr’s epoxy resin. An ultramicrotome was used to cut ultrathin sections (~ 70 nm), stained with uranyl acetate followed by lead citrate to enhance electron contrast and viewed under a CRYO-TEM (TALOS S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) [37].

Assessment of oxidative damage: malondialdehyde (MDA), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and electrolyte leakage

H2O2 was quantified spectrophotometrically using the method of Alexieva et al. [38], based on its reaction with potassium iodide (KI). Leaf tissues (0.3 g) were homogenised in ice-cold 0.5 mL 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and mixed with 0.5 ml 100 mM potassium-phosphate buffer and 2 ml KI reagent (1 M KI w/v in fresh double-distilled water). After an hour of incubation in the dark, absorbance was measured at 390 nm, and values were expressed as mg g⁻¹ fresh weight using an H2O2 calibration curve. Malondialdehyde (MDA), an indicator of lipid peroxidation, was quantified by the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction, as outlined by Thordal-Christensen et al. [31]. Leaf tissue (0.3 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of 5% TCA and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of 0.5% TBA in 20% TCA and heated at 95 °C for 25 min. After cooling, the reaction mixture was centrifuged at 7,500 rpm for 5 min to obtain a clear supernatant for absorbance measurement at 532 and 600 nm. To calculate electrolyte leakage (EL), an indicator of membrane damage, leaf tissue (100 mg) was cut into uniform pieces and immersed in 10 mL of deionized water. Samples were incubated at 32 °C for 2 h, and the initial conductivity (E1) was measured using a conductivity meter (Model 215-R). The tubes were then autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min to release all electrolytes, cooled to 25 °C, and the final conductivity (E2) was recorded. EL was expressed as a percentage using the formula EL (%) = (E1 / E2) × 100 [39]. All assays were performed on leaf samples collected at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after pathogen or toxin treatment.

Cell death assay

Cell death in leaf tissues was assessed using Evans blue staining following the method of Baker and Mock [33], with minor modifications. Potato leaves were exposed to the pathogen and its associated toxins, TeA and AOH, with both toxins applied at a concentration of 200 µg mL-1. After treatment, the leaves were immersed in 0.25% (v/v) aqueous Evans blue solution for 15 min, followed by gentle washing with distilled water for 30 min to remove unbound dye. The stained leaf segments were then incubated in 500 µL of N, N-dimethylformamide for 1 h at room temperature to extract the bound dye. The absorbance of the extract was measured spectrophotometrically at 600 nm to quantify Evans blue retention, which reflects the extent of cell death.

Enzymes activity assays

Antioxidant enzymes

Activities of key antioxidant enzymes (APX, SOD, CAT and GR) were quantified from fresh potato leaves using standard spectrophotometric methods using a UV-B spectrophotometer (Shimadzu 1900i, Japan). For ascorbate peroxidase (APX, EC 1.11.1.11) activity, leaf tissue (0.2 g) was homogenized in 1.5 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8) containing 1 mM EDTA, 1 g polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and 5 mM ascorbate, and the supernatant obtained after centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 30 min was used immediately. Activity was assessed by monitoring the ascorbate-dependent decline in absorbance at 290 nm in a reaction containing 0.1 mM H2O2 and 0.5 mM ascorbate, and 0.1 mM EDTA [40, 41].

Superoxide dismutase (SOD, EC 1.15.1.1) activity was assessed according to the method of Beauchamp and Fridovich [42], by homogenizing 0.1 g of tissue in 5 mL of ice-cold phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.5) containing 0.5 mM EDTA, centrifuging at 15,000 rpm for 15 min. The photoreduction of NBT was measured at 560 nm after exposing the assay mixture (100 − 300 µL of the plant extract, 3.5 mL of an O-2 generating solution, comprising 14.3 mM methionine, 82.5 µM NBT, and 2.2 µM riboflavin) to fluorescent light for 10 min, with one unit defined as 50% inhibition of NBT reduction.

For catalase (CAT, EC 1.11.1.6) activity, 0.6 g of leaf tissue was extracted in 2 mL of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8) containing 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 g PVP and 0.5% Triton X-100, centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 30 min, and the decomposition of H2O2 was followed at 240 nm. The reaction mixture contained 200 µL of enzyme extract, 1.5 mL of 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.8), 300 µL of 0.1 M H2O2, and 1.0 mL of distilled water. The alteration in absorbance at 240 nm per minute, serving as a measure of CAT activity, was recorded [43].

Glutathione reductase (GR, EC 1.8.1.7) activity was measured following Hossain et al. [44] using a reaction mixture containing potassium phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.8), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM GSSG and 0.2 mM NADPH, with the decline in NADPH absorbance recorded at 340 nm (for a duration of 1 min) using an extinction coefficient of 6.2 mM-1 cm-1.

Defense related enzymes

For peroxidase (POX, EC 1.11.1.7) activity, leaf tissue (0.5 g) was gently homogenized in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and clarified by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C). The reaction mixture consisted of 1.5 mL of 0.05 M pyrogallol, 0.1 mL of enzyme extract, and 0.5 mL of 1% H2O2. Activity was monitored by following the H2O2-dependent oxidation of pyrogallol, with changes in absorbance at 420 nm recorded every 30 s for 3 min [45].

Polyphenol oxidase (PPO, EC 1.10.3.1) activity was measured from extracts prepared in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.2 mL of 0.01 M catechol as the substrate in the reaction mixture (1.5 mL 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) and 0.1 mL enzyme extract). PPO activity was monitored by measuring the increase in absorbance at 495 nm at 30-second intervals for a total of 3 min at room temperature [46].

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL, EC 4.3.1.5) activity was quantified from 0.5 g tissue homogenized in phosphate buffer (pH 8.8) by measuring the formation of cinnamic acid after incubating the extract with 1 ml 12 mM L-phenylalanine at 32 °C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 0.5 mL of 2 N HCl. A corresponding blank was prepared by adding phenylalanine after the addition of HCl. The absorbance was recorded at 290 nm [47]. Enzyme activities for all assays were expressed relative to fresh leaf weight. All activity assays were performed on leaf samples collected at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after pathogen or toxin treatment.

Protein profiling by SDS − PAGE

Total soluble proteins were extracted from potato leaf tissues (100 mg) using an extraction buffer that contained 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT. The homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Protein concentration was measured by the Bradford assay [48]. Equal amounts of protein (15–30 µg) from each sample were mixed with Laemmli loading buffer [49], denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, and run on SDS-PAGE. The preparation of the stacking gel, resolving gel, and sample loading followed the protocol outlined by Rajik et al. [50]. Electrophoresis was done on polyacrylamide gels with a 12% resolving gel and 4% stacking gel, using a Tris-glycine-SDS running buffer. Gels were run at 80 V through the stacking gel and 120 V through the resolving gel until the dye front reached the bottom. After electrophoresis, the gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 and destained in a methanol-acetic acid solution until clear banding patterns appeared. Protein profiles were documented using a gel documentation system, and a comparative analysis of banding patterns was performed to assess treatment induced variation.

Isoenzyme analysis of CAT and SOD by native PAGE

A Mini-Protean-II system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) was used in native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) to resolve the isoenzymatic profiles of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD). SOD isoenzymes were characterized following the method of Beauchamp and Fridovich [42]. A 10% native PAGE was run using 40 µg protein per sample, and electrophoresis was carried out at 4 °C for 4 h at a constant current of 25 mA. After electrophoresis, gels were incubated in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 8.2) containing 12.5 mg NBT and 5 mg riboflavin for 15–20 min in the dark, followed by a brief incubation in 0.1% TEMED for an additional 15 min. SOD activity bands were visualized by exposing the gels to light for approximately 15 min.

CAT isoforms were analyzed using the method described by Anderson et al. [51]. Protein extracts (30 µg) were loaded onto 3.5% stacking and 7.5% resolving gels and electrophoresed in Tris − glycine buffer (pH 8.3) at a constant 25 mA. After electrophoresis, gels were equilibrated in 5 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and incubated in 5 mM H₂O₂ to activate CAT. Isoenzymes were visualized by staining with 2% potassium ferricyanide and 2% ferric chloride, producing clear CAT bands on a dark blue background. Gel images were captured using a Bio-Rad Gel Doc™ EZ Imager.

Expression analysis of antioxidant defense genes (Cu/Zn-SOD, CAT1, and APX)

RNA extraction and complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from 0.1 g of potato leaf tissue frozen in liquid nitrogen using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), followed by DNase I treatment according to the manufacturer’s instructions [52]. RNA integrity was verified by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel containing 0.5 µg mL-1 ethidium bromide [53]. RNA purity and concentration were further assessed using a NanoPhotometer (Implen, CA, United States) by evaluating the A230/A260/A280 absorbance ratios. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1.0 µg of total RNA using the iScript™ cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, United States) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Temporal expression of Cu/Zn-SOD, CAT1, and APX was analyzed using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) across all treatments. The synthesized cDNA was diluted to 50 ng µL⁻1 and used as the template for qRT-PCR. The oligonucleotide primers used in this study are listed in Table 1. qRT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicate for each of the three biological replicates using SsoFast™ EvaGreen® Supermix (Bio-Rad) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, United States). Fluorescence detection was carried out using SYBR Green dye (Qiagen, United States), and data acquisition was performed with iQ5 Optical System Software version 2.0 (Bio-Rad, CA, United States). Each reaction was set up in a total volume of 20 µL, containing 2 µL of cDNA (20 ng), 10 µL of 2× SsoFast™ EvaGreen® Supermix, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (0.2 µM), and 1 µL of nuclease-free water. The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s.

Statistical analyses

Three biological replicates are used to present all data as mean ± SD. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD test (p ≤ 0.05) were used to determine statistical significance [57]. Graphs were made using a paired comparison on Origin Pro 2024. Multivariate analyses were explored using principal component analysis (PCA) on the standardised correlation matrix to identify major sources of variance and treatment-based clustering [58]. Spearman’s rank correlation was employed to assess monotonic relationships between pathogenicity-related parameters, antioxidant enzymes, and stress indicators. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), based on Pearson distance, was performed to classify treatments and variables, and the resulting similarity patterns were visualised through heatmaps and dendrograms.

Results

Morpho-molecular characterization and pathogenicity of the pathogen

Microscopic study of the fungal isolate associated with potato leaves revealed morphological traits characteristic of A. alternata. Genomic DNA extracted from the pathogen displayed a single high-molecular weight band, indicating good quality. PCR amplification of the ITS1-5.8 S-ITS4 region produced a ~ 570 bp product (Supplementary Fig. S1). The sequencing and BLAST analysis of the amplicon verified its identity as A. alternata, the sequence was submitted to GenBank with accession number PQ060510. The phylogenetic tree created with ClustalW and MEGA11 software indicated that the isolate falls into the A. alternata clade, thereby confirming its taxonomic position (Supplementary Fig. S2). The pathogenicity was assessed on potted potato plants in accordance with Koch’s postulates. Within 7 − 10 days after inoculation, necrotic lesions with brown spots developed and subsequently covered almost three-quarters of the leaf surface. Disease severity, assessed at 7 days post-inoculation using the 0 − 5 scale, indicated a high level of pathogenicity, with infected leaves reaching a score of 4, corresponding to approximately 74.10 ± 4.62% leaf area affected (high pathogenicity category, 60 − 100%). Upon re-isolation from symptomatic tissue, the pathogen matched the original isolate in terms of morphological and pathogenic traits, thereby verifying its identification (Fig. 1).

Identification of AOH and tea by HPLC

The mycotoxin profile of the representative isolate was analysed using HPLC. Standard compounds of AOH and TeA showed retention times (RT) of 4.46 and 6.27 min, respectively (Fig. 2a, b). The chromatographic peaks in the sample appeared at the same retention times as the standards, with AOH eluting at RT 4.373 min and TeA at RT 6.303 min, confirming the presence of both toxins in the isolate (Fig. 2c, d).

Phytotoxic effects of A. alternata and its toxins on potato leaves

Necrotic lesions with brown spots developed on leaves treated with A. alternata, while control leaves (infiltrated with deionized water) remained symptomless (Fig. 2e). TeA and AOH infilteration also induced necrosis, although the severity was lower than that caused by the pathogen. Affected leaves showed chlorosis, indicating loss of chlorophyll. The results confirmed the phytotoxicity of both the isolate and its toxins.

Pigment degradation under pathogen and toxin stress

Potato plants challenged with the pathogen and its toxins (AOH and TeA) had much lower levels of pigments than control plants. Chlorophyll a, b, and total chlorophyll levels were lowest in leaves inoculated with pathogen (3.54, 4.37, and 8.47 mg g-1 FW, respectively). They were followed by TeA treated (5.24, 6.44, and 12.36 mg g-1) and AOH treated leaves (7.47, 13.31, and 17.38 mg g-1), while control leaves had the highest levels (9.25, 15.26, and 24.39 mg g-1) (Fig. 3a). Carotenoid levels declined over time under all treatments except the control. By 48 h, they dropped to 2.32 mg g-1 (pathogen), 4.37 mg g-1 (TeA), and 6.34 mg g-1 (AOH), compared to 10.19 mg g-1 in control (Fig. 3b). At 96 h, the levels further declined; with the pathogen causing the greatest reduction. This pigment loss corresponded with necrotic severity, highlighting the phytotoxicity of A. alternata and its toxins.

Differential ROS generation, cell death, and callose deposition in potato leaves

Histochemical staining showed differential accumulation of oxidative stress markers in potato leaves that were exposed to A. alternata and its toxins. H2O2 accumulation visualized as deep brown precipitates was highest in pathogen inoculated leaves, followed by leaves treated with TeA and AOH, with no sign in controls (Fig. 4I). NBT staining showed superoxide anion (O2·−) as blue formazan deposits, most prominent in samples treated with pathogens, moderate in TeA, and lowest in AOH, with no deposits in controls (Fig. 4II). Lipid peroxidation, which was shown by pinkish malondialdehyde (MDA) spots, followed a similar pattern: it was highest in tissue infected with pathogens, moderate with TeA, and lowest with AOH (Fig. 4IV). Evans blue staining confirmed elevated cell death under pathogen stress, followed by TeA and AOH, while the controls remained unstained (Fig. 4III).

Aniline blue staining showed a clear and consistent pattern of callose deposition across treatments. The pathogen-inoculated leaves displayed the most prominent fluorescence, with bright callose deposits forming around the infection sites, reflecting a strong cell wall-reinforcement response. Toxin treatments also induced callose accumulation, with TeA causing more noticeable deposits than AOH, indicating differences in their elicitation strength. In comparison, control leaves showed only faint, basal levels of callose. Overall, the pattern of callose deposition aligns with the severity of stress imposed, highlighting the activation of structural defenses in response to A. alternata and its toxins (Fig. 4V).

SEM and TEM reveal cellular impact of Alternaria and its toxins on potato leaves

Different ultrastructural changes suggestive of toxin-specific and pathogen-driven damage were revealed by SEM analysis of potato leaves (Fig. 4VI). The leaf surface of the control samples showed a well-preserved epidermis with a smooth cuticle and intact stomata, reflecting normal cellular architecture (Fig. 4VIa). By contrast, leaves treated with AOH showed modest perturbations, suggesting early oxidative stress responses (Fig. 4VIb), including localised epidermal deformation and mild cuticular undulations. TeA treatment caused noticeably more severe damage marked by collapsed epidermal cells, ruptured cuticle layers, and distorted stomatal structures, emphasising TeA’s strong phytotoxicity (Fig. 4VIc). The most severe damage was observed in leaves infected with A. alternata, where dense fungal hyphae colonised the surface, accompanied by extensive tissue collapse, stomatal penetration, and epidermal disintegration, highlighting the combined mechanical invasion and chemical damage (Fig. 4VId).

TEM revealed progressive ultrastructural degradation in potato leaves exposed to Alternaria toxins and infection (Fig. 4VII). Control cells showed intact architecture with well-defined intact membranes, well-defined organelles, and clear cytoplasm (Fig. 4VIIa). AOH treatment caused partial plasmolysis, swollen organelles, uneven cytoplasmic texture, chloroplast disorganisation, small vesicular bodies, and cytoplasmic condensation, indicating oxidative stress-induced damage (Fig. 4VIIb). TeA triggered severe cytoplasmic vacuolation, disrupted tonoplasts, large vacuoles, partial degradation of chloroplasts, electron-dense cytoplasmic inclusions, and mitochondrial fragmentation, reflecting its strong phytotoxicity (Fig. 4VIIc). Alternaria-infected tissues exhibited the most severe degeneration, characterized by complete cellular collapse, severe membrane rupture, organelle disintegration, and the presence of large amounts of necrotic debris, underscoring the combined effects of enzymatic degradation, mechanical invasion, and toxin synergy (Fig. 4VIId).

H2O2 levels, MDA content, and electrolyte leakage

Lipid peroxidation produces malondialdehyde (MDA), a by-product that indicates the degree of oxidative damage to cellular membranes. With the highest levels in pathogen-inoculated samples (12.23-fold), TeA (9.14-fold), and AOH (4.17-fold), its accumulation peaked at 48 h, suggesting significant ROS-induced membrane damage (Fig. 5a). Following pathogen inoculation and toxin exposure, H2O2 levels dramatically increased and peaked after 24 h. When compared to control plants, pathogen-treated leaves showed the largest increase (34.85-fold), followed by TeA (31.24-fold) and AOH (26.18-fold) (Fig. 5b).

Electrolyte leakage, an indicator of membrane integrity loss, increased under stress. TeA caused the most marked electrolyte efflux at both 48 h (28.91%) and 96 h (32.48%) indicating its strong influence in membrane disruption. While control plants remained stable, pathogen and AOH treated samples also showed elevated leakage (23.21% and 18.67% at 96 h, respectively) (Fig. 5c). These findings underline TeA’s strong membrane-damaging effect, presumably by means of oxidative damage caused by ROS.

Cell death determination

Cell death was quantified using the Evans blue uptake assay, and a steady rise in cellular damage was observed from day 1 to day 6 across all treatments (Fig. 5d). Pathogen-treated leaves consistently showed the highest levels of cell death, followed by TeA and AOH. Over the six-day period, pathogen exposure resulted in an approximately 12-fold increase in cell death, TeA showed an ~ 11-fold rise, and AOH exhibited a higher proportional increase (~ 22-fold) due to its low baseline. Despite this, the absolute extent of cell damage followed the clear pattern: Pathogen > TeA > AOH.

Temporal changes in enzyme activities

The SOD, CAT, APX, and GR antioxidant enzyme activities all responded strongly to the pathogen and toxin treatments, indicative of the detoxification processes for accumulated reactive oxygen species (Fig. 6a − d). SOD activity peaked at 24 h with maximum increase in pathogen treated samples (9.79-fold) followed by TeA (7.48-fold) and AOH (5.91-fold) (Fig. 6a). CAT activity showed a progressive increase up to 72 h, peaking in pathogen inoculated leaves (21.0-fold) (Fig. 6b), while APX and GR peaked at 48 h suggesting their major contribution in H2O2 scavenging and the maintenance of redox balance during cellular respiration (Fig. 6c, d). GR exhibited the strongest response in pathogen treated samples with 36.67-fold increase, followed by TeA (31.63-fold) and AOH (19.96-fold) (Fig. 6d).

In a similar manner, defense enzymes (PAL, POX, and PPO) were all significantly upregulated. Following pathogen exposure, PAL activity peaked at 48 h post-inoculation and showed the strongest response in pathogen exposed leaves (7.12-fold) followed by TeA with a response of (5.86-fold) and AOH (4.22-fold) compared to control (Fig. 6e). POX activity also peaked at 48 h where it reached 6.83-fold in the pathogen treatment group while recording 5.73-fold in TeA and 4.65-fold in AOH treated groups (Fig. 6f). Protein estimation

The protein profile was studied using the SDS − PAGE technique. Total proteins were extracted from both pathogen-infected and toxin-treated plants. The approximate molecular weights of the isolated proteins were determined with marker proteins of medium range (5 − 260 kDa). Control extracts showed only weak, consistent bands. In contrast, AOH, TeA, and pathogen treatments caused additional polypeptides and stronger band intensities (Fig. 7a). A common inducible mid-molecular-weight band (~ 40 − 50 kDa) was observed in all treatments, but it was most prominent in AOH-treated samples. TeA and pathogen treatments uniquely induced a very intense high molecular weight band (~ 75 kDa), with this band being more intense in the pathogen treated samples. More low-molecular-weight bands (12 − 30 kDa) were seen in TeA and pathogen-treated samples. TeA produced a banding pattern that was between those of AOH and pathogen treatments. These patterns suggest an increase in defense-related proteins during infection.

Isoenzyme analysis of CAT and SOD

Isoenzyme assay clearly displayed the different CAT and SOD banding patterns under pathogen and toxin treatments (Fig. 7b). SOD isoenzyme analysis revealed treatment-specific differences in antioxidant activation. Pathogen-inoculated leaves exhibited strong induction of four SOD isoforms, corresponding to Cu/Zn-SOD I, Cu/Zn-SOD II, Fe-SOD, and Mn-SOD. TeA treatment produced the same isoforms but with comparatively lower band intensity, while AOH induced only three faint isoforms (Cu/Zn-SOD I, Cu/Zn-SOD II, and Fe-SOD). Control leaves showed only very weak basal Cu/Zn-SOD activity (Fig. 7b). In the pathogen inoculated leaves, three CAT isoforms appeared with strong intensity, and only one band was detected in the toxin treated samples. The density of the band was stronger in TeA treated leaves (Fig. 7b). These isoform profiles are in line with the quantitative enzyme activities.

Expression patterns of antioxidant genes under pathogen and toxin treatments

The expression of Cu/Zn-SOD, CAT1 and APX increased progressively in response to pathogen, TeA and AOH, with clear differences among treatments (Fig. 8a − c). By 12 h, pathogen triggered a noticeable rise (1.69 − 1.98-fold), while TeA caused moderate increases (1.32 − 1.59-fold) and AOH only slight changes (1.14 − 1.30-fold). The strongest activation occurred between 24 and 48 h, when pathogen produced the highest expression across all genes (24 h: 6.07-, 4.87- and 3.86-fold; 48 h: 4.65-, 8.05- and 8.40-fold for Cu/Zn-SOD, CAT1 and APX, respectively). TeA resulted in intermediate induction (24 h: 3.13 − 3.91-fold; 48 h: 2.88 − 5.48-fold), whereas AOH consistently showed lower increases (24 h: 2.29 − 2.86-fold; 48 h: 3.41 − 3.99-fold). Although expression gradually decreased by 72 h, pathogen-treated leaves still maintained higher values (3.43 − 5.99-fold) compared to TeA (2.50 − 3.98-fold) and AOH (2.09 − 3.20-fold). By 96 h, expression remained elevated under pathogen stress (3.02 − 4.40-fold), moderate under TeA (2.10 − 3.21-fold), and lowest under AOH (1.58 − 2.06-fold). Overall, the responses consistently followed the order: Pathogen > TeA > AOH > Control, with the most pronounced increases occurring between 24 and 48 h for all three antioxidant genes.

Discussion

This study provides an integrative assessment of the phytotoxic impact of A. alternata and two of its major mycotoxins TeA and AOH, on potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) using morphological, ultrastructural, biochemical, and multivariate approaches. The molecular identification of the pathogen through ITS1 and ITS4 sequencing confirmed the pathogen as A. alternata, which is consistent with the earlier documentation of brown spot disease caused by Alternaria in solanaceous crops such as tomato and potato [59–61]. Pathogenicity tests showed typical necrotic lesions alongside chlorosis and foliar senescence indicating toxicity and virulence. Confirmed production of two phytotoxins, TeA and AOH by HPLC aligns with the known mycotoxin profile of A. alternata and underscores their role in symptom development [5, 6]. The symptoms caused by the infection were more severe than those caused by individual toxins. This supports the idea that fungal virulence involves mechanical penetration, enzymatic breakdown, and phytotoxin synergism [62–64]. While AOH interferes with DNA damage leading to genotoxic activity, TeA mostly works through inhibition of protein synthesis and inducing ROS accumulation [10, 65].

The impact of toxins and pathogen stress significantly reduces the concentration of chlorophyll a, b, and carotenoids, consistent with Meena et al. [28] and Sadhu et al. [66], who explained loss of pigments due to chloroplast disintegration and oxidative damage. TeA caused greater pigment reduction compared to AOH, reflecting its higher phytotoxicity. Quantitative assessment revealed that the pathogen caused the greatest impairment in total chlorophyll and carotenoid levels, which could be due to oxidative damage and inhibited pigment biosynthesis [67]. TeA’s disruption of photosystem II, as well as plastid structure [32], supports this, where the loss of chlorophyll is a marker of stress-induced photosynthetic impairment [9].

Histochemical staining provided further evidence of oxidative stress, wherein marked accumulation of H2O2 as well as O2·−, particularly in pathogen and TeA treated samples, was observed. These results were corroborated by lipid peroxidation and Evans blue staining for membrane integrity. The severity of oxidative damage followed the trend: pathogen > TeA > AOH. This gradient corresponds to previous studies on necrotrophic pathosystems like Alternaria brassicicola, wherein a strong host response was observed under live infection compared to exposure to purified toxins [68]. Elevated callose deposition in infected leaves further supports the development of a stress-induced defence response to strengthen cell walls and stop the spread of the pathogen [69].

SEM and TEM observations from this study reveal a distinct severity gradient from AOH → TeA → A. alternata, indicating growing disruption in organelle integrity and epidermal structure. These results are in line with ROS-driven cytotoxic mechanisms that were described by Felipini et al. [70] in citrus and Ahmad et al. [71] in cherry fruit post-harvest infections, where they reported hyphal colonisation and membrane collapse. The concept of toxin synergy, which has been noted in multi-omics studies of potato early blight, is supported by the combined mechanical and biochemical damage caused by A. alternata infection [72]. The significant vacuolation and mitochondrial impairment induced by TeA correspond also align with its role in cell death [69]. Samples treated with AOH showed moderate signs of ultrastructural damage, such as vesicle accumulation, chloroplast disorganisation, and plasmolysis. To our knowledge, previous research has only examined the biochemical or genotoxic effects of AOH; this is the first report of AOH-induced subcellular changes in plant tissues, especially potatoes.

The noticeable increase in ROS levels and membrane leakage under all treatments reflect membrane destabilisation and redox imbalance. Under TeA stress, electrolyte leakage peaked, demonstrating TeA’s greater ability to disrupt membranes than AOH. This finding is consistent with earlier research on the mechanism of action of TeA, which includes the disruption of photosynthetic machinery and inhibition of protein synthesis [9]. As demonstrated in the work of Li et al. [69] and ShuJun et al. [73], MDA trends reflected ROS generation, confirming its role as reliable oxidative damage marker.

A well-coordinated plant response to oxidative stress is demonstrated by the strong induction of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, APX, and GR). The function of these enzymes in counteracting oxidative stress from necrotrophic pathogens is further supported by increased SOD, CAT, POX, and PPO activities in Brassica juncea during Alternaria blight [74, 75]. The enzyme GR, which is essential for glutathione recycling, demonstrated the largest fold change, highlighting its crucial function in maintaining redox balance. Additionally, there was a marked upregulation of defence enzymes (PAL, POX, and PPO). While POX and PPO facilitate the oxidative cross-linking of cell wall proteins and inhibit pathogens, PAL is a crucial enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway, leading to the synthesis of lignin and phytoalexin [76–79]. Isoenzyme profiling further supported the biochemical findings, with increased band intensity and isoform diversity under pathogen and TeA treatments, paralleling findings in tomatoes under Alternaria stress [28, 80]. When exposed to pathogens and toxins, different SOD isoforms are produced, with A. alternata infection causing the most noticeable expression. The findings are consistent with previous research that demonstrated pathogen-specific induction of SOD isoenzymes in Phaseolus vulgaris, wherein two Mn-SODs, two Cu/Zn-SODs, and two Fe-SODs were identified, and one isoform was specifically upregulated upon infection with Botrytis cinerea [81]. The compartment-specific activation of superoxide dismutases (SODs) reinforces the hypothesis that different isoforms are mobilized to regulate localized reactive oxygen species (ROS) build-up and maintain redox equilibrium during biotic stress. This isoform modification is a well-documented adaptation strategy in plants following pathogen invasion [82, 83]. Under pathogen and TeA treatments, at least two CAT isoforms were prominently upregulated, while under AOH, induction was comparatively mild. The differential expression signifies isoform-specific activation of catalase enzymes located in peroxisomes and cytoplasm, which are crucial for decomposing H2O2 during the oxidative burst [84, 85]. Interestingly, the stronger band intensity in pathogen-infected leaves suggests a higher demand for hydrogen peroxide scavenging, which is consistent with the histochemical detection of elevated ROS levels.

The strong and time-dependent induction of Cu/Zn-SOD, CAT1, and APX gene expression observed in potato under A. alternata, TeA, and AOH treatments is consistent with the oxidative stress-driven defense strategies widely documented in Solanaceae-pathogen interactions. A. alternata and its toxins are known to provoke intense ROS accumulation and membrane damage [21, 86], and the early transcriptional activation detected at 12 h, most pronounced under pathogen challenge reflects an immediate requirement for ROS detoxification through SOD-CAT-APX pathways, similar to responses reported in tomato and pepper under necrotrophic attack [87, 88]. The marked expression peaks between 24 and 48 h align with the second, more sustained antioxidative phase described during fungal invasion, where elevated detoxification capacity is essential to limit unchecked oxidative bursts [89]. These expression patterns parallel findings in other pathosystems, where resistant genotypes show stronger antioxidant activation than susceptible ones, as demonstrated for tomato challenged with A. alternata [90] and bean genotypes inoculated with Colletotrichum lindemuthianum [91]. Moreover, the coordinated rise in SOD, CAT, and APX transcripts mirrors the efficient antioxidative regulation observed in resistant potato cultivars during Phytophthora infestans infection [92] and corresponds with enhanced defense signaling seen in StRbohA-overexpressing potato, where increased ROS-triggered PR gene activation restricts pathogen growth [56]. Together, the treatment-specific expression profiles in our study demonstrate that A. alternata induces a classical, transcriptionally regulated antioxidant defense cascade in potato, with responses consistently following the order Pathogen > TeA > AOH, underscoring pathogen-driven oxidative pressure and the centrality of ROS-antioxidant homeostasis in Solanaceae immunity.

Multivariate analyses highlighted the host responses’ temporal dynamics by clearly separating treatments and time points. PCA demonstrated clear temporal and treatment-specific clustering and provided a potent method of dimensionality reduction [93]. ROS parameters (H2O2 and O2·−), MDA, EL, and antioxidant enzymes were the main factors influencing PC1 and PC2, which accounted for a significant portion of the overall variance. Pathogen and TeA samples clustered together at later time points, with treatments separated according to severity and time (Fig. 9). This supports the hypothesis that both induce persistent oxidative stress and elicit potent detoxification reactions. These relationships were further elucidated by Spearman’s correlation plot (Fig. 10), which showed highly significant negative correlations between ROS markers and photosynthetic pigments. These correlations were consistent with altered chloroplast stability and oxidative bleaching under Alternaria stress. Heatmap visualisation and hierarchical clustering further differentiated between pigment-related characteristics and ROS/enzyme responses, suggesting two principal nodes of stress manifestation: early photosynthetic inhibition and subsequent oxidative damage (Fig. 11). Similar patterns have been reported in Arabidopsis infected with Botrytis cinerea [94] and in abiotic stress contexts, such as salinity and lead (Pb) stress in maize genotypes [95], reaffirming the usefulness of multivariate tools in deciphering complex plant stress responses.

In summary, the present research reveals a distinct toxicodynamic gradient of A. alternata and its toxins on potatoes, with TeA exhibiting higher phytotoxicity than AOH, yet still secondary to the full pathogenic effect. These findings support the potential use of biochemical markers and ultrastructural alterations as early indicators of infection severity and emphasise the necessity of early detection of Alternaria toxins in crop systems. The method used here provides a comprehensive framework for studying interactions between plants, pathogens, and toxins, which may aid in breeding and management strategies for cultivars resistant to brown spot.
