On the Proofs of the Predictive Synthesis Formula
Riku Masuda and Kaoru Irie

Abstract
Bayesian predictive synthesis is useful in synthesizing multiple predictive distributions coherently. However, the proof for the fundamental equation of the synthesized predictive density has been missing. In this technical report, we review the series of research on predictive synthesis, then fill the gap between the known results and the equation used in modern applications. We provide two proofs and clarify the structure of predictive synthesis. Key words and phrases: Bayesian predictive synthesis, forecast density combination, agent opinion theory.



1
Introduction

Suppose that a decision maker (DM) is interested in predicting quantity
y
. This DM has
K
agents (statistical models, human experts, deterministic computer algorithms, etc) who report their forecasts of
y
or related quantities. Specifically, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, the
k
-th agent provides the DM with forecast density function
h_{k}
. The DM has her/his own prior for
y
, denoted by
p(y)
. In this context, the DM is interested in updating prior
p(y)
to the posterior, conditioning information set
\mathcal{H}\equiv\{h_{1},\dots,h_{K}\}
. However, the formal Bayes rule is not available unless the DM fully specifies her/his belief on
y
and
\mathcal{H}
jointly, which is often a difficult task in practice.

Thus, a coherent rule of updating the DM’s forecast of
y
based on the agents’ opinion
\mathcal{H}
, other than the direct posterior computation by the Bayes rule, is needed. It is claimed that such a rule must be of the form

p(y|\mathcal{H})=\int\alpha(y|\bm{x})\prod_{k=1}^{K}h_{k}(x_{k})d\bm{x},

(1)

where
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{K})
and
\alpha(y|\bm{x})
is a probability density function. This model, known as
Bayesian predictive synthesis
, has been utilized in modern forecasting problems, including the predictions of macroeconomic variables such as GDP and inflation rates
(
McAlinn et al., 2020
;
McAlinn, 2021
;
Chernis et al., 2023
;
Chernis, 2024
;
Chernis et al., 2024
)
, oil prices
(
Aastveit et al., 2023
)
, returns from financial assets
(
Tallman and West, 2024a
;
Tallman and West, 2024b
)
, prices of real estates
(
Cabel et al., 2022
)
, and public health data
(
Sugasawa et al., 2023
;
Kobayashi et al., 2024
;
Babasaki et al., 2024
)
. The comparison with other methods for model synthesis, such as stacking, logarithmic pooling and discounting, has also been investigated
(
McAlinn et al., 2018
;
Carvalho et al., 2022
;
Bernaciak and Griffin, 2024
)
. Examples of synthesis density
\alpha(y|\bm{x})
are provided and studied in
Johnson and West, 2024
. Once the synthesis density is specified, the DM’s forecast can be computed by the Markov chain Monte Carlo or sequential Monte Carlo methods
(
Masuda and Irie, 2023
)
.

Equation (
1
) appeared in
McAlinn and West, 2019
(or
McAlinn, 2017
) for the first time, referring
West and Crosse, 1992
as its origin. However, the proof of (
1
) cannot be found in
West and Crosse, 1992
and the reference therein, including
Genest and Schervish, 1985
and
West, 1988
;
West, 1992
. In this technical report, we try to fill the gap between the foundational results on predictive synthesis obtained in 1980s-90s and equation (
1
) used today as the Bayesian predictive synthesis formula. We review the known results in Section 2, then prove equation (
1
) in two ways in Section 3.

2
Review of established results

The original work of
Genest and Schervish, 1985
starts their analysis from the coherent synthesis of a single agent (
K=1

West and Crosse, 1992

1
) with
K=1
is obtained as the limit when the number of events increases. The predictive synthesis of multiple agents has been discussed in
Genest and Schervish, 1985
, but limited to the single event case. The question is whether the same extension to the multiple events is feasible, so that (
1
) is proven for general
K\geq 1
. Table

2.1
Single agent, single event

Suppose that the DM’s interest is in the occurrence of an event,
E\subset\Omega
. The DM’s prior for
E
is
p\equiv\mathbb{P}(E)\in[0,1]
. There is an agent who assesses the probability that event
E_{1}
, which is not necessarily identical to
E
, will happen at
f_{1}\in[0,1]
. The DM is supposed to have a prior for
f_{1}
, or a probability distribution on
[0,1]
. For simplicity, we assume the existence of a density function
p(f_{1})df_{1}
. Since we (and the DM) are unable, or reluctant, to specify the joint model of
E
and
f_{1}
, the conditional probability,
\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1})
, is not computable by the Bayes rule. Then, our goal is to obtain another formula that defines the probability of
E
given
f_{1}
, denoted by
p^{\ast}(f_{1})
. We require this probability to satisfy the
Consistency Condition
, which makes the joint, marginal and conditional probabilities compatible.

Definition

(Consistency Condition)
.

For any
p
and
p(f_{1})
, the updating rule
p^{\ast}(f_{1})
must satisfy

p=\int_{0}^{1}p^{\ast}(f_{1})p(f_{1})df_{1},

and
p^{\ast}(f_{1})=\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1})
, in addition to other initial specifications.

“Other initial specifications” are typically the condition for the (marginal) prior moments of
f_{1}
. Here, we focus on the case of partially specified prior with mean
\mu_{1}
.

Definition

(Partially specified prior)
.

Prior
p(f_{1})
is partially specified if the DM does not fully specifies
p(f_{1})
but its mean by
\mu_{1}
. That is,

\mu_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}f_{1}p(f_{1})df_{1}.

Thus, for given
\mu_{1}
, the Consistency Condition must be satisfied for any prior with mean
\mu_{1}
.

Genest and Schervish, 1985
showed in their Theorem 2.1 that, under the Consistency Condition, the
p^{\ast}(f_{1})
must be of the form,

p^{\ast}(f_{1})=p+\lambda_{1}(f_{1}-\mu_{1}),

where
\lambda_{1}
is a constant and contained in some finite interval so that
p^{\ast}(f_{1})\in[0,1]
.
West and Crosse, 1992
pointed out in their Corollary 1 that this expression can be rewritten as,

p^{\ast}(f_{1})=\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1}=1)f_{1}+\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1}=0)(1-f_{1}).

(2)

To evaluate
p^{\ast}(f_{1})
in practice, the DM must specify either of the following sets of quantities:

•

p\equiv\mathbb{P}(E)
and
\mu_{1}
: DM’s prior event probability and expected agent’s forecast.

•

\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1}=1)
and
\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1}=0)
: event probabilities when extreme forecasts are reported.

2.2
Single agent, multiple events

West and Crosse, 1992
extended the above result to the case where the agent assesses the probabilities of
n
events,
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
, that are mutually disjoint, while the DM is interested in another event,
E
, with prior
p\equiv\mathbb{P}(E)
. Let
E_{n+1}\equiv(\cup E_{i})^{c}
. Denote the agent’s forecast probabilities of
E_{i}
by
f_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n,n{+}1\}
. Note that
f_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}f_{i}=1
, hence
\bm{f}\equiv(f_{1},\dots,f_{n},f_{n+1})^{\prime}
is on the
n
-simplex. The DM has a prior for
\bm{f}
, denoted by density
p(\bm{f})
, whose marginal density is
p(f_{i})
. The DM specifies its marginal mean by
\mu_{i}
:

\mu_{i}=\int_{0}^{1}f_{i}\ p(f_{i})\ df_{i},\ \ \ \ \ \ i\in\{1,\dots,n,n+1\}

Then, the Consistency Condition determines the DM’s probability of
E
given
\bm{f}
by,

p^{\ast}(\bm{f})=p+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(f_{i}-\mu_{i}),

(3)

or, equivalently,

p^{\ast}(\bm{f})=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\Pi_{i}f_{i},\ \ \ \ \ \mathrm{where}\ \ \ \ \Pi_{i}\equiv\mathbb{P}(E|f_{i}=1).

(4)

Note that
(n{+}1)
-th event, or the complement of the
n
events, is included in the latter expression of (
4
). The
(n{+}1)
-th term,
\Pi_{n+1}f_{n+1}
, is the “residual,” or the variation of
y
that is unexplained by the forecasts of
n
events, serving as the intercept term in (
1
) as a part of
\alpha(y|x)
.

The synthesis formula in (
1
) is obtained for
K=1
as follows. Let quantity of interest be
Y
. This is a random variable whose probability law represents the DM’s uncertainty about this quantity. For arbitrary value
y
, set
E=\{Y\leq y\}
. In this context, the prior probability of event
E
is the DM’s distribution function of
Y
, or
p(y)\equiv\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y)
. The agent forecasts quantity
X
, which is not necessarily equal to
Y
. For arbitrary points
-\infty=q_{0}<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{n}<q_{n+1}=\infty
, set
E_{i}=\{q_{i-1}<X\leq q_{i}\}
. Denote the agent’s distribution and density function of
X
by
H
and
h
, respectively. Then the forecast made by the agent is written as
f_{i}=H(q_{i+1})-H(q_{i})
for each
i
. This is the agent’s probability that
X
falls in interval
(q_{i-1},q_{i}]
. The DM has a prior for
\bm{f}
, but specifies only the expectation of
f_{i}
as
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[f_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[H(q_{i})-H(q_{i-1})]
for each
i
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}\equiv\{H(q_{i})|i=1,\dots,n\}
. When being conditioned, this information set is equivalent to
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}
. Then equation (
4
) becomes,

\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y|\mathcal{H}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\Pi_{i}(y)\{H(q_{i})-H(q_{i-1})\},\ \ \ \ \ \mathrm{where}\ \ \ \ \Pi_{i}(y)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y|H(q_{i})-H(q_{i-1})=1).

Now, consider the equal-length grids by setting
q_{i}-q_{i-1}=\epsilon_{n}>0
for
i\in\{2,\dots,n\}
, and suppose that
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then, the expression above can be viewed as the Riemann sum and has the limit:

\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y|\mathcal{H}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\Pi_{i}(y)\frac{H(q_{i})-H(q_{i-1})}{\epsilon_{n}}\epsilon_{n}\to\int\Pi(y|x)h(x)dx=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y|\mathcal{H}),

where
x
is the point at which intervals degenerate in the limit.
The symbols in the right are defined as the limit of those in the left, as explained below.

•

\mathcal{H}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}_{n}
. This information set contains almost all values of
H(x)
. Hence, having information set
\mathcal{H}
is equivalent to knowing density function
h
.

•

\Pi(y|x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi_{i}(y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y|H(q_{i})-H(q_{i-1})=1)
. This is the limit of the DM’s distribution function when the agent reports that
X
falls in interval
(q_{i-1},q_{i}]
. Note that this interval degenerates at point
x
as
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
. Thus,
\Pi(y|x)
is the DM’s distribution function when the agent is 100% sure that
X=x
.

Computing the derivatives of both sides in
y
yields

p(y|\mathcal{H})=\int\alpha(y|x)h(x)dx,\qquad\mathrm{where}\quad\alpha(y|x)=\frac{d}{dy}\Pi(y|x).

This is equivalent to (
1
) when
K=1
.

2.3
Multiple agents, multiple events

We now assume
K\geq 1
. Each of
K
agents forecasts
n
events. For
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, these events are denoted by
E_{k,1},\cdots,E_{k,n}
and are mutually disjoint. Then, the
k
-th agent assesses the probability that
E_{k,i_{k}}
will happen at
f_{k,i_{k}}\in[0,1]
for
i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, while satisfying
\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n}f_{k,i_{k}}\leq 1
. Set
E_{k,n+1}\equiv(\cup_{i_{k}}E_{k,i_{k}})^{c}
and
f_{k,n+1}\equiv 1-\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n}f_{i_{k},k}
. Let
\bm{f}_{k}\equiv(f_{k,1},\dots,f_{k,n},f_{k,n+1})^{\prime}
and
\bm{f}_{1:K}\equiv\{\bm{f}_{1},\dots,\bm{f}_{K}\}
. Note that
\bm{f}_{k}
is
(n{+}1)
-dimensional and includes
f_{k,n+1}
, hence sums to unity.

The DM has a joint prior for
\bm{f}_{1:K}
, but does not fully specify its distributional form. Only their marginal means are specified as
\mu_{k,i_{k}}=\mathbb{E}[f_{k,i_{k}}]
for each
k
and
i_{k}
. The DM is interested in the occurrence of event
E
and has a prior probability
p\equiv\mathbb{P}(E)
. After hearing the agents’ opinions
\bm{f}_{1:K}
, the DM updates her/his belief on
E
to
p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})
so that the Consistency Condition is satisfied.

Similarly to (
3
), for any partially specified prior
p(\bm{f}_{1:K})
, the
p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})
must be of the form,

p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})=p+\sum_{{i_{1}}=1}^{n}\lambda_{1,i_{1}}(f_{1,i_{1}}-\mu_{1,i_{1}})+\cdots+\sum_{{i_{K}}=1}^{n}\lambda_{K,i_{K}}(f_{K,i_{K}}-\mu_{K,i_{K}}).

(5)

The coefficient,
\lambda_{k,i_{k}}
, must satisfy many inequalities so that
p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})\in[0,1]
.
Genest and Schervish, 1985
proved this expression for
n=1
in their Theorem 3.2.

The proof of (
5
) completely parallels to that of Lemma 2 in
West and Crosse, 1992
. That is, we first apply Lemma 3.1 in
Genest and Schervish, 1985
to obtain the form of (
5
), treating this problem as the “single agent,
nK
events” case without the sum-to-unity condition for each
\bm{f}_{k}
. Then restrict the support of the DM’s prior for
\bm{f}_{1:K}
to the product of
K
simplices. Such a prior exists; a typical example is the independent Dirichlet distributions.

3
Proof of general synthesis equation

Our goal is to prove by using (
5
) that, for any partially specified prior
p(\bm{f}_{1:K})
, we have

p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\cdots\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n+1}\Pi_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{K})}\prod_{k=1}^{K}f_{k,i_{k}},

(6)

where the coefficient is the conditional probability:

\Pi_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{K})}\equiv\mathbb{P}\left(E\left|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{K,i_{K}}=1\right.\right).

Clearly, this is the multiple agents version of (
2
). Similarly to the single agent case, this form is viewed as the Riemann sum that converges to (
1
). Thus, to obtain equation (
1
), we need to prove (
6
). We provides two approaches to the proof, each of which clarifies the mechanism of predictive synthesis in different ways.

3.1
Proof by marginalization

Our first proof explicitly addresses the update of the DM’s information. Mathematically, it is the proof by induction. Note that this equation has been shown for
K=1
in (
4
). Suppose that the DM initially has
K{-}1
agents (
K\geq 2
), then adds another agent to her/his information set. The DM’s prior must be consistent in the sense that we should be able to recover
p(\bm{f}_{1:K-1})
by marginalizing
\bm{f}_{K}
in the prior
p(\bm{f}_{1:K})
.

Using (
5
) for each
k
individually, we have the DM’s probability conditional on
k
agents as,

p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:k})=p+\bm{\lambda}_{1|k}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{1})+\cdots+\bm{\lambda}_{k|k}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{k}),

(7)

where we set
\bm{\lambda}_{j|k}\equiv(\lambda_{j,1|k},\cdots,\lambda_{j,n+1|k})^{\prime}
and
\bm{\mu}_{j}\equiv(\mu_{j,1},\dots,\mu_{j,n+1})^{\prime}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. By construction, coefficient
\lambda_{j,i_{j}|k}
might be dependent on
k
, or the number of agents in the DM’s information set. Note that
\lambda_{j,n+1|k}=0
,
f_{j,n+1}=1-f_{j,1}-\cdots-f_{j,n}
and
\mu_{j,n+1}=1-\mu_{j,1}-\cdots-\mu_{j,n}
. Under this notation, if
f_{j,n+1}=1
, then
f_{j,1}=\cdots=f_{j,n}=0
and

\bm{\lambda}_{j|k}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{j}-\bm{\mu}_{j})=-\lambda_{j,1|k}\mu_{j,1}-\cdots-\lambda_{j,n|k}\mu_{j,n}=-\bm{\lambda}_{j|k}^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{j}.

Next, we define
\Pi_{k}
for each
k
as,

\Pi_{k}\equiv\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,n+1}=1,\cdots,f_{k,n+1}=1)=p-\bm{\lambda}_{1|k}^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{1}-\cdots-\bm{\lambda}_{k|k}^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{k},

where the second equality is obtained by setting
f_{1,n+1}=1,\cdots,f_{k,n+1}=1
in the expression of
p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:k})
in (
7
).
Then, observe the following relationship:

\Pi_{k}=\Pi_{k-1}+(\bm{\lambda}_{1|k-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{1|k})^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{1}+\cdots+(\bm{\lambda}_{k-1|k-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{k-1|k})^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{k-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{k|k}^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{k}.

Similarly, by setting
f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{k,i_{k}}=1
in (
7
), we have

\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{k,i_{k}}=1)=\Pi_{k}+\lambda_{1,i_{1}|k}+\cdots+\lambda_{k,i_{k}|k}.

Using these notations and relationships, we obtain the following identities:

\begin{split}\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{k,i_{k}}=1)f_{k,i_{k}}&=\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n+1}[\Pi_{k}+\lambda_{1,i_{1}|k}+\cdots+\lambda_{k,i_{k}|k}]\ f_{k,i_{k}}\\
&=[\Pi_{k}+\lambda_{1,i_{1}|k}+\cdots+\lambda_{k-1,i_{k-1}|k}]+\bm{\lambda}_{k|k}^{\prime}\bm{f}_{k}.\end{split}

Writing
\Pi_{k}
by using
\Pi_{k-1}
, then using
\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{k-1,i_{k-1}}=1)
, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}(E|&f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{k,i_{k}}=1)f_{k,i_{k}}=\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{k-1,i_{k-1}}=1)\\
&\ \ \ \ +(\bm{\lambda}_{1|k-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{1|k})^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{1}+\cdots+(\bm{\lambda}_{k-1|k-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{k-1|k})^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{k-1}+\bm{\lambda}_{k|k}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{k})\\
&\ \ \ \ +(\lambda_{1,i_{1}|k}-\lambda_{1,i_{1}|k-1})+\cdots+(\lambda_{k-1,i_{k}|k}-\lambda_{k-1,i_{k}|k-1}).\end{split}

(8)

Now, assume that (
6
) holds for
K{-}1
:

p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K-1})=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\cdots\sum_{i_{K-1}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}=1)f_{1,i_{1}}\cdots f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}.

Then, we confirm that (
6
) also holds for
K
as follows:

\begin{split}&\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\cdots\sum_{i_{K}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{K,i_{K}}=1)f_{1,i_{1}}\cdots f_{K,i_{K}}\\
&=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\cdots\sum_{i_{K-1}=1}^{n+1}f_{1,i_{1}}\cdots f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}\sum_{i_{K}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{K,i_{K}}=1)f_{K,i_{K}}\\
&=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\cdots\sum_{i_{K-1}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}=1)f_{1,i_{1}}\cdots f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}\\
&\ \ \ \ +(\bm{\lambda}_{1|K-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{1|K})^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{1}+\cdots+(\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K})^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{K-1}+\bm{\lambda}_{K|K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K}-\bm{\mu}_{K})\\
&\ \ \ \ +\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}f_{1,i_{1}}(\lambda_{1,i_{1}|K}-\lambda_{1,i_{1}|K-1})+\cdots+\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}(\lambda_{K-1,i_{K-1}|K}-\lambda_{K-1,i_{K-1}|K-1})\\
&=p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K-1})\\
&\ \ \ \ +(\bm{\lambda}_{1|K-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{1|K})^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{1}+\cdots+(\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K})^{\prime}\bm{\mu}_{K-1}+\bm{\lambda}_{K|K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K}-\bm{\mu}_{K})\\
&\ \ \ \ +(\bm{\lambda}_{1|K}-\bm{\lambda}_{1|K-1})^{\prime}\bm{f}_{1}+\cdots+(\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K}-\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K-1})^{\prime}\bm{f}_{K-1}\\
&=p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K-1})\\
&\ \ \ \ -[\ p+\bm{\lambda}_{1|K-1}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{1})+\cdots+\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K-1}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K-1}-\bm{\mu}_{K-1})\ ]\\
&\ \ \ \ +[\ p+\bm{\lambda}_{1|K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{1})+\cdots+\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K-1}-\bm{\mu}_{K-1})+\bm{\lambda}_{K|K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K}-\bm{\mu}_{K})\ ]\\
&=p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K-1})-p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K-1})+p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})\\
&=p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K}).\end{split}

This completes the proof by induction.

3.2
Proof by using independent prior

The Consistency Condition requires
any
prior whose marginal means are
\bm{\mu}_{1:K}
to satisfy the same equations. If we specifically consider the following independent prior,

q(\bm{f}_{1:K})\equiv\prod_{k=1}^{K}q(\bm{f}_{k}),

where
q(\bm{f}_{k})
has mean
\bm{\mu}_{k}
, then equation (
5
), or (
7
), must hold even for this prior. Our second proof is based on this fact, and uses the independent prior to derive the expression in (
6
).

Using the independence,
q(\bm{f}_{K}|\bm{f}_{1:K-1})=q(\bm{f}_{K})
, and the Consistency Condition, we have

p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K-1})=\int p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})q(\bm{f}_{K})\ d\bm{f}_{K}.

Relating this result to (
7
), we have

\begin{split}p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K-1})&=p+\bm{\lambda}_{1|K-1}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{1})+\cdots+\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K-1}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K-1}-\bm{\mu}_{K-1})\\
&=p+\bm{\lambda}_{1|K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{1})+\cdots+\bm{\lambda}_{K-1|K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K-1}-\bm{\mu}_{K-1}),\end{split}

which shows
\bm{\lambda}_{k|K-1}=\bm{\lambda}_{k|K}
for any
k\leq K
, meaning that the coefficients of the
k
-th agent is invariant even if other agents are added to, or omitted from, the information set. We write
\bm{\lambda}_{k}\equiv\bm{\lambda}_{k|k^{\prime}}
for
k^{\prime}\in\{k,\dots,K\}
. Since equation (
5
), or (
7
), holds for any prior, coefficient
\bm{\lambda}_{k}
does not depend on the number of agents even when we use priors other than
q(\bm{f}_{1:K})
.

Now, let the DM’s prior be an arbitrary
p(\bm{f}_{1:K})
(but with mean
\bm{\mu}_{1:K}
). The result on coefficients greatly simplifies (
8
) as,

\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{k,i_{k}}=1)f_{k,i_{k}}=\mathbb{P}(E|f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{k-1,i_{k-1}}=1)+\bm{\lambda}_{k}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{k}).

Using this equation recursively, we obtain

\begin{split}&\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\cdots\sum_{i_{K-1}=1}^{n+1}\sum_{i_{K}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}\left(E\left|\ f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{K,i_{K}}=1\right.\right)f_{1,i_{1}}\cdots f_{K,i_{K}}\\
&=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\cdots\sum_{i_{K-1}=1}^{n+1}f_{1,i_{1}}\cdots f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}\sum_{i_{K}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}\left(E\left|\ f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{K,i_{K}}=1\right.\right)f_{K,i_{K}}\\
&=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\cdots\sum_{i_{K-1}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}\left(E\left|\ f_{1,i_{1}}=1,\cdots,f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}=1\right.\right)f_{1,i_{1}}\cdots f_{K-1,i_{K-1}}+\bm{\lambda}_{K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K}-\bm{\mu}_{K})\\
&=\cdots\\
&=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}\left(E\left|\ f_{1,i_{1}}=1\right.\right)f_{1,i_{1}}+\bm{\lambda}_{2}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{2}-\bm{\mu}_{2})+\cdots+\bm{\lambda}_{K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K}-\bm{\mu}_{K})\\
&=p+\bm{\lambda}_{1}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{1})+\bm{\lambda}_{2}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{2}-\bm{\mu}_{2})+\cdots+\bm{\lambda}_{K}^{\prime}(\bm{f}_{K}-\bm{\mu}_{K}),\end{split}

where the last line follows from the result for the single agent, or (
4
). The last expression is exactly
p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})
in (
7
). This completes the proof of (
6
).

3.3
Remarks

On prior independence

It is worth emphasizing that we do
not
assume the independence of agents in the DM’s prior. Rather, the prior independence of agents
might
be true, but is not certain. The Consistency Condition must be satisfied in any case, regardless of the prior independence. This allows us to use the independent prior in the proof to compute the posterior probability, because it must be identical for any choice of priors.

If the DM does assume the independence across agents— if the DM excludes any other priors but decides to use
q(\bm{f}_{1:K})
only— then such an assumption must be considered explicitly when developing the predictive synthesis equation. In this case, the Consistency Condition must be modified, and the DM obtains a different form of updating rule
(
Genest and Schervish, 1985
, Theorem 4.1)
.

Random number generation and independence

To generate a random variable
y
from the DM’s posterior
p(y|\mathcal{H})
in (
1
), one should generate
x_{k}
from each agent forecast distribution
h_{k}(x_{k})
independently, then generate
y
from
\alpha(y|\bm{x})
. Similarly, to generate a binary variable whether event
E
occurs or not from the DM’s posterior
p^{\ast}(\bm{f}_{1:K})
in (
6
), one should generate index
i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,n,n{+}1\}
from the discrete distribution with probability
\bm{f}_{k}
for each
k
independently, then generate a binary variable from the Bernoulli distribution with probability
\Pi_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{K})}
.

The step of generating
x_{k}
or
i_{k}
independently is due to the product of agent forecast densities
h_{k}(x_{k})
in (
1
), or the product of agent forecast probabilities
f_{k,i_{k}}
in (
6
). However, for the same reason stated in the previous remark, this does not mean that we assume the independence of agents. These products appear in the probability of event
E
where the agent forecasts are conditioned. The (in)dependence of agents is stated in the DM’s prior
p(\bm{f}_{1:K})
, but we avoided specifying this prior completely. To reiterate, we did specify its mean
\bm{\mu}_{1:K}
, not the (in)dependence.

Further extensions: multivariate forecasts by agents

For simplicity, assume
K=1
in this subsection. In proving (
1
) by using (
4
), we assumed that the agent forecasts a single quantity
X
. The extension to the multivariate case, where
p
quantities
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{p})^{\prime}
are forecast by the agent, is straightforward. Consider the same equal-length grids in Section
2.2
, then define,

\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}\equiv(q_{i_{1}-1},q_{i_{1}}]\times\cdots\times(q_{i_{p}-1},q_{i_{p}}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{p},

where
i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and each interval has length
\epsilon_{n}>0
. Note that, for
i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\in\{2,\dots,n{-}1\}
, the
\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}
is the
p
-dimensional hypercube with volume
\epsilon_{n}^{p}
. If we set
E_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}=\{\bm{X}\in\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}\}
, then this problem becomes the synthesis of “single agent,
np
events.” The synthesis function implied by (
4
) is,

\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y|\mathcal{H}_{n})=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\cdots\sum_{i_{p}=1}^{n}\Pi_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}(y)H\!\left(\bm{X}\in\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}\right),

where
H
is the agent’s distribution function of
\bm{X}
, and the coefficient is given by

\Pi_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}(y)\equiv\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y|\bm{X}\in\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}).

Viewing this expression as the Riemann sum, letting
n\to\infty
and
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
and computing the derivatives in
y
, we have

p(y|\mathcal{H})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\alpha(y|\bm{x})h(\bm{x})d\bm{x},

where
h
is the density function of distribution
H
. The similar expression can be found for
K\geq 1
in the same way.

In the discussion above, we have implicitly assumed that the DM has a prior for
h
, or the agent’s joint distribution of
\bm{X}
, and specifies its mean. If the agent does not report the joint density
h(\bm{x})
, but the the marginal densities,
h_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,h(x_{p})
, the synthesis formula becomes

p(y|\mathcal{H})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\alpha(y|\bm{x})\prod_{j=1}^{p}h_{j}(x_{j})d\bm{x}.

This synthesis formula also applies to the case where the agent reports joint density
h
, but the DM specifies the means of marginals
(h_{1},\dots h_{p})
, not the mean of joint
h
. Such a scenario has been considered in
McAlinn, 2021
.
