Dynamic changes of endogenous hormones and transcriptome analysis reveal the regulatory mechanism of gibberellin-promoted male cone formation in Platycladus orientalis
Authors: Ting Liao, Liqin Guo, Kai Qu, Na Li, Guobin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlatycladus orientalis is a unique monoecious coniferous tree species in China that plays an important role in urban landscaping and mountain afforestation. However, its large amount of pollen can easily trigger pollen allergy during spring. Clarifying the mechanisms underlying male cone formation and development in P. orientalis is essential for better controlling male cone production. Gibberellin (GA) is known to regulate various reproductive and developmental processes in plants. Although the regulatory role of GA in the reproductive transition of angiosperms has been relatively well characterized, its function in coniferous plants remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study elucidated gene expression mechanisms following GA treatment.ResultsThe male cone quantity after GA application was statistically analyzed. The results showed that treatment with 150 mg/L GA3 produced the highest increase in male cone number, reaching 84.2%. Endogenous hormone contents and transcriptomic profiles of male cones at different stages after GA3 spraying were compared with those of the control group. During the reproductive transition and development, GA3 and IAA concentrations in the treatment group were markedly higher than in the control. While the contents of ABA and ZT were lower than those of the control group, and there was no significant difference in the differentiation stage. In total, 13,661 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified across developmental stages. Among them, 201 DEGs were involved in endogenous hormone biosynthesis and signal transduction, and 85 DEGs were related to reproductive transition and development. These findings indicate that the reproductive transition in P. orientalis is regulated by interactions among multiple endogenous hormones. Furthermore, GO, KEGG, and WGCNA analyses were performed to construct a co-expression network associated with male cone initiation and development. Core regulatory genes, including GAI1, AGL8, GGM13, AP1, and AGL6, were identified and their expression patterns were revealed among different developmental stages.ConclusionsA gene regulatory network model describing GA-induced male cone formation and development in P. orientalis was established, revealing that the regulatory network from the vegetative to reproduction is more complex. This research provides a systematic and comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying male cone formation and development in P. orientalis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08706-4.

Background

Platycladus orientalis (L.), a monoecious species of the genus Platycladus within the Cupressaceae family, is one of the most important and unique coniferous tree species for urban greening and ecological restoration in northern China [1, 2]. It is also widely cultivated as the city tree of Beijing. However, its use is limited by the excessive production of male cones and the relatively low number of female cones, which leads to severe pollen-induced allergic symptoms in spring and restricts its landscape applications. According to allergen assessments conducted in our laboratory, P. orientalis pollen contains abundant allergenic proteins. Previous studies primarily focused on anatomical, physiological, and cultivation aspects of pollen structure and development [3]. In broad-leaved model species such as Arabidopsis thaliana, the molecular mechanisms underlying flower formation and development have been comprehensively elucidated [4–6]. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing male cone formation in coniferous trees—particularly in P. orientalis—remain poorly understood. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms and developmental characteristics of male cone formation in P. orientalis is crucial for reducing pollen pollution and controlling the source of pollen sensitization.

At present, the research on the reproductive regulation in gymnosperms mainly draws on the research in angiosperms. There are large number of genes in coniferous trees that are homologous to the flowering regulatory genes of angiosperms and have similar expression and function [7]. On the other hand, the FT and AP1 that play a core regulatory role in A. thaliana do not have direct homologous genes in the genomes of Norwegian spruce and Pinus tabulifomis [8, 9]. No direct FLC homologous genes were found in the whole genomes of P. tabilaegii and P. taeda either [9, 10]. In addition, some MADS-box genes are specific to coniferous trees. DAL10 and DAL21 are specifically expressed in cones, which may be involved in the reproductive organ formation and development in Norwegian spruce [11]. Based on the expression patterns of different developmental stages of male and female cones, new regulation models of reproductive development were proposed in Norwegian spruce and P. tabulatus [11, 12]

However, what are the underlying mechanisms and key genes for the male cone formation and development in P. orientalis? Based on the question, we previously carried out detailed morphological and anatomical studies on the cone development process in P. orientalis [3]. These studies revealed that, in the early stages of cone development, only unisexual primordia are present in P. orientalis, which differs from the hermaphroditic origin of flowers in angiosperms [3]. Before cone primordium initiation, spraying with different types and concentrations of exogenous hormones altered the number of male and female cones, indicating that cone formation in P. orientalis is malleable.

Endogenous hormones are closely associated with floral differentiation and formation [13–16]. The GA pathway is one of the six major pathways involved in flowering regulation [17, 18]. The GA regulatory pathway in model angiosperms has been largely elucidated [19, 20]. GA strongly influences the flowering process in trees by activating cell-elongation signaling pathways and breaking physiological dormancy [21]. Its mechanism functions through two major aspects: first, GA can substitute for low-temperature vernalization or long-day conditions and induce earlier flowering in some woody plants [22]. For example, exogenous GA directly induced flower bud differentiation and shortened the vegetative growth period in apples [23]. Second, GA promotes the expression of flowering-related genes (e.g., FT), which directly activate the expression of flower meristem genes such as SOC1, AP1, and LFY to initiate flower bud differentiation, thereby accelerating the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth [24–26]. However, due to differences in reproductive development patterns between coniferous plants and angiosperms [27, 28], how GA influences male cone formation in P. orientalis, as well as its regulatory genes and mechanisms, remains unclear.

The flowering-induction effect of GA is highly species-specific and depended on the types. GA3 has been reported to stimulate female flower production in spinach and corn and to promote male flower development in cucumber [29]. Its concentration in male flowers of poplar is significantly higher than in female flowers [30]. In Taxodiaceae and Cupressaceae, GA3 significantly promotes cone formation, whereas in Pinaceae, the less polar GA4+7 has a stronger effect [31]. Studies in Pseudotsuga menziesii have shown that injection of GA4+7 increases endogenous auxin (IAA) levels and slightly decreases abscisic acid (ABA) content. In Cryptomeria japonica D. Don, GA treatment in July strongly induces the male strobili and C-N ratio [32] These findings suggest that cone initiation and cone yield in coniferous trees may be regulated by endogenous hormones at specific tissue sites and developmental stages [33].

GA also interacts with other hormones. Studies in cucumber demonstrated that exogenous GA significantly reduced ethylene (ETH) synthesis at the stem tip, and expression levels of ETR1 and ACS genes were markedly decreased, suggesting that GA may regulate sex determination through the ETH pathway [34]. During female strobili differentiation in Ginkgo biloba, miR2950 suppressed the participation of its target gene ERF in the ETH metabolic pathway, thereby indirectly influencing the GA pathway [35]. Meanwhile, miR159 and miR858 inhibited the involvement of their target gene GAMYB in the GA metabolic pathway, jointly regulating female strobili formation in G. biloba [35].

In this study, endogenous hormone levels were quantified, and comparative transcriptomic analyses were performed on male cones at different developmental stages following exogenous GA application to identify the main genes and regulatory networks involved in promoting male cone formation in P. orientalis. This work is of great significance for understanding reproductive transition, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of male cone formation, and developing artificial hormonal regulation strategies for adjusting male-to-female cone ratios in P. orientalis. Moreover, it provides a foundation for molecular-assisted breeding and for addressing issues such as pollen pollution and allergy in landscape applications.

Methods

Plant materials, growth conditions and experimental treatment

P. orientalis used in this study was obtained from the coniferous plant resource nursery at the Institute of Forestry and Pomology, Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences, Beijing, China. The trees were selected from a batch of clones produced by cuttings in 2018 and were 5-year-old individuals. According to meteorological records, the nursery soil was alkaline (pH 7.1–8.2), with an average annual temperature of 11–13 °C and an annual rainfall of approximately 626 mm [3].

Following the developmental timeline of male cones in P. orientalis [3], cones prior to differentiation were sprayed twice with four different concentrations of GA3 (100, 150, 200 and 250 mg/L), with a 7-day interval between treatments. Each spray was applied until runoff from the tree crown, and water-sprayed clones were used as controls. Five trees were treated per concentration.

Statistics of male cone number and sample collection

The number of male cones after GA treatment was recorded and compared. In March of the following year, before pollen shedding began, the number of male cones on each small branch of the treated and control groups was counted. For each treatment, 10 branches oriented in different directions were selected for statistical analysis. Based on the results, the treatment yielding the greatest increase in male cone production (150 mg/L GA3) was selected for subsequent experiments.

Sampling began one week after GA application. The entire male cone for endogenous hormone determination and transcriptome analysis were collected at the pre-sex differentiation (June 20), sex differentiation (July 20), and male cone development (August 20) stages following a previous study [3]. These were designated S1, S2, and S3, respectively. Water-sprayed controls collected at the same time points were designated M1, M2, and M3. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for further analysis.

Determination of endogenous hormone contents

Endogenous phytohormone contents in male cones were quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS) following previously described methods with minor modifications [36]. The hormones measured included GA3, auxin (IAA), zeatin (ZT), and abscisic acid (ABA). Samples were ground in liquid nitrogen, and 50 mg of powder was transferred to a 2 mL centrifuge tube. A total of 500 μL of extraction solution (2-propanol:water:hydrochloric acid = 2:1:0.002) was added, followed by internal standards (2 ng/μL GA3, 5 μL; 2 ng/μL IAA, 5 μL; 0.25 ng/μL ABA, 15 μL; and 0.25 ng/μL ZT, 10 μL). Tubes were shaken at 4 °C at 100 rpm for 30 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of dichloromethane was added and shaken for another 30 min. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The lower organic phase was transferred to clean tubes, evaporated under nitrogen, and re-dissolved in 200 μL of methanol. HPLC–MS/MS analysis was performed using an AB Sciex QTRAP 5500 LC system (AB Sciex Pte. Ltd., USA). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and 0.4% triethylamine solution (pH 3.5). The injection volume was 10 μL. Each sample had three technical replicates and three biological replicates.

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by purification using the RNase-Free DNase Set (Qiagen China, Shanghai, China). RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and RNA integrity was examined via agarose gel electrophoresis. Enriched mRNA was fragmented with fragmentation buffer and reverse transcribed into cDNA using random primers. cDNA fragments were purified with the QiaQuick PCR extraction kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands), screened by agarose gel electrophoresis, amplified by PCR, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq™ 4000 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

Data filtering, unigene assembly, and quantification of gene abundance

Raw sequencing data potentially contained ribosomal RNA sequences, low-quality reads, and reads containing primers and adapters, which could affect downstream analyses. Therefore, raw reads were filtered using Fastp software (version 0.18.0) [37] to remove adapter sequences, ribosomal RNA sequences, and low-quality reads. The resulting high-quality reads were designated as clean reads. Clean reads were then mapped to the reference transcriptome obtained by third-generation sequencing of P. orientalis [2] using RSEM [38]. Gene abundances were calculated and normalized to RPKM (Reads Per kb per Million mapped reads).

Transcript annotation and analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [39]. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute fold change (FC) ≥ 2 were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To analyze the function of unigenes and determine the sequence orientation, the functional annotation of DEGs was performed against multiple nucleic acids and protein public databases, contained the Nr (NCBI Non-Redundant Protein Sequences), JGI (http://jgi.doe.gov/), Tair (http://www.arabidopsis.org/), GO (Gene Ontology, http://geneontology.org/), KOG and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, www.kegg.jp) databases [40–45].

Construction of co-expression network for DEGs

Based on RPKM values, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for each gene pair. Co-expression networks were visualized using Cytoscape 3.4. The degree value represented the number of connections a node had with other nodes, while the K-core value indicated the centrality of a regulatory gene within the network. Higher values indicated a more central position.

TheWGCNA was performed on all the samples using the standard package in R [28]. Intramodular connectivity and module correlation degree of each gene was calculated. Pearson correlation between each gene and trait data under the module is also calculated for the most relevant module corresponding to each phenotype data. Then the module that was most significantly correlated with GA3 content for further analysis in the coexpression network.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) verification of DEGs

qRT-PCR was performed to validate transcriptome data and analyze expression patterns of 14 DEGs associated with reproductive transition in P. orientalis. RNA extraction was conducted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Reverse transcription was performed with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR was carried out using the 7500-Fast instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA).

Primers were designed using Primer-BLAST [46] and are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The 20 μL PCR mixture contained 10 μL of 2 × SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.6 μL of each primer, 4 μL of 10 × diluted cDNA, and 5.4 μL RNase-free ddH2O. The reaction program consisted of pre-denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s, and ending with melting curve analysis from 72–95 °C. All genes were analyzed using three biological replicates. The UBC gene served as the reference gene, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [47].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Product and Service Solution (SPSS) software (version 25.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Means were compared using the t-test at the 5% significance level. Each treatment included three replicates. Data visualization was performed using OriginPro23, GraphPad Prism 8.0, and TBtools.

Results

Effect of GA3 treatment on the number of male cones in P. orientalis

Exogenous GA3 was sprayed prior to male bud differentiation in P. orientalis at concentrations of 100, 150, 200, and 250 mg/L. Statistical analysis showed that GA3 promoted male cone formation to varying degrees depending on the concentration (Fig. 1A). Among all treatments, 150 mg/L GA3 resulted in the greatest increase, reaching 84.2%, which was significantly higher than the other concentrations (Fig. 1C, D). To assess the stability of GA3 treatment, a continuous 4-year experiment was conducted using 150 mg/L GA3. Results demonstrated a consistent and significant increase in male cone number across all years compared with the corresponding controls. Analysis of endogenous hormone contents during the male cones formation and development

Endogenous hormone contents at different developmental stages of male cones in P. orientalis are shown in Fig. 2. Spraying GA3 before male cone differentiation significantly increased endogenous GA3 levels throughout development (S1, S2, and S3). GA3 concentrations in the treatment group were markedly higher than in the control. However, there was no significant differences between S2 and S3 stage in treatment and control groups, respectively. The IAA content showed a significant upward trend at different developmental stages after GA3 treatment. However, in the S3 stage, the IAA content in control male cones significantly decreased to the level of the S1 stage. In contrast, ABA and ZT showed similar decreasing patterns. During S1 and S3 stages, the contents of ABA were significantly higher than those in the control. During S2 stage, the content of ABA decreased, resulting in no significant difference from the control. While the ZT level was significantly lower than the control during S1, and no significant differences were observed during S2 and S3. There were no significant changes in the ABA contents in the control groups of the three stages, so as ZT contents. These findings suggest that GA3 plays a key role during male cone formation and early differentiation in P. orientalis. Global analysis of transcriptome sequencing of the male cones in response to GA3 treatment

Raw transcriptome sequencing data ranged from 40,641,608 to 93,053,036 reads. After removing adapter-containing reads, reads with > 10% N, poly-A reads, and low-quality reads, the clean data yield exceeded 98% for all samples (Supplementary Table S2). Isoforms assembled by third-generation sequencing were quantified using RSEM. Second-generation reads were aligned to these isoforms to obtain transcript abundance. The overall mapping rate was approximately 80%. Q20 and Q30 base calling rates exceeded 97% and 93%, respectively, and average GC content was approximately 43.59% (Supplementary Table S3). These metrics indicate that the sequencing quality met the requirements for downstream analyses.

The DEGs were analyzed across developmental stages (Fig. 3). A total of 13,661 DEGs were identified between GA3-treated and control groups (Fig. 3B). The most DEGs occurred during S1, with 8,981 genes (3,597 up-regulated and 5,384 down-regulated) (Fig. 3A). S2 contained 7,760 DEGs (3,936 up-regulated and 3,824 down-regulated), while S3 had 6,715 DEGs (3,630 up-regulated and 3,085 down-regulated). Within the GA3-treated group, 4,442 DEGs were detected across developmental stages (Fig. 3D). Between S1 and S2, 2,071 DEGs were identified (1,153 up-regulated, 918 down-regulated). The highest number of DEGs was observed between S1 and S3, with 3,590 DEGs (1,868 up-regulated, 1,722 down-regulated). The fewest DEGs occurred between S2 and S3, with 1,277 DEGs (463 up-regulated, 814 down-regulated) (Fig. 3C). To explore the biological roles of DEGs, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the 13,661 DEGs identified across developmental stages (Fig. 4). DEGs were enriched across 23 biological process (BP), 12 molecular function (MF), and 17 cellular component (CC) categories. In the BP category, the largest groups were Metabolic process (GO:0008152, 7,081 DEGs), Cellular process (GO:0009987, 7,000 DEGs), and Single-organism process (GO:0044699, 6,386 DEGs) (Fig. 4A, Table S4, Figure S1). Additionally, 982 DEGs were enriched in the growth category (GO:0040007), 850 in signaling (GO:0023052), 2,229 in developmental processes (GO:0032502), and 994 in reproductive processes (GO:0022414). DEGs involved in male cone formation and development

GO enrichment analysis identified 18 GO terms related to floral formation and development, comprising 85 DEGs responsive to GA3 treatment in P. orientalis (Fig. 6, Table S7). Floral organ development in angiosperms is divided into four whorls, and most ABCDE floral organ identity genes belong to the MADS-box family. In this study, AP1 (APETALA1), GGM13 (Graphical Gaussian Model 13), and AG1 (AGAMOUS1), corresponding to A-, B-, and C-class floral identity genes, respectively, were up-regulated following GA3 treatment. Other MADS-box DEGs included MADS6 and MADS50 (up-regulated), while AGL19 and AGL8 were down-regulated. Several kinase-, auxin-response-, and receptor-like kinase–related genes such as KIN14R, ARF4, RLK7, and GAI1 were also differentially expressed. Developmental regulators WUS and UFO, known to function with MADS-box genes, were also differentially expressed after GA3 treatment. Screening of hub genes for the formation and development of male cones

To identify hub regulatory genes responsive to GA3 treatment, two strategies were used. The first constructed co-expression networks using DEGs related to hormone signaling and male cone development; the second applied WGCNA to hormone-associated gene modules. Hub gene expression was then correlated with endogenous hormone contents.

After removing duplicates from the 201 genes related to hormone signal transduction and the 85 genes related to cones development mentioned above, 36 and 49 genes, respectively, were selected. Relationship pairs with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.9 were used to construct the co-expression networks (Fig. 7). From the network analysis, IAA, GA, and ABA were primarily involved in the hormone-related network. Within this network, LAX and SAUR genes in the IAA pathway, PIL genes in the GA pathway, and ABF genes in the ABA pathway formed the highest number of relationship pairs and occupied core node positions (Fig. 7A). Although GAI1 formed fewer connections, it exhibited strong relationships with the core nodes, suggesting that cross-talk between IAA and ABA may be mediated through the GA pathway. According to the gene expression patterns, GAI1 expression was positively correlated with LAX, SAUR, and PIL, and negatively correlated with ABF. Consistent with these patterns, endogenous hormone contents showed similar trends: IAA and GA3 varied in parallel, whereas ABA exhibited the opposite pattern during male cone formation and development. Based on these two screening strategies, dozens of hub genes were identified after GA3 spraying. Correlation analysis showed that the expressions of these genes were significantly positively or negatively associated with hormone content (Figs. 7C and 8F), suggesting potential hub regulatory roles in GA3-mediated male cone formation and development in P. orientalis. Notably, GAI1, AGL8, GGM13, AGL6, and MADS6 were identified as hub genes by both methods, indicating their crucial roles in the regulatory process.

To verify the accuracy of the transcriptome sequencing results and to characterize the expression patterns of genes involved in male cone formation and development in P. orientalis, the spatiotemporal expression of selected hub genes was analyzed (Fig. 9). Twelve DEGs related to hormone signaling, male cones development, and sex determination—GAI1, GID1, MADS2, WUS, AP1, AGL19, AP2, AG1, AGL8, MADS6, MADS1, and GGM13—were examined across the three developmental stages by RT-qPCR. Discussion

Due to the long generations, large and rare genomes, and unstable genetic transformation systems in gymnosperms, up to now, research on the reproductive organ development of gymnosperms is far less than that of angiosperms. P. orientalis provides substantial ecological benefits in northern China. The highly allergenic pollen makes it necessary to conduct deeper research on the male cone formation and development. We have made it clear about male and female primordium differentiation, strobilus initiation, and cone development through two years of continuous observation [3]. This study analyzed the changes in male cone formation and development stages following GA₃ treatment and explored the core genes involved in male cone formation and development in P. orientalis.

The effect of GA3 treatment on male cone formation and inhibition strategies in P. orientalis

The reproductive transformation of plants by GA depends on its concentration. In short-day plants (e.g. Citrus), excessive GA may inhibit flower bud formation and promote vegetative growth [48]. Furthermore, the concentration threshold and timing of GA application are critical factors. Low concentrations (1–10 mg/L) of GA could effectively promote flower bud formation, while higher concentrations may cause excessive vegetative growth and reduce flowering. For instance, in Myrica rubra, spraying 20–50 mg/L GA during the flower bud differentiation period (June–August) for 3–4 applications inhibited flower bud formation [49]. The concentration tolerance threshold for GA₃ appears higher in tree species compared with fruit crops. For example, 500 mg/L of GA3 significantly enhanced flower bud development and promoted earlier blooming in Camellia oleifera [50]. In the present study, the application of 150 mg/L GA₃ showed the strongest effect on promoting male cone formation in P. orientalis (Fig. 1), providing a theoretical basis for future efforts to suppress male cone production.

In coniferous trees, GA displays unique species-specific regulatory characteristics during the reproductive transition, and its effect is closely associated with GA polarity. Highly polar GAs (e.g., GA₃) significantly promote strobilus differentiation by activating key reproductive transition genes and inducing the development of female cones in Taxodiaceae and Cupressaceae [31]. By contrast, less-polar GAs (e.g., GA₄/GA₇ mixtures) have a stronger inductive effect on the reproductive transition of Pinaceae species, effectively shortening the vegetative phase and accelerating reproductive organ formation [31]. These differences may arise from distinct patterns of interaction between the GA signaling pathway and endogenous flowering regulators across conifer species. GA₃ may promote flowering by inducing the degradation of DELLA repressor proteins, thereby relieving their inhibition on flowering genes, whereas GA4/GA7 may exert a stronger effect through the photoperiod response pathway. Similarly, GA concentration thresholds and application timing play decisive roles in the reproductive transition and cones development of coniferous species. According to the results of this study, GA₃ application before male primordium differentiation (S1 stage) increased male cone formation, whereas GA₃ application after male cone development (S3 stage) stimulated vigorous vegetative growth in P. orientalis.

In practical applications, the allergenicity of pollen can be reduced by decreasing the number of male cones. GA inhibitors or plant hormones that have the opposite effect of GA are good choices. To investigate the dwarfing and flowering effects of GA biosynthesis inhibitors on Ricinus communis, three GA biosynthesis inhibitors with three concentrations: paclobutrazol, mepiquat chloride and chlorocholine chloride were sprayed. It was demonstrated that paclobutrazol could produce the opposite effect compared to treatment with GA [51]. On the other hand, GA and CTK generally show an antagonistic relationship with plant flowering. Therefore, relevant cytokinin-based products can be used for treatment. In lodgepole pine, application of a paste containing GA4 and GA7 combined with thidiazuron (TDZ) to long-shoot buds altered endogenous phytohormone profiles and induced female cone bud formation in the proximal portion of the long-shoot bud, where male cone buds normally occur. Meanwhile, it led to increased concentrations of endogenous zeatin-type CTKs [52]. It indicates that CTK may have an opposite effect to GA in changing the sex of flowers. In the present study, the content of GA and CTK showed basically opposite changing trends (Fig. 2). Hence, it can be considered to use CTK-related products when suppressing pollen in applications.

Exogenous GA treatment alters endogenous phytohormone levels and the expression of biosynthesis- and signaling-related genes

GA has been demonstrated to be a key hormone promoting male flower and seed formation in tree species such as C. henryi [53, 54], C. oleifera [50], and P. orientalis. Exogenous GA significantly alters endogenous hormone balance by directly supplying active GA molecules, activating negative feedback regulation, and modulating hormone interaction networks [55]. Exogenous GA₃ reduces endogenous GA synthesis by inhibiting the expression of GA20ox (a key biosynthetic enzyme) and activating GA2ox (a GA-degrading enzyme) [56–58]. For example, in melon, although exogenous GA₃ increased endogenous GA levels, a dynamic balance was achieved by suppressing GA20ox expression [59]. In this study, GA contents in the treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig. 2), indicating that exogenous GA rapidly activated related synthases. However, no DEGs associated with GA synthesis were detected among the S1, S2, and S3 stages. This may be due to the sampling time, as the first collection occurred one week after GA treatment.

In addition to GA biosynthetic genes, genes in plant hormone signal transduction pathways are also crucial. After exogenous GA binds to the GID1 receptor, the GA–GID1–DELLA complex forms and triggers SCF-mediated ubiquitination to degrade DELLA proteins, releasing the inhibition of transcription factors such as PIF [60]. The GID1–DELLA–SCF pathway is highly conserved in conifers, although species-specific differences exist in DELLA inhibitory domains. Meanwhile, the expression levels of components of the SCF complex, such as SLY1/GID2 and RGL1, directly influence GA sensitivity. In Rosa rugosa, downregulation of GID1 increased DELLA accumulation and ultimately suppressed expression of the downstream gene GASA [61]. In this study, DEGs related to the receptor gene GID1 and the DELLA gene GAI1 were detected across developmental stages. After GA₃ treatment, GID1 expression was significantly upregulated, while GAI1 (a DELLA protein) was significantly downregulated, ultimately affecting the expression of PIF. This suggests that GA regulation of male cone formation in P. orientalis is closely associated with the GID1–DELLA–SCF pathway. GASA genes function downstream of GA signaling [62, 63] and are strongly expressed in young tissues and rapidly growing organs [64]. In A. thaliana, GASA participates in floral meristem determination and promotes flowering [65]. In this study, GASA1, GASA2, GASA6, and GASA7 were identified as DEGs in both treatment versus control and among developmental stages, indicating their involvement in GA signal transduction. It is worth noting that 4, 6, 4, 5, 6, and 6 DEGs—including 7, 12, 5, 8, 10, and 10 transcripts—related to GA signal transduction were identified in S1 vs. S2, S1 vs. S3, S2 vs. S3, S1 vs. M1, S2 vs. M2, and S3 vs. M3 comparisons, respectively (Fig. 5), suggesting that GA signaling is more complex in the early stages of male cone formation than during later development.

Exogenous GA indirectly regulates plant hormone levels by promoting the synthesis or degradation of other hormones such as IAA, CTK, ABA, ETH, and JA, forming synergistic or antagonistic interactions [66]. This interaction becomes particularly pronounced following DELLA degradation. GA and IAA synergistically promote flower bud differentiation and floral organ development. GA enhances IAA accumulation by degrading DELLA proteins and relieving repression of auxin biosynthetic genes (e.g., YUC) and jointly induces the expression of flowering genes such as SOC, initiating floral induction [67]. In this study, IAA content increased progressively and remained significantly higher than the control during male cone formation and development (Fig. 2), indicating that exogenous GA enhanced IAA accumulation. Similar increases in IAA content during the flowering transition have been observed in R. odorata, Crocus sativus, and R. rugosa [61, 68, 69]. Among IAA synthesis-related genes, eight transcripts representing three genes—AO1, IAMT1, and YUC2—were identified as DEGs. AO1 expression at S1 was significantly higher than at M1 (Fig. 5), consistent with elevated IAA levels in the treatment group. Meanwhile, IAMT1 expression in the control during the same period was significantly higher than in the treatment group (Fig. 2). YUC2 expression was significantly higher at S2 and S3 compared with M2 and M3, and it steadily increased from S1 to S3. These results indicate that exogenous GA treatment significantly elevated the expression of IAA synthesis-related genes.

Beyond synthesis, IAA levels are also influenced by transport proteins such as PINs, AUX1/LAXs, and ABCBs [70]. In this study, three PIN family efflux carriers (PIN1, PIN2, PIN3), represented by ten transcripts, were identified as DEGs (Fig. 5). Their expression was significantly lower at S1 than at M1, but higher at S2 and S3 compared with M2 and M3. Across developmental stages, PIN expression first increased and then decreased. This pattern differed slightly from hormone content trends, likely due to the temporal offset between gene expression and protein synthesis [71]. The IAA signaling pathway includes IAA receptors (TIR1/AFB), transcriptional repressors (AUX/IAA), auxin response factors (ARF), and downstream target genes (GH, SAUR) [72]. Here, three AUX1/LAX influx carriers and the IAA receptor gene TIR were both upregulated in GA3-treated samples relative to controls (Fig. 5). Hence, the increased expression of PIN and AUX1/LAX may alter intracellular IAA transport, subsequently influencing the expression of other genes in the IAA transduction pathway. Following AUX/IAA degradation, ARF activates early auxin-responsive genes (e.g., AUX/IAA), transmitting the signal to SAUR and GH, thereby promoting growth and development. Across developmental stages, numerous DEGs related to ARF, IAA, SAUR, and GH were detected. Although these genes have not been definitively linked to reproductive transition, their roles warrant further investigation.

CTK is a key hormone for flower bud differentiation, directly activating meristematic cell division and acting synergistically with IAA or GA to promote flowering. In A. thaliana, CTK and IAA jointly regulate biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways [73]. In this study, cytokinin-related biosynthesis and signal transduction genes were differentially expressed between GA-treated and control samples, while most showed no significant differences among S1, S2, and S3 (Fig. 5). This pattern was largely consistent with hormone content measurements (Fig. 2).

GA and ABA typically act antagonistically. GA reduces ABA levels by inhibiting ABA synthesis genes (e.g., NCED), breaking dormancy and promoting floral initiation [74]. Conversely, ABA inhibits flowering by reducing active GA content and promoting DELLA accumulation. Two ABA synthesis-related genes, AO1 and NCED, were identified. Their expression patterns were generally negatively correlated with ABA content. This differs from patterns observed in several Rosaceae species [61, 68, 75]. This may be due to the AO1 also participates in IAA synthesis, which may be more active than ABA synthesis during male cones formation and development in P. orientalis. For NCED, four transcripts exhibited opposite expression patterns; determining which is functionally relevant requires further study. PP2C, a negative regulator in ABA signaling, showed high expression in S1 vs. M1 and a relatively stable trend across S1, S2, and S3, consistent with its negative relationship with ABA levels. Additionally, several other ABA pathway genes (PYR, PYL, ABF) were identified, forming a useful basis for subsequent studies on ABA regulation of male cones formation and development in P. orientalis.

For ETH, BR, SA, and JA, multiple DEGs in their signaling pathways were identified, including MYC2, a key regulator of JA signaling and hormone crosstalk. These results indicate that GA interacts with multiple endogenous hormones to modulate the expression of biosynthesis- and transduction-related genes, ultimately regulating the formation and development of male cones in P. orientalis.

The regulatory effect of GA on the male reproductive organ development

The regulatory effect of GA on floral organ development in angiosperms is mainly achieved through the DELLA protein degradation pathway and the direct regulation of flowering genes [76]. In A. thaliana, GA is perceived by the GID1 receptor, which induces DELLA protein degradation and releases SPL proteins from suppression [77, 78]. SPL proteins then activate the expression of floral-inducing genes such as SOC1 and FT, which subsequently activate key flowering genes such as LFY, promoting flower meristem formation [79]. Floral organ development is further regulated by MADS-box genes. However, conifers lack the typical FT–TFL1 competitive mechanism, and their reproductive transition differ significantly from those of angiosperms [9]. In this study, in addition to DELLA proteins, genes related to reproductive organ development were identified, including AP1, AG1, MADS6, MADS1, GGM13, AGL6, AGL8, AGL19, and other homologs. AP1 acts as a floral meristem identity gene [80, 81], determining the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. After GA3 spraying, AP1 expression increased significantly in S1 compared with M1. While the expression of AP1 decreased from S1 to S2, which was different from the expression pattern of M1 to M2 under normal conditions, indicating that GA3 may initiate male strobilus formation at S1 stage in P. orientalis. This coincides with findings in the conifer C. japonica [82]. Additionally, AG genes involved in floral organ formation were also significantly differentially expressed. Previous studies showed that AG can promote the expression of genes involved in active GA biosynthesis, and GA signaling can in turn upregulate AG expression [83]. In the gai-3 mutant, AG expression was lower than in the wild type, while AG overexpression partially restored floral phenotypes. In the present study, AG1 showed differently expressed between S3 and M3 stage, while no differences at S3 vs M3 and S2 vs M2 stages. These findings indicate that AG is essential for subsequent reproductive organ development and may be a GA-responsive gene during cones development. AG and active GAs regulate stamen development through a positive feedback loop [84].

Within the MADS-box gene family, the AGAMOUS-LIKE 6 (AGL6) subfamily exhibits diverse expression patterns and functions across plant species [85–88]. AGL6 genes play important roles in floral meristem regulation and the formation of floral organs and seeds in angiosperms [85, 86, 89]. Ectopic expression of AGL6 promotes flowering by activating FT and negatively regulating FLC/FAM in Oncidium Gower [90] and rice [86]. However, the functions and regulatory mechanisms of the AGL6 subfamily have not been fully elucidated in higher plants. Within the AGL family, AGL12 has been reported to regulate flowering transition in A. thaliana [91]. In this study, AGL6, AGL8, and AGL19 were identified as DEGs after GA3 treatment. These genes may be closely associated with male cone formation in P. orientalis, although their precise roles in the reproductive organ development remain unclear.

In conifers, DAL10 exhibits high expression in seeds and pollen of Norway spruce and is closely associated with reproductive development in Picea abies [11, 27]. SVP-like, SOC1-like, and SPL genes form regulatory networks distinct from those found in angiosperms and control female and male cone development in P. tabuliformis [92]. PtDAL1 is homologous to the AGL6 subfamily. DAL1 and MADS11 also play important roles in reproductive transition in P. tabuliformis [28]. In the present research, AGL6 and AGL19 showed the same gene expression pattern, it dropped after GA3 treatment at S1 vs M1, while subsequently rise at S2 and S3 stages. However, AGL8 had the opposite expression pattern. Although AGL6 family gene expression varies among distantly related plants, it is typically associated with reproduction and strobilus development [93].

DAL6 belongs to the GGM13 (Gnetum gnemon MADS) branch. GGM13 was one of 13 MADS-box genes identified in G. gnemon [94, 95], representing a gene subpopulation absent in dicotyledonous plants. The DEF/AP3, GLO/PI, and GGM13 branches cluster with multiple gymnosperm genes in P. abies [11]. These genes have been identified as B-class–related genes [93], suggesting that GGM13 may contribute to petal and stamen differentiation. GGM13 was also identified as a DEG in the present study (Fig. 6). During the M1 stage, its expression level was very low, with an FPKM value of 0.03. After treatment with GA3, although the expression level increased, the absolute value was still relatively low, at 0.3. During the male cone development, the expression level in S2 was significantly higher than that in M2, while there was no difference between S3 and M3. Therefore, GGM13 may be an "encoder of the cone organ identity", and it determines which tissues develop into petal-like or stamen-like structures in conifers. This indirectly indicates that reproductive transition genes in conifers may operate within a unique regulatory network involving DELLA proteins, functional divergence from angiosperms, and age-related epigenetic regulation. These findings provide important clues for understanding evolutionary adaptation and molecular breeding in coniferous species.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that exogenous GA spraying promotes male cone formation in P. orientalis. Endogenous hormone profiling and transcriptomic analyses were performed on treatment and control groups at different developmental stages. Genes in hormone biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways related to reproductive organ transition and development were identified. The homologs of these candidate core genes have been shown to participate in reproductive transition in other plant species; whether they play similar roles in P. orientalis requires further study. This research provides a foundation for elucidating the mechanisms underlying male strobilus formation and male cone development in conifers.
