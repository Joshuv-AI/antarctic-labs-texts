A statistical mechanical view of complex economies under the green transition
Filippo Marcuccini, Giacomo Livan

Abstract
We propose a stylized model of a complex economy to explore the economic tradeoffs imposed by the so called ‘green transition’ — the shift towards more sustainable production paradigms — using tools from the Statistical Mechanics of disordered systems. Namely, we promote the parameters of a standard input-output economic model to random variables, in order to characterize the typical statistical properties of its equilibria. A central feature of our work is the explicit inclusion of a waste variable as a proxy for the unwanted byproducts, such as emissions, generated by different production processes. We find that the interplay between economic development and waste gives rise to a double phase transition, separating a region of viable economic activity from two distinct shutdown regimes. Notably, more wasteful (‘brown’) economies support a larger number of technologies per good but fail to activate most of them, limiting their productive capacity. In contrast, ‘greener’ economies, while having access to fewer technologies, tend to achieve higher economic welfare, up to a critical point where rapid growth under tight waste constraints may destabilize them. Our results highlight the structural tensions between technological development and environmental sustainability, suggesting that the green transition may require careful navigation to avoid abrupt collapses in economic activity. The nature of the phase transitions we observe in the model is linked to the shrinkage of feasible economic configurations under increasing constraints, akin to algorithmic phase transitions studied in Computer Science.



I
Introduction

Modern developed economies have reached remarkable levels of complexity. Nowadays, technologically sophisticated products are delivered through intricate supply chains that often involve the interaction of hundreds, or even thousands, of heterogenoues actors, ranging from designers and developers, to factories and logistics companies
Pichler et al. 2023
;
Carvalho et al. 2021
.

As is well known by now, such economic development has come at the price of significant changes to our climate
Shukla et al. 2022
. As a consequence, our economies now face the daunting task of seeking to transition to cleaner paradigms of mass production, while at the same time retaining the economic benefits delivered by past growth. Such a challenge is often broadly referred to as the ‘green transition’, i.e., the reorientation of modern economies towards low-emission and resource-efficient ways of delivering goods to the global population.

From a high level perspective, the green transition amounts to an unprecedented attempt at ‘reconfiguring’ a large complex system. There is extensive debate on the pros and cons of different pathways towards greener economies
Shayegh et al. 2023
, due to the importance of the tradeoffs involved and the impact they may have on the lives of countless people. On the one hand, it is rather widely accepted that — on top of the obvious gains in terms of global health — the green transition will open up new economic opportunities in the medium-long term, favouring technological innovation
Acemoglu et al. 2012
and leading to the creation of new jobs
Lehr et al. 2012
. At the same time, though, it is also understood that the transition itself will not be painless in the short run, as it will likely result in job losses and/or in significant skill mismatches in the workforce
Hanna et al. 2024
, as well as in the exacerbation of preexisting socio-economic inequalities
Fremstad and Paul 2019
.

Getting a precise quantitative understanding of the above tradeoffs and their
net
effects on the global economy is an almost hopeless task, due to practical impossibility of collecting fine-grained data about economic production networks from different countries
Pichler et al. 2023
;
Bacilieri et al. 2023
, which often can only be inferred at best
Mungo et al. 2023
. Even worse, should those data be readily available, there would still be no obvious way of aggregating them into accurate predictions of the global economy’s future trajectory, due to the unprecedented nature of the course we are on.

In this paper, we seek to shed some light on the above tradeoffs by leveraging approaches that are typically used in the Statistical Mechanics of disordered systems
Stein and Newman 2013
, which provides a natural framework to characterize the
average
behavior of large complex interacting systems. The key insight provided by Statistical Mechanics is that the microscopic details of a large interacting system often become irrelevant to its macroscopic statistical description, resulting in so called self-averaging behavior. Such insight has led to massive advances in our understanding of a great variety of both physical and non-physical complex systems, ranging from spin glasses
Mézard et al. 1987
and quantum fields
Verbaarschot and
Wettig 2000
to ecosystems
Allesina and Tang 2012
, machine learning algorithms
Carleo et al. 2019
, and game theory
Galla and Farmer 2013
, just to name a few.

In the following, we will consider a standard
General Equilibrium Theory
(GET) model of an economy where profit-maximizing firms interact with each other and with utility-maximizing consumers, and seek to derive its statistical properties upon averaging over all possible interactions — within certain constraints — between such actors. Broadly speaking, GET refers to the analysis of the conditions under which supply and demand match via the interaction between rational economic agents (see, e.g.,
Lancaster 2012
). Despite being increasingly criticized for their somewhat unrealistic assumptions
Kirman 1992
;
Chang et al. 2013
, GET models remain very much in use among leading global economic institutions. Furthermore, a handful of studies — which our work builds upon — have already demonstrated how recasting GET models in a statistical mechanical framework leads to the emergence of results, such as sharp transitions between different economic regimes, that are in qualitative agreement with empirical observations but cannot be produced via classical GET modelling
De Martino and Marsili 2005
;
De Martino and Marsili 2006
;
De Martino et al. 2007
;
Bardoscia et al. 2012
;
Marsili 2014
;
Bardoscia et al. 2017
;
Moran and Bouchaud 2019
.

Within this stream of literature, our main contribution will be that of explicitly modelling and accounting for the
waste
generated by an economy, which in our simplified setting represents a proxy for, e.g., greenhouse gas emissions and other pollutants generated by modern economies.

In Section
II
, we illustrate our model, and in Section
III
we present its analytical solution (which we detail in full in the Appendices). In Section
IV
, we present our main results, highlighting two phase transitions that the economy described by our model undergoes in certain settings. The nature of such phase transitions is detailed in Section
V
.

II
Large Random Economies

We consider an economy with
N
firms,
C
goods and one representative consumer, a concept often used in GET modelling to capture average/typical consumer behaviour
Lancaster 2012
. Each firm is endowed with a technology that allows it to produce certain goods by consuming others. This is captured by a matrix with entries
q_{n}^{c}
that are positive (negative) when firm
n
produces (consumes) good
c
(
n=1,\ldots,N
;
c=1,\ldots C
). Each firm generates a certain amount of waste
\epsilon_{n}>0
, i.e.,

\sum_{c=1}^{C}q_{n}^{c}=-\epsilon_{n}\ ,

(1)

which effectively quantifies a firm’s efficiency as the discrepancy between the output produced by its technology and the input required to produce it. Firms operate their technologies at a scale
s_{n}>0
, with
s_{n}=0
indicating that firm
n
is inactive. When active, firms make a profit

\pi_{n}=s_{n}\sum_{c=1}^{C}q_{n}^{c}p^{c}=s_{n}\ \bm{p}\cdot\bm{q}_{n}\ ,

where
\bm{p}=(p^{1},\ldots,p^{C})
is the set of equilibrium prices for all goods (
p^{c}\geq 0,\ \forall\ c
) and
\bm{q}_{n}=(q_{n}^{1},\ldots,q_{n}^{C})
.

The representative consumer’s welfare is described by a utility function

U_{N}(\bm{x})=\sum_{c=1}^{C}u(x^{c})\ ,

(2)

where

x^{c}=x_{0}^{c}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}q_{n}^{c}\geq 0

(3)

denotes the amount of good
c
consumed at equilibrium. In the above expression,
x_{0}^{c}
represent the economy’s initial endowment of good
c
, i.e., goods for which
x_{0}^{c}>0
are primary goods (e.g., crops, raw materials), whereas goods for which
x_{0}^{c}=0
are entirely produced via technologies. The term
\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{n}q_{n}^{c}
quantifies the net production/consumption of good
c
by the firms’ technologies. In the following, we will denote the fraction of primary goods in the economy as
f_{0}
, i.e.,

f_{0}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\Theta(x_{0}^{c})\ ,

(4)

where
\Theta(x)=1
for
x>0
, and
\Theta(x)=0
otherwise.

The economy’s equilibrium prices
\bm{p}
are determined by jointly solving two optimization problems. On the supply side, firms seek to maximize their profits by determining optimal scales of production, i.e, each firm seeks to solve
\max_{s_{n}\geq 0}\pi_{n}
. On the demand side, the representative consumer seeks to maximize utility compatibly with a budget constraint:
\max_{\bm{x}\in B}U_{N}(\bm{x})
, where
B=\{x^{c}\geq 0\ :\ \bm{p}\cdot\bm{x}\leq\bm{p}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}\}
denotes the set of feasible consumption bundles, i.e., non-negative consumption vectors
\bm{x}=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{C})
(see Eq. (
3
)) that do not require the consumer to spend more than they would without any active technology.

It can be shown
Lancaster 2012
;
De Martino et al. 2007
;
Marsili 2014
;
Bardoscia et al. 2017
that the joint solution of the two above optimization problems, coupled with the condition in Eq. (
3
), is equivalent to solving

\bm{s}^{\star}=\argmax_{\bm{s}\geq 0}\ U_{N}(\bm{s}|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})=\argmax_{\bm{s}\geq 0}\ U_{N}\left(\bm{x}_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}\bm{q}_{n}\right)\ ,

(5)

which determines the optimal scale of production of each technology
\bm{s}^{\star}=(s_{1}^{\star},\ldots,s_{N}^{\star})
and, via Eq. (
3
), the optimal consumption bundle for the consumer
\bm{x}^{\star}=(x_{1}^{\star},\ldots,x_{C}^{\star})
for a
fixed
production matrix
\bm{q}=\{q_{n}^{c}\}_{n=1,\ldots,N}^{c=1,\ldots,C}
, waste vector
\bm{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{N})
(see Eq. (
1
)), and initial endowment
\bm{x}_{0}
.

Our goal in the following is to characterize the economy’s properties irrespective of its details. In order to do so, we will ‘promote’
\bm{q}
,
\bm{\epsilon}
and
\bm{x}_{0}
to random variables, and take the ‘thermodynamic’ limit
N,C\rightarrow\infty
with
\nu=N/C
fixed. As we will see, under such a limit the economy’s main statistical properties display self-averaging behaviour and attain typical values that do not depend on the economy’s specificities.

Let us reformulate the optimization problem in Eq. (
5
) as

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\max_{\bm{s}\geq 0}\ U_{N}(\bm{s}|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\beta N}\log Z_{N}(\beta|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})\ ,

(6)

where

Z_{N}(\beta|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})=\int\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\ \mathrm{e}^{\beta U_{N}(\bm{s}|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})}\ .

The above expression obviously mirrors a canonical partition function in Statistical Mechanics, and the optimization problem in Eq. (
6
) has been converted into a steepest descent one by the limit on the ‘inverse temperature’
\beta\rightarrow\infty
that selects the values of the utility function
U_{N}
that dominate the integral above. Reasonably, we can expect the maxima of the function
U_{N}
to grow with
N
, so that the quantity
U_{N}/N
in Eq. (
6
) will be an intensive one. Our position is that, for a reasonable choice of the utility function, the maxima of
U_{N}/N
will cease to depend on the economy’s details as it grows large, i.e.,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\max_{\bm{s}\geq 0}\ U_{N}(\bm{s}|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\max_{\bm{s}\geq 0}\ U_{N}(\bm{s}|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})\right\rangle_{\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0}}\ ,

where
\langle\cdots\rangle_{\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0}}
denotes averaging over the probability density functions describing the statistical properties of the economy’s production matrix, waste, and initial endowment.

From a practical standpoint, the above expression looks rather inconvenient, as it requires averaging over the logarithm of the partition function
Z_{N}
. Such a rather cumbersome task is usually circumvented via the so-called replica trick, which converts the average over
\log Z_{N}
into an average over
r
replicas of the system through the following formal identity

\langle\log Z_{N}\rangle=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\langle Z_{N}^{r}\rangle-1}{r}\ ,

where
r
is taken to be an integer number throughout all calculations, and restored to be a real number only at the end when taking the above limit.

All in all, the optimization problem in Eq. (
6
) can be solved as

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\max_{\bm{s}\geq 0}\ U_{N}(\bm{s}|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})\right\rangle_{\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0}}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\beta Nr}\log\langle Z_{N}^{r}(\beta|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})\rangle_{\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0}}=h(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\ ,

(7)

where the last equality comes from the fact that the replica method typically allows to identify
order parameters

\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k})
such that

\langle Z_{N}^{r}(\beta|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})\rangle_{\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0}}\approx\int\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}\ \mathrm{e}^{\beta Nrh(\bm{\theta})}

(8)

for a suitable function
h
, and the optimal order parameter values in Eq. (
7
) are found by solving the set of saddle point equations

\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right)_{\theta_{i}=\theta_{i}^{\star}}=0\ \qquad\ i=1,\ldots,k

which extract the maximum of the function in the exponential in the above integral.

III
Solution and typical properties

In Appendix
A
we provide a detailed analytical solution to the optimization problem in Eq. (
7
) under the following assumptions:

•

As customary
Lancaster 2012
, we choose the utility function in Eq. (
2
) to be concave and monotonically increasing by choosing
u(x^{c})=\log(x^{c})

•

We assume the amount of waste generated by each technology (see Eq. (
1
)) to be distributed according to a Gaussian distribution with mean
\epsilon_{0}
and variance
\lambda>0
:

P(\epsilon_{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(\epsilon_{n}-\epsilon_{0})^{2}}{2\lambda}}\ .

(9)

Throughout the rest of the paper we choose
\epsilon_{0}=g\lambda
with
g=10
in order to effectively make sure that
\epsilon_{n}>0
, as it should be. Albeit inelegant, this assumption is a necessary compromise to retain the Gaussian form of the above probability distribution, which allows to make analytical progress. Thus, in the following, larger values of
\lambda
will correspond to higher waste, as they lead both to a shift in the mean and to a broader variation in the distribution in Eq. (
9
).

•

We assume each row of the production matrix to be drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean zero and variance
1/C
, constrained to match the constraint in Eq. (
1
) for fixed
\epsilon_{n}
(drawn as explained in the above point):

P(\bm{q}_{n})=\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N}\delta\left(\sum_{c=1}^{C}q_{n}^{c}+\epsilon_{n}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{C}{2}(q_{n}^{c})^{2}}}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\bm{q}_{n}^{\prime}\ \prod_{n=1}^{N}\delta\left(\sum_{c=1}^{C}(q_{n}^{c})^{\prime}+\epsilon_{n}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{C}{2}(q_{n}^{c})^{\prime 2}}}\ ,

(10)

where
\delta(\cdot)
is a Dirac delta.

•

We assume each good to be primary with probability
f_{0}
(see Eq. (
4
)), and, if so, to have unit initial endowment:

P(x_{0}^{c})=f_{0}\delta(x_{0}^{c}-1)+(1-f_{0})\delta(x_{0}^{c})\ .

(11)

With these positions, the solution to Eq. (
7
) reads

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\max_{\bm{s}\geq 0}\ U_{N}(\bm{s}|\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0})\right\rangle_{\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0}}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\max_{\bm{s}\geq 0}\ U_{N}\left(\bm{x}_{0}+\sum_{n=^{N}}s_{n}\bm{q}_{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\bm{q},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0}}=h(\Omega^{\star},\kappa^{\star},p^{\star},\sigma^{\star},\chi^{\star},\hat{\chi}^{\star})\ ,

where

\displaystyle h\left(\Omega,\kappa,p,\sigma,\chi,\hat{\chi}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega\hat{\chi}}{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}\chi}{2\nu}-\frac{\chi\left(1+\lambda\right)p^{2}}{2\nu}+\frac{p\kappa}{\nu}+\left<\max_{s\geq 0}\left[-\frac{\hat{\chi}}{2}s^{2}+\left(t\sigma-p\epsilon_{0}\right)s\right]\right>_{t}

(12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu}\left\langle\max_{s\geq 0}\left[u\left(x\right)-\frac{1}{2\chi}\left(x-x_{0}+t\sqrt{\nu\Omega}+\frac{\kappa}{1+\lambda}\right)^{2}\right]\right\rangle_{t,x_{0}}\ .

We provide a detailed derivation of the above result in Appendix
A
. In the above expression,
\bm{\theta}=(\Omega,\kappa,p,\sigma,\chi,\hat{\chi})
denote the six order parameters that characterize the solution, whose optimal values are to be determined by solving the saddle point equations

\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial\Omega}\right)_{\Omega=\Omega^{\star}}=\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial\kappa}\right)_{\kappa=\kappa^{\star}}=\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial p}\right)_{p=p^{\star}}=\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial\sigma}\right)_{\sigma=\sigma^{\star}}=\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial\chi}\right)_{\chi=\chi^{\star}}=\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial\hat{\chi}}\right)_{\hat{\chi}=\hat{\chi}^{\star}}=0\ .

(13)

The averaging with respect to
t
in Eq. (
12
) simply denotes an average with respect to a standard Gaussian variable, i.e.,
\langle\cdots\rangle=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t(\cdots)\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/2}
.

V
Nature of the phase transitions

In this Section, we provide some numerical evidence on the nature of the phase transitions illustrated in Fig.
1
. Their origins can be traced back to Eq. (
3
), which effectively identifies a set of constraints. Namely, the economy’s production network matrix
q_{n}^{c}
and the optimal scales of production at equilibrium
s_{n}^{\star}
must guarantee that the production level of each good is a non-negative quantity, i.e.,
(x^{c})^{\star}\geq 0
. Clearly, at finite
N
and
C
, this depends on the specific draw of
q_{n}^{c}
from the probability density in Eq. (
10
). It may very well be that, for some ‘unlucky’ draw of the input-output matrix, no production scale values exist to guarantee strictly positive consumption levels (at least for some goods), making the ‘shut down’ solution
s_{n}^{\star}=0
,
\forall\ n
(and therefore
(x^{c})^{\star}=0
,
\forall\ n
) the only viable one.

In the thermodynamic limit
N,C\rightarrow\infty
, with
\nu=N/C
fixed, the above process results in a sharp separation between regions where all draws of the input-output matrix are ‘unlucky’, resulting in systematic economic shut down, from regions where they all are ‘lucky’, resulting in some non-trivial level of economic activity. More formally, the former correspond to regions where the volume

V=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\prod_{c=1}^{C}\Theta\left(x_{0}^{c}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}q_{n}^{c}\right)

(14)

shrinks to zero in the thermodynamic limit.

We numerically corroborate the above picture by solving the following linear programming problem at finite
N
and
C
values

\max_{\bm{s}}\ \bm{s}\cdot\bm{\rho}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mathrm{subject\ to}\ \ \ \ \begin{cases}x_{0}^{c}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}q_{n}^{c}\geq 0&\forall\ c\\
s_{n}\geq 0&\forall\ n\ ,\end{cases}

(15)

where
\rho
is a random vector of positive numbers (e.g.,
N
i.i.d. draws from the uniform distribution in
[0,1]
). Our focus in the above problem is not on its solution, but rather on the
existence
of a non-trivial solution, hence our use of a random vector. In other words, the above linear programming problem looks for solutions within the polytope identified by its constraints. Should non-trivial solutions exist, then the constraints do not shrink the volume of possible solutions in Eq. (
14
) to just the origin (
s_{n}=0
,
\forall\ n
).

VI
Discussion

In this paper, we propose a stylized model of a complex economy to characterize the average behavior of a standard General Equilibrium Theory (GET) model when its parameters are treated as random variables, following the approach of the Statistical Mechanics of disordered systems. Our main focus is on the waste generated by complex economies, which we use as a proxy for emissions and other unwanted byproducts released by industrial production.

Our model is, of course, a simplified representation of the real-world complexity that characterizes modern economies and supply chains. To begin with, it inherits from GET the limitation of not capturing temporal dynamics, as GET focuses on static equilibria. Nonetheless, these equilibria can be interpreted in a ‘meta-stable’ sense — that is, as economic states that may persist for extended periods before transitioning to a new equilibrium due to shifts in the economy’s fundamental parameters.

Within this framework, our model serves as a useful tool for evaluating the tradeoffs imposed on modern economies by the so called green transition. In this context, the model’s phase diagrams (Fig.
1
) illustrate the possible trajectories an economy may follow during the course of this transition. The central trade-off highlighted by our model lies between economic development and economic welfare, very much in line with the literature on ‘green growth’ and its alternatives
D’Alessandro et al. 2020
;
Hickel and Kallis 2020
. Specifically, we consistently find that more wasteful (i.e., brown) economies have access to higher values of
\nu=N/C
— that is, a greater number of available technologies per good produced. However, much of this technological potential remains unrealized, as these economies exhibit a monotonic decline in the fraction
\phi
of active technologies with increasing
\nu
. At the same time, greener economies, despite having access to lower values of
\nu
, tend to generate higher economic welfare, reflected in higher production scales, higher GDP, and higher consumption levels (see Fig.
2
).

One of the most notable features of our model is that it reveals a double phase transition, separating the region where an economy can function from two regions of economic shutdown. These shutdown regions correspond to scenarios where either economic development is too advanced to be compatible with low levels of waste, or too limited for the economy to function. Interestingly, greener economies tend to approach the former boundary along a path marked by substantial growth, particularly in terms of operation scale (see, e.g., the top-left panel in Fig.
1
). This suggests that rapid growth under low-waste conditions may require careful management to prevent abrupt disruptions in economic activity.

On a more technical note, we were able to explain the aforementioned phase transitions with rather convincing numerical evidence, linking them with the ‘phase space shrinkage’ that occurs as an economy is subjected to an increasing number of constraints. This behavior is reminiscent of algorithmic phase transitions commonly studied in Computer Science problems
Monasson et al. 1999
;
Mézard et al. 2002
.
