ALPs production from Light Primordial Black Holes: the role of Superradiance
Marco Manno, Daniele Montanino

Abstract
Light Primordial Black Holes (LPBHs) with masses in the range 10 10 g ≤ M BH ≤ 10 9 \leq M_{\rm BH}\leq 10^{9} g, although they evaporate before Big Bang Nucleosynthesis, can play a significant role in the production of both Dark Matter and Dark Radiation. In particular, LPBHs can evaporate into light axions or axion-like particles (ALPs) with masses m a ≲ m_{a}\lesssim MeV, contributing to the effective number of neutrino species, Δ ​ N eff \Delta N_{\rm eff} . Additionally, heavy scalar particles known as moduli , predicted by String Theory, can be produced both via Hawking evaporation and through amplification by a mechanism called Superradiance Instability in the case of spinning PBHs. These moduli can subsequently decay into ALPs, further amplifying their abundance. In this work, we calculate the number density of ALPs in the presence of moduli enhanced by Superradiance for Kerr PBHs. Using current limits on Δ ​ N eff \Delta N_{\rm eff} from Planck satellite observations, we derive updated constraints on this scenario.

Introduction

Besides stellar-collapse black holes, Primordial Black Holes (PBHs), produced by density fluctuations during post-inflationary eras, have been proposed for a long time
Zel’dovich and Novikov 1967
;
Hawking 1971
;
Carr and Hawking 1974
;

. Unlike stellar-collapse black holes, PBHs can, in principle, have a wide range of masses, from
\sim 1

;
Bird et al. 2023
. However, strong constraints on PBH masses and abundances exist due to various observations

;
Green 2024
(updated bounds can also be found in
Kavanagh

10\lesssim M_{\rm BH}\lesssim 10^{9}
g, the constraints are much weaker
Papanikolaou et al. 2021
;
Domènech et al. 2021
. In the following, we refer to these PBHs as Light Primordial Black Holes (LPBHs). This is because LPBHs would evaporate via
Hawking radiation

Hawking 1975
before neutrino decoupling, and their radiation products would enter into thermal equilibrium with the environment.

In addition to standard particles, LPBHs can also evaporate into non-standard heavy or light (or massless) particles. In the former case, if these particles are stable, they can serve as cold or warm dark matter candidates. This scenario has been extensively analyzed in the literature (see
Fujita et al. 2014
;
Lennon et al. 2018
;
Hooper et al. 2019
;

;

;

;

;
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Chen et al. 2024b
for a non-comprehensive list). In the latter case, light particles could contribute to dark radiation. Essentially, there are two possible types of particles: gravitons
Papanikolaou et al. 2021
;
Domènech et al. 2021
;
Arbey et al. 2021
;
Hooper et al. 2020
or axion-like particles (ALPs)

;

;

Besides evaporation, another mechanism that can enhance particle production from spinning black holes is
superradiance
(see
Brito et al. 2015
for a review). This is a phenomenon of amplification by coherent emitters, which is ubiquitous in many physical systems. For instance, waves scattering off a rotating cylinder can increase in amplitude. If a mirror surrounds the cylinder, the waves bounce back and forth, exponentially increasing their energy at the expense of the cylinder’s kinetic energy. This phenomenon is called
superradiant instability
. In the case of spinning black holes, the role of the scattering waves can be played by massive bosons, while the mirror is provided by gravity itself
Press and Teukolsky 1972
;
Teukolsky and Press 1974
;
Cardoso et al. 2004
. This leads to the formation of a
gravitational atom
, where a boson cloud is exponentially amplified. The key parameter governing this phenomenon is the
gravitational fine structure constant
,
\alpha=r_{s}/2\lambda_{c}
, where
r_{s}=2GM_{\rm BH}
is the Schwarzschild radius of the black hole and
\lambda_{c}=1/m_{S}
is the Compton wavelength of the boson mass
Zouros and Eardley 1979
;
Detweiler 1980
;
Dolan 2007
;
Witek et al. 2013
;
Roy et al. 2022
;
Bernal et al. 2022
;
Bao et al. 2022
;
Hoof et al. 2024
;
Siemonsen et al. 2023
. Hereafter, we adopt natural units (
\hbar=c=k_{B}=1
) and use
M_{\rm pl}=1/\sqrt{8\pi G}
. For
M_{\rm BH}<10^{9}
g, superradiance is effective for boson masses
m_{B}>10^{5}
GeV. If these particles are stable and feebly interacting with ordinary matter, they would not thermalize and could now constitute dark matter
Bernal et al. 2022
. Alternatively, if the massive bosons are unstable, they might decay into very weakly interacting light particles, such as axions or ALPs, leaving an excess of dark radiation beyond neutrinos.

String theory models often predict the existence of heavy moduli fields that behave like bosons. In such scenarios, moduli fields produced after inflation could decay into ALPs
Cicoli et al. 2013
;
Higaki and Takahashi 2012
;
Higaki et al. 2013
;
Conlon and Marsh 2013a
;
Conlon and Marsh 2013b
;
Angus et al. 2014
;
Evoli et al. 2016
. In addition, these particles can induce superradiant instabilities in primordial Black Holes, thereby enhancing the production of ALPs.
In this work, we analyze this scenario. In Sec.
II
, we illustrate the main mechanism responsible for the mass and spin loss of Black Holes, namely Hawking radiation. In Sec.
III

VII

Hawking radiation, a theoretical prediction made by physicist Stephen Hawking
in 1974

;
Hawking 1975

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta-a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}dt^{2}+\Sigma\left(\frac{dr^{2}}{\Delta}+d\theta^{2}\right)

III
Superradiance

In addition to Hawking radiation, rotating black holes exhibit another remarkable phenomenon: superradiance, which can lead to significant energy
extraction and even instabilities. Superradiance instabilities occur when the conditions for wave amplification lead
to a runaway effect.
The instabilities can be triggered in various ways (see
Brito et al. 2015
), but here we consider the more natural mechanism called
gravitational atom
, i.e. the system of a black hole and a massive bosonic field.
Massive scalar fields with mass
m_{S}
can be trapped in Hydrogen-like bound states:

E_{n}=\omega_{n}\simeq m_{S}-\frac{\alpha^{2}m_{S}}{2n^{2}}\;,

(10)

where we have defined a useful parameter called gravitational coupling:

\alpha=\frac{r_{s}}{2\lambda_{c}}=GM_{\rm BH}m_{S}\simeq 0.38\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{10^{7}g}\right)\left(\frac{m_{S}}{10^{7}\text{GeV}}\right)\;,

(11)

in which
\lambda_{c}=1/m_{S}
is the Compton length of the boson.
For superradiance to occur, the wave frequency
\omega
must satisfy a specific condition relative to the black hole’s spin. This condition is expressed as:

\omega<\mu\Omega\;,

(12)

where
\mu
is the azimuthal quantum number of the field.
When this condition is met, an incident wave is amplified, extracting rotational energy from the black hole. This amplification leads to the growth of bound modes in the case of massive bosonic fields. The growth rate can be approximated as
Bernal et al. 2022
;
Detweiler 1980

\Gamma_{\text{sr}}(M_{\rm BH},a_{\star})\simeq\frac{m_{S}}{24}\left(\frac{m_{S}M_{\rm BH}}{8\pi M_{\rm pl}^{2}}\right)^{8}(a_{\star}-2m_{S}r_{+})\;,

(13)

where the dominant unstable mode
n=2
and
l=\mu=1
was considered.

We notice that
\Gamma_{sr}
is calculated at order
{\cal O}(\alpha)
in the limit of
\alpha\ll 1
, i.e. the non relativistic regime. Correction are calculated in the literature
Hoof et al. 2024
up to
{\cal O}(\alpha^{5})
or even numerically
Siemonsen et al. 2023
. However, the approximately Eq. (
13
) is enough to our purposes.
It’s straightforward now to generalize Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
) in presence of superradiance.

Considering the fact that superradiant instabilities can amplify the scalar field, we can indicate with
{\cal N}_{S}
the number of scalar particles gravitationally bounded to a PBH. For nearly stable scalar particles, the dynamics of the system can be described as follows
Bernal et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle\frac{d{\cal N}_{S}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{sr}(M_{\rm BH},a_{\star}){\cal N}_{S}\;,

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\rm BH}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon(M_{\rm BH},a_{\star})\frac{M_{\rm pl}^{4}}{M_{\rm BH}^{2}}-m_{S}\frac{d{\cal N}_{S}}{dt}\;,

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{da_{\star}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-a_{\star}[\gamma(M_{\rm BH},a_{\star})-2\varepsilon(M_{\rm BH},a_{\star})]\frac{M_{\rm pl}^{4}}{M_{\rm BH}^{3}}+

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{GM_{\rm BH}^{2}}\left(\sqrt{2}-2\alpha a_{\star}\right)\frac{d{\cal N}_{S}}{dt}\;,

(16)

being the factor
\sqrt{2}=\sqrt{l(l+1)}
in the last term the angular momentum carried away by a single boson in the dominant mode
l=1
. It has been shown that the initial number of scalar particles does not affect the final result, so one can set
{\cal N}_{S}(0)=1
.
As we will see later, the superradiant phase is much faster compared to the evaporation timescale. For this reason, one can, in principle, neglect evaporation during the superradiant phase, although no approximations are made in our calculations.
We highlight that numerical simulations indicate that for
a_{\star}\simeq 1
, the superradiant instability reaches its maximum at
\alpha\sim 0.42

Dolan 2007
;
Witek et al. 2013
.

IV
ALPs production from moduli decay

In this work, we consider the heavy particles called moduli,
\Phi
. These fields are real scalar fields predicted by string theory, with couplings suppressed by the Planck constant
M_{\rm pl}
, and naturally arise from the complex Calabi-Yau geometry (see
McAllister and Quevedo 2023
for a recent review). These are naturally long-lived particles because their coupling with matter particles is suppressed by
1/M_{\rm pl}
. The moduli
\Phi
can serve as the scalar particles responsible for inducing superradiance in primordial black holes (PBHs). From Eq. (
11
), we observe that for a moduli mass
10^{5}\lesssim m_{\Phi}\lesssim 10^{13}
GeV, superradiance is effective, which aligns with the Large Volume Compactification scenario
Cicoli et al. 2013
;
Higaki and Takahashi 2012
;
Higaki et al. 2013
;
Conlon and Marsh 2013a
;
Conlon and Marsh 2013b
;
Angus et al. 2014
;
Evoli et al. 2016
. For simplicity, we assume that no other heavy scalar particles contribute to the superradiant instability besides moduli.
As we will demonstrate in the following section (see also
Bernal et al. 2022
), the timescale of moduli production via superradiance is much shorter than the moduli decay lifetime,
\tau_{sr}\ll\tau_{\Phi}
.

A population of ALPs can be produced by the decay of moduli particles. The decay rate of moduli
\Phi
is
Cicoli et al. 2013
:

\Gamma_{\Phi}=\tau_{\Phi}^{-1}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{m_{\Phi}^{3}}{(M_{\rm pl}/k)^{2}}\;,

(17)

with
k
a constant of order
{\cal O}(1)
(in the following we choose for simplicity
k=1
).
Since moduli have gravitational couplings, they can decay into various sectors, including both standard and non-standard particles. Without constraints on the mass of the moduli, the universe could remain dominated by these scalar particles, delaying the onset of primordial nucleosynthesis (BBN). To align with BBN predictions, reheating must occur before nucleosynthesis, requiring a reheating temperature
T_{\text{reheat}}>{\cal O}(1)\,\text{MeV}
, which imposes an upper limit on the moduli mass,
m_{\Phi}\gtrsim 30\,\text{TeV}

Cicoli et al. 2013
. Moduli don’t decay instantly and the energies of the produced ALPs particles scales differently depending on when the moduli decayed. ALPs are produced by processes

\Phi\rightarrow aa
with
\Gamma_{\Phi\rightarrow aa}=B_{a}\Gamma_{\Phi}
,
where
B_{a}
is the branching ratio, which quantifies how many moduli decay in ALPs. Equivalently
1-B_{a}
quantifies how many moduli decay in standard model particles.

V
Complete scenario

Taking into account all the production channels, the evolution equations for the energy densities can be cast as follow

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm SM}+4H\rho_{\rm SM}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{\rm SM}\frac{M_{\rm pl}^{4}}{M_{\rm BH}^{2}}n_{\rm BH}+\Gamma_{\Phi}(1-B_{a})\rho_{\Phi}\;,

(18)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{a}+4H\rho_{a}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{a}\frac{M_{\rm pl}^{4}}{M_{\rm BH}^{2}}n_{\rm BH}+\Gamma_{\Phi}B_{a}\rho_{\Phi}\;,

(19)

where
\rho_{\Phi}=m_{\Phi}n_{\Phi}
, with
n_{\Phi}=n_{\rm BH}{\cal N}_{\Phi}
number density of moduli and
n_{\rm BH}(t)=n_{{\rm BH},i}/a^{3}(t)
is the number density of PBHs.
\varepsilon_{\rm SM}
is the sum of the
\varepsilon_{i}
in Eq. (
8

\varepsilon_{a}
for the ALP field.

Similarly, we can write the equation for
\rho_{\Phi}

\dot{\rho}_{\Phi}+3H\rho_{\Phi}=\Gamma_{sr}\rho_{\Phi}+\varepsilon_{\Phi}\frac{M_{\rm pl}^{4}}{M_{\rm BH}^{2}}n_{\rm BH}-\Gamma_{\Phi}\rho_{\Phi}\;,

(20)

where the first term takes into account the superradiant production, the second term the evaporation and the last one the decay. Previous equations must be supplemented by the Friedmann-Robertson-Walker equation:

H^{2}(t)=\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\left(\rho_{\rm SM}+\rho_{a}+\rho_{\Phi}+\rho_{\rm BH}\right)\;,

(21)

with
\rho_{\rm BH}(t)=M_{\rm BH}(t)n_{\rm BH}(t)
.
This equation allows to solve the evolution equation in terms of the cosmological scale factor
a
instead of the time

\frac{d}{dt}=\frac{da}{dt}\cdot\frac{d}{da}=aH(a)\cdot\frac{d}{da}\;,

(22)

being
a=1
the scale factor at

when PBH formed. This time roughly correspond to the time when
the Schwarzschild radius of the perturbation was of the order of the horizon scale,
2GM_{\rm BH}\sim 2t_{i}

VI
Extra effective number of neutrinos

The inclusion of superradiance not only amplifies the production of moduli
but also leaves a lasting impact on the axion population, which have observational
consequences for the Cosmological Axion Background (CAB)
Conlon and Marsh 2013a
. This axion population can contribute to the extra effective number of neutrino species:

\Delta N_{\rm eff}=\frac{\rho_{a}(T_{0})}{\rho_{r}(T_{0})}\left[N_{\rm eff}+\frac{8}{7}\left(\frac{11}{4}\right)^{4/3}\right]\;,

(24)

where
T_{0}=2.73
K is the current temperature of the cosmic microwave background (CMB),
N_{\rm eff}=3.043
the effective numbers of neutrinos
Cielo et al. 2023
, and
\rho_{r}
denote the ‘standard” radiation made of photons and neutrinos.
Now we want to redshift the latter equation to the time at which the decay of the moduli particles
t_{d}\gtrsim\Gamma_{\Phi}^{-1}
. We denote this time as
t_{d}
, and the temperature at this time as
T_{d}
.

As the universe expands the standard radiation energy density gets diluted as

\frac{\rho_{r}(T_{0})}{\rho_{\rm SM}(T_{d})}=\frac{g_{\rho}(T_{0})}{g_{\rho}(T_{d})}\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{d}}\right)^{4}\;.

Further, total entropy must be conserved, so we have

\frac{T_{0}}{T_{d}}=\frac{a(T_{d})}{a(T_{0})}\left(\frac{g_{S}(T_{d})}{g_{S}(T_{0})}\right)^{1/3}\;,

(26)

where
g_{S}

\frac{\rho_{r}(T_{0})}{\rho_{\rm SM}(T_{d})}=\frac{g_{\rho}(T_{0})}{g_{\rho}(T_{d})}\left(\frac{g_{S}(T_{d})}{g_{S}(T_{0})}\right)^{4/3}\left(\frac{a(T_{d})}{a(T_{0})}\right)^{4}\;.

(27)

Differently, the dark radiation density just gets diluted by the expansion of the universe:

\frac{\rho_{a}(T_{0})}{\rho_{a}(T_{d})}=\left(\frac{a(T_{d})}{a(T_{0})}\right)^{4}\;.

(28)

Then the ratio between the present energy densities is expressed in terms of the energy densities at the time of decay as

\frac{\rho_{a}(T_{0})}{\rho_{r}(T_{0})}=\frac{g_{\rho}(T_{d})}{g_{\rho}(T_{0})}\left(\frac{g_{S}(T_{0})}{g_{S}(T_{d})}\right)^{4/3}\frac{\rho_{a}(T_{d})}{\rho_{\rm SM}(T_{d})}\;.

(29)

Inserting the latter in Eq. (
24
) we get the extra effective number of neutrinos:

\Delta N_{\rm eff}=7.446\times\frac{g_{\rho}(T_{d})}{g_{\rho}(T_{0})}\left(\frac{g_{S}(T_{0})}{g_{S}(T_{d})}\right)^{4/3}\frac{\rho_{a}(T_{d})}{\rho_{\rm SM}(T_{d})}\;.

(30)

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we explored the interplay between Hawking radiation and superradiance in the context of Light Primordial Black Holes (LPBHs) and their implications for axion-like particles (ALPs) as contributors to Dark Radiation. We focused on the production of heavy scalar particles, known as
moduli
, via superradiance instabilities in Kerr black holes and their subsequent decay into ALPs.

We demonstrated that the combined effects of Hawking radiation and superradiance significantly amplify the production of ALPs, particularly when moduli fields are present. Using current constraints on the extra effective number of neutrino species,
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
, from Planck satellite observations, we derived updated bounds on the parameter space of this scenario. Our analysis showed that the inclusion of superradiance extends the range of
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
values, potentially shifting the allowed parameter space compared to scenarios without superradiance. Future experiment CMB-S4 will be able to prove values of
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
up to 0.06
Baumann et al. 2016
, thus hinting or further constraining present scenario. This underscores the importance of superradiance as a mechanism for enhancing the production of non-standard particles in the early universe.

In future work, we plan to further constrain the parameter space by studying the impact of ALPs on reionization or the diffuse X-ray background, resulting from ALPs reconversion in cosmic magnetic fields or decay into photons. Moreover, the study can be extended to other types of Dark Radiation, such as Gravitational Waves or Dark Photons
Fabbrichesi et al. 2020
.
