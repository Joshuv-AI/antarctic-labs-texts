Aoudad (Ammotragus lervia) as disease reservoirs for bighorn sheep (Ovis canadensis): experimental and epidemiological evidence
Authors: Logan F Thomas, Dallas Clontz, Chase M Nunez, Jack J Richison, Robert O Dittmar, J Hunter Reed, Sara R Wyckoff, Froylán Hernandez, Shawn S Gray, Raquel R Rech, Walter E Cook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the role of aoudad (Ammotragus lervia) as a maintenance host and reservoir for significant respiratory pathogens hindering bighorn sheep (Ovis canadensis) conservation; Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae and leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae. An experimental commingling trial and regional disease surveillance revealed significant interspecies transmission risks and epidemiological patterns. Experimentally, 80% of bighorn sheep exposed to aoudad inoculated with M. ovipneumoniae and leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae died of pneumonia under both indirect and direct contact conditions. Conversely, aoudad exhibited prolonged relatively asymptomatic shedding of M. ovipneumoniae without severe clinical outcomes. Surveillance of 351 free-ranging aoudad revealed 9.4% with M. ovipneumoniae DNA in nasal swabs and M. ovipneumoniae-specific antibodies in 55.8%. Aoudad diagnostic profiles were heterogeneous across populations, including variations in strain diversity and distribution. Shedding rates were higher among juveniles than adults. Aoudad can sustain and transmit M. ovipneumoniae under free-ranging and experimental conditions, presenting significant risks to bighorn sheep populations. Research on strategies to mitigate pathogen transmission, such as reducing shared water access and aoudad removal, is critical for predicting complex biological outcomes for multi-species management. Introduction

Diseases, ranging from non-infectious conditions like nutrient deficiencies and excessive stress to highly infectious diseases caused by microbes, have become increasingly important to wildlife conservation1–4. When disease-associated mortalities occur, they are often followed by density-dependent increases in survival and/or recruitment, contributing to traditional views that wildlife diseases act merely and/or predominantly as compensatory mortality5–8. However, a growing body of research acknowledges that the roles of wildlife diseases in population dynamics and conservation have been overlooked9,10. For example, native host–pathogen systems can drive population dynamics and species distributions at both immediate and evolutionary scales11–13. When non-indigenous hosts and their pathogens are introduced outside their native ranges, spillover to local native hosts can occur, potentially altering native host exposure patterns to an agent beyond direct spillover events14. Specific examples include nonindigenous species serving as alternative maintenance hosts of both native and introduced microbes and parasites15–18, altered behavior/habitat use causing an increase in contact/disease transmission rates19, and simply introducing novel pathogens to ecological communities in which native species are not coadapted20.

The bighorn sheep (Ovis canadensis; BHS) pneumonia complex is perhaps one of the most ecologically significant and well-studied disease systems illustrating the challenges of etiological studies investigating an introduced non-indigenous host–pathogen system with cascading effects for a native species21–26. Specifically, Old World Caprinae, particularly domestic sheep (Ovis aries; DS), brought to North America are regarded as the origin of the non-indigenous host–pathogen system affecting BHS25–27. BHS frequently experience all-age bronchopneumonia epizootics with variable mortality rates, followed by annual summer lamb mortality events (which can vary in duration and severity) due to contact with chronically infected (adult) individuals, ultimately causing recruitment issues26,28. While contacts with DS and domestic goats (Capra aegagrus hircus; DG) are typically the greatest concern, contacts with other BHS play a crucial role in transmission and epizootic initiation29–32. Additionally, mountain goats (Oreamnos americanus) have been identified as a transmission risk for BHS33.

The etiology of bronchopneumonia in BHS is complex, but Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae (Movi) is commonly carried by domestic Caprinae and frequently predisposes BHS to secondary bacterial colonization by either opportunistic commensals (e.g., Pasteurella multocida) or through failed mucociliary clearance of more pathogenic, leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae species (e.g., Mannheimia haemolytica)34–36. Many factors influence how BHS populations respond to respiratory pathogen exposures like Movi and Pasteurellaceae spp. These factors can include host genetics37,38, historical pathogen exposure39, pathogen genetics29,40, nutrition41, respiratory pathogen co-exposures42, demographic state28, and social dynamics43.

We recently discovered that aoudad/Barbary sheep (Ammotragus lervia; AD), a nonindigenous Caprid introduced to the southwestern United States in the 1950s, are capable of harboring and intraspecifically transmitting both Movi and Pasteurellaceae spp. important in BHS pneumonia etiology44. The increasing abundance and distribution of AD in Texas BHS habitat elevates the epidemiological concern that AD may serve as a maintenance and spillover host for pneumonic pathogens negatively affecting BHS45.

We present results from experimental commingling of AD inoculated with leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae spp. and/or Movi under both indirect and direct contact regimes with BHS. Additionally, we present two years of data from the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department’s (TPWD) big game disease surveillance program for AD and BHS. Our objectives included: (1) experimentally assessing the ability of AD to transmit Movi and leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae to BHS, and (2) identifying individual and population-level patterns of immunological exposure, shedding, and transmission of Movi among free-ranging Texas AD. Addressing these objectives is important for estimating the impact of free-ranging infected AD on BHS populations as well as understanding potential outcomes of commonly implemented practices like establishing artificial water sources, translocating BHS to areas with variable AD densities, and AD removal.

Results

Pathogen and antibody detection dynamics in experimental animals

Pre-study pathogen exposure

All AD and BHS tested positive for Bovine respiratory syncytial virus-specific antibodies upon capture and at acclimatization on day 39 (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S7). One Wash BHS tested positive for Bovine Herpes Virus and Bovine Viral Diarrhea Virus-specific antibodies upon capture. One Movi BHS and two control BHS tested positive for Parainfluenza-3-specific serum antibodies (all neutralizing immunoglobulins regardless of subtype) at capture and again upon acclimatization. Four Movi AD tested positive for M. haemolytica-specific antibodies upon capture. M. haemolytica antibody detection in BHS increased from zero in all groups to one Control BHS, one Movi BHS, and two Wash BHS between capture and acclimatization. All AD and BHS lacked both detectable Movi in nasal swabs and Movi-specific antibodies upon capture (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S7).

Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae

Although Movi was undetected in BHS and AD upon capture, two Wash BHS tested qPCR positive and one Movi BHS tested qPCR indeterminate after acclimatization (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S7). All BHS were seronegative before and after acclimatization. At validation, Movi DNA was detected by qPCR in 83% (5 of 6) and 100% (6 of 6) of Movi and Wash AD, respectively. At validation, 67% (4 of 6) of Movi AD and 60% (3 of 5) of Wash AD were seropositive. Upon necropsy, Movi DNA was detected in nasal swabs of all Control BHS, 80% of both Movi (n = 5) and Wash BHS (n = 5), 33% (1 indeterminate; n = 6) of Movi AD, and 67% of Wash AD (n = 6; 1 indeterminate result); reflecting greater Movi DNA positivity in BHS than AD (OR = 2.41, p = 0.038). Lung samples tested Movi qPCR positive in 80% (n = 5) of Control and Wash BHS, 40% of Movi BHS (n = 5; 1 indeterminate result), and 17% of Movi (n = 6; 1 indeterminate result) and Wash (n = 6; 2 indeterminate results) AD. Study wide Movi DNA detection rates did not differ by treatment (33 to 48%; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.77; pairwise ORs = -1.4 to 1.6) or species (AD 45.7%, BHS 34.7%; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.38; pairwise ORs = -1.23 to 0.69). Movi DNA was detected in the upper respiratory tract of AD (nasal swabs collected upon necropsy) up to 171 days post-inoculation with a median of 115 days post-inoculation.

Pasteurellaceae and leukotoxin-A

Pasteurellaceae were cultured more frequently in BHS (83%) than AD (43%) tonsil swabs (Fisher’s exact test, OR = 6.1, 95% CI = 2 to 20, p = 0.0003; Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S7). M. haemolytica was cultured from Wash BHS (38%) more frequently than Movi AD (5.6%; OR = 9.5, 95% CI = 0.95 to 494, p = 0.035). Conversely, Movi BHS (33%) had higher P. multocida culture rates than Wash BHS (12%; OR = Inf, 95% CI = 1.2 to Inf, p = 0.02). B. trehalosi was cultured more frequently from BHS (67%) than AD (23%; OR = 6.7, 95% CI = 2.3 to 22, p = 0.00011).

Pasteurellaceae detection rates (assessed across all cultured species of this family) did not change across study phases at the study-wide, species, or treatment group levels. As the study progressed, M. haemolytica detection frequency increased significantly in Wash BHS (acclimatization 0% versus necropsy phase 80% ; Fisher’s exact test, OR = Inf, 95% CI = 1.02 to Inf, p = 0.047), P. multocida detection frequency increased significantly in both Movi AD (capture 0% versus necropsy phase 83%; Fisher’s exact test, OR = Inf, 95% CI = 1.7 to Inf, p = 0.015) and Movi BHS (capture and acclimatization 0% versus necropsy phase 100%; Fisher’s exact test, OR = Inf, 95% CI = 2.3 to Inf, p = 0.008), and B. trehalosi detection decreased significantly in Wash BHS (capture 83%, acclimatization 100% versus necropsy phase 20%; Fisher’s exact test, OR = 0, 95% CI = 0 to 0.98, p = 0.047). Six of the eight DS nasal washes used to create inocula tested positive for leukotoxin-A gene. In experimental animals, the leukotoxin-A gene (assessed only upon necropsy) was only detected in Wash AD (n = 6, 17%) and Wash BHS (n = 5, 80%). Notably, all individuals testing positive for the leukotoxin-A gene detection also had M. haemolytica detected within these same respiratory tissue samples.

Experimental clinical scores, and mortalities

Prior to AD arrival and subsequent inoculation, two Movi (BHS 4; BHS 7), two Wash (BHS 9; BHS 10), and one control (BHS 17) experienced peri-anesthetic or peri-parturient mortality. Consequently, five Movi, five Wash, and five Control BHS were included in the analysis (Supplementary Table S11). Most (80%; 4 of 5) Wash BHS died of pneumonia during the water sharing period (prior to planned commingling) on days 55, 61, 71, and 72, while one death occurred on day 78 (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table S11). Consequently, no Wash BHS were available for the planned commingling with Wash AD. Five Movi BHS died of pneumonia and were euthanized during the commingling period on days 110 or 111. Two Movi AD died on days 69 and 72 from Haemonchus contortus infestation and peri-anesthetic mortality, respectively. The remaining AD were euthanized on days 155, 196, or 211 (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table S11). Between days 77 and 84, Control BHS began displaying clinical signs of pneumonia. Two events preceded this finding: 1) the vinyl barrier on the fenceline separating Wash BHS from Control BHS and preventing direct aerosol transmission was damaged on day 36 by the wash BHS group and could not be safely repaired, and 2) a Control BHS lamb was born on day 72 and escaped containment for up to three hours before returning to the Control BHS pen for the duration of the study. Control BHS group euthanasia occurred on days 110, 113, and 133. Mortality risk, which was assessed beginning at day 40, varied significantly by treatment (Mantel–Haenszel test, X2 = 35.3, df = 4, p = 4E-7; daily hazard rate 0.00609 to 0.0148 mortalities per animal-day) and species (Mantel–Haenszel test, X2 = 13.4, df = 1, p = 2E-4), with BHS (daily hazard rate = 0.00988 mortalities per animal-day) experiencing mortality earlier than AD (daily hazard rate = 0.0065 mortalities per animal-day). Wash BHS (daily hazard rate = 0.0148 mortalities per animal-day) experienced mortality earlier than all other treatments of either species (daily hazard rate 0.00609 to 0.00906 mortalities per animal-day; Mantel–Haenszel test, X2 > 10.76, df = 1, p < 0.046; Fig. 2a). Cumulative mean clinical scores (CMS) were higher in BHS (0.0206 to1.14) than AD (0 to 0.518) in days 50 through 112 (two-sample Wilcoxon test, K = 2 to 41.5; p = 0.0001 to 0.015; Fig. 2b). Wash BHS CMS (0.033 to 1.14) were significantly greater than those of Movi AD (0 to 0.11) between days 50 and 71 (two-sample Wilcoxon test, K = 0 to 0.5; p = 0.011 to 0.038; Fig. 2b). Movi BHS (0.19 to 0.74) had significantly greater CMS than Control BHS (0.063 to 0.268; two-sample Wilcoxon test, K = 0 to 2.0; p = 0.008 to 0.032), Movi AD (0.0 to 0.11; two-sample Wilcoxon test, K = 0 to 3.0; p = 0.004 to 0.03), and Wash AD (0.023 to 0.19 ;two-sample Wilcoxon test, K = 20; p = 0.02 to 0.03) between days 81 and 109. While time-to-achieve maximum CMS did not differ by species (Mantel–Haenszel test, X2 = 0.1, df = 1, p = 0.7), Movi BHS (median 110 days) achieved their maximum CMS significantly later in the study compared to Wash BHS (median 71 days) and Wash AD (median 66 days; Mantel–Haenszel test, X2 = 9.34, df = 1, p = 0.009).

Pathological findings

We categorized four diagnostic lesion patterns of pneumonia (Supplementary Table S11): (1) Subacute to chronic, lymphohistiocytic bronchointerstitial pneumonia with perivascular, bronchial, and bronchiolar-associated lymphoid tissue (BALT) hyperplasia (Supplementary Fig. S4; Supplementary Table S11); (2) acute suppurative bronchopneumonia (Fig. 3a,b); (3) superimposition of patterns one and two (Fig. 3e,f); (4) acute intravascular neutrophilia with congestion and multifocal fibrin deposition (Supplementary Table S11).

Control group, not directly exposed to experimentally inoculated aoudad (BHS16; e,f). Histologically, the main lesions were necrosuppurative bronchopneumonia (b, d) with intralesional bacteria (inset d, gram stain) and superimposed bronchointerstitial bronchopneumonia with bronchiolar-associated lymphoid hyperplasia (f).

Compared to AD , BHS were more likely to have acute lesions (AD 17%, BHS 100%; OR = 0, 95% CI = 0 to 0.17, p = 9.382E-5), necrosuppurative bronchopneumonia (AD 10%, BHS 80%; OR = 29.25, 95% CI = 2.58 to 168, p = 9.8E-4), and pleuritis (AD 0%, BHS 80%; OR = Inf, 95% CI = 4.75 to Inf, p = 0.00011), and less likely to have bronchointerstitial pneumonia (AD 70%, BHS 27%; OR = 0.17, 95% CI = 0.018 to 1.2, p = 0.04862) and lesion pattern one (AD 80%, BHS 0%; OR = 0, 95% CI = 0.0 to 0.17, p = 4.1E-5).

Experimental transmission inference and strain characterization

Of the 25 DS sampled for Movi shedding, 12 (48%) had qPCR positive nasal swabs (Supplementary Table S2). Multi-locus Sanger sequencing results suggest either six or seven distinct Movi strains were present in our nasal washings. Specifically, one pair of DS (DS9 and DS11) were separated by three nucleotide mismatches across all four loci. Sequence information for DS19 was only available at the IGS locus, which differed by fewer than five nucleotide base pairs from both DS22 (4 basepairs) and DS17 (2 basepairs). Consequently, we cannot confirm whether sequence information from the non-amplified loci for DS19 would have yielded an additional distinct Movi strain.

Considering the number of distinct Movi source strains and availability of amplified loci sequence data from our samples, we determined there were eight discernible intraspecific Movi transmission events possible among AD (Supplementary Tables S3-S6). Between inoculation and validation (32 days), all genetic distances between all AD and DS source strains representing possible transmission opportunities exceeded the five base pair threshold for direct strain assignment. However, available sequences for AD11 consistently aligned most closely with source strains from DS13 and DS22. Specifically, cumulative Levenshtein’s distance from AD 11 across all comparable loci was 54 for DS22 (IGS, LM, rpoB, and gyrB) and 26 for DS13 (IGS, LM, and gyrB).

Levenshtein’s distances between sequences obtained from BHS and AD exceeded the 5 basepair threshold for strain assignment. However, sequences from BHS8 had a cumulative Levenshtein’s distance of four across three loci (IGS, LM, gyrB) from AD6. Levenshtein’s distances among BHS were typically lower than or equivalent to those between BHS and AD pairs, particularly for the IGS and gyrB loci.

Epidemiology of Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae in free-ranging aoudad

In the Trans-Pecos region of Texas, 9.4% of free-ranging Texas AD (n = 351) tested qPCR positive (detection of Movi DNA) for Movi, and 55.8% had anti-Movi antibodies (seropositive) detected by cELISA (Table 1). Of the 351 AD, 40.7% were seronegative and tested Movi qPCR negative, 49.9% were seropositive and tested Movi qPCR negative, 3.4% were seronegative and tested Movi qPCR positive, and 6.0% were seropositive and tested Movi qPCR positive.

Movi DNA detection did not vary by year, sex, or cELISA inhibition level. Movi DNA detection rate varied significantly by surveillance location (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.035; Likelihood ratio test, X2 = 23.0, df = 11, p = 0.018; Table 1; Fig. 4). Specifically, Movi detection rate was significantly higher in the Del Norte range than the Big Bend range (OR = Inf, 95% CI = 1.35 to Inf, p = 0.011). Nine of the 12 surveillance locations had AD with Movi DNA detected. All else being equal, juvenile AD were 8.8 times more likely than adults to have Movi DNA in nasal swab samples (Fisher’s exact test, OR = 8.9, 95% CI = 3.9 to 20.6, p = 3.4E-8; Likelihood ratio test, X2 = 30.9, df = 1, p = 2.6E-8; Logistic regression, B = 2.19, SE = 0.39, z = 5.62, p = 1.9E-8; Supplementary Fig. S1). While Movi detection did not differ by serological status globally, age class interacted significantly with serological status (Likelihood ratio test, X2 = 4.6, df = 1, p = 0.033) such that Movi was more likely to be detected in seropositive adults (Logistic regression, B = 1.59, SE = 0.77, p = 0.04) and less likely to be detected in seropositive juveniles (Logistic regression, B = -1.88, SE = 0.95, p = 0.048; Supplementary Fig. S2).

Seropositivity was significantly higher in 2023 than in 2022 (Likelihood ratio test, X2 = 4.77, df = 1, p = 0.029; Logistic regression, B = 0.53, SE = 0.24, z = 2.18, p = 0.03; Fisher’s exact test, OR = 1.7, 95% CI = 1.0 to 2.8, p = 0.03) and varied significantly by location (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.001; Likelihood ratio test, X2 = 23, df = 11, p = 2.3E-9). Seropositivity rates differed significantly across locations, with odds ratios ranging from 5.9 to 28.5 and p-values between 1.3E-6 and 0.0015, indicating lower odds of seropositivity in Hueco and Big Bend ranges compared to Chinati, Davis, EMWMA, Glass, Quitman, and Wylie. While serological status differed by neither age class nor sex alone, these variables interacted significantly to influence seropositivity (Likelihood ratio test: X2 = 8.5, df = 1, p = 0.035). Specifically, adult AD exhibited similar seropositivity rates by sex, whereas juvenile males were significantly more likely to be seropositive than juvenile females (Logistic regression: B = 1.75, SE = 0.61, z = 2.85, p = 0.0044; Supplementary Fig. S3).

We observed four Movi diagnostic categories: cELISA negative and qPCR negative, cELISA negative and qPCR positive, cELISA positive and qPCR positive, and cELISA positive and qPCR negative (Fig. 4a). The proportion of animals in each diagnostic category varied considerably across locations (Fisher’s test, p = 0.0005) and age classes (Fisher’s test, p = 2.8E-8; Fig. 4b). Adults were significantly more likely than juveniles to be cELISA positive and qPCR negative (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.00 to 3.37, p = 0.048), whereas juveniles were more likely than adults to be both cELISA positive and qPCR positive (OR = 4.00, 95% CI: 1.40 to 10.91, p = 0.0043) and cELISA negative and qPCR positive (OR = 27.8, 95% CI: 5.7 to 267.0, p < 0.000001). Among juveniles, males were more likely to be cELISA positive and qPCR positive while females were more likely to be cELISA negative and qPCR negative (OR = 12.45, 95% CI = 1.24 to 655, p = 0.015).

Regarding Movi exposure history and population-level responses of AD, two populations (Big Bend and Hueco ranges) were in the pre-epizootic phase, seven were in the epizootic phase (Chalk, Chinati, Davis, Del Norte, Elephant Exterior, Quitman, and Wylie ranges), two were in the enzootic phase (Eagle and Glass), and one was in the post-epizootic phase (Santiago range). All locations assigned epizootic status (n = 7) met epizootic criteria based on patterns of relative per-capita infection hazard ratio (FOIR > 1.5) and minimum seroprevalence thresholds for each age class (Sjuvenile > 0.2 and Sadult > 0.2). While the Chalk, Davis, Elephant exterior, Quitman, and Wylie ranges met epizootic criteria based on age-specific seroprevalence differences between juveniles and adults (ΔS > 0.0; juvenile serology driven classification; Supplementary Tables S9 and S10; Supplementary Fig. S6), the Chinati and Del Norte ranges were assigned epizootic based on observed patterns of the ratio of qPCR positive, seronegative to qPCR negative, seropositive juveniles (AJI > 1.0; juvenile qPCR driven classification; Supplementary Tables S9 and S10; Supplementary Fig. S6). Both locations assigned enzootic met the epidemiological phase criteria based on observed patterns of seroprevalence (Sjuvenile > 0.25 and Sadult > 0.25), AJI ≤ 1.0, and ΔS ≤ 0.0, but not FOIR ≤ 1.0. However, model-based assumptions about the mean pathogen exposure window for adults and juveniles (based on age in years) altered FOIR estimates for the Eagle and Glass ranges (Supplementary Dataset 1). Specifically, more extreme differences between adult and juvenile exposure windows increased FOIR. All pre-epizootic (FOIR < 1.0, Sjuvenile = 0.0, Sadult < 0.25, and AJI = 0.0) criteria were met by both the Big Bend and Hueco ranges. Finally, all post-epizootic criteria (FOIR < 1.0, Sjuvenile = 0.0, Sadult > 0.50, and AJI = 0.0) were met by the Santiago range. The directionality of FOIR estimates (whether above or below 1.0) for all locations assigned pre-epizootic, epizootic, and post-epizootic epidemiological phases were unaltered by differences in juvenile and adult exposure windows (ages in years).

Strain characterization using Levenshtein’s distances

Levenshtein’s distances of Movi sequences obtained from free-ranging AD (n = 12) revealed at least six distinct Movi strains (Supplementary Table S8). Of the twelve AD samples subjected to multi-locus Sanger sequencing, three had all four loci amplify, five had three loci amplify, and the remaining four AD had two or fewer loci amplify. Sequence information was only available from select AD in the Elephant Mountain Exterior, Del Norte, Davis, and Wylie Mountain ranges. Two AD in the Elephant Mountain Exterior (n = 2; haplotype AAAA; Supplementary Fig. S5) and two AD in the Del Norte range (n = 2; haplotype AABB/AUBB) each shared a Movi strain. Three distinct strains were identified in AD from the Wylie range (n = 4; haplotypes ABBB, ABBC, and BBBC), and one of these strains (haplotype BBBC) was shared with a single AD in the neighboring Davis mountain range. The remaining Davis Mountains AD for which sequence data was available had at least one additional unique strain (haplotype CBCC). The diversity and geographical relationships between Movi strains in AD varied across populations. The adjacent Elephant Mountain Exterior and Del Norte ranges did not appear to share a Movi strain. However, there were both shared (BBBC) and unique strains (ABBB; CBCC) among the Wylie and Davis ranges (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Sequences for SD25 were sufficient to assign the CBCC haplotype, but cumulative Levenshtein’s distance (six) across all four loci was above the threshold (five) for strain distinction. Sequences for SD31 could not be accurately assigned a haplotype due to incomplete sequence amplification. Taken together, however, all three Sierra Diablo range BHS (Supplementary Fig. S5) had a Levenshtein’s distance of zero between the “C” allele identifier at the IGS locus, which was unique to free-ranging AD in the Davis range. Both SD25 (83.97% inhibition) and SD31 (58% inhibition) Sierra Diablo BHS also tested seropositive upon Movi cELISA.

Discussion

Limitations and considerations

The development of respiratory disease in Control BHS suggests that direct contact with AD was not necessary for Control BHS to develop respiratory disease. However, this was likely due to vinyl barrier disruption by wash BHS, which may have allowed long-distance aerosol transmission across the pen facility, as observed in previous studies46,47. When distress is noted as a driver of respiratory disease development in ungulates, it is generally acute48–50 (4–14 days in these examples). If respiratory disease primarily resulted from stress, we would have expected it to develop early in the acclimatization period, before day 40 (AD arrival). Additionally, there was a temporal association between respiratory clinical signs, mortality, and contact opportunities with AD for both Movi and Wash BHS groups. While the Control BHS group did not serve as a valid control throughout the study, there remains strong support implicating AD contacts as the most plausible exposure driving respiratory disease in these captive BHS. The infection of Control BHS most likely resulted from the high transmissibility of Movi among BHS via an extended aerosol route47,51.

After the acclimatization period, two wash BHS nasal swabs tested qPCR positive for Movi, and one Movi BHS swab tested indeterminate on day 39. However, all BHS were seronegative for Movi on both day zero and day 39, suggesting that no animals had been previously exposed to infectious levels of the agent. Given the reported assay sensitivity in BHS (90.7%), the probability of failing to detect Movi antibodies at both capture and acclimatization in each BHS was 0.87%, even if only one BHS had been truly exposed prior to study initiation. For reasons yet to be discovered, it is thought some BHS with known/ suspected exposure to Movi will not develop detectable humoral responses. Additionally, antibody levels may wax and wane with repeated exposures52. While it is possible that some BHS were previously exposed to Movi, the highest antibody inhibition in BHS upon capture and acclimatization were 5.9% and 16.6%, respectively; well below minimum diagnostic cutoff value for indeterminate samples (40%), suggesting animals had not been previously exposed with waning titers. Finally, the source population of BHS in our study was Elephant Mountain Wildlife Management Area, which has yielded no Movi detections by serology or qPCR since consistent surveillance began in 2014. This herd’s intensive monitoring with a lack of observed Movi-induced respiratory disease epizootic supports a lack of true pre-study Movi exposure for BHS utilized in our experiment. We recognize that methods to detect Movi and Pasteurellaceae (qPCR and culture) may have produced imperfect and/or temporally variable detection rates, especially for longitudinal pathogen assessments. Additionally, the low sample size of experimental animals may limit the interpretive resolution these results can reasonably support.

We recognize that the decision to not administer tranquilizing drugs to Wash AD may have increased stress patterns relative to Movi AD, possibly leading to differential pathogen shedding between AD groups regardless of the inoculation protocol experienced. However, we expect the use of AD previously adapted to captivity prior to study initiation, the selective use of younger individuals, and the relatively even ages of animals across AD treatments decreased overall stress and acted to mitigate the effects of captivity on experimental animals53. We did not consistently obtain complete Sanger sequence reads for all four loci utilized. Due to the high strain variability of Movi54 and documented detection issues this causes for similar Mycoplasma spp.55, this may have been caused by poor strain-specific primer affinity and a subsequent lack of sufficient PCR product available for Sanger MLST sequencing, and/or low bacterial loads in samples. Additionally, each AD was inoculated with multiple Movi strains to maximize colonization opportunities, which significantly reduced our ability to quantify inter- and intraspecific Movi transmission events. Finally, we did not attempt to isolate Movi from shared water sources to confirm its potential role in transmission. However, it is important to emphasize that vinyl barriers and pen designs were intended to prevent direct aerosol transmission. Ultimately, we cannot verify whether water served as a transmission fomite nor rule out other forms of indirect transmission, such as aerosol contamination of vinyl barriers or other structures.

Our evaluation of evidence for various epidemiological phases required assumptions of similar post-exposure development and persistence of Movi-specific antibodies across AD ages, sexes, and locations. Additionally, the coarse age classification (juvenile and adult) limited our ability to detect temporal infection hazard (FOI) changes among adult AD to distinguish between enzootic transmission and multiple epizootics occurring during/shortly prior to the surveillance periods. For populations classified as enzootic, the FOIR was sensitive to differences in exposure periods between age classes, suggesting our ability to distinguish enzootic from epizootic transmission dynamics is explicitly limited by the population age structure. Specifically, metrics and samples needed to estimate the age of AD in years were not collected/available due to personnel safety and logistical constraints.

Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae transmission, mechanisms, and epidemiology

Aoudad can transmit Movi (and leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae) to BHS under experimental contacts, as well as under free-ranging conditions. In both the Movi and Wash BHS groups, respiratory clinical signs clearly developed after ten days of sharing water (day 50) with AD. However, it is unknown to what degree transmission occurred via water sharing and/or aerosol transmission through pen barriers. While we cannot unequivocally attribute the clinical progression in BHS to Movi alone, the observed clinical signs and pathological findings are commonly associated with the agent in BHS23,25,26,29,34,52,56,57. The detection of Movi in BHS supports interspecific transmission, and in the case of Wash BHS, direct contacts with AD were not necessary for transmission. The early progression of clinical signs in BHS suggests that transmission likely occurred during the water-sharing period. Recent work supports shared water sources as a transmission mechanism for Movi among BHS51. However, the survival of many Mycoplasma spp. in water is thought to be no more than two hours51,58, demonstrating an increased need to further assess shared water as a potential pathogen transmission mechanism.

We acknowledge that widespread Movi infection in experimental BHS could be explained by a single transmission event from AD followed by multiple transmission events among BHS. Regardless of the dominant transmission mechanism(s) in our study, direct interspecific interactions appear unnecessary for the transmission of Movi or leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae from AD to BHS.

Although sharing a Movi strain between AD and BHS suggests free-ranging interspecific transmission, we cannot definitively ascertain the directionality of transmission. However, the greater number of AD relative to BHS in Texas59, the higher apparent Movi strain diversity, and the lack of clinical signs in experimental and free-ranging systems all suggest that AD may be more likely to sustain Movi transmission than BHS. Importantly, the harboring of multiple Movi strains within a single AD population may reflect signatures of host–pathogen coevolution for reservoir hosts like DS60. AD in the present study also had the capacity to harbor Movi DNA in the upper respiratory tract for extended periods, suggesting they could harbor and transmit Movi chronically. It is not currently known how long AD can harbor Movi in the upper respiratory tract for, as our experiment did not continue until all AD had negative nasal swabs. Given the importance of BHS chronic Movi carriers in driving transmission dynamics at the population level61,62, formally assessing AD Movi shedding durations and associated tendencies is crucial for evaluating the risk they pose to BHS.

While our sequencing results only suggested one of eight discernible intraspecific transmission events may have occurred among experimental AD, we emphasize that the strain composition of our inocula greatly reduced our resolution to detect intraspecific transmission events. If our observations reflect true transmission, then this suspected transmission event occurred after only 32 days of intragroup contact among AD. We previously found serological evidence of intraspecific Movi transmission from Movi-inoculated to uninoculated control AD44.

Further support for AD as a maintenance reservoir with high intraspecific Movi transmission rates comes from our epidemiological findings. Among Movi-exposed AD populations, up to 28% (5 of 18) of individuals in the Del Norte range had Movi DNA present in nasal swab samples. Additionally, Movi seropositivity can be as high as 82% (24 of 29) in the Elephant Mountain Exterior range. We found that AD can harbor Movi DNA for between 115 days (four of seven AD euthanized on day 155) to 171 days (one of two AD euthanized on day 211) after experimental exposure. While we could not determine whether these animals could transmit an infectious dose at these times, these findings suggest AD can serve as chronic Movi carriers, similar to some individual BHS44. While the observed Movi strain diversity in AD was far lower than that reported for DS flocks35, the presence of multiple Movi strains within and across free-ranging AD populations suggests that this species may threaten BHS populations with multiple Movi strains39.

Our epidemiological findings suggest that juvenile AD were more likely to harbor Movi DNA, even with similar levels of seropositivity. Several non-exclusive potential explanations for this exist. It is possible that Movi DNA detection probability in AD is related to age-specific pathogen load and subsequently eliminating (clearing) the agent from the upper respiratory tract63. This elimination may vary by age due to time since exposure23,64, differences in immunological responses, and the suitability of the upper respiratory tract for Movi54. The wide variation in the effect size (OR CI = 3.9–20.6) of age class on Movi detection makes interpreting the epidemiological mechanisms and potential transmission dynamics in AD challenging. This varied estimate may have stemmed from a low sampling rate of juveniles compared to adults (17.4% versus 82.6% of total samples, respectively). Consequently, a higher proportion of juvenile samples may allow a more precise assessment of how Movi epidemiology varies by age class by narrowing the effect size estimate. It is unknown whether there was sampling bias based on age in this study, but previous studies have found 30–42% of AD in monitored populations were juveniles65. Little is known about the diagnostic accuracy of Movi qPCR and cELISA in AD, or how host–pathogen relationships vary by time since exposure, age, sex, season, or environment; further, the Movi cELISA is not validated in AD. However, our conceptual and qualitative assessment of AD diagnostics reveals considerable heterogeneity in overall seropositivity and Movi DNA detection probability across locations and age classes suggesting that the qPCR and cELISA results reflect meaningful epidemiological patterns in this species. Responses of AD populations to Movi vary and include: Low seropositivity with little apparent ongoing transmission (Movi qPCR), marked Movi DNA detection in up to 20–30% of individuals, or high seropositivity with little apparent Movi DNA detection. One possible explanation for these variations is the duration of time each AD population has experienced since initial exposure to Movi. Alternatively, AD populations in the Trans-Pecos region of Texas may be in various stages of exposure to secondary Movi strains. Indeed, the relatively high prevalence of Movi qPCR-positive, seronegative juvenile AD in the Chinati and Del Norte ranges suggests these were potentially early epizootics for which seroconversion had not yet reflected cumulative exposure at the time of disease surveillance. Interestingly, the juvenile AD in the Chalk range were ubiquitously Movi seropositive without any Movi qPCR-positive AD detected. This may suggest the Chalk range experienced a more prolonged epizootic at the time of disease surveillance. While we recognize multiple potential explanations for our epidemiological categorizations may exist, we present them here as a baseline for future research on AD respiratory disease ecology and to provide wildlife managers with a basis for formal testing as AD disease surveillance continues. Given recent findings that BHS lambs born to chronic Movi carriers may be central to rapid pathogen transmission61, juvenile free-ranging AD may similarly drive observed epidemiological dynamics.

Clinical manifestations

As in previous studies exposing BHS to DS and DG, BHS developed fatal respiratory disease24,29,66 (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), the progression and severity of which were affected by co-exposure status of Pasteurellaceae34,67,68. In our study, Movi BHS exposed to AD inoculated with only Movi exhibited a more prolonged respiratory disease versus wash BHS exposed (indirectly) to AD inoculated with Movi plus leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae. Although Movi BHS had higher peak CMS than Wash BHS, this was primarily due to their more prolonged pathogenesis. That is, Wash BHS experienced a shorter apparent incubation period and mortality window.

All BHS experienced more severe and acute disease than AD, suggesting that AD directly infected via unnatural routes of exposure (inoculation) are less susceptible to developing respiratory disease than naturally exposed BHS. Importantly, the route of infection has been noted as an important factor driving pathobiological response to Movi infection69. While we did not formally assess Movi dose in the inocula26, we expect AD received a higher dose than those experienced by BHS through natural transmission. It should be noted that BHS were wild-caught, while AD were acclimated to captivity. While AD were not allowed an acclimatization period prior to inoculation, we cannot determine the full effects of stress and acclimatization on species-specific susceptibility to Movi and/or leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae. The extensive exposure of free-ranging AD across multiple populations, in addition to the mild clinical signs observed here, suggest AD are unlikely to experience clinical disease or population-level declines due to Movi. Indeed, AD in this study were less likely to develop grossly apparent respiratory lesions, and more likely to develop chronic interstitial pneumonia. This suggests that AD may be more likely than BHS to recover from resulting pathologies after exposure to Movi.

The increasing frequency of Pasteurellaceae, P. multocida most notably, in tonsil swabs in our experimental animals suggests that Movi dysregulated the upper respiratory tract of AD and BHS36,63,68. This increase in detection is presumably due to Movi disruption of the mucociliary apparatus, allowing subsequent bacterial co-infection.

For Wash BHS, M. haemolytica detection increased throughout the study, suggesting that indirect contacts with wash AD increased their exposure to M. haemolytica and permitted lower respiratory tract colonization due to co-infection with Movi34,68. Overall, pathogen detection patterns of both Movi and Pasteurellaceae support previously described etiologic patterns in Caprinae.

Future conservation and research efforts

Our experimental and epidemiological efforts demonstrate that: (1) AD are a potential (experimental results) and realized (epidemiological surveillance) reservoir that can transmit Movi to BHS with only indirect contacts and readily transmit the agent to other AD, (2) the clinical effects and associated mortality risk of Movi exposure differ between AD and BHS and by co-exposure status to leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae, (3) Post-exposure Movi DNA detection and Movi-specific antibody detection in AD can be prolonged, but this varies interindividually and by age class, (4) Movi DNA detection and seroprevalence in free-ranging AD varies spatially, and 5) free-ranging AD populations in Texas may have different (temporal) patterns of exposure and/or longitudinal responses to Movi exposure.

Future efforts directed at BHS conservation in areas with AD should focus on determining risk factors for interspecific transmission, including the potential role of shared water sources. Contact patterns between AD and BHS are not fully understood but are of utmost importance for determining where and when BHS may be at the greatest risk of pathogen exposure. Additionally, tailoring disease surveillance efforts to identify spatiotemporal trends of AD Movi exposure and carriage in free-ranging populations is necessary to assess risks to BHS. Efforts should also focus on identifying the sources and strain composition(s) of Movi in AD and any surrounding DS and DG flocks (though few operations exist). The carriage of multiple Movi strains within and across different AD populations highlights the need for strain typing because both short-term clinical presentations as well as population level responses of BHS are mediated by historical strain exposures39. Taken together, disease surveillance and fundamental epidemiological research are needed to fully assess how, where, and when AD may pose a disease threat to BHS. This is especially crucial because current disease mitigation efforts across much of the BHS range rely on geographic separation from DS and DG flocks, an option impractical and improbable for free-ranging AD given their widespread distribution and economic value to rural communities. Lethal removal is the primary current tool used to mitigate disease risk from AD. Understanding whether AD removal is followed by compensatory increases in AD reproductive parameters that decrease the average AD age70 may be necessary to fully assess pre- and post-removal transmission likelihood alongside the benefits BHS experience from reduced AD density and interspecific competition71. The variable success of selective removal of persistent Movi carriers for managing BHS herds72 further highlights the need to understand Movi dynamics in AD to predict and achieve BHS disease risk mitigation targets.

Methods

Experimental design and animal housing

Study activities were approved by the Texas A&M AgriLife Animal Care and Use Committee- IACUC permit number 2020-038A. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant state and federal regulations, international guidelines, and best veterinary practices for anesthesia and euthanasia were employed. Methodological descriptions and results presentation comply with the ARRIVE 2.0 Essential 10 Guidelines. We captured 20 (10 female, 10 male; approximately 10 months to 4 years old; Supplementary Table S1) BHS from Elephant Mountain Wildlife Management Area, Texas (30°02′03.2" N 103°33′57.6″ W) and transported them to the study site at Mason Mountain Wildlife Management Area, Texas (30°49′18.8″N 99°13′20.0″W).

Upon capture (day 0; Fig. 5), BHS were sampled for pathogen exposure, ear-tagged, and assigned to one of three treatments: indirect and direct contact with AD experimentally inoculated with isolated DS-origin Movi (Movi BHS, n = 5), indirect and direct contact with AD experimentally inoculated with DS-origin Movi and Pasteurellaceae (Wash BHS, n = 5), and a control group with no intergroup contacts (control BHS, n = 5). Each animal (AD or BHS) represents an experimental unit. Sample sizes were selected based on a balance of facility capacity, spatial requirements for animal welfare, and consideration of the native population from which the BHS were captured75. True randomization of experimental animals was not achieved, though an unbiased Texas Parks and Wildlife Department employee obtained and distributed animals from the trailer to treatment-specific facility pens. However, because all animals were fit with ear tags upon capture, and were retained throughout the experiment, research personnel were privy to animal allocation immediately. Authors were aware of individuals’ treatment group memberships prior to sample processing and data analyses.

Experimental activity timeline. Timeline depicts what biological samples were collected at each time-point, the corresponding species, and the assays used to generate data from the biological samples. Created in Biorender.com by Thomas, LF.

BHS were allowed 40 days to acclimatize to captivity (acclimatization period; Day 0 to Day 40; Fig. 5). On day 39, BHS were captured and sampled for pathogen exposure. On day 40, 12 captive-reared AD (eight female, four male; estimated one to two years old; Supplementary Table S1) were trailered to the study site, physically restrained, sampled for pathogen exposure, ear-tagged with unique marks, and randomly assigned to one of two treatments: inoculation with DS-origin Movi (Movi AD), or inoculation with DS-origin Movi and Pasteurellaceae (Wash AD).

Inoculated AD were placed in pens adjacent to the corresponding BHS treatment group (Fig. 6a). Fenceline borders separating AD and BHS were modified with vinyl (1.83 m tall, 0.15 mm thick) across the length of the shared pen border to reduce the probability of aerosol pathogen transmission, prevent direct contacts, and mitigate psychological distress (Fig. 6b). Additionally, the pen borders separating AD and BHS were modified to allow a water trough to bisect each pen only during select periods of the study. Movi and Wash AD were allowed to share a common water source with their respective BHS groups from days 40 to 75 (water sharing period; Fig. 5; Fig. 6a). On day 72, AD were captured and sampled to assess apparent Movi colonization. Shared water sources were removed on day 76, and animals were observed on days 76 to 95 (post-water observation period). On day 95, Movi AD were placed in direct contact with Movi BHS for 16 days; days 95–111. All BHS were removed from the study by either mortality or euthanasia by day 155; mortalities occurring on or before day 46 were excluded from the study. Seven of the ten available AD were euthanized on day 155, one was euthanized on day 168, and two were euthanized on day 211.

We observed and recorded signs of disease progression in all available animals throughout the entire 211-day study period. Body condition was visually estimated daily and with manual palpation at each capture event. We used a daily clinical scoring system to evaluate health and animal welfare throughout the study29. A score of > 5 for 5 consecutive days at any point during the 211-day study period necessitated euthanasia. Animals had unlimited access to native vegetation, coastal fescue hay, alfalfa hay, livestock mineral blocks, lactating sheep and goat pellets, and water throughout the study.

Animal restraint and handling

We initially procured BHS via helicopter netgun and physically restrained them with leg hobbles and blindfolds. Azaperone (immediately; 15 mg regardless of mass, 0.30–0.55 mg/kg based on estimated mass) and sustained release haloperidol (30 to 45 min after capture; 0.2–0.7 mg/kg based on estimated mass; ZooPharm, Laramie, Wyoming 82070, USA) were administered intramuscularly to all BHS and Movi AD upon initial capture to alleviate stress and facilitate acclimatization73. All animals were subcutaneously administered 50 mg (1.0–1.83 mg/kg based on estimated mass) of eprinomectin upon initial capture (50 mg/mL; LongRange; Boehringer Ingelheim Animal Health, Duluth, GA 30096, USA). After initial animal procurement, all animal captures were conducted via chemical immobilization following previously described methods74.

Inoculum preparation and administration

We used previous findings on the genetic structure and prevalence of Movi in DS to guide our decision and inoculation protocols. Approximately 60% of DS carry and shed Movi in the upper respiratory tract, 77% of those DS carrying Movi harbor a unique strain, and generally no more than three DS in a given flock will carry the same strain35.

On day 21, we collected nasal swabs from 25 DS and submitted samples to the Washington Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory for the detection of Movi by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). We selected eight Movi-positive DS for nasal washing based on the qPCR cycle threshold value from each DS nasal swab (Fig. 7a). Prior to AD arrival and subsequent sampling on day 40 (after sampling BHS on day 39), inocula were obtained by irrigating the nares of eight Movi-positive DS with sterile phosphate-buffered saline, yielding DS nasal washes (Fig. 7b). Domestic sheep were housed in a private flock in Mason County, Texas and were trailered to the study site at Mason Mountain Wildlife Management Area for nasal washing activities, which took place approximately 300 m from the pen facilities. All personal protective equipment and footwear used in DS nasal washing activities were discarded and replaced with new equipment before sampling and inoculating AD on day 40.

Detection of pathogens and pathogen exposure in experimental animals

Blood was collected via jugular venipuncture and placed into 3 mL EDTA vacutainer tubes and 5 mL vacutainer tubes without additive, both of which were held at approximately 4 ℃. A smear from whole blood was made within three hours of collection and, after allowing to clot for at least one hour (but no more than three hours), blood tubes without additive were centrifuged for 15 min at 1500g to separate serum. Blood smears were subjected to relative leukocyte quantification and serum was frozen at -80 ℃ then submitted to 1) detect antibodies to Mannheimia haemolytica, Bovine Herpesvirus type 1, Bovine Viral Diarrhea types 1a/1b/2, Parainfluenza 3 virus, and Bovine Respiratory Syncytial Virus by virus neutralization at the Texas Veterinary Medical Diagnostic Laboratory (TVMDL) and 2) detect antibodies to Movi by competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (cELISA) at the Washington Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory (WADDL; proprietary protocol). Rayon-flocked swabs (Puritan Medical Products, Guilford, Maine, USA 04443) were collected from the nares (nasal swab), placed in two mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB; 0.03 g/mL tryptic soy broth powder, 15% glycerol, 85% deionized water), held at approximately 4 ℃, frozen at -80 ℃ within three hours of collection, and submitted to WADDL for the detection of Movi by quantitative polymerase chain reaction within 90 days of collection75. To detect Pasteurellaceae of the upper respiratory tract, palatine tonsil swab samples were collected with sterile rayon-flocked swabs and placed in Ames gel media with charcoal (Becton Dickson, Franklin Lakes, NJ 07417, USA) and cultured under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions on cation adjusted Mueller–Hinton agar for 24 to 48 h with speciation by matrix assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry at TVMDL. Tonsil swab samples were held at approximately 4 ℃ for no more than two days prior to TVMDL submission and analysis.

Upon euthanasia via intravenous pentobarbital sodium (85.8 mg/kg; 390 mg/mL; Vortech Pharmaceuticals, Dearborn, MI 48126, USA) or natural mortality, necropsies were performed. Lesions in the lungs were assessed through a combination of written records and photographs. Representative samples of the lungs were collected in 10% buffered formalin and routinely processed for histological examination and analyzed by a board-certified anatomic pathologist. If gross lesions were present, tissues were collected at margin of apparently diseased and healthy tissues. Otherwise, sections from both the cranioventral and dorsocaudal lungs were collected. Samples for histology were not available from AD8 and AD10. Gross and histologic findings were categorized according to respiratory lesion pattern scheme previously described for pathological assessment of AD inoculated with Movi with and without leukotoxigenic Pasteurellaceae44. This approach focused on lesions indicative of bronchopneumonia, pleuritis, bronchiolar-associated lymphoid tissue (BALT) hyperplasia, and oat cells.

Lung samples were also collected for the detection of pathogens and were selected using the same criteria as samples selected for histological examination. However, these samples were placed in 2 mL of TSB and frozen at -80 ℃ and submitted to WADDL for the detection of Movi by qPCR and Pasteurellaceae by culture on both Columbia blood agar (5%) under 5% carbon dioxide conditions and MacConkey agar with no gas modifications at 35 °C both for 24 to 72 h. Any potentially leukotoxigenic Pasteurellacea species isolated from tissues were subjected to leukotoxin-A PCR at WADDL (proprietary protocol).

Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae sequencing and experimental transmission analyses.

All DS washes and AD and BHS nasal swabs collected on or after day 72 (validation) that tested qPCR-positive for Movi were subjected to multi-locus Sanger sequencing (Figs. 5, 7d) of the IGS, LM, gyrB, and rpoB loci at the Washington Animal Disease Diagnostic Lab to characterize strains present in DS-source inocula and to assess potential transmission events (Supplementary Tables S2-S6). Specifically, we utilized locus-specific Levenshtein’s genetic distance between sequences aligned with using the ClustalOmega algorithm. We used previous knowledge on the genetic structure of Movi in DS to set our strain assignment threshold of 5 basepairs35. Because AD were intentionally exposed to multiple Movi strains, our assessment of intraspecific Movi transmission among AD was based on genetic distances between sequences obtained after the validation phase with those of source strains assessed directly from DS nasal washes.

Epidemiology of Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae in free-ranging Texas aoudad

We compiled disease surveillance data from free-ranging AD (n = 351). This data includes sample collection year, mountain range area (location), species, age, sex, Movi serological exposure status as determined by cELISA performed by WADDL, and Movi nasal swab detection status as determined by qPCR at WADDL. For our analyses, we considered indeterminate results for both cELISA and qPCR as positives. This decision was made based upon: 1) ease of results interpretation (eliminating an indeterminate category from models and comparisons), 2) a lack of validation for either assay for AD, and 3) previous findings that AD with known exposure to Movi can fluctuate between cELISA and qPCR “positive” and “indeterminate” statuses within relatively short timeframes44.

Statistical analyses

All statistics were performed with program R, version 4.3.2. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed with a significance level of alpha < 0.05.

Captive experiment analyses

We compared the detection frequencies of Movi, Pasteurellaceae species, Movi specific antibodies, and antibodies to bovine respiratory pathogens using Fisher’s exact test with Holm’s adjustments focusing on study wide and longitudinal differences between treatment groups and species. The CMS were longitudinally compared between species and treatment groups throughout the study from day 40 through 155 using the unpaired two-sample Wilcoxon test with Holm’s adjustments. Differences in survival and time-to-clinical score onset between treatment groups were assessed using a fixed-design two arm sample size calculation for time-to-event data using nSurvival in the gsDesign package in program R76 . Additionally, median time to natural mortality or necessary euthanasia and maximum cumulative mean clinical score were compared across treatment groups and species with survival analysis via the log-rank/Mantel–Haenszel test77. Lesion patterns were compared between species and treatment groups using Fisher’s exact test.

Epidemiological analyses

We compared the detection frequencies of Movi, and Movi specific antibodies using Fisher’s exact test with Holm’s adjustments to assess differences based on year, age, sex, and location. Epidemiological diagnostic categorizations were mutually exclusive and assessed using a Chi-square test for homogeneity to compare locations, ages, and years.

These diagnostic categories were used to identify and describe Movi transmission and exposure metrics consistent with distinct epidemiological phases under an age-specific serocatalytic model. Diagnostic errors, seroconversion rates, and Movi shedding duration were assumed to be similar across locations and sampling periods to allow inferential comparisons. We estimate force of infection (λadult and λjuvenile, index of average per-capita hazard of infection) using the “classic” constant time-invariant serocatalytic model to assess relative differences in cumulative infection hazard between age classes at each location78,79. The basic assumption for the use of this model to evaluate epidemiological patterns is that seroprevalence patterns reflecting per-capita infection hazard (λ) are elevated in the juvenile age class in the case of epizootics, while λ is equivalent between age classes during under enzootic transmission. We extend our epidemiological evaluation to include Movi qPCR data, primarily to assess support for epizootics not yet reflected by the serocatalytic model (Movi serological data only) due to potential lag times in seroconversion. We evaluated support for an epidemiological phase associated with each location by considering 1) Movi shedding prevalence (indicated by qPCR nasal swabs), 2) the ratio of λjuvenile to λadult (FOIR), 3), the difference between juvenile and adult Movi seroprevalence (ΔS; juvenile seroprevalence minus adult seroprevalence), 4) age-specific Movi seroprevalence, and 5) the ratio of seronegative, qPCR positive to seropositive, qPCR negative (acute juvenile index; AJI). Specifically, the proposed epidemiological phases have the following characteristics under the assumptions of the constant serocatalytic model and/or available Movi qPCR data: 1) pre-epizootic populations will have an FOIR < 1.0, juvenile Movi seroprevalence = 0 (ΔS inherently ≤ 0; AJI undefined or 0), and adult seroprevalence < 0.25, 2) epizootic populations will have juvenile and adult Movi seroprevalence > 0.2 combined with either AJI > 1.0 (applicable to distinguish recent epizootics not reflected with age-specific serology) or FOIR > 1.5 and ΔS > 0, 3) enzootic populations will have adult and juvenile Movi seroprevalence ≥ 0.25, AJI ≤ 1.0 and a lack of age-specific serological exposure differences indicated by either FOIR ≤ 1.0 or ΔS ≤ 0, and 4) post-epizootic populations will have FOIR < 1.0, no juveniles seropositive for Movi (ΔS inherently ≤ 0; AJI undefined or 0), and adult Movi seroprevalence > 0.5; Supplementary Fig. S6). The value of the FOIR epizootic threshold was selected based on previous studies demonstrating marked differences in juvenile and adult FOI in cases of known epidemics80–82 while the enzootic FOIR threshold was selected based upon assumptions central to the null serocatalytic model (enzooticity) and evidence that FOI in wildlife disease systems does not vary by age under enzootic transmission83. Thresholds of ΔS were based on age-specific seroprevalence expectations (directionality) under the “classic” serocatalytic model with null enzootic expectations. The AJI reflects the relative recency of pathogen circulation across populations (via Movi qPCR detection) under the assumptions that 1) qPCR status consistently precedes seroconversion (to Movi seropositive status) in juveniles due to their shorter relative exposure window compared to adults, 2) the rate and duration of Movi shedding (and subsequent detection) does is similar between sampling periods and locations, and 3) Movi seroconversion (changing from seronegative to seropositive for Movi) and reversion (waning Movi antibodies; reverting from seropositive to seronegative) are similar between sampling periods and locations. Values of AJI ≤ 1.0 suggest enzootic transmission because it reflects a lack of relative excess of Movi seronegative, Movi qPCR positive juveniles relative to Movi seropositive, Movi qPCR negative juveniles expected after/during recent epizootic transmission prior to seroconversion. Movi seroprevalence thresholds were selected for comparative epidemiological interpretability that distinguishes observations consistent with epizootics or enzootics of varying intensities from observations consistent with pre-epizootic and post-epizootic epidemiological phases. The pre-epizootic adult Movi seroprevalence threshold was selected to allow relatively low seroprevalence due to immigration/emigration from surrounding populations when no other apparent evidence of transmission is present. Conversely, the post-epizootic adult Movi seroprevalence threshold was selected based on the assumption that a previous epizootic with no current evidence of sustained transmission would be detected prior to extensive Movi seroreversion to seronegative status (seropositive adults accumulate within the exposure-to-sampling timeframe). The epizootic Movi seroprevalence thresholds were conservatively selected such that relatively early and/or low-intensity epizootics could be described. Seroprevalence thresholds for the enzootic phase assignment were selected to reflect cumulative Movi exposure expected of persistent pathogen circulation without age-class skew. For our model-based λadult and λjuvenile infection hazard index calculations, we assumed a mean age of 0.5 for juveniles and 2.0 for adults. Mean juvenile age was based on sampling period timing relative to peak breeding and subsequent parturition from observations made by TPWD personnel. Our conservative mean adult age was selected to avoid inflating FOIR by increasing the difference between juvenile and adult exposure windows. Because adult ages were not known, FOIR should be interpreted as an index of infection hazard scaled to relative exposure windows. To assess the reliance of our results on assumed age structure, we assessed FOIR across a series of mean juvenile ages (0.5 or 0.75 years) and mean adult ages (1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, and 5.0 years).
