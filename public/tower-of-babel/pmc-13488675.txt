Optimization and Implementation of a Primary Care Nurse Training Program Using an Action Research Framework
Authors: Jia-Yi Lin, Qi-Qi Gong, Ying-Shi Chen, Wan-Ting Xiang, Ya-Ting Huang, Chen-Ling Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the optimization and implementation of a primary care nurse training program using an action research framework.MethodsGuided by the theoretical principles of action research, three iterative cycles were conducted to address identified gaps among nurses working in primary care settings, with the aim of refining the training program. Program evaluation was performed using a satisfaction questionnaire and semi-structured interviews.ResultsA needs-based training program for nurses in primary care settings was developed. Across the three action research cycles, mean participant satisfaction scores were 41.43 ± 11.829, 43.73 ± 10.784, and 46.12 ± 5.995, respectively. Welch’s ANOVA demonstrated statistically significant differences among the cycles (F = 24.186, p < 0.001), and pairwise comparisons indicated significant differences in satisfaction scores between cycles (p < 0.05). Additionally, 92.9% (221/238) of participating nurses reported applying acquired knowledge and skills in clinical practice.ConclusionThe action research approach facilitates problem identification and continuous iterative refinement of the training programme throughout the implementation process, thereby enhancing its relevance and practicality, as reflected in the increased satisfaction among primary care nurses. Future research should incorporate objective competency assessments to evaluate the actual effectiveness of the training programme in improving nursing competence.

Introduction

Against the background of global population aging, the burden of chronic disease has become increasingly pronounced, necessitating the development of a responsive healthcare service system that expands nursing resources and broadens the scope of nursing practice. In particular, increasing both the number and competency of nurses in primary healthcare settings is essential to address the care needs of individuals with chronic conditions. Many countries have expanded the scope of practice for primary care nurses to enhance the accessibility, quality, and cost-effectiveness of healthcare services.1,2 Evidence indicates that providing primary healthcare professionals with training tailored to their specific knowledge and skill requirements is a critical strategy for strengthening primary care capacity,3 and represents an important pathway toward achieving the integration of medical care and disease prevention at the primary care level.4

Within the context of the national initiative to advance the Healthy China Initiative and the implementation of Guangdong Province’s “Hundred Counties, Thousand Towns, Ten Thousand Villages High-Quality Development Program”, and in alignment with national priorities to strengthen the primary healthcare system, the Health Commission of Guangdong Province issued the Five-Year Work Plan for the “Xinhuo Peiji Project” to Enhance the Competency of Primary Healthcare Personnel in Guangdong Province.5 The Primary Care Nursing Skills Enhancement Project constitutes one of six specialized training programs designated under this plan and is administered by the School of Nursing, Southern Medical University. The project aims to provide comprehensive competency-based training for healthcare personnel across township health centers, village health stations, and community health service centers throughout the province over an approximately five-year period. The training encompasses three domains: foundational theoretical knowledge, common clinical nursing operational skills, and professional theoretical and practical competencies. The primary objectives are to strengthen nursing service capacity within the primary healthcare system, improve the overall competency and quality of care delivered by nursing personnel, refine the training framework for nurses in primary care settings, and develop a cohort of nursing management and clinical nursing personnel committed to and capable of sustained service at the primary care level.

Research has shown that targeted training and education for primary care nurses remain relatively underdeveloped. Most courses they attend are organized and managed by hospitals or other healthcare institutions, with limited assessment of the learning needs of primary care nurses; consequently, the training needs of primary healthcare workers remain largely unmet.3,6 Training for primary care nursing is not a simplified version of hospital nursing training, as this is determined by the orientation and nature of primary care services. Therefore, training programmes for primary care nursing should be further refined and optimized in a manner that is oriented towards the training needs of primary care nurses.

The term “action research” was coined by psychologist Lewin and was first applied in the field of education.7 Action research is a problem-oriented approach that involves analyzing and reflecting on problems in practice, developing and implementing targeted interventions, with the aim of solving problems and changing behaviors.8 In recent years, action research has gained widespread application and validation in international nursing education. Mohsen et al9 adopted the action research model in Iran, systematically improving a continuing nursing education program through the cyclical process of planning, action, observation, and reflection, with a significant increase in participant satisfaction scores. Ryder et al10 used action research to redesign a continuing professional education framework in a large Irish teaching hospital, with 90% of learners reporting acquisition of new knowledge and skills. Ryan et al11 employed action research to evaluate a workshop training involving 20 clinical nurse educators from Queensland, Australia, and found that the learning experiences generated by action research could effectively stimulate educators to apply educational theory and enhance their teaching competencies in the affective and nuanced dimensions of clinical training. Collectively, these studies indicate that action research can effectively respond to trainees’ needs, improve training satisfaction, and enhance participants’ relevant competencies.

Therefore, our research team adopted the action research approach as the theoretical framework. Based on problem identification, we collaboratively designed and implemented training action plans with the study participants, and through the continuous cycle of planning, action, observation, and reflection, we optimized and refined the training programme for primary care nurses. Unlike the traditional “top-down” training model, the novelty of this study lies in the application of an iterative action research cycle that continuously adjusts training content in response to the evolving and context-specific needs of participants, a process that is often overlooked in conventional training programme design.

Study Participants

Participants were selected using convenience sampling from nurses in primary care settings enrolled in Guangdong Province’s “Xinhuo Peiji Project”.

Inclusion criteria: (1) possession of a valid nursing license within the current registration period; (2) employment as a nurse in primary care settings, including community health service centers, township health centers, or village health stations; (3) provision of informed consent for participation in the study.

Exclusion criteria: (1) nursing personnel who did not complete the full “Xinhuo Peiji Project” training program.

Removal criteria: (1) incomplete questionnaire responses; (2) evidence of patterned responding.

Methods

Establishment of the Research Team

The research team comprised six members, including five master’s-level graduate students and one associate professor, aged 22 to 52 years. All members had received relevant training prior to study implementation.

Study Design

Action research was adopted as the guiding methodological framework. Three action research cycles of primary care nurse training were conducted between 2024 and 2025, with 400, 387, and 398 participants enrolled in each round, respectively. Each cycle represented a complete action research cycle and adhered to the four-step process of “plan–act–observe–reflect”. Multiple data collection methods, including situational surveys, semi-structured interviews, team meetings, and field observations, were employed to identify existing issues, refine intervention strategies, and optimize the training program within each cycle. This iterative process facilitated the development of a dynamic and progressively evolving training program. The overall action research process is presented in Figure 1. Development of the Practice Training Program

Problem Identification

(1) Phase one. To obtain a comprehensive understanding of the training needs of nurses in primary care settings with respect to nursing procedures and clinical specialty areas, a pre-training survey was conducted using convenience sampling. A total of 42 nurses from primary care settings in Shaoguan and Meizhou, Guangdong Province, were included as participants. The questionnaire was developed with reference to the nursing training syllabus of the “Xinhuo Peiji Project”.12 Common nursing procedures were categorized into four groups according to clinical application context, while specialty departments were classified into three categories—surgery, internal medicine, and medical specialties—for survey purposes. The findings indicated that, among the four groups of nursing procedures, the highest demand proportions were observed for aseptic technique (92.9%), humanistic care and psychological nursing skills (97.6%), basic trauma care techniques (97.6%), and vascular access device maintenance (81.0%). Regarding departmental training needs, within the internal medicine category, the Department of Respiratory Medicine demonstrated the highest demand (97.6%); within the surgery category, orthopedics ranked highest (71.4%); and within the medical specialties category, the emergency department exhibited the greatest demand (100.0%).

(2) Phase two. Purposive sampling was employed to conduct in-depth interviews with nurses in primary care settings. In accordance with the principle of data saturation, a total of 15 participants were included. An interview guide was developed in advance by the research team to ensure that the interviews remained focused on the study objectives. Prior to each interview, the venue was arranged with the participant, and a quiet, well-lit environment was selected. With participant consent, interviews were audio-recorded in full; where consent for audio recording was not provided, detailed written notes were taken. Following each interview, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and participant data were coded. Colaizzi’s seven-step method was applied to organize and analyze the data. The interview guide included the following questions: (1) description of experience in primary care nursing practice; (2) perceived competencies and attributes required for nurses in primary care settings; (3) adequacy of current knowledge and skills in meeting patient care needs; (4) recommended content for primary care nurse training programs; and (5) perceived effectiveness of previously attended training programs.

Development of the Preliminary Practice Training Program

Based on the interview findings and the nursing training syllabus of the Guangdong Province “Xinhuo Peiji Project”, the research team convened an expert panel to develop a preliminary training program for nurses in primary care settings. The training objectives were defined such that, upon completion, nurses in primary care settings would demonstrate mastery of foundational theoretical knowledge, essential clinical knowledge, and basic nursing skills required for practice, as well as competency in professional ethics, communication, and emergency response. The training content included: (1) nurse–patient communication, humanistic care, and psychological nursing skills; (2) fall risk assessment and prevention among older adults; (3) commonly used health assessment techniques in primary care; (4) perinatal healthcare techniques, neonatal emergency care, and child healthcare; (5) common emergency care skills in primary care settings; and (6) essential traditional Chinese medicine nursing skills applicable to primary care. The training faculty comprised two specialist nurses and eight university faculty members, six of whom held the rank of associate professor or higher. Training modalities included didactic instruction, group discussion, procedural demonstration, and scenario-based simulation. The program consisted of six sessions, each lasting 45 to 60 minutes.

Based on the findings obtained during the initial problem-identification phase, this study adopted the ADDIE model,13 a commonly used training framework in the field of education, to conduct comprehensive analysis and discussion. Combined with expert panel meetings, we completed the draft design of a training programme for primary care nurses. The specific steps were as follows: 1) Analysis: The researchers systematically reviewed the domestic and international literature published over the past five years on primary care nurse training, and integrated the nursing training outline of the Guangdong “Xinhuo Peiji” Project, along with the results of our previous quantitative and qualitative studies, to provide direct evidence for the development of the training programme. 2) Design: Based on the training content, the curriculum was divided into six modules, each lasting 40–60 minutes. The training content included: (a) nurse–patient communication, humanistic care and psychological nursing techniques; (b) fall assessment and prevention in older adults; (c) commonly used health assessment techniques in primary care; (d) perinatal care techniques, neonatal emergency care and child health care; (e) common emergency techniques in primary care; and (f) essential basic nursing skills in traditional Chinese medicine for primary care. The training objectives were set to enable primary care nurses to acquire the fundamental theories, basic knowledge and essential skills required for clinical nursing practice, as well as to develop good professional ethics, communication abilities and emergency response capabilities. To achieve these objectives, each module adopted appropriate teaching strategies according to the characteristics of its content. 3) Development: The course was developed using PowerPoint presentations as the primary teaching medium; the slides were designed for easy viewing with clear emphasis on key and difficult points. Operational demonstrations were conducted on site by the instructors, who provided explanations and guidance on the key and difficult aspects. 4) Implementation: The training implementation received strong support from the local Health Commission. The training venues were classrooms at a local medical university, equipped with complete multimedia teaching facilities and training materials, and provided a comfortable learning environment. 5) Evaluation: After each round of training, satisfaction surveys and semi-structured interviews were conducted to assess training satisfaction and effectiveness.

Preliminary Implementation and Refinement of the Practice Training Program

Training was conducted from 2024 to 2025, with each cycle representing a complete action research cycle. Prior to the initiation of each cycle, a needs assessment survey was administered using a structured questionnaire. Training Needs of Primary Care Nurses Across Three Action Research Cycles

Regarding departmental training needs, orthopedics and the emergency department consistently ranked among the highest priorities across all three rounds. Within the internal medicine category, the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine demonstrated the highest training demand in the first cycle, while the Department of Respiratory Medicine ranked highest in the second and third cycles and in the aggregated results. During the training process, data were collected through participant observation by members of the research team. Upon completion of each cycle, satisfaction questionnaires were distributed to assess participant satisfaction, and semi-structured interviews were conducted to obtain feedback.

Based on the integrated findings, existing issues in the training program were identified, targeted corrective measures were proposed, and the program was further refined prior to the subsequent cycle.

(1) Planning: Prior to each cycle, the training program for that cycle was formulated by integrating findings from the needs assessment survey, results from the preceding action research cycle, and the training syllabus, with explicit specification of instructional content, teaching methods, and evaluation strategies.

(2) Action: Training was implemented in accordance with the established plan; evaluation components were assessed against predefined criteria at designated time points, and issues arising during the training process were identified and addressed in a timely manner.

(3) Observation: Members of the research team conducted continuous participant observation throughout the training process, monitoring performance indicators such as class engagement, technical proficiency during skills practice, and overall classroom dynamics. At the conclusion of each session, a classroom evaluation questionnaire was administered to assess participant satisfaction and collect feedback.

(4) Reflection: Upon completion of each cycle, a team meeting was convened to synthesize observational records and evaluation data, systematically analyze identified issues, and propose strategies for program optimization.

Observation and Reflection

It should be noted that after categorizing and summarizing the trainee feedback, the research team identified certain discrepancies in the needs expressed by different subgroups of trainees. To address this, the team established three prioritization principles in advance: (1) the principle of demand frequency. Content was prioritized if it was rated as needed by ≥80% of trainees in the questionnaire survey or mentioned by ≥3 interviewees; (2) the principle of job relevance. Priority was given to content directly related to the core daily responsibilities of primary care nurses and with high application frequency (eg, primary emergency nursing techniques), while content with lower relevance to the primary care setting was appropriately deferred; and (3) the principle of resource availability. Preference was given to training content that could be feasibly delivered within the existing faculty and teaching hour constraints. All three rounds of revision were informed by decisions based on these principles. Taking the first round of revision as an example, “emergency nursing techniques” was rated as “highly needed” by 96.9% of trainees in the needs survey and is a frequently applied skill in primary care settings; therefore, it was prioritized for increased teaching weight.

Evaluation of the Primary Care Nurse Training Program

Assessment of Training Effectiveness

Semi-structured interviews were conducted to evaluate training effectiveness. To ensure the authenticity and accuracy of the data, principal members of the research team served as interviewers. During the interview process, subjective guidance and leading prompts were avoided to preserve the integrity of participants’ responses. Any ambiguities arising during the interviews were clarified in a timely manner. The interviews addressed topics including the implementation of the primary care nurse training program, learning outcomes, and recommendations for program improvement.

Interview data were systematically organized and comprehensively analyzed to identify and extract key themes. The interview guide included the following: 1) Please describe your experience of participating in this training program. 2) Which instructional content was most impactful, and for what reasons? 3) What were the key gains from participation in this training? 4) What impact has the training had on clinical practice? 5) What suggestions are proposed for improvement, and what are the expectations for future training programs?

Training Satisfaction Survey

The survey instrument was developed by the researchers based on the training content of Guangdong Province’s “Xinhuo Peiji Project” and comprised 10 items across four dimensions: training content, teaching faculty, curriculum design, and training value. A five-point Likert scale was employed, with scores ranging from 1 (“very dissatisfied”) to 5 (“very satisfied”), where higher scores indicated greater levels of participant satisfaction with the training. During the questionnaire development process, the research team generated an initial item pool based on an extensive literature review. Five experts were then invited to form an expert panel for content validity review, including two nursing education experts with over 15 years of teaching experience, two clinical nursing experts with extensive experience in primary care, and one expert in questionnaire design and psychometrics. The experts rated each item for relevance, clarity, and dimensional attribution, and provided comments for revision. Based on the expert feedback, the research team modified the wording of three items, resulting in a final formal questionnaire comprising 10 items. Subsequently, a pilot survey was conducted among 42 trainees using this questionnaire to evaluate its reliability. The pilot survey results showed that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the total scale was 0.897, exceeding the threshold of 0.70, indicating good internal consistency of the questionnaire.

Impact of Training on Clinical Practice

Six months following completion of the training program, a questionnaire survey was administered to evaluate whether the knowledge, skills, and attitudes acquired during training had been applied in routine nursing practice.

Quality Control

Prior to study initiation, all members of the research team underwent standardized training in action research methodology to ensure a consistent understanding of study procedures, assigned roles, and data collection methods. During the implementation phase, a research advisor or quality control officer who was not directly involved in the specific action activities was designated to periodically review study progress for deviations from the original protocol and to verify the completeness and standardization of data collection. During data management and analysis, two team members independently cross-checked the extracted data and excluded invalid questionnaires exhibiting evident logical inconsistencies or patterned responses. Double data entry was employed to ensure data accuracy. Audio recordings were transcribed within 24 hours following each interview, and the transcripts, together with field notes, were organized and returned to participants for verification. Two researchers who were not involved in the interview process applied Colaizzi’s seven-step method to code the transcribed data and extract themes.

Research Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University (Approval No.: 2025096). Prior to participation, all individuals were fully informed of the study objectives, methods, procedures, and timeline, and participation was voluntary. Interview content was audio-recorded with participant consent, and coded data were coded to ensure the protection of personal privacy.

Statistical Methods

Study data were entered into Excel and, following verification for accuracy, analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered indicative of statistical significance. Quantitative data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (øverline x ±s). For data demonstrating homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, with pairwise comparisons conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) method. For data with heterogeneity of variance, Welch’s ANOVA was employed, and pairwise comparisons were performed using the Tamhane’s T2 method.

Results

Pre-Training Interview Findings

The interview findings were as follows: 1) Primary care nursing is highly comprehensive in nature, with complex and varied job responsibilities, requiring nurses to simultaneously assume multiple functional roles. 2) Primary care nurses should possess emergency response capabilities, good communication skills, solid professional operational skills, a systematic nursing knowledge base, and an awareness of proactive service. 3) Most primary care nurses perceived that their current nursing knowledge and skills still require improvement in order to better meet the health needs of primary care patients. 4) Primary care nurses articulated several training needs, primarily focusing on emergency techniques, nurse–patient communication and humanistic care, and traditional Chinese medicine nursing. 5) Primary care nurses reported that current training opportunities are relatively scarce. Previous training mainly consisted of advanced studies at higher-level hospitals, but the content of such training was often disconnected from the actual practice in primary care settings. In addition, internally organized training within the facilities was largely confined to theoretical lectures, with limited introduction of new knowledge and new techniques, resulting in suboptimal training outcomes. Therefore, it is recommended that future training should be oriented towards the training needs of primary care nurses and should emphasize the integration of theory and practice to enhance the practicality of the training.

Post-Training Interview Findings

Participants were selected using purposive sampling from nurses in primary care settings who participated in the primary care nursing skills enhancement training under the “Xinhuo Peiji Project”. Selection was guided by the principle of maximum variation, considering factors such as region, sex, age, marital status, years of work experience, first academic degree, highest academic degree, and employment type. Data collection was discontinued upon reaching data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes or perspectives emerged from subsequent interviews. A total of 15 participants were included.

1) The majority of participants reported a high overall evaluation of the “Xinhuo Peiji Project”, indicating that the training content was practical and well aligned with the requirements of primary care nursing practice.

2) Among the training components, emergency care skills in primary care and essential traditional Chinese medicine nursing skills received particular attention, with participants indicating that both areas demonstrated strong practical relevance and, when combined with hands-on training, resulted in notable learning outcomes.

3) Most participants reported substantial gains from the training, with acquired knowledge and skills effectively applied in routine nursing practice, thereby contributing to improvements in the quality of care. In terms of nursing assessment, one participant stated: In the past, limited attention was given to environmental safety factors related to falls among older adults; following the training, improvements were made to the ward environment as well as patients’ clothing and footwear.

In the past, limited attention was given to environmental safety factors related to falls among older adults; following the training, improvements were made to the ward environment as well as patients’ clothing and footwear.

Another participant noted: Previously, assessment was limited to identifying the presence of anxiety or pain; following the training, standardized assessment tools were used to evaluate the severity of anxiety, pain, and related parameters, providing enhanced support for nursing practice.

Previously, assessment was limited to identifying the presence of anxiety or pain; following the training, standardized assessment tools were used to evaluate the severity of anxiety, pain, and related parameters, providing enhanced support for nursing practice.

Regarding nurse–patient communication, one participant reported: Older adults represent a substantial proportion of patients in primary care settings, and communication was previously challenging; following the training, communication from the patient’s perspective improved, resulting in more effective interactions.

Participants also reported improvements in updating nursing knowledge and techniques, as well as in traditional Chinese medicine nursing practices.

4) With respect to future training optimization, participants expressed a need for increased training opportunities and appropriately extended training duration. Recommendations also included integrating instructional content with real clinical cases to facilitate comprehension and application of knowledge and skills. Additionally, some participants recommended incorporating training related to nursing management for personnel in managerial roles within primary care settings to enhance practice efficiency.

Survey Results on the Impact of Training on Clinical Practice

A follow-up survey conducted six months after completion of the training yielded 238 valid questionnaires. The findings indicated that 92.9% (221/238) of participants reported applying the professional knowledge and skills acquired during the training in clinical practice.

Discussion

Methodological Innovation of Iterative Action Research in Primary Care Training

The core innovation of this study lies in the introduction of an iterative action research framework, rather than adopting a conventional static training design approach. Existing training programmes are typically constructed based on a single needs assessment or expert experience, and lack a dynamic response mechanism to accommodate trainee feedback or changes in the clinical context during the implementation phase. Consequently, they are often insufficiently adaptable to the growing complexity of the primary health care system and the diverse practice needs across different regions.15,16 In contrast, the iterative action research framework follows a cyclical process of “planning–action–observation–reflection–replanning”, which enables the training programme to be dynamically adjusted. Such a dynamic matching mechanism helps ensure that the training content remains closely aligned with the real-world work context of primary care nurses and their evolving competency gaps, thereby effectively avoiding the disconnect between training and clinical practice. Moreover, the iterative process itself confers on primary care nurses the role of “participatory improvers”. By orienting training towards the actual needs of primary care nurses, it transforms training from an externally imposed task into an internally driven process of need fulfilment, thereby strengthening nurses’ sense of agency and intrinsic motivation for learning—a feature that markedly differentiates this approach from the traditional “top-down” model of knowledge transmission. Accordingly, this study has not only produced an optimised training programme, but has also explored a sustainable and transferable mechanism for training optimisation, enabling the training programme to continuously evolve in response to shifts in primary health care policy, changes in the epidemiological profile, and the professional development needs of the nursing workforce. This constitutes the key innovation of the present study compared with previous training research. Therefore, this model has the potential for generalisability across different regions and levels of primary care training; its core value lies not in the specific course content, but in the mechanism of “continuous feedback–dynamic adjustment” itself, which provides a feasible pathway for building a resilient and adaptive continuing education system for primary care.

Interestingly, the iterative action research framework bears a deep structural resemblance to the developmental logic of care bundle interventions in the field of healthcare quality improvement. The core tenet of care bundle interventions is to systematically integrate and standardise a set of evidence-based interventions, and to monitor implementation adherence in order to enhance the reliability and predictability of clinical outcomes. A study from Italy noted that one of the main challenges currently facing healthcare quality improvement is not the validation of intervention effectiveness, but rather the construction of such transparent and reproducible development and implementation pathways.17 From this perspective, the action research framework adopted in this study may not only represent an educational strategy; it may also provide a methodological foundation for designing standardised competency-building pathways in primary care. Consequently, future research could further explore whether the iterative process presented in this study can be translated into structured educational care bundles to support uniform implementation, adherence monitoring, and ultimately improve professional practice and patient-related outcomes. Promoting the evolution of action research from a tool for single-project optimisation to a generalisable and replicable paradigm for primary care human resource development may represent an important trajectory for future advancement.

Action Research–Oriented Identification of Training Needs in Primary Care Nursing

Studies have demonstrated that, in the absence of a well-established educational framework for nurses in primary care settings, training programs often exhibit limited alignment with actual clinical needs.14,18,19 This misalignment is reflected in narrowly focused and repetitive training content that does not adequately address the practical demands of primary care or adhere to the principle of needs-based education. In recent years, China has increasingly emphasized the strengthening of primary care service capacity;20–22 consequently, training programs for nurses in primary care settings require continuous refinement through practice to meet the evolving demands of workforce development. This aligns with the core characteristic of action research, which integrates inquiry and improvement through iterative cycles of problem identification, planning, action, observation, and re-planning, thereby enabling dynamic and sustained program enhancement.8 In recent years, action research has been widely applied in the field of medical education and training.23–25

Based on this, the present study conducted three rounds of action research and identified that the training needs of primary care nurses in China were concentrated in the areas of emergency nursing, fall assessment in older adults, pressure injury prevention, traditional Chinese medicine nursing techniques, and chronic disease management. However, these needs may reflect more than mere training requirements. Further analysis revealed that the observed clustering of these needs actually mirrors the most salient and challenging core task that primary care nurses encounter in their daily clinical practice—namely, the ability, through professional assessment, to identify subtle, ambiguous, yet clinically significant signals of fragility before a patient’s condition deteriorates acutely. A study encompassing 42,735 hospitalised patients confirmed that nursing diagnoses such as fall risk, impaired skin integrity, acute pain, and risk for injury were significant predictors of transfer to the intensive care unit in both adult and paediatric patients.26 Notably, the nursing diagnoses identified as high-risk warning signals in that study were highly consistent with the training needs repeatedly emphasised by primary care nurses in our investigation. This suggests that the training demands expressed by primary care nurses may fundamentally reflect their adaptive response to the increasing complexity of patients in contemporary healthcare settings. Therefore, future research should move beyond evaluating satisfaction and self-reported application of knowledge, and instead investigate whether action research–oriented training programmes can enhance nurses’ ability to recognise the aforementioned high-risk nursing characteristics, anticipate clinical deterioration, and ultimately improve patient safety outcomes, thereby promoting a shift in training objectives from skill transmission to the empowerment of risk anticipation capacity. Such capacity building centred on risk anticipation holds significant practical value for primary care institutions in achieving early warning and reducing the incidence of avoidable deterioration events under resource-constrained conditions.

Further comparison of relevant studies across different countries reveals that the training needs of primary care nurses in Lebanon are focused on community health promotion, patient-centred care models, and self-management support for patients with chronic diseases,27 among which chronic disease management aligns with the research priorities identified in China. A review from Spain28 explicitly identified significant training gaps in emergency techniques among primary care practitioners, a need that is also consistent with the high demand for emergency nursing skills reported by primary care nurses in our study. In contrast, research from Kenya29 indicates that the training priorities for primary health workers in that country are concentrated in areas such as infectious disease care, mental health, oral health, and ophthalmic services, displaying a training needs profile markedly different from that of the present study. This discrepancy can be attributed to variations in epidemiological profiles, health system structures, and public health priorities across countries. From an international comparative perspective, it is evident that training needs in primary care nursing are characterised by distinct geographic specificity and system-oriented features. Their distribution is not only directly shaped by the spectrum of diseases but is also closely associated with health resource allocation, service accessibility, and regional health policies. Therefore, in developing training plans, it is essential to fully ground them in localised disease burdens and service gaps to ensure that training content precisely aligns with actual health needs. At the same time, the similarities and differences across countries suggest that the design of primary care training programmes should place great emphasis on cultural appropriateness and contextual sensitivity, avoiding the uncritical adoption of universal approaches.

Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that primary care nurses encounter the direct obstacle of “complex learning materials” during the learning process, indicating that training content lacking a graded difficulty gradient and exceeding their cognitive load can directly impede learning outcomes,30 a finding consistent with the observations of the present study. During the implementation of the training, our research team found that primary care nurses experienced considerable difficulties in learning electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation and management, and exhibited low interest in this topic. Nevertheless, given the accelerating population ageing and the rising annual prevalence of cardiovascular diseases, ECG, as a simple and rapid detection tool, can provide objective, real-time clinical data for critically ill patients and those with severe arrhythmias.31 Currently, ECG examination and cardiac monitoring techniques have been widely applied in clinical practice. The ability of nurses to quickly and accurately identify ECG changes, particularly life-threatening malignant arrhythmias, is directly associated with the timeliness of resuscitation and improvement of outcomes in critically ill patients. Based on the above context and the teaching feedback from primary care nurses, our research team adjusted the teaching content of the module “ECG Interpretation and Management”, refocusing the instructional emphasis on standardised ECG operating procedures and the recognition and interpretation of common critical ECG patterns, so as to lower the learning threshold and enhance the practicality and relevance of the training.

After three cycles of action research, the research team progressively identified the training needs of primary care nurses and systematically gained insights into the learners’ learning status, class participation, and overall teaching atmosphere. Throughout the process, we systematically collected trainee feedback, convened thematic seminars, and aggregated and analysed observational records and evaluation data to identify major problems in training implementation, based on which corresponding optimisation strategies were proposed. Thus, action research enables training organisers to identify problems in a timely manner during the training process and to optimise the training programme to meet trainees’ needs, as has been demonstrated in previous studies.32,33

The Optimized Primary Care Nurse Training Program Demonstrates Greater Relevance and Practicality

Action research is characterized by the identification of practice-based problems, reflective analysis, implementation of targeted interventions, and evaluation of outcomes, thereby forming a dynamic and iterative cycle of continuous improvement. In the present study, following each training cycle, participant feedback was systematically collected and integrated with observations of the instructional process and evaluations of training effectiveness. Based on these data, the content of subsequent training cycles was promptly refined, resulting in progressive enhancement of the relevance and practical applicability of the training program.

Survey findings indicated that, across all three cycles of training needs assessments, the proportion of participants identifying CPR as a training need consistently exceeded 94.0%. When data from all cycles were aggregated, 96.0% of nurses in primary care settings identified training related to the “emergency department” within the medical specialties module as necessary. In response to findings from participant observation and semi-structured interviews, corresponding adjustments to the training curriculum were implemented: instructional time allocated to “common emergency care skills in primary care” was increased, and two additional components— “hemostasis, bandaging, and immobilization” and “triage”—were incorporated; instructional time for the “essential basic traditional Chinese medicine nursing skills for primary care” module was extended, with the inclusion of additional content such as “postpartum follow-up”, “chronic disease management in primary care”, “wound and ostomy care”, and “orthopedic nursing care”. The optimized training program more closely aligns with the needs of nurses in primary care settings, thereby enhancing the relevance and practical value of the training content, effectively addressing learning needs, and strengthening engagement in the learning process.

Furthermore, the alignment between training content and clinical practice, together with the application of acquired knowledge and skills, supports the practical effectiveness of the program. Evidence indicates that the transfer of knowledge and skills is a key indicator of training effectiveness, with direct implications for improvements in clinical nursing practice and service quality.34 The present findings demonstrated that 92.9% (221/238) of participants applied the professional knowledge acquired during training in clinical practice, indicating that the program may have contributed to the integration of theoretical knowledge and practical application. Therefore, continuous refinement of training content and reinforcement of a practice-oriented approach may be essential for enhancing the professional competencies of nurses in primary care settings and improving the quality of nursing services.

Preliminary Outcomes and Reflections on the Action Research–Optimized Training Program

Action research is characterized by practitioners identifying problems in real-world contexts, conducting reflection, implementing interventions, and evaluating outcomes, thereby constituting a dynamic cyclical mechanism for continuous improvement. In this study, after each training session, we systematically collected trainee feedback and integrated data from teaching process observations and outcome evaluations to make targeted adjustments to the subsequent training content, with the aim of continuously enhancing the relevance and effectiveness of the training programme. Based on the survey findings, the research team made the following optimizations to the curriculum: First, we increased the teaching hours for the “Common Emergency Techniques in Primary Care” module and added two new teaching units on “Hemostasis, Bandaging, and Immobilization” and “Triage”; Second, we expanded the teaching hours for the “Essential Basic Nursing Skills in Traditional Chinese Medicine for Primary Care” module and incorporated additional content including “Postnatal Follow-up”, “Chronic Disease Management in Primary Care”, “Intravenous Catheter Maintenance”, and “Bone and Joint Care”. The optimized training programme, oriented towards the actual needs of primary care nurses, further enhanced the specificity and practicality of the training content.

Furthermore, the high degree of alignment between the training content and clinical practice, along with the positive application of acquired knowledge and skills by the trainees, further corroborates the practical value of this training programme. Research has demonstrated that effective transfer of knowledge and skills is a key indicator of training effectiveness, and the extent of such transfer directly influences improvements in clinical nursing behaviours and service quality.34 The results of this survey showed that 92.9% (221/238) of the trainees reported that they were able to apply the professional knowledge acquired from the training to clinical practice. However, it should be noted that these data are entirely derived from the trainees’ subjective self-assessments, and can only indirectly reflect their subjective recognition and positive acceptance of the practicality of the training content.

At the primary care service level, primary care nurses constitute the largest component of the primary health care workforce and serve as gatekeepers of population health.35 The International Council of Nurses has emphasized that nurses play a pivotal role in primary care, being the primary providers of primary health care services and the bridge connecting primary and higher-tier healthcare systems, and has identified increased investment in nursing education as a key pathway to achieving high-quality primary care.1 In this study, the training programme for primary care nurses, optimized through three rounds of action research, demonstrated improvements in both relevance and practicality. In terms of curriculum design, we strengthened the teaching of high-frequency skills used in primary care settings, including emergency management, chronic disease management, and appropriate traditional Chinese medicine techniques. The relatively high rate of knowledge application after training suggests that a “needs-driven” curriculum design can effectively reduce the disconnect between learning and practice. However, it should be cautiously noted that the self-reported enhancement of skill reserves among trained nurses may not necessarily correspond directly to their procedural competence and decision-making accuracy in actual clinical scenarios. Nevertheless, in terms of content relevance and subjective trainee acceptance, the action research–optimized programme showed positive effects in improving trainees’ self-efficacy and learning satisfaction. Although this study was implemented only in a specific region, and its external generalizability requires further validation, its core philosophy of “needs-driven and continuous optimization” still offers valuable insights for rethinking the prevalent issues of “rigid content and detachment from practice” in current primary care training. Future research should incorporate objective competency assessments and patient outcome data to further validate the actual effectiveness of this training programme.

The Optimized Primary Care Nurse Training Program Enhances Training Satisfaction

Through three cycles of action research, the primary care nurse training program underwent continuous refinement. The findings demonstrated a progressive increase in overall participant satisfaction scores with successive program improvements. The mean satisfaction score increased from 41.43 ± 11.829 in Round 1 to 43.73 ± 10.784 in Round 2, and further to 46.12 ± 5.995 in Round 3. Welch’s ANOVA indicated statistically significant differences (F = 24.186, p < 0.001), and pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences in satisfaction scores between cycles (p < 0.05), indicating that iterative program optimization was associated with improved participant satisfaction. This finding was consistent with the results of an action research study conducted by Iranian scholars, which similarly employed the action research approach as an intervention for nurse training and reported a marked improvement in participant satisfaction.9 The gradual increase in satisfaction scores can be attributed to the iterative removal of mismatched content and the addition of high-demand modules. Such responsiveness likely enhanced participants’ perception of the value and practicality of the training, thereby contributing to higher overall satisfaction. Notably, although the effect size in this study was small to moderate, given that nursing education and training are often influenced by multiple confounding factors, the specific translational value of this effect size for clinical nursing management still warrants further investigation.

These findings indicate that the action research–based “participation–collaboration–action” model may have contributed positively to the optimization of the training program. In comparison with the traditional “research–development–dissemination” model, action research places greater emphasis on participant feedback and interactive engagement, facilitating the timely identification of issues and implementation of targeted improvements, thereby enhancing training effectiveness.36,37 Conventional training approaches typically adopt a “top-down” model of knowledge transmission, which may insufficiently address learner needs and contextual relevance, potentially resulting in a disconnect between training content and clinical practice. In contrast, the action research approach positions participants as active collaborators in the training process, enabling improved alignment with practice needs and greater adaptability through multi-stakeholder engagement. In the present study, the observed increase in satisfaction scores across successive cycles provides preliminary empirical support for the effectiveness of this approach.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, the evaluation of outcomes relied primarily on self-reported data, such as satisfaction and perceived usability, without the inclusion of objective competency measures. This makes it difficult to ascertain whether the observed improvements in satisfaction truly reflect enhancements in clinical skills; the observed improvements may simply represent trainees’ subjective positive perceptions of the training experience rather than actual gains in competence. Second, the sample was derived from a single province, which limited the generalisability of the findings. Training needs may vary across regions due to differences in healthcare infrastructure and policy environments. Third, although trainees across the three rounds were all primary care nurses from the same province, there may have been some variability in baseline characteristics among participants, which could introduce unmeasured confounding. However, the study design was primarily focused on collecting qualitative feedback and did not require complete baseline equivalence across rounds.

Given these limitations, while we are confident in reporting improvements in training acceptability and perceived utility, caution is warranted when drawing conclusions about objective effectiveness. Future research should prioritise the introduction of objective competency assessment tools, such as the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) or standardised patient assessments, before and after training, to complement self-reported data. In addition, multi-centre studies across provinces would help enhance the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, the associations between improved satisfaction and trainees’ knowledge acquisition, behavioural changes, and patient care outcomes should be further explored to provide a more comprehensive, evidence-based evaluation of the training programme’s implementation effects.

Conclusion

In summary, the application of action research to the optimisation of training programmes can effectively enhance participant satisfaction. The dynamic adjustment strategy has been shown to align training content with the actual needs of primary care, as reflected in the high rate of self-reported clinical application among participants. However, the evaluation framework of this study relied primarily on self-reported data, making it difficult to ascertain whether the observed improvements were truly attributable to substantive gains in professional competence. Therefore, future research should incorporate objective competency assessment indicators to further validate the actual effectiveness of the training intervention.
