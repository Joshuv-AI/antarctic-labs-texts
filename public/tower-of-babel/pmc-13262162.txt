Longitudinal changes in developmental trajectory following early hemispherotomy in early infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy
Authors: Tetsuya Ueda, Hiroharu Suzuki, Takumi Mitsuhashi, Kazuki Nishioka, Kazuki Nomura, Takato Akiba, Shimpei Matsuda, Ayuko Igarashi, Hidenori Sugano, Yuri Urabe, Eri Inaba, Yasushi Iimura, Akihide Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (EIDEE) associated with extensive unilateral structural abnormalities is frequently characterized by drug-resistance and poor developmental outcomes. Although epilepsy surgery is recognized as a treatment option, the optimal timing of intervention in early infancy and detailed longitudinal postoperative developmental trajectories remain insufficiently characterized.We report a male infant with EIDEE due to extensive unilateral hemispheric malformation who underwent hemispherotomy at four months of age for persistent epileptic spasms with a suppression–burst pattern. The patient achieved sustained seizure freedom over five years and discontinued antiseizure medications two years postoperatively. Longitudinal developmental assessments showed continued acquisition of cognitive, language, and motor skills. Raw developmental measures increased steadily over time, whereas age-normed standardized scores remained below age-adjusted norms, indicating a gradual shift in developmental trajectory rather than normalization within the expected range. This case suggests that early consideration of surgical intervention may be associated with a favorable developmental trajectory in selected infants with EIDEE due to congenital structural abnormalities, particularly when drug-resistance is anticipated. Further investigation is needed to define optimal timing and patient selection.

Introduction

Early infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (EIDEE) is a severe early-onset epilepsy presenting within the first three months of life and characterized by frequent drug-resistant seizures, suppression–burst electroencephalogram (EEG) patterns, and profound developmental impairment, including developmental delay, regression, and intellectual disability [1], [2], [3]. In EIDEE, early seizure onset, severe electroclinical abnormalities such as a suppression–burst pattern on EEG, and the presence of extensive structural brain abnormalities are associated with a high likelihood of drug-resistance and poor neurodevelopmental outcomes [1], [4]. Antiseizure medications (ASMs) are typically used as first-line therapy. Although evidence specifically supporting adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) use in EIDEE remains limited, when epileptic spasms (ES) are present, ACTH may be considered based on evidence for infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS) [5], [6]. However, previous studies have shown that relapse may occur even after an initial response to ACTH, with one recent series reporting relapse in 42.6% of ACTH responders, particularly among patients with structural MRI abnormalities [7]. In selected infants with extensive unilateral structural pathology, early surgical evaluation may be warranted when drug-resistance is anticipated.

Epilepsy surgery, including hemispherotomy, has been reported to achieve long-term seizure freedom and support developmental improvement in selected patients [8], [9], [10], [11]. Previous studies have suggested that shorter preoperative seizure duration and earlier surgical intervention may be associated with more favorable developmental outcomes in children undergoing hemispheric surgery [12], [13]. At the same time, surgical intervention during the neonatal and early infant periods carries unique perioperative risks and potential postoperative complications, necessitating careful individualized assessment [12], [13]. This balance between potential developmental benefit and surgical risk underscores the complexity of determining the optimal timing of intervention in structurally defined EIDEE. Furthermore, although developmental outcomes after hemispheric surgery in infancy have been reported [9], [14], [15], longitudinal neuropsychological evaluation including both composite standard scores and raw developmental age measures, remains less frequently described in very young infants.

Here, we present an infant with EIDEE due to extensive unilateral hemispheric malformation who underwent hemispherotomy at four months of age. The patient achieved sustained seizure freedom and demonstrated a favorable developmental trajectory on longitudinal assessment. This case underscores the potential role of early hemispherotomy in altering the clinical course and may be associated with a favorable developmental trajectory in carefully selected infants with EIDEE due to congenital structural abnormalities at high risk of drug-resistance.

Case presentation

A boy currently aged 5 years and 8 months was born at 37 weeks of gestation via elective cesarean section due to placenta previa, with a birth weight of 3170 g. He had no history of perinatal complications or neurodevelopmental delays.

ES were first observed on day 10 of life. By day 18, the frequency of ES had increased, prompting admission to a referring hospital. Scalp EEG demonstrated high-amplitude slow waves in the left hemisphere. Valproic acid was initiated at 5 mg/kg/day and titrated up to 20 mg/kg/day; however, seizures persisted despite dose escalation. From 1 month of age, the frequency of ES gradually increased, reaching approximately five clusters per day. He was referred to our epilepsy center for further evaluation and consideration of surgical treatment.

Axial and coronal fluid-attenuated inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated extensive gyral malformation involving the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1A). Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) demonstrated diffuse hypermetabolism throughout the left hemisphere. The patient continued to experience frequent ES, and EEG frequently demonstrated a suppression-burst pattern. Although the exact temporal relationship between FDG-PET acquisition and ES could not be precisely determined, the hypermetabolism was considered to reflect ongoing peri-ictal epileptic activity rather than a typical interictal pattern (Fig. 1B). Prolonged scalp video-EEG (vEEG) monitoring at 3 months of age revealed an asymmetric suppression–burst pattern with the left hemispheric predominance during both wakefulness and sleep. (Fig. 1C). Habitual ES followed by tonic seizure were captured, demonstrating high amplitude triphasic slow waves with low amplitude fast activity originating from the left hemisphere (Fig. 1D). In addition, ES followed by tonic seizures and behavioral arrest were observed. At 3 months of age, the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (Bayley-III) showed composite scores of 55 (cognitive), 59 (language), and 55 (motor). Preoperative Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (Vineland-II) demonstrated composite scores of 63 (communication), 87 (daily living skills), 76 (socialization), and 94 (motor skills), with an adaptive behavior composite of 72. Given the early seizure onset, extensive unilateral structural abnormality, suppression–burst EEG pattern, and multiple seizure types, this case was considered highly likely to be drug-resistant and unlikely to achieve sustained seizure control with medical therapy including ACTH, prednisolone or vigabatrin. Accordingly, although the patient had received only valproic acid monotherapy, early surgical intervention was considered. Following multidisciplinary discussion involving pediatric neurologists and neurosurgeons, early hemispherotomy was offered after shared decision-making with the family. The left hemispherotomy was performed at 4 months and 10 days of age without perioperative complications. The pathological diagnosis was focal cortical dysplasia type I.

At 5 years and 4 months of postoperative follow-up, the patient remained seizure-free. Valproic acid monotherapy was discontinued 2 years after surgery. Postoperative MRI showed complete disconnection without any other findings. (Fig. 2A). The suppression–burst pattern resolved on EEG performed one week after hemispherotomy, although interictal epileptiform discharges persisted over the left hemisphere (Fig. 2B). Postoperatively, right hemiparesis was observed, predominantly affecting the upper extremity. Continuous rehabilitation was initiated on the day after hemispherotomy. Over longitudinal follow-up, gross motor function gradually improved, and the patient achieved ambulation with a lower-extremity orthosis. Neurodevelopment was longitudinally assessed using the Bayley-III and the Vineland-II according to the institutional neurodevelopmental follow-up protocol, with evaluations conducted at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively. In the present case, an additional assessment was performed at 3 and 5 years postoperatively because continued developmental progress was observed. On the Bayley-III, standardized composite scores initially remained low but showed gradual improvement over time, while raw scores demonstrated continuous increases across cognitive, language, and motor domains. On the Vineland-II, standardized composite scores transiently declined in the early postoperative period but subsequently recovered, whereas raw scores consistently increased across all domains. Discussion

This case suggests that early hemispherotomy in EIDEE associated with unilateral structural abnormalities may be associated with a favorable developmental trajectory rather than solely achieve seizure control. Although seizure freedom was sustained for five years postoperatively, the more notable observation was the longitudinal pattern of developmental change. In EIDEE with extensive unilateral structural abnormalities, drug-resistance is common, and the developmental course typically follows a pattern of progressive divergence [5].

A number of clinical characteristics have been consistently linked to unfavorable developmental trajectories in EIDEE. Earlier seizure onset, particularly during the neonatal period, has been associated with more severe neurodevelopmental impairment in developmental and epileptic encephalopathies [16], [17]. In pediatric cohorts with malformations of cortical development, drug-resistant seizures and neurodevelopmental delay frequently co-occur [18], indicating that congenital structural brain abnormalities are often associated with an unfavorable developmental course. In addition, the suppression–burst pattern is characteristic of severe forms of EIDEE and has been associated with increased mortality and generally poor developmental outcomes [19]. The coexistence of epileptic spasms with other seizure types, such as tonic or focal seizures, has been associated with a more refractory seizure course in some studies [20], [21]. This patient exhibited multiple established poor prognostic factors, suggesting a substantial risk of persistent seizures and developmental divergence under continued medical therapy. Taken together, these observations suggest that extensive structural abnormalities and severe electroclinical features may drive progressive developmental divergence in EIDEE, providing the rationale for early surgical intervention aimed not only at seizure control but also at attenuating the expected progressive divergence and promoting a more favorable developmental trajectory.

A key finding was the longitudinal dissociation between standardized and raw developmental measures observed across serial Bayley-III and Vineland-II assessments, as illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4 and detailed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. On the Bayley-III, standardized composite scores remained below age-adjusted normative expectations in the early postoperative period but gradually improved over time, while raw scores showed steady increases across cognitive, language, and motor domains. Similarly, on the Vineland-II, age-normed standardized scores transiently declined after surgery, whereas raw scores demonstrated consistent increases across domains. The discrepancy between Bayley-III and Vineland-II scores may reflect differences in the developmental domains assessed by each instrument. Bayley-III evaluates developmental abilities in a structured testing environment, whereas Vineland-II is based on caregiver-reported adaptive functioning in daily life. In the present case, developmental skill acquisition continued during longitudinal follow-up, whereas adaptive functioning in daily life may have been influenced by postoperative motor impairment and differences from the age-appropriate developmental level. Thus, the discrepancy between these scores may reflect the distinction between developmental capacity and functional adaptation in daily life. This dissociation suggests that the transient decline in standardized scores did not reflect true developmental regression. The sustained increase in raw scores, including motor scores, suggests continued acquisition of functional abilities and indicates that early surgical intervention may promote adaptive reorganization and functional compensation despite the hemiparesis typically associated with hemispherotomy. From a long-term developmental perspective, the benefits of seizure freedom and prevention of epileptic encephalopathy appear to outweigh the impact of postoperative motor impairment.

Rather than immediate normalization of standardized scores, the patient demonstrated a gradual change in developmental slope over time. Although progressive divergence might be anticipated in EIDEE with multiple poor prognostic indicators, the sustained rise in raw scores suggests continued acquisition of functional abilities, consistent with a potential modification of the expected developmental trajectory. Thus, the longitudinal dissociation between standardized and raw measures provides a more informative framework for interpreting postoperative developmental trajectories beyond seizure control. Developmental outcomes in EIDEE are likely influenced by at least two factors: the underlying genetic or structural condition and the secondary deterioration caused by ongoing epileptic encephalopathy. As emphasized by Mastrangelo et al., severe epileptic activity during the critical period of synaptogenesis may lead to irreversible deviation of the developmental trajectory [17]. In the present case, the sustained increase in raw developmental scores suggests that early interruption of epileptic activity may have mitigated the progressive developmental deterioration.

Longitudinal volumetric neuroimaging studies in children undergoing hemispherotomy have demonstrated progressive structural changes in the contralateral hemisphere [22], findings that are consistent with sustained developmental acquisition observed after early hemispherotomy. Although derived from a different pathological context, these data suggest that early hemispherotomy during a sensitive developmental period may be associated with adaptive reorganization of preserved neural networks. Within this framework, the sustained increase in raw developmental scores may reflect not only seizure suppression but also gradual functional reorganization. While causality cannot be established in a single case, the convergence of clinical course and longitudinal developmental data raises the possibility that early intervention may be associated with a favorable developmental trajectory. Taken together, this case illustrates the potential impact of early surgical intervention on developmental trajectories in selected infants with EIDEE. Further systematic investigation is required to determine optimal timing and indications.

Conclusion

This case suggests that early hemispherotomy may be associated with a favorable developmental trajectory in selected infants with congenital structural EIDEE. Surgery at four months of age resulted in sustained seizure freedom and continued developmental acquisition over five years. Although limited to a single case, the longitudinal findings support timely surgical evaluation in infants with anticipated drug-resistance.
