Fortified Proximal Causal Inference with Many Invalid Proxies
Myeonghun Yu, Xu Shi, Eric J. Tchetgen Tchetgen

Abstract
Causal inference from observational data often relies on the assumption of no unmeasured confounding, an assumption frequently violated in practice due to unobserved or poorly measured covariates. Proximal causal inference (PCI) offers a promising framework for addressing unmeasured confounding using a pair of outcome and treatment confounding proxies. However, existing PCI methods typically assume all specified proxies are valid, which may be unrealistic and is untestable without extra assumptions. In this paper, we develop a semiparametric approach for a many-proxy PCI setting that accommodates potentially invalid treatment confounding proxies. We introduce a new class of fortified confounding bridge functions and establish nonparametric identification of the population average treatment effect (ATE) under the assumption that at least γ \gamma out of K K candidate treatment confounding proxies are valid, for any γ ≤ K \gamma\leq K set by the analyst without requiring knowledge of which proxies are valid. We establish a local semiparametric efficiency bound and develop a class of multiply robust, locally efficient estimators for the ATE. These estimators are thus simultaneously robust to invalid treatment confounding proxies and model misspecification of nuisance parameters. The proposed methods are evaluated through simulation and applied to assess the effect of right heart catheterization in critically ill patients.



Keywords:
Causal inference, Invalid proxies, Semiparametric inference, Unmeasured confounding

1
Introduction

The assumption of no unmeasured confounding is a common assumption of causal inference in observational studies, which requires that all confounders are measured accurately and accounted for in the analysis. However, this assumption is often violated in practice, as some confounders may be unobserved or imperfectly measured. Violations of this assumption can lead to biased estimates and invalid inference about causal effects.

To address this challenge, a variety of methods have been developed over the years, including recent approaches that leverage so-called proxy variables which have drawn growing interest
(
Miao et al. 2018
;
Shi et al. 2020b
;
Tchetgen Tchetgen et al. 2024
;
Cui et al. 2024
)
.
Proximal causal inference (PCI) provides a formal framework that uses proxy variables to identify and estimate causal effects despite the presence of unmeasured confounding.
In addition to having potentially measured a subset of relevant confounders, it relies on having also measured two types of proxy variables: (1) treatment confounding proxies; and (2) outcome confounding proxies.
Treatment confounding proxies must be associated with the outcome only through a shared unmeasured confounder with the treatment, while outcome confounding proxies must be associated with the treatment only through a shared unmeasured confounder with the outcome.
We refer readers to Section
2.1
for formal definitions.
Prominent examples of proxies of types (1) and (2) are so-called negative control exposures and negative control outcomes, respectively.
Importantly, both types of proxies should share the same unmeasured confounding structure as the primary treatment and outcome of interest.

However, the assumption that proxy variables are valid, in the sense of satisfying either (1) or (2), is not empirically testable without additional assumptions. Assessments of proxy validity often rely on domain knowledge
(
Lipsitch et al. 2010
;
Shi et al. 2020b
)
, which may be subjective and potentially unreliable. Existing data-driven validity tests rely on stringent structural equation models, and are limited to validating one particular type of proxies, known as
disconnected
proxies
(
Kummerfeld et al. 2024
)
.
In practice, identifying valid outcome confounding proxies may be more straightforward than selecting valid treatment confounding proxies.
For example, pre-treatment outcome measures are widely used as outcome confounding proxies since they cannot be causally affected by the treatment.
A case in point is
Jackson et al. 2006
, who used pre-season influenza hospitalizations as an outcome confounding proxy to detect residual confounding bias in evaluating the effect of vaccination among the elderly. More broadly, the widely popular difference-in-differences methodology uses pre-treatment outcome measurements as valid outcome confounding proxies to debias causal effect estimates
(
Sofer et al. 2016
)
.
In contrast, identifying valid treatment confounding proxies may be considerably more challenging in practice.
The presence of invalid proxies can result in biased estimation and misleading inference, yet existing PCI literature offers limited methodological tools for dealing with this problem.

In this paper, assuming that valid outcome confounding proxies are available, we develop a semiparametric framework for PCI about the population average treatment effect (ATE) that accommodates possibly invalid treatment confounding proxies.
After introducing a new class of
fortified
confounding bridge functions
(
Miao et al. 2018
)
, we establish nonparametric identification of the ATE under the assumption that at least
\gamma
out of
K
candidate treatment confounding proxies are valid, for
1\leq\gamma\leq K
a priori set by the analyst, without necessarily knowing their identity.
As such, our proposed approach does not rely on the stringent assumption common in the proximal literature that all specified proxies are valid, providing identifiability conditions as long as
\gamma
is a valid lower bound for the actual number of valid treatment confounding proxies. Beyond identification, we also consider estimation and inference in the proposed framework, and we characterize a locally semiparametric efficiency bound for the ATE under our proposed model and derive a rich class of multiply robust, locally efficient estimators.
These estimators remain consistent if one of several working models is correctly specified, and attain the semiparametric efficiency bound in the union model when all working models are correctly specified. Notably, our method avoids an explicit model selection step, that is, it does not attempt to identify which proxies are invalid. However, as the true number of valid proxies may not be available, the analyst can probe the robustness of empirical results by evaluating whether estimated causal effects remain stable across different values of
\gamma
over a certain range in a form of sensitivity analysis, given that the identified parameter should be constant provided that
\gamma
is a valid lower bound for the underlying true number of valid proxies. A similar strategy may be adopted when many outcome confounding proxies are available, to evaluate whether some are invalid, by repeating the analysis and assessing the sensitivity of results across different subsets of outcome confounding proxies.

Section

5
illustrates a real data application evaluating the effectiveness of right heart catheterization in critically ill patients admitted to the intensive care unit.
Additional discussion and all technical proofs are provided in the Supplementary Material.

This view has generalized Robins’ g-formula to the

;
Miao et al. 2024

(

In this paper, we relax the validity requirement of treatment confounding proxies by assuming that while

Assumption 2

.

Let

(i)

For example, if

Completeness is a foundational concept in statistical inference, closely related to the notion of sufficiency

Recently, it has been applied to identification problems across various areas, including nonparametric instrumental variable regression
(

(

(

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\nu^{*}\subseteq[K]
be the set of indices of valid treatment confounding proxies defined in Definition
2.1
and Assumption
1

(i)

Suppose that Assumption
2
(i) is satisfied and there exists a function
\tilde{h}
satisfying the following integral equation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y|Z,A,X)=\mathbb{E}\{h(W,A,X,Z_{-\nu^{*}})|Z,A,X\}.

(2.1)

Then, we have that
\tau^{*}=\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{h}(W,1,X,Z_{-\nu^{*}})-\tilde{h}(W,0,X,Z_{-\nu^{*}})\}
.

(ii)

Suppose that Assumption
2
(ii) is satisfied and there exists a function
\tilde{q}
satisfying the following integral equation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{q(Z,A,X)|W,A,X,Z_{-\nu^{*}}\}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(A|W,X,Z_{-\nu^{*}})}.

(2.2)

Then, we have that
\tau^{*}=\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{q}(Z,1,X)AY-\tilde{q}(Z,0,X)(1-A)Y\}
.

A function
\tilde{h}
(not necessarily unique) satisfying (

) is referred to as an outcome confounding bridge function, which stands-in as a well-calibrated forecast of the potential outcome; likewise,
\tilde{q}
(not necessarily unique) satisfying (
2.2
) is referred to as a treatment confounding bridge function, and plays a similar role as inverse propensity score weighting under unconfoundness
(
Cui et al. 2024

Miao et al. 2024

but in the proximal setting.
Proposition

demonstrates that in the presence of unmeasured confounders,
\tau^{*}
can be identified leveraging

outcome and treatment confounding proxies via outcome bridge or treatment bridge functions strategies.

Proposition

depends crucially on the accurate knowledge of
\nu^{*}
.
If

do not constitute valid treatment confounding proxies, the identification result breaks down, and applying Proposition
2.1
with invalid proxies will generally lead to the biased estimation of
\tau^{*}
.
Therefore, it is important to select valid
\nu^{*}
for valid estimation and inference about
\tau^{*}
.
However, verifying the validity of
\nu^{*}
in the presence of

is challenging.
For instance, directly testing the exclusion restriction of
Z_{\nu^{*}}
on

conditional on both observed and unobserved confounders (Definition
2.1

(
Kummerfeld et al. 2024
)
.
Given these difficulties, the validity of proxies is often assessed using domain-specific knowledge, which can be subjective and unreliable.

2.2
Nonparametric identification with invalid proxies

To address the aforementioned limitations in the current literature, we develop nonparametric identification of
\tau^{*}
, when the set of indices for valid treatment confounding proxies,
\nu^{*}
, is unknown.
In the absence of knowledge of
\nu^{*}
, we assume that, out of the
K
candidates, at least some of them are valid, as formalized below.

Assumption 3
.

The unknown set of indices of valid treatment confounding proxies (defined in Definition
2.1
),
\nu^{*}
, satisfies
|\nu^{*}|\geq\gamma
for some
\gamma\in[K]
.

Although
\nu^{*}
is unknown, Assumption
3
ensures that at least
\gamma
out of
K
candidates are valid, for
\gamma
a priori known to the analyst. Note that
\gamma
need not be greater than
K/2
(or the largest number of invalid proxies), which distinguishes our assumption from the majority (or plurality) rule in the instrumental variable literature
(
Han 2008
;
Kang et al. 2016
;
Guo et al. 2018
)
.
Identification under this setting can be interpreted as identification within a union of viable causal models, each corresponding to a subset
\nu^{*}\subseteq[K]
satisfying
|\nu^{*}|\geq\gamma
.

A key contribution of the paper is to propose novel classes of bridge functions that identify the ATE under Assumption
3
.
Specifically, we present two identification results: one based on a new class of outcome confounding bridge functions, and an alternative approach based on a new class of treatment confounding bridge functions which also provides robustness to invalid treatment confounding proxies.
Building on these results, we develop semiparametric estimators of
\tau^{*}
in Section
3
.

2.2.1
Identification via fortified outcome confounding bridge functions

To introduce an outcome confounding bridge function under Assumption
3
, we first define the subspace
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
of
L_{2}(Z,A,X)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}:=\{d\in L_{2}(Z,A,X):\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu},X\}=0,\penalty\ \forall\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\geq\gamma}([K])\}.

(2.3)

That is, the subspace
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
contains all functions of
(Z,A,X)
that satisfy
\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu},X\}=0
, for all possible partitions
Z=(Z_{\nu},Z_{-\nu})
with
|\nu|\geq\gamma
. It is characterized solely by the number
\gamma
and thus does not require knowledge of
\nu^{*}
.
By the Law of Iterative Expectations,
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
is equivalently characterized as

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}:=\{d\in L_{2}(Z,A,X):\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu},X\}=0,\penalty\ \forall\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}([K])\}.

.

As an illustrative example of

That is, the set of possible valid proxies is

Moreover, assume

Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}=

\displaystyle\{\bar{A},\bar{A}\bar{Z}_{1},\bar{A}\bar{Z}_{2},\bar{A}\bar{Z}_{1}\bar{Z}_{2},\bar{A}\bar{Z_{1}^{2}},\bar{A}\bar{Z_{2}^{2}},\bar{A}\bar{Z_{1}^{2}}\bar{Z}_{2},\bar{A}\bar{Z}_{1}\bar{Z_{2}^{2}},\bar{A}\bar{Z_{1}^{2}}\bar{Z_{2}^{2}},

Assumption 4
.

There exists a solution

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d(Z,A,X)\{Y-h^{*}(W,A,X)\}]=0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}.

Assumption 5

For any

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)g(U,A,Z_{-\nu^{*}},X)\}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mbox{ for all }\penalty\ d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}+L_{2}(Z_{-\nu^{*}},X)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)g(U,A,Z_{-\nu^{*}},X)\}=0\penalty\ \mbox{ for all }d\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)

Thus, Assumption

Remark 2.4
.

Our nonparametric identification results under Assumption
3
can be interpreted as identification within a union of viable causal models, each corresponding to a subset
\nu^{*}\subseteq[K]
satisfying
|\nu^{*}|\geq\gamma
.
In this respect, our approach is conceptually similar to that of
Sun et al. 2023
, who developed a g-estimation framework for identifying the ATE under a semiparametric structural mean model with additive treatment effect
(
Holland 1988
;
Small 2007
)
, in the presence of unmeasured confounding. Their method assumes that at least
\gamma
of
K
candidate instruments are valid, without requiring prior knowledge of which instruments are invalid.
Like our approach, their framework enables identification and semiparametric theory without relying on model selection, thereby avoiding assumptions such as the majority rule
(
Han 2008
;
Kang et al. 2016
)
or the plurality rule
(
Guo et al. 2018
)
.
Despite this similarity, our setup presents distinct challenges from
Sun et al. 2023
, in part because proximal inference must integrate both treatment and outcome confounding proxies, and therefore a treatment confounding proxy may be invalid either because it has a direct causal effect on the primary outcome, or because it has a causal effect on an outcome confounding proxy, while a candidate instrument in
Sun et al. 2023
can only be invalid in a single way, due to a direct effect on the primary outcome.
In addition,
Sun et al. 2023
’s results largely rely on an important assumption that, whether valid or not, the set of candidate instruments must be both mutually independent and jointly independent of
U
. Neither of these conditions is required of valid or invalid treatment confounding proxies in our framework. Finally, they require homogeneity of the causal effect with respect to the hidden confounders, which we also do not require.

These fundamental differences call for new technical and methodological developments that distinguish our contributions from existing work on robust instrumental variables and robust proximal methods.

2.2.2

3
Semiparametric theory and inference

3.1
Semiparametric local efficiency bound

In this Section, we study efficient and robust estimation for
\tau^{*}
in the presence of potentially invalid treatment confounding proxies.
For a fixed
\gamma\in[K]
, let
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}
denote the collection of regular laws of the observed data, which only assumes the existence of a solution

to (

) and is otherwise unrestricted.
For a formal definition of a regular law, we refer readers to Chapter 3 of

Let

denote the observed data.

To characterize the semiparametric local efficiency bound for

under model

Assumption 8

(Surjectivity)
.

For a fixed
\gamma\in[K]
, let

We note that

Thus, identifying

.

Recall that
h^{*}
solves (
2.4
) if and only if there exists

such that

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
3
–

.

when

and

are viewed as treatment confounding proxies and baseline covariates, respectively.
This observation further illustrates the similarity between our influence function and that of

However, a key distinction is that

in our framework is constructed without knowledge of
\nu^{*}
, whereas the conventional outcome confounding bridge function assumes

is known.

Theorem
3.1
shows that the estimator
\tilde{\tau}
defined by the estimating equation
0=\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathtt{IF}(O;h^{*},q^{*},\ell^{*},\tilde{\tau}))
attains the semiparametric local efficiency bound of
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}
at
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm eff},\gamma}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{n}
is the empirical average operator, i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{n}(V)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}
for any random variable
V
.
However, this estimator is infeasible in practice since it depends on the unknown nuisance functions
h^{*},q^{*}
, and
\ell^{*}
.
Note that the definitions of fortified bridge functions in (
2.4
) and (
2.9
) involve solving Fredholm integral equations of the first kind, which are well-known to often be ill-posed problems.
To illustrate this, for a fixed
\gamma\in[K]
, recall the definition of
\Pi_{\gamma}
in Assumption
8
and let
\Pi_{\gamma}^{*}:\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}\to L_{2}(W,A,X)
denote the conditional expectation given by
\Pi_{\gamma}^{*}(d)=\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)|W,A,X\}
for
d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
.
As previously discussed, the definition of
h^{*}
in (
2.4
) is equivalent to the condition
\Pi_{\gamma}(h^{*})=\Pi_{\gamma}(Y)
, and similarly, the definition of
q^{*}
in (
2.9
) corresponds to the condition
\Pi^{*}_{\gamma}((-1)^{1-A}q^{*})=(-1)^{1-A}/\mathbb{P}(A|W,X)
.
Thus, identifying these functions reduces to solving these inverse problems.
Even if the operators are injective under suitable regularity conditions ensuring the existence and uniqueness of a solution to these equations, the problems may still be effectively ill-posed.
For instance, if
\Pi_{\gamma}
(or
\Pi^{*}_{\gamma}
) is a compact operator and zero is a limit point of its eigenvalues, as can sometimes happen in practice, then the inverse operator
\Pi_{\gamma}^{-1}
(or
(\Pi^{*}_{\gamma})^{-1}
) may become discontinuous.
This discontinuity can make the resulting solutions highly sensitive to small perturbations in the observed data due to sampling variability, leading to instability
(
O’Sullivan 1986
)
.

In the context of conventional PCI, several approaches have been introduced to construct nonparametric estimators of bridge functions (see, e.g.
Singh 2020
;
Kallus et al. 2021
;
Mastouri et al. 2021
;
Ghassami et al. 2022
).
However, as the subspace
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
depends on the unknown conditional law
f(Z,A\mid X)
, directly extending these nonparametric approaches to our setting presents significant new challenges.
To address this issue, we adopt a practical semiparametric modeling strategy that effectively regularizes the problem.
Similar to the approach of
Cui et al. 2024
, we proceed by a priori restricting unknown nuisance functions to follow certain smooth parametric working models, yet still allowing the observed data distribution to otherwise remain unrestricted.
In Section
3.2
, we introduce a rich class of multiply robust estimators for
\tau^{*}
when the parameters of these models are estimated appropriately, without prior knowledge of the valid treatment confounding proxies.

3.2
Estimation and inference of the average treatment effect

We introduce three strategies based on working submodels for the nuisance functions.
We assume that the conditional law

, and that

\hat{\tau}_{{\rm fPMR}}
defined in (
3.5
) is a consistent and asymptotically normal estimator of
\tau^{*}
under the semiparametric union model
\mathcal{M}_{1,\gamma}\cup\mathcal{M}_{2,\gamma}\cup\mathcal{M}_{3,\gamma}
. Furthermore,
\hat{\tau}_{{\rm fPMR}}
is semiparametrically locally efficient under the semiparametric union model, at the intersection submodel
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm eff},\gamma}\cap\mathcal{M}_{1,\gamma}\cap\mathcal{M}_{2,\gamma}\cap\mathcal{M}_{3,\gamma}
.

Theorem
3.2
proves that our proposed estimator is multiply robust without requiring correct specification of a set of valid treatment confounding proxies.
It delivers valid inference provided that at least one of the following holds: the conditional distribution of
(Z,A)
given
X
, and the fortified outcome confounding bridge function are correctly specified; both the fortified outcome confounding bridge function and the conditional expectation of a corresponding residual are correctly specified; or the conditional distribution of
(Z,A)
given
X
and the fortified treatment confounding bridge function are correctly specified.

We now describe two separate approaches for constructing functions in
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbf{a}})
, each based on a specific mapping
d(\cdot;\hat{\mathbf{a}})
from
L_{2}(Z,A,X)
to
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbf{a}})
.
The following lemma generalizes Theorem 1 of
Tchetgen Tchetgen et al. 2010
and provides an explicit representation of
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f^{*}(Z,A|X)
be any fixed user-specified conditional law of
(A,Z)
given
X
such that
(A,Z)
are conditionally independent given
X
under the law
f^{*}
. Moreover, suppose that
f^{*}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the true law
f(Z,A|X)
and
f/f^{*}\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)
.
Then, the set of functions
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
is
\{d^{\dagger}(g)(Z,A,X):g\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)\}
, where

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}(g)(Z,A,X)=\frac{f^{*}}{f}\bigg\{g-\sum_{i=\gamma}^{K}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}^{*}(g|Z_{-\nu},X)\bigg\}.

Here, the coefficients
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=\gamma}^{K}
are explicitly specified in the proof, and the conditional expectations
\mathbb{E}^{*}(\cdot|\cdot)
are taken with respect to the probability law
f^{*}
.

Alternatively, since
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
is the intersection of the closed subspaces, any function
d^{(0)}(Z,A,X)\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)
can be mapped to an element in
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
using the alternating conditional expectations (ACE) algorithm
(
Breiman and Friedman 1985
;
Bickel et al. 1993
)
.
Let
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{M}
enumerate all subsets of
[K]
of size
\gamma
, where
M=\binom{K}{\gamma}
.
Starting from
d^{(0)}
, the ACE algorithm iteratively computes the sequence

\displaystyle d^{(M\cdot m+j)}(Z,A,X)=d^{(M\cdot m+j-1)}(Z,A,X)-\mathbb{E}\{d^{(M\cdot m+j-1)}(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu_{j}},X\}

(3.6)

for
1\leq j\leq M
and
m\geq 1
.
This iterative procedure converges to a function
d^{\ddagger}(d^{(0)})(Z,A,X)
in
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
as
m\to\infty
.

Given a function
d^{(0)}\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)
, let
d^{\dagger}(d^{(0)};\hat{\mathbf{a}})(Z,A,X)
and
d^{\ddagger}(d^{(0)};\hat{\mathbf{a}})(Z,A,X)
denote the functions
d^{\dagger}(d^{(0)})(Z,A,X)
and
d^{\ddagger}(d^{(0)})(Z,A,X)
, respectively, with expectations taken under the law
f(Z,A\mid X;\hat{\mathbf{a}})
.
Then, both
d^{\dagger}
and
d^{\ddagger}
define valid mappings from
L_{2}(Z,A,X)
to
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbf{a}})
.

Remark 3.1
.

When constructing a function in
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}(\hat{\mathbf{a}})
, the mapping
d^{\dagger}
involves weighting by the inverse of the joint conditional law
f(Z,A\mid X;\hat{\mathbf{a}})
,
which can lead to instability if the estimated law is either inaccurate or reaches values near zero.
To mitigate this, we use the
d^{\ddagger}
mapping implemented via the ACE algorithm in both simulations and data analysis.
Following the approach of
Breiman and Friedman 1985
and
Vansteelandt et al. 2008
, we adopt a practical strategy that specifies separate working models for the conditional expectations in equation (
3.6
), fitting each iteratively using standard regression methods.
A limitation of this approach is that the functions obtained via the ACE algorithm lack closed-form expressions, preventing direct computation of the asymptotic variance through derivatives of the estimating equations.
Consequently, following
Vansteelandt et al. 2008
, we employ the nonparametric bootstrap to conduct inference under this practical implementation of the ACE algorithm.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we developed a general semiparametric framework for causal inference in the presence of unmeasured confounding by leveraging possibly invalid treatment confounding proxies through the introduction of new classes of confounding bridge functions, which generalize the standard confounding bridge functions of
(
Miao et al. 2024
)
and
Cui et al. 2024
. We proposed a new class of multiply robust estimators for the ATE, which are locally efficient. The utility of our approach was demonstrated through simulation studies and a real data application.

There are several promising directions for future research. While our framework accommodates invalid treatment confounding proxies, it still relies critically on the validity of the outcome confounding proxies, in the sense that
W
and
A
must be conditionally independent given
U
,
X
, and
Z_{-\nu^{*}}
. A natural extension is to develop identification strategies for the ATE when some of the candidate outcome confounding proxies are invalid, or when both treatment and outcome confounding proxies may be partially invalid; we plan to further explore these strategies in future work.

Another important direction is to explore the use of modern machine learning methods to estimate the fortified confounding bridge functions, as alternatives to positing parametric working models. In the context of conventional PCI, adversarial inference frameworks incorporating reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) or neural networks have shown promise
(
Singh 2020
;
Kallus et al. 2021
;
Mastouri et al. 2021
;
Ghassami et al. 2022
)
. However, extending these approaches to our setting is nontrivial, as the structure of
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
is not readily approximated by conventional function classes such as RKHSs or neural networks.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)\ell^{\prime}(Z_{-\nu^{*}},X)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu^{*}},X\}\ell^{\prime}(Z_{-\nu^{*}},X)]=0.

Thus,

solves the class of moment equations (
2.4
).

Now, let

be any solution to (
2.4
).
Since

and

are solutions to (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d(Z,A,X)\{h(W,A,X)-h^{\prime}(W,A,X)\}]=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}.

Then, by the completeness assumption, it follows that

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(W,A,X)-h(W,A,X)=\mathbb{E}\{h^{\prime}(W,A,X)-h(W,A,X)|X\}=:\ell^{\prime}(X).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\tau^{*}=\mathbb{E}\{h^{\prime}(W,1,X)-h^{\prime}(W,0,X)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[h(W,1,X)+\ell^{\prime}(X)-\{h(W,0,X)+\ell^{\prime}(X)\}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{h(W,1,X)-h(W,0,X)\},

establishing the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A.2
.

In the proof of Theorem
2.2
, we have shown that such

satisfies (

Thus, we only need to show that

also solves (

Recall that
W\perp(Z_{\nu^{*}},A)|U,Z_{-\nu^{*}},X
.
Then, by the Law of Iterative Expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{q^{\prime}(Z,A,X)|W,A,X\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}\{q^{\prime}(Z,A,X)|U,W,A,Z_{-\nu^{*}},X\}|W,A,X]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}\{q^{\prime}(Z,A,X)|U,A,Z_{-\nu^{*}},X\}|W,A,X]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(A|U,Z_{-\nu^{*}},X)}\bigg|W,A,X\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(A|U,W,Z_{-\nu^{*}},X)}\bigg|W,A,X\bigg\}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(A|W,X)}.

Thus,

solves (
2.9
).

Now, to show the uniqueness, suppose that both

and

satisfy (

Then, by the completeness assumption,

, that is,

almost surely.
Thus, the solution

to (

A.3
Sufficient conditions for the existence of the bridge functions

By following a similar approach as in
Miao et al. 2018
, we provide sufficient conditions that ensure the existence of a solution to (
2.4
).
Sufficient conditions that guarantee the existence of a fortified treatment confounding bridge function can be established in a similar fashion and are omitted.
We first state Picard’s theorem, which gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of the solution to the first-kind equations.

Theorem A.3

(Theorem 15.18 of
Kress 1989
)
.

Let
A:X\to Y
be a compact linear operator with singular system
(\mu_{n},\varphi_{n},g_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
The equation of the first kind

\displaystyle A\varphi=f

is solvable if and only if
f\in N(A^{*})^{\perp}=\{f^{\prime}:A^{*}(f^{\prime})=0\}^{\perp}
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu_{n}^{-2}|\langle f,g_{n}\rangle|^{2}<\infty
.

We first show that the existence of a solution to (
2.4
) is equivalent to the existence of solutions to some non-homogeneous equations.
For each
x
, let
L_{2}(W,A|X=x)
and
L_{2}(Z,A|X=x)
be the spaces of square-integrable functions of
(W,A)
and
(Z,A)
given
X=x
, respectively, which are equipped with the
L_{2}
inner product.
Similarly, let
L_{2}(Y,W,Z,A|X=x)
be the space of square-integrable functions of
(Y,W,Z,A)
given
X=x
.
Define

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}:=\{d\in L_{2}(Z,A|X=x):\mathbb{E}(d|Z_{-\nu},X=x)=0\penalty\ \forall\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}([K])\},

and let
\Pi_{\gamma,x}:L_{2}(Y,W,Z,A|X=x)\to\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
.

Lemma A.1
.

There exists a solution to (
2.4
) if and only if for each
X=x
, there exists a solution to the non-homogeneous equation
\Pi_{\gamma,x}(h)=\Pi_{\gamma,x}(Y)
for
h\in L_{2}(W,A|X=x)
.

Proof.

First, we assume that there exists a solution to (
2.4
).
This means that
\Pi_{\gamma}(Y-h^{*})=0
, that is,
\Pi_{\gamma}(h^{*})=\Pi_{\gamma}(Y)
.
Choose any function
d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
.
Then, it can be shown that
\mathbbm{1}(X=x)d(Z,A)\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
so that for any
d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}(X=x)d(Z,A)\{Y-h^{*}(W,A,X)\}]=0,

which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d(Z,A)\{Y-h^{*}(W,A,x)\}|X=x]=0.

Since this equation holds for all
d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
,
h^{*}(W,A,x)
solves the non-homogeneous equation
\Pi_{\gamma,x}(h)=\Pi_{\gamma,x}(Y)
.

Conversely, assume that for each
x
, there exists
h_{x}\in L_{2}(W,A|X=x)
satisfying
\Pi_{\gamma,x}(h)=\Pi_{\gamma,x}(Y)
.
Let
h^{*}\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)
be given by
h^{*}(W,A,x)=h_{x}(W,A)
for each
x
.
Now, for any
d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma},\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}([K])
and
x
,
\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu},X=x\}=0
,
implying that
d(Z,A,x)\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d(Z,A,X)\{h^{*}(W,A,X)-Y\}|X=x]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[d(Z,A,x)\{h_{x}(W,A)-Y\}|X=x]

\displaystyle=0.

This equality holds for any
d\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}
and
x
, implying that
h^{*}
satisfies (
2.4
), completing the proof.
∎

Abusing some notations, denote
\Pi_{\gamma,x}
to be the operator by restriction the domain of
\Pi_{\gamma,x}
to
L_{2}(W,A|X=x)
for each
x
.
Then, by Lemma
A.1
, we need to show that
\Pi_{\gamma,x}(h)=\Pi_{\gamma,x}(Y)
is solvable.
To show this, we assume the following conditions for each
x
:

(i)

\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}}\int\int f_{W|Z,A,X}(w|Z,a,x)f_{Z|W,A,X}(Z|w,a,x){\rm d}w{\rm d}Z<\infty
;

(ii)

For
g\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
,
\mathbb{E}\{g(Z,A)|W,A,X=x\}=0
implies
g=0
almost surely;

(iii)

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i,x}^{-2}|\langle g_{Y,x}(Z,A),g_{i,x}\rangle_{L_{2}(Z,A|X=x}|^{2}<\infty
, where
\{\mu_{i,x},\phi_{i,x},g_{i,x}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is the singular system of
\Pi_{\gamma,x}
and
g_{Y,x}=\Pi_{\gamma,x}(Y)
.

We first show that under the above assumptions, the operator
\Pi_{\gamma,x}:L_{2}(W,A|X=x)\to\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
is compact.
To see this, let
\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*}:\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}\to L_{2}(W,A|X=x)
be the conditional expectation operator defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*}(g)=\mathbb{E}\{g(Z,A)|W,A,X=x\}.

Then,
\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
\Pi_{\gamma,x}
.
To verify this, for any
h\in L_{2}(W,A|X=x)
and
g\in\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
,

\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{\gamma,x}(h),g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\Pi_{\gamma,x}(h)g(Z,A)|X=x\}=\mathbb{E}\{h(W,A)g(Z,A)|X=x\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[h(W,A)\mathbb{E}\{g(Z,A)|W,A,X=x\}|X=x]

\displaystyle=\langle h,\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*}(g)\rangle_{L_{2}(W,A|X=x)},

which proves the claim.
Moreover, we remark that
\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*}
is the restriction of the conditional expectation operator
\Pi_{x}^{*}:L_{2}(Z,A|X=x)\to L_{2}(W,A|X=x)
to
\mathbb{H}_{\gamma,x}
.
Therefore,
\Pi_{\gamma,x}
is compact if and only if
\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*}
is compact, and
\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*}
is compact given that
\Pi_{x}^{*}
is compact (see, e.g., Theorem 4.4 in Chapter II of
Conway 1990
).
To show that
\Pi_{x}^{*}
is compact, note that for any
(w,a)
,

\displaystyle\Pi_{x}^{*}(g)(w,a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{g(Z,A)|W=w,A=a,X=x\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}}\int g(Z,a^{\prime})f(Z,a^{\prime}|x)\frac{\mathbbm{I}(a=a^{\prime})f(w,Z,a|x)}{f(Z,a^{\prime}|x)f(w,a|x)}{\rm d}Z

\displaystyle=\langle k(\cdot,(w,a)),g\rangle_{L_{2}(Z,A|X=x)},

where the kernel
k
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle k((Z,a^{\prime}),(w,a))=\frac{\mathbbm{I}(a=a^{\prime})f(w,Z,a|x)}{f(w,a^{\prime}|x)f(Z,a|x)}.

Therefore, as shown in page 5659 of
Carrasco et al. 2007
,
\Pi_{x}^{*}
is compact given that

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}}\int\int\frac{\mathbbm{I}(a=a^{\prime})f^{2}(w,Z,a|x)}{f^{2}(w,a^{\prime}|x)f^{2}(Z,a|x)}f(w,a^{\prime}|x)f(Z,a|x){\rm d}w{\rm d}Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}}\int\int f_{W|Z,A,X}(w|Z,a,x)f_{Z|W,A,X}(Z|w,a,x){\rm d}w{\rm d}Z<\infty,

which is assumed in the above.

Next, we show that
N(\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*})^{\perp}=L_{2}(Z,A|X=x)
, which ensures that
\Pi_{\gamma,x}(Y)
belongs to
N(\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*})^{\perp}
trivially.
To see this, remark that
g\in N(\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*})
if and only if
\mathbb{E}\{g(Z,A)|W,A,X=x\}=0
.
But by the above assumptions, this implies
g=0
, so that
N(\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*})
contains only the zero function, leading to
N(\Pi_{\gamma,x}^{*})^{\perp}=L_{2}(Z,A|X=x)
.
Putting the pieces together with the above assumption on the singular system of
\Pi_{\gamma,x}
, the conditions of Theorem 15.18 of
Kress 1989
are satisfied.
Consequently, for each
x
, there exists a solution
h_{x}\in L_{2}(W,A|X=x)
satisfying
\Pi_{\gamma,x}(h_{x})=\Pi_{\gamma,x}(Y)
, which, establishes the existence of a solution to (
2.4
) by Lemma
A.1
.

.

By comparing the terms in (

\alpha_{\gamma}=1

Now, consider
i>\gamma
and fix
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])
with
\tilde{\nu}\subseteq\nu
.
To determine
b_{\nu}

such that
\nu^{\prime}\cup\tilde{\nu}=\nu

For each
\gamma\leq j\leq i
, we count the number of subsets
\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{j}([K])

\nu^{\prime}\cup\tilde{\nu}=\nu
in the following.
Note that such
\nu^{\prime}

\nu\setminus\tilde{\nu}
, where

\nu^{\prime}

\tilde{\nu}
.
The number of such subsets

Thus, the coefficients of

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=\gamma}^{i}\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K]),\nu^{\prime}\cup\tilde{\nu}=\nu}\alpha_{i}=-\sum_{j=\gamma}^{i}\binom{\gamma}{i-j}\alpha_{i}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}\supseteq\bigg\{d(Z,A,X)-\sum_{i=\gamma}^{K}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu},X\}:d\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)\bigg\}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}\subseteq\bigg\{d(Z,A,X)-\sum_{i=\gamma}^{K}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}\{d(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu},X\}:d\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)\bigg\},

Now, we consider the general setting where
(Z,A)
may not be conditionally independent given
X
.
Let

v(Z,A,X)\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)

v

\displaystyle v(Z,A,X):=\frac{f^{*}}{f}\bigg\{d-\sum_{i=\gamma}^{K}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}^{*}(d|Z_{-\nu},X)\bigg\}.

Since
f^{*}

f

f^{*}/f
is the Radon-Nikodým derivative of
f^{*}
with respect to
f
.
Thus, for any
\tilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}([K])

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(v|Z_{-\nu},X)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{f^{*}}{f}\bigg\{d-\sum_{i=\gamma}^{K}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}^{*}(d|Z_{-\nu},X)\bigg|Z_{-\tilde{\nu}},X\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f(Z_{-\tilde{\nu}},X)}\mathbb{E}^{*}\bigg\{d-\sum_{i=\gamma}^{K}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}^{*}(d|Z_{-\nu},X)\bigg|Z_{-\tilde{\nu}},X\bigg\}=0.

This implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\gamma}\supseteq\bigg\{\frac{f^{*}}{f}\bigg\{d-\sum_{i=\gamma}^{K}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}^{*}(d|Z_{-\nu},X)\bigg\}:d\in L_{2}(Z,A,X)\bigg\}\cap L_{2}(Z,A,X).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{*}(d(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu},X)=\frac{f(Z_{-\nu},X)}{f^{*}(Z_{-\nu},X)}\mathbb{E}\{v(Z,A,X)|Z_{-\nu},X\}=0,

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{f^{*}}{f}\bigg\{d-\sum_{i=\gamma}^{K}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{i}([K])}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}^{*}(d|Z_{-\nu},X)\bigg\}=\frac{f^{*}}{f}d(Z,A,X)=v(Z,A,X).

This completes the proof.
