Cognitive dispersion profiles and prediction of cognitive change in early‐onset dementias: Results from LEADS

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONResearch into cognitive dispersion – a cognitive process score measuring the intra‐individual variability (IIV) across a single testing session – suggests utility in neurodegenerative populations. Given widespread deficits observed in sporadic early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (EOAD), however, it is unclear if examining cognitive dispersion shows benefit in this condition.METHODSA total of 309 participants (188 amyloid‐positive EOAD, 43 amyloid‐negative early‐onset dementia [EOnonAD], 78 cognitively normal [CN]) completed neuropsychological testing twice over 12 months. Dispersion‐related differences among groups were assessed, as was cognitive dispersion's capacity to predict domain‐specific cognitive trajectories, and its convergence with imaging biomarkers.RESULTSEOAD participants displayed higher cognitive dispersion than EOnonAD participants, and associations with EOAD‐specific biomarkers. Additionally, cognitive dispersion was associated with 12‐month reliable change across several cognitive domains.DISCUSSIONThese preliminary results examine cognitive dispersion in sporadic EOAD. When used with baseline cognitive performance, cognitive dispersion measures may enrich future clinical trials in EOAD by enhancing treatment monitoring over time.

BACKGROUND

Clinical neuropsychology has historically focused on aggregated summary scores to interpret cognitive test results. 1 Cognitive process scores, however, can inform “how” an individual performs in addition to their magnitude of difficulty. 2 When paired with aggregated summary scores, process scores can improve clinical decision making, 2 enhance monitoring of treatment response, 3 and identify subtleties in performance that would otherwise be overlooked. 4 Intra‐individual variability (IIV) is one such cognitive process score that has developed broad support. Conceptualized as the variation observed in cognitive performance, IIV reconsiders performance inconsistency from psychometric noise to being clinically meaningful. 5 Several methods exist to measure IIV, with cognitive dispersion – the intra‐session deviation/scatter in an individual's test performance around a central ability level – being the focus currently. 5 , 6 Whereas some performance variability is natural, 7 lower levels of cognitive dispersion are desirable.

Research on cognitive dispersion has suggested its clinical utility in populations ranging from cognitively normal to impaired. Recent meta‐analyses of IIV suggest that participants with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia due to Alzheimer's disease (AD) often display higher dispersion than those who are cognitively intact. 5 , 8 IIV is also associated with AD‐related biomarkers of cerebral β‐amyloid and tau deposition. 8 Meta‐analytic findings of longitudinal studies have observed that IIV is associated with subsequent cognitive decline and/or conversion to MCI/dementia. 9 Although increases in IIV are associated with a variety of mechanisms, the primary drivers appear to be overall brain health/integrity 10 , 11 and attention/executive functioning. 12 Specifically, greater inconsistency/dispersion are associated with reduced attention and white matter connectivity in regions associated with cognitive flexibility (e.g., superior longitudinal fasciculus). 13 , 14 Greater dispersion additionally discerns patients with frontotemporal dementia from cognitively normal (CN) individuals, and is associated with greater functional declines in such patients. 15

Given the executive contribution to cognitive dispersion, it may be a particularly relevant process tool in early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (EOAD). EOAD is a rare condition developing prior to the age of 65, 16 with recent findings revealing that patients with sporadic EOAD display disproportionately worse impairments in executive functioning, processing speed, and visuospatial skills than other domains (and than late‐onset AD). 17 Additionally, individuals with EOAD experience greater atrophy in the inferior and superior parietal lobules, posterior cingulate, and mid‐ and caudal‐lateral temporal cortices 18 – regions associated with attentional and executive control, and that have increased connectivity to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. 19 Further, research has observed higher frequency of non‐amnestic clinical phenotypes in sporadic EOAD compared to late‐onset AD (e.g., dysexecutive‐AD, primary progressive aphasia [PPA], and posterior cortical atrophy [PCA]), 16 despite common amyloid‐ and tau‐pathology.

While these findings suggest that cognitive dispersion may be heightened in EOAD, other studies have revealed widespread deficits across the cognitive profile in EOAD 20 , 21 – which may diminish capacity to observe IIV across domains. At present, it is subsequently unclear the extent to which EOAD participants are susceptible to IIV. The current purpose is therefore to characterize cognitive dispersion in participants with early‐onset dementia. Based on the implicated anatomical and cognitive profiles of EOAD, it is hypothesized that cognitive dispersion will be elevated in early‐onset dementia patients, and higher in participants with sporadic amyloid‐positive EOAD compared to CN or amyloid‐negative cognitively impaired participants (“early‐onset non‐AD” [EOnonAD]). Also, higher cognitive dispersion is anticipated to be associated with greater 12‐month reliable cognitive change 22 across a variety of domains. Further, higher dispersion is expected to be associated with worse AD imaging biomarker outcomes (β‐amyloid and tau deposition, hippocampal volume, and EOAD atrophy signature). These results will provide information about the utility of IIV as a process tool in early‐onset dementia. Such tools could complement traditional summary scores in future clinical trials with EOAD populations to enhance monitoring of treatment responses by considering both changes within and the interplay between cognitive domains. 3 Finally, as research into reliable change methods have primarily focused on baseline cognition and demographic/trial‐related factors influencing cognitive change over time, 23 current findings will extend this literature by highlighting the role that intra‐session variance across domains can play on predicting individuals’ cognitive trajectories.

METHODS

Participants

The current study involved 309 participants from the ongoing Longitudinal EOAD Study (LEADS), 24 including 188 amyloid‐positive EOAD, 43 amyloid‐negative EOnonAD, and 78 CN participants. All participants possessed baseline and 12‐month data that passed quality‐control checks from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) for completeness and accuracy. The reader is encouraged to review Apostolova et al. 24 for a thorough discussion of details regarding LEADS protocols and inclusion/exclusion criteria. Briefly, LEADS includes 18 sites throughout the United States and five sites internationally and was initiated in 2018 with a primary goal of comprehensively characterizing sporadic EOAD from clinical, biomarker, genetic, and pathologic perspectives. Participants are included in LEADS if they are diagnosed with cognitive impairment between 40 and 64 years of age, are in adequate general health and do not have a primary psychiatric or non‐cognitive neurological disorder, are fluent in English, and possess a knowledgeable informant. The presence of confirmed pathogenic mutations in amyloid precursor protein (APP), presenilin‐1 (PSEN1), presenilin‐2 (PSEN2), microtubule associated protein tau (MAPT), granulin precursor aka progranulin (GRN), or chromosome 9 open reading frame 72 (C9ORF72) genes are exclusionary, to reduce the likelihood of non‐sporadic forms of EOAD in LEADS. Each site is responsible for diagnostic classification of their participants at enrollment, with independent and formal consensus reviews occurring locally with some combination of cognitive neurologists, geriatric psychiatrists, and neuropsychologists using available clinical information (e.g., clinical, genetic, biomarker, cognitive). Cerebral β‐amyloid burden via positron emission tomography (PET) is used to classify EOAD versus EOnonAD in cognitively impaired participants, with EOAD participants being amyloid‐positive at baseline and EOnonAD participants being amyloid‐negative. EOAD and EOnonAD participants are additionally required to have a baseline Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR®) 25 scale global score ≤ 1.0 at enrollment, and CN participants have a Mini‐Mental Status Examination (MMSE) 26 score ≥ 24 and a CDR global score of 0. Regulatory oversight is managed by the Indiana University School of Medicine Single Institutional Review Board, and informed consent is obtained in writing from study participants or their legally authorized representatives.

Procedure

Cognitive battery

The specifics about the LEADS neuropsychological battery have been previously documented. 24 All participants received the NACC Uniform Data Set (UDS) 3.0, 27 which includes the Benson Complex Figure Recall Test (Immediate and Delayed), 28 Craft Story 21 Memory Test (Immediate and Delayed), 29 Multilingual Naming Test (MINT), 30 Animal (Semantic) Fluency, 31 Vegetable (Semantic) Fluency, 27 Letter (Phonemic; F & L) Fluency, 27 Trail Making Test Parts A and B (TMT‐A and TMT‐B, 32 Number Span Test (Forward and Backward), 27 and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). 33 Supplemental measures included the AD Assessment Scale‐Cognitive (ADAS‐Cog), 34 the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT; Total and Delayed), 35 the CDR, and the MMSE; the latter two were administered exclusively for inclusion criteria. As these measures are likely familiar to most clinically‐focused AD specialists, readers are referred to the respective test manuals for more detail. This comprehensive neuropsychological battery was administered both at baseline and approximately 12 months later, with the same test version administered each time.

Calculation of cognitive dispersion

Cognitive dispersion is operationalized in the current study using the Intra‐individual Standard Deviation (ISD), which is defined as the average variance observed across cognitive measures within a single test session for an individual. Using data from the 12 primary cognitive measures in the UDS (listed above, minus the MoCA), we computed the ISD separately for each participant following previously established methods. 6 , 36 First, raw scores were converted to normative z‐scores after demographic correction (age‐, sex‐, and education‐adjustment) using established norms. 27 Next, the z‐scores were transformed into T‐scores (mean = 50, SD = 10) and the average T‐score across all measures was calculated (“Mean T”). This was used to compute the deviation between the session‐wide “Mean T” and the subtest‐specific T score. The ISD reflected the square root of the average of these squared deviations across all 12 subtests, which can be mathematically represented as follows: ISD = ((∑(T–Mean T) 2 )/12)^0.5. Higher ISD values reflect greater cognitive dispersion, or IIV. Of note, although the coefficient of variation (CoV) – which is ISD divided by the “Mean T” – is commonly calculated in the cognitive dispersion literature to control for mean level of performance, this practice has been questioned 12 because the “Mean T” is used in the original calculation of the ISD, thus the dividend and divisor of the CoV equation are dependent. We have currently followed recommendations to exclude the CoV and instead include an independent measure of global cognition when modeling regression analyses. 12

PET imaging

Procedures used for amyloid and tau PET imaging followed established protocols for LEADS. 37 For amyloid, 18F‐Florbetaben PET had an acquisition time of 90 to 110 min post‐injection of ∼8 mCi of ligand (four 5‐min frames). For tau, 18F‐Flortaucipir PET had an acquisition time of 75 to 105 min post‐injection of ∼10 mCi of ligand (six 5‐min frames). 37 For amyloid and tau PET, image acquisition, reconstruction parameters, quality control, and image standardization aligned with previously published ADNI‐3 PET procedures. 38 PET data were uploaded to the Laboratory of Neuroimaging (LONI) at the University of Southern California. Amyloid burden was defined as a global composite centiloid value following correction for whole cerebellum uptake, and tau burden was defined as a meta‐Temporal region of interest standardized‐uptake value ratio (SUVR) after correction for inferior cerebellar gray matter.

Magnetic resonance imaging

The reader is referred to Touroutoglou et al. for a thorough description of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) procedures in LEADS. 18 MRI data were acquired via 3.0 Tesla scanners using a three‐dimensional accelerated MPRAGE/IRSPGR T1‐weighted sagittal sequence. Parameters were set at repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.98 ms, flip angle = 9°, field of view = 240×256 mm, in‐plane voxel size = 1 mm isotropic, 208 sagittal slices, and double‐time acceleration, with some slight variation of parameters based on vendor and system type. 18 Quality‐controlled MPRAGE data were then normalized and processed using FreeSurfer v6.0. Baseline bilateral hippocampal volumes were defined as age‐, sex‐, and intracranial volume‐adjusted w‐scores, and the LEADS‐specific EOAD Atrophy Signature was defined by age‐ and sex‐adjusted w‐scores from a composite of regions of interest (ROIs) including the caudal lateral temporal cortex, inferior parietal lobule, and posterior cingulate and precuneus cortices. 3 , 18

Analyses

Demographic analyses

For analyses comparing ISD between diagnostic (CN, EOAD, and EOnonAD) or phenotypic (amnestic, dysexecutive [frontal‐variant‐AD], PPA, and PCA) groups, analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted between the respective groups for continuous demographic (e.g., age and education), baseline cognitive performance, and AD biomarker variables. Significant omnibus test results were followed with independent samples t tests between individual groups. Two‐way chi squared analyses were conducted between respective diagnostic or phenotypic groups to compare categorical demographic variables (e.g., sex, racial/ethnic diversity, APOE ε4 status). Variables with significant differences between the respective groups were subsequently used as covariates in group‐based comparisons of cognitive dispersion.

Additionally, bivariate correlations between demographic variables and ISD were examined to determine covariates for subsequent hierarchical regression and partial correlation analyses.

Diagnostic and phenotypic discrimination by cognitive dispersion

To determine differences in ISD between diagnostic groups, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted after accounting for relevant demographic variables. Following significant omnibus testing, post‐hoc ANCOVAs were conducted for each diagnostic group comparison.

To formally assess the ability of cognitive dispersion to discriminate EOAD versus EOnonAD groups, receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC‐AUC) analyses were conducted with ISD and relevant covariates. For the interpretation of ROC‐AUC values, the current study followed previously established guidelines: 39 ROC‐AUC values < 0.600 being a “failure”, 0.600 ≤ values ≤ 0.699 being “poor”, 0.700 ≤ values ≤ 0.799 being “fair”, 0.800 ≤ values ≤ 0.899 being “good”, and values ≥ 0.900 being “excellent”.

To determine differences in ISD between clinical phenotypes, ANCOVA was again conducted after accounting for relevant demographic variables and diagnosis, with post‐hoc ANCOVAs being run for each phenotypic group comparison. Given that sample sizes among some clinical phenotypes were small and that CN participants did not possess a clinical phenotype, these analyses were conducted only on EOAD and EOnonAD participants and were considered supplementary.

Cognitive dispersion estimating 12‐month change in early‐onset dementia

Calculation of change scores. Reliable change methods were used in the current design to assess change over time given their improved statistical rigor relative to other methods (including a basic difference method: Time 2 – Time 1). 40 To calculate 12‐month reliable change, previously published standardized regression‐based (SRB) prediction equations 3 were applied to the current baseline and 12‐month follow‐up cognitive performances of participants in LEADS. This resulted in individual normalized deviations of session‐to‐session change (or Z‐scores) for each of the 16 primary variables in the LEADS neuropsychological battery. Specifically, these Z‐score values reflect the difference between a participant's Observed 12‐month follow‐up score (T2 ) and their Predicted 12‐month follow‐up score (T2'), normalized by the standard error of the estimate (SEest ) of the regression (Z = [T2 – T2']/ SEest ). 22 , 41 Using this equation, positive Z‐scores reflect follow‐up performances that are higher than predicted, and negative Z‐scores reflect follow‐up performances that are lower than predicted. To ensure ease of interpretation, the directionality of Z‐scores was reversed for TMT‐A, TMT‐B, and ADAS‐Cog, so that a positive Z‐score has the same interpretation regardless of task. Z‐score cognitive composites were then calculated by taking the average of the domain‐specific individual Z‐scores for the following domains, based on previous LEADS studies: Episodic Immediate Memory, Episodic Delayed Memory, Global Cognition, Language, Attention/ Processing Speed, Executive Functioning, and Visuospatial skills (see Table S1 for listing of specific variables per domain). 20

Estimation of change. Following the calculation of these composite Z‐scores, a series of hierarchical linear regression analyses were conducted to determine whether cognitive dispersion could incrementally predict domain‐specific 12‐month reliable cognitive change. First, with cognitive change being the dependent variable, Step 1 of each analysis included relevant predictor variables of demographics and diagnosis (EOAD, EOnonAD, or CN), and Step 2 included ISD across all participants in the LEADS cohort. These results would establish whether cognitive dispersion incrementally predicts cognitive change beyond diagnosis. Next, to assess whether ISD was a significant predictor of 12‐month change above and beyond the impact of diagnosis and global cognition, a supplementary set of hierarchical regression analyses were conducted across all three diagnostic groups with Step 1 including relevant demographics, diagnosis, and the baseline Global Cognition composite, and Step 2 including ISD. Note, these latter analyses are considered supplementary given the high multi‐collinearity known to exist between baseline global cognition and cognitive change. 42 Finally, sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating the primary hierarchical regression analyses after removing participants with non‐amnestic phenotypes; this was designed to ensure that primary results were not driven by phenotypic differences.

Association of cognitive dispersion and imaging biomarkers in early‐onset dementia

Finally, to examine the relationships between cognitive dispersion and AD‐related imaging biomarkers, partial correlations were conducted between ISD and amyloid mean global Centiloid, tau meta‐Temporal ROI SUVR, bilateral hippocampal w‐score, and bilateral EOAD Atrophy Signature w‐score values after accounting for relevant demographic variables and diagnosis.

Measures of effect size were expressed throughout as η 2 (ANOVA/ANCOVA), r2 (regression and partial correlations), and AUC‐ROC values. To protect against multiple comparisons, the use of multivariate methods and Holm's sequential Bonferroni method of family‐wise adjustment 43 was applied to the two‐tailed alpha level for all primary analyses.

RESULTS

Demographic analyses

Table 1 reports the demographic and clinical characteristics of participants. Significant differences were observed between diagnostic groups for age, education, sex, and racial/ethnic diversity (ps = 0.001–0.04), whereas no difference was seen for APOE ε4 (p = 0.10). Specifically, CN participants were younger, had greater educational exposure, and greater racial/ethnic diversity than the EOAD group (ps < 0.001). They also had greater racial/ethnic diversity and a greater proportion of females than the EOnonAD group (ps = 0.02–0.03). No differences were evident between the EOAD and EOnonAD groups for age, education, sex, nor racial/ethnic diversity (ps = 0.31–0.99). Additionally, differences were observed between diagnostic groups for amyloid and tau burden, hippocampal atrophy, cortical thickness in ROIs associated with the EOAD atrophy signature, and a composite of baseline global cognition (ps < 0.001). The EOAD group was more impaired than both the CN and EOnonAD groups on the global composite (ps < 0.001), and possessed greater amyloid and tau burden, and greater atrophy in the EOAD signature ROIs (p < 0.001). The EOAD group possessed a greater proportion of participants with more severe CDR Global scores (p = 0.02) at baseline. The EOnonAD group was more impaired than the CN group on the global composite (p < 0.001), and possessed greater hippocampal atrophy (p = 0.04). No differences were observed between EOAD and EOnonAD groups for hippocampal atrophy (p = 0.32). As a reminder, the calculation of the ISD variable already included adjustment for age, education, and sex, therefore these variables were not included as additional covariates. Consequently, racial/ethnic diversity and global cognition were used as covariates in diagnosis‐related ANCOVA analyses.

The proportions of clinical phenotypes were similar between EOAD and EOnonAD groups (p = 0.20), consequently the groups were combined for the supplemental clinical phenotype‐related analyses. To assess for covariates, demographic analyses revealed that there were no differences between clinical phenotypes for age, education, sex, nor racial/ethnic minority (ps = 0.11–0.91). APOE ε4 status and racial/ethnic diversity were subsequently used as covariates, as were diagnostic status and baseline cognition.

Diagnostic and phenotypic discrimination by cognitive dispersion

Results of omnibus ANCOVA analyses revealed that significant differences existed for ISD between the diagnostic groups, even after accounting for racial/ethnic diversity and global cognition (F(2,286) = 3.86, p = 0.02, η 2 = 0.026). Post‐hoc comparisons revealed that the EOAD group displayed greater cognitive dispersion (i.e., a higher ISD value) than the EOnonAD group (Figure 1A; p = 0.03, η 2 = 0.022). Although Figure 1A reveals a sizable difference between EOAD and CN group means for ISD values (p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.269), the difference in baseline cognition between these two groups was so large (η 2 = 0.300) that incorporating it in the analyses resulted in only a trend for ISD (p = 0.06, η 2 = 0.014). There was similarly no ISD difference between CN and EOnonAD groups (p = 0.83, η 2 = 0.001) after baseline cognition adjustment. Additionally, AUC‐ROC analysis (Figure 2) revealed that a model including ISD and covariates (racial/ethnic diversity and global cognition) discriminated between EOAD and EOnonAD group membership at an AUC value of 0.795 (95% CI of 0.709–0.882).

Cognitive dispersion estimating 12‐month change in early‐onset dementia

Hierarchical linear regression indicated that cognitive dispersion can estimate 12‐month reliable cognitive change across a host of cognitive domains (Table 2). A series of regression models including demographic and clinical variables (racial/ethnic diversity and APOE ε4 status), diagnostic group, and ISD significantly predicted 12‐month change on all five cognitive domain composites, plus the composite of global cognition (all ps < 0.001, r 2s = 0.18–0.50). Specifically, higher ISD values were associated with greater 12‐month declines across domain composites of Immediate Memory, Delayed Memory, Language, Processing Speed/Attention, Executive Functioning, Visuospatial Skills, and Global Cognition. Examination of r 2 values in Table 2 reveals that ISD incrementally predicted longitudinal change for each domain above and beyond demographics and diagnostic classification (ps < 0.001, r 2s = 0.09–0.37). When a sensitivity analysis was conducted by repeating the models after participants with non‐amnestic phenotypes were excluded, the results were the same across domains (ps = 0.001–0.002) except for Language (r 2 value of 0.019, p = 0.07). Additionally, when supplementary analyses repeated the primary hierarchical linear regression analyses by further including an independent baseline global cognitive composite as a covariate, all models remained significant (ps < 0.001, r 2s = 0.32–0.72). ISD continued to incrementally predict longitudinal change for the domains of Delayed Memory, Processing Speed/Attention, Visuospatial Skills, and Global Cognition, above and beyond demographic and clinical variables, diagnostic classification, and global cognition (ps = 0.001–0.004, r 2s = 0.01–0.11). A trend was observed in the expected direction for Executive Functioning (p = 0.08, r 2 = 0.007). Conversely, ISD was no longer incrementally predictive for domains of Immediate Memory and Language (ps = 0.42–0.56, r 2s = 0.001) .

DISCUSSION

The current study examined cognitive dispersion as a cognitive process tool in early‐onset dementia. Participants with sporadic EOAD and EOnonAD displayed elevated levels of IIV at present. Recently published demographically‐corrected clinical norms for ISD 6 reveal just how severely elevated these ISD values are relative to CN age‐, education‐, and race/ethnicity‐similar peers. Specifically, average ISD values of 19.0 and 11.6 (for EOAD and EOnonAD, respectively) are 2.85SD and 1.50SD below the mean for their demographically‐similar peers, suggesting a clinical deficit (< 1st and 7th percentiles, respectively). Consequently, these conditions reflect impairments in both mean and dispersion of cognitive performances at baseline. These results coincide with research on IIV in later‐onset AD that consistently suggest that participants with MCI and AD display higher dispersion than CN samples. 5 , 8 Although comparing raw ISD values between studies is challenging given the limited reporting and variety of methods used, current mean values for EOAD and EOnonAD exceed those of physically frail older adults 44 and a cohort of patients with Dementia with Lewy Bodies. 6 As such, IIV appears to be quite pronounced in early‐onset dementia.

Our results also reflect initial documentation of differences in IIV in early‐onset dementia samples. Hypotheses were supported showing that ISD values were higher in EOAD than EOnonAD (Figure 1A), and that the discrimination of EOAD from EOnonAD by ISD and covariates was considered “fair” (AUC = 0.795, with 95% CI ranging from “fair to good” [0.709–0.882]; Figure 2). Although these effect sizes were small (η 2 = 0.022), differences persisted after accounting for disease severity, suggesting that IIV is not just a proxy for clinical severity and reduced neuronal integrity. 11 Relatedly and consistent with expectations, Table 3 displays that cognitive dispersion may be sensitive to pathology associated with EOAD – including small but positive relationships between ISD and amyloid and tau deposition, and the EOAD Atrophy Signature. Associations were stronger with tau burden than amyloid, likely because amyloid accumulates earlier in the disease course than tau and therefore the rise with ISD is asynchronous. 45 These results coincide with currently‐reviewed findings suggesting that in cognitively impaired participants with presumed AD, cerebrospinal markers of pTau181 and amyloid beta positively correlated with IIV, with patterns replicated in amyloid PET. 46 Conversely, ISD had no relationship with hippocampal atrophy, which coincides with earlier findings that pathology in sporadic EOAD is often hippocampal‐sparing. 18 Instead, these findings support work in older adults showing cognitive dispersion being associated with executive functioning 13 , 14 and dorsal attention networks. 47 Of all biomarkers examined, ISD was associated strongest with the EOAD Atrophy Signature seen on MRI (r = −0.31, r 2 = 0.10). ROIs comprising this index are known to play important roles in executive, attentional, and visuoexecutive processing, and possess strong connections with dorsolateral prefrontal networks. 19 It is not surprising, therefore, that atrophy in these regions was directly and positively correlated with variance across cognitive testing. Although these relationships accounted for limited variance with imaging biomarkers, accounting for 10% of variance can be meaningful if added to models with other significant predictors. 48 In particular, these cognitive dispersion relationships existed above and beyond the sizable contributions of diagnosis and global cognition (r2 s = 0.47–0.48 for Tau PET and EOAD Atrophy Signature). Consequently, while the contribution of cognitive dispersion was not as large currently as global cognition, our results support its role as an outcome measure in future clinical trials with EOAD populations to complement traditional summary scores to identify unique variance and subtleties in performance that may otherwise be overlooked. Future work is planned to directly compare cognitive dispersion magnitudes between EOAD and LOAD populations.

Results further revealed preliminary findings suggesting significant differences in dispersion among phenotypic manifestations of early‐onset dementia, with the PCA phenotype possessing the highest ISD values and PPA possessing the lowest (Figure 1B). Composite means and SDs for global cognition were comparable between groups (Table S2), suggesting that these findings were not driven by broad discrepancies in global performance (p = 0.51). Rather, they were likely impacted by the range of impairments observed in each condition. Participants with PCA tend to display notable impairments on measures of visuospatial skills, attention, and processing speed (zs = −5.76 to −6.11 relative to intact peers), whereas decrements on other domains are more modest (e.g., z = −0.87). 20 The sizable but selective impairments observed for a few domains result in large IIVs for the PCA group. Conversely, the small ISD values for PPA suggest either globally intact or globally impaired performances. Logopenic‐variant PPA (lvPPA) is the PPA variant most commonly associated with amyloid positivity (i.e., AD), 49 which often displays widespread impairments in language, memory, visuospatial reasoning, attention, and mental flexibility skills. 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 Working memory and attentional dysfunction – associated with altered frontal‐lobe circuitry 55 – are suggested to account for this globally impaired pattern in lvPPA, 54 which would manifest in diminished ISD values. Additionally, the LEADS battery tends to be language‐dependent; thus, aphasia would negatively impact numerous cognitive tasks. These supplementary results should be viewed with caution, given that small sizes for non‐amnestic phenotypes (ns = 9–15 per group) may have increased our likelihood of observing spurious findings. However, the rarity of non‐amnestic sporadic EOAD phenotypes in research has led us to feel that their inclusion as preliminary findings is warranted. Future studies focused on non‐amnestic EOAD samples are encouraged to permit more comprehensive investigation of cognitive dispersion among EOAD phenotypes.

Finally, higher cognitive dispersion at baseline predicted greater 12‐month reliable cognitive declines across all assessed cognitive domains. Results remained significant for most domains after accounting for covariates (including racial/ethnic diversity, APOE ε4 status, diagnostic group, and baseline global cognition), excepting immediate memory and language. These results extend previous work from older adults showing that IIV predicts either conversion to dementia or cognitive decline. 9 , 56 Despite similarities with IIV research in older‐onset populations, these findings are novel in several ways. First, we incorporate reliable change methods into the assessment of IIV's predictive capacity of cognitive trajectories, providing a more robust measure of change (e.g., relative to T2 – T1). 40 Additionally, these results build upon the relatively limited knowledge currently known about longitudinal cognitive change in sporadic EOAD – namely that cognitive declines tend to be steep following diagnosis. 3 , 57 , 58 Further, our analysis of individual cognitive composites reveals that after accounting for baseline cognition, IIV is more sensitive to cognitive change in some domains than others. Except for delayed memory, cognitive change in all domains predicted by IIV was influenced by executive and attentional processing, which as described above is highly associated with IIV and cognitive processing in EOAD. 13 , 14 , 17 , 19 , 47 Lastly, although comparison of r 2 values in Table 2 reveals that baseline cognition remains the largest predictor of future cognitive performance (consistent with longstanding research 59 , 60 ), cognitive dispersion added unique variance beyond baseline cognition. This suggests possible benefit of updating SRB prediction equations for commonly‐used neuropsychological batteries (e.g., UDS 61 or AD Neuroimaging Initiative 62 test battery) to include cognitive dispersion as a predictor variable alongside baseline cognition and demographics/test variables to predict future expected performance with greater accuracy.

The current results should be interpreted in light of limitations. First, although attempts were made to calculate the ISD variable in a consistent fashion with other studies using the UDS, 6 , 36 these results may not generalize to those using other cognitive dispersion calculations (including CoV) or cognitive test batteries. For example, while in our sample ISD was unrelated to age, education, or sex (rs = −0.06–0.00; ps = 0.26–0.97), sex‐related differences have recently been observed when using un‐normed IIV values in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) participants from the American South. 63 Our use of demographically‐adjusted norms 6 to calculate ISD likely removed possible age‐, education‐, and sex‐relationships in our study. Additionally, it was surprising that no differences were observed currently between ISD values for CN and EOnonAD groups, given previous literature suggesting that IIV can differentiate participants with frontotemporal dementia, 15 dementia with Lewy bodies, 64 HIV, 65 traumatic brain injury, 66 and schizophrenia 67 from healthy control participants. There are likely two explanations for this null finding. First, EOnonAD represents a constellation of non‐AD neurodegenerative etiologies and non‐neurodegenerative etiologies, which may have masked possible condition‐specific effects. Second, in a large number of IIV studies, authors either did not account for global cognition, or used the CoV variable as a control for global cognition – which previous literature has suggested to avoid. 12 Overall, the smaller effect sizes observed in the current study relative to others are likely in part attributable to this more stringent control for baseline cognition. As a result, we echo calls for greater consistency in IIV calculations to permit comparisons across studies. 12

Next, the combination of younger age and greater severity of cognitive impairment in our EOAD sample resulted in extreme T scores (e.g., T < 0) for some measures, which confounded the calculation of ISD. This phenomenon required “winsorization” techniques 68 used in other ISD studies of dementia 36 to adjust extreme performances to T = 9 to ensure that ISD values could be interpreted properly. In addition, the reliable change prediction equations used herein are specific to the LEADS battery, and for individuals ≤ 65 years old assessed twice over 12 months; attempts to replicate these results in older samples (or different batteries or retest intervals) may not generalize. Further, as the LEADS battery was developed to complement the UDS, some cognitive domains were constrained by measures that were either bimodal in their distributions or contained only a single measure (visuospatial skills). Finally, the cognitively impaired samples in LEADS were rather homogenous for sex, racial/ethnic diversity, and education. Emphasis of future LEADS recruitment (Diversity Supplement: LDRFP‐21‐818464) is to optimize ways to overcome these issues commonly observed in large‐scale studies. 69 , 70

CONCLUSIONS

Cognitive dispersion complements traditional aggregated cognitive summary scores by enabling a more nuanced characterization of cognitive performance patterns in sporadic EOAD and explaining more variance in predicted cognitive trajectories. This may permit enrichment of future clinical trials in EOAD by enhancing the ability to identify deviations in performance or track treatment changes over time. Further, the incremental sensitivity of cognitive dispersion towards biomarkers of EOAD suggests that it may support traditional approaches of clinical decision making related to disease modifying treatments for AD.
