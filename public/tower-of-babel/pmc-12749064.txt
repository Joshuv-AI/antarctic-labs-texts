Annealing strategy for enhancing the fracture toughness of MoSiBTiC alloys
Authors: Nobuaki Sekido, Tetsuya Saito, Kyosuke Yoshimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An optimal annealing process for enhancing the room-temperature fracture toughness of MoSiBTiC alloys was investigated in this study. The alloy with a composition of Mo–5Si–10B–10Ti–10C (at%) was subjected to three annealing conditions to control its microstructure. Quantitative microstructural analysis and mechanical testing revealed that the toughness of the MoSiBTiC alloys increased with the size of the Moss phase and decreased with its hardness. Annealing at 1800 °C for 24 h promoted microstructural coarsening but also led to increased solution hardening and reduced ductility of the Moss phase. In contrast, annealing at 1600 °C for 100 h improved the ductility of the Moss phase by reducing solution hardening, although the coarsening of the Moss phase was relatively slow. Based on these findings, a two-step annealing process that combines the benefits of lower-temperature and higher-temperature annealing conditions was proposed to effectively enhance the fracture toughness of MoSiBTiC alloys.

Introduction

Ultrahigh-temperature materials with temperature capabilities exceeding Ni-based superalloys have long been anticipated in the aerospace industry1. For turbine jet engines, efficiency increases with higher turbine inlet temperature. However, recent trends in jet engine performance indicate that the efficiency gains from raising turbine inlet temperatures have stagnated due to efficiency losses caused by the cooling of turbine blades2. Over the past decades, refractory metal silicides have been considered promising candidate materials because of their high melting points and excellent oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures3–6. Following the discovery that small boron additions significantly improve the oxidation resistance of Mo5Si37–9, Mo-Si-B alloys with Mo-rich compositions have attracted considerable interest for potential high-temperature applications2,10–12. Despite their excellent oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures2,11,13–19, Mo–Si–B alloys exhibit accelerated oxidation in the intermediate temperature range between 700 and 900 °C. To address this limitation, alloying strategies involving fourth-element additions have been explored in various studies, as reviewed elsewhere20,21. Among these elements, Ti has been shown to be a promising alloying element as it stabilizes the BCC solid solution phase12,22 and improves oxidation resistance at intermediate temperatures by forming TiO2 instead of the volatile MoO323, and enhances the creep strength24,25. Nonetheless, the intrinsically low ductility and fracture toughness of the alloys remain major limitations for practical use10,26–31.

Recently, TiC-added Mo-Si-B alloys, known as “MoSiBTiC” alloys, have attracted significant attention due to their excellent high-temperature strength and adequate room-temperature fracture toughness32–36. It has been demonstrated that the oxidation resistance of MoSiBTiC alloys at intermediate temperatures can also be improved by suitable compositional control37–39. MoSiBTiC alloys consist of the Mo solid solution (Moss), Mo5SiB2 (T2), and TiC phases, and exhibit complex microstructures due to their intricate solidification paths32,40–42. Despite containing brittle TiC, MoSiBTiC alloys show higher fracture toughness than Mo-Si-B alloys when compared at the same volume fraction of Moss. However, the factors that determine the toughness of the alloys have not been fully clarified35,43. For Mo-Si-B alloys, crack trapping by Moss has been shown as a key toughening mechanism31; however, fracture toughness is not solely determined by the volume fraction of Moss, but is strongly influenced by microstructural factors such as continuity44 and the size of Moss10. When the Moss phase becomes more continuous, cracks initiated in the brittle phases can be more effectively arrested, thereby increasing toughness43–45. In addition, it is accepted that larger ductile grains can provide a higher toughening effect10,45,46. Indeed, high-temperature annealing is reported to promote Moss coarsening, which then improves the toughness of the alloys47.

These findings suggest that the toughness of the MoSiBTiC alloys could be improved through optimal microstructural control via annealing. Nevertheless, limited fracture toughness data are available for MoSiBTiC alloys35, and the optimal annealing strategies for enhancing toughness have not been established. Therefore, this study focuses on identifying the microstructural factors that influence the room-temperature fracture toughness of MoSiBTiC alloys by controlling the annealing conditions.

Results

Microstructural changes during annealing

Previous studies on Mo–Si–B–Ti–C quinary alloys (or MoSiBTiC alloys) have suggested that the alloy with the composition 65Mo–5Si–10B–10Ti–10C (at%) exhibits a good combination of room-temperature toughness and high-temperature strength35. Figure 1 shows SEM-BSE micrographs of the MoSiBTiC alloy under the following conditions: (a) as-cast, (b) annealed at 1800 °C for 24 h, (c) annealed at 1600 °C for 100 h, and (d) subjected to two-step annealing, which consists of the initial annealing at 1800 °C for 24 h followed by the secondary annealing at 1600 °C for 100 h. Note that annealing at 1800 °C for 24 h is a typical annealing condition for MoSiBTiC alloys to approach thermodynamic equilibrium32,33,35,36, whereas annealing at 1600 °C for 100 h is a representative condition used to achieve equilibrium in Mo–Si–B alloys12,22,48. Consistent with our previous reports32,35,36, the alloy primarily consists of three phases: the Mo solid solution phase (Moss) with an A2 structure (brightest contrast), the Mo5SiB2 phase (T2) with a D8l structure (gray contrast), and the TiC phase with a B1 structure (darkest contrast). The as-cast microstructure shown in Fig. 1a indicates that dendritic TiC grains initially formed from the liquid, followed by the formation of Moss grains and a Moss/TiC two-phase microstructure. This was subsequently followed by the formation of rod-shaped T2 grains, a Moss/T2 two-phase microstructure, and a ternary-eutectic-like Moss/TiC/T2 three-phase microstructure. The formation of a small amount of Mo2C was also confirmed in the as-cast microstructure. This sequence of phase formation has been validated by previously reported in-situ electromagnetic levitation experiments49. Figure 1b shows the microstructure of the alloy after annealing at 1800 °C for 24 h. In agreement with previous reports32,33, the microstructure coarsened, and most Mo2C grains decomposed into Moss and TiC lamellae, while some remained undecomposed even after the annealing. This behavior is clearly visible in a magnified image shown in Fig. 2b. Annealing 1600 °C for 100 h (Fig. 1c) resulted in the complete decomposition of Mo2C, although the degree of microstructure coarsening was less pronounced compared to the alloy annealed at 1800 °C. In addition, the precipitation behavior within the Moss and TiC grains was altered by annealing under different conditions. Figure 2 presents magnified micrographs of the alloys shown in Fig. 1. In the as-cast alloy, no precipitates were observed in the dendritic Moss grains. However, TiC have precipitated within the Moss matrix of the annealed alloys (Figs. 2b–d). The volume fraction of TiC precipitates was higher in the alloy annealed at 1600 °C than in the alloy annealed at 1800 °C. This is attributed to the lower solubility of Ti and C in the Moss phase at lower temperatures, which promotes the formation of finer TiC precipitates with higher number densities. In passing, the number density of TiC precipitates appeared to be smaller in the two-step annealed alloy because TiC precipitates had already formed and coarsened during the first annealing step (1800 °C/24 h) of the two-step annealing. Furthermore, many Moss precipitates were observed in the primary TiC phase in the two-step annealed alloys. It has been demonstrated that the TiC phase exhibits some degree of off-stoichiometry toward a Ti–rich composition, and the solubility limit in equilibrium with the Ti solid solution phase varies with temperature50,51. A (Mo + Ti)-rich TiC phase forms during solidification in MoSiBTiC alloys; however, the degree of off-stoichiometry that develops during annealing at 1800 °C is larger than that in the as-cast or 1600 °C-annealed conditions. Therefore, when the as-cast alloy is directly annealed at 1600 °C, the precipitation of Moss seldom occurs within the TiC phase because the difference in the degree of off-stoichiometry between the as-cast and 1600 °C conditions is small. In contrast, when the alloy is first annealed at 1800 °C and subsequently annealed at 1600 °C, Moss precipitation occurs within the TiC phase to relieve the supersaturation (i.e., off-stoichiometry) developed during annealing at 1800 °C.

The phase fractions in the two-step annealed alloy were similar to those of the alloy annealed at 1600 °C for 100 h: 53% Moss, 36% T2, and 11% TiC, with no Mo2C detected. Quantitative analysis on the coarsening of Moss during annealing

It has been demonstrated that room-temperature toughness is significantly influenced by the size of the ductile inclusions in a ductile-phase-toughened alloy: toughness increases with the size of ductile inclusions for a given volume fraction46. As shown in Figs. 1 and 3, the microstructural coarsening behavior is different when annealed at 1600 °C and 1800 °C. Therefore, quantitative analyses of the size of the Moss phase are necessary to clarify its contribution to toughness.

As can be seen in the micrographs of the present alloy, Moss grains do not exhibit a spherical morphology but sometimes appear as a block composed of a eutectic-like Moss/TiC two-phase product. Therefore, in this study, the size of Moss grains was characterized as a "thickness" defined by the intersection spacing between a measurement line and the hetero-phase boundary around Moss grains. The intersection method was applied to Moss-segmented images processed from the phase maps obtained via EBSD. It should be noted that, since the present alloy exhibits a complex and heterogenous microstructure, the line intersection measurements were repeated by rotating the images in 10° increments up to 90° to minimize errors caused by anisotropic microstructures. The interspacing for the vertical and horizontal measurement lines was set to be 5 µm.

Figure 5 shows the frequency distributions and the corresponding cumulative distributions of Moss thickness obtained by the above method. All the distributions exhibit positive skewness, where the right tail of the distribution is longer than the left tail. The arithmetic mean and the median (the middle value in the dataset) are also represented in each figure. For the as-cast alloy, the mean Moss thickness was dmean = 1.8 µm, and the median Moss thickness was dmedian = 1.1 µm, respectively. After annealing at 1800 °C for 24 h, the mean thickness increased to dmean = 3.3 µm, and the median thickness to dmedian = 2.5 µm. The alloy annealed at 1600 °C for 100 h showed dmean = 3.5 µm and dmedian = 2.3 µm, while the two-step annealed alloy showed the largest values, dmean = 4.3 µm and dmedian = 3.2 µm. In all cases, the mean values were larger than the median values. This trend can be attributed to the formation of large-scaled Moss grains near the primary TiC dendrites, which raised the mean values. The median values were close to the peak values of the frequency distributions. In this study, both the median and mean values were used to represent the size of the Moss phase, providing complementary insights into its contribution to the alloy’s toughness.Fig. 5Frequency distributions and the corresponding cumulative distributions for the Moss thickness measured by the line-intersection method for (a) as-cast, (b) annealed at 1800 °C for 24 h, (c) annealed at 1600 °C for 100 h, and (d) two-step annealed alloys.

Fracture toughness of the as-cast and annealed alloy

Fracture toughness (KQ) at room temperature was evaluated by three-point bending tests with chevron-notched specimens, and the results are summarized in Fig. 6. The as-cast alloy exhibited a room-temperature fracture toughness of 13.0 MPa√m. The alloys annealed at 1800 °C for 24 h showed an improved toughness of 15.3 MPa√m, which is consistent with the previous report for alloys of the same composition and annealing condition (15.2 MPa√m)35. The alloy annealed at 1600 °C for 100 h exhibits a similar toughness to that of the alloy annealed at 1800 °C. The highest toughness improvement was attained in the two-step annealed alloys, reaching 17.4 MPa√m.Fig. 6Room-temperature fracture toughness (KQ) of the MoSiBTiC alloy evaluated by three-point bending tests with chevron-notched specimens.

To investigate the fracture behavior, the fracture surfaces of the specimens after fracture toughness measurements were analyzed. Figure 8 shows the BSE (a, b) and SE (c, d) fractographs taken at the same location on both sides of the fracture surfaces of the two-step annealed alloy, which exhibited the highest fracture toughness in this study. Following the procedure described in the previous study35, phase identification in the fractographs was performed using EDS in addition to BSE imaging. Since these images were taken at corresponding positions on the two opposing fracture surfaces, the left and right images (a–b and c–d) correspond to each other as mirror positions. When the same phase appears at the corresponding positions, the fracture is classified as transgranular. In contrast, when different phases are observed, it indicates that interfacial debonding occurred. The fractographs indicate that transgranular cleavage fracture was dominant across all constituent phases. The TiC and T2 phases showed smooth, flat surfaces upon cleavage fracture. The Moss phase also failed in a brittle manner; however, some signs of plastic deformation during crack propagation, such as uneven surfaces and river patterns, were observed on the fracture surfaces. Interfacial debonding was rarely observed at the Moss/TiC interfaces, which is consistent with the previous study35. In contrast, debonding was often observed at the T2/TiC interfaces, as indicated by the arrows in Fig. 8.Fig. 8BSE (a, b) and SE (c, d) images taken at corresponding positions on the two opposing fracture surfaces of the two-step annealed alloy.

Similar fractographic features were observed in the other alloys; i.e., the as-cast alloy, the alloy annealed at 1600 °C for 100 h, and the alloy annealed at 1800 °C for 24 h. In all cases, cleavage fracture was dominant in the T2 and TiC phases, and the Moss phase also failed in a brittle manner, while exhibiting small traces of plastic deformation. As mentioned previously, fracture toughness was evaluated using chevron-notched specimens. However, these specimens are not suitable for observing crack propagation behavior. Therefore, in this study, straight-notched specimens were used to investigate crack propagation by examining the side surface of the specimen after the bending test. Figure 9 shows the crack propagation behavior near the crack tip after the bending test for (a) the as-cast alloy, and (b) the two-step annealed alloy. The crack propagation behaviors of the as-cast and the two-step annealed alloys are similar in the following points; (i) cracks are often deflected at relatively large Moss grains, (ii) microcracks are sometimes bridged by Moss grains as indicated by Arrow II, and (iii) cracks tend to propagate along the boundary between T2 grains and Moss/TiC eutectic-like products, as indicated by Arrow I. The latter behavior explains why interfacial debonding was frequently observed at the TiC/T2 interfaces, as shown in Fig. 8.Fig. 9Crack propagation behavior near the crack tip in straight-notched bend specimens: (a) as-cast alloy and (b) two-step annealed alloy.

However, the toughening contribution of the eutectic Moss phase seems to be different in the as-cast and the two-step annealed alloys. In the as-cast alloy, Moss grains in the eutectic microstructure were fine and isolated, allowing cracks to bypass the Moss grains and propagate linearly through the brittle phases. In such a case, the contribution of the Moss grains to toughening would be minimal. In contrast, in the two-step annealed alloy, the eutectic microstructure was coarsened and the Moss phase formed a continuous network, in which TiC grains were enclosed by Moss grains. This Moss/TiC two-phase product appears to act as a single block, serving as an obstacle to crack propagation. Such a microstructure can increase the likelihood that cracks are bridged and/or deflected by the Moss grains, resulting in a more tortuous crack path and thereby increasing the overall toughness.

Discussions

Effect of the size of the Mo solid-solution phase on toughness

The present study has demonstrated that the size of Moss in MoSiBTiC alloys can be controlled by selecting appropriate annealing conditions. Here, the focus is placed on the effect of Moss size on toughness. Figure 10 illustrates the relationship between room-temperature fracture toughness and the square root of the Moss phase thickness, in the same manner as plotted in Fig. 7. It has been demonstrated that the toughness increment, ΔKC, caused by crack bridging through ductile particles, is described by the following Equation46:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {K}^{C}=E\sqrt{C{V}_{f}\frac{{\sigma }_{0}}{E}{d}_{0}}$$\end{document}where E, Vf, σ0, and d0 are the Young’s modulus, volume fraction, tensile strength, and particle size of the ductile phase, respectively. This model assumes that ductile inclusions embedded within a brittle matrix undergo plastic deformation and fail at a chisel point, and the toughness increment is derived from the energy consumption during the plastic deformation of the ductile phase. Interface debonding plays a significant role, as it reduces the constraints imposed on the ductile inclusions. In Eq. (1), C is a material constant representing the degree of constraint from the matrix. When no interface debonding occurs, C equals 1.6, while C increases to 6 when partial interfacial debonding occurs. The toughness increment for the two-step annealed alloy was estimated from Eq. (1) using the following parameters for the Moss phase: a Young’s modulus of E = 298 GPa52, a yield stress of σ0 = 623 MPa52, a volume fraction of Vf = 0.53 (from Fig. 4), and a size (thickness) of either dmean = 4.3 µm or dmedian = 3.2 µm (from Fig. 5). Since interface debonding upon crack-bridging by Moss was not often observed in the fractographs shown in Fig. 9, C was set to 1.6. Using these values, the estimated toughness increment is ΔKC = 22.4–26.0 MPa√m, depending on the selected Moss thickness for the calculation. This estimated value is higher than the experimentally obtained value of 17.4 MPa√m, but it seems reasonable when considering that the fracture toughness of T2 is 1.5–3.3 MPa√m53,54 and TiC 1.5–3.6 MPa√m55, respectively. In this context, the toughness increment is primarily attributed to the crack bridging by Moss, and the observed linear relationship between the square root of Moss size and toughness in Fig. 10 would be reasonable.

The difference between the predicted and practical values of fracture toughness may be caused by (i) the frequency of crack bridging by Moss, and (ii) differences in the fracture manner of Moss grains between the model and the practical. As shown in Fig. 9, cracks are often deflected at the interface between T2 and Moss/TiC two-phase product. The crack deflection is another toughening mechanism, where toughness increases due to reduced stress concentration from the decreased net crack-tip opening displacement, as well as increased work of fracture from increased fracture surfaces56. However, if the toughening contribution from crack bridging is higher than that from crack deflection, a larger toughness increment can be expected by promoting the former. This can be achieved through microstructural control by manipulating the morphology of the ductile phase into a continuous rod shape (e.g., via directional solidification45). Moreover, the model expressed by Eq. (1) assumes that ductile inclusions fail in chisel-point fracture, whereas the Moss phase exhibits cleavage fracture with some river patterns. Therefore, improving the ductility of the Moss phase is another critical factor for further enhancing the toughness of MoSiBTiC alloys.

Relationship between toughness and nano-hardness of the Mo solid solution phase

Figure 11 shows the relationship between the fracture toughness and nano-hardness of the Moss phase. Nano-hardness was measured avoiding precipitates to more strongly reflect the strength of the Moss matrix. The fracture toughness increases as the nano-hardness of Moss decreases. Since hardness is often related to the yield strength in alloys, this trend appears to contradict the prediction of the ductile-phase toughening model, as expressed by Eq. (1), which indicates the toughness increases with the yield strength of the ductile phase. As discussed in the previous section, the predicted toughness increment for MoSiBTiC alloys is higher than the experimentally observed value, partly because the Moss phase does not exhibit the high level of ductility assumed in the model. This implies that the increase in toughness associated with a decrease in nano-hardness can be attributed to the ductility improvement (or increased work of fracture) of the Moss phase, since strength and ductility usually follow a trade-off relationship.Fig. 11Relationship between fracture toughness and nano-hardness of the Moss phase. Nano-hardness was measured while avoiding precipitates to reflect the intrinsic strength of Moss.

Relationship between fracture toughness and nano-hardness of the Moss phase. Nano-hardness was measured while avoiding precipitates to reflect the intrinsic strength of Moss.

It is well known that Si acts as a strong solid solution hardener for Mo57,58, and its solubility in Moss decreases as the temperature decreases59. To the best of our knowledge, no reports are available on the solution hardening behavior of C in Mo. However, in α-iron, which has the same BCC structure as Mo, the dissolution of interstitial elements causes significantly larger lattice distortions compared to that of substitutional elements, resulting in a much higher solid solution hardening capability60. The decrease in C content in the Moss matrix, which is evidenced by TiC precipitation in alloys annealed at the lower temperature of 1600 °C, as shown in Fig. 2, may reduce the solution hardening and could contribute to ductility improvement in the Moss phase. This observation is consistent with the fact that the nano-hardness of the Moss phase increases with higher annealing temperatures. Consequently, annealing at lower temperatures leads to reduced solution hardening of Moss, which enhances the work of fracture of the Moss phase and thus the toughness of MoSiBTiC alloys.

Annealing strategy for toughness enhancement

In general, effective toughening of a brittle matrix by ductile-phase inclusions depends on several microstructural factors, including the size, volume fraction, morphology, spatial distribution of the ductile phase, and its interfacial strength with the brittle matrix. The volume fraction of the ductile Moss phase varied with the annealing condition, as shown in Fig. 4, however, the magnitude of this variation was not significant. In fact, the as-cast alloy, which exhibits the highest volume fraction of Moss, shows the lowest toughness among all the alloys examined in this study. This indicates that the volume fraction of Moss was not a dominant factor, and that other microstructural characteristics play a more important role. In this study, both the size and the ductility of the Moss phase are identified as key parameters that can be controlled through annealing. As noted above, the optimal characteristics for the Moss phase are a larger size and lower hardness, which presumably corresponds to higher ductility. High-temperature annealing promotes microstructural coarsening, but can also enhance solid-solution hardening and reduce ductility due to increased solubility of solute atoms at higher temperatures. The present study demonstrates that these conflicting requirements can be balanced by a two-step annealing process: the first step at 1800 °C promotes coarsening, whereas the subsequent annealing at 1600 °C reduces solid-solution hardening and thereby improves the ductility of the Moss phase. It should be noted that the solute concentration in the Moss phase is expected to be comparable between the alloy annealed at 1600 °C for 100 h and the two-step annealed alloy. However, the size of the Moss phase is different between the two conditions, which leads to the variation in fracture toughness. It should also be noted that the two-step annealing induces precipitation of the Moss phase within the primary TiC phase, as shown in Fig. 2, which serves as an additional factor that increases the toughness of the alloy. In this regard, the two-step annealing strategy is highly effective in enhancing the toughness of MoSiBTiC alloys.

Methodology

A MoSiBTiC alloy with a nominal composition of Mo–5Si–10B–10Ti–10C (at%) was prepared by arc-melting high-purity raw materials (Mo: 99.99%, Si: 99.9999%, B: 99.95%, TiC: 99% in wt%) under an Ar atmosphere. To clarify the effect of annealing conditions on the microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloys, three types of annealing conditions were examined: (i) 1800 °C for 24 h, (ii) 1600 °C for 100 h, and (iii) two-step annealing, where the first step involved annealing at 1800 °C for 24 h, followed by annealing at 1600 °C for 100 h as the second step. Microstructures were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD).

Room-temperature fracture toughness was measured using three-point bending tests with chevron-notched specimens under a crosshead speed of 10 μm/s. The dimensions of the bending test specimens were 5 × 5 × 25 mm3, and the Chevron notch was introduced using electro-discharge machining (EDM) with a 0.1 mm diameter wire. The notch geometry was triangular with an apex angle of 90°. The fracture toughness, KQ, was evaluated from the strain energy release rate using Irwin’s similarity relationship as follows35:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{Q}=\sqrt{{G}_{C}E^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\frac{U}{A}\cdot \frac{E}{1-{\nu }^{2}}}$$\end{document}

Here GC is the critical energy release rate, U is the energy absorbed during the fracture, and A is the projected area swept by the crack. E and ν are the Young’s modulus and the Poisson’s ratio of the specimen, respectively. In this study, the reported values of E = 358 GPa and ν = 0.26 were used for evaluating the room-temperature fracture toughness. Besides the toughness measurements, three-point bending tests using straight-notched specimens were conducted to observe the crack propagation behavior. Nanoindentation was used to evaluate the hardness of the constituent phases under the load control mode at a maximum load of 2 mN.

Conclusions

Microstructural factors affecting the room-temperature fracture toughness of MoSiBTiC alloys with a composition of Mo–5Si–10B–10Ti–10C (at%) were investigated by controlling the annealing process. The alloy mainly consists of three phases, Moss, TiC, and T2, and the plastic deformation of the Moss phase was found to contribute significantly to the toughening of the alloy. The fracture toughness was found to improve with an increase in the size of the Moss grains and a decrease in its hardness, leading to increased ductility. Annealing at higher temperatures promotes microstructural coarsening but also increases the solubility of the Moss phase, resulting in reduced ductility due to the enhanced effects of solution hardening. Conversely, annealing at lower temperatures improves the ductility of the Moss phase by reducing its solubility, although the rate of microstructural coarsening becomes slower. Based on these findings, the "two-step annealing" was proposed as an effective process to enhance the toughness of the MoSiBTiC alloy by controlling the Moss characteristics. The first step involves annealing at 1800 °C for 24 h to promote coarsening, followed by annealing at 1600 °C for 100 h in the second step to reduce the effects of solution hardening and enhance the ductility of the Moss phase. This process successfully improved the fracture toughness of the alloys up to 17.4 MPa√m.
