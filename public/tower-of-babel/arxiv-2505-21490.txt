Bayesian Dynamic Clustering Factor Models
Tsering Dolkar, Marco A. R. Ferreira, Hwasoo Shin, Allison N. Tegge

Abstract
We propose novel Bayesian Dynamic Clustering Factor Models (BDCFM) for the analysis of multivariate longitudinal data. BDCFM combines factor models with hidden Markov models to concomitantly perform dimension reduction, clustering, and estimation of the dynamic transitions of subjects through clusters. We develop an efficient Gibbs sampler for exploration of the posterior distribution. An analysis of a simulated dataset shows that our inferential approach works well both at parameter estimation and clustering of subjects. Finally, we illustrate the utility of our BDCFM with an analysis of a dataset on opioid use disorder.



Keywords—
Bayesian
Dynamic Models; Factor Models; Hidden Markov Models; Opioid Use Disorder.

1
Introduction

We propose a novel method for the analysis of multivariate longitudinal data.
This method is motivated by our previous work on recovery from opioid use disorder (OUD)
[
Craft et al., 2023
]
, where we used a combination of principal component analysis (PCA) and
k
-means clustering
[
Yeung and Ruzzo, 2001
,
Hastie et al., 2009
]
.
While providing dimension reduction and clustering, these latter combined methods do not provide uncertainty quantification.
We recently developed Bayesian Clustering Factor Models (BCFM), a framework that combines factor models
[
Lopes and West, 2004
,
Shin and Ferreira, 2023
]
with mixtures of Gaussians
[
Frühwirth-Schnatter, 2006
,
Fruhwirth-Schnatter et al., 2019
]
to achieve concomitant dimension reduction and clustering for cross-sectional data
[
Shin et al., 2025
]
.
To extend this to multivariate longitudinal data, here we propose Bayesian Dynamic Clustering Factor Models (BDCFM).

BDCFM combines latent factor models (FM) and hidden Markov models (HMM) for concomitant dimension reduction, clustering, and estimation of the dynamic transitions of subjects among clusters.
To provide full account of uncertainty, we develop a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm
[
Gelfand and Smith, 1990a
,
Robert and Casella, 2005
,
Gamerman and Lopes, 2006
]
.
For fast and convenient computations, we consider conditionally conjugate priors.
To deal with issues of label switching, we employ an empirical Bayes approach inspired by unit information priors
[
Kass and Wasserman, 1995
,
Steele and Raftery, 2010
]
to choose the prior hyperparameters for the cluster parameters.

Previous Bayesian approaches have combined FMs with mixtures of Gaussians for concomitant dimension reduction and clustering
[
Fokoué and Titterington, 2003
,
Papastamoulis, 2018
,
Chandra et al., 2023
,
Ghilotti et al., 2025
,
Shin et al., 2025
]
.
These approaches assume for each vector of observations a factor model with vector of common factors coming from a mixture of Gaussian distributions.
While useful for the analysis of cross-sectional data, these approaches are not adequate for the analysis of longitudinal data.
To fill this gap, BDCFM assumes that the vector of common factors for each subject evolves through time according to a hidden Markov model. As a result, in addition to providing dimension reduction and clustering, BDCFM also models the dynamic transitions of subjects among clusters.

We illustrate the usefulness and flexibility of BDCFM with two applications.
The first application to a simulated dataset shows that our BDCFM framework accurately and precisely estimates the parameters of the model and the cluster assignments.
The second application, to a longitudinal dataset on recovery from OUD, shows that our BDCFM framework may provide useful information on the latent factors related to the recovery process, the recovery subgroups, and the transitions of subjects among subgroups.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces BDCFM and the conditionally conjugate prior distributions.
Section
3
presents the MCMC algorithm for exploration of the posterior distribution, including the full conditional distributions used in a Gibbs sampler.
Section
4
evaluates the performance of our BDCFM framework using a simulated dataset.
Section
5
illustrates the application of BDCFM to a real-world dataset on recovery from OUD.
Finally, Section
6
concludes with a summary of our findings and possible future research directions.
The Appendix contains the empirical Bayes specification of priors for cluster parameters and development of the full conditional distributions.
Supplementary Table 1 provides the notation used throughout this paper.

2
The Model

2.1
Bayesian Dynamic Clustering Factor Models

Consider an
R
-dimensional data vector
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{it}
for subject
i
at time
t
, where

i=1,\dots,S
and
t=1,\dots,T
.
Thus, we have
S\times T
observations for each of the
R
variables. BDCFM relates the
R
observed variables to
L
latent factors using the factor model

\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{it}=\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{it}\text{, \; }\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{it}\sim N(0,\mbox{\boldmath$V$}),\\

(1)

where
B
is an
R\times L
factor loadings matrix, and
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}
is an
L
-dimensional vector of latent common factors for subject
i
at time
t
.
In addition, the covariance matrix of the error term
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{it}
is
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}=diag(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{R}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{R}^{2}
are known as idiosyncratic variances or uniquenesses, henceforth referred to as uniquenesses.
The factor loadings matrix
B
is associated with how much of the variance in an observed variable is explained by the latent common factors. To ensure identifiability of the model, we assume that the factor loadings matrix
B
follows a hierarchical structural constraint such that

\mbox{\boldmath$B$}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\dots&0\\
b_{2,1}&1&0&\dots&0\\
b_{3,1}&b_{3,2}&1&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{L,1}&b_{L,2}&b_{L,3}&\dots&1\\
b_{L+1,1}&b_{L+1,2}&b_{L+1,3}&\dots&b_{L+1,L}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{R,1}&b_{R,2}&b_{R,3}&\dots&b_{R,L}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, we assume that the factor loadings matrix
B
in Equation (
1
) is lower triangular with main diagonal elements set to 1. The order of the variables in
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{it}
is chosen carefully to preserve the properties of the hierarchical structural constraint
[
Geweke and Zhou, 1996
,
Aguilar and West, 1999
]
.
Prado et al. [2021]
and
Shin and Ferreira [2023]
provide recommendations on how to choose the order of the variables.

Let
Z_{it}
be a latent variable that indicates the cluster to which subject
i
belongs at time
t
.
Denote by
G
the total number of clusters.
BDCFM assumes that if subject
i
belongs to cluster
g
at time
t
, then
Z_{it}=g
and
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}
follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g}
and covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}
, that is

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}|Z_{it}=g\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g},\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g})
.
To make BDCFM identifiable, we assume the first cluster has a diagonal covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{1}
.
In addition, BDCFM assumes that
Z_{it}
follows a hidden Markov model with the transition probability from cluster
j
at time
t-1
to cluster
g
at time
t
equal to
q_{jg}=P(Z_{it}=g|Z_{i,t-1}=j)
,
t=2,\dots,T
.
Further, at time
t=1
, the probability of being assigned to cluster
g
is
p_{g}=P(Z_{i1}=g)
.
Thus, we define
Q
to be the
G\times G
matrix of transition probabilities, where the element in row
j
and column
g
is
q_{jg}
.

Therefore, the joint probability density function of all the random quantities in BDCFM is

\displaystyle p(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$},\mbox{\boldmath$B$},\mbox{\boldmath$V$},\mbox{\boldmath$X$},\mbox{\boldmath$Z$},\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$},\mbox{\boldmath$p$},\mbox{\boldmath$Q$},\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{2})=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{S}\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{it}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$B$},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it},\mbox{\boldmath$V$})p(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}\mid Z_{it},\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$})p(Z_{it}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$p$},\mbox{\boldmath$Q$})\right]

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{g=1}^{G}p(\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g})p(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g})p(\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{g.})\right]p(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})p(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{2})p(\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{2}),

where
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{g},\dots,p_{G})
,
\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{g.}=(q_{g1},\dots,q_{gG})
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{2}=(\tau^{2}_{1},\dots,\tau^{2}_{L})
. In addition,
p(\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g})
,
p(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g})
,
p(\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{g.})
,
p(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
,
p(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{2})
, and
p(\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{2})
are prior densities; we discuss the choice of prior distributions in the next section.

2.2
Prior distributions

We propose using conditionally conjugate prior distributions that allow a wide range of prior information to be represented and facilitate MCMC computations.

For the factor loadings matrix
B
, we assign independent normal priors with mean 0 and variance
\tau_{l}
to the unconstrained elements of the
l
-th column (the
l
-th factor).
In addition, we assign for the variance
\tau_{l}
an inverse gamma prior distribution
IG(n_{\tau}/2,n_{\tau}s_{\tau}^{2}/2)
, where
n_{\tau}=1
, and
n_{\tau}s_{\tau}^{2}=1
. Further, as recommended by
Lopes and West [2004]
, for the uniqueness variances
\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{R}^{2}
, we assign independent inverse gamma priors
IG(n_{\sigma}/2,n_{\sigma}s_{\sigma}^{2}/2)
, where
n_{\sigma}=2.2
, and
n_{\sigma}s_{\sigma}^{2}=0.1
. This choice of hyperparameters results in a prior mean of 0.5 and infinite prior variance.

Next, we discuss the prior distributions for the cluster parameters. We assume for the mean vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g}
of cluster
g
a normal prior distribution with
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{g}^{\mu},\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu})
,
g=1,\dots,G
. We discuss the choice of
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{g}^{\mu}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu}
in the Appendix. Recall that the covariance matrix of the first cluster
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{1}
is diagonal. Denote by
\omega_{l}
the
l
-th diagonal element of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{1}
which corresponds to the
l
-th factor.
We assume for
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{L}
independent inverse gamma priors with
\omega_{l}\sim IG(n_{\omega_{l}}/2,n_{\omega_{l}}s_{\omega_{l}}^{2}/2)
.
We set the shape parameter of the prior to
n_{\omega_{l}}/2=2
, which implies that the prior mean of
l
-th element
\omega_{l}
is
s_{\omega_{l}}^{2}
.
The Appendix discusses the choice of
s_{\omega_{l}}^{2}
.
Further, we assume that the covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}
of cluster
g
follows an inverse Wishart prior with
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}\sim IW(n_{\Omega_{g}},\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{\Omega_{g}})
,

g=2,\dots,G
where
n_{\Omega_{g}}=2+L
.
This choice of hyperparameters implies that the prior mean of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}
is
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{\Omega_{g}}
.
We discuss the choice of
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{\Omega_{g}}
in the Appendix.

Lastly, we discuss the prior distributions for the initial cluster probabilities and the transition probability matrix.
Recall that the probability of a subject starting in cluster
g
at time point 1 is
p_{g}
.
We denote the vector of initial probabilities by
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{G})
. We assume a
priori
that
p
follows a Dirichlet distribution with parameter vector
(\alpha_{1},..,\alpha_{G})=(2,...,2)
.
Let
q_{j.}=(q_{j1},\dots,q_{jG})
be the vector of probabilities of transitioning from cluster
j
at one-time point to each of the
G
clusters at the next time point.
Note that
q_{jj}
is the probability of staying in the cluster
j
at the next time point and that
q_{j1}+\dots+q_{jG}=1
. We assume that
q_{j.}\sim Dirichlet(\alpha_{j1},\dots,\alpha_{jG})
where
\alpha_{jg}=2
,
g=1,...,G
.
Note that both for the initial probabilities and the transition probabilities, the choice of exponents
\alpha=2
implies that the prior density is equal to zero if any element of the probability vector is equal to zero.

3
Statistical Inference

We propose a Gibbs sampler
[
Gelfand and Smith, 1990b
]
to obtain a sample from the joint posterior distribution of all the parameters in BDCFM. In this section, we present the full conditional distributions of the parameters and the Gibbs sampler.

The full conditional distribution of the vector of common factors
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}
depends on the cluster assignment
Z_{it}
of subject
i
at timepoint
t
. Specifically, given
Z_{it}=g
, the full conditional of
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}
is
N(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{it},\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{it})
with

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{it}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}^{{}^{\prime}}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}^{-1})^{-1},

(2)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{it}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{it}(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}^{{}^{\prime}}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{it}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g}),

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}
are the prior mean vector and the prior covariance matrix of the
g
-th cluster. In addition, recall that
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}=diag(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{R}^{2})
.

The full conditional distribution of the mean of the
g
-th cluster,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g}
, is
N(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{g}^{\mu*},\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu*})
, with

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu-1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}n_{tg})^{-1},

(3)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{g}^{\mu*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu*}(\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu-1}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{g}^{\mu}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{tg}}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}),

where
\mathcal{C}_{tg}
is the set of subjects that belong to cluster
g
at time
t
, and
n_{tg}
is the cardinality of
\mathcal{C}_{tg}
.

Recall that the covariance matrix of the first cluster is
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{1}=diag(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{L})
.
The full conditional distribution of
\omega_{l}
,
l=1,\dots,L
, is
IG(n_{\omega_{l}}^{*}/2,n_{\omega_{l}}^{*}s_{\omega_{l}}^{*2}/2)
, with

\displaystyle n_{\omega_{l}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\omega_{l}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}n_{t1},

(4)

\displaystyle n_{\omega_{l}}^{*}s_{\omega_{l}}^{*2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\omega_{l}}s_{\omega_{l}}^{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{t1}}\left(x_{itl}-\mu_{1l}\right)^{2},

where
x_{itl}
is the
l
-th element of
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}
, and
\mu_{1l}
is the
l
-th element of the mean vector of cluster 1.

The full conditional distribution of the covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}
for
g=2,\dots,G
is an inverse Wishart distribution
IW(n_{\Omega_{g}}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{\Omega_{g}}^{*})
with parameters

\displaystyle n_{\Omega_{g}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\Omega_{g}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}n_{tg},

(5)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{\Omega_{g}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{\Omega_{g}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{tg}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g})(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g})^{\prime}.

We simulate the factor loadings matrix
B
by row.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}_{r.}
be the
r
-th row of
B
.
Due to the hierarchical structural constraint, the first row of
B
is fixed.
For the remaining rows of
B
, we consider two cases; Case 1:
1<r\leq L
, and Case 2:
r>L
.
For Case 1, the last
L-r+1
elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}_{r.}
are fixed.
This implies that there are
r-1
free elements in
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}_{r.}
.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}_{r.}^{*}
be a vector that contains the first
r-1
elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}_{r.}
.
In addition, let
\mbox{\boldmath$\Upsilon$}=diag(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{L})
.
Then for Case 1, the full conditional distribution of
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}^{*}_{r.}
is
N(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}^{b}_{r},\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{b}_{r})
, with

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{b}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Upsilon$}_{1:(r-1),1:(r-1)}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{r}^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it,1:(r-1)}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it,1:(r-1)}^{\prime}\right)^{-1},

(6)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$m$}^{b}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{r}^{b}\frac{1}{\sigma_{r}^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it,1:(r-1)}(y_{itr}-x_{itr}),

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\Upsilon$}_{1:(r-1),1:(r-1)}
is a submatrix of the first
r-1
rows and columns of the covariance matrix
\Upsilon
.
For Case 2,
r>L
, the full conditional distribution of
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}_{r.}
is
N(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}^{b}_{r},\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{b}_{r})
, with

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{b}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Upsilon$}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{r}^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}^{\prime}\right)^{-1},

(7)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$m$}^{b}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{r}^{b}\frac{1}{\sigma_{r}^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}y_{itr}.

The full conditional distribution of the
r
-th uniqueness
\sigma_{r}^{2}
, for
r=1,\dots,R
, is an inverse gamma distribution
IG(n_{\sigma}^{*}/2,n_{\sigma}^{*}s_{\sigma}^{*2}/2)
with

\displaystyle n_{\sigma}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\sigma}+ST,

(8)

\displaystyle n_{\sigma}^{*}s_{\sigma}^{*2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\sigma}s_{\sigma}^{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{S}(y_{itr}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{it}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}_{r.})^{2}.

Now let us consider the full conditional distribution of the variance
\tau_{l}
of the factor loadings of the
l
-th factor,
l=1,\dots,L
.
Due to the hierarchical structural constraint of
B
, the first
l

-th factor are fixed at either 0 or 1.
Thus, the full conditional distribution of
\tau_{l}
is an inverse gamma distribution
IG(n_{\tau}^{*}/2,n_{\tau}^{*}s_{\tau}^{*2}/2)
, with

\displaystyle n_{\tau}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\tau}+R-l,

(9)

\displaystyle n_{\tau}^{*}s_{\tau}^{*2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\tau}s_{\tau}^{2}+\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{(l+1):R,l}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{(l+1):R,l},

\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{(l+1):R,l}

Recall that the initial probability that subject

g
at time

is

. The full conditional distribution of the vector of initial probabilities
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{G})

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$p$}\mid.\sim Dir(\mbox{\boldmath$n$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}),

(10)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}_{1}=(n_{11},\dots,n_{1G})
,

at time

, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{G})
.

Let us now consider the simulation of the transition probability matrix
Q
. The full conditional distribution of the
j
-th row of

is a Dirichlet distribution

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{j.}\mid.\sim Dir(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{j.}+\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{j.}),

(11)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{j.}=(m_{j1},\dots,m_{jG})

4
Analysis of a Simulated Dataset

We have simulated a dataset with
S=200
subjects,
R=20
variables,
T=5
time points,
G=4
clusters, and
L=3
factors. The
200
subjects at the first time point are randomly assigned to one of the
4
clusters according to the probability vector
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=(0.45,0.26,0.16,0.13)
. Then, in the subsequent time points, subjects transition among clusters according to the transition probability matrix

\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}=\begin{bmatrix}0.75&0.15&0.10&0.00\\
0.20&0.55&0.15&0.10\\
0.25&0.15&0.50&0.10\\
0.10&0.15&0.20&0.55\end{bmatrix}.

The true mean vectors of each cluster are
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}=(7,4,5)
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{2}=(-7,3,-3)
,

6
Discussion

Here we have introduced our novel BDCFM framework that
combines HMMs and factor models to capture the temporal dynamics in multivariate longitudinal data. BDCFM provides cluster assignments for each subject at each time point, while permitting subjects to transition among clusters over time.
We have developed a Gibbs sampler to explore the posterior distribution of the parameters, which allows our BDCFM framework to provide both estimates of the unknown quantities and uncertainty quantification.

We evaluated the performance of our BDCFM framework through a simulated dataset.
BDCFM accurately estimated the parameters.
In addition,
BDCFM properly quantified the uncertainty and was well calibrated. Specifically, the 95% credible intervals contained the true values of the parameters more than 95% of the time.
Lastly, BDCFM correctly assigned subjects to clusters at each time point with a very low misclassification rate.

We have demonstrated the utility of our BDCFM framework with an analysis of a dataset on OUD recovery. This dataset had
S=252
subjects across
T=4
time points, and the BDCFM framework completed this analysis in 6.2 minutes. Lastly, we have created an R package that will be available on CRAN upon publication.

There are several promising directions for future research. One such direction is the development of methods for the choice of the number of factors and the number of clusters. Another possible future direction is the inclusion of regressors in the BDCFM framework. This may be used to estimate the impact of regressors such as changes in individual-level psychosocial factors on the probability of transition among clusters.

7.1
Empirical Bayes specification of priors for cluster parameters

BDCFM inherits some computational challenges from hidden Markov models.
Specifically, if the prior distribution on the cluster parameters is the same for each cluster, then the posterior distribution has several modes corresponding to different label orders of the clusters.
In that case, MCMC implementations may suffer from label switching, where two or more clusters may switch labels.
To prevent label switching, similarly to
Steele and Raftery [2010]
, we implement a prior specification for the parameters of each cluster based on empirical Bayes.

The empirical Bayes procedure combines factor analysis with
k
-means clustering.
First, we stack all transposed observed vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{it}^{\prime}
by row into a matrix
Y
with
n=S\times T
rows and
R
columns.
After that, we obtain an estimate
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}
of the factor loadings matrix using the function
fa
from the
R
package
psych

[
Revelle, 2024
]
.
While using
fa
, we set the method argument to
“minimum residual”
and the rotate argument to
“none”
.
After that, we compute a matrix
M
that transforms
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}
into a matrix
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}=\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}\mbox{\boldmath$M$}
that satisfies the hierarchical structural constraint.
From the output of the function
fa
, we also obtain a preliminary estimate
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}=diag(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{R})
, where
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{r}
is the estimated uniqueness that is equal to one minus the estimated communality of the
r
-th variable.

To compute preliminary estimates of the vectors of common factors, our empirical Bayes approach uses the weighted least squares estimate
[
Thomson, 1951
]

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}=(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}^{-1}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}})^{-1}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$},

where each row of
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}
corresponds to an estimated vector of common factors for a given subject at a specific time point.
Note that the computation of
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}
does not perform any shrinkage towards a common mean vector, which would be undesirable in the case when the vectors of common factors come from a mixture of Gaussians as assumed by BDCFM.
We then cluster the estimated vectors of common factors with
k
-means clustering
[
Tibshirani et al., 2001
]
.
After that, we order the clusters in decreasing order of cluster size.
Let
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{g}
and
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}}_{g}
,
g=1,\dots,G
, respectively be the sample mean vector and sample covariance matrix of the estimated vectors of common factors assigned to cluster
g
.

To respect the constraint that the first cluster has a diagonal covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{1}
, we compute the LDL decomposition
[
Trefethen and Bau III, 1997
]
of
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}}_{1}
, that is,
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}^{\prime}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}
is a lower triangular matrix and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{1}
is a diagonal matrix.
Then, we premultiply all preliminary estimates of vectors of common factors by
\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}^{-1}
.
As a result, the sample covariance matrix of the estimated vectors of common factors allocated to the first cluster becomes the diagonal matrix

\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{1}^{\mu}=\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}^{-1}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}^{-1^{\prime}}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{1}
.
Denote by
s_{\omega_{1}}^{2},\dots,s_{\omega_{L}}^{2}
the diagonal elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{1}^{\mu}
.
Recall that the covariance matrix of the first cluster is
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{1}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{L})
.
Then, we assign for
\omega_{l}
a prior
IG(n_{\omega_{l}}/2,n_{\omega_{l}}s_{\omega_{l}}^{2}/2)
, where
n_{\omega_{l}}/2=2
implies that the mean of the
l
-th element
\omega_{l}
is
s_{\omega_{l}}^{2}
.

Further, the sample covariance matrix of the estimated vectors of common factors assigned to the
g
-th cluster becomes

\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu}=\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}^{-1}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}}_{g}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}^{-1^{\prime}}.

Thus, we assume for the covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}
of the
g
-th cluster an inverse Wishart prior with parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{\Omega_{g}}=\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu}
and
n_{\Omega_{g}}=L+2
, implying that the prior mean of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{g}
is
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{\Omega_{g}}
.
Finally, the mean vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{g}
of the
g
-th cluster is assigned prior
N(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{g}^{\mu},\mbox{\boldmath$C$}_{g}^{\mu})
where

\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{g}^{\mu}=\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{1}^{-1}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{g}.
