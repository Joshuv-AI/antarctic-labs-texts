A new mechanism underlying mitochondrial dysfunction in schizophrenia – attenuated mRNA transport of the complex I subunit NDUFV2 by its pseudogene NDUFV2P1
Authors: Rachel Karry, Dorit Ben-Shachar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile schizophrenia (SZ) etiology remains unclear, accumulating evidence implicates mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly complex-I of the respiratory chain and its essential free-electron scavenger subunit, NDUFV2, as a contributor to neuronal and behavioral impairments observed in SZ. Our recent studies suggest a potential role for the NDUFV2 pseudogene (NDUFV2P1) in NDUFV2 deficits. Here, we describe a mechanism by which NDUFV2P1 negatively controls NDUFV2 mRNA transport and its protein levels in SZ-derived lymphocyte cell lines (SZ-LCLs). We found increased NDUFV2P1 transcript levels in SZ frontal cortex postmortem specimens (SZ-FCX) and across all studied SZ-LCLs subcellular fractions. However, NDUFV2 levels were reduced in SZ-FCX and in all cell compartments, except for the nucleus, as compared to healthy subjects-derived LCLs (CTL-LCLs), suggesting its impaired nuclear export. Concomitantly, we observed increased NDUFV2P1, yet decreased NDUFV2 mRNA binding to NXF1, a key player in nuclear mRNA export. Overexpression of NDUFV2P1 in CTL-LCLs mimicked the SZ-state, reducing NDUFV2 levels and its binding to NXF1. The interactome of both mRNAs revealed an opposite binding profile for most RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) in SZ-LCLs compared to CTL-LCLs. Pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially bound RBPs to both transcripts revealed additional potential interference sites for NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1, including ribosomal-, spliceosome-, and RNA transport-related RBPs. This study uncovers a new mechanism in which NDUFV2P1 interferes with RBPs involved in regulating NDUFV2 transport from the nucleus to mitochondrial-bound ribosomes. While further validation is necessary to substantiate this mechanism, the findings highlight NDUFV2P1 potential as a means for regulating mitochondrial function and consequently energy metabolism in SZ.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Biomarkers

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) most probably originates from inadequate development of the brain instigated by interwoven genetic and environmental factors1,2. Mitochondrial dysfunction and deficits in energy metabolism in the brain and periphery have been repeatedly reported as etiopathological factors in this disorder3–9. Recent genetic studies show a causal link between mitochondria-related genes, including those of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and SZ6,8,10,11. Previously and recently, we have shown a direct link between mitochondria and SZ-related biological and behavioral pathologies both in-vitro and in-vivo12,13.

A repeatedly reported important contributor to mitochondrial dysfunction in SZ is the first complex (complex I - CoI) of the electron transport system and, in particular, its core subunit, NDUFV2, which is the most severely and consistently affected subunit in SZ3,14–19. NDUFV2 is an essential, labile subunit and acts as an electron acceptor antioxidant17. Genetic studies suggested NDUFV2 as a risk gene in the disorder20,21. In rats, down-regulation of NDUFV2 was associated with reduced ATP levels, increased ROS production and neuronal mal-structure in the hippocampus22. Our recent data suggest that an alteration in the pseudogene of NDUFV2 (NDUFV2P1) may underly the deficits observed in NDUFV2, in SZ19.

NDUFV2P1 (ENSG00000267809; GRCh37 (hg19), chr19: 53,727,226–53,728,132, reverse) is a processed pseudogene of NDUFV2 (ENSG00000267809; GRCh38p14, chr18:9,102,628-9,134,343) originating from NDUFV2 mRNA that has been reverse-transcribed and integrated into the genome at a new location23. Pseudogenes are non-coding genes, ubiquitous, unevenly distributed, and abundant in the human genome, comparable in number to coding genes, and have evolved during the genome evolution by different processes24–26. An increasing number of pseudogenes retain biological functions and are involved in cellular regulatory mechanisms and development. Such pseudogenes can regulate their protein-coding parental genes by several mechanisms. Due to their high sequence similarities, some pseudogenes can competitively bind miRNA or RNA binding proteins (RBPs) and thereby interfere with the regulation processes of their parent-gene RNAs at the level of transcription, mRNA transport, and translation24,27,28. Others can generate endogenous small interfering RNAs29, or can function as trans-acting antisense RNAs30. Various physiological (e.g., cell differentiation, epigenetic modifications) or pathological conditions may lead to alterations in pseudogene expression31. Indeed, over the last decades, dysregulation of pseudogenes was repeatedly shown to be associated with various human diseases, such as cancer, inflammation, and type II diabetes25,31,32. For example, Phosphatase and TENsin homolog Pseudogene1 (PTENP1) was shown to be involved in tumor biology27,33 and High Mobility Group AT-Hook1 Pseudogene1 (HMGA1P1) in type II diabetes28.

Our previous data in SZ-derived postmortem specimens and lymphocyte cell line (LCLs) showed a significant increase in NDUFV2P1 transcripts as compared to healthy subjects. A meta-analysis transcriptomic study showed an increase in transcript levels of four mitochondrial and three ribosomal pseudogenes in the PFC of SZ female and male patients, respectively34. We have also shown a statistically significant, high inverse correlation between NDUFV2P1 transcripts and NDUFV2 protein levels and cell oxygen consumption in SZ- LCLs and CTL-LCLs, suggesting that NDUFV2P1 may interfere with its parent gene, thereby contributing to CoI deficits and mitochondrial dysfunction in the disorder19. The present study investigated whether NDUFV2P1 interferes with its parent gene transcript and explored the underlying molecular mechanisms leading to reduced levels of NDUFV2 in SZ.

Experimental procedures

Human samples

Frozen frontal cortex specimens (FCX-BA 46/9) of SZ patients (DSM-IV criteria n = 10) and of healthy controls (n = 10) matched for age, sex, race, postmortem interval, pH, and brain side, were provided by the Stanley Foundation Neuropathology Consortium (Bethesda, MD). Detailed description of the Stanley Brain Collection and protection of human rights was previously reported35.

EBV-transformed human lymphocyte cell lines (LCLs) derived from DSM-IV diagnosed SZ patients (6 males, 4 females, average age 45.4 ± 8.1 years old, range 31–60 years) and healthy control subjects with no prior history of psychiatric illness (7 males, 3 females, average age 45.9 ± 7.8 years old, range 33–60 years) were used in the study and were kindly provided by Prof. Peter Zill, Psychiatry Center, Ludwig-Maximilians-University of Munich, Germany. The average Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) scores of patients were: Negative scores 31.8 ± 4.9, Positive scores 22.1 ± 7.4, Global scores 54.4 ± 14.5. LCLs. The study was approved by the Center’s Helsinki Committees. All participants gave written informed consent. Five lines/group were randomly selected from the cohort for this study. LCLs were cultured as previously described12.

Details of the following methodologies are provided in the Supplementary Method Section.

RT-PCR

RNA was extracted from LCLs lysates or their subcellular fractions and reverse transcribed by RT-PCR and qRT-PCR as previously described. Supplementary Table 1 lists primers sequences. cDNA samples were analyzed by Sanger sequencing as previously described19.

Immunoblotting

Protein was extracted from LCLs lysates or the subcellular fractions using the radio-immunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) containing protease-phosphatase inhibitor cocktails, as previously described19. Supplementary Table 2 lists the antibodies used in this study. Band optical density was assessed by TotalLab Quant software.

Subcellular fractionation

Mitochondria were isolated from LCLs (1–1.5 × 107 cells/line) and purified on a Percoll gradient as previously described36,37.

Ribosomes were isolated by sucrose cushion centrifugation based on previous reports38,39. LCLs (1–2 × 107 cells/line) were incubated in the presence of 100 µg/ml cyclohexamide (CHX) for 10 min 37 °C. Cells were centrifuged 500 × g, 5 min, at 4 °C, and the pellet was washed twice with ice-cold PBS-CHX. Pellet was lyzed with 100–300 µl fresh lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.4, 5 mM MgCL2, 100 mM KCl, 200 µg/ml heparine, 1% triton x 100, 2 mM DTT, and 100 µg/ml CHX). For RNA isolation RNASe inhibitor 1000 U/ml, and for protein isolation anti-proteases cocktail were added. Lysates were incubated for 10–15 min on ice and centrifuged 12,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant 250–350 µl was placed on a similar volume of cushion buffer (4% sucrose, 20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.4, 150mMKCl 5 mM MgCL2) and was centrifuged for 60–70 min at 535,000 × g (Sorval Micro-Ultracentrifuge RC-M150) at 4 °C. The supernatant was stored at −80 °C. For RNA or protein isolation, the ribosomal pellet was homogenized in a glass/glass homogenizer in Tri-reagent or protein lysis buffer, respectively.

Mitochondria-bound ribosomes were isolated based on a previous report40. LCLs (5 × 107 cells/line) were incubated in the presence of 100 µg/ml CHX for 10 min, centrifuged, and washed twice as described above. Cells were re-suspended in the homogenization buffer (10 mM Hepes-KOH pH 7.4, 0.25 M sucrose, 1 mM EDTA-KOH, 100 µg/ml CHX), homogenized in conical glass/glass Patter Elvejen homogenizer, and centrifuged at 1600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was centrifuged at 22,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and the obtained pellet was re-suspended with homogenization buffer and centrifuged at 1600 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The final supernatant containing the mitochondria was centrifuged at 22,000 x g for 15 min at 4 °C and the mitochondria-bound ribosomal fraction was isolated by sucrose cushion centrifugation as described above.

Nuclear and cytosolic fractions from LCLs (5–10 × 106 cells/line) were isolated as previously described41 and according to BioSource C-070276 1107 1-Jan-2007. Subcellular fractions were stored at –80 °C until use. The purity of the subcellular fractions was verified by immunoblotting with sub-fraction-specific antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In-vitro transcription/translation

35S-methionine labeled proteins were obtained using the TNT® T7 Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation Systems kit (Cat# L1170 Promega, USA) in the presence of 35S-methionine-cysteine (>1000 Ci/mmol, Cat# NEG009T PerkinElmer, NEN Radiochemicals, MA). Linearized pGEM-T vector containing NDUFV2 or NDUFV2P1 sequences or the T7-promoter containing RT-PCR products of both transcripts obtained from four LCLs/group were used as templates. The reaction was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions with minor adaptations as previously described19. [35S]-methionine labeled protein products were loaded on a 10%SDS–PAGE gel, subjected to electrophoresis, and blotted. The radiolabeled proteins were then visualized by phosphorimaging.

RNA pulldown

mRNAs were synthesized in-vitro from the T7-promoter RT-PCR products, obtained from an equal amount of total RNA extracted from n = 2–3 LCLs/group of full-length NDUFV2 and the short cytosolic spliced variant of NDUFV2P1 using the TranscriptAid T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (Cat# K0441 ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sanger sequencing showed no difference in the sequences of the RT-PCR products from the control and SZ samples.). The obtained mRNAs were extracted and purified using the GeneJET RNApurification kit (Cat# K0731 ThermoFisher Scientific). The Biotin labeling mix Mix (Cat# 11685597910 Sigma-Aldrich) was used to incorporate Biotin-16-UTP to approximately every 20–25 nucleotides of the transcripts as previously described42. Labeling efficiency in each sample was estimated using the Biotin Chromogenic Detection Kit (Cat# K061 ThermoFisher Scientific). Equal levels of biotin-labeled NDUFV2 or NDUFV2P1 mRNAs from SZ-LCLs and CTL- LCLs were used for pull-down experiments. Finally, to enrich for RBPs, the Pierce™ Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-Down Kit (Cat# 20164 ThermoFisher Scientific) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA-bound beads were equilibrated in protein RNA binding buffer and then incubated with 100 µg cell lysate protein/100 µl reaction buffer for 60 min at 4 °C. The extracted RBPs were assessed by Liquid Chromatography-Andem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and data analyzed as previously described40. Based on previous multiple testing corrections methods in proteomic43, the criteria for significantly altered proteins were an effect size cut-off of log2-ratio ≥ |0.32| and false discovery rate <0.05. To assess the differentially expressed RBPs P < 0.05 and q < 0.05 values were used for NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 respectively, as the overall significant difference between groups was lower for NDUFV2 (n = 2 LCLs/group) than for NDUFV2P1 (n = 2 CTL-LCLs; n = 3 SZ-LCLs). P values were used, as applying q‑value thresholds in the context of NDUFV2 would have resulted in the exclusion of all signals, including those showing biologically meaningful enrichment. The differentially expressed proteins, their biological functions and interactions were analyzed using Webgestalt, String and Perseus softwares. For more details see Supplementary Methods.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP was performed using the Magna RIP kit (Cat#17-701 Millipore) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In short, 5 × 107 cells, n = 4–5 LCLs/group were used. Reverse crosslinking was performed following cell washing with PBS by 1% formealdehyde (F-8775 Molecular Biology). Slow cell shaking was performed at RT for 15 min. Crosslinking was stopped by the addition of 0.2 M glycine for 5 min. Samples were centrifuged 2500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed twice with cold PBS. Nuclear fraction was purified as described above. RNA immunoprecipitation was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions using 5 µg of NXF1 antibody (anti-TAP (NXF1) (Invitrogen Cat. # MA1-5882), anti-IgG-negative control and anti-hSNRNP70 positive control, both provided in the Magna RIP kit. In short, RNA beads were prepared and antibodies bound. Nuclear lysates were thawed and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, resuspended in RIP IP buffer, added to the antibody bound beads and rotated overnight at 4 °C. Ten microliters of the nuclear lysates were kept for assessing total mRNA input for each sample and 10 µl for assessing protein levels of mRNA bound NXF1 by western blotting. Each immunoprecipitated was washed thoroughly, then treated with RIP wash buffer containing proteinase K and 10% SDS to digest the proteins. RNA was purified from the beads’ supernatant and the input samples according to the manufacturer instructions and specific bound transcripts were assessed by qRT-PCR. The relative expression of NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 was normalized to NXF1 bound GAPDH, 18 s or input levels (nuclear mRNA) of the respective transcripts.

RNA stability

LCL cells (3 × 107) from five LCLs/group were grown and split to 5 × 106cells/time point (0, 30, 60, 120, 240, and 360 min). Actinomycine D (Sigma-Akdrich A9415) 5 µg/ml was added to each well at time 0. RNA was extracted at each time point, and 1 µg RNA was taken for qRT-PCR. RNA stability was assessed for each of the following mRNAs: β-actin, GAPDH, 18sRNA, NDUFV2, and NDUFV2P1. T1/2 and kdecay (K) were calculated using the one-phase decay analysis in GraphPad Prism as previously described44.

Plasmid construction and cell infection

For pNDUFV2P1 Lentivirus, the entire transcript sequence (NG_001161.4) was cloned into the pGEM-T vector (Promega, WI, USA), and its sequence was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Next, the NDUFV2P1 fragment was cut by adequate enzymes (BamHI and EcoRI) purified, and ligated into pEF-ENTR A vector (Invitrogen, CA, USA), to generate the pEF-ENTR A-NDUFV2P1 expressing vector. pEF-ENTR A-NDUFV2P1 was used to construct the final pLenti-CMV-GFP-DEST-NDUFV2P1 plasmid employing the GATEWAY™ cloning technique with GFP and NDUFV2P1 having different promoters (Supplementary Fig. 2). The final plasmid was packed with a 3rd generation Lentivirus packaging system. The same plasmid lacking NDUFV2P1 serves as a control (empty vector). LCLs were infected in the presence of 1:20 (w:v) polybrene (10 mg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich TR-1003) for 3 h, washed, and analyzed 5 days after infection. For RIP experiments, the nuclear fraction was isolated from each of the five LCLs/group after crosslinking and then pooled into one sample/group for IP experiment with anti-NXF1 antibody.

Statistics

To determine whether each variable met the requirements for parametric testing, we first evaluated normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Data were normally distributed and exhibiting homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test p > 0.05) and group means were compared using Student’s t-test. When the assumption of equal variances was violated (Levene’s test p < 0.05), as in the RNA pulldown data, we applied Welch’s t-test, which adjusts for unequal variances. For comparisons involving more than two groups, we used one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD test. On the postmortem data, we conducted Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA). Age, sex, brain pH, and postmortem interval were included as covariates to determine whether covariates modified the primary group effects. Pearson’s correlation coefficients with r ≥ |0.7| (r2 ≥ 0.49) were classified as strong and reliable, as this threshold indicates that at least ~50% of the variance is shared between variables. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a threshold of P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.1.1 or IBM SPSS statistics 24 software. A power analysis was performed to estimate the required sample size. Assuming an expected minimum of 20% difference in NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 levels between healthy and SZ samples (based on our previous reports), with α = 0.05 and power = 0.8, the calculation suggested that a minimum of four samples (n = 4) per group could be sufficient to detect a statistically significant effect.

Results

NDUFV2P1 characteristics in SZ and CTL specimens

In human FCX postmortem specimens, the transcript level of NDUFV2P1 is significantly increased (P < 0.0137) and associated with a significant decrease in NDUFV2 transcript levels (P < 0.0001) as compared to healthy subjects derived specimens (Fig. 1A). Age, gender, and brain pH had no effect on the significance of the difference between the groups for both mRNAs. Postmortem interval (PMI) had a significant effect (P < 0.02) on the difference between the two groups for both NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1, but the difference between SZ and CTL remained significant (adjusted P < 0.005 and 0.035 for NDUFV2 and NDUV2P1, respectively) as the interaction [group X covariate] was not significant (P > 0.5) for both mRNAs.

In the FCX postmortem specimen, an increase in NDUFV2P1 mRNA levels is associated with reduced levels of NDUFV2 mRNA (A). Values are means ± SEM *P < 0.0001;**P < 0.0137. Unlike NDUFV2, which full length sequence is observed both in the nuclear and cytosol fraction the full transcript length of NDUFV2P1 (1010 bp) is observed in the nuclear fraction (B), while its spliced variant (~521 bp) is observed in the cytosol (C). In-vitro transcription translation of RT-PCR and pGEM-T products of NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 full-length transcripts from 4LCLs/group resulted in a 35S-methionine-labeled NDUFV2 protein, while NDUFV2P1 transcripts were unable to produce a protein in both CTL and SZ samples (D).

Prior to studying a potential interaction between the two transcripts, we assessed NDUFV2P1 properties. NDUFV2P1 transcript in our hands is 1010 bp long and has an identical sequence in both SZ and healthy (CTL) subjects’ derived LCLs. NDUFV2P1 is unique to humans, as neither the NCBI genome browser nor nucleotide sequence alignment detected its entire mRNA sequence in other organisms. The entire NDUFV2P1 transcript showed 72% similarity with its parent gene NDUFV2. Downstream of its first 314 bp, NDUFV2P1 is almost identical to the NDUFV2 sequence (98% similarity), starting at 50 bp downstream of the ATG of NDUFV2. Similar to other long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), NDUFV2P1 is spliced. Unlike NDUFV2, its full-length transcript is observed in the nuclear fraction but not in the cytosolic fraction (Fig. 1B, C and Supplementary Fig. 3). Stepwise RT-PCR, using the same forward prime (F-PG5) and different reverse primers (listed in Supplementary Table 1) revealed that a spliced variant of ~521 bp is expressed in the cytosolic fraction, its 260 bp 5’ end showing 99% homology with NDUFV2. To verify that NDUFV2P1 cannot produce a protein in SZ cells, we performed in-vitro transcription/translation of the linearized plasmid pGEM-T containing full-length NDUFV2P1 and NDUFV2 sequences, and of their T7-RT-PCR full-length products. Unlike NDUFV2, the NDUFV2P1 transcript from both SZ and CTL-LCLs (n = 4/group), failed to produce 35S-methionine labeled protein (Fig. 1D).

The subcellular distribution of NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 transcript levels and NDUFV2 decay rates are altered in SZ

Our previous studies in an Israeli SZ-LCLs cohort showed lower transcript and protein levels of NDUFV2, while higher levels of NDUFV2P1 transcripts. In the present study, using a German SZ-LCLs cohort, similar alterations were observed. Thus, NDUFV2 transcripts and protein levels were significantly reduced (P < 0.001, P < 0.028, respectively), while NDUFV2P1 transcript levels were increased (P < 0.014) in SZ-LCLs as compared to CTL-LCLs (Fig. 2A–C). A significant decrease in NDUFV2 protein levels was also observed in isolated SZ-derived mitochondria (P < 0.006), which, as previously reported, lead to complex I deficits (Fig. 2M, Q). Based on the hitherto findings, we hypothesized that the deficits observed in the NDUFV2 protein and mitochondrial function stem from NDUFV2P1 interference with its parent gene. To verify this hypothesis, we overexpressed NDUFV2P1 in CTL-LCLs (n = 5 LCLs/group) and observed that the increase in NDUFV2P1 levels (P < 0.019) was associated with a significant reduction in transcript and protein levels of NDUFV2 (P < 0.01 and P < 0.03, respectively) as compared to empty virus-infected cells, mimicking the findings in SZ-LCLs and suggesting that the pseudogene plays a role in the regulation of its parent gene mRNA and protein (Fig. 2D).

To investigate potential intracellular interacting sites between NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1, we first assessed their subcellular distribution in SZ- and CTL-LCLs (n = 4–5 LCLs/group) in the following fractions: nuclear, cytosolic, cytosol-ribosomal, and mitochondria-bound ribosomal fractions.

As depicted in (Fig. 2I–P), in the nuclear fraction, NADUFV2 transcript and protein levels were similar in both cohorts. NDUFV2P1 transcript levels, however, were significantly higher (P < 0.004) in SZ-LCLs as compared to CTL-LCLs (Fig. 2I, M, N). In the cytosolic fraction of SZ-LCLs, NDUFV2 mRNA and protein levels were significantly lower (P < 0.002; P < 0.008, respectively), and NDUFV2P1 transcript levels remained higher than in the controls (P < 0.0006) (Fig. 2J, M, O). In the cytosol-ribosomal fractions, no change was observed between both cohorts in both NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 mRNAs and NDUFV2 protein levels (Fig.2K, M, P). However, in mitochondria-bound ribosomal fraction, the primary site for NDUFV2 translation43, both transcripts were significantly lower in the SZ cohort as compared to the control (P < 0.02, P < 0.0007, respectively) (Fig. 2L), suggesting that NDUFV2P1 transport to the mitochondria-bound ribosome is hindered in SZ-LCLs.

The observed reduction in NDUFV2 and the increase in NDUFV2P1 mRNA levels in total cell lysates are not due to parallel differences in their mRNA stability. Hence, no significant change was observed in the half-life time (T1/2) and Kdecay (K) of NDUFV2P1 and GAPDH, yet NDUFV2 transcripts showed a significantly reduced degradation rate in SZ-LCLs as compared to CTL-LCLs (T1/2 = 116.74 ± 35.42 vs. 24.57 ± 4.19, P < 0.03; KDecay = 0.009 ± 0.002 vs. 0.031 ± 0.005, P < 0.01; n = 5 LCLs/group) (Fig. 2R and Table 1). The latter is in line with our previous findings of reduced degradation rates of NDUFV2 protein in the cytosolic and mitochondrial fractions in SZ-LCLs, which probably contributed to the malfunctioning of NDUFV2 protein19. Taken together, these data suggest an intact transcription process of NDUFV2 and point at abnormal nuclear/cytosolic export process of NDUFV2 mRNA in SZ, which can be one possible site of interference between both transcripts.

NDUFV2P1 transcript interferes with NDUFV2 binding to Nuclear RNA Export Factor 1 (NXF1) in SZ

Given the subcellular fractionation findings, we next studied whether NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 mRNAs interact at the nuclear export machinery. We observed a high and significant inverse correlation between the mRNA levels of nuclear NDUFV2P1 and cytosolic NDUFV2 (r = −0.839, P = 0.009), as well as between NXF1-bound transcript levels of NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 (r = −0.986, P = 0.014) (Fig. 3A, B). TRanscription-Export complex (TREX) is a protein complex that links mRNA transcription with its exit from the nucleus processes. NXF1 coordinates transcriptional dynamics, 3′ end processing, and nuclear export of mRNAs and lncRNAs. NXF1 dimers with nuclear transport factor 2 like export factor 1 (NXT1) to facilitate the last step in mRNA nuclear transport to the cytoplasm through direct interaction with the nuclear pore complex40,45,46. No other TREX complex-related RBPs, such as CHTOP ALYREF (Fig. 3C, D), and THOC2/3/5 and DDX39 (data not shown) showed a significantly high inverse correlation. We, therefore, performed nuclear RNA immunoprecipitation to explore the efficiency of binding of the NDUFV2 entire transcript and the 512 bp long NDUFV2P1 mRNA to NXF1. We first assessed the nuclear levels (input) of NDUFV2, NDUFV2P1, and GAPDH mRNAs following the crosslinking procedure (n = 4 LCLs/group). Similar to our finding in the subcellular fractionation experiment, there was a significant increase in nuclear NDUFV2P1 (P < 0.0001) while no change in NDUFV2 and GAPDH transcripts’ levels in SZ-LCLs as compared to CTL-LCLs (n = 4 LCLs/group) (Fig. 3E). NXF1-bound NDUFV2 transcript level was significantly lower in SZ as compared to controls (3.10 ± 0.86 vs. 28.67 ± 11.1; P < 0.05), while that of NDUFV2P1 was increased (11.59 ± 5.79 vs. 2.35 ± 0.29; P < 0.03) (Fig. 3F). To investigate whether NDUFV2P1 is responsible, at least in part, for the decrease in NDUFV2 binding to NXF1 in SZ samples, we overexpressed NDUFV2P1 or its empty vector in CTL-LCLs (n = 5 LCLcs/group). After isolation of the nuclear fraction, we pooled together all five samples/group into one sample/group to receive a measurable amount of NXF1-bound mRNA in the infected cells and to increase reliability. The NXF1-bound mRNA levels of NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 in CTL-LCLs overexpressing NDUFV2P1 were compared to CTL-LCLs treated with empty virus and with those of pooled naïve SZ-LCLs and CTL-LCLs (n = 5 LCLs/group). Overexpression of NDUFV2P1 in CTL-LCLs resulted in 180% increase in its mRNA levels in the nuclear fraction, with no change in NDUFV2 transcript levels as compared to empty virus-infected cells (Fig. 3G). Following NDUFV2P1 overexpression in CTL-LCLs, NXF1-bound NDUFV2P1 mRNA levels were 300% increased, while those of NXF1-bound NDUFV2 were reduced by 50% as compared to cells infected with empty virus (Fig. 3H). Comparing pooled naïve SZ-LCLs to CTL-LCLs (n = 5 LCLcs/group) revealed a similar pattern of change both in the nuclear fraction and in the NXF1-bound mRNAs for both genes (Fig. 3I, J). These data strongly support NDUF2P1 interference with its parent gene on NXF1-mediated nuclear export.

The interactome pattern of NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 mRNAs is oppositely altered in SZ

Finally, to elucidate additional intracellular interaction sites of NDUFV2 mRNA and its pseudogene we assessed their interactomes. In-vitro synthesized biotinylated mRNAs from T7-RT-PCR products of SZ and healthy subjects’ LCLs (2-3 LCLs/group) were incubated with their respective protein lysates, mimicking the in-vivo conditions. Levels of the RBPs were assessed by LC-MS/MS. A total of 1848 RBPs (FDR < 0.01) binding to NDUFV2 and/or NDUFV2P1 were identified. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the significantly different NDUFV2-RBPs (n = 197; p < 0.05) and NDUFV2P1-RBPs (n = 945; q < 0.05) and principal component analysis (PCA) distinguished between SZ and healthy subjects for both mRNAs (Fig. 4A–C). Overall, the bound RBPs to SZ-derived NDUFV2 transcript showed lower levels than those bound to healthy control-derived mRNA, while an opposite result was observed for those bound to SZ-derived NDUFV2P1 mRNA, which showed increased levels as compared to healthy controls (Fig. 4A, B and Supplementary Table 3), further supporting the potential of NDUFV2P1 to interfere with its parent gene binding to specific RBPs. Over-representation analysis (ORA) of significant RBPs followed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified three main pathways for NDUFV2-bound RBPs: ribosome (FDR = 2.005e-2; P = 1.366e-4), spliceosome (FDR = 2.005e-2; P = 1.845e-4) and RNA transport (FDR = 2.005e-2; P = 7.538e-5) (Fig. 4D). For NDUFV2P1-bound RBPs four top pathways were identified: spliceosome (FDR = 2.2e-16; P = 2.2e-16), ribosome (FDR = 2.2e-16; P = 2.2e-16), mRNA surveillance (FDR = 3.619e-13; P = 3.3307e-15) and RNA transport (FDR = 3.619e-13; P = 3.331e-15) (Fig. 4E). Interestingly, 109 RBPs did not bind to CTL-NDUFV2P1 mRNA yet did bind with high abundance to mRNAs of SZ-NDUFV2P1 as well as to NDUFV2 from both cohorts. Pathway enrichment analysis of these RBPs identified two pathways - RNA polymerase (FDR = 4.7163e-2; P = 2.228e-4) and RNA transport (FDR = 4.2432e-3; P = 6.9e-6), further emphasizing RNA transport machinery as a site of interaction between the pseudogene and its parent gene (Fig. 4F). In contrast, only four RBPs bound to CTL-NDUFV2P1 but not to SZ-NDUFV2P1 mRNAs, while equally to CTL and SZ NDUFV2 mRNA. The differentially expressed RBPs of NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 mRNAs and those comprising each of the pathways are presented in (Supplementary Tables 3–5). Despite the inherent limitations of an in-vitro assessment of mRNA interactome, and the lack of multiple-testing correction for NDUFV2, the similar pathways obtained for both transcripts and the decreased RBPs binding to NDUFV2 transcripts, while increased binding to NDUFV2P1 in SZ samples, suggest that NDUFV2P1 is a trans-acting factor interfering with NDUFV2 mRNA processing, its transport from the nucleus to the ribosome, and its translation.

Discussion

The main finding of the present study is the uncovering of a previously unrecognized mechanism whereby the pseudogene NDUFV2P1 interferes with the mRNA of its parent gene NDUFV2, upstream of transcription, as is graphically depicted in Fig. 5. Consequently, NDUFV2 protein levels and function are reduced, which as previously demonstrated lead to CoI deficits and mitochondrial dysfunction in SZ19. Here, we show that in SZ-FCX specimens, the increase in NDUFV2P1 transcripts is associated with a decrease in NDUFV2 transcripts. This relationship remains statistically significant after controlling potential confounders, including age, gender, brain pH, and PMI, thereby reinforcing the presence of disease-dependent main effects. Our data demonstrate that, similar to other lncRNAs47,48, NDUFV2P1 and primarily its spliced variant, act as trans-acting negative modulators of NDUFV2 expression. Notably, there is no experimental evidence for miRNAs or siRNAs targeting NDUFV2 (refs. 49,50 miRTarBase 2025), excluding competition of small RNAs as regulators of NDUFV2 expression.

A graphical summary of NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 state and interaction in SZ as compared to CTL samples.

Nuclear NDUFV2 mRNA levels are similar in SZ and CTL cells however, NDUFV2P1 mRNA levels are significantly increased in the SZ nucleus (A). NDUFV2 mRNA binding to NXF1, which facilitates the final step of the mRNA transport process from the nucleus to the cytoplasm through a direct interaction with the nuclear pore complex, is hindered by NDUFV2P1 in SZ-LCLs. Hence, NDUFV2 mRNA binding to NXF1 is decreased, while NDUFV2P1 binding is increased in SZ cells (B). The altered binding to NXF1 underlies the cytosolic reduction of NDUFV2 and the increase of NDUFV2P1 mRNAs levels in SZ-LCLs as compared to CTL-LCLs (C). NDUFV2 mRNA transport by RBPs through the cytosol to the ribosomes is probably also hindered as higher levels of RBPs bind NDUFV2P1 mRNA, while lower levels bind to NDUFV2 mRNA in SZ as compared to CTL (D). In mitochondrial bound ribosome, the main site of NDUFV2 mRNA translation, reduced level of NDUFV2 mRNA is observed, which leads to reduced levels of NDUFV2 protein observed in mitochondria, while NDUFV2P1 transcript levels are higher in CTL than in SZ (E), suggesting that at this site NDUFV2P1 is bound to translation inhibitory RBP, such as FMRP1, thereby reducing the inhibitory effect in CTL, yet less so in SZ, contributing to the decrease in NDUFV2 protein levels in SZ. Reduced levels of intra-mitochondrial NDUFV2 protein lead to deficits in complex I activity and finally to mitochondrial dysfunction in the disease.

Subcellular distribution of both transcripts shows that NDUFV2P1 transcript levels are elevated in the nuclear fraction of SZ-LCLs as compared to CTL-LCLs, whereas NDUFV2 levels are similar in both cohorts, suggesting an intact transcription of NDUFV2 in SZ-LCLs. In the cytosolic fraction, levels of NDUFV2 mRNA and protein are reduced, while NDUFV2P1 mRNA levels remain high in SZ-LCLS, demonstrating a high inverse and significant correlation between nuclear NDUFV2P1 and cytosolic NDUFV2 transcripts. Interestingly, a role for a pseudogene in the regulation of its parent gene mRNA stability was demonstrated for Makorin1-P1 and Makorin1 mRNA51. However, in our case, the decrease in cytosolic NDUFV2 mRNA is not due to its impaired mRNA stability, as its mRNA decay rate is significantly lower in SZ-LCLs than in CTL-LCLs. These findings are in line with the significant reduction in NDUFV2 protein degradation rate in SZ-LCLs cytosolic and mitochondrial fractions19. Taken together, the above findings point at the nuclear export machinery as a candidate site for interaction between both mRNAs. Indeed, our data show that in SZ-nuclear fraction, the level of NDUFV2P1 mRNA bound to NXF1 is increased, while that of NDUFV2 mRNA is decreased as compared to CTL-LCLs. NXF1 binding to GAPDH mRNAs, which is partially transported through the TREX complex52 was similar in both cohorts, suggesting no overall impairment in NXF1 binding capacity in SZ. Overexpression of NDUFV2P1 in CTL-LCLs, caused elevated binding of NDUFV2P1 mRNA associated with a reduction in the binding of NDUFV2 mRNA to NXF1, mimicking our observation in naïve SZ-LCLs. These data provide direct evidence for the interference of the pseudogene with the export of its parent gene mRNA from the nucleus to the cytosol. NXF1 is the last protein in the chain of events of the export process of mRNA from the nucleus into the cytosol through the nuclear pore complex52,53. Therefore, any interference in NDUFV2 mRNA binding upstream to NFX1, such as binding to proteins forming the TREX complex, which links mRNA transcription to its export, will be reflected in the extent of NDUFV2 binding to NXF1. Probably, multiple sites of interaction exist between NDUFV2 and its pseudogene mRNAs during mRNA processing, transport, and binding to the ribosomes. Indeed, analyzing the interactome of both mRNAs showed an overall reduction in RBPs binding to SZ-NDUFV2 mRNA, whereas an enhanced binding to SZ-NDUFV2P1 transcripts as compared to their CTL mRNAs. To simulate the in-vivo conditions, we assessed SZ and CTL NDUFV2 and NDUFV2P1 mRNAs interactome in the presence of their respective cellular homogenates. These conditions limit our ability to differentiate the relative contribution of each of the assay components to the extent of RBPs-mRNAs binding in SZ and CTL cells. Nevertheless, these findings further indicate that NDUFV2P1 acts as a trans-acting factor on RBPs which bind NDUFV2, specifically on those involved in spliceosome, RNA transport, and ribosome pathways, as demonstrated by pathway enrichment analysis of the significantly different RBPs bound to both mRNAs. These pathways align with our findings in SZ cells of reduced binding of NDUFV2 mRNA to NXF1, its reduced levels in the cytosol and in mitochondrial-bound ribosomal fraction, the main site of NDUFV2 translation site40. The constellation of our findings implies that in SZ cells, NDUFV2P1 interference with NDUFV2 mRNA probably leads to reduced levels of NDUFV2 protein in the cytosol and the mitochondria, and its impaired folding and decreased uptake rate into the mitochondria19. Unexpectedly, in the mitochondria-bound ribosomal fraction, NDUFV2P1 mRNA levels were significantly lower in SZ than in the CTL fraction. A tempting explanation is that NDUFV2P1 interferes with NDUFV2 binding to RBPs that inhibit translation, thereby increasing the availability of NDUFV2 to the translation process in CTL-LCLs. One possible candidate is the Fragile X Mental Retardation Protein (FMR1), an RBP involved in RNA transport and localization. In addition, FMR1 regulates translation by blocking essential components of the translational machinery, such as the 5S-RNA-L5 in the 80S initiation complex, hindering the binding of its bound mRNA to the ribosome54. Indeed, FMRP1 binding to SZ-NDUFV2P1 mRNA was significantly enhanced (Supplementary Table 3), as compared to its CTL mRNA.

Given that schizophrenia is a highly heterogeneous disorder with multiple genetic subtypes and the relatively modest sample size used in this study, it is possible that distinct biological mechanisms operate across different patient groups. As a result, the molecular interactions identified here may not be generalized to all individuals with schizophrenia. In addition, individuals’ genetic data for each sample were not available, which limited our ability to assess the contribution of specific genetic variants to the observed molecular signatures. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts with available genomic information will be essential for validating these findings and determining how genetic background influences the regulatory mechanism described in this study.

Collectively, as mentioned above, our data indicate that NDUFV2P1 is a trans-acting factor that negatively regulates NDUFV2 mRNA by interacting with RBPs. Our data do not rule out the possibility that NDUFV2P1 affects or is affected by additional molecular pathways in SZ. Further studies are required to comprehensively investigate alternative interaction sites between both mRNAs and to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the increased expression of NDUFV2P1 in SZ. Regardless, this study proposes a bottom-up mechanism by which NDUFV2P1 modulates NDUFV2, an essential subunit for complex I activity and consequently for the maintenance of intact mitochondrial function, neurons' optimal functioning, and ultimately behavior12,13,19,55. Modulation of NDUFV2P1 expression, its targets, and/or effectors may turn into a therapeutic approach in SZ, particularly for a subset of patients exhibiting elevated levels of the pseudogene.
