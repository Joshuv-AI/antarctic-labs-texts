To Study the Effect of Nilotinib, a Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor, on Learning and Memory and Its Comparison with Nitric Oxide Synthase Inhibitor in an Animal Model of Alzheimer’s Disease
Authors: Anurag Kumar, Nasreen Fatima, Pramod Kumar, Rajendra Nath, Amod Kumar Sachan, Rakesh Kumar Dixit, Rishi Pal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a gradual neurodegenerative disorder presenting with cognitive and non-cognitive impairment. Currently approved therapies alleviate symptoms but do not modify disease progression and may be associated with adverse effects.ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare the effect of nilotinib (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor) and Nω-nitro-l-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME, a nitric oxide synthase inhibitor) as agents that improve learning and memory processes in a scopolamine-induced rat model of AD with memantine.MethodsThirty adult male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 6 in each group). Scopolamine hydrobromide 3 mg/kg i.p. once daily for 7 days was used to induce AD-like cognitive deficits. Behavioural testing (elevated plus maze [EPM] and Morris water maze [MWM]) was performed on days 8 and 9. From day 10, rats were treated with nilotinib (10 mg/kg p.o.), L-NAME (10 mg/kg i.p.), memantine (10 mg/kg p.o.) or saline for 21 days. Behavioural testing was repeated on days 29 and 30. Then, animals were sacrificed, and brain homogenates were prepared for estimation of acetylcholinesterase (AChE), amyloid-beta 1-42 (Aβ1-42) and PARK-7 levels.ResultsScopolamine increased transfer latency in the EPM and escape latency in the MWM and increased AChE and Aβ1-42 levels, but it decreased PARK-7. Both nilotinib and L-NAME significantly reduced transfer latency and escape latency compared to scopolamine (all p < .001). Both treatments reduced AChE and Aβ1-42 levels and increased PARK-7. Nilotinib produced significantly greater improvements than L-NAME (p < .01) with effects comparable to memantine.ConclusionNilotinib and L-NAME improved cognitive function and modulated cholinergic, amyloid and autophagy-related markers in a rat model of AD. Nilotinib demonstrated superior efficacy to L-NAME and may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for AD. Further studies are warranted to evaluate long-term effects and clinical applicability.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative cause of dementia. Extracellular amyloid-beta plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles consisting of hyperphosphorylated tau, region-specific cerebral hypometabolism, synaptic dysfunction and mitochondrial impairment are the defining pathologies of AD.1–4 Amyloid-beta peptides, particularly amyloid-beta 1-42 (Aβ1-42), are generated through sequential cleavage of amyloid precursor protein by β- and γ-secretases.1–4

The non-receptor tyrosine kinase Abelson (Abl) is expressed in the cytoplasm and nucleus that regulates apoptosis, among other cellular processes. Increased amounts of phosphorylated Abl at tyrosine 412 (T412) are present in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus of AD brains, and Abl phosphorylates tau at multiple tyrosine residues. Inhibition of Abl has been shown to activate parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase critical for proteasomal and autophagic protein clearance, and to promote neuronal survival. In models expressing amyloid, parkin solubility and autophagy are reduced, indicating that amyloid stress impairs parkin stability and lysosomal clearance. Decreased solubility of parkin and its decreased interaction with beclin-1, a key autophagic protein, are associated with impaired clearance of β-amyloid and phosphorylated tau in AD.5–9

Nilotinib, which acts as an Abl inhibitor, facilitates the interaction between parkin and beclin-1, boosts the levels of endogenous parkin and its ubiquitination, and improves the proteasomal recycling of parkin. This could lead to the restoration of the autophagic clearance of amyloid and tau.

The brain accounts for ∼2% of body mass but consumes ∼20% of total oxygen. Approximately 15% of cerebral oxygen is reduced to superoxide (O2⁻), a primary source of oxidative stress. The brain also generates high concentrations of nitric oxide (NO).10–12 NO reacts rapidly with superoxide to form peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), a potent oxidant and cytotoxic agent that contributes to nitroxidative stress. Chronic neuroinflammation, driven by amyloid plaques and tau, upregulates inducible NO synthase (iNOS), leading to excess NO and peroxynitrite formation. Peroxynitrite damages neuronal membranes and promotes neurodegeneration. Therefore, Nω-nitro-l-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME), an iNOS inhibitor, may attenuate neurodegeneration by reducing iNOS activity in AD.

The N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor antagonist memantine and cholinesterase inhibitors like donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine are currently the first-line medications for AD.

The present study evaluated the effects of nilotinib and L-NAME on behavioural and biochemical parameters in a scopolamine-induced rat model of AD, and compared their efficacy with memantine.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Ethics

The Indian Institute of Toxicology Research in Lucknow, India, provided 30 adult male Wistar rats weighing between 160 and 200 g. In the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA)-approved animal facility at King George’s Medical University in Lucknow, animals were kept in cages of six, with a controlled temperature of 25 °C ± 2 °C, a 12-h light/dark cycle and unlimited access to water and a standard pellet diet. Before the experiment, the rats were acclimated for 7 days. A veterinarian checked the animals’ health every day.

The Institutional Animal Ethics Committee at King George’s Medical University in Lucknow approved each procedure (Ref. No. 175/IAEC/2021) and the procedures were carried out in compliance with the directives of the CCSEA.

Drugs and Chemicals

Scopolamine hydrobromide, nilotinib, Nω-nitro-l-arginine methyl ester hydrochloride (L-NAME) and memantine hydrochloride were procured from Sigma–Aldrich, USA. All other reagents were of analytical grade. Drugs were freshly dissolved in 0.9% normal saline before use.

Drug Administration

Doses were calculated based on body weight. For oral administration, a 16 G ball-tipped gavage cannula was used. Rats were restrained manually, and the cannula was inserted via the oral cavity into the oesophagus. Intraperitoneal injections were administered in the right lower abdominal quadrant using a 26-G needle.

The EPM transfer latency task is a standard test of learning and memory in rodents based on avoidance learning:

Day 8 (acquisition trial): First exposure. Rodents naturally avoid open arms and prefer closed arms. The time taken to move from the open to the closed arm is recorded as initial transfer latency. This reflects learning of the safe zone.

Day 9 (retention trial): Second exposure 24 h later. A significant decrease in transfer latency in normal animals indicates memory retention of the learned task. Failure to reduce latency reflects impaired memory consolidation/retrieval. 3. To differentiate drug effect from the model induction effect

Behavioural Assessment

On days 8, 9, 29 and 30, behavioural tests were conducted. To avoid stress to the animals, they were maintained in a quiet room for 1 h before the tests.

Elevated Plus Maze Test 21

The rats were positioned apart from the platform at the end of an open arm, and the duration of time it took for them to enter the enclosed arm was recorded (transfer latency, or TL). The length of time the animal was kept in the open arm until each leg crossed the thin white line that ran through the centre of the enclosed arm was known as TL.

Morris Water Maze 22

The day after the group as a whole completed the Y-maze test, experiments began. After an hour of tranquil confinement, the animals underwent four trials a day for 4 days, with the platform immersed in quadrant two. There was a 20-min intertrial period and a 60-s time restriction in each trial. If the animal was unable to locate the platform, it was left on the platform for 10 s. One trial without the platform made up the retention phase, which began the day after the acquisition was completed. Software was used to analyse swim speed, the amount of time spent in quadrant two during the retention phase (24 h after the acquisition phase ends), and the time it took to find the platform during the acquisition phase.

Biochemical Analysis

On day 30, after the behavioural test, rats from Groups 1 to 5 were euthanised with a high dose of pentobarbitone (100 mg/kg i.p.) according to CCSEA guidelines. 23

Brain Homogenate Preparation

After being cleaned with ice-cold saline (0.9% NaCl), whole brain samples were homogenised in chilled tissue lysis buffer (RIPA + PI cocktail). The homogenates were centrifuged at 800g for 5 min at 4 °C to extract the radioactive waste. The lysate was then centrifuged for 30 min at 4 °C at 10,000g to obtain the supernatant. The biochemical and molecular parameters were estimated in this way.

Colourimetric Determination of Acetylcholinesterase Levels in Brain Homogenate: Ellman’s Assay 24

The acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity of brain homogenate can be measured photometrically. The enzyme activity is measured by following the increase in yellow colour produced from thiocholine when it reacts with the dithiobisnitrobenzoate ion.

Determination of Amyloid-beta 1-42 Level in Brain Homogenate by Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay Kit 25

A colourimetric enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Sigma) was used to determine the quantities of soluble and insoluble Aβ42 in cortical and hippocampal homogenates. At 450 nm, the assay was read in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The unit of data is in nanograms per milligram of tissue.

Determination of PARK-7 Level in Brain Homogenate by Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay Kit 26

Using a colourimetric ELISA kit, the concentration of soluble and insoluble parkin was assessed in cortical and hippocampal homogenates. The test was conducted as directed by the manufacturer and read at 450 nm. The data have been presented as one nanogram of tissue per milligram.

Procedure of Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay Test

Seven standard wells and one blank well were prepared. Each of the standard, blank and sample dilutions was applied to the corresponding well in a quantity of 100 µL. At 37 °C, the incubation process lasted for 2 h. After adding 100 µL of biotinylated antibody, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After another washing, 100 µL of streptavidin-HRP was added, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. 90 µL of TMB substrate was added after another round of washing. After that, the solution became blue, and it was incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. The solution turned yellow when 50 µL of stop reagent was finally added. An optical density measurement was then obtained using a microplate reader that was calibrated to 450 nm.

Analysis of Statistics

One-way analysis of variance was used to analyse the data, and the Newman–Keuls post hoc test was used for statistical analysis within the group. A ‘p’ value of less than .05 was deemed statistically significant across all parameters, and the data were displayed as mean ± standard error.

Results

Behavioural Parameter Assessment

Elevated Plus Maze: Transfer Latency on Days 8 and 9

Behavioural Assessment: Transfer latency was measured using the EPM on days 8 and 9 to confirm scopolamine-induced impairment of learning and memory before test drug administration. Day 8 served as the acquisition trial and day 9 as the retention trial 24 h later. A significant reduction in transfer latency on day 9 in control animals indicates intact memory, while failure to reduce latency in scopolamine-treated groups confirms amnesia. Test drugs were administered from day 10 onwards, and behavioural testing was repeated on days 29 and 30 to evaluate treatment effects.

On day 8, transfer latency did not differ significantly among groups (F(4,25) = 0.67, p = .617).

On day 9, transfer latency differed significantly across groups (F(4,25) = 43.14, p < .001). Control animals exhibited a significant reduction in latency from day 8 to day 9 (16.33 ± 2.50 vs 32.17 ± 3.49 s, t = 8.91, p < .001). Scopolamine-treated animals showed no significant reduction (28.17 ± 2.48 vs 29.83 ± 2.93 s, t = 5.00, p = .004).

Tukey’s post hoc analysis on day 9 revealed that transfer latency in the control group was significantly lower than in all other groups (all p < .001). No significant differences were observed among the scopolamine, nilotinib, L-NAME and memantine groups (all p > .05) (Figure 2).

On day 29, transfer latency did not differ significantly among groups (F(4,25) = 0.92, p = .466).

On day 30, transfer latency differed significantly across groups (F(4,25) = 59.24, p < .001). All groups showed significant reductions from day 29 to day 30 (all p ≤ .009). Control decreased to 12.17 ± 2.32 s, scopolamine to 28.67 ± 1.37 s, nilotinib to 17.67 ± 2.16 s, L-NAME to 22.33 ± 2.34 s and memantine to 14.17 ± 2.23 s.

Memantine produced the greatest reduction, not significantly different from control (p = .488) or nilotinib (p = .058) (Figure 3).

Effect of Nilotinib and Nω-nitro-l-arginine Methyl Ester on Acetylcholinesterase Activity

AChE activity in brain homogenates differed significantly across groups (F(4,25) = 323.1, p < .001). When compared to the control, scopolamine 3 mg/kg significantly increased AChE activity (790.48 ± 21.75 vs 432.26 ± 8.97 mU/g tissue, p < .001). When compared to scopolamine alone, treatment with nilotinib 10 mg/kg significantly decreased AChE activity (482.97 ± 23.88 mU/g tissue, p < .001). Although not as much as nilotinib, L-NAME 10 mg/kg also reduced AChE activity (598.09 ± 14.49 mU/g tissue, p < .001). Memantine 10 mg/kg produced a reduction comparable to nilotinib (470.58 ± 15.62 mU/g tissue, p < .001 vs scopolamine). All groups showed significant differences according to post hoc comparisons. Nilotinib-treated animals showed significantly lower AChE activity than L-NAME (mean difference = 115.12, p < .001) and did not differ significantly from memantine (mean difference = 12.39, p < .001), though the latter comparison was statistically significant due to small variance. These results indicate that nilotinib was more effective than L-NAME in restoring cholinergic activity and comparable to standard therapy (Figure 5).

Effect of Nilotinib and Nω-nitro-l-arginine Methyl Ester on PARK-7 Levels

PARK-7 concentrations in brain homogenates varied significantly among groups (F(4,25) = 26.42, p < .001). Scopolamine significantly reduced PARK-7 levels compared to control (2.70 ± 1.75 vs 9.20 ± 0.58 ng/mL, p < .001). Nilotinib treatment restored PARK-7 levels (8.27 ± 0.39 ng/mL), which were not significantly different from control (p = .824) and were significantly higher than scopolamine (p < .001). Although the effect was not as strong as that of nilotinib, L-NAME also raised PARK-7 levels (5.36 ± 2.43 ng/mL, p = .002 vs control; p = .04 vs scopolamine). Rats given memantine had PARK-7 levels that were comparable to the control group (8.65 ± 0.79 ng/mL, p = .969). Nilotinib significantly outperformed L-NAME in raising PARK-7, according to pairwise comparisons (mean difference = 2.91, p = .022). No significant difference was observed between nilotinib and memantine (mean difference = −0.38, p = .992). According to these results, nilotinib significantly increased PARK-7 expression, suggesting enhanced autophagic/ubiquitination capacity (Figure 6).

Effect of Nilotinib and Nω-nitro-l-arginine Methyl Ester on Amyloid-beta 1-42 Levels

Brain Aβ1-42 levels differed significantly across groups (F(4,25) = 32.7, p < .001). Scopolamine administration markedly increased Aβ1-42 compared to control (785.46 ± 67.88 vs 127.77 ± 20.59 pg/mL, p < .001). Nilotinib significantly reduced Aβ1-42 levels relative to scopolamine (352.22 ± 199.57 pg/mL, p < .001). L-NAME also decreased Aβ1-42 (400.58 ± 98.90 pg/mL, p < .001 vs scopolamine), though levels remained higher than with nilotinib. Memantine produced the greatest reduction among treatments (207.92 ± 46.10 pg/mL, p < .001 vs scopolamine). Nilotinib and L-NAME (mean difference = −48.36, p = .889) and nilotinib and memantine (mean difference = 144.30, p = .206) did not differ significantly, according to the post hoc analysis. In contrast to L-NAME, memantine dramatically decreased Aβ1-42 (mean difference = 192.66, p = .032). According to these findings, memantine and nilotinib both successfully reduce the amyloid burden brought on by scopolamine, with nilotinib showing an effect comparable to that of conventional treatment (Figure 7).

Discussion

The current research work assessed the cognitive impacts of nilotinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, alongside L-NAME, a non-selective NO synthase inhibitor, using a rat model of AD induced by scopolamine, and also compared their effectiveness to that of memantine.

A common tool for simulating amnesia in rodents is scopolamine, a centrally acting muscarinic cholinergic antagonist that reduces cholinergic neurotransmission.18, 19, 27 In line with earlier findings, scopolamine 3 mg/kg i.p. considerably reduced memory and learning, as shown by longer escape latency in the Morris water maze (MWM) and increased transfer latency in the EPM.

Nilotinib 10 mg/kg treatment considerably enhanced behavioural performance in both MWM and EPM. These effects were comparable to memantine 10 mg/kg and better than L-NAME 10 mg/kg. Nilotinib’s benefits for cognition are probably mediated by c-Abl inhibition. Amyloid-beta deposition in AD triggers c-Abl, which phosphorylates tau and lowers parkin levels. Amyloid-beta and neurofibrillary tangle ubiquitination and autophagic clearance are hampered by decreased parkin. Nilotinib enhances autophagy and amyloid clearance by inhibiting c-Abl, increasing soluble parkin and promoting parkin–beclin-1 interaction.28–31 In the present study, nilotinib increased PARK-7 levels, supporting activation of this pathway.32–34

L-NAME also improved cognition, though to a lesser extent than nilotinib. Amyloid-beta induces iNOS expression and NO production in the hippocampus. Excess NO reacts with superoxide to form peroxynitrite, a potent oxidant that contributes to neuronal damage. By inhibiting NOS, L-NAME reduces NO and peroxynitrite formation, attenuating oxidative-nitrosative stress.35–38

Biochemically, scopolamine increased AChE activity and Aβ1-42 levels, indicating cholinergic hypofunction and amyloid accumulation. Nilotinib and L-NAME both reduced AChE activity and Aβ1-42, with nilotinib producing greater effects. 39

Our findings are consistent with prior studies. Curcumin reduced transfer latency in scopolamine-treated rats, and L-NAME improved cognition in streptozotocin-induced models. Nilotinib has been shown to promote amyloid clearance via parkin-dependent autophagy.20, 28, 37

Limitations include the use of a pharmacological rather than transgenic AD model and the absence of histopathological analysis of amyloid and tau deposition.

Conclusion

In the current study, learning and memory were enhanced in a rat model of AD induced by scopolamine by nilotinib (10 mg/kg, p.o.) and L-NAME (10 mg/kg, i.p.). Compared to L-NAME, nilotinib significantly improved cognition, with effects similar to those of memantine (10 mg/kg, p.o.).

Both nilotinib and L-NAME increased PARK-7 and decreased AChE activity and Aβ1-42 levels, suggesting that cholinergic, amyloid and autophagy-related pathways are modulated. Nilotinib decreased central amyloid burden and improved spatial recognition memory, indicating therapeutic potential against important pathological features of AD.

These results lend credence to the potential of NO synthase inhibition and c-Abl inhibition as treatment approaches for cognitive impairment. To clarify molecular mechanisms and assess long-term efficacy and safety, more animal research and clinical trials are needed.
