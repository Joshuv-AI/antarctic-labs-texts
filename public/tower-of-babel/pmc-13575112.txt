Value of preoperative peripheral blood inflammation markers in predicting prognosis after radical gastric cancer surgery
Authors: Xiang Li, Wei Dai, Feng Wen, Jinming Xu, Ke Li, LianWei Wang, Yajun Chen, Zhongxue Fu, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to systematically investigate the association between ten preoperative peripheral blood inflammatory markers and the clinicopathologic characteristics and outcomes of gastric cancer (GC) and develop a prediction model for overall survival (OS) based on these markers. We conducted retrospective analyses of the clinicopathological data of 921 patients with gastric cancer who underwent radical gastrectomy, adjusting baseline characteristics using propensity score matching (PSM). Patients were divided into high and low inflammation categories using optimal cutoff values to examine variations in clinicopathological characteristics and OS between the groups. Subsequent regression analyses were performed to determine the independent prognostic factors of OS, and a nomogram for predicting OS was developed and validated. After balancing the baseline characteristics of deceased and surviving patients by PSM, we identified significant differences in the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, systemic immune inflammation index, inflammation-combined prognostic index, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio-CA 19 − 9 score (NCS), and hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocytes, and platelet scores between the two groups. Furthermore, the high inflammation group had poorer clinicopathologic features and prognosis than the low inflammation group. Multivariate regression analysis revealed that age, body mass index, American Joint Committee on Cancer T-stage, lymph node ratio, and NCS were significant prognostic factors influencing OS. Ultimately, we created a nomogram using these factors and a web calculator for estimating OS. Patients with higher inflammation scores often exhibit unfavorable clinicopathological features and have a poorer prognosis. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), a highly prevalent malignant tumor worldwide, has consistently been one of the primary contributors to cancer-related deaths. According to the World Health Organization, in 2022, nearly one million new cases of GC were reported globally, accounting for approximately 4.9% of all cancer cases, while deaths from the disease reached approximately 660,000, making up 6.8% of all cancer-related deaths and positioning GC as the fifth most common cause of cancer mortality1. Especially in East Asia, the incidence and mortality rates of GC are notably elevated compared to other regions, posing a major threat to public health and affecting life expectancy2. Although medical advances in early diagnosis, radical surgery, and (neo) adjuvant therapy for GC have occurred in recent years and there has been a decline in both its incidence and mortality rates, the long-term survival rate remains relatively low3,4. Therefore, effective predictive tools and biomarkers are urgently needed to guide postoperative management and the development of individualized treatment strategies1,5.

Inflammation significantly contributes to the advancement and progression of malignant tumors by modifying the tumor microenvironment (TME)6. Chronic inflammation is strongly associated with GC and may significantly influence tumor invasion and metastasis, which in turn affects the overall prognosis of patients7. Recent research indicates that peripheral blood inflammatory markers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR)8, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR)9, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR)10, and systemic immune inflammation index (SII)11, reflect systemic inflammatory status and are strongly correlated with the prognosis of different malignant tumors. These markers have been extensively studied and have been confirmed to affect the prognosis of various cancers, including gastric, colorectal, thyroid, lung, and breast cancers12,13. Specifically, inflammatory markers like elevated NLR, PLR, and SII are frequently linked to worse outcomes, likely because inflammation releases biologically active molecules into the TME14, fostering tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis15. Given the ease of use, affordability, and reproducibility of these inflammatory markers, an increasing number of clinicians are using them as indirect markers of systemic inflammatory response in the TME16.

Although studies have explored how inflammatory markers affect the prognosis of GC, most studies have not thoroughly considered the interaction between inflammatory markers or their independent effects using multivariate analyses. Moreover, while many types of inflammatory markers exist in peripheral blood, no study has systematically evaluated the correlation between these markers and patients’ clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis. Additionally, the optimal cutoff values for inflammatory markers are not standardized, partly due to imbalances in patient baseline characteristics17. Existing studies seldom use methods like propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline characteristics, potentially introducing bias and impacting result reliability18, leading to significant variability between studies, which hinders the broader applicability of the findings in clinical settings.

To address these challenges, we performed a retrospective analysis of the clinicopathological data of patients with GC while balancing baseline characteristics through the PSM method, ensuring that the optimal cutoff values obtained were stable and reliable. We systematically analyzed the relationship between peripheral blood inflammatory parameters and the clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis of patients with GC. We then identified key risk factors among these markers that independently affected overall survival (OS) using a regression analysis. Finally, we created and validated a prediction model for OS using these markers and designed a corresponding web-based calculator. Our goal is to offer a scientific basis for personalized treatment decisions in GC, optimizing postoperative management, and improving long-term survival. This also promotes a new understanding of the biology of GC and provides direction for future research.

Patients and methods

Study population and inclusion criteria

A retrospective study was conducted on patients hospitalized at the gastrointestinal surgery departments of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University and Chongqing University Fuling Hospital between July 1, 2016, and April 30, 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) postoperative pathological diagnosis of GC; (2) radical gastric surgery; and (3) age ≥ 18 years.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were: (1) individuals with prior or simultaneous tumors at multiple sites; (2) individuals who received preoperative neoadjuvant therapy; (3) individuals with pre-existing conditions, including systemic immune disorders or blood disorders; (4) individuals experiencing acute infections, those with a recent blood transfusion history, or those suffering from gastrointestinal bleeding; (5) individuals who have recently used hormonal treatments, anti-inflammatory drugs, or antiplatelet agents; and (6) individuals with insufficient clinicopathological data.

Clinicopathological characteristics

The clinicopathological characteristics assessed in this study included patients’ gender, age, comorbidities, smoking status, alcohol intake, body mass index (BMI), tumor size, histological differentiation, pathological classification, the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) T-stage, AJCC N-stage, TNM stage, lymph node ratio (LNR), number of positive lymph nodes, total number of lymph nodes, vascular invasion, neurological invasion, HER2 status, primary site, postoperative chemotherapy, complications, and OS. BMI was calculated as the weight (kg) divided by the height squared (m²). We did not exclude patients with chronic conditions that were well-managed and stable because there is no consistent conclusion on the effect of chronic diseases on peripheral blood markers19. The comorbidity score was defined based on the presence of five common chronic diseases: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, non-acute phase), and a history of cerebral infarction. The score was 0 if none of these conditions were present, 1 for the presence of one condition, 2 for two conditions, and so on. AJCC TNM Staging: the AJCC/Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging system (8th edition)20 was used to determine the TNM stage of patients with GC. The LNR is calculated as the number of positive lymph nodes divided by the total number of lymph nodes assessed. Postoperative adjuvant therapy was administered according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines21, with specific treatment plans based on the patient’s TNM stage, pathological findings, and overall health condition. The key postoperative complications monitored included fistulas (such as anastomotic leaks), infections (including pulmonary, incision, and abdominal infections), and other complications (such as postoperative bleeding and bowel obstruction). These complications significantly impact patient recovery and prognosis, making close observation and documentation essential. OS was defined as the duration from the surgery date to the date of death from any cause, the last follow-up for patients lost to follow-up, or the conclusion of the study’s follow-up period.

The Glasgow Prognostic Score (GPS)26 is scored as follows: elevated CRP (> 10 mg/L) and hypoalbuminemia (< 35 g/L) are assigned a score of 2, elevated CRP (> 10 mg/L) or hypoalbuminemia (< 35 g/L) a score of 1, and the absence of both is scored as 0. The upper reference value for CA 19 − 9 in our laboratory was set at 27 U/ml.

Follow-up

Follow-up appointments were planned every three months during the first two years after surgery, followed by semiannual visits thereafter. Information, including the dates of missed appointments, the last confirmed survival status, or the date of death, was collected from hospital records or through direct contact with the patient or their family. Overall mortality was defined as any death occurring after the surgical procedure, regardless of the cause.

Determination of optimal cutoff values and survival analysis in inflammatory parameter evaluation

To establish reliable optimal thresholds for the inflammatory markers, we first performed PSM with nearest-neighbor matching28 using the MatchIt package (Version 4.5.5), adjusting the caliper to 0.38, and achieving a 1:1 matching between deceased and surviving patient groups. We utilized the cobalt package (Version 4.5.3) to calculate the standardized mean difference (SMD) and evaluate whether the initial characteristics of the deceased and surviving patient groups were equally distributed before and after PSM, considering the baseline balanced when the SMD was < 0.129. ROC curves were plotted for each inflammatory parameter, and the optimal cutoff values were established by identifying the peak of the Youden index30. Each parameter was further analyzed, with its area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and other statistical metrics calculated. The inflammatory parameters were categorized into groups of high (> cutoff) and low (≤ cutoff) inflammation, and the clinicopathological differences between these groups were compared. The rms package (Version 4.3.2) was used to generate restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves, allowing for the evaluation of the dose-response relationship between the inflammatory parameters and OS. Simultaneously, we performed Kaplan–Meier curve analyses and log-rank tests using the survival (version 3.5-5) and survminer (version 0.4.9) packages to evaluate and compare the OS between the two patient groups.

Cox and LASSO regression analysis for independent prognostic factors

To assess whether any of the inflammatory parameters independently influenced the outcomes of GC, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses utilizing the survival package to examine the relevant factors. In addition, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was conducted using the glmnet package (version 4.1-8) to enhance variable selection and model performance.

Initially, univariate Cox regression analysis was used to examine how inflammatory parameters correlate with OS in patients with GC. Given the potential correlations among the included variables, we then performed LASSO regression analysis to refine the selection of prognostic parameters. The minimum λ value was used as the basis for determining the ideal number of variables to incorporate into the model. Finally, multivariate Cox regression analysis was employed to derive hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the selected parameters, assessing if these variables were independent prognostic factors for OS in patients with GC.

Establishment and validation of a predictive model

Initially, we randomly divided the patient data into a training group and a validation group in a ratio of 8:2. We examined the variations between the two groups for statistical significance, and if notable differences emerged (p < 0.05), we regrouped the data until no significant differences existed.

Next, using the survival and regplot (version 1.1) packages, we developed a nomogram to estimate the OS at 1, 3, and 5 years using the independent prognostic factors identified in our earlier analysis.

To assess the effectiveness of the model, we initially evaluated its discriminative power against the conventional TNM staging using the concordance index (C-index). Additionally, the timeROC (version 0.4) package was used to compute time-dependent AUC values for the training and validation groups at the 1-, 3-, and 5-year marks, thereby assessing the predictive power.

Calibration curves were created to assess how well the predicted survival aligns with the actual outcomes observed. Furthermore, we conducted decision curve analysis (DCA) with the ggDCA (version 1.2) package to evaluate the model’s predictive capability against the traditional TNM staging system regarding OS at 1, 3, and 5 years within both the training and validation groups.

Lastly, each patient’s total score was computed, and X-tile software31 (https://medicine.yale.edu/lab/rimm/research/software/) was utilized to categorize patients into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups. The survival differences among these three groups were then compared to further validate the model’s prognostic utility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.3.1, https://www.r-project.org/). Categorical variables were represented by frequencies and percentages, while differences were evaluated using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables are expressed as means with standard deviations. To assess differences between the groups for continuous variables that adhere to a normal distribution, we utilized the independent samples t-test (student’s t-test) for analysis. To compare groups of continuous variables that exhibited a non-normal distribution, we utilized the Mann–Whitney rank sum test, also known as the Mann–Whitney U test, for our analysis. The results of the Cox regression analyses were expressed as the HRs with their corresponding 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined by a p-value of less than 0.05 in a two-tailed test.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

We conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinicopathological information of patients who underwent radical gastric surgery and were pathologically diagnosed with GC from July 1, 2016, to April 30, 2023, in the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University and Chongqing University Fuling Hospital. Based on our inclusion and exclusion criteria, we ultimately obtained information on 921 patients (Supplementary Fig. 1), of whom 227 had died and 694 were still alive. We used the SMD to assess whether the baseline characteristics of the deceased and surviving groups were balanced. We identified an imbalance (SMD > 0.1) in patient age, BMI, tumor size, AJCC T-stage, AJCC N-stage, LNR, number of positive lymph nodes, vascular invasion, and postoperative chemotherapy (Supplementary Table 1). Therefore, we performed PSM to balance the covariates between the two groups (Supplementary Table 1), resulting in the inclusion of information on 201 deceased and 201 surviving patients. After balancing the two groups at baseline, our analysis revealed that the PLR, SII, and ICPI levels were significantly elevated in the cohort of deceased patients compared to those in those who survived. Furthermore, the percentage of patients exhibiting elevated NCS scores was notably greater in deceased patients than in the group of surviving patients. Furthermore, the HALP levels were markedly reduced in deceased patients compared to those who survived, with these differences reaching statistical significance. However, the remaining five inflammatory markers were not significantly different between the two groups (Fig. 1).

Obtaining optimal cutoff values for inflammatory parameters

To assess the role of inflammatory indicators in the prognosis of patients with GC and the prediction of GC outcome, we first plotted the ROC curves of NLR, PLR, and MLR to obtain the optimal cutoff values using the data of the 402 PSM-matched patients. Subsequently, we calculated the ICPI and NCS using the specified formulas. Next, we plotted ROC curves for each inflammatory parameter (Supplementary Fig. 2). Our analysis revealed that ICPI had the highest AUC value, followed by NCS and SII with values of 0.6, 0.59, and 0.57, respectively. However, HALP, CALLY, and PNI had the lowest AUC values, which were less than 0.5; therefore, we determined that these three parameters lacked predictive capability for patient prognosis, and they were therefore excluded from further analysis (Table 1). Relationship between inflammatory parameters and patients’ clinicopathologic characteristics

We categorized the patient data into two groups based on the optimal cutoff value of the inflammatory parameter: high inflammation (> cutoff) and verified low (≤ cutoff). The clinicopathological characteristics of the two groups were then analyzed to determine whether there were significant differences. We found that NLR was associated with patient prognosis, age, comorbidities, smoking status, tumor size, T stage, TNM stage, LNR, and the number of positive lymph nodes. PLR was associated with age, comorbidities, tumor size, and HER2 status. MLR was associated with prognosis, gender, age, comorbidities, smoking status, tumor size, T stage, TNM stage, and the total number of lymph nodes. SII was correlated with gender, T stage, and TNM stage. ICPI was correlated with patient prognosis, age, comorbidities, BMI, tumor size, T stage, N stage, TNM stage, LNR, the number of positive lymph nodes, and vascular invasion. GPS was correlated with patient prognosis, gender, age, smoking status, BMI, T stage, N stage, and TNM stage. NCS was correlated with patient prognosis, gender, age, comorbidities, smoking status, tumor size, T stage, N stage, TNM stage, LNR, the number of positive lymph nodes, and vascular invasion (Supplementary Table 2).

Survival analysis of inflammatory parameters

We performed a survival analysis using the previously collected data to evaluate survival outcomes between the groups categorized by high inflammation (> cutoff) and low inflammation (≤ cutoff) inflammation. Our findings indicated that, with NLR > cutoff value, the likelihood of mortality among patients with GC significantly increased with an increase in NLR (Fig. 2a). Survival analysis indicated that individuals in the high NLR group had a significantly poorer prognosis than those in the low NLR group (Fig. 2b). Similarly, an increase in PLR was correlated with a higher risk of death (Fig. 2c), although the prognosis was not significantly different between the high and low PLR groups (Fig. 2d). The risk of death also increased with an increase in MLR (Fig. 2e), and survival analysis indicated that patients in the high MLR group had a poorer prognosis than those in the low MLR group (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, as SII increased beyond the cutoff value, the risk of death also rose (Fig. 2g), with the low SII group demonstrating notably superior outcomes to those in the high SII group, albeit without statistical significance (Fig. 2h). When the ICPI exceeded the cutoff value, the risk of death escalated significantly, and patients with elevated ICPI levels had worse prognoses than those with lower ICPI levels (Fig. 2i and j). Finally, patients with GPS and NCS scores of 0 had significantly better prognoses than those with scores of 1 or 2 (Figs. 2k-l).

Determination of independent risk factors for OS in GC

To determine the independent prognostic factors in GC, we performed univariate Cox regression, LASSO, and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Initially, we performed a univariate Cox regression analysis, which revealed that age, comorbidities, BMI, tumor size, histological differentiation, AJCC T stage, AJCC N stage, LNR, the number of positive lymph nodes, vascular invasion, neurological invasion, HER2 status, primary site, postoperative chemotherapy, and inflammatory parameters such as NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, ICPI, GPS, and NCS—totaling 21 parameters—were correlated with the outcomes of patients with GC. To reduce the risk of overfitting in the multivariate model, we first applied LASSO regression to these 21 parameters before advancing to the multivariate Cox analysis. Using the minimum λ value, we filtered out 10 parameters for the subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 3). Ultimately, we identified age, BMI, AJCC T stage, LNR, and NCS as the independent risk factors for OS in GC (Table 2).

Building prognostic models

We first divided the patient data into a training group and a validation group in a ratio of 8:2. The training group was used for model construction, while the validation group was used to test the model’s robustness. After separating the data, we determined if any significant disparities existed between the training and validation groups; if any differences were detected, regrouping was necessary (Supplementary Table 3).

Subsequently, we developed a nomogram to estimate the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS for patients with GC using a regression analysis of the six identified independent prognostic factors (age, BMI, AJCC T stage, LNR, and NCS) (Fig. 4a). For example, a patient with GC with an NCS of 0, LNR of 0.067, AJCC T stage of T4, BMI of 21.63, and age of 74 had a total score of 200, corresponding to overall mortality rates from all causes of 0.056, 0.216, and 0.304 for 1-, 3-, and 5 years, respectively. The corresponding survival rates were 0.944, 0.784, and 0.696 (Fig. 4a).

Validation of the model

First, we employed the C-index as a means to assess the accuracy of the prognostic model. In the training group, the model achieved a C-index of 0.768 (95% CI: 0.74–0.81) in contrast to the traditional TNM model, which had a C-index of 0.692 (95% CI: 0.66–0.72). In the validation group, the predictive model’s C-index was 0.761 (0.72–0.85), compared to 0.693 (0.62–0.76) for the traditional TNM model.

To evaluate the predictive performance of the nomogram, we analyzed time-dependent ROC curves for 1, 3, and 5 years in both the training and validation groups, calculating the corresponding AUC values to quantify the model’s discriminatory ability. The AUC values were 0.76 (95% CI: 0.7–0.82), 0.81 (0.77–0.85), and 0.82 (0.78–0.87) for 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively, in the training group (Figs. 4b-c). In the validation group, the AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were 0.73 (0.56–0.89), 0.72 (0.63–0.82), and 0.77 (0.67–0.88), respectively (Figs. 4d-e), demonstrating the model’s robustness and generalizability across different patient populations.

To determine the reliability and precision of the nomogram model, we utilized calibration curves. The estimated survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years for GC closely matched the actual observed rates in both the training and validation groups. These findings demonstrate that the prediction model maintains a high level of accuracy and reliability across all time points, suggesting its potential for clinical application (Figs. 4f-k).

To assess the model’s clinical utility, we plotted decision curves for 1, 3, and 5 years in both groups. The predictive model provided a notably greater net benefit than the traditional TNM staging model across nearly all time intervals and threshold ranges (Figs. 5a-b). However, in the 3-year decision curves of the validation group, we observed that the conventional TNM model had a slight advantage over the nomogram model at threshold probabilities of approximately 0.15–0.3 (Fig. 5b). This may be due to the TNM model’s higher sensitivity in specific patient populations, at certain time points, and within particular risk thresholds. Nevertheless, the nomogram model demonstrated greater applicability and accuracy across a broader range of clinical scenarios.

Finally, we calculated a composite score for every patient, categorizing them into high, medium, and low-risk categories using the X-Tile software to assess survival differences. We observed that the survival curves for the three groups were distinctly separable, with higher total scores correlating with worse overall prognoses, and the differences were statistically significant (Figs. 5c-d).

Web model calculator

To facilitate quick and easy use of the nomogram prediction model in assessing 1-, 3-, and 5-year patient survival in clinical practice, we have developed an online web tool based on the model (https://jasonidea.shinyapps.io/gc_inflammation/). This tool serves as an effective resource for clinical decision-making and aids in individualized treatment planning (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

GC is a malignant neoplasm that has a significant impact on global morbidity and mortality rates, making early diagnosis and treatment crucial for improving patient prognosis. In recent years, the influence of inflammatory responses on tumor progression has emerged as a significant area of interest in cancer research. Chronic inflammation is known to drive tumorigenesis, disease progression, and metastasis through various mechanisms7. Consequently, the significance of inflammatory markers in the progression of cancer has become increasingly recognized.

Peripheral blood inflammatory markers can provide insights into systemic inflammation through straightforward, non-invasive, and economical blood tests. This study comprehensively analyzed the correlation between ten common peripheral blood inflammatory markers and the prognosis and clinicopathological characteristics of patients with GC. These markers provide insights into the body’s inflammatory state from different perspectives. Our results indicated that elevated inflammation scores were significantly associated with poor clinicopathological features and prognosis. Regression analyses revealed that age, BMI, T stage, LNR, and NCS were the independent prognostic factors. Ultimately, the nomogram model developed from these inflammatory markers demonstrated enhanced accuracy in predicting OS at 1, 3, and 5 years. This finding underscores the role of inflammation in GC prognosis and suggests that these markers could serve as valuable tools for assessing patient prognosis in clinical settings.

In the current study, we used the PSM approach primarily to minimize the impact of potential confounding variables, thereby enhancing comparability between the groups. PSM simulates the effects of a randomized controlled trial by matching patients with similar baseline characteristics, which helps to avoid systematic bias arising from imbalanced patient characteristics. Before PSM matching, we observed notable variations in ten inflammatory parameters between the deceased and surviving patient groups (p < 0.05). However, there were imbalances in baseline characteristics such as age, BMI, tumor size, AJCC T stage, AJCC N stage, and the number of positive lymph nodes. This raised concerns about the reliability and stability of our results. By applying PSM, we effectively minimized these biases, making our findings more reliable and precise when evaluating the association between peripheral blood inflammatory markers and the clinicopathological features and prognosis of GC29.

After balancing the preliminary attributes of both groups using the PSM method, we found that PLR, SII, HALP, NCS, and ICPI remained significantly different between the groups. Although the ROC curves had low AUC values (≤ 0.6) for all ten indicators, we believe that this methodology not only improved the precision of our statistical analysis but also enhanced the interpretability and applicability of our results32. In contrast, markers such as HALP, CALLY, and PNI demonstrated poor predictive ability in this study (AUC < 0.5). This may be attributed to the biological heterogeneity of these markers. While the study by Duzkopru et al.33 indicated that HALP has some prognostic value in some patients with GC, our findings did not support its broad applicability, suggesting potential variations in marker efficacy across different populations.

In this study, patients with elevated peripheral blood inflammation scores typically exhibited poorer clinicopathological characteristics, including advanced tumor stages, larger tumor volumes, and a higher number of positive lymph nodes. The unfavorable prognosis of these patients may be closely linked to the chronic inflammatory response present in their bodies. Existing research indicates that the inflammatory state correlates with tumor progression and metastasis. Inflammatory cells, such as neutrophils34 and monocytes35, along with inflammatory factors like interleukin (IL)−6, IL-8, and vascular endothelial growth factor, can inhibit immune system function within the TME. Additionally, platelets contribute to immune suppression by promoting neoangiogenesis, degrading the extracellular matrix, and releasing adhesion molecules and growth factors36, which synergistically facilitate tumor growth and metastasis14,37. Furthermore, patients with high inflammation scores may experience diminished tumor suppression by the immune response in vivo, thereby accelerating tumor progression and spread38.

In addition, numerous studies have established peripheral blood inflammation markers as valuable indicators for evaluating the prognosis of patients with cancer8–11. Higher inflammation scores are typically significantly and negatively correlated with OS in these individuals. These markers provide insights into the body’s overall inflammatory condition, and elevated levels often indicate increased immunosuppression and the release of inflammatory factors within the TME. This environment can promote tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance, ultimately leading to tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis39,40. Additionally, elevated levels of peripheral blood inflammatory markers are frequently associated with a decrease in lymphocyte counts41, suggesting a weakened immune system. This decline can further impair the body’s ability to resist cancer, influencing postoperative recurrence and long-term survival. Our study confirmed that higher NLR, MLR, SII, ICPI, NCS, and GPS scores correlate with poorer prognoses in patients with GC. Thus, a high peripheral blood inflammation score not only indicates the systemic inflammatory response in patients but also correlates with adverse clinicopathological features and prognosis. This underscores the need for more rigorous disease management and the development of personalized treatment strategies for patients with high inflammation scores42.

In this study, we performed an in-depth analysis of the relationship between ten peripheral blood inflammatory markers and the clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis of gastric cancer. Our regression analyses, including univariate Cox regression, LASSO, and multivariate Cox regression analyses, indicated that only the NCS (which includes CA 19 − 9 and NLR) was an independent prognostic factor for OS in GC. The NCS effectively integrates the tumor marker CA 19 − 9 and the NLR, a peripheral blood inflammation parameter, allowing it to reflect both tumor burden and systemic inflammatory status; therefore, it had a stronger predictive capability. Elevated CA 19 − 9 levels are frequently linked to higher TNM stages and adverse clinical features, such as deeper tumor infiltration, larger tumor volume, and lymph node metastasis43. Furthermore, patients with GC and elevated preoperative CA 19 − 9 levels are more likely to experience postoperative recurrence44, which typically predicts lower disease-free survival (DFS) and OS45. The NCS provides a more accurate assessment of patient prognosis than single peripheral blood inflammation parameters like NLR or PLR. Other inflammatory parameters did not qualify as independent risk factors, potentially due to sample size, statistical effects, and multivariate interactions39,40.

In clinical practice, the relevant parameters can be obtained from the patient’s admission records, routine preoperative blood tests, and pre-treatment imaging studies such as CT or MRI. These variables can be directly entered into the web-based calculator we developed to rapidly and intuitively estimate the patient’s OS probability. This prediction provides valuable guidance for the formulation of individualized treatment plans, postoperative follow-up strategies, and enhanced physician–patient communication. For patients identified as high-risk (lower predicted OS) by the model, and whose physical condition permits, clinicians may consider adopting more aggressive interventions. These may include more extensive tumor resection, broader lymphadenectomy, intensified perioperative management, and more frequent adjuvant therapy and follow-up, with the aim of improving long-term survival outcomes. Conversely, for low-risk (higher predicted OS) patients with good general health status, standard treatment and follow-up protocols per current guidelines may suffice. For those who suffer from severe comorbidities such as cardiopulmonary disease or malnutrition and are intolerant to standard therapies, personalized conservative treatment and surgical strategies may be adopted to minimize postoperative complications while preserving survival expectations. It is important to emphasize that the nomogram serves as a predictive and decision-support tool for survival estimation. Final treatment decisions should still be based on a comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation, taking into account the patient’s overall health status, local tumor progression, economic conditions, and family support, to achieve truly individualized and precision medicine.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, being a retrospective analysis, selective bias and some missing data may be unavoidable, which impacts the broad applicability of the findings. Second, the relatively small number of patients with well differentiated and stage T3 GC in the study sample may be related to the population composition, resulting in the underrepresentation of these subgroups. Additionally, this study used only single measurements of preoperative peripheral blood inflammatory markers and lacked dynamic monitoring of these markers over time, which may have underestimated their prognostic impact. While there are various inflammation-related cells and cytokines in peripheral blood, only some were selected for this study; therefore, the influence of all inflammatory markers on OS in GC was not fully evaluated. Moreover, the specific regimen and course of adjuvant therapy were not recorded in detail, potentially introducing bias into the prognostic analysis. Finally, recurrence data were missing for some patients, limiting further exploration of the impact of peripheral blood inflammatory markers on DFS. Future studies should focus on dynamic monitoring, incorporate multi-omics analysis, use prospective study designs, and involve multiple centers to comprehensively confirm the significance and utility of these markers in the prognostic evaluation of GC.

Conclusion

In this study, ten peripheral blood inflammatory markers were analyzed in depth to investigate their association with the clinicopathological features and the outcome of GC. The results indicated that elevated inflammation scores were associated with unfavorable clinicopathological features and poorer prognoses. Multivariate regression analysis further indicated that age, BMI, T stage, LNR, and NCS were independent prognostic factors for OS in patients with GC. Additionally, the nomogram model and web calculator constructed based on these inflammatory markers were capable of more precisely forecasting the survival rates of patients at 1, 3, and 5 years than the traditional TNM model. These findings provide a new reference for the clinical application of inflammatory markers in the prognostic assessment of GC. However, further validation through multicenter, prospective studies is necessary.
