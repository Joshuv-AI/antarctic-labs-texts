Discrete-Time Linear Dynamical System Control Using Sparse Inputs With Time-Varying Support
Krishna Praveen V. S. Kondapi, Chandrasekhar Sriram, Geethu Joseph, Chandra R. Murthy

Abstract
In networked control systems, communication resource constraints often necessitate the use of sparse control input vectors. A prototypical problem is how to ensure controllability of a linear dynamical system when only a limited number of actuators (inputs) can be active at each time step. In this work, we first present an algorithm for determining the sparse actuator schedule , i.e., the sequence of supports of the input vectors that ensures controllability. Next, we extend the algorithm to minimize the average control energy by simultaneously minimizing the trace of the controllability Gramian, under the sparsity constraints. We derive theoretical guarantees for both algorithms: the first algorithm ensures controllability with a minimal number of control inputs at a given sparsity level; for the second algorithm, we derive an upper bound on the average control energy under the resulting actuator schedule. Finally, we develop a novel sparse controller based on Kalman filtering and sparse signal recovery that drives the system to a desired state in the presence of process and measurement noise. We also derive an upper bound on the steady-state MSE attained by the algorithm. We corroborate our theoretical results using numerical simulations and illustrate that sparse control achieves a control performance comparable to the fully actuated systems.

II
Actuator Scheduling Problem

We consider a discrete-time LDS
(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})
whose dynamics are governed by

\boldsymbol{x}(k+1)=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}(k)+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{u}(k),

(1)

where
k=0,1,\ldots
is the integer time index,
\boldsymbol{x}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{u}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the system state and the input vectors, respectively, at time
k
. The matrices
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are the system transfer matrix and the input matrix, respectively. In
[
16
,
20
,
29
]
, the input matrix is taken as
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{I}
. In the sequel, we assume
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{B}\right\}=n
; no other conditions on
\boldsymbol{B}
are assumed. We consider the input
\boldsymbol{u}(k)
to have at most
s
nonzero entries (
s
-sparse inputs), i.e.,
\|\boldsymbol{u}(k)\|_{0}\leq s
, for
k=\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}
.

A classical control design problem is to estimate the control inputs
\{\boldsymbol{u}(k)\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
that drive the system state to a desired state
\boldsymbol{x}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
If the support of
\boldsymbol{u}(k)
is known, we can easily find the sparse inputs using conventional methods. However, sparsity constraints make finding such an actuator schedule (support of the sparse inputs) challenging. Further, it is known that a sparse actuator schedule that ensures controllability exists if and only if the system is
s
-sparse controllable

[
13
]
. Yet,
[
13
]
does not provide a method to determine the actuator schedule for
s
-sparse controllable systems. In this work, we address this gap by designing a sparse actuator schedule that can be used to drive the system to any given desired state in
K
time steps (
\boldsymbol{x}(K)=\boldsymbol{x}_{f}
).

The
s
-sparse controllability is equivalent to the following two conditions
[
13
]
: the system is controllable, and
s\geq n-\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\right\}
.
So, in the sequel, to ensure that the problem is feasible, we assume that these two conditions hold.
Further, for an
s
-sparse controllable LDS, the minimum number of time steps
K
required to ensure controllability is bounded as

\frac{n}{s}\leq K\leq\min\left(q\left\lceil\frac{\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{B}\right\}}{s}\right\rceil,n-s+1\right).

(2)

where
q
is the degree of the minimal polynomial of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Hence, with
\boldsymbol{R}
denoting the controllability matrix, there exists a finite
K
and an actuator schedule
\mathcal{S}\triangleq(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{K-1})
with
|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\leq s
such that,

\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}_{0}}&\boldsymbol{A}^{K-2}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}_{K-1}},\end{bmatrix}

(3)

satisfies
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}}\right\}=n
. Once
\mathcal{S}
is determined, the system can be driven from any initial state to a desired final state by applying inputs
\boldsymbol{u}(k)
with support
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, for
k=0,1,\ldots,K-1
. Thus, we aim to find
\mathcal{S}\in\Phi
such that
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}}\right\}=n
, where

\Phi\triangleq\left\{(\mathcal{S}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{K-1}):\mathcal{S}_{k}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\},|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\leq s\,\forall k\right\}.

(4)

We observe that any actuator schedule of length
K
can be represented using a subset of

\mathcal{V}=\{(k,j)|k=0,1,\ldots,K-1,\;j=1,2,\ldots,m\}.

(5)

Here,
k
and
j
represent the time and actuator index, respectively. It is clear that there is a natural bijection between any
\mathcal{T}\in 2^{\mathcal{V}}
and the corresponding actuator schedule:

\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T})=(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{K-1})\;\;\text{with}\;\;\mathcal{S}_{k}=\{j:(k,j)\in\mathcal{T}\}.

Also, including the tuple
(k,j)
in the schedule
\mathcal{S}
corresponds to including the
j
th actuator in the
k
th time step, i.e., the column
\boldsymbol{A}^{K-k-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{j}
is included in
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Clearly, brute-force search for
\mathcal{S}\in\Phi
is infeasible since
|\Phi|=\left(\sum_{l=0}^{s}\binom{m}{l}\right)^{K}
can be prohibitively large. In the next section, we present an algorithm that returns an actuator schedule that guarantees controllability subject to (
4
).

III
Actuator Scheduling Algorithm to Guarantee Controllability

We consider an actuator scheduling algorithm that starts with an empty set
\mathcal{S}
and incrementally adds entries to it until
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}}\right\}=n
. Since
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{B}\right\}=n
, we have
\mathcal{CS}({\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}})\subseteq\mathcal{CS}({\boldsymbol{A}^{j}\boldsymbol{B}})
for
i\geq j\geq 0
. This subspace structure allows for the addition of at most
s
columns from
\boldsymbol{A}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{B}
first, then adding at most
s
columns from
\boldsymbol{A}^{K-2}\boldsymbol{B}
, and so on, increasing the rank till
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}}\right\}=n
. The main result of this section is that the schedule returned by such a procedure ensures
s
-sparse controllability.
First, for each
0\leq i\leq K-1
, consider a set
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
containing column indices (expressed as tuples of the form defined above) of
\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{B}&\boldsymbol{A}^{K-2}\boldsymbol{B}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}\end{bmatrix}
such that the columns indexed by
\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)}
are linearly independent (LI) and
\mathcal{T}^{(K)}=\emptyset
. Let
\mathcal{I}(i)
denote a set containing
l(i)
indices corresponding to columns of
\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}
such that
\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)}\cup\mathcal{I}(i)
contain indices of LI columns in
\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{B}&\boldsymbol{A}^{K-2}\boldsymbol{B}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}\end{bmatrix}
. We note that
\mathcal{I}(i)
contains
l(i)
distinct tuples:
\mathcal{I}(i)=\left\{(K-i-1,j_{l})\in\mathcal{V}\right\}_{l=1}^{l(i)}
,
j_{l}\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,m\right\}
and satisfies

\mathcal{NS}\left(\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{I}(i)\cup\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)}\right)}\right)=\emptyset.

(6)

The following proposition specifies the value of
l(i)

Proposition 1

If
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{B}\right\}=n
, then there exist
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}\right\}-\left|\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)}\right|\geq 0
linearly independent columns in
\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}
that do not belong to
\mathcal{CS}(\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)})})

We get the above result by noting that
\mathcal{CS}\{\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)})}\}\subseteq\mathcal{CS}\{\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}\}
and
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)})}\right\}=\left|\mathcal{T}^{{i+1}}\right|
.

The above proposition ensures that we can set

at each
i
and ensure that (
6

\mathcal{I}(i)

l(i)
indices of LI columns from the matrix

satisfying (

to
\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)}
is guaranteed to increase the rank by

Definition 1
(Supermodularity)

The set function
f:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\alpha
-submodular if
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the largest number for which the following holds
\forall\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and
\forall e\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{B}
:

f(\mathcal{A}\cup\{e\})-f(\mathcal{A})\geq{\alpha[f(\mathcal{B}\cup\{e\})-f(\mathcal{B})]}.

(11)

Also, the function
-f(\cdot)
is said to be
\alpha
-supermodular.

Definition 2
(Matroid)

A matroid is a pair
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{I})
where
\mathcal{U}
is a finite set and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{U}}
satisfies the following three properties:
(i)
\emptyset\in\mathcal{I}
; (ii) For any two sets,
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, if
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{I}
, then

VI
Sparse Control of Noisy Systems

The algorithms presented in
Section

III
and
Section

provide a sparse actuator schedule that can be used to drive the LDS from any given initial and final state, under a noiseless setting. If the same schedule is employed in LDSs affected by noise, the resulting trajectory may stray away from the desired final state. So, we need feedback control to compensate for both process noise and measurement noise. Specifically, we consider a system with additive Gaussian noise in the state evolution and measurement equations:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}(k+1)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}(k)+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{u}(k)+\boldsymbol{v}(k)

\boldsymbol{x}(0)

We aim to reach a target state

VIII
Conclusion

We studied the problem of finding an energy-efficient actuator schedule for controlling an LDS in a finite number of steps, with at most
s
active inputs per time step. To address this problem, we presented a greedy scheduling algorithm for minimizing the average control energy,
\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}_{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}\right\}
. We presented several interesting theoretical guarantees for the algorithm when the input matrix has full row rank. Simulations showed that the algorithm returns a feasible schedule, with the average energy increasing modestly (compared to the case without sparsity constraints), proportional to the inverse of the fraction of active actuators. Thus, sparse inputs can drive an LDS to a desired state without significantly increasing energy requirements at moderate sparsity levels. We also developed a controller based on the Kalman filter for noisy LDSs and derived an upper bound on its
steady-state MSE
. Simulations indicated that increasing the number of available actuators
m
improves the tracking performance when the sparsity level is fixed. Finally, we showed that, even under sparsity constraints, we can stabilize the system around an equilibrium point in the presence of noise. Future work could look at assessing the sparse controllability under adversaries and under unknown system matrices, especially since data-driven control has been gaining focus recently.

Proof of

By construction in

corresponds to linearly independent columns in matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{B}&\boldsymbol{A}^{K-2}\boldsymbol{B}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}\end{bmatrix}
. To ensure controllability, i.e.,
\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}\right\}=n

. To this end, it suffices to show that

. The remaining

, corresponding to linearly independent columns from

1
)

\left|\mathcal{T}^{(i)}\right|=\min\left\{\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}\right\},\left|\mathcal{T}^{(i+1)}\right|+s\right\}.

(29)

This is because we only add linearly independent columns in each iteration and

Rewriting

Therefore, for

\mathsf{Rank}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{B}\right\}+(K-2)s,(K-1)s\}.

Now, to establish that

Now, we show that the remaining terms in (

\displaystyle\hskip-51.21504pt\leq\chi\mathbb{E}\left\{\|\boldsymbol{d}(k-1)\|^{2}\right\}+(1+3\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B}))\mathbb{E}\left\{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}(k-1)\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+2\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{f}-\boldsymbol{x}_{f}\|^{2}+\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{v}\right\},

(46)

\displaystyle\hskip-51.21504pt=\chi^{k}\|\boldsymbol{d}(0)\|^{2}+\left(1+3\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\right)\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\chi^{k-i-1}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}(i)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\chi^{i-1}\left[2\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{f}-\boldsymbol{x}_{f}\|^{2}+\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{v}\right\}\right].

(47)

We note that
\chi<1
since
s\geq\max\{-\frac{\log(2\|\boldsymbol{A}\|^{2})}{\log(\xi(\boldsymbol{B}))},0\}
by assumption. So, the first term of (
47
) is a decreasing function of
k
, which is lower bounded by 0. Hence, we can always find an integer
k_{1}
such that

\chi^{k}\|\boldsymbol{d}(0)\|^{2}\leq\frac{\eta}{3},

(48)

for all
k\geq k_{1}
.
Also, for the second term of (
47
), we note that

\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}(i)\|^{2}=\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}.

(49)

Substituting (
48
) and (
49
) into (
47
) gives

\hskip-14.22636pt\mathbb{E}\left\{\|\boldsymbol{d}(k)\|^{2}\right\}<\frac{\eta}{3}+\left(1+3\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\right)\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\chi^{k-i-1}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}\\
+\frac{2\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{f}-\boldsymbol{x}_{f}\|^{2}+\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{v}\right\}}{1-2\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\|\boldsymbol{A}\|^{2}},

(50)

where we use the property that, for any integers
0\leq i_{i}\leq i_{f}
,

\sum_{i=i_{i}}^{i_{f}}\chi^{i}<\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\chi^{i}=\frac{1}{1-\chi},

(51)

because
0<\chi<1
. Now, to establish the desired bound in (
25
), it remains analyze the second term in (
50
). To this end, we use (
26
) and (
27
) to obtain
\lim_{i\to\infty}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}=\boldsymbol{P}
. The limit
\boldsymbol{P}
exists when the system
(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{v}^{1/2},\boldsymbol{C})
is controllable and observable. Hence, we can find an integer
k_{2}
satisfying

\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}<\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}+\frac{\eta(1-\chi)}{3(1+\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B}))},

(52)

for all
i\geq k_{2}
. Therefore, for
k>k_{2}
, the second term of (
50
) simplifies as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\chi^{k-i-1}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip-108.12054pt\leq\!\sum_{i=0}^{k_{2}-1}\chi^{k-i-1}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\!\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!\right\}\!+\max_{i\geq k_{2}}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\!\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!\right\}\!\sum_{i=k_{2}}^{k-1}\!\chi^{k-i-1}

\displaystyle\hskip-108.12054pt\leq\!\sum_{i=0}^{k_{2}-1}\chi^{k-i-1}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\!\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!\right\}\!+\frac{\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\!\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!\right\}}{1-\chi}+\frac{\eta}{3(1+\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B}))},

(53)

due to (
51
) and (
52
). Next, we use
\chi<1
to choose an integer
k_{3}>k_{2}
such that

\chi^{k_{3}-k_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{k_{2}-1}\chi^{k_{2}-i-1}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}<\frac{\eta}{3\left(1+3\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\right)}.

Hence, for all
k>k_{3}
, we bound the first term of (
53
) as

\sum_{i=0}^{k_{2}-1}\chi^{k-i-1}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k_{2}-1}\chi^{k_{3}-i-1}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}\\
<\frac{\eta}{3\left(1+3\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\right)}.

(54)

Now (
53
) and (
54
) jointly lead to

\left(1+3\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\right)\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\chi^{k-i-1}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}\\
<\frac{1+3\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})}{1-2\xi^{s}(\boldsymbol{B})\|\boldsymbol{A}\|^{2}}\mathsf{Tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}+\frac{2\eta}{3}.

(55)

Combining (
50
) and (
55
), we arrive at (
25
). Hence, the proof is complete.
