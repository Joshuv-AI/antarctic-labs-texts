Étale algebras over finite Heyting algebras
Evgeny Kuznetsov

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the concept of local homeomorphism in Esakia spaces. We introduce the notion of étale Heyting H H -algebra and establish category-theoretic duality for étale Heyting H H -algebra in the case of finite Heyting algebra H H . Furthermore, we give an identity that axiomatizes the variety of étale Heyting H H -algebras when H H is finite. We also show that the category of Stone space-valued (co)presheaves over a finite Esakia space X X is equivalent to the slice category of local homeomorphisms over X X . The fact is used to show that, in comparison with the case of general Heyting H H -algebras, it is easier to compute finite colimits in the category of étale Heyting H H -algebras.

\end{itemize}\par\par The underlying set of an Esakia space $X$ is finite if and only if $H_{X}$, the Esakia dual Heyting algebra of $X$, is finite; see, e.g., \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{esakiaBook}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Theorem 3.4.17)}.

\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Local homeomorphisms}
This paper's major goal is to investigate the local invertibility of Esakia morphisms around each element of the domain (often referred to as \emph{local homeomorphisms}; see Definition \ref{localHomeomorphismDefinition} below). We then investigate possible algebraic characterizations linked to them via Esakia duality.
\par Note also that local homeomorhisms of topological spaces can be described in terms of sheaves \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{maclane-moerdijk-sheaves}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}, a machinery widely used throughout mathematics for almost a century. Sheaves originated in mathematical analysis and geometry, where they serve as cohomology coefficients and representations of functions suitable for different types of manifolds. On the other hand, logic uses sheaves as vehicles for models of set theory.
\par We hope that the study of local homeomorphisms in Esakia spaces contributes to a better understanding of Heyting algebras and aligns with broader trends in the area of research.

\par For notational convenience, for a map $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$, we consider two functions between corresponding power sets, namely ${f_{*}\colon\mathcal{P}(X^{\prime})\to\mathcal{P}(X)}$ and ${f^{*}\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(X^{\prime})}$ defined as follows: ${f_{*}(U)=\{f(x)\mid x\in U\}}$ and ${f^{*}(V)=\{x\mid f(x)\in V\}}$.
\par These two functions, $f_{*}$ and $f^{*}$\!, preserve the subset inclusion order on powersets. If they are seen as functors between poset categories (see the Definition \ref{poset-category} below), they make an obvious adjunction, meaning $U\subseteq f^{*}(V)$ if and only if $f_{*}(U)\subseteq V$.
\par We assume the reader is familiar with the notions of \emph{open map}, \emph{closed map}, and \emph{homeomorphism}. For details related to these notions, please refer to, e.g., \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{engelking}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, \lx@sectionsign 1.4)}.
\par\par\begin{definition}A map $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ between topological spaces $X^{\prime}$ and $X$ is said to be \emph{local homeomorphism} (or in other terms \emph{locally invertible} around every element of its domain $X^{\prime}$), if for every element $x\in X^{\prime}$ there exists an open subset $U_{x}\subseteq X^{\prime}$ such that the image $f_{*}(U_{x})$ is an open subset in $X$ and the restriction of $f$ to $U_{x}$ is a homeomorphism between $U_{x}$ and $f_{*}(U_{x})$ equipped with the subspace topology induced from $X^{\prime}$ and $X$ respectively.
\end{definition}\par We also encounter the following terminology in the subject literature: a continuous map $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ is referred to as a \emph{space over} $X$ (or sometimes simply \emph{bundle}), while a local homeomorphism $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ is termed an \emph{\'{e}tale space} over $X$ (or \emph{\'{e}tale map} over $X$). This terminology justifies both the title of the article and the name of the algebraic construction of \'{E}tale Heyting algebra that will be discussed below.
\par One example is the category $$, which consists of all the local homeomorphisms over the fixed topological space $X$. \par The objective of the present study arose in search of a new class of morphisms of Esakia spaces called \emph{Esakia local homeomorphisms}, providing some evidence that for an Esakia space $X$, the corresponding category $/X$ of Esakia local homeomorphisms over $X$ enjoys many useful properties.
\par In this paper, the role of local homeomorphisms over Esakia spaces is played by continuous strict $p$-morphisms. We will explain and elaborate on the rationale for doing so. In the sequel, $/X$ denotes the category with objects, strict $p$-morphisms over a fixed Esakia space $X$ in the category of Esakia spaces. The arrows in $/X$ are standard Esakia morphisms between objects' domains, forming commutative triangles.
\par It is well known that if $f\colon Y\to X$ and $f^{\prime}\colon Y^{\prime}\to X$ are local homeomorphisms of topological spaces and $g\colon Y\to Y^{\prime}$ is a continuous map such that $f^{\prime}\circ g=f$, then $g$ is a local homeomorphism. Similarly, if $f\colon Y\to X$ and $f^{\prime}\colon Y^{\prime}\to X$ are strict $p$-morphisms, and $f^{\prime}\circ g=f$ for the continuous order-preserving map $g\colon Y\to Y^{\prime}$, then $g$ is also a strict $p$-morphism.
\par As we will see below, the categories $/X_{H}$ provide useful tools for studying general Heyting algebras, where $X_{H}$ is the dual Esakia space for a Heyting algebra $H$. In particular, if $H$ is a finite Heyting algebra, then finite limits in the category $/X_{H}$ are well-behaved and can be explicitly described.
\par On the Heyting algebra side, this provides a class of Heyting algebra homomorphisms with manageable amalgamation properties; see, e.g., the Corollary \ref{finite-colimits-cor}. This is important because pushouts of general Heyting algebra homomorphisms are notoriously difficult to describe.
\par\par The following well-known lemma characterizes order-preserving maps in terms of preservation upward-closedness by $f_{*}$; see, e.g., \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{esakiaBook}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Lemma 1.4.12)} for proof.
\par\begin{lemma}Let $X^{\prime}$ and $X$ be partially ordered sets and $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ be a map, then
\par\begin{enumerate}\enumerate@item@$f$ is order-preserving if and only if $f_{*}({\uparrow}x)\subseteq{\uparrow}f(x)$.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\enumerate@item@$f$ satisfies the `back' condition of a $p$-morpism if and only if ${\uparrow}f(x)\subseteq f_{*}({\uparrow}x)$.
\par\end{enumerate}\end{lemma}\par We also use a well-known fact relating the properties of being an order-preserving map, a continuous map, a $p$-morphism, and an open map; see, e.g., \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{esakiaBook}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Lemma 3.3.2)}.
\par\par\par\begin{lemma}Let $X^{\prime}$ and $X$ be partially ordered sets and $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ be a map, then
\begin{enumerate}\enumerate@item@$f$ is continuous with respect to upsets topologies on $X^{\prime}$ and $X$ if and only if it is order-preserving;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\enumerate@item@$f$ is open with respect to upsets topologies on $X^{\prime}$ and $X$ if and only if the `back' condition of a $p$-morpism holds.
\end{enumerate}\end{lemma}\par\par\begin{lemma}Let $f\colon(X^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\leqslant^{\prime})\to(X,\tau,\leqslant)$ be a map between partially ordered topological spaces. Let for any $x\in X^{\prime}$ there exist an open upset neighborhood $U_{x}$ of $x$ such that $f_{*}(U_{x})$ is an open upset and the restriction $f|_{U_{x}}$ is a homeomorphism with respect to both ordinary and upset topologies. Then $f$ is an open, continuous, strict $p$-morphism.
\end{lemma}\@proof First, let us show that $f$ is continuous. Let $V$ be an open subset of $X$ and $x\in f^{*}(V)$. By assumption, there exists an open neighborhood $U_{x}$ of $x$ such that $f|_{U_{x}}$ is a homeomorphism between $U_{x}$ and $f_{*}(U_{x})$.
\par Subsets $V$ and $f_{*}(U_{x})$ are open subsets in $X$, so $V\cap f_{*}(U_{x})$ is an open subset in $X$. $f|_{U_{x}}$ is a homeomorphism, and as a result is continuous, then $f|_{U_{x}}^{*}(V\cap f_{*}(U_{x}))=\{x\in U_{x}\mid f(x)\in V\cap f_{*}(U_{x})\}=\left(f^{*}(V)\cap U_{x}\right)$ is open in $X$ and $x\in\left(f^{*}(V)\cap U_{x}\right)\subseteq f^{*}(V)$. Hence, every $x\in f^{*}(V)$ has an open neighborhood contained in $f^{*}(V)$, and $f^{*}(V)$ is open.
\par Similarly, we can show that $f$ is order-preserving. Namely, using Lemma \ref{contunuity-and-openness-is-monotonicity-and-pmorhismness}, it is easy to see that a map is order-preserving if and only if for any upset $V$ in $X$ the subset $f^{*}(V)$ is an upset in $X^{\prime}$.
\par Let us now prove that $f$ is open. Let $A$ denote an open subset of $X^{\prime}$. For each $x\in A$, choose $U_{x}\subseteq X$ and $V_{x}\subseteq X^{\prime}$ so that $f|_{U_{x}}$ is a homeomorphism between $U_{x}$ and $V_{x}$. For each $x\in A$, the subset $f_{*}(U_{x}\cap A)$ is an open subset in $V_{x}$, so it is open in $X$ as well. Therefore, $\bigcup_{x\in A}f_{*}(U_{x}\cap A)=f_{*}(A)$ is an open subset of $X$.
\par In the same way, we can demonstrate that $f$ is a $p$-morphism since an order-preserving map is a $p$-morphism if and only if the subset $f_{*}(A)$ is an upset in $X$ for each upset $A$ in $X^{\prime}$. Lemma \ref{contunuity-and-openness-is-monotonicity-and-pmorhismness} is used again to see this.
\par Now let us show that $f$ is strict. Let $x\in X^{\prime}$ and $f(x)\leqslant x^{\prime}$. Since $f$ is a $p$-morphism, there exists $y\in{\uparrow}x$ such that $f(y)=x^{\prime}$. Because there exist open upset neighborhoods $U_{x}$ and $V_{f(x)}$ such that $f|_{U_{x}}$ is a homeomorphism between them (with respect to upset topologies) and ${\uparrow}x\subseteq U_{x}$, it follows that such $y\in{\uparrow}x$ is unique.
\qed\end@proof
\par Recall that according to Corollary \ref{continuous-inverse-lemma}, for a continuous mapping $f\colon X^{\prime}$ to be a local homeomorphism, for every point $x\in X^{\prime}$ there must exist an open neighborhood $U_{x}$ of $x$ such that $f_{*}(U_{x})$ is an open subset and the restriction map $f|_{U_{x}}$ is a closed/open bijection.
Also, by Lemma \ref{esakia_space_pointed_upset_is_closed}, in an Esakia space (and in a Priestley space in general, see \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{priestley-1972}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}), the upset ${\uparrow}x$ is always a closed subset.
\par With this fact in mind, if $f$ is a strict $p$-morphism between Esakia spaces, we can relax the assumptions on $f$ by dropping the openness and closeness restrictions without losing the property of being a local homeomorphism of underlying topological spaces.
\par\begin{lemma}Suppose $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ is a continuous strict $p$-morphism between Esakia spaces $X^{\prime}$ and $X$. Then $f|_{{\uparrow}x}$ is a continuous bijective $p$-morphism between ${\uparrow}x$ and $f_{*}({\uparrow}x)$, with inverse $f|_{{\uparrow}x}^{-1}$ a continuous $p$-morphism.
\end{lemma}\@proof Indeed, because $f$ is strict, it establishes a bijection between ${\uparrow}x$ and $f_{*}({\uparrow}x)$. Because $f$ is a $p$-morphism, $f_{*}({\uparrow}x)={\uparrow}f(x)$. By Lemma \ref{esakia_space_pointed_upset_is_closed} ${\uparrow}x$ is a closed subset of a compact space; hence, ${\uparrow}x$ is compact. Now, $f_{*}({\uparrow}x)$ is a subspace of a Hausdorff space; hence, it is a Hausdorff space. By leveraging Lemma \ref{closed-function-lemma}, along with Corollary \ref{continuous-inverse-lemma} and assuming that $f$ is a continuous $p$-morphism, we can see that the inverse $f^{-1}$ is also a continuous $p$-morphism.
\qed\end@proof
\par The \emph{local sections} of a function $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ over a subset $U\subseteq X$ are functions $s\colon U\to X^{\prime}$ such that $f\circ s=id_{U}$. It's important to note that in more standard situations, like, e.g., topological spaces, the images $s_{*}(U)$ of continuous local sections $s$ for \'{e}tale function $f$ form a base for the topology of the total space $X^{\prime}$; see, e.g., \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{maclane-moerdijk-sheaves}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Ch. 2, \lx@sectionsign 6)}. In other words, every member of the Heyting algebra of open subsets of $X^{\prime}$ is a union of images $s_{*}(U)$.
\par\par\par In our case, we have demonstrated that continuous strict $p$-morphisms are invertible on principal upsets ${\uparrow}x$. Because $f$ is a $p$-morphism, we also know that $f_{*}({\uparrow}x)={\uparrow}f(x)$. For arbitrary $x\in X^{\prime}$, the function $s=f|_{{\uparrow}x}^{-1}$, which is the inverse of $f|_{{\uparrow}x}\colon{\uparrow}x\to f_{*}({\uparrow}x)$, serves as a local section for $f$. In the case when $X$ is a finite Esakia space, principal upsets are clopen subsets because the Stone topology on a finite Esakia space is discrete. And finally, if $X$ is an Esakia space and $C$ is a clopen upset in $X$, then it is evident that $C=\bigcup_{c\in C}{\uparrow}c$. This demonstrates that every element in the Heyting algebra of clopen upsets is a union of images $s_{*}({\uparrow}f(x))$ of local sections of $f$. This is a crucial fact to be aware of.
\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{\'{E}tale Heyting algebras}
In this section, we introduce some notions from universal algebra serving algebraic duals to local isomorphisms of Esakia spaces. The analogues of these notions appear in various fields of mathematics and are studied in different contexts. Structures under consideration in this section can be thought of as an expansion of algebraic structure by a substructure of constants. It is worth noting that a concept seemingly closely related yet distinct in appearance, known as the $\forall$-subalgebra, has been recently considered by \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{almeida-2023-pi2rule}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Definition 3.3)}.
\par In what follows, we will utilize the notion of the \emph{variety} of algebras of some fixed signature. Readers not familiar with the notion of variety can refer to \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{burrisAndSankappanavar}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Ch. 2, Definition 9.3)} for clarification.
\par\par Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a variety of universal algebras, and let $A_{0}\in\mathcal{V}$ be an algebra in it. We consider the category ${}_{0})$ of $A_{0}$-algebras.
\par\par Namely, objects of ${}_{0})$ are $\mathcal{V}$-homomorphisms $A_{0}\to A$, and a morphism from $\alpha\colon A_{0}\to A$ to $\alpha^{\prime}\colon A_{0}\to A^{\prime}$ is represented by a $\mathcal{V}$-homomorphism $\beta\colon A\to A^{\prime}$ such that $\beta\circ\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}$.
\par Upon closer inspection, a reader may recognize the idea of expansion of the signature by subalgebra of constants here. The idea of expansion of the signature by constants is not novel and is used fruitfully in different contexts in mathematics, e.g., see \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{taylor2017}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}, \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{nouri2020}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}, \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{daniyarova2012}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, \textsection 3)}. Note that indeed ${}_{0})$ forms the category of all algebras in another variety with an enriched signature, which we will also denote by ${}_{0})$. Operations of ${}_{0})$ are operations of $\mathcal{V}$ together with constants (nullary operations) $c_{a}$, one for each $a\in A_{0}$, and identities of the variety ${}_{0})$ are given by identities of $\mathcal{V}$ together with variable-free identities of the form $w(c_{a_{1}},\ldots,c_{a_{n}})=c_{w(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})}$ for every generating $n$-ary operation $w$ of $\mathcal{V}$.
\par\par\par Let finally ${}_{0})$ be the subvariety of ${}_{0})$ generated by $A_{0}$, i.e., by the identity map of $A_{0}$ viewed as the \emph{initial object} of the category ${}_{0})$. For the definition of the \emph{variety generated} by a class of algebras, please refer to \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{burrisAndSankappanavar}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Ch. 2, Definition 9.4)}. Also, recall that an object $A$ of a category $scalled\emph{initial}ifthereisexactlyonemorphismfrom$A$toanyobject$B$of$\par\par We refer to algebras from ${}_{0})$ as \emph{\'{e}tale $A_{0}$-algebras}, and the $\mathcal{V}$-homomorphisms $A_{0}\to A$ that define \'{e}tale $A_{0}$-algebras as \emph{\'{e}tale homomorphisms}. As we will see, the name \'{e}tale will be subsequently justified for finite Heyting $H$-algebras due to their close relation with strict Esakia morphisms. In this context, the category of \'{e}tale $A_{0}$-algebras is the smallest subcategory of ${}_{0})$ containing $A_{0}$ and closed under products, subalgebras, and homomorphic images. According to Tarski's theorem \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{tarski1946}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}, \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{burrisAndSankappanavar}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Ch. 2, Theorem 9.5)}, ${}_{0})=\operatorname{HSP}(A_{0})$ and by Birkhoff's theorem \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{birkhoff1935}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}} it is an equational class, see also \cite[cite]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{burrisAndSankappanavar}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Ch. 2, Theorem 11.9)}.
\par\par\par For Heyting algebra $H$, the corresponding category $)$ of \'{e}tale $H$-algebras, i.e., Heyting algebras $A$ equipped with an \'{e}tale homomorphism $H\to A$, admits a nicer and more explicit description compared to the category of arbitrary $H$-algebras $)$, i.e., those given by arbitrary Heyting algebra homomorphisms $H\to A$. For instance, pushouts in this category are much simpler to describe than pushouts of general Heyting algebras. They coincide with pushouts computed in the category of distributive lattices and lattice homomorphisms; see Corollary \ref{pushout-corrolary}.

\par\begin{lemma}If for elements $x,y,z$ in a Heyting algebra $H$ holds $(y\wedge x)=(y\wedge z)$, then $y\leq(x\Leftrightarrow z)$.
\end{lemma}\@proof If $(y\wedge x)=(y\wedge z)$, then $(y\wedge x)=(y\wedge z)\leq z$ and $(y\wedge z)=(y\wedge x)\leq x$. Hence $y\leq(x\Rightarrow z)$ and $y\leq(z\Rightarrow x)$. The last holds if and only if $y\leq(x\Leftrightarrow z)$.
\qed\end@proof
\par\begin{lemma}Let $c\colon H\to A$ be a Heyting $H$-algebra where $H$ is complete. Let $x\in A$ be a fixed element of $A$ and $G\subseteq A$ be a subset of $A$ such that $1_{A}\in A$ is representable as a join $1_{A}=\bigvee_{y\in G}y$ (possibly infinite). Suppose also that for arbitrary elements $y\in G$ there exists $h_{y}\in H$ such that $y\wedge x=y\wedge c(h_{y})$, then $\underset{h\in H}{\bigvee}\left(x\Leftrightarrow c(h)\right)=1$ holds for $c$.
\end{lemma}\par\@proof Fix some element $x\in A$. Using the Lemma \ref{aux-lemma-1-identity}, for an arbitrary element $y\in G$, there exists $h_{y}\in H$ such that $y\leq(x\Leftrightarrow c(h_{y}))$. Then $1=\bigvee_{y\in G}y\leq\underset{h_{y}\in H}{\bigvee}\left(x\Leftrightarrow c(h_{y})\right)$.
\qed\end@proof
\par\begin{proposition}Let ${f\colon X^{\prime}\to X}$ be a $p$-morphism between partially ordered sets $X^{\prime}$ and $X$. $f$ is a strict $p$-morphism if and only if for every upset $U$ in $X^{\prime}$, in the Heyting algebra of upsets of $X^{\prime}$ the following identity holds: $$\underset{W\in)}{\bigvee}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\left(U\Leftrightarrow f^{*}(W)\right)=X^{\prime}.$$\end{proposition}\par\@proof First, suppose that $f$ is a strict $p$-morphism. By the Lemma \ref{aux-lemma-2-identity}, to demonstrate the validity of the identity \text{$(\acute{E}t)_{H}$} \!{\!,} it is sufficient to, for any $y\in X^{\prime}$ and any fixed upset $U$ of $X^{\prime}$, find an upset $W$ such that ${\uparrow}y\cap U={\uparrow}y\cap f^{*}(W)$.
\par Let $W=f_{*}({\uparrow}y\cap U)$. Note that $W$ is upset because $f$ is a $p$-morphism. It is also obvious that ${\uparrow} y\cap U\subseteq f^{*}(W)$. Hence ${\uparrow} y\cap U\subseteq{\uparrow} y\cap f^{*}(W)$. On the other hand, since $f$ is strict, ${\uparrow}y\cap f^{*}(W)\subseteq U$, hence ${\uparrow}y\cap f^{*}(W)\subseteq{\uparrow}y\cap U$.
\par Now suppose $f$ validates the identity \text{$(\acute{E}t)_{H}$} \!\!. $f$ is a $p$-morphism. To demonstrate the strictness of the $p$-morphism $f$, it is sufficient to show that, for any $y\in X^{\prime}$ and any elements $y\leqslant y_{1},y\leqslant y_{2}$ above $y$, if $f(y_{1})=f(y_{2})$, then $y_{1}=y_{2}$.
\par\par\par Suppose, to the contrary, that $f(y_{1})=f(y_{2})$ and $y_{1}\neq y_{2}$. Without loss of generality, we assume $y_{1}\nleqslant y_{2}$. Since \text{$(\acute{E}t)_{H}$} is valid for $f$, for $U={\uparrow}y_{1}$, there should exist an upset $W$ in $X$ such that ${\uparrow}y\cap f^{*}(W)={\uparrow}y\cap U={\uparrow}y_{1}$. Since $y_{2}\notin{\uparrow}y_{1}$, we also have $y_{2}\notin{\uparrow}y\cap f^{*}(W)$. Since $y_{2}\in{\uparrow}y$, we conclude that $y_{2}\notin f^{*}(W)$, and herefore, $f(y_{2})\notin W$. Recall that we also assumed $f(y_{1})=f(y_{2})$; hence, $y_{1}\notin f^{*}(W)$. Therefore, $y_{1}\notin{\uparrow}y\cap f^{*}(W)={\uparrow}y_{1}$, leading to a contradiction.
\qed\end@proof

\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{General case}
A natural follow-up question is whether the categories $)$ and $/X$ are still dually equivalent in the case of arbitrary, infinite Heyting algebra $H$. Unfortunately, the answer to this question appears to be negative.
\par Nonetheless, first note the following:
\par\begin{proposition}For an arbitrary continuous strict $p$-morphism $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ the Esakia dual of $f$ belongs to ${}_{X})$.
\end{proposition}\@proof Similar to the approach used in Proposition \ref{StonePresheavesOnSierpinskiToEtaleVariety}, we can demonstrate that $H_{X}^{\prime}$ is isomorphic to a subalgebra of $\prod_{\begin{subarray}{l}x\in X\\
\xi\in f^{*}(\{x\})\end{subarray}}{H_{{\uparrow}x}}$.
\par Let $X$ be an arbitrary Esakia space and $f\colon X^{\prime}\to X$ a strict Esakia morphism. The elements of $X^{\prime}$ can be indexed by pairs $(x,\xi)$ where $x\in X$ and $\xi\in f^{*}(\{x\})$. Since $f$ is a strict $p$-morphism, then $f|_{{\uparrow}\xi}$ is a continuous bijection between ${\uparrow}\xi$ and ${\uparrow}f(\xi)$.
\par Recall that ${\uparrow}\xi$ is the smallest upset containing $\xi$, which, by Lemma \ref{esakia_space_pointed_upset_is_closed}, is always closed in an Esakia space. A closed subspace of a Hausdorff space is compact Hausdorff as well. Hence, $f|_{{\uparrow}\xi}$ is an isomorphism because it is a continuous bijection of compact Hausdorff spaces.
\par The map $\left(m_{x,\xi}\right)_{\!}\colon\uparrow x\xrightarrow{f^{-1}}\uparrow\xi\xhookrightarrow{\uparrow\xi\cap f^{*}({x})}X^{\prime}$ is clearly a continuous map.
\par Let's suppose that $y\leqslant z$ in $\uparrow x$. Then, the map $\left(m_{x,\xi}\right)_{$}isanorder-preservingmapduetothefactthat$f$isa$p$-morphism.Giventhat$f$isorder-preserving,itfollowsthat$\left(m_{x,\xi}\right)_{ $}isa$p$-morphism.\par Onceagain,tocompletetheproofthattheEsakiadualof$f$isinthevariety$_X) $,weneedtoverifythatforarbitraryelements$x\in X$,thealgebra$H_{{\uparrow}x}$belongsto$_X) $.Thisistriviallythecasesince,forany$x\in X$,theinclusion${\uparrow}x\hookrightarrow X$isacontinuous$p$-morphism,andhence$H_{{\uparrow}x}$isahomomorphicimageof$H_X$.\qed\end@proof\par Theinversedirectionmakesthingsharder.Namely,forinfiniteEsakiaspaces$X$,thereexistnon-strictp-morphisms$f\colon X'\to X$whoseEsakiadualsdobelongto$_X) $.\par\begin{example}Consider the ordinals $X=\omega+1$ and $X^{\prime}=\omega+2$, along with their natural orderings and ordinal topologies. It is straightforward to verify (and well known) that both are Esakia spaces. Consider the map $\pi\colon X^{\prime}\to X$, sending all elements of $\omega+1$ to themselves and the element $\omega+1\in X^{\prime}$ to $\omega\in X$. It is evident that $\pi$ is a continuous order-preserving $p$-morphism that is not strict. Indeed, under a strict $p$-morphism, inverse images of points are incomparable, while $\pi(\omega)=\pi(\omega+1)=\omega$ with $\omega<\omega+1$.
\par On the other hand, the dual homomorphism $\widehat{\pi}\colon H_{X}\to H_{X^{\prime}}$ belongs to ${}_{X})$.
\par To see this, we will explicitly represent $H_{X^{\prime}}$ as a subquotient of a product of several copies of $H_{X}$, in a way compatible with $\widehat{\pi}$ and the diagonal embedding of $H_{X}$ into that product.
\par For that, it will be convenient for us to identify $H_{X}$ with the set of all natural numbers with reverse ordering, together with an extra bottom element,
$$H=\{1\succ 2\succ 3\succ\cdots\succ\bot\}.$$Here $n\in H$, $n=1,2,3,\ldots$ corresponds to the principal clopen upset ${\uparrow}n$ of $X$, and $\bot$ to the empty clopen upset.
\par Similarly, $H_{X^{\prime}}$ can be identified with $H^{\prime}=H\cup\{\bot^{\prime}\}$, where $\bot^{\prime}$ is an element with $h\succ\bot^{\prime}$ for all $h\in H$ distinct from $\bot$ and $\bot^{\prime}\succ\bot$. This $\bot^{\prime}$ corresponds to the clopen upset $\{\omega+1\}$ of $X^{\prime}$.
\par Under this identification, $\widehat{\pi}$ corresponds to the identical embedding of $H$ into $H^{\prime}$. So let us show that the $H$-algebra $H^{\prime}$ defined by $\widehat{\pi}$ indeed belongs to $)$. Namely, it is a quotient of a subalgebra of the product of an infinite number of copies of $H$.
\par Let $N\subset H$ be $H\setminus\{\bot\}$. Let $H^{N}$ be the product of $N$ many copies of $H$, which we will view as the algebra of all maps from $N$ to $H$ with pointwise Heyting algebra structure. Let $A\subset H^{N}$ be the Heyting subalgebra of $H^{N}$ generated by the identical embedding $\boldsymbol{x}\colon N\hookrightarrow H$, together with all constant maps $\boldsymbol{c}_{h}$, $h\in H$, with $\boldsymbol{c}_{h}(n)=h$ for all $n\in N$. Note also that $\neg\boldsymbol{x}=\neg\boldsymbol{c}_{h}=\boldsymbol{c}_{\bot}$.
\par Since the image of the diagonal embedding $d\colon H\hookrightarrow H^{N}$ consists precisely of the constant maps $\boldsymbol{c}_{h}$ above, $A$ contains the image of $d$, so $a\colon H\hookrightarrow A$ belongs to $)$.
\par Let $\mathcal{F}\subseteq A$ be the subset of $A$ consisting of those $f\in A$ which satisfy $f(n)=1$ for almost all $n$, i.e., there exists an $n_{0}\in N$ such that $f(n)=1$ for all $n\prec n_{0}$. This $\mathcal{F}$ is a filter of $A$.
\par Note that,
$$(\boldsymbol{x}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{c}_{h})(n)=\begin{cases}h,\text{ if }n\succ h\\
1,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}$$which belongs to $\mathcal{F}$ for all $h\neq\bot$ and is equal to $\boldsymbol{c}_{\bot}$ for $h=\bot$.
\par Moreover
$$\boldsymbol{x}\cong_{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{c}_{h}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{x})(n)=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if }n\succ h\text{ or }n=h\\
n,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases};$$\par$$\boldsymbol{c}_{h}\cong_{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{c}_{h}\vee\boldsymbol{x})(n)=\begin{cases}n,\text{ if }n\succ h\text{ or }n=h\\
h,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases};$$\par$$\boldsymbol{x}\cong_{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{c}_{h}\wedge\boldsymbol{x})(n)=\begin{cases}h,\text{ if }n\succ h\text{ or }n=h\\
n,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.$$\par\par It follows that in the quotient algebra $A/\mathcal{F}$ we have $[\boldsymbol{x}]\prec[\boldsymbol{c}_{n}]$ for all $h\neq\bot$ and $[\boldsymbol{x}]\succ[\boldsymbol{c}_{\bot}]=[\bot]$. Also, $[\boldsymbol{c}_{h}]\succ[\boldsymbol{c}_{h+1}]$ for all $h\neq\bot$ as $\boldsymbol{c}_{h}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{c}_{h+1}\notin\mathcal{F}$, and $[\boldsymbol{x}]\neq[\bot]$ since $\boldsymbol{x}\Rightarrow\bot\notin\mathcal{F}$. Also note that from the computations shown above, it follows that $A/\mathcal{F}$ does not contain any other elements because $A$ is generated by the $\boldsymbol{c}_{h}$ and by $\boldsymbol{x}$.
\par We therefore conclude that $A/\mathcal{F}$ is isomorphic to $H^{\prime}$. Moreover, this isomorphism is compatible with the homomorphisms $d\colon H\hookrightarrow A$ and $\widehat{\pi}\colon H\to H^{\prime}$, so that indeed $H^{\prime}$ belongs to $)$.
\end{example}\par\par\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{section}{Acknowledgments}
