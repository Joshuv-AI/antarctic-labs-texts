Quinazolinone derivatives as suitable mitigator for corrosion inhibition of carbon steel in hydrochloric acid environment
Authors: Ahlam A Al-Surmi, Munira S Shaaban, Ahmed El-Mekabaty, Abd El-Aziz S Fouda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe corrosion inhibition behavior of carbon steel (CS) in 1.0 M HCl in the presence of 4-(2-(4-oxo-3-phenyl-3,4-dihydroquinazolin-2-yl) vinyl) phenyl benzenesulfonate (4-OPB) and4-(2-(3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-4-oxo-3,4-dihydroquinazolin-2-yl)vinyl)phenyl benzenesulfonate (4-HPB) was investigated through chemical evaluation via weight loss (WL) measurements, as well as electrochemical techniques, including AC impedance (EIS), and potentiodynamic polarization (PDP). The inhibition efficiency (IE) increased progressively with higher concentrations of the tested compounds and with temperature elevation, reaching a maximum of 93.2% and 90.1% at 21 × 10⁻3 M of 4-HPB and 4-OPB, respectively from WL tests at 25C. In the other hand, it reached 96.5%, 95.9% for 4-HPB and 4-OPB at 45oCand the same concentration, respectively. The findings indicated that these compounds adhere on the CS surface and create a protective film whose formation conforms to the Langmuir adsorption isotherm, consistent with chemisorption, as supported by the relatively high adsorption energy values (ΔG°ads < – 46 kJ mol⁻1), rise in % inhibition by raising the temperature and the lowering in activation energy (E*) in presence of inhibitors than in its absence. These chemical compounds function as mixed-type inhibitors, according to PDP studies. Surface characterization of the inhibited CS using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) demonstrated significant improvement in surface morphology. Introduction

Carbon steel (CS) is widely recognized as a key material in numerous manufacturing applications, attributed to its outstanding mechanical characteristics and low production cost 1 It is extensively employed in industrial installations, including heat exchangers, piping systems, and storage vessels yet it remains highly vulnerable to corrosion 2 This is especially evident in the case of hydrochloric acid, which is widely utilized for eliminating surface deposits and rust 3 Mineral acids, particularly hydrochloric acid, are frequently employed in industrial processes such as acid cleaning and acid pickling4 These acids generate an environment that is highly aggressive toward carbon steel, promoting severe corrosion5, significantly influencing both their economic value and operational safety. Corrosion is an electrochemical phenomenon characterized by simultaneous anodic and cathodic reactions occurring on the metal surface by the gradual reversion of metals to their native state 6. It encompasses any modification in the physical properties of metals arising from physicochemical interactions with their surrounding environment, ultimately leading to the degradation of the metal’s inherent characteristics7 Various approaches have been developed to mitigate metal corrosion, including optimizing alloy composition and smelting techniques, applying organic or inorganic protective coatings, and incorporating corrosion inhibitors. Among these methods, the use of corrosion inhibitors remains the most cost-effective and widely adopted strategy 8. The development of corrosion inhibitors is closely associated with the relative ease of incorporating novel functional groups into their molecular structures, thereby enabling the synthesis of new compounds with enhanced inhibition efficiencies in consequence contributing to the prolonged service life of metallic surfaces9,10 . Heterocyclic organic compounds bearing multiple bonds and heteroatoms, including sulfur or oxygen, demonstrate remarkable corrosion-inhibiting properties. The effectiveness of these inhibitors is attributed to their high polarity and low electronegativity, as the lone electron pairs associated with these heteroatoms readily interact with the metal surface, enabling robust adsorption 11,12. This process effectively blocks active sites and establishes a thin protective film, thereby minimizing the diffusion of corrosive agents into the underlying metal 13,14. The adsorption process is affected by the characteristics and condition of the metal’s functional properties, the nature of the corrosive environment, and the molecular configuration of the inhibitors 15,16. A study by Toghan et al. 17 investigated the corrosion inhibition properties of a synthetic compound, (3aR,7S,7aS)-4,5,6,7,8,8-hexachloro-1,3-dioxo-1,3,3a,4,7,7a-hexahydro-2H-4,7-methanoisoindol-2-yl) benzenesulfonamide, or MBSI. The research focused on how effectively MBSI prevents the corrosion of C-steel in a 0.5 M sulfuric acid solution at temperatures ranging from 25 to 55 °C. The findings showed that a greater inhibitory efficiency results from raising the MBSI concentration. The maximum effectiveness was observed at a concentration of 37 × 10⁻⁶ M and a temperature of 25 °C, where the corrosion of the steel alloy was reduced by 94%.By using electrochemical techniques, weight loss, and a plasma atomic emission spectrometer , the synthetic material’s ability to inhibit corrosion 4-(((1E)-4) 4-(4-dicyano-3)4-(((E)-4,4-dicyano-3-(2-oxo-2H-chromen-3-yl)buta-1,3-dien-1-yl)-4-hydroxyphenyl) diazenyl) benzene sulfonic acid (Dye 2) and 4-(((E)-4,4-dicyano-3-(2-oxo-2H-chromen-3-yl)-4-hydroxyphenyl)diazenyl) benzene sulfonic acid (Dye 3) were examined for corrosion inhibition against steel in acidic environments 18. The potentiodynamic polarization approach at 7.5 × 10–4 M concentration for inhibitors of dyes two and three produced the maximum corrosion efficiency of 96.5% and 96.1%, respectively. This study examined two novel quinazoline derivatives as corrosion inhibitors for mild steel in 1 M hydrochloric acid: 3-dimethyl-12-(4-nitrophenyl)-3,4,5,12-tetrahydrobenzo [4, 5] imidazo [2,1-b] quinazolin-1(2H)-one (Q-NO2) and 12-(4-methoxyphenyl) (Q-OMe) 19. When the inhibitors’ performance was assessed by potentiodynamic polarization, the highest inhibition efficiencies of 94.7% for QNO2 and 96.7% for Q-OMe were attained. Two synthetic carboxamide derivatives, 1-benzyl-5-methyl-N-(o-tolyl)-1H-pyrazole-3-carboxamide (BPCP) and 1-benzyl-N-cyclohexyl-5-methyl-1H-pyrazole-3-carboxamide (BPCC), were studied as corrosion inhibitors for CS in 1 M HCl 20. The highest inhibitory efficiency of BPCP and BPCC were determined to be 88.2% and 84.5%, respectively, at a concentration of 300 ppm at room temperature 298 K. The effectiveness of 1H-pyrazole–3,5-dicarboxylic acid 5-benzyl ester 3-phenyl ester (PCBPE) as a preventer for deterioration of IS 513 Gr. D steel in 1 M HCl medium was evaluated21. The shielding efficiency of PCBPE has been enhanced at elevated concentrations reached 90.02% at 100 ppm, 302 K, and it has been diminished at amplified temperatures and reached 82.08% at the same concentration and 332 K. Pyrazoles have achieved in recent eras huge prominence because of their effectiveness in obstructing metallic corrosion. Therefore, 5-Acetyl-2H-pyrazole-3-carboxylic acid ethyl ester (APCE) have intended to study as an anticorrosive agent for the metal exposed in 1 M HCl at distinct temperatures22, electrochemical measurements confirmed the adsorption of APCE on mild steel with 90.75% corrosion inhibition efficiency at 100 ppm. This study explores the ability of 4–nitro–1–octyl–5–phenyl–1H–1,2,3–triazole (NOPT) to inhibit corrosion. The anticorrosive properties of NOPT on mild steel samples immersed in 1.0 M HCl were investigated by electrochemical and chemical methods23. The inhibitor reaches its maximum inhibition efficiency of 88.78% at 150 ppm when the temperature is 302 K.

Quinazolinone-based compounds are known for their broad biological and pharmaceutical activities, their application in corrosion science remains relatively limited compared to traditional heterocyclic inhibitors such as triazoles, imidazoles, benzimidazoles, and quinoline derivatives. Currently, there is no research available on the use of these derivatives as anti-corrosion agents. The use of these Quinazolinone derivatives as corrosion inhibitors based on: i) Quinazolinone derivatives have rarely been explored in acidic corrosion environments ii) the specific structural modifications introduced in the synthesized derivatives provide enhanced adsorption or inhibition efficiency iii) theoretical calculations (DFT/MD) reveal new mechanistic insights into their adsorption behavior and iv) the compounds offer advantages such as improved environmental compatibility, stronger surface binding, or superior performance compared to previously studied inhibitors.

In this study, the corrosion inhibition efficiency of 4-(2-(4-oxo-3-phenyl-3,4-dihydroquinazolin-2-yl) vinyl) phenyl benzenesulfonate (4-OPB) and 4-(2-(3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-4-oxo-3,4-dihydroquinazolin-2-yl)vinyl) phenyl benzenesulfonate (4-HPB) was evaluated through a series of experimental techniques, including weight loss measurements, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), potentiodynamic polarization (PDP), and surface characterization using FT-IR, SEM, and EDX analyses The inhibition mechanism of these two derivatives was investigated and contrasted using a simulation method based on density functional theory (DFT) computational chemistry. Additionally covered are the relationships between the molecule’s computed electronic characteristics and its inhibitory efficacy24. The Monte Carlo molecular simulation approach was used to calculate and assess the adsorption energy that accompanied the molecule’s interaction with the steel surface 25.

Materials and solutions

Materials

The carbon steel (CS) employed within the scope of this research consisted of: C 0.2%, Mn 0.35%, P 0.024%, Si 0.003%, with Fe as the balance. Concentrated hydrochloric acid (BDH grade) was diluted with bidistilled water to create the corrosive medium, 1.0 M HCl.

Weight loss (WL) method

The corrosion rate (kcorr) and inhibition efficiency (IE %) were determined in accordance with the static WL method specified in ASTM G1-03 (reapproved 2017)27. Seven specimens, each measuring 2 × 2 × 0.2 cm, were sectioned and successively polished using sandpapers of varying grit sizes (400, 600, 800, and 1200). They were subsequently cleaned, dried, and rinsed with acetone to eliminate grease and other organic residues, after which they were immersed in 100 mL of 1.0 M HCl solution, with and without of varying concentrations of the inhibitors (4-OPB and 4-HPB). Each sample was fully submerged in a 250 mL beaker containing the prepared solution for 3 h within a temperature range of 25–45 °C. Prior to soaking, and after 30 min intervals, the specimens were removed, dried, and weighed before being re-immersed, with this cycle repeated until the completion of the experiment. WL was recorded at each interval triplets and plotted to ensure measurement repeatability. Electrochemical measurements

The corrosion behavior of CS in 1.0 M HCl solution, with and without the utilized inhibitors, was investigated using PDP, and EIS techniques. Measurements were conducted in a standard three-electrode electrochemical cell, consisting of saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode, a platinum counter electrode (CE), and CS was utilized as the working electrode (WE). The preparation process of WE involved attaching one side of a CS sheet (dimensions: 10 mm × 10 mm × 20 mm) to a copper wire to facilitate electrical connection. The copper wire, connected to the CS sheet, was then inserted into a glass tube and secured using epoxy resin, ensuring that only an exposed region of 1 cm2 of the CS was in contact with the testing medium. Prior to electrochemical measurements, the sample was pretreated as previously described, and the electrode potential was allowed to stabilize for 30 min to attain a steady state. Data acquisition and control were achieved via a Multichannel Potentiostat operated with Corrtest software. EIS measurements involved applying a 10 mV AC signal over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 10 mHz under the same temperature conditions. PDP experiments were performed by scanning the potential from − 250 to + 250 mV versus SCE, relative to the open-circuit potential, at a rate of 0.2 mV s⁻1, and maintained at 25 °C 28. By utilizing a low scan rate, pitting initiation can occur at lower noble potentials 29. Surface inspection

The carbon steel specimens were prepared following the same procedure as in the WL method and subsequently submerged in the test solution, both with and without of 4-OPB and 4-HPB at a concentration of 21 × 10⁻⁶ M, for a duration of one day at 298 K. Upon completion of the immersion period, the specimens were carefully removed, dried using a hair dryer, and coated with a gold–palladium layer using an SPI Module sputter coater. Surface morphology was examined via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-6510LV, Japan) operated at 30 kV, while elemental composition was determined using an energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) unit operated at 20 kV, and EFM analyses were carried out using dual excitation frequencies of 2.0 Hz and 5.0 Hz to assess the surface roughness 30.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

The FTIR spectra were recorded for both the pure inhibitor and carbon steel specimens dipped for one day in 1.0 M HCl containing the maximum of focus employed utilizing an Alpha II Bruker ATR spectrophotometer.

Findings and discussion

WL approach

Impact of concentrations of PBs

WL measurements for CS corrosion were conducted in a blank solution of 1.0 M HCl and in media containing various concentrations of the investigated inhibitors, within the range of 1 × 10–6—21 × 10–6 M (Fig. 2) the findings demonstrated that, at 25°C, both WL and corrosion rate declined with increasing inhibitor concentration, as illustrated in the corresponding Table 2. This enhancement in IE % is attributed to the formation of a protective barrier by the inhibitor molecules on the metal surface, which restricts metal dissolution 33. The order of IE % of the inhibitors is: 4-HPB > 4-OPB at 21 × 10–6 M. The adsorption of BPs molecules onto the CS surface is responsible for the superior inhibitory performance. The massive molecular structure of BPs and the abundance of heteroatoms (N) and (O) support this. Moreover, the type of substituent group affixed to the pyrazolo ring accounts for the variation in corrosion inhibition effectiveness between HBP and OBP. Specifically, in contrast to OPB, the hydroxyl (OH) group on the HPB molecule boosts the electron density at the active sites, encouraging a greater contact with the CS surface, which lacks a substituent group 34.

Adsorption isotherms

The interaction between a metal surface and corrosion inhibitors can be more effectively interpreted through adsorption isotherm models. The inhibition of metallic corrosion by various organic compounds is generally ascribed to their adsorption onto the metal surface, a phenomenon typically confirmed by fitting experimental results to different adsorption isotherms, such as Langmuir, Flory–Huggins, Temkin, and Freundlich models41 (Fig. S1). The degree of surface coverage at varying concentrations of PBs was utilized to determine which isotherm most accurately depicts the adsorption mechanism. The experimental data best conformed to the Langmuir isotherm, demonstrated by a regression coefficient (R2) near unity (R2 > 0.999), which indicates good fittings. PDP technique

A study of PDP provides important information about the anodic and cathodic reaction kinetics. Based upon the resulting corrosion potential, it also aids in identifying the kind of inhibition action that corrosion inhibitors take. Numerous research articles report that corrosion inhibitors suppress both the anodic and cathodic electrochemical reactions on the metal surface, which consequently lowers the corrosion current (icorr). This observed decrease in icorr is indicative of the corrosion inhibition phenomenon 50. The inhibitors significantly influence the hydrogen evolution and anodic dissolution processes, indicating the interact with the metal that they function as mixed-type inhibitors (Fig. 9). Extrapolation of the polarization curves provided key electrochemical parameters, including (Ecorr, βa, βc, icorr and IE %) βa, βc and Ecorr are the anodic Tafel slope, cathodic Tafel slope, and corrosion potential, respectively, which are presented in Table 6. The icorr values were employed to calculate IE % through Eq. 3. The data in Table 6 demonstrate that the inclusion of inhibitors significantly reduces the corrosion current density, with a noticeable improvement in inhibition efficiency at elevated inhibitor concentrations. The introduction of 4-OPB to the 1 M HCl caused a significant positive shift in the corrosion potential with respect to the blank medium (Fig. 9). But on the other hand, by addition of 4-HPB to the corrosive solution caused a negative shift with respect to the blank (Fig. 9). The maximum shift observed was more than 85 mV, which is above 85 mV, meaning that 4-OPB act as anodic inhibitor and 4-HPB functioned as cathodic inhibitor51. The cathodic arms of all PDP curves exhibited parallel trends, showing that the incorporation of PBs did not amend the cathodic reaction mechanism 52. The PBs reduced current densities, with the effect increasing with concentration. This is illustrated by the mitigation of anodic and cathodic reactions at the carbon steel surface. Consequently, carbon steel dissolution and hydrogen reduction were significantly reduced at their respective reaction sites53. The nearly unchanged values of Both cathodic (βc) and anodic (βa) Tafel slopes indicate that the fundamental corrosion mechanism remains unaltered 54, and the inhibition occurs primarily through surface adsorption at active sites. Therotical calculations

Quantum chemical parameters

Further information on the phenomenon of inhibition can be obtained through the use of quantum chemical calculations. EHOMO, ELUMO, ΔE, η, σ, χ, and ΔN are estimated quantum chemical variables that are linked to an inhibitor’s corrosion inhibition efficacy. The optimization of the investigated inhibitor compounds allows for the observation of these parameters 67. Predicting metal-inhibitor interactions is aided by these characteristics. Every quantum parameter for both protonated and neutral 4-OPB, 4-HPB molecules in the gas phase were computed in the current investigation. Table 11 provides a summary of the findings. Figures 17 & 18 display the neutral and protonated 4-OPB and 4-HPB optimized geometries. The EHOMO and ELUMO of their frontier molecular orbital energies enable the realization of the inhibitor reactivity. The EHOMO energy value generally indicates the inhibitor’s ability to donate electrons. While the ELUMO values show the metal’s ability to take electrons from the inhibitor, the high EHOMO value suggests that the inhibitor has a high affinity to offer electrons to appropriate acceptor molecules. In general, a lower ELUMO value indicates that the inhibitor may undoubtedly back donate electrons from the metal. Good inhibitory performance of the molecule is indicated by a decreased value of ΔE (ΔE = ELUMO − EHOMO). Other significant parameters that provide information on the stability and reactivity of molecules are the global softness (σ) and global hardness (η). Generally, an inhibitor with a greater hardness (η) value and a bigger energy gap (ΔE) exhibits lower chemical reactivity and is consequently less effective at inhibition. Conversely, an inhibitor characterized by a greater softness (σ) value and a smaller energy gap ΔE is associated with higher chemical reactivity and a greater level of inhibition efficiency 68. A soft molecule has a small energy gap, while a hard molecule has a large one. Therefore, higher corrosion inhibition efficiency is suggested by the high (η) and low (σ) values. in molecules of neutral inhibitors (Fig. 17). Table 11 demonstrates that ΔE value of HPB (0.814 eV) is higher than OPB (0.965 eV), both of which have a strong correlation with the experimental findings. Additionally, the values of global softness (σ) and the proportion of transported electrons (ΔN) support the experimental findings, and global hardness (η) for HPB > OPB and HPB < OPB, respectively. The EHOMO value of OPB (− 5.275 eV) is slightly higher than that of HPB (-5.160 eV), which is somewhat at odds with the experimental findings. Computational analysis of the protonated inhibitor molecules (Fig. 18) confirms that HPB is the more effective inhibitor. The calculated ΔEa value for HPB (0.809 eV) is slightly greater than that of OPB (0.793 eV). Additionally, σ, ΔN, and η, follow the same relationships observed for the neutral molecules: σ and ΔN are higher for HPB than OPB, while is lower for HPB than OPB. The EHOMO of HPB (-5.521 eV) is also higher than that of OPB (-5.510 eV). All these theoretical findings correlate well with the experimental results, specifically the inhibition efficiencies, where HPB (93.2%) performs better than OPB (90.1%), suggesting HPB establishes stronger bonds with the Fe metal surface. The protonation of the inhibitor molecules (Fig. 18) causes a notable decrease in their EHOMO values, dropping to -5.521 eV (for OPB) and -5.510 eV (for HPB) from the neutral values (-5.275 eV and -5.160 eV). This reduction means the electron-donating ability via the EHOMO orbital is diminished. As a result, the dominant bonding interaction between the inhibitor and the metal surface becomes back-donation (electron sharing from the metal to the inhibitor). Crucially, HPB is a more effective inhibitor than OPB, according to the quantum chemical parameters ΔE, ΔN, η, and σ. The strong correlation between these calculated parameters and the experimental data confirms the higher corrosion inhibition efficiency of HPB.

Mechanism of inhibition

The adsorption of inhibitors onto a metallic surface is influenced by the characteristics of the inhibitor’s active sites, the electron density, and the charge of the metal surface. The combination of electrochemical experiments and quantum chemical calculations provided the data necessary to propose the following probable adsorption and inhibition mechanisms for the inhibitor studied. When placed in an acidic solution, the inhibitor molecules become easily protonated. This protonated form is subsequently adsorbed onto the CS surface through electrostatic interactions. Although the CS surface is anticipated to be positively charged, the simultaneous presence of an excess of negatively charged Cl- ions adjacent to this positively charged CS surface is also instrumental in promoting the inhibitor’s adsorption onto the metal (see Fig. 2169–71). Several active sites on the inhibitor molecules are capable of donating electrons to the positively charged carbon steel (CS) surface. The sites that provide these electrons include: The lone pairs of electrons associated with heteroatoms (specifically S, N, and O atoms). The pi electrons located in unsaturated (double and triple) bonds and the conjugated systems of the aromatic moieties 72. This electron donation results in a chemical bond that creates a stable protective layer on the metallic surface. Furthermore, retro-donation (or back donation) can take place, involving electron transfer from the steel to the inhibitor molecules. Conclusions

4-OPB and 4-HPB are efficient, eco-friendly corrosion inhibitors for carbon steel in 1 M HCl, effective even at very low doses. Their inhibition efficiency improves with increased concentration but diminishes with rising temperature. The adsorption of both compounds adheres to the Langmuir adsorption isotherm model. Potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) experiments suggest a chemisorption mechanism for their action. The presence of a protective inhibitor film on the carbon steel surface was confirmed via SEM and AFM analysis. Theoretical calculations (using DFT and Monte Carlo Simulations) show strong agreement with the experimental results from both weight loss (WL) and electrochemical measurements.
