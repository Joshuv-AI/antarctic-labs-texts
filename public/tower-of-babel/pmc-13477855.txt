The Mediating Role of Prenatal Attachment Between Pregnancy Distress and Prenatal Coping Among Chinese Women in the Third Trimester: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Ya Ma, Yuying Yan, Lidan Xu, Yuehong Lv, Zhongbo Cai, Lanfeng Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the status of pregnancy distress, prenatal attachment, and prenatal coping among women in the third trimester, and to analyze the mediating role of prenatal attachment in the relationship between pregnancy distress and prenatal coping.MethodsThis study employed a cross-sectional design. A convenience sampling method was utilized to select 351 women in the third trimester who visited the obstetric outpatient clinic of a tertiary Grade A obstetrics and gynecology hospital in mainland China from January 2024 to January 2025 as participants. The age of the 351 participants was 32.02 years (SD = 3.76), and 77.5% of the participants were under 35 years old. A questionnaire survey was conducted using a general demographic questionnaire, the Tilburg Pregnancy Distress Scale, the Prenatal Attachment Inventory, and the Revised Prenatal Coping Inventory. The scales demonstrated good internal consistency reliability in this sample, with Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.891 to 0.904. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software was utilized for descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and mediation effect testing using the Bootstrap method (with 5,000 resamples). Multiple linear regression models were used to determine factors influencing prenatal coping among women in the third trimester.ResultsThe mean scores for pregnancy distress, prenatal attachment, and prenatal coping among the 351 women in the third trimester were (28.27 ± 7.07), (50.59 ± 10.68), and (55.19 ± 14.12), respectively. Correlation analysis revealed that pregnancy distress was negatively correlated with prenatal attachment (r = −0.123, P < 0.05) and prenatal coping (r = −0.335, P < 0.05), whereas prenatal attachment was positively correlated with prenatal coping (r = 0.603, P < 0.05). Multiple linear regression analysis identified residence, educational level, history of threatened miscarriage hospitalization, pregnancy distress, and prenatal attachment as significant predictors of prenatal coping in women in the third trimester (standardized β = −3.348 to 3.518, all P < 0.05). The mediation effect test revealed that prenatal attachment plays a partial mediating role between pregnancy distress and prenatal coping, with the mediating effect accounting for 20.96% (95% CI: −0.270 to −0.075).ConclusionPrenatal attachment plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between pregnancy distress and prenatal coping among women in the third trimester. These findings suggest that interventions aimed at enhancing prenatal attachment can help mitigate the adverse effects of pregnancy distress. Therefore, healthcare providers should routinely assess the level of pregnancy distress and implement strategies to strengthen prenatal attachment, thereby improving prenatal coping strategies and maternal–infant mental health.

Introduction

The third trimester (≥ 28 weeks) is a period marked by peak functional burden on women’s organs and prominent physical changes.1 During this period, significant physiological changes are superimposed on multiple stressors involving family, occupation, and social support, which easily trigger psychological crises in pregnant women.2 If these stressors are not addressed promptly and effectively, they not only affect maternal and fetal physical health but also lead to psychological issues such as prenatal anxiety and postpartum depression.3,4 Globally, pregnancy distress is a significant concern, with international studies reporting a prevalence ranging from 41.7% to 51.0%.5 In Mainland China, emerging evidence indicates that pregnant women face a substantial psychological burden. Domestic studies have reported levels of pregnancy distress and negative affect that are significantly higher than those observed in Western populations.6 This discrepancy suggests that pregnant women in China encounter unique socio-cultural challenges, such as the family structure, high expectations for fetal health, and the pressure of balancing career with motherhood which may exacerbate psychological distress. Consequently, there is an urgent need to understand the mechanisms underlying this distress to develop effective, culturally sensitive support systems.

Attachment theory provides an important perspective for understanding psychological adaptation mechanisms during this period. The theory posits that attachment is a core mechanism for individuals to seek security when facing threats or uncertainty, functioning to regulate emotions and cope with stress.7 Beyond general emotion regulation, attachment theory specifically suggests that an individual’s internal working model guides their cognitive appraisal of stress, thereby influencing the specific coping strategies they employ.8 For instance, secure attachment has been linked to more active and problem-focused coping, while insecure attachment is often associated with avoidance or emotion-focused strategies.9 Coping strategies as a key component of stress and coping theory, refer to the cognitive and behavioral modes employed by individuals to avoid harm when facing stressful events.10 As an important factor influencing individual mental health, different women in the third trimester adopt various coping strategies when facing pregnancy pressure. Previous studies have demonstrated that positive coping strategies have a direct effect on mitigating pregnancy-related stress;11 whereas negative coping strategies not only exacerbate physical and psychological distress and aggravate the adverse effects of pregnancy pressure but also undermine trust in healthcare providers among women in the third trimester, adversely affecting birth outcomes, including delivery mode, labor progress, and neonatal status.12 Furthermore, within the specific context of pregnancy, the theory extends to the development of prenatal attachment—the emotional bond between mother and fetus—which may further shape these coping responses13. Thus, attachment theory indicates that the coping strategies an individual adopts are not random but depend on their sense of attachment security.14

Pregnancy distress is often a key factor hindering the adaptive process of preparing for motherhood and managing the challenges of pregnancy. Pregnancy distress refers to the manifestation of depression, anxiety, and stress related to fetal health, physical changes, and childbirth,15 with a prevalence ranging from 41.7% to 51.0% among pregnant women.5 Existing studies indicate that high levels of pregnancy distress not only impair the mental health of pregnant women16 but may also distract their attention and weaken their emotional investment in the fetus, thereby hindering the formation and development of prenatal attachment.17

Specifically, prenatal attachment serves as a direct application of attachment theory during the perinatal period. It refers to the intimate emotional bond established between a pregnant woman and the fetus.18 This attachment typically begins in early pregnancy, develops gradually with the gestational process, peaks in third trimester, and transforms into a lasting mother-infant attachment after delivery, serving as a key factor for mothers to successfully adapt to pregnancy.19 Relevant studies have shown that high-quality prenatal attachment can provide pregnant women with a sense of security, making them more psychologically resilient when facing pregnancy pressure and inclined to adopt positive strategies for coping; conversely, impaired attachment may lead to a decline in coping ability.20

Although existing literature has separately confirmed the impact of pregnancy distress on coping strategies21,22 and the relationship between pregnancy distress and prenatal attachment,17 current research lacks an integrated theoretical perspective connecting the three. Furthermore, most existing studies have been conducted in Western cultural contexts; there is a paucity of research exploring these specific psychological mechanisms within the unique socio-cultural environment of Mainland China. The distinct family dynamics and high social expectations placed on pregnant women in China may influence how distress impacts attachment and coping. Therefore, this study was conducted to bridge this gap by examining the interplay of these factors in a local context. Particularly from the perspective of attachment theory, the internal mechanism of how pregnancy distress hinders women’s coping strategies by disrupting prenatal attachment—a core emotional bond—remains unclear. This gap limits the in-depth understanding of the psychological adaptation process in third trimester and hinders the formulation of precise clinical intervention strategies.

To address these issues and ensure the findings are representative of the population most in need of care, this study was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University. Zhejiang Province was selected as the research site because it represents a rapidly developing region in Eastern China with a distinct demographic profile.23 As an economic hub, Zhejiang presents a unique convergence of modern lifestyle stressors and traditional family expectations, which may intensify pregnancy-related distress. The hospital was chosen specifically as it is a tertiary Grade A medical institution with a high volume of obstetric patients, ensuring a diverse sample that captures a wide spectrum of pregnancy experiences. Based on this, this study uses attachment theory as a framework to hypothesize that prenatal attachment is a key mediating variable connecting pregnancy distress and prenatal coping, and explores the mechanisms among the three variables in women in the third trimester. The findings of this study not only expand the research scope of perinatal psychology but also provide important implications for clinical practice: suggesting that healthcare professionals should improve pregnant women’s coping strategies through the dual pathways of alleviating pregnancy distress and enhancing maternal-fetal emotional bonding, thereby providing a scientific reference for improving maternal and infant health levels.

Methods

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional design and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.24

Participants

This study was conducted in Zhejiang Province, mainland China, between January 2024 and January 2025. Participants were recruited from the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Women with a singleton pregnancy at 32–36 weeks of gestation, aged ≥ 20 years; (2) informed consent and voluntary participation in the survey. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Women with a history of mental illness, intellectual or language impairments, or severe physical diseases were excluded as these conditions act as confounding factors that significantly bias psychological self-reporting;25 (2) fetuses with malformations were excluded to avoid the influence of acute pathological stress; (3) recent experience of major traumatic events were excluded to ensure measured distress was pregnancy-related; (4) concurrent participation in other studies to prevent data contamination from external interventions. For structural equation modeling, the recommended sample size is 5 to 10 times the number of observed variables, with a minimum of 200 participants.26 This study included 14 observed variables, thus, the minimum required sample size was 200. Considering an estimated 20% rate of invalid questionnaires, we aimed to recruit at least 250 participants. Ultimately, 351 participants were included in this study, which exceeds the minimum requirement and ensures sufficient statistical power for the analysis.

Measures

General Information Questionnaire

A general demographic questionnaire was developed by the researchers based on a comprehensive review of literature regarding sociodemographic and obstetric factors influencing perinatal mental health,27–29 combined with expert consultation. It was used to collect sociodemographic and obstetric-related data of women in the third trimester. The questionnaire included the following: (1) Demographic characteristics: age, place of residence, educational level, occupation, per capita monthly household income; (2) social support and family status: living status with partner, self-rated family relationships; (3) obstetric clinical characteristics: gravidity, parity, pregnancy complications.

Tilburg Pregnancy Distress Scale (TPDS)

TPDS was developed by Pop30 and later translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Liu.31 This self-report scale is primarily used to assess pregnancy-related distress. TPDS contains 16 items across two dimensions: negative affect (11 items) and partner involvement (5 items). Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scoring method, with scores ranging from 0 to 3 for “never” to “always,” respectively. Items 3, 5–7, 9–14, and 16 are reverse-scored. The total scale score ranges from 0 to 48, with a higher total score indicating greater pregnancy-related distress and poorer partner involvement. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.891, indicating good reliability.

Revised Prenatal Coping Inventory (NuPCI)

NuPCI was revised by Hamilton32 by supplementing PCI; “Nu” is an abbreviation for “New,” derived from the Greek letter ν, emphasizing its status as an updated version. It was later translated into Chinese by Li.33 This scale is primarily used to assess the coping strategies used by pregnant women when facing stress and comprises 30 items. The scale divides prenatal coping into four dimensions: Planning-Preparation-Psychological (7 items), Planning-Preparation-Knowledge (7 items), Avoidance (12 items), and Spirituality-Positive Coping (4 items). The items are rated on a 5-point Likert scoring method, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 for “never” to “always,” and a total score ranging from 0 to 120. Higher scores indicate greater use of the corresponding coping strategies. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.899, indicating good reliability.

Prenatal Attachment Inventory (PAI)

PAI scale was developed by Muller,34 and later translated and validated in Chinese by Qi.35 It primarily assesses the mother’s psychological activities regarding feelings and emotional bonding with the fetus during pregnancy. The questionnaire contains three dimensions: Interaction (10 items), Thoughts (8 items), and Enjoyment (3 items), totaling 21 items. Each item uses a 4-point Likert scoring method, with scores ranging from 1 to 4 for “never” to “always.” The total scale score ranges from 21 to 84, with higher scores indicating a higher level of prenatal attachment in pregnant women. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.904, indicating good reliability.

Data Collection

Data were collected between January 2024 and January 2025 using a self-reported, anonymous online survey platform (Wenjuanxing; https://www.wjx.cn/). The questionnaire link was shared with patients attending the obstetric outpatient clinic during pre-check triage. Before participation, the purpose and significance of the study were comprehensively explained to the patients. The questionnaire explicitly stated that the collected information would be kept strictly confidential. Participation was voluntary, and questionnaires were submitted anonymously after informed consent was obtained. To ensure data quality, each Internet Protocol (IP) address was permitted to access the questionnaire only once to prevent duplicate responses. Additionally, the questionnaire platform was configured to allow submission only after all items had been completed to ensure data completeness. Based on a pilot study, the average time required to complete the full survey was approximately 10–15 minutes, indicating a reasonable burden that minimized the risk of respondent fatigue. During the data cleaning process, two patients refused to participate, seven questionnaires were deemed invalid due to consistent consecutive answer choices, and another five questionnaires were excluded because the completion time was less than 120 s. The 120s threshold was determined based on a pilot study, which indicated that this duration was insufficient for participants to read and thoughtfully answer the questions, thus indicating random or invalid responding. Overall, out of 365 women in the third trimester, 351 submitted valid questionnaires, resulting in a valid response rate of 96.16%. This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical Analysis

Microsoft Excel was utilized to establish the database, and the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 25.0) was used for statistical data analysis. Descriptive statistical analysis was first conducted for all variables, and measurement data were confirmed to follow a normal distribution. General data on women in the third trimester, along with the scores for prenatal attachment, pregnancy distress, and prenatal coping, were comprehensively described using frequencies, percentages, and mean ± standard deviation (SD). To investigate the differences in prenatal coping among women in the third trimester based on general data groupings, two independent samples t-tests and analysis of variance were implemented. Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis was utilized to analyze the associations among prenatal attachment, pregnancy distress, and prenatal coping in women in the third trimester and to further investigate the relationships between prenatal attachment, pregnancy distress, and prenatal coping. A multiple linear regression model was further performed to identify factors associated with prenatal coping. Mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro in SPSS software to examine the mediating effect of pregnancy distress. The level of statistical significance in this study was set at α = 0.05, with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Mediating Effect of Prenatal Attachment Between Pregnancy Distress and Prenatal Coping

To examine the proposed mechanism and test the hypothesis that prenatal attachment mediates the relationship between pregnancy distress and prenatal coping, a mediation analysis was conducted. Model 4 of the PROCESS macro in SPSS was utilized for the mediation analysis. The results revealed that the independent variable had a significant effect on the dependent variable (β = −0.668, P < 0.001) in the first step of the test, indicating that the total effect was established. In the second step, the independent variable exhibited a significant impact on the mediating variable (β = −0.185, P = 0.0214). In the third step, the independent variable had a significant effect on the dependent variable (β = −0.529, P < 0.001). Prenatal attachment exerted a significant effect on prenatal coping (β = 0.754, P < 0.001), thereby establishing the mediating role of prenatal attachment in this model (Table 4). Finally, to identify specific predictors of prenatal coping in women in the third trimester of pregnancy, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted. The prenatal coping inventory score was set as the dependent variable, while variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were entered as independent variables. The results revealed that place of residence, educational level, history of hospitalization for fetus preservation, total pregnancy distress score, and total prenatal attachment score were significant predictors. Collectively, these variables explained 45.20% of the total variance in prenatal coping among women in the third trimester of pregnancy. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first empirical investigation in mainland China to specifically examine the mediating role of prenatal attachment in the relationship between pregnancy distress and prenatal coping among women in the third trimester. While previous studies have explored related factors in other cultural contexts or focused on general prenatal anxiety,37 there is a paucity of data systematically clarifying these particular mechanisms within the Chinese population. Using a mediating effect model, we found that pregnancy distress directly and negatively predicts prenatal coping and indirectly affects coping ability by reducing prenatal attachment, indicating that prenatal attachment plays a partial mediating role between the two. Specifically, higher levels of pregnancy distress in women in the third trimester were associated with lower prenatal coping ability and diminished prenatal attachment, which further weakened coping ability. The mediating effect of prenatal attachment in the relationship between pregnancy distress and prenatal coping accounted for 20.96%. This substantial effect size not only provides strong empirical support for the association model of the three but also aligns with and extends previous findings in the field, which have similarly highlighted the interplay between maternal emotional states and adaptive behaviors. These findings advance the theoretical understanding of the interplay between psychological well-being and perinatal health in the interdisciplinary field of positive psychology and maternal medicine. Clinically, they provide a scientific basis for clinical practice to block negative pathways by strengthening the mother-infant bond and implementing targeted psychological interventions.

First, this study confirmed that pregnancy distress directly and negatively predicts prenatal coping, consistent with the results of a previous study.38 To explain why this relationship exists, we draw upon Lazarus’s stress and coping theory.39 This theory posits that individuals’ cognitive appraisal of stressors is critical in determining coping strategies. Pregnancy distress faced in the third trimester acts as a significant stressor, depleting psychological resources. The third trimester represents a critical transition period in women’s physical and mental development, during which they face the physiological demands of impending childbirth and multiple psychological pressures related to role transition and family relationship reconstruction.40 Elevated pregnancy distress can overwhelm the psychological resources of pregnant women, impairing cognitive appraisal abilities. Consequently, women find it difficult to mobilize effective problem-solving strategies, leading to negative coping or a general decline in coping ability. These findings indicate that pregnancy distress is not just a concurrent symptom but an independent risk factor that actively weakens a woman’s capacity to cope.

Second, this study identified a partial mediating effect of prenatal attachment between pregnancy distress and prenatal coping, expanding the understanding of their interrelationship. This finding suggests that the impact of distress on coping is twofold: it occurs both directly and indirectly by disrupting the mother-fetal bond. To explain this mechanism, we apply attachment theory,41 when a mother is preoccupied with negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, her psychological energy is directed toward self-protection, reducing attention and emotional investment in the fetus and leading to lower prenatal attachment. Prenatal attachment serves as a key internal psychological resource, supports positive coping behaviors. Low prenatal attachment reflects limited deep maternal-fetal interaction and emotional connection, which may hinder a secure transition to the maternal role and limit the activation of protective motivation;41 consequently, the mother is more inclined to adopt avoidant or negative coping styles when facing pregnancy challenges. Conversely, strong prenatal attachment enhances maternal identity and confidence, promoting proactive coping styles. This theoretical perspective explains why distress leads to poor coping not only through resource depletion but also through the erosion of the specific emotional bond with the fetus.

It is noteworthy that prenatal attachment mediated approximately 21% of the total effect, indicating a partial mediation. The remaining 79% suggests that pregnancy distress exerts a strong direct influence on prenatal coping, independent of attachment levels. Furthermore, this substantial unexplained variance implies that other unmeasured factors—such as social support, personality traits, or marital satisfaction—may also play crucial roles in how pregnant women cope with stress. This finding reinforces the complexity of the coping mechanism and suggests that while enhancing attachment is vital, clinical interventions should also address other direct pathways to effectively mitigate the impact of distress.

Based on the established pathways linking distress, attachment, and coping, our findings suggest that healthcare providers should adopt integrated, theory-driven approaches. While existing interventions like mindfulness-based programs42 and CBT effectively target distress,43 our results underscore the necessity of explicitly including components that strengthen prenatal attachment. We recommend that clinical interventions move beyond solely reducing distress to actively fostering the maternal-fetal bond. For instance, integrating guided mother-fetal bonding activities into standard distress-reduction treatments could activate the protective motivation mediated by attachment. Furthermore, clinical staff should identify high-risk groups—particularly those with a history of hospitalization for fetal preservation or lower educational backgrounds—and provide personalized support. By simultaneously alleviating distress and bolstering attachment, healthcare providers can more effectively break the vicious cycle of high distress-low attachment-low coping, offering a new pathway to improve perinatal psychological adaptation.

This study also demonstrated that a history of hospitalization for fetal preservation is a risk factor for lower prenatal coping in women during the third trimester of pregnancy, while educational level and urban residence serve as positive predictors. These findings are in line with previous research indicating that clinical risk factors and socioeconomic status significantly influence psychological adaptation.44–46 Specifically, the negative impact of a history of fetal preservation hospitalization can be explained by the physical and psychological burden associated with uncertain outcomes. Treatment for fetal preservation often involves uncertainty regarding pregnancy outcomes, prolonged bed rest, and restricted activity. These challenges can diminish a woman’s sense of control and self-efficacy, leading to an avoidant coping style when facing childbirth pressure.

In contrast, the positive association between higher education and prenatal coping aligns with the understanding that socioeconomic factors play a crucial role in psychological resilience. Women with lower educational attainment often face greater socioeconomic burdens, such as financial instability and limited access to resources, which deplete the reserves needed for effective coping.45 Higher education is typically associated with better economic security and health literacy, enabling women to adopt more positive strategies. Similarly, urban residence likely reflects better access to medical resources and social support systems,46 providing a buffer that facilitates knowledge acquisition and psychological support, thereby enhancing coping abilities. These findings suggest that clinical nursing staff should identify high-risk groups, particularly women with a history of hospitalization for fetal preservation, and formulate personalized support strategies based on educational level and place of residence to optimize the parental coping level of women during the third trimester of pregnancy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, a convenience sampling method was used, and participants were recruited from a single tertiary Grade A hospital in mainland China, which might have introduced sample selection bias and limited the generalizability of the results. Future studies should expand the sample scope to multi-center, cross-regional studies to enhance external validity. Second, the data were self-reported. Although the scales used demonstrated good reliability and validity, subjective recall bias could not be excluded. Future studies could consider combining clinical interviews and physiological indicators to achieve a comprehensive assessment and improve data objectivity. Third, the cross-sectional study design limited causal inference. Although the mediation model verified the association among pregnancy distress, prenatal attachment, and prenatal coping, it failed to establish the temporal sequence or dynamic changes among these variables. Future research should adopt a longitudinal tracking design to more deeply investigate the causal mechanisms among the variables. Furthermore, this study focused solely on pregnancy distress and prenatal attachment and prenatal coping, without accounting for other potential variables such as social support and family function, which may affect the explanatory power of the model. Future studies could consider additional factors to develop a more robust prediction model.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that pregnancy distress is negatively associated with prenatal attachment and prenatal coping, although prenatal attachment is positively associated with prenatal coping. Mediation effect analysis using the Bootstrap method confirmed that prenatal attachment mediates the relationship between pregnancy distress and prenatal coping. These findings reveal that pregnancy distress reduces prenatal coping directly and indirectly by impairing prenatal attachment. Notably, the remaining variance highlights the substantial direct impact of distress and the potential influence of other unmeasured factors, suggesting a complex mechanism that warrants a multifaceted clinical approach. These findings have significant implications for clinical practice and hospital management. Medical staff should integrate psychological health monitoring into routine perinatal care by adopting comprehensive intervention strategies. Specifically, this involves implementing routine screening for pregnancy distress during prenatal check-ups to identify high-risk women and provide timely referral or counseling to mitigate the direct negative impact on coping.

Furthermore, to strengthen the protective role of prenatal attachment, providers should actively facilitate maternal-fetal bonding through specific, evidence-based techniques. These fetal bonding interventions may include encouraging mothers to engage in daily talks with the fetus, practice guided imagery focusing on the baby, or maintain a pregnancy journal. Moreover, hospital administrators and policymakers are encouraged to institutionalize these bonding-enhancement protocols as a standard component of maternity services to ensure sustainable support for expectant mothers. By systematically combining distress alleviation with these practical attachment-enhancement activities, healthcare providers can more effectively improving the prenatal coping ability among women in the third trimester. Regarding future research, studies should build upon these findings by conducting longitudinal studies to verify the causal relationships between these variables over time. Additionally, intervention studies are needed to evaluate the long-term efficacy of the proposed bonding-enhancement strategies on maternal and infant outcomes. Finally, investigating these associations in more diverse cultural and demographic populations would enhance the generalizability of the results.
