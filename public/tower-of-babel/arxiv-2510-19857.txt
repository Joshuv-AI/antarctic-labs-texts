Simple Analytic Estimate of Black Hole Shadow Size in an Expanding Universe
Debarshi Mukherjee

Abstract
The apparent shadow of a black hole provides one of the most direct probes of strong-field general relativity. While the shadow size in asymptotically flat spacetimes is well understood, the influence of cosmic expansion on its apparent angular diameter remains less explored. In this work, we present a simple analytic framework to estimate the shadow size of a non-rotating black hole embedded in an expanding universe. By combining the local Schwarzschild geometry with large-scale cosmological dynamics through the McVittie and Kottler metrics, we derive a compact relation between the shadow angular size and the angular diameter distance D A ​ ( z ) D_{A}(z) . This approach captures the essential dependence on cosmological parameters such as the Hubble constant H 0 H_{0} and the cosmological constant Λ \Lambda , while remaining analytically tractable. We further perform numerical estimates to quantify the redshift dependence of the apparent shadow size, showing that the effect of cosmic expansion is negligible for nearby sources but becomes relevant for high-redshift black holes. Our results demonstrate a clear conceptual connection between strong-gravity optics and cosmological expansion, providing a pedagogically transparent and physically motivated extension of black hole shadow theory to a cosmological context.



I
Introduction

The study of black hole shadows has gained significant attention following the first direct imaging of the supermassive black holes M87* and Sgr A* by the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) collaboration
(
EHT Collaboration 2019a
;
EHT Collaboration 2019b
;
EHT Collaboration 2022
)
. The apparent silhouette or ”shadow” of a black hole represents the boundary separating photons captured by the event horizon from those that escape to a distant observer
(
Bardeen 1973
;
Chandrasekhar 1983
)
. The size and shape of this shadow depend on both the spacetime geometry near the black hole and the properties of the surrounding environment, making it an important probe of general relativity in the strong-field regime.

In most theoretical analyses, the black hole shadow is computed assuming an asymptotically flat spacetime,neglecting any large-scale cosmological expansion. This approximation is valid for astrophysical black holes whose Schwarzschild radius is many orders of magnitude smaller than the Hubble radius. However, as observational precision improves, it becomes meaningful to ask:
how does the expansion of the universe modify the apparent size of a black hole’s shadow?
While the effect is extremely small for nearby sources like Sgr A* or M87*, it could become non-negligible for black holes at cosmological distances or in the early universe
(
Perlick et al. 2018
;
Tsupko & Bisnovatyi-Kogan 2020
)
.

The interplay between black hole physics and cosmological expansion has been explored through various toy models and exact solutions, such as the McVittie metric, which describes a point mass embedded in an expanding Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) background
(
McVittie 1933
;
Nolan 1998
)
. Analytical approaches to this problem are challenging due to the nontrivial coupling between local and global spacetime curvature. However, approximate models allow for tractable insights. One may consider how light trajectories from the photon sphere are modified when the observer is comoving with the cosmological expansion, introducing corrections proportional to the Hubble parameter
H
.

The present work aims to derive a simple, analytic estimate of the black-hole shadow size in an expanding universe, using perturbative arguments that connect the static Schwarzschild shadow with the dynamic, expanding background. Rather than performing a full general-relativistic ray-tracing simulation, we pursue a leading-order correction analysis where the shadow’s angular size
\alpha
is expressed as a function of redshift
z
and the Hubble rate
H(z)
. This allows us to understand qualitatively how cosmic expansion and geometry conspire to affect the observed shadow.

This approach not only highlights the subtle connection between local gravitational lensing and the cosmological metric but also provides a pedagogical framework accessible to undergraduate and graduate students interested in gravitational physics and cosmology. Despite the simplicity of the model, the resulting expression captures the essential dependence of the apparent angular diameter on both the black-hole mass and the large-scale expansion rate. Furthermore, by applying the model to realistic black holes such as Sgr A*, M87*, and hypothetical stellar-mass black holes at cosmological distances, we show that cosmological corrections become significant only for extremely distant objects.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we summarize the basic formalism for computing the shadow radius in Schwarzschild and cosmological spacetimes. Section
III
presents our analytic estimates and their numerical evaluation for typical astrophysical cases. Finally, Section
IV
discusses the implications and limitations of our results in the context of observational cosmology and black-hole physics.

II
Theoretical Framework

The apparent angular size of a black hole shadow as seen by a distant observer depends on the geometry of null geodesics near the photon sphere and the background spacetime in which the black hole is embedded. In the simplest case of a non-rotating, asymptotically flat Schwarzschild black hole, the problem reduces to the computation of the critical impact parameter corresponding to unstable circular photon orbits. To extend this idea to an expanding universe, we introduce a perturbative treatment that accounts for the effect of cosmic expansion on the observed angular diameter.

II.1
Shadow size in Schwarzschild spacetime

The Schwarzschild metric in natural units (
G=c=1
) is given by

ds^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}),

(1)

where
M
is the mass of the black hole. For photon trajectories (
ds^{2}=0
), the conserved quantities are the energy
E
and the angular momentum
L
, leading to an effective potential of the form

V_{\text{eff}}(r)=\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right).

(2)

The unstable circular photon orbit, known as the photon sphere, occurs at the extremum of
V_{\text{eff}}(r)
, satisfying
dV_{\text{eff}}/dr=0
, which yields

r_{\text{ph}}=3M.

(3)

The critical impact parameter
b_{c}
separating captured from scattered photons is given by

b_{c}=\frac{L}{E}=\frac{r_{\text{ph}}}{\sqrt{1-\dfrac{2M}{r_{\text{ph}}}}}=3\sqrt{3}M.

(4)

Hence, for a static observer at a coordinate distance
r_{o}\gg M
, the angular radius of the shadow is

\sin\alpha=\frac{b_{c}}{r_{o}}\sqrt{1-\frac{2M}{r_{o}}}\approx\frac{3\sqrt{3}M}{r_{o}},

(5)

where the approximation holds for large
r_{o}
. This defines the classical shadow size in an asymptotically flat spacetime
(
Bardeen 1973
;
Chandrasekhar 1983
)
.

II.2
Embedding in an expanding background

To incorporate cosmological expansion, one can consider the black hole as being embedded in an isotropic and homogeneous Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) background. A simplified approach is to use the McVittie metric
(
McVittie 1933
)
, which generalizes the Schwarzschild solution by including a time-dependent scale factor
a(t)
:

ds^{2}=-\left(\frac{1-\dfrac{2M}{a(t)r}}{1+\dfrac{2M}{a(t)r}}\right)^{2}dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)\left(1+\frac{2M}{a(t)r}\right)^{4}(dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}),

(6)

where
d\Omega^{2}=d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}
. For small
M/(ar)
, this metric smoothly transitions to the standard FLRW form. In the limit where the observer is comoving with the expansion, the proper angular-diameter distance
D_{A}
between the observer and the black hole satisfies the standard cosmological relation

D_{A}(z)=\frac{1}{1+z}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{c\,dz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})},

(7)

where
z
is the redshift and
H(z)
is the Hubble parameter. The apparent angular size of any object of proper radius
R
is then

\alpha(z)=\arcsin\left(\frac{R}{D_{A}(z)}\right).

(8)

For the case of a black hole shadow, we substitute
R=b_{c}=3\sqrt{3}GM/c^{2}
, giving

\alpha_{\text{shadow}}(z)=\arcsin\left[\frac{3\sqrt{3}\,GM/c^{2}}{D_{A}(z)}\right].

(9)

Equation (
9
) provides a remarkably simple connection between the local relativistic geometry of the photon sphere and the global cosmological expansion. The only cosmological dependence enters through
D_{A}(z)
, which is determined by the background cosmology. In a flat
\Lambda
CDM universe with parameters
(H_{0},\Omega_{m},\Omega_{\Lambda})
, the angular-diameter distance is

D_{A}(z)=\frac{c}{(1+z)H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m}(1+z^{\prime})^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}}}.

(10)

II.3
Small-
z
analytic approximation

For nearby black holes (
z\ll 1
), the integral in Eq. (
10
) can be expanded as

D_{A}(z)\approx\frac{cz}{H_{0}}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}(1+q_{0})z+\mathcal{O}(z^{2})\right],

(11)

where
q_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{m}-\Omega_{\Lambda}
is the deceleration parameter. Substituting this into Eq. (
9
) yields

\alpha_{\text{shadow}}(z)\approx\frac{3\sqrt{3}GMH_{0}}{c^{3}z}\left[1+\frac{1}{2}(1+q_{0})z+\cdots\right].

(12)

This expression shows that the observed angular diameter of the shadow decreases approximately as
1/z
with distance, modulated by small cosmological corrections. Although the overall effect of expansion is negligible for nearby sources such as Sgr A* (
z\sim 10^{-6}
), it becomes conceptually interesting for black holes at cosmological distances (
z\gtrsim 1
), where the angular-diameter distance saturates and even decreases due to the geometry of the Universe
(
Perlick et al. 2018
;
Tsupko & Bisnovatyi-Kogan 2020
)
.

II.4
Interpretation

The dependence of
\alpha_{\text{shadow}}
on cosmological parameters demonstrates how the large-scale structure of spacetime can influence local gravitational observables. While the shadow radius in units of
GM/c^{2}
remains invariant, the apparent angular size projected on the sky encodes information about the background expansion. The simplicity of Eq. (
9
) makes it an instructive tool for teaching the connection between general relativity and cosmology: the black hole shadow, often considered a purely local feature, subtly reflects the cosmological metric through the angular-diameter distance.

III
Numerical Implementation and Results

To complement the analytic estimates of Section
II
, we perform a direct numerical evaluation of the black-hole shadow’s apparent angular size
\alpha_{\text{shadow}}(z)
using the expressions derived above. This serves two purposes: (i) to validate the analytic small-
z
approximation, and (ii) to illustrate the cosmological dependence of the shadow size for sources located at moderate to high redshift.

III.1
Numerical setup

All computations were performed using
Python 3.10
with the
NumPy
and
SciPy
libraries for numerical integration, and
Matplotlib
for visualization. The key relation to be evaluated is Eq. (
9
), which requires the angular-diameter distance
D_{A}(z)
given by Eq. (
10
). The numerical integration of the distance–redshift relation is carried out as

D_{A}(z)=\frac{c}{(1+z)H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m}(1+z^{\prime})^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}+\Omega_{r}(1+z^{\prime})^{4}}},

(13)

where a small radiation term
\Omega_{r}=9.2\times 10^{-5}
is included for completeness, although it has a negligible effect for
z<5
.

We adopt the cosmological parameters consistent with the
Planck 2018
results
(
Planck Collaboration 2020
)
:

H_{0}=67.4~\text{km s}^{-1}\text{Mpc}^{-1},\quad\Omega_{m}=0.315,\quad\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.685.

(14)

For the black-hole mass, we consider three representative cases:

1.

The supermassive black hole Sgr A* at the Galactic center, with
M=4.3\times 10^{6}~M_{\odot}
.

2.

The M87* black hole, with
M=6.5\times 10^{9}~M_{\odot}
.

3.

A hypothetical intermediate-mass black hole of
M=10^{5}~M_{\odot}
placed at cosmological distances (
z\sim 1
–
5
).

The angular size is computed from Eq. (
9
) in microarcseconds (
\mu
as):

\alpha_{\text{shadow}}(z)=\frac{206265\times 10^{6}}{\text{rad}}\,\arcsin\!\left[\frac{3\sqrt{3}\,GM/c^{2}}{D_{A}(z)}\right].

(15)

For all practical cases,
\alpha\ll 1
rad, so the small-angle approximation
\sin\alpha\approx\alpha
is used in the numerical evaluation.

III.2
Verification of analytic approximation

At very low redshift (
z\lesssim 10^{-3}
), Eq. (
12
) accurately reproduces the numerical results from Eq. (
15
). For example, the Sgr A* shadow subtends an angular radius of approximately

\alpha_{\text{SgrA*}}\approx 26.4~\mu\text{as},

(16)

in excellent agreement with the EHT measurement
(
EHT Collaboration 2022
)
. The difference between the static Schwarzschild estimate and the full cosmological expression is smaller than
10^{-10}
, confirming that cosmological corrections are negligible for local sources. In contrast, for hypothetical black holes located at cosmological distances (
z\sim 1
–
5
), the shadow angular diameter decreases sharply with increasing redshift. The variation of
\alpha(z)
is driven primarily by the angular-diameter distance
D_{A}(z)
, which initially increases with
z
, reaches a maximum near
z\approx 1.6
in a
\Lambda
CDM universe, and then decreases for higher redshift
(
Hogg 1999
)
. Consequently, the shadow size exhibits a non-monotonic trend: it first shrinks, then slightly grows beyond
z\approx 2
.

shows the computed shadow angular size
\alpha_{\text{shadow}}(z)
for black holes of different masses as a function of redshift, assuming a flat
\Lambda

R>D_{A}(z)
, which corresponds to unphysical cases where the small-angle approximation breaks down.

\alpha_{\text{shadow}}(z)
for black holes of different masses in a
\Lambda
CDM universe with
H_{0}=67.4~\text{km s}^{-1}\text{Mpc}^{-1}
,
\Omega_{m}=0.315
, and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.685
. The shaded region marks the redshift range where
D_{A}(z)
reaches its maximum value, producing a turning point in
\alpha(z)
.

The results show that:

•

For nearby supermassive black holes (
z<10^{-3}
), cosmological expansion has a completely negligible effect on the shadow size.

•

For intermediate-mass black holes (
M\sim 10^{5}~M_{\odot}
) at cosmological distances, the apparent angular diameter can drop below
10^{-3}~\mu\text{as}
, rendering such shadows undetectable even for future high-resolution interferometers.

•

The non-monotonic dependence of
D_{A}(z)
implies that a black hole of fixed mass would appear slightly larger beyond the angular-diameter distance maximum (
z\gtrsim 1.6
).

III.4
Comparison with alternative cosmologies

To further explore the role of cosmological expansion, we also computed
\alpha(z)
under two limiting cosmologies:

1.

A matter-dominated Einstein–de Sitter (EdS) universe, with
\Omega_{m}=1
and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0
.

2.

A de Sitter universe dominated by vacuum energy, with
\Omega_{m}=0
and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=1
.

The EdS model yields smaller
D_{A}(z)
values at a given redshift, resulting in slightly larger apparent shadow sizes compared to
\Lambda
CDM. Conversely, the de Sitter model predicts a faster increase of
D_{A}(z)
, leading to smaller apparent shadows at all redshifts. These qualitative differences, though tiny in absolute terms, demonstrate the sensitivity of
\alpha_{\text{shadow}}
to the background expansion rate and curvature
(
Tsupko & Bisnovatyi-Kogan 2020
;
Perlick et al. 2018
)
.

III.5
Numerical stability and uncertainty

Since the integral in Eq. (
13
) is smooth and well-behaved for all
z>0
, numerical uncertainties are minimal. The integration was verified with adaptive quadrature methods, achieving fractional precision
\lesssim 10^{-8}
. For all plots, the error bars are smaller than the line thickness. The dominant source of physical uncertainty arises from the mass measurement of the black hole itself, not from cosmological modeling.

Overall, these numerical experiments confirm that while the expansion of the universe does influence the apparent shadow size at very large redshifts, the effect is completely negligible for the astrophysical black holes currently observed. Nonetheless, the analytic relations developed here serve as a valuable conceptual bridge between strong-field general relativity and cosmological geometry.

IV
Discussion and Conclusion

The analysis presented in this work provides a simplified yet physically insightful understanding of how cosmic expansion influences the apparent size of black hole shadows. The numerical results confirm that, while the Schwarzschild geometry dominates the near-horizon behavior, the contribution from cosmological expansion becomes increasingly relevant for distant observers and at late cosmic times. This aligns with the general relativistic intuition that local physics is unaffected by large-scale expansion, yet the optical appearance of distant compact objects may still encode cosmological information
(
Faraoni 2015
)
.

Our results show that the apparent angular diameter of the shadow depends weakly on the Hubble parameter
H_{0}
for nearby sources, but begins to deviate measurably for redshifts
z\gtrsim 1
. This deviation arises from the modification of the photon trajectories in a dynamic background metric such as the McVittie or Kottler spacetime
(
McVittie 1933
;
Kottler 1918
)
. The angular size
\theta_{\text{sh}}
can therefore be treated as a sensitive probe of cosmological parameters in principle, although practical detection of such effects would require unprecedented angular resolution.

It is worth emphasizing that the inclusion of cosmic expansion effectively rescales the observer’s angular diameter distance
D_{A}(z)
, leading to the approximate relation

\theta_{\text{sh}}(z)\simeq\frac{3\sqrt{3}GM}{c^{2}D_{A}(z)},

(17)

which is consistent with the purely Schwarzschild result in the static limit
H_{0}\to 0

(
Bardeen 1973
;
Perlick 2021
)
. The corrections obtained from our analysis thus interpolate between local strong-gravity effects and the large-scale structure of the Universe.

From a phenomenological standpoint, the impact of cosmic expansion on shadow size remains subtle compared to other astrophysical sources of uncertainty, such as accretion disk emission, scattering, and spin effects
(
Falcke et al. 2000
;
EHT Collaboration 2019a
)
. Nonetheless, our analytic framework offers a compact and tractable method to quantify these cosmological influences, which could become relevant for future very long baseline interferometry (VLBI) observations of supermassive black holes at higher redshifts.

An interesting avenue for future research lies in extending this approach to more general spacetimes, such as rotating black holes embedded in expanding universes (the Kerr–de Sitter case). Moreover, including the effects of inhomogeneous expansion or dark energy models beyond
\Lambda
CDM could reveal additional dependencies of the shadow profile on cosmological dynamics . Such extensions would bridge the gap between observational cosmology and black hole imaging, offering a new theoretical channel through which the geometry of the Universe can leave its imprint on observable strong-gravity phenomena.

In conclusion, the analytic and numerical results obtained here highlight the interplay between cosmological expansion and local gravitational lensing near black holes. While the effect on the shadow size is small for currently observed systems, the theoretical implications underscore the deep connection between general relativity and cosmology. The methodology developed in this study provides a foundation for more sophisticated future work aimed at constraining cosmological parameters using black hole shadow measurements.
