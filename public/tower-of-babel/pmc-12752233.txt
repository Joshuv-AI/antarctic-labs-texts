Therapeutic effects and potential mechanisms of focused ultrasound in neuropathic pain: a narrative review of preclinical studies
Authors: Mao-Yu Chen, Pin-Yuan Chen, Chen-Nen Chang, Ti-Hsuan Chien, Jiun-Lin Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuropathic pain is a complex condition that can be challenging to manage, despite the availability of a variety of pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic treatment approaches. This narrative review aimed to synthesize preclinical evidence on the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of focused ultrasound (FUS) in neuropathic pain models.MethodsWe conducted a narrative literature search using PubMed to identify preclinical studies investigating the use of FUS in the context of neuropathic pain. Studies were retrieved from PubMed using keyword combinations “focused ultrasound” and “neuropathic pain,” with no restrictions on country of origin. Studies published in English from inception to December 2024 were considered. Eligible studies were limited to those involving animal models or in vitro cell models relevant to neuropathic pain. Clinical studies, review articles, editorials, and studies unrelated to neuropathic mechanisms were excluded.ResultsA total of 28 preclinical studies were identified, all employing either animal or cell-based models of neuropathic pain. Across various nerve injury models, both high-intensity (HIFU) and low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) were associated with behavioral improvements indicative of pain reduction, partial restoration of nerve function, and modulation of inflammatory cytokine profiles. Although the precise mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated, current evidence suggests that FUS may exert its effects through thermal, mechanical, and neuromodulatory pathways, including modulation of inhibitory neurotransmission, suppression of neuroinflammation, and regulation of ionic homeostasis.ConclusionsPreclinical studies indicate that FUS may alleviate neuropathic pain via thermal, mechanical, and neuromodulatory mechanisms. However, clinical translation remains uncertain, highlighting the need for further investigation in human models.

Background

Neuropathic pain is a complex condition that arises from dysfunction of the somatosensory nervous system [1–4]. The pathophysiology of neuropathic pain is believed to be due to damage or pathological changes to nerve fibers, which distort nerve function at the site of the injury and in connected nervous system areas [1–4]. These alterations in nerve structure and function typically lead to pain that manifests spontaneously and is disproportionately amplified in response to both noxious (painful) and innocuous (non-painful) stimuli [1, 3–5].

The prevalence of neuropathic pain has been reported to range from 3 to 17% [3]. The incidence of neuropathic pain varies significantly depending on the underlying condition. For instance, post-herpetic neuralgia occurs at a rate of 3.9 to 42.0 cases per 100,000 person-years, trigeminal neuralgia at 12.6 to 28.9 cases per 100,000 person-years, painful diabetic neuropathy at 15.3 to 72.3 cases per 100,000 person-years, and glossopharyngeal neuralgia at 0.2 to 0.4 cases per 100,000 person-years [3]. Estimating the incidence and prevalence of neuropathic pain globally presents significant challenges, primarily due to inconsistencies in defining the condition [6]. These discrepancies in definition can complicate comparisons and analyses across different studies.

The primary focus of the management of neuropathic pain is symptom relief [1, 4]. In cases where the underlying pathological cause is identifiable, targeted treatment can alleviate the pain [1, 4]. Recommended pharmacotherapy typically involves oral anticonvulsants or antidepressants; however, topical agents, nerve blocks, injections, and neuromodulation devices are also utilized. Despite these multiple options, it is estimated that more than half of patients do not achieve adequate pain control [1, 4].

There is growing interest in alternative treatment strategies for neuropathic pain, including neurostimulation and neuromodulation approaches [7–10]. Established techniques such as spinal cord stimulation (SCS), dorsal root ganglion (DRG) stimulation, and peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) have been successfully utilized to alleviate neuropathic pain in selected patients. More recently, transcranial focused ultrasound (FUS) has emerged as a novel, noninvasive neuromodulation technique capable of modulating pain signaling pathways projecting to the brain [11, 12]. FUS may offer a therapeutic option for patients who do not achieve adequate relief with recommended pharmacologic treatments.

While Seol et al. [13] primarily focused on low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) in chronic pain models, the present review provides a broader perspective by encompassing both high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) and LIFU in neuropathic pain of diverse etiologies. We further emphasize experimental outcomes, mechanisms of action, and the challenges that need to be addressed for clinical translation.

Methods

Search approach

Relevant literature was identified through a descriptive PubMed search covering publications up to December 2024. Only two primary keywords—“focused ultrasound” and “neuropathic pain”—were used to capture representative preclinical and in vitro studies. addressing therapeutic mechanisms. Clinical studies, review articles, editorials, and studies unrelated to neuropathic mechanisms were excluded from the review.

Results and discussion

The targeted PubMed search initially retrieved 54 records, of which 28 met the relevance criteria after excluding review and clinical papers and focusing on preclinical evidence. These studies, which investigated the effects and mechanisms of FUS for the relief of neuropathic pain, are discussed below by category.

Neuropathic pain is a chronic disorder characterized by abnormal sensory processing and limited response to conventional pharmacologic or neuromodulation therapies [1, 8]. These limitations have driven growing interest in noninvasive alternatives such as FUS, which aims to modulate pain pathways without surgical intervention or permanent tissue damage.

Preclinical studies on FUS treatment

FUS is an alternative, noninvasive approach to treating neuropathic pain. However, the use of both HIFU and LIFU for treating neuropathic pain is currently in the early stages of development. It is primarily investigated in clinical feasibility studies and preclinical experiments to demonstrate its safety and efficacy [2, 5, 9, 11, 12].

Several animal experiments have been developed to examine FUS as a potential treatment for neuropathic pain [14–16]. Overall, studies have demonstrated significant improvements in mechanical and thermal nociceptive thresholds, along with a reduction in allodynia, as evidenced by behavioral tests conducted across various species, including rodents and pigs. HIFU has been primarily used for precise thermal ablation and is considered a potential approach for neuropathic pain relief. However, no validated preclinical studies have applied ablative HIFU in neuropathic pain models. Existing evidence is limited to large-animal feasibility studies, such as Kaye et al. [17], who demonstrated that MRI-guided HIFU ablation of the medial branch nerve in swine produced effective focal necrosis without behavioral deficits. These studies confirm the technical feasibility and safety of ablative HIFU, providing a foundation for clinical translation, but lack behavioral or mechanistic validation.

Building upon the limitations of ablative HIFU, which may cause irreversible tissue damage, recent research has increasingly explored LIFU as a safe, non-ablative neuromodulatory approach for neuropathic pain. Prabhala et al. [15] developed a common peroneal nerve injury (CPNI) model using rats to examine the effect of external LIFU treatment. The neuropathic phenotype was identified by Von Frey Fibers (VFF) test. External LIFU was applied to the left L4, 5 DRG and resulted in significantly increased VFF thresholds. Histological analysis conducted 24 h after FUS treatment revealed no evidence of short-term structural damage in nerve sections. Hellman et al. [18] also used a CPNI rat model to examine the effects of modulatory LIFU on behavioral responses and sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs). The results showed significant increases in mechanical thresholds and reductions in SNAP latency after LIFU treatment, indicating a reduction in pain sensitivity. They also noted that the anatomical features of domestic swine more closely resemble those of humans than those of mice and rats, and thus they developed a swine CPNI model to examine the effect of LIFU [19]. Briefly, LIFU eliminated pain guarding in all animals for 1 month and reduced allodynia for 5 day post-procedure. Accordingly, LIFU may reduce neuropathic pain without the need for tissue ablation at the target.

Furthermore, several studies found that the sub-ablation temperature induced by pulsed-HIFU, also referred to as sub-ablative FUS, can also alleviate neuropathic pain without inducing irreversible tissue damage. Unlike conventional continuous HIFU that produces thermal coagulation, pulsed-HIFU operates at lower average intensities with intervals, allowing tissue cooling between pulses and preventing ablation. Walling et al. [20] investigated the effects of pulsed-HIFU in a rat chronic migraine model associated with mechanical allodynia and reported that this treatment significantly increased VFF thresholds 3 days after intervention. Notably, compared to pulsed-HIFU, ablative lesioning with HIFU showed no improvement. Similarly, Youn et al. [21] applied pulsed-HIFU on validated vincristine-induced neuropathy (VIN) rat model to examine the effect. VIN was induced and mechanical allodynia was confirmed by nociceptive testing. After treatment, VIN rats had significantly increased mechanical thresholds. Histological examination of the L5 DRG of the treated rats indicated that transient cellular edema resolving within 48 h, indicating that no irreversible adverse effects. Collectively, these findings indicate that pulsed or sub-ablative FUS may offer a reversible and safe neuromodulatory window between destructive ablation and low-intensity stimulation.

Biological mechanisms

Although the precise biological mechanisms underlying FUS-induced analgesia remain incompletely understood, accumulating evidence suggests that FUS may modulate nociceptive processing through several pathways operating at different spatial and temporal levels. HIFU primarily exerts its therapeutic effect through thermal ablation, which induces localized tissue coagulation and irreversible neural disruption. The rise in temperature within the focal zone can transiently or permanently block nerve conduction, depending on exposure parameters. Colucci et al. [22] investigated this phenomenon using isolated bullfrog sciatic nerves exposed to HIFU. They observed a temperature-dependent reduction in compound action potentials, which recovered completely, partially, or not at all according to the applied intensity and duration. These findings suggest that thermal elevation plays a dominant role in mediating the conduction block produced by HIFU. In the context of neuropathic pain, such focal denervation or conduction interruption may disrupt aberrant nociceptive transmission, thereby providing analgesia. However, the irreversible nature of this mechanism underscores the importance of precise targeting and energy control to avoid off-target injury.

On the other hand, LIFU has been explored as a non-ablative and reversible approach for modulating neuropathic pain. While its exact mechanism also remains to be elucidated, current evidence points to multifaceted effects involving peripheral and spinal pathways. For example, Bao et al. [23] demonstrated that LIFU applied to the L5 DRG suppressed aberrant neuronal firing across the pain transmission pathway, including the spinal dorsal horn, primary somatosensory cortex (S1), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). This electrophysiological evidence implies that peripheral ultrasound stimulation may propagate inhibitory effects through spinal and supraspinal networks.

At the molecular signaling level, Lin et al. [24] reported that DRG-targeted LIFU enhanced the GABAergic inhibitory signaling, suggesting that ultrasound stimulation may restore inhibitory neurotransmission disrupted in neuropathic conditions. In line with this interpretation, prior studies have observed increases in GAD65 after LIFU treatment, and GAD65/67 catalyzes the conversion of glutamate to GABA. Such changes would be expected to enhance inhibitory tone. (e.g., Chen et al. [25]; Wang et al. [26]). Hellman et al. further observed that LIFU reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and CNTF within the DRG, indicating that LIFU may attenuate local neuroinflammatory responses [27]. Beyond these peripheral effects, Hellman et al. also demonstrated that LIFU targeting the dorsal horn reduced neuroinflammatory activity, accompanied by decreased expression of glial activation markers and normalization of sensory-evoked responses. Similarly, Song et al. [28] reported that trans-spinal LIFU reduced microglial activation, evidenced by decreased Iba1 expression, further supporting the anti-inflammatory potential of ultrasound within spinal circuits. These findings indicate that ultrasound may exert central anti-inflammatory and modulatory effects within spinal pain-processing networks. In addition, Liao et al. [29] observed a marked reduction of KCC2 expression in the spinal dorsal horn with their PNI model, disrupting chloride extrusion and impairing GABAergic inhibition. Following LIFU treatment, this downregulation was reversed through suppression of the CaMKIV–pCREB signaling cascade, thereby restoring KCC2-mediated Cl⁻ homeostasis and alleviating neuropathic pain. This pathway suggests that LIFU may alleviate neuropathic pain by normalizing inhibitory neurotransmission through Ca2⁺-dependent regulation of ion transport and synaptic signaling within spinal circuits.

Previous studies have demonstrated that CaMKIV activity is regulated by intracellular calcium levels, suggesting that LIFU may influence neural excitability through Ca2⁺-dependent pathways. It is, therefore, plausible that ultrasound stimulation modulates ion flux by acting on membrane ion channels. Supporting this hypothesis, Tyler et al. [30] reported that low-intensity ultrasound induced transient depolarization in hippocampal neurons, which in turn activated voltage-gated sodium (Naᵥ) and calcium (Caᵥ) channels without measurable temperature elevation. The authors proposed that these effects are likely mediated by mechanical perturbation of the neuronal membrane, which influences the gating of mechanosensitive ion channels and consequently modulates neuronal excitability and synaptic activity.

Building upon the mechanical perturbation hypothesis, Kubanek et al. [31] further investigated how ultrasound interacts with mechanosensitive ion channels. Using C. elegans and Xenopus oocyte models, they demonstrated that ultrasound stimulation can deform the neuronal membrane and modulate the activity of mechanosensitive channels, such as K2P and NaV1.5, resulting in altered transmembrane currents. These findings support the concept that mechanical stress from LIFU may be converted into bioelectrical signals through localized membrane tension changes. Expanding on this framework, Ryu et al. [32] proposed that ultrasound-induced mechanical forces may also activate canonical mechanotransduction cascades. Specifically, integrin–FAK–Src signaling was shown to couple extracellular matrix deformation to intracellular pathways involving MAPK, Rho-GTPase/ROCK, and PI3K–Akt, ultimately influencing cytoskeletal remodeling and gene transcription. Collectively, these studies suggest that FUS may engage membrane-level mechanosensitive ion channels, providing a potential mechanistic framework for its neuromodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects.

Taken together, current evidence indicates that FUS influences neuropathic pain through multi-level processes: HIFU achieves analgesia via thermal ablation and conduction block, whereas LIFU and pulsed-HIFU appear to modulate nociception reversibly across DRG, spinal, and supraspinal circuits, engaging GABAergic inhibition, restoration of chloride homeostasis (KCC2), and attenuation of microglial-mediated neuroinflammation (Fig. 1). Nevertheless, the proposed mechanisms are still largely hypothetical, and current studies vary widely in terminology, experimental parameters, and levels of validation. As a narrative review, our synthesis integrates recent preclinical advances but does not constitute a systematic appraisal of all studies. Against this backdrop, we outline key priorities to accelerate safe and effective clinical translation.

Future directions

Current studies on FUS for neuropathic pain are mostly short-term and preclinical, with marked variability in ultrasound parameters, target selection, and outcome measures. Despite encouraging findings, major gaps remain—most notably, the absence of standardized protocols, limited large-animal validation, and few mechanistic studies that differentiate thermal, mechanical, and neuromodulatory effects. Future work should clearly define three acoustic regimes: ablative HIFU causing irreversible thermal lesions, sub-ablative or pulsed-HIFU within a partially reversible thermal–mechanical window, and LIFU achieving non-ablative neuromodulation. Establishing these energy domains through systematic dose–response and safety mapping will be critical for reproducibility and clinical translation. To move beyond correlative observations, interventional validation is needed to confirm causal mechanisms linking FUS stimulation to molecular and cellular responses. Pharmacological or genetic perturbations, such as blockade of voltage- or mechano-sensitive ion channels, could help determine whether these pathways mediate the observed analgesic effects. Given that FUS acts across multiple levels of the nervous system, further study may consider integrating multi-scale assessments spanning the peripheral, spinal, and supraspinal networks. Combining electrophysiology, neuroimaging, and behavioral outcomes may clarify how peripheral ultrasound modulation propagates through ascending pain circuits.

Limitations

The main limitation of this review is its reliance on preclinical models, which may not fully replicate human neuropathic pain. While animal studies provide valuable mechanistic insights, they fall short in modeling the diverse etiologies, chronicity, and psychological components of pain in human populations. In addition, only the PubMed database was searched using two primary keywords. This narrow scope, together with the exclusion of grey literature and non-PubMed-indexed studies, may introduce selection bias and potentially omit relevant findings. Anatomical and physiological differences between humans and commonly used animal models present major barriers to clinical translation. For example, the DRG, a common FUS target, lies only 3–5 mm beneath the skin in rodents, but may be located up to 5–8 cm deep in adult humans. The included studies also exhibit methodological heterogeneity in FUS parameters and outcome measures, hindering direct comparison and meta-analytic synthesis. Moreover, most studies focused on short-term effects, leaving the long-term safety and efficacy of repeated FUS exposure uncertain. Finally, the mechanistic interpretations summarized here are based primarily on correlational data rather than interventional validation. Well-designed early phase clinical trials are needed to determine human safety profiles, confirm biological mechanisms, and evaluate efficacy in defined subtypes of neuropathic pain.

Conclusions

FUS is an emerging noninvasive approach for neuromodulation in neuropathic pain, supported by growing preclinical evidence. Across various models, both high- and low-intensity FUS have shown analgesic effects through mechanisms ranging from thermal ablation to reversible neuromodulation with minimal tissue injury. However, translation to humans remains limited due to anatomical differences, heterogeneous parameters, and lack of standardized protocols. Carefully controlled early clinical studies integrating mechanistic validation, dose optimization, and safety monitoring are essential to establish FUS as a clinically reliable therapy for neuropathic pain.
