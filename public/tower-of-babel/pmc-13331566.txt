Recent advances in the construction of humanized animal models and applications in translational medicine
Authors: Yanan Lyu, Yong‐Guang Yang, Zheng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past several decades, humanized mouse models have undergone significant refinement and become essential tools in biomedical research. Advances in genetic engineering, particularly the targeted knock‐in of human cytokines, have enabled robust development and function of human immune cells in these models. Recent breakthroughs—including the directed differentiation of human pluripotent stem cells into thymic epithelial cells and the efficient expansion of hematopoietic stem cells—have alleviated the constraint of scarce human tissue sources. Furthermore, the reconstruction of lymph node structures and successful engraftment of solid organ tissues have significantly improved the efficiency and physiological relevance of human immune system reconstitution. These models now provide a unique platform for in vivo studies in cancer immunotherapy, infectious diseases, regenerative medicine, and autoimmune disorders under near‐physiological conditions. Beyond rodents, substantial progress in humanized large animals, such as pigs, offers promising avenues for large‐scale immune system reconstruction and mass production of immunotherapeutic cells. This review presents a comprehensive overview of the development, optimization, and expanding applications of humanized animal models, highlighting their transformative role in advancing translational medicine.

INTRODUCTION

Reproducing human biology in vivo remains a central objective in translational biomedical research. However, substantial interspecies differences between humans and commonly used laboratory animals, particularly mice, limit the predictive accuracy of current models. At the immune level, mice differ from humans in immune cell subset composition, cytokine networks, and immune functions. 1 Moreover, murine major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules do not fully correspond to the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) system, frequently resulting in discordant responses to immunomodulators and vaccines. 1 Beyond immunity, marked differences in tissue architecture, cellular composition, metabolism, and gene expression further constrain the ability of murine models to recapitulate human‐specific development, disease phenotypes, and drug metabolism. 2 , 3 Although organoids partially address these limitations by reconstructing aspects of human organ structure and development, 4 , 5 they inherently lack a functional immune system, precluding the study of dynamic immune–tissue interactions. 6 Moreover, in vitro organoid systems are intrinsically limited in their ability to capture the complexity of physiological and pathological processes occurring in vivo. Humanized mouse models—established through reconstitution of a human immune system and, in advanced designs, co‐engraftment of human tissues—provide a critical platform to bridge this gap. By integrating human immune functionality with organ‐level context, these systems enhance translational fidelity, improve predictive power, and reduce conventional animal usage.

Over the past few decades, humanized mouse models have evolved from early immunodeficient strains such as nude 7 and SCID 8 mice to next‐generation platforms such as NSG, 9 NSG‐SGM3, 10 and MISTRG 11 mice. Genetically engineered to support robust human immune system reconstitution, these strains have significantly advanced our understanding of cancer immunotherapy, 12 infectious diseases, 13 and regenerative medicine. 14

However, limitations of murine models, such as small size, short lifespan, and interspecies physiological differences, restrict their use in modeling complex organ‐level interactions and scaling up for translational applications. To address these challenges, attention has increasingly shifted to humanizing larger animals, including rats 15 , 16 and pigs, 17 , 18 which share more anatomical and physiological similarities with humans. These models offer promising platforms for long‐term studies, organ‐specific modeling, and scalable cell and tissue therapies.

Moving beyond the mouse‐centric perspective of previous studies, this review delineates the evolution of humanized animal models from rodent systems to large‐animal platforms, integrating advances in immune system reconstitution, hematopoietic stem cell engineering, and organ humanization. Particular emphasis is placed on recent breakthroughs in large‐animal (pig) models, which have emerged as a critical bridge toward clinically relevant systems. This perspective provides a forward‐looking basis for rational model selection in translational research.

HUMANIZED MOUSE MODELS

Development history of humanized mice with a human immune system

Since the 1980s, the construction of humanized mouse models with a human immune system (HIS) has progressively evolved, beginning with the SCID‐hu mouse model, 19 followed by the humanized peripheral blood leukocyte (hu‐PBL) model, 20 the humanized‐SCID‐repopulating cell (hu‐SRC) model, 21 , 22 , 23 and the Neonatal‐HIS model. 24 Despite their widespread applications in biomedical research, these models still have critical limitations (Figure 1).

Evolution of humanized immune system animal models. The development of humanized immune system animal models have progressed through four key stages. (1) Early exploration stage: Early models such as SCID‐hu, hu‐PBL and hu‐SRC exhibited low human cell engraftment, limited multilineage differentiation, and short survival durations. (2) Improvement stage: The Neonatal‐HIS and BLT‐HIS models marked a significant advancement, enabling more robust reconstitution of T cells, B cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells, as well as the enhancement of lymphoid tissue architecture and stronger T cell‐mediated immunity. (3) Optimization stage: Characterized by the incorporation of human cytokines to support full immune cell reconstitution, HSC transplantation without pre‐conditioning, and lymph node reconstruction to improve humoral immune responses; (4) Humanized large animal models: Offering greater physiological relevance for translational applications by more closely mimicking human anatomy and immunobiology, thus providing unique advantages for preclinical research.

In 2004, our group demonstrated that intravenous administration of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and progenitor cells, combined with human thymic/liver tissue under the mouse renal capsule, enhanced peripheral T‐cell reconstitution and function. This approach resulted in systemic hematopoietic engraftment and multilineage immune reconstitution, overcoming the limitations of the SCID‐hu mice. 25 Two years later, this model was designated as the bone marrow–liver–thymus (BLT) humanized mouse model, which was comprehensively characterized in subsequent reports. 26 , 27 BLT mice exhibit reconstitution of peripheral human T cells, B cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells, along with improved lymphoid tissue architecture. Notably, this model supports robust human thymopoiesis, leading to highly functional T cell‐mediated immune responses. 28

A key challenge for humanized mice is whether the reconstituted human immune cells can develop and function properly. Cytokines play an essential role in hematopoiesis and immune homeostasis. However, many human cytokine receptors respond poorly to mouse cytokines, which limits the development, survival, and function of human immune cells. 29 This defect may arise from insufficient supply of human cytokines, lack or inefficiency of cross‐reactive mouse cytokines, and competition from mouse cells for resources and “niche” space.

To address this aspect, researchers have tested various methods for delivering or expressing human cytokines in vivo. As early as 1992, administering recombinant human cytokines, such as SCF, IL‐3, and GM‐CSF, was found to significantly improve the multilineage differentiation and maturation of human HSCs in SCID mice. 30 Chen et al. 31 used hydrodynamic tail vein injection of plasmids carrying human cytokine genes to temporarily enhance reconstitution of specific hematopoietic lineages in humanized mice. Huang et al. 32 applied adeno‐associated virus (AAV) to deliver human cytokine genes, improving immune reconstitution after HSC transplantation.

With progress in gene editing, more advanced strategies have emerged. Early approaches relied on random integration of human cytokine transgenes into the genomes of immunodeficient mice. For example, NSG‐SGM3 transgenic mice, which express human SCF, GM‐CSF, and IL‐3, better support human HSC maintenance and differentiation, particularly promoting myeloid cell maturation. 10 However, transgenic expression often leads to supraphysiological or ectopic cytokine levels, which can skew immune cell development.

To address this aspect, targeted gene knock‐in methods have been developed to replace mouse cytokine genes with their human counterparts under natural regulatory control, allowing human cells to gain a developmental and functional advantage. These knock‐in models are gaining increasing attention due to their higher accuracy and biological relevance, making them more promising for both research and clinical applications (Table 1).

Despite their usefulness, constitutive expression of human cytokines in immunodeficient mice can lead to adverse effects that limit their experimental value. For example, in MISTRG mice, functional human macrophages can phagocytize mouse red blood cells, resulting in progressive anemia. 11 Similarly, NSG‐SGM3 mice develop severe anemia and thrombocytopenia, as well as bone marrow dysplasia. Additionally, these mice exhibit elevated levels of human proinflammatory cytokines, indicating a persistent systemic inflammatory response. 38 These effects underscore the importance of achieving physiologically regulated cytokine expression to minimize off‐target effects and ensure model stability for long‐term studies.

To reduce competition for resources and niche space, researchers have used gene editing to limit endogenous mouse cells, thereby creating conditions that favor the reconstitution of human immune cells such as dendritic cells and neutrophils. For example, Flt3‐knockout BRGF mice show reduced numbers of mouse dendritic cells and increased levels of Flt3L, while the addition of exogenous human Flt3L selectively promotes the expansion of human dendritic cells. 39 As human Flt3L can also act on mouse Flt3, FL Tg/KO mice—carrying human FLT3L transgenes together with Flt3 knockout—were developed to eliminate this cross‐reactivity and further improve the niche for human dendritic cells. 40 In the case of neutrophils, humanized G‐CSF alone shows limited effects due to similar cross‐species interactions. Additional knockout of mouse Csf3r in mice markedly reduces mouse neutrophils, thereby freeing niche space for human neutrophil development. 37 Together, these strategies provide a foundation for building next‐generation humanized mouse models that more closely reflect human immune cell composition.

Recent advances in the construction of humanized mouse models

Implantation of human thymic epithelial cells differentiated from pluripotent stem cells

Although BLT mice exhibit a robust reconstitution of human immune function, their reliance on human fetal tissues limits broader use. Thymic epithelial cells (TECs), the dominant stromal population in the thymus, play a critical role in T‐cell lineage commitment and functional maturation. 41 , 42 Therefore, generating thymic epithelial progenitor cells (TEPs) from human pluripotent stem cells and transplanting them into immunodeficient mice presents a promising strategy to expand the utility of humanized mouse models in both basic research and translational applications.

In 2013, Parent et al. 43 and Sun et al. 44 demonstrated that human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) could be directed to differentiate into TEPs by mimicking thymus organogenesis in vitro. When transplanted into the renal capsule of athymic nude mice, these TEPs matured into functional TECs and promoted T‐cell development. Furthermore, Sun et al. 44 confirmed that these hESC‐derived TECs supported the development of human T cells when co‐engrafted with human HSCs in NOD‐SCID mice.

In recent years, human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have gained traction as a more ethically and practically viable source for generating TECs. Ramos et al. 45 established a protocol to generate TEPs from multiple iPSC lines. When transplanted into nude mice, these iPSC‐derived TEPs differentiated into functional TECs and promoted T cell development. Building on this, Zeleniak et al. 46 developed a strategy to construct functional human thymus organoids by co‐seeding hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) and iPSC‐derived TECs into decellularized murine thymic scaffolds. These organoids successfully supported the differentiation of HPCs into functional human T cells in vivo.

However, this technology still faces several key challenges. Differentiation efficiency remains low, and it is difficult to reliably generate functional cTEC and mTEC subsets. Current three‐dimensional culture systems are also not yet fully developed—for example, they lack adequate vascularization and multicellular co‐culture—thus limiting the functionality of the resulting organoids. In addition, issues such as the tumorigenic risk of residual undifferentiated cells, potential immunogenicity, and difficulties in large‐scale production remain largely unresolved.

Consequently, pluripotent stem cell‐derived TEC regeneration strategies are still mainly used for mechanistic studies, and their broader application in humanized model construction and regenerative medicine remains limited.

Differentiation and expansion of human hematopoietic stem cells

The limited availability of human HSCs remains a significant bottleneck in both biomedical research and clinical applications involving humanized mouse models. Two main approaches have been pursued to overcome this limitation: directed differentiation of pluripotent stem cells (ESCs or iPSCs) into HSCs and in vitro expansion of primary HSCs.

In 2008, Ledran et al. 47 showed that co‐culturing hESCs with stromal cells derived from the murine aorta‐gonad‐mesonephros (AGM) region or fetal liver enhanced spontaneous hematopoietic differentiation; this method yielded hESC‐derived cells that engrafted NSG mice with an engraftment rate of approximately 16%. More recently, Piau et al. 48 used embryoid bodies derived from hiPSCs to generate a population resembling early AGM‐region HSCs; upon transplantation into NSG mice, these cells achieved an engraftment rate of 13%. However, no strategy to date has successfully generated humanized mice with high levels of chimerism or robust functional immune reconstitution, highlighting the ongoing challenge of generating truly engraftable HSCs from pluripotent stem cells.

In vitro expansion of primary HSCs also presents challenges, including maintaining stemness and the high cost of cytokine‐rich culture conditions. In 2010, Himburg et al. 49 identified pleiotrophin as a potent factor for promoting HSC expansion and engraftment in SCID mice. Fares et al. 50 later showed that UM171, a small molecule, effectively expanded cord blood‐derived HSCs while preserving long‐term capacity in NSG mice. More recently, in 2023, Sakurai et al. 51 established a cytokine‐free culture system using chemical agonists and a caprolactam‐based polymer matrix; this system, combining PI3K activators, thrombopoietin receptor agonists, and UM171, enabled sustained HSC expansion and supported serial transplantation in NOG‐W41 mice.

In summary, although substantial progress has been made in the directed differentiation and ex vivo expansion of HSCs, the stable and efficient generation of human HSCs with both high engraftment potential and full functionality remains a critical bottleneck for translating humanized mouse models into broader clinical applications. Future efforts should integrate strategies such as stem cell engineering, niche simulation, and gene editing to standardize and scale HSC production, thereby laying the foundation for the widespread application of humanized models.

Optimization of lymph node formation in humanized mice

A key limitation of Il2rg‐deficient immunodeficient mouse models is impaired lymph node (LN) development, which severely compromises T‐cell‐dependent antibody responses and antigen‐specific T cell reactions. 52 This defect stems from the loss of IL‐7 signaling in lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells, which are crucial for LN organogenesis. 53 , 54

To address this limitation, three distinct strategies have been developed. Takahashi et al. 55 engineered NOG‐pRORγt‐γc transgenic mice by driving Il2rg expression under the control of the RORγt promoter, restoring IL‐7 signaling specifically in LTi cells. These mice developed multiple LNs and supported antigen‐specific IgG responses. Li et al. 56 generated BRGS mice that overexpress human thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), a cytokine functionally similar to IL‐7 that signals via IL‐7Rα and TSLPR independently of Il2rg. 56 , 57 , 58 This model exhibited extensive LN development, with well‐organized B and T cell zones and functional T follicular helper responses upon immunization.

In 2024, Chupp et al. 59 introduced the THX model, wherein human umbilical cord blood CD34+ cells were transplanted into bone marrow‐ablated NBSGW and NSGW41 neonatal mice treated with estradiol (E2). These strains harbor mutations in the Kit gene, which allow human HSC engraftment without irradiation. 60 , 61 E2 treatment, shown to enhance hematopoietic and lymphoid differentiation, 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 facilitated the development of functional LNs with enriched human plasma cells, memory B cells, and marginal zone B cells, resulting in high‐affinity antibody production. The THX model represents a major advancement in modeling fully functional human immunity in vivo.

Human red blood cells and platelet reconstitution

Human red blood cells (RBCs) are among the most abundant cell types in the human blood, with their production and function exhibiting species‐specific characteristics. 66 Therefore, developing an in vivo model that accurately mimics human erythropoiesis is essential. However, no immunodeficient mouse model has yet been able to stably maintain mature human RBCs in circulation, which limits research on hematopoietic stem cell disorders and erythrocyte‐targeting pathogens, such as Plasmodium.

In 2011, our group reported that although human erythropoiesis occurred actively in the bone marrow of humanized mice, mature human RBCs were absent from the peripheral circulation due to clearance by murine phagocytes. Depletion of phagocytes using clodronate liposomes effectively prevented this rejection, and when combined with EPO and IL‐3, allowed 25%–30% of circulating erythrocytes to be of human origin. However, the high toxicity of clodronate limits this approach to short‐term applications. 67 In 2017, we identified the mouse complement system as a key factor in promoting human RBC adhesion to phagocytes. 68 Yamaguchi et al. 69 developed a C3‐deficient immunodeficient strain to enhance human RBC survival, although significant improvements were observed only when combined with clodronate‐mediated phagocyte suppression. Song et al. 70 established a MISTRG6‐Fah −/− humanized liver model, which eliminated C3 and reduced mouse Kupffer cells, achieving up to 12% peripheral human RBC chimerism. However, its requirement for HLA matching between liver cells and HSCs restricts its broader applicability. More recently, Patel et al. 71 demonstrated that heme oxygenase‐1 (HO‐1) deficiency in mice elevated circulating human RBCs after transfusion, likely due to reduced macrophage activity in the liver and spleen. However, compensatory macrophage expansion in the kidneys appeared to limit the overall efficiency of human RBC chimerism. 72

Similarly, human platelet levels are typically very low in conventional humanized mouse models. This deficiency is not due to impaired megakaryocyte differentiation or platelet biogenesis, but rather to xenogeneic phagocytosis by murine macrophages. Clodronate‐liposome treatment has been shown to significantly increase the proportion of circulating human platelets to over 70%. 73 Recent studies also found that THX mice support 30%–40% circulating human platelets, although mature human RBCs remain nearly undetectable. 59

Of note, early studies combined anti‐neutrophil antibodies with clodronate liposomes to mitigate rapid clearance of infused human RBCs. 74 Recent findings indicate that clodronate liposomes not only deplete macrophages but also induce a “shock” state in neutrophils. Thus, the improved engraftment observed after clodronate treatment in humanized models may involve neutrophil shock in addition to macrophage depletion. 75 Broader insight into this mechanism offers new directions for optimizing human RBC and platelet chimerism models.

Humanization of non‐hematopoietic tissues and organs

Solid organs are critical in regulating immune responses to infections, inflammation, and tissue injury. Humanized models of organs such as the liver, lung, skin, intestine, spleen, bone marrow, and tumors have become valuable tools for mechanistic and translational studies 76 , 77 , 78 (Figure 2).

Immunodeficient mice can receive a wide range of human tissues and cells via diverse transplantation routes. These models serve as powerful platforms for investigating human immune responses, including those related to transplantation immunity, autoimmunity, infectious diseases, and tumor immunology.

Liver

Humanized liver models are typically established by inducing liver injury in recipient mice to facilitate the engraftment and expansion of human hepatocytes, thereby generating chimeric livers. Commonly used models include Fah‐knockout, 79 uPA‐, 80 tk‐, 81 and AFC8‐ 82 transgenic mice, which support human hepatocyte engraftment through mechanisms such as toxic metabolite accumulation, urokinase overexpression, or drug‐induced apoptosis. These models have been widely applied in studies of hepatitis virus infection, 80 , 82 , 83 drug metabolism, 84 , 85 , 86 and liver disease pathogenesis. 84 , 85 , 86

The choice of hepatocyte source critically determines reconstitution efficiency and applicability. Adult hepatocytes, owing to their robust expansion capacity, achieve over 70% humanization in FRG or uPA models and fully support the HBV/HCV life cycle. 87 However, their limited availability and difficulty in HLA matching with human immune cells restrict their use in immunological studies. In contrast, fetal hepatoblasts can be co‐transplanted with autologous CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells, making them a useful cell source for dual immune–liver humanized models. However, their repopulation efficiency is relatively low and often needs to be improved by using stronger liver injury models or cytokines such as oncostatin M. 88 , 89 Recently, hepatocyte‐like cells derived from iPSCs or ESCs have attracted increasing attention, as they help overcome limitations in tissue availability and allow for gene editing. These features make them a promising option for humanized model development. 90 However, their maturation and repopulation efficiency still require further improvement.

Lung

Lung‐humanized mouse models are important in vivo platforms for recapitulating human lung structure and function. Conventional mice fail to support infection by human respiratory pathogens or reproduce human lung pathology, highlighting the need for such models to study infection mechanisms, evaluate antivirals and vaccines, and explore lung regeneration strategies.

Current construction strategies can be broadly divided into two categories: cell and tissue transplantation. Cell transplantation models induce lung injury to create a niche for human lung progenitor cells, enabling chimeric lung formation; this approach is suitable for studying epithelial regeneration but is limited by host–human cell compatibility. 91 , 92 Tissue transplantation models involve implanting human lung fragments subcutaneously or under the renal capsule of immunodeficient mice, generating functional grafts that retain native three‐dimensional architecture, cellular polarity, and stromal components, and support multiple respiratory pathogens. 93 , 94 , 95 , 96

Recent advances in tissue preservation have reduced dependence on fresh tissue. Optimized cryopreservation protocols incorporating fibroblast growth factor (FGF) and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) significantly enhance early proliferation, alveolar differentiation, and structural maturation. Tissue cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen for up to 1 year achieves efficient engraftment under these conditions, establishing a technical foundation for standardized and scalable generation of lung‐humanized models. 97

Heterotopic organ regeneration in the spleen

Wang et al. 98 demonstrated that relocating the spleen subcutaneously and injecting tumor extracts transformed its microenvironment, supporting the engraftment of human or iPSC‐derived hepatocytes. These cells proliferated and maintained function without toxicity. More recently, konjac glucomannan‐modified silica nanoparticles were used to engineer the spleen with ECM‐rich, immunosuppressive features, enabling the engraftment of human islets and restoring normoglycemia in macaques. 99 These findings highlight spleen‐based tissue engineering as a promising strategy for regenerating large human organs.

Dual‐humanized mouse models

Dual‐humanized mouse models combining a functional human immune system with human target tissues are key to overcoming species barriers and faithfully recapitulating human immune–tissue interactions. These models are useful for studying pathogen infection mechanisms, immunopathology, and metabolic diseases, and for evaluating novel therapies.

Conventional models lack a human immune system and fail to recapitulate the progression from NAFLD to NASH or glucose intolerance. 100 To address this, immune–liver dual‐humanized models have been developed. Her et al. 101 established HIL‐NSG mice by transplanting human fetal liver‐derived CD34+ cells into NSG mice, which develop steatosis and fibrosis under high‐fat diet. Kaffe et al. 102 co‐engrafted human liver tissue and hematopoietic stem cells into MISTRG‐Fah−/− mice, generating models containing human bile ducts, endothelium, stellate cells, and immune cells that self‐assemble into region‐specific liver microenvironments and exhibit NASH features upon Western diet administration.

In the lung field, models with human lung tissue alone lack the immune component essential for studying infection‐induced immune activation, inflammation, and repair. To introduce a human immune system, Wahl et al. 93 engrafted human lung tissue into BLT mice to generate BLT‐lung mice, enabling autologous human immune cell reconstitution and functional antigen‐specific responses against HCMV. Human immune system–lung models have also provided platforms for studying immune cell recruitment, activation, and effector functions during influenza virus and SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. 94 , 95 , 97

Overall, dual‐humanized mouse models are evolving from simple tissue reconstitution toward coordinated immune–tissue engineering.

Improvement of human neutrophil development

Neutrophils are the most abundant human immune cells in blood and play essential roles in infection control, inflammation, and tumor immunity. However, significant species differences in neutrophil development, function, and lifespan between humans and mice have long hindered the stable reconstitution of mature human neutrophils in conventional humanized mouse models. Recent advances have overcome this limitation through three distinct strategies. Zheng et al. 37 established the MISTRGGR model by knock‐in of human G‐CSF combined with knockout of mouse G‐CSFR, freeing up the bone marrow niche and enabling functional human neutrophil reconstitution with proven anti‐infective capacity. Sefik et al. 103 generated MaGIC mice by humanizing CD47 together with multiple human cytokines, leveraging a “niche remodeling” mechanism wherein irradiated mouse GMPs lacking CD47 are cleared by macrophages, thereby achieving efficient human neutrophil reconstitution without the need for mouse neutrophil depletion, resulting in a more stable model. Liu et al. 104 focused on developmental stages, generating NCG‐Gfi1−/− HIS mice via knockout of the key neutrophil transcription factor Gfi1, enabling robust human neutrophil reconstitution and revealing that myelocyte and metamyelocyte‐stage neutrophils dominate the tumor microenvironment and exhibit immunosuppressive properties. Together, these models provide critical platforms for studying human neutrophils in infection, cancer immunity, and targeted therapy.

In summary, recent advances in pluripotent stem cell‐derived thymic epithelial cells and in the differentiation and ex vivo expansion of hematopoietic stem cells have reduced the dependence on primary human tissues. Improvements in lymph node formation, as well as in the reconstitution of red blood cells, platelets, and neutrophils, have helped to address key limitations. Meanwhile, humanization of non‐hematopoietic tissues and the development of dual‐humanized models now enable more physiologically relevant immune–tissue interactions.

Application of humanized mice

Cancer immunotherapy

Immune checkpoints

HIS mice serve as crucial preclinical tools for studying immune checkpoint therapies by replicating interactions between therapeutic antibodies and human immune cells. These models have been extensively applied in research on anti‐PD1/PD‐L1, 105 , 106 , 107 , 108 anti‐CTLA‐4, 109 anti‐4‐1BB, 105 , 110 and anti‐ICOS 111 antibodies.

Lin et al. 112 developed EGFR‐CD3‐PD‐L1 Multi‐TAC9 using triple orthogonal linker technology and validated it in humanized mice reconstituted with CD34+ HSCs. Ho et al. 113 revealed that PD‐L1 stability depends on competition with CD58 for CMTM6 binding, and using humanized mice, demonstrated that CD58 deficiency promotes immune evasion by upregulating PD‐L1. Xia et al. 114 identified PILRα as an immunosuppressive ligand on tumor cells and showed in NCG mice reconstituted with human PBMCs that anti‐PILRα antibodies enhance T‐cell‐mediated tumor clearance.

CAR‐T cells

CAR‐T cell therapy offers potent tumor‐killing capacity but is challenged by off‐target effects and cytokine release syndrome. HIS mouse models have been widely used to study CAR‐T therapy for both hematological malignancies (e.g., CD19, 115 , 116 CD27, 117 GD2, 118 ICAM‐1, 119 and CD44v6 120 ) and solid tumors (e.g., PSMA, 121 HER2, 122 GPC3, 123 and mesothelin 124 ). HSC‐HIS models enable evaluation of CAR‐T cell efficacy and toxicity within a functional human immune context. Casucci et al. 120 showed that CD44v6 CAR‐T cells eradicated AML and MM without harming normal HSCs. Pizzitola et al. 125 tested CAR‐modified cytokine‐induced killer cells against CD33/CD123, using similar models. To address alloreactivity, a fully autologous HIS model was developed, combining functional human immunity with naturally developed human B‐ALL and recapitulating CD19 CAR‐T responses with high fidelity. 12

In recent years, the application of CAR‐T therapy has expanded to autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and systemic sclerosis. Lu et al. 126 utilized a humanized mouse model recapitulating lupus‐like symptoms to evaluate a novel adeno‐associated virus variant, AAV‐M2‐CD19CAR, which exhibits high specificity for human T cells. A single administration of this vector enabled in vivo generation of CAR‐T cells, leading to effective B cell depletion and reversal of lupus nephritis and other organ damage, thereby providing robust preclinical evidence supporting the clinical translation of CAR‐T therapy for autoimmune diseases. Collectively, these findings establish humanized mouse models as a key platform for extending CAR‐T therapy from cancer immunotherapy to autoimmune diseases.

Oncolytic viruses

Tsoneva et al. 127 injected GLV‐1 h376 (a vaccinia virus encoding anti‐CTLA4 antibody) into HIS mice bearing human lung tumors, confirming viral oncolysis and immune activation. Watanabe et al. 128 showed that adenovirus expressing TNF‐α and IL‐2 improved CAR‐T efficacy against pancreatic cancer. Kuryk et al. 129 combined GM‐CSF‐expressing adenovirus and anti‐PD‐1 therapy in a humanized melanoma model. Recently, Chen et al. 130 engineered an oncolytic virus encoding bystander T‐cell epitopes (TBYS) to activate pre‐existing virus‐specific T cells within tumors, achieving effective tumor clearance in NCG mice.

Pathogen infection

Humanized mice have been pivotal in studying human‐specific infectious diseases and are widely used in research on pathogens such as SARS‐CoV‐2, hepatitis viruses, 89 , 131 , 132 human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), malaria parasites, 70 , 133 , 134 dengue virus, 135 , 136 , 137 and respiratory syncytial virus. 93 , 138

SARS‐CoV‐2

Wahl et al. 96 successfully implanted human lung tissue subcutaneously into NSG mice infected them with SARS‐CoV‐2, demonstrating efficient viral replication and the early diffuse alveolar damage, similar to that observed in human patients. This model was also used to assess the antiviral efficacy of EIDD‐2801 against SARS‐CoV‐2. Similarly, we observed hemorrhage and immune cell infiltration in the grafted lung tissue of HIS mice after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, which were absent in NSG mice with lung tissue grafts alone. 94

SARS‐CoV‐2 enters host cells via hACE2 and infects lung epithelial cells. Liu et al. 139 created transgenic mice with inducible, lung‐specific hACE2 expression, which developed severe pneumonia upon infection. Sefik et al. 140 infected MISTRG6‐hACE2 humanized mice and observed exacerbated and persistent lung inflammation, along with a strong inflammatory macrophage response. Previous studies have shown that circulating human monocytes can differentiate into lung‐resident macrophages in mice. 141 , 142 Using this model, Sefik et al. 140 revealed that SARS‐CoV‐2 replication in human macrophages activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to IL‐1β and IL‐18 release, pyroptosis, and enhanced lung inflammation. Inhibiting the NLRP3 pathway mitigated chronic lung damage, which is consistent with findings from COVID‐19 patient autopsies. 143

HIV

HIV establishes persistent infection in humanized mice due to the presence of key host cell types, including CD4+ T cells and macrophages. These models have been crucial for evaluating therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, 144 HIV gene therapy, 145 , 146 , 147 CAR‐T immunotherapy, 148 , 149 , 150 and vaccine development. 150 , 151 Recently, Sungur et al. 13 recently developed the MISTRG‐6‐15 mouse model, wherein HIV‐1 infection led to rapid NK cell expansion in non‐lymphoid organs and triggered cytotoxic and inflammatory responses. Wang et al. 152 demonstrated that transplantation of gene‐edited HSCs lacking CARD8 reduced CD4+ T cell depletion, highlighting the inflammasome's role in HIV pathogenesis.

Regenerative medicine

Stem cell therapy offers hope for curing various refractory diseases (e.g., degenerative, ischemic, and autoimmune disorders), yet its clinical translation has long been hindered by graft immune rejection. 153 , 154 Humanized mouse models have become key preclinical platforms for evaluating the immunogenicity and tolerability of immune‐engineered stem cell derivatives. In hESC studies, Rong et al. 14 recapitulated immune rejection of hESC‐derived grafts (e.g., teratomas, cardiomyocytes) and demonstrated that sustained expression of CTLA4‐Ig and PD‐L1 enhances graft protection. Zhu et al. 155 further restricted immunomodulatory gene expression to differentiated cells, reducing teratoma formation while promoting immune tolerance toward hepatocytes. In iPSC research, Hu et al. 156 showed that hypo‐immune (B2M−/−CIITA−/−CD47+) iPSCs and derived islet cells survived long term in fully immunocompetent allogeneic humanized mice, effectively evading T, NK, and macrophage responses while ameliorating diabetes. Gravina et al. 157 engineered synthetic SIRPα agonistic engagers combined with truncated CD64 in HLA‐deficient iPSC‐derived endothelial cells, achieving complete protection from allogeneic cellular and antibody‐mediated rejection in humanized mouse models. Wang et al. 158 epigenetically repressed a β2‐microglobulin super‐enhancer to reduce HLA‐I to a “Goldilocks” level, enabling MSCs to evade T and NK cells and survive up to 40 days in humanized mice while treating acute lung injury. These studies underscore the utility of humanized mice for validating next‐generation universal iPSC products.

Autoimmune diseases

Type 1 diabetes (T1D)

Despite the extensive use of NOD mice in T1D research, many mechanisms and therapeutic strategies remain unvalidated in human contexts. 159 Viehmann Milam et al. 160 demonstrated that transferring CD4+ T cells or PBMCs from HLA‐DR4+ T1D patients into NSG.DR4 mice—after antigen‐specific stimulation—resulted in islet infiltration and β‐cell destruction. However, xenogeneic graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD) complicates the interpretation of these models. Recently, our group successfully developed a HIS model by transplanting HLA‐DQ8+ fetal thymus and HSCs into HLA‐DQ8‐transgenic NOD‐SCID mice. Subsequent transfer of CD4+ T cells expressing insulin peptide‐specific TCRs (InsB:9–23) resulted in significant islet infiltration, β‐cell destruction, and hyperglycemia. 161

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

SLE is a multifactorial autoimmune disease characterized by autoantibody production and systemic inflammation. 162 Andrade et al. 163 transplanted PBMCs from patients with SLE into BRG mice, which developed hallmark features of SLE, including proteinuria, human IgG deposition in the kidneys, and a shortened lifespan. Similarly, Gunawan et al. 164 induced lupus‐like nephritis in HIS mice by injecting pristane, which led to the production of anti‐dsDNA and anti‐histone antibodies, as well as glomerular Ig deposition. While conventional HIS mice exhibit limited humoral immune reconstitution, recent THX models show enhanced functionality. In response to pristane injection, THX mice developed “butterfly rash”‐like lesions, elevated levels of human IgG/IgA, accumulation of somatic hypermutations, and lupus‐like renal pathology. 59

Additionally, Lu et al. 126 utilized R848 to activate TLR7/8 signaling, achieving a highly faithful recapitulation of human SLE pathology in an HIS mouse model. This approach induced the production of anti‐dsDNA and anti‐Sm antibodies and led to clear histopathological changes, thereby providing a clinically relevant platform for evaluating targeted therapies for SLE.

Metabolic diseases

Hyperlipidemia

Chronic hyperlipidemia is a risk factor for atherosclerosis and contributes to immune dysregulation and systemic inflammation. 165 , 166 To model hyperlipidemia, Proto et al. 167 used AAV8‐PCSK9 combined with a high‐fat Western diet to induce hypercholesterolemia in HIS mice. These mice exhibited increased levels of human CD4+/CD8+ effector memory T cells and reduced levels of regulatory T cells in the blood, spleen, and lungs.

Non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease

Kaffe et al. 102 showed that MISTRG‐Fah −/− mice, engrafted with both human hepatocytes and immune cells, developed hallmark histological features of non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), including ballooning, Mallory body formation, zone 3 steatosis, and lobular inflammation upon Western diet feeding; they also identified a non‐parenchymal cell‐dependent regulatory axis in which WNT2, secreted by human endothelial cells, modulates hepatocyte cholesterol uptake via Frizzled class receptor 5. This model provides an advanced platform for investigating human hepatic metabolism and the interactions between liver and immunity in metabolic diseases.

Transplantation

Humanized mouse models, reconstructed with functional human immune system, serve as a key platform linking mechanistic research to clinical translation in transplantation immunology. Early studies that used these models have established fundamental principles of human T cell tolerance to porcine xenoantigens. Lan et al. 25 showed that mixed hematopoietic chimerism induces donor‐specific T cell non‐responsiveness; Tonomura et al. 168 revealed that pig islet xenograft rejection is largely T cell‐dependent; and Habiro et al. 169 demonstrated that porcine thymus transplantation enables donor‐specific tolerance and de novo human thymopoiesis. More recently, in allogeneic cell therapy, McCallion et al. 170 showed that unmodified regulatory T cells are rapidly cleared by host CD8+ T cells, whereas CRISPR‐mediated HLA‐I/II disruption combined with HLA‐E knock‐in enhanced immune evasion to levels comparable with autologous Tregs, supporting an off‐the‐shelf strategy. In xenotransplantation, transgenic porcine islets expressing either the CTLA‐4 variant LEA29Y 171 or PD‐L1 172 achieved long‐term graft survival in humanized mice, with the latter suppressing IFN‐γ responses without systemic immunosuppression. Collectively, these findings establish humanized mouse models as an indispensable preclinical platform for advancing transplantation immunotherapies.

HUMANIZED RAT MODELS

Compared to mice, rats offer closer metabolic and physiological similarities to humans, making them more suitable for modeling cardiovascular, metabolic, and pharmacological responses. 173 , 174 Their slower heart rate and larger size also facilitate surgical procedures and long‐term monitoring in regenerative medicine and cell therapy studies. 175

Yang et al. 15 developed an immunodeficient rat model (hSIRPA+ Prkdc−/−Il2rg−/−, or NSG‐like rat) lacking B, T, and NK cells. This model supported the engraftment of human pluripotent stem and cancer cells, and fetal liver‐derived HSCs. When co‐transplanted with fetal thymic tissue, these rats exhibited robust reconstitution of human T and B cells, with NK cells and macrophages detectable in the bone marrow. However, peripheral blood chimerism remained low at 1%–2% without thymus transplantation and increased to 17% with thymic tissue transplantation.

More recently, Ménoret et al. 16 reported a chimerism of approximately 45% in the peripheral blood of neonatal immunodeficient rats (hSIRPA+Rag1−/−Il2rg−/−) following the hepatic injection of human HSCs, with most of the chimerism being T cells. While these humanized rat models show potential, they still face limitations such as inefficient immune system reconstitution, incomplete lymphoid lineage development, and abnormal human immune functionality. These challenges highlight the need for further improvement of the platform further.

HUMANIZED PIG MODEL

Pigs offer unique advantages over rodent models for humanization due to their closer anatomical, physiological, and genetic resemblance to humans, as well as their larger body size and longer lifespan, which enable long‐term and complex preclinical studies. Moreover, their amenability to genome editing and scalability make them particularly attractive as bioreactors for generating human immune and blood cells. The following sections summarize the generation and breeding of immunodeficient pig strains, followed by recent progress in developing humanized pig models with stable and functional human immune system reconstitution.

Generation and breeding of immunodeficient pigs

Pigs share greater anatomical, hematological, physiological, and genetic similarities with humans compared to mice. Their larger body size and longer lifespan make them more suitable for long‐term and complex biomedical studies. 176 Additionally, pigs are relatively easy to breed and amenable to genome editing technologies. 177 However, as with humanized mice, the transplantation of human cells into pigs triggers robust xenogeneic immune rejection. Therefore, generating immunodeficient pigs is essential for enabling the reconstruction of human hematopoiesis.

In 2012, Suzuki et al. 178 developed the first immunodeficient pig model by generating IL2RG −/Y male cloned pigs through the targeted deletion of the IL2RG gene in fibroblasts. These pigs exhibited thymic aplasia and profound deficits in immunoglobulin, T cell, and NK cell development. Basel et al. 179 reported a naturally occurring immunodeficient pig strain capable of supporting human tumor growth. Histological analysis revealed severely depleted lymphoid tissues, including a near‐complete absence of T and B lymphocytes, although NK cell function was retained. This phenotype was later attributed to mutations in the ARTEMIS gene. 180

In 2014, Lee et al. 181 introduced a RAG2‐mutant immunodeficient pig model that lacked T and B cells but retained NK cells. Concurrently, Huang et al. 182 and Ito et al. 183 independently developed RAG1/2 and RAG1‐knockout pigs, respectively. Building upon these models, Lei et al. 184 and Boettcher et al. 18 created double‐knockout pigs targeting RAG2/IL2RG and ARTEMIS/IL2RG, both of which displayed profound deficiencies in T, B, and NK cells.

Despite the establishment of multiple immunodeficient pig strains, their severe immune dysfunction renders them highly susceptible to infections, which often leads to early mortality. This poses significant challenges for long‐term breeding and research applications. 18

Development of humanized pig models

Initial studies explored intrauterine transplantation of human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) into immunocompetent pigs to circumvent postnatal immune rejection. However, these attempts yielded minim human cell chimerism. 185 In 2020, Boettcher et al. 18 transplanted human HSPCs into ART −/− IL2RG −/Y pigs; although low levels of human chimerism were detected in umbilical cord blood, follow‐up was limited to 1 week postpartum, and engraftment in the bone marrow and circulation remained minimal.

A significant advancement was made with the development of a triple‐knockout pig model lacking RAG1, IL2RG, and CD47. 17 The deletion of CD47, which normally acts as a “self” recognition signal to macrophages, markedly reduced macrophage‐mediated phagocytic rejection of human cells. 186 This innovation created large animal models with profound immunodeficiency, capable of supporting the engraftment of human lymphohematopoietic cells.

By employing strict pathogen‐free husbandry, isolator systems, 184 and cesarean delivery techniques, 187 we achieved consistent long‐term survival of immunodeficient pigs, with lifespans exceeding 500 days. We then compared human HSPC engraftment in Rag1 −/− IL2RG −/− (RG) and CD47 −/− Rag1 −/− IL2RG −/− (RGD) pigs. In RG pigs, high levels of human HSPCs were detected in the bone marrow, but peripheral blood and spleen chimerism remained minimal. In contrast, RGD pigs receiving optimized human HSPC transplantation achieved over 90% human chimerism across bone marrow, peripheral blood, spleen, and other organs, with the engraftment persisting for up to 200 days. 17

The reconstituted human immune system in RGD pigs encompassed nearly all major hematopoietic lineages, including T, B, NK, myeloid dendritic cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and monocytes. Notably, the pig thymus supported robust human T cell development, with both T and B cells displaying diverse TCR and BCR repertoires. Functional assays revealed that human CD4+ T cells developed in humanized pigs could respond to TCR stimulation with strong activation, proliferation, and cytokine secretion. These T cells could also differentiate into Th1, Th2, Th17, and Treg subsets. Moreover, human CD8+ T cells transduced with anti‐CD19 CAR vectors, displayed potent cytotoxic activity against human B cell tumors. 17

These findings represent a major breakthrough in the development of large animal models for immunology, hematology, oncology, and cell‐based therapies, and underscore the potential of the humanized pig platform for advancing research in these areas.

CHALLENGES AND PROSPECTS

Despite recent progress, humanized mouse models still have several important limitations. The development and tissue residency of myeloid cells and NK cells remain weaker than that in humans. Most models also lack secondary lymphoid structures, which affects T cell‐dependent antibody responses, class switching, and germinal center formation, making it difficult to evaluate vaccines and antibody‐based therapies. In addition, human red blood cells and platelets do not persist well in circulation, limiting studies of conditions such as anemia, malaria, and thrombocytopenia. Although dual‐humanized models combine the human immune system with target tissues, the distribution, activation, and function of human immune cells are still not optimal. The lack of human stromal cells further limits the local microenvironment. Finally, widely used high‐performance models such as BLT depend on fetal tissues, which are limited in availability and raise ethical concerns.

The future development of humanized animal models will follow a dual‐track paradigm: refinement in mice and translational breakthroughs in large animals.

Mouse models will continue to advance in functional refinement, multisystem integration, and personalized modeling, serving as the primary platform for basic research and early drug screening. Precise gene editing enabling inducible expression of human cytokines can mimic physiological levels spatiotemporally while avoiding systemic side effects (Figure 3A). Co‐engraftment of human stromal cells with immune cells and target organs may reconstitute a more complete tissue microenvironment, enhancing immune cell maturation and residency. Multisystem humanized mice incorporating immune, hepatic, pulmonary, intestinal, and skin components could model systemic cross‐organ interactions and drug distribution. Combining human organoids with immune‐humanized mice offers a hybrid platform that merges high‐throughput screening with physiologically relevant immune interactions (Figure 3B). Personalized models derived from patient iPSCs or tumors hold promise for predicting immunotherapy responses, identifying sensitive drugs, and elucidating resistance mechanisms, thereby advancing precision medicine (Figure 3C).

Development and future applications of humanized mouse models. (A) Leveraging gene‐editing technologies to fully restore essential human immune cell functions in mice. (B) Constructing multi‐system humanized models to more accurately recapitulate human physiology. (C) Facilitating personalized medicine through patient‐specific modeling approaches.

Large animal models, owing to their human‐scale anatomy and physiology, offer unique advantages for modeling human diseases and evaluating therapeutics in a clinically relevant context. A central focus is regeneration—particularly hematopoietic regeneration. Humanized large animals can serve as platforms for the scalable production of off‐the‐shelf (universal) CAR‐T, CAR‐NK, and TCR‐T cells, addressing the high cost and batch‐to‐batch variability associated with conventional ex vivo manufacturing. Furthermore, achieving stable reconstitution of human red blood cells and platelets in pigs—through optimized macrophage depletion and complement inhibition—would provide sustainable, donor‐independent sources for transfusion medicine and hematology research, representing a key step toward blood regeneration. Beyond hematopoiesis, advances in cell regeneration and tissue engineering enable humanized pigs to function as “living bioreactors” for the regeneration of humanized livers, pancreata, and kidneys, offering novel strategies to alleviate donor organ shortages (Figure 4). The synergistic advancement of rodent and large animal humanized models will drive progress in elucidating human disease mechanisms, developing novel therapeutics, and advancing regenerative medicine. Future development will focus on optimizing multi‐gene editing platforms (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9 combined with somatic cell nuclear transfer) to generate pigs with additional human cytokine knock‐ins, further enhancing human immune cell development and functionality. Ultimately, humanized pigs carrying both a human immune system and specific human organs may enable physiologically relevant evaluation of immunotherapies, gene therapies, and vaccines, effectively bridging the translational gap between mice and humans.

Application potential of humanized pigs as bioreactors for human cell and organ regeneration. The successful reconstitution of the human hematopoietic system and the generation of humanized organs in RGD immunodeficient pigs offer significant potential for addressing the critical shortage of donor resources in blood transfusion, cell therapy, and organ transplantation. These advances pave the way for transformative developments in regenerative medicine and future clinical applications.

CONCLUSION

The development of humanized animal models has advanced from early immunodeficient mice to more complex, multi‐system platforms. Strategies including gene editing of human cytokines, co‐transplantation of fetal tissues with hematopoietic stem cells, reconstruction of lymphoid structures, and dual‐humanized immune–tissue systems have improved the ability to model human biological processes in vivo.

Nevertheless, these models differ in immune reconstitution efficiency, cellular functionality, tissue specificity, construction complexity, and scalability. Model selection should therefore be tailored to the specific research objective.
