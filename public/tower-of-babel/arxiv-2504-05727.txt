SAP-CoPE: Social-Aware Planning using Cooperative Pose Estimation with Infrastructure Sensor Nodes
Minghao Ning, Yufeng Yang, Shucheng Huang, Jiaming Zhong, Keqi Shu, Chen Sun, Ehsan Hashemi, and Amir Khajepour

Abstract
Note to Practitioners Indoor autonomous mobile robots operating in public facilities such as hospitals must navigate crowded environments where onboard sensing is often challenged by occlusions and limited fields of view, while also accounting for human comfort in addition to collision avoidance. This paper presents SAP-CoPE, a deployable framework that integrates cooperative infrastructure sensing with social-aware motion planning. Ceiling-mounted sensor nodes provide expanded and occlusion-robust perception through uncertainty-aware 3D human pose estimation from cameras, optionally supplemented by sparse LiDAR data. Leveraging these pose estimates, an MPC-based planner incorporating a human personal space field generates trajectories that balance social comfort, efficiency, and safety while respecting the robot’s kinematic constraints. The proposed architecture is modular and compatible with existing indoor robotic platforms using standard hardware, and real-world experiments demonstrate smoother navigation and improved clearance and travel-time performance compared with conventional baseline methods.

Introduction

Intelligent indoor mobility systems are gaining increasing attention in healthcare, logistics, and the automotive industry
[
1
,

3
]
. These systems reduce manual transportation tasks, minimize workplace injuries, and improve operational efficiency
[
4
]
. In restricted environments, mobile robots primarily focus on avoiding collisions with objects and are programmed to stop when humans are nearby. However, this strategy is less effective in public areas like hospitals and shopping malls, where interactions with people are unavoidable. As shown in Fig.
1
, to enable safe and efficient human-robot interaction, such a system requires real-time obstacle detection, human state and motion-cue estimation, and a robust motion planning algorithm for safe and smooth navigation.

Fig. 1

Recent research has improved robot perception using high-performance onboard sensors; however, onboard-only systems remain limited by perception range, field of view (FOV), and occlusions in crowded indoor spaces
[
5
]
. Infrastructure-supported perception can mitigate these limitations by observing shared spaces from complementary viewpoints. However, for human-aware navigation, object-level information such as pedestrian positions or bounding boxes is often insufficient, because it provides limited cues about human body orientation, posture, and short-term motion tendency.

Articulated 3D human pose provides richer information for social-aware planning. Human heading and body configuration can indicate whether a pedestrian is facing the robot, crossing its path, or beginning to turn. Such cues are useful for constructing an orientation-aware personal-space (PS) field, rather than treating each human as an isotropic obstacle. This is particularly important for large indoor robotic platforms, whose rectangular footprints and kinematic constraints require the planner to balance human comfort, physical safety, travel efficiency, and motion feasibility.

This work builds upon our preliminary studies on infrastructure-supported perception and MPC-based indoor robot navigation
[
6
,
7
]
. Those studies established the foundations for cooperative infrastructure perception and physically safe MPC navigation, but they focused mainly on obstacle-level perception and collision-oriented planning. In contrast, this article develops a unified pose-aware and social-aware navigation framework. The proposed SAP-CoPE framework estimates human 3D pose and heading in the world coordinate system using uncertainty-aware camera projection, joint-coherence constraints, and optional sparse LiDAR associations. The estimated pose and heading are then incorporated into an MPC-based planner through an asymmetric PS field, enabling the robot to generate socially acceptable motion while satisfying its kinematic constraints.

The key contributions of this article are as follows.

1.

Uncertainty-aware and sparse-measurement-compatible 3D human pose estimation:
A lightweight optimization-based pose estimation formulation is proposed to account for uncertainty propagation from camera projection while enforcing human joint coherence. The sparse LiDAR term is activated only when reliable LiDAR-to-joint associations are available, allowing the method to operate in both camera-only and camera-LiDAR configurations.

2.

Extension from physically safe MPC to social-aware MPC navigation:
A human-orientation-dependent PS field is incorporated into the MPC formulation for a large omnidirectional indoor robot. Instead of treating humans only as generic obstacles, the planner uses estimated human pose and heading to generate socially acceptable motion that balances clearance, comfort, travel efficiency, and robot kinematic feasibility.

3.

Practical validation and scope demonstration in human-populated indoor environments:
The proposed SAP-CoPE framework is validated through simulation and real-world experiments with human participants. The results demonstrate its applicability to infrastructure-supported indoor autonomous mobility tasks, where the robot must navigate efficiently while maintaining human-comfortable interaction distances.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work. Section III presents an overview of the proposed methodology. Section IV details the perception algorithm. Section V formulates the social-aware motion planner. Section VI presents the experimental results. Finally, Section VII concludes the paper and discusses future research directions.

Related Work

II-A

Perception

Infrastructure-Based Perception Systems:

Reliable perception is fundamental for intelligent indoor mobility systems. Traditional infrastructure-based approaches, particularly those using vision sensors, have been widely explored. Works such as
[
8
,

,
10
,
11
]
employ ceiling or wall-mounted cameras to detect and track pedestrians. However, purely independent camera-driven systems can be sensitive to lighting conditions and occlusions, and they are generally limited in their ability to obtain precise 3D object positions without additional depth information.
To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored multi-sensor fusion within the infrastructure. Brščić et al.
[
12
]
integrated RGB-D cameras, LiDAR, and marker-based tracking to enhance spatial accuracy and robustness. Though this approach improves performance, its scalability is hindered by high equipment and installation costs. Similarly, high-frequency motion capture systems
[
13
]
offer ground-truth level accuracy but are restricted to controlled spaces, limiting their practical use in real-world deployments.
Overall, these limitations emphasize the demand for indoor perception systems that are both reliable under challenging conditions and feasible for cost-effective, large-scale deployment.

Cooperative Perception Systems:

Cooperative perception improves environmental awareness by integrating sensory data from multiple sources, including other robots and infrastructure. This collaborative approach effectively addresses challenges such as occlusions and the limited FOV inherent in individual sensors. Recent advancements highlight the significance of Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) in the autonomous driving domain
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
Huang et al.
[
15
]
highlight the critical role of data sharing between agents and infrastructure while identifying real-time performance and communication delay as key bottlenecks.
Inspired by this concept, we propose a real-time cooperative perception system utilizing ceiling-mounted ISNs. Each node performs local perception and transmits lightweight observations to a central fusion module in real time. This architecture not only expands FOV and reduces occlusions in crowded indoor spaces but also ensures timely and coherent perception by mitigating latency-induced errors in the fusion pipeline.

3D Human Pose Detection:

Accurately estimating human 3D poses is vital for autonomous systems to interpret and predict human behaviour, ensuring safe and social-aware interactions.
Single-camera-based methods face challenges such as depth ambiguity and occlusions
[
18
]
; therefore, estimation accuracy can be improved by leveraging multi-modal sensor data.
Zanfir et al. introduced HUM3DIL, a semi-supervised multi-modal approach that embeds LiDAR points into pixel-aligned multi-modal features and employs a Transformer architecture for prediction
[
19
]
. Similarly, Bauer et al. proposed a weakly supervised method that integrates camera and LiDAR data, enabling accurate 3D human pose estimation without extensive 3D annotations
[
20
]
. However, this method requires dense point clouds to generate high-accuracy pseudo-3D keypoint labels by projecting point clouds onto the image plane.
Different from learning-based RGB–LiDAR pose estimation methods such as HUM3DIL and Bauer et al., our method does not assume dense LiDAR observations or learned multimodal feature fusion. In our deployed infrastructure layout, a pedestrian is usually observed by one top-down camera, while LiDAR returns on the human body may be sparse or intermittently available. Therefore, we formulate 3D pose estimation as a lightweight probabilistic optimization problem in the world coordinate frame. The method explicitly propagates calibrated camera-projection uncertainty, enforces joint-coherence constraints, and incorporates LiDAR only as optional weighted geometric residuals when valid associations exist. This allows the same framework to operate in camera-only, camera–LiDAR, single-node, and cooperative configurations.

II-B

Planning

social-aware Planning:

Recent human-aware planning algorithms focus primarily on modeling human–robot interactions while often assuming the robot has a simple circular shape for computational convenience, without explicitly accounting for rectangular or articulated platform geometries
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. Among these algorithms, the Social Force Model (SFM) is widely used to generate socially acceptable robot motion. Kamezaki et al.
[
25
]
introduced a planning algorithm based on the inducible Social Force Model, while Ratsamee et al.
[
26
]
developed the Extended SFM (ESFM) by incorporating human pose and orientation to improve conventional SFM. Despite their effectiveness, SFM-based approaches often model human influence as symmetric in all directions. While this assumption allows the robot to maintain distance from personal spaces in sparse environments, it can lead to frequent unexpected deceleration or even stopping in crowded settings, thereby reducing efficiency and increasing the risk of collisions.

Planning based on Information Types:

Social-aware planning algorithms can be broadly categorized into two types: individual state-based information and group-based information
[
27
]
. Individual state-based methods use detailed information such as position, pose, orientation, and velocity; for example, Stefanini et al.
[
28
]
proposed a novel planning algorithm that incorporates the articulated 3D human poses, semantic labels, and trajectory prediction to describe human behaviour. However, such approaches usually require reliable perception and tracking algorithms, and their performance can degrade significantly under occlusions, which are common in cluttered environments.

In contrast, group-based methods treat multiple humans as a single entity. Hoang et al.
[
29
]
proposed a proactive social motion model that accounts for socio-spatiotemporal characteristics of human groups, allowing the mobile robot to plan a safe trajectory in various situations. Nevertheless, group-level representations may oversimplify complex interactions among individuals, potentially causing the robot to react conservatively or ignore subtle cues such as interpersonal distances, leader–follower structures, or individuals breaking away from the group.

On the other hand, although the above approaches have demonstrated strong performance in human-aware planning, they may not generalize well to large, rectangular robots with omnidirectional maneuverability, potentially resulting in unrealistic or impractical motions. Moreover, these methods often lack explicit constraints on robot kinematics and control limits, which may produce motion commands that are smooth in simulation but difficult to track reliably on actual hardware.

III
Methodology

Fig. 2
:
SAP-CoPE Structure Overview

The proposed SAP-CoPE method consists of two main components: real-time cooperative perception and social-aware motion planning (Fig.
2
). The perception system employs a group of multi-modal ISNs equipped with LiDAR, cameras, and edge computing for local object detection and 3D human pose estimation. A synchronized clock system aligns LiDAR scans with camera operations, ensuring temporally consistent data, which is transmitted to a Central Layer (CL) for global fusion, multi-object tracking, and delay compensation. For the motion-planning system, an MPC-based planner is designed to generate optimal poses and is implemented in the CL, enabling the robot to compute a social-aware motion that respects humans’ personal space while remaining close to a predefined reference motion. To achieve this, the PS field, inspired by psychological studies, is incorporated into the MPC’s objective function.

Furthermore, the controller then tracks the generated motion to obtain optimal control commands, which are transmitted to the Application Layer (AL) for execution. In this study, communication between the ISNs, CL, and AL is achieved using a Wi-Fi network.

Propagation of Uncertainty to 3D World Coordinates

Building upon the computed world coordinates and the derived Jacobian matrix, we
propagate the uncertainties of the input parameters
(x_{p},y_{p},z_{w})

Using the Jacobian matrix

P^{i}

P^{j}

\|\mu_{P^{i}}-\mu_{P^{j}}\|\approx l_{ij}
,
where
l_{ij}

i
and
j

is also incorporated, ensuring a consistent and biologically plausible pose.

The scalar weights

Since each residual is already weighted by its corresponding uncertainty, these scalar weights only regulate the relative importance of different information sources. The LiDAR term is activated only when valid LiDAR-to-joint associations are available. If LiDAR measurements are sparse or absent,

IV-B

Delay-aware Global Perception

In the ISN system, computational and communication delays can introduce inconsistencies in perception, severely degrading the performance of the subsequent motion-planning task.
To address this, our previous work proposed a delay-aware cooperative perception pipeline that effectively mitigates latency effects and provides consistent, real-time perception outputs
[
6
]
. It contains three key components: global fusion across sensor nodes, multi-object tracking, and delay compensation.

Each ISN sends its local perception results to the CL, and the detections are matched based on spatial proximity and class labels. For objects detected by multiple ISNs, instead of simply averaging their positions like previous works
[
6
]
, the CL refines the 3D human pose estimation via Eq.
6
at the CL by fusing multi-view pose constraints from different ISNs to enhance robustness against occlusions and partial views.

Then, CL performs multi-object tracking using an extended Kalman filter (EKF) for each detected object to keep track of their states over time. Human trajectories, in particular, are modelled using a non-linear dynamic system capable of capturing changes in walking direction and speed, which enhances robustness in crowded and dynamic environments.

To further improve real-time responsiveness, the system incorporates timestamp-based delay compensation. Each ISN assigns a local timestamp to detected objects, allowing the CL module to compute the delay relative to its current time. Object positions are then extrapolated forward using motion prediction models, effectively aligning stale data with the current system state. This compensation mechanism ensures that the fused perception remains temporally consistent, even under variable network or processing latencies.

This paper adapts the concept of personal space (PS)
[
32
]
to model human behaviour in interaction with the robot. Compared with using the APF to represent the human, the PS model captures an individual’s non-verbal and non-contact communication channels, influencing how they perceive proximity to others. Based on studies by Edward T. Hall
[
33
]
and R. Sommer
[
34
]
, PS can be divided into four regions: public distance, social distance, personal distance, and intimate distance. These regions define the various zones of comfort and social interaction. The graphical representation and the relative distances can be found in Fig.

.

By assigning appropriate values to each region, as shown in Fig.
4
b), the robot is able to maintain a psychologically safe distance from nearby humans. In this work, we adopt the asymmetric field proposed in
[
32
]
, where the field is intentionally shaped to be asymmetric along the human’s facing direction. This design reflects the natural psychological comfort zones observed in human interactions, where individuals are more sensitive to being disturbed from the front. As a result, the robot can anticipate and steer earlier to avoid potential discomfort.

In contrast, the conventional SFM and Artificial Potential Field (APF) are normally symmetrical in both
x
and
y
directions, which requires enlarging the overall field size to achieve a similar early-dodging behavior from the robot. However, because the conventional field covers an equal area in front of and behind the human, it can lead to inefficient navigation behavior, such as unnecessary deceleration or even being trapped when operating in densely populated environments. The proposed PS field, therefore, enables more natural and efficient avoidance while ensuring safe interpersonal distances.

V-C

QP-Based MPC Approximation

To achieve real-time computational efficiency, both the nonlinear kinematic model and the PS field are approximated before formulating the MPC problem. The nonlinear kinematic model is approximated using a first-order Taylor expansion around the current measured state and previous control input. Since the PS field depends on the relative position between the robot and the surrounding humans, the nominal trajectories of both the robot and humans need to be predicted over the prediction horizon. In this work, the nominal robot and human states are predicted using the Constant Velocity and Turning Rate (CVTR) model. The PS field is then approximated using a second-order Taylor expansion around these nominal predicted states. Specifically, for the robot position
z=[X,Y]^{\top}
, the nominal point
z_{0}
, and

\Delta z=z-z_{0}
, the PS field is approximated as

\displaystyle\Omega(z)\approx

\displaystyle\Omega(z_{0})+\nabla\Omega(z_{0})^{\top}\Delta z

(21)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Delta z^{\top}\nabla^{2}\Omega(z_{0})\Delta z.

Here,
z_{0}
is updated at each prediction step according to the predicted robot state and the corresponding predicted human state. The constant term
\Omega(z_{0})
is omitted from the QP objective because it does not affect the optimizer. The gradient term contributes to the linear cost vector, while the Hessian term contributes to the quadratic cost matrix. To preserve the convexity of the QP, the PS-field Hessian is projected onto its positive semi-definite component before being included in the objective function. This quadratic approximation allows the PS-field cost to be incorporated into the QP formulation and efficiently solved using the OSQP solver
[
36
]
. The analysis of the QP approximation of the PS field can be found in the supplementary material.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed motion planner in a larger and crowded scenario, simulations were conducted and compared with three baseline methods. The first baseline is the conventional Social Force Model (SFM)
[

[

The simulation environment was designed as a
12\mathrm{m}\times 12\mathrm{m}

Each method was evaluated over 100 trials, with randomized initial and goal positions for the pedestrians. The same random seeds were used across all methods for each trial to ensure a fair comparison.

Four evaluation metrics were used to assess the performance of the proposed motion planning algorithm against other baselines.
a)
Collision and Stuck Rates
: this metric evaluates the task-completion performance of each method. A successful trial is defined as one in which the robot reaches the goal without collision or becoming stuck. A collision case refers to physical contact between the robot and a human (boundary-to-
boundary distance), while a stuck case refers to the robot failing to reach the goal within the allowed time due to local minima, excessive stopping, or infeasible motion.
b)
Clearance
: this metric measures the minimum boundary-to-boundary distance between the robot and the human. Lower values indicate that the robot moves closer to the human, which may increase psychological discomfort and collision risk.
c)
Travel Distance
: this metric measures the total traveled distance of the robot from the starting position to the goal, reflecting travel efficiency.
d)
Travel Time
: this metric measures the total traveling time from the starting position to the goal, reflecting motion efficiency and smoothness. A shorter travel time indicates that the robot can maintain the desired speed with fewer unnecessary decelerations or stops.

IV
shows the simulation results. Statistical significance among the methods was evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (a paired, non-parametric test) with Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The resulting groupings are indicated by superscript letters in Table
IV
. For each performance metric, methods that share at least one superscript letter are not significantly different, whereas methods with no letter in common are significantly different. Since failed trials may yield incomplete or non-comparable path and time measurements, only successful trials were used in the statistical comparison of total traveled distance and total traveled time.

Compared with the baseline methods, the proposed method achieved the best overall success performance, reducing the collision rate to 7% and eliminating stuck cases. In contrast, both SFM and SARL resulted in a 50% collision rate, while MPC+PF produced a 10% collision rate and a 3% stuck rate. These results indicate that the proposed method provides improved robustness in dense human-populated environments, including when compared with the evaluated learning-based SARL baseline.

In terms of safety-related performance, the proposed method maintained a clearance distance statistically comparable to MPC+PF, while both methods significantly outperformed SFM and SARL. Although MPC+PF achieved a slightly higher average clearance, its higher collision/stuck rate and longer travel time indicate that increased clearance alone does not necessarily lead to better overall navigation performance. For navigation efficiency, the proposed method achieved the shortest average traveled distance and traversal time among all evaluated methods. The statistical results show that the proposed method significantly reduced the traveled distance compared with all baselines. In addition, based on the post-hoc statistical grouping, the proposed method achieved significantly shorter travel time than the other evaluated methods.

To improve computational efficiency in dense environments, only pedestrians within a 5 m radius of the robot are considered when constructing the MPC optimization problem. This local-neighbour selection strategy limits the number of active pedestrian-related terms while retaining the most relevant agents for collision avoidance and social interaction. In the 15-pedestrian dense-crowd simulation, the proposed method achieved an average computation time of 41.69 ms, which remains below the MPC sampling period of 100 ms. Although higher than the baseline methods, this result demonstrates that the proposed formulation remains computationally feasible under the tested dense-crowd condition.

Overall, the dense-crowd simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method provides a favourable trade-off among safety, robustness, navigation efficiency, and computational feasibility. Some simulation quantitative results can be found in the repository.

VI-D

Discussion

VI-D
1
Motion Planning Simulation vs Experiment

The experimental observations are generally consistent with the simulation results, particularly in clearance and travel time. In both cases, the proposed method maintained strong clearance performance while achieving the lowest average travel time in most scenarios. The traveled-distance results are more scenario-dependent: although the simulation showed a clearer advantage for the proposed method, the experiments showed statistically similar or mixed trends in the 2- and 4-participant cases, with the shortest distance achieved by the proposed method only in the 6-participant case. This difference is reasonable due to the limited experimental trials and sparse human density.

In summary, SFM showed the weakest safety performance because it does not explicitly consider the robot’s omnidirectional maneuverability when generating avoidance behaviour. MPC+PF performed competitively in sparse scenarios; however, its symmetric field representation can lead to conservative deceleration and reduced efficiency in denser settings. By contrast, the proposed method maintained a better balance between interaction-aware clearance, travel efficiency, and real-time computational feasibility, demonstrating its suitability for social-aware navigation in human-populated indoor environments.

VI-D
2
Component-wise Ablation Discussion

The conducted evaluations provide component-wise evidence for the contribution of the main modules in SAP-CoPE. First, the contribution of LiDAR fusion is isolated in Table
II
by comparing the Cam-only and Cam+LiDAR variants. Adding sparse LiDAR measurements reduces the position MAE from 0.1098 m to 0.0892 m in the single-node case, while the cooperative Cam+LiDAR configuration further reduces the error to 0.0771 m. This confirms that LiDAR fusion improves spatial localization accuracy by providing additional depth constraints.

Second, the effect of delay compensation is evaluated in Table
III
, where the delay-aware pipeline is compared with the baseline fusion method under different artificial delays. As the delay increases to 200 ms, the baseline displacement error increases to 0.1439 m, whereas the delay-aware method limits the error to 0.0993 m, demonstrating that timestamp-based correction and motion prediction improve temporal consistency under communication latency.

Third, the contribution of the asymmetric PS field is isolated by comparing the proposed method with MPC+PF, which uses a symmetric potential field formulation. In both the dense-crowd simulation and the physical experiments, the proposed method achieves lower failure rates and improved navigation efficiency compared with MPC+PF. Specifically, the proposed method achieves the lowest collision/stuck rate, and the shortest average traveled distance and time in the dense-crowd simulation, while maintaining comparable minimum clearance. In the physical experiments, it also achieves the lowest average travel time across all tested participant numbers while maintaining safe clearance. These results indicate that the asymmetric PS field provides a more direction-aware representation of human personal space than the symmetric potential field, improving socially compliant avoidance behaviour without sacrificing navigation efficiency or real-time feasibility.

VII
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we have proposed SAP-CoPE, a social-aware navigation framework for autonomous mobile robots operating in human-populated indoor environments. The framework has integrated cooperative infrastructure-based perception with MPC-based social-aware motion planning. In the perception module, an uncertainty-aware 3D human pose estimation method has been developed to estimate human pose and heading from camera observations, with optional sparse LiDAR-based geometric constraints. In the planning module, the estimated human pose and orientation have been used to construct an asymmetric PS field, enabling the robot to generate trajectories that balance physical safety, human comfort, travel efficiency, and kinematic feasibility.

The proposed framework has been evaluated through perception experiments, dense-crowd simulation, and real-world robot experiments with human participants. The results have demonstrated improved pose estimation accuracy, robust delay-aware perception, and social-aware navigation performance compared with baseline methods. The computation-time results have also shown that the proposed planner has satisfied the real-time requirement under the tested indoor scenarios. These results have indicated that SAP-CoPE is suitable for infrastructure-supported indoor public environments where sensor nodes can provide cooperative perception.

Several limitations have been identified in this work. First, the physical experiments have been conducted in controlled hallway-like scenarios; therefore, larger-scale validation in more complex and dynamic environments, such as hospital wards or open lobbies, is still needed. Second, the current prior-height-based pose initialization has assumed approximately upright pedestrians, which is reasonable for the walking scenarios considered in this study but may be less accurate for sitting, bending, or other unusual postures. Finally, the CVTR method has been used to predict the robot and human poses as nominal points for the quadratic approximation of the PS field. However, sudden changes in pedestrian motion may introduce a mismatch between the predicted and actual PS fields, causing a potential collision. Future work will address these limitations by extending validation to more complex real-world environments and incorporating learning-based human intention and motion prediction to improve robustness under dynamic human motion.
