Cohomology of the hyperplane complement of a quaternionic reflection group Thanks: We thank Gwyn Bellamy, Gerhard Röhrle, Johannes Schmitt, Don Taylor, and Ulrich Thiel for comments on a preliminary version of this article.
Stephen Griffeth, David Guevara

Abstract
. We compute the graded rank of the cohomology of the hyperplane complement associated with a quaternionic reflection group, and observe that it factors into irreducible factors with positive integer coefficients. For an irreducible group, these irreducible factors are all linear except at most one irreducible quadratic factor, which occurs for precisely three of the exceptional groups.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Reflection groups

Each compact Lie group
K
with maximal torus
T
produces a
Weyl group

W
, which is a finite linear group
W=N_{K}(T)/T
acting on the dual of the Lie algebra of
T
and stabilizing the lattice of characters of
T
. This representation of the Weyl group is in particular defined over
\mathbf{Q}
, and one shows that
W
is generated by the reflections it contains. In this way compact Lie groups are related to reflection groups defined over
\mathbf{Q}
, with many of the properties of
K
encoded by the corresponding Weyl group
W
. Extending scalars from
\mathbf{Q}
to
\mathbf{R}
and considering those finite
\mathbf{R}
-linear groups generated by reflections produces the class of finite Coxeter groups, and further enlarging the class of groups under study to allow a group
W
generated by reflections acting on a
\mathbf{C}
-vector space
V
results in complex reflection groups, characterized as those finite linear groups whose corresponding quotient space
V/W
is smooth. The sequence
\mathbf{Q}\subseteq\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{C}
of fields may be extended to

\mathbf{Q}\subseteq\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{C}\subseteq\mathbf{H}

by considering the division ring
\mathbf{H}
of quaternions, and while the resulting class of reflection groups is far less well-studied than the class of complex reflection groups, it has recently developed (see
[
CaGr
]
) that the natural generality for the study of diagonal coinvariant rings of complex reflection groups seems to be in the context of these
quaternionic reflection groups
(also known as
symplectic reflection groups
). The purpose of this paper is to explore another aspect of the theory of complex reflection groups that, quite mysteriously, also extends to quaternionic reflection groups. Specifically, we compute the Poincaré polynomials of the hyperplane complements for quaternionic reflection groups and observe that they exhibit some extremely striking behavior similar to the case of complex reflection groups (as first proved in
[
OrSo
]
): they factor into irreducible (in
\mathbf{Z}[t]
) factors of degree at most two with positive integer coefficients. Our calculation relies on Cohen’s classification
[
Coh
]
of quaternionic reflection groups (see also
[
Tay
]
and
[
Wal
]
). Specifically, we show that for the infinite family
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
(see
3.4
for the definition), the hyperplane complement is an iterated fiber bundle, while for the exceptional groups we compute the structure of the lattice of parabolic subgroups explicitly enough to determine the Poincaré polynomial. This calculation should also be of use in questions having to do with the structure of the class of parabolic subgroups, for instance in the study of symplectic reflection algebras.

1.2.
Numerical invariants from polynomial invariants and the coinvariant ring

Given a complex reflection group
W
with reflection representation
\mathfrak{h}
, the ring
\mathbf{C}[\mathfrak{h}]^{W}
of
W
-invariant polynomial functions on
\mathfrak{h}
is generated by
n=\mathrm{dim}(\mathfrak{h})
algebraically independent homogeneous polynomials of degrees
d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}
. This sequence
d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}
is independent of the choice of generators, and the integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
are called the
degrees
of
W
. The ring
\mathbf{C}[\mathfrak{h}]^{W}
is the ring of functions on the quotient scheme
\mathfrak{h}/W
, and the
coinvariant ring
of
W
is the ring
\mathbf{C}[\pi^{-1}(0)]
of functions on the scheme-theoretic fiber
\pi^{-1}(0)
of the quotient map
\pi:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{h}/W
. The structure of the graded
W
-module
\mathbf{C}[\pi^{-1}(0)]
provides another family of numerical invariants of
W
: upon forgetting the grading it is isomorphic to the regular representation
\mathbf{C}W
and so for each irreducible
\mathbf{C}W
-module
E
there is a well-determined sequence
m_{1}\leq m_{2}\leq\cdots\leq m_{p}
of
p=\mathrm{dim}(E)
non-negative integers known as the
exponents
of
E
with
E
occurring in degrees
m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{p}
in
\mathbf{C}[\pi^{-1}(0)]
. In particular, the representation
\mathfrak{h}
appears in degrees
d_{1}-1,d_{2}-1,\dots,d_{n}-1
in
\mathbf{C}[\pi^{-1}(0)]
, and the numbers
e_{1}=d_{1}-1,\dots,e_{n}=d_{n}-1
are known simply as the
exponents
of
W
. The
co-exponents
of
W
are the integers
1=f_{1}\leq f_{2}\leq\cdots\leq f_{n}
so that
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
appears in degrees
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
in
\mathbf{C}[\pi^{-1}(0)]
. If
\mathfrak{h}\cong\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, or in other words if
W
is a real reflection group, then the exponents and co-exponents coincide.

1.3.
Exponents and co-exponents via topology of the hyperplane complement and the combinatorics of the intersection lattice

These numerical invariants arise in another context. Namely, given a complex reflection group
W
we define two polynomials

c_{W}(t)=\sum_{w\in W}t^{\mathrm{codim}(\mathrm{fix}(w))}

and

p_{W}(t)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{dim}(H^{d}(\mathcal{M}_{W}))t^{d},

where
\mathcal{M}_{W}
is the hyperplane complement for the group
W
, obtained by removing all its reflecting hyperplanes from the space
V
on which it acts. These polynomials factor in terms of the exponents and coexponents of
W
:

c_{W}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+e_{i}t)\quad\text{and}\quad p_{W}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+f_{i}t).

Thus if
W
is a real reflection group, we have
p_{W}(t)=c_{W}(t)
. Both of these definitions still make sense for a quaternionic reflection group, and the purpose of this paper is to point out that while the algebraic definitions of the exponents and coexponents no longer work, the polynomials
c_{W}(t)
and (especially!)
p_{W}(t)
exhibits fascinating and mysterious behavior very similar to the case of a complex reflection group.

1.4.
The codimension and Poincaré polynomials for a quaternionic reflection group

Given a quaternionic reflection group
W
with reflection representation
V
, we search for numerical invariants. The paper
[
CaGr
]
introduced three variants of the Coxeter number
h
of an irreducible real reflection group,
g=2N/n,

h=(N+N^{*})/n
, and
k=2N^{*}/n
, where
N
is the number of reflections,
N^{*}
is the number of reflecting hyperplanes and
n
is the rank. Here we compute the ranks of the cohomology groups with coefficients in
\mathbf{Z}
of the hyperplane complement
V^{\circ}
associated with
W
, with the hope of discovering an analog of the coexponents.

We encode this information in the Poincaré polynomial
p_{W}(t)
, which is the generating function for these ranks:

p_{W}(t)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{rk}\left(H^{3d}(V^{\circ},\mathbf{Z})\right)t^{d}.

We note that the cohomology groups are always free
\mathbf{Z}
-modules, and are zero in degrees not divisible by
3
(essentially because the
3
-sphere
S^{3}
has these properties), so
p_{W}(t)
contains all the information about the cohomology groups up to isomorphism. We also define the codimension generating function
c_{W}(t)
just as in the complex case (but taking
\mathbf{H}
-codimension).

Our main theorem is the computation of these polynomials for all quaternionic reflection groups. Since they are multiplicative,
p_{W_{1}\times W_{2}}=p_{W_{1}}p_{W_{2}}
and
c_{W_{1}\times W_{2}}=c_{W_{1}}c_{W_{2}}
, it suffices to compute them for irreducible groups, which we do using Cohen’s
[
Coh
]
classification. We may assume our group
W
is not complex.

Theorem 1.1
.

(a)

For a quaternionic group
W
of rank
2
containing
N
reflections and
N^{*}
reflecting hyperplanes,

p_{W}(t)=(1+t)(1+(N^{*}-1)t)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{W}(t)=1+Nt+(|W|-N-1)t^{2}.

(b)

For a positive integer
n\geq 3
, a finite non-abelian subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mathbf{H}^{\times}
with order
|\Gamma|=m
, and a normal subgroup
\Delta
of
\Gamma
such that
\Gamma/\Delta
is abelian, the Poincaré polynomial
p_{W}(t)
of
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
is

p_{W}(t)=\left(1+t\right)\left(1+(1+m)t\right)\left(1+(1+2m)t\right)\cdots\left(1+(1+(n-1)m)t\right),

and the polynomial
c_{W}(t)
is

c_{W}(t)=\left(1+(m-1)t\right)\left(1+(2m-1)t\right)\cdots\left(1+((n-1)m-1)t\right)\left(1+(np-1)t\right),

where
p=|\Delta|
is the order of
\Delta
.

(c)

For the exceptional irreducible quaternionic groups of rank at least

The polynomials
p_{W}(t)
factor into irreducible factors with positive integer coefficients, all of which are linear except for a single quadratic factor for the three exceptional groups of type

, and

. The factor
(1+t)

c_{W}(t)

2.
Topological preliminaries: the cohomology of a quaternionic hyperplane complement

2.1.
Summary

The purpose of this section is to collect the basic facts about hyperplane complements we will use, in the form we will use them. Most of this has appeared in
[
Sch
]
. For us the important point is that, just as for complex arrangements, the Poincaré polynomial of the complement of a quaternionic hyperplane arrangement may be computed in terms of the Möbius function of the intersection lattice of the arrangement. The proof of this is actually easier than the corresponding fact for complex arrangements: the splitting of the long exact sequence for a complex arrangement equipped with a distinguished hyperplane is automatic for dimension reasons in the quaternionic case, as we explain below. In fact, it seems likely that one could turn this around, give an
a priori
proof that the cohomology of the quaternionification of a complex hyperplane complement is isomorphic (up to changing the grading) to the cohomology of the complex complement, and thereby obtain a more efficient proof of the results in the complex case by use of the quaternionic case.

2.2.
Setup

We write
\mathbf{H}
for the division ring of quaternions, which is the
\mathbf{R}
-vector space with basis
1,i,j,k
and
\mathbf{R}
-linear multiplication determined by

i^{2}=j^{2}=k^{2}=ijk=-1.

We write
\overline{h}=a-bi-cj-dk
for the conjugate of a quaternion
h=a+bi+cj+dk\in\mathbf{H}
with
a,b,c,d\in\mathbf{R}
, and
\mathbf{H}^{\times}
for the group of non-zero quaternions.

We fix a finite-dimensional right
\mathbf{H}
-vector space
V
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite collection of linear (that is, containing
0
) hyperplanes in
V
(sometimes called a
central
hyperplane arrangement), and let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}
be the corresponding hyperplane complement

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}=V\setminus\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{A}}H.

We are interested in the cohomology with integer coefficients of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}
. It turns out that this is entirely controlled by the structure of the poset
L(\mathcal{A})
whose elements are the finite intersections
X=H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{m}
of elements of
\mathcal{A}
, with the order
X\geq Y
if
X\subseteq Y
. Thus
V
(the empty intersection) is the unique minimal element of
L(\mathcal{A})
, and the intersection of all the elements of
\mathcal{A}
is the unique maximal element (often
\{0\}
) of
L(\mathcal{A})
. This poset is ranked by codimension: we define
\mathrm{rk}(X)=\mathrm{codim}_{V}(X)
for
X\in L(\mathcal{A})
.

2.3.
The inductive approach

Fix
H_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
. We obtain two new arrangements: first, the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{0}
in the vector space
H_{0}
consists of the set
H_{0}\cap H
of hyperplanes in
H_{0}
obtained by intersecting
H_{0}
with those
H\in\mathcal{A}
such that
H_{0}
is not contained in
H
. Second, the arrangement
\mathcal{A}^{0}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\{H_{0}\}
is obtained simply by omitting
H_{0}
from
\mathcal{A}
. We write
\mathcal{M}_{0}
for the complement in
H_{0}
of the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}^{0}
for the complement in
V
of the arrangement
\mathcal{A}^{0}
. Thus
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}
is an open subset of
\mathcal{M}^{0}
with complement equal to
\mathcal{M}_{0}
(which is therefore a closed subset of
\mathcal{M}^{0}
). We will use this configuration to study the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
and its complement
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}
by induction on the cardinality of
\mathcal{A}
.

2.4.
Cohomology

We will use only cohomology with integer coefficients. Thus for a topological space
X
we write simply
H^{d}(X)=H^{d}(X;\mathbf{Z})
for the
d
th cohomology group with integer coefficients. For an abelian group
A
, we write
\mathrm{rk}(A)
for its rank (which is a non-negative integer or
\infty
). Given a quaternionic hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we define the
Poincaré polynomial

p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
by

p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=\sum_{d}\mathrm{rk}(H^{3d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}))t^{d}.

As we will see below, the cohomology groups of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}
are finitely generated free abelian groups and are zero except in degrees divisible by
3
, so
p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
contains all the information about their isomorphism classes.

2.5.
The topological side of the induction

The key observation for the topological side of this inductive approach is that
\mathcal{M}_{0}
has a tubular neighborhood
U
in
\mathcal{M}^{0}
, so that the pair
(U,U\setminus\mathcal{M}_{0})
is homeomorphic to
(\mathcal{M}_{0}\times\mathbf{H},\mathcal{M}_{0}\times\mathbf{H}^{\times})
. Thus by excision, the Künneth formula, and the fact that the relative cohomology of the pair
H^{d}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{H}^{\times})
is zero for
d\neq 0
and isomorphic to
\mathbf{Z}
for
d=4
,

H^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{0},\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})\cong H^{d}(U,U\setminus\mathcal{M}_{0})\cong H^{d}(\mathcal{M}_{0}\times\mathbf{H},\mathcal{M}_{0}\times\mathbf{H}^{\times})\cong H^{d-4}(\mathcal{M}_{0})\otimes H^{4}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{H}^{\times})\cong H^{d-4}(\mathcal{M}_{0}).

The long exact sequence for the pair
(\mathcal{M}^{0},\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})
is

H^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{0},\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})\to H^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{0})\to H^{d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})\to H^{d+1}(\mathcal{M}^{0},\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}),

which by the preceding isomorphism produces an exact sequence

H^{d-4}(\mathcal{M}_{0})\to H^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{0})\to H^{d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})\to H^{d-3}(\mathcal{M}_{0}).

If
\mathcal{A}
contains only one hyperplane, then
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}
is homotopic to
S^{3}
and the cohomology is non-zero only in degrees
0
and
3
. By using the exact sequence above and induction on
|\mathcal{A}|
, it follows that
H^{d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})
is non-zero only for
d
divisible by
3
. Moreover, when
d
is divisible by
3
, the integer
d-4
is not, and it follows that we have an exact sequence

0\to H^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{0})\to H^{d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})\to H^{d-3}(\mathcal{M}_{0})\to 0.

Now induction shows furthermore that
H^{d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})
is a free
\mathbf{Z}
-module of finite rank, isomorphic to the direct sum
H^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{0})\oplus H^{d-3}(\mathcal{M}_{0})
and so the Poincaré polynomial
p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
satisfies the recursion

p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=p_{\mathcal{A}^{0}}(t)+tp_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}(t).

2.6.
The combinatorial side of the induction

Here we simply restate the results from chapter 2 of
[
OrTe
]
, the proofs of which go through without change for arrangements in a vector space over a division ring. We recall that the
Möbius function
of the intersection lattice
L(\mathcal{A})
is the function
\mu:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbf{Z}
determined by the normalization
\mu(X,X)=1
for all
X\in L(\mathcal{A})
,
\mu(X,Y)=0
unless
X\leq Y
, and for
X<Y

0=\sum_{X\leq Z\leq Y}\mu(X,Z).

Just as in
[
OrTe
]
, chapter 2, section 2.2, we write
\mu(X)=\mu(V,X)
for
X\in L(\mathcal{A})
. We then have
(-1)^{\mathrm{rk}(X)}\mu(X)>0
for all
X\in L(\mathcal{A})
, and defining the polynomial
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=\sum(-1)^{\mathrm{rk}(X)}\mu(X)t^{\mathrm{rk}(X)}
we obtain the recursion

\pi_{A}(t)=\pi_{\mathcal{A}^{0}}(t)+t\pi(\mathcal{A}_{0},t),

which together with the initial condition
\pi_{\emptyset}(t)=1
shows that
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
. Thus

\mathrm{rk}(H^{3d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in L(\mathcal{A})\\
\mathrm{rk}(X)=d\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\mathrm{rk}(X)}\mu(X).

(2.2)

2.7.
The reformulation in terms of line systems

Each hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in an
\mathbf{H}
-vector space
V
equipped with a positive definite Hermitian form produces a
line system

L
: we take
L
to be the set of lines consisting of the lines in
V
that are orthogonal to the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
. The subspace lattice
S(L)
generated by
L
then consists of the orthogonal complements of the elements of the intersection lattice
L(\mathcal{A})
. The elements of
S(L)
are the
flats
of
L
, and we refer to the elements of
S(L)
of dimension
d
as the
d
-flats
of
L
.

Given an element
X\in S(L)
we define
\mu(X)=(-1)^{\mathrm{rk}(X^{\perp})}\mu(X^{\perp})
. Thus (
2.2
) may be written

\mathrm{rk}(H^{3d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in S(L)\\
\mathrm{dim}(X)=d\end{subarray}}\mu(X).

(2.3)

This is the form in which we will compute the Poincaré polynomials for the line systems associated with the exceptional quaternionic reflection groups.

The definition of the Möbius function implies the following recursion for computing
\mu(X)
for
X
in
S(L)
:
\mu(0)=1
and

\mu(X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in S(L)\\
Y\subsetneqq X\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\mathrm{dim}(X)-\mathrm{dim}(Y)-1}\mu(Y),

(2.4)

where
\mathrm{dim}(X)
is the
\mathbf{H}
-dimension of the subspace
X
.

2.8.
A map to
(\mathbf{H}^{\times})^{\mathcal{A}}

We will not use the remaining assertions in the body of the paper, but we include them here because they fit well with the theme. For each hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{A}
we choose a linear form
\alpha_{H}:V\to\mathbf{H}
. Together these define a map
V\to\mathbf{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
sending
v\in V
to the point of
\mathbf{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
with coordinates
\alpha_{H}(v)
for
H\in\mathcal{A}
, restricting to a map
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}\to(\mathbf{H}^{\times})^{\mathcal{A}}
which commutes with the right
\mathbf{H}^{\times}
-action on both. Now a space with an
\mathbf{H}^{\times}
-action inherits a degree
-3
differential on its cohomology algebra. In the case of
(\mathbf{H}^{\times})^{\mathcal{A}}
, its cohomology algebra is an exterior algebra on degree three variables
\omega_{H}
in bijection with the hyperplanes
H\in\mathcal{A}
, and the derivation
d
is the unique one sending
\omega_{H}
to
1
for all
H\in\mathcal{A}
.

The map
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}\to(\mathbf{H}^{\times})^{\mathcal{A}}
induces a map on cohomology
H^{*}((\mathbf{H}^{\times})^{\mathcal{A}})\to H^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})
that commutes with the differentials induced by the
\mathbf{H}^{\times}
-actions.
Moreover one checks that for any dependent set
H_{1},\dots,H_{p}
in
\mathcal{A}
, the element
\omega_{H_{1}}\cdots\omega_{H_{p}}
is in the kernel of this map on cohomology. Since this map commutes with differentials, its kernel contains
\partial(\omega_{H_{1}}\cdots\omega_{H_{p}})
for any dependent set
H_{1},\dots,H_{p}
in
\mathcal{A}
, where
\partial
is the derivation induced by the
\mathbf{H}^{\times}
-action.

2.9.
The Orlik-Solomon algebra and the algebraic side of the induction

The
Orlik-Solomon algebra

A(\mathcal{A})
is the quotient of the exterior
\mathbf{Z}
-algebra on degree
3
generators
\omega_{H}
for all
H\in\mathcal{A}
by the ideal generated by all
\partial(\omega_{H_{1}}\cdots\omega_{H_{p}})
for dependent sets
H_{1},\dots,H_{p}
in
\mathcal{A}
. The defining ideal of
A(\mathcal{A})
is homogeneous, so
A(\mathcal{A})
is a graded algebra with graded pieces that we write as
A(\mathcal{A})^{d}
, and there are exact sequences

0\to A(\mathcal{A}^{0})^{d}\to A(\mathcal{A})^{d}\to A(\mathcal{A}_{0})^{d-3}\to 0

of
\mathbf{Z}
-modules. Using these and induction on
|\mathcal{A}|
one proves that the natural maps
A(\mathcal{A})^{d}\to H^{d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})
are isomorphisms.

2.10.
The graded trace of an automorphism of
\mathcal{A}

If
g
is an automorphism of
V
that preserves the set
\mathcal{A}
of hyperplanes, then
g
induces automorphisms of the cohomology ring of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}
and on the Orlik-Solomon algebra
A(\mathcal{A})
, and the isomorphism
A(\mathcal{A})\to H^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})
constructed above is
g
-equivariant. Just as in chapter 6 of
[
OrTe
]
we obtain the graded trace of
g
on
H^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}})
:

\mathrm{tr}(g,H^{3d}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}))=(-1)^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in L(\mathcal{A})^{g}\\
\mathrm{rk}(X)=d\end{subarray}}\mu^{g}(V,X),

where
L(\mathcal{A})^{g}
is the poset of flats that are fixed (setwise) by
g
and
\mu^{g}
is its Möbius function.

3.
Reflection groups and line systems

3.1.
Hermitian forms and angles between lines

Given a right
\mathbf{H}
-vector space
V
, a
Hermitian form
on
V
is a function
(\cdot,\cdot):V\times V\to\mathbf{H}
such that

(v_{1},v_{2})=\overline{(v_{2},v_{1})}\quad\text{and}\quad(v_{1},v_{2}a+v_{3}b)=(v_{1},v_{2})a+(v_{1},v_{2})b\quad\hbox{for all $v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in V$ and all $a,b\in\mathbf{H}$.}

Thus
(\cdot,\cdot)
is
\mathbf{H}
-linear in the second variable and satisfies

(av_{1}+bv_{2},v_{3})=\overline{a}(v_{1},v_{3})+\overline{b}(v_{2},v_{3})\quad\hbox{for all $a,b\in D$ and all $v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in V$.}

Evidently
(v,v)\in\mathbf{R}
for all
v\in V
, and we say that the form is
positive definite
if
(v,v)>0
for all
v\in V\setminus\{0\}
.

For example, the form on
\mathbf{H}^{n}
given by the formula

((x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{x_{i}}y_{i}

is a positive definite Hermitian form. We will assume throughout that
V
is equipped with a fixed positive definite Hermitian form.

The
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
is

|(v_{1},v_{2})|\leq|v_{1}||v_{2}|\quad\hbox{for all $v_{1},v_{2}\in V$, with equality if and only if $v_{1}\mathbf{H}=v_{2}\mathbf{H}$.}

This holds for any positive definite Hermitian form, and allows us to define the
angle

\alpha
between two one-dimensional spaces
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
of
V
by

\mathrm{cos}(\alpha)=\frac{|(v_{1},v_{2})|}{|v_{1}||v_{2}|}

(3.1)

for any non-zero
v_{1}\in\ell_{1}
and
v_{2}\in\ell_{2}
.

3.2.
The general linear and unitary groups

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional right
\mathbf{H}
-vector space equipped with a positive definite Hermitian form
(\cdot,\cdot)
. The
general linear group
of
V
is the group
\mathrm{GL}(V)
of all invertible
\mathbf{H}
-linear transformations
g:V\to V
. It acts on
V
on the left, and if the dimension of
V
is
n
then upon choosing a basis of
V
may be identified with the group of all invertible
n
by
n
matrices with entries in
\mathbf{H}
, acting on
\mathbf{H}^{n}
by left multiplication. The
unitary group
of
V
is

U(V)=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(V)\ | \ (g(v_{1}),g(v_{2}))=(v_{1},v_{2})\ \hbox{for all $v_{1},v_{2}\in V$}\}.

3.3.
Reflection groups

Let
V
be a right
\mathbf{H}
-vector space equipped with a positive definite Hermitian form
(\cdot,\cdot)
, and let
W\subseteq U(V)
be a finite group of unitary
\mathbf{H}
-linear transformations. A
reflection
in
W
is an element
r\in W
such that

\mathrm{codim}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathrm{fix}_{V}(r))=1.

We write
R
for the set of all the reflections in
W
, and we say that
W
is a
reflection group
if it is generated by
R
. Given a reflection group
W
, we let

\mathcal{A}_{W}=\{\mathrm{fix}(r)\ |\ r\in R\}

be the reflection arrangement for
W
, and we let

\mathcal{M}_{W}=V\setminus\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{A}_{W}}H

be the corresponding (quaternionic) hyperplane complement. We also obtain a line system
L
associated with
W
whose elements are the orthogonal complements of the reflecting hyperplanes.

3.4.
The definition of
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)

Here we define the infinite family of reflection groups that accounts for all but
7
of the irreducible quaternionic reflection groups in rank at least
3
. We fix a finite subgroup
\Gamma\leq\mathbf{H}^{\times}
of the quaternions together with a normal subgroup
\Delta\leq\Gamma
such that the quotient group
\Gamma/\Delta
is abelian. If
\Gamma
is cyclic then every subgroup
\Delta
of
\Gamma
satisfies these properties. It is often most convenient to specify
\Delta
by specifying the character group of
\Gamma/\Delta
, which is a subgroup of the group of linear characters of
\Gamma
.

The group
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
is the subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{H})
generated by the group
S_{n}
of permutation matrices together with those diagonal matrices
\mathrm{diag}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{n})
such that
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma
and
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{n}\in\Delta
. When
\Delta=\Gamma
we abbreviate by writing simply
W_{n}(\Gamma)=W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
. For readers more familiar with complex reflection groups, we note that it
\Gamma
is cyclic of order
\ell
and
\Delta
is the subgroup of
\Gamma
of order
\ell/m
for some divisor
m
of
\ell
, then the group we denote
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
is often written as
G(\ell,m,n)=W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
.

We will use the following notation: given
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
1\leq m\leq n
, we write
\gamma_{m}
for the diagonal matrix with
\gamma
in the
m
th position and
1
’s elsewhere. We will also identify
W_{n-1}(\Gamma)
with the subgroup of
W_{n}(\Gamma)
fixing the vector
e_{n}=(0,0,\dots,0,1)
.

3.5.
Parabolic subgroups

Let
W
be a reflection group with reflection representation
V
. A subgroup
W^{\prime}
of
W
is
parabolic
if there exists
v\in V
such that
W^{\prime}=W_{v}
is the stabilizer of
v
in
W
. It is a consequence of the work
[
BST
]
of Bellamy-Schmitt-Thiel that each parabolic subgroup
W^{\prime}
of
W
is itself a reflection group.

Given a parabolic subgroup
W^{\prime}
of
W
, we may decompose
V
as
V^{W^{\prime}}\oplus V^{\prime}
, where
V^{\prime}
is the orthogonal complement to the fixed space
V^{W^{\prime}}
. We refer to
V^{\prime}
as the
support
of
W^{\prime}
, and the
rank
of
W^{\prime}
is by definition the dimension of
V^{\prime}
. In particular the rank of
W
is the dimension of
V
minus the dimension of
V^{W}
(so it is equal to the dimension of
V
if
W
is non-trivial and
V
is an irreducible representation of
W
).

The support of
W^{\prime}
may also be described as follows: for each
H\in\mathcal{A}_{W}
containing
V^{W^{\prime}}
we let
\ell_{H}
be its orthogonal complement. The span of all of these lines is the support of
W^{\prime}
. In this way parabolic subgroups of
W
are in bijection with elements
X
of the subspace lattice
S(L)
of the line system of
W
.

3.6.
The numerical invariants
\mu
and
e

Given a quaternionic reflection group
W
with reflection representation
V
, we define an element
w\in W
to be
elliptic
if
\mathrm{fix}_{V}(w)=\{0\}
. We then define
e(W)
to be the number of elliptic elements of
W
. Evidently we have

e(W)=|W|-\sum e(W^{\prime}),

where the sum runs over all proper parabolic subgroups
W^{\prime}
of
W
, each of which we regard as acting on its support in
V
. It is also an immediate consequence of the definitions that

e(W_{1}\times W_{2})=e(W_{1})e(W_{2}).

The invariant
\mu(W)
is defined to be the dimension of the top cohomology group of the hyperplane complement
\mathcal{M}_{W}
of
W
. More specifically, if
W
is a group of rank
n
then we define

\mu(W)=\mathrm{dim}(H^{3n}(\mathcal{M}_{W})).

Just as for
e(W)
there is a recurrence determining
\mu(W)
:

\mu(W)=\sum(-1)^{\mathrm{rk}(W)-\mathrm{rk}(W^{\prime})-1}\mu(W^{\prime}),

where the sum runs over all parabolic subgroups
W^{\prime}
of
W
(by using (
2.3
) and the recursion for the Möbius function). And just as for
e(W)
the invariant
\mu(W)
is multiplicative (by the Künneth formula):

\mu(W_{1}\times W_{2})=\mu(W_{1})\times\mu(W_{2}).

3.7.
The codimension generating function

We consider the generating function

c_{W}(t)=\sum_{w\in W}t^{\mathrm{codim}(\mathrm{fix}(w))}.

Evidently only the identity has fixed space of codimension
0
. At the other extreme, if
n=\mathrm{dim}(V)
then the coefficient of
t^{n}
in
c_{W}(t)
is the number
e(W)
of elliptic elements of
W
. By
[
BST
]
the fixed space of each
w\in W
is an element
X
of the intersection lattice: each
w\in W
can be written as a product of reflections in (some of) the hyperplanes of the arrangement of
W
containing its fixed space, and it follows that its fixed space is precisely the intersection of those hyperplanes of the arrangement of
W
that contain it.

The elements with fixed space of codimension
1
are the reflections, and those with fixed space of codimension
2
are the elements of a rank two parabolic subgroup that are not reflections or the identity; in other words, they are the elliptic elements of some rank two parabolic subgroup of
W
. In general, the number of elements of
W
with fixed space of codimension
d
is the total number of elliptic elements of rank
d
parabolic subgroups of
W
. Thus

c_{W}(t)=\sum_{d}\left(\sum_{\mathrm{rk}(W^{\prime})=d}e(W^{\prime})\right)t^{d},

(3.2)

where the inner sum runs over all rank
d
parabolic subgroups
W^{\prime}
of
W
.

3.8.
Lines and angles

As above, we let
V
be a finite-dimensional right
D
-vector space equipped with a positive definite Hermitian form
(\cdot,\cdot)
. A
line

\ell
in
V
is a one-dimensional subspace
\ell\leq V
. Given two lines
\ell
and
k
, spanned by vectors
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, as above the angle between them is the real number
\alpha
defined by

\cos(\alpha)=\frac{|(v_{1},v_{2})|}{|v_{1}||v_{2}|}\quad\hbox{and $0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2$.}

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
k
and
\ell
are lines and the angle between them is
\pi/3
. Then there is a unique line
m
in the plane spanned by
k
and
\ell
that is at angle
\pi/3
to both
k
and
\ell
.

Proof.

Choose basis elements
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
for
k
and
\ell
, such that
(v_{1},v_{1})=2=(v_{2},v_{2})
. Existence: by scaling
v_{1}
we may assume
(v_{1},v_{2})=-1
. Put
v=-v_{1}-v_{2}
. Then

(v,v)=(v_{1},v_{1})+2(v_{1},v_{2})+(v_{2},v_{2})=2-2+2=2\quad\text{and}\quad(v,v_{1})=-(v_{1},v_{1})-(v_{2},v_{1})=-2+1=-1=(v,v_{2}).

It follows that the line spanned by
v
is at angle
\pi/3
to
k
and
\ell
.

Now we prove uniqueness. Suppose
m
is a line at angle
\pi/3
to each of
k
and
\ell
, and choose a basis element
v
of
m
such that
(v,v)=2
. By scaling, we may assume

(v_{1},v_{2})=-1\quad\text{and}\quad(v_{1},v)=-1.

Since the angle between the lines spanned by
v_{2}
and
v
is
\pi/3
we furthermore have

|(v_{2},v)|=1.

Write
v=v_{1}a+v_{2}b
for
a,b\in\mathbf{H}
. We obtain

-1=(v_{1},v)=(v_{1},v_{1}a+v_{2}b)=2a-b\quad\implies\quad b=2a+1

and

2=(v,v)=(v,v_{1})a+(v,v_{2})b=-a+(v,v_{2})b.

Now

(v_{2},v)=-a+2b\quad\implies\quad(v,v_{2})=-\overline{a}+2\overline{b}\quad\implies\quad 2=-a+(-\overline{a}+2\overline{b})b.

Substituting
b=2a+1
into this gives

2=-a+(-\overline{a}+2(\overline{2a+1}))(2a+1)=-a-\overline{a}-2|a|^{2}+2(2\overline{a}+1)(2a+1).

Simplifying this implies

a+\overline{a}=-2|a|^{2}.

But also

1=|(v_{2},v)|^{2}=|-a+2b|^{2}=|-a+2(2a+1)|^{2}=|3a+2|^{2}.

Thus if
a=x+yi+zj+wk
for real numbers
x,y,z,w
then
x=\frac{a+\overline{a}}{2}=-|a|^{2}
and

1=(3x+2)^{2}+9y^{2}+9z^{2}+9w^{2}=9(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+w^{2})+12x+4=9|a|^{2}-12|a|^{2}+4.

It follows that
|a|=1
, and hence
x=-1
and finally
a=-1
,
b=2a+1=-1
.
∎

3.9.
Hyperplanes, and reflections of order
2

A
hyperplane

H
in
V
is a codimension-one subspace
H\leq V
. Our choice of form induces a bijection between lines and hyperplanes, given by the rule

\ell^{\perp}=\{v\in V\ |\ (v,x)=0\quad\hbox{for all $x\in\ell$}\}.

Given a hyperplane
H
in
V
, we choose a non-zero vector
\alpha
in the line
H^{\perp}
orthogonal to
H
and define the
reflection of order
2
in
H
by the formula

r_{H}(v)=v-\alpha\frac{2}{(\alpha,\alpha)}(\alpha,v).

Given a line
\ell
we will also write
r_{\ell}
for the reflection of order
2
in the hyperplane
H=\ell^{\perp}
. The first part of the next lemma justifies omitting the choice of
\alpha
from the notation for
r_{H}
:

Lemma 3.2
.

(a)

The reflection
r_{H}
is independent of the choice of
\alpha
.

(b)

We have
(r_{H}(v_{1}),r_{H}(v_{2}))=(v_{1},v_{2})
for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
.

(c)

We have
r_{H}(v)=v
for all
v\in H
.

(d)

We have
r_{H}(v)=-v
for all
v\in H^{\perp}
.

(e)

If
\ell
and
k
are lines at angle
\pi/3
to one another, then
r_{\ell}(k)
is the unique line in their span at angle
\pi/3
to both.

Proof.

This is all straightforward. We prove only (e). We may choose
v_{1} \in\ell
and
v_{2}\in k
with
(v_{1},v_{2})=-1
and
(v_{1},v_{1})=2=(v_{2},v_{2})
. Then by definition

r_{\ell}(v_{2})=v_{2}-v_{1}(v_{1},v_{2})=v_{1}+v_{2},

so that
r_{\ell}(k)
is at angle
\pi/3
to both
\ell
and
k
. By Lemma
3.1
we have proved (e).
∎

The order of the product
r_{\ell}r_{\ell^{\prime}}
of two reflections of order two is determined by the angle between
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
; the following lemma is a straightforward check.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
be lines in
V
.

(a)

r_{\ell}
and
r_{\ell^{\prime}}
commute if and only if
\ell\perp\ell^{\prime}
.

(b)

The order of
r_{\ell}r_{\ell^{\prime}}
is
3
if and only if the angle between
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
is
\pi/3
.

(c)

The order of
r_{\ell}r_{\ell^{\prime}}
is
4
if and only if the angle between
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
is
\pi/4
.

(d)

The order of
r_{\ell}r_{\ell^{\prime}}
is
5
if and only if the angle between
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
is either
\pi/5
or
2\pi/5
.

3.10.
Line systems for reflection groups

Given a reflection group
W
with the property that each reflection has order
2
, we may recover
W
from its line system:
W
is the group generated by the reflection
r_{\ell}
as
\ell
ranges over all lines perpendicular to a reflecting hyperplane of
W
. Conversely, given a star-closed line system
L
in
V
, the group generated by the reflections
r_{\ell}
is a finite reflection group
W
. It turns out that for the line system
L
of one of the exceptional groups of rank at least
3
specified by Cohen
[
Coh
]
, every reflection in the resulting group is the reflection with respect to some line in
L
. But this is not immediately obvious and we do not know a proof that does not use the classification and a computer check.

3.11.
Star-closed line systems

Let
L
be a finite set of lines in
V
(via the correspondence
\ell\mapsto\ell^{\perp}
, specifying
L
is the same thing as specifying a finite collection of hyperplanes in
V
). Then
L
is
star-closed
if for all pairs
\ell,\ell^{\prime} \in L
, we have

r_{\ell}(\ell^{\prime})\in L.

Assuming
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
, the lines
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
span a plane (a
2
-dimensional subspace of
V
). By the formula for
r_{\ell}
, the line
r_{\ell}(\ell^{\prime})
lies in this plane.

3.12.
k
-systems

Let
L
be a star-closed line system in
V
that contains at least
2
lines and let
k\geq 2
be an integer. We say that
L
is a
k
-system if the product
r_{\ell}r_{\ell^{\prime}}
is of order at most
k
for all
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in L
, and there exist some
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in L
so that the order of
r_{\ell}r_{\ell^{\prime}}
is exactly
k
. It is straightforward to check that
L
is a
2
-system if and only if all the lines in
L
are orthogonal to one another, and that
L
is a
3
system if and only if each pair of lines in
L
is either orthogonal to one another or at angle
\pi/3
to one another, and there is at least one pair at angle
\pi/3
.

3.13.
3
-systems

Because of part (e) of Lemma
3.2
, the star-closed line systems such that every pair of lines is at angle
\pi/2
or
\pi/3
play a special role. Those that arise in real vector spaces are classified by the simply-laced Dynkin diagrams. In
\mathbf{C}
-vector spaces, one additional infinite family arises: for the groups of type
G(3,3,n)
, the corresponding set of root lines gives a
3
-system. Moreover, there are two additional exceptional groups:
W(K_{5})
and
W(K_{6})
(the groups
G_{33}
and
G_{34}
in the Shephard-Todd notation), corresponding to lines systems
K_{5}
and
K_{6}
. Passing to quaternionic line systems produces, according to the classification theorem from Cohen
[
Coh
]
, no additional infinite family of
3
-systems and two new exceptional line systems of ranks
4
and
5
, denoted
S_{1}
and
U
in Cohen’s notation.

3.14.
The Goethals-Seidel decomposition for a
3
-star in a
3
-system

Here we describe the quaternionic analog of some of the material from chapter 7 of
[
LeTa
]
, and which is especially useful in our analysis of the flats for the groups of type
S_{1}
and
U
.

Suppose that
L
is a
3
-system in a right
\mathbf{H}
-vector space
V
equipped with a positive definite Hermitian form
(\cdot,\cdot)
, and that
a,b,c\in L
are lines at angle
\pi/3
to one another and together spanning a
2
-dimensional subspace of
V
. It follows that there are no other lines of
L
in this subspace, which we will call a
3
-star of
L
. We define the following subsets of
L
:

(a)

The set
\Delta
consists of the lines of
L
that are perpendicular to all three of
a,b,
and
c
.

(b)

The set
\Lambda
consists of the lines of
L\setminus\{a,b,c\}
that are not perpendicular to any of
a,b,
and
c
.

(c)

The set
\Gamma_{a}
consists of the lines of
L
that are perpendicular to
a
but not to
b
or
c
; the sets
\Gamma_{b}
and
\Gamma_{c}
are defined analogously.

It is clear that
L
is the disjoint union

L=\{a,b,c\}\cup\Delta\cup\Lambda\cup\Gamma_{a}\cup\Gamma_{b}\cup\Gamma_{c}.

Evidently the cardinalities of these sets depend only on the
W(L)
orbit of the
3
-star
\{a,b,c\}
. But they may well vary across such orbits, as the examples we compute next show. As a first very simple example we consider the line system of type
A_{3}
, consisting of the six lines spanned by the six vectors

a=(1,-1,0,0),\ b=(1,0,-1,0),\ c=(0,1,-1,0),\ d=(1,0,0,-1),\ e=(0,1,0,-1),\ \text{and}\ f=(0,0,1,-1).

Taking our
3
-star to be
\{a,b,c\}
we evidently have
\Delta=\emptyset=\Lambda
, while
\Gamma_{a}=\{f\}
,
\Gamma_{b}=\{e\}
, and
\Gamma_{c}=\{d\}
.

Now we show that the GS decomposition determines the
3
-flats of
L
containing the
3
-star
\{a,b,c\}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
L
be a
3
-system and let
\{a,b,c\}
be a
3
-star of
L
.

The
3
-flat generated by
\{a,b,c\}
and an element
\ell
of
\Delta
is of type
A_{2} \times A_{1}
, and the number of flats of
L
of type
A_{2}\times A_{1}
containing
\{a,b,c\}
is the cardinality of
\Delta
.

The
3
-flat generated by
\{a,b,c\}
and an element
\ell
of
\Lambda
is of type
G(3,3,3)
, and the number of flats of
L
of type
G(3,3,3)
containing
\{a,b,c\}
is the cardinality of
\Lambda
divided by
6
(in particular, the cardinality of
\Lambda
is a multiple of
6
).

The
3
-flat generated by
\{a,b,c\}
and an element
\ell
of
\Gamma_{a}
is of type
A_{3}
, and the number of flats of
L
of type
A_{3}
containing
\{a,b,c\}
is the cardinality of
\Gamma_{a}
. Moreover, for each element
d\in\Gamma_{a}
there are unique
e\in\Gamma_{b}
and
f\in\Gamma_{c}
such that the lines in the
3
-flat generated by
d
and
\{a,b,c\}
are precisely
a,b,c,d,e,
and
f
.

Proof.

This follows from Cohen’s classification: the only reflection groups of rank three containing only reflections of order two and such that the order of a product of two reflections is either
2
or
3
are the groups of types
A_{1}^{\times 3}
,
A_{2}\times A_{1}
,
A_{3}
, and
G(3,3,3)
. The group of type
A_{1}^{\times 3}
doesn’t contain a copy of
A_{2}
, and the other three are distinguished by the angles their lines make with a given subgroup of type
A_{2}
.
∎

4.
First examples of
\mu
and

4.1.
Structure of our counting techniques

In order to apply (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) to compute the
\mu
-invariant we classify and count the flats in the line systems of the exceptional reflection groups. In our count of the flats of the exceptional reflection groups, we repeatedly employ slight variants the following strategy. Given that we have counted all flats of rank
d
, we count flats of rank
d+1
by counting, in two ways, pairs
(X,Y)
such that
X
is a rank
d
flat of a given type,
Y
is a rank
d+1
flat of a given type, and
X\subseteq Y
. To carry this out, we must know how many flats of a certain type contain a given flat
X
, and know, given a flat
Y
of a certain type, how many flats of a certain type it contains. We collect some of this information here for a number of small examples that will be useful later on, while at the same time illustrating the strategy in some simple cases.

4.2.
Notation for line systems

We use the usual notation
A_{n}
for the line system of the symmetric group
S_{n+1}
acting on its irreducible
n
-dimensional reflection representation, and similarly use the notation
B_{n}
for the line system of the type
B_{n}
Weyl group. We recall that in
3.4
we have defined the complex reflection groups of type
G(\ell,m,n)
. We will also use this notation below.

4.3.
A_{1}^{\times 3}

We consider first the line system
L=A_{1}\times A_{1}\times A_{1}
: for this line system
S(L)
is a boolean algebra on three generators, of cardinality
8
. There are three elements of
S(L)
of rank two, all isomorphic to
A_{1} \times A_{1}
. It thus follows from the definition that

\mu(L)=3-3+1=1

and
e(L)=1
, in accordance with the fact that
e(A_{1})=\mu(A_{1})=1
and these functions are multiplicative.

4.4.
A_{1}\times A_{2}

Next, for the line system

, the rank one elements of

are four lines, and the rank two elements are three line systems of type
A_{1}\times A_{1}
and one line system of type

4.5.
A_{3}

For the line system

are six lines and the rank two elements are three line systems of type

4.6.
B_{3}

For the line system

are the nine lines and the rank two elements come in three types. Firstly, there are six subsystems of type

. And finally, there are four subsystems of type

4.7.
G(3,3,3)

For the line system of the group

are the nine lines, on which the group acts transitively. Fixing one of these lines, every other line is at angle

to it, and the other eight lines are therefore partitioned into four sets of two lines each, forming together with our fixed line a system of type

To compute

4.8.

For the line system of the group

(b)

and sixteen

To compute

4.9.

For the line system of the group

5.1.
A product decomposition of
W_{n}(\Gamma)

The following lemma is the key point in our first computation of
c_{W}(t)
for the groups
W_{n}(\Gamma)
, and explains the factorization we obtain. It seems likely that for the exceptional groups
W
, the existence of similar product decompositions explains the limited factorizations occurring for
c_{W}(t)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Each element
w\in W_{n}(\Gamma)
may be written uniquely as a product
w=xv
, where
v\in W_{n-1}(\Gamma)
and

x\in\{1 \}\cup\{\gamma_{n}(mn)\gamma_{n}^{-1}\ |\ 1\leq m<n,\ \gamma\in\Gamma\}\cup\{\gamma_{n}\ |\ \gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{1\}\}.

Moreover the fixed space of
w
is the intersection of the fixed spaces of
v
and
x
, and its codimension is the sum of their codimensions.

Proof.

For existence, if
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
are the canonical basis elements of
\mathbf{H}^{n}
, then
w(e_{n})=\gamma e_{m}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
1\leq m\leq n
. If
m=n
then we have
\gamma_{n}^{-1}w\in W_{n-1}(\Gamma)
. Otherwise
\gamma_{n}^{-1}(mn)\gamma_{n}w\in W_{n-1}(\Gamma)
. Uniqueness follows by comparing cardinalities.

Evidently the intersection of the fixed spaces of
x
and
v
is contained in the fixed space of
w
. Conversely, if
e_{1}a_{1}+e_{2}a_{2}+\cdots+e_{n}a_{n}
belongs to the fixed space of
w=xv
and
x=\gamma_{n}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
then comparing coefficients of
e_{n}
on both sides of

e_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+e_{n}a_{n}=xv(e_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+e_{n}a_{n})=\cdots+e_{n}\zeta a_{n}

shows that
x
fixes
e_{1}a_{1}+\cdots e_{n}a_{n}
and our claim follows. If on the other hand
x=\gamma_{n}(mn)\gamma_{n}^{-1}
then comparing coefficients of
e_{m}
on both sides of the same equation shows that
x
fixes
e_{1}a_{1}+\cdots e_{n}a_{n}
.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

For
W=W_{n}(\Gamma)
with
|\Gamma|=m
, the polynomial
c_{W}(t)
is

c_{W}(t)=\prod_{p=1}^{n}(1+(pm-1)t).

Proof.

This follows from the previous lemma by induction on

.
∎

5.2.
The polynomial
c_{W}(t)
for the groups
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
.

Here we observe that for the groups
W=W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
, the polynomial
c_{W}(t)
depends only on
n
and the orders of
\Gamma
and
\Delta
. Since
W
is a complex group (for which the claimed formula is well-known) when
\Gamma
is cyclic, we obtain the formula in general. First, each element
w \in W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
may be written uniquely as
w=\gamma^{(1)}_{1}\cdots\gamma^{(n)}_{n}v
for

and
\gamma^{(1)},\dots,\gamma^{(n)}\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma^{(1)}\cdots\gamma^{(n)}\in\Delta
. The element

may be decomposed uniquely as a product of disjoint cycles with lengths determining a partition
\lambda
called the
cycle type
of
v
. Fixing a cycle
c=(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{\ell})
with
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots p_{\ell}
appearing in this decomposition, we call the product
\gamma^{(p_{1})}\cdots\gamma^{(p_{\ell})}
of the corresponding elements of
\gamma
the
cycle product
of

. Ordering the cycles of
v
by length and then breaking ties according to the smallest element in each produces an ordered list
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{\ell(\lambda)})
of the cycle products, and it is straightforward to check the following lemma:

Lemma 5.3
.

The fixed space of
w
is of dimension equal to the number of cycle products in the list
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{\ell(\lambda)})
that are equal to the identity
1
of
W
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
d
be an integer with
0\leq d\leq n
. The number of elements of
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
with fixed space of dimension
d
depends only on
d
and the orders
|\Gamma|
and
|\Delta|
of the groups
\Gamma
and
\Delta
.

Proof.

We count the number
a(\Gamma,\Delta,n,d)
of elements of
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
with fixed space of dimension
d
as follows: for each cycle type
\lambda\vdash n
, let
c_{\lambda}
be the number of
v\in S_{n}
with cycle type
\lambda
. We must count the number of sequences
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})
of elements of
\Gamma
that have exactly
d
cycle products (with respect to
v
) equal to the identity. We may count these sequences by first specifying what the cycle products are, and then counting then number of ways to factor each cycle product appropriately. For the first step, we must choose which
d
of the
\ell(\lambda)
cycle products are equal to the identity, and then count the number of ways to factor each element of
\Delta
into
\ell(\lambda)-d
non-identity elements of
\Gamma
. Finally there are
|\Gamma|^{n-\ell(\lambda)}
ways to factor the cycle products into products of elements of
\Gamma
of the appropriate length. We thus have

a(\Gamma,\Delta,n,d)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\lambda}{\ell(\lambda)\choose d}|\Gamma|^{n-\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{\delta\in\Delta}f(\delta,\ell(\lambda)-d),

where
f(\delta,k)
is the number of sequences
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k})
of elements of
\Gamma
such that
\gamma_{i}\neq 1
for all
i
and
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2} \cdots\gamma_{k}=\delta
. This lemma is now a consequence of the next one.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Fix an integer
m
There exist functions
a,b:\mathbf{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
with the following property: for any group
G
with
|G|=m
, define a function
f(g,k):G\times\mathbf{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
by setting
f(g,k)
equal to the number of sequences
(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{k})
of elements of
G
such that
g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{k}=g
and
g_{i}\neq 1
for all
1 \leq i\leq k
. Then
f(g,k)=a(k)
if
g\neq 1
and
f(g,k)=b(k)
if
g=1
.

Proof.

We must prove that
f(g,k)
depends only on
k
and whether or not
g
is the identity, and not on the specific group
G
. We have
f(1,1)=0
and
f(g,1)=1
for all
g\in G
with
g\neq 1
, establishing the claim for
k=1
. Next observe that the inductive hypothesis implies

f(1,k)=\sum_{h\in G\setminus\{1\}}f(h^{-1},k-1)=(|G|-1)a(k-1)

and for

f(g,k)=f(1,k-1)+\sum_{h\in G\setminus\{1,g\}}f(gh^{-1},k-1)=b(k-1)+(|G|-2)a(k-1),

establishing the lemma by induction.
∎

For the group

5.3.
A fibration implies the product formula for
p_{W}(t)

Here we fix a finite subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mathbf{H}^{\times}
and let
n
vary. Write
X_{n}
for the hyperplane complement corresponding to the group
W_{n}(\Gamma)
, which is also the hyperplane complement of
W_{n}(\Gamma,\Delta)
when
\Delta\neq 1
(which is always the case if
\Gamma
is not abelian). Thus

X_{n}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{H}^{n}\ |\ x_{i}\neq 0\ \text{and}\ x_{p}\neq\gamma x_{q}\ \hbox{for all $1\leq p\neq q\leq n$ and all $\gamma\in\Gamma$}\}.

The projection onto the last coordinate defines a map
\pi_{n}:X_{n}\to X_{n-1}
. The fiber of
\pi_{n}
over
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})\in X_{n-1}
consists of all points
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x)
of
X_{n}
such that
x\neq 0
and
x\neq\gamma x_{p}
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and all
1\leq p\leq n-1
. Thus the fiber is the complement in
\mathbf{H}
of a set of
1+(n-1)|\Gamma|
points. In fact it is a fiber bundle, as follows,
mutatis mutandis
, from the proof of Theorem 5.111 in
[
OrTe
]
.

Now we consider the Serre spectral sequence of the fibration
\pi_{n}
. Since the base is simply connected, the local system of cohomology of the fibers is the constant sheaf
H^{q}(C)
, where
C
is the complement in
\mathbf{H}
of a set of
1+(n-1)|\Gamma|
points. In particular
H^{q}(C)=0
unless
q=0
or
q=3
. Moreover, the cohomology of the base
X_{n-1}
is zero except in degrees divisible by
3
, which implies that the differential
d_{2}:H^{p}(X_{n-1},H^{q}(C))\to H^{p+2}(X_{n-1},H^{q-1}(C))
of the
E_{2}
page of the spectral sequence is zero and thus that the spectral sequence degenerates. This implies that the Poincaré polynomial

of

is that of
X_{n-1}
times
(1+(1+(n-1)|\Gamma|)t)
. It therefore follows by induction on

that the Poincaré polynomial of
X_{n}
, or in other words of

, is

p_{W}(t)=(1+t)(1+(1+|\Gamma|)t)(1+(1+2|\Gamma|)t)\cdots(1+(1+(n-1)|\Gamma|)t).
