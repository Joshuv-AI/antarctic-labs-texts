Schistosoma japonicum peptide SJMHE1 promotes peripheral nerve regeneration via macrophage migrasome-derived miR-26b-5p targeting the PTEN/AKT axis
Authors: Qian Wang, Wei Zhang, Kai Zhao, Yue Liu, Shang Wang, Xingwen Chen, Dan Zhou, Juan Wang, Yu Feng, Tao Li, Wenchao Zhang, Liyang Dong, Yongbin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral nerve injury (PNI) is a major clinical challenge with limited effective therapeutic options. Our prior work has demonstrated that SJMHE1, a peptide derived from Schistosoma japonicum, promotes peripheral nerve regeneration by regulating M2 macrophages; however, its downstream molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Migrasomes are newly identified migration-dependent extracellular vesicles that mediate intercellular communication, and are abundantly produced by highly migratory macrophages. The present study investigates whether SJMHE1 promotes peripheral nerve regeneration through macrophage-derived migrasomes, with a focus on the role of these migrasomes in macrophage-Schwann cell crosstalk.MethodsThe formation of migrasomes was observed using an in vivo rat model of sciatic nerve transection injury and in vitro macrophage-Schwann cell co-culture systems. SJMHE1-induced macrophage-derived migrasomes (SM-M) were systematically characterized: transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for morphological analysis, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) for size distribution, and Western blotting (WB) for detecting migrasome marker proteins (e.g., TSPAN4, PIGK). The regulatory effects of SM-M on Schwann cell function were evaluated using multiple approaches: CCK-8 and EdU assays to assess cell proliferation, Transwell assays to measure cell migration in vitro; and a rat sciatic nerve crush injury model to verify in vivo regenerative effects (via immunofluorescence staining for S100 and NF200). Additionally, miRNA sequencing was performed on SM-M to identify key miRNAs and subsequent bioinformatics analysis was conducted to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms by which SM-M regulates Schwann cell function.ResultsSJMHE1 treatment significantly enhances migrasome production in macrophages under both the in vivo and in vitro conditions. Functional experiments revealed that SM-M improve Schwann cell function in vivo and significantly promote the proliferation and migration of Schwann cells in vitro. Mechanistically, SM-M deliver miR-26b-5p to directly target and inhibit PTEN expression, thereby activating the AKT signaling pathway to regulate Schwann cell proliferation and migration.ConclusionOur findings reveal a novel mechanism by which SJMHE1 promotes peripheral nerve regeneration through macrophage-Schwann cell communication mediated by miRNA delivery via macrophage migrasomes. This study highlights SJMHE1 as a promising therapeutic peptide for PNI and indicates that targeting migrasome-mediated communication between macrophages and Schwann cells may represent a new therapeutic strategy for PNI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07328-y.

Background

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) often causes motor and sensory dysfunction and remains a persistent clinical challenge [1]. Although the peripheral nervous system possesses intrinsic regenerative capacity, this potential is limited by intrinsic regulatory mechanisms and the post-injury microenvironment, making effective nerve repair difficult [2–5]. Advances have been made in surgical techniques such as nerve anastomosis and autologous nerve transplantation have improved outcomes but fail to activate the regenerative potential of nerves, leading to incomplete functional recovery [6]. Therefore, elucidating the intrinsic mechanisms of nerve regeneration and developing novel therapeutic strategies to overcome bottlenecks in PNI repair remain essential.

Precise intercellular crosstalk is plays a central role in nerve regeneration, and interactions between macrophages and Schwann cells are critical for coordinating axonal regrowth after PNI [7–9]. Traditionally, these interactions were attributed to rely on cytokines and extracellular vesicles (EVs) [10, 11]. However, recent studies have identified migrasomes, large membrane vesicles (0.5–3 μm) released during cell migration, as a novel mode of intercellular communication [12]. Migrasomes originate from retraction fibers at the trailing edge of migrating cells, where they accumulate cellular components before being released and internalized by neighboring cells, influencing multiple pathological processes [12, 13]. As highly migratory immune cells, Macrophages are major producers of migrasomes in vivo and in vitro [14, 15]. Emerging evidence suggests that macrophage-derived migrasomes can regulate the phenotype and function of recipient cell by transferring bioactive molecules [14]. However, whether migrasomes mediate macrophage-Schwann cell communication during peripheral nerve regeneration remains unknown.

SJMHE1, a small peptide derived from Schistosoma japonicum heat shock protein 60 (HSP60), exhibits broad anti-inflammatory effects in animal models, including inhibition of collagen-induced arthritis [16], acute and chronic colitis [17], and allergic rhinitis [18]. Excessive inflammation disrupts Schwann cell function and impairs peripheral nerve regeneration [19]. Our previous study demonstrated that SJMHE1 promotes PNI repair by inducing anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages [20]. Notably, SJMHE1 also accelerates Schwann cell maturation and myelin regeneration in vivo, but exerts no direct effect on Schwann cell proliferation or migration in vitro, suggesting the existence of indirect regulatory pathways in vivo. Whether SJMHE1 promotes nerve regeneration by modulating Schwann cell function through macrophage-derived migrasomes remains to be determined.

Here, we investigated the role of SJMHE1-induced macrophage-derived migrasomes (SM-M) in regulating Schwann cell function. We found that SJMHE1 induces macrophages to produce migrasomes that enhance Schwann cell proliferation and migration by delivering miR-26b-5p, which targets the PTEN/AKT signaling axis. These findings reveal a novel mechanism through which SJMHE1 promotes peripheral nerve regeneration and provide a foundation for its development as a therapeutic agent for PNI.

Materials and methods

SJMHE1 peptide synthesis

The SJMHE1 peptide derived from Schistosoma japonicum HSP60 (residues 437–460; sequence: VPGGGTALLRCIPVLDTLSTKNED), was synthesized and purified by SYNPEPTIDE CO., LTD Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The peptide, with a purity exceeding 99%, was used for all experiments.

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats weighing 180–200 g were purchased from Changzhou Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. All animals received humane care, and experimental procedures followed the Guidelines for the Protection and Use of Experimental Animals and the Measures for the Administration of Animal Use of Jiangsu University. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Jiangsu University (Approval No.:UJS-IACUC-AP-20190305056).

Sciatic nerve transection injury models

Rats were acclimated for one week and then randomly divided into two groups (n = 7 per group): Control group (PBS) and SJMHE1 treatment group. All animals were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane for induction and maintained with continuous inhalation of 2% isoflurane during surgery. The rat sciatic nerve transection injury model was established following our previous protocol [20]. Briefly, the sciatic nerve was transected and bridged with a silicone tube to form a 5-mm gap. A 5 µL mixture of PBS or 5 µL of SJMHE1 (10 µg) and Matrigel (BD Biosciences, MA) in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio, and each mixture was injected into the nerve conduit using a pre-cooled microsyringe. On day 7 after conduit implantation, tissue samples from the proximal stump were collected for CD68 and S100 immunofluorescence staining to assess macrophage activity; Western blotting (WB) analysis was also performed to examine the expression of migrasome-related markers in the tissues.

For localization studies, FITC-labeled SJMHE1 was locally injected at the injury site. At the 24 h post-injury, the rats were euthanized, and the injured nerves were collected for frozen sectioning and immunofluorescence staining with CD68 and S100. Fluorescence colocalization analysis was used to identify the target cells of SJMHE1.

Adoptive transfer of macrophages

The adoptive transfer protocol was based on previous studies [21]. Rat peritoneal macrophages were isolated using an established extraction method [21] and transfected with TSPAN4 small interfering RNA (TSPAN4-siRNA; GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Cells were divided into two groups: negative control (NC-siRNA) and TSPAN4-siRNA. After treatment with 0.5 µg/mL SJMHE1 for 24 h, macrophages (2 × 10⁵ cells/50 µL DMEM) were injected into the injury site of the rat sciatic nerve crush injury model. The experimental groups (n = 3 per group) included PBS, SJMHE1 + NC-siRNA, and SJMHE1 + TSPAN4-siRNA. Fourteen days after model establishment, rats were euthanized, and injured nerve tissues were collected for hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining to assess morphology, and for NF200 and S100 immunofluorescence to evaluate axonal regeneration and Schwann cell activation.

Cell culture

The mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7, rat Schwann cell line RSC96 and rat pheochromocytoma cell line (PC12, a neuronal cell model) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Typical Culture Preservation Committee, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Beyotime, Nantong, China) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Formation of migrasomes

RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with 0.5 µg/mL SJMHE1 for 24 h, and untreated cells served as controls. Subsequently, 5 × 10³ treated or untreated RAW264.7 cells were cocultured with 5 × 10³ RSC96 cells (1:1 ratio) on glass coverslips for 24 h. Following standard preparation procedures for scanning electron microscopy (SEM), migrasome formation was observed using a SEM (JSM-7900 F, JEOL, Japan).

To further assess SJMHE1-induced migrasome formation, RAW264.7 cells treated or untreated with 0.5 µg/mL SJMHE1 for 24 h were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and examined by SEM for retraction fibers and attached migrasomes. As described previously [22], cells were also stained with 1 µg/mL Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and imaged under a confocal microscope to visualize migrasome structures around the cell periphery.

For ultrastructural analysis, RAW264.7 cells treated or untreated with 0.5 µg/mL SJMHE1 for 24 h were digested with 0.25% trypsin and collected for transmission electron microscopy (TEM). After standard TEM sample preparation, migrasome morphology was examined using a TEM (JEM-1200EX; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Isolation and characterization of migrasomes

Macrophages treated or untreated with 0.5 µg/mL SJMHE1 were separately seeded into 100 mm culture dishes and maintained in conditioned medium containing serum-depleted EVs. When cell confluence reached 40–50%, cells were washed twice with PBS, digested with 0.25% trypsin, and collected into 50 mL centrifuge tubes. All subsequent steps were conducted at 4 °C. Migrasomes were isolated following a previously described protocol [15]. Briefly, the samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min and then at 4000 × g for 20 min to remove cells and debris. The supernatant after centrifugation was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, which was subsequently inverted and flushed with PBS using a medical syringe containing PBS. The resulting filtrate was transferred to a 100 kDa ultrafiltration tube and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min to collect the migrasomes. Migrasomes derived from SJMHE1-treated macrophages were designated as SM-M, and those derived from untreated macrophages were designated as M-M.

Migrasome morphology and ultrastructure were examined using TEM (JEM-1200EX; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Particle size distribution was determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using a NanoSight NS300 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK). Protein concentration in the migrasome suspension was quantified with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime), and the expression of migrasome-specific markers was analyzed by WB.

Treatment with SM-M or M-M in vivo

The rat sciatic nerve crush injury model was established according to previous studies [23]. Experimental groups (n = 3 per group) included control, M-M, and SM-M. Briefly, the exposed sciatic nerve was crushed three times with hemostatic forceps for 15 s each, producing complete transverse axonal rupture whereas preserving the epineurium. A total of 100 µL of PBS, M-M, or SM-M (containing 40 µg of M-M or SM-M suspended in 100 µL PBS) was injected into the crush site using a microinjector. Fourteen days after injury, the hindlimbs were photographed for macroscopic evaluation, and footprint analysis was conducted. Subsequently, rats were euthanized, and neural tissues were harvested for HE staining and immunofluorescence for NF200 and S100.

Footprint analysis

Sciatic nerve function was evaluated using the sciatic functional index (SFI) as previously described [23]. The hind paws of rats were coated with red ink, and each rat was allowed to walk along a paper-lined runway. Clear footprints were selected for analysis, and SFI was calculated using the following equation: SFI = (-38.3 × (EPL-NPL)/NPL) + (109.5 × (ETS-NTS)/NTS) + (13.3 × (EIT-NIT)/NIT) − 8.8. where “E” denotes the experimental foot, and “N” the normal foot; EPL, ETS, and EIT represent footprint length, toe width, and intermediary toe spacing of the experimental foot, respectively, and NPL, NTS, and NIT represent the corresponding parameters of the normal foot. The SFI value ranges from − 100 to 0, where 0 indicates normal neural function and − 100 indicates complete loss of neural function.

Immunofluorescent staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed following our previously described protocol [20].

For the transection injury model, neural tissues from the proximal stump collected on day 7 were sectioned longitudinally after paraffin embedding. Sections were incubated with rabbit anti-CD68 antibody (1:5000, GB11067, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and rabbit anti-S100 antibody (1:5000, GB13359, Servicebio), followed by goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated to CY3-tyramine(1:500, G1223, Servicebio)/iF488-tyramine(1:500, G1231, Servicebio). Each washing step was performed with PBS for 5 min.

For the crush injury model, neural tissues were collected 14 days post-surgery, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and processed for paraffin embedding system. Sections were stained with rabbit anti-rat S100 (1:200, GB113884, Servicebio) and mouse anti-rat NF200 (1:100, GB12144, Servicebio), followed by Cy3 goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:300, GB21303, Servicebio) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse (1:400, GB25301, Servicebio) secondary antibodies. Each washing step was repeated three times in PBS for 5 min.

To assess migrasome uptake by Schwann cells, RSC96 cells were first labeled with the green fluorescent dye DIO and co-incubated for 2 h with migrasomes pre-labeled with the red dye CM-Dil. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. All reagents were obtained from Beyotime. Fluorescent images were captured using a CKX-53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

To further simulate in vitro migrasome uptake, CM-Dil-labeled SM-M was co-cultured for 2 h with RSC96 and PC12 cells (1:1 seeding ratio). Red fluorescence in these cells was observed under a CKX-53 fluorescence microscope to evaluate SM-M internalization.

Cell proliferation assay

The proliferation of RSC96 cells was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Biosharp, Hefei, China) assay and 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) kit (Beyotime). For the CCK-8 assay, RSC96 cells were seeded in a 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well and cultured overnight. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing SM-M at different concentrations and incubation for 24 h. CCK-8 reagent was added, and the plates were incubated for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For the EdU assay, RSC96 cells (5 × 104) were plated in 24-well plates and treated with SM-M or M-M for 24 h, followed by staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured using an Olympus CKX-53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The EdU was calculated as: [EdU stained cells (red fluorescence) / DAPI stained cells (blue fluorescence)] ×100%.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration was analyzed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; Corning, Cambridge, MA, USA). For the transwell assay, RSC96 cells (1 × 105 in 200 µL serum-free medium) were seeded in the upper chamber of Transwell (8 μm pore size; Corning, Cambridge, MA, USA), whereas the lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently removed, and cells adhering to the lower surface of the Transwell membrane were then fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Images were obtained using a Leica ICC50 HD microscope (Wetzlar, Germany).

For chemotaxis assays, the procedure was similar except that the lower chamber contained culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS and either M-M or SM-M, which served as chemoattractants. After 24 h, the cells on the lower surface were gently removed. cells adhering to the lower surface of the Transwell membrane were fixed and stained as described above, and images were recorded using the same microscope.

WB analysis

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime) and quantified with a BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime). WB analysis was performed as previously described [15]. The following primary antibodies were used: TSPAN4 (1:1000, A10253, Abclonal, Wuhan, China); PIGK (1:1000, ab201693, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA); EOGT (1:1000, ab190693, Abcam); NDST1 (1:1000, 26203-1-AP, Proteintech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA); CPQ/PGCP (1:1000, 16601-1-AP, Proteintech); β-actin (1:2000, 4970T, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); PTEN (1:1000, 9188T, Cell Signaling Technology), AKT (1:1000, 4691T, Cell Signaling Technology); phospho-AKT (1:1000, 4060T, Cell Signaling Technology); and GAPDH (1:2000, HY-P80137, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:2000, 7074P2, Cell Signaling Technology) was used as the secondary antibody.

MiRNA sequencing and analysis

To profile miRNA cargoes of SM-M and M-M, total RNA was extracted and subjected to miRNA sequencing. Library preparation, sequencing, and bioinformatic analyses were performed by LC-Bio Technology (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the GenePharma reverse transcription kit (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (GenePharma). Relative gene expression was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to U6 for miRNAs and β-actin for mRNAs.

Cell transfection

RSC96 cells at approximately 60% confluency were transfected with 30 nM miRNA/siRNA or 1 µg PTEN plasmid using GenePharma transfection reagent in Opti-MEM medium (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Synthetic miR-26b-5p mimic, miR-23a-3p mimic, mimic control, miR-26b-5p inhibitor, inhibitor control; siRNA targeting PTEN, and nonspecific siRNA control, PTEN overexpression plasmid and control vector plasmid were purchased from GenePharma.

Luciferase reporter assay

To validate the interaction between miR-26b-5p and the 3’-UTR of PTEN, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with 100 nM miRNA mimics and 1 µg of dual-luciferase reporter plasmid containing either the wild-type (WT) or mutant-type (MUT) PTEN 3’-UTR (GenePharma). At 48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using the Luc-Pair™ Dual-Luciferase Assay Kit 2.0 (GenePharma). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase for each sample.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between two groups was evaluated using the Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SJMHE1 mediates macrophage-Schwann cell communication via inducing macrophage-derived migrasomes

In our previous studies, we have demonstrated that: (1) SJMHE1 promotes peripheral nerve regeneration through a macrophage-dependent mechanism [20]; (2) macrophages are capable of generating migrasomes [15]; (3) migrasomes are membrane vesicle structures dependent on cell migration, which participate in multiple pathological processes [13]. Based on these foundations, we hypothesized that SJMHE1 may modulate migrasome formation in macrophages, and these migrasomes contribute to nerve regeneration.

To verify this hypothesis, we established a rat sciatic nerve transection model with local SJMHE1 injection. At 7 days post-injection, compared with the control group, the SJMHE1 group exhibited a marked increase in macrophage numbers in proximal nerve tissues, accompanied by higher fluorescence intensity of S100, a Schwann cell marker (Fig. 1A). Numerous round vesicle-like structures were observed between Schwann cell-formed myelin fibers, most displaying a “tadpole-like” morphology (Fig. A1, A2, a1 and a2). These structures were CD68-positive, confirming their macrophage origin, and were internalized by Schwann cells (Fig. A3, a3). Given their larger size and characteristic morphology, distinct from conventional EVs, these structures closely resembled migrasomes as previously described [24]. Thus, we inferred that SJMHE1 may induce macrophages to form migrasomes, which then participate in intercellular communication with Schwann cells.

To further validate, we simulated the in vivo microenvironment using an in vitro coculture system. SEM revealed that, compared with the control group, SJMHE1-treated macrophages formed denser retraction fiber networks at their tailing edges, with a markedly higher number of migrasomes attached to these retraction fibers. In contrast, Schwann cells, which exhibited a flattened morphology, rarely produced retraction fibers or migrasomes (Fig. 1B). Importantly, migrasomes at the tips of macrophage-derived retraction fibers were observed in direct contact with the borders of Schwann cell bodies, corroborating the in vivo findings. WB analysis further showed that SJMHE1 administration significantly upregulated the expression of migrasome-related markers [25], including TSPAN4, PIGK, EOGT, NDST1, and CPQ in proximal nerve tissues (Fig. 1C-D). Notably, as shown in Supplementary Figure S1, we constructed a rat sciatic nerve injury model and locally injected FITC-labeled SJMHE1 (green). Co-localization analysis revealed that SJMHE1 primarily overlapped with macrophages (labelled by CD68), while minimal co-localization was observed with other key cell types involved in peripheral nerve regeneration, such as Schwann cells (labelled by S100). This partly suggests that SJMHE1 may specifically regulate migrasome production in macrophage.

To further verify that macrophage-derived migrasomes mediate the pro-regenerative effect of SJMHE1 on peripheral nerves, we first knocked down TSPAN4 (via TSPAN4-siRNA) in macrophages (Supplementary Figure S2A). TSPAN4 is a key gene essential for migrasome biogenesis, and its silencing has been shown to significantly suppress migrasome formation [26, 27]. Subsequently, we performed adoptive transfer of two distinct macrophage cell populations to the sciatic nerve injury site in rat: SJMHE1-treated macrophages transfected with either TSPAN4-siRNA or negative control NC-siRNA (called SMTSPAN4−siRNA and SMNC−siRNA, respectively). SMNC−siRNA treatment effectively promoted the process of nerve regeneration (higher number of nerve fibers at the injury site, and the upregulated S100 and NF200 expression), yet this promotional effect was impaired when adoptively transferring SMTSPAN4−siRNA (Supplementary Figure S2B and S2C). This result at least partially confirms that migrasomes released by SJMHE1-treated macrophages in vivo are functionally involved in promoting nerve regeneration.

Collectively, these data strongly indicate that, beyond directly promoting peripheral nerve regeneration by inducing M2 macrophages, SJMHE1 may mediate macrophage-Schwann cell communication by regulating the formation of macrophage-derived migrasomes.

SJMHE1 promotes the formation of macrophage migrasomes in vitro

We previously reported that SJMHE1 enhances macrophage migration [20]. As migrasome formation is closely associated with cell motility [28], we inferred that SJMHE1 treatment might increase migrasome production. As expected, we found that SJMHE1-treated macrophages formed longer retraction fiber networks in their tail regions and exhibited a significantly higher number of migrasomes (Fig. 2A-C). However, the particle size of migrasomes did not differ significantly between the SJMHE1 and control groups (Fig. 2D). To further confirm these results, we used WGA staining to visualize migrasomes. The results showed that SJMHE1-treated macrophages displayed numerous retraction fibers, with WGA-positive migrasomes localized at fiber branching points or termini. The particle size of these migrasomes was similar to that of the control group (Fig. 2E-H), consistent with SEM findings. Taken together, these data demonstrate that SJMHE1 promotes the formation of migrasomes in macrophages.

Isolation and characterization of SJMHE1-induced macrophage-derived migrasomes

To visualize migrasome formation at the ultrastructural level, SJMHE1-treated macrophages were digested, fixed, and sectioned for TEM. Results showed that migrasomes were observed as discrete structures within the intercellular spaces. Compared with the control group, SJMHE1-treated macrophages produced significantly more migrasomes containing variable numbers of smaller internal vesicles, a typical feature of migrasomes [12] (Fig. 3A).

Next, using the previously established separation and purification method [15], we isolated macrophage migrasomes (M-M) and SJMHE1-induced macrophage migrasomes (SM-M) (Fig. 3B). TEM imaging showed that both purified M-M and SM-M presented as large elliptical vesicles with surface folds and attached retraction fibers with smaller migrasomes visible nearby (indicated by red arrows) (Fig. 3C). NTA revealed distinct peaks above 800 nm in both groups (Fig. 3D), consistent with previously reported migrasome size distributions [29]. Furthermore, WB confirmed that both M-M and SM-M expressed characteristic migrasome-specific markers, including TSPAN4, PIGK, EOGT, NDST1, and CPQ. These results confirm that the large vesicles isolated from macrophages are indeed migrasomes.

SM-M promotes Schwann cell function after peripheral nerve injury

To assess whether SJMHE1 regulates Schwann cell function through macrophage-derived migrasomes, we established a rat sciatic nerve crush injury model and locally injected PBS, M-M, or SM-M at the crush site (Fig. 4A-B). Because nerve regeneration typically begins around 14 days after injury [30], we evaluated the macroscopic morphology of the ipsilateral hind paws and performed footprint analyses at this time point. Although partial spreading of the hind paw toes was observed in the SM-M group, no significant differences were detected compared with the PBS or M-M groups, as confirmed by footprint analysis (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S3). This likely reflects the early regenerative stage at day 14, when regenerating axons have not yet formed complete functional connections across the injury site, resulting in limited behavioral recovery.

Next, tissue samples collected from the crush site (Fig. 4D) showed distinct histological differences. HE staining revealed a markedly greater number of regenerating nerve fibers at the injury site in the SM-M group (Fig. 4E). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated increased expression of the Schwann cell marker S100 and the axonal marker NF200 in SM-M-treated nerves. Notably, S100 fluorescence indicated that Schwann cells established extensive connections at the lesion site, whereas axonal regrowth remained relatively delayed (Fig. 4F). This pattern is consistent with the typical sequence of peripheral nerve repair, in which Schwann cells first proliferate, migrate, and extend longitudinally to form aligned cords that guide subsequent axonal regrowth [31]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SM-M enhances peripheral nerve regeneration by promoting Schwann cell function.

Because EVs often regulate recipient cell function through internalization [32], and SM-M are membrane vesicles of similar nature, we hypothesized that SM-M might act via cellular uptake. To test this, SM-M were labeled with CM-Dil (red) and co-incubated with RSC96 cells pre-labeled with DiO (green). Following confirmation of SM-M uptake, we explored its effects on Schwann cell function. Both CCK-8 and EdU assays showed that SM-M significantly increased RSC96 cell proliferation (Fig. 5C-E). Transwell assays demonstrated enhanced RSC96 cell migration under SM-M treatment (Fig. 5F-G). Considering that migrasomes are known to exert localized signaling effects that guide directional cell movement [33], we further examined their role in Schwann cell chemotaxis. When SM-M were added to the lower chamber of a Transwell system, a markedly greater number of RSC96 cells migrated toward the lower chamber compared with controls (Fig. 5H-I). These results indicate that SM-M not only promote Schwann cell proliferation and migration but also direct their migration through signal localization, providing indirect evidence that SJMHE1 regulates Schwann cell function via communication mediated by macrophage-derived migrasomes.

miR-26b-5p functions as an effector of SM-M in promoting Schwann cell proliferation and migration

Our previous work showed that macrophage-derived migrasomes are enriched in miRNAs [15]. To elucidate the mechanism by which SM-M promote Schwann cell proliferation and migration, we performed miRNA sequencing on SM-M and M-M. The analysis identified 178 upregulated and 161 downregulated miRNAs in SM-M compared with M-M (Fig. 6A). The most differentially expressed miRNAs were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 6B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of target genes revealed significant involvement of the PI3K-AKT pathway, which regulates cell proliferation and migration (Fig. 6C). Additional pathways associated with axonal growth and regeneration, including Wnt, ErbB, and mTOR signaling, were also enriched. Given that miRNAs carried by EVs generally exert biological functions through upregulation, we focused on the five most highly upregulated candidates: miR-26b-5p, miR-17-5p, miR-20a-5p, miR-23a-3p, and miR-191-5p. Bioinformatics analysis using TargetScan and miRDB predicted that PTEN is a common target of all five miRNAs (Fig. 6D).

We then performed validation via qRT-PCR to identify core regulatory miRNAs in SM-M, and found that miR-26b-5p was the most significantly upregulated miRNA (Fig. 6E); thus, it was selected as the primary focus for subsequent analyses. Consistently, compared with the control group, SM-M treatment notably increased miR-26b-5p expression levels in RSC96 cells (Fig. 6F). To explore its function, RSC96 cells were transfected with miR-26b-5p mimics or inhibitors, and transfection efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR (Fig. 6G). Cell growth curves and EdU assays showed that miR-26b-5p mimics significantly enhanced RSC96 cell proliferation, whereas inhibition of miR-26b-5p reduced proliferation (Fig. 6H-J). Similarly, migration assays indicated that miR-26b-5p overexpression promoted, whereas inhibition of miR-26b-5p impaired RSC96 cell migration (Fig. 6K-L). We also explored the function of miR-23a-3p, another upregulated miRNA in SM-M. As shown in Supplementary Figure S4, miR-23a-3p mimics dramatically enhanced RSC96 cell proliferation and migration; however, compared with miR-23a-3p mimics, miR-26b-5p mimics exhibited a stronger ability to promote Schwann cell proliferation and migration. These findings indicate that miR-26b-5p is not only the most upregulated miRNA in SM-M, but also exerts the most prominent regulatory effect on Schwann cell functions.

Given that PTEN was a predicted target of miR-26b-5p, we next examined their expression relationship in Schwann cells. Both SM-M treatment and miR-26b-5p mimics reduced PTEN protein levels, whereas miR-26b-5p inhibition increased PTEN levels (Fig. 6M-N). Because PTEN negatively regulates the PI3K/AKT pathway, we further measured AKT phosphorylation. miR-26b-5p overexpression significantly elevated phosphorylated AKT (P-AKT) levels, consistent with the activation observed in SM-M-treated cells (Fig. 6O).

miR-26b-5p mediates SM-M-induced Schwann cell function by targeting the PTEN/AKT axis

Apart from TargetScan and miRDB, analyses using miRTarBase and miRmap also identified PTEN as a potential target of miR-26b-5p (Fig. 7A). The PTEN-encoding gene is highly conserved among humans, rats, and mice, and contains a seed sequence complementary to miR-26b-5p (Fig. 7B). To confirm whether the 3’-UTR of PTEN is a direct binding site for miR-26b-5p, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Overexpression of miR-26b-5p markedly reduced luciferase activity in constructs containing the wild-type (WT) PTEN 3’-UTR binding site, but had no effect on constructs carrying a mutant binding site (Fig. 7C). These findings confirm that PTEN is a direct target of miR-26b-5p.

We next assessed the functional role of PTEN in Schwann cells. To minimize off-target interference, four PTEN-targeting small interfering RNAs (siPTEN1-4, corresponding to siRNA-333, siRNA-653, siRNA-1069, and siRNA-282, respectively) and a negative control (si-NC) were designed and transfected into RSC96 cells. Knockdown efficiency was verified by real-time quantitative PCR and WB (Fig. 7D-E). Among these, si-PTEN1 (siRNA333) showed the strongest inhibition and was selected for further experiments. RSC96 cells were transfected with either si-PTEN1 or a PTEN overexpression plasmid (OE-PTEN) (Fig. 7F). Compared with controls, PTEN knockdown significantly enhanced RSC96 cell proliferation and migration, whereas PTEN overexpression suppressed both processes (Fig. 7G-I), indicating that PTEN acts as a negative regulator of Schwann cell proliferation and migration.

To clarify the regulatory relationship between miR-26b-5p and PTEN, a rescue experiment was conducted in which RSC96 cells were co-transfected with miR-26b-5p mimics and the OE-PTEN plasmid. PTEN overexpression alone significantly inhibited RSC96 cell proliferation and migration, whereas co-transfection with miR-26b-5p mimics reversed this inhibition (Fig. 7J-L). We further examined whether miR-26b-5p regulates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in Schwann cells via PTEN targeting. As shown in Fig 7M, PTEN overexpression significantly reduced P-AKT levels, whereas PTEN knockdown upregulated P-AKT expression. Co-transfection of miR-26b-5p mimics with OE-PTEN restored P-AKT levels compared with OE-PTEN alone.

Combined with the above results, miR-26b-5p is likely a key effector of SM-M function, promoting Schwann cell proliferation and migration by activating the AKT signaling pathway through suppression of PTEN.

Discussion

PNI remains a major clinical challenge, as the intrinsic regenerative potential of the peripheral nervous system is often insufficient for full functional recovery [1, 2]. A critical obstacle is the excessive inflammatory response that occurs during the early post-injury phase, which disrupts the microenvironment homeostasis required for regeneration and directly impairs Schwann cell function [19, 34]. Thus, strategies that simultaneously modulate inflammation and preserve Schwann cell function are essential for effective PNI treatment. Our previous work demonstrated that SJMHE1, a peptide derived from Schistosoma japonicum, promotes PNI repair by inducing anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization, thereby establishing a pro-regenerative microenvironment [20]. In this study, we extend these findings by identifying a second complementary mechanism in which SJMHE1 enhances Schwann cell function via macrophage-derived migrasomes. This “dual-mode” of action, combining anti-inflammatory regulation with functional promotion of Schwann cells, distinguishes SJMHE1 from single-target interventions and highlights its potential as a multifaceted therapeutic candidate for PNI.

As a novel cell-to-cell communication vesicle, migrasomes have attracted considerable attention in recent years [13]. Although several publications referred to migrasomes as EVs, migrasomes differ from traditional EVs (such as exosomes and microvesicles) considering the biogenesis, size, markers and functional roles [35]: (1) Biogenesis: migrasomes are uniquely formed along the retraction fibers of migrating cells and released upon fiber breakage, whereas exosomes originate from endosomal sorting complexes required for transport and are released via multivesicular body fusion, and microvesicles bud directly from the plasma membrane; (2) Size: migrasomes typically range from 500 to 3000 nm, which is significantly larger than exosomes (30–150 nm) and microvesicles (100–1000 nm); (3) Markers: migrasomes are specifically labeled by TSPAN4, while exosomes are characterized by CD63/CD81/TSG101 and microvesicles by CD40/Annexin V; (4) Functional roles: unlike exosomes/microvesicles that mainly mediate cargo delivery in static cell microenvironments, migrasomes are tightly coupled to cell migration and excel at “spatiotemporally targeted communication” during dynamic processes (e.g., guiding cell migration via directional cargo release) [26]. The rationale for focusing on migrasomes in this study is twofold: first, our previous work confirmed that SJMHE1 exerts nerve repair effects through a macrophage-dependent mechanism [20], and macrophages, a kind of highly migratory cells, are proven to generate migrasomes [15]; second, the “migration-dependent biogenesis” of migrasomes aligns with the dynamic cellular remodeling process of peripheral nerve regeneration (e.g., macrophage migration to injury sites, Schwann cell migration to form guidance tracks), making them more likely to participate in intercellular communication during nerve repair than statically released exosomes/microvesicles. Here, we observed that macrophages in the injured rat sciatic nerve release migrasomes at 7 days post-injury, and local SJMHE1 treatment significantly increased their number. Co-labeling for CD68 and S100 indicated that these migrasomes interacted with Schwann cells, suggesting a role in cell-to-cell communication. Although endogenous Schwann cell-derived migrasomes cannot be entirely excluded, our in vitro co-culture experiments confirmed that macrophages are the primary source of migrasomes, with minimal contribution from Schwann cells, consistent with our in vivo findings. Additionally, SJMHE1 also upregulated migrasome-specific markers (e.g., TSPAN4, a regulator of migrasome formation) in sciatic nerve tissue. Importantly, we further found that the effect of SJMHE1-treated macrophages in promoting nerve regeneration was attenuated following TSPAN4 gene knockdown, reinforcing migrasomes as key effectors of SJMHE1-mediated regeneration. It is well known that migrasome biogenesis is closely associated with both cell motility [28], thus, combined with our previous discoveries: (1) SJMHE1 treatment significantly enhances macrophage migration [20]; (2) the biological functions of SJMHE1 rely on its binding to receptors on target cells [16, 36], we reasonably speculate that SJMHE1 may regulate macrophage migrasome production through certain migration-related signaling pathways, which certainly requires further verification.

Directed collective migration of Schwann cells is fundamental to nerve repair and functional reconnection [37–40]. Macrophages are known to guide Schwann cell migration via vascular polarization (e.g., through VEGF-A secretion) [40], and migrasomes can act as signal effectors that regulate cell migration through spatial signal transmission [26, 33]. Here, our data reveal that SM-M exert potent signal-localizing effects, actively recruiting Schwann cells to migrate directionally. Moreover, SM-M are internalized by Schwann cells, promoting both proliferation and migration, which is consistent with the established role of Schwann cell dynamics as a critical target for PNI repair [41, 42]. Interestingly, M-M lacked these effects, contrasting with previous reports that macrophages-derived extracellular vesicles enhance Schwann cell function [43]. This discrepancy likely reflects fundamental differences between migrasomes and traditional EVs in biogenesis [24]. Notably, we found that CM-Dil-labelled SM-M can be dually internalised by RSC96 and PC12 cells (Supplementary Figure S5) in vitro, indicating that SM-M internalisation in the nerve microenvironment is non-selective. The potential direct combination of SM-M on PC12 cells suggests a possible “Schwann cell + neuronal cell” dual-target mechanism for SM-M-mediated nerve regeneration, which is a direction meriting future study.

Migrasomes are a type of membrane vesicle structure originating from the cell membrane, with phospholipid bilayers as their core component [12]. This structure can fuse with the membrane of recipient cells, thereby delivering its carried contents into the recipient cells. As a fundamental biological process, membrane fusion is universally present in various organisms. Beyond membrane fusion, recipient cells can also take up migrasomes through multiple endocytic pathways, including clathrin-dependent endocytosis and clathrin-independent pathways (e.g., caveolin-mediated uptake, macropinocytosis, phagocytosis, and lipid raft-mediated internalization) [44]. Based on the aforementioned biological mechanisms, we hypothesize that Schwann cells may take up migrasomes through multiple modes. However, the specific uptake mechanism may depend on the proteins and glycoproteins of migrasomes and recipient cells, which requires further investigation in subsequent studies.

Our previous studies found that migrasomes are enriched in miRNAs [15]. As a conserved non-coding RNA, miRNAs are known to participate in nerve injury repair [32]. Therefore, in the present study, we focused on miRNAs in SM-M. Our miRNA sequencing analysis identified a distinct miRNA profile in SM-M, with miR-26b-5p as the most significantly upregulated candidate. Functional assays confirmed that SM-M deliver miR-26b-5p to Schwann cells, where it enhances proliferation and migration, establishing miR-26b-5p as a principal effector of SM-M function. This finding expands the known roles of miR-26b-5p, which has previously been linked to cartilage protection in osteoarthritis and neural recovery after spinal cord injury via extracellular vesicle-mediated delivery [45, 46], but had not been implicated in PNI. Importantly, miR-26b-5p function is cell- and tissue-specific; while it promotes Schwann cell activity here, it can drive tumor progression in hematological and solid malignancies [47, 48]. This duality necessitates careful evaluation of SJMHE1 or miR-26b-5p-based therapies in PNI patients with concurrent malignancies to mitigate potential oncogenic risks.

To unravel the molecular mechanism underlying miR-26b-5p-mediated Schwann cell regulation, we combined bioinformatic predictions with dual-luciferase reporter assays to confirm PTEN as a direct target. PTEN, a classical tumor suppressor and negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway, inhibits cell proliferation and migration by suppressing AKT phosphorylation [49]. Downregulation of PTEN is known to enhance Schwann cell activity via PI3K/AKT activation [50], and PTEN has been termed a molecular “brake” on peripheral nerve regeneration [51]. Our data align with these findings: SM-M-derived miR-26b-5p targets PTEN, reducing its expression and activating AKT to promote Schwann cell proliferation and migration. While the PTEN/AKT axis is a key mediator, miR-26b-5p may regulate additional targets in Schwann cells, warranting further investigation.

Despite these insights, our work has limitations. First, we only used a single dose of both SJMHE1 and SM-M in animal studies. Further research is therefore required to elucidate the dose-dependent responses, half-lives, optimal therapeutic frequencies, and administration routes of SJMHE1 and SM-M. This will facilitate the determination of the optimal dosage and administration schedule for SJMHE1 or SM-M when applied as clinical agents. Second, SM-M is a complex membrane-bound structure that may contain not only miR-26b-5p, but also potentially functional proteins and other non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) [52, 53], which may synergistically regulate Schwann cell function. Dissecting the contributions of these components and their interactions with miR-26b-5p is essential. Third, the dynamic processes of migrasomes in the injured neural microenvironment in vivo remain unclear, including the spatiotemporal patterns of their release from macrophages, the efficiency and specificity of their uptake by Schwann cells, and their ultimate clearance mechanisms. Characterizing these properties is a prerequisite for evaluating its clinical translation potential. Fourth, while SJMHE1 shows promise in crush injuries and short-gap defects, long-distance nerve defects (a major clinical challenge) may require combination strategies, such as integrating SJMHE1 with engineered scaffolds (e.g., hydrogels [54]) to functionalize nerve guides, an approach needing further validation.

Conclusions

Our study uncovers a novel mechanism underlying SJMHE1-mediated peripheral nerve regeneration: SJMHE1 induces macrophages to secrete migrasomes, which serve as carriers to deliver miR-26b-5p to Schwann cells. This miR-26b-5p then targets and inhibits PTEN, thereby activating the AKT pathway to enhance Schwann cell proliferation and migration, the key processes for nerve repair (Fig. 8). Additionally, this work identifies “macrophage-derived migrasomes” as a previously unrecognized mediator of macrophage-Schwann cell crosstalk during peripheral nerve regeneration and highlights SJMHE1 as a promising therapeutic peptide for PNI.
