Assessing clinical medicine students’ acceptance of large language model: based on technology acceptance model
Authors: Fuze Liu, Xiao Chang, Qi Zhu, Yue Huang, Yifei Li, Hai Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
While large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated significant potential in medical education, there is limited understanding of medical students’ acceptance of LLMs and the factors influencing their use. This study explores medical students’ acceptance of LLMs in learning and examines the factors influencing this acceptance through the lens of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). A questionnaire survey conducted among Chinese medical students revealed a high willingness to use LLMs in their studies. The findings suggest that attitudes play a crucial role in predicting medical students’ behavioral intentions to use LLMs, mediating the effects of perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and perceived risk. Additionally, perceived risk and social influence directly impact behavioral intentions. This study provides compelling evidence supporting the applicability of the TAM to the acceptance of LLMs in medical education, highlighting the necessity for medical students to utilize LLMs as an auxiliary tool in their learning process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06232-1.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has played a significant role in the digital world, serving as a transformative force across various fields [1, 2]. Large language models (LLMs), such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT and its more recent version, GPT-4, are examples of generative AI chatbots. These models can autonomously learn from data and, after being trained on vast volumes of text data, generate advanced, humanlike language [3–5]. After extensive debates following the release of ChatGPT, there is a consensus that LLMs have a significant impact on medical education. Given that physicians will interact with patients across diverse healthcare settings and continually update their medical knowledge throughout their careers, it is essential for medical education to evolve. LLMs have been proposed as valuable tools for study assistance, medical writing, curriculum development, medical training, and clinical decision-making, serving as supplements to traditional medical education methods [6–8]. With the assistance of LLMs, physicians can enhance their ability to diagnose, prognosticate, and treat patients with complex comorbidities, a growing necessity due to the aging population [9]. However, obvious drawbacks accompany LLMs, including supplying fictitious information, encouraging academic fraud, lack of consistency, and algorithmic bias [2, 3, 10]. In the digital era, students differ significantly from previous generations. With the widespread availability of LLMs to the public, many medical students have already experimented with LLMs in their daily studies. For medical students, it is often necessary to memorize a large amount of information in a short period, LLMs can help students efficiently find relevant information on any desired medical topic, and provide personalized learning programs for students based on their situation [7, 11].

It is undeniable that LLMs are double-edged swords, offering both opportunities and challenges. While numerous reports have discussed the potential goals and directions for the application of LLMs, there has been limited practical investigation into whether and how the next generation plans to use LLMs and the key factors influencing their adoption in medical education [12, 13]. Through preliminary investigation, the usage of LLMs in medical students mainly includes helping to complete assignments or preparing examinations, finding research topics, helping to write papers, and assisting clinical decision-making [7, 11]. Guided by the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), we aimed to investigate the intention of medical students to accept LLMs and identify the influential factors affecting their acceptance [14]. The results from this investigation can illuminate the current state of LLMs usage in medical education and open new avenues for exploration with this interactive tool.

Theoretical framework and hypothesis development

Technology acceptance model

User acceptance refers to the prospective users’ willingness to adopt a technology [15]. TAM, a management psychology model derived from the theory of reasoned action, is used to investigate potential users’ predispositions toward adopting new technology [15–18]. TAM considers that individuals’ behavior is influenced by behavioral intentions, which are determined by subjective norms and attitudes [19]. The attitudes are shaped by the core factors: perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. It has been reported that TAM effectively predicts individual behavior by examining the causal relationships among attitudes, intentions, and behaviors [20]. As the central role in the field of technology acceptance, TAM is the fundamental basis for numerous later extended models, which were added new external influencers or combined with contextually associated theories. In this study, we employed TAM as the primary theoretical framework. In addition to perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, two other external factors, perceived risk and social impact are also involved in investigating their influence on medical students’ attitudes and intentions to the LLMs. As a new technology, the research into LLMs is still in its early stage. As peer-to-peer communication is an important way for people to obtain new technology, the adoption of LLMs could be influenced by social impact with network externalities [21, 22]. The potential risks related to LLMs, including bias, privacy, and academic integrity, are still notable. Risk perception, which is influenced by students’ knowledge, beliefs, and experience, could further affect their attitudes and intentions toward LLMs [8, 23]. The framework employs these factors to comprehensively elucidate the usage of LLMs in an educational setting [24]. The following sections will explore in depth the formulation of the assumptions underpinning this model.

Hypothesis development

Relationship amongst constructs

The model concept diagram is shown in Fig. 1. TAM posits that individuals’ actual use of technology is influenced by their behavioral intentions, which are determined by their attitudes, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use [14]. Attitudes toward technology reflect individuals’ affective reactions to and evaluations of its use, which are linked to intrinsic motivation [25]. The more positive individuals’ attitudes are toward technology, the more likely they are to have a strong intention to use it [26, 27]. Therefore, this study proposes the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1

Attitudes toward using LLMs in medical learning would significantly and positively influence students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Moreover, TAM suggests that attitudes are influenced by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Perceived usefulness is the belief that technology will enhance performance. When students believe that using technology will improve their efficiency and outcomes, they are generally more inclined to use it positively. Consequently, perceived usefulness is hypothesized to have a positive effect on attitude and, in turn, on behavioral intentions. Additionally, TAM identifies perceived ease of use as a crucial factor, referring to how simple and user-friendly the technology is [14]. It has a significant influence on attitudes and further causes an increase in behavioral intention [26, 28]. The ease of using technology will make students feel that it is helpful and foster a positive attitude, leading to a stronger inclination to use it in their studies [28]. Subsequently, the following hypotheses are accordingly proposed.

Hypothesis 2

Perceived usefulness of using LLMs in medical learning would significantly and positively influence students’ attitudes towards using LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 3

Perceived usefulness of using LLMs in medical learning would significantly and positively influence students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 4

Perceived ease of use would significantly and positively influence students’ attitudes towards using LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 5

Perceived ease of use would significantly and positively influence students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Furthermore, the TAM framework also incorporates perceived risk and social influence in assessing the acceptance and usage of technology [14, 29]. Risk perception is often included as an additional variable in the TAM model, affecting students’ attitudes toward and usage of new technology [24, 30–32]. If students perceive that using the technology poses a significant risk to their academic achievements or clinical work, it negatively impacts their attitudes and reduces their likelihood of using it. Additionally, social impact, which encompasses the opinions of peers and the social circle, can significantly affect individuals’ attitudes toward and usage of technology [33, 34]. This finding is not surprising, as individuals’ beliefs about new technologies are well-established as significant factors shaping their attitudes toward and usage of these technologies [35, 36]. Subsequently, the following hypotheses can be proposed.

Hypothesis 6

Perceived risk of using LLMs in medical learning would significantly and negatively influence students’ attitudes towards using LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 7

Perceived risk of using LLMs in medical learning would significantly and negatively influence students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 8

Social impact would significantly and positively influence students’ attitudes towards using LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 9

Social impact would significantly and positively influence students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Mediating role of attitude towards use

In the TAM model, attitude toward use is considered a mediating factor between external influences and behavioral intention. The mediating role of attitudes has been established in prior research. Studies have shown that attitude toward use mediates the relationship between perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use on one hand, and behavioral intention on the other [26, 37]. Additionally, it has been reported that attitude toward use is a significant mediating variable between perceived risk and social impact on one hand, and behavioral intention on the other [38–41]. Therefore, the following assumptions have been put forward based on the discussion above.

Hypothesis 10a

Attitude towards use has a mediating role between perceived usefulness and students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 10b

Attitude towards use has a mediating role between perceived ease of use and students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 10c

Attitude towards use has a mediating role between perceived risk and students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Hypothesis 10d

Attitude towards use has a mediating role between social impact and students’ behavioral intention to use LLMs in medical learning.

Materials and methods

Participants and data collection

A total of 238 medical students participated in the study, which was conducted across medical colleges in China. The participants, selected through convenience sampling, comprised undergraduates, master’s students, and doctoral candidates.

Measures

To assess students’ acceptance of LLMs and the influencing factors, an online survey was conducted in May 2024. This survey aims to investigate the usage of LLMs in medical students, including assignments, examinations, academic research, clinical work, and associated influential factors. The survey instrument comprised two sections: demographic information and the constructs from TAM, which were adapted from previous studies.

In our study, the independent variables include perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, perceived risk, and social impact. The questionnaire is provided in the supplement file 1. The items related to perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are adapted from Davis (1989) and Malik Sallam (2023) [14, 24]. The items related to perceived risk are adapted from Srivastava(2014) and Malik Sallam (2023) [24]. The items related to social impact are adapted from Venkatesh & Davis (2000) and Malik Sallam (2023) [24, 42]. The items related to attitude towards use are adapted from Edmunds (2012) [43]. The items related to behavioral intention are adapted from Moon & Kim(2001) [44]. The agreement degree on every level of respondents was demonstrated by a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “1” (Strongly Disagree) to “5” (Strongly Agree).

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 and Amos 28.0 software. Initially, SPSS was employed for descriptive and correlation analyses. Subsequently, the fitness and validity of the model constructs were assessed through reliability (Cronbach’s alpha), convergent validity, and discriminant validity, which included factor loadings, composite reliabilities (CRs), average variance extracted (AVE), the square root of AVE, and the correlations for each construct [45–47]. Then the common bias was evaluated with the methodology provided by Kock and Lynn(2012) [48]. All variables were regressed against a random variable, and their Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). Lastly, Amos software was utilized to construct structural equation modeling (SEM), which involved calculating the associations among focus variables and performing mediation analysis. For mediation analysis, a bias-corrected bootstrapping method with 2000 iterations was used to estimate direct and indirect effects and their 95% confidence intervals. Additionally, the model fit was assessed using various fit indices, including chi-square, the Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Non-Normed Fit Index (NFI), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) [36, 49].

Discussion

Although LLMs have aroused great controversy in medical education, previous studies have mostly targeted all student groups, and there is a lack of specific surveys on medical students’ attitudes and willingness to use LLMs and related influencing factors, the specific situation is still unknown, our article fills this gap [56]. This study investigated medical students; acceptance of learning and its key influential factors. The results of the empirical analysis demonstrate strong support for most of the hypotheses. Positive attitudes and high intention to use LLMs for medical students in learning were revealed with TAM. The high intention is strongly influenced by perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, perceived risk, social impact, and attitude towards using LLMs. The findings help us better understand medical students’ acceptance inclination towards LLMs in learning.

In our study, the direct relationship between perceived usefulness and behavioral intention, and the direct association between perceived ease of use and behavioral intention are not significant. However, consistent with previous studies, these psychological factors, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use significantly influence medical students’ attitudes towards using LLMs. Perceived usefulness of the new technologies in achieving destination and high user-friendly level, which have been reflected by the general agreement of the participants in this study, could encourage people to have a more positive attitude towards their application [57–59]. In our study, a significant indirect path existed between these psychological factors and behavioral intention, mediated by attitude towards use [28, 57, 60, 61]. The finding is inconsistent with previous studies of new educational technology, but consistent with a study of doctoral students’ acceptance to write using ChatGPT [4, 27, 28]. This may be due to some studies often removing attitudes from TAM regarding its weak role in mediating the effects of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use on behavioral intention [15, 62]. Besides, it may also because LLMs are still a new technology and the enjoyable experience provided by the instruments, in other words, the intrinsic motivation, facilitates the early use [25, 63, 64]. Therefore, positively assessed outcomes and user-friendly management of LLMs can increase users’ affective reactions towards LLMs and strengthen their sense of efficacy, further develop their positive attitude, and boost behavioral intention [65–67]. Besides, it’s also been found that attitude towards use directly affects behavioral intention. When medical students have a more positive evaluation of using LLMs in medical learning, they are more willing to perform the behavior. The above results demonstrated that medical students’ attitudes towards using LLMs, influenced by the perceived usefulness and ease of use, have an important effect on student’s intention to use LLMs in learning, as the mediator.

We also investigated the effect of perceived risk and social impact on the acceptance of LLMs in medical students. As LLMs depend on huge training datasets lacking transparency, it may generate biased and wrong content, or affect academic integrity, which can lead to severe consequences in clinical work and medical learning [8, 68–72]. Our study showed that perceived risk, the most influential factor among all factors, has a significantly negative impact on medical students’ attitudes towards the usage of LLMs, which emphasized that the potential technological drawbacks needed to be addressed to prevent potential severe consequences. What’s more, we also found that attitude towards using LLMs is a vital mediator between perceived risk and behavioral intentions. As a result, perceived risk counterbalances individuals’ inclination to use technologies which may lead to harm in various ways. Transparent risk management policies should be made before the application of LLMs in medical education [8, 73, 74]. For medical students, education on the limitations and potential risks of LLMs, the guidelines for using LLMs, and high-quality training datasets are necessary to address the perceived risks of using LLMs [8, 23].

Besides, social impact, including the advice of the social cycle and peers, has been proven to significantly influence behavioral intention and widespread adoption of LLMs [33, 34]. The results showed that compared with other factors, social impact is the most influential factor in the medical students’ intentions, which is consistent with previous studies, as people’s beliefs about innovative technologies have been established as the vital factor shaping people’s intentions toward new technologies [38, 75, 76].

In the future of health care, the application of LLMs will be more widespread, and the outputs of LLMs for medical uses could become more indispensable. As a result, the new technology will be a useful tool for medical students, particularly for students preferring student-centered learning [6, 7, 11, 77]. For now, the reliability of LLMs, especially for questions requiring advanced skills and knowledge is still skeptical. For some medical students who cannot correctly discern answers to professional questions, caution is advised [11]. It is worth noting that traditional assessment strategies, may not be applicable for LLMs. For example, when medical students are asked to write a self-reflection or firsthand experiences piece, LLMs are not appropriate [78, 79]. What’s more, although LLMs could help supplement medical students’ experience during the clinical phase through augmented reality, interaction with real patients and professionals is still necessary, as their reactions in real time are difficult to predict [80]. The interpersonal skills and humanistic care should be cultured in the clinical setting. Besides, because the underlying training dataset may be biased, or the inherent bias may exist in the algorithms designing process, the bias could remain and lead to biased behaviors, amplifying underlying inequalities and discriminatory outcomes [7, 81, 82]. It could aggregate existing social cultural or historical bias and result in unfair discrimination against certain individuals. Growing up in the digital age, medical students who have limited background knowledge may have too much reliance on LLMs and cannot identify errors [7, 83, 84]. As a result, they are more easily to be influenced by the bias, compared with older physicians. Therefore, algorithmic transparency for all LLMs is necessary to allow external researchers to assess and identify potential bias.

LLMs will be used more widely and have a significant impact in the field of medical education. LLMs could generate diverse medical education resources and tailored learning programs based on the students’ learning styles and comprehension levels [77]. As a result, further research on how LLMs can adjust real-time content to meet each student’s specific needs and become more interactive and context-aware learning mentors or assistants is important. Remarkably, simplifying the use of LLMs and providing use permission will let more students use them. In the area lacking educational resources, it’s necessary to focus on how LLMs can bridge the gap and distribute educational opportunities more fairly [10]. Besides, apart from academic tutoring, LLMs could be used to identify and respond to students’ emotional needs, thereby providing emotional and psychological support [7]. It’s also vital to investigate how LLMs could reduce learning anxiety and improve learning enthusiasm through a more nuanced emotional recognition and dialogue system. Given the privacy, ethical and data reliability challenges that remain with LLMs, particularly security, data transparency, and bias issues, it is significant to develop more transparent algorithms, establish clear ethical norms, improve data reliability, and provide students with courses on the correct use of LLMs in the future [11]. In addition, how to avoid the lack of personal and emotional interactions necessary to communicate with patients among medical students trained by the large language model should also be considered [73].

Several limitations are acknowledged in this study. First, while the study highlights a high willingness among medical students to use LLMs in their learning, it is exploratory and relies solely on questionnaires to measure acceptance. However, the study lacks a survey of the actual usage of LLMs by medical students, and future research should investigate medical students’ actual processes and outcomes of LLM usage to provide deeper insights. Second, as LLMs raise increasing ethical and informational concerns, and as medical students’ experience, knowledge, and skills with LLMs evolve, their attitudes and intentions toward using these tools may change over time [85, 86]. Longitudinal studies could be valuable in tracking the development of medical students’ knowledge about using LLMs for learning and how this knowledge impacts their attitudes and intentions toward LLMs. Given the strong willingness of medical students to use these tools, alongside the growing concerns about inaccurate information and ethical risks associated with LLMs, it is also promising to explore effective strategies for integrating LLMs into medical education. Developing relevant models to ensure that students use LLMs in an effective, ethical, and responsible manner is crucial.

Conclusion

LLMs have attracted more and more attention in medical education and their transformative implications make their application seemingly inevitable [87–89]. Despite the limited research on the acceptance of LLMs among medical students, our study provides valuable insights. Utilizing descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and SEM grounded in TAM, we investigated the acceptance of LLMs and the factors influencing this acceptance among medical students. Our findings reveal that medical students strongly intend to use LLMs for learning. This intention is significantly influenced by their attitudes, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, social impact, and perceived risk. This research contributes to the field by advancing both theoretical and practical understanding of LLMs in medical education. It enriches the theoretical framework by incorporating additional factors into the TAM and provides practical guidance for integrating LLMs into medical learning environments.

From a theoretical perspective, perceived risk and social impact were integrated into extended TAM to investigate acceptance and utilization. We reveal multiple insights into the acceptance and utilization of LLMs by medical students. This approach enriches the existing theoretical framework by identifying key factors that influence medical students’ attitudes, and intentions toward LLMs. It reveals nuanced relationships among perceived usefulness, perceived risk, social influence, attitudes, and behavioral intention, thereby deepening our understanding of the dynamics of LLMs adoption. Additionally, the study advances theoretical discussions about the role of LLMs in medical learning, highlighting the shifts in the learning environment due to these technologies. The theoretical significance of this study highlights the evolving nature of medical students’ learning experiences with the development of LLMs and underscores the necessity for practical frameworks that address the multifaceted aspects of LLMs integration.

Practically speaking, the results offer valuable insights for technology developers, policy-makers, and medical students on the effective use of LLMs for learning. The strong willingness of medical students to integrate LLMs into their educational activities indicates that these tools can significantly enhance their learning experience as complementary aids in the medical field. Given the substantial influence of attitudes on students’ behavioral intentions to use LLMs, it is crucial for teachers, policy-makers, and technology developers to understand and positively influence these attitudes. By improving the positive evaluation and emotional response of medical students toward LLMs, stakeholders can foster a more effective and supportive learning environment. For example, technology developers can enhance LLMs by making them more innovative, engaging, and functionally diverse, which would foster more positive attitudes and boost learners’ intrinsic motivation. Given the growing concerns about information accuracy, ethical issues, and potential learning risks associated with LLMs, it is essential to implement measures to mitigate these negative impacts. For instance, developers should focus on improving quality control mechanisms for the content generated by LLMs to ensure reliability and accuracy. Similarly, teachers must provide training on the effective, ethical, and responsible use of LLMs in medical learning. Since social impact significantly affects the adoption of LLMs, highlighting the benefits of these tools through public activities, such as collating topic reports or paper outlines, and polishing papers, may support the use of LLMs in medical students’ learning. In addition, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness were found to have significant impacts on students’ attitudes, which may further influence students’ intention to use LLMs. Sequential and cyclic relationships among variables indicate that technology developers should focus on enhancing both the functionality and ease of use of LLMs to ensure they are user-friendly and effective. For instance, developers can continuously refine and simplify LLM operations based on user feedback and provide clear, practical instructions or examples for their effective and ethical application in learning. Policymakers should support the use of LLMs in the medical field by considering students’ needs and integrating these technologies with educational objectives rather than imposing restrictions. Teachers and university administrators should work towards incorporating LLMs into medical curricula and training programs, leveraging these tools to enhance medical students’ learning experiences and capabilities.
