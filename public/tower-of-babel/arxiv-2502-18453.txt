Shift orbifolds, decompactification limits, and lattices
Yann Proto

Abstract
We describe the general shift orbifold of a Narain CFT and use this to investigate decompactification limits in the heterotic Narain moduli space. We also comment on higher rank theories and describe some applications to the CFT based on the Leech lattice and its shift orbifolds.



1
Introduction

The main purpose of this note is to clarify the consistency conditions in a large class of orbifold conformal field theories (CFTs). A familiar way to identify necessary conditions is by demanding that the partition function of the orbifold theory is modular invariant, but the arguments for modular invariance can be subtle, nor is it clear that they are sufficient: for instance, a putative partition function can have negative or non-integer coefficients in its expansion while being modular-invariant. A recent discussion of such issues can be found in
[
1
]
.

We will consider a particularly simple class of orbifolds, where the parent theory is just the Narain heterotic CFT
[
2
]
. In such a theory the Hilbert space decomposes into sectors
{\cal H}=\oplus_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}{\cal H}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
labeled by lattice points
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\Gamma
, an even self-dual lattice of signature
d,d+16
, and we take a “shift orbifold,” by which we mean that we gauge the action of a finite symmetry group
G
, and this action is block-diagonal with respect to the decomposition.

Typically we use orbifold CFTs to construct new theories with new spectra of operators and new global symmetries. General shift orbifolds of Narain CFT do not really provide us with new theories: if the orbifold is consistent, then it yields another Narain CFT, which we could also obtain by moving in the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{d,d+16}
of the parent theory. Nevertheless, we think the results obtained here are instructive for several reasons. First, we hope that a complete solution in this case can be extended to more general asymmetric orbifolds of Narain CFTs
[
3
]
to recover and clarify the known consistency conditions; the perspective could potentially be useful in the more ambitious goal of classifying such orbifolds. Second, we expect that the lessons learned can also be applied to more general parent theories where the orbifold action involves a shift in a toroidal sector which is combined with another action in a different sector, which might be realized by a non-linear sigma model with a curved target space or a Gepner model.

3.1
Orbifold consistency conditions

Having described the most general symmetry we wish to consider, we now turn to gauging
G

G

In the heroic days the most concrete check of the consistency of such a procedure was obtained by calculating the partition function of the orbifolded theory and checking its modular invariance. In certain simple situations one could use modular orbits to construct a modular--invariant partition function and then interpret it as a sum over twisted sectors.
6
6

6

A lucid early review of this can be found in

The more modern categorical perspective gives a more satisfying in-principle answer: we can think of the gauging as an introduction of a background field for the discrete group

7
7

7

A useful discussion and additional references can be found in

We can follow a less technical approach because there is a particularly simple way to directly construct the unprojected twisted Hilbert spaces for each

Although it is not as general, this construction has the advantage over the approach based on modular orbits of the partition function: the Hilbert space interpretation of the twisted sector operators is built–in from the start, and there is no need to check positivity conditions on expansion coefficients in a putative partition function. Moreover, since we will verify that the resulting vertex operators are labeled by an even self-dual lattice, we can be sure that the operators are mutually local and have an associative OPE that closes.

At any point in the moduli space the CFT has a Kac-Moody symmetry

An orbifold theory is always equipped with a

4.3
Decompactification limits and T-dualities

We now have the tools to resolve our initial puzzle of determining which ten-dimensional shift orbifolds switch between the two lattices
\Lambda_{8}+\Lambda_{8}
and
\Lambda_{16}

Starting from a given point

and a Weyl symmetry

\displaystyle=\frac{R({{A}})}{r^{2}+\frac{1}{2}{{A}}\cdot{{A}}}+R(\boldsymbol{q})~

\displaystyle R(\tfrac{2}{{{A}}\cdot{{A}}}{{A}}+\boldsymbol{q})~.

Our choice of

1.

2.

The second possibility is when

for the

When

3.

Outside the two special cases above, (

An iterative method

Applying the above procedure to the Wilson line parameter (
4.4
) will identify the
r\to 0
decompactification limit with one of the two ten-dimensional heterotic strings. In fact, we can forget about the nine-dimensional description and rephrase this method at the level of the lattice
\Lambda
. We start with a given shift vector
s={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}/k
associated with a cyclic shift orbifold of order
k
. Since translations of
s
by lattice vectors do not affect the phase by which the orbifold acts on vertex operators, we can take
s
to be in the maximal torus of the gauge group. Moreover, instead of
s
, we could pick any Weyl rotated shift vector
R(s)
—this amounts to working in a T-dual frame. Hence, without loss of generality, we can restrict to a shift vector inside the fundamental alcove:

\displaystyle s=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{k}\;\in\mathfrak{M}~.

(4.23)

We now define a sequence
\{s_{\text{i}}\}_{\text{i}\in\mathbb{N}}
of vectors
s_{\text{i}}\in\mathfrak{M}
as follows. We start with
s_{0}=s
. At each step, if
s_{\text{i}}
is non-zero, then we take
s_{\text{i}+1}
to be a representative in the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}
of the vector

\displaystyle\frac{2s_{\text{i}}}{s_{\text{i}}\cdot s_{\text{i}}}~.

(4.24)

If
s_{\text{i}}=0
, we instead define
s_{\text{i}+1}=0
. We claim that this sequence converges to a fixed point after a finite number of steps. In fact, for
\text{i}\geq k
we either have
s_{\text{i}}=0
or
s_{\text{i}}={\mathfrak{m}}
. In the first case, the orbifold theory is described by the same lattice
\Lambda
as the parent theory. In the second case, the shift orbifold leads to an interchange of the two lattices
\Lambda_{8}+\Lambda_{8}
and
\Lambda_{16}
.

To prove this statement, we first observe that at each step we can write
s_{\text{i}}
in the form

\displaystyle s_{\text{i}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}}{k_{\text{i}}}~,

(4.25)

where
k_{\text{i}}
is a positive integer and
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}
is a lattice element satisfying the condition
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}\in 2k_{\text{i}}{\mathbb{Z}}
. This is simply shown by induction. The initial case follows from the orbifold consistency conditions (
3.16
). Then at each step, we define
16
16

16

When
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}=0
, we instead take
k_{\text{i}+1}=1
. Since
s_{\text{i}+1}=0
the equality (
4.25
) is trivial in this case.

\displaystyle k_{\text{i}+1}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}}{2k_{\text{i}}}~.

(4.26)

By the induction hypothesis,
k_{\text{i}+1}
is a positive integer. We can use it to write the norm of
s_{\text{i}}
as
s_{\text{i}}\cdot s_{\text{i}}=2k_{\text{i}+1}/k_{\text{i}}
. By construction,
s_{\text{i}}
belongs to the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}
, and consequently its norm is bounded by
s_{\text{i}}\cdot s_{\text{i}}\leq 2
. Therefore, we must have

\displaystyle k_{\text{i}+1}\leq k_{\text{i}}~,

(4.27)

and the equality
k_{\text{i}+1}=k_{\text{i}}
holds if and only if

. Since
s_{\text{i}+1}
represents the vector
\frac{2}{s_{\text{i}}\cdot s_{\text{i}}}s_{\text{i}}
in the fundamental domain, we can write it as

, for some Weyl symmetry

and a given lattice vector
\boldsymbol{q}_{\text{i}}\in\Lambda
. This allows us to recast
s_{\text{i}+1}
as

\displaystyle s_{\text{i}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}+1}}{k_{\text{i}+1}}~,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}+1}

\displaystyle=R_{\text{i}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}+k_{\text{i}+1}\boldsymbol{q}_{\text{i}})~.

(4.28)

Using (

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}+1}=2k_{\text{i}+1}(k_{\text{i}}+{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_{\text{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{q}_{\text{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_{\text{i}}k_{\text{i}+1})

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\text{i}+1}\in 2k_{\text{i}+1}{\mathbb{Z}}~,

(4.29)

achieving the proof of the statement (
4.25

\{s_{\text{i}}\}
follows: if at the i-th step the vector

is not equal to
0

{\mathfrak{m}}
, then at the next step we have

with

a positive integer

than

equals
0

{\mathfrak{m}}

and lattice elements

appears as a decompactification limit.

An aside: rational Wilson lines and decompactification

Using the above construction we can characterize for which values of
{{A}}
the
r\to 0
limit is a decompactification limit. This will happen if and only if the Wilson line parameter
{{A}}
is
rational
—i.e. if it has rational coefficients when expanded in a basis of simple roots (or equivalently, of coweights).

When the sequence of T-dualities introduced at the beginning of this subsection converges,
{{A}}
is related to
0
or
{\mathfrak{m}}
by a finite number of lattice vectors shifts and Weyl transformations. As a consequence, such an
{{A}}
must be rational. Conversely, consider an arbitrary rational Wilson line

\displaystyle{{A}}=\sum_{i=1}^{16}\frac{m_{i}}{n_{i}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}~,

(4.30)

where
m_{i}
and
n_{i}
are coprime integers for all
i=1,\dots,16
. We define
k=\text{lcm}(n_{1},\dots,n_{16})
as the least common multiple of the integers in the denominators. In terms of
k
, the Wilson line parameter takes the form
{{A}}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}/k
for some lattice vector
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
. Let us apply the algorithm introduced above to
{{A}}
. We obtain a sequence of Wilson line parameters
\{{{A}}_{\text{i}}\}_{\text{i}\in\mathbb{N}}
, related to the initial one by a chain of T-dualities parametrized by
\boldsymbol{q}_{\text{i}}
and
R_{\text{i}}
.

In order to prove the convergence of such a sequence, we relied on the condition
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in 2k{\mathbb{Z}}
—it allowed us to define
k_{1}
at the first step by the equation
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=2kk_{1}
. For a generic choice of rational Wilson line (
4.30
), this condition is not satisfied. Nevertheless, this is easily remedied by defining
k_{1}
to be the numerator of
\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2k}\in\mathbb{Q}
when expressed as an irreducible fraction. Doing so,
s_{1}
can be recast in the form (
4.25
), and the corresponding lattice element
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}
satisfies the condition
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}\in 2k_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}
. The rest of the proof carries over, and the sequence of Wilson line converges to either
0
or
{\mathfrak{m}}
. This completes the analysis of
r\to 0
limits of
[
4
]
: we see that in the presence of a rational Wilson line, the
r\to 0
limit of the nine-dimensional heterotic theory is always a decompactification limit.

Let us note that the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{1,17}
of the Narain CFT is a locally symmetric space: its simply-connected cover
\operatorname{Gr}(1,17)
is the quotient of the Lie group
\operatorname{O{}}(1,17)
by its maximally compact subgroup
\operatorname{O{}}(1)\times\operatorname{O{}}(17)
. As explained in
[
24
,
25
]
, the boundary of such spaces can be characterized in terms of rational parabolic subgroups of the underlying group—here,
\operatorname{O{}}(1,17)
. The analysis of
[
24
,
25
]
applies more generally to heterotic compactifications on
T^{d}
and the associated Narain CFTs, whose moduli space is
\mathfrak{M}_{d,d+16}
. It would be interesting to explore the relation between shift orbifolds and decompactification limits in this lower dimensional setting, and to characterize in detail rational parabolic subgroups of
\operatorname{O{}}(d,d+16)
and their T-duality orbits.

Shift orbifolds of Niemeier CFTs

In this final section we generalize some of our previous constructions to higher rank lattices and show that they lead to a systematic understanding of shift orbifolds of the Leech CFT.

One of the most interesting results of
[
10
]
is that any two even self-dual lattices of the same rank and signature can always be connected by the shifting method. Euclidean even self-dual lattices exist in any dimension divisible by
8
. The lattice
\Lambda_{8}
is the unique example in dimension
8
. The statement of
[
10
]
becomes non-trivial in dimension
16
, where there are two possibilities:
\Lambda_{8}+\Lambda_{8}
and
\Lambda_{16}
. We confirmed in section
4
the existence of shift orbifolds between the corresponding Narain theories. The next simplest examples can be found in dimension
24
.

Euclidean even self-dual lattices of rank
24
have been classified in
[
26
]
and are known as Niemeier lattices. The list comprises twenty-four distinct lattices, including the famous example discovered by Leech in
[
27
]
. To every Niemeier lattice
\Lambda
we can associate a conformal field theory: the free theory of twenty-four holomorphic chiral bosons compactified on
\Lambda
. These theories appear in the Schellekens classification of meromorphic
c=24
CFTs
[
28
]
.

Shift orbifolds of Niemeier CFTs, or closely related constructions disguised under various names, have been used to great effect in the physics literature. Several constructions of the Leech lattice—including the “holy constructions” of
[
29
]
—find a simple interpretation in the orbifold context, as we will describe below. CFT generalizations of such constructions have been used in the study of vertex operator algebras, and involve the so-called “generalized deep holes” of the Leech vertex operator algebra
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. A recent application to constructions of isolated CFTs and heterotic islands is given in
[
33
]
, and this reference also includes an overview of some additional appearances of the Leech lattice in physics.

Our aim will be to generalize methods from the previous section to the
c=24
Niemeier theories. In particular, we will obtain a pragmatic answer to the following question: when performing a cyclic shift orbifold of the Leech CFT, which Niemeier theory does one obtain? To frame the question, we first review some well-known results from the theory of even self-dual lattices, emphasizing the connections to shift orbifolds along the way.

5.1
Niemeier lattices

There are twenty-four even self-dual lattices with positive definite signature and rank
24
. Any such lattice
\Lambda
is fully characterized by its root system

\displaystyle\left\{\left.{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\in\Lambda~\right|~{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\cdot{{\boldsymbol{L}}}=2\right\}~.

(5.1)

The Leech lattice
\Lambda_{\text{L}}
is the unique Niemeier lattice without roots. In the other twenty-three cases the roots of
\Lambda
span a sublattice

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}=\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}+\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}+\dots+\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}_{M}~,

(5.2)

where each factor denotes the root lattice associated to a simply laced Dynkin diagram. This sublattice has full rank
\operatorname{rk}\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}+\dots+\operatorname{rk}\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}_{M}=24
, and all of its components share the same Coxeter number
h
. The lattice
\Lambda
can be reconstructed by gluing to
\mathfrak{g}
appropriate elements of the discriminant group
\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ast}/\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}
.
18
18

18

We will not need the detailed form of these glue vectors. Their expression can be found in the classic reference
[
34
]
.
We denote the corresponding Niemeier lattice by
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
. The gluing construction does not add any new root to the lattice, so the total number of roots of
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
is equal to
24\,h
. The twenty-four Niemeier lattices are the nodes in figure

For each Niemeier lattice with roots
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
, the Weyl vector
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, defined as half the sum of all positive roots, is an element of the lattice

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=2h(h+1)~.

(5.3)

The inner product of

with any root of

is equal to the height of the root, and any positive root

satisfies

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\left\{1,2,\dots,h-1\right\}~.

(5.4)

The Borcherds construction

Starting from any of the twenty-three Niemeier lattices with roots
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
, the Weyl vector can be used to construct the Leech lattice as a cyclic shift orbifold of
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
of order
h
—the Coxeter number of
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
. The twenty-three resulting descriptions of the Leech lattice are known as its “holy constructions”
[
29
,
35
]
, and each can be understood as a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{h}
shift orbifold with shift vector
s=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{h}
, which, as a result of (
5.3
), satisfies the orbifold consistency condition
s\cdot s\in\frac{2}{h}{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Setting
\zeta=-1/h
, the invariant lattice takes the form

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}})_{\text{inv}}=\left\{\left.{{\boldsymbol{L}}}+\tfrac{\ell}{h}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}~\right|~{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\in\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}~,~\ell=0,1,\dots,h-1~,~{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in h{\mathbb{Z}}-\ell\right\}~.

(5.5)

The roots of the Niemeier lattice do not survive the projection to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{h}
-invariant states, due to the property (
5.4
), and the twisted sectors do not contain any root; since
(\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}})_{\text{inv}}
is an even-self dual lattice of rank
24
without roots, it must be isomorphic to the Leech lattice.

5.2
Orbifolds of the Leech CFT

We now turn our attention to the Leech CFT and its shift orbifolds. We saw in the previous section that the Leech theory can be obtained as a cyclic orbifold of any of the other Niemeier CFTs, using the Weyl vector of the Niemeier lattice. Following the discussion in section
3.3
, the corresponding quantum symmetries provide us with twenty-three shift orbifolds of the Leech CFT, leading to distinct
c=24
Niemeier theories.

Our aim will be to characterize, given a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{k}
shift symmetry of the Leech CFT, which of the twenty-four Niemeier CFTs describes the orbifold theory. For this purpose it is useful to introduce additional lattice machinery. Given a Euclidean lattice
\Lambda
, its Voronoi cell
V_{\Lambda}
is the convex polytope in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
defined as

\displaystyle\text{V}_{\Lambda}=\left\{\left.v\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}~\right|~v\cdot v\leq(v-{{\boldsymbol{L}}})\cdot(v-{{\boldsymbol{L}}})\quad\text{ for all }{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\in\Lambda\right\}~.

(5.8)

The Voronoi cell contains all the points which are closer to the origin than to any other lattice point, and it provides a fundamental region for the lattice
\Lambda
that is preserved by lattice automorphisms:
R(\text{V}_{\Lambda})=V_{\Lambda}
for any
R\in\operatorname{O{}}(\Lambda)
. A point whose distance from the lattice is a local maximum is a vertex of
V_{\Lambda}
(or its translate by a lattice element), and is called a
hole
. The points which are the furthest away from the lattice are called
deep holes
, and their distance from the lattice is the covering radius
r_{\Lambda}
:
19
19

19

This is the smallest radius such that spheres centered at lattice points cover the whole vector space.

\displaystyle\text{r}_{\Lambda}=\operatorname{sup}\left\{\left.\sqrt{v\cdot v}~\right|~v\in V_{\Lambda}\right\}~.

(5.9)

The lattice points located at distance
\text{r}_{\Lambda}
of a deep hole are the vertices of this deep hole.

We already encountered two examples of Voronoi cells in our description of the heterotic moduli space in section
4.2
:

\displaystyle V_{\Lambda_{8}+\Lambda_{8}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{R\in\operatorname{O{}}(\Lambda_{8}+\Lambda_{8})}R\left(\mathfrak{M}_{(\operatorname{E}_{8}\times\operatorname{E}_{8})\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}\right)~,

\displaystyle V_{\Lambda_{16}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{R\in\operatorname{O{}}(\Lambda_{16})}R\left(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{Spin}(32)/{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}\right)~.

(5.10)

The deep holes of
\Lambda_{8}+\Lambda_{8}
which share a vertex at the origin are the Weyl images of
\mathfrak{m}_{\text{\tiny{E}}}
, while the deep holes of
\Lambda_{16}
near the origin are the Weyl images of
\mathfrak{m}_{\text{\tiny{S}}}
. The covering radius of both lattices is equal to
\text{r}_{\Lambda_{8}+\Lambda_{8}}=\text{r}_{\Lambda_{16}}=\sqrt{2}
. The above description of the Voronoi cell of a lattice as a the union of all images of a simplex
\mathfrak{M}
under the Weyl group is common to all root lattices, and the region
\mathfrak{M}
is called a fundamental simplex. We refer the reader to
[
38
,
39
]
for additional details.

The deep holes of the Leech lattice
\Lambda_{\text{L}}
exhibit a rich structure uncovered in
[
40
]
. The covering radius of
\Lambda_{\text{L}}
is
\text{r}_{\Lambda_{\text{L}}}=\sqrt{2}
, and hence any deep hole
\mathfrak{m}
near the origin satisfies

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\cdot\mathfrak{m}=2~.

(5.11)

For a given deep hole
\mathfrak{m}
, its vertices (i.e. the lattice points
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\Lambda_{\text{L}}
satisfying
(\boldsymbol{\nu}-\mathfrak{m})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}-\mathfrak{m})=2
) satisfy remarkable properties. The distance between two distinct vertices
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}
can only take the following values:

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\nu})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\nu})\in\{4,6,8\}~.

(5.12)

To any deep hole
\mathfrak{m}
, we can assign a
hole diagram
as follows. We label the vertices of
\mathfrak{m}
as
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i}
, where the index
i
runs over the total number of vertices. We associate a node of the diagram to each vertex. The number of lines between two nodes is given by

\displaystyle N_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j})-2~.

(5.13)

The hole diagram of any deep hole is an extended Coxeter–Dynkin diagram, and there are twenty-three possibilities for the corresponding root system, in exact correspondence with the twenty-three root systems of Niemeier lattices
[
40
]
.

The extended Coxeter-Dynkin diagram associated to
\mathfrak{g}

\mathfrak{g}
by the corresponding extended diagram.
Any two holes with the same hole diagram can be related by automorphisms of the Leech lattice, and hence under
\operatorname{O{}}(\Lambda_{\text{L}})
there are exactly twenty-three orbits of deep holes close to the origin. Finally, for any a deep hole
\mathfrak{m}
with diagram having Coxeter number

is a primitive element in

.

Leech deep holes and shift orbifolds

Consider a shift orbifold of the Leech theory, where we take
s
to be a deep hole of type
\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}
, i.e. whose hole diagram is the extended Coxeter–Dynkin diagram of
\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}
and has Coxeter number
h
. Without loss of generality we assume that
s
is in the Voronoi cell, and hence that
s\cdot s=2
. The vertices of the deep hole are located at a distance
({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i}-s)\cdot({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i}-s)=2
from
s
and therefore satisfy

\displaystyle s\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}~.

(5.14)

Since
h\,s\in\Lambda_{\text{L}}
, we can perform a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{h}
shift orbifold of the Leech CFT using
s
, leading to

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{\text{L}})_{\text{inv}}=\left\{\left.{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\in\Lambda_{\text{L}}~\right|~{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\cdot s\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}+\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\{s\}~.

(5.15)

The invariant lattice contains the roots
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}=-{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i}+s
that span a root system isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}
, and since the invariant lattice is even self-dual, it must be isomorphic to the Niemeier lattice

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{\text{L}})_{\text{inv}}\simeq\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}~.

(5.16)

Hence, any shift orbifold of the Leech theory with
s
a deep hole leads to one of the twenty-three other Niemeier CFTs
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
, with
\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}
given by the hole diagram of
s
. This shift symmetry is in fact the quantum symmetry of the Borcherds construction reviewed above, where the Leech theory is recovered as a specific shift orbifold of the
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
theory.

A characterization of Leech shift orbifolds

We can now obtain a complete picture of shift orbifolds of the Leech CFT. Consider a cyclic
{\mathbb{Z}}_{k}
orbifold with shift vector
s
. Which of the Niemeier CFTs describes the orbifold theory?

The answer is obtained using an algorithmic method, similar to the one described in section
4
. We can take
s
to be in the Voronoi cell, since for any vector
{{\boldsymbol{L}}}
of the Leech lattice,
s
and
s+{{\boldsymbol{L}}}
describe the same orbifold. In particular, the shift vector satisfies

\displaystyle s\cdot s\leq 2~.

(5.17)

We fix an embedding of the Leech lattice in the Lorentzian lattice

\displaystyle\Gamma=\Lambda_{1,1}+\Lambda_{\text{L}}~,

(5.18)

such that any
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\Gamma
is uniquely written as
{\boldsymbol{p}}=\text{w}{{\boldsymbol{e}}}+\text{n}{{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{\ast}+{{\boldsymbol{L}}}
, where
\text{w},\text{n}
are integers and
{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\in\Lambda_{\text{L}}
. The Leech lattice can be obtained by considering the sublattice
{{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{\perp}\subset\Gamma
of points
{\boldsymbol{p}}
orthogonal to
{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
(those with
\text{n}=0
), and modding out this sublattice by
{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
(which allows to also set
\text{w}=0
). Hence, we trivially have the isomorphism

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{L}}\simeq{{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{\perp}/{{\boldsymbol{e}}}~.

(5.19)

Similarly, we can describe the invariant lattice
(\Lambda_{\text{L}})_{\text{inv}}
as a sublattice of
\Gamma
. To each lattice point of the orbifold theory
\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}={{\boldsymbol{L}}}+\ell s
, with
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
, we associate the element
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}}\in\Gamma
defined as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}}=\ell{{\boldsymbol{e}}}+(s\cdot{{\boldsymbol{L}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\ell s\cdot s){{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{\ast}+{{\boldsymbol{L}}}~,

(5.20)

which has norm
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}}=\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}\cdot\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}
. Such a point is orthogonal to the primitive null vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}=k\left({{\boldsymbol{e}}}-\tfrac{s\cdot s}{2}{{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{\ast}-s\right)~.

(5.21)

Moreover, any vector
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\Gamma
orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{f}
can be uniquely written as
{\boldsymbol{p}}={\boldsymbol{p}}_{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}}+\widetilde{\text{w}}\boldsymbol{f}
, for some integer
\widetilde{\text{w}}
and some
\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{L}}}}\in(\Lambda_{\text{L}})_{\text{inv}}
. Hence, the invariant lattice is isomorphic to

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{\text{L}})_{\text{inv}}\simeq\boldsymbol{f}^{\perp}/\boldsymbol{f}~.

(5.22)

We can proceed as in section
4
. Since the shift vector
s
is in the Voronoi cell and is subject to the orbifold consistency condition
s\cdot s\in\frac{2}{k}{\mathbb{Z}}
, its norm can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{s\cdot s}{2}=\frac{k^{\prime}}{k}~,

(5.23)

for some
k^{\prime}\leq k
, with
k^{\prime}=k
if and only if
s
is a deep hole. The Lorentzian lattice
\Gamma
has automorphisms that are the analogues of the heterotic Wilson line shifts (
4.9
) and factorized duality (
4.10
), and for each vector of the Leech lattice
{\boldsymbol{q}}
we define an automorphism
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma)
via

\displaystyle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\text{w}{{\boldsymbol{e}}}+\text{n}{{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{\ast}+{{\boldsymbol{L}}})=-\text{n}{{\boldsymbol{e}}}-(\text{w}-{\boldsymbol{q}}\cdot{{\boldsymbol{L}}}-\tfrac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{q}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{q}}\text{n}){{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{\ast}+{{\boldsymbol{L}}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}\text{n}~.

(5.24)

This duality maps the null vector
\boldsymbol{f}
to
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}=\varphi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{f})
, which we rewrite as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=k^{\prime}\left({{\boldsymbol{e}}}-\tfrac{s^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime}}{2}{{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{\ast}-s^{\prime}\right)~,

\displaystyle\text{with }\qquad s^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{k^{\prime}}s+{\boldsymbol{q}}~.

(5.25)

We can choose
{\boldsymbol{q}}
such that
s^{\prime}
is in the Voronoi cell of the Leech lattice. By construction, the invariant lattice
(\Lambda_{\text{L}})_{\text{inv}}
is isomorphic to
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\perp}/\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}
. Hence we obtain an equivalent description of the orbifold theory, as a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{k^{\prime}}
orbifold with shift vector
s^{\prime}
. This is exactly the dual shift orbifold described in section
3.4
.

Repeating this procedure, we obtain a sequence of null vectors
\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime},\dots
, and accordingly a sequence of shift vectors
s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime},\dots
, that lead to isomorphic orbifold theories. After a finite number of steps, the shift vectors converge to a point that is either the origin, or one of the deep holes of the Leech lattice, of type
\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}
. In the first case, the orbifold theory is the Leech CFT. In the other cases, the invariant lattice is isomorphic to the Niemeier lattice
\Lambda_{\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}}
. We note in passing that, since the integers
k\geq k^{\prime}\geq k^{\prime\prime}\geq...
decrease at each step and reach a final value
h
(the Coxeter number of the invariant lattice), we recover the Chenevier–Lannes bound
k\geq h
.

Consequently, we can unambiguously characterize all possible shift orbifolds of the Leech CFT, by applying the above algorithm to the corresponding shift vectors. The convergence of this algorithm, just like in the heterotic setting, relies on the fact that the Voronoi cell of the Leech lattice fits in a sphere of radius
\sqrt{2}
. This is not the case for the other Niemeier lattices, and it is unclear how to extend our results to those remaining
c=24
theories. Compared to the rank
16
case, the underlying difficulties can be traced back to the automorphism group of the Lorentzian lattice
\Gamma_{1,25}
, described in
[
41
]
, which is considerably more intricate than the T-duality group
\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma_{1,17})
.
