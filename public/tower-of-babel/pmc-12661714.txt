Prevalence of tooth wear and associated risk factors among people living with HIV/AIDS in Rwanda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Julienne Murererehe, Franҫois Niragire, Jeannine Ahishakiye, Dorcus Dorée Nishimwe, Eliane I. Harelimana, Gabriel Tuyishime, Pierre Nkundimfura, Eustache Ntigura, Ineza Marie Claire, Sifa Nayihiki, Yolanda Malele-Kolisa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTooth wear has been reported to be on the increase worldwide. More importantly, tooth wear was found to be considerably high and severer among People Living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) compared to HIV negative counterparts. Moreover, there is scarcity of information on tooth wear and associated risk factors. Specifically, no single study has been done in Rwanda to provide baseline data on this topic.AimThe aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of tooth wear and associated risk factors among PLWHA in Rwanda.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among two hundred and thirty-seven (237) PLWHA aged 18 years and above attending HIV clinics in Kigali (Muhima hospital) and outside Kigali (Nyamata and Kabgayi hospitals) in Rwanda. Oral examination was done by calibrated examiners and tooth wear was assessed using Tooth wear index (TWI). Descriptive statistics, Chi square, and multiple binary logistic regression analysis were used for data analysis using Stata version 15 software.ResultsOverall, our study found a high prevalence of tooth wear 200 (92.83%) among PLWHA in Rwanda. More than half of the study participants (56.12%, 133) had dentinal wear up to 1/3 of the crown, 54 (22.78%) had enamel wear to dentine in a single spot, and 33(13.92%) had dentinal wear that extended to more than 1/3 of the crown. Our results showed that 142 (60%) of the participants had tooth wear in 4 to 6 sextants, 160 (67.51%) had tooth wear in both anterior and posterior teeth, and 219 (81.02%) had tooth wear in both upper and lower jaws. The predictors of tooth wear were older age of 55+ (OR: 20.872; 95% CI:1.704-255.733), longer duration on HIV medication (OR: 6.594; 95%CI:1.053–40.719); and horizontal brushing technique. Although not statistically significant, males were 3.547 times more likely to have tooth wear than females. In addition, PLWHA who grind teeth were 2 times more likely to have tooth wear than those who do not grind teeth.ConclusionAccording to our study results, tooth wear is a highly prevalent and a notably severe condition among PLWHA in Rwanda. Longer duration on HIV medication, older age, horizontal brushing technique were associated with tooth wear. Therefore, there is a need to raise awareness on tooth wear and its risk factors in this population and health workers (dental professionals, nurses/ physicians in HIV services). Hence the need to integrate oral health services to HIV services towards provision of oral health care to this high-risk group of PLWHA.

Introduction

The burden of oral diseases and problems associated with them are a major public health concern worldwide [1]. In particular, the prevalence of tooth wear has been reported to be on the increase [2]. tooth wear was found to be considerably high (more than 90%) among PLWHA [2]. PLWHA have also been reported to be at risk of severe tooth wear compared to HIV-negative counterparts [2, 25]. Furthermore, the literature reports that patients with severe tooth wear experience difficulties with mastication, swallowing, speaking, sleeping and social interaction [3].

According to the existing literature, thorough diagnosis of tooth wear and identification of its risk factors are essential for developing preventative measures and successful tooth wear treatment [4]. In addition, the proper management of tooth wear requires a holistic and multidisciplinary approach that involves dental, medical and mental professionals [5]. Several studies have reported different risk factors for tooth wear including dietary factors such as excessive consumption of acidic foods and beverages, sweets, soft drinks, acidic snacks, and drinking habits like swishing or holding drinks in the mouth [6, 7] as well as mechanical factors such as oral hygiene practices, tooth grinding, anterior contact [6–9].The term tooth wear is defined as loss of substance from hard tooth tissue in response to mechanical, chemical or a combination of both responses and not caused by dental caries [10]. Hence, this study looked at tooth wear in general without focusing on any specific types.

In sub-Saharan Africa, there is scarcity of data regarding oral diseases, especially among PLWHA [11]. In particular, there is no data on tooth wear status and its risk factors among PLWHA in Rwanda, which constitutes a major problem for prevention and treatment of tooth wear. There is no single published research in Rwanda that investigated the prevalence and risk factors of tooth wear. Therefore, this study seeks to determine/establish the prevalence and identify risk factors associated with tooth wear among PLWHA in Rwanda. This study will provide data that can be used to inform policy making and develop intervention programmes aimed at improving the oral health and wellbeing of PLWHA in Rwanda.

Materials and methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at three randomly selected hospital-based HIV clinics in Rwanda, which included Muhima, Nyamata and Kabgayi hospitals. Muhima hospital is an urban district hospital in Kigali and serves a considerable number of PLWHA. Kabgayi and Nyamata hospitals are district hospitals located at 130 and 50 km from Kigali respectively. According to hospital electronic health records, Muhima hospital offers care or services to a total of 1900 PLWHA whereas Kabgayi hospital serves 1385 PLWHA and Nyamata hospital takes care of 846 PLWHA. Thus, the total population of PLWHA served by these 3 hospitals is 4131.

Study design and participants

This study used a cross-sectional design. The study population were PLWHA aged 18 years and above attending three randomly selected hospital-based HIV care clinics within and outside Kigali in Rwanda.

Sample size, and sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated using the Cochran formula for proportions [12]. The proportion of tooth wear in the population was considered unknown. Therefore, a proportion of p = 50% was considered to maximize the sample size n, and a confidence interval of 7% (we expect the true proportion is within 7 units below or above the estimated proportion (margin error of 7%), thus CI width = 14%). where N is the population size and the sample size for each hospital clinic was determined in proportion to its population size at the time of data collection. This study sample also takes into consideration a design effect of 1.2 as the similarities in the responses provided between study participants from the same facility is expected to be very small given the nature of research design and tools, hence a minimum sample size of 225 participants. We also expected that PLWHA were more likely to respond to our oral health study given their health seeking behavior and adherence to HIV treatment visits. The minimum sample size was increased by 5% to allow for non-response. Thus, this study aimed to recruit 236 PLWHA. A consecutive sampling technique was used to get targeted sample size.

Data collection tools and procedures

The study participants were selected from PLWHA who attend the HIV clinics in the 3 selected hospitals. In order to achieve our recruitment target, we collaborated with nurses and physicians who take care of these PLWHA on a regular basis to inform them about the study. The PLWHA who accepted to participate in the study were introduced to the study team for the informed consent process.

To determine the prevalence of tooth wear, all consenting participants were examined for tooth wear. Clinical examination was done by two calibrated experienced dental professionals (DDN and GT) and inter-examiner reliability score was 0.72 using Tooth wear index (TWI). Data collectors had a Bachelor’s degree in dentistry and master’s degree in Public Health. The TWI is the most frequently used index to identify tooth wear [13]. JM and YMK calibrated data collectors through organized trainings. JM and YMK hold doctor of philosophy degrees in Community dentistry and Public Health respectively. The clinical examination was done in a research room at the HIV clinics of the selected hospitals. Participants were seated in semi-supine position and then examined under natural light using mouth mirror and explorer. (0) was recorded for “no wear or negligible wear of the enamel” (1) was scored for “obvious wear through the enamel to dentin in single spots”, (2) was recorded for “wear of dentin up to 1/3 of the crown height”, (3) was recorded for “wear of dentin more than 1/3 of the crown height and or excessive wear of tooth restorative materials. To be able to calculate the overall tooth status among our participants, people who scored 0 were considered as not having tooth wear. Those who scored 1, 2 and 3 were combined and considered as having tooth wear.

We used a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire to capture demographic characteristics and the risk factors for tooth wear. The questionnaire on risk factors of tooth wear was developed based on items of a tool used by the National Association of Community Health Centers (NACHC) and factors from the Protocol for Responding and Assessing Patients ‘Assets, Risks, and Experiences (PREPARE) [14, 15]. The questionnaire was piloted prior to data collection to improve its clarity and quality.

Statistical analysis

Data entry and cleaning of data was done through checking of any missing value or typo. Descriptive statistics comprising frequencies, percentages of tooth wear were calculated. Multiple binary logistic regression tests were done to determine the relationships between the tooth wear (dichotomous outcomes) with different factors. The data was analyzed using Stata version 15.

Discussion

According to our study findings, tooth wear is highly prevalent 220 (92.83%) and severe (dentinal wear up to 1/3 of the crown and excess tooth wear than extends to more than 1/3 of the crown) among People Living with HIV/AIDs (PLWHA) in Rwanda. Longer duration on HIV medication, older age, horizontal brushing techniques were significantly associated with tooth wear. These results are similar to other findings in the literature that reported a high prevalence of tooth wear among PLWHA and highlighting duration of HIV medication, older age and brushing technique among predictors of tooth wear [2, 9].

Our study results of tooth wear of 92% among PLWHA compare well with what Seghal et al. found in Saudi Arabia. This further supports the previous argument in the literature highlighting that PLWHA live with severe and untreated oral health problems including tooth wear [2, 16]. Severe tooth wear has an impact on oral pain levels, dental appearance and function [16, 17]. Thus, as emphasized by previous researchers, medical interventions that consider involvement of dental providers and health professionals in HIV services are essential to comprehensively and successfully prevent and treat tooth wear among PLWHA [2].

Our study results showed generalized tooth wear in the oral cavity where most respondents had tooth wear in 4 up to 6 sextants (60%), in both upper and lower teeth (80.01%) and among both anterior and posterior teeth (67.51%). Having many teeth, in various sextants and jaws affected by tooth wear can lead to functional impairment, tooth sensitivity or pain, and aesthetic problems (7). In addition, tooth wear can affect a person’s ability to live comfortably, work successfully, enjoy life, form relationships, and can have a negative impact on one’s quality of life [18]. Hence the need for effective and contextual oral health promotion interventions among this high risk group of PLWHA in Rwanda.

After performing multiple binary logistic regression analysis, the predictors of tooth wear were older age, longer duration on HIV medication and horizontal brushing technique. These findings are in line with other studies reported in the literature. For an instance older age was shown to be associated with tooth wear by several researchers [9, 19–21]. Tooth wear increases during a person’s life course, and its severity increases with age [22]. As the life expectancy of PLWHA increases, the prevalence and severity of tooth wear among this high risk group also increases. Therefore, it is essential for oral health care professionals to collaborate with medical professionals involved in HIV care to ensure early tooth wear risk assessment and clinical examination.

Our study has shown that longer duration on HIV medication to be significantly associated with tooth wear. Similar results have been reported in the literature [2, 23]. It is postulated that many oral health issues experienced by PLWHA are related to the side effects of the medications themselves, including dry mouth or xerostomia which is highlighted as a significant complication of HIV and/or side effect of ART medications [24]. Also, mood and sleeping disturbances are reported among side effects of taking antiretroviral therapy [25, 26]. The combination of insomnia and mood disturbances increases the likelihood of clenching and grinding teeth leading to increased risk of tooth wear among PLWHA [2, 25]. Although not statistically significant the results have shown that PLWHA were more likely to grind teeth and have tooth wear within the first 5 years than within 6 to 10 years of HIV infection. This may be explained by increased psychological problems in the earlier years after being informed about their HIV status. This may decrease with time while PLWHA learn to cope with this issue and then increases again with due to HIV treatment side effects.

This study has also shown that participants who used horizontal brushing movements were more likely to develop tooth wear. These results are similar to the findings of a study done by Kan sun and colleagues in Beijing [20]. In general, improper rushing techniques accelerate dental hard tissue defects [27]. For example, horizontal brushing movement is known to be one of the most important reasons for wedge-shaped defects in teeth [20]. At the same time Keeping good oral hygiene is very central to prevent different oral diseases including tooth wear. Thus, educating PLWHA on how to properly brush their teeth is paramount to reduce the risk of tooth wear development in this high risk group.

Though not statistically significant, participants who reported to grind teeth were more likely to have tooth wear than the ones who were not grinding teeth. These results are in line with previous studies done [2]. Scholars have reported that most PLWHA on antiretroviral therapy experience dry mouth, bruxism and teeth grinding that all result in tooth wear [2, 6]. Others researchers have highlighted that Antiretroviral medicine can expose to xerostomia, mood, and sleeping difficulties [26]. Insomnia and medicines increase the likelihood of clenching and grinding teeth, which exacerbates tooth wear among PLWHA [26]. Also, the results of a study done in Brasil by Pomarico and colleagues highlighted that HIV medication can have a significant risk of tooth wear among PLWHA [23]. In addition to HIV medication side effects, PLWHA are prone to psychological disorders [27]. The presence of psychological disorders is known to result in various oral health problems [24] including as bruxism which in turn leads to tooth wear [6].

Moreover, the findings in this research have shown that males were more likely to develop tooth wear than females. Similar results were reported in previous studies [2, 9, 21]. For instance, in a study done by Khalifa S Al-Khalifa in Saudi Arabia, males were reported with higher prevalence of tooth wear than females [26]. Bernhardt and colleagues has reported that men are more likely show bruxism behavior than females [28] which may explain why greater amounts of tooth wear is found in this group of people.

The literature has highlighted that tooth wear-related early diagnosis and intervention are vital because the treatment of tooth wear is very challenging (12). At the same time, there is a limited access to oral health services mostly due to shortage of dental personnel and infrastructures in Rwanda [29]. This context can amplify the problem of tooth wear among Rwandans especially among PLWHA. Therefore, Oral health advocacy is essential to help timely diagnosis and management of tooth wear and to ensure good oral health and better quality of life of PLWHA in Rwanda.

Limitation of the study

A cross-sectional design used in this study do not allow to establish causality. Future longitudinal study on tooth wear topic are recommended to better understand its association with various factors. In addition, we used a tooth wear index and the results could not specify the type of tooth wear. Further studies on tooth wear that specify the types of tooth wear among PLWHA are needed in Rwanda.

Conclusion

According to study results, tooth wear is a highly prevalent and a notably severe condition among PLWHA in Rwanda. Longer duration on HIV medication, older age, horizontal brushing techniques were strongly associated with tooth wear. Therefore, there is a need of efforts from multidisciplinary health workers (dental professionals, nurses/physicians in HIV services to raise awareness on tooth wear and its risk among this high risk group of people. In addition, tooth wear in PLWHA are left untreated. Hence the need to integrate oral health services to HIV services to provide oral health care to this group of people.
