RewardRank: Optimizing True Learning-to-Rank Utility
Gaurav Bhatt Kiran Koshy Thekumparampil Tanmay Gangwani Tesi Xiao, Leonid Sigal, [1em] The University of British Columbia, Amazon

Abstract
Traditional ranking systems rely on proxy loss functions that assume simplistic user behavior, such as users preferring a rank list where items are sorted by hand-crafted relevance. However, real-world user interactions are influenced by complex behavioral biases, including position bias, brand affinity, decoy effects, and similarity aversion, which these objectives fail to capture. As a result, models trained on such losses often misalign with actual user utility, such as the probability of any click or purchase across the ranked list. In this work, we propose a data-driven framework for modeling user behavior through counterfactual reward learning. Our method, RewardRank, first trains a deep utility model to estimate user engagement for entire item permutations using logged data. Then, a ranking policy is optimized to maximize predicted utility via differentiable soft permutation operators, enabling end-to-end training over the space of factual and counterfactual rankings. To address the challenge of evaluation without ground-truth for unseen permutations, we introduce two automated protocols: (i) KD-Eval , using a position-aware oracle for counterfactual reward estimation, and (ii) LLM-Eval , which simulates user preferences via large language models. Experiments on large-scale benchmarks, including Baidu-ULTR and the Amazon KDD Cup datasets, demonstrate that our approach consistently outperforms strong baselines, highlighting the effectiveness of modeling user behavior dynamics for utility-optimized ranking. Our code is available at: https://github.com/GauravBh1010tt/RewardRank

Introduction

The goal of any ranking system is to model human decision-making in a way that maximizes user engagement and utility. However, real-world user behavior is shaped by subtle, context-dependent cognitive biases that traditional ranking losses fail to capture. Engagement often drops when users are presented with redundant or overly similar items, whereas introducing diversity or strategically positioning items can significantly enhance interest. For example, the decoy effect—where the presence of a less-attractive item increases preference for a similar alternative—has been observed in search interactions and shown to meaningfully influence user choices
Wang et al. 2025a
. Other well-documented biases include position bias
Chen et al. 2024
;
Hager et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2022
, brand bias
Li et al. 2025
, and similarity aversion
Tversky & Simonson 2004
. In online advertising, the goal is often to maximize the probability that a user clicks on any item in the list, rather than just the top-ranked one. If data shows that users tend to click on the second position, it may be optimal to place the most engaging ad there to improve overall performance. Likewise, in recommendation scenarios, users may prefer a diverse mix of product styles or brands over a cluster of nearly identical, albeit highly relevant, items. Traditional ranking losses, which emphasize relevance at individual positions (typically the top), are ill-suited for modeling such list-level behaviors (Figure
1
). They overlook the fact that user utility depends not just on which items are shown, but how they are arranged, highlighting the limitations of handcrafted objectives in capturing the interactive and comparative nature of real user decision-making.

A natural way to model user behavior is by learning preferences over full permutations of items within a query group (i.e., a query and its associated items). The ideal objective is to identify and rank those permutations that are most likely to drive user engagement, which can be formulated as a likelihood maximization problem: maximizing the probability of observing high-engagement permutations while minimizing that of unengaged ones.

However, the combinatorial explosion of the permutation space quickly renders this approach intractable; for instance, ranking 10 items results in
10!
(over 3.6 million) possible arrangements. To address this, recent approaches adopt a utility-based framework
Feng et al. 2021
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Xi et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025b
, where a utility model is trained to score permutations based on user preferences, and a ranker is subsequently optimized to generate item orders that maximize the predicted utility. While this framework reduces the combinatorial burden, it introduces two key challenges. First is the classic exploration–exploitation dilemma: the ranker must leverage known high-utility arrangements while also exploring novel permutations that may yield higher engagement. Second is the vulnerability to utility model misspecification: if the learned reward model fails to accurately reflect true user preferences, the ranker may be misled, resulting in poor exploration and degraded overall performance.

In operational ranking systems, user interactions are logged for only a small fraction of the total permutation space. For example, in Figure
1
, only 3 out of the 120 possible arrangements of 5 items for the query "laptop bag" are observed, constituting the factual space. These observed interactions define the
factual/observed
space, whereas the vast majority of unexposed, yet potentially high-utility, permutations form the
counterfactual/unobserved
space. The optimal arrangement that maximizes user engagement may exist anywhere within the full permutation space of 120 arrangements. One of the major challenges in counterfactual ranking lies in reliably evaluating unobserved permutations. Even if the full permutation space is modeled, evaluating ranking strategies under counterfactual settings remains challenging due to the lack of explicit supervision
Agarwal et al. 2019
;
Gupta et al. 2024b
;
Gupta et al. 2024a
;
Buchholz et al. 2024
. For instance, in Figure
1
, 117 out of 120 possible arrangements remain unobserved, making their evaluation inherently counterfactual. Existing approaches, such as offline A/B testing, inverse propensity scoring, or other debiasing techniques, are often costly, statistically unstable, or difficult to scale, making counterfactual evaluation a central bottleneck in listwise utility optimization.

To address these challenges, we propose RewardRank, a counterfactual utility maximization framework that models user behavior over full item permutations. Rather than scoring items in isolation, we learn a permutation-aware utility function that captures user preferences at the list level. To enable differentiable optimization over permutations, we employ the
SoftSort
operator
Prillo & Eisenschlos 2020
to construct soft position embeddings, allowing end-to-end training of the ranking model with respect to utility gradients. To mitigate the effects of reward model misspecification—where the learned utility may diverge from actual user preferences—we introduce a correction term in the ranker’s training objective that improves robustness during optimization. For evaluation, we present two scalable, fully automated protocols that assess counterfactual performance without requiring human labels.
KD-Eval
(Knowledge Distillation Evaluation) uses a pretrained, position-aware oracle to provide soft supervision and serve as a proxy for user behavior.
LAU-Eval
(LLM-as-User Evaluation) leverages large language models to simulate user preferences and assess ranking quality across unobserved permutations. Together, these methods enable efficient benchmarking of counterfactual ranking strategies and help align learned rankings with actual or simulated user utility.

Our key contributions can be summarized as:

•

We present RewardRank, a counterfactual utility maximization framework that models human behavior by learning a permutation-aware utility function. This function captures list-level preferences and contextual effects, including position bias, brand bias, and decoy phenomena.

•

We enable differentiable, end-to-end optimization over item permutations by employing the
SoftSort
operator. Additionally, we introduce a correction term in the training objective to improve robustness against reward model misspecification.

•

We propose two scalable, fully automated evaluation protocols for counterfactual ranking:
KD-Eval
, which uses a position-aware oracle for soft supervision, and
LAU-Eval
, which leverages large language models as user simulators, both eliminating the need for human-labeled data.

1.1
Related Work

Counterfactual Learning-to-Rank.

Prior work in counterfactual learning-to-rank (CLTR) primarily addresses position bias in implicit feedback using methods such as inverse propensity scoring (IPS)
Joachims et al. 2017
and doubly robust estimation
Oosterhuis 2023
. Extensions include modeling trust bias
Agarwal et al. 2019
and jointly correcting for both position and trust biases
Vardasbi et al. 2020
. Recent approaches explore policy optimization via proximal updates
Gupta et al. 2024b
and extend this to trust-aware CLTR through proximal ranking objectives
Gupta et al. 2024a
. While effective, these methods often focus narrowly on position bias or make strong assumptions, underscoring the need for broader utility-driven ranking frameworks, as pursued in this work.

Utility-oriented counterfactual reranking
.
Reranking methods enhance an initial ranked list by applying a secondary model to better optimize downstream objectives such as user utility, fairness, or diversity
Xi et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025b
. Recent work in counterfactual ranking predominantly follows a two-stage framework consisting of a generator and an evaluator
Xi et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025b
. For example, URCC
Xi et al. 2024
trains a set-aware utility model and employs a context-sensitive pairwise LambdaLoss to guide the ranker. NLGR
Wang et al. 2025b
leverages neighboring lists within a generator-evaluator setup for utility optimization. PRS
Feng et al. 2021
adopts beam search to generate candidate permutations and evaluates them using a permutation-wise scoring model, while PIER
Shi et al. 2023
uses SimHash to select top-K candidates from the full permutation space efficiently.

Reranking methods rely on a strong base ranker trained on factual data and typically explore counterfactuals near these initial permutations, leading to conservative exploration that limits discovery of globally optimal rankings. Moreover, they often overlook correcting for reward misspecification, which is crucial for reliable counterfactual learning.

Differential approximation to ranking
.
A key challenge in learning-to-rank is the mismatch between evaluation metrics (e.g., NDCG, MAP) and surrogate loss functions amenable to gradient-based optimization, due to the non-differentiable nature of sorting operations. To address this, prior work has either proposed smooth approximations to the rank function (e.g., ApproxNDCG
Qin et al. 2010
) or introduced differentiable approximations to argsort using soft permutation matrices
Grover et al. 2019
;
Prillo & Eisenschlos 2020
; for instance, PiRank
Swezey et al. 2021
and NeuralNDCG
Pobrotyn & Białobrzeski 2021
utilize NeuralSort as a temperature-controlled surrogate. Another line of work leverages the Plackett–Luce distribution to model ranking policies in a differentiable manner
Oosterhuis 2021
. Methods like PGRANK
Gao et al. 2023
use policy gradients to optimize the expected reward over the Plackett–Luce distribution based on REINFORCE, while ListNet
Cao et al. 2007
and ListMLE
Xia et al. 2008
employ the Plackett–Luce framework to derive smooth list-wise objectives.

Existing utility-based counterfactual ranking methods often rely on proxy mechanisms such as pairwise or neighbor-wise swaps to approximate sorting, without addressing reward misspecification, limiting both exploration and optimization. Although the Plackett–Luce distribution provides a principled framework for modeling ranking policies, it introduces optimization challenges due to the absence of closed-form gradients. In contrast, soft permutation matrices offer a more direct and differentiable way to model counterfactual permutations, making them well-suited for utility-maximizing ranking based on user behavior.

Problem Formulation

We define a
query group
as an i.i.d. sample from the logged off-policy dataset
\{q,\{i\}_{L},\pi,y\}\sim\mathcal{D}
, where
q
denotes the query features,
\{i\}_{L}
is the set of feature vectors (each of dimension
d
) corresponding to the
L
items associated with the query,
\pi
represents the item ranking generated by the production policy (i.e., the order in which
\{i\}_{L}
was presented to the user), and
y
denotes the user feedback observed for that specific arrangement. Assume that there is a true ranking utility function
U(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi):\mathbb{R}^{(L+1)\times d}\rightarrow[0,1]
which takes a query
q
, the matchset
\{i\}_{L}
and corresponding ranking
\pi
to generate the expected user utility of production policy in form of a scalar score.
We estimate the utility function
U(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)
using off-policy data via a parametric reward model
g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi;\phi)
, where
g
is a neural network parameterized by
\phi
. The learning objective is to ensure that the model-predicted utility
g(q,{i}_{L},\pi;\phi)
aligns with the observed user behavior in the logged interaction data
y
, thereby enabling accurate off-policy utility estimation:

\displaystyle\min_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{L}(g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi;\phi),y)]

(1)

The reward model
g
is trained to minimize a loss function
\mathcal{L}
that measures the discrepancy between its predicted output and the observed user feedback
y
for each query group. The loss
\mathcal{L}
can be instantiated as either cross-entropy or mean squared error (MSE), depending on the task formulation.

Let
f(q,\{i\}_{L};\theta)
be a ranker that takes as input a query
q
and its associated matchset
\{i\}_{L}
, and outputs scores
s
for each of the items; where,
f
is a neural network parameterized by
\theta
. The final ranking is obtained by sorting the items in descending order of their predicted scores. Given the factual (
\pi\sim\Omega^{f}
) and counterfactual permutations (
\bar{\pi}\sim\Omega^{c}
), the goal of ranking
f
is to find the ideal permutation
\pi^{*}\in\{\Omega^{f}\cup\Omega^{c}\}
that maximize the expected reward:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(q,\{i\}_{L})\sim\mathcal{D}}[g(q,\{i\}_{L},\hat{\pi};\phi)]\textrm{ where }\hat{\pi}=f(q,\{i\}_{L};\theta)

(2)

Here,
\pi\sim\Omega^{f}
denotes a factual permutation, that is, an observed ordering of the matchset
\{i\}_{L}
. Conversely,
\bar{\pi}\sim\Omega^{c}
is a counterfactual permutation not seen during data collection. The ranker output is denoted by
\hat{\pi}\sim\{\Omega^{f}\cup\Omega^{c}\}
, representing any possible ordering generated by
f
.

3
Proposed Method

We propose a general utility-oriented counterfactual ranking framework designed to learn from logged user interactions. In the first stage, a utility (reward) model is trained to estimate user preferences over item permutations based on observed feedback. In the second stage, a ranker is optimized to generate arrangements that maximize the predicted utility. To enable end-to-end optimization over permutations, we use soft permutation operators such as
SoftSort

Prillo & Eisenschlos 2020
, allowing differentiable approximation of ranking behavior (Figure
2
). We refer to our proposed method as
RewardRank
. The framework is model- and task-agnostic, supporting various architectures and feedback signals. For instance, the reward model can be instantiated using a transformer-based encoder and trained with binary click labels using cross-entropy, or with continuous-valued labels (e.g., purchase probability, satisfaction score) using mean squared error loss. This flexibility allows RewardRank to generalize across domains such as e-commerce, advertising, and content recommendation, where different types of user utility signals may be available.

RewardRank framework.

RewardRank first applies a transformer-based ranker to score each item in the input set
\{i\}_{L}
. These scores are passed through a SoftSort operator to generate a soft permutation matrix
\Pi
, which is then used to compute soft position embeddings. These embeddings are combined with item representations and fed into the reward model to estimate utility.

3.1
Stage 1: Learning the Utility Function

We define the reward model as
g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi;\phi)
, which estimates a scalar utility for a query group
\{q,\{i\}_{L},\pi\}
. Here,
q
denotes the query, and
\pi=\{\pi_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
is a permutation over the matchset
\{i_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
, where
\pi_{\ell}
indicates the position assigned to item
i_{\ell}
in the ranking. The reward model is instantiated as a transformer encoder
Enc
and trained solely on logged (factual) permutations
\Omega^{f}
. For each query group, the input to the encoder is constructed by concatenating the query embedding
q
with each item embedding
i_{\ell}
at its assigned position
\ell
, followed by adding a learned position embedding
\mathbf{p}_{\ell}\in\mathbf{P}
corresponding to that position. In addition to query and item position, the reward model can also incorporate auxiliary contextual signals such as device type, page layout, or user-specific features (e.g., browsing history), enabling more accurate modeling of user preferences in real-world ranking environments.

Training Objective.

The reward model is trained on factual permutations from the logged dataset
\mathcal{D}
by minimizing the cross-entropy loss:

\displaystyle\min_{\phi}\,\mathbb{E}_{(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\text{CE}\big(g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi;\phi),y\big)\right]

(5)

where the label
y\in\{0,1\}
represents binary user feedback, such as click versus no-click or purchase versus no-purchase, defined at the level of the entire query group.
After training, the reward model parameters
\phi
are frozen for use in Stage 2.

Unlike RLHF, which uses pairwise preference labels and models preferences via the Bradley–Terry framework
Sun et al. 2025
, our setting observes only scalar rewards for factual permutations. When rewards are continuous, the model is trained via regression
Wang et al. 2024
, but minimizing prediction error (e.g., MSE) is not the primary goal. Instead, the reward model must effectively distinguish good permutations from poor ones.

3.2
Stage 2: Training the Ranker with Soft Permutations

We define the ranker as
f(q,\{i\}_{L};\theta)
, which maps a query and an unordered set of items to a permutation over those items. Similar to the reward model, each item
i_{\ell}
is concatenated with the query
q
to obtain embeddings
\mathbf{e}_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle f(q,\{i\}_{L};\theta)

\displaystyle=\sigma\Big[\mathbf{w}^{\top}\text{Enc}_{\text{ranker}}\left(\left\{\mathbf{e}_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{L}\right)\Big]

(6)

where
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
projects the item embeddings into scores:
\{s_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
,
\sigma
is sigmoid activation, and
\theta=\{\text{Enc}_{\text{ranker}},\mathbf{w}\}
.
To ensure permutation invariance, we don’t add position encoding to the input of the ranker.

Soft Permutation via SoftSort.

Given item scores
\{s_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
produced by the ranker, we compute a soft permutation matrix
\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
using the
SoftSort
operator
Prillo & Eisenschlos 2020
.
\Pi
is a unimodal row-stochastic matrix, where each entry
\Pi_{k,\ell}
approximates the probability of assigning item
i_{\ell}
to position
k
, and is defined as:

\displaystyle\Pi_{k,\ell}=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\tau}\left|s_{\ell}-s_{[k]}\right|\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\tau}\left|s_{j}-s_{[k]}\right|\right)}

(7)

where
s_{[k]}
denotes the
k
-th largest value in the score vector
\mathbf{s}
, and
\tau>0
is a temperature parameter that controls the smoothness of the approximation. As
\tau\to 0
, the soft permutation matrix
\Pi
approaches a hard permutation matrix.

The soft permutation matrix enables differentiable ranking and is used downstream to construct soft position embeddings, which are added to item embeddings before being passed to the reward model.

Soft Position Embeddings.

We denote the reward model’s position embedding matrix as
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
, where
\mathbf{P}=[p_{1},\dots,p_{L}]
consists of learned positional vectors. To compute soft position embeddings, each item receives a weighted combination of these vectors based on the soft permutation matrix
\Pi
:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Pi_{k,\ell}\cdot p_{k}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\hat{\pi}=\Pi^{\top}\mathbf{P}

(8)

These soft position embeddings are then added to the item embeddings before being passed to the frozen reward model:

\displaystyle g(q,\{i\}_{L},\hat{\pi};\phi)=\sigma\Big[\mathbf{v}^{\top}\text{Enc}_{\text{reward}}^{[\texttt{CLS}]}\left(\left\{\mathbf{e}_{\ell}+\hat{\pi}_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{L}\right)\Big]

(9)

Utility Maximization Objective.

The ranker is trained to maximize the utility predicted by the frozen reward model:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{(q,\{i\}_{L})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[g(q,\{i\}_{L},\hat{\pi};\phi)\right]

(10)

This objective searches for the permutation
\pi^{*}
that maximizes the reward
g(q,\{i\}_{L},\hat{\pi};\phi)
; where
\hat{\pi}\sim\{\Omega^{f}\cup\Omega^{c}\}
. It allows the model to generalize beyond historical rankings and discover better item orderings in the entire permutation space.

Mitigating reward misspecification.

A key challenge in counterfactual ranking lies in the potential misspecification of the reward model. The ranker’s ability to effectively explore the counterfactual space critically depends on how accurately the reward model captures user preferences. When the reward model is misaligned with actual user behavior, the ranker may explore uncertain or misleading regions of the counterfactual space, leading to suboptimal learning and degraded performance. To mitigate this issue, we introduce a correction term to the reward maximization objective defined in Eqn
10
, resulting in instance-weighted expected reward maximization:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\end{subarray}}\ w\cdot g(q,\{i\}_{L},\hat{\pi});w=1-\lambda|y-g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)|

(11)

where
\lambda|y-g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)|
denotes the residual error introduced by reward model misspecification on the factual/observed permutations (
\pi
), and
\lambda
serves as a regularization coefficient controlling its influence. Reward misspecification arises when the reward model fails to align with user preferences on factual permutations—a limitation that is expected, as some degree of prediction error is inevitable in practice. By down-weighting the rewards for these unreliable cases during ranker training, the objective emphasizes confidently estimated rewards, leading to more stable counterfactual exploration and improved learning.

Experimentation

4.1
Datasets

Baidu-ULTR.
The Baidu-ULTR dataset
Hager et al. 2024
, a large-scale subset of the Baidu-ULTR corpus
Zou et al. 2022
, contains user click interactions over web search queries. It includes 1.8M query groups (11.7M query-document pairs) and 590K validation/test sessions (4.8M pairs), BERT-based CLS embeddings, and traditional lexical features such as BM25, TF-IDF, and query-likelihood scores. The authors of
Hager et al. 2024
provide BERT-based CLS embeddings for each query-document pair.

Amazon KDD-cup dataset.
The KDD-Cup dataset
Reddy et al. 2022
includes 130K queries and 2.6M annotated query-product pairs across English, Japanese, and Spanish. Each query is associated with up to 40 products and enriched with metadata such as titles, descriptions, and bullet points, making it particularly suitable for LLM-based evaluations due to the availability of textual features, unlike Baidu-ULTR. The dataset primarily contains mostly-relevant query-item pairs that were displayed to users, but it does not record the presentation order. To construct our train/validation/test splits, we randomly sample five permutations of length 8 from the set of candidate products for each query, resulting in 400K query groups for training and 50K samples for validation/testing.

4.2
Implementation Details and Compared Baselines

We use a BERT-base architecture to design the encoders for both the reward model and the ranker. The default hyperparameters were used for BERT-base, which consists of 12 transformer layers, 768 hidden dimensions, 12 attention heads, and approximately 110M parameters. For comparison, we implement two utility-based counterfactual ranking approaches: URCC, which uses LambdaLoss-based pairwise objective for counterfactually training rankers
Xi et al. 2024
, and PG-rank, which employs Plackett–Luce modeling with policy gradient optimization
Gao et al. 2023
. Our variant, URCC*, employs the proposed transformer-based reward model and ranker, which outperforms the BiLSTM architecture used in the original implementation
Xi et al. 2024
. Similarly, unlike the original PG-rank
Gao et al. 2023
, which was explored with known deterministic NDCG utility, we create a variant, PG-rank
∗
, which is trained with the same reward model used in RewardRank. We also report results using different numbers of Monte Carlo samples in our implementation of PG-rank
∗
(
\text{MC}=1,5,10
).
Additionally, we train a Naive-ranker using a traditional proxy objective that relaxes NDCG, following the formulation in PiRank
Swezey et al. 2021
. Note that all baselines share the same BERT-based transformer architecture, allowing for a consistent comparison of supervision methods across different counterfactual ranking strategies. In all our experiments with RewardRank, we set the temperature parameter to
\tau=1.0
for computing soft permutations. Refer to Appendix (Section

) for additional details. When learning RewardRank with real click signals from the Baidu-ULTR dataset, we compare it against point-wise, pair-wise, list-wise, and position-debiased ULTR baselines reported in
Hager et al. 2024

4.3
Knowledge-Distillation Evaluation Strategy (KD-Eval)

KD-Eval focuses on modeling position-bias in user behavior, one of the most prominent and well-understood forms of bias in ranking systems, making it crucial for evaluating how well a ranker aligns with user attention patterns. To enable this, we use a pre-trained Inverse Propensity Scoring model (IPS-Oracle)
Hager et al. 2024
1
1

1

https://github.com/philipphager/baidu-bert-model
, a position-dependent click probability estimator, as the ground-truth counterfactual model for the Baidu-ULTR dataset. We use this model to generate soft click probability for a ranked query group. Naturally, they encode position bias and can be used to train any counterfactual ranking model. Moreover, the IPS-Oracle serves as an evaluation proxy, allowing us to assess how effectively a trained ranker has modeled the user attention patterns reflected in the distilled supervision to maximize the click probability.
The IPS-Oracle models the click probability at position
\ell
as the product of two factors: the probability that the position is examined
P(E_{\ell})
and the relevance-based click probability of the item at that position
P(R_{q,i_{\pi_{\ell}}})
if it were to be examined. Given a ranked query group
(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)\sim\mathcal{D}
, with
\pi=\{\pi_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
as the observed permutation, the click probability of the item
i_{\pi_{\ell}}
at position
\ell

\displaystyle P(C_{q,i_{\pi_{\ell}},\ell})=P(E_{\ell})\cdot P(R_{q,i_{\pi_{\ell}}})\,.

(12)

Click-based Utility.

The IPS-Oracle models item clicks independently and does not capture preference over entire permutations (Eqn
12
). To bridge this gap, we define a permutation-level utility that estimates the probability of at least one click within a query group, serving as a proxy for overall user engagement, where higher click likelihood indicates a better-ranked arrangement:

\displaystyle U_{\text{IPS}}(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)=1-\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(1-P(E_{\ell})\cdot\sigma\left(R_{q,i_{\pi_{\ell}}}\right)\right)

(13)

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function used to normalize relevance scores as probabilities. The reward model is trained to approximate this utility, using distilled soft utility
\hat{y}=U_{\text{IPS}}(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)
as supervision.

4.4
LLM-as-User Evaluation Strategy (LAU-Eval)

While KD-Eval captures position bias via IPS-Oracle supervision, it does not account for broader behavioral patterns such as brand bias, similarity aversion, or irrelevance bias. To complement KD-Eval and more fully assess human-centered ranking behavior, we introduce the LAU-Eval framework. In this framework, soft utility are generated by a large language model (LLM) prompted to simulate user shopping behavior over a query group provided in the Amazon KDD-cup dataset. Given a query and an ordered product list, the LLM estimates (i) the probability of purchasing at least one item, and (ii) the item it would choose, without explicit constraints on how relevance is computed. The instruction prompt is designed to incorporate behavioral biases such as position bias, brand preference, irrelevance filtering, similarity aversion, and color bias, encouraging the LLM to reason about both relevance and context-dependent preferences. We use Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2 with a temperature of 0.1 and a context window of 5,000 tokens. Additional details on the instruction prompt design, modeled biases, dataset statistics, sample LLM responses, and rearrangement examples from RewardRank and other baselines are included in the Appendix (Sections

4.6
Limitations

While both KD-Eval and LAU-Eval provide valuable insights into ranking quality and user preferences, there are inherent limitations in each approach.

KD-Eval Limitations:
While KD-Eval provides a robust baseline for position-debiasing, it is limited in behavioral scope. It primarily focuses on mitigating position bias without considering other nuanced user preferences, such as brand bias or contextual relevance, which can lead to suboptimal performance in more complex scenarios.

LAU-Eval Limitations:
LAU-Eval captures richer heuristics and offers more context-aware ranking, but it depends heavily on the quality and stability of the LLM outputs. Inconsistent or noisy outputs from the LLM can negatively affect the reliability of the evaluation, as the method assumes that the LLM accurately reflects user preferences in all scenarios.

It is important to note that LAU-Eval is used to simulate user behavior dynamics that may influence
user decisions. Our selection of biases and instruction prompt serves as a proof-of-concept demon-
strating that an LLM can be used as a proxy user to study counterfactual ranking strategies. We
acknowledge that there are likely many variants of instruction prompts that could be designed to
simulate user behavior. This area of exploration could be a direction for future work.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we presented RewardRank, a general framework for counterfactual learning-to-rank that combines a learned permutation-aware utility model with differentiable soft permutation optimization with reward corrections. To address the challenge of evaluation without ground-truth supervision for unseen permutations, we introduced two scalable protocols: KD-Eval, which uses a position-aware IPS oracle for learning and evaluation, and LAU-Eval, which leverages LLMs to simulate user preferences beyond position bias.

While KD-Eval provides a robust baseline for position-debiasing, it is limited in behavioral scope. LAU-Eval captures richer heuristics but depends on the quality and stability of LLM outputs.
Empirical results across Baidu-ULTR and Amazon KDD Cup show that RewardRank outperforms strong baselines in both factual and counterfactual settings. These findings highlight RewardRank’s potential to advance human-aligned ranking by enabling flexible modeling and evaluation of utility-driven behavior. Future work could explore interactive evaluation, uncertainty-aware learning, and broader user modeling beyond current LLM capabilities.

B.1
Reward misspecification mitigation

Proposition 1

(Reward misssepecification provides a pessimistic lower-bound to learned reward)
.

Let
g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)>0
is non-negative,
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
\epsilon=|y-g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)|
be the absolute error of the reward model on the logged permutations
\pi
. Then, for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
, the instance-weighted reward satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[w(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi,y)\cdot g(q,\{i\}_{L},\hat{\pi})]\leq\mathbb{E}[g(q,\{i\}_{L},\hat{\pi})]\quad

Proof.

By definition of the residual-based weighting:

w=1-\lambda\cdot\epsilon

Let the utility be defined as the sum of true reward (
g
) and model error (
\Delta
):
U=g+\Delta
. Under ideal conditions,
\Delta=0
, thereby allowing
g
to capture true user utility from the logged data.
We can define the deviation from
U
for a given reward scheme as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[U]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[g]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[g+\Delta]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[g]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\Delta]

(14)

Similarly, we can measure the deviation using our correction scheme:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[U]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[w\cdot g]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[g+\Delta]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[w\cdot g]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[(1-w)\cdot g]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\Delta]

(15)

From
14
and
15
we can show that:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[w\cdot g]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[g]

This demonstrates that our correction scheme mitigates model bias by down-weighting unreliable reward estimates, effectively balancing the bias–variance trade-off and enhancing generalization when exploring the counterfactual permutation space.
∎

B.2
Ideal IPS-Oracle: Rearrangement inequality

Theorem 1

(Ideal Ranking Maximizes Utility via Rearrangement Inequality)
.

Let
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be a vector of predicted relevance scores, and let
\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be a non-increasing sequence of examination probabilities:
e_{1}\geq e_{2}\geq\ldots\geq e_{n}
. Let
\pi^{*}
be the permutation that sorts
\mathbf{r}
in descending order:
r_{\pi^{*}(1)}\geq r_{\pi^{*}(2)}\geq\ldots\geq r_{\pi^{*}(n)}
. Then, for any permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we have:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\cdot r_{\pi^{*}(i)}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\cdot r_{\pi(i)}

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the classical rearrangement inequality
Day 1972
. Among all permutations
\pi
of the relevance scores, the weighted sum
\sum_{i}e_{i}\cdot r_{\pi(i)}
is maximized when the
r_{\pi(i)}
are ordered in the same way as the
e_{i}
, i.e., both decreasing. Hence, sorting
\mathbf{r}
in descending order and aligning it with the already sorted
\mathbf{e}
gives the maximal utility.
∎

Above analysis shows that ideal ranking order under the IPS Oracle is ordering the items such the sorting of item relevance scores and examination probabilities result in the same permutation.

B.3
SoftSort with Straight-Through Estimation.

The SoftSort operator
Prillo & Eisenschlos 2020
provides a continuous relaxation of the discrete sorting operator by mapping a score vector
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
to a doubly-stochastic matrix
\hat{P}\in[0,1]^{L\times L}
that approximates the permutation matrix corresponding to
\text{argsort}(\mathbf{s})
. The output matrix
\hat{P}
satisfies the following conditions:

•

Row-Stochasticity:
Each row of
\hat{P}
sums to 1, i.e.,
\sum_{j=1}^{L}\hat{P}_{i,j}=1
for all
i
. This ensures every item receives a probabilistic position.

•

Column-Stochasticity:
Each column of
\hat{P}
also sums to 1, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{L}\hat{P}_{i,j}=1
for all
j
. This guarantees that each rank is assigned probabilistically to one item.

•

Limit Consistency:
As the temperature parameter
\tau\to 0
, the soft permutation matrix
\hat{P}
converges to a hard permutation matrix (i.e., one-hot rows and columns). As
\tau\to\infty
, the matrix approaches a uniform distribution over all permutations.

•

Differentiability:
The SoftSort operator is differentiable almost everywhere with respect to the input scores
\mathbf{s}
, making it suitable for use in gradient-based learning pipelines.

These properties make SoftSort a principled and effective tool for modeling permutations in end-to-end trainable ranking systems.

We also experiment with
hard permutations
using a
straight-through estimator
(STE)
Yin et al. 2019
. Specifically, we apply the hard (non-differentiable) sorting operator in the forward pass, while retaining the soft permutation matrix
\hat{P}
from SoftSort for the backward gradient computation. The final STE update takes the form:

P_{\text{STE}}=\texttt{stop\_grad}(P_{\text{hard}}-\hat{P})+\hat{P}

where
P_{\text{hard}}
is obtained via hard sorting, and
stop_grad
denotes gradient detachment. This allows our model to learn from discrete permutation outputs without sacrificing gradient flow during training. We provide ablation studies with hard permutations in Section
E
.

B.4

B.4.1
PG-Rank*: PG-rank with Learned Reward Model.

We extend the PG-Rank framework
Gao et al. 2023
by replacing the handcrafted reward (e.g., NDCG) with a learned reward model
g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)
that scores entire permutations based on user utility. The goal is to maximize the expected reward under the Plackett–Luce distribution induced by the ranker’s scores:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{PG-reward}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}\left[g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)\right]

(16)

where
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\pi)
is the Plackett–Luce distribution over permutations, parameterized by model scores
s_{1},\dots,s_{L}
for each item in the query group. To enable backpropagation through the sampled permutations, we adopt the Gumbel-Softmax trick as in the original PG-Rank implementation, which provides a continuous relaxation of the discrete sampling process.

The gradient of this objective is estimated using the REINFORCE trick with a baseline
b
for variance reduction (adopted from PG-rank
Gao et al. 2023
;
Kool et al. 2019
):

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{PG-reward}}\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\left(g(\pi^{(k)})-b\right)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\pi^{(k)})\right]

(17)

where
\pi^{(k)}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
are
K
Monte Carlo samples drawn from the Plackett–Luce model.

The log-probability of a sampled permutation
\pi
under this model is given by:

\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left[s_{\pi(k)}-\log\sum_{j=k}^{L}\exp(s_{\pi(j)})\right]

(18)

This formulation allows us to train the ranking model directly on learned, utility-aligned reward signals using fully differentiable, sample-based policy gradients.

B.4.2
URCC* with Learned Reward Model.

URCC
Xi et al. 2024
proposes a two-stage counterfactual reranking framework that jointly learns a set-aware utility function and a context-aware reranker. The utility model in URCC is itself learned from data and used to guide the optimization of the reranker via a pairwise ranking loss over permutations. Since the official implementation of URCC is not publicly available, we re-implemented the method using our own architecture.

In our version of URCC*, we retain the core two-stage structure but implement the utility model
g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)
as a Transformer-based encoder trained to predict user utility over full permutations. Given a query
q
and a set of items
\{i\}_{L}
, the reward model assigns a scalar score to a permutation
\pi
:

S_{\pi}=g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi)

(19)

Following URCC, we then train the ranker
f_{\theta}
to maximize this learned reward by optimizing a context-aware pairwise loss. For a pair of permutations
(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})
such that
g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi^{+})>g(q,\{i\}_{L},\pi^{-})
, we minimize the following objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{URCC-reward}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\log\left(1+\exp\left(-(S_{\pi^{+}}-S_{\pi^{-}})\right)\right)\right]

(20)

Here,
\mathcal{P}
denotes the set of sampled permutation pairs with preference orderings induced by the reward model. Our implementation uses neighborhood-based sampling (e.g., pairwise swaps) to construct
\pi^{+}
and
\pi^{-}
from the base ranking.

Thus, while our training procedure is structurally consistent with the original URCC framework, we employ a more expressive Transformer-based reward model to capture user behavior better and align optimization with utility-oriented objectives.

C.3
Implementation details

We use a BERT-base encoder for both the reward model and the ranker across all methods to ensure a consistent architectural backbone. The BERT-base model contains 12 transformer layers, 768 hidden dimensions, 12 attention heads, and approximately 110M parameters. All models are trained with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
using the AdamW optimizer
Loshchilov & Hutter 2017
with a weight decay of
10^{-2}
. We use a batch size of 512 and train for 21 epochs, applying a learning rate decay at epoch 12 via a step-based learning rate scheduler. All experiments are conducted using 2 NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40GB each).

For our method, RewardRank, we use a soft permutation temperature
\tau=1.0
. In the PG-rank
∗
baseline, which replaces the handcrafted NDCG utility with our learned reward model, we apply Gumbel-Softmax sampling with temperature
0.1
to approximate permutation sampling from the Plackett–Luce distribution. We report PG-rank
∗
results for different Monte Carlo samples (
\text{MC}=1,5,10
) to evaluate variance in reward estimation.

In our URCC* implementation, we follow the original two-stage design: a set-aware utility model and a pairwise ranker. The utility model is trained with a binary cross-entropy loss computed over per-item logits derived from the transformer encoder outputs. Specifically, for each item in the permutation, we pool its embedding from the encoder, apply dropout, and project it through a shared per-item classifier. The per-item predictions are matched to click labels, and their aggregated loss forms the utility supervision.

As an additional baseline, we include a Naive-ranker trained with a relaxed NDCG objective following the PiRank formulation
Swezey et al. 2021
, allowing listwise supervision using soft permutation matrices. All baselines are trained using the same reward data and input embeddings to isolate the impact of the learning objective.

Representative code for our implementations of RewardRank, PG-Rank*, URCC* baselines, and evaluation procedures is included in the supplementary material.

Appendix E
Further ablation studies

In this section, we present additional ablation studies that analyze the impact of different experimental setups on the performance of the ranking models. Specifically, we evaluate the pretrained ranker, hard permutations, and PG-Rank* with temperature 0.

Pretrained Ranker.

Table
7
presents the results for the pretrained ranker, which is the naive ranker trained with PiRank. We compare various configurations of RewardRank with different
\lambda
values, ranging from 0.0 to 1.0. URCC*, being dependent on the pretrained ranker, demonstrates larger performance improvements. However, the gains from the naive ranker are not as significant, suggesting that URCC*’s performance is more sensitive to the quality of the pretrained model. On the other hand, RewardRank and PG-rank* show limited improvements when using the pretrained ranker, as their performance is not heavily reliant on the presence of a strong pretrained model. These methods are more robust in their ranking capabilities and do not exhibit substantial gains from a pretrained ranker.

Hard Permutations.

Table
7
shows the results of using hard permutations. The model evaluates permutations where the decision on the ranking is discrete, as opposed to using soft permutations. The results indicate that the use of hard permutations reduces the performance slightly, as compared to using continuous relaxation methods, such as soft permutations. However, the reduction in performance is minimal, suggesting that the model can still generate reasonable rankings even with hard permutations.

PG-Rank* with Temperature 0.

In Table
7
, we present the results for PG-Rank* with temperature 0. This configuration samples from a hard ranking distribution by setting the temperature parameter to 0. As expected, this approach leads to lower
\mathbb{E}[U_{\text{IPS}}(q,\{i\}_{L})]
and
\text{NDCG}_{\text{rel}}
values compared to the softer temperature values used in PG-Rank. This confirms that softer temperature settings are more effective for generating high-quality rankings, as they allow the model to explore a broader set of permutations.

These ablation studies highlight the importance of using pretrained models, experimenting with hard versus soft permutations, and selecting an optimal temperature for PG-Rank to balance between discrete and continuous ranking choices. These insights are valuable for fine-tuning the ranking systems for real-world applications.

Appendix F
Inference cost, limitations, and broader societal impacts

Inference Cost.

The main inference cost in our work arises from using large language models (LLMs) for ranking and purchase probability estimation. These models require significant computational resources, especially for large datasets and permutations of items. Optimizations like batch processing and multi-GPU use help manage costs, but scalability remains a challenge. Caching frequently accessed queries can further reduce repeated computation costs.

Limitations.

While both KD-Eval and LAU-Eval provide valuable insights into ranking quality and user preferences, there are inherent limitations in each approach. These limitations arise from their reliance on specific biases and the quality of input data, which may affect their performance in diverse real-world scenarios. Below, we outline the key limitations of each method:

•

KD-Eval Limitations:
While KD-Eval provides a robust baseline for position-debiasing, it is limited in behavioral scope. It primarily focuses on mitigating position bias without considering other nuanced user preferences, such as brand bias or contextual relevance, which can lead to suboptimal performance in more complex scenarios.

•

LAU-Eval Limitations:
LAU-Eval captures richer heuristics and offers more context-aware ranking, but it depends heavily on the quality and stability of the LLM outputs. Inconsistent or noisy outputs from the LLM can negatively affect the reliability of the evaluation, as the method assumes that the LLM accurately reflects user preferences in all scenarios.

These limitations highlight areas for future improvement, such as incorporating additional user behavior modeling and enhancing the robustness of the LLM outputs.

Societal Impacts of Our Work.

While our work advances personalized recommendations, it also raises important issues around bias, privacy, trust, and sustainability that must be carefully managed.
Our work improves shopping experiences by providing personalized product recommendations, may lead to higher customer satisfaction and conversion rates Algorithmic bias may favor certain products or brands. Careful mitigation strategies might be needed to ensure fairness.
The lack of interpretability in AI-driven rankings could reduce user trust. Transparent reasoning behind recommendations is essential to maintain confidence.
The use of customer data for training models raises privacy concerns. Compliance with data protection regulations and techniques like differential privacy are may be needed to safeguard personal information.
