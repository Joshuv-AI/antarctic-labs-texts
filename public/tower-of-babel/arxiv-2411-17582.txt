From Fairness to Infinity: Outcome-Indistinguishable (Omni)Prediction in Evolving Graphs
Cynthia Dwork, Chris Hays, Nicole Immorlica, Juan C. Perdomo, Pranay Tankala

Abstract
Professional networks provide invaluable entree to opportunity through referrals and introductions. A rich literature shows they also serve to entrench and even exacerbate a status quo of privilege and disadvantage. Hiring platforms, equipped with the ability to nudge link formation, provide a tantalizing opening for beneficial structural change. We anticipate that key to this prospect will be the ability to estimate the likelihood of edge formation in an evolving graph. Outcome-indistinguishable prediction algorithms ensure that the modeled world is indistinguishable from the real world by a family of statistical tests. Omnipredictors ensure that predictions can be post-processed to yield loss minimization competitive with respect to a benchmark class of predictors for many losses simultaneously, with appropriate post-processing. We begin by observing that, by combining a slightly modified form of the online K29 ∗ \text{K29}^{*} algorithm of Vovk (2007) with basic facts from the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, one can derive simple and efficient online algorithms satisfying outcome indistinguishability and omniprediction, with guarantees that improve upon, or are complementary to, those currently known. This is of independent interest. We apply these techniques to evolving graphs, obtaining online outcome-indistinguishable omnipredictors for rich — possibly infinite — sets of distinguishers that capture properties of pairs of nodes, and their neighborhoods. This yields, inter alia , multicalibrated predictions of edge formation with respect to pairs of demographic groups, and the ability to simultaneously optimize loss as measured by a variety of social welfare functions.

Introduction

Professional networks provide invaluable entree to opportunity through referrals and introductions.
A rich literature shows they may also serve to entrench and even exacerbate a status quo of privilege and disadvantage. For example, in a network with two disjoint groups with equal ability distribution, homophily can, through job referrals, result in the draining of opportunity from the smaller group to the larger

Remedies are few. Hiring platforms, equipped with the ability to nudge link formation, provide a tantalizing opening for beneficial structural change.

Key to this prospect is the ability to estimate edge formation in an evolving network.

To complicate matters even further, these features change over time as individuals grow their networks, switch jobs,

We treat edge prediction in a social network as an online, distribution-free problem and aim to make predictions that are valid and useful,

Since one of our overarching goals is fairness in networking, we certainly want these predictions to satisfy a rich collection of “fair accuracy” criteria, which we express in the language of

Outcome indistinguishability
(OI) frames learning not as loss minimization – the dominant paradigm in supervised machine learning — but instead as satisfaction of a collection of “indistinguishability” constraints. Outcome indistinguishability considers two alternate worlds of individual-outcome pairs: in the natural world, individuals’ outcomes are generated by Real Life’s true distribution; in the simulated world, individuals’ outcomes are sampled according to a predictive model. Outcome indistinguishability requires the learner to produce a predictor in which the two worlds are computationally indistinguishable. This is captured by specifying a class of distinguishers to be fooled by the predictor.

Simplifying for ease of exposition, one may define a class of distinguishers corresponding to a (possibly infinite) collection of (possibly intersecting) demographic groups and prediction values, in which case outcome indistinguishability ensures that the predictor is calibrated simultaneously on each group when viewed in isolation. This is
multicalibration
, defined in the seminal work of Hébert-Johnson, Kim, Reingold, and Rothblum
[
HKRR18
]

A full treatment of fairness in networking requires understanding which kinds of links will advance social and/or individual welfare and which nudges are likely to be most beneficial. We hope our work serves as an important first step towards addressing these questions. In addition, as it is infeasible to make predictions for all non-edges and a random nudge may likely be useless, platform-assisted fair networking will require policies for focusing the platform’s attention, a subject for future work.

1.1
Our contributions and related work.

We initiate the study of online outcome indistinguishability and omniprediction for link formation. Our technical starting point is a novel, randomized variant of Vovk’s
K29^{*}
online prediction algorithm
[
Vov07
]

2

2

Informally for now, RKHSs are potentially very rich classes of non-parametric functions.

To our knowledge, our work is the first in the multigroup fairness literature to use kernel methods (see, however,

PSGL+23
]

Building on this new algorithm, we design efficient kernel functions that capture rich information necessary for the fair link prediction criteria mentioned above.

In particular, using the

Link predictions may be used for a variety
of downstream decisions; for example loss functions may be used to measure predictive accuracy or desirability of outcomes. Moreover the precise loss function may not be known at prediction time. In particular, a predictive system may need to be fixed in advance of A/B testing to determine which of several candidate loss functions encourages desirable behavior. We show how to address these problems by using the

Finally, we extend our results to quantile regression and high-dimensional regression, which will be of general interest in forecasting, and we examine the relationship of

When the comparator class is contained in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space whose corresponding kernel function is efficiently computable, this learning problem has an efficient solution. This yields efficient methods for finding outcome-indistinguishable predictors in both the batch and online cases, even in settings where the distinguisher class is infinite.

1.2
Overview of technical results.

Our work has two main sets of technical results. The first set concerns online outcome indistinguishability and the second set concerns efficient,
\sqrt{T}
, online omniprediction. In both cases, we focus on developing machinery for online prediction that we later specialize to link prediction. As a byproduct of these investigations, we also arrived at new results for online quantile and vector regression, as well as kernel batch algorithms and notions of distance to multicalibration that are of independent interest.

Online outcome indistinguishability
[
DKR+21
]
.

The technical starting point of our paper is a result by Vovk
[
Vov07
]
which guarantees online outcome indistinguishability with respect to specific classes of functions

that form an RKHS, or reproducing kernel Hilbert space. We review both of these concepts below.

An algorithm guarantees online outcome indistinguishability with respect to a class
{\cal F}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}

satisfying the following guarantee:

\displaystyle\left|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E(y_{t}-p_{t})f(x_{t},p_{t})\right|\leqslant{o}(T)\text{ for all }f\in{\cal F}.

Here,
(x_{t},y_{t})
are an arbitrary sequence of (feature, outcome) pairs in
\in{\cal X}\times\{0,1\}

can be chosen with knowledge of the entire history

and in some cases

(see
Section

2
for details).

In other words, a sequence of predictions is outcome-indistinguishable if no distinguisher in
{\cal F}
can reliably (with constant advantage) tell the difference between outcomes drawn according to the learner’s predictions

(see

2.1.1

RKHSs, the
\text{K29}^{*}
algorithm, and the
Any Kernel algorithm

A reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)

is a class of functions that can be defined over arbitrary domains (

Functions in an RKHS have the property that they can be

represented by a kernel function
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Indeed, each kernel

represents a unique RKHS

3
3

3

Common classes of functions like linear functions or polynomials are an RKHS, but we will see many others.

The kernel representation enables one to design computationally efficient learning algorithms with guarantees that hold over all functions in the RKHS

f\in{\cal F}
(
e.g.

k

In addition to their computational benefits, RKHSs can be very expressive. By carefully designing the kernel function
k
, one can guarantee that the corresponding RKHS of functions
{\cal F}_{k}
contains specific classes of distinguishers of interest.
4
4

4

See

3

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{HOI}({\cal L},{\cal H})=\{\partial\ell(x,h(x_{t})):\ell\in{\cal L},h\in{\cal H}\}\text{ where }\partial\ell(x,\hat{y})=\ell(x,\hat{y},1)-\ell(x,\hat{y},0).

(3)

Similarly, we say that a online algorithm satisfies online decision OI if it is outcome indistinguishable with respect to the following class of tests:

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{DOI}({\cal L})=\{\partial\ell(x,\pi_{\ell}(x)):\ell\in{\cal L}\}\text{ where }\pi_{\ell}(x,p)=\argmin_{\hat{y}}\E_{\tilde{y}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\ell(x,\hat{y},\tilde{y}).

(4)

Using these definitions, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 1.3

(Informal)
.

Let
{\cal L}
be a class of loss functions and
{\cal H}
be a comparator class. If
{\cal A}
is online outcome indistinguishable with respect to the union of
{\cal F}_{DOI}({\cal L})
and
{\cal F}_{HOI}({\cal L},{\cal H})
with indistinguishability error bounded by
\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}
, then
{\cal A}
is an online omnipredictor with regret rate
{\cal O}(\mathcal{R}_{\cal A})
.

While it is interesting that this relationship, first identified in
[
GHK+23
]
, carries over to the online setting, it is not quite useful without also knowing that the necessary indistinguishability requirements are also efficiently achievable. The main technical contributions of our work towards establishing online omniprediction is the design of efficiently computable kernel functions whose corresponding RKHSs contain the requisite distinguishers for hypothesis and decision OI.

We defer a detailed presentation of these constructions to
Section

4
.
However, the main technical ideas behind these results rely heavily on the theory behind reproducing kernel Hilbert space and the fact that it is relatively simple to compose kernel functions together. This ease of composition also allows one to characterize their corresponding (composed) function spaces. Being able to reason about composition is fundamental to these constructions since decision and hypothesis OI are both defined in terms of composition of functions (
i.e.
,
\partial\ell(x,\pi_{\ell}(p)),~\partial\ell(x,h(x))
).
A technical challenge of our work is showing how certain RKHS remain closed under post-processing. In particular, as a stepping stone to proving the necessary decision OI guarantees, we identify natural conditions on RKHSs
{\cal F}
which guarantee that if
\ell(x,p,y)
is in
{\cal F}
then so is
\ell(x,\pi_{\ell}(p),y)
.

Our results can be used to guarantee
\sqrt{T}
online omniprediction with respect to various different kinds of comparator classes
{\cal H}
and losses
{\cal L}
. However, in the following theorem we instantiate this general recipe to provide an end to end guarantee for classes
{\cal H}
and
{\cal L}
that are commonly considered in the literature. We refer the reader to Section

(Informal)

There exist an efficient kernel

Any Kernel algorithm

)-online omnipredictor for the following settings,

–

The comparator class
{\cal H}
contains all low-depth regression trees taking values in
[-1,1]
and all functions
h^{\prime}
in a pre-specified finite set
{\cal H}^{\prime}
.

–

The set of losses

is any smooth, proper scoring rule
9
9

9

Proper scoring rules
\ell

\E_{y\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\ell(x,\hat{y},y)

In the link prediction context, one can in particular choose losses mapping onto the utility of a range of different decisions, including predictive performance (e.g.,

Other results.

As a serendipitous consequence of our investigation into kernel methods for online indistinguishability and omniprediction, we obtain algorithms for other online prediction problems. These are not directly related to the link prediction problem which is our main focus, but are of independent interest.

We design a new algorithm for online multicalibrated quantile regression. In quantile regression, outcomes

Quantile regression is a common problem in domains like weather forecasting or financial prediction, where one is interested in deriving confidence intervals or predicting the likely range of outcomes, rather than the average outcome. In

, which satisfies the following guarantee for the online setting where

from a different distribution

at every time step:

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t},y_{t}\sim o_{t}}[({1}\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)f(x_{t},p_{t})]\leqslant\|f\|\sqrt{T}\text{ for all }f\in{\cal F}

Like the
Any Kernel algorithm
, the

works for any RKHS
{\cal F}
and runs in polynomial time whenever the associated kernel
k
is efficiently computable.
Furthermore, using our previous results relating kernels

to their corresponding RKHSs
{\cal F}_{k}

{\cal F}
. These results complement those in
[

]

[
Rot22
]
since the functions

In addition to quantiles, one can also extend the algorithm to high dimensional regression, where

is now a vector in a compact set

instead of a scalar in

. Drawing on the theory of matrix valued kernels

2
The Link Prediction Problem

Data.

We represent a professional network as a graph
G_{t}
consisting of nodes (people) and edges (connections between people) that evolve over time. Each node
i
is associated with a features
z_{i,t}
containing information that pertains specifically to
i
, such as their employment and demographic information. This can vary over time. In addition to this node-level information, the graph
G_{t}
is defined by a set of undirected edges detailing which individuals are connected at time
t
.
Edges can be added to or removed from the graph arbitrarily at every time step and need not follow any predefined dynamic or process such as triadic closure
[
Sim08
]
. The underlying set of nodes can also change.
The only restriction we will make is that the platform has the ability to
observe
the entire graph
G_{t}
as it evolves over time.
11
11

11

While the platform has the ability to examine all of
G_{t}
, algorithms need not read the entire input
G_{t}
. They only examine the subset of
G_{t}
relevant to the distinguishers.

Prediction protocol.

At every time step
t
, the platform is presented with a pair of individuals

a_{t}=(i,j)
and generates a prediction
p_{t}
regarding the likelihood that
i
and
j
will be connected at the next time step (
i
and
j
may or may not be connected at time
t
). After producing the prediction, the platform then observes a binary outcome
y_{t}
, which is 1 if
i
and
j
are connected at time
t+1
and 0 otherwise. As per our earlier observability comment, the platform observes the outcome
y_{t}
before having to make a prediction at time
t+1
.
Variants of this prediction problem were proposed as early as 2003
[
LNK03
]
.

In our setting, we allow the outcome
y_{t}
to also depend on the distribution
\Delta_{t}
where
p_{t}
is drawn from.
12
12

12

The difference between
y_{t}
depending on the distribution
\Delta_{t}
versus the draw
p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}
is relatively neglible since in all our algorithms,
\Delta_{t}
is only ever supported on 2 points which are very close together. For intuition, one can essentially assume that Nature chooses
y_{t}
while knowing
p_{t}
up to some small rounding error.
That is, predictions can be
performative

[
PZMH20
]
and influence the likelihood of the outcome. This dynamic naturally occurs whenever the platform uses predictions to inform recommendations. For instance, a platform such as LinkedIn may opt to recommend that a pair of individuals connect via the “People You May Know” panel if
p_{t}
is above some threshold. Forecasts in this setting are hence likely to be self-fulfilling (although our results hold for any dynamic).

Notation.

We denote by
{\cal Z}
the set of possible node-level features of an individual, at any point in time.
We define the graph
G_{t}
to be a set
\{(v,z_{v,t},\Gamma_{t}(v))\}_{v\in V_{t}}
, where
v\in\mathbb N
is the id of a node,
z_{v,t}\in{\cal Z}
are the node-level features of
v
at time
t
, and
\Gamma_{t}(v)\subseteq V_{t}
is the set of nodes containing
v
and its immediate neighbors at time
t
.
Here,
V_{t}\subseteq\mathbb N
is the set of nodes present in the graph at time
t
. We will use
\Gamma_{G}^{(r)}(v)
to denote the set of nodes that are at distance at most
r
from
v
in
G
.
If the sequence of graphs
\{G_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is clear from context, we will write
\Gamma_{t}(v)=\Gamma_{G_{t}}(v)
, and adopt the shorthands
\Gamma_{G}^{(1)}(v)=\Gamma_{t}(v)
for
v
’s immediate neighborhood.

Furthermore, we will (exclusively) use
\mathcal{U}=(\mathbb N\times\mathbb N)\times{\cal G}
to refer to the universe of possible elements
u=(a,G)
consisting of pairs of individuals
a=(u,v)
and graphs
G\in{\cal G}
. We will use
{\cal X}
to refer to a general set.

2.1
Formal desiderata.

The dynamics underlying professional networking are complex. In this paper, we address the challenge of efficiently generating forecasts that are guaranteed to be
a) valid
and
b) useful
, without imposing any modeling assumptions regarding how networks evolve.

2.1.1
Validity and outcome indistinguishability.

Defining what it means for a forecast of arbitrary, non-repeatable events to be valid is in and of itself a challenging task.
However, one common perspective within the sciences is that a theory, or prediction, is valid if it withstands efforts to falsify it. This viewpoint was recently formalized in the computer science literature by
[
DKR+21
]
who introduced the notion of
outcome indistinguishability
(OI).
Briefly, a predictor is outcome indistinguishable if no analyst can refute the validity of the predictor on the basis of a particular set of computational tests.

This idea of the analyst is operationalized via a class
{\cal F}_{A}
of
distinguishers
that take in a set of observation information
x
, a prediction
p
, a binary outcome
y
, and return a score (think True/False).
13
13

13

This corresponds sample-access OI, the second level in the OI hierarchy presented in
[
DKR+21
]
. For ease of presentation, we assume that all distinguishers
A
are deterministic.
A sequence of predictions
p_{t}
is outcome indistinguishable with respect to
{\cal F}_{A}
if, when averaged over the sequence, all distinguishers
A\in{\cal F}_{A}
give (approximately) the same output in the case where they are given
(a)
the synthetic outcome
\tilde{y}_{t}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{t})
sampled according to the learner’s prediction
p_{t}
and
(b)
the true outcome
y_{t}
revealed by "Real Life". That is,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{\tilde{y}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{t})}A(x_{t},p_{t},\tilde{y}_{t})\approx\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}A(x_{t},p_{t},y_{t}).

(5)

In their initial work,
[
DKR+21
]
focused on the batch, or distributional setting, where features are sampled from a fixed,
static
distribution
x\sim{\cal D}
, and outcomes
y
are sampled from some conditional distribution,
y\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p^{*}(x))
.
As discussed previously, networking dynamics are complex and the likelihood of a link forming between any pair of individuals changes as networks evolve. Assuming any kind of static, or slowly moving distribution over
(x,y)
is a non-starter for the link prediction problem.

Instead of generating predictions that are indistinguishable under a specific choice of static distribution, we tackle the challenge
of (efficiently) producing predictions that are outcome indistinguishable against arbitrary sequences
\{(x_{t},p_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
. That is, “Real Life”’ can choose outcomes
y_{t}\in\{0,1\}
arbitrarily, and the choice of
y_{t}
may even depend on the learners predictions.
Formally, we aim to generate link predictions that satisfy the following online outcome indistinguishability guarantee:

Definition 2.1
.

An algorithm
{\cal A}
is
({\cal F},\mathcal{R}_{\cal A})
-online outcome indistinguishable if it generates a transcript
\{(x_{t},\Delta_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that for all distinguishers
f\in{\cal F}

\displaystyle\bigg|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}(y_{t}-p_{t})f(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg|\leqslant\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}(T,f)

(6)

where the indistinguishability error rate
\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}:\mathbb N\times{\cal F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
is
o(T)
for every
f
.

Although stated differently, the condition above is essentially equivalent to that presented in
Equation

5
since,

\displaystyle A(x_{t},p_{t},y_{t})-\E_{\tilde{y}_{t}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{t})}A(x_{t},p_{t},\tilde{y}_{t})=(y_{t}-p_{t})(A(x_{t},p_{t},1)-A(x_{t},p_{t},0))=(y_{t}-p_{t})f_{A}(x_{t},p_{t}),

for
f_{A}(x,p)=A(x,p,1)-A(x,p,0)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\bigg|\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}\E_{\tilde{y}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{t})}A(x_{t},p_{t},\tilde{y}_{t})-A(x_{t},p_{t},y_{t})\bigg|=0\iff\bigg|\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})f_{A}(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg|=0.

Although initially defined with respects functions
f
that are binary valued — where
f
was the characteristic function of a set or demographic group
[
HKRR18
]
— the distinction between binary and real-valued functions has since been blurred in the multicalibration literature. In this work, we keep to earlier conventions and refer to the above guarantee (
Equation

6
) as indistinguishability since we focus mostly on real-valued
f
and because we work with a formulation of omniprediction that is expressed in terms of outcome indistinguishability
[
GHK+23
]
. However, we do so with the understanding that both terms are very tightly linked.

Returning to the intuition that predictions will be regarded as valid (for now!) if they cannot be falsified, we note that predictions satisfying
Equation

6
with
\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}(T,f)={\cal O}(\sqrt{T})
cannot be refuted on the basis of a common class of tests based on the theory of martingales. To see this, assume that the outcomes
y_{t}
are the realizations of a stochastic process
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
where the binary random variables
Y_{t}
are not necessarily independent nor identically distributed, but satisfy
\E Y_{t}=p^{*}_{t}
. Then, it’s not hard to check that
Z_{t}=\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}-p^{*}_{t}
is a martingale with bounded differences.
By Azuma-Hoeffding, the best one can guarantee on the deviations
|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}y_{t}-p^{*}_{t}|
is that they scale at
{\cal O}(\sqrt{T})
rates.
Therefore, a sequence of predictions
(p_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
that are OI with respect to the constant function
f=1
and satisfy
|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}-p_{t}|\leqslant{\cal O}(\sqrt{T})
behave
as if
they were the true sequence
(p^{*}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
that generate the data. We cannot refute them on the basis of these martingale tests.

The above online OI guarantee is stronger, it holds not just on average over the sequence but even with respect to distinguishers that also examine information present in
x_{t}
and the prediction
p_{t}
itself. We will develop link prediction algorithms that fool distinguishers which examine a wide variety of information about the pair of individuals including their node-level features, their mutual connections, and the features of people to whom they are connected.

2.1.2
Utility and omniprediction.

In addition to the notion of empirical validity above, we aim to generate predictions that are
useful
for decision-making.
We will thus move beyond analysis of predictions
p_{t}
and consider
decisions

\hat{y}_{t}
made on the basis of a prediction
p_{t}
and the relevant context
x_{t}
.

We will also assume that decision-makers’ utilities can be specified by a (class of)
loss function
(s).
For example, decision-makers may want to forecast outcomes, so that predictions closely match outcomes, or steer them, so that desirable outcomes occur more often.
In such cases, a loss function will encode some notion of distance between predictions and outcomes. Or, it might simply produce higher outputs when outcomes are undesirable and lower outputs when they they are desirable.
As we noted previously, our “platform” setting allows for performativity, meaning that outcomes
y
can depend on decisions
\hat{y}
— this is the power of the platform that we wish to exploit and what gives us hope that the latter goal of steering subjects towards desirable outcomes may be attainable.

We will focus on minimizing loss with respect to the best fixed action in retrospect:
An algorithm
{\cal A}
generating a transcript of (feature, decision, outcomes) tuples
\{x_{t},\hat{y}_{t},y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
achieves
\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}(T)
regret with respect to a comparison, or benchmark, class of functions
{\cal H}
and loss
\ell
if

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t},\hat{y}_{t},y_{t})\leqslant\min_{h\in{\cal H}}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t},h(x_{t}),y_{t})+\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}(T).

In the equation above, we note that loss functions can depend on
features

x_{t}
as well as predicted and realized outcomes.
This is because many loss minimization settings in complex domains depend on the object we are making predictions about as well as on the prediction and realized outcome.
For example, one may wish to more heavily weight decisions that affect disadvantaged demographic groups, in which case the loss function will depend on the features of individuals.
However, one can always drop the
x
argument to
\ell
for losses that do not depend on features (as in in prior work on omniprediction
[
GHK+23
,
GJRR24
]
).

In link prediction, a platform may want to determine which links are likely to form or make recommendations that nudge certain links towards forming.
The utility of a decision in an evolving network may also depend on characteristics of the decision subjects, such as the demographic group membership of the pair of individuals across a potential connection.
We allow for loss functions that take into account characteristics of pairs of individuals (and also their neighborhoods and neighbors’ features).

Finally, we will focus on creating predictors that can be efficiently post-processed so as to minimize loss, with respect to a given comparator class, for any in
large classes of loss functions
. These are called omnipredictors
[
GKR+22
,
GJN+22
]
.
Online omnipredictors can be defined formally as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

An algorithm
{\cal A}
is an
({\cal L},{\cal H},\mathcal{R}_{\cal A})
-online omnipredictor if it generates a transcript
\{(x_{t},\Delta_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that for all
\ell\in{\cal L}
there exists a
\pi_{\ell}:{\cal X}\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}\ell(x_{t},\pi_{\ell}(x_{t},p_{t}),y_{t})\leqslant\inf_{h\in{\cal H}}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t},h(x_{t}),y_{t})+\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}(T).

(7)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
is
o(T)
.

In particular, we will take
\pi_{\ell}
to be

\displaystyle\pi_{\ell}(x,p)

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\hat{y}\in[0,1]}\E_{y\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)}[\ell(x,\hat{y},y)],

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\hat{y}\in[0,1]}p\cdot\ell(x,\hat{y},1)+(1-p)\cdot\ell(x,\hat{y},0),

which is a simple optimization problem over the unit interval that can be efficiently solved.
(We will assume argmin returns the set of values achiving a minimum, and that
\pi_{\ell}
is an arbitrary member of this set.)
Finally if
\ell
is invariant to
x
, the
x
argument to
\pi_{\ell}
can also be dropped.

We focus on omnipredictors for two reasons.
First, link predictions may be used for a variety of downstream decisions on a platform.
As mentioned previously, a class of loss functions can simultaneously be used to measure
predictive quality
(
e.g.
, squared loss:
\ell(x,\hat{y},y)=(y-\hat{y})^{2}
) or
desirability of outcomes
(
e.g.
, link formation:
\ell(x,\hat{y},y)=1-y
, which is minimized when an edge forms).
Additionally, platforms may use link predictions within different “People You May Know” recommendations serving different goals (
e.g.
, different types of connections), and they may hope to tailor other on-platform experiences on the basis of the predicted evolution of the network.
Second, the loss function may not be known at prediction time: for example, a predictive system may need to be fixed in advance of A/B tests determining which loss function in a certain class gives the best proxy for some long-term objective.

In Section
4
, we discuss learning algorithms which are omnipredictors with respect to large classes of losses (
e.g.
, all bounded differentiable loss functions) and with expressive comparator classes, like deep neural nets.

3
Online Outcome Indistinguishability and Applications to
Link Prediction

In this section, we consider the first task detailed in
Section

2.1
of generating link predictions for an evolving network that satisfy the following outcome indistinguishability guarantee:

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(p_{t}-y_{t})f(x_{t},p_{t})\leqslant o(T)\text{ for all }f\in{\cal F}.

We are specifically interested in designing online algorithms that are
(a)
computationally-efficient,
(b)
indistinguishable with respect to rich classes of functions
{\cal F}
defined on complex, graph-based domains
\mathcal{U}
, and
(c)
achieve the optimal
{\cal O}(\sqrt{T})
outcome indistinguishability error, henceforth
OI error
.

We present a more detailed comparison to prior work later on. However, briefly, previous online algorithms for this problem which achieved the optimal
\sqrt{T}
OI error bound were either computationally inefficient for super polynomially sized sets
{\cal F}

[
FK06
,
GJN+22
]
, could only achieve the above guarantee for restricted classes of functions
f
that were continuous in the forecast
p

[
Vov07
]
, or which where binary valued
[
GJN+22
]
. Our algorithm overcomes these issues and achieves all three of the above desiderata. This will enable new possibilities for
omniprediction
as we detail in
Section

4
, accomplished by appropriate choice of the kernel function, folding the benchmark functions into the corresponding RKHS
{\cal F}
.

Technical approach.

We develop new, general-purpose algorithms guaranteeing online outcome indistinguishability and then specialize them to the link prediction setting.
In particular, we focus on developing algorithms which guarantee calibration with respect to sets
{\cal F}
that form a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS). Intuitively, an RKHS is a set of functions
{\cal F}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
that are implicitly represented by a kernel function
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for a universe
{\cal X}
.

This kernel based viewpoint is useful for our link prediction problem because it provides a computationally efficient way to guarantee calibration with respect to rich classes of functions defined on graphs.
Building on the theory of RKHSs, we design computationally efficient kernels that guarantee indistinguishability with respect to classes of distinguishers that take into account graph topology (
e.g.
, number of mutual connections, isomorphism class of the local neighborhoods), or functions computable by arbitrary finite sets of pre-specified functions, like graph neural network link predictors.

Our technical approach is directly builds on a result by Vovk
[
Vov07
]
that is in turn inspired by the breakthrough work of
[
FV98
]
.
In his paper, which predates the definition of multicalibration by
[
HKRR18
]
or OI
[
DKR+21
]
, Vovk introduces an algorithm that guarantees indistinguishability with respect to any RKHS of functions
f(u,p)
that are continuous in
p
.
Drawing on ideas from
[
FH21
]
, we introduce the
Any Kernel algorithm
, which guarantees indistiguishability with respect to
any
RKHS
{\cal F}
, not just those that are continuous in
p
.

3.1
The algorithm.

We now formally present our online
Any Kernel algorithm
, which forms the backbone of our later results. The algorithm builds on the earlier
\text{K29}^{*}
algorithm from
[
Vov07
]
that is in turn inspired by Kolmogorov’s 1929 proof of the weak law of large numbers
[
KC29
]
.
The reader familiar with reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces can skip the brief background highlights outlined below.

Background on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

Our guarantees are stated in terms of a kernel
k
and its associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space
{\cal F}_{k}
. We drop the subscript when it is clear from context. We briefly review the basic facts behind RKHSs here and provide a self-contained formal review of the facts we need. In
Appendix

A
, we list out various kernels and RKHS that we then use to instantiate the algorithm. We refer the reader to texts such as
[
PR
,
Ste08
]
for further background on this material.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\cal X}
be an arbitrary set. A function
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
kernel
on
{\cal X}
if it satisfies

1.

Symmetry:
k(x,x^{\prime})=k(x^{\prime},x)
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}
.

2.

Positive Definiteness:
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})\geqslant 0
for all
n\in\mathbb N
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\cal X}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Every kernel
k
is associated with a unique Hilbert space
{\cal F}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
of real-valued functions. By virtue of being a Hilbert space,
{\cal F}
is equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\cal F}}:{\cal F}\times{\cal F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that defines a norm on the elements
f\in{\cal F}
,
\|f\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}=\langle f,f\rangle_{{\cal F}}
. The set is called a
reproducing
kernel Hilbert space since for every element
x\in{\cal X}
, there exists an element
\Phi(x)\in{\cal F}
such that

\displaystyle f(x)=\langle f,\Phi(x)\rangle_{{\cal F}}\text{ for all }f\in{\cal F},

where
\langle\cdot,\Phi(x)\rangle_{{\cal F}}
is continuous.
The function
\Phi:{\cal X}\rightarrow{\cal F}
is called the reproducing kernel or feature map. It also satisfies the property that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}
,

\displaystyle k(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\Phi(x),\Phi(x^{\prime})\rangle_{{\cal F}}.

Given any kernel
k
, or equivalently a feature map
\Phi
, the Moore-Aronszajn theorem provides an explicit characterization of the set of functions
{\cal F}
. In particular,

\displaystyle{\cal F}=\overline{\mathsf{span}}\{\Phi(x):x\in{\cal X}\},

where,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{span}}\{\Phi(x):x\in{\cal X}\}=\bigg\{f\;:\;f=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\Phi(x_{i})\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb N,x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{U}\text{ and }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\bigg\},

and the overline denotes the completion of the set.
That is,
{\cal F}
is the set of all finite linear combinations of feature maps
\Phi
augmented with the limits of any Cauchy sequences of such linear combinations.

Throughout our work we will use the fact that kernels
compose
.
That is, if
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are kernels for RKHSs
{\cal F}_{1}\subseteq\{{\cal X}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
and
{\cal F}_{2}\subseteq\{{\cal X}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
. Then
k_{1}+k_{2}
is a kernel for
{\cal F}_{1}+{\cal F}_{2}
and
k_{1}\cdot k_{2}
is a kernel for
{\cal F}_{1}\cdot{\cal F}_{2}
where,

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{1}+{\cal F}_{2}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})\;:\;x_{1}\in{\cal X}_{1},x_{2}\in{\cal X}_{2},f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1},f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}\},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{1}\cdot{\cal F}_{2}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{f_{1}(x_{1})f_{2}(x_{2})\;:\;x_{1}\in{\cal X}_{1},x_{2}\in{\cal X}_{2},f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1},f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}\}.

A direct implication of the first line is that two different RKHSs on the same domain can be combined to make a new one, where the set of functions in the RKHS contains the union of functions in each of the RKHSs.
Further details are deferred to
Lemma

A.5
and
Lemma

A.6
.
However, the key point is that these composition properties make it easy to “mix and match” various indistinguishability guarantees.

Description of algorithm.

The algorithm is at a high-level very simple. It only takes as input a kernel function
k
,

\displaystyle k:({\cal X}\times[0,1])\times({\cal X}\times[0,1])\to\mathbb{R}.

At every round
t
, it constructs a function
S_{t}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined from the history
\{(x_{i},p_{i},y_{i})\}_{j=1}^{t-1}
.
If the kernel is continuous, it chooses a prediction
p_{t}
that is a zero of
S_{t}
,
S_{t}(p_{t})\approx 0
. If the kernel
k
is discontinuous in
p
, it instead finds two points
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
which are very close together (i.e.,
|q_{1}-q_{2}|\approx 0
) and outputs a distribution
\Delta_{t}
supported on
q_{1},q_{2}
such that the expectation of
S_{t}
over
\Delta_{t}
is approximately 0. Both of these search problems are efficiently solved via binary search. The algorithm in which the kernel
k
is continuous is the same as in Vovk’s
\text{K29}^{*}
algorithm, while the discontinuous case is new. In particular, the procedure in the discontinuous case draws on ideas from
[
FH21
]
and their results on near deterministic calibration.

Guarantees of algorithm.

With these preliminaries out of the way, we now state the main guarantees of the theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
k
be a kernel with associated RKHS
{\cal F}
. Then, the
Any Kernel algorithm
(
Figure

1
) instantiated with kernel
k
generates a transcript
\{(x_{t},\Delta_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that for any

\displaystyle\left|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}f(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\right|\leqslant\|f\|_{{\cal F}}\sqrt{1+\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}p_{t}(1-p_{t})k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))}.

If
k
is forecast-continuous, then the guarantee is deterministic since
\Delta_{t}
is a point mass. Otherwise, it is near-deterministic. The distribution
\Delta_{t}
is supported on points that are
{\cal O}(t^{-3})
apart.
14
14

14

One could change this from
{\cal O}(t^{-3})
to
{\cal O}(t^{-\alpha})
for any
\alpha>3
without changing the asymptotic runtime.

If the kernel is bounded by
B

\sup_{(x,p)\in{\cal X}\times[0,1]}k((x,p),(x,p))\leqslant B,

then the per round runtime of the algorithm is bounded by
{{\cal O}}(t\cdot\log(tB)\cdot\mathsf{time}(k))
, where
\mathsf{time}(k)
is a uniform upper bound on the runtime of computing the kernel function
k
.

Proof.

If
\mathrm{sign}\,S_{t}(0)=\mathrm{sign}\,S_{t}(1)\neq 0
in round
t
, selecting
p_{t}=(1+\mathrm{sign}\,S_{t}(0))/2
guarantees that,

\displaystyle S_{t}(p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\leqslant 0,

regardless of whether
y_{t}
is 1 or 0. Otherwise,
p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}
where
\Delta_{t}
places probability
\tau
on
q_{t}
and
1-\tau
on
q_{t}^{\prime}
. In this case, letting
\tau^{\prime}=1-\tau
, we can write:

\displaystyle\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}[S_{t}(p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})]

\displaystyle=\tau S_{t}(q_{t})(y_{t}-q_{t})+(1-\tau)S_{t}(q_{t}^{\prime})(y_{t}-q_{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=[\tau S_{t}(q_{t})+\tau^{\prime}S_{t}(q_{t}^{\prime})](y_{t}-q_{t}^{\prime})+\tau S(q_{t})(q_{t}^{\prime}-q_{t})

By choice of
\tau=|S_{t}(q_{t}^{\prime})|/(|S_{t}(q_{t})|+|S_{t}(q_{t}^{\prime})|)
, and the fact that
S_{t}(q_{t}^{\prime})
and
S_{t}(q_{t})
have opposite signs, the term inside the brackets is equal to 0 (this is the forecast hedging idea from
[
FH21
]
). Summarizing, we have that:

\displaystyle\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}[S_{t}(p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})]=\tau S_{t}(q_{t})(q_{t}^{\prime}-q_{t})\leqslant|S_{t}(q_{t})||q_{t}-q_{t}^{\prime}|\leqslant|q_{t}-q_{t}^{\prime}|\cdot t\cdot\max_{t^{\prime}\leqslant t}k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t})).

Since
|q_{t}-q_{t}^{\prime}|\leqslant\varepsilon_{t}=1/(10B_{t}t^{3})
where
B_{t}=\max_{t^{\prime}\leqslant t}k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))
, we conclude that regardless of whether
y_{t}
is 0 or 1,

\displaystyle\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}[S_{t}(p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})]\leqslant\frac{1}{10t^{2}}.

(8)

We now seek an upper bound on the expected value of

\left\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\right\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})(y_{s}-p_{s})\langle\Phi(x_{t},p_{t}),\Phi(x_{s},p_{s})\rangle_{{\cal F}}.

To this end, first observe the symmetry of the summands in
(s,t)
, so the right side simplifies to

\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{2}\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}+2\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{t-1}k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{s},p_{s}))(y_{s}-p_{s})\right).\\

Next, we apply the identity
(y_{t}-p_{t})^{2}=p_{t}(1-p_{t})+(1-2p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})
, which holds for all
y_{t}\in\{0,1\}
and
p_{t}\in[0,1]
and rewrite the above expression as:

\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}(1-p_{t})\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}+2\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{t-1}k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{s},p_{s}))(y_{s}-p_{s})+\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}(1-2p_{t})\right).

Since the rightmost parenthesized term is, by definition, precisely
S_{t}(p_{t})
, we have shown that

\displaystyle\E\left\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\right\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}=\E\left[\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}(1-p_{t})\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}\right]+2\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E\left[S_{t}(p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\right].

Now, using our earlier result (
Eq.

8
), we conclude that:

\displaystyle\E\left\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\right\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\left[\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}(1-p_{t})\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}\right]+2\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{10t^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\left[\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}(1-p_{t})\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}\right]+\frac{2}{10}\cdot\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}

where we used the fact that
\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{\infty}t^{-2}=\pi^{2}/6
. Noting that

\displaystyle p_{t}(1-p_{t})\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}=p_{t}(1-p_{t})k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t})),

and applying Jensen’s inequality, the above equation implies that:

\displaystyle\E\left\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\right\|_{{\cal F}}\leqslant\sqrt{1+\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}p_{t}(1-p_{t})k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))}.

(9)

To conclude the proof, we use the reproducing property
f(x,p)=\langle f,\Phi(x,p)\rangle_{\cal F}
, which, along with Cauchy-Schwarz, relates the indistinguishability error to the above expression as follows:

\displaystyle|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}(y_{t}-p_{t})f(x_{t},p_{t})|

\displaystyle=\big|\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}[\langle f,\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})\Phi(p_{t},x_{t})\rangle_{\cal F}]\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f\|_{\cal F}\E\left\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\right\|_{\cal F}.

∎

Discussion.

The bound guarantees non-asymptotic OI error of at most
\sqrt{T}
for all functions
f
that lie in the RKHS
{\cal F}
induced by a pre-specified kernel
k
.

15
15

15

In particular, the bound holds for all values of
T
.

While the bound holds for all functions in the RKHS, it is
adaptive
. For each
f
, it depends on the norm
\|f\|_{{\cal F}}
but not on the number of functions
|{\cal F}|
(which is in fact infinite for every choice of kernel
k
).
The norm of a function in an RKHS can often be interpreted as an instance-specific notion of complexity. Consequently, the OI error bound satisfies the intuitive property that it is smaller for simple functions, and larger for more complicated functions.

The guarantees are also adaptive since they depend on norms of the features in the sequence,
k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))=\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}
, and the variance of the predictions
p_{t}(1-p_{t})
. Adapting to the variance is particularly useful in the link prediction setting since we expect most edges in professional networks to be unlikely to form, meaning that the OI error bound is smaller.

We also note that neither the run-time of the algorithm nor the associated regret bounds have any explicit dependence on number of functions
|{\cal F}|
. Both of these properties are determined by the kernel function
k
.

Comparison to prior work.

As per our earlier discussion, the closest work to ours is
[

]

The computation complexity of our algorithm and Vovk’s are essentially identical.

Also closely related to our work, the algorithm in

. Both of these are determined by the kernel

As seen in

and both the runtime and OI error scale polynomially with

The principal strength of

Specializing the Any Kernel algorithm to the link prediction problem

Online Omniprediction and Applications to Link Prediction

Up until this point, we have focused on designing online algorithms which satisfy online outcome indistinguishability with respect to various classes of tests.
In this section, we illustrate how these previous insights and algorithms also imply
loss minimization
with respect to many different objectives
{\cal L}
and infinitely large benchmark classes
{\cal H}
.

That is, we show how simple adaptations of techniques developed in the previous section expand the scope of possibilities for
online omniprediction
.
We recall definition of online omnipredictors from
Section

2
:

Definition 4.1
.

An algorithm
{\cal A}
is an
({\cal L},{\cal H},\mathcal{R}_{\cal A})
-online omnipredictor if it generates a transcript
\{(x_{t},\Delta_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that for all
\ell\in{\cal L}
there exists a
\pi_{\ell}:{\cal X}\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}\ell(x_{t},\pi_{\ell}(x_{t},p_{t}),y_{t})\leqslant\inf_{h\in{\cal H}}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t},h(x_{t}),y_{t})+\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}(T).

(16)

where the regret bound,
\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
, is
o(T)
.

Omnipredictors were initially defined by
[
GKR+22
]
for the offline setting and then extended to the online case by
[
GJRR24
]
. Intuitively, omnipredictors are efficient “menus of optimality”: They provide a single prediction that can be postprocessed (via
\pi_{\ell}
) to guarantee lower loss than that achievable by any function in some comparator class
{\cal H}
.
Briefly, the main contribution of this section is we introduce the first algorithm which guarantees online omniprediction with respect to comparator classes
{\cal H}
that are real-valued and of infinite cardinality. These constructions are also unconditionally computationally efficient.

To do this, we build on the insight established by
[
GHK+23
]
which shows that, in the distributional (offline) setting, given any set of losses
{\cal L}
and comparator class
{\cal H}
, one can always construct a set of distinguishers
{\cal F}({\cal H},{\cal L})
such that indistinguishability with respect to
{\cal F}({\cal H},{\cal L})
implies omniprediction.

The results we present in this section differ from prior work both in their substance and in the techniques used to prove them.

In the same paper, using ideas rooted in online minimax optimization

4.1
Efficient,
\sqrt{T}
online omiprediction with respect to rich comparison classes
{\cal H}
.

In this subsection, we present our main result demonstrating how outcome indistinguishability implies omniprediction in the online setting and illustrating how these indistinguishability conditions can be efficiently achieved via the
Any Kernel algorithm
.

The following two OI definitions, hypothesis and decision OI, were first introduced (in the batch setting) by
[
GHK+23
]
. We now adapt them to the online case.
Decision outcome indistinguishability (DOI) is defined with respect to a class of losses
{\cal L}
. It states that prediction must be approximately indistinguishable with respect to the class of test functions constructed from pairs of loss functions
\ell\in{\cal L}
and post-processed predictions
\pi_{\ell}
:

Definition 4.4

(Decision OI)
.

For a loss class
{\cal L}
and regret bound
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{DOI}}(T)
, an algorithm satisfies
({\cal L},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{DOI}}(T))
-
decision outcome indistinguishability (DOI)
if it generates a transcript
\{(x_{t},\Delta_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that,

\displaystyle\left|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}\partial\ell(x_{t},\pi_{\ell}(x_{t},p_{t}))(p_{t}-y_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{DOI}}(T),

\displaystyle\forall\ell\in{\cal L}.

(19)

The second OI condition, hypothesis outcome indistinguishability (HOI), requires that predictions must be approximately indistinguishable with respect to functions constructed from pairs of comparator functions
h\in{\cal H}
and loss functions
\ell\in{\cal L}
:

Definition 4.5

(Hypothesis OI)
.

For a loss class
{\cal L}
, comparator class
{\cal H}
, and regret bound
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HOI}}(T)
, an algorithm satisfies
({\cal L},{\cal H},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HOI}}(T))
-
hypothesis outcome indistinguishability (HOI)
if it generates a transcript
\{(x_{t},\Delta_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that:

\displaystyle\left|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}\partial\ell(x,h(x_{t}))(p_{t}-y_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HOI}}(T),

\displaystyle\forall(\ell,h)\in{\cal L}\times{\cal H}.

(20)

Having introduced these two definitions, the result that OI implies omniprediction is almost immediate. The following lemma formally adapts the ideas from
[
GHK+23
]
to the online setting.

Lemma 4.6
.

Fix a comparator class
{\cal H}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\to[0,1]\}
, a class of losses
{\cal L}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\times[0,1]\times\{0,1\}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}
and regret bounds
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{DOI}}(T),\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HOI}}(T)\;:\;{\mathbb{N}}\to R
. If an algorithm
{\cal A}
satisfies

1.

({\cal L},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{DOI}}(T))
-decision OI (
Definition

4.4
)

2.

and
({\cal L},{\cal H},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HOI}}(T))
-hypothesis OI (
Definition

4.5
),

then,
{\cal A}
is an
({\cal L},{\cal H},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{DOI}}(T)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HOI}}(T))
-online omnipredictor.

Proof.

First, we observe that for all
x\in{\cal X}
and any pair
(\hat{y},y)
where
y\in\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle\ell(x,\hat{y},y)=y\ell(x,\hat{y},1)+(1-y)\ell(x,\hat{y},0)=y(\ell(x,\hat{y},1)-\ell(x,\hat{y},0))+\ell(x,\hat{y},0)

A similar expression holds for the following expectation version,

\displaystyle\E_{y\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\ell(x,\hat{y},y)=p\ell(x,\hat{y},1)+(1-p)\ell(x,\hat{y},0)=p(\ell(x,\hat{y},1)-\ell(x,\hat{y},0))+\ell(x,\hat{y},0).

Therefore,

\displaystyle|\ell(x,\hat{y},y)-\E_{\tilde{y}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\ell(x,\hat{y},\tilde{y})|=|(y-p)(\ell(x,\hat{y},1)-\ell(x,\hat{y},0))|=|(y-p)\partial\ell(x,\hat{y})|

Using this decomposition, by the Decision OI guarantee
Definition

4.4
, we know that

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t},\pi_{\ell}(x_{t},p_{t}),y_{t})\leqslant\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{\tilde{y}_{t}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{t})}\ell(x_{t},\pi_{\ell}(x_{t},p_{t}),\tilde{y}_{t})+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{DOI}}(T).

Furthermore, since
\pi_{\ell}
is the argmin (see
Equation

17
), by definition it satisfies the following inequality for any
h
,

\displaystyle\E_{\tilde{y}_{t}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{t})}\ell(x_{t},\pi_{\ell}(x_{t},p_{t}),\tilde{y}_{t})\leqslant\E_{\tilde{y}_{t}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{t})}\ell(x_{t},h(x_{t}),\tilde{y}_{t})

Lastly, by the Hypothesis OI guarantee (
Definition

4.5
),

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{\tilde{y}_{t}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{t})}\ell(x_{t},h(x_{t}),\tilde{y}_{t})\leqslant\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t},h(x_{t}),y_{t})+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HOI}}(T).

Combining all three inequalities, we get our desired result:

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t},\pi_{\ell}(x_{t},p_{t}),y_{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t},h(x),y_{t})+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{DOI}}(T)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HOI}}(T).\quad\forall h\in{\cal H}\qed

The advantage of this loss OI viewpoint is that it provides a neat template for algorithm design. More specifically, to achieve omniprediction, we only need to design kernels whose corresponding RKHS contain the required distinguishers and then run the
Any Kernel algorithm
with these kernels.
While the main idea is simple, to prove a formal non-asymptotic regret bound we also need to ensure that corresponding function norms of the distinguishers
\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}}
and feature norms
k((x,p),(x,p))=\|\Phi(x,p)\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}
are appropriately bounded. If these quantities are not appropriately bounded, then the guarantees from the
Any Kernel algorithm
can become vacuous (recall the bound from
Theorem

3.2
).

To address this issue, we further specialize the OI definitions above to the RKHS domain. These specializations, kernel decision and hypothesis OI, are representational conditions on the kernel
k
and the corresponding RKHS
{\cal F}_{k}
. Intuitively, they require that a kernel
k
be efficiently computable, bounded, and that certain functions are contained (and have small norm) in
{\cal F}_{k}
.

Definition 4.7

(Kernel Decision OI)
.

Let
{\cal L}
be a set of loss functions. A kernel
k
with corresponding RKHS
{\cal F}
is
{\cal L}
-kernel decision OI (KDOI) with parameter
B
if,

\displaystyle\{\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell}\;|\;\ell\in{\cal L}\}\subseteq{\cal F}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\},

(21)

where
\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell}(x,p)=\ell(x,\pi_{\ell}(p),1)-\ell(x,\pi_{\ell}(p),0)
and:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sup_{\ell\in{\cal L}}\|\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell}\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}\cdot\sup_{x\in{\cal X},p\in[0,1]}k((x,p),(x,p))}\leqslant B.

The condition states that the composition of the discrete derivative of each loss composed with its post-processing function is in the corresponding RKHS and that both the function
\|\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell}\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}
and feature norms
k_{{\cal L}}((x,p),(x,p))=\|\Phi(x,p)\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}
are uniformly bounded.
We note that, by
Lemma

A.4
, if a function
f
is in
{\cal F}
, then so is its negation,
-f
(RKHSs are closed under scalar multiplication).
Thus, a sufficient condition for KDOI is that
\ell(\pi_{\ell}(\cdot),y)\in{\cal F}
for all
\ell\in{\cal L},y\in\{0,1\}
.
Next, we define an analogous condition for losses composed with comparator functions.

Definition 4.8

(Kernel Hypothesis OI)
.

Let
{\cal H}
be a comparator class and let
{\cal L}
be a class of loss functions. A kernel
k
with corresponding RKHS
{\cal F}
satisfies
({\cal L},{\cal H})
-kernel hypothesis OI (KHOI) with parameter
B
if,

\displaystyle\{\partial\ell\circ h\;|\;h\in{\cal H},\ell\in{\cal L}\}\subseteq{\cal F}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\},

(22)

where
\partial\ell\circ h(x)=\ell(x,h(x),1)-\ell(x,h(x),0)
and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sup_{h\in{\cal H},\ell\in{\cal L}}\|\partial\ell\circ h\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}\cdot\sup_{x\in{\cal X},p\in[0,1]}k((x,p),(x,p))}\leqslant B.

As in the previous setting, a sufficient condition for KHOI is that
\ell(h(\cdot),y)\in{\cal F}
for all
h\in{\cal H},\ell\in{\cal L},y\in\{0,1\}
.
We also note that the kernel version of decision and hypothesis OI are qualitatively different from other conditions in the omniprediction literature, since they allow for infinite and real-valued comparison classes but require the existence of a suitable RKHS containing compositions of loss, post-processing and comparator functions.

With these definitions in hand, we can now state our main theorem which provides a general recipe for online omniprediction via the
Any Kernel algorithm
.

Theorem 4.9

(Corollary to
Lemma

4.6
)
.

Let
{\cal H}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\rightarrow[0,1]\}
be a class of comparison functions and let
{\cal L}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\times[0,1]\times\{0,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
be a set of losses.

Let
k_{{\cal L}}
and
k_{{\cal L},{\cal H}}
be efficient kernels with corresponding RKHSs
{\cal F}_{{\cal L}}
and
{\cal F}_{{\cal L},{\cal H}}
that satisfy
{\cal L}
-KDOI and
({\cal L},{\cal H})
-KHOI with parameters
B_{\mathrm{KDOI}}
and
B_{\mathrm{KDOI}}
. Then, the
Any Kernel algorithm
with kernel
k_{{\cal L}}+k_{{\cal H},{\cal L}}
runs in polynomial time and is an
({\cal L},{\cal H},2(B_{\mathrm{KDOI}}+B_{\mathrm{KHOI}})\sqrt{T})
-online omnipredictor.

Proof.

Define the function
k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}k_{{\cal L}}+k_{{\cal L},{\cal H}}
.
From
Lemma

A.5
, it holds that
k
is a kernel and that the functions

\displaystyle\{f_{1}+f_{2}\;|\;f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{{\cal L}};f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{{\cal L},{\cal H}}\}

are in the corresponding RKHS, which we will call
{\cal F}
.
Also, since
k_{{\cal L}}
and

can be evaluated in polynomial time, so can

Now, by the fact that
{\cal F}_{{\cal L}}
and

are closed under scalar multiplication (by

), the zero function is in
{\cal F}_{{\cal L}}
and
{\cal F}_{{\cal H},{\cal L}}
.
This implies for all

and

Now by the main guarantee for the

\displaystyle\left|{\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}(p_{t}-y_{t})(\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell})(x_{t},p_{t})}\right|

Discussion.

We note that the above theorem establishes a precise, non-asymptotic regret bound. It in particular guarantees that for any

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}\ell(x_{t},p_{t},y_{t})

for every

.
Furthermore, the algorithm is very simple and easy to implement. As presented previously in

, you only need to be able to evaluate the kernel and solve a small binary search problem at every iteration.
In the next sections, we instantiate our results for several common comparator and loss classes and show how the relevant parameters

More specifically, in

, we demonstrate how to construct kernels that satisfy KDOI and in

Since the kernels for each condition can be constructed separately and then combined (added) to create a kernel to pass into the

that satisfies both conditions jointly, the constructions in each subsection can be mixed and matched according to the prediction problem at hand.

4.2
Loss classes satisfying kernel decision OI.

In this subsection, we present several broad classes of loss functions satisfying kernel decision OI, which says that the composition of the discrete derivatives of loss functions with their associated post-processing functions must be in an RKHS and have bounded function and feature norms.

Throughout these next two subsections, we restrict our attention to a particular class of losses: those that depend only on decisions
\hat{y}
and outcomes
y
, and not on features
x
. We will call these loss classes
feature-invariant
.
This is the typical setting for omniprediction in prior work
[
GKR+22
,
GJRR24
]
(and for loss or regret minimization).
Since all of the loss functions in this section will be assumed to be invariant to the feature vectors, we will drop
x
from the notation and consider
{\cal L}\subset\{[0,1]\times\{0,1\}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}
.
We will also drop the argument for
x
from each post-processing function
\pi_{\ell}
. Later on, in
Section

4.4
, we will bring the dependence on
x
back in when we generalize these constructions to
separable
losses.

A naive strategy.

A first attempt to achieve kernel decision OI is to find a rich, expressive RKHS
{\cal F}
such that
\partial\ell\in{\cal F}
then hope that the composition
\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell}
is also in
{\cal F}
.
20
20

20

Recall that
\partial{\cal L}
is defined as the set
\{\partial\ell\;|\;\ell\in{\cal L}\}
, and
\partial\ell(x,p)
is defined as
\ell(x,p,1)-\ell(x,p,0)
.

In fact, it is generally straightforward to find such RKHSs that contain
\partial\ell
for many natural loss classes.
For example, the set of losses where
\ell(\hat{y},y)
is Lipschitz in
\hat{y}
for each
y\in\{0,1\}
is contained in an RKHS. This is the Sobolev space mentioned in the preliminaries of this section.
Lipschitz loss functions include squared/absolute error on a bounded domain, Huber, exponential, and the hinge loss, among others.

Unfortunately, the mere fact that
\partial{\cal L}
is contained in an RKHS
{\cal F}
does not imply that
\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell}
is in
{\cal F}
.

Proposition

4.10
shows a formal counterexample for the case where
{\cal F}
is the Sobolev space.

Proposition 4.10
.

There exists a kernel
k
with RKHS
{\cal F}
and a set of losses
{\cal L}
such that
\partial{\cal L}\subseteq{\cal F}
, but

\{\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell}\;|\;\ell\in{\cal L}\}\nsubseteq{\cal F}.

Proof.

Let
{\cal L}\subseteq\{[0,1]\times\{0,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
be the set of functions that just depend on
\hat{y}
and
y
such that for all
\ell\in{\cal L}
and
y\in\{0,1\}
,
\ell(\cdot,y)\;:\;[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is differentiable and for which both
\ell
and its derivative with respect to
\hat{y}
are square integrable over
[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\ell(t,y)^{2}dt+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\ell^{\prime}(t,y)^{2}dt<\infty

Notice that
\partial{\cal L}
is the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}([0,1])
, which is an RKHS that has an efficient kernel. (See
Example

A.13
for a definition of Sobolev spaces relevant to our context.)
We will show that the postprocessing of a function
\partial\ell\circ\pi_{\ell}\in{\cal L}
may
not
be in the Sobolev space.
Take
\ell(x,\hat{y},1)=-(\hat{y}-1/2)^{2}
and
\ell(x,\hat{y},0)=(\hat{y}-1/2)^{2}
, which are each in
W^{1,2}([0,1])
.
Next, we will argue the postprocessing
\pi_{\ell}

Intuitively, the previous says that if a loss class satisfies common regularity conditions like truthfulness (i.e. a proper scoring rule), smoothness/convexity, or is finite, then there exists a kernel satisfying KDOI.

Comparator and loss classes satisfying kernel hypothesis OI.

Having analyzed how one can guarantee kernel

Regression trees.

Our first result in this section shows one can guarantee kernel hypothesis OI for the class

Proposition 4.12
.

Let

Proof.

We first note that regression trees on binary features are low degree polynomials, which are contained an RKHS

associated with the degree

Example

A.10

To see this, we can write each tree in the following form:
For a given regression tree, let

\displaystyle h(x)=\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in\{0,1\}^{d}}c_{b}\prodop\displaylimits_{m=0}^{d-1}((1-x_{i_{b,m}})(1-b_{m})+x_{i_{b,m}}b_{m})

\displaystyle h(x)=\sumop\displaylimits_{I\in{\cal I}}a_{I}x_{I}

Asymptotic KHOI for all continuous functions.

RKHSs can contain very rich function classes which can be used as benchmark classes.
Indeed, some RKHSs are
universal approximators
in the sense that they contain arbitrarily precise approximations of all continuous functions.

Formally, an RKHS
{\cal F}
is a universal approximator if, for all
\varepsilon
and continuous
g:{\cal X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, there exists some
f\in{\cal F}
such that
\sup_{x}|f(x)-g(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon
. Several common kernels like the Gaussian (or RBF) kernel,
k(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2})
fall into this class.
We refer the reader to
[
Ste08
]
, Section 4.6 for further examples and background.

Universal approximators can be used to guarantee KHOI with respect to any continuous benchmark function
h
and loss
\ell
. However, the result is best understood in an asymptotic sense since it is not always tractable to control relevant function norms in the RKHS.

Here, we outline a general approach for doing so. The template matches those of similar results in the literature (see e.g. the discussion in Section C of
[
FK06
]
).
Let
{\cal H}
be a comparison class of continuous functions and
{\cal L}
be a class of continuous losses. Since the composition of continuous functions is continuous, the functions in
\partial{\cal L}\circ{\cal H}
are also continuous.
For a universal approximator
{\cal F}
, denote by
{\cal F}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq{\cal F}
a set such that for all
\partial\ell\circ h\in\partial{\cal L}\circ{\cal H}
, there exists some
f\in{\cal F}_{\varepsilon}
such that
\|f-\partial\ell\circ h\|_{\infty}\leqslant\varepsilon
.
Define

B_{\varepsilon}=\inf_{{\cal F}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq{\cal F}}\sup_{f\in{\cal F}_{\varepsilon}}\|f\|_{{\cal F}}

be the infimum of a uniform upper bound on the norm of subsets
{\cal F}_{\varepsilon}
satisfying the property.
Notice that
B_{\varepsilon}\geqslant B_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
for all
\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon^{\prime}
since any
{\cal F}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
satisfying the
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-approximation property also satisfies
\varepsilon
-approximation.
Then, one can chose a sequence
\varepsilon_{T}
for
T=1,2,\dots
such that
\lim_{T\to\infty}\varepsilon=0
and
B_{\varepsilon_{T}}=o(\sqrt{T})
.
Then, the universal approximator can be used to satisfy an asymptotic, approximate version of KHOI with respect to
{\cal H}
and
{\cal L}
.

4.4
Generalizing kernel OI to separable losses.

So far, we’ve established structural properties of losses
\ell(\hat{y},y)
that guarantee kernel decision and hypothesis OI. Here, we generalize these analyses to include losses that also depend on the features
x
.
In particular, we prove that these requisite OI conditions also for a wide variety of
separable
loss functions
\ell(x,\hat{y},y)
: those where each loss function can be factorized into a function of the feature vector
x
and of the decision-outcome pair
(\hat{y},y)
.

Definition 4.14

(Separable Losses)
.

A loss function
\ell(x,\hat{y},y)
is separable if there exists functions
\ell_{x}:{\cal X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
\ell_{y}:[0,1]^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that for all
(x,\hat{y},y)
,

\displaystyle\ell(x,\hat{y},y)=\ell_{x}(x)\ell_{y}(\hat{y},y).

Similarly, we say that a set of losses
{\cal L}
For a separable loss class
{\cal L}
, we will define two new sets
{\cal L}_{x}
and
{\cal L}_{y}
to consist of the sets of the feature and decision-outcome components of the losses, respectively:

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\{\ell_{x}(x)\ell_{y}(\hat{y},y):\ell_{x}\in{\cal L}_{x},\ell_{y}\in{\cal L}_{y}\}.

We refer to
{\cal L}_{x}
and
{\cal L}_{y}
as the
factors
of the separable class
{\cal L}
.

Separable loss classes capture many important examples of loss functions that depend on features.
For example,
{\cal L}_{x}
may consist of indicator functions for set membership, so that the loss only accumulates for members of a certain set.
More generally,
{\cal L}_{x}
can be interpreted to consist of any (re)weighting of the loss function over feature vectors
x
.
These kinds of losses will be important for our results on link prediction at the end of this section.

We next state a simple result showing how to construct kernels for separable loss classes.
Intuitively, the result says that any of the feature-invariant losses in the previous subsection can be reweighted by functions of the features
x
, as long as these functions are themselves in an RKHS with bounded norms.

Proposition 4.15

(Corollary to
Lemma

A.6
)
.

Let
{\cal L}
be a separable class of losses with factors
{\cal L}_{x},{\cal L}_{y}
and let
{\cal H}
be a comparator set of functions. Assume that
k_{x}
has an RKHS
{\cal F}_{x}
such that
{\cal L}_{x}\subseteq{\cal F}_{x}
and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sup_{\ell_{x}\in{\cal L}_{x}}\|\ell_{x}\|_{{\cal F}_{x}}^{2}\cdot\sup_{x\in{\cal X}}k_{x}(x,x)}\leqslant B_{x}.

1.

If
k_{y}
is a kernel that is
({\cal L}_{y},{\cal H})
-KHOI with parameter
B_{y}
. Then, then the product kernel,

k((x,p),(x^{\prime},p^{\prime}))=k_{x}((x,p),(x^{\prime},p^{\prime}))\cdot k_{y}((x,p),(x^{\prime},p^{\prime})),

({\cal L},{\cal H})
-KHOI with parameter
B_{x}B_{y}
.

2.

If
k_{y}
is a kernel that is
({\cal L}_{y})
-KDOI with parameter
B_{y}
. Then, then the same product kernel is
({\cal L},{\cal H})
-KDOI with parameter
B_{x}B_{y}
.

Proof.

The result follows directly from
Lemma

A.6
, which says that the product of functions in an RKHS are contained in an RKHS and that the norm of the product function is no more than the product of norms of component the functions.
∎

Letting the separable loss class be functions where
{\cal L}_{x}
is composed of a set membership kernel (as described in
Lemma

A.8
or any of the examples in
Section

{\cal L}_{y}
consist of loss functions

In particular,
{\cal F}_{x}
could consist of any collection of functions indexed by a set

and
\ell_{x}\in{\cal L}_{x}\subseteq{\cal F}_{x}

These could include, but are not limited to any finite set of group membership indicators.
In this case,

{\cal F}_{y}
could consist of any of the classic loss functions considered in

We leave exploration of non-separable loss functions where
\ell(x,\hat{y},y)

4.5
Guarantees for online regression.

Before moving onto to discussing the application of these techniques in the link prediction context, we briefly remark on how these ideas apply to the specific problem of online regression.

4.7
Connections to Performative Prediction

We close this section with some brief remarks interpreting these loss minimization guarantees within the context performative prediction.

Recall that in the online prediction protocol,
x_{t}\in{\cal X}
can chosen arbitrarily and in particular as a function of the history
\pi_{t-1}=\{(x_{i},p_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
. Outcomes
y_{t}
can be chosen as a function both of the history
\pi_{t-1}
and the current distribution over predictions
\Delta_{t}
. Hence in this setup, both the features
x_{t}
and the outcomes
y_{t}
can be performative. That is, they can be a
function of the predictive model
. Furthermore, no restrictions are made regarding how Real Life responds to realized sequence of predictions. Please see
[
PZMH20
,
HMD23
,
PS23
]
for further background on the performative prediction literature.

In particular, given an algorithm
{\cal A}
, let
\{(x_{t}({\cal A}),\hat{y}_{t}({\cal A}),y_{t}({\cal A}))\}_{t=1}^{T}
be the sequence of features, decisions and outcomes that are induced by making predictions
p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}
according to
{\cal A}
in the online protocol where
\hat{y}_{t}=\pi_{\ell}(x_{t},p_{t})
. Similarly,let
\{(x_{t}(h),\hat{y}_{t}(h),y_{t}(h))\}_{t=1}^{T}
be the sequence of features, predictions and outcomes that are induced by making predictions according to some other function
h
.
The algorithms we introduce in this section satisfy the following guarantee:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t}({\cal A}),\hat{y}_{t}({\cal A}),y_{t}({\cal A}))\leqslant\min_{h\in{\cal H}}\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t-1}^{T}\ell(x_{t}({\cal A}),h(x_{t}({\cal A})),y_{t}({\cal A}))+o(1)

This condition states that, in hindsight over the sequence of data induced by the algorithm
{\cal A}
, no alternative
h
in the comparator class would have higher loss. We think of this as a version of online performative stability (see
[
PZMH20
]
for a formal definition of performative stability).

This is different than performative optimality.
22
22

22

Also note that both guarantees are the same if the data sequence
(x_{t},y_{t})
is not influenced by the predictions.
The most natural definition for an algorithm
{\cal A}
to guarantee performative optimality would be the following statement where we change the dependency structure on the right hand side of the bound above:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x_{t}({\cal A}),\hat{y}_{t}({\cal A}),y_{t}({\cal A}))\leqslant\min_{h\in{\cal H}}\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t-1}^{T}\ell(x_{t}(h),h(x_{t}(h)),y_{t}(h))+o(1).

(31)

While stability is about making good predictions in hindsight over the data that you induce, optimality is inherently a counterfactual statement. To achieve performative optimality, one compares performance not on the same data sequence, but on the data that
would have resulted
by making decisions according to some other function
h
. Our algorithms guarantee the former, but not the latter.

In the batch setting, we by now know how to achieve performative optimality (see
e.g.

[
MPZ21
]
) and even performative omniprediction
[
KP23
]
. We believe it is an interesting direction for future work to understand how one might guarantee online performative omniprediction.
That is, algorithms which achieve the guarantee in

simultaneously over many losses.

5
New Algorithms for Online Quantile & Vector Regression, Distance to Multicalibration, and Extensions to the Batch Case

As an addeded benefit of our investigation into kernel methods for online indistinguishability and omniprediction, we obtain algorithms for other, seemingly different, online prediction problems.
In this section, we illustrate how to generalize the ideas presented previously beyond the binary setting to quantile regression and vector-valued predictions. As was true previously, the RKHS perspective provides a computationally efficient way to generate predictions that are indistinguishable with respect to rich classes of real-valued test functions in these settings.

In addition to these new algorithms, we also initiate the study of distance to multicalibration and prove that the classical problem of weak agnostic learning of a function class
{\cal F}
can be solved efficiently whenever
{\cal F}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

5.1
Quantile regression.

Unlike the binary case where means (i.e
\E[y\mid X=x]
) provide a complete description of the conditional distribution over outcomes, knowing the mean of a real-valued outcome
y
often provides a misleading picture of the future. In domains like finance and weather prediction where outcomes are noisy and heavy-tailed,
y
and
\E[y\mid x]
can be very different. In these cases, we often often want estimates of best or worst case outcomes for
y_{t}
. Quantile prediction provides a rigorous way to estimate these best/worst case outcomes and quantify uncertainty.

Prediction protocol.

The online protocol for quantile calibration mirrors that of binary prediction. At every round

x_{t}\in{\cal X}
arbitrarily, the learner chooses a distribution
\Delta_{t}
over outcomes
p_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Finally, Nature selects a distribution

over outcomes
y_{t}\in[Y_{\min},Y_{\max}]

\Delta_{t}
and

. Throughout this section, we will assume that Real Life selects outcomes from a Lipschitz distribution. This is a technical condition, standard in online quantile prediction
[

]
, which requires that small changes in predictions also imply small changes in the CDF of

Definition 5.1

.

A conditional label distribution

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{y\sim o}[y\leqslant p_{1}]-\mathrm{Pr}_{y\sim o}[y\leqslant p_{2}]|\leqslant\rho\cdot|p_{1}-p_{2}|.

(v-q)^{2}=q(1-q)+(1-2q)(v-q)
, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{T}k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{i},p_{i}))(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)(1\{y_{i}\leqslant p_{i}\}-q)-\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))q(1-q).

Finally, since
k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{i},p_{i}))(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)(1\{y_{i}\leqslant p_{i}\}-q)
is equal to

\langle\Phi(x_{i},p_{i})(1\{y_{i}\leqslant p_{i}\}-q),\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)\rangle_{{\cal F}},

we arrive at the identity that:

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E[S_{t}^{q}(p_{t})(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)]=\frac{1}{2}\bigg\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)\cdot\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}-\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}q(1-q)\bigg\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}.

Lastly, by our assumption that
\E[S_{t}^{q}(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)]\leqslant\varepsilon_{t}
, we get our desired result:

\displaystyle\E\bigg\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)\cdot\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg\|^{2}_{{\cal F}}\leqslant 2\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon_{t}+\E\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}q(1-q)\bigg\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}.

∎

Given this result, the final step in the analysis is to show that the Quantile Any Kernel Algorithm generates predictions such that
\E[S_{t}^{q}(p_{t})(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)]\approx 0
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that the learner makes predictions
p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}
according to the Quantile Any Kernel algorithm and that Real Life selects outcomes
y_{t}
from a
\rho
-Lipschitz conditional distribution
o_{t}
, then

\displaystyle|\E_{y_{t}\sim\Delta_{t},p_{t}\sim o_{t}}S_{t}^{q}(p_{t})(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)|\leqslant\frac{1}{10t^{2}}\rho.

Proof.

If
S_{t}^{q}(Y_{\min})
and
S_{t}^{q}(Y_{\max})
are both non-negative or non-positive then the inequality,

\displaystyle S_{t}^{q}(p_{t})(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)\leqslant 0,

holds trivially regardless of the outcome
y_{t}.
If they have opposite signs, recall that by definition of the algorithm, the learner plays
p_{t,1}
with probability
r_{1}=\tau
and
p_{t,2}
with probability
r_{2}=1-r_{1}
. With his in mind,

\displaystyle\E_{y_{t},p_{t}}\left[S_{t}^{q}(p)(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)\right]=r_{1}\cdot S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})\E[1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,1}\}-q]+r_{2}\cdot S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,2})\E[1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,2}\}-q].

By adding and subtracting,
r_{1}\cdot S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})\E[1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,2}\}-q]
, we can rewrite this as,

\displaystyle[r_{1}S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})+r_{2}S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,2})]\cdot\E[1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,2}\}-q]+r_{1}S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})\E[1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,1}\}-1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,2}\}].

By choice of
r_{1},p_{t,1}
and
p_{t,2}
, we have that,
r_{1}S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})+r_{2}S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,2})=0
, so the first term drops out. Then, since Real Life is required to select outcomes from a Lipschitz distribution,

\displaystyle r_{1}S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})\E[1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,1}\}-1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,2}\}]

\displaystyle\leqslant|S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})|\cdot|\mathrm{Pr}[y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,1}]-\mathrm{Pr}[y_{t}\leqslant p_{t,2}]|

\displaystyle\leqslant|S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})|\cdot\rho\cdot|p_{t,1}-p_{t,2}|.

The bound follows from the fact that
|S_{t}^{q}(p_{t,1})|\leqslant B_{t}
and
|p_{t,1}-p_{t,2}|\leqslant 1/(10\cdot B_{t}\cdot t^{3})
.
∎

Taken together, these lemmas establish the following theorem which summarizes the final guarantee of the
Quantile Any Kernel algorithm
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
k
be a kernel with associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space
{\cal F}
. If outcomes
y_{t}
are drawn from a
\rho
-Lipschitz conditional distribution, then, the
Quantile Any Kernel algorithm
generates a transcript
\{(x_{t},\Delta_{t},y_{t})\}
such that for all
f\in{\cal F}
,

\displaystyle\big|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E(1\{y_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\}-q)f(x_{t},p_{t})\big|\leqslant\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\rho+\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}q(1-q)\E k((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))}

Furthermore, if the kernel is bounded by
B
,

\sup_{(x,p)\in{\cal X}\times[0,1]}k((x,p),(x,p))\leqslant B,

then the per round runtime of the algorithm is bounded by
{{\cal O}}(t\cdot\log(tB)\cdot\mathsf{time}(k))
, where
\mathsf{time}(k)
is a uniform upper bound on the runtime of computing the kernel function
k
.

Discussion.

To the best of our knowledge this is the first online algorithm for quantile regression with respect to functions spaces
{\cal F}
that are an RKHS. As was the case with the
Any Kernel algorithm
, the algorithm is very simple to implement. At every time step, one only needs to solve a binary search problem over the unit interval. Furthermore, the guarantees are adaptive and illustrates how certain quantiles
q
(those closer to 0 or 1) lead to lower OI error bounds than those closer to 1/2. Lastly, the algorithm is hyperparameter free, one does not need to know the Lipschitz constant
\rho
ahead of time. The only requirement is that we know bounds
Y_{\min},Y_{\max}
on the outcome
y
.

5.2
Vector-valued, high-dimensional regression.

In addition to quantile regression, the RKHS and defensive forecasting viewpoint also provides a simple way of generating indistinguishable predictions in settings where outcomes are high-dimensional. That is, instead of binary or scalar-valued outcomes, in this subsection we consider the case where
y_{t}\in{\cal Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\cal Y}
is a compact, convex set (e.g
{\cal Y}=[-1,1]^{d}
).

Formal setup.

The online protocol is identical to that of scalar prediction. At every round
t
, Real Life chooses features
x_{t}\in{\cal X}
arbitrarily, the learner chooses a distribution
\Delta_{t}
over
p_{t}\in{\cal Y}
. Finally, Nature selects a distribution
o_{t}
over outcomes
y_{t}\in{\cal Y}
, possibly as a function of
\Delta_{t}
and
x_{t}
.

Definition 5.6

(Online Vector-Valued Indistinguishability)
.

An algorithm
{\cal A}
guarantees online high-dimensional indistinguishability with respect to class of functions
{\cal F}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
if it is guaranteed to generate a transcript satisfying the following guarantee,

\displaystyle\big|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t},y_{t}\sim o_{t}}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}f(x_{t},p_{t})\big|\leqslant\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}(T,f)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\cal A}:\mathbb N\times{\cal F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
o(T)
for every
f
.

Note that in this setting the test functions
c(x_{t},p_{t})
are
vector-valued
. High-dimensional indistinguishability asks that, when averaged over the sequence, prediction errors
y_{t}-p_{t}
are uncorrelated with any test function
f\in{\cal F}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}f(x_{t},p_{t})=0.

Background on vector-valued RKHSs

As was the case previously, the algorithm has guarantees with respect to set functions that form an RKHS, but in this functions take values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
rather than
\mathbb{R}
. A vector-valued RKHS is a set of functions
{\cal F}\subset\{{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, where the set
{\cal F}
is itself a Hilbert space, equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\cal F}}
.

A kernel
K
for a vector-valued RKHS is a mapping from
{\cal X}\times{\cal X}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
To disambiguate from the scalar case, we use capital
K
to denote matrix-valued kernels and lower case
k
to denote a scalar-valued kernel.

For a more comprehensive background on vector-valued kernels, we refer the reader to the excellent survey by Alvarez, Rosasco, and Lawrence
[
ÁRL12
]
. For the context of our results, we will only need two main facts. First, as with the scalar case, the kernel
K
has the reproducing property such that for any function
f:{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the RKHS and vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

\displaystyle f(z)^{\top}v=\langle f,\Phi(z)v\rangle_{{\cal F}}

(34)

Here
\Phi(x)
is the feature map of
x
. For any fixed
x
,
\Phi(x)
is a mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
{\cal F}
. The last property we need is part a) from Proposition 2.1 in
[
MP05
]
which states that for any
x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}
and
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle v^{\top}K(z,z^{\prime})v^{\prime}=\langle\Phi(z^{\prime})v^{\prime},\Phi(z)v\rangle_{{\cal F}}

(35)

The Vector Any Kernel Algorithm

Input:
A compact, convex set
{\cal Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a kernel
K:({\cal X}\times{\cal Y})^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

For
t=1,\dots,
:

1.

Given history
\{(x_{i},p_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
and current features
x_{t}
define

\displaystyle S_{t}(p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{t-1}K((x_{t},p),(x_{i},p_{i}))(y_{i}-p_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

2.

If
K
is continuous in
p
,
return

\Delta_{t}=p_{t}\in{\cal Y}
that solves the variational-inequality:

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in{\cal Y}}(y-p_{t})^{\top}S_{t}(p)\leqslant 0

(36)

For discontinuous kernels,
return

\Delta_{t}
, where
\Delta_{t}
is a distribution over
p_{t}\in{\cal Y}
satisfying

\displaystyle\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}\sup_{y\in{\cal Y}}(y-p_{t})^{\top}S_{t}(p)\leqslant 0

(37)

Figure 3:
Extension of the
Any Kernel algorithm
for high-dimensional prediction. For simplicity, we state the algorithm assuming that the variational inequalities are solved exactly. However, as illustrated previously in quantile and binary prediction, the analysis can be easily modified to accomodate approximate solutions. The behavior of the algorithm for continuous kernels is the same as in
[
VNTS05
]
. The extension to the discontinuous case is new.

Algorithmic guarantees.

As before, the advantage of this approach is that the final algorithm has strong guarantees of performance, and is additionally very simple to state and analyze. The main computational difference relative to previous settings is that the learner needs to solve a
variational inequality
(
Eqs.

36
and
37
). Variational inequalities are a rich and well-developed area of research within the optimization literature
[
KS00
,
Noo88
]
, with earliest work dating back to the papers by Signori and Fichera
[
Fic63
]
.
These optimization problems always have a solution. Furthermore, these solutions can be found efficiently in various settings.

However, before discussing these ideas further, we state the final end-to-end result for the
Vector Any Kernel algorithm
:

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
K
be a kernel for a vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space
{\cal F}
. Then, the
Vector Any Kernel algorithm
is guaranteed to generate a transcript such that for any
f\in{\cal F}
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}f(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg|\leqslant\|f\|_{{\cal F}}\sqrt{\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t}}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}K((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))(y_{t}-p_{t})}.

If we further assume that the kernel K is uniformly bounded by
B
over
{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
, and that the diameter of the set
{\cal Y}
is at most
D
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\cal X},p\in{\cal Y}}\|K((x,p),(x,p))\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant B,\quad\sup_{p,p^{\prime}\in{\cal Y}}\|p-p^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}\leqslant D

then, the above guarantee implies that:

\displaystyle\bigg|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}c(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg|\leqslant\|c\|_{{\cal F}}\sqrt{BDT}.

Furthermore, the per round runtime of the algorithm is at most
{\cal O}(t\mathsf{timeVE)})
where
\mathsf{timeVE)}
is an upper bound on the time it takes solve the variational inequality problems in
Equation

36
and
Equation

37
.

Proof.

We start the analysis by again showing that it suffices to bound the correlation between the features
\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})
and the errors
(y_{t}-p_{t})
. Using the reproducing property for vector-valued RKHSs,
Eq.

34
, we first show the following bound:

\displaystyle\bigg|\E\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}c(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\E\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\langle c,\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\rangle_{{\cal F}}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\langle c,\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\rangle_{{\cal F}}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|c\|_{{\cal F}}\cdot\big\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E\left[\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\right]\big\|_{{\cal F}}.

(38)

Next, we show that the
Vector Any Kernel algorithm
bounds the second term. In particular, by construction, the algorithm guarantees that:

\displaystyle\E_{p_{t}\sim\Delta_{t},\Delta_{t}\sim o_{t}}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}S_{t}(p_{t})\leqslant 0\text{ where }S_{t}(p)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{t-1}K((x_{t},p),(x_{i},p_{i}))(y_{i}-p_{i}).

Summing up this quantity over all
T
rounds,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geqslant\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{t-1}\E\left[(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}K((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{i},p_{i}))(y_{i}-p_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{T}\E\left[(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}K((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{i},p_{i}))(y_{i}-p_{i})\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E\left[(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}K((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))(y_{t}-p_{t})\right].

Hence,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{T}\E\left[(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}K((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{i},p_{i}))(y_{i}-p_{i})\right]\leqslant\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E\left[(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}K((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))(y_{t}-p_{t})\right].

(39)

Now, by applying
Eq.

35
, we see that,

\displaystyle(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}K((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{t},p_{t}))(y_{t}-p_{t})

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t}),\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\rangle_{{\cal F}}

\displaystyle=\big\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\big\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}

(40)

And,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}K((x_{t},p_{t}),(x_{i},p_{i}))(y_{i}-p_{i})=\big\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\big\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}

(41)

Combining
Eqs.

39
,
40
and
41
(and Jensen’s inequality) we get that the Vector Any Kernel algorithm generates sequence satisfying,

\displaystyle\big\|\E\left[\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\right]\big\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}\leqslant\E\left[\Big\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\Big\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}\right]\leqslant\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E\left[\big\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\big\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}\right]

Together with the first inequality,
Eq.

38
, we get our desired data-dependent guarantee,

\displaystyle\bigg|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}c(x_{t},p_{t})\bigg|\leqslant\|c\|_{{\cal F}}\sqrt{\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\E\left[\big\|\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\big\|_{{\cal F}}^{2}\right]}.

∎

Variational inequalities.

As seen from the description of the algorithm, the main computational step is the Vector Any Kernel algorithm is to solve for a vector
p_{t}
, or a distribution
\Delta_{t}
over vectors
p_{t}
that satisfies,

\displaystyle(y-p_{t})^{\top}S_{t}(p)\leqslant 0\quad\forall y\in{\cal Y}.

From a first glance, it is not obvious that such a
p_{t}
exists. However, in a recent, related paper on online calibration, Foster and Hart show that these “outgoing fixed points” exists under very mild conditions. We restate their result below:

Proposition 5.8

(Theorem 4 & Corollary 6 in
[
FH21
]
)
.

Let
{\cal Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact, convex set and let
S:{\cal Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a continuous function. Then, there exists a point
p_{*}\in{\cal Y}
such that,

\displaystyle(y-p_{*})^{\top}S(p_{*})\leqslant 0\quad\forall y\in{\cal Y}.

If
S:{\cal Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is not necessarily continuous, but bounded in the sense that,

\sup_{y\in\hat{\mathcal{Y}}}\|S(y)\|_{2}<\infty,

then, for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists a distribution
\Delta
supported on at most
d+3
points in
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
such that,

\displaystyle\E_{p_{*}\sim\Delta}(y-p_{*})^{\top}S(p_{*})\leqslant\varepsilon\quad\forall y\in\hat{\mathcal{Y}}.

Not only do these fixed points exists, but by now there is an increasingly extensive literature on algorithms for finding them
[
CGR12
,
BI98
,
KS00
,
Noo88
]
under various regularity conditions on the function
S
.

Discussion.

The
Vector Any Kernel algorithm
is most closely related to the K29 (not star) algorithm from Vovk
[
VNTS05
,
Vov07
]
. By using the forecast hedging idea from
[
FH21
]
, we extend the algorithm so that it works for any matrix valued kernel. Modulo this extension, the regret guarantees are nearly identical.

To the best of our knowledge, the other most closely related work is the recent paper by Noarov, Ramalingam, Roth, and Xie
[
NRRX23
]
. Using different techniques to ours (from online minimax optimization), they introduce an algorithm that achieves the following guarantee,

\displaystyle\|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-p_{t})^{\top}f(x_{t},p_{t})\|_{\infty}\leqslant{\cal O}(\sqrt{T}).

This is essentially the same goal we consider (up to poly d factors). However, their result holds with respect to functions
f
taking values in
\{0,1\}^{d}
(they refer to
f
as events) and sets
{\cal F}
which are finite. In our case,
|{\cal F}|
is infinite and
{\cal F}
is real-valued since it is an RKHS.

Furthermore, their runtime is guarateed to be polynomial whenever
|{\cal F}|
is polynomially sized whereas our results are best understood as being oracle efficient. The algorithm runs in polynomial time whenever there exists an efficient oracle that can solve the corresponding variational inequality. These efficent algorithms exist for instance when the functions
S
are
monotone
, however they may be computationally difficult in general.

Please see the supplemantary material for results on how one can design matrix valued kernels whose corresponding RKHS contain an arbitrary finite set of functions
{\cal F}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
.

5.3
Distance to online multicalibration.

In this subsection, we show that instantiating the
Any Kernel algorithm
with a particular kernel
k
achieves small
distance to online multicalibration
, a novel extension of the canonical notion of
distance to (online) calibration
from
[
BGHN23
,
QZ24
]
which we introduce in this paper.

To start, we start by recalling what it means for a predictor to be perfectly calibrated and restate the definition of distance to calibration from
[
BGHN23
,
QZ24
]
.

Definition 5.9

(Perfect Online (Multi)Calibration)
.

Suppose we are given fixed sequences of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{T})\in[0,1]^{T}
, features
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})\in{\cal X}^{T}
, outcomes
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{T})\in\{0,1\}^{T}
, and a collection
{\cal C}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\cal X}
of group indicator functions.
We say that
\bm{p}
is
perfectly multicalibrated with respect to the collection
{\cal C}
if for all
v\in[0,1]
and
c\in{\cal C}
,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-v)c(x_{t})\bm{1}[p_{t}=v]=0.

Likewise, we say that a prediction is perfectly calibrated if it is multicalibrated with respect to the collection
{\cal C}
that just contains the constant 1 function.

Given a function
c:{\cal X}\to\{0,1\}
, let
\mathrm{PC}(c)
denote the set of prediction sequences
\bm{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{T})\in[0,1]^{T}
that are perfectly calibrated on
c
. Let
\mathrm{PC}({\cal C})
be the intersection of
\mathrm{PC}(c)
for all
c\in{\cal C}
.

While defining perfect calibration is relatively straightforward, defining distance to calibration is not. In their recent work,
[
BGHN23
]
propose a unifying notion of distance to calibration. Here, we state the online version of their definition as presented in
[
QZ24
]
.

Definition 5.10

(Distance to Online Calibration
[
QZ24
]
)
.

Suppose we are given fixed sequences of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{T})\in[0,1]^{T}
, features
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})\in{\cal X}^{T}
, outcomes
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{T})\in\{0,1\}^{T}
. The
distance to online calibration
is

\mathrm{dCE}_{\bm{y}}(\bm{p})=\inf_{\bm{q}\in\mathrm{PC}({1})}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}|p_{t}-q_{t}|,

where
{1}:{\cal X}\to\{0,1\}
denotes the all-ones function.

With these definitions in hand, we now introduce our definition of distance to (online)
multicalibration
. Given a collection
{\cal C}
of group indicator functions there are several ways of defining distance to multicalibration. Here, we present two such versions, showing how one is efficiently achievable and the other is in fact impossible to achieve in general.

Definition 5.11

(Distance to Online Multicalibration, Standard and Strong Variants)
.

Suppose we are given fixed sequences of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{T})\in[0,1]^{T}
, features
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})\in{\cal X}^{T}
, outcomes
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{T})\in\{0,1\}^{T}
, and a collection
{\cal C}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\cal X}
of group indicator functions.

We define the
distance to online multicalibration

\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}
and
strong distance to online multicalibration

\mathrm{dMCE}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\sup_{c\in{\cal C}}\inf_{\bm{q}\in\mathrm{PC}(c)}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}|p_{t}-q_{t}|,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dMCE}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\bm{q}\in\mathrm{PC}({\cal C})}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}|p_{t}-q_{t}|,

where
\mathrm{PC}({\cal C})
is as defined in
Definition

5.9
.

Several remarks about
Definition

5.11
are in order. First, it is easy to see that even the first of these two notions of distance to multicalibration is still stronger than a global notion of distance to calibration. For example, in the online setting, consider a single subsequence indicator
c:{\cal X}\to\{0,1\}
such that for each
t=1,\ldots,T
,

c(x_{t})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $t$ is odd}\\
0&\text{if $t$ is even.}\end{cases}

Suppose the outcome sequence
y
follow the same pattern, so
y_{t}=c(x_{t})
, but we predict
p_{t}=1/2
for all time steps
t\in[T]
. In this case,
\bm{p}
will be perfectly calibrated with respect to
\bm{y}
in a global sense, but
\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},\{c\}}(\bm{p})=T/4=\Omega(T)
.

Next, observe that in the definition of distance to online multicalibration, the constraint
q\in\mathrm{PC}(c)
only restricts the values that
q
takes during time steps
t\in[T]
such that
c(x_{t})=1
. In other words, during time steps for which
c(x_{t})=0
, it is clearly optimal to take
q_{t}=p_{t}
if the goal is to minimize the sum on the right side, because this ensures that the
t
th
term satisfies
|p_{t}-q_{t}|=0
. Consequently, we have the equality

\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})=\sup_{c\in{\cal C}}\inf_{\bm{q}\in\mathrm{PC}(c)}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}|p_{t}-q_{t}|c(x_{t})

Next, we establish the relationship between our standard and strong notions of distance to online multicalibration:

Theorem 5.12
.

For any prediction, feature, and outcome sequences, and for any collection
{\cal C}
,

\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})\leqslant\mathrm{dMCE}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p}).

Moreover, this inequality can be strict; in fact, there exists a distribution over feature and outcome sequences, as well as a collection
{\cal C}
, such that for any prediction algorithm used to generate
\bm{p}
,

\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})\leqslant O(1)

but with high probability,

\mathrm{dMCE}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})\geqslant\Omega(T).

Proof.

Using the fact that
\bm{q}\in\mathrm{PC}({\cal C})
necessarily belongs to
\mathrm{PC}(c)
for each
c\in{\cal C}
, it is clear that

\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})\leqslant\mathrm{dMCE}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})

for any prediction sequence
\bm{p}
. To see that this inequality can be strict, consider a setting in which
{\cal X}=\mathbb N
and
x_{t}=t
at each time step
t\in[T]
. Consider the collection
{\cal C}_{\mathrm{singleton}}
consisting of all “singleton” indicator functions
c_{t}
of the form
c_{t}(s)=\bm{1}[s=t]
for some fixed
t\in[T]
. In this case, being perfectly calibrated on the set
\{t\}
amounts to exactly predicting the
t
th
bit—in other words, the event that
p_{t}=y_{t}\in\{0,1\}
. Consequently, the set
\mathrm{PC}({\cal C}_{\mathrm{singleton}})
of perfectly
{\cal C}
-multicalibrated prediction sequences is a singleton set that only contains the true outcome sequence
\bm{y}
, which implies that

\mathrm{dMCE}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}_{\mathrm{singleton}}}(\bm{p})=\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}|p_{t}-y_{t}|.

On the other hand, using the aforementioned characterization of the standard notion of distance to online multicalibration, we see that

\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}_{\mathrm{singleton}}}(\bm{p})=\max_{t\in[T]}\,|p_{t}-y_{t}|,

the
maximum
error made at any particular time step. In particular, in this example, we have that
\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}_{\mathrm{singleton}}}(\bm{p})\leqslant 1
for any prediction sequence
\bm{p}
. However, if
y_{t}\in\{0,1\}
is sampled uniformly and independently of the history of predictions and outcomes before time step
t
, we have
\mathrm{dMCE}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}_{\mathrm{singleton}}}(\bm{p})\geqslant\Omega(T)
with high probability, regardless of the algorithm used to make the predctions at each time step.
∎

To conclude this section, we show that the
Any Kernel algorithm
can be used to achieve small distance to online multicalibration, provided that we aim for the standard notion, as opposed to the strong notion.

Theorem 5.13
.

Given a collection
{\cal C}
of indicator functions for subpopulations of a population
{\cal X}
, let
k_{\mathrm{Lap}}=k_{\mathbb{R}}
be the
Laplace kernel
as defined in
Example

A.13
, let
\mathsf{Int}_{{\cal C}}:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the intersection kernel

\mathsf{Int}_{{\cal C}}(x,x^{\prime})=|\{c\in{\cal C}:c(x)=c(x^{\prime})=1\}|,

and let
k_{\mathrm{MC}}:(\mathbb{R}\times{\cal X})\times(\mathbb{R}\times{\cal X})\to\mathbb{R}
denote the product kernel

k_{\mathrm{MC}}((p,x),(p^{\prime},x^{\prime}))=k_{\mathrm{Lap}}(p,p^{\prime})\cdot\mathsf{Int}_{\cal C}(x,x^{\prime}),

which is uniformly bounded by

m=\max_{x\in{\cal X}}|\{c\in{\cal C}:c(x)=1\}|.

Let
\pi_{1:T}=\{(x_{t},p_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
denote the transcript at the end of the
Any Kernel algorithm
when instantiated with the kernel
k_{\mathrm{MC}}
. Then,

\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})\leqslant{\cal O}(\sqrt{mT}).

Proof.

Theorem

3.2
guarantees that the transcript ultimately satisfies

\left|\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}(p_{t}-y_{t})f(p_{t})c(x_{t})\right|\leqslant\sqrt{mT+1}

for all
f
with norm at most
1
in the RKHS corresponding to
k_{\mathrm{Lap}}
, and for all
c\in{\cal C}
(these have norm at most
1
in the RKHS corresponding to
\mathsf{Int}_{\cal C}
by
Lemma

A.8
). Next, we fix a particular function
c\in{\cal C}
and rewrite this inequality as

\left|\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[T]\\
c(x_{t})=1\end{subarray}}(p_{t}-y_{t})f(p_{t})\right|\leqslant\sqrt{mT+1}.

Letting
\bm{y}_{c},\bm{p}_{c}\in[0,1]^{|S|}
denote the restriction of
\bm{y},\bm{p}\in[0,1]^{T}
to the set
S
of
t\in[T]
for which
c(x_{t})=1
, this implies that the
kernel calibration error

\sqrt{mT+1}
:

\mathrm{kCE}^{k_{\mathrm{Lap}}}_{\bm{y}_{c}}(\bm{p}_{c}):=\sup_{f:\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{Lap}}\leqslant 1}\left|\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[T]\\
c(x_{t})=1\end{subarray}}(p_{t}-y_{t})f(p_{t})\right|\leqslant\sqrt{mT+1}.

By Lemma 7.3 of
[
BGHN23
]

\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[T]\\

Since our initial choice of

was arbitrary, we conclude that

\mathrm{dMCE}_{\bm{y},{\cal C}}(\bm{p})=\sup_{c\in{\cal C}}\inf_{\bm{q}\in\mathrm{PC}(c)}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}|p_{t}-q_{t}|={\cal O}(\sqrt{mT}).\qed

On measuring distance to multicalibration.

A priori, it is not clear from

5.4
Offline results: weak agnostic learning and online to batch conversions.

In this section, we shift our attention to the offline setting where samples are drawn i.i.d from some fixed distribution
{\cal D}
. We prove two main results.

The first shows that one can efficiently solve weak agnostic learning over function classes
{\cal F}
that are an RKHS. Given the tight connection between weak agnostic learning and multicalibration
[
HKRR18
]
, this result shows that any multicalibration algorithm that relies on the existence of a weak agnostic learner is unconditionally efficient whenever
{\cal F}
is an RKHS.

Second, we show to convert the online learning algorithms into offline algorithms with strong guarantees for the batch setting. This adaptation in particular implies omniprediction and outcome indistinguishability algorithms for the batch case with end-to-end computational efficiency and near-optimal statistical guarantees.

Efficient (strong) learning over an RKHS.

We start by recalling the definition of weak agnostic learning. Here, we state the definition as presented in
[
GKR24
]
:

Definition 5.14

(Weak Agnostic Learning)
.

Let
{\cal D}
be a distribution over
{\cal X}\times[-1,1]
. Given a comparator class
{\cal H}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\rightarrow[-1,1]\}
, a weak agnostic learner for
{\cal H}
solves the following promise problem: Given an accuracy parameter
\gamma
, if there exists
h\in{\cal H}
such that

\displaystyle\E_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[h(x)y]\geqslant\gamma

then weak agnostic learner returns a function
h^{\prime}:{\cal X}\rightarrow[-1,1]
(not necessarily in
{\cal H}
) such that

\displaystyle\E_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[h(x)y]\geqslant{\rm poly}(\gamma).

Using the representer theorem, we prove that one can efficient solve a stronger version of the optimization problem above when
{\cal H}
is an RKHS.

Proposition 5.15

(Existence of a Strong Learner over an RKHS)
.

Let
k
be a efficiently computable kernel with associated RKHS
{\cal F}\subseteq\{{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
with
\sup_{x}k(x,x)\leqslant 1
and let
{\cal F}_{B}\subseteq{\cal F}
be the subset of functions with norm at most
B
,

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{B}=\{f\in{\cal F}:\|f\|_{{\cal F}}\leqslant B\}.

Then, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm such that for any
\gamma\geqslant 0
, given
n\geqslant{\rm poly}(1/\gamma,\log(1/\delta))
samples
(x,y)\sim{\cal D}
, returns a function
f^{\prime}\in{\cal F}
such that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[\max_{f\in{\cal F}_{B}}\E_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[f(x)y]-\E_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[f^{\prime}(x)y]\geqslant\gamma\right]\leqslant\delta.

Proof.

The proof consists of two parts. First, we show that the corresponding empirical risk minimization problem can be solved in polynomial time. Second, we prove a uniform convergence bound showing that the empirical risk and the true risk of the functions in this class are close.
Let
S_{n}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
for
x_{i}\in{\cal X}
and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
be a dataset.

Starting with the first part, let
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be set of samples drawn i.i.d from
{\cal D}
. By the Moore-Aronszajn theorem (
Theorem

A.3
), we can write any function
f\in{\cal F}
as
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\Phi(x_{i})+v
where
v
lies in the orthogonal complement to

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{span}}\{\Phi(x):x\in\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\}\}.

Therefore, using the representer theorem,
f(x_{i})=\langle f,\Phi(x_{i})\rangle_{{\cal F}}
,we can write the following optimization problem over a Hilbert space
{\cal F}

\displaystyle\argmax_{f\in{\cal F}_{B}}\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})y_{i}

as an optimization problem over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\argmax_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\langle\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\Phi(x_{j})+v,\Phi(x_{i})\rangle_{{\cal F}}

\displaystyle\text{s.t}\quad\langle\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\Phi(x_{i}),\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\Phi(x_{i})\rangle_{{\cal F}}\leqslant B^{2}.

If we let
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the matrix with
k(x_{i},x_{j})=\langle\Phi(x_{i}),\Phi(x_{j})\rangle_{{\cal F}}
as its
(i,j)
th entry, this becomes,

\displaystyle\argmax_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{n}\alpha^{\top}Ky

(42)

\displaystyle\text{s.t}\quad\alpha^{\top}K\alpha\leqslant B^{2}.

This is a convex optimization problem (linear objective, quadratic constraints) and can hence be solved to any tolerance
\gamma
in time polynomial in
n
and
1/\gamma
.

To finish the proof, we prove a uniform convergence bound showing that all of the functions in
{\cal F}_{B}
are close to their empirical counterparts with high probability:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sup_{f\in{\cal F}_{B}}|\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})y_{i}-\E f(x)y|\geqslant B\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right]\leqslant\delta.

(43)

The proof of this fact follows from observing that by applying the representer theorem and linearity of inner products, we can avoid union bounding over all
f\in{\cal F}_{B}
and instead just bound a quantity involving the feature vectors:

\displaystyle|\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})y_{i}-\E[f(x)y]|

\displaystyle=|\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\langle f,\Phi(x_{i})\rangle_{{\cal F}}y_{i}-\E[\langle f,\Phi(x)\rangle_{{\cal F}}y]|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f\|_{{\cal F}}\|\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\Phi(x_{i})y_{i}-\E\Phi(x)y\|.

Now, since
\sup_{x}k(x,x)=\|\Phi(x)\|_{{\cal F}}\leqslant 1
and
y\in[-1,1]
, the vectors
z=\Phi(x)y
are sub-Gaussian (have norm bounded by 1 a.s). Therefore, we can just apply standard concentration bounds for sub-Gaussian vectors. In particular, we apply Proposition 7 in
[
MP21
]
(
Lemma

5.18
) to get that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\Phi(x_{i})y_{i}-\E\Phi(x)y\|_{{\cal F}}\leqslant 8e\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

This completes the proof of the claim in
Equation

43
. The proof of the main result then follows directly by combining this concentration result with the optimization fact from
Equation

42
. In particular, let
f^{\prime}
be an
\gamma
approximate optima for
Equation

42
(which can be computed in polynomial time), and let
f
be any other function in
{\cal F}_{B}
. Then,

\displaystyle\E[f^{\prime}(x)y]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}f^{\prime}(x_{i})y_{i}-{\cal O}(B\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})y_{i}-{\cal O}(B\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n})-\gamma

\displaystyle\geqslant\E[f(x_{i})y_{i}]-{\cal O}(B\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n})-\gamma.

Letting
n\geqslant{\rm poly}(B,\gamma^{-1},\log(1/\delta)))
, we get that
\E[f^{\prime}(x)y]\geqslant\sup_{f\in{\cal F}_{B}}\E[f(x)y]-{\cal O}(\gamma)
.
∎

Online to batch conversions.

For the sake of completeness, we also illustrate how one can convert any of the online algorithms we study in this paper into batch algorithms. The proof of the following result is somewhat standard and uses classical martingale decompositions, but we include it for completeness.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
k
be a kernel with RKHS
{\cal F}
satisfying

\sup_{x\in{\cal X},p\in[0,1]}k((x,p),(x,p))\leqslant B<\infty

and let
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a dataset of i.i.d samples drawn from a fixed distribution
{\cal D}
over
{\cal X}\times\{0,1\}
.

Furthermore, let
S=\{(x_{i},y_{i},p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be transcript generated from running the
Any Kernel algorithm
on the samples
(x_{i},y_{i})
and
h_{i}:{\cal X}\rightarrow[0,1]
be the randomized function induced by the
Any Kernel algorithm
conditioned on
\pi_{1:i-1}=\{(x_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
.

If we define
\overline{h}_{S}
be the randomized predictor which selects a function from the set
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
uniformly, then with probability
1-\delta
over the randomness of the
n
samples and the predictor
\overline{h}_{S}
, the following inequality holds for all
f\in{\cal F}
,where
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are universal constants:

\displaystyle\E_{S\sim{\cal D}^{(n)},(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[(y-\overline{h}_{S}(x))f(x,\overline{h}_{S}(x))]\leqslant c_{0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}}B+c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{1+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

Proof.

We use a similar decomposition as in the previous results. We start by using the reproducing property of the RKHS, linearity of expectation and then applying Cauchy-Schwarz:

\displaystyle\E[(y-\bar{h}_{S}(x))f(x,\bar{h}_{S}(x))]

\displaystyle=\E[(y-\bar{h}_{S}(x))\langle f,\Phi(x,\bar{h}_{S}(x))\rangle_{{\cal F}}]

\displaystyle=\langle f,\E[(y-\bar{h}_{S}(x))\Phi(x,\bar{h}_{S}(x))]\rangle_{{\cal F}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\|\E[(y-\bar{h}_{S}(x))\Phi(x,\bar{h}_{S}(x))]\|_{\mathcal{F}}.

Having done this, the proposition follows by combining the following two statements:

\displaystyle\E[(\overline{h}_{S}(x)-y)\Phi(\overline{h}_{S}(x),x)]\lesssim\|\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-y_{i})\Phi(x_{i},p_{i})\|_{\mathcal{F}}+\sqrt{\frac{1+\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

(44)

\displaystyle\|\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-y_{i})\Phi(x_{i},p_{i})\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leqslant\sqrt{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\E p_{i}(1-p_{i})}\leqslant\sqrt{n}

where the second one is exactly the guarantee shown for the
Any Kernel algorithm
from
Theorem

3.2
(see
Equation

9
). We hence now focus on establishing the bound in
Equation

44
. By definition of
\bar{h}_{S}
,

\displaystyle\E[(\overline{h}_{S}(x)-y)\Phi(\overline{h}_{S}(x),x)]

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\E[(h_{i}(x)-y)\Phi(x,h_{i}(x))\mid h_{i}]\mathrm{Pr}[\overline{h}_{S}=h_{i}]

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{t}\E[(h_{s}(x)-y)\Phi(x,h_{s}(x))\mid h_{s}].

Now consider the following Hilbert-space valued martingale sequence
V_{i}
adapted to the filtration
{\cal B}_{i}=\sigma(\{(x_{i},y_{i}),p_{o}\}_{i=1}^{n})
where
V_{0}=0
and

\displaystyle V_{i+1}=V_{i}+\E_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[(h_{i}(x)-y)\Phi(x,h_{i}(x))\mid{\cal B}_{i-1}]-(p_{i}-y_{i})\Phi(x_{i},p_{i}).

We can easily check that this process is indeed a martingale. Clearly,
V_{t}
is adapted to
{\cal B}_{t}
. Furthermore, since
\|(p_{t}-y_{t})\Phi(x_{t},p_{t})\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leqslant B
, then
\E\|V_{i}\|_{\mathcal{F}}<\infty
. Lastly, since

\displaystyle\E[(p_{i}-y_{i})\Phi(x_{i},p_{i})\mid{\cal B}_{i-1}]=\E_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[(h_{i}(x)-y)\Phi(x,h_{i}(x))\mid{\cal B}_{i-1}],

then,

\displaystyle\E[V_{i+1}\mid{\cal B}_{i}]=\E[V_{i}\mid{\cal B}_{i}]+0=V_{i}.

Rewriting
V_{i}
as

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\E_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[(h_{i}(x_{i})-y_{i})\Phi(x_{i},h_{i}(x_{i}))\mid{\cal B}_{i-1}]-(p_{i}-y_{i})\Phi(x_{i},p_{i})

Using the Azuma-Hoeffding deviation inequality from
[
Nao12
]
(
Lemma

5.17
), there exists a universal constant
c^{\prime}
such that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|V_{i}\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leqslant c^{\prime}\sqrt{t\log(e^{3}/\delta)},

and hence by the reverse triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{t}\E_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}[(h_{s}(x)-y)\Phi(x,h_{s}(x))\mid{\cal B}_{s-1}]\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leqslant\|\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{t}(\tilde{p}_{s}-y_{s})\Phi(x_{s},\tilde{p}_{s})\|_{\mathcal{F}}.

Plugging this into the decomposition from
Equation

45
, we get that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\E[(\overline{h}_{t}(x)-y)\Phi(\overline{h}(x),x)]\leqslant\|\frac{1}{t}\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{t}(\tilde{p}_{s}-y_{s})\Phi(x_{s},\tilde{p}_{s})\|_{\mathcal{F}}+c_{0}^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\log(e^{3}/\delta)}{t}}.

This establishes our two previous conditions and hence concludes the proof of the result.
∎

Appendix A
Background on Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

A.1
Definition and properties.

We start with a more detailed definition of an RKHS and some of its key properties.

Definition A.1

(Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces)
.

A set of functions
{\cal F}\subseteq\{f\;:\;{\cal X}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) if it satisfies the following properties.

1.

There exists an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\cal F}}\;:\;{\cal F}\times{\cal F}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. That is,
\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\rangle_{{\cal F}}
is symmetric, linear in its first argument, and positive definite (for all
f
,
\langle{f},{f}\rangle_{{\cal F}}\geqslant 0
, and
\langle{f},{f}\rangle_{{\cal F}}=0
if and only if
f=0
).

2.

The space is complete with respect to the norm
\|f\|_{{\cal F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\langle{f},{f}\rangle_{{\cal F}}}
. That is, for all Cauchy sequences
f_{1},f_{2},\dots\in{\cal F}
, it holds
\lim_{i\to\infty}f_{i}\in{\cal F}
.

3.

For all
x\in{\cal X}
, there exists a function
K_{x}\in{\cal F}
such that

\displaystyle f(x)=\langle{f},{K_{x}}\rangle_{{\cal F}}

for all
f\in{\cal F}
where
\langle{\cdot},{K_{x}}\rangle_{{\cal F}}
is continuous.

The map
\langle{\cdot},{K_{x}}\rangle_{{\cal F}}\;:\;{\cal F}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called the evaluation functional. The function
K(x,x^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\langle{K_{x}},{K_{x^{\prime}}}\rangle_{{\cal F}}
is called the
reproducing kernel
(or
kernel
for short) of
{\cal F}
.
Next, we define positive semi-deminite functions, which will be used in
Theorem

A.3
.

Definition A.2

(PSD function)
.

A symmetric function
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is positive semi-definite if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})\geqslant 0

for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in{\cal X}
and
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

The next theorem states that each positive semi-definite function corresponds to a unique RKHS.

Theorem A.3

(Moore-Aronszajn Theorem)
.

Let
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a positive semi-definite function. Then, there is a unique RKHS
{\cal F}\subseteq\{f\;:\;{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
for which
k
is the reproducing kernel. Moreover,
{\cal F}
consists of the completion of the linear span of
\{k(\cdot,x)\;|\;x\in{\cal X}\}
, i.e., the set

\displaystyle\left\{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k(\cdot,x_{i})\;\;\middle|\;\;\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}},x_{i}\in{\cal X},\lim_{m\to\infty}\sup_{n\geqslant m}\left\|{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=m}^{n}\alpha_{i}k(\cdot,x_{i})}\right\|_{{\cal F}}=0\right\}.

For example, if
|{\cal X}|<\infty
then, the RKHS induced by
k
is

\displaystyle{\cal F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\sumop\displaylimits_{x_{i}\in{\cal X}}\alpha_{i}k(\cdot,x_{i})\;:\;\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

Next, we state several lemmas that are useful for our analysis.

Lemma A.4

(Corollary to
Theorem

A.3
)
.

Let
{\cal F}
be a RKHS on
{\cal X}
. Then the zero function
x\mapsto 0
is in
{\cal F}
, and, more generally, for all
f\in{\cal F}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, any linear function
x\mapsto\alpha f(x)
is in
{\cal F}
.

Lemma A.5

(Theorem 5.4,
[
PR
]
)
.

Let
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
be positive semi-definite kernels on
{\cal X}
with associated RKHSs
{\cal F}_{1}
and
{\cal F}_{2}
then
k=k_{1}+k_{2}
is a valid kernel with associated RKHS
{\cal F}
equal to the completion of the span of

\{f_{1}+f_{2}:f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1},f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}\}.

Moreover, direct implication of the above result is that, for
f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1},f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}
,
\|f_{1}+f_{2}\|_{\cal F}\leqslant\|f_{1}\|_{{\cal F}_{1}}+\|f_{2}\|_{{\cal F}_{2}}
.

A direct implication of the above result, since the zero function
x\mapsto 0
is in every RKHS, is that
{\cal F}_{1}\cup{\cal F}_{2}\subseteq{\cal F}
.

Lemma A.6

(Theorem 5.11,
[
PR
]
)
.

Let
k_{1}:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
k_{2}:{\cal Y}\times{\cal Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be positive semi-definite kernels with associated RKHSs
{\cal F}_{1}
and
{\cal F}_{2}
then
k((x,y),(x,y^{\prime}))=k_{1}(x,x^{\prime})k_{2}(y,y^{\prime})
is a valid kernel. Furthermore, its associated function space is the completion of the span of the set

\{f_{1}\cdot f_{2}\;:\;f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1},f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}\}

where for any
f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1},f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}
we define
f_{1}\cdot f_{2}\;:\;{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}\to{\mathbb{R}}
to be the function
(f_{1}\cdot f_{2})(x,y)=f_{1}(x)f_{2}(y)
for all
(x,y)\in{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
.
Moreover, for
f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1},f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}
,
\|f_{1}\cdot f_{2}\|_{\cal F}\leqslant\|f_{1}\|_{{\cal F}_{1}}\|f_{2}\|_{{\cal F}_{2}}
.

Lemma A.7

(Theorem 5.7,
[
PR
]
)
.

For any function
\phi\;:\;{\cal X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and RKHS
{\cal F}_{0}\subseteq\{f\;:\;{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}
associated with kernel
k
, there exists an RKHS
{\cal F}_{1}
equal to the completion of the span of the set
\{f\circ\phi\;:\;f\in{\cal F}_{0}\}
and
associated with kernel
k\circ\phi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}k(\phi(\cdot),\phi(\cdot))
.
Moreover, it holds
\|f\circ\phi\|_{{\cal F}_{1}}\leqslant\|f\|_{{\cal F}_{0}}
.

Lemma A.8
.

Let
{\cal X}
be any set and let
{\cal I}
be any index set. Let
{\cal F}=\{f_{i}\}_{i\in{\cal I}}
be a collection of functions
f_{i}:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}
indexed by
{\cal I}
. Suppose that for each
x\in{\cal X}
, we have

\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in{\cal I}}f_{i}(x)^{2}<m

(46)

for some constant
m
,
in which case the function
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

k(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in{\cal I}}f_{i}(x)\,f_{i}(y)

is a valid kernel. Then, the RKHS
{\cal F}
corresponding to
k
contains
{\cal F}
, and
\|f_{i}\|_{{\cal F}}\leqslant 1
for each
i\in{\cal I}
.

Proof of
Lemma

A.8
.

We introduce several pieces of notation:

–

Let
{\cal H}
be the Hilbert space of “coefficient sequences”
\alpha:{\cal I}\to\mathbb{R}
that are
L^{2}
bounded by
m
with respect to the counting measure on
{\cal I}
, which means that
\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in{\cal I}}\alpha(i)^{2}<m
.

–

For each
x\in{\cal X}
, define a coefficient sequence
\Phi_{x}:{\cal I}\to\mathbb{R}
by the formula
\Phi_{x}(i)=f_{i}(x)
. Note that
\Phi_{x}\in{\cal H}
by the assumption that
\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in{\cal I}}f_{i}(x)^{2}
is finite. Note also that the kernel function
k
satisfies

k(x,y)=\langle\Phi_{x},\,\Phi_{y}\rangle_{{\cal H}}

for any
x,y\in{\cal X}
.

–

Given a coefficient sequence
\alpha\in{\cal H}
, let
f_{\alpha}:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the function

f_{\alpha}(x)=\langle\alpha,\,\Phi_{x}\rangle_{{\cal H}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in{\cal I}}\alpha(i)\,f_{i}(x).

–

Let
V\subseteq{\cal H}
be the closure in
{\cal H}
of the subspace
\mathrm{span}\{\Phi_{x}:x\in{\cal X}\}
. In other words, let
V
be the set of all finite linear combinations of coefficient sequences
\Phi_{x}
for
x\in{\cal X}
, together with their limit points in
{\cal H}
. Relatedly, let
\mathrm{proj}_{V}
denote the orthogonal projection of
{\cal H}
onto
V
, which satisfies
\mathrm{proj}_{V}(\alpha)\in V
and

\big\langle\alpha-\mathrm{proj}_{V}(\alpha),\,\Phi_{x}\big\rangle_{{\cal H}}=0

(47)

for each
\alpha\in{\cal H}
and
x\in{\cal X}
.

Rephrased in this language, Moore-Aronszajn theorem and its proof simply show that the map
\alpha\mapsto f_{\alpha}
is a distance-preserving, one-to-one correspondence (
i.e.
an isometric isomorphism) between
V
and the RKHS
{\cal F}
corresponding to the kernel
k
. Next, by
Eq.

47
with
\alpha=e_{i}
, we see that for all
x\in{\cal X}
and
i\in{\cal I}
,

f_{i}(x)=\langle e_{i},\,\Phi_{x}\rangle_{{\cal H}}=\big\langle\mathrm{proj}_{V}(e_{i}),\,\Phi_{x}\big\rangle_{{\cal H}}=f_{\mathrm{proj}_{V}(e_{i})}(x).

Here,
e_{i}
denotes the
i
th
standard basis coefficient sequence

e_{i}(j)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=j,\\
0&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

Using the aforementioned distance-preserving correspondence between
V
and
{\cal F}
, we see that

\|f_{i}\|_{{\cal F}}=\big\|f_{\mathrm{proj}_{V}(e_{i})}\big\|_{{\cal F}}=\|\mathrm{proj}_{V}(e_{i})\|_{{\cal H}}\leqslant\|e_{i}\|_{{\cal H}}=1,

which concludes the proof.
∎

We also remark that if
{\cal F}
is a (not necessarily finite) collection of indicator functions for subsets
S_{i}\subseteq{\cal X}
but each
x\in{\cal X}
belongs to at most finitely many such
S_{i}
, then
Eq.

10
is satisfied, so
Lemma

A.8
implies that the RKHS corresponding to the intersection kernel

k(x,y)=\mathsf{Int}_{\cal F}(x,y)=\big|\{i\in{\cal I}:x,y\in S_{i}\}\big|

contains all functions in
{\cal F}
and that their norms in
{\cal F}
are at most
1
.

A.2
Key examples.

Example A.9

(Linear functions)
.

Let
{\cal X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
{\cal F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
, the space of all linear functions from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
, defined as,

{\cal F}_{\mathrm{lin}}=\{f_{w}:w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},f(x)=x\cdot w\}\subseteq\{\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}

is an RKHS with corresponding kernel
k_{\mathrm{lin}}(x,x^{\prime})=x\cdot x^{\prime}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime}
equal to the standard inner product. The feature mapping is just the identity function
\Phi(x)=x
. Note that each element
f\in{\cal F}
could be thought of both as a function from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
as well as an element in the Hilbert space (which in this case is just
\mathbb{R}^{d}
). However, going back to our earlier comment, we see that we could have equivalently written out
{\cal F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
as,

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{\mathrm{lin}}=\mathsf{span}\left\{\sumop\displaylimits_{x_{i}\in{\cal X}}\alpha_{i}k_{\mathrm{lin}}(\cdot,x_{i})\;:\;\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=\mathsf{span}\left\{\sumop\displaylimits_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\alpha_{i}x_{i}\;:\;\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Example A.10

(Polynomial functions)
.

Consider the set of polynomials of degree
\leqslant k
on
d
variables with the inner product defined as the inner product of the coefficients on each monomial.
In this case,
{\cal X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since the space of coefficients is just
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
for some appropriate
\ell
(depending on the dimension of the input space
d
and
k
), it is complete and the inner product satisfies all the necessary properties.

Then, to show that this has the reproducing property, let
K_{x}
be the polynomial where the coefficient on a given monomial is determined by multiplying together the corresponding entries of
x
. So the coefficient on the
x_{1}x_{2}^{3}
term is the first entry of
x
times the cube of the second entry of
x
.
Then, notice that for all
f\in{\cal H}
,
f(x)=\langle{f},{K_{x}}\rangle_{{\cal H}}
.
It can be shown that the corresponding kernel is

k(x,y)=(1+\langle x,y\rangle)^{k}.

Example A.11

(Boolean functions)
.

Consider the set of functions taking the form
f\;:\;\{-1,1\}^{d}\to\{-1,1\}
.

First, notice that we can write
f
as a polynomial. For
a,x\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
, define the indicator polynomial

\displaystyle 1_{a}(x)

\displaystyle=(\frac{1+a_{1}x_{1}}{2})(\frac{1+a_{2}x_{2}}{2})\cdot\cdot\cdot(\frac{1+a_{d}x_{d}}{2})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\;\text{if }a=x\\
0&\;\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then, notice

\displaystyle f(x)=\sumop\displaylimits_{a\in\{-1,1\}^{d}}f(a)1_{a}(x).

This is just the sum of
2^{d}
different order
d
polynomials and therefore a polynomial of order
d
.
Thus, Boolean functions are a subset of the polynomials and we can use the kernel
k(x,y)=(1+\langle x,y\rangle)^{d}
.
The inner product is also the same as for the polynomials: the inner product is just the inner product of the coefficients on each monomial.

In fact, if we distribute the products in
1_{a}(x)
, we can see that every Boolean function can be written as

\displaystyle f(x)=\sumop\displaylimits_{I\in 2^{d}}\alpha_{I}x_{I}

for
\alpha_{I}\in\mathbb{R}
a constant and
x_{I}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\prodop\displaylimits_{i\in I}x_{i}
. See
[
O’D21
]
for more discussion of Boolean functions.

Example A.12

(Regression trees)
.

As a special case of Boolean functions, we will write down the functions representing regression trees on Boolean inputs.
For a given regression tree, let
b\in\{0,1\}^{k}
represent the path down the decision tree, where
b_{i}=0
means go to the left child (
i.e.
, the the decision variable in the
i
th decision following path
b
is 0) and
b_{i}=1
means go to the right child at depth
i
.
Let
c_{b}
be the leaf assigned to path
b
. Let
i_{b,j}
represent the index of the decision variable at the
j
th decision following path
b
. Then any decision tree can be specified by
\{c_{b}\}_{b\in\{0,1\}^{k}}
and
\{i_{b,j}\}_{b\in\{0,1\}^{k},j\in[k]}
:

\displaystyle f(x)=\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in\{0,1\}^{k}}c_{b}\prodop\displaylimits_{\ell=0}^{k-1}((1-x_{i_{b,\ell}})(1-b_{\ell})+x_{i_{b,\ell}}b_{\ell})

Example A.13

(Sobolev spaces
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
for
\Omega\in\{[0,1],{\mathbb{R}}\}
, )
.

This example comes from
[
BTA11
]
, Section 7.4, Examples 13 and 24.
Consider the set of functions
{\cal F}_{0}\subseteq\{\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\}
for
\Omega\in\{[0,1],{\mathbb{R}}\}
such that

1.

each function is differentiable almost everywhere and continuous, and

2.

each function and its derivative are square integrable.

The completion of
{\cal F}_{0}
with respect to the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\cal F}_{0}}^{2}=\intop\nolimits(f(x))^{2}\;dx+\intop\nolimits(f^{\prime}(x))^{2}\;dx.

is an RKHS
{\cal F}
(usually denoted
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
) where, if
\Omega=[0,1]
, the kernel is

\displaystyle k_{[0,1]}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{(e^{x}+e^{-x})(e^{1-x^{\prime}}+e^{x^{\prime}-1})}{2(e-e^{-1})}<3.

for
0\leqslant x\leqslant x^{\prime}\leqslant 1
and
k_{[0,1]}(x,x^{\prime})=k_{[0,1]}(x^{\prime},x)
if
0\leqslant x^{\prime}\leqslant x\leqslant 1
. If
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}
, the kernel is

\displaystyle k_{{\mathbb{R}}}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp\{-\lvert x-x^{\prime}\rvert\}.

The inner product in
{\cal F}
for differentiable functions
f,g\in{\cal F}
is

\displaystyle\langle{f},{g}\rangle_{{\cal F}}=\intop\nolimits f(x)g(x)\;dx+\intop\nolimits f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(x)\;dx.

Next, we state the following simple lemma about the composition of functions in
W^{1,2}([0,1])
.
For a set of differentiable functions
{\cal F}
, let
{\cal F}^{\prime}=\{f^{\prime}\;|\;f\in{\cal F}\}
denote the set of derivatives.

Lemma A.14
.

Suppose that there exists a universal constant
B\geqslant 1
and sets of differentiable functions
{\cal F}_{0},{\cal F}_{1}
with
\mathrm{Im}({\cal F}_{0})\subseteq[0,1]
,
\|{\cal F}_{0}\|_{W^{1,2}([0,1])}\leqslant B
,
\mathrm{Im}({\cal F}_{1})\subseteq[-B,B]
, and
\mathrm{Im}({\cal F}_{1}^{\prime})\subseteq[-B,B]
.
Then,
\{f_{1}\circ f_{0}\;|\;f_{0}\in{\cal F}_{0},f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1}\}\subseteq{\cal F}
and
\|f_{1}\circ f_{0}\|_{{\cal F}}\leqslant 2B^{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
f_{0}\in{\cal F}_{0},f_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1}
.
Notice that by the uniform boundedness of
f_{1}
,
\|f_{1}\circ f_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\leqslant B
. Also,
\|f_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\leqslant\|f_{0}^{\prime}\|_{W^{1,2}([0,1])}\leqslant B
. Then,

\displaystyle\|(f_{1}^{\prime}\circ f_{0})f_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f_{1}^{\prime}\circ f_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\|f_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle\leqslant B^{2}

where the first line comes from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the second line comes from the plugging in the bounds on each norm.
Also, by the uniform boundedness of
{\cal F}_{1}
,
\|f_{1}\circ f_{0}\|\leqslant B
, which implies the desired bound.
See, e.g.,
[
Eva18
]
, Theorem 4.4, part (ii) for more general conditions on the composition of functions in a Sobolev space.
∎

(Low-degree functions on
\{0,1\}^{n}

, Section 9.2)
.

Consider the set of functions
{\cal F}_{0}\subseteq\{\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]\}
whose Fourier spectrum is supported on monomials of degree at most

.
The kernel associated with the completion of

A.3
Matrix-valued kernels

We now introduce two standard definitions related to
matrix-valued
kernels and their corresponding vector valued reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. These standard facts can be found, for example, in
[
ÁRL12
,
Min16
]
.

Definition A.16
.

We say that a matrix-valued function
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a valid
kernel
if the following two “positive semidefiniteness” properties hold:

–

For all
x,y\in{\cal X}
, we have
k(x,y)=k(y,x)^{\top}
.

–

For all
n\in\mathbb N
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\cal X}
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\sumop\displaylimits_{a=1}^{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{b=1}^{n}\langle w_{a},k(x_{a},x_{b})w_{b}\rangle\geqslant 0.

Definition A.17
.

Given a matrix-valued kernel
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the
reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
{\cal F}
corresponding to
k
is a Hilbert space consisting of vector-valued functions
f:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Specifically,
{\cal F}
is the completion of the space of all linear combinations of functions of the form

x\mapsto\sumop\displaylimits_{a=1}^{n}k(x,x_{a})w_{a}

for some
n\in\mathbb N
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\cal X}
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is imbued with the unique inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\cal F}:{\cal F}\times{\cal F}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following property: for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\cal X}
and
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the inner product of the functions
f_{1}(x)=k(x,x_{1})w_{1}
and
f_{2}(x)=k(x,x_{2})w_{2}
is

\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{\cal F}=\langle w_{1},k(x_{1},x_{2})w_{2}\rangle,

where the inner product on the right hand side is the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The following result illustrates how one might represent any finite set of vector valued functions using a matrix valued kernel:

Lemma A.18
.

Let
{\cal X}
be any (not necessarily finite) population set and let
{\cal I}
be any (not necessarily finite) index set. Let
{\cal C}=\{c_{i}\}_{i\in{\cal I}}
be a collection of functions
c_{i}:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
indexed by
{\cal I}
. Suppose that for each
x\in{\cal X}
, we have

\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in{\cal I}}\lVert c_{i}(x)\rVert^{2}<\infty,

(
*
)

in which case the matrix-valued function
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
given by

k(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in{\cal I}}c_{i}(x)\,c_{i}(y)^{\top}

is a valid kernel. Then, the RKHS
{\cal F}
corresponding to
k
contains
{\cal C}
, and
\|c_{i}\|_{{\cal F}}\leqslant 1
for each
i\in{\cal I}
.

Proof.

Given a fixed element
y\in{\cal X}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, consider the following vector-valued function from
{\cal X}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

x\mapsto k(x,y)a.

By
Definition

A.17
, we know that the RKHS
{\cal F}
corresponding to the matrix-valued kernel
k
is the completion of the set of all linear combinations of vector-valued functions of the above form. Next, consider the following related
scalar-valued
kernel
k_{\mathrm{scalar}}:({\cal X}\times[d])\times({\cal X}\times[d])\to\mathbb{R}
, defined as follows:

k_{\mathrm{scalar}}((x,a),(y,b))=k(x,y)_{ab}.

The RKHS
{\cal F}_{\mathrm{scalar}}
corresponding to
k_{\mathrm{scalar}}
is given by the Moore-Aronszajn Theorem (
Theorem

A.3
), and comparing this description to the aforementioned description of
{\cal F}
, it becomes clear that
{\cal F}
and
{\cal F}_{\mathrm{scalar}}
are isometrically isomorphic, i.e. there is a one-to-one, length-preserving correspondence between elements of
{\cal F}
and elements of
{\cal F}_{\mathrm{scalar}}
. Specifically, the isomorphism maps a function
f:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
{\cal F}
to the function
f_{\mathrm{scalar}}:{\cal X}\times[d]\to\mathbb{R}
given by

f_{\mathrm{scalar}}(x,a)=f(x)_{a}

for each
x\in{\cal X}
and
a\in[d]
. By
Lemma

A.8
, the space
{\cal F}_{\mathrm{scalar}}
contains the function
c_{\mathrm{scalar}}:{\cal X}\times[d]\to\mathbb{R}
for each
c\in{\cal C}
, and these functions all have norm
\lVert c_{\mathrm{scalar}}\rVert_{{\cal F}_{\mathrm{scalar}}}\leqslant 1
. Consequently,
{\cal C}\subseteq{\cal F}
and
\lVert c\rVert_{{\cal F}}\leqslant 1
for each
c\in{\cal C}
, as well.
∎
