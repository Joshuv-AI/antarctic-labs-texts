Prevalence and risk factors of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts among young people in the MENAT region: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammed A Alhassan, Mohammed A Alarabi, Waled M Albalawi, Abdulaziz Alkhodairi, Khalil A Alghalayini, Ahmad K Alsomari, Hamzah A Al Fiaar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide is one of the leading causes of mortality among adolescents and young adults, with suicidal ideation and attempts representing critical predictors of suicide-related outcomes. Although global evidence highlights rising trends in suicidal behaviors, regional patterns remain varied. In the MENAT region (Middle East, North Africa, and Turkey), where cultural stigma, conflict, and insufficient mental health infrastructure may exacerbate vulnerability, evidence remains fragmented. This study systematically reviewed and meta-analyzed the prevalence, risk factors, and protective factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts among young people in the MENAT region.MethodsThe study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251101524) and conducted following PRISMA guidelines. A systematic search of six electronic databases (PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and PsycINFO) was conducted for studies published between January 2010 and June 2025. Eligible studies were observational in design, published in English, and reported the prevalence of suicidal ideation or suicide attempts among individuals aged ≤25 years in the MENAT region. Many included studies relied on single-item measures of suicidality rather than validated multi-item scales, which was considered when interpreting results. Pooled prevalence and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random-effects models. Heterogeneity, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias were examined.ResultsA total of 47 studies encompassing 115,152 participants were included. The pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was 25% (95% CI: 0.19–0.31), while suicide attempts were reported by 12% (95% CI: 0.06–0.19). Suicidal ideation reached as high as 52.7% in individual studies from Jordan, while suicide attempts were most prevalent in studies from Kuwait. Female gender, bullying (OR = 1.6), loneliness (OR = 2.2), substance use, including smoking (OR = 2.0), alcohol (OR = 3.0), and amphetamines (OR = 3.5), as well as depression (OR = 1.5), anxiety (OR = 2.1) and history of sexual abuse (OR = 2.0) were significantly associated with suicidal ideation. Suicide attempts were strongly linked to alcohol (OR = 3.0), cannabis use (OR = 1.7), anxiety (OR = 3.1), depression (OR = 1.5), and a history of sexual abuse (OR = 2.2). Evidence on protective factors such as family connectedness, religiosity, and social support was limited and inconsistently reported across studies, highlighting a major research gap in understanding resilience factors among youth in the MENAT region.ConclusionSuicidal ideation and attempts are prevalent among young people in the MENAT region and were significantly associated with psychosocial stressors and substance use. However, the reliance of many included studies on single-item assessments of suicidality limits the precision and comparability of estimates. These findings highlight the urgent need for culturally tailored, evidence-based suicide prevention strategies and enhanced mental health services in this vulnerable population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07585-7.Keywords: Adolescents, Youth, Young people, Suicidal ideation, Suicide attempt, MENAT

Introduction

Suicidal behavior is a major global public health concern and is the second leading cause of non-natural death worldwide [1]. It is characterized by suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and death by suicide. Suicidal ideation is defined as “thinking about, considering, or planning suicide,” whereas suicide attempts refer to “nonfatal, self-directed, potentially injurious behavior with an intent to die as a result of the behavior.” Suicide is defined as “death resulting from self-directed injurious behavior with the intent to die” [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that over 700,000 people die by suicide annually and an estimated 20 suicide attempts occur for every death [3].

Suicidal ideation is recognized as a significant risk factor for suicide and is the third leading cause of death among adolescents [4]. Specifically, among adolescents aged 15 to 19 years, suicide is the third leading cause of death in girls and the fourth leading cause in boys. Adolescence represents one of the most challenging and stressful periods of human development [5, 6]. At this stage, individuals are exposed to a range of biological, social, and psychological pressures. For instance, experiences of bullying and cyberbullying, as well as drug or alcohol use, sleep disturbances have been associated with higher rates of suicidal ideation [7–9]. Numerous studies have reported a rise in suicide rates among adolescents over the past two decades.

Large-scale international studies underscore the burden of suicidal behaviors among adolescents. For instance, a survey across 17 European countries found a 10.5% prevalence of suicide attempts among high school students [10], while a provincial survey in China reported suicidal ideation in 39.1% of secondary school adolescents [11]. Similar associations between family disharmony, substance use, and suicidal ideation have been documented elsewhere [12]. Recent evidence, including studies during the COVID-19 pandemic, further suggests that social isolation and loneliness exacerbate adolescent mental health risks [6, 13–15].

In the Middle East, North Africa, and Turkey (MENAT) region, several studies have reported varying rates of suicidal ideation and attempts. However, the findings remain fragmented, methodologically inconsistent, and difficult to compare across settings. The MENAT region includes countries affected by conflict, displacement, and limited mental health infrastructure, all of which may increase vulnerability among young people. Cultural differences and the stigma surrounding suicide may further hinder reporting of suicidal thoughts and behaviors, reducing the reliability of cross-cultural comparisons [16]. Despite the urgency of the issue, there is currently no comprehensive synthesis of the prevalence, risk factors, and protective factors associated with suicidal ideation and attempts among adolescents and young adults in the MENAT region. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to address this gap by providing pooled prevalence estimates and examining key risk and protective factors. The findings are intended to inform public health planning, guide culturally relevant suicide prevention efforts, and support the development of mental health services across the MENAT region.

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [17]. The associated PRISMA checklist is displayed in Supplementary Table S1. This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published studies; it did not involve human or animal participants. Therefore, ethical approval, informed consent, and an ethics committee protocol number were not required. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251101524).

A comprehensive search was performed across six major electronic databases, PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and PsycINFO, from database inception to June 2, 2025. The search strategy, developed in consultation with a medical librarian, combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) with free-text keywords related to: (1) suicidal ideation and suicide attempts; (2) young people (we defined ‘young people’ as individuals aged 10–24 years, in line with WHO classifications. However, studies that reported data including participants up to 25 years were also considered eligible); and (3) countries within the Middle East, North Africa, and Turkey (MENAT) region. A detailed description of the database-specific queries and search outcomes is presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Eligibility was restricted to peer-reviewed studies published in English from 2010 onward. This timeframe was chosen to ensure inclusion of contemporary data, reflecting recent sociopolitical changes and methodological advances in the assessment of suicidal behaviors in the MENAT region. Restriction to English was based on feasibility, as translation resources were unavailable. Two independent reviewers conducted the literature search and screened all retrieved records using RAYYAN software. Duplicate records were automatically removed and manually verified. Title and abstract screening were followed by full-text assessment to determine final inclusion. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or, when necessary, consultation with a third senior reviewer.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) reported the prevalence of suicidal ideation or suicide attempts separately among adolescents and young adults in the MENAT region, and (2) employed an observational design (cross-sectional, prospective cohort, or retrospective). Exclusion criteria included case reports, review articles, conference abstracts, study protocols, duplicate publications, and studies lacking full-text availability.

Definitions of suicide ideation and suicide attempts

Suicidal ideation is defined as thoughts of self-inflicted death, irrespective of intent or accompanying behaviors. Suicide attempts are defined as deliberate acts of self-harm undertaken with the intention of ending one’s life but not resulting in death. In many studies, both suicidal ideation and suicide attempts are assessed using single-item questions rather than validated psychometric scales. Examples include: “In the past 12 months, did you seriously consider attempting suicide?” and “In the past 12 months, how many times did you attempt suicide?” For the purposes of this review, suicidal ideation and suicide attempts were examined across three timeframes: current, past 12 months, and lifetime.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Data extraction was independently performed using a standardized data collection form. Extracted variables included author (year), study design, country, population type, recruitment period, number of participants reporting suicidal ideation or suicide attempts, total sample size, gender distribution, eligible age range, and the mean and standard deviation of participant age. Information on the measurement tools used to assess suicidal ideation and suicide attempts was extracted. Several studies employed single-item questions rather than validated instruments, which may contribute to variability in prevalence estimates. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third author. In addition, studies reporting prevalence were reexamined for the presence of associated risk factor data.

The risk of bias in included studies was assessed using Hoy’s risk of bias tool [18], which is specifically designed for prevalence and incidence studies. The tool comprises 10 items, of which items 1–4 assess external validity and items 5–10 assess internal validity. Each item is rated as either “yes” (1) or “no” (0), and total scores are categorized into three levels of overall risk: high risk (0–4), moderate risk (5–7), and low risk (8–10).

Statistical analysis

Prevalence estimates for suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were pooled using a random-effects model to account for substantial heterogeneity across studies. To address potential non-normality, the arcsine square-root transformation was applied. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic and the I2index, with p < 0.10 for Cochran’s Q or I2 ≥ 25% indicating significant heterogeneity [19]. Psychiatric inpatient populations that met the inclusion criteria were analyzed separately to enhance clarity in subgroup interpretation.

To examine risk factors for suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% CIs were extracted from eligible studies. Pooled ORs were calculated only when at least three studies reported data for a given risk factor; when fewer than three studies contributed, no pooled estimate was generated. Reporting of ORs varied across studies, with some presenting adjusted ORs and others reporting unadjusted ORs. Adjusted ORs were prioritized whenever available; if only unadjusted ORs were reported, these were included to maximize data coverage. As a result, pooled estimates for certain risk factors reflect a combination of adjusted and unadjusted data, which is indicated in Table 1.

Characteristics of the included studies (n = 47)

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall pooled effect size [67]. Subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by age group and country. Meta-regression analyses based on year of publication and gross domestic product (GDP) were also performed. Publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry and formally tested using Egger’s regression test, with p < 0.10 indicating potential bias [68]. When publication bias was suggested, the trim-and-fill method was applied to adjust pooled estimates accordingly.

Results

Study selection

The initial database search yielded 4,568 records from 6 databases. After removing 2877 duplicates, 1691 titles and abstracts were screened for relevance and 119 were retrieved for full-text review. Studies were excluded if they included participants older than 25 years, employed irrelevant study designs, were conducted outside the MENAT region, did not report prevalence data, or were duplicate publications. Ultimately, 47 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The PRISMA flow diagram visually summarizes the screening process (Fig. 1).

prisma flow diagram of study selection process

Among these, three studies involved psychiatric inpatient populations. Suicide ideation was reported in 41 studies, including two studies of psychiatric inpatient populations, while suicide attempts were reported in 26 studies, including all three psychiatric inpatient studies. Twenty-seven studies were classified as low risk of bias, while the remaining 20 were deemed to have moderate risk (Supplementary Table S3).

Descriptive characteristics of included studies

In total of 47 studies involving 115,152 participants were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Across these studies, 52% of participants were female, with a mean age of 15.7 years (SD = 1.8). The majority of studies were conducted in Turkey (n = 11), followed by Iran (n = 5), Lebanon (n = 5), Morocco (n = 4), Israel (n = 4), Saudi Arabia (n = 3), Kuwait (n = 3), and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) (n = 3). Two studies each were conducted in Egypt, Iraq, and Palestine, while one study was conducted in each of the following countries: Oman, Tunisia, and Jordan. Detailed characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1.

Pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation or suicide attempts

The pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was 25%, (95% CI: 0.19–0.31), with significant heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 99.7%, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2), indicating variability in populations, measures, and settings. A heat map illustrates the distribution of suicidal ideation prevalence across MENAT countries (Fig. 3). Jordan reported the highest prevalence (52.7%), derived from a single study with 860 participants out of the total 97,569 included in the analysis. This estimate should therefore be interpreted with caution due to its limited representativeness.

For suicide attempts, the pooled prevalence was 12.0% (95% CI: 0.06–0.19), also demonstrating significant heterogeneity (I2 = 99.8%, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4). The country-level prevalence of suicide attempts across the MENAT region is shown in Fig. 5, with the highest rate observed in Kuwait (61.1%), based on three studies with a combined sample of 6,565 participants among the total 49,297 included. Conversely, the lowest prevalence was reported in Egypt (2.4%). These country-level comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, as the observed differences likely reflect variability in study design, population characteristics, and reporting methods rather than true epidemiological disparities.

Suicidal ideation prevalence was similar among adolescents (25%) and young adults (27%) (Supplementary Fig. S1). In contrast, suicide attempts were slightly more common in young adults (17%) than in adolescents (12%) (Supplementary Fig. S2). Furthermore, psychiatric inpatient adolescents showed a higher prevalence of suicide attempts (33.0%, 95%: CI 0.24–0.42) (Supplementary Fig. S3). Suicidal ideation was not significantly correlated with year of publication (p = 0.64) or GDP (p = 0.08) (Supplementary Figs. S4, S5). Sensitivity analyses using the leave-one-out approach confirmed the robustness of the pooled estimates, indicating no single study exerting disproportionate influence on the overall effect size for either suicidal ideation or suicide attempts (Supplementary Figs. S6, S7).

Pooled risk factors of suicidal ideation or suicide attempts

Table 2 shows the detailed pooled estimates of the risk factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts. Males were significantly less likely to report suicidal ideation compared to females (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.1–1.4); however, there was no significant gender difference in the risk of suicide attempts. Suicidal ideation was significantly more prevalent among participants exposed to frequent bullying (OR = 1.6, 95% CI: 1.2–2.0) and those experiencing loneliness (OR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.3–3.7). Substance use, including smoking (OR = 2.0, 95% CI: 1.4–2.7), cannabis (OR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.4–3.6), amphetamines (OR = 3.5, 95% CI: 2.8–4.5), and alcohol (OR = 3.0, 95% CI: 2.3–3.8), was significantly associated with increased risk of suicidal ideation. Furthermore, patients with a history of anxiety (OR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.3–3.3), or depression ((OR = 1.5, 95% CI: 1.1–1.9)) had a significantly elevated risks of suicidal ideation. Similarly, sexual abuse also increased suicidal ideation (OR = 2.0, 95% CI: 1.6–2.4).

In terms of suicide attempts, significant associations were observed with cannabis use (OR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.1–2.7) and alcohol consumption (OR = 3.0, 95% CI: 1.9–4.6). Psychiatric conditions were also significantly linked to increased risk of suicide attempts, including anxiety (OR = 3.1, 95% CI: 2.4–4.2), and depression (OR = 1.5, 95% CI: 1.1–1.9). Similarly, sexual abuse also increased suicidal attempts (OR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.4–3.5).

In contrast to the well-documented risk factors, evidence regarding protective factors was scarce and heterogeneous. Only a few studies briefly mentioned potential buffers such as family connectedness, religiosity, and perceived social support, but these were not consistently measured or analyzed, highlighting a critical gap in the regional evidence base.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of funnel plots suggested asymmetry for both suicidal ideation (Supplementary Fig. S8) and suicide attempts (Supplementary Fig. S9), indicating potential publication bias. However, Egger’s regression tests did not detect statistically significant small-study effects (ideation: t = 0.20, p = 0.84; attempts: t = −0.90, p = 0.38). This discrepancy may reflect the limited power of Egger’s test and the influence of high heterogeneity and proportion-based data on funnel plot shape; thus, results should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized findings from 47 studies across 15 MENAT countries on the prevalence and risk factors of suicidal ideation and attempts among young people. We found a substantial burden, with a pooled prevalence of 25% for suicidal ideation and 12% for suicide attempts. Consistent with our findings, a study by Zygo et al. in Eastern Poland among 2,615 adolescents aged 13–19 years reported a suicidal ideation prevalence of 24.66% [16]. These estimates are broadly comparable to those reported in youth populations internationally, highlighting suicide-related behaviors as a pressing and under-addressed public health concern in the region.

The prevalence of suicidal ideation was approximately similar among adolescents (25%) and young adults (27%), consistent with a study conducted in Germany, which found no significant difference in suicidal ideation between individuals aged 14–17 years and those aged 18–21 years [16], whereas suicide attempts were higher in young adults (17% vs 12%) [69]. This pattern may reflect differences in exposure and opportunity (e.g., access to means), help-seeking patterns, or measurement/reporting effects rather than developmental causation per se. Furthermore, in young adults, suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts usually begin after 20s, which may reflect both retrospective bias and new episodes occurring in adulthood [69, 70]. However, some studies suggest that suicidal behavior during adolescence and young adulthood may vary by geography or culture. Adolescents in certain regions may face less stigma or have greater access to suicide prevention resources compared with others [71].

Our analysis also identified key sociodemographic and behavioral risk factors for suicidal ideation and attempts. Female gender was associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation, while no clear gender difference was observed for suicide attempts. Girls may be more likely to attempt suicide in response to feelings of helplessness, loneliness, rejection, guilt, or conflicts with parents and peers [16]. However, given known gender differences in help-seeking and disclosure, as well as potential stigma around reporting among boys and men in MENAT contexts, these findings should be interpreted cautiously [71].

In this study, we demonstrated that suicidal ideation was significantly more prevalent among young people exposed to frequent bullying and experiencing loneliness. Furthermore, a history of anxiety, depression, or sexual abuse significantly elevated the risk of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts. Notably, a history of sexual abuse was consistently associated with both outcomes, highlighting the long-term impact of trauma and underscoring the importance of trauma-informed services and safeguarding pathways [72]. Our findings are consistent with previous reports on risk factors for suicidal ideation in similar regions. For example, a systematic review conducted in the Eastern Mediterranean region identified adverse life events, bullying, depression, anxiety, and other mental health conditions as major risk factors for suicidal ideation among university students [73]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in Muslim-majority countries revealed that the pooled lifetime prevalence of suicidal ideation among students overall was 21.9%, with high school students demonstrating the highest prevalence at 13.4% [74]. Frequent bullying and loneliness were strongly associated with ideation, highlighting peer environments and social connectedness as priority targets for school-based prevention and digital/peer support interventions [75]. Recent studies have also shown that sleep disturbances are an emerging factor that has gained increasing attention and are significantly associated with suicidal ideation and behaviors among adolescent psychiatric inpatients [9, 76].

Our findings revealed that substance use, including smoking, cannabis, amphetamines, and alcohol, was significantly associated with an increased risk of suicidal ideation. However, suicide attempts were only significantly associated with cannabis use and alcohol consumption. This may be described by the social context of drug use in young adults, as heavy use, such as binge drinking, often takes place within peer groups or at social gatherings [77]. These findings support prior research suggesting that substance use may serve as both a coping mechanism and a disinhibitor that facilitates suicidal behavior [77]. Youth suicide remains a persistent issue without clear improvement or worsening across the time window (2010–2025), highlighting that global/national prevention efforts have not yet translated into measurable population-level change. The near-significant GDP effect indicates that economic context may play a role in shaping youth suicide risk (e.g., through access to education, health care, social support, or cultural attitudes), but the relationship is complex and not straightforward.

Evidence on protective factors was limited/inconsistent across studies; variables such as family connectedness, school engagement, religiosity/spirituality, and perceived social support were under-reported or measured heterogeneously. This represents a critical evidence gap in the MENAT region [78] and highlights the need for future research to explore culturally grounded resilience factors that could inform effective prevention and intervention strategies.

This review delivers the most comprehensive, region-focused picture of youth suicidal ideation and attempts in the MENAT region to date, aggregating evidence across 15 countries and > 115,000 participants. Beyond quantifying burden, it identifies actionable, modifiable correlates (bullying, loneliness, substance use) and consistently vulnerable groups (e.g., psychiatric inpatients, female adolescents), providing clear levers for prevention. The cross-country mapping and age-stratified estimates offer decision-relevant signals for policymakers, informing schoolwide anti-bullying and connectedness initiatives, integrated substance-use/mental-health services, trauma-informed pathways, and post-discharge safety planning. The synthesis also highlights critical evidence gaps, particularly around protective factors and the under-representation of out-of-school youth and forcibly displaced populations, thereby setting a concrete research and surveillance agenda for the region.

At the same time, between-study heterogeneity was extreme (I2 > 99%), likely reflecting variation in sampling frames, measurement approaches, recall periods, and country contexts; estimates should therefore be interpreted as region-level signals rather than precise country benchmarks. Most studies were cross-sectional, limiting causal inference; English-language and post-2010 restrictions may have excluded relevant evidence; school-based samples predominated; and covariate adjustment was inconsistent, leaving residual confounding possible. Although visual inspection of funnel plots suggested potential small-study effects, Egger’s regression tests were non-significant. This discrepancy likely reflects the limited power of Egger’s test and the challenges of applying traditional publication bias diagnostics to prevalence data with high heterogeneity. Funnel plot asymmetry in this context may reflect genuine variability in study precision or methodological differences rather than selective publication. Therefore, while evidence of bias is not conclusive, pooled estimates should still be interpreted with caution.

The marked heterogeneity in prevalence across MENAT countries may partly reflect regional realities such as cultural stigma surrounding mental illness and suicide, which often lead to underreporting and concealment of suicidal behaviors. For example, in Iran, stigma toward mental illness remains a significant barrier to accessing psychiatric care, contributing to the underutilization of mental health services and ultimately obstructing timely psychiatric intervention, which can lead to serious consequences for affected individuals [79]. In many countries within the region, suicide is regarded as both a religious and legal taboo, discouraging individuals and families from disclosing suicidal thoughts or attempts. While religiosity may act as a protective factor against suicide, partly due to the prohibition of suicide in Islam and other faiths, it may also reinforce silence and concealment in some contexts [80].

Furthermore, political instability across parts of the region may exacerbate psychosocial distress while simultaneously restricting access to mental health services and hindering reliable data collection. Migration-related stressors, socioeconomic inequalities, and weak surveillance systems further contribute to the variability observed across studies. These contextual factors highlight the importance of culturally sensitive, contextually grounded prevention and intervention strategies tailored to the unique social realities of the MENAT region [81].

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to examine suicidal ideation and attempts among young people in the MENAT region. Our findings indicate a substantial burden, with a pooled prevalence of 25% for suicidal ideation and 12% for suicide attempts. Key risk factors included female gender, bullying, loneliness, substance use, anxiety, depression, and a history of sexual abuse. Religion and cultural values may contribute to stigma and underreporting, masking the true extent of suicidality in this population [82]. These findings highlight the urgent need for culturally sensitive prevention strategies, strengthened mental health services, and contextually relevant interventions to address the psychosocial and behavioral factors driving youth suicidality in the region.
