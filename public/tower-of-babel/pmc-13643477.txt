Septal Artery Injury During Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing
Authors: Jing Song, Xueqiang Guan, Jia Li, Sheng Ye, Xuliang Chen, Jiafeng Lin, Jin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) requires deep septal lead implantation and carries a risk of septal vascular injury.Case SummaryA 58-year-old man with binodal disease underwent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation using LBBAP. Initial high septal lead placement was associated with markedly elevated impedance and suboptimal pacing parameters. After repositioning, parameters normalized. However, the patient developed acute chest pain, ST-segment elevation, and ventricular tachycardia. Coronary angiography demonstrated septal branch perforation with contrast extravasation, and echocardiography confirmed an interventricular septal hematoma. Transcatheter coil embolization achieved complete hemostasis with favorable recovery.DiscussionAbnormal pacing parameters and ventricular arrhythmias may represent early electrophysiological markers of septal vascular injury during LBBAP, providing important insights into its recognition, management, and procedural implications.Take-Home MessagesSeptal branch injury is a rare but potentially serious complication of LBBAP. Early recognition and prompt imaging-guided intervention are essential to ensure procedural safety.

Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) is increasingly used as a physiological pacing strategy that preserves ventricular synchrony.1 Despite its favorable electrical performance, deep septal lead implantation introduces potential risks related to adjacent septal structures, including coronary perforator arteries.2 Clinically significant septal vascular injury remains rare and incompletely characterized. We report a case of septal artery injury during LBBAP and highlight its early electrophysiological warning signals and management.Take-Home Messages•Septal branch injury is a rare but potentially serious complication of left bundle branch area pacing.•Early recognition and prompt imaging-guided intervention are essential to ensure procedural safety.

History of Presentation

A 58-year-old man presented with a 2-month history of chest discomfort and fatigue. Electrocardiogram (ECG) and ambulatory monitoring revealed sinus arrest, junctional escape rhythm, and intermittent high-grade atrioventricular block, consistent with binodal disease (Figure 1). Baseline coronary angiography revealed moderate multivessel coronary artery disease without hemodynamically significant stenosis.Figure 1Baseline Electrocardiogram Demonstrating Sinus Arrest With Junctional Escape Rhythm

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus and coronary artery disease.

Differential Diagnosis

In addition to chest discomfort and fatigue due to binodal disease, other potential etiologies for presentation include acute coronary syndrome, heart failure, and noncardiac reasons for the symptoms.

Investigations

Venous access was obtained via fluoroscopically guided puncture of the left axillary vein. An LBBAP lead (Medtronic SelectSecure 3830) was delivered using a C315HIS sheath (Medtronic). Initial mid to high septal implantation was associated with markedly elevated impedance (approximately 2,300 Ω) and a high capture threshold (4.5 V), suggesting suboptimal lead positioning. Management

Emergency coronary angiography demonstrated contrast extravasation from the third septal branch of the left anterior descending artery at the site of the initial lead deployment, consistent with septal branch perforation (Video 1, Figure 5). A microcatheter was advanced into the injured vessel, and coil embolization was successfully performed (Video 2, Figure 6). Discussion

Rather than reiterating the established role of LBBAP, the present case highlights a less well-defined aspect of this technique—procedure-related intraseptal vascular injury. Although LBBAP has demonstrated favorable safety and efficacy in large cohorts, including the MELOS study, coronary septal branch injury remains insufficiently characterized and likely under-recognized in clinical practice.3 This gap underscores the importance of identifying early and clinically actionable warning signals.

This case illustrates septal coronary artery injury leading to interventricular septal hematoma and extends prior reports by emphasizing abnormal pacing parameters and ventricular arrhythmias as early indicators of vascular injury.4 Previous cases have primarily focused on late manifestations such as pericardial effusion, hemodynamic compromise, or tamponade.5 In contrast, our findings suggest that electrophysiological changes may precede overt structural complications, offering a critical window for early detection.

From an anatomical perspective, septal perforator arteries—typically originating from the left anterior descending artery—run in close proximity to the intended pacing target. Variability in their distribution, caliber, and trajectory results in an unpredictable “safe zone” during lead implantation. This is particularly relevant when the lead is positioned too anteriorly or at a higher septal level, increasing the likelihood of vascular injury.6

From a procedural standpoint, markedly abnormal pacing parameters should not be interpreted solely as technical failure. In this case, the elevated impedance and high capture threshold were observed during the initial high septal implantation and prompted immediate lead repositioning. These findings may reflect engagement of nonmyocardial structures, including vascular channels, or early tissue disruption. Importantly, these abnormalities preceded clinical deterioration and angiographic confirmation of septal branch perforation, despite normalization of pacing parameters after repositioning. Although this observation is hypothesis generating, it provides a practical electrophysiological clue that may facilitate earlier recognition of vascular injury during LBBAP.

Clinically, the combination of chest pain, ST-segment elevation, and ventricular arrhythmias during implantation should prompt immediate evaluation for coronary injury. Ventricular tachycardia in this context may result from local ischemia, intramyocardial hematoma formation, or mechanical irritation of the conduction system. Coronary angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosis, while echocardiography plays a complementary role in identifying interventricular septal hematoma and excluding pericardial complications.

Interventricular septal hematoma is an uncommon but potentially serious complication that has been reported in various clinical settings, including coronary interventions, septal ablation, and cardiac trauma. Its pathogenesis is most commonly attributed to injury of septal perforator arteries, resulting in intramyocardial bleeding.7 Although rare, septal hematoma may lead to outflow tract obstruction, conduction disturbances, or cardiac tamponade, necessitating close monitoring and timely intervention.8

Management strategies for septal vascular injury during LBBAP are not standardized. Conservative management may be appropriate in hemodynamically stable patients without active bleeding; however, ongoing contrast extravasation warrants prompt intervention. In the present case, coil embolization achieved rapid and definitive hemostasis while avoiding surgical repair, consistent with previous reports supporting its safety and efficacy.9 Clinical decision-making should be individualized based on clinical presentation, hemodynamic status, and imaging findings.

Finally, this case underscores the importance of integrating electrophysiological parameters with anatomical and imaging guidance to improve procedural safety. Electrocardiographic morphology, fluoroscopic positioning, and, when necessary, contrast injection should be used to optimize lead placement and avoid high-risk trajectories. Taken together, these findings support a pragmatic safety framework for LBBAP: Recognize abnormal pacing parameters as potential warning signals, maintain vigilance for intraprocedural symptoms, and initiate early multimodality evaluation to enable timely intervention.

Conclusions

Septal vascular injury during LBBAP may be heralded by abnormal pacing parameters and ventricular arrhythmias.
