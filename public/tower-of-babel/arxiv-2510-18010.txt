On the expansion of Hanoi graphs
David Eppstein, Daniel Frishberg, William Maxwell

Abstract




Abstract

The famous
Tower of Hanoi
puzzle involves moving
n
discs of distinct sizes from one of
p\geq 3
pegs (traditionally
p=3
) to another of the pegs, subject to the constraints that only one disc may be moved at a time, and no disc can ever be placed on a disc smaller than itself. Much is known about the
Hanoi graph

H_{p}^{n}
, whose
p^{n}
vertices represent the configurations of the puzzle, and whose edges represent the pairs of configurations separated by a single legal move. In a previous paper, the present authors presented nearly tight asymptotic bounds of
O((p-2)^{n})
and
\Omega(n^{(1-p)/2}(p-2)^{n})
on the
treewidth
of this graph for fixed
p\geq 3
. In this paper we show that the upper bound is tight, by giving a matching lower bound of
\Omega((p-2)^{n})
for the
expansion
of
H_{p}^{n}
.

1
Introduction

1.1
Background and motivation

In the well-studied
Tower of Hanoi
puzzle, one is given
n
discs
\{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\}
of increasing size, sitting on the first of
p
pegs. The object is to move all of the discs to some specified peg, while moving one disc at a time, and without ever putting one disc
d_{i}
on a smaller disc
d_{j}
,
j<i
. The
Hanoi graph

H_{p}^{n}
is the graph whose vertices correspond to the
p^{n}
possible configurations of the puzzle, and whose edges correspond to the pairs of configurations that differ by a single valid move.

Treewidth
(see
Section

2.2
) is a graph connectivity property with broad applications in algorithm design and analysis (see e.g.
[
4
]
). Intuitively, treewidth measures how difficult it is to recursively decompose a graph via small vertex separators. The treewidth of large graphs having a natural combinatorial definition—such as Hanoi graphs—is of independent theoretical interest. Some results are known e.g. for
Kneser
graphs
[
9
]
and their generalizations
[
15
]
.

Expansion
is a graph connectivity property that in some sense is stronger than treewidth; it measures the robustness of a graph with respect to “bottlenecks”, or sparse cuts. Expansion has a close connection to
multicommodity flow
problems via a kind of flow-cut duality. Prior work on the expansion of large graphs
[
3
,
5
,
13
]
connects closely to the study of the
mixing
properties of Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms for counting and sampling problems
[
18
,
14
,
1
]
via the relationship between (combinatorial) expansion and
spectral
expansion. Algorithmic questions involving expansion are also of interest (see e.g.
[
2
]
).

Certain graph properties of the special case
p=3
of
H_{p}^{n}
have been somewhat easier to analyze than the case
p\geq 4
. The Hanoi graph
H_{3}^{n}
on three pegs is known to have treewidth exactly four
[
7
]
, and the diameter of
H_{3}^{n}
is
2^{n}-1

[
11
]
. On the other hand, for
p\geq 4
, only (nearly tight) asymptotic bounds are known on the treewidth
[
7
]
, and the diameter of
H_{p}^{n}
is unknown for
p\geq 4

[
11
]
although values have been computed for small
n
when
p=4
, and compared with theoretical bounds
[
10
]
.

1.2
Markov chain decomposition

Our technique builds on existing decomposition techniques developed in the analysis of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms for sampling problems. These algorithms are simple random walks that converge to a target distribution of interest. The
mixing time
of such an algorithm—the time it takes to converge to the target distribution—is of interest, and often difficult to bound. A common means of obtaining such a bound is to use a standard theorem that relates the
Cheeger constant
—essentially the expansion of the state space of the chain, viewed as a graph, up to normalization factors—to the mixing time.

In a prior work
[
5
]
, motivated by the question of the mixing time of a well-studied
[
16
,
17
]
chain for sampling
polygon triangulations
via a
flip walk
, the first and second author developed a framework for bounding the expansion of a graph by recursively decomposing the graph into subgraphs, and analyzing the mutual connectivity of the subgraphs at each level. Prior decomposition techniques existed in the MCMC setting, but these were too lossy to be of use for the triangulation flip walk. The triangulation flip walk has, as its state space, a well-studied graph known as the
associahedron
. Thus the problem of bounding the triangulation flip walk mixing time is equivalent to the problem of (lower-)bounding the expansion of the associahedron.

In a companion paper
[
6
]
, the first and second authors further developed the framework and applied it to a number of other Markov chains.

1.3
Our contribution

In this paper, we apply the previous Markov chain decomposition framework to a large graph family that does not admit a natural Markov chain interpretation, showing that this framework is of independent interest beyond MCMC analysis. Furthermore, the family of graphs we are studying—Hanoi graphs—do not admit a direct application of that framework, as written. We further develop the framework and show it can nonetheless be applied to Hanoi graphs.

The best known treewidth upper bound for
H_{p}^{n}
when
p\geq 4
is
O((p-2)^{n})

[
7
]
. Combining this known upper bound with a new lower bound, we prove the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

The expansion of the Hanoi graph
H_{p}^{n}
, for fixed
p\geq 3
, is
\Theta(((p-2)/p)^{n})
.

In Section
2
we will define expansion and treewidth and observe a relationship between the two (
Remark

2.7
); from this relationship it will be immediate that if the expansion of a graph in a given family of bounded-degree graphs (such as Hanoi graphs)
G=(V,E)
is
\Omega(f(|V|))
, then the treewidth of the same graph is
|V|\cdot\Omega(f(|V|))
. From this relationship, Theorem
1.1
, and the upper bound on treewidth in
[
7
]
the following corollary is immediate (here
|V|=p^{n}
):

Corollary 1.2
.

The treewidth of the Hanoi graph
H_{p}^{n}
, for fixed
p\geq 3
, is
\Theta((p-2)^{n})
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Hanoi Graphs

By
D=\{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\}
we denote the set of
n
discs in the Tower of Hanoi puzzle such that whenever
i>j
the disc
d_{i}
is larger than the disc
d_{j}
. By
[p]=\{1,\dots,p\}
we denote the
p
pegs. A
configuration
in the Tower of Hanoi puzzle is given by a function mapping discs to pegs, i.e.
\tau\colon D\rightarrow[p]
. Equivalently write
\tau
as a tuple,
\tau=(\tau(d_{1}),\tau(d_{2}),\dots,\tau(d_{n}))
. Since no disc
d_{i}
can be placed on a smaller disc
d_{j}
, each
\tau
indeed uniquely specifies a puzzle state. By
H_{p}^{n}
we denote the Hanoi graph associated with the Tower of Hanoi puzzle on
n
vertices and
p
pegs. The vertices of
H_{p}^{n}
are given by the configurations (the functions
\tau\colon D\rightarrow[p]
). There is an edge between two configurations
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
if and only if a player can transition from
\tau_{1}
to
\tau_{2}
by making exactly one legal move (that is, moving a disc from one peg to another, and placing it on top of a larger disc).

We define a decomposition of a Hanoi graph
H_{p}^{n}
into
p
induced Hanoi graphs isomorphic to
H_{p}^{n-1}
as in
[
7
]
. For a configuration
\tau
let
\tau_{n}
denote its
n
th component. That is,
\tau_{n}
denotes the peg on which the largest disc is placed in the configuration
\tau
. For
i=1,\dots,p
the sets
\{\tau\mid\tau_{n}=i\}
partition the vertices of
H_{p}^{n}
and the subgraphs induced by these sets are isomorphic to
H_{p}^{n-1}
. See
Fig.

1
for an illustration.

When referring to a Hanoi graph
H\cong H_{p}^{n}
, we will denote by
\{H_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq p\}
the induced Hanoi subgraphs of
H
that are induced by the vertex sets
\{\tau\mid\tau_{n}=i\}
. These subgraphs are mutually vertex-disjoint and are each isomorphic to
H_{p}^{n-1}
. We call an edge in
H^{n}_{p}
a
boundary edge
if its endpoints lie in two distinct subgraphs
H_{i},H_{j}
, and we call a vertex a
boundary vertex
if it is incident to a boundary edge. Let
\partial_{e}(H_{i},H_{j})=\{(u,v)\mid u\in H_{i},v\in H_{j},(u,v)\in E(H)\}
be the set of boundary edges between a pair of subgraphs, and let
\partial_{v}(H_{i},H_{j})=\{u\in H_{i}\mid\exists v\in H_{j},(u,v)\in E(H)\}
be the set of boundary vertices in
H_{i}
with a neighbor in
H_{j}
.

We now analyze the structure of these subgraphs and the boundaries between them. First we define what we call a
facet
:

Definition 2.1
.

If
H
is a Hanoi graph isomorphic to
H_{p}^{n}
, then given
1\leq i<j\leq p
, let
\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H)=\mathcal{F}_{ji}(H)
be the set of vertices in
H
that represent puzzle states having no discs on peg
i
or peg
j
. Call
\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H)
a
facet
.

Under this definition, the restriction of a facet
\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H)
to a Hanoi subgraph
H_{k}
of
H
is itself a facet, namely
\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H_{k})
. Furthermore, by the symmetry among pegs, for all
1\leq k,l\leq p
with
k,l\notin\{i,j\}
, the restrictions
\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H_{k})
and
\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H_{l})
have the same cardinality. That is:

Remark 2.2
.

Every nonempty facet
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H)
within a graph
H\cong H_{p}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, is a union
\bigcup_{k\notin\{i,j\}}\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H_{k})
(where
\forall k,H_{k}\cong H_{p}^{n-1}
) of facets in
H_{p}^{n-1}
copies. All of these facets have the same size,
|\mathcal{F}|/(p-2)
.

We now show that the boundary between each
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
induces a facet within each of
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
:

Lemma 2.3
.

Partition
H_{p}^{n}
into
p
Hanoi subgraphs as
V(H_{p}^{n})=\bigcup_{k=1}^{p}V(H_{k})
,
H_{k}\cong H_{p}^{n-1}\forall k
. Then
\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H_{i})=\partial_{v}(H_{i},H_{j})\forall i,j.

Proof.

The boundary set
\partial_{v}(H_{i},H_{j})
is precisely the set of vertices in
H_{i}
whose configurations in the
H_{p}^{n-1}
graph isomorphic to
H_{i}
have no disc on peg
i
or peg
j
. This is because those are the configurations in which the largest disc in
H_{p}^{n}
can be moved from peg
i
to peg
j
. This set is precisely
\mathcal{F}_{ij}(H_{i})
, by Definition
2.1
.
∎

We now have the following nice structure:

Lemma 2.4
.

Every pair among the
p
Hanoi subgraphs comprising
H_{p}^{n}
has as its set of boundary edges a matching of size
(p-2)^{n-1}
, i.e.

|\partial_{v}(H_{i},H_{j})|=|\partial_{v}(H_{j},H_{i})|=|\partial_{e}(H_{i},H_{j})|=(p-2)^{n-1}

(Our previous paper
[
7
]
gives essentially the same proof of this fact.)

2.2
Expansion and treewidth

Our aim in this paper is to give asymptotically tight bounds on two key parameters for Hanoi graphs:
expansion
and
treewidth
. A graph with large expansion has no sparse cuts; a graph with large treewidth may have sparse cuts, but in a sense we will describe shortly, cannot be recursively decomposed via sparse cuts. Therefore, a lower bound on expansion induces a lower bound on treewidth, and an upper bound on treewidth induces an upper bound on expansion.

We now make these notions precise: given a graph
G=(V,E)
, define the
expansion
, or
edge expansion

h(G)
as

h(G)=\min_{S\subseteq V:|S|\leq|V|/2}\{|\partial S|/|S|\}

where
\partial S=\{(u,v)\mid u\in S,v\in V\setminus S,(u,v)\in E\}
.

Define the
vertex expansion

h_{v}(G)
as

h_{v}(G)=\min_{S\subseteq V:|S|\leq|V|/2}\{|\partial_{v}S|/|S|\}

where
\partial_{v}S=\{v\in V\setminus S\mid\exists u\in S,(u,v)\in E\}
.

Unless specified as vertex expansion, we mean by “expansion” the edge expansion.

Treewidth, while closely related, is defined with respect to a
tree decomposition
: given
G=(V,E)
, let a
tree decomposition
of
G
, denoted
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E})
be a tree, whose nodes are a collection of
bags
(a term referring simply to subsets of
V
)
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{k}\}
, satisfying the following conditions:

1.

For all
v\in V
, some bag
X_{i}
contains
v
.

2.

For all
(u,v)\in E
, some bag
X_{i}
contains
u
and
v
.

3.

For all
v\in V,
the set of bags containing
v
induces a nonempty (and connected) subtree of
\mathcal{T}
.

Define the
width
of a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E})
to be one less than the cardinality of the largest bag in
\mathcal{X}
. Define the
treewidth
of a graph
G
to be the smallest possible width of a tree decomposition of
G
.

Define a
balanced vertex separator

X\subseteq V
in a graph
G=(V,E)
as a set of vertices such that
V\setminus X
can be partitioned into two subsets
A
and
B
with
|V|/3\leq|A|\leq|B|\leq 2|V|/3
, where no vertex in
A
has a neighbor in
B
.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
8
]

Given a graph
G
, let
t
be the treewidth of
G
. then
G
has a balanced vertex separator of size at most
t+1
.

Lemma

2.5
immediately implies the following, by the definition of a balanced separator and of vertex expansion:

Corollary 2.6
.

If a graph
G=(V,E)
has treewidth
t
, then the vertex expansion of
G
is at most
\frac{3(t+1)}{|V|}
.

We observe the following consequence of the definitions of treewidth, expansion, and vertex expansion and of
Corollary

2.6
:

Remark 2.7
.

In a graph
G
of degree
\Delta
and treewidth
t
we have
h_{v}(G)\leq h(G)\leq\Delta\cdot h_{v}(G)\leq\frac{3\Delta(t+1)}{|V|}
.

2.3
Expansion and multicommodity flows

Multicommodity flows
are a standard tool for lower-bounding the expansion of a graph
[
18
,
13
]
. Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. Replace each (undirected) edge
(u,v)\in E
by two directed edges
(u,v)
and
(v,u)
. Denote the resulting set of directed edges by
A
. A multicommodity flow
f
is a collection of functions
\{f_{st}:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid(s,t)\in V\times V\}
obeying the standard flow axioms:

1.

\sum_{v\in N(s)}f_{st}(s,v)=\sum_{v\in N(t)}f_{st}(v,t)=1

2.

for all
v\in V\setminus\{s,t\}
,
\sum_{u\in N(v)}f_{st}(u,v)=\sum_{w\in N(v)}f_{st}(v,w)

By
N(v)
we mean
\{u\in V:(u,v)\in E\}
.

Define the
congestion
of a multicommodity flow
f
as
\frac{1}{|V|}\max_{(u,v)\in A}\sum_{(s,t)\in V\times V}f_{st}(u,v)
.

The following standard inequality relates multicommodity flows to expansion:

Lemma 2.8
.

If a graph
G=(V,E)
admits a multicommodity flow
f
with congestion at most
\rho
, then
h(G)\geq 1/(2\rho)
.

3
Extending a prior framework

In two prior works
[
5
,
6
]
the first and second author presented a framework consisting of a set of conditions that guarantee
rapid mixing
for the natural random walk on a family of graphs on
N=f(n)
vertices, where the family is parameterized by
n<<N
. This condition is equivalent to the graph having expansion
\Omega\left(1/n^{O(1)}\right)
. They applied the framework to obtain rapid mixing results for random walks on the
triangulations
of a convex point set, as well as for a number of graph-theoretic sampling problems in bounded-treewidth graphs.

As we discussed in that paper, the framework is an analogue of the
projection-restriction
technique of Jerrum, Son, Tetali, and Vigoda
[
12
]
. The idea is to recursively partition the vertices of the graph into a small number of induced subgraphs in the same family, allowing a recursive decomposition. One can then construct a multicommodity flow recursively, by first specifying the amount of flow to send across each edge between a pair of the subgraphs, and then solving the resulting flow subproblems recursively.

The framework in
[
5
]
is as follows:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\dots\}
be an infinite family of connected graphs, parameterized by a value
n
. Suppose that for every graph
\mathcal{M}_{n}=(\mathcal{V}_{n},\mathcal{E}_{n})\in\mathcal{F}
, for
n\geq 2
, the vertex set
\mathcal{V}_{n}
can be partitioned into a set
\mathcal{S}_{n}
of classes inducing subgraphs of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
that satisfy the following conditions:

1.

Each subgraph is isomorphic to a smaller graph
\mathcal{M}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
,
i<n
.

2.

The number of classes is at most
n^{O(1)}
.

3.

For every pair of classes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the set of edges between the subgraphs induced by the two classes is a matching of size at least
\frac{|\mathcal{C}||\mathcal{C^{\prime}}|}{|\mathcal{V}_{n}|}.

4.

Given a pair of classes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the set of vertices in
\mathcal{C}
having a neighbor in
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
induces a subgraph of
\mathcal{C}
that is isomorphic to a smaller graph
\mathcal{M}_{j}\in\mathcal{F}
,
j<n
.

Suppose further that
|\mathcal{V}_{1}|=1
. Then the expansion of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is
\Omega(1/(\kappa(n)n))
, where
\kappa(n)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\mathcal{S}_{i}|
is the maximum number of classes in any
\mathcal{M}_{i},i\leq n
.

In fact, Eppstein and Frishberg
[
5
]
proved a stronger version of
Lemma

3.1
that holds if the subgraphs in the decomposition are isomorphic to
Cartesian products
of smaller graphs in the family. However, these Cartesian products are not relevant to the case of Hanoi graphs.

In our case, the induced subgraphs are straightforward to find: in an
H_{p}^{n}
input graph, we define the subgraphs to be the
p
copies of
H_{p}^{n-1}
that correspond to playing the puzzle on the smallest
n-1
discs given each of the
p
possible placements of the largest disc.

Lemma

3.1
requires a strong lower bound (condition
3
) on the number of edges between a given pair of the subgraphs, namely that the number of edges must be at least equal to the product of the cardinalities of the two subgraphs, divided by the total number of vertices in the graph. This condition fails for Hanoi graphs: this ratio is inverse exponential in
n
. Nonetheless, we show how to repair the framework and apply it to Hanoi graphs. Informally, we use the fact that although the number of edges is much smaller than the stated bound, the
boundary set
between two subgraphs—the set of vertices in one subgraph
H_{p}^{n-1}
having neighbors in a given other subgraph—is well distributed among the
H_{p}^{n-2}
subgraphs that comprise
H_{p}^{n-1}
. We require also that each subgraph admits a decomposition into “facets” (
Section

2
) allowing the construction of many “parallel” paths from such a boundary set to the other vertices within the subgraph. This avoids too much concentration of flow within a given vertex.

On the other hand, the decomposition of the Hanoi graph that we give here is simpler than those in
[
5
]
: that framework allowed each subgraph to be a
Cartesian product
of smaller graphs; here we have simply a smaller similar graph—and that graph is in fact completely determined by its isomorphism to
H_{p}^{n-1}
.

Thus the main contribution of this paper, in addition to establishing tight expansion and treewidth bounds for
H_{p}^{n}
, is to give an extension of the framework where some of the conditions fail.

4
Large expansion for Hanoi graphs

In this section we adapt the framework of
[
5
]
to Hanoi graphs. We will prove the following:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p\geq 3
be fixed. Suppose there exists a uniform multicommodity flow
f
in
H_{p}^{n-1}
with congestion
\rho
. Then there exists a multicommodity flow in
H_{p}^{n}
with congestion
\rho+O((p/(p-2))^{n})
.

Then Theorem
1.1
will immediately follow via induction on
n
(with a trivial base case).

The construction in this section largely follows the construction used in
[
5
]
to prove
Lemma

3.1
. However, applying their construction breaks down in our case due to the small sets of edges between Hanoi subgraphs. We address this with the new machinery in
Section

5
.

The inductive structure comes from dividing
H_{p}^{n}
into
p
subgraphs as described in Section
3
, each of which is isomorphic to
H_{p}^{n-1}
. We then reuse the fractional paths given by the flow
f
for pairs of vertices lying in the same
H_{p}^{n-1}
copy. The rest of this section is devoted to routing flow between those pairs lying in different
H_{p}^{n-1}
copies.

Let
H_{1},\dots,H_{p}
be the
p
copies of
H_{p}^{n-1}
. Suppose a vertex
s\in H_{1}
wants to send an equal amount of flow to all vertices
t\in H_{2}
. We first find some set of paths along which
s
sends its flow to the
boundary vertices
in
H_{1}
—those having neighbors in
H_{2}
—so that the flow is equally distributed among those boundary vertices. We let those boundary vertices simply send the flow across their edges to
H_{2}
; then the boundary vertices in
H_{2}
need to find a way to distribute the flow to the rest of the vertices in
H_{2}
.

We call this last step
distribution
; we call sending the flow across the boundary the
transmission
step; we will divide the first part—having
s
send its flow to the boundary—into two steps, one of which we call
shuffling
and one of which we call
concentration
. The shuffling step, which we will describe shortly, takes advantage of the inductive structure we described above.

Figure
2
shows the transmission and distribution steps in
H_{3}^{3}
. (The distribution step is almost trivial in this case, since the boundary matching has size one.)

Figure 2:

H_{3}^{3}
as described in Section
4
.
The top vertex in
H_{1}
routes
|V(H_{1})||V(H_{2})|
flow to its neighbor in
H_{2}
. That neighbor, the bottom-left vertex in
H_{2}
, then routes
|V(H_{1})|\cdot 3
flow to its topmost neighbor (similarly right neighbor) that is bound for the topmost (similarly bottom-right) 3-clique in
H_{2}
, and distributes an additional
|V(H_{1})|\cdot 1
flow to each neighbor in its own 3-clique.

To formalize the division of this multicommodity flow into “steps,” we define the steps as a more general type of flow, as in
[
5
]
:

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, denote by
A
the set of directed arcs obtained by directing the edges of
E
in both directions as in the definition of a multicommodity flow in
Section

2.3
.
Define a
multi-way single-commodity flow
(MSF)
[
5
]
as a function
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
source
set
S\subseteq V
,
sink
set
T\subseteq V
, and a set of
surplus
and
demand
amounts
\sigma:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\delta:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, as a flow
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in
G
, such that:

1.

for all
s\in S\setminus T
we have
\sum_{v\in N(s)}(f(s,v)-f(v,s))=\sigma(s)

2.

for all
t\in T\setminus S
we have
\sum_{v\in N(t)}(f(v,t)-f(t,v))=\delta(t)

3.

for all
u\in S\cap T
we have
\sum_{v\in N(u)}(f(u,v)-f(v,u))=\sigma(u)-\delta(u)

4.

for all
u\in V\setminus(S\cup T)
we have

Given a vertex

•

\pi_{dist}=(\partial_{v}(H_{2},H_{1}),V(H_{2}),\sigma_{dist}=|V(H_{2})|/|\partial_{v}(H_{2},H_{1})|,\delta_{dist}=1)

The composition of these problems is
\pi_{s}=\pi_{dist}\circ\pi_{tran}\circ\pi_{conc}\circ\pi_{shuf}

Proof.

The claim follows from comparing source and sink sets, as well as the

,
\pi_{conc}
,
\pi_{tran}
, and
\pi_{dist}
.

The idea of the shuffling step

is, given a vertex

that needs to send flow to every

To send flow from

across the edges

between

and

Lemma

states that to solve

We now show how to solve

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose there exists a uniform multicommodity flow

in

\rho\cdot\frac{|V(H_{1})|}{|V\left(H_{p}^{n}\right)|}

Proof.

To solve all of the
\pi_{shuf}
problems (one for each

, every

must send a single unit (

. By the assumption that

by

to a flow defined over

; since the congestion is defined to be normalized by the number of vertices in the graph

(

Let

∎

Next we solve

:

Lemma 4.5
.

The MSF problem
\pi_{tran}
for each vertex

can be solved, such that the total congestion for all such flows is at most

Let

simply send an equal amount

5
The distribution step

In this section we introduce new machinery that allows us to repair the framework in
[
5
]
. As we stated in
Section

4
, that framework breaks down for Hanoi graphs. In particular, Condition 3 of
Lemma

3.1
fails, as the number of edges between a pair of Hanoi subgraphs is much smaller than needed for that condition.

Condition 3 requires that the number of edges between a pair of subgraphs be at least equal to the product of the cardinalities of the vertex sets of the subgraphs, divided by the total number of edges in the graph. This would give congestion at most one in the
f_{tran}
flow defined in
Section

4

However, since Condition 3 fails for Hanoi graphs, the
f_{tran}
flow has congestion (much) greater than one, causing an exponential blowup in the recursive construction that is needed to solve the
\pi_{conc}
and
\pi_{dist}
subproblems.

We address this problem by more carefully specifying the flow

that solves the
\pi_{dist}
subproblem. To do so, we use the facets we defined in
Section

2

Recall that we defined

|V(H_{2})|

.

Define the following MSF problems:
