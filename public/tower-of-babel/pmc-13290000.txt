Patient Engagement in Treatment and its Determinants among Rural Indian Adults with Hypertension and Diabetes
Authors: Udita Singh, Anand Krishnan, Mohan Lal Bairwa, Baridalyne Nongkynrih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Patient engagement enhances safety, improves quality, and leads to better treatment compliance and disease control, especially in chronic noncommunicable diseases. However, it continues to be poor in many developing countries. We assessed patient engagement in disease management and associated factors among people with diabetes and hypertension in a rural population of Faridabad, Haryana, India.Materials and Methods:This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among adults aged 30–65 years with known diabetes and/or hypertension, identified randomly from 20 villages under 10 primary health centers (PHC) in Faridabad, Haryana. Patient engagement was measured by the 20-item Patient Assessment for Chronic Illness Care (PACIC) tool with five subscales using a 5-point Likert scale administered at their homes. Socio-demographic factors and treatment seeking were also asked.Statistical Analysis Used:A multivariate linear regression was done using the PACIC score as the dependent variable and other socio-demographic factors and place of treatment as independent variables.Results:Among the 300 participants (56% women), the mean (SD) age was 51.8 (10.2) years; 64.3% were unemployed, and 31.7% were illiterate. The overall mean (SD) PACIC score was 2.1 (0.63) out of 5; domain-wise scores were patient activation (1.4), decision support (2.5), goal setting (2.2), problem solving (3.0), and follow-up (1.2). In the multivariate analysis, patients who were literate or belonged to a higher socioeconomic status had significantly higher PACIC scores, and those working as laborers or in caste-based occupations or cultivation had lower PACIC scores as compared to the unemployed.Conclusion:Patient engagement in patients with diabetes and hypertension in the rural population of Haryana was low and was associated with poor literacy, occupation (laborer), and socioeconomic status.

INTRODUCTION

Following the epidemiological transition, the burden of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) is growing worldwide. They contribute significantly to morbidity and mortality, hence remain a primary focus for public health efforts. While the prevalence of NCDs is on the rise, their control rates remain poor. Among the two most common NCDs are hypertension and diabetes mellitus.

The National NCD Monitoring Survey (NNMS) 2017–18 showed the prevalence of raised blood pressure and raised blood glucose among those aged 18–69 years to be 28.5% and 9.3%, respectively. In contrast, the control rate was only 16.3% and 12.3%, respectively.[1] Similar findings were seen in the ICMR-INDIAB study[2] and the India Hypertension Control Initiative.[3]

These poor control rates are not only because of a lack of access to medication, but also reflect a combination of patient-, provider-, and system-level challenges. Many studies have shown that factors such as low awareness, poor treatment adherence, health illiteracy, cost of care, and limited counseling or follow-up by providers are barriers to disease control.[456] A systematic review by Kamath et al.[7] identified health system limitations, workforce shortages, and out-of-pocket expenditures as major barriers in India. Tripathy et al.[8] found that patients often discontinued treatment because of financial constraints, lack of awareness, and limited behavior change. Other studies done in low- and middle-income countries also show that fragmented care, inadequate infrastructure, and lack of patient activation contribute to poor outcomes.[91011]

Effective management of chronic conditions like hypertension and diabetes requires more than diagnosis and prescription—it demands sustained patient involvement and follow-up. The Chronic Care Model (CCM), proposed by Wagner et al.,[12] emphasizes a people-centered, team-based approach for chronic disease management. Optimizing care delivery through this model has been shown to result in activated patients, responsive health teams, improved outcomes, and cost-effectiveness.[1213]

The World Health Organization defines patient engagement as building the capacity of patients, caregivers, and providers to work collaboratively for safer, higher-quality, and more person-centered care.[14] Patient engagement is said to be the cornerstone of CCM. Research has shown that educational interventions can enhance patient engagement and improve medication safety.[14]

Studies have also shown that patient engagement is influenced by multiple factors such as health literacy, beliefs, family support, provider practices, and social norms.[1516] Measuring patient engagement also presents challenges, due to conceptual, linguistic, and contextual limitations.[17181920] Tools used to measure patient engagement include the Patient Assessment of Chronic Illness Care (PACIC) or the Patient Activation Measure (PAM).[2122] PACIC, developed by Glasgow et al.,[21] assess engagement across five domains: Patient Activation, Decision Support, Goal Setting, Problem Solving, and Follow-up. This tool has been used in multiple Indian and international studies.[17202324]

We conducted a study that looked at the association between patient engagement and treatment adherence and disease control among rural adults living with hypertension or diabetes in Faridabad district, Haryana. The present paper reports the results of an assessment of patient engagement and its determinants.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This was a community-based, cross-sectional study conducted in Faridabad district, Haryana. Study participants were adults aged 30 to 65 years, with known diabetes and/or hypertension for at least one year, residing in the selected villages in Faridabad, Haryana, and who had consulted with an allopathic doctor in the last 6 months, with or without documentation for the same.

A sample size was calculated with the assumptions 80% power, 5% Alpha error, 25% Control in subjects with high PACIC score (above median), and 12.5% among those with lower PACIC scores (median and below), using the prevalence ratio, then the sample size is 298.

There are 14 rural PHCs in Faridabad district, out of which 10 were randomly selected for the study, with two villages per PHC. Along with the PHC village, one additional village was selected randomly from all the villages under that PHC. A list of known patients with diabetes and/or hypertension was prepared for each selected village with the help of health workers and/or Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHAs). From this list, 15 patients were randomly selected. These patients were visited at their houses with the help of ASHA/ANM.

Patient engagement was assessed using the PACIC tool. It consists of 20 items across five subscales (Patient Activation, Decision Support, Goal Setting, Problem Solving, and Follow-up). The “Patient Activation” domain evaluates whether healthcare providers encouraged and involved patients in managing their condition. “Delivery System Design/Decision Support” assesses if care was well-organized and included clear, evidence-based guidance. “Goal Setting/Tailoring” examines whether care providers helped patients set specific, personalized goals. “Problem-Solving/Contextual Counselling” evaluates support provided to address barriers in daily life and the practical management of illness. Finally, “Follow-up/Coordination” assesses whether there was consistent follow-up and effective coordination of care. Together, these domains reflect the extent to which care aligns with the CCM. Each item is scored on a Likert scale from 1 to 5. The total and average score per subscale is calculated. The mean score is reported.

The interview schedule for the study comprised of seven sections: socio-demographic and risk profile, knowledge about the disease (adapted from Diabetes Knowledge Questionnaire[26]), self-efficacy for disease management (adapted from Stanford diabetes self-efficacy scale (DSES)[27]), family support, health-seeking behavior, patient engagement (PACIC[21]), and treatment adherence (Hill-Bone compliance to high blood pressure therapy scale[28]). Uday Pareekh scale[29] was used to calculate socioeconomic status. The questionnaire was administered by the interviewer with a verbatim reading. Waist circumference was also measured for all participants as per standard protocols.[30] Waist circumference cut off for men was 90 cm and for women was 80 cm.[31]

Statistical analysis

Data collection and entry were done on Epicollect5. The data was extracted into MS Excel. Statistical analysis used Stata software version 16 (16.1, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). The mean (SD) of overall and construct-specific PACIC scores were calculated for each patient from all the 20 questions and summated for the average population-level measure. Bivariate analysis by comparison of mean scores between subgroups was done using a t-test. Determinants of PACIC score were identified by multiple linear regression using socio-demographic, awareness, self-efficacy, and health-seeking variables as explanatory variables and PACIC score as the dependent variable. Model diagnostics were performed to ensure the validity of the regression analyses. Model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²) (or pseudo-R², as appropriate for the model). The assumptions of regression were evaluated by examining the distribution of residuals for normality and assessing homoscedasticity through residual plots. Multicollinearity among independent variables, particularly education, socioeconomic status, and occupation, was assessed using variance inflation factors, with a threshold of <10 considered acceptable.

Ethical Clearance (Annexure) was obtained from the Institute Ethics Committee of All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi, Ref. no. IECPG-117/24.02.2022.

RESULTS

The study included 300 participants who were selected from the 20 shortlisted villages. Of the 300 participants enrolled in the study, 56% were women [Table 1]. The participants’ mean (SD) age was 51.8 (10.2) years, 64.3% were unemployed, 12.3% employed in the informal sector, and 31.7% were illiterate. About 72% of the participants belonged to the lower class as per the Uday Pareek scale [Table 1]. Among the enrolled patients in this study, 41% had only hypertension, 35% had only diabetes, and 24% had both hypertension and diabetes.

Of all the participants, 20.7% were daily tobacco users, and 2% consumed alcohol daily. The median (IQR) servings per day of fruits and vegetables was 2.3 (2.0–2.6), with 97.3% of the participants consuming inadequate (<5 servings) fruits and vegetables per day. About one-third of the participants had low physical activity, defined as <150 minutes of moderate physical activity per week [Table 1]. The majority of the participants sought treatment from the private sector, that is, 55.7%, while 29% took it from the PHC.

The overall mean (SD) PACIC score was 2.07 (0.63) out of 5. The highest mean score was in the problem-solving domain, that is, 3.04 (1.09), and the lowest mean score was for the follow-up domain [1.22 (0.47)]. Patient activation also scored relatively low at 1.38 (0.66) [Figure 1]. Overall, there was inadequate patient engagement across all domains of PACIC. The majority of participants reported minimal participation in treatment decisions, with 94% stating that they were almost never asked for their inputs when treatment plans were made, 82% were never given choices about treatment options, and nearly 69% were almost never asked about any issues related to their medication [Table 2]. For delivery system design, 78% were almost never given written suggestions on improving their health [Table 2]. About 38% of participants felt that the care was organized well most of the time. Goal setting and tailoring were lacking, with 79% were almost never asked about their personal goals or supported in setting goals for diet and exercise, and 97% were not encouraged to participate in support groups [Table 2]. For problem-solving, less than half of the participants felt that their doctor considered their values and traditions while planning for daily care. Follow up and coordination were poor, with more 90% were almost never contacted after visits, referred to dietitians or counselors, or encouraged to join community programs [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first community-based study in India to assess patient engagement using the PACIC among adults with hypertension and/or diabetes. Our study population primarily included adults residing in rural areas with low-literacy levels and limited formal employment. A large proportion of participants were either daily wage laborers or homemakers. The demographic characteristics in our study are similar to those of other rural areas of North India. These are important to understand the patterns of health behavior and system engagement.

The mean PACIC score in our study was found to be 2.07 (0.6). This score is lower than the studies conducted in other countries.[171920232532] The score, in our study, was particularly low in domains related to patient activation and follow-up. Most patients responded that they were almost never asked about their ideas for treatment plans or given choices about treatment options, reflecting a doctor-driven consultation.[33] Nearly 79% of participants reported that they were never asked about their goals or supported in setting specific targets. This may be because of the limited training of healthcare providers in behavioral counseling and self-management support.[34] Follow-up and coordination were also inadequate. Over 90% of patients were not contacted after their OPD visits or referred to allied health professionals. This may be due to the absence of structured chronic care models, referral, follow-up systems, and multidisciplinary teams in the public health system.[3536] This is especially true for the primary care setting. This indicates that the current delivery of care does not fully support continuity, shared decision making, or self management, which are important for improving patient experience and satisfaction.[37] These are some of the components that are essential for effective management of chronic conditions like hypertension and diabetes. An underlying systemic challenge, such as high patient load, time constraint, inadequate training in patient centered communication, and fragmented healthcare services, might be the reason for this, as is evident from other studies.[3839]

A hospital-based study by Koley et al.[23] in West Bengal reported a higher mean PACIC score of 3.46 (1.76). This could be attributed to their hospital setting, consistency in healthcare delivery, more structured care pathways, better follow-up, and availability of support services. In high-income countries like the United States and Canada, PACIC scores typically range from 2.7 to 3.2, indicating better implementation of chronic care principles in those settings.[2040]

We also found that socio-demographic characteristics influenced engagement. Participants with higher education and socioeconomic status were more engaged in their care, possibly due to better health literacy and more confidence in interacting with healthcare providers. On the contrary, those engaged in daily wage labor reported lower engagement. This may be because seeking care often meant giving up a day’s wages, navigating time constraints, or prioritizing urgent survival needs over health. Interestingly, even though more participants sought care from the private sector, their engagement scores were low. This could reflect the more transactional and hurried nature of private consultations, as has been observed in other global and Indian studies.[4041]

While PACIC is a useful tool and has been used internationally, it was originally developed in a high-income, English-speaking setting. It has not been formally validated in rural north Indian context. Some questions in the tool, such as those on referrals to dieticians, support groups, or counselors, were not relevant in our setting, where such services are often unavailable. This issue was also raised by Koley et al.,[23] who recommended adapting the PACIC to better suit Indian contexts. The prerequisite for using the PACIC tool is that the patient should have had a consultation with an allopathic doctor in the last 6 months. This excludes the patients who were lost to follow-up or non-engagers. There were also challenges in administering the tool to rural participants. For example, the term “treatment plan” was frequently interpreted as simply a written prescription, which reflects the limited exposure to structured chronic care planning in routine practice. These observations point to the need for culturally and linguistically appropriate tools, especially when working with low-literacy populations.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of the ongoing national efforts to strengthen the chronic care disease care in India. The NP-NCD program emphasizes early diagnosis, standardized treatment, and regular follow-up of individuals with chronic conditions. Furthermore the operationalizations of Ayushman Bharat Health and Wellness Centers also aims to strengthen primary care delivery through comprehensive services, including screening, treatment adherence support, and longitudinal follow-up for NCDs. These initiatives incorporate several components aligned with the CCM, particularly in terms of proactive care, continuity, and patient engagement.

Overall, our findings point to a broader issue that the Indian health system still tends to be provider-driven and focused on managing acute episodes, rather than supporting patients over the long term. For chronic conditions like hypertension and diabetes, this model is insufficient. Improving patient engagement will require multiple steps—training providers in communication and patient-centered care, building systems for regular follow-up, and developing tools that are accessible, relevant, and meaningful to patients in diverse Indian settings.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we found that patient engagement among adults with diabetes and/or hypertension was low. This was poorest for patient activation and follow-up. While literacy and higher socioeconomic status were positively associated to better engagement levels, those working as laborers, in caste-based occupation or cultivation showed lower engagement. This highlights the need to strengthen the approaches to chronic care management at primary care level, with an emphasis on improving communication, shared decision-making, and structured follow-up, especially among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups.

Key messages

Patient engagement can play an important role in chronic disease management; however, its potential remains underexplored. Evidence from high-income countries suggests that active patient participation can improve disease control and health outcomes. There is a pressing need for more context-specific research in India to better understand the impact of patient engagement on care delivery and outcomes.
