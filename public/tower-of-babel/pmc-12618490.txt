Evaluation of antibiotic resistance, Staphylococci carriage and biochemical parameters of intensive care unit patients
Authors: Ercan Aslan, Songul Cetik Yildiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarriers and diseases caused by Staphylococci, one of the important hospital pathogens, are an important problem in the emergence of antibiotic resistance and finding effective treatments. Our study aimed to investigate the relationship between changes in hematological and biochemical parameters and the development of antibiotic-resistant Staphylococcus infections in patients hospitalized in the intensive care unit (ICU). The study included the 1st and 5th day data of 100 patients who had been hospitalized in the adult ICU for at least 5 days. Demographic data, blood and biochemical parameters and culture samples of the patients were evaluated. The higher NLR, CRP, LAC, and WBC values and lower HGB values observed on the 5th day compared to the 1st day suggest that patients in the ICU were likely exposed to hospital-acquired Staphylococcus infections, which adversely affected their prognosis. Indeed, the 31 Staphylococcus strains identified were isolated from blood (18), sputum (6), wound (4), urine (0), stool (0) and, catheter (3) cultures. It was determined that the most isolated strains, S. aureus, were resistant to levofloxacin, erythomycin, tetracycline, nitroforantoin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, oxacillin MIC, clindamycin, fusidic acid and oxsf. Based on our results, the observed increase in hematological and biochemical values on day 5 relative to day 1 reinforces the probability of hospital-acquired infection. Since this situation will put the life of the patient and the individuals related to the patient at risk, monitoring these values, early diagnosis and initiation of treatment are vital.Keywords: Staphylococci, Antibiotic resistance, Intensive care unitSubject terms: Microbiology, Infectious-disease diagnostics

Introduction

Intensive care units (ICU) are multidisciplinary units that accommodate quite different patient profiles. In these units, life-threatening conditions seen during the course of both acute and chronic diseases are treated and the follow-up of patients here is very important. The population and distribution of patients treated here also change over time. Due to the increase in the elderly population, developments in malignancy-related treatments, advances in diagnostic techniques and surgery, increased expectations of society and many other factors, patients in ICUs are more complex and consist of elderly patients with comorbidities than before. The carriage, diseases and development of antibiotic resistance of Staphylococcus aureus and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), which are important hospital pathogens, have caused the problem of finding effective treatments. Staphylococcus aureus is one of the most common and dangerous bacterial infectious agents among both hospital and community-acquired infections.

Since the initiation of antibiotic therapy in the treatment of infectious diseases, resistance has developed in Staphylococci in a short time due to the selective pressure created by antibiotics1. Staphylococci are being isolated at an increasing rate due to the frequent use of antibiotics effective against gram (-) bacilli in hospitals and the intensive application of interventional methods2. Although methicillin is no longer produced today and more durable anti-staphylococcal penicillins such as oxacillin and cefoxitin are used in susceptibility tests, the abbreviation “MRSA” continues to be used in general for strains of S. aureus that are resistant to such antibiotics3. Patients infected with MRSA are hospitalized in ICUs for a long time for treatment. During this process, many antibiotics effective against MRSA are being tried. However, the continuous and high mortality rate due to invasive MRSA infection requires high-quality studies to determine the most appropriate method for these patients. In order to conduct such studies, the clinical impact of this pathogen should be reduced by establishing a clinical research network and expanding the field of research4. The carrier state, which occurs when MRSA colonizes the skin and nasal mucosa of hospital personnel, is important because these people are potential sources of infection and play a key role in hospital-acquired epidemics5. Resistance data highlight the need for stronger infection control practices, prudent antibiotic use, and development of new treatment options to prevent the spread of these pathogens and protect public health6.

Results

According to the demographic data of our study, the average age of the 100 patients we included in the study was 71, while 53% of the patients were male and 47% were female. The hospital diagnoses of the patients whose clinical data we evaluated showed wide variation when they were admitted to the hospital. When we separate the patients according to their hospitalization diagnoses, 19% were treated in the ICU due to pneumonia, 20.1% due to various cardiovascular diseases, 12% due to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and respiratory failure, 15.1% due to cerebrovascular diseases, 13.5% due to renal failure, 6% due to gastrointestinal diseases, and 14.3% due to other complaints (Fig. 1).

Again, while the average CRP value of the patients on the 1st day was 85, this value was found to be much higher at 140 on the 5th day (Fig. 2, Table 1).

Among the isolates, Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequently detected species, found in blood (8), sputum (6), and wound cultures (4). This species exhibited resistance to a wide range of antibiotics, including levofloxacin, erythromycin, tetracycline, nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, oxacillin (MIC), clindamycin, fusidic acid, and others.

Staphylococcus hominis was isolated from 4 blood and 2 catheter cultures. These strains showed resistance to levofloxacin, erythromycin, fusidic acid, oxacillin (MIC), tetracycline, and clindamycin.

Staphylococcus epidermidis was found only in 3 blood cultures and demonstrated resistance to multiple antibiotics, including levofloxacin, erythromycin, fusidic acid, oxacillin (MIC), tetracycline, and clindamycin.

Staphylococcus haemolyticus was isolated from 3 blood and 1 catheter culture and exhibited extensive resistance, including to vancomycin, daptomycin, teicoplanin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (Table 6).

Discussion

The average age of the 100 patients included in this study was 71, while 53% of the patients were male and 47% female, indicating that patients receiving treatment in adult ICUs are generally elder patients in need of care. Due to the increasing elderly population, developments in malignancy treatments, diagnostic techniques, advances in surgery, increased expectations of society and many other factors, patients in ICUs are more complex and older than before.

Due to its increased synthetic ability within hours following infection or inflammation, CRP is known to contribute to defense, particularly through the innate immune response. Measurement of the acute phase response to CRP provides important clinical information regarding the presence of tissue damage or inflammation and subsequent response to treatment. According to our biochemical findings, the CRP values ​​of the patients on the 5th day of their ICU stay (99% ≥ 5 mg/L) were found to be significantly higher than the 1st day (81%(≥ 5 mg/L). Again, the fact that the average CRP value of the patients on the 1st day was 85, while this value was much higher on the 5th day as 140, confirms the presence of a hospital-acquired infection risk. In a prospective cohort study of 313 patients in ICU, patients with high serum CRP levels at the time of admission to the ICU had more severe organ dysfunction, longer ICU stay, and higher mortality rates than patients with normal serum CRP levels. It has also been reported that CRP levels correlate with the presence and number of organ failures7.

NLR is a good indicator for the evaluation of ICU patients and for determining bacteremia. A study has suggested that if the NLR value is above 5, it is a more suitable marker than CRP8. When we look at the NLR values ​​in our study, while 45% of the patients were above this reference value on the first day of hospitalization, this value became 77% by the 5th day. According to our data, the increase in the NLR level on the 5th day compared to the first day shows the infection picture of patients. In a study conducted on patients with suspected bacteremia and sepsis, a correlation between CRP and NLR was investigated and a positive correlation between CRP and NLR was reported9.

LAC, whose elevation is basically an important indicator of hypoxia, is one of the important laboratory parameters that continue to be used for early diagnosis and clinical prognosis. It is well known that the elevation of LAC, especially in patients with sepsis, is important for the severity of the disease. The normal value is between 0.5–1.8 mmol/L. A study reported that patients with LAC levels > 2 mmol/L had a higher mortality rate10. According to our data, in terms of LAC level, 26% of the patients were > 2 mmol/L on the 1st day, while 38% were > 2 mmol/L on the 5th day. This is an important parameter in terms of LAC elevation indicating sepsis in patients hospitalized in the ICU. According to the studies, it has been reported that the increase in LAC level can be considered as an important biomarker in determining the severity of diseases11,12.

WBC is an important parameter used in many scoring systems as an indicator of inflammation. Sepsis and bacteremia are infections that are frequently encountered in patients hospitalized in ICUs and cause significant mortality. Since early treatment can be life-saving for these patients and bacterial growth in blood culture is a time-consuming procedure, evaluation of WBC is extremely important. When the WBC levels of the patients in the ICU in our study were evaluated, it was seen that 53% of the patients were above the reference value (≥ × 103/μL) on the 1st day, while this value was found to be above the reference value by 82% on the 5th day. While the average WBC ​​of the patients on the day of hospitalization were 12.3, this value was found to be 15.5 on the 5th day of hospitalization.

Decreased HGB values ​​measured in ICU patients can be considered as a poor prognostic indicator. According to our data, 86% of patients showed a decrease in HGB compared to day 1. While 83% of patients were 10 and above 10 g/dL on day 1 of hospitalization, only 39% of these patients were above this value from day 5 of hospitalization onwards. In a study conducted with patients followed in the surgical ICU, it was reported that patients with low HGB levels had higher mortality rates and longer ICU and hospitalization times13. In another study, the mean HGB level in deceased patients was reported as 11.5 g/dl, while in surviving patients this rate was reported as 12.6 g/dl14. An epidemiological study confirmed that anemia is common in critically ill patients in ICU, while lower HGB levels were associated with longer length of stay and increased mortality rates15.

As a result, the high biochemical values ​​such as NLR, CRP, LAC, WBC on the 5th day and the low HGB values ​​indicate that patients in ICUs are exposed to hospital care-associated infections and therefore their prognosis may be adversely affected. Since it is known that mortality increases as the duration of hospitalization increases, we can say that monitoring these values ​​will be beneficial in terms of early diagnosis and initiation of treatment. As a matter of fact, in a study conducted on patients in the ICU, it was reported that the high WBC and NLR​​on the day of admission and the day of discharge were associated with an increase in mortality, while the decrease in the HGB on the day of discharge was associated with an increase16.

In this study, we identified 31 Staphylococcus strains from various culture samples of patients hospitalized in an adult ICU. The distribution of isolates was as follows: 18 blood, 6 sputum, 4 wound, and 3 from catheter cultures. No growth was detected in urine and stool cultures. Among these isolates, S. aureus was the most frequently detected species, followed by S. hominis, S. epidermidis, and S. haemolyticus. In a study where the reliability of culture tests was studied, it was reported that one of the highest positivity rates from different samples was sputum samples with a rate of 72%. A positivity of 29% was detected in stool samples, while significant positivity was detected in blood and urine samples17. Since bloodstream infections are among the most effective and critical infectious diseases worldwide with high mortality and morbidity rates, the first standard test applied is blood culture. Since rapid and targeted treatment approaches can improve prognosis, it is vital to distinguish between the real agent and the contaminant and to quickly complete antibiotic susceptibility tests18. Catheter-related bloodstream infections can develop approximately 48 h after intravascular catheterization. These infections constitute approximately 30% of all bloodstream infections and are common in ICUs. Sputum culture is preferred because it is quick and easy to apply in most patients. Although the possibility of contamination, one of the leading routine problems in this culture, continues to be significant, the risk of contamination can be minimized when the necessary procedures are applied during the sampling process19. Since wound infections can cause a wide variety of both healthcare-associated and community-acquired infections, such as pneumonia and meningitis, endocarditis, and peritonitis in immunosuppressed patients20, it is important to obtain culture samples.

The observed increases in inflammatory and hematological parameters such as CRP, NLR, WBC, and LAC, along with the decrease in HGB, are consistent with the systemic inflammatory response commonly associated with Staphylococcus infections. S. aureus is known to trigger strong pro-inflammatory cytokine release, leading to elevated CRP and leukocytosis, while persistent infection and inflammation can contribute to anemia of chronic disease, reflected by lower HGB levels. The marked increase in these biomarkers in our ICU patients, together with the isolation of multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus strains from blood, wound, and catheter cultures, suggests that these biochemical changes may be directly linked to the presence and pathogenic activity of Staphylococcus species. These findings highlight the clinical importance of early detection and continuous monitoring of these markers as potential indicators of Staphylococcus-related infections and their impact on patient prognosis.

When the presence of antibiotic resistance was examined, S.aureus strains were found to be resistant to the antibiotics levofloxacin, erythomycin, tetracycline, nitroforantoin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, oxacillin MIC, clindamycin, fusidic acid and oxsf. One of the most important problems in infections caused by S.aureus is the resistance mechanisms it develops to antimicrobials. Due to inappropriate antibiotic treatment, resistance to new antibiotics can develop in a very short time21. In a study evaluating a total of 392 patients hospitalized in ICUs, it was reported that MRSA colonization was detected in 21and MRSA infection was detected in 1322. Methicillin resistance was determined as 68.9% for S.aureus strains isolated from patients hospitalized in ICU for 1 year23. Increasing resistance rates of S.aureus and its multidrug-resistant strains suggest that there is a narrow range of therapeutic options for the treatment of such infections in both clinical and community settings24.

S. hominis, though less frequently isolated, demonstrated significant resistance to multiple antibiotics, including levofloxacin, erythromycin, fusidic acid, oxacillin (MIC), tetracycline, and clindamycin. This is in line with recent studies highlighting the increasing resistance of S. hominis to antibiotics, particularly its methicillin resistance associated with the mecA gene25.

S. epidermidis, often considered a contaminant, was found to be a significant pathogen in our ICU setting, isolated from blood cultures of 3 patients. Resistance to multiple antibiotics, including levofloxacin, erythromycin, fusidic acid, oxacillin (MIC), tetracycline, and clindamycin, was observed. This finding supports recent studies that emphasize the role of S. epidermidis in bloodstream infections and septic shock in ICU patients, particularly in immunocompromised individuals26.

S. haemolyticus, isolated from blood and catheter cultures, exhibited extensive resistance, including to vancomycin, daptomycin, teicoplanin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. These findings are consistent with emerging reports of multidrug-resistant S. haemolyticus in neonatal ICU settings, underscoring the need for vigilant surveillance and infection control measures27. Each hospital, and even different ICUs within the same hospital, has its own pathogen flora and antibiotic resistance pattern. These differences can affect clinicians’ perspective on hospital infections and determine approaches to empirical antibiotic therapy planned to be implemented.

Multiple antibiotic resistance index, this could indicate that the isolates originate from a high-risk contamination source, probably due to the overuse and misuse of antibiotics28. Inappropriate antibiotic therapy applications may lead to the development of resistant pathogen types. Antibiotic resistance in microorganisms can be minimized by regulating the use of antibiotics in ICUs29,30.

Limitations

One important limitation of our study is the difficulty in consistently following patients throughout their entire ICU stay. The length of stay varied considerably among patients, ranging from a few days to up to one year or more. As a result, it was not feasible to systematically track clinical outcomes such as duration of intubation, discharge status, or mortality. The absence of these data inevitably restricts the extent to which our findings can be interpreted in terms of clinical outcomes. An other limitation, culture samples were not taken from all sources regularly, the values ​​of all patients could not be provided.

Conclusion

In this study, blood, biochemistry and culture results obtained from patients hospitalized in an adult ICU and their resistance to various antibiotics were evaluated. The fact that the patients’ blood and biochemistry values ​​on the 5th day were higher than those on the 1st day strengthens the possibility of developing a hospital-acquired infection. In addition, the observation of various infections and antibiotic resistance in the culture results supports these findings. Based on our results, it is thought that these patients were exposed to these infections due to hospitalization, and therefore their treatment duration may be prolonged. It is very important to prevent the spread of infections acquired by patients in ICUs and the development of resistance to antibiotics. In this case, it is important to be able to predict the patients in the risk group in advance. Therefore, the patient’s hospitalization history, antibiotherapy, questioning of additional diseases, differentiating hospital-acquired or community-acquired infections and treatment selection will be more important.

Methods

The study included the 1st and 5th day results of 100 patients who had been hospitalized in the adult ICU of Mardin/Kiziltepe State Hospital for at least 5 days between January 2023–2024. To conduct this study, Institutional Permit and Approval were obtained from the Mardin Provincial Health Directorate for the use of patient data. Also, our study was approved by the Health Sciences University, Gazi Yaşargil Training and Research Hospital, Clinical Research Ethics Committee with the number 378/2023. Our study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent forms (signed) were obtained from all patients. The results of 100 patients were randomly selected regardless of age and gender. Demographic data of the patients such as age and gender, blood and biochemistry parameters, and culture (blood, wound, sputum, catheter, urine, stool) samples were retrospectively obtained from patient files and hospital records and evaluated.

Inclusion Criteria: The study population consisted of patients admitted to the ICU with a confirmed diagnosis of, cardiovascular diseases, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pneumonia, respiratory failure, cerebrovascular diseases, renal failure, or gastrointestinal diseases.

Exclusion Criteria: Since the primary objective of our study was to evaluate whether patients developed ICU-acquired infections, those with pre-existing chronic infectious diseases or known chronic conditions associated with infection were excluded from the analysis.

Culture samples

Culture samples included blood, sputum, wound, urine, stool, and catheter specimens. Stool samples were also included to investigate the possible intestinal carriage of Staphylococcus species, as gastrointestinal colonization may contribute to the dissemination of antibiotic-resistant strains in ICU settings.

Blood culture samples were studied in the microbiology laboratory using the BACTEC FX fully automated blood culture system (Becton Dickinson, USA). Samples inoculated into RTA brand aerobic blood culture bottles were monitored in the device for 5 days. Specimens that signaled growth were subcultured: urine samples were plated directly onto 5% sheep blood agar and Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar, while other samples were subcultured on 5% sheep blood agar, EMB agar, and Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) media, and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Colonies grown on the media were identified to the genus and/or species level by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (Maldi Biotyper 3.1, Bruker, Germany). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the BD Phoenix 100 automated system (Becton Dickinson, USA) and interpreted according to EUCAST version 9.0 criteria. For certain antibiotics, including vancomycin, results from the automated system were confirmed using reference methods such as E-test or disk diffusion in accordance with EUCAST recommendations.

Biochemical evaluations

Neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate (LAC) and white blood cell (WBC) levels were measured by colorimetric method using an Otto Scientific brand Mindray-BS400 model fully automatic biochemistry device. Colorimetric assay in accordance with a standardized method.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 was used for data analysis. First, descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values, were calculated for continuous variables, while frequency and percentage distributions were calculated for categorical variables. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to assess the normal distribution of all continuous variables in the data set. The normality test results revealed that the majority of the variables did not meet the normal distribution assumption (p < 0.05). Therefore, it was decided to use nonparametric tests in the analyses.

To test the significance of the difference between the patients’ laboratory measurements on day 1 and day 5, the Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test was used for comparisons of two dependent groups.

Spearman Rank Correlation Coefficient (rho) analysis was performed to examine the direction and strength of the monotonic relationship between the variables. Delta (Δ) values, which represent the amount of change between days 1 and 5, were calculated for each laboratory parameter. Delta values ​​were obtained for each patient using the formula (Day 5 Value–Day 1 Value). This approach aims to better understand the dynamics of the process by examining the relationship between absolute changes (increases or decreases) in parameters.
