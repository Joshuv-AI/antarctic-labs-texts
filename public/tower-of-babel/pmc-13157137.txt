Reverse cupping–like appearance on CT: A beam hardening–related phenomenon with phantom correlation
Authors: Rohini Choudhary, Sameer Peer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interaction of polychromatic X-ray beam with highly attenuating material leads to beam hardening which may manifest as “cupping” artifact, where the central part of the image appears relatively hypodense as compared to the periphery, a phenomenon arising out of the monochromatic beam assumption of the reconstruction algorithms. The presence of a central hyperattenuating structure with a surrounding homogeneous medium and a sufficient peripheral-to-central distance may result in a “reverse cupping-like” variant of the beam-hardening artifact, in which the central part of the image appears relatively hyperdense compared to the peripheral part. In this report, we describe a “reverse cupping-like” artifact observed in CT scans of the abdomens of 2 patients and reproduce the findings in a phantom experiment. We attempt to unearth the physical underpinnings of this phenomenon.Keywords: Beam hardening, Cupping, Reverse cupping, Artifact, Computed tomography

Introduction

Since the advent of Computed tomography (CT) in the 1960s, many technical advancements have led to better generations of CT, resulting in improved image quality [1]. Artifacts in CT refer to any mismatch in the actual attenuation value versus the registered attenuation value of the imaged anatomy [2]. CT image acquisition requires 3 steps: data acquisition, image reconstruction, and image post-processing. Adjustments can be made at every step to reduce artifacts [3]. Compared to conventional radiography, CT is inherently more susceptible to artifacts because the final image is generated from millions of detector measurements. The reconstruction process assumes internal consistency among these data points, so even minor inaccuracies can translate into noticeable errors in the reconstructed image [4]. Different types of artefacts can be encountered during image acquisition in CT imaging, which can be broadly classified into 4 categories: Physics-based artefacts arise from the inherent processes of CT acquisition, including beam hardening, photon starvation, scatter, and partial volume effects, all of which can reduce image quality and can mimic pathology. Patient-based artifacts result from patient movement and respiratory motion, leading to blurring and obscuring anatomical detail. Scanner-based artifacts result from technical imperfections, such as detector miscalibration and faulty detector elements that produce ring artifacts, which can often be minimized through regular system calibration and maintenance. Finally, reconstruction-related artifacts are made during the reconstruction process, particularly with helical and multidetector CT techniques, and may present as interpolation errors, cone-beam distortions, or misregistration between slices. Although modern iterative reconstruction algorithms have reduced their frequency, these artifacts still pose diagnostic challenges in clinical practice [4].

In this report, we describe a variant of the beam-hardening artifact, in which the central part of the image appears hyperdense. In contrast, the peripheral part appears hypodense, essentially a reversal of the “cupping artifact.” This artifact was noted around the centrally placed hyperattenuating structures (small bowel loops filled with orally administered iodinated contrast and the vertebral body), which were surrounded by a relatively homogeneous tissue interface from the skin surface to the center of the image, thereby increasing the skin-to-center distance. We also performed a phantom experiment as a proof-of-concept to explain the physics related to this variation in the beam hardening artifact due to a centrally placed hyperattenuating structure.

Materials and methods

CT scan acquisition protocol

The CT examinations were performed using a 256-slice Siemens SOMATOM Definition Flash scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). The standard protocol was followed, in which approximately 2-3 hours before the examination, each patient is given a mixture of oral iodinated contrast [300 mgL/mL] (15–20 mL) diluted in 2 L of water to drink for optimal bowel opacification. The scan was performed in triple-phase — unenhanced, late arterial, and portal venous. Informed and written consent was obtained from the patient’s next of kin before proceeding for the CT scan. Description of the “reverse cupping-like” artifact

We noted the “reverse cupping-like” appearance on contrast-enhanced CT scan of a 56-year-old woman, who had a known history of decompensated cirrhosis with portal hypertension and was admitted with worsening hepatic encephalopathy. The artifact appeared as a hyperdense area around the contrast-filled small bowel loops, which were surrounded by ascitic fluid (Fig. 1). A similar appearance was also noted in the CT of the abdomen of a 55-year-old patient with morbidly obese body habitus (Fig. 2). In this case, the vertebral column was pointed out at the approximate center of the image, with hyperdensity in its central portion and relative hypodensity in its periphery. In both cases, a gradual change in pixel attenuation was noted, with pixels in the center of the image showing higher attenuation than those at the periphery.

Phantom experiment

To investigate our hypothesis and serve as a proof of concept, an experimental setup was created using an ice-based phantom (Fig. 3). The phantom material was molded in 2 identical containers—1 steel (diameter 14 cm, height 12 cm) and one plastic of the same size. Initially, the phantom in the steel container was positioned within the CT gantry and scanned using the acquisition parameters listed in Table 2. Subsequently, the phantom molded in the plastic container was placed in the same position within the CT bore. A 20 mL syringe filled with contrast medium (300 mg, L/mL) diluted 1:50 (contrast-to-water) was positioned at the center of the phantom, and scanning was performed again using the same parameters as outlined in Table 2.

Result of phantom experiment

The scan acquired using a phantom molded in a steel container showed the classic “cupping artifact” (Fig. 4A), as the x-ray beam encounters more attenuating material in the periphery (steel), which hardens the polychromatic beam by attenuating the low-energy photons. So, this “hardened” beam with high mean energy interacts less with the central part of the phantom and reaches the detector, which causes a difference in the actual and expected attenuation, resulting in an image that appears hyperdense at the periphery and hypodense at the center.

(A) Axial image obtained with ice phantom surrounded by a steel container. Note the characteristic “cupping” due to beam hardening, with central part of the image appearing hypodense (white arrow) and peripheral part of the image appearing relatively hyperdense (yellow arrow). (B) Axial image of the ice-phantom kept in a plastic cover with syringe filled with iodinated contrast medium (1:50 dilution) in the center. The central part of the image surrounding the syringe appears relatively hyperdense (yellow arrow) as compared to the periphery of the image (white arrow).

When we acquired the scan using an ice-based phantom molded in the plastic container with a diluted (1:50) contrast-filled 20 mL syringe in the center, the image shows hyperdensity at the center, around the contrast-filled syringe, and relative hypodensity at the periphery (Fig. 4B). This creates the “reverse” cupping like appearance, as the X-rays are attenuated more in the center by the contrast-filled syringe than at the periphery.

The above equation holds for a monochromatic X-ray beam and a homogeneous interaction medium. For a polychromatic X-ray beam, the low-energy X-ray photons attenuate to a greater extent than higher-energy photons, increasing the mean energy of the X-ray beam as it traverses the matter, thus resulting in lesser attenuation. This results in “beam hardening,” which forms the basis of the beam-hardening artifact [5].

One of the manifestations of the beam hardening artifact is the so-called “cupping artifact”, where a highly attenuating material at the periphery of the area of interest results in the absorption of the low-energy X-ray photons, thus effectively increasing the mean energy of the X-ray beam passing through the central parts of the structure. Since reconstruction algorithms, such as filtered back projection, assume the X-ray beam to be monochromatic, this leads to an erroneous assignment of lower-than-actual attenuation values to the pixels in the center of the structure, resulting in the “cupping” artifact [6].

This can be illustrated. Consider a homogeneous spherical structure, S, with a composition similar to that of water (Fig. 5). A cross-section of the structure through the diameter is shown in the figure below, with 3 points along its diameter, A, B, and C.

Schematic representation of the cross-section of a homogenous water attenuation sphere with 3 points (A, B, and C) along the diameter of the sphere.

The linear attenuation coefficients at points A, B, and C may be denoted µA, µB, and µC, respectively. Now, if a hyperattenuating structure, such as a metallic ring with a diameter equal to that of the sphere, is placed at the periphery of the structure, then the linear attenuation coefficients at each of the points shall change, such that at point A

µA’ > µA, (where, µA’ is the new linear attenuation coefficient).

In accordance with the Beer-Lambert law, the output intensity at point A shall thus decrease, and the pixel corresponding to A shall be assigned a higher value.

Considering the X-ray beam to be polychromatic, the low-energy photons shall be absorbed at point A by the metallic structure. This will increase the mean energy of the X-ray beam transmitted to the center of the structure. Due to this beam hardening, the attenuation of X-rays at point B shall be less. Thus, at point B:

µB’ < µB (Where, µB’ is the new linear attenuation coefficient).

This results in a higher apparent intensity of the output X-ray beam at point B than expected under the monochromatic assumption, as given by the Beer-Lambert equation. Thus, the pixel at point B is assigned a lower attenuation value than the one at point A.

Now, again, consider point C, which is noted as diametrically opposite to point A, and thus within the perimeter of the metallic ring placed at the periphery of the sphere. Thus, at point C:

µc’> µc (Where, µC’ is the new linear attenuation coefficient).

Thus, the intensity of the transmitted X-ray at point C is lower than expected, and the pixel at point C is assigned a higher-than-expected attenuation value.

This results in the typical “cupping” artifact, in which the center of the structure appears darker than its periphery.

After discussion of the classical cupping artifact, let us now assume that a hyperatenuating cylindrical structure of uniform density, such as a cylinder filled with a dilute iodinated contrast medium (dilution of 1:50), is placed at the centre of the sphere at point B. Thus, now at point B, the linear attenuation coefficient shall increase such that:

µB” > µB’ > µB (where, µB” is the new linear attenuation coefficient)

Thus, the X-ray beam shall attenuate more at the center, and the pixel at point B shall be assigned a higher attenuation value than points A and C. Since there is no significant attenuation at points A and C, the linear attenuation coefficients at A (µA”) and C (µc”) shall decrease, since the points A and C receive a beam hardened by interaction at point B. Thus, the assigned HU values at points A and C shall be lower than expected. Thus, we have:

µA” < µA< µA’ (Where, µA” represents the new attenuation coefficient at point A)

µc” < µc< µc’ ((Where, µC” represents the new attenuation coefficient at point C)

This gives rise to an artifact in which the pixels towards the centre of the structure have a higher assigned attenuation value than those in the periphery, and the centre of the image appears brighter than the periphery, resulting in the “reverse cupping-like ” appearance on CT image. The observed appearance may overlap with previously described phenomena such as capping artifacts, which arise due to overcorrection of beam hardening during reconstruction [7]. Additionally, partial volume averaging and subtle streak artifacts may contribute to the observed attenuation gradients. Therefore, this pattern is best interpreted as a combined effect of beam hardening and attenuation geometry.

We performed a phantom experiment as a proof-of-concept for the “reverse cupping-like appearance.” We placed region of interest (ROI) of equal area at 3 points (corresponding to point A, B, and C, in the above discussion) on the images of each of the phantoms, that is, phantom with peripheral ring of hyperdensity, phantom with no radiodense material, and phantom with centrally placed radiodensity (Fig. 8).

It is pertinent to note that, in certain conditions, misalignment of the patient (or phantom) with respect to the bow-tie filter, may result in variations in the density of the HU values on CT image [8]. However, in our case, the position of the patients with respect to the bow-tie filter of the CT gantry was consistent, since the artifacts were not noted in any of the other scans acquired on the same scanner on the same day. Also, the phantom experiment further validates the explanation of the genesis of the “reverse cupping-like” artifact, since the postion of the phantom with respect to the isocenter of the CT scanner was consistent in all the acquisitions, and introduction of radiodense material in the periphery resulted in the typical cupping artifact, while the centrally placed radiodense structure resulted in the “reverse cupping-like” artifact. The quantitative analysis of the HU values across the phantom experiments also lends further support to the explanation of the “reverse cupping-like” artifact as a variant of the beam hardening artifact presented (vide supra) (Fig. 9).

It is of note that streak artifacts from oral contrast administration or barium residue in the bowel are well known [9]. Thus, for the reverse cupping artifact to manifest, the dilution of oral contrast in the bowel lumen may also be an essential factor, leading to beam hardening without streaking or significant photon starvation. This “reverse cupping-like” artifact is demonstrated in our phantom experiment, in which we used a homogeneous water-attenuation medium with an iodinated contrast-filled syringe (dilution of iodinated contrast 1:50, iodine concentration 300 mg/mL). It has been shown that a 1:50 dilution provides good contrast without streak artifacts; hence, this concentration was chosen for our phantom experiment [10]. In this context, we may infer that for the “reverse cupping-like” variant of the beam hardening artifact to occur, the following conditions may be necessary:

1.Presence of a homogenous soft tissue or water attenuation medium around a hyperattenuating medium.

Presence of a homogenous soft tissue or water attenuation medium around a hyperattenuating medium.

2.The central hyperattenuating medium must not cause streaking or significant photon starvation.

The central hyperattenuating medium must not cause streaking or significant photon starvation.

3.The medium may be of sufficient thickness for the attenuation of a polychromatic X-ray beam to occur.

As far as the clinical relevance of the “reverse cupping-like” artifact is considered, this artifact may erroneously be interpreted as hemorrhagic contents within the body fluids, such as pleural fluid, ascites, or pericardial effusion. Also, presence of this reverse cupping-like appearance around bowel loops after oral contrast administration may erroneously be interpreted as contrast leak out of the bowel loops. The recognition of this artifact as a variant of beam hardening, along with the plausible explanation described in this paper, may help understand it.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The phantom model was simplified and does not fully replicate in vivo conditions. Contributions from partial volume effects and subtle streak artifacts cannot be completely excluded. Additionally, quantitative validation across different scanners and reconstruction algorithms was not performed. Hence, further phantom studies as well as more clinical cases need to be studied to fully understand the reverse-cupping appearance as a variant of beam hardening artifact.

Conclusion

We have described a reverse cupping appearance as a variant of the beam-hardening artifact in which the presence of a central hyperattenuating structure within a homogeneous medium of sufficient thickness results in hyperdensities in the central part of the image and relative hypodensities in the periphery. This may be considered the “reverse cupping-like” artifact and may be explained by the polychromatic nature of the X-ray beam and its interactions with the matter it interacts with. Recognition of this artifact may help avoid an erroneous interpretation of hemorrhage in body fluids.
