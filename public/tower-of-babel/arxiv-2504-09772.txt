Two Heads are Better Than One: Test-time Scaling of Multi-agent Collaborative Reasoning
Can Jin, Hongwu Peng, Qixin Zhang, Yang Zhou, Yujin Tang, Tong Che, Dimitris N. Metaxas

Abstract
Test-Time Scaling has emerged as a powerful method to extend the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models. However, single-agent TTS faces significant scalability bottlenecks, as excessively long reasoning traces lead to increased inference costs and stability issues caused by context management failures. To address these limitations, we propose leveraging Multi-Agent Systems as a structural upgrade to standard TTS. By decomposing monolithic reasoning chains into distinct, manageable contexts across multiple agents, MAS offers a more robust framework for scaling reasoning. We validate this approach by introducing M500, a dataset comprising 500 high-quality multi-agent, multi-turn collaborative reasoning traces generated via DeepSeek-R1. Through Supervised Fine-Tuning on M500, we enable open-source models to internalize collaborative reasoning patterns and show improved TTS performance in MAS. Furthermore, we propose an adaptive scaling strategy incorporating a “CEO” agent to dynamically guide the reasoning process and optimize collaboration depth. Extensive experiments within the AgentVerse framework demonstrate that our fine-tuned models, Qwen2.5-32B-MAS and Phi4-14B-MAS, significantly outperform their base counterparts. Codes are available at https://github.com/jincan333/MAS-TTS.

Introduction

The advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs)

;
Hurst et al., 2024
;

;
Yang et al., 2024
;
Abdin et al., 2024
;
Jin et al., 2024
)
has significantly extended the capabilities of artificial intelligence. Recently, Test-Time Scaling (TTS) has emerged as an effective approach to further enhance LLM performance, particularly for complex mathematical reasoning tasks
(
Guo et al., 2025
;
Snell et al., 2025
;
Liu et al., 2025
;
Muennighoff et al., 2025
;
Wu et al., 2025
;
Jin et al., 2026
)
. Techniques such as Monte Carlo Tree Search
(
Gao et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024b
)
, large-scale reinforcement learning
(
Guo et al., 2025
;
OpenAI, 2024
;
Zeng et al., 2024
)
, and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on detailed reasoning chains
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
;
Ye et al., 2025
)
have been widely applied to extend the inference process. However, extending the reasoning trace for a single agent introduces significant challenges. As the reasoning chain grows excessively long, the inference cost increases substantially
(
Dao et al., 2022
)
, and the model becomes prone to divergence and hallucination due to the difficulty of managing a massive context window effectively
(
Liu et al., 2024b
;
Dziri et al., 2023
;
Turpin et al., 2023
)
.

To address the limitations of single-agent TTS, we propose leveraging Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) as a structural extension to standard TTS. The core advantage of MAS in this context is the ability to decompose a potentially unstable, monolithic reasoning trace into distinct segments managed by different roles
(
Feng et al., 2025
;
Li et al., 2023
)
. By assigning specific contexts and sub-tasks to different agents, MAS can maintain coherence over long-form reasoning processes that would otherwise overwhelm a single agent
(
Chen et al., 2024
;
Qian et al., 2024
;
Schmidgall et al., 2025
;
Lu et al., 2024
)
. Consequently, MAS serves as a more robust framework for TTS, allowing for effective scaling of reasoning capabilities without the context management failures observed in single-agent settings.

Building on the concept of Simple-Scaling
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
)
, we hypothesize that the collaborative reasoning capabilities required for this enhanced TTS can be established via SFT on multi-agent Chain-of-Thought (CoT) interaction data. To investigate this, we first curate a high-quality multi-agent collaborative reasoning dataset by solving a diverse set of challenging problems using an open-source MAS. Each instance in the dataset comprises the interactive sequence of multi-agent, multi-turn data generated by the MAS to solve a specific problem. To ensure dataset quality and support long-form CoT, we filter low-quality examples and use DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al., 2025
)
to generate robust reasoning traces for every agent. We designate this dataset as M500, which contains 500 diverse and detailed multi-agent collaborative reasoning traces. We then perform SFT on M500 using standard open-source, instruction-tuned LLMs. To further optimize adaptive reasoning and scaling in the MAS, we introduce a “
CEO
” agent powered by the SFT model, inspired by the observation that leaderless groups in human societies often lack effectiveness and coherent direction
(
Cruz et al., 1999
;
Hackman, 2002
)
. This agent dynamically guides discussions, effectively managing collaborative efforts and reasoning depth to improve the overall performance of the system.

We conduct extensive experiments to validate our approach by fine-tuning Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct
(
Hui et al., 2024
)
and Phi4-14B
(
Abdin et al., 2024
)
on M500, yielding the models Qwen2.5-32B-MAS and Phi4-14B-MAS. We then integrate these models into the AgentVerse
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
multi-agent framework. Testing across various task categories—including general understanding, mathematical reasoning, and coding—demonstrates that our Qwen2.5-32B-MAS and Phi4-14B-MAS significantly surpass the baseline Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and Phi4-14B within the MAS. For example, our method achieves a 12% improvement on GPQA-Diamond
(
Rein et al., 2024
)
, a 41% improvement on AIME2024
(
MAA, 2024
)
, and a 10% improvement on MBPP-Sanitized
(
Austin et al., 2021
)
, while matching the performance of DeepSeek-R1 on MATH-500 and MBPP-Sanitized.

In summary, our contributions are: ❶ We develop M500, a comprehensive multi-agent, multi-turn collaborative reasoning dataset created via an automatic generation pipeline; ❷ We demonstrate the collaborative reasoning capability of LLMs in MAS can be effectively improved by SFT on multi-agent, multi-turn reasoning datasets, serving as an effective implementation of MAS-TTS; ❸ We propose an adaptive scaling strategy that incorporates a CEO agent powered by the obtained LLMs to dynamically guide and scale multi-agent collaborative reasoning; and ❹ We show through extensive experiments that our method significantly outperforms baseline models and achieves performance comparable to DeepSeek-R1 on specific tasks.

Related Works

2.1
Test-time Scaling

A wide range of methods have been developed to improve reasoning in LLMs by leveraging Test-Time Scaling (TTS). Recent work explores techniques including hierarchical hypothesis search, which enables inductive reasoning through structured exploration
(
Wang et al., 2023b
;
Li et al., 2026
)
. They also examine tool augmented inference, which enhances downstream performance by allowing models to interact with external environments
(
Gao et al., 2023
;
Qin et al., 2024
)
. Other approaches focus on internal mechanisms, such as learning thought tokens in an unsupervised manner
(
Zelikman et al., 2024
;
Goyal et al., 2023
)
, allowing models to better utilize extended reasoning sequences. Among the most studied scaling paradigms are parallel and sequential TTS approaches. Parallel methods generate multiple solution candidates independently and select the best one using a scoring criterion, such as majority voting or outcome-based reward models
(
Brown et al., 2024
;
Irvine et al., 2023
;
Snell et al., 2025
)
. In contrast, sequential methods condition each new attempt on the previous ones, allowing iterative refinement based on prior outputs
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
;
Snell et al., 2025
;
Hou et al., 2025
;
Lee et al., 2025
)
. Bridging these strategies, tree-based techniques such as Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)
(
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Zhou et al., 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2024a
;
Wu et al., 2025
)
and guided beam search
(
Xie et al., 2023
)
enable structured exploration through branching and evaluation. Central to many of these methods are reward models, which provide feedback signals during generation. These can be categorized as outcome reward models, which evaluate entire solutions
(
Xin et al., 2024
;
Ankner et al., 2024
)
, or process reward models, which assess intermediate reasoning steps
(
Lightman et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2023a
;
Wu et al., 2025
)
, guiding the model toward more effective reasoning paths.

, software engineering

3.1
Automatic Generation of Multi-Agent Collaborative Reasoning Data

Question Sampling Based on Difficulty, Diversity, and Interdisciplinarity.

When selecting questions for our multi-agent collaborative reasoning dataset, we consider three main aspects: ❶ Difficulty, ❷ Diversity, and ❸ Interdisciplinarity. We begin with the full Simple-Scaling dataset
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
)
, which includes diverse questions sourced from historical AIME problems, OlympicArena
(
Huang et al., 2024
)
, and AGIEval
(
Zhong et al., 2024
)
, among others. These questions span multiple domains, including physics, geometry, number theory, biology, and astronomy. To promote difficulty and interdisciplinarity, we use DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al., 2025
)
to determine whether solving each question requires interdisciplinary knowledge, excluding those that DeepSeek-R1 answers using fewer than 1024 tokens. Questions selected through this process are generally challenging and demand knowledge integration from multiple disciplines. For example, solving a complex mathematics problem might benefit from collaboration between algebra and geometry specialists, whereas an advanced astronomy question may require input from astronomers, physicists, and mathematicians.

Generating Multi-Agent Collaborative Reasoning Traces.

To generate collaborative reasoning traces, we employ the open-source MAS frameworks and reasoning models, specifically AgentVerse
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
and DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al., 2025
)
, to process previously selected questions. This MAS framework involves multiple roles: Expert Recruiter (e.g., Human Resources), Problem Solvers (e.g., Scientists and Engineers), Executor (e.g., Quality Assurance Engineers), and Evaluator (e.g., Product Manager). The agents collaborate in the following manner: the Expert Recruiter identifies and assigns suitable experts for the task, while keeping the
total agent number
fixed and independent of question complexity or available information. These recruited experts function as Problem Solvers, proposing and iteratively refining solutions through multi-turn discussions with a predetermined
number of critic iterations
. Once a consensus is reached, or if the maximum number of critic iterations is reached, the resulting solution is passed to the Executor, who runs the necessary code or invokes external tools if required. The Evaluator then reviews both the solution and the results produced by the Executor, providing feedback for further refinement. This iterative process continues until either the solution is deemed correct or the predefined
maximum total number of iterations
is reached. Each MAS role produces distinct inputs and outputs, generating reasoning traces from its expert viewpoint. We collect all input-output pairs from each agent and compile the entire collaborative reasoning history into a comprehensive trace for each question.

Data Filtering.

To ensure high-quality collaborative reasoning traces, we filter data based on three criteria: ❶
Consensus Reached:
Traces where Problem Solvers fail to reach consensus within the maximum allowed critic iterations are discarded. This criterion ensures effective discussion convergence and minimizes unresolved reasoning. ❷
Format Compliance:
Samples that deviate from the required format are excluded. Specifically, each agent’s reasoning must be enclosed within
<think>
and
</think>
tags, and the final answers must be encapsulated within
boxed{}
. This maintains output consistency and facilitates automated parsing and answer extraction. ❸
Correctness:
We extract the final answer from each collaborative trace and compare it against the ground-truth solution, discarding any traces with incorrect answers.

Through this method, we iteratively sample 500 questions along with their corresponding multi-agent collaborative reasoning traces, forming the
M500
dataset. This dataset contains 500 challenging and diverse questions requiring interdisciplinary collaboration, accompanied by comprehensive and high-quality reasoning traces that document the complete multi-agent problem-solving process. The pseudocode detailing this sampling procedure is provided in Algorithm

in the Appendix.

3.2
Enhancing LLM Collaborative Reasoning through Supervised Fine-Tuning

Inspired by Simple-Scaling
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
)
, which shows that long-form CoT reasoning capabilities in LLMs can be developed through SFT on detailed reasoning traces, we apply SFT to an LLM
f
using the M500 dataset. The goal is to enable
f
to produce long-form CoT reasoning that supports the collaboration in a MAS. Specifically, the SFT objective is to minimize the following loss:

\textstyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\text{M500}}[-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{y}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\mathbf{y}|}\log P_{f}(\mathbf{y}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{<t})]

where
P_{f}(\mathbf{y}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{<t})
denotes the probability the model
f
assigns to token
\mathbf{y}_{t}
given input
\mathbf{x}
and previous tokens
\mathbf{y}_{<t}
.

For each question
q
in the M500 dataset, we collect a series of input-output pairs
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
corresponding to the reasoning traces from all participating agents. During training, we ensure all reasoning traces for
q
,
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, are grouped within the same batch and ordered according to the original generation sequence in the MAS. This approach helps the model learn collaborative reasoning in a coherent and temporally consistent manner.

Finally, we integrate the fine-tuned model back into the MAS and introduce a CEO agent to adaptively scale reasoning by dynamically adjusting collaboration intensity and reasoning depth.

3.3
Adaptive Test-time Scaling

Recently, TTS has emerged as an effective method for enhancing the performance of LLMs. Models such as OpenAI’s o-series and DeepSeek-R1 have shown considerable improvements by employing scaled reasoning during inference. However, the application of TTS within MAS remains relatively unexplored. Previous studies in single-agent scenarios indicate that the optimal TTS strategy depends on question difficulty
(
Liu et al., 2025
;
Wu et al., 2025
)
. In MAS, choosing an appropriate TTS strategy is even more critical due to the significantly higher computational and time costs involved in collaboration compared to single-agent.

To address this issue, we propose an adaptive TTS strategy for MAS by introducing a dedicated "
CEO
" agent, which dynamically manages collaboration and resource allocation based on the task’s ongoing progress. As shown in Figure
3
, the CEO agent evaluates the question, current solution state, evaluation feedback, and available resources to determine whether a proposed solution should be accepted or needs further refinement. Additionally, this agent directs subsequent discussions, decides how many agents to involve, and sets appropriate reasoning depth (i.e., the token budget for each agent’s response).

Unlike static MAS configurations, which have fixed numbers of agents, iteration limits, and reasoning depths, our adaptive approach allows the MAS to dynamically adjust its settings. This capability enables more effective scaling of collaborative reasoning by modifying agent participation, termination conditions, and reasoning depth according to the evolving requirements of the problem.

Experiments

To validate that our method effectively improves collaborative reasoning in MAS, we conduct experiments using state-of-the-art (SOTA) open-source and closed-source LLMs within the AgentVerse framework. We evaluate performance across general understanding, mathematical reasoning, and coding tasks. Additionally, we analyze the emergent behavior and scaling properties of the proposed method.

4.2
Main Results

We demonstrate that our method significantly improves the performance of LLMs within MAS compared to both the original models and SFT models trained on single-agent datasets. The experimental results are presented in Table
1
. We observe several key findings: ❶ Our method achieves substantial improvements across all evaluated tasks relative to Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and Phi4-14B. This demonstrates that Qwen2.5-32B-MAS and Phi4-14B-MAS, particularly when integrated with the CEO agent, effectively improve general question answering, writing, mathematical reasoning, and coding capabilities within MAS. Specifically, Qwen2.5-32B-MAS w. CEO improves performance by 12%, 41%, 11%, and 10% on GPQA, AIME2024, MATH-500, and MBPP-S, respectively, compared to Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct. Moreover, our method achieves performance comparable to SoTA open-source and closed-source models, such as DeepSeek-R1 and o3-mini, on MATH-500, Commongen, and MBPP-S, underscoring the effectiveness of our approach. ❷ Our approach significantly improves collaborative reasoning in MAS compared to single-agent SFT and Simple-Scaling
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
)
. For instance, Qwen2.5-32B-MAS with CEO outperforms s1.1-32B by 4% on GPQA and 9% on AIME2024. Additionally, while s1.1-32B experiences performance degradation in coding tasks compared to Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, our method yields notable improvements in this domain, highlighting the advantage over Simple-Scaling. ❸ The MAS models outperform the single-agent SFT models when evaluated within the MAS framework, indicating that collaborative reasoning traces are more effective than single-agent reasoning data for enhancing multi-agent capabilities. ❹ The introduction of the CEO agent consistently improves the performance of Qwen2.5-32B-MAS and Phi4-14B-MAS across all tasks. This highlights that collaborative reasoning in MAS is effectively scaled when guided by a CEO agent powered by Qwen2.5-32B-MAS or Phi4-14B-MAS.

4.3
Aha Moment in MAS

In MAS, we observe that when using Qwen2.5-32B-MAS, agents sometimes exhibit emergent behaviors that actively contribute to validating and refining collaborative processes, even when it is not explicitly required. For example, as illustrated in Figure
4
, the Problem Solver initially fails to recognize an error in its solution. After reviewing this solution, the CEO agent actively checks its validity and identifies the Problem Solver’s error, noting that it results in a negative length for a line segment. The CEO agent then proposes an alternative and correct solution, prompting the Problem Solver to revise its original response accordingly. This collaborative interaction, where one agent assists others by verifying solutions, exploring alternative approaches, and suggesting corrections, occurs even when other agents are unaware of their own mistakes. A plausible reason for this emergent behavior is that the CEO agent, having been trained on multi-agent collaborative reasoning traces and observing other agents’ discussions, actively validates and corrects solutions based on learned collaborative patterns and insights gained from the reasoning of other agents.

4.4
Additional Investigation

Scaling Collaborative Reasoning in MAS.

We investigate how scaling collaborative reasoning affects the performance of Qwen2.5-32B-MAS in MAS by systematically adjusting the total iterations, critic iterations, total agent numbers, and maximum token limits. The results are presented in Figures
5
and
6
. Our observations are as follows: ❶ Enhancing collaboration by increasing the interactions among Problem Solvers significantly improves performance. This can be achieved either by raising the critic iteration limit to allow more extensive discussion toward consensus or by increasing the total number of Problem Solvers. However, involving too many Problem Solvers may reduce performance due to divergent discussions among agents. Additionally, merely increasing the total iterations does not improve MAS performance. ❷ Enhancing reasoning capabilities by increasing the maximum allowed tokens per agent effectively improves MAS performance. Furthermore, optimal token limits vary by task; for example, 16384 tokens yield optimal results for AIME2024, whereas 8192 tokens are sufficient for GPQA. This finding supports our motivation for using the CEO agent to dynamically manage token allocation based on specific task requirements.

5
Conclusion

We propose Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) as a structural upgrade to single-agent Test-Time Scaling (TTS). Through Supervised Fine-Tuning on our M500 dataset, we demonstrated that open-source models can effectively internalize collaborative reasoning patterns. The integration of an adaptive CEO agent further optimized this process, yielding significant improvements in mathematical and coding tasks in MAS settings. Our results confirm that MAS-based TTS enables smaller models to match state-of-the-art baselines, establishing a robust pathway for scalable inference.

6
Limitations

The M500 dataset size is relatively small, consisting of 500 high-quality traces. While this data volume proves sufficient to elicit collaborative behaviors in the evaluated models, scaling the dataset to cover a broader range of domains and more diverse interaction topologies could yield further improvements. Additionally, while the CEO agent effectively guides the reasoning process, it introduces a centralization risk. If the CEO agent hallucinates or provides incorrect directives early in the collaboration, it may lead the group toward an incorrect solution path, limiting the ability of other agents to self-correct. Future work could explore decentralized coordination mechanisms to mitigate this dependency.

7
Ethical Considerations

In this work, we propose that LLMs supervised fine-tuning on MAS collaborative reasoning traces achieves better scaling performance on the MAS. Our work focuses on technical contributions to deep learning and AI. Therefore, the potential social impacts of AI in general apply to our work, including fake information, toxic content, fairness concerns, and misuse of AI. For example, toxic content like hate speech can lead to data contamination and therefore have harmful impacts on society, which has been observed in large-scale pretrained models. By employing our method, such harmful behavior can potentially be amplified.
