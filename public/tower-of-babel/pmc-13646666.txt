Revisiting the Temperature Dependence of the Photorespiratory CO2 Compensation Point (Γ*)
Authors: Darwin L Moreno‐Echeverry, Miko U F Kirschbaum, Margaret M Barbour, Lìyǐn L Liáng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAccurate estimation of the photorespiratory CO2 compensation point (Γ*) is essential for describing the balance between Rubisco carboxylation and oxygenation and for parameterising biochemical models of photosynthesis. Γ* and the rate of CO2 release in the light (D L) are commonly estimated using the Laisk method, based on measurements of net CO2 assimilation rate (A net) at low chloroplastic CO2 concentrations (cc), under several sub‐saturating irradiance levels. However, many widely used temperature dependence relationships for Γ* (Γ*(T)) were derived using conventional linear implementations of the Laisk method, despite the intrinsically nonlinear behaviour of the A net–cc response predicted by the photosynthetic theory. Here, we revisited the temperature dependence of Γ* and D L using the improved Laisk–FvCB framework that simultaneously constrains the nonlinear A net–cc response across multiple irradiance levels. Gas exchange of sunflower leaves was measured across a wide temperature range from 3.9°C to 42.0°C. The conventional linear implementation generated highly dispersed pairwise intersections and unstable estimates of both Γ* and D L, including some physiologically unrealistic negative D L values at low temperatures. In contrast, the mechanistically constrained Laisk–FvCB framework produced physiologically meaningful temperature responses and substantially reduced methodological artefacts associated with linear extrapolation. Using this framework, we derived a revised in vivo Γ*(T) relationship described by an Arrhenius‐type function with Γ*(25) = 43.4 μmol mol−1 and an apparent activation energy of 27.7 kJ mol−1, such that Γ*(T) = 43.4 exp[11.176 ((T − 25)/(T + 273.15))], where T is leaf temperature in °C. Comparison with other widely used Γ*(T) formulations showed substantial divergence at temperature extremes, often exceeding the variability expected from realistic interspecific differences in Rubisco specificity among C3 species.Keywords: Arrhenius temperature function, C3 photosynthesis, CO2 release in the light, DL, FvCB model, gamma‐star, gas exchange, Laisk method, Rday, Rlight

Introduction

Quantifying the difference between CO2 assimilation and CO2 release is essential for understanding the carbon balance of C3 plant leaves. While plants assimilate CO2 through photosynthesis, a portion of the fixed carbon is released through two independent but connected processes: photorespiration and decarboxylation in the light (D L) (Dusenge et al. 2019; Moreno‐Echeverry, Kirschbaum, et al. 2026; Pärnik et al. 2007; Tcherkez et al. 2017). Photorespiration comes from the dual substrate specificity of Rubisco, which can bind both CO2 and O2 as substrates and catalyse either carboxylation or oxygenation of ribulose‐1,5‐bisphospate (RuBP) (Farquhar et al. 1980). The competition between carboxylation and oxygenation determines the photorespiratory CO2 compensation point (Γ*), defined as ‘the CO2 concentration at which CO2 uptake from carboxylation and CO2 loss from photorespiration are equal’ (Brooks and Farquhar 1985). Therefore, when the chloroplast CO2 concentration equals Γ*, the net assimilation rate (A net) equals D L.

As Γ* represents the CO2 compensation point in the absence of non‐photorespiratory respiration, it is determined by Rubisco specificity for CO2 relative to O2 (S c/o) and the O2 concentration at the site of Rubisco activity (Brooks and Farquhar 1985; Jordan and Ogren 1984; Walker et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2024). Rubisco specificity for CO2 declines as temperature rises, and consequently oxygenation increases relative to carboxylation, resulting in an increase in Γ* (Galmés et al. 2016; Hermida‐Carrera et al. 2016; Jordan and Ogren 1984). Γ* is a fundamental parameter in the most widely used biochemical model developed by Farquhar, von Caemmerer, and Berry, also known as the FvCB model (Farquhar et al. 1980; Walker and Ort 2015; Yin, Busch, et al. 2020), so quantification of environmentally determined variation in the parameter is vital for accurate model predictions (Bernacchi et al. 2001).

Biochemical studies have quantified the temperature dependence of S c/o in vitro and calculated the temperature dependence of Γ* [Γ*(T)] by combining the temperature dependence of Rubisco specificity with the relative change in solubility of CO2 and O2 (Jordan and Ogren 1984; Perdomo et al. 2015; Zhu et al. 1998). Alternatively, Γ*(T) can be determined in vivo using the Laisk method by measuring the CO2 evolution under different light levels at low CO2 concentrations (Laisk 1977; Brooks and Farquhar 1985).

The Laisk method has become one of the standard approaches for estimating Γ* and D L from leaf gas exchange measurements, and its correct application is now widely discussed in methodological syntheses of photosynthetic measurements (Berghuijs et al. 2019; Busch et al. 2024; Villar et al. 1994; Walker and Cousins 2013). Although D L has also been estimated using alternative approaches, including Kok‐type analyses (Gauthier et al. 2020; Way et al. 2019; Yin, Niu, et al. 2020) and combined gas exchange fluorescence techniques (Yin et al. 2011), the Laisk method remains widely used because it enables the simultaneous estimation of both Γ* and D L.

Estimates of Γ*(T) functions derived from enzyme assays of Rubisco and gas exchange measurements at the leaf level are broadly consistent (Jordan and Ogren 1984; Brooks and Farquhar 1985; Bernacchi et al. 2001), reflecting the relatively conserved nature of S c/o among C3 plants (Galmés et al. 2016; Hermida‐Carrera et al. 2016). However, while being broadly consistent, notable discrepancies among published Γ*(T) relationships remain, particularly at low and high temperatures.

The importance of those discrepancies is illustrated in Figure 1, where we show an A net–cc curve measured on a sunflower leaf at 20°C. The FvCB model was fitted to the data with Γ* set to different values based on three widely used literature sources, as indicated in the figure. Figure 1a shows the overall fit to the data, and Figure 1b shows an enlargement in the cc range around Γ*. Similarly, good agreement between modelled and observed data could be obtained for the different assumed values of Γ*, but the choice of different Γ* values leads to different best parameter fits of other key parameters of the FvCB model, particularly for D L (Figure 1b). The potential for parameter compensation within the FvCB framework becomes evident when Γ* is poorly constrained. The selected value of Γ* on the abscissa of the A net–cc space, consequently, determines the value of D L on the ordinate since D L equals A net at Γ*. Therefore, any error in estimating Γ* will lead to quantitatively important errors in D L estimates (Gong et al. 2018; Moreno‐Echeverry, Kirschbaum, et al. 2026; Yin et al. 2011).

In addition, previous estimates of Γ*(T) and D L(T) based on gas‐exchange measurements mainly relied on classical implementations of the Laisk method, in which linear regressions of A net plotted against intercellular or chloroplast CO2 concentration were fitted over wide CO2 ranges. Previous work has shown that the nonlinear behaviour of the A net–cc curve could have led to serious estimation errors (Moreno‐Echeverry, Kirschbaum, et al. 2026; Schmiege et al. 2023; Walker and Ort 2015). In most cases, these linear implementations may generate multiple pairwise intersections and unstable Γ* estimates across temperatures. Use of assumed linear relationships is likely to have introduced systematic bias in estimating Γ*, particularly at low and high temperatures, which will have propagated directly into estimates of D L. We have elaborated on these problems below.

In the present work, we have built on a recently developed integration of the Laisk method with the FvCB model that captures the nonlinearity of the A net–cc curve (Moreno‐Echeverry, Kirschbaum, et al. 2026) and have used the modified approach to revisit the temperature dependence of Γ* and D L using leaf gas exchange measurements. We compared conventional linear and nonlinear implementations of the Laisk method across a wide temperature range and evaluated whether methodological artefacts associated with linear fitting could contribute to discrepancies among published Γ*(T) relationships. We further assessed whether the variability among published Γ*(T) functions exceeded the range expected from interspecific variation in Rubisco specificity among C3 plants.

Materials and Methods

Plant Growth Conditions and Gas Exchange Measurements

Sunflower (Helianthus annuus ‘Russian Giant’) plants were grown in a plant growth unit. Plants were watered daily to field capacity and fertilised weekly with a complete nutrient solution. The photoperiod was 14/10 h, day/night, air temperatures were 24°C/19°C, and relative humidity was 40% during the day and 65% at night. Photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) at the leaf surface during the growth period was about 400 µmol photons m−2 s−1.

Sunflower was selected because it provides several practical and physiological advantages for temperature‐dependent gas exchange analyses. The species develops large, anatomically homogeneous leaves that facilitate stable IRGA measurements across broad temperature ranges while maintaining relatively high photosynthetic activity across short‐term thermal treatments and throughout the day.

Gas exchange was measured on fully expanded, upper canopy leaves using a LI‐6400XT portable photosynthesis system (LI‐COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Throughout the experiment, standard LI‐COR procedures were followed for flow rate control, IRGA matching, and H2O and CO2 calibration.

To enable measurements across a wide temperature range, the leaf cuvette of the LI‐6400XT was housed inside a purpose‐built temperature‐controlled enclosure. This insulated box allowed the ambient air surrounding the cuvette to be cooled or warmed independently of room temperature, ensuring stable and uniform thermal conditions during measurements. This configuration made it possible to achieve and maintain leaf temperatures as low as 3.9°C and as high as 42°C without condensation forming in the tubing or on optical surfaces. Air entering the system was equilibrated within the enclosure before reaching the analyser, which prevented humidity and temperature transients that could otherwise compromise IRGA stability at extreme temperatures.

Chamber air temperatures within the enclosure were adjusted over an approximate range of 5°C to 49°C, generating corresponding measured leaf temperatures ranging from 3.9°C to 42°C. All analyses were based on the measured leaf temperatures, which represent the physiologically relevant variable controlling photosynthetic kinetics. Differences between chamber air temperature and leaf temperature arose naturally from variation in leaf energy balance and transpirational cooling during measurements, particularly at temperature extremes. For each temperature response, leaves were acclimated for 20 min at the target leaf temperature, with chamber relative humidity maintained at approximately 60%, reference CO2 set to 400 μmol mol−1, and O2 maintained at the ambient atmospheric concentration of 21%. Within a given temperature treatment, successive A net–cc curves were measured without returning leaves to 400 μmol mol−1 for a full reacclimation period between individual curves. Stable A net values were confirmed before initiating each A net–cc curve.

Because the application of the Laisk method requires three A net–cc curves with distinct initial slopes, gas exchange measurements were conducted under three different sub‐saturating PPFD levels at each temperature. PPFD combinations were adjusted among leaves and temperature treatments to account for variation in leaf photosynthetic capacity and to ensure adequate separation among A net–cc slopes under sub‐saturating conditions. A net–cc curves were generated using stepwise decreases in reference CO2 concentration, ranging from approximately 200 down to 40 μmol mol−1, depending on temperature. Each Γ* and D L estimate was therefore derived from a dataset consisting of three A net–cc curves measured on the same leaf at a given temperature. In total, 56 Γ*– D L datasets were obtained across the full range of temperature and irradiance conditions.

Determination of Γ* and D L Based on Gas‐Exchange Measurements

At each target leaf temperature, Γ* and D L were estimated using both the conventional Linear–Laisk method and a refined implementation of the Laisk method integrated with the FvCB photosynthesis model, as described by Moreno‐Echeverry, Avadie, et al. (2026). This implementation incorporates the smoothed transition between Rubisco‐limited and RuBP regeneration‐limited photosynthesis proposed by Kirschbaum and Farquhar (1984). In both approaches, three A net–cc curves were measured over a low‐CO2 range under three sub‐saturating PPFD levels.

For the conventional Laisk method, linear regressions were fitted to the low CO2 region of each A net–cc curve. Γ* and D L were estimated from the mean of the pairwise intersections between regressions obtained under different levels within each dataset, where Γ* was taken as the x‐intercept and D L as the y‐intercept. To assess the sensitivity of the conventional Laisk implementation to the arbitrary selection of the fitting interval, additional analyses were performed by fitting the linear regressions using the lowest three, four or five CO2 observations and comparing the resulting estimates with those obtained using the six‐point reference analysis. The corresponding results are presented in Figure S1.

In the nonlinear Laisk–FvCB method, values of Γ* and D L were jointly estimated from the three curves and constrained to be identical across irradiance levels at a given temperature. Under these constraints, the fitted A net–cc curves converged to a single common intersection in (cc, A net) coordinates, from which Γ* was defined as the x‐intercept and D L as the y‐intercept. In both approaches, D L was expressed as a positive flux corresponding to net CO2 release in the light. Model fitting simultaneously estimated the photosynthetic capacity and curvature parameters of the FvCB model (V cmax, J max, α, θ and g m), together with Γ* and D L. The optimisation procedure and parameter constraints are described in detail in Note S1.

Statistical Comparison Between Methods

To evaluate whether the conventional Laisk–linear and Laisk–FvCB implementations produced significantly different parameter estimates, paired comparisons were performed using the 56 datasets analysed in this study. For each dataset, the difference between both implementations (Laisk–FvCB − Laisk–linear) was calculated for Γ* and D L. Normality of the paired differences was assessed using the Lilliefors test. When the paired differences followed a normal distribution, differences between methods were evaluated using a paired Student's t‐test; otherwise, a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used. To quantify the uncertainty of the estimated mean and median paired differences, bias‐corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals were obtained from 10 000 paired bootstrap resamples. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen's dz .

Agreement between both implementations was additionally assessed using Deming regression, which accounts for measurement uncertainty in both variables. Under perfect agreement, the regression intercept equals 0, and the slope equals 1 (Y = X). Bootstrap confidence intervals (10 000 paired resamples) were calculated for the intercept and slope, and a joint Wald test was used to evaluate the null hypothesis that the intercept equals 0 and the slope equals 1 simultaneously.

Results

Comparison of Linear and Nonlinear Implementations of the Laisk Method

The two implementations of the Laisk method produced fundamentally different representations of the A net–cc relationship. The conventional Laisk–linear approach estimates Γ* and D L from pairwise intersections among irradiance levels, whereas the Laisk–FvCB approach simultaneously constrains all irradiance levels using the nonlinear FvCB model (Figure 2). The differences between the two approaches were particularly evident at low temperatures. For example, at 4°C (Figure 2a), the pairwise linear regressions generated markedly different intersection points depending on the irradiance combination considered, whereas the Laisk–FvCB approach yielded a single physiologically consistent estimate.

Although this discrepancy was most pronounced at low temperatures, the conventional implementation remained dependent on user‐defined linear regressions across all temperatures, whereas the Laisk–FvCB approach consistently provided a single mechanistically constrained estimate using the complete dataset. This dependence was further confirmed by repeating the linear analysis using different numbers of low‐CO2 observations. Estimates of both Γ* and D L varied substantially with the selected fitting interval, whereas the nonlinear Laisk–FvCB approach was unaffected because it simultaneously fitted the complete dataset (Figure S1).

These methodological differences resulted in markedly different estimates of the temperature dependence of Γ* (Figure 4). The Laisk–FvCB approach generated a smooth and consistent increase in Γ* from approximately 20 μmol mol−1 at 3.9°C to 79 μmol mol−1 at 42°C (Figure 4a). In contrast, the conventional Laisk–linear approach produced substantially greater dispersion, particularly at lower temperatures, where several estimates approached 0 or became erroneously negative (Figure 4b). Although the mean Γ* values obtained with the linear approach also increased with temperature, the variability among datasets remained high across the entire temperature range.

Comparison of the Derived Temperature Dependence of D L

The contrasting behaviour observed for Γ* between the two implementations of the Laisk method also propagated into the estimation of D L (Figure 5). The Laisk–FvCB approach produced a consistent increase in D L with leaf temperature across the entire experimental range (Figure 5a). D L remained positive at all temperatures and increased from 0.2 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1 at 3.9°C to 5 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1 at 42°C.

In contrast, D L estimates derived from the conventional Laisk–linear approach showed substantially greater variability (Figure 5b). Although the average D L values generally increased with temperature, the dispersion among estimates remained high across the measured temperature range. Several D L estimates became erroneously negative, particularly at low temperatures, reflecting the limitation of the conventional Laisk–linear method in accurately estimating D L. These negative values emerged directly from the inconsistent Γ* estimates generated by the multiple linear intersections observed in Figures 3 and 4.

These differences in D L estimates were further confirmed when alternative published Γ*(T) were used during FvCB parameter estimation (Table S1). Whereas fitted V cmax and J max values remained comparatively stable across functions, D L estimates showed substantial sensitivity to the selected Γ*(T) relationship, particularly at low and high temperatures.

Statistical Comparison of Parameter Estimates Obtained Using the Laisk–Linear and Laisk–FvCB Methods

To formally assess whether the two implementations produced significantly different parameter estimates, paired statistical comparisons were performed across the 56 gas exchange datasets analysed in this study (Table 1). The Laisk–FvCB implementation yielded systematically higher Γ* estimates than the conventional Laisk–linear implementation (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, p = 1.95 × 10−8), with a mean paired difference of 9.91 µmol mol−1 (BCa bootstrap 95% confidence interval: 7.10–13.75 µmol mol−1). Likewise, D L estimates were significantly higher using the Laisk–FvCB method (paired Student's t‐test, p = 2.32 × 10−4), with a mean paired difference of 0.180 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1 (BCa bootstrap 95% confidence interval: 0.091–0.268 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1).

At 25°C, we obtained a best‐fit Γ* of 43.4 μmol mol−1, compared with 42.8 μmol mol−1 from Bernacchi et al. (2001), 42.7 μmol mol−1 from Brooks and Farquhar (1985) and 44.7 μmol mol−1 from Jordan and Ogren (1984). We then examined how Γ* derived from the improved Laisk–FvCB fitting approach varied with leaf temperature. To provide a comparison with existing Γ*(T) functions from Jordan and Ogren (1984), Brooks and Farquhar (1985) and Bernacchi et al. (2001), we plotted the Γ*(T) function derived in the current study together with these functions (Figure 7a). Brooks and Farquhar (1985) reported Γ* values over the temperature range from 15°C to 30°C, whereas Jordan and Ogren (1984) and Bernacchi et al. (2001) covered wider temperature windows from 5°C to 40°C and from 10°C to 40°C, respectively.

To better compare the implied temperature sensitivities of these Γ*(T) functions, the same data and functions are shown in Arrhenius coordinates, with ln Γ* plotted against 1000/T (Figure 7b), showing clear differences in the slope of each function. Our Γ*(T) estimates closely followed a linear Arrhenius relationship over the full temperature range, yielding an apparent E a of 27.7 kJ mol−1. The formulation of Bernacchi et al. (2001) produced a substantially steeper Arrhenius slope corresponding to a higher E a of 37.8 kJ mol−1. By contrast, the polynomial functions of Brooks and Farquhar (1985) and Jordan and Ogren (1984) show marked curvature, especially at low temperatures, indicating departures from simple Arrhenius behaviour. This curvature reflects the limitations of applying polynomial Γ*(T) models outside their calibration domains and results in substantial divergence at the cold end of the range.

Although the alternative Γ*(T) functions diverged substantially from the relationship derived in this study, part of this variation could theoretically arise from interspecific differences in S c/o. To assess whether the observed deviation fell within the range expected from plausible biological variation among C3 species, we compared the relative differences among Γ*(T) functions with a reference envelope based on published variation in S c/o (Figure 8).

The ±7% envelope represents a reference benchmark derived from the average interspecific variation in S c/o compiled by Galmés et al. (2016). Their compilation included in vitro measurements of the temperature response of Rubisco specificity for 24 plant species spanning diverse C3 taxa and climatic origins (Table S2). This reference envelope is intended to provide an approximate indication of the magnitude of biologically realistic interspecific variation in S c/o, rather than to define the full range of variation observed among all species or genera. Indeed, larger differences have been reported for some taxonomic groups. For example, Orr et al. (2016) reported approximately 11% variation in S c/o among Oryza species at 20°C, illustrating that the extent of natural variation can differ among taxa.

Because Γ* is inversely proportional to S c/o (Γ* = 0.5 O/S c/o), a relative change in S c/o propagates approximately one to one, but in the opposite direction, to Γ* when O2 concentration is assumed constant. Accordingly, a ± 7% change in S c/o corresponds approximately to a ± 7% change in Γ*. Using the Γ*(T) function derived in this study as a reference, we constructed the corresponding ± 7% reference envelope and compared it with the relative differences produced by alternative Γ*(T) functions.

In general, the relative differences among Γ*(T) functions remained within the 7% reference envelope over moderate temperatures but increasingly exceeded this benchmark towards both low and high temperatures. The formulation of Bernacchi et al. (2001) showed the greatest divergence, reaching approximately ± 25% at the temperature extremes. The Brooks and Farquhar (1985) function reached 35% at the coolest temperature, and the Jordan and Ogren (1984) function also exceeded the expected range of variability, particularly at temperature extremes.

Discussion

Importance of a Methodologically Sound Basis for Reliable Γ*(T) Estimation

The Laisk method has become one of the most widely used gas exchange approaches for estimating Γ* and D L (Yin and Amthor 2024; Zaragoza‐Castells et al. 2007). Estimates of Γ* are fundamental for parameterising photosynthetic models, and D L plays a central role in understanding respiratory carbon losses and the coordination between photosynthesis and respiration under illuminated conditions (Farquhar et al. 1980; Tcherkez et al. 2017). Previous analyses have suggested that the conventional linear implementation of the Laisk method may introduce substantial methodological instability into the estimation of both Γ* and D L (Walker and Ort 2015; Moreno‐Echeverry, Kirschbaum, et al. 2026).

The large dispersion observed among pairwise intersections demonstrates that this instability originates directly from the mathematical structure of the conventional linear implementation itself rather than from isolated experimental noise (Figures 2 and 3). Similar patterns emerged when the same methodological comparison was applied to the independent datasets of Way et al. (2019) and Crous et al. (2017). In both studies, like in the recent study (Figures 2 and 3), the conventional Laisk–linear implementation produced highly dispersed pairwise intersections across temperatures (Figures S2 and S3), which subsequently propagated into unstable and highly variable Γ* estimates (Figure S4). Together, these observations strongly indicate that the uncertainty is systematic and intrinsic to the linear approximation itself rather than dataset‐specific.

This uncertainty is mechanistically expected because the conventional Laisk implementation relies on linear regressions fitted over sections of the A net–cc response, despite the intrinsically nonlinear behaviour predicted by the FvCB model (Farquhar et al. 1980). Under the traditional framework, the estimated intersection becomes highly sensitive to the selected CO2 range, irradiance level and measurement noise, particularly under low temperature conditions (Moreno‐Echeverry, Kirschbaum, et al. 2026), where photosynthetic rates decline and signal‐to‐noise ratios are inherently reduced. Consequently, part of the variability historically reported for Γ* (Walker and Ort 2015), likely reflects methodological uncertainty associated with the linear implementation rather than genuine physiological variation.

This dependence on the selected CO2 range introduces a fundamental trade‐off in the conventional implementation. Although restricting the fitting interval to observations closest to Γ* is theoretically desirable because this region is expected to be approximately linear, our sensitivity analysis showed that the estimated parameters remained highly sensitive to the selected fitting interval (Figure S1). Because the regression is fitted over a very narrow CO2 range, small changes in slope can produce large changes in the extrapolated intersection. Expanding the fitting interval improves slope constraint but progressively incorporates curvature into the fitted region, compromising the assumption of linearity. Consequently, no single choice of fitting interval consistently eliminates this source of uncertainty, as the conventional implementation must balance preserving local linearity against obtaining robustly constrained regressions.

The improved Laisk–FvCB framework substantially reduced these artefacts by embedding the Laisk logic directly within the mechanistic structure of the FvCB model and simultaneously constraining Γ* across multiple irradiance levels using the nonlinear A net–cc response (Moreno‐Echeverry, Kirschbaum, et al. 2026).

Propagation of Γ* Uncertainty Into D L Estimation

Accurate estimation of D L is increasingly important for understanding daytime carbon balance because growing evidence indicates that CO2 release in the light is dynamic rather than constant and reflects the combined contributions of multiple metabolic processes (Tcherkez et al. 2017; Gong et al. 2018; Moreno‐Echeverry, Kirschbaum, et al. 2026). Despite these complexities and the continuing uncertainty surrounding its quantification, the Laisk framework remains one of the most widely used gas exchange approaches for estimating D L in vivo.

The present results demonstrate that uncertainty in Γ* estimation propagates directly into estimates of D L. The conventional Laisk–linear implementation frequently generated highly variable D L estimates and even produced negative values, particularly at low temperatures (Figure 5). Similar behaviour also emerged when the published datasets from Way et al. (2019) and Crous et al. (2017) were analysed using the same conventional implementation (Figure S5a), reinforcing the conclusion that these negative values most likely originate from methodological artefacts associated with unstable linear intersections. These observations strongly suggest that at least part of the physiologically unrealistic behaviour historically reported for D L at low temperatures (Atkin et al. 2000; Tcherkez and Atkin 2021), may originate from uncertainty in Γ* estimation rather than from respiratory responses.

Under the improved Laisk–FvCB framework, D L remained positive across the full temperature range examined and exhibited physiologically plausible thermal responses. This behaviour is consistent with current understanding of respiratory and photorespiratory carbon metabolism in illuminated leaves, where mitochondrial decarboxylation, photorespiration and carbon export processes interact dynamically with photosynthetic activity (Tcherkez et al. 2017; Gong et al. 2018).

Temperature Dependence of Γ*(T) and Its Implications for Photoynthesis Modelling

Temperature‐dependent Γ*(T) functions are widely used in photosynthesis models because Γ* quantifies the balance between Rubisco carboxylation and oxygenation within the FvCB framework. Many of the most commonly used Γ*(T) parameterisations originated either from implementations of the Laisk method (Brooks and Farquhar 1985; Bernacchi et al. 2001) or from biochemical approaches based on measurements of Rubisco specificity (Jordan and Ogren 1984). Consequently, uncertainties associated with the estimation methods propagate directly into subsequent modelling applications.

The Γ*(T) function derived here using the improved Laisk–FvCB framework exhibited a clear and monotonic increase with temperature across the measured range. This temperature response is broadly consistent with patterns reported in classical and more recent studies examining Rubisco specificity and photorespiratory kinetics (Brooks and Farquhar 1985; Galmés et al. 2016; Jordan and Ogren 1984; Perdomo et al. 2015; von Caemmerer et al. 1994).

In biochemical terms, Γ* increases with increases in the effective O2 concentration at the carboxylation site and decreases as S c/o increases. Consequently, temperature influences Γ* through at least two coupled mechanisms: changes in the relative solubility of CO2 and O2, and changes in S c/o itself. The increase in Γ* with warming, therefore, reflects well‐established biochemical and physical processes governing Rubisco kinetics, including the stronger temperature‐driven decline in CO2 solubility relative to O2, the increasingly favourable oxygenation transition state, and the progressive decline in S c/o at higher temperatures (Bathellier et al. 2020; Cen and Sage 2005; Tcherkez et al. 2006; von Caemmerer et al. 1994). Together, these processes shift the balance of Rubisco reactions towards oxygenation at higher temperatures, resulting in a higher Γ*.

However, the Γ*(T) relationship derived using the Laisk–FvCB framework differed substantially from widely used published formulations, particularly at temperature extremes (Figure 6). Although part of this divergence could theoretically arise from genuine interspecific variation in S c/o of the different species used in the studies, comparison with the variability compiled by Galmés et al. (2016) and Orr et al. (2016) demonstrated that the divergence among several published Γ*(T) functions exceeded the range expected solely from plausible biological variation among C3 species (Figure 7). These observations suggest that methodological differences among estimation frameworks likely contributed substantially to the variability among published Γ*(T) formulations. The Bernacchi et al. (2001) function showed the largest deviations, reaching approximately ± 25% relative to our reference relationship at the temperature extremes. At low temperatures, the Brooks and Farquhar (1985) function similarly diverged strongly from our reference relationship. These results do not demonstrate that any individual parameterisation is necessarily incorrect. Rather, they show that the divergence among widely used Γ*(T) functions can exceed the magnitude of variation typically reported for S c/o among C3 species, particularly when the functions are applied outside the temperature ranges over which they were originally calibrated.

The contrasting behaviour of the two Laisk implementations further illustrates the magnitude of the methodological uncertainty introduced by the conventional linear approach. When individual Γ* estimates were expressed relative to the fitted Γ*(T) relationship derived from the Laisk–FvCB framework, their estimates remained tightly clustered around the reference relationship across the full temperature range (Figure S6a). In contrast, Γ* estimates obtained using the conventional Laisk–linear implementation showed extreme dispersion, with nearly all estimates falling outside the ± 7% variability range associated with realistic interspecific variation in S c/o among C3 species (Figure S6b). Deviations commonly exceeded several tens of percent and reached values approaching 150% in some cases, demonstrating that the uncertainty introduced by the linear implementation greatly exceeds the magnitude of biologically plausible variation in S c/o.

These divergent trends become clearer in Arrhenius coordinates (Figure 6b), where the temperature response of the Γ*(T) function derived in this study was approximately linear across the measured temperature range. By contrast, the quadratic formulations displayed pronounced curvature towards the temperature extremes. This divergence became particularly evident outside the temperature ranges over which the original functions were calibrated, where extrapolation amplified differences among formulations. Consequently, although the quadratic functions may adequately describe Γ*(T) within their respective calibration domains, they produced increasingly divergent predictions at temperature extremes. Given the close relationship between Γ* and S c/o (Jordan and Ogren 1984; Galmés et al. 2016; Perdomo et al. 2015), the approximately linear behaviour observed in Arrhenius coordinates suggests that an Arrhenius‐type formulation provides a robust empirical description of Γ*(T) across a broad thermal range.

Although the Arrhenius formulation of Bernacchi et al. (2001) also remained linear in Arrhenius space, it implied a substantially stronger temperature sensitivity than the relationship derived in the present study. Consequently, the Bernacchi formulation produced lower Γ* values at cool temperatures and higher values at warm temperatures, primarily because of its substantially higher apparent activation energy (E a of 37.8 kJ mol−1) compared with the value estimated here (E a of 27.7 kJ mol−1).

These methodological differences become particularly important when fitting FvCB models to conventional gas exchange datasets, where Γ*(T) is typically prescribed rather than estimated directly because the measurements do not contain sufficient information to constrain Γ* independently (Gu et al. 2010; Walker and Ort 2015). Under these conditions, the selected Γ*(T) relationship becomes a structural assumption of the model rather than an experimentally constrained variable. Consequently, any mismatch between the prescribed Γ*(T) function and the physiological Γ* consistent with the measured A net–cc response must be compensated through adjustments in other fitted parameters.

The numerical analysis performed here demonstrated that alternative Γ*(T) formulations propagated strongly into fitted D L estimates (Figure 1b), whereas V cmax and J max remained comparatively robust across the temperature range examined (Table S1). This behaviour is mechanistically expected because D L acts as the principal compensatory parameter when Γ*(T) is imposed during fitting, whereas V cmax and J max are constrained more strongly by the broader structure of the A net–cc response.

These findings have important implications for photosynthesis modelling because FvCB parameterisations commonly rely on previously published Γ*(T) functions rather than species‐specific estimates (Bernacchi et al. 2001; Sharkey et al. 2007; Walker and Ort 2015; Zaragoza‐Castells et al. 2007). The present results demonstrate that uncertainties associated with Γ*(T) estimation propagate into D L, thereby directly influencing the interpretation of daytime respiratory carbon fluxes and photorespiratory metabolism in model simulations.
