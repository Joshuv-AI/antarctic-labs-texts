Goat and cow milk merits distinct nutritional and functional properties based on simulated gastrointestinal digestion of infant
Authors: Jiaxin Chen, Ruikai Zhu, Linjun Wu, Jian Kuang, Xiangyu Bian, Xiaoqiong Li, Weiwei Wang, Wenjie Kang, Shanbo Mu, Bryan Yanwen Liu, Ke Zhao, Jinjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe nutritional and functional properties of goat and cow milk in digestive tract of infant is unclear. Applying simulated infant gastrointestinal digestion method, this study aims to systematically compare digestibility, peptide profiling, and immunomodulatory role of goat and cow milk, and further characterize their role in regulating homeostasis of gut microbiota and metabolism. It's found that the degree of hydrolysis and number of detected peptides was higher in goat milk (GM), while the relative abundance of predicted allergic peptides was higher in cow milk (CM). In addition, the expression of IL-1β and/or IL-6 in colonic organoids was elevated after GM or CM digesta intervention. Furthermore, GM and CM showed distinct effects on the composition of gut microbiota and metabolite profiles in an in vitro hindgut fermentation system using infant fecal inocula. The results provide basis for future applications of GM and CM in infant feeding, and further validation is required.Keywords: Goat milk, Cow milk, Infant, Simulated gastrointestinal digestion, Peptides, Gut microbiota, Metabolites

Introduction

Milk is good source of protein for human. Although there is little difference regarding the main composition of different mammalian milk, the relative abundance of different proteins varied among animals, and the amino acid sequence and structure of proteins were different (Ma et al., 2019). Recently, non-bovine milk has attracted much attention due to their rich nutrition, antibacterial, immunity enhancement and potential anticancer ability (Bakry et al., 2021). Non-bovine milk refers as the milk from lactating livestock other than cow, such as goat milk (GM), yak milk, camel milk, buffalo milk, horse milk, donkey milk, etc., which has unique taste and nutritional value. Compared to cow milk (CM), GM has higher content of casein, horse, donkey and human milk has lower content of casein, while camel milk contains more α-lactalbumin and lacks of β-lactoglobulin, which is considered to be the main allergen in CM (Ho et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018; Shahriari-Farfani et al., 2019).

Among those non-bovine milk, GM exerts high production and nutritional values, and is known as “the king of milk” in the international nutrition community (Turck, 2013). The protein and mineral content (especially calcium and phosphorus) of GM are slightly higher than that of CM, and the vitamin A and B content are higher than that of CM. In addition, the average diameter of fat globules in GM is smaller than that in CM, while the specific surface areas (SSA) of GM are larger than those of CM. The larger the SSA, the more they come into contact with lipase in the gut, and thus are easier to be digested. This difference gives GM more advantages in digestion and absorption and delicacy of taste (Attaie & Richter, 2000). It's reported that the digestibility of GM in babies can reach to more than 94% (Yibin, 2012). At present, the GM and CM were the two major resources for infant formula production (Carlo Agostoni et al., 2012). GM has been suggested to show superior digestibility compared to CM for infants, probably due to differences in casein composition and milk fat globule size (Dhasmana et al., 2022; Attaie & Richter, 2000). However, most prior investigations have primarily concentrated on comparing the constituents among various types of milk, little was known about composition and function of their digesta, especially in infant (Bourlieu et al., 2014). Maathuis et al. (2017) reported that the kinetics of protein digestion of goat milk-based infant formula was more superior to that of the cow milk-based infant formula, and the mice colonized by healthy infant feces was applied to elucidate the different gut microbial and metabolic patterns between goat milk-based and cow milk-based infant formula (Chen et al., 2023). However, the complex composition of infant formula did affect the digestion and function of milk and pure GM and CM require further research. For the milk research, Hodgkinson et al. (2018) found that the peptide profiles from GM were distinct from CM after gastric digestion, while the intestinal digestion was not included and the detail information of the peptide profiling was not further investigated. In addition to provide nutrients, the peptides released from milk proteins also exerting multiple bioactive function, including antioxidant, antimicrobes, allergy, ACE inhibition, et al. (Borges et al., 2025).

Furthermore, the undigested components of breast milk in the small intestine also affected the homeostasis of microbiota in hindgut, which played close relationship with multiple aspects of health status of baby (Ito et al., 2025). It's reported that the dynamic interaction between the gut microbiota and the mother's body is crucial for the physiological development and disease resilience of infants (Nunez et al., 2025). Therefore, the homeostatis of gut microbiota is another key point in evaluating the health status of baby. The effects of different types of milk in shifting gut microbes had been investigated in vivo and in vitro (Song et al., 2024; Maximino et al., 2024), and it's well accepted that different milk did exert distinct regulatory roles. However, most of the studies applied mice model, which limited the precise interpretation of human. Simulated hindgut fermentation method provided an applicable way in investigating the gut microbiota of human using fecal samples (Xu et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2021).

Overall, applying simulated infant gastrointestinal digestion, the objective of this study was to carry out a systematic comparison of digestibility, peptide profiles, and immunomodulatory functions between GM and CM, then further characterize their regulatory effects on the homeostasis of gut microbiota and metabolism of infants. The findings of this study will offer a detailed nutritional and functional characterization of both GM and CM during digestion in the infant, which will pave the way for their precise application and health promotion in infant feeding in future.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Pepsin, trypsin, sodium taurocholate, sodium glycydesoxycholate, AEBSF, and DL-dithiothreitol were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., a company located in Shanghai, China. Phthalaldehyde and sodium tetraborate were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich, a brand of Merck KGaA located in Darmstadt, Germany. Analytical-grade NaCl, NaHCO3 and CaCl2 were obtained from Shanghai Lingfeng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Analytical-grade KCl was acquired from Wuxi Zhanwang Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Wuxi, China). Matrix Gel from Corning Inc. (Corning, NY, USA). Cell culture medium and DPBS from Stemcell Technologies Inc.(Vancouver, BC, Canada).

Sample preparation

Fresh GM and CM were collected from the milk tank of dairy farms of Yeeper Dairy Group Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China) and freeze-dried for further analysis. Fecal samples of 10 infants were collected from Ningbo Mingzhou Hospital (Ningbo, China), with the age ranging from 4 to 11 months (mean age: 6.7 ± 2.9 months; mean body wight: 8.9 ± 2.5 kg; 6 female, 4 male). All infants were exclusively formula-fed, had no history of antibiotic use in the 4 weeks prior to sampling, and had no diagnosed gastrointestinal or metabolic disorders. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating infants. The cultivation and treatment follows the method established previously in our lab (Pi et al., 2022; Liao et al., 2025), with the colonic organoids were supplied by the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University following the ethical standards of the clinical research committee.

The freeze-dried milk samples were diluted using sterile water to milk solutions containing 1.5 g/100 mL protein. The simulated infant gastric digestion was conducted following previous reported methods with modification (Liu et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023). Briefly, simulated gastric digestion was initiated by mixing 5 mL of the milk solutions with simulated gastric fluid (SGF) at a 63:37 (v/v) ratio. The SGF, which contained 268 U/mL pepsin, was used under conditions of pH 5.3 and 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, intestinal digestion was simulated by supplementing the gastric digesta with 3.2 mL of pre-warmed simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) containing bile salt (3.1 mM) and trypsin (16 U/mL). These mixtures were incubated under conditions of pH 6.6 and 37 °C for 2 h. Finally, the mixture of the digesta was heated at 95 °C for 5 min to stop the reaction. Then the gastrointestinal digestive fluid was centrifuged at 4 °C and 8000 × g for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C for detection of the degree of hyrolysis. It should be noted that this static, two-step digestion model does not fully replicate dynamic infant gastrointestinal conditions (e.g., gradual pH change, peristalsis, gastric emptying).

Detection of the degree of hydrolysis (DH)

The o-Phthaldehyde(OPA) method was applied in this study to determine the DH of milk proteins (Li et al., 1994). Briefly, 3 mL OPA reagent was added to 400 μL of the gastric and intestinal digesta derived from GM and CM solutions. This mixture was then left to stand at room temperature for 2 min, and the OD value of the mixture were immediately read at 340 nm. Peptidomics detection and analysis

The supernatant of the intestinal digestive solution that filtrated through 3 kDa ultrafiltration tubes were freeze-dried and subjected for peptidomics analysis employing LC-MS/MS method. The complete details have been provided in a previous study (Wu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). Briefly, after desalted, the same amount of samples were analyzed using a 25 cm × 75 μm, RP-C18 liquid chromatography Column, sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). The liquid phase was liquid A (0.1% formic acid) and liquid B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), and the flow rate was 250 nL/min for 60 min. After the mass charge ratio of peptides and peptide fragments was collected, the software MaxQuant 1.5.5.1 was used to search the milk protein database of corresponding species (Capra hircus and Bos taurus) for peptide identification and quantification. The peptides that were detected in all the three replication samples were regarded as detected peptides and used for further analysis. The detected peptides were then analyzed using BIOPEP (https:// biochemia.uwm.edu.pl/biopep/start_biopep.php). Then intensity of peptides was normalized by the total intensity of all the peptides in each sample. For allergenicity prediction, the amino acid sequences of identified peptides were submitted to the AllerTOP v2.0 server (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/) using the default k-nearest neighbor (kNN) algorithm with a threshold of 0.8. Peptides predicted as “allergen” or “probable allergen” were classified as predicted allergenic peptides, while the number and normalized abundance (based on MS intensity) of these peptides were compared between GM and CM groups.

Evaluation the immune response of colonic organoids

Following the previous reported method (Rahmani et al., 2019), the colonic organoids were rapidly thawed at 37 °C and washed with solution containing 1% BSA, and then re-suspended in the solution (matrix gel: complete culture medium = 1:1) and inoculated into 24-well plates. After incubated at 37 °C 30 min, 500 μL of DMEM medium containing 0.1% Y-27632 was added to each well for treatment of 24 h. Then the medium without Y-27632 was used for culture and intervention. The organoids were passaged at the ratio of 1:2 to 4 when reaching a diameter of 100–200 μm each. The treatment was conducted when the organoids completed budding and differentiation.

Supernatant of the gastrointestinal digestive fluid of GM and CM was diluted with HBSS at a volume ratio of 1:4 to obtain the sample working solution (Fotschki et al., 2015). Subsequently, the working solution of the digestive fluid of GM and CM were filter-sterilized using a 0.22 μm membrane. These sterile solutions were then added to the colonic organoid culture medium, achieving a final protein concentration of 0.25 mg/mL at pH 6.8. After 24 h intervention, the organoids from different groups were harvested and the total RNA was extracted using Novozyme RNAprep Pure kit from TransGen Biotech (Beijing, China). The relative expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10 and TNF-α genes were quantified via RT-qPCR and normalized by the internal reference gene GAPDH.

In vitro fermentation

Following the method of a previous study (Shiowatana et al., 2006), the gastrointestinal digesta of GM and CM was dialysed in the sodium bicarbonate solution at a 1:100 (v/v) ratio under ambient temperature conditions. After dialysis, the contents in the dialysis bag were collected, freeze-dried and stored at −20 °C for the subsequent fermentation test. Then the in vitro fermentation of different milk samples was carried out according to the method previously reported in our lab (Xu et al., 2024). Briefly, 10% (wt/vol) infant fecal suspension was firstly prepared (0.8 ± 0.02 g, n = 10), and then were injected into YCFA medium containing dialysed digest of GM and CM, with the blank YCFA medium was set as the control group. After 24 h fermentation, the total gas production was detected using barometer from Beijing Xinsite Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and the culture medium was preserved at −80 °C for subsequent analyses, including 16S rRNA sequencing and non-targeted metabolomics.

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

Total DNA was first isolated from the fermented samples described above. Subsequently, the V3-V4 regions of the extracted DNA were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using universal primers targeting bacteria, and DNA libraries were constructed and sequenced after purification (Lc-Bio Technologies, Hangzhou, China). Following sequencing, the raw reads (RawData) were merged based on overlapping regions. Subsequent quality filtering and chimera elimination yielded a set of high-quality clean reads (CleanData). DivisiveAmplicon Denoising Algorithm (DADA2) was used to denoise by “Dereplication” (equivalent to clustering with 100% similarity), and then the representative sequence with single base accuracy was obtained. Then, the Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) approach was employed to generate ASV feature table along with the corresponding feature sequences. Further analyses were then conducted, including diversity assessment, taxonomic annotation, and differential abundance testing.

Non-targeted metabolomics sequencing and analysis

The samples were firstly extracted using methanol and centrifuged for 10 min at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C (n = 10 in each group), and then supernatant was concentrated and dried. After dissolved in 150 μL 80% methanol containing L-2-Chlorophenylalanine (4 μg/mL), the samples were filtered by 0.22 μm membrane and applied for further analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling 10 μL of each sample, and were injected every 10 tests to monitor the stability of the analysis.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was employed for non-targeted metabolomics analysis. Briefly, 2 μL samples were injected into a Vanquish Ultra High Performance liquid phase system (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), coupled with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) from Waters (Milford, MA, USA). The flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and the column temperature of 40 °C were employed. The mobile phase used in the positive ionization mode contained 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B2) and 0.1% formic acid in water (A2), with the gradient elution conditions of: 0–1 min at 8% B2, 1–8 min from 8% to 98% B2, 8–10 min at 98% B2, 10–10.1 min from 98% to 8% B2, and 10.1–12 min at 8% B2. In the negative ion mode, acetonitrile (B3) and 5 mM ammonium formate (A3) served as the mobile phase, with a gradient of: 0–1 min at 8% B3, 1–8 min from 8% to 98% B3, 8–10 min at 98% B3, 10–10.1 min from 98% to 8% B3, and 10.1–12 min at 8% B3.

Data were collected respectively in positive and negative ionization modes using a Thermo Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) fitted with an electrospray ion source (ESI). The parameters were as follow: ion spray voltage +3.50 kV/−2.50 kV, sheath gas 40 arb, the auxiliary gas 10 arb, capillary temperature 325 °C, and Full-scan MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 over an m/z range of 100–1,000. Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was employed for MS/MS fragmentation, with a collision energy of 30 eV and a resolution of 17,500. The three most intense ions were selected for fragmentation prior to signal acquisition. Dynamic exclusion was applied to discard redundant MS/MS information. For data processing, raw mass spectrometry files were converted into mzXML format using the MSConvert tool within the Proteowizard software package (v3.0.8789). Peak detection, filtering, and alignment were subsequently performed using the XCMS (v3.12.0) software package, with parameters configured as follows: bw = 2, ppm = 15, peakwidth = c(5, 30), mzwid = 0.015, mzdiff = 0.01, and method = “centWave”. Metabolites identification was performed using public database, including HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), massbank (https://massbank.eu/MassBank/), LipidMaps ((https://www.lipidmaps.org/), mzcloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Gnomes (KEGG, https://www.kegg.jp/). Missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbor (kNN) algorithm (k = 10). Batch correction was performed using LOESS normalization based on QC samples. A quantitative list of features was thereby generated for bioinformatics analysis (Nomi Metabolism, Hangzhou, China). The MetaboAnalyst (www.metaboanalyst.ca) was applied for KEGG pathway analysis. The R ropls (v1.22.0) package was applied for multivariate statistic analysis, with FDR < 0.05 and VIP > 1 was considered as significant difference.

Statistics and analysis

All statistical evaluations were carried out using GraphPad Prism V10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Comparisons of data from each experimental group were conducted via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's post hoc test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. For genus-level intestinal microbial analysis, the Kruskal-Wallis test coupled with LEfSe (absolute LDA score threshold > 3) was applied to identify taxa with significant abundance differences.

Results

Digestibility of goat and cow milk

As shown in Fig. 1-A, the freeze-dried GM and CM dissolved in water exerted good water solubility and formed uniform and granular emulsions. After simulated gastric digestion, both GM and CM samples formed obvious curd particles, and the curd particles from GM appeared smaller than those from CM based on visual inspection. No obvious curd granules were observed in both GM and CM after simulated small intestinal digestion. The limitation of the study is lack of the quantitative particle size measurement. In addition, the DH of GM was significantly higher than that of CM underwent simulating infant gastrointestinal digestion (p < 0.01, Fig. 1-B).

Peptide profiling of GM and CM after intestinal digestion

After simulated intestinal digestion, a total of 215 and 206 detected small peptides were observed in the digesta of GM and CM, respectively, with 41 peptides were shared (Fig. 1-C, Table S1 and S2). Moreover, the small peptides generated from the digestive hydrolysis of goat and cow milk were primarily in the range of 5 to 13 amino acid residues (73.02% in GM and 79.13% in CM) (Fig. 1-D). The peptides with lengths of 8 to 23 in GM after digestion are more than those in cow milk, while the peptides with lengths of 3, 5, 6, and 7 are fewer than those in cow milk (Fig. 1-D). Furthermore, the functions of the detected active peptides were most enriched in ACE inhibitors, followed by antioxidative and antibacterial peptides, with more types of functions were enriched in CM than that in GM (Fig. 1-E). Immunomodulating peptides were identified in both GM (NPWDQVKR) and CM (FFSDK, AMKPWIQPK, RPKHPIKHQGLPQEVLNENLLRF, LLYQEPVLGPVRGPFPIIV, and NPWDQVKR) (Fig. 1-E). In addition, it was found that the number of sensitizing small peptides observed in GM was higher than that in CM, but the abundance of these predicted sensitizing peptide were lower than that in CM (Fig. 1-F, Table S3). It should be noted that experimental validation of allergenicity require further research in future.

Expression of inflammatory factors treated with diegesta of goat and cow milk

As shown in Fig. 2-A, the epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM) and the mucin 2 (MUC-2) were highly expressed in the differentiated colonic organoids. The morphology and size were in line with expectations, and a closed intestinal-like cavity structure was formed, indicating that the colonic organoid model was successfully constructed and could be used for subsequent experiments. Relative to the control group (CON), treatments of GM or CM digesta resulted in a marked upregulation of the gene expression of pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β (p < 0.05). However, a significant elevation in IL-6 expression was observed exclusively following CM intervention (p < 0.05, Fig. 2-B). On the other hand, GM or CM digesta intervention did not affect the expression of IL-10 and TNF-α (p > 0.05).

Undigested goat and cow milk shift the gut microbiota of infant

Relative to the CON group, supplementation of undigested GM significantly reduced the total intestinal gas production of infants (p < 0.05, Fig. 3-A). As for the microbiota, the Chao1 index was significantly lower in the GM group than in CON (p < 0.05), while either GM or CM intervention did not affect the Shannon and Simpson index (p > 0.05, Fig. 3-B). Furthermore, the profiling of microbes among the three groups showed clear clusters, and the interpretability of PCo1 and PCo2 is 17.46% and 8.99%, respectively (Fig. 3-C).

At the phylum level, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Verrucomicrobiota were the predominant bacterial phyla in the fermentation products derived from GM and CM (Fig. 3-D). Compared to the CON group, supplementation of undigested GM significantly decreased the relative abundance of Desulfobacterota and Cyanobacteria (FDR < 0.05), whereas undigested CM markedly reduced the relative abundance of Desulfobacterota alone (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 3-D). At the genus level, taxa with a detectable relative abundance in more than 50% of samples in at least one of the three groups were defined as detected genera, yielding a total of 98 detected genera in this study. Compared to the CON group, the top 30 genera exhibiting the largest absolute fold changes in the GM or CM group were shown in Fig. 3-E.

Furthermore, 25 and 29 differentially abundant genera were identified in the CON vs. GM and CON vs. CM comparisons, respectively (FDR < 0.05), with 22 genera overlapping (Fig. 4-A). As shown in Fig. 4-B, compared to the CON group, the relative abundance of Ligilactobacillus was significantly enriched following GM and CM intervention. Meanwhile, the abundance of Lacticaseibacillus was increased in GM, while that of Salinivibrio and Acinetobacter was elevated in CM group. Conversely, the remaining 28 genera were depressed or remained unchanged after treatment with undigested GM or CM (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 4-B). Among those 32 differentially abundant genera, 10 exhibited distinct alteration patterns compared to the CON group, including Lacticaseibacillus, Salinivibrio, Acinetobacter, Sphingobium, Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Mitochondria_unclassified, UCG-005, Ruminococcaceae_unclassified, and Desulfovibrio (Fig. 4-B). Additionally, LEfSe analysis identified Ligilactobacillus as the characteristic biomarker for both GM and CM groups (Fig. 4-C, 4-D).

The relative abundance of the top 10 differential abundant metabolites in the CON vs. GM and CON vs. CM comparison were illustrated in Fig. 5-D. Specifically, compared to the CON group, GM or CM intervention elevated and depressed the relative abundance of 11 and 3 metabolites, respectively. Furthermore, distinct metabolic profiles and 15 differential abundant metabolites were observed between GM and CM groups (Fig. S1).

Discussion

Milk protein is mainly composed of casein and whey protein, with casein being the principal protein in fresh milk, accounting for about 80% of the total protein, while whey protein accounts for about 20% of the total protein. Under the action of gastric acid, milk protein undergoes coagulation to form curds. Then, under the action of pepsin, it undergoes hydrolysis and enters the small intestine for further digestion and absorption. The size of curd particles is closely related to the digestion and absorption of milk. Our results suggest that GM is more readily digested than CM under the simulated infant conditions. The DH is a parameter reflecting the degree to which a protein is hydrolyzed, that is, the proportion of peptide bond breaks in the protein. In addition, the DH of GM is significantly higher than that of CM. Together with the the characteristics of digestive granules, the results above indicates that GM merits the characteristic of being more easily digested than CM in the in vitro simulated upper digestive tract of infants. The results was consistent with previous gastric digestion of CM and GM, which revealed that caseins from GM tended to be more efficiently digested compared to caseins from CM (Hodgkinson et al., 2018). Similarly, it's reported that the digestibility of GM protein was closer to that of breast milk than cow milk is, with the bioavailability of nitrogen was higher for GM than for CM in the digestion process of first hour, and true ileal protein digestibility was 78.3% ± 3.7% (goat) and 73.4% ± 2.7% (cow) (Maathuis et al., 2017). However, the static, two-step digestion model employed does not fully replicate the dynamic conditions of the infant gastrointestinal tract, including gradual acidification, peristalsis, gastric emptying, or bile salt flux. Considering the limitation mentioned above, our findings therefore provide a preliminary in vitro data requiring validation in more physiological models.

The small peptides produced after the hydrolysis of GM and CM are mainly concentrated in the range of 5 to 13 amino acid residues, suggesting that the digestion of their digestible proteins is relatively high. Since this experiment mainly focused on small peptides, peptides or proteins with a length greater than 28 amino acid residues were not included in the scope of this study. We acknowledge that larger polypeptides (> 3 kDa), including major milk allergens such as αs1 casein and β -lactoglobulin, may retain allergenic epitopes and survive gastrointestinal digestion (Roncada et al., 2002; Jung et al., 2017), while this limitation should be taken into consideration especially for the allergenic analysis. Interestingly, one immunomodulatory peptide was commonly observed in the digesta of GM and CM, NPWDQVKR. Considering the important role of immune function for infant, the precise function of this peptides require further research. In addition, it's found that the number of predicted allergic small peptides observed in GM group was higher than that in CM, while the abundance (MS intensity) of these predicted species specific allergenic peptides was lower in GM than that in CM. It should be noted that the interpretation is speculative without functional validation (e.g., IgE binding, basophil activation, or mast cell degranulation assays). However, it is consistent with previous research that CM αS1 casein had higher allergenicity than GM αS1 casein via increasing s-IgE and Th2 cell-related inflammatory factors (Zhang et al., 2022).

The intestine is not only an important site for the digestion and absorption of nutrients, but also an important immune organ. The innate immunity of the intestine is tightly associated with the mucosal defense mechanisms of the human body (Yap & Mariño, 2018). Allergy is essentially an immune response (Ado & Gushchin, 1980). Therefore, when studying dairy product allergies, the intestine is a very important research object. Therefore, in this project, a human colon organoid model was utilized, and qPCR was conducted using inflammatory factor primers to analyze and compare the sensitization effects of goat milk and cow milk and investigate their possible mechanisms. Regarding immune activation, it's observed that digesta from both GM and CM upregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β in adult-derived colonic organoids, while digesta from CM also upregulated pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6. It is important to clarify that induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines does not equate to allergenicity, which involves specific IgE and Th2 responses. Our data instead demonstrate differential pro-inflammatory potential. Although obvious differences exist between infant and adult intestine regarding the barrier function, immune maturation, and receptor expression, et al., the organoid is a preferential three-dimensional model for functional study of gut (Wu et al., 2020). Of course, the limitation of the current study should not be neglected, and future studies should employ infant-derived intestinal models and measure protein-level cytokine secretion.

Within the anaerobic environment of the intestine, numerous gases participate in various biological activities, including oxygen, hydrogen, carbon monoxide, ammonia, methane, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide and nitrogen. The composition and dynamic changes of these intestinal gases serve as indicators of the intestinal environment, enabling the monitoring of local metabolic alterations and the assessment of digestive system disease progression (Tozzi & Minella, 2024). Compared with CON group, supplementation of GM digesta significantly reduced total gas production in the closed in vitro fermentation system. However, the gap between in vitro model and clinic symptoms enforce the further investigation via animal models or clinical trials.

In addition, GM and CM intervention altered the community composition of the infant fecal microbiota in the in vitro fermentation system. At the phylum level, although no significant difference was observe among the three group, supplementation of GM or CM numerically elevated the abundance of Actinobacteriota and decreased that of Verrucomicrobiota (Fig. 3-D). Similar results had been reported that goat and cow milk-based infant formula significantly increased the Actinobacteria and decreased Verrucomicrobiota compared with human milk (Chen et al., 2023). These two phyla mentioned above have been implicated in intestinal barrier regulation and immune modulation (Liu et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). Notably, the relative abundance of Desulfobacterota was significantly lower in both GM and CM group than that in CON group (Fig. 3-D). Few study concerned Desulfobacterota regarding the gut microbiome driven by different types of milk, but it's found that camel milk could significantly reduce relative abundance of Desulfovibrio (54.55%) in high fat diet feeding mice (Wang, Zhang, et al., 2018). Considering the health beneficial role of camel milk, it's indicated that the GM and CM might exert microbial regulatory role via Desulfobacterota.

At the genus level, supplementation of GM and CM tended to increase the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium, and significant increased that of Ligilactobacillus. Bifidobacterium and Ligilactobacillus are generally beneficial to infant intestinal health (Mills et al., 2023; Slattery et al., 2019). Similar results had been reported in mice feeding goat or cow milk (Wang et al., 2018). However, recent study showed that goat milk-based infant formula could selectively increase the relative abundance of Blautia, Roseburia, Alistites and Muribaculum in (Chen et al., 2023). The discrepancy of the results might come from the difference of infant donor and the in vivo vs. in vitro study. On the other hand, the relative abundance of Streptococcus and Colidextribacter associated with colonic inflammation or disease (Pompilio et al., 2019; Raygoza Garay et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2021), were decreased via GM or/and CM intervention. In addition, a marked increase of Lacticaseibacillus was observed exclusively in the GM group, which contributed to the microbial habitation, synthesis of bacteriocins, and elevation of the content of beneficial bacteria in the gut (Bujdoš et al., 2026; Dillen et al., 2026). Overall, the microbial results above indicate the health promotion potential of GM and CM in shifting gut microbiota. However, it must be emphasized that the microbial analysis is based on a static in vitro fermentation system inoculated with infant feces, and varied with the in vivo dynamic status.

It's well known that the food ingested is metabolized by the intestinal microbiota to generate small molecules that play important physiological roles. To further explore the potential nutritional role of GM and GM, the metabolites of the fermented liquid were analyzed. Substantial shifts of metabolic profiling were observed among CON, GM, and CM groups (Fig. 5). The most enriched pathways of differential metabolites in GM vs. CON and CM vs. CON was “Phenylpropapnoid biosynthesis”, followed by “Tryptophan metabolism” in GM vs. CON and “ABC transporters” in CM vs. CON. Previous study identified 25 phenolic metabolites in goat and cow milk via untargeted metabolomics, revealing a diverse array of phenolic metabolites in milk, and goat's milk contained higher concentrations of these metabolites than cow's milk (Agulló et al., 2024). It's well accepted that tryptophan and its regulatory pathway play vital roles in inflammatory responses (Zelante et al., 2013), and the enrichment of Tryptophan metabolism after GM intervention suggests potential distinct inflammatory response of GM and GM.

Several compounds exhibited significant abundance differences between GM and CM groups. Adenosine monophosphate (AMP), a central metabolite in energy and nucleotide metabolism, was significantly lower in the CM group compared with GM. AMP is known to activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a master regulator of cellular energy homeostasis, and has been linked to metabolic disorders (Kim & Park, 2016). The lower AMP level in the CM group might imply differential energy substrate utilization by the gut microbiota, but this interpretation remains speculative without concurrent measurements of ATP/ADP ratios or flux analysis. Other differential metabolites, including Alditol 6-phosphate and (3R)-Sophorol, have been occasionally reported in bacterial metabolism or plant secondary metabolism (Maloney et al., 1984; Volk et al., 2009), but their roles in the infant gut ecosystem are largely unknown and require further characterization.

Correlation analysis between differentially abundant microbial genera and differential metabolites in the CON versus GM/CM comparisons revealed several notable associations (Fig. 6-A, B). For instance, 2-Amino-3-carboxymuconic acid semialdehyde and Quinolinic acid, which are downstream metabolites of tryptophan kynurenin pathway (Chen et al., 2025), exhibited positive correlations with Lacticaseibacillus and negative correlations with Ruminococcaceae_unclassified and Mitochondria_unclassified. This suggests an enhanced tryptophan catabolism in the GM group. Regarding the CM intervention, the elevation of melatonin and its associated microbes may underpin the health-promoting effects of CM in infant. This is supported by the fact that the gut microbiota can synthesize melatonin from tryptophan, and melatonin exerts beneficial effects on gut barrier function and microbial homeostasis (Ahmadi et al., 2024; Zimmermann et al., 2024). Furthermore, L-Aspartic acid, which possesses multiple biological activities such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects (Zhang et al., 2026), was significantly elevated following CM intervention. The relative abundance of L-Aspartic acid was positive correlated with Salinivibrio and Acinetobacter, but negatively correlated with Lactobacillus and UCG-005. Overall, both GM and CM interventions strengthened tryptopan metabolism, whereas the L-Aspartic acid metabolism was specifically upregulated in the CM group. The distinct shift in differential genera might contribute to the divergent role of GM and CM in regulating microbial metabolism of infant hindgut in vitro. However, it must be emphasized that these correlation analyses are purely descriptive and do not imply causal or mechanistic relationships. The observed associations may be confounded by numerous factors inherent to the in vitro fecal fermentation system, and they cannot be extrapolated to in vivo physiological conditions. Future studies employing targeted metabolomics, flux analysis, and functional gene profiling are necessary to disentangle the complex microbe-metabolite interactions suggested by our data.

In summary, this study provides an in-depth in vitro characterization of the digestibility, peptide profiles, immunomodulatory potential, and microbiota-modulating effects of GM and CM under simulated infant conditions. GM exhibited higher digestibility and a lower abundance of predicted allergenic small peptides, while CM induced a more pronounced pro-inflammatory response in adult colonic organoids. Both milks differentially modulated gut microbiota composition and metabolite profiles in an infant fecal fermentation system. However, these findings are subject to major limitations, including the use of static digestion, adult organoids, and in silico predictions, as well as the exclusion of > 3 kDa peptides. Future studies should employ dynamic infant digestion models, infant-derived intestinal models, functional allergenicity assays, and ultimately animal studies and clinical trials to validate the direct relationship of these observations.
