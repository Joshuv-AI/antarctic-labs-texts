Oxidative stability as a guiding principle for durable ionomer cations in pure-water electrolysis
Authors: Yiqi Jin, Bo Wu, Wenzheng Li, Chunlei Wang, Li Fan, Lixian Wen, Jiawei Li, James Wang, Jinggui Lei, Jiantao Fan, Hui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cation degradation in ionomers critically limits the durability of pure-water-fed anion exchange membrane water electrolysis, particularly at the anode where high overpotentials and near-neutral pH induce harsh oxidative stress. However, prevailing cation design remains rooted in alkali-stability frameworks, which fall short under these oxidative conditions. Here, a multiscale approach combining molecular degradation and operando diagnostics identifies electrochemical oxidation as the dominant anodic cation degradation pathway in anion exchange membrane water electrolysis, prompting a shift toward oxidation-resistant cation design. Electrolyzers incorporating oxidation-tolerant trimethylammonium-functionalized ionomers achieve 360-hour stability at 1 A cm⁻2, doubling the durability of alkali-stable N,N-dimethylpiperidinium-based ionomers. Mechanistic studies under simulated anode conditions reveal electrochemical oxidative degradation pathways of cations, establishing a molecular-level evaluation framework. Operando diagnostics with reference-integrated electrolyzers reveal that cation oxidation elevates ohmic and ionic transport resistances. This multiscale study directly traces device failure to cation oxidation, redefining cation design around oxidative stability to enhance durability in pure-water-fed anion exchange membrane water electrolysis.

Introduction

The development of sustainable hydrogen technologies demands water electrolysis systems that combine high efficiency with long-term durability1,2. KOH-fed anion exchange membrane water electrolysis (AEMWE) can operate stably for over 10,000 hours, enabled by the KOH-induced formation of highly conductive hydroxide ion (OH⁻) transport pathways3,4. However, the corrosive nature of KOH, along with carbonate precipitation, complicates system engineering and increases operational cost1,5. In contrast, pure-water-fed AEMWE avoids recirculated alkaline solutions, offering benefits in terms of system simplicity, integration, and cost1. Yet, under near-neutral pH and high anodic overpotentials, a pure-water system suffers from severe stability issues3–7, often operating for 10–100 times shorter durations than KOH-fed AEMWE (Fig. 1). In the absence of the supporting electrolyte, OH⁻ transport at the anion exchange membrane (AEM)/catalyst interface depends almost exclusively on the structural integrity and functionality of the anion exchange ionomer (AEI), especially the stability of its cationic groups8. Once the AEI cations degrade, OH⁻ conduction declines, reaction interfaces deteriorate, and electrolyzer performance rapidly drops9. The structural optimization of AEI cations in the anode has largely followed alkaline stability principles that are already well established in alkaline systems such as AEM fuel cells and KOH-fed electrolysis. Representative strategies include sterically protected imidazolium frameworks developed by Steven Holdcroft’s group10, and our collaborative work with Young Moo Lee group11, where γ-substitution with electron-donating groups improved the alkaline stability of N-heterocyclic ammonium cations. However, the feasibility of alkali-stable ionomers in the anode catalyst layer (CL) under pure-water-fed AEMWE remains unclear. Surveying commercial polyelectrolytes tethered with trimethylammonium (TMA), N,N-dimethylpiperidinium (DMP), or imidazolium cations reveals markedly different durability trends under KOH and pure-water conditions (Fig. 1). AEMWE incorporating DMP- or imidazolium-functionalized polyelectrolytes generally demonstrate competitive durability in alkaline media but suffer from substantial performance degradation in pure-water operation. In contrast, although TMA exhibits relatively lower alkaline stability, electrolyzers employing TMA-based anode AEIs demonstrate a more pronounced durability trend under pure-water operation. This trend raises a key question: do cation design principles optimized for alkaline environments apply to pure-water electrolysis? The answer likely lies in the fundamentally distinct operational environments: KOH-fed systems operate under high pH and moderate potentials, whereas anode AEIs in pure-water-fed AEMWE experience near-neutral pH, coupled with substantially higher potentials.

These unique operating conditions in pure-water AEMWE introduce a different degradation stress. Although the near-neutral environment in pure-water AEMWE alleviates alkaline degradation1,12,13, it does not enhance ionomer stability. On the contrary, limited OH⁻ transport raises ohmic resistance14,15, while suppressed catalytic activity under neutral conditions increases kinetic overpotentials16,17. The resulting high anodic voltage may not only directly oxidize cations18, but may also generate hydroxyl (HO˙)19 and hydroperoxyl (HO2˙) radicals20,21, which might accelerate cation oxidation through multiple pathways22,23. Such degradation impairs OH⁻ conduction and collapses of the triple-phase boundary, thereby compromising electrocatalysis and mass (gas and water) diffusion, which collectively increase operating voltage22,24. This vicious cycle between cation electrochemical oxidation and increased voltage ultimately accelerates device performance drop.

Despite its importance, systematic research on the electrochemical oxidative stability of cationic groups remains limited. While several radical-mediated degradation pathways have been proposed25–28, most existing studies focus on individual cation and lack generalizable structure–stability relationships. In addition, most degradation assessments rarely replicate the electrochemical conditions present in operational AEMWE9,22,29,30. This limited understanding of degradation under practical AEMWE conditions leaves two key issues unresolved: how different cation structures respond to electrochemical stresses in pure water AEMWE, and how their degradation directly affects long-term device performance.

To systematically explore the electrochemical oxidative stability, this study combines polymer-level analysis, molecular mechanistic insights, and operando validation to construct a structure–stability framework for designing durable cations. We demonstrate that electrochemical oxidative resistance, rather than alkaline tolerance, dominates the long-term durability of anode ionomers under pure-water electrolysis. Moreover, we provide a preliminary molecular-level platform for systematically assessing the electrochemical robustness of specific cations and revealing their oxidative degradation mechanisms. Operando measurements using reference-integrated electrolyzers further link cation-degradation behavior to device-scale voltage loss. Collectively, this work refines cation design principles for pure-water AEMWE and provides a practical strategy for advancing oxidation-resistant ionomer materials.

Results and discussion

Cation-dependent trade-offs between oxidative and alkaline stability

Two representative series of polyaromatics functionalized with TMA and DMP (Fig. 2a), respectively, as anode ionomers were synthesized through a two-step process involving superacid-catalyzed polymerization followed by quaternization. Details of the synthesis procedure and structural confirmation are provided in Supplementary Figs. S1–S2. Specifically, each series utilized an identical polymer backbone, ensuring that the TMA and DMP cations were positioned within equivalent chemical environments. This strategic design minimized backbone-related interference and enhanced the comparability of cation intrinsic oxidative stability. To evaluate their resistance to oxidative stress in anode CLs, the oxidative stability of these polyelectrolytes was quantitatively assessed under Fenton’s conditions. TMA-functionalized polyelectrolytes exhibited higher conductivity retention (96.0% for QBPMTP-TMA and 88.6% for QPTP-TMA) than DMP-substituted ones (90.1% for QBPMTP-DMP and 69.2% for QPTP-DMP), indicating TMA’s enhanced resistance to radical-induced oxidative degradation. (Fig. 2b) Consistently, mass retention in Fig. 2b followed the same trend. In contrast, DMP-based polyelectrolytes retained higher conductivity after alkaline treatment (Fig. 2c), consistent with DMP intrinsic alkaline stability31. Oxidative degradation of ionomers driven by cations in pure-water AEMWE

Device-level impact of cation stability was assessed by incorporating TMA- and DMP-functionalized polyelectrolytes into anode membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs) operated under pure-water conditions (Fig. 3a). As shown in Fig. 3b, the MEA with QBPMTP-TMA achieved 0.9 A cm⁻2 at 1.8 V, which is 1.6 times the current density of the QPTP-DMP-based MEA under the same conditions. A pronounced contrast in operational durability was observed (Fig. 3c): under pure-water electrolysis at 1 A cm⁻2, TMA-MEA maintained their functionality beyond 360 hours, whereas DMP-based systems prematurely failed within 150 hours. Notably, this durability significantly exceeded that of an electrolyzer utilizing commercial DMP-functionalized PiperION® ionomers, which exhibited durability of approximately 100 hours under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. S5). Post-mortem X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed significantly higher retention of quaternary ammonium (QA) groups in TMA-based electrodes (Fig. 3d), corroborating its greater chemical stability. This aligns with the comparable oxidative resistance of TMA-functionalized polyelectrolytes observed under Fenton’s condition (Fig. 2b), linking the over two-fold disparity in device lifetime directly to cation-dependent oxidative stability. Given that the near-neutral anode environment under pure-water conditions suppresses alkaline degradation pathways, these findings collectively identify electrochemical oxidation of QAs as the dominant cation degradation mechanism, limiting AEI's durability in pure-water-fed AEMWE. This insight motivated a systematic, molecular-level investigation into the electrochemical oxidation of QA cations under AEMWE-relevant conditions.

Electrochemical oxidative stability of quaternary ammoniums

In pure-water-fed AEMWE, the electrochemical oxidative stress at the anode drives cation degradation through electron-transfer and radical-mediated pathways. To probe the structure–stability relationship of QAs under these oxidative conditions, we selected five representative QA model compounds—TMAH, DMPH, BTMAH, ASUH, and HTMAH—for electrochemical testing (Fig. 4). Each compound was exposed to a constant potential of 2.0 V vs. Hg/HgO in aqueous solution (Supplementary Fig. S6), simulating the oxidative environment at the AEMWE anode. Differential pulse voltammetry (Supplementary Fig. S7) confirmed that this applied potential exceeded the oxidation onset of all tested QAs. Concurrently, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy detected the formation of HO˙ radicals (Supplementary Fig. S8), supporting the presence of a radical-mediated degradation pathway. 1H NMR spectroscopy and mass spectrometry (Supplementary Figs. S11–S12) revealed characteristic oxidative degradation products, including lower-order amines (2.20–2.25 ppm in Supplementary Fig. S11) and formaldehyde (8.4 ppm in Supplementary Fig. S11). Mechanistically, HO˙ radicals initiate hydrogen abstraction, forming carbon-centered radicals that lead to demethylation and N–C bond cleavage34,35. In the case of TMAH, degradation proceeds via ˙CH2N⁺(CH3)3 formation, its conversion to (CH3)3N⁺˙, and then eventual release of formaldehyde and trimethylamine (Supplementary Fig. S13)36–40.

TMAH exhibited the greatest oxidative stability among the tested cations (Fig. 4), attributed to its lower rate constant for HO˙-mediated degradation34. In addition to radical oxidation, cations with extended alkyl chains or cyclic substituents (e.g., HTMAH, ASUH, DMPH) underwent direct electrochemical oxidation via electron loss. Such substituents stabilize intermediate carbocations through hyperconjugation, thereby promoting electrochemical oxidation18. Aromatic oxidation was observed for BTMAH, leading to phenol and quinone-type products (Supplementary Fig. S11; proposed pathway in Supplementary Fig. S14)41,42. Crucially, these results reveal a design paradox (Fig. 4): cations like DMPH, prized for their alkaline resilience11,31, exhibited pronounced oxidative vulnerability. This trade-off underscores the need to design ionomers with cations resistant to electrochemical oxidative degradation, as conventional alkali-focused strategies may fail under oxidative AEMWE conditions.

From molecular-level oxidation to AEMWE performance decay

Although molecular-level insights have clarified QA group oxidation behavior (Fig. 4), their translation to device-level failure pathways remains fully elucidated. Therefore, we incorporated a Pt/Ir wire reference electrode (Pt/Ir-WRE) into the MEA, enabling time-resolved tracking of both ohmic and polarization resistance (Rp) across the electrodes (Fig. 3a)43. During operation, operando impedance tracking revealed a sharp increase in anode resistance, while the impedance in the cathode remained relatively stable, pinpointing the anode as the dominant site of failure (Fig. 5a, b, d, e). Consistently, within the dual-membrane electrolyzer, the decline in ionic conductivity was found to be much more pronounced for the anode-facing membrane than for the cathode-facing one (Supplementary Fig. S15). Decoupled electrochemical resistance analysis (Fig. 5g) attributed the majority of this increase to intensified ionic transport resistance (Rionic), with a minor contribution from elevated ohmic resistance—both closely linked to the ionomer’s functional integrity, particularly its cation. The high oxidative stability of TMA-functionalized ionomers led to a smaller anode HFR increase (21.6%) than that of DMP-based systems (58.8%) (Supplementary Table S5). The rising polarization resistance, as revealed by the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis (Fig. 5c and f), stemmed from impaired OH⁻ transport in CLs44,45. 1H NMR analysis (Supplementary Fig. S16) of post-mortem ionomers revealed electrochemical oxidation of both cationic groups and the polyaromatic backbone. Cation degradation diminishes charge density, weakening electrostatic repulsion and facilitating AEI aggregation, as evidenced by morphological changes in Fig. 5a and d (insets). Meanwhile, oxidized polyaromatic fragments exhibit stronger catalytic surface adsorption than their pristine counterparts, leading to active site poisoning and accelerated electrode decay17,42,46,47. Ionomer electrochemical oxidation induced CL interface degradation, as evidenced by post-operation scanning electron microscopy, which revealed pronounced ionomer agglomeration and pore occlusion in the CLs (Fig. 5a and d, insets). Consistently, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy analysis (Supplementary Fig. S17) indicated the degradation of the ionomer. CL interface deterioration accounted for the increased overpotential in devices. These findings highlight a coherent multiscale degradation pathway: molecular-level oxidative instability of the cation triggers mesoscale morphological failure and culminates in macroscopic performance decay.

This work identifies electrochemical oxidative stability of cationic groups as a key factor limiting anode ionomer durability in pure-water-fed AEMWE. The oxidation-resistant TMA-functionalized ionomer enables stable operation of electrolyzers for over 360 hours at 1 A cm⁻2 under pure-water conditions, substantially outperforming alkali-stable ionomers. Systematic molecular-level studies elucidate the structure–stability relationships of QA cations, guiding functional group selection for oxidation-resistant ionomers. Moreover, reference-integrated electrolyzers enabled direct correlation of ionomer cation degradation with device electrochemical performance, demonstrating that oxidation-resistant groups effectively mitigate both ohmic losses and ionic transport resistance within the catalyst layer. By bridging the molecular degradation pathways, ionomer stability, and device failure modes, this multiscale study establishes electrochemical oxidative stability as a pivotal yet underappreciated design criterion for durable, high-performance ionomers in pure-water-fed AEMWE.

Methods

Materials

High-purity TMAH (0.1 mol L⁻1), BTMAH (40 wt% in H2O), 1,5-dibromopentane (98%), N-methylpiperidine (97%), N,N-dimethylpiperidinium chloride (95%), biphenyl (99%), hexyltrimethylammonium bromide (98%), p-terphenyl (99.5%), m-terphenyl (99.5%), trifluoromethanesulfonic acid (TFSA, 98%), 7-bromo-1,1,1-trifluoro-2-heptanone (95%), trimethylamine solution (30 wt% in H2O), dichloromethane (DCM, 99.5%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.7%), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 98%), potassium hydroxide (KOH, 95%), sodium 3-(trimethylsilyl)propane-1-sulfonate (98%), and diethyl ether (99%) were obtained from Macklin. 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (97%), tetrahydrofuran (THF, 99.5%), methanol (99.5%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 95%), ethanol (99.5%), n-propyl alcohol (99.5%), and Amberlite® IRA-900 anion exchange resin were purchased from Aladdin. High-purity piperidine was acquired from Chengdu Kelong Chemical Co., Ltd. Deuterated solvents, including DMSO-d6 (99.9%-D), CD3OD-d4 (99.8%-D), and D2O (99.9%-D), were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of polyaromatic precursors

A typical synthesis procedure of BPMTP is as follows: biphenyl (0.9 g, 6 mmol) and m-terphenyl (1.4 g, 6 mmol) were dissolved in DCM (15 mL) in a flask. The mixture was cooled to 0 °C in an ice bath, after which 7-bromo-1,1,1-trifluoroheptan-2-one (4.2 g, 16.8 mmol) and TFSA (15.1 g, 100.8 mmol) were added dropwise sequentially. The reaction was stirred for 5–6 h, during which a significant increase in viscosity indicated polymerization. The resulting mixture was precipitated into methanol, yielding a white fibrous polymer. The product was washed with deionized water until neutral and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h. PTP was synthesized following the BPMTP procedure with modifications to the monomer composition. p-terphenyl (2.8 g, 12.0 mmol) was used as the sole aromatic monomer, maintaining identical stoichiometric ratios for 7-bromo-1,1,1-trifluoroheptan-2-one (4.2 g, 16.8 mmol) and TFSA (15.1 g, 100.8 mmol). The drying protocols matched those described for BPMTP.

Quaternization of polyaromatics

For QBPMTP-DMP, BPMTP (4.51 g, 10.7 mmol) was dissolved in NMP (90 mL) in a flask, treated with 1-methylpiperidine (13 mL, 107 mmol), and stirred for 30 hours. The product was precipitated in ethyl ether, filtered, and vacuum-dried at 60 °C. To synthesize QBPMTP-TMA, BPMTP (4.51 g, 10.7 mmol) in NMP (100 mL) was reacted with a 30 wt% aqueous solution of trimethylamine (13.3 g, 53.7 mmol) for 24 hours. The reaction was stirred at 25 °C and subsequently precipitated in ethyl acetate. The white polymer was filtered and dried under vacuum to yield QBPMTP-TMA. The quaternization of the QPTP series followed the above synthesis procedures.

Radical-induced degradation test for anion exchange polyelectrolytes

Polyaromatic electrolytes were immersed in Fenton’s reagent (2 ppm Fe2+ in 3 wt% H2O2) at 80 °C for 48 h. Mass retention was recorded in the initial state, after 24 h, and after 48 h. Each sample was rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, dried under vacuum, and weighed. Before conductivity testing, polyelectrolytes were exchanged into Cl⁻ form.

Alkaline stability test for anion exchange polyelectrolytes

The alkaline stability of the QBPMTP series and QPTP series was tested by monitoring variations in conductivity under alkaline conditions. The membrane samples were immersed in a 10 mol L⁻1 KOH solution at 80 °C for 120 h. Before conductivity testing, anions were exchanged into Cl⁻ form.

BDE calculation

All density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using Gaussian 1648. The geometries of M1 and M2 were optimized at the ωB97X-D/6-31 G(d) level of theory49. Using the same functional and basis set, harmonic vibrational frequency analyses were conducted to verify that the optimized structures corresponded to local minima; no imaginary frequencies were found. Bond dissociation energies were then estimated for selected C–H bonds in M1 and M2 to compare their relative bond strengths. The atomic coordinates of the optimized structures are provided in Supplementary Data 1, along with a corresponding legend file (Supplementary Data 1 Legend).

Membrane electrode assembly fabrication for pure-water-fed AEMWE

The MEA (4 cm2) comprised a Pt-coated corrosion-resistant sintered Ti plate as anode and a carbon paper as cathode (Avcarb®). A PiperION® membrane (40 µm) was employed, and the same commercial material was used as the cathode ionomer. The cathode catalyst layer was prepared by ultrasonically dispersing Pt/C catalyst (52.1 wt% Pt, Tanaka) in n-propanol/water (7:3 v/v) with PiperION® ionomer (25 wt%). The ink was spray-coated onto the carbon paper at 80 °C, achieving 0.5 mgPt cm⁻2. For the anode, IrO2 was mixed with QPTP or QBPMTP ionomer (5 wt% in DMSO), then concentrated and coated onto the Ti plate. The electrodes were dried at 80 °C, resulting in an Ir loading of 8.0 mg cm⁻2. Catalyst loadings were controlled and calculated by measuring the electrode mass difference before and after coating. All components were activated in 1 mol L⁻1 KOH (80 °C, 12 h) for complete OH⁻ exchange, followed by thorough rinsing to neutral pH.

Performance evaluation of pure-water-fed electrolyzers

Each MEA was assembled with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) gaskets on both anode and cathode sides and compressed to a torque of 7 N·m, corresponding to a compression ratio of 10–12%. The cells were operated at 80 °C with deionized water supplied to both electrodes at a flow rate of 3 mL min⁻1. Electrolyzer activation was performed at 0.05 A cm⁻2 for 30 min. Galvanostatic polarization curves were acquired from 0.05 to 1.0 A cm⁻2 in 0.05 A cm⁻2 increments (using LAND CT6002A). Durability testing was carried out at 1 A cm⁻2 at 80 °C (using LAND CT6002A). EIS was collected in the frequency range of 100 kHz to 1 Hz at various current densities with 10% AC amplitude (using Gamry Interface 5000E). Data were fitted using ZView software with equivalent circuits. The original EIS data were subsequently processed using the DRTtools MATLAB package. In the three-electrode AEMWE configuration, a Pt/Ir-WRE was used. The wire reference was insulated with PTFE (Pt 25 µm, Pt 10 µm) and sandwiched between two PiperION® membranes, thereby positioning it at the center of the MEA active area. This configuration allows direct tracking of electrode-specific electrochemical responses during operation. MEA fabrication for durability testing in KOH AEMWE

This section corresponds to Supplementary Fig. I. The MEA used for stability testing at 1 A cm⁻2 was prepared as follows. The anode ink was prepared by homogenizing lab-synthesized NiCo2 catalyst with 15 wt% polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) binder in deionized water by ball milling for 48 hours. The slurry was concentrated and uniformly applied to platinum-plated nickel foam (5×5 cm2), then dried at 180 °C for 30 min. The cathode ink, containing Pt/C and Nafion® in a mixture of isopropanol and deionized water, was ultrasonically dispersed and spray-coated onto carbon paper, achieving a Pt loading of 0.5 mg cm⁻2. For stability testing at 0.5 A cm⁻2, platinum-plated nickel foam was used as both anode and cathode. AEMWE durability was evaluated under a circulation of 1 mol L⁻1 KOH.

Synthesis of quaternary ammonium hydroxides

The model compounds were prepared through a two-step procedure involving (i) preparation of quaternary ammonium salts and (ii) subsequent anion exchange. Commercially available QAs (TMA, BTMA, DMP, and HTMA) were used directly, while ASU was synthesized via the Hoffmann alkylation reaction11. Piperidine (5.1089 g, 0.06 mol) and NaOH (2.4 g, 0.06 mol) were dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (30 mL). 1,5-dibromopentane (13.797 g, 0.06 mol) was added dropwise over 30 minutes, this was followed by refluxing at 60 °C for 24 h. The reaction mixture was concentrated, and the crude product was purified through sequential ethanol extraction, diethyl ether washing, and recrystallization from isopropanol to yield ASU bromide as white crystals. To obtain quaternary ammonium hydroxides, halogenated salts were exchanged for anions using an ion exchange resin (Amberlite® IRA-900). Prior to use, the resin was pre-treated with 1 mol L⁻1 KOH and rinsed with deionized water until a neutral pH was achieved. The electrolyte was prepared immediately before use and was not stored.
