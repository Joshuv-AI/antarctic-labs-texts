Serum untargeted metabolomics analysis of colorectal cancer
Authors: Yanan Yi, Yaning Cao, Yong Guo, Ya Cui, Chongxu Han, Wei Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent gastrointestinal tract disease with atypical manifestations. The objective of this study was to employ serum metabolomics for the identification of potential biomarkers in CRC diagnosis and treatment.MethodsA total of 136 healthy controls and 134 CRC patients were enrolled in our study. In total, 270 serum samples were analyzed using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and divided into the discovery group and validation group. Meanwhile, we collected 65 CRC patients one week after surgery to systematically monitor postoperative metabolite changes. Statistical analysis and functional annotation were conducted to identify potential biomarker panels and altered metabolic pathways. Mfuzz expression pattern clustering analysis was employed to reveal the changing trends of metabolites across stages 1–4 of CRC.ResultsA total of 118 differential metabolites were identified in this study, demonstrating distinct separation between patients and controls based on serum metabolic profiles. Further functional annotation revealed associations between the differential metabolites and lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, nucleic metabolism, as well as pentose and glucuronate interconversions. Moreover, a panel consisting of Guanosine and Tyr Ser exhibited excellent predictive performance for CRC diagnosis with an AUC of 0.961 in the discovery group and an AUC of 0.948 in the validation group. The analysis of these differential metabolites in pre-and postoperative CRC serum samples showed that their levels returned to a healthy state after the operation, suggesting their potential specificity for CRC and highlighting their potential utility in monitoring therapeutic response. Furthermore, clustering analysis using Mfuzz identified dynamic metabolic expression patterns across CRC stages I to IV, with several metabolites exhibiting consistent upregulation or downregulation trends, implying a close association with CRC progression.ConclusionThis study highlighted significant differences in CRC serum metabolomic profiles, which may have potential value for distinguishing CRC patients from controls and for assessing pre- and post-operative states. The establishment of potentially diagnostic biomarker panels and alerted metabolic pathways provided valuable insights into deeper metabolic disorders associated with CRC. Our data showed that serum metabolomics might be used for CRC diagnosis, prognosis and classification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15433-w.Keywords: Biomarker, Colorectal cancer, Serum metabolomics, Prognosis, Classification

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors, ranking third in global cancer incidence and second in terms of cancer-related mortality [1]. Notably, the stage at diagnosis represents the most significant prognostic factor for survival outcomes in CRC. Studies have shown that the 5-year survival rate exceeds 90% for patients diagnosed at stage I or II, while it drops to approximately 14% in stage IV disease with distant metastases [2]. Currently, commonly used non-invasive screening methods for colorectal cancer include fecal occult blood test (FOBT), stool multi-target DNA testing, and detection of blood tumor markers. FOBT, which includes guaiac-based and fecal immunochemical tests (FIT), is prone to interference by dietary and physiological factors, resulting in a high false-positive rate [3]. Multi-target stool DNA testing detects CRC-associated mutations from exfoliated epithelial cells within the colorectal lumen. However, its limited specificity for advanced adenomas, demanding sample collection procedures, and high cost have hindered widespread clinical implementation [4]. Blood tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9), have been widely applied in clinical practice. However, both markers suffer from suboptimal sensitivity and specificity, significantly limiting their utility in early detection and diagnosis [5, 6]. Clinically, the monitoring of CRC recurrence and metastasis heavily relies on colonoscopy and imaging techniques. A large-scale prospective cohort study conducted in Germany demonstrated a significant reduction in CRC incidence and mortality attributable to screening colonoscopy [7]. Nonetheless, the widespread use of colonoscopy is limited by its invasiveness, high cost, and the need for stringent bowel preparation. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remain the standard procedures for detecting distant metastasis and guiding radiotherapy [8, 9]. However, their limitations, such as high cost, suboptimal sensitivity for small lesions (< 1 cm), and potential radiation exposure, have restricted their application in large-scale screening [9, 10]. Therefore, the development of minimally invasive or non-invasive diagnostic approaches with high specificity and sensitivity is urgently needed to improve early detection and clinical outcomes in CRC.

Metabolomics has emerged as a powerful tool in clinical diagnostics and therapeutics by enabling the comprehensive analysis of metabolic alterations in human biofluids, thereby providing insights into disease pathophysiology and progression [11, 12]. Even subtle physiological or pathological changes can lead to significant shifts in metabolite concentrations, thereby rendering the metabolome as an amplified manifestation of a biological system [13]. Among various biofluids, blood is particularly advantageous due to its accessibility and minimal invasiveness, allowing for dynamic monitoring of disease progression and therapeutic responses [14]. Currently, metabolomics offers a novel, non-invasive approach for CRC screening, facilitating early detection through the identification of specific metabolic biomarkers [15]. In previous studies, 153 patients samples with metastatic CRC and 139 control samples were analyzed using 1H NMR to identify valid biomarkers and the metabolite profiles may offer a potential tool to predict patient survival [16]. Kim et al. employed NMR-based metabolomics to identify potential diagnostic biomarkers (taurine, alanine, and 3-aminoisobutyrate) capable of distinguishing advanced colorectal adenoma, early-stage colorectal cancer, and healthy controls [17]. In 2025, an NMR-based metabolomics study was conducted to profile metabolic differences in colorectal cancer tissues across various stages and anatomical sites, in comparison with matched distant noncancerous tissues. The study identified five metabolite biomarkers (glucose, glutamate, alanine, valine, and histidine) that effectively distinguished early-stage from advanced-stage tumors. Additionally, distinct metabolic profiles were observed across tumor locations, with glucose, glycerol, glutamine, inositol, succinate, and citrate serving as discriminative metabolites for different anatomical sites of CRC [18].

Răchieriu et al. performed integrated metabolomic profiling on serum samples from 25 CRC patients and 16 controls, uncovering distinct lipid classes—including phosphatidylcholines (PCs), phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs), and ceramides—linked to tumor progression via altered lipogenesis and lipolysis pathways [19]. A comprehensive untargeted metabolomic analysis using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS) on paired CRC and adjacent normal tissues (n = 35) revealed elevated levels of amino acid derivatives such as N-α-acetyl-ε-(2-propenal)-Lys, cyclo(Glu-Glu), and cyclo(Phe-Glu) in tumor tissues, indicating disrupted amino acid metabolism in CRC [20]. In 2024, Runhao Xu et al. implemented a screening panel consisting of ApoA1, ApoA2, lithocholic acid (LCA), and CEA, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.995, surpassing the diagnostic performance of individual lipid, bile acid, or tumor marker analyses [21]. A 2024 large-scale cohort study integrated plasma and fecal metabolomics to identify key metabolites, including oleic acid and allocholic acid, associated with CRC progression from normal mucosa to adenoma to carcinoma. Additionally, a 17-metabolite plasma panel was established, demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy across three independent validation cohorts (AUC = 0.848–0.987) [22]. Recent studies have demonstrated that specific metabolic signatures in biofluids, such as blood and urine, can serve as biomarkers for surgical efficacy. Our prior study demonstrated that analysis of four specific metabolites (N-Phenylacetylasparticacid, Tyrosyl-Gamma-glutamate, Tyr-Ser and Sphingosine) in serum samples of CRC patients before and after surgery indicated a return to healthy levels after an intervention [23]. Serum metabolomics has also shown promise in monitoring CRC progression and staging. Studies have demonstrated that specific metabolite profiles correlate with different CRC stages, offering potential biomarkers for disease staging. A prospective cohort study showed surgery-induced phospholipids and amino acid dysregulation is associated with the severity of CRC postoperative complications, showing the potential of metabolomics being reliably prognosticate the risk of severe complications, which would be of use to clinical practice [24]. Alterations in amino acid and nucleotide metabolism have been associated with tumor progression, and certain metabolites have been identified as stage-specific indicators [25]. These findings suggest that metabolomic profiling can aid in distinguishing between early and advanced stages of CRC, thereby informing treatment decisions and prognostic assessments.

Given the advancements in metabolomic technologies, prior studies have emphasized the potential of metabolomics as a non-invasive diagnostic approach for CRC. This study aimed to identify serum diagnostic biomarkers and elucidate the underlying metabolic mechanisms associated with CRC detection using untargeted metabolomics. Based on post-operative pathological results, CRC patients were categorized into stages I-IV, and the trend of specific metabolite changes across these stages was systematically monitored. This study might offer an innovative perspective insight into the diagnosis and progression of CRC.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital (2025ky142). Patients were recruited between September 2021 and July 2022 at the Department of Gastrointestinal surgery. Serum samples from CRC patients were collected immediately before surgery, whereas post-operative serum samples were obtained one week following the surgical procedure. The pathological diagnosis for all patients was confirmed by two professional pathologists after surgery. While the samples of the healthy control group were obtained from the physical examination center. Clinical information, including age, sex, cancer biomarkers, and other pertinent data, was obtained from medical and laboratory reports.

Sample collection

Serum samples: Following an overnight fast, blood samples were collected from all participants in the early morning between 7:00 AM and 9:00 AM. Subsequently, the blood was drawn into 5 ml vacutainer serum separator tubes containing a clot activator for serum separation. These samples were allowed to clot for 15 min at room temperature before undergoing centrifugation. The clotted blood samples were then centrifuged at a speed of 3000×g for 5 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected. All collected blood samples were stored at -80 °C until further analysis.

Sample preparation

The serum sample (50 µl) was supplemented with 150 µL of acetonitrile and vortexed for 30 s. Subsequently, the resulting solution underwent centrifugation at 15,000×g for a duration of 10 min. The supernatant was then subjected to vacuum drying, yielding a powder. Next, the dried powder was reconstituted in 100 µL of 2% acetonitrile and centrifuged again at 15,000×g for 15 min. Finally, we utilized a specifically designed chromatography instrument sample plate (Waters, USA) with a capacity of 700 µL to collect the post-centrifugation liquid which was subsequently transferred to an autosampler. Quality control (QC) samples were generated by pooling equal volumes from all samples across different groups. To evaluate the stability and repeatability of our analytical process, QC samples were randomly injected resulting in a total of nine serum injections. QC standard was prepared by pooling 2 µL aliquots from all serum samples. The QC samples were injected in a randomized sequence throughout the analytical run to continuously monitor the stability and performance of the LC-MS system. A tight cluster of quality control samples demonstrated strong stability of the platform (Figure S1). The results demonstrated that the significant variations observed among the subgroups were attributable to authentic alterations in metabolite profiles rather than technological errors.

UPLC-MS analysis

The Waters ACQUITY UHPLC system (Waters, USA) coupled with a LTQ Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) was utilized for the UPLC-MS analysis of serum samples. Serum metabolites were separated on a Waters HSS T3 column (3 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) using a 13-minute linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. The mobile phase A was composed of water containing 0.1% formic acid and the mobile phase B was constituted by 100% acetonitrile. The elution procedure was set as follows: 0–1 min, 2% B; 1–3 min, 2%-55% B; 3–8 min, 55%-100% B; 8–11 min, 100% B; 11–11.1 min, 100%–2% B; 11.1–13 min, 2% B. The injection volume was 20 µl and the temperature in the autosampler was maintained at 40 °C. The mass spectrometer was analyzed in ESI (+) mode with a m/z range of 100–1000. The instrument parameters were set as follows: sheath gas flow rate of 40 Arb, capillary temperature of 350℃, full MS resolution set at 60,000, MS/MS resolution set at 15,000, collision energy set at 20/35/60 in Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) mode, and spray voltage of 3.2 kV (positive). The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to achieve a value of 5 × 105 and the maximum injection time (IT) was 100 ms.

Data pre-processing and statistical analysis

The mass spectrometry raw data were processed according to the methodology outlined by Liu et al. [26]. Briefly, Progenesis QI software (Waters, USA) was utilized for peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration of the raw data. Metabolite annotation was subsequently performed at using HMDB, METLIN, and an in-house standard database. The peak area of each chromatographic peak represents the relative content of the corresponding substance. Further analysis included missing value estimation, log10 transformation, and Pareto scaling in MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca). Variables with more than 50% missing values across samples were excluded from the analysis. The remaining missing values were imputed using the K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) algorithm as implemented on the ‘Wu Kong’ platform(https://www.omicsolution.com/wkomics/main/).

Intergroup age comparisons were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while gender-based comparisons were assessed through chi-square tests. Non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests were conducted to evaluate the significance of the variables. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using SIMCA14.1 (Umetrics, Sweden). The criteria for identifying statistically significant variables were as follows: (1) adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05; (2) fold change ≥ 1.5; and (3) variable importance in projection (VIP) value >1.0. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and external biomarker validation were performed using the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform to evaluate the prediction accuracy. A logistic regression algorithm was utilized to conduct multivariate ROC analysis, aiming to develop a more robust predictive model. Visualization of these models was generated using R software (version 4.2). The heatmap analysis of metabolites after surgery was performed based on the differential metabolites selected in the discovery group in three subgroups (control, pre-operation and post-operation). Line chart were analyzed using the log10-transformed intensities of each biomarker obtained from patient samples (pre-operation and post-operation). Statistically significant differences in metabolites shown in violin plots were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test in R software, with *, **, and *** indicating p-values < 0.05, < 0.01, and < 0.001, respectively, between the two groups. To investigate the overall metabolites expression patterns, a soft clustering method was performed using the Mfuzz package in the ‘Wu Kong’ platform.

Results

Study workflow

A total of 270 participants were included in this study, consisting of 134 patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer (CRC) and 136 healthy controls. Table 1 illustrated the distribution of participants across groups, along with their baseline clinical characteristics. All cases were randomly divided into a discovery group (n = 183) and a validation group (n = 87), maintaining a ratio of 2:1. Age and sex matching was performed within subgroups, revealing no statistically significant differences. Differential analysis was performed in the discovery group to distinguish CRC patients from healthy controls, comprising 92 CRC cases and 96 HC. Biomarker panel was subsequently validated in the independent validation group (42 CRC vs. 40 HC). Subsequently, functional annotation of these differential metabolites was explored through databases such as HMDB. Furthermore, a heatmap analysis was generated to compare the metabolic profiles of postoperative patients with those of healthy controls and preoperative patients, thereby dynamically assessing metabolic changes following surgical intervention. A schematic overview of the study design was presented in Fig. 1.

The workflow of this study

Distinguishing CRC from healthy controls by serum metabolomics

A total of 1003 spectral features were identified based on the LC-MS analysis of serum metabolome from CRC patients and healthy controls. The selection of potential biomarkers in this study was facilitated by the application of multivariate statistical analysis models. In the discovery cohort, both the PCA scatter plot and the OPLS-DA model (R2 = 0.926, Q2 = 0.89) demonstrated a clear and distinct separation trend, indicating significant differentiation between CRC cases and healthy controls (as illustrated in Fig. 2A and Figure S2a, respectively). Two hundred permutation tests showed the model stability in Figure S2b. Similarly, an OPLS-DA model was constructed for the validation group (R2 = 0.974, Q2 = 0.90), as shown in Figure S2c. The results of 200 permutation tests revealed no signs of overfitting, thereby further substantiating the robustness of the model (Figure S2d). Based on the criteria of Fold change ≥ 1.5, VIP > 1, and FDR < 0.05, a total of 118 differential metabolites were identified. Among them, 70 metabolites were upregulated whereas 48 were downregulated in CRC serum (Detailed in Table S1). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these differential metabolites were significantly involved in several key metabolic pathways, including β-alanine metabolism, purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and pentose and glucuronate interconversions (Fig. 2B). Additionally, we further classified the 118 differential metabolites by chemical structural subclasses. The dominant groups consisted of amino acid derivatives, glycerophosphocholines and carbohydrates—together accounting for about 80% of all differential metabolites (Figure S2e).

Diagnosis biomarker of CRC

We utilized ROC curves to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of each differential metabolite. To further improve predictive performance, a logistic regression model was constructed, and multivariate ROC curve-based exploratory analysis was conducted. Notably, a panel consisting of Guanosine and Tyr-Ser demonstrated excellent performance in distinguishing CRC from HC within the discovery cohort, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.961, with a sensitivity of 0.844 and a specificity of 0.946 (Fig. 2E and F, Table S2 and Figure S3). External validation of the model yielded an AUC of 0.948, with sensitivity and specificity rates of 0.825 and 0.952, respectively (Fig. 2D; Table 2). Additionally, the line charts demonstrated that significant differences in the intensity levels of Guanosine and Tyr Ser in both discovery group and validation group (Fig. 2E and F and Figure S3). Notably, the preoperative and postoperative metabolite data were collected from the same individuals, allowing dynamic assessment of metabolic alterations following surgical intervention. Collectively, these findings confirmed the promising potential of Guanosine and Tyr-Ser as non-invasive serum biomarkers for the diagnosis of CRC.

Surveillance of postoperative metabolic alterations

A total of 118 significantly differential metabolites were identified between pre-operative patients and healthy controls in the discovery cohort (Table S2). A heatmap visualization of these metabolites revealed marked differences in serum metabolic profiles between CRC patients and healthy individuals (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, while postoperative samples collected one week after surgery remained distinguishable from healthy controls, they exhibited a clear trend toward normalization, indicating partial metabolic recovery. Further analysis showed that 45 out of the 118 differential metabolites, which were elevated in CRC patients preoperatively, displayed a tendency to return toward baseline levels following surgical resection. Notably, these metabolites included Phe Thr, His Ile Gly, and Histidinyl-Tryptophan (Fig. 3B, C and D, Figure S3 and Table S3). Functional annotation of these up-regulated metabolites (Fig. 3E) indicated that they were primarily associated with biotin metabolism and pentose and glucuronate interconversions.

These findings reinforced the biological relevance of these metabolites to CRC, highlighting their potential value for dynamically monitoring the post-surgical status of CRC patients.

MFUZZ expression pattern clustering of CRC progression-related metabolites

The fluctuation patterns of serum metabolites across different stages of CRC (Stages I–IV) were classified using MFUZZ clustering analysis, a method based on the fuzzy c-means clustering algorithm. A total of nine distinct serum metabolite clusters were identified, each exhibiting specific expression trends across CRC stages (Fig. 4A and Table S4).

We subsequently focused on the proteins in clusters 1 and 8, which are closely associated with tumor progression. Metabolites in cluster 1 were gradually downregulated during disease progression. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that cluster 1 metabolites were predominantly involved in Histidine metabolism, Glycerophospholipid metabolism, Sphingolipid metabolism, and related biological processes (Fig. 4B). Consistently, the violin plot analysis demonstrated a progressive decrease in L-histidine levels across CRC stages (Fig. 4C). In contrast, metabolites in cluster 8 were continuously upregulated, and pathway analysis indicated that these metabolites were associated with Histidine metabolism, Pentose and glucuronate interconversions, and so on (Fig. 4D). Urocanic acid, a representative metabolite from cluster 8, exhibited a consistent increase with cancer progression (Fig. 4E, Figure S3 and Table S5). These findings further substantiated the biological relevance of these metabolites to CRC, highlighting their potential value as dynamic biomarkers for CRC staging and monitoring.

Discussion

Metabolic reprogramming is a fundamental hallmark that drives both the initiation and progression of cancer [27]. Consequently, the identification of reliable biomarkers for CRC diagnosis and the elucidation of its underlying metabolic mechanisms remain urgent priorities. Leveraging the unique advantages of serum—such as its accessibility and rich biochemical information-we performed an in-depth characterization of the serum metabolomic profile in CRC patients. The differential metabolites identified in this study were implicated in multiple metabolic pathways, including those related to carbohydrates, amino acids, lipids, nucleotides, and hormones. Notably, we established a diagnostic panel consisting of guanosine and Tyr-Ser, which exhibited robust predictive power for CRC diagnosis, with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) exceeding 0.90 in both discovery and validation cohorts. Furthermore, we observed that the levels of several serum metabolites tended to normalize following surgical resection, suggesting their potential utility not only in diagnosis but also in monitoring postoperative prognosis. Through the integration of MFUZZ-based soft clustering with machine learning algorithms, we identified distinct metabolite clusters (Cluster1 and Cluster8) that showed progressive upward or downward trajectories. These clusters may offer additional value in the staging and stratification of CRC, highlighting the relevance of dynamic metabolic alterations throughout disease progression.

Metabolomics characteristics of CRC patients compared with the healthy controls

Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these differentially expressed metabolites were significantly enriched in several key metabolic pathways, including β-alanine metabolism, purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and the pentose and glucuronate interconversion pathway.

Nucleotide metabolism plays a pivotal role in the process of carcinogenesis and cancer progression, not only by fulfilling the demands of cell proliferation but also by exerting influence on diverse activities including tumor immunity, resistance to anti-tumor drugs, and the interplay between tumor cells and the microenvironment [28]. Consistent with previous studies, guanosine levels were significantly elevated in CRC patients compared to healthy controls (HCs) [29, 30]. Cancer cells rely on de novo nucleotide biosynthesis as an essential mechanism to sustain dysregulated cell division [31, 32]. Insufficient supply of nucleotide precursors is considered a limiting factor for CRC cell growth [20]. Unlike lipids or amino acids, nucleotides are not readily available in the tumor microenvironment, which may constrain tumor growth [27]. Guanosine functions as a substrate for guanosine triphosphate (GTP) synthesis via the salvage pathway, and intracellular GTP levels are critical for sustaining cell proliferation [30, 33, 34]. Previous research found that Guanosine significantly inhibited the proliferation of the breast cancer cell line MCF-7, indicating its potential as a therapeutic target for the development of novel cancer treatment strategies [33]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that CRC patients may exhibit increased purine metabolism to offset the heightened utilization of biomolecular substances resulting from the rapid proliferation and growth of tumor cells. In our study, guanosine—a key precursor for nucleic acid synthesis—was significantly elevated in CRC patients relative to HCs. Additionally, the level of guanosine was consistently observed to decrease in both preoperative and postoperative comparisons, indicating its potential as a promising candidate for biomarker discovery in CRC.

We also classified differential metabolites by chemical subclass. The dominant categories were amino acid derivatives, glycerophosphocholines and carbohydrate. This compositional pattern reflects a coordinated reprogramming of amino acid, carbohydrate and lipid remodeling, which together sustain tumor proliferation and microenvironmental adaptation.

Amino acids serve not only as substrates for protein synthesis but also as critical intermediates in energy production, nucleoside synthesis, and the maintenance of cellular redox balance [35]. The alteration in various amino acid profiles indicates a higher demand for energy to support increased cellular proliferation [30]. Notably, the expression level of the dipeptide Tyr-Ser is significantly elevated in CRC and has been established as one of the biomarkers for diagnosis in this study. Consistently, Long et al. analyzed the differences in the metabolism of CRC with different clinicopathological features and reported the early tumors of CRC have higher abundance of dipeptide characteristics [36]. While the origin and function of dipeptides in CRC remain unclear, further studies are warranted to elucidate their mechanistic roles.

In our subclass analysis, carbohydrates emerged as one of the major categories, indicating an important role for carbohydrate metabolism in CRC pathophysiology. Various evidences supports that colorectal cancer cells often reprogram glucose metabolism to favor glycolysis—even in the presence of oxygen (the Warburg effect)—thereby enhancing biosynthetic precursor supply and redox balance to fuel proliferation and survival [37].

In addition, lipid subclasses including sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids were significantly altered, consistent with previous reports linking lipidomic remodeling to tumor invasiveness and immune evasion. These lipid metabolic alterations may affect membrane fluidity, signaling cascades, and cell death resistance, collectively contributing to tumor progression [38, 39]. In our subclass-based metabolomic profiling, a significant proportion of differential metabolites fell into the glycerophosphocholine (GPC) subclass. GPC is a key intermediate in phosphatidylcholine (PC) turnover and choline metabolism, serving as a reservoir for intracellular choline pools and linking membrane lipid dynamics with cellular signaling [40]. In colorectal cancer specifically, dysregulated PC metabolism—driven by enzymes such as choline kinase, phospholipases, and glycerophosphodiesterases—could support membrane biosynthesis, proliferation, and oncogenic signaling [41]. Notably, previous studies have proved that alternation of sphingolipid metabolism in CRC, which aligns with our emphasis on lipid subclass perturbations [42]. Sphingolipid pathways, centered on the balance between ceramide and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), are well recognized for their opposing roles in regulating apoptosis versus cell survival, thus critically modulating tumor fate [43].

Metabolites analysis in healthy Control, CRC and Post-operative group

Based on differential metabolomic analyses of preoperative and postoperative CRC patients, differentially expressed metabolites were found to be significantly enriched in the pentose and glucuronate interconversion pathways.

The pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway plays a crucial role not only in biosynthetic pathways such as gluconeogenesis but also in the fundamental metabolism of tumor cells, including Nucleic acid precursor formation and maintenance of redox balance [44, 45]. Increasing evidence from metabolomics and genomics studies has confirmed that dysregulation of this pathway occurs in colorectal cancer, suggesting its involvement in tumor initiation and progression. Cancer cells frequently undergo metabolic reprogramming to support their rapid proliferation. In both colorectal and gastric cancers, meta-analyses have identified significant alterations in the pentose and glucuronate interconversion pathways. These changes may enhance nucleotide biosynthesis and detoxification capacity, thereby facilitating tumor growth [46].

Furthermore, a pan-cancer analysis of metabolic gene expression across 26 different tumor types, compared with matched normal tissues, revealed both expected and novel patterns of metabolic reprogramming. In addition to well-established alterations such as enhanced glycolysis—consistent with the Warburg effect—this analysis uncovered unexpected dysregulation in additional metabolic circuits. Among these, the pentose and glucuronate interconversion pathway emerged as one of the most significantly disrupted metabolic routes, showing marked alterations in 20 cancer types, though not universally across all cancers [47].

Metabolomic alterations throughout the course of CRC progression

MFUZZ analysis of different stages of CRC identified some cluster profiles is associated with CRC progression, suggesting that these metabolite profiles could serve as potential indicators for disease monitoring.

Histidine, an essential amino acid, is enzymatically converted by histidine decarboxylase (HDC) into Histamine, a critical signaling molecule and immunomodulatory agent. Previous studies have shown that both HDC activity and Histamine levels are significantly elevated in tumor tissues, correlating with tumor metastasis [48]. Notably, a large cohort study conducted by Geijsen et al. demonstrated that stage III-IV patients exhibited significantly lower concentrations of Histidine and phosphatidylcholine (PC) compared to stage I-II patients, which is consistent with our findings [49]. Decreased Histidine level was associated with systemic inflammation and poorer cancer-specific survival [50]. Additionally, Investigators reported that L-Histidine concentrations were significantly lower in Stage IV compared to Stage I CRC patients, possibly due to increased hepatic uptake related to liver metastases [49, 51].

Contrary to L-Histidine, we observed a gradual increase trend in Urocanic acid levels during tumor progression after analysing cluster 8. Histidine is enzymatically converted to Urocanic acid by Histidase. Additionally, Urocanic acid is further metabolized into glutamate, which serves as a key intermediary in energy metabolism and redox regulation [52]. Studies have demonstrated that Urocanic acid may exhibit anti-inflammatory properties [52, 53]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the increase in Urocanic acid was primarily due to the enhanced enzymatic conversion of Histidine and was intricately linked to the inflammatory response associated with tumor progression.

L-Histidine metabolism is a vital biochemical pathway in humans, involved in several key metabolites processes, including the catabolism of L-histidine and the formation of urocanic acid. This pathway plays significant roles in various physiological processes and has implications in certain metabolic disorders.Analysis of two large prospective cohorts—the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC) and the UK Biobank—revealed that higher pre-diagnostic plasma histidine concentrations are associated with a reduced risk of developing CRC, suggesting a protective role of histidine against tumorigenesis [54]. Histidine is metabolized into histamine via the enzyme histidine decarboxylase (HDC). In CRC tissues, both HDC expression and histamine levels are significantly elevated compared to normal mucosa, with a positive correlation to tumor stage. Histamine can promote tumor growth by enhancing cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and modulating the immune response [55]. Previous research has indicated that serum amino acid levels are associated with systemic inflammatory status and disease progression, potentially reflecting inflammation-induced muscle catabolism, which in turn leads to specific alterations in serum metabolite profiles [50]. Our study revealed that decreasing L-histidine levels may be associated with poor prognosis, but it may not be linked to inflammation status of tumor stages (supplementary figure/ detailed data not shown). Therefore, the experimental results should be validated through a larger cohort study, and the underlying mechanisms associated with altered L-histidine levels during tumor progression warrant further investigation.

Consistently, our enrichment analysis of serum metabolites from CRC patients at different stages revealed that histidine metabolism pathways were highly enriched in both Cluster 1 and Cluster 8. This finding not only suggests that nucleotide metabolism pathways are directly or indirectly involved in CRC progression but also provides avenues for the development of biomarkers and therapeutic targets. The inverse relationship between circulating Histidine levels and CRC risk/staging underscores its potential as a non-invasive biomarker for early detection and disease monitoring. Notably, as Histidine is an essential amino acid obtained from the diet, nutritional interventions to modulate its levels could be explored as adjunctive measures in CRC prevention and management.

The association between microbiota and metabolites

More and more studies have shown that gut microbiota can not only promote the occurrence of cancer through the changes of specific flora, but also affect the occurrence and development of CRC through a wider range of microbial tribes, such as metabolites.

The results of a multi-omics study showed that CRC with intestinal flora imbalance had significant disorders in the metabolism of aromatic amino acids in serum and feces, of which Phe was commonly up-regulated [56]. Meanwhile, p-cresol, which is associated with Phe/ Tyr fermentation, is a typical microbial protein decomposition product with mucosal genotoxicity and barrier damage potential, and is considered to be a suspected cancer-promoting co-factor in CRC [57]. Threonine is a key amino acid in the synthesis of mucin O-glycan. An earlier in vivo experiment confirmed that supplementation of Thr, Ser, Pro, and Cys could promote mucin synthesis, improve intestinal microecology, and promote mucosal healing [58]. Various studies have also shown that ImP is elevated in CRC and adenoma sequences and is one of the characteristic metabolites in serum in cross-cohort CRC integration studies [59]. In general, the up-regulation of ImP/ histamine pathway suggests that microbial metabolites promote tumor microenvironment remodeling through inflammatory and immunosuppressive pathways. Thus, it promotes the occurrence and development of tumors such as CRC.

In summary, CRC-associated microbiota imbalance leaves measurable systemic signals at the serum level through amino acid and dipeptide metabolites, forming a multi-layer connection of “gut microbiota-serum metabolism-host metabolic network”. The relationship between the two not only provides a new explanation framework for the molecular mechanism of CRC occurrence and development, but also suggests that combined detection of flora characteristics and serum metabolites is expected to become a new strategy for early diagnosis, risk stratification and prognosis evaluation. At the same time, interventions targeting these pathways, such as increasing dietary fiber, prebiotics/probiotics modulation, targeted supplementation or inhibition of metabolites, have potential operability and translational prospects. Future multi-center, large-sample prospective studies will help to verify the stability and clinical utility of these markers.

Limitation and future direction

Despite the valuable insights obtained, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration in future work.The relatively modest sample size and single-center design may limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings.Moreover, unmeasured confounders—such as dietary habits, lifestyle factors, and comorbid conditions-might have contributed to the observed metabolic variations. Finally, the short follow-up period, with postoperative serum samples collected only one week after surgery, limited our ability to evaluate long-term metabolic restoration and the potential association with disease recurrence.

To overcome these limitations, future research should aim to recruit larger, multi-center, prospective cohorts to enhance the statistical robustness and external validity of the results. Comprehensive data integration—including clinical parameters, molecular profiles, and long-term follow-up information—will be crucial for clarifying the prognostic and predictive significance of serum metabolomic biomarkers. In particular, longitudinal studies with extended postoperative monitoring are warranted to elucidate the temporal dynamics of metabolic alterations and their association with recurrence and patient survival. Furthermore, future in vivo studies using relevant animal models are needed to confirm the functional roles of key metabolites in colorectal cancer progression and support their translation into clinically applicable biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Conclusions

In this study, we applied LC–MS to comprehensively profile the serum metabolome of CRC patients. The results demonstrated that the serum metabolome could effectively distinguish CRC patients from healthy controls and reflect the distinct states of the disease both preoperatively and postoperatively. We established a diagnostic panel comprising guanosine and Tyr-Ser, which showed strong discriminatory power and holds promise for clinical diagnosis and postoperative monitoring of CRC. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed metabolites were mainly involved in β-alanine metabolism, purine and pyrimidine metabolism, and pentose and glucuronate interconversions—pathways known to be critical in tumor biosynthesis, energy metabolism, and microenvironmental regulation. Furthermore, MFUZZ-based clustering revealed progressive and stage-associated patterns of metabolic alteration, offering additional insights into CRC progression and potential avenues for patient stratification. Collectively, our findings emphasize the utility of LC–MS-based serum metabolomics in the discovery of robust diagnostic biomarkers and the dissection of CRC-associated metabolic pathways. The integration of metabolomic profiling with advanced clustering approaches provides a powerful framework for improving early diagnosis, tracking disease progression, and informing personalized therapeutic strategies in colorectal cancer.
