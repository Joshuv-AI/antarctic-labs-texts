Phylogeny Drives Carrion Consumption by Vertebrate Scavengers Towards Distantly Related Species
Authors: Tatiana Pessano‐Serrat, Roberto C Rodríguez‐Caro, Daniel Redondo‐Gómez, Adrian Orihuela‐Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCarrion is a highly nutritious resource that attracts diverse organisms and plays a key role in ecosystem functioning. However, it could also pose pathogen risks, especially from phylogenetically close carcass‐scavenger pairs sharing traits that facilitate transmission. Although phylogenetic distance has long been suggested to influence scavenger feeding patterns, evidence remains scarce and limited at specific scales. To address this gap, we used camera‐trap monitoring to record scavenging events on fish, bird, and mammal carcasses in a Mediterranean wetland and evaluated how phylogenetic distance between vertebrate scavengers and available carcasses shaped feeding patterns. Our results revealed a consistent pattern across the assemblage, showing that scavengers consumed carcasses that were more phylogenetically distant than expected by chance, although this pattern varied slightly between taxonomic classes and at the species level. Our findings suggest the existence of a natural mechanism by which scavengers may reduce pathogen exposure when selecting carcasses.Keywords: carcass, disease, facultative scavenger, foraging behaviour, landscape of disgust, pathogen avoidance, phylogenetic relatedness, scavenging, transmission, wetland

Introduction

Carrion, that is, dead animal matter‐ is an unpredictable, ephemeral and highly nutritious resource that attracts a diverse assemblage of vertebrates, invertebrates and microorganisms (Barton et al. 2013; DeVault et al. 2003). These organisms exploit carcasses not only for feeding (Anderson et al. 2019; Carter et al. 2007; Selva et al. 2019), but also for preying—on vertebrates and invertebrates attracted to the carrion (Chin et al. 2007; Frank et al. 2020; Redondo‐Gómez et al. 2025), breeding ‐using carrion as a site for egg laying and larval development‐ (Szpila et al. 2015), or even for collecting nesting materials ‐such as feathers or hair‐ (Moleón and Sánchez‐Zapata 2016), among other purposes that further highlight the multifaceted ecological role of carrion. Consequently, carrion is essential to ecosystems, functioning as a biodiversity hotspot that shapes complex ecological interactions, structures trophic networks, and facilitates energy flow through food webs (Barton et al. 2013; Wilson and Wolkovich 2011). At a finer scale, carrion serves as a hotspot for diverse biotic interactions ‐from competition to facilitation‐ giving rise to an intricate, multidirectional network of relationships that emerge around it (Naves‐Alegre et al. 2022; Quinn et al. 2018; Redondo‐Gómez et al. 2023).

These ecological interactions that arise around carrion often carry significant risks for the species involved, as carcass sites can expose animals to both predators and pathogens (Moleón and Sánchez‐Zapata 2021). Carcasses may serve as a point for transmission of pathogens ‐including some species of viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, helminths, and arthropods (Balloux and van Dorp 2017; Huang et al. 2014)‐ that may affect not only scavengers feeding on the carrion but also non‐scavenging species that interact with the carcass in other ways or use the surrounding area (Gonzálvez, Martínez‐Carrasco, and Moleón 2021; Hashizume et al. 2024; Turner et al. 2014). These pathogens may originate from the carcass itself (autochthonous) or be introduced by visiting species (allochthonous), and can be transmitted via trophic pathways (e.g., ingestion of infected tissues; Gonzálvez, Martinez‐Carrasco, et al. 2021) or non‐trophic contact with contaminated surfaces or fluids (Gonzálvez, Martínez‐Carrasco, and Moleón 2021). Importantly, these infective agents typically exhibit reduced survival and abundance with temporal progression and spatial separation from carcasses (Singh et al. 2018; Valseth et al. 2017), establishing a spatiotemporal risk gradient. Because of the dual risk posed by predation and infection, carcass sites play a role in two important risk‐based frameworks: the landscape of fear, which relates to the risk of predation and the landscape of disgust, which concerns the risk of infection by pathogens (Moleón and Sánchez‐Zapata 2021).

The landscape of disgust describes a three‐dimensional perception of infection risk associated with carcasses, where peaks and valleys represent areas of higher or lower probability of pathogen exposure, respectively (Curtis 2014; Moleón et al. 2017). This perception of risk strongly shapes animal movement, spatial behaviour, and decision‐making in their environment (Buck and Ripple 2017; Weinstein et al. 2018; Wood and Johnson 2015). Animals assess this risk using different cues, primarily indirect signals (e.g., olfactory) coming from the carcass, or by previous negative experiences with similar contexts (Weinstein et al. 2018). Across evolutionary timescales, hosts have evolved three main defence strategies against pathogens: avoidance (behaviours that reduce contact with pathogens), resistance (physiological or immunological mechanisms that limit pathogen load), and tolerance (strategies that reduce the fitness costs of infection without affecting pathogen load) (Antonovics et al. 2017; Van Valen 1977; Rivas et al. 2014; Sarabian et al. 2023). Based on this framework, scavenger species may have evolved behavioural counterstrategies to mitigate infection from carrion, such as delayed consumption until pathogenic load has decreased or avoidance of carcasses perceived as highly infectious (Gonzálvez, Martinez‐Carrasco, et al. 2021). From an adaptive perspective, avoidance of infectious carcasses represents one of the main strategies to mitigate fitness costs, as pathogen exposure through ingestion or contact can directly impact morbidity, reproductive success, survival and social interactions (Buck et al. 2018; Weinstein et al. 2018). Pathogen persistence in carcasses reinforces these behaviours, as both non‐trophically transmitted agents (such as Sarcoptes scabiei, Bacillus anthracis , Lyssavirus type 1; Escobar et al. 2022; Jiranantasak et al. 2022; McElhinney et al. 2014; Niedringhaus et al. 2019) and trophically transmitted pathogens such as Trichinella spp. can remain viable in remains for days, enduring in skin, fluids or decaying tissues (Badagliacca et al. 2016; Farina et al. 2017; Pozio 2000; Raines and Stewart 1988). However, despite clear evidence that many pathogens can persist for days in decaying tissues, documented cases of actual transmission from carcasses to scavengers are scarce (Hanbury et al. 1986) and the behavioural adaptations that might mitigate such risks remain poorly understood.

Avoidance behaviour may be especially important when scavenging on phylogenetically proximate carcasses, because of host–parasite compatibility (Davies and Pedersen 2008). Phylogenetically close carcasses can be perceived as presenting a high risk of pathogen transmission due to shared physiological traits (Huang et al. 2014; Stephens et al. 2016). In line with this, several studies have reported that carnivorous mammals tend to avoid scavenging on the carcasses of other carnivores, particularly conspecifics, but also, to a lesser extent, phylogenetically related heterospecifics (Moleón et al. 2017; Gonzálvez, Martinez‐Carrasco, et al. 2021; Gonzálvez, Martínez‐Carrasco, and Moleón 2021). Cases of conspecific scavenging have been documented (Evans et al. 2022; Muther et al. 2024; Olson et al. 2016), but they are rare, particularly compared with heterospecific consumption, and typically occur under specific circumstances (e.g., unhealthy or young individuals or resource scarcity) (Fodrie et al. 2012; Mukherjee and Heithaus 2013). The low frequency of conspecific and closely related scavenging has been hypothesized to reflect an evolutionary trade‐off, where animals must balance the high nutritional value of related carcasses against the increased risk of infection they pose (Moleón et al. 2017; Pfennig et al. 1998; Pfennig 2000). Despite these, the role of phylogenetic proximity in shaping scavenging decisions remains largely untested. This reveals a key gap in our understanding of the ecological and evolutionary dynamics of scavenging behaviour and the potential role of phylogenetic distance in shaping them.

To address this knowledge gap, our study experimentally evaluated whether a general pattern exists in nature whereby vertebrate scavengers preferentially consume carrion from species that are phylogenetically more distant from themselves, as an adaptative strategy to reduce pathogen transmission risk. Using field data from 35 carcass species monitored in a Mediterranean wetland, we assessed consumption patterns by 24 vertebrate scavenger species with three objectives: (1) to determine if vertebrate scavengers avoid consuming phylogenetically proximate carcasses more than expected by chance; (2) to assess if this potential pattern of avoidance differs between the main vertebrate scavenger taxonomic classes (Aves (hereafter birds) vs. Mammalia (hereafter mammals)); and (3) to evaluate variation among species in this potential avoidance behaviour. We predict that vertebrate scavengers are more likely to feed on carcasses of phylogenetically distant species than would be expected by chance. Furthermore, we hypothesize that this behaviour will vary between taxonomic classes (birds and mammals), due to their separate evolutionary histories that could influence their risk assessment strategies. We also hypothesize variation among species within each class, reflecting differences in physiology and behaviour, which could influence the expression of this potential pattern of carcass selection. Understanding these patterns is not only essential for advancing the behavioural ecology of scavengers ‐especially within the framework of the landscape of disgust‐ but also carries significant implications for human and ecosystem health, especially in the current challenges posed by zoonoses and emerging diseases affecting domestic animals, wildlife and humans (e.g., Avian influenza; SARS‐CoV‐2) (Kalthoff et al. 2010; Lerner and Berg 2015; Leroy et al. 2020; Wong et al. 2020).

Methods

Study Area

This research was carried out in El Hondo Natural Park (38°11′16″ N, 0°47′28″ W; hereafter “El Hondo”), a 2387 ha mesohaline wetland in southeastern Alicante, Spain. Notably, El Hondo is a highly productive system (Mitsch et al. 2015), characterized by abundant resources that concentrate a high diversity and density of species within a relatively small area, thereby leading to frequent carrion availability (Orihuela‐Torres et al. 2023; pers. obs.). Although of natural origin, the system has been extensively modified by human activities and today comprises two large ponds ‐primarily used for crop irrigation‐ and over 20 smaller ponds dedicated to species conservation, sport fishing, and waterfowl hunting (Abellán‐Contreras 2019; Orihuela‐Torres, Pérez‐García, et al. 2022). Our work focused exclusively on the irrigation and conservation ponds areas, since the fishing and hunting areas are under private ownership. The park is composed of several habitat types: extensive reedbeds ‐especially around the ponds‐ dominate the landscape; marshes featuring aquatic plants like Schoenoplectus litoralis and Bolboschoenus maritimus ; and salt flats that remain dry year‐round, supporting halophytes such as Salicornia spp., Halocnemum strobilaceum , Limonium spp., and Tamarix spp. (Sedín 2005). The region has a dry thermo‐Mediterranean climate, receiving on average 300 mm of rainfall per year, with mean temperatures around 18°C.

El Hondo is designated a Ramsar wetland and protected under the European Union Habitats and Birds Directives (92/43/EEC and 2009/147/EC) as part of the Natura 2000 network. It's internationally recognized for supporting an important number of waterfowls during both wintering and breeding seasons (Servei de Vida Silvestre i Xarxa Natura 2000 2022). The park also provides important habitat for various raptors, gulls and medium‐sized carnivores, all of which can act as facultative scavengers (Orihuela‐Torres et al. 2023). However, no obligate scavengers (i.e., vultures) or large predators are present at El Hondo.

Field Methods

Between February 2020 and May 2021, we placed 185 carcasses (Orihuela‐Torres, Sebastián‐González, and Pérez‐García 2022) in El Hondo Natural Park and monitored them using camera traps (Browning Strike Force Pro HD model). Of these, 11 carcasses were excluded from analyses due to technical problems that prevented obtaining complete consumption data. Thus, a total of 174 carcasses were included in the analyses (Table S1; Figure 1). These carcasses encompassed 35 different carcass species present in the park, including 84 fish (one species; common carp Cyprinus carpio ; 100–3975 g; x̄ = 648.22 g), 60 birds (29 species; turtledoves, quails, cuckoos, passerines, wading birds and ducks; 10–3450 g; x̄ = 543.87 g) and 30 mammals (five species; rodents, shrews, hares and rabbits; 12–2254 g; x̄ = 420.19 g) (Table S1; Figure 1). A higher proportion of fish carcasses was used, reflecting the dominant contribution of this group to the animal biomass in the study area (Cyanowater 2019).

Phylogenetic tree of scavengers and carcass species. Species‐level phylogenetic tree constructed using data from the Open Tree of Life. Light blue indicates scavenger species, brown represents carcass species and dark blue shows species that acted both as scavengers and carcasses.

Terrestrial vertebrate carcasses (birds and mammals) were collected from fresh (< 12 h since death) roadkill around the park or obtained from the regional wildlife rehabilitation centre (Centro de Recuperación de Fauna Santa Faz, Alicante), while fish were captured by park staff as part of invasive species control efforts. Carcasses were strategically placed to simulate natural mortality: waterbirds near ponds, fish along shorelines, and mammals and terrestrial birds distributed across the park. To account for seasonal variation in scavenger communities, carcasses were placed throughout 1 year (February 2019–May 2021; Orihuela‐Torres et al. 2023), and at least 200 m apart (Turner et al. 2020). In a few instances, carcasses were set less than 25 m apart but separated temporally by at least 1 month to prevent scavengers from getting used to the supply of carrion. Each carcass was monitored with two camera traps placed 1.5–2 m from the carrion ‐one configured for taking photos and the other for recording video ‐to confirm carrion consumption in case of doubt (i.e., actual ingestion, excluding bites, pecking, and exploratory behaviours). Photo‐mode cameras were programmed to capture three images every 30 s upon motion detection, while video‐mode cameras recorded 20‐s clips at 1‐min intervals when detecting movement. Monitoring continued until the carcass was totally consumed, leaving only bones, skin, or feathers. All carcasses were checked at least every 3 days to ensure accurate data collection and to check the condition of the camera traps, including battery levels and memory cards.

Data Analysis

Our analyses aimed to test whether phylogenetic relatedness between scavengers and carcasses influences carrion consumption patterns. To this end, we first constructed a species‐level phylogenetic tree including all scavenger and carcass species recorded in the study. The consensus tree was obtained from the Open Tree of Life (OTL; https://tree.opentreeoflife.org; Hinchliff et al. 2015), which publicly provides taxonomic and phylogenetic information across taxa. Branch lengths were computed to account for (1) phylogenetic distance between pairs of species (Revell 2012) and the resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized using the plot.phylo function from the ggtree package (Yu et al. 2017; Figure 1).

We then assessed the effect of phylogenetic distance on carrion consumption using three complementary approaches. First, once pairwise phylogenetic distance between scavenger and carcass species was obtained, we calculated for each scavenger the (2) observed phylogenetic distance as the weighted average distance to the carcass species actually consumed, with weights corresponding to the number of carcasses of each species consumed. To establish a baseline under a random scenario, we also computed the (3) expected phylogenetic distance which corresponded to the weighted average distance to all available carcass species, weighted by carcass availability – number of carcasses of each species available. Thus, for each scavenger species, we obtained two values: observed phylogenetic distance and the expected phylogenetic distance. We then used these paired values to perform a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (stats package, function wilcox.test; R Core Team 2023), which allowed us to assess whether, at the community level, scavengers tended to consume carcasses that were more or less phylogenetically distant than expected by chance. Results of the Wilcoxon test were visualized with a boxplot created using the ggplot function from the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016).

Second, to explore how phylogenetic distance influenced the likelihood of carcass consumption, we fitted binomial Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) using the glmer function from the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015), modelling all possible scavenger‐carcass interactions (n = 841) as the response variable (4) probability of scavenging, defined for each interaction as a binary indicator of whether the carcass was consumed (1) or not (0). Fixed effects included phylogenetic distance and (5) scavenger taxonomic class; indicating whether the scavenger involved in each interaction was a bird or a mammal, and scavenger species was included as a random effect to account for interspecific variation (Table S2). Variance inflation factors indicated low collinearity among predictors of both models (VIF = 1.19).

Third, to examine whether the number of carcasses consumed by each species varied with phylogenetic distance, we fitted Poisson Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) using the glm function from the base stats package (R Core Team 2023). The response variable, (6) number of consumed carcasses, was defined for each interaction in which consumption occurred, representing the total number of carcasses of that species consumed in that interaction. Analyses were therefore restricted to these interactions, as our interest was to explore patterns among effective consumers. This approach focuses on the recurrence of scavenger consumption across carcasses rather than on carcass availability, thereby minimizing potential biases. Explanatory variables included phylogenetic distance and scavenger taxonomic class (Table S3).

For both GLMMs and GLMs, we compared additive and interaction models and selected the best‐fitting model based on Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC). When the difference between competing models was less than two AIC units, we selected the model with fewer parameters, following the principle of parsimony. Model explanatory power was quantified using pseudo‐R 2 indices. For GLMMs, we computed marginal (R 2m) and conditional (R 2c) coefficients of determination using the r.squared GLMM function (MuMIn package; Bartoń 2024). For GLMs, McFadden's pseudo‐R 2 was calculated using the pR 2 function (pscl package; Jackman 2024), since other indices (Cox & Snell and Nagelkerke) do not apply for Poisson models. Lastly, the selected models were visualized using the ggplot function from the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016).

Finally, to explore species‐specific patterns, we calculated for each scavenger species the difference between the observed phylogenetic distance and the expected phylogenetic distance, indicating whether a species consumed phylogenetically closer (negative values) or more distant (positive values) carcasses relative to random expectations. These values were then visualized using the ggplot function from the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016) to describe the direction and magnitude of phylogenetic selectivity among scavenger species.

Results

A total of 26 facultative scavenger species were recorded feeding on carrion by camera traps, including nine mammals, 16 birds, and one reptile ( Malpolon monspessulanus ). For the analyses, domestic dog ( Canis lupus familiaris ) and feral cat ( Felis catus ) were excluded, as their scavenging behaviour may be influenced by human‐related factors, resulting in a final dataset of 24 wild scavenger species (Table S4). Notably, five species acted both as scavengers and as carcasses: the birds Erithacus rubecula , Gallinula chloropus , Porphyrio porphyrio , and Ardea cinerea , as well as the mammal Rattus norvegicus (Figure 1; Tables S1 and S4). No cases of conspecific scavenging were observed.

At the scavenger assemblage level, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test indicated that scavengers tended to consume carcasses that were more phylogenetically distant than expected under a random scenario (W = 177, p‐value = 0.02; Figure 2).

Error bars represent the standard deviation, and shaded areas depict the data distribution. The boxes represent the interquartile range (IQR), showing the middle 50% of the data, while the horizontal black line inside each box indicates the median value.

Among the models tested, the Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) that best explained the probability of scavenging ‐the likelihood that a scavenger feeds on a given carcass species‐ included phylogenetic distance as the only explanatory variable, without accounting for the scavenger taxonomic class (Table S2). The model indicated that the likelihood of a scavenger species feeding on a given carcass increased significantly with phylogenetic distance (p‐value < 0.001; Figure 3A). The model showed a marginal R 2 of 0.137, indicating the variance explained by fixed effects, and a conditional R 2 of 0.316, reflecting the variance explained by both fixed and random effects (Table S2).

(B) Number of consumed carcasses ‐the number of consumed carcasses of a given carcass species‐ as a function of phylogenetic distance.

Moreover, the Generalized Linear Model (GLM) that best accounted for variation in the number of consumed carcasses–the number of consumed carcasses of a given carcass species‐ by scavenger species included phylogenetic distance, scavenger taxonomic class, and their interaction (Table S3). The model revealed an overall positive relationship between the number of consumed carcasses and phylogenetic distance (p‐value < 0.001; Figure 3B). However, the pattern differed between birds and mammals: at short and intermediate phylogenetic distances, mammals consumed fewer carcasses than birds, whereas at high phylogenetic distances, carrion consumption increased sharply in mammals and more gradually in birds. Both the effect of scavenger taxonomic class and its interaction with phylogenetic distance were statistically significant (p‐value < 0.001). The model showed a McFadden's pseudo‐R 2 of 0.267 (Table S3).

While we observed a general tendency for scavengers to feed on phylogenetically distant carcasses, species‐level patterns ‐quantified as the difference between the observed and the expected phylogenetic distance‐ revealed substantial interspecific variation (Figure 4; Table S4). Among the 24 species in the assemblage, half of them (50%, n = 12) consumed carcasses that were more distantly related than expected by chance, 29.2% (n = 7) fed on phylogenetically closer carcasses, and the remaining 20.8% (n = 5) showed no clear preference.

Phylogenetic patterns of carrion consumption at the species level. The plot shows the difference in phylogenetic distance (in million years, myr) between the expected weighted mean distance based on available carcasses and the observed weighted mean distance based on consumed carcasses, for each scavenger species of birds (blue), mammals (green) and reptiles (grey). Dots represent the mean difference in phylogenetic distance, with the total number of carcasses consumed by each species indicated inside the dots, and horizontal lines indicate confidence intervals (likely 95%). Positive values indicate that species consumed carrion from more distantly related taxa than expected by chance, while negative values indicate consumption of more closely related taxa.

Within the group that preferentially consumed more phylogenetically distant carcasses, Rattus norvegicus stood out as the main consumer of the assemblage, feeding on 38 carcasses, mostly fish. A comparable pattern was observed in Gallinula chloropus (12 carcasses) and Larus michahellis (six carcasses), both of which primarily consumed fish but occasionally scavenged on mammals and birds. Similarly, Ardea cinerea (five carcasses) and Chroicocephalus ridibundus (seven carcasses) also fed exclusively on fish, matching this trend.

Conversely, among the species that fed on more phylogenetically closely related carcasses, two bird species were especially notable: Circus aeruginosus and Lanius meridionalis . Circus aeruginosus scavenged nearly equally on birds and mammals (seven carcasses), while Lanius meridionalis fed exclusively on birds (six carcasses).

Species showing no clear phylogenetic preference included Sus scrofa , which tended to consume phylogenetically distant carcasses (12 carcasses), although its confidence interval overlapped with the expectation under a random scenario. Similarly, two of the main scavengers – Vulpes (30 carcasses) and Pica (27 carcasses)‐ did not deviate from random expectations and exhibited no apparent phylogenetic bias in carcass selection.

Finally, for 37.5% (n = 9) of the species, only a single scavenged carcass was recorded. Among these, four species consumed carcasses more distantly related than expected by chance (e.g., Erithacus rubecula , Porphyrio porphyrio ), whereas five fed on more closely related carcasses (e.g., Falco tinnunculus , Malpolon monspessulanus , Martes foina ).

Discussion

Here, we provide the first empirical evidence from a diverse vertebrate scavenger assemblage that phylogenetic distance is a key driver of selective carrion consumption. Our study addresses a key gap in scavenger foraging ecology. While previous research has explored how resource characteristics such as carcass size, weight, and type influence scavenger behaviour (e.g., DeVault et al. 2003; Moleón et al. 2015; Orihuela‐Torres et al. 2025), much less attention has been given to whether phylogenetic relationships between scavengers and carcass species shape carrion use (e.g., Moleón et al. 2017; Gonzálvez, Martinez‐Carrasco, et al. 2021). We found a consistent pattern across the assemblage: scavengers generally avoided consuming carcasses of phylogenetically related species. This trend held for both the probability of consuming a given carcass species and the total number of carcasses consumed, although the strength of the relationship varied between taxonomic classes (birds vs. mammals). While most species adhered to this pattern, a few consumed carcasses of closely related species, indicating notable species‐level variation. Overall, such selective avoidance behaviour may reflect an evolutionary strategy to reduce pathogen‐transmission risk (Huang et al. 2014; Sarabian et al. 2023).

These findings can be framed within the conceptual model of host defence strategies ‐avoidance, resistance, and tolerance‐ where behavioural avoidance represents a highly effective first line of defence. By detecting and steering clear of potential sources of infection before contact, hosts can effectively reduce both infection risk and the energetic costs associated with immune responses (Poirotte et al. 2019; Sarabian et al. 2023). We therefore hypothesize that the general avoidance of phylogenetically related carcasses by vertebrate scavengers is a manifestation of this evolutionary strategy. The underlying mechanism is explained by disease ecology; phylogenetically proximate species are more likely to share physiological traits and, consequently, pathogens, facilitating cross‐species transmission (Davies and Pedersen 2008; Huang et al. 2014; Stephens et al. 2016). This principle, supported by multiple experimental studies (Faria et al. 2013; Longdon et al. 2011; Streicker et al. 2010), establishes phylogenetic similarity as a major predictor of host shifts for vertebrate pathogens (Shaw et al. 2020). While other factors like geographic overlap further modulate this risk (Albery et al. 2020; Davies and Pedersen 2008) and exceptions exist where pathogens jump to phylogenetically distant hosts (Cooper et al. 2012), the overall pattern is consistent with the trends observed in our study.

However, when examining this general pattern at finer taxonomic scales (class and species), we observed some variations. At the class level, mammals consumed fewer carcasses than birds at short and intermediate phylogenetic distances, but their consumption increased sharply at larger distances, whereas birds exhibited a gradual increase across the phylogenetic gradient. These differences likely reflect class‐specific sensory and behavioural traits, such as reliance on sight versus olfaction, which influence risk assessment strategies (Moleón et al. 2019). At the species level, the general pattern of avoiding phylogenetically close carcasses was mostly maintained, although some species occasionally fed on closely related carcasses. For several species, only a few feeding events were recorded, highlighting that increased sampling would capture more consumption events and allow a better understanding of individual variation in carrion selection. It is worth highlighting the pattern observed among species with a higher number of recorded feeding events. Among those with more than one documented event, only two species ‐Circus aeruginosus and Lanius meridionalis ‐ showed a tendency to consume phylogenetically close carcasses, likely due to their major consumption of bird carcasses.

Conversely, a notable pattern of consuming phylogenetically distant carcasses was observed in Rattus norvegicus and Gallinula chloropus , the two species with the highest number of feeding events showing this pattern. Similarly, Sus scrofa ‐although its confidence interval overlapped with the expected value under a random scenario‐ tended to feed on phylogenetically distant carcasses. Supporting this, a study deploying 91 Sus scrofa carcasses reported that wild pigs visited 33% of the carcasses but did not consume any of them (Leivers et al. 2023). Other species with a high number of recorded feeding events, such as Pica pica and Vulpes vulpes , exhibited no apparent phylogenetic bias in carcass selection.

We hypothesize that species‐level differences in this pattern may reflect not only phylogenetic factors but also individual‐scale influences such as behavioural tendencies (e.g., dietary preferences, degree of sociability), physiological conditions (e.g., health status; life stage) or circumstantial factors (e.g., food scarcity; Fodrie et al. 2012; Mukherjee and Heithaus 2013). Such factors could also explain occasional events of conspecific consumption, as documented in previous studies (Gonzálvez, Martínez‐Carrasco, and Moleón 2021; Gonzálvez, Martinez‐Carrasco, et al. 2021; Muther et al. 2024). This pattern may be more evident in facultative scavengers that rely on carrion at low or moderate levels, as they may adopt stronger behavioural avoidance to mitigate infection risk. In contrast, species that depend heavily or exclusively on carrion, such as vultures, might not exhibit this avoidance pattern due to specific physiological adaptations that reduce pathogen transmission risk during carcass consumption (Chen et al. 2023; Chung et al. 2015; Houston 1975; Shivik 2006; Wise and Kent 2025; Zou et al. 2021). Instead, these species may rely more on pathogen resistance or tolerance than on avoidance (Sarabian et al. 2023).

While our results provide novel insights into carrion consumption patterns among avian and mammalian facultative scavengers, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, one potential concern is that fish are absent as scavengers in the terrestrial component of our study system. However, this does not affect our conclusions, because fish carcasses represent a resource that is equally ‐or even more‐ available to terrestrial scavengers, which can freely choose which carcasses to consume. Moreover, these carcasses were placed along shorelines, where fish naturally accumulate upon death, ensuring realistic availability for scavengers. Importantly, although the fish species used (common carp) is non‐native, it has been present in the Segura River basin ‐which directly supplies water to El Hondo wetland‐ since at least 1930 (Oliva‐Paterna et al. 2014), and has been established in the Iberian Peninsula since its introduction during the 16th‐17th centuries (Vilizzi 2012). It is now a well‐established component of the natural park community, making it unlikely to represent a novel resource for native scavengers or to bias their feeding behaviour. Second, the relatively low number of feeding events recorded for some species limits species‐level inference of this pattern and highlights the inherent difficulty of fully capturing all trophic interactions in a natural system. In addition, our study was conducted within a single geographical area, which constrains the generalization of these findings to other regions and scavenger communities. Expanding future research to include multiple study areas and ecosystem types will be essential to assess whether similar phylogenetic patterns occur elsewhere. Nevertheless, working within a single, well‐defined area and time period allowed us to control for spatial and temporal variation in local scavenger populations, habitat structure, and resource availability, ensuring that the observed patterns primarily reflect responses to carcass phylogenetic distances. Future research should build on this approach by adopting coordinated and standardized sampling designs across regions. This would ideally involve deploying carcasses from multiple vertebrate classes ‐including birds, mammals, reptiles, amphibians (e.g., Abernethy et al. 2017) and fish‐ across different ecosystems and along complete annual cycles, ensuring comparability in carcass type, placement protocols, and monitoring effort. Importantly, incorporating ecosystems where obligate scavengers are present will also be essential, as their inclusion may significantly influence scavenging dynamics and community structure, due to their adaptations for carrion consumption (Roggenbuck et al. 2014). Increasing the diversity and number of carcasses would improve the representation of potential trophic interactions and enable more robust testing of phylogenetic preferences at broader scales. Moreover, unmeasured variables such as decomposition stage (Wenting et al. 2022), and temporal changes in microbiological and parasitological load (Badagliacca et al. 2016; Jiranantasak et al. 2022) could also influence carrion selection patterns. Integrating these aspects in future research would provide a more comprehensive understanding of scavenger behaviour and its potential implications for zoonotic disease dynamics and ecosystem health (Lerner and Berg 2015).

Our findings suggest the existence of a natural mechanism by which scavengers may avoid pathogens when selecting carcasses, as evidenced by the general preference for phylogenetically distant carcasses. This behaviour may have important implications for both zoonotic and public health, potentially reducing pathogen transmission risks through natural scavenging processes. From a conservation perspective, understanding these selective patterns highlights the ecological role of scavengers not only as key agents in nutrient cycling and food‐web stability but also as contributors to disease regulation (Barton et al. 2013; Vicente and Vercauteren 2019; Wilson and Wolkovich 2011). Recognizing and preserving healthy scavenger communities may therefore provide both ecosystem‐level benefits and contribute to mitigating zoonotic risks (Lerner and Berg 2015). These insights highlight the importance of integrating scavenger species into broader wildlife management and conservation frameworks.

Table S2: Results of generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) testing the probability of scavenging events by vertebrate scavengers in relation to phylogenetic distance from the carcass species and scavenger taxonomic class (Aves [bird] vs. Mammalia [mammal]). Both additive and interaction effects were evaluated, with estimates, variance of random effect (scavenger species), standard errors (SE), standard deviation of random effect (SD), z‐values, p‐values (significant p‐values are highlighted in bold), Model Akaike Information Criterion (AIC; the AIC of the best‐fitting model is highlighted in bold), marginal R 2 (R 2m; variance explained by fixed effects), and conditional R 2 (R 2c; variance explained by both fixed and random effects).

Table S3: Results of the generalized linear models (GLMs) testing the number of carcasses of each species consumed by vertebrate scavengers as a function of phylogenetic distance and scavenger taxonomic class (Aves [bird] vs. Mammalia [mammal]). Both additive and interaction effects were evaluated, with estimates, standard errors (SE), z‐values, p‐values (significant p‐values are highlighted in bold) and Model Akaike Information Criterion (AIC; the AIC of the best‐fitting model is highlighted in bold) reported for each model. McFadden's pseudo‐R 2 is provided as a measure of model fit.
