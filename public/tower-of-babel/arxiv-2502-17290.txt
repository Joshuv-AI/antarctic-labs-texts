Tunneling between magnetic wells in two dimensions
Søren Fournais, Yannick Guedes Bonthonneau, Léo Morin, Nicolas Raymond

Abstract
. The two-dimensional magnetic Laplacian is considered. We calculate the leading term of the splitting between the first two eigenvalues of the operator in the semiclassical limit under the assumption that the magnetic field does not vanish and has two symmetric magnetic wells with respect to the coordinate axes. This is the first result of quantum tunneling between purely magnetic wells under generic assumptions. The proof, which strongly relies on microlocal analysis, reveals a purely magnetic Agmon distance between the wells. Surprisingly, it is discovered that the exponential decay of the eigenfunctions away from the magnetic wells is not crucial to derive the tunneling formula. The key is a microlocal exponential decay inside the characteristic manifold, with respect to the variable quantizing the classical center guide motion.



1.
Introduction and statement of the result

1.1.
The tunneling effect

This article considers the spectral theory of the magnetic Laplacian in two dimensions. It focuses on the description of a fundamental phenomenon arising in quantum physics, which has no classical counterpart, namely the tunneling effect.
The standard quantity measuring this effect is the spectral gap between the first two eigenvalues in a double-well situation.

In the purely electric situation, i.e. when considering the Schrödinger operator
-h^{2}\Delta+V

and assuming that the electric potential has two identical wells, mapped to each other by a symmetry,
the spectral gap between the first eigenvalues has been accurately calculated in the semiclassical limit
h\to 0
in the seminal papers by Simon
[
36
,
37
]
and by Helffer-Sjöstrand
[
24
,
25
]
. These authors have strongly contributed to our understanding of the eigenfunctions in the semiclassical limit. In particular, the localization properties near the minima of
V
have been quantified thanks to the famous Agmon-Lithner estimates
[
1
]
cast into the semiclassical regime, and very accurate WKB approximations have been established. These were key steps in the understanding of the exponential smallness of the eigenfunctions far from the minima – the wells – of
V
, that is where the interaction between the wells occurs. A paradigmatic example of a tunneling estimate in this context can be given in one dimension, say when
V
is even and has non-degenerate minima at
\{\pm a\}
with
a>0
. In this case, the lowest eigenvalues group in pairs that are exponentially close, each pair being separated from the others by gaps of order
h
. For instance, we have (see the original article
[
16
]
or the pedagogical articles
[
35
,
4
]
):

\lambda_{2}(h)-\lambda_{1}(h)=(1+o(1))ch^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-S/h}\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad S=\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{V}\mathrm{d}x\,,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad c>0\,,

where the coefficient
c
can be explicitly computed in terms of
V
. The key elements of the Helffer-Sjöstrand theory are described in the lecture notes
[
17
]
and in
[
8
]
.

1.2.
Tunneling in the presence of magnetic fields

Shortly after their analysis of the purely electric tunneling effect, Helffer and Sjöstrand considered the case when a small magnetic field is added
[
26
]
. More precisely, they considered the operator
(-ih\nabla-b\mathbf{A})^{2}+V
with
b
small enough and with
V
satisfying similar assumptions as in their previous works. This allowed them to establish similar tunneling formulas in weak magnetic fields but the general question of tunneling in magnetic fields remained open. Very recently, this situation has been reconsidered in
[
10
]
(in two dimensions) where the authors were able in a special setup to remove the smallness assumption on
b
and to prove that the spectral gap does not close in the semiclassical limit. These authors consider the special case when
V
has radial wells and the magnetic field is constant between them. Soon after followed
[
20
]
where the exponential smallness of the gap was calculated (see also
[
21
]
). In
[
31
]
, the one-term asymptotics of the spectral gap was established by means of a concise and elementary presentation inspired by the original strategy of Helffer-Sjöstrand and by the key remark of
[
10
]
that in this example, the eigenfunctions coincide with Kummer special functions away from the wells.

However, the whole analysis in the latter works relied on the presence of
V
and on a reduction of dimension due to radiality. This left open the purely magnetic case when
V=0
.

Purely magnetic tunneling has been considered—still under an assumption of radial symmetry of the single-well problems—in the recent works
[
13
,
12
,
19
]
.

These works were preceded by a breakthrough understanding of magnetic tunneling in the case where the reduction of dimension was induced by a Neumann boundary condition (see
[
3
,
5
]
).
This reduction was based on the multiscale behavior of the eigenfunctions, with a stronger localization to the boundary than within the boundary itself. This gives rise to an effective Schrödinger-like operator along the boundary, which carries the tunneling effect.
This method was later succesfully applied to situations where the mechanism behind the dimensional reduction was slightly different (see
[
11
,
2
]
).
In the case we consider in this article, such a multiscale localization of the eigenfunctions is not true, and we cannot reduce to an effective one-dimensional Schrödinger operator.

1.3.
What do we know about magnetic wells?

Surprisingly, the situation in two dimensions when
B
is positive and has non-degenerate minima has been open up until rather recently. An important step in this direction was made in
[
34
]
, where the asymptotics of the lowest eigenvalues is related to the classical dynamics in a magnetic field, which is governed by the cyclotron and center guide motions. These Hamiltonian considerations, their quantization via Egorov theorems and semiclassical Birkhoff normal forms led to the following result.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
22
]
&
[
34
]
)
.

Assume that
B
is smooth and positive and that it has a unique minimum
b_{0}
at
0
, which is non-degenerate
1
1

1

In this case, we say that
B
has a ”well” at
0
.
. Consider a smooth magnetic potential
\mathbf{A}
, i.e. a function such that
\nabla\times\mathbf{A}=B
and let
\mathscr{L}_{h}=(-ih\nabla-\mathbf{A})^{2}
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
. For
h
small enough, the
n
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}(h)
exists and belongs to the discrete spectrum of
\mathscr{L}_{h}
and

\lambda_{n}(h)=b_{0}h+((2nd_{0}+d_{1})h^{2}+o(h^{2})\,,

where

d_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{\det H}}{b_{0}}\,,\quad d_{1}=\frac{(\mathrm{Tr}H^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}}{2b_{0}}\,,\quad H=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Hess}B(0)\,.

In
[
34
]
, it is established that the eigenfunctions are microlocalized in phase space near
(x,\xi)=(0,0)
at the scale
h^{\frac{1}{2}}
(say if
\mathbf{A}(0)=0
). In particular, their space localization near the minimum of
B
is isotropic contrary the multiscale cases evoked in the previous section. Note also that these localization estimates are not exponential estimates
à la
Agmon: they only give that the eigenfunctions are
\mathcal{O}(h^{\infty})
away from
D(0,h^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta})
, with
\eta>0
. By working a little more, we can prove a decay of order
\mathscr{O}(e^{-C/\sqrt{h}})
, see
[
15
, Section 1.4]
. This decay can be used to get a rough estimate of tunneling when
B
has a symmetric double well (see the discussion in
[
13
, Prop. 3.1 & 3.2]
):

(1.1)

\lambda_{2}(\mathscr{L})-\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{L})=\mathscr{O}(h^{\infty})\,.

Such rough estimates were already known in
[
23
, Remark 3.4]
, where tunneling between pure magnetic wells is mentioned as an open problem.

Under analyticity assumptions on
B
, it has been possible to prove a quasi-optimal exponential decay of the eigenfunctions away from the minimum of
B
in
[
15
]
by means of the Fourier-Bros-Iagolnitzer transform, but without exhibiting an explicit and natural Agmon distance. Let us describe this estimate. Let
M>0
. There exist
h_{0},C>0
such that, for all
h\in(0,h_{0})
, any eigenfunction
\psi
associated with
\lambda\leqslant b_{0}h+Mh^{2}
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{2d(x)/h}|\psi(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leqslant C\|\psi\|^{2}\,,

where
d
is a smooth function quadratic near
0
. Obtaining such an estimate was motivated by the question of the optimality of WKB constructions done in
[
6
,
14
]
in the case of a non-degenerate minimum of
B
.

The case when
B
is radial is considered in
[
14
]
. In this case, we can show that the groundstate is radial and coincides with the groundstate of an electric Schrödinger operator. Thanks to the Helffer-Sjöstrand theory, this shows that the groundstate is approximated by the WKB Ansatz. This key property, reducing the analysis to the electric situation, and the observation in
[
10
]
about the Kummer functions motivated the article
[
13
]
, which establishes the first tunneling formula between pure radial magnetic wells.

1.4.
Main result: a generic magnetic tunneling formula

The present article aims at going beyond the radiality assumption. In this case we do not dispose of sufficiently good approximations of the eigenfunctions since we can neither use explicit special functions nor suitable WKB approximations inherited from the electric situation.

We consider the
2
-dimensional magnetic Laplacian

\mathscr{L}=(-ih\nabla-\mathbf{A})^{2}\,,

on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
and denote the coordinates in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by
q=(q_{1},q_{2})
.
Due to the gauge invariance, we may assume that
\mathbf{A}
has the form

(1.2)

\mathbf{A}=(0,A_{2})\,,\quad A_{2}(q)=\int_{0}^{q_{1}}B(s,q_{2})\mathrm{d}s\,,

where
B(q_{1},q_{2})
denotes the magnetic field.
We work with a magnetic field that has precisely two non-degenerate minima. We assume symmetry of the magnetic field as well as partial analyticity in one of the coordinates (Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
). Furthermore, there is an important assumption on the complex level curve connecting the two minima (Assumption
1.5
). Finally, we also impose the technical Assumption
1.7
.

More precisely the statements are as follows.

Assumption 1.2
.

We consider a smooth magnetic field
B=B(q_{1},q_{2})
with exactly two minima, which are positive and not attained at infinity.
We assume that
B
is symmetric under reflection in the two coordinate axis,

B(-q_{1},q_{2})=B(q_{1},-q_{2})=B(q)\,,\quad\forall q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

We also assume that the two minima are
C_{u}=(0,c_{u})
and
C_{d}=(0,c_{d})
with
c_{u}>0
and
c_{d}=-c_{u}
, that
B(C_{u}):=b_{0}>0
, and that
C_{u}
is a non-degenerate minimum, that is

\mathrm{Hess}_{C_{u}}B>0\,.

Note that the symmetry assumptions imply that
\mathrm{Hess}_{C_{u}}B
is diagonal. Our choice of gauge is compatible with the symmetry of
B
in the sense that

U\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{L}U\,,

where
Uf(q)=f(-q)
.

Let us now describe our other general assumptions.

Assumption 1.3

(Partial analyticity and mild variations)
.

There exist
r,\eta_{0}>0
such that the following holds.

—

The functions
q_{1}\mapsto B(q_{1},q_{2})
and
q_{1}\mapsto\partial_{2}A_{2}(q_{1},q_{2})
are analytic in the strip
U_{r}:=\{q_{1}\in\mathbb{C}:|\mathrm{Im}\,q_{1}|<r\}
, uniformly in
q_{2}
, and their complex extensions belong to the symbol class

S(U_{r}\times\mathbb{R})=\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U_{r}\times\mathbb{R}):\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\,,\partial^{\alpha}f\in L^{\infty}(U_{r}\times\mathbb{R})\}\,.

—

The complex extension of
B
has mild variations in the sense that, for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we have, for all
q\in U_{r}\times\mathbb{R}
,

(1.3)

|B(q_{1},q_{2})-b_{0}|\leqslant\varepsilon b_{0}\,,\quad|\partial_{2}B(q_{1},q_{2})|\leqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)b_{0}}{2r}\,.

Remark 1.4
.

Assumption
1.3
implies, for all
q\in U_{r}\times\mathbb{R}
,

(1.4)

\mathrm{Re}\,B(q_{1},q_{2})\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)b_{0}\,.

Then, we assume that there exists a unique complex path connecting the minima
C_{u}
and
C_{d}
, which are disconnected in the real plane.

Assumption 1.5

(Complex path connecting the minima)
.

There exists a smooth function
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\|\gamma\|_{\infty}<r
and

B(i\gamma(q_{2}),q_{2})=b_{0}\,,\quad\gamma(c_{u})=\gamma(c_{d})=0\,,\quad\gamma^{\prime}(c_{d})>0\,,\quad\gamma^{\prime}(c_{u})<0\,.

Moreover,
B(q_{1},q_{2})-b_{0}
vanishes only on the curves
q_{1}=\pm i\gamma(q_{2})
, and in a non-degenerate way:

\partial_{1}B(i\gamma(q_{2}),q_{2})\neq 0,\quad\forall q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{c_{d},c_{u}\}.

This assumption turns out to be crucial since
\gamma
appears in our tunneling formula. It is a natural assumption in the following sense. Since
{\rm Hess}_{C_{u}}B
is diagonal and non-degenerate, there must always be exactly two complex curves along which
B-b_{0}
vanishes on a small neighborhood of the wells. Due to the
q_{1}
-symmetry we also have
B(q_{1},q_{2})=b_{0}\Leftrightarrow B(-\overline{q_{1}},q_{2})=b_{0}
, and therefore such a curve must be purely imaginary. We simply assume that these curves defined on a neighborhood of
c_{u}
and
c_{d}
match each other following a global curve
q_{1}=\pm i\gamma(q_{2})
.

Remark 1.6
.

Note that the function
\gamma
is even due to the horizontal symmetry
B(q_{1},q_{2})=B(q_{1},-q_{2})
. Moreover, Assumption
1.5
implies

(1.5)

i\partial_{1}B(i\gamma(q_{2}),q_{2})\gamma(q_{2})<0,\quad q_{2}\neq c_{u},c_{d}\,.

Indeed, since
{\rm Hess}_{C_{u}}B
is diagonal and non-degenerate, one easily checks that (
1.5
) holds on a neighborhood of
c_{u}
. Since
i\partial_{1}B
does not vanish along the curve
\gamma
, it must stay negative for all
q_{2}
.

Finally, we will also work under the following simplifying assumption.

Assumption 1.7
.

For all
(q_{1},q_{2})\in U_{r}\times\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathrm{Re}\,q_{1}\geqslant 0\Longrightarrow\mathrm{Re}\,\frac{\partial_{1}B}{B}(q_{1},q_{2})\geqslant 0\,.

For real
q_{1}
, Assumption
1.7
tells us that
B
increases away from the vertical axis. In fact, this assumption could probably be removed using additional microlocal estimates in the proof of Lemma
4.2
. However, since it significantly simplifies the proof and does not hide any other interesting features on the tunneling effect, we decide to work under Assumption
1.7
.

Under the above assumptions we are able to calculate the tunneling.

Theorem 1.8
.

Under Assumptions
1.2
,
1.3
,
1.5
and
1.7
, there exists
c_{0}>0
such that, in the semiclassical limit
h\to 0
,

(1.6)

\lambda_{2}(\mathscr{L})-\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{L})=c_{0}h^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-S/h}(1+o(1))\,,

where

(1.7)

S=\int_{c_{d}}^{c_{u}}\int_{0}^{\gamma(q_{2})}B(it,q_{2})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}q_{2}>0\,.

Remark 1.9
.

Let us make some comments on Theorem
1.8
.

(i)

Tunneling between purely magnetic wells remained an important open problem left open after the works of Helffer-Sjöstrand in the 1980’s. It is for example mentioned as an open problem in
[
23
, Remark 3.4]
. Theorem
1.8
actually disproves their conjecture that the order of magnetic tunneling should be
\mathscr{O}(e^{-C/\sqrt{h}})
.

(ii)

We deduce that, when
h
is small enough, the lowest eigenvalue is simple.

(iii)

We have an explicit but complicated formula for
c_{0}
, see (
A.5
).

(iv)

The action
S
is a flux of
B
through a surface in the complex plane.
In the formula (
1.7
) one can also interpret
S
as a purely magnetic Agmon distance. This quantity has never appeared in the literature before, to the best of our knowledge.

(v)

The tunneling formula (
1.6
) is the first general formula measuring optimally the exponentially small gap between the eigenvalues of a Laplacian with pure magnetic wells. Thereby, it is the only general magnetic analogue of the electric tunneling results
à la
Helffer-Sjöstrand. We explain in Section
1.5
below how strongly the proof of Theorem
1.8
deviates from the original strategy in
[
24
,
25
]
. In particular, our strategy allows to establish optimal tunneling results for a class of pseudodifferential operators in two dimensions.

Example 1.10
.

As an example, we consider the following familly of magnetic fields which satisfy our assumptions. Let
b_{0}>0
and define, for
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\in(0,1]
,

B(q)=b_{0}(1+\varepsilon_{1}g(q_{1})+\varepsilon_{2}\langle q_{1}\rangle^{-2}W(q_{2}))\,,

with

g(q_{1}):=1-\langle q_{1}\rangle^{-2}\,,

where
0\leqslant W\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
is a smooth, even function with a double minimum at
c_{u}>0
and
c_{d}=-c_{u}
, which is non-degenerate, and with minimal value
0
. In this case,
A_{2}
and
B
satisfy Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
, if
\varepsilon_{2}
is small enough.

Assume that
\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}\varepsilon_{2}\leqslant 1
.
We want to solve
B(q)=b_{0}
, that is

q_{1}^{2}=-\varepsilon_{3}W(q_{2})
with
\varepsilon_{3}=\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}\varepsilon_{2}
.
We find the curves

q_{1}=\pm i\sqrt{\varepsilon_{3}W(q_{2})}\,.

In particular, we have

|\mathrm{Im}\,q_{1}|=|q_{1}|\leqslant\sqrt{\varepsilon_{3}}\sqrt{W(q_{2})}\,.

Then, the smooth function

\gamma(q_{2})=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{3}W(q_{2})}&\text{ when }q_{2}\in(c_{d},c_{u})\,,\\
-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{3}W(q_{2})}&\text{ else },\end{cases}

solves the equation
B(i\gamma(q_{2}),q_{2})=b_{0}\,.
Moreover, for
q_{2}>c_{u}
,
\gamma^{\prime}(q_{2})=-\frac{\varepsilon_{3}W^{\prime}(q_{2})}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{3}W(q_{2})}}
and thus, when
q_{2}\to c_{u}
with
q_{2}>c_{u}
,

\gamma^{\prime}(c_{u})=-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{3}}\sqrt{\frac{W^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})}{2}}<0\,.

We finally check that
Assumption
1.7
is satisfied if
\varepsilon_{2}\leqslant\varepsilon_{1}
. Indeed, we have

\frac{\partial_{1}B}{B}(q_{1},q_{2})=\frac{2a(q_{2})q_{1}}{(1+q_{1}^{2})(1+q_{1}^{2}-a(q_{2}))}\,,\quad\mbox{ with }\quad a(q_{2})=\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}W(q_{2})}{1+\varepsilon_{1}}\,.

Note that
a(\mathbb{R})\subset[0,\varepsilon_{1}]
. Then, take
(q_{1},q_{2})\in U_{r}\times\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{Re}\,q_{1}\geqslant 0
. The sign of
\mathrm{Re}\,\frac{\partial_{1}B}{B}(q_{1},q_{2})
is the same as that of

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Re}\,\left(q_{1}(1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2})(1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2}-a)\right)\\
&=x\mathrm{Re}\,\left((1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2})(1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2}-a)\right)-y\mathrm{Im}\,\left((1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2})(1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2}-a)\right)\,,\end{split}

with
q_{1}=x+iy
. We have

\begin{split}\mathrm{Re}\,\left((1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2})(1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2}-a)\right)&=(1+x^{2}-y^{2})(1-a+x^{2}-y^{2})-4x^{2}y^{2}\,,\\
\mathrm{Im}\,\left((1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2})(1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2}-a)\right)&=-2ixy(2-a+2x^{2}-2y^{2})\,.\end{split}

We deduce that, if
r>0
is small enough,
\mathrm{Re}\,\left(q_{1}(1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2})(1+\overline{q}_{1}^{2}-a)\right)\geqslant 0
and thus
\mathrm{Re}\,\frac{\partial_{1}B}{B}(q_{1},q_{2})\geqslant 0
.

1.5.
Organization, heuristics and strategy

Let us now describe our strategy. As observed in
[
34
,
32
]
, a key to describing the spectrum of the magnetic Laplacian is to use suitable coordinates in which the magnetic field is constant. Indeed, the structure of the low-lying spectrum is related to the characteristic manifold
M:=\{(q,p):p-A(q)=0\}
, which also determines the Hamiltonian dynamics at low energy. The manifold
M
is symplectic since
\mathrm{d}p\wedge\mathrm{d}q_{|M}=B(q)\mathrm{d}q_{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}q_{2}\neq 0
. This suggests to use the diffeomorphism given by
\iota(q)=(A_{2}(q_{1},q_{2}),\,q_{2})=x
so that
\iota^{-1}_{*}(\mathrm{d}p\wedge\mathrm{d}q_{|M})=\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{2}
. The analyticity properties of
\iota
are analyzed in Section
2.1
. This change of variable conjugates
\mathscr{L}
to a differential operator whose principal symbol is

\mathcal{B}(x_{1},x_{2})^{2}\xi_{1}^{2}+\left(\xi_{2}-x_{1}-\alpha(x_{1},x_{2})\xi_{1}\right)^{2}\,,

with
\mathcal{B}=B\circ\iota^{-1}
.
This suggests to use the symplectic change of variable
y_{1}=x_{1}-\xi_{2}
,
\eta_{1}=\xi_{1}
,
y_{2}=x_{2}-\xi_{1}
,
\eta_{2}=\xi_{2}
, which can be quantized by the metaplectic Egorov theorem (see
[
27
, Theorem 18.5.9]
) so that
\mathscr{L}
is unitarily conjugated to a pseudodifferential operator whose principal symbol is

\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+x_{1},x_{2}+\xi_{1})^{2}\xi_{1}^{2}+\left(x_{1}+\alpha(\xi_{2}+x_{1},x_{2}+\xi_{1})\xi_{1}\right)^{2}\,.

Since the first eigenvalues of the magnetic Laplacian are of order
\mathcal{O}(h)
, the eigenfunctions are microlocalized in
(x_{1},\xi_{1})
near
(0,0)
at the scale
h^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Thus, we make the rescaling
(x_{1},\xi_{1})\mapsto(h^{\frac{1}{2}}x_{1},h^{\frac{1}{2}}\xi_{1})
and we get the operator
h\mathscr{M}
in Proposition
2.3
(which was already established in
[
32
]
). After a change of quantization in
x_{1}
, the principal part of the operator is (with the standard notation for the Weyl quantization):

h\mathscr{M}\approx h\mathsf{Op}^{w,2}_{h}\mathsf{Op}^{w,1}_{1}\left(\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2},x_{2})^{2}\xi_{1}^{2}+\left(x_{1}+\alpha(\xi_{2},x_{2})\xi_{1}\right)^{2}\right)\,,

which is quadratic in
(x_{1},\xi_{1})
. In the variables
(x_{1},\xi_{1})
, we have a harmonic oscillator corresponding to the well-known cyclotron motion of classical dynamics. The variables
(x_{2},\xi_{2})
represent the center guide motion, which carries the tunneling phenomenon.

The rest of the article is devoted to the study of the pseudodifferential operator
\mathscr{M}
. The fact that, from the very beginning of the analysis, one casts the problem into a pseudodifferential framework is not common at all in the literature about the magnetic Laplacian. This idea of combining a change of coordinates and a metaplectic transform might a priori seem the source of technical troubles. These canonical coordinates, which are adapted to the magnetic field, will in fact reveal an
optimal
exponential decay of the eigenfunctions that is responsible for the tunneling effect and that it would be hard to describe in the original space coordinates.

In order to understand the interaction between the magnetic wells, one needs to establish a very accurate description of the eigenfunctions when there is only one well. To achieve this, we consider the one well operator
\mathscr{M}_{u}=\mathscr{M}+\Sigma(x_{2})
by sealing the
down
well by simply adding an electric potential, see (
2.9
). Since the tunneling effect is an exponentially small effect, we must describe as accurately as possible the decay of the eigenfunctions away from the well and prove exponentially good approximations. For that purpose, we consider the conjugated operator
\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}:=e^{\Phi(x_{2})/h}\mathscr{M}_{u}e^{-\Phi(x_{2})/h}
, which is still a pseudodifferential operator with operator-valued symbol, whose principal symbol is

M_{0}(x_{2},\xi_{2})=\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})^{2}D^{2}_{x_{1}}+(x_{1}+\alpha(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})D_{x_{1}})^{2}+\Sigma(x_{2})\,,

see Proposition
2.5
and Lemma
2.7
. Note that the eigenvalues of the non-self-adjoint operator
M_{0}(x_{2},\xi_{2})
are the Landau levels (see Section
3.2.1
)

(2n-1)\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})\,,\quad n\geqslant 1\,.

The behavior of the resolvent near
b_{0}
is studied in Lemma
3.4
. We emphasize that the conjugation by
e^{-\Phi/h}
amounts to considering a
complex
magnetic field
\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})
. Whereas complex electric potentials are rather common in the literature (as in
[
32
]
), the case with complex magnetic fields seems to be widely open (see
[
29
]
). The present article gives a new motivation to study this situation.

The reader might be surprised that only weights in
x_{2}
(and not also in
x_{1}

2.1.
New coordinates

Inspired by
[
32

, we prefer to work in the new coordinates

which are adapted to the geometry of the magnetic field. They are defined through the diffeomorphism

\iota:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{q}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}_{x}
which reads

(2.1)

\iota(q)=(A_{2}(q_{1},q_{2}),\,q_{2})=x\,.

In fact, this defines Darboux coordinates for the magnetic
2
-form, meaning that
\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{2}=B(q)\mathrm{d}q_{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}q_{2}
. We recall that we chose a gauge where

, see (
1.2
). Note that these coordinates preserve the symmetries of the problem, in the sense that
\iota(-q)=-\iota(q)
and
\iota(q_{1},-q_{2})=(A_{2}(q_{1},q_{2}),-q_{2})
. The wells are also preserved since

\iota(C_{u})=(A_{2}(C_{u}),c_{u})=(0,c_{u})=C_{u}

and by symmetry
\iota(C_{d})=C_{d}
.

In these coordinates, the magnetic field is
\mathcal{B}(x)=B\circ\iota^{-1}(x)
.
The function
\mathcal{B}
still satisfies the symmetries
\mathcal{B}(-x_{1},x_{2})=\mathcal{B}(x_{1},-x_{2})=\mathcal{B}(x)
. In particular, the Hessian of
\mathcal{B}
at

is still diagonal.

We also define

(2.2)

\alpha(x)=\partial_{2}A_{2}\circ\iota^{-1}(x).

We notice that

and

satisfy Assumption
1.3

Lemma 2.1
.

If

satisfies Assumption
1.3
, then there exists
\tilde{r}>0
such that the functions
x_{1}\mapsto\mathcal{B}(x_{1},x_{2})
and

have holomorphic extensions to

for all

. Moreover, these extensions belong to

Proof.

Let us consider, for all

, the natural extension of

\tilde{A}(q_{1};q_{2}):=\int_{[0,q_{1}]}B(s,q_{2})\mathrm{d}s\,.

Then

is holomorphic in

and

because of the small variations of

(Assumption
1.3
). Thus, the range of

is open. In fact, we can show that it is a biholomorphism.

Let us consider the injectivity. We have

(2.3)

\tilde{A}(\tilde{q}_{1};q_{2})-\tilde{A}(q_{1};q_{2})=(\tilde{q}_{1}-q_{1})\int_{0}^{1}B(q_{1}+t(\tilde{q}_{1}-q_{1}),q_{2})\mathrm{d}t\,.

We notice that, for all

and all

Thus,

|\tilde{A}(\tilde{q}_{1};q_{2})-\tilde{A}(q_{1};q_{2})|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)b_{0}|\tilde{q}_{1}-q_{1}|\,,

which shows the injectivity. Let us now explain why the range contains a strip

about

. Thanks to (

) we get, for all

\mathrm{Im}\,\left(\tilde{A}(q_{1};q_{2})-\tilde{A}(\mathrm{Re}\,q_{1};q_{2})\right)=\mathrm{Im}\,q_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Re}\,B(\mathrm{Re}\,q_{1}+it\mathrm{Im}\,q_{1},q_{2})\mathrm{d}t\,,

and, for all

,

|\mathrm{Im}\,\tilde{A}(q_{1};q_{2})|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)b_{0}|\mathrm{Im}\,q_{1}|\,.

This shows the existence of the desired strip

.

Therefore, by the implicit function Theorem we can find a smooth function

on

such that

\tilde{A}(v(x_{1},x_{2}),x_{2})=x_{1}.

Taking the

-derivative we also have

. The function

h^{\frac{J+1}{2}}S(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathcal{L}(B^{J+3}(\mathbb{R}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R})))
.
∎

There is an extension of Proposition
2.5
to slightly more general weights, involving the class of
h
-dependent functions

(2.12)

S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})=\big\{\Phi(\cdot;h)\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\,;\forall n,\quad|\partial_{x}^{n}\Phi(x;h)|\leqslant C_{\alpha}h^{-\frac{n}{2}}\big\}.

Such weights will be useful when proving accurate approximation of the one-well eigenfunctions.

Lemma 2.7
.

Consider a smooth bounded real-valued function
x_{2}\mapsto\Phi(x_{2})
with
\Phi^{\prime}\in S(\mathbb{R})+h^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
such that
|\Phi^{\prime}|\leqslant\tilde{r}
. Then,

\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}:=e^{\Phi(x_{2})/h}\mathscr{M}_{u}e^{-\Phi(x_{2})/h}
is a pseudodifferential operator that can be written in the form

\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}=\mathsf{Op}^{w,2}_{h}(M_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}M_{1})+h\mathscr{R}_{h}\,,

where the symbol of
\mathscr{R}_{h}
belongs to

S_{\frac{1}{2},0}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathcal{L}(B^{4}(\mathbb{R}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\big)\\
=\big\{a\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}:\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\,,\exists C_{\alpha}>0:\|\partial^{\alpha}_{X_{2}}a\|_{\mathcal{L}(B^{4}(\mathbb{R}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))}\leqslant C_{\alpha}h^{-\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}}\big\}\,.

Proof.

Under the assumptions on
\Phi
, each time we differentiate
\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})
with respect to
x_{2}
we loose a power of
h^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Hence the same holds for the symbol
m_{u}^{\Phi}
. Proceeding as in Proposition
2.5
with
J=1
, the result follows.
∎

3.
Functional inequalities

3.1.
Statements

The inequality in the following Proposition
3.2
is the core of the Agmon-type decay estimates and of a WKB-like approximation of the groundstate of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
. It can be established under the following assumption on the weight
\Phi
. Let
\chi_{0}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
be a fixed cutoff function, equal to
1
on a neighborhood of
0
.

Assumption 3.1

(Coercivity)
.

There exists
c>0
such that the following hold. For all
R\geqslant 1
we can find
h_{0}=h_{0}(R)>0
such that, for
h\in(0,h_{0})
and
(x_{2},\xi_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the function
\Phi
satisfies

\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})-b_{0}\geqslant cR^{2}h\Big(1-\chi_{0}\left(\frac{x_{2}-c_{u}}{Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\Big)\,.

We emphasize that
\Phi
may depend on
R
and
h
. For simplicity we will use the notation
\chi_{R,h}(x)=\chi(x/Rh^{\frac{1}{2}})
. We also recall that
\mathcal{B}
has small variations, meaning that
|\mathcal{B}-b_{0}|\leqslant\varepsilon b_{0}
. The main result of this section is

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume
\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}<(1-3\varepsilon)b_{0}
, with
\varepsilon
from Assumption
1.3
. Let
C_{0}>0
, and let
\Phi
be a smooth bounded real-valued function with
\Phi^{\prime}\in S(\mathbb{R})+Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, satisfying Assumption
3.1
. Then there exists
C>0
such that for
R
large enough, we can find
h_{0}=h_{0}(R)>0
such that

R^{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant Ch^{-1}\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}+CR^{2}\|\chi_{R,h}(x_{2}-c_{u})f\|_{L^{2}}+C\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\,,

for all
h\in(0,h_{0})
, all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and all
z
satisfying
|z-b_{0}|<C_{0}h
.

Note that, when we say
\Phi^{\prime}\in Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we implicitly assume that the seminorms are uniformly bounded with respect to
R\in[1,\infty)
. This is the case for the weight we use later, see Lemma
4.3
.
From Proposition
3.2
, we deduce the following functional inequality, which avoids the complicated remainders related to the
B^{N}(\mathbb{R})
-norm.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
C_{0}>0
. If
\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}<(1-3\varepsilon)b_{0}
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds.
Let
\Phi
be a smooth, bounded, real-valued function with
\Phi^{\prime}\in S(\mathbb{R})+Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying Assumption
3.1
. Then there exist
R,h_{0},C>0
such that

\|f\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}\leqslant Ch^{-1}\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}+CR^{2}\|\chi_{R,h}(x_{2}-c_{u})f\|\,,

for all
h\in(0,h_{0})
, all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and all
z
satisfying
|z-b_{0}|<C_{0}h
.

Proof of Corollary
3.3
.

We start by establishing, for all
N\geqslant 0
,

(3.1)

\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}+1)f\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}\geqslant C\|f\|_{B^{N+2}\otimes L^{2}}.

Using the weight
W_{N}=(1+x_{1}^{2}+\xi_{1}^{2})^{\frac{N}{2}}
and its quantization
W_{N}^{w}=\mathsf{Op}_{1}^{w,1}W_{N}
, it is enough to prove

(3.2)

\|W^{w}_{N}(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}+1)(W^{w}_{N+2})^{-1}g\|_{L^{2}}\geqslant C\|g\|_{L^{2}}.

By standard Weyl calculus, using that the symbol
m_{u}^{\Phi}
behaves quadratically in
\xi_{1}
and
x_{1}
, the symbol of the operator
\mathscr{N}=W_{N}^{w}\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}(W^{w}_{N+2})^{-1}
is in
S(\mathbb{R})
, and the principal part of
\mathscr{N}^{*}\mathscr{N}
is

|W_{N}W_{N+2}^{-1}(m_{u}^{\Phi}+1)|^{2}=W_{-4}\big|m_{u}^{\Phi}+1\big|^{2}\geqslant C.

The bound (
3.2
) follows thanks to the Gårding inequality for operator-valued symbols (see
[
28
, Theorem 2.1.18]
in the case
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{B}
), and thus (
3.1
) follows. Note that the involved constant
C
can be chosen independent of
R
and
\Phi
.

Now, we use Proposition
3.2
and (
3.1
) with
N\geqslant 2
, and we deduce

R^{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant Ch^{-1}\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}+CR^{2}\|\chi_{R,h}(x_{2}-c_{u})f\|_{L^{2}}\\
+C\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}+1)f\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}},

and since
z
is bounded,

R^{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant Ch^{-1}\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}+CR^{2}\|\chi_{R,h}(x_{2}-c_{u})f\|_{L^{2}}+C\|f\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}.

Using (
3.1
) recursively, we can replace the
B^{N}
-norm of
f
by the
L^{2}
norm of
f
, up to increasing the constant
C
. Hence,

R^{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant C_{N}h^{-1}\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}+CR^{2}\|\chi_{R,h}(x_{2}-c_{u})f\|_{L^{2}}+C\|f\|_{L^{2}}.

We choose
R^{2}>C
, and the result follows for
L=0
. For larger values of
L
, we use (
3.1
) recursively again.
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

3.2.
About the principal operator symbol

We discuss basic properties of
M_{0}(X_{2})
, the principal symbol of
\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}
given in Proposition
2.5
.

3.2.1.
A harmonic oscillator with almost real coefficients

The operator
M_{0}(X_{2})
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{x_{1}})
is not selfadjoint. It has compact resolvent and its (discrete) spectrum can be computed explicitly. The spectrum is given by

\mathrm{sp}(M_{0}(X_{2}))=\big\{(2n-1)\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})\,,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}\,.

These eigenvalues are algebraically simple, in the sense that the corresponding Riesz projector is of rank one.
To see this, we can write

\begin{split}M_{0}(X_{2})&=(\mathcal{B}^{2}+\alpha^{2})D_{x_{1}}^{2}+\alpha(x_{1}D_{x_{1}}+D_{x_{1}}x_{1})+x_{1}^{2}+\Sigma\\
&=(\mathcal{B}^{2}+\alpha^{2})\left(D_{x_{1}}+\frac{\alpha}{\mathcal{B}^{2}+\alpha^{2}}x_{1}\right)^{2}+\frac{\mathcal{B}^{2}}{\mathcal{B}^{2}+\alpha^{2}}x_{1}^{2}+\Sigma\,,\end{split}

and we find a total family of eigenfunctions with the help of the Hermite functions. Note that here the functions
\mathcal{B}
and
\alpha
are taken at the point
(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})
,
even though we do not write it explicitly to lighten notations.
The normalized eigenfunction of
M_{0}
associated with the eigenvalue
\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})
is

(3.3)

\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}(x_{1})=C(X_{2})\exp\Big({-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2(\mathcal{B}-i\alpha)}}\Big)\,,

where we choose the constant
C(X_{2})>0
such that
\|\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}\|=1
. Note that this normalization is possible because
{\rm Re}\,(\mathcal{B}+i\alpha)
stays positive, see Remark
2.2
.

Taking the adjoint of
M_{0}
amounts replacing
\mathcal{B}
by
\overline{\mathcal{B}}
and
\alpha
by
\overline{\alpha}
. Thus, an eigenfunction of
M_{0}^{*}
associated with
\overline{\mathcal{B}}+\Sigma
is

(3.4)

\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}(x_{1})=c(X_{2})\exp\Big({-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2(\overline{\mathcal{B}}-i\overline{\alpha})}}\Big)\,,

where we choose the constant
c(X_{2})\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\langle\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}},\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}\rangle=1
, for later convenience.

3.2.2.
Inverting the principal operator symbol at fixed
X_{2}

Lemma 3.4
.

The operator
\langle\cdot,\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}\rangle\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}
is the Riesz projector
\Pi_{X_{2}}
of
M_{0}(X_{2})
associated with the eigenvalue
\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})
. Moreover, the operator

R_{0}(z):=(M_{0}(X_{2})-z)^{-1}(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi_{X_{2}})\,,

is a holomorphic function of
z
, for
|z-b_{0}|<b_{0}
.

Proof.

The Riesz projector is

\Pi_{X_{2}}=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\mathscr{C}}(\zeta-M_{0}(X_{2}))^{-1}\mathrm{d}\zeta

where
\mathscr{C}
is a contour encircling the isolated eigenvalue
\mathcal{B}+\Sigma
. Then, for all
z\neq\mathcal{B}+\Sigma
inside
\mathscr{C}
we have

\begin{split}\mathrm{Id}-\Pi_{X_{2}}&=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\mathscr{C}}\left((\zeta-z)^{-1}-(\zeta-M_{0}(X_{2}))^{-1}\right)\mathrm{d}\zeta\\
&=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\mathscr{C}}(\zeta-z)^{-1}(z-M_{0}(X_{2}))(\zeta-M_{0}(X_{2}))^{-1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\end{split}

so that

(3.5)

(M_{0}(X_{2})-z)^{-1}(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi_{X_{2}})=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\mathscr{C}}(\zeta-z)^{-1}(M_{0}(X_{2})-\zeta)^{-1}\mathrm{d}\zeta

is holomorphic inside
\mathscr{C}
, including at
z=\mathcal{B}+\Sigma
.

Since
\langle\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}},\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}\rangle=1
, the operator
\langle\cdot,\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}\rangle\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}
is a projector. It has the same range as
\Pi_{X_{2}}
,
i.e.

\mathrm{span}\,\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}
. Moreover, it has the same kernel since
\ker\Pi_{X_{2}}=(\mathrm{ran}\,\Pi_{X_{2}}^{*})^{\perp}=(\mathrm{span}\,\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}})^{\perp}
. Therefore
\Pi_{X_{2}}=\langle\cdot,\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}\rangle\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}
.

Finally, note that we can choose
\mathscr{C}
to be the circle of center
b_{0}
and radius
b_{0}
, as soon as
\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}<(1-3\varepsilon)b_{0}
. Indeed, this path encircles
\mathcal{B}+\Sigma
because

|\mathcal{B}+\Sigma-b_{0}|\leqslant\varepsilon b_{0}+|\Sigma|<b_{0},

and it does not encircles
(2n+1)\mathcal{B}+\Sigma
for
n\geqslant 1
since

\displaystyle|(2n+1)\mathcal{B}+\Sigma-b_{0}|

\displaystyle\geqslant 2nb_{0}-|\Sigma|-(2n+1)|\mathcal{B}-b_{0}|

\displaystyle>(2n(1-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon-1+3\varepsilon)b_{0}\geqslant b_{0}.

∎

The functions
\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}
and
\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}
are convenient for the construction of an augmented matrix of
M_{0}(X_{2})-z
, which is bijective when
z
is close to the first eigenvalue
\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})
of
M_{0}(X_{2})
.

Proposition 3.5
.

We let

\mathsf{P}_{0}(X_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}M_{0}(X_{2})-z&\cdot\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}\\
\langle\cdot,\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}\rangle&0\end{pmatrix}:B^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}\,.

If
|z-b_{0}|<b_{0}
, then
\mathsf{P}_{0}(X_{2})
is bijective and its inverse is given by

(3.6)

\mathsf{P}_{0}(X_{2})^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}R_{0}(z)&\cdot\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}\\
\langle\cdot,\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}\rangle&z-\mathcal{B}-\Sigma\end{pmatrix}\,.

Moreover,
\mathsf{P}_{0}(X_{2})
and
\mathsf{P}_{0}(X_{2})^{-1}
are bounded symbols.

Proof.

One easily checks the formula for the inverse by taking the product of the two matrices. Clearly,
M_{0}(X_{2})
,
\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}
and
\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}
are in the class of bounded symbols. It only remains to estimate
R_{0}(z)
as function of
X_{2}
.
R_{0}(z)
is given by formula (
3.5
), where the contour
\mathscr{C}
is independent of
X_{2}
. Therefore, it is enough to notice that
X_{2}\mapsto(M_{0}(X_{2})-\zeta)^{-1}
is a smooth bounded function of
X_{2}
with values in
\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),B^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, which depends continuously on
\zeta
. Similarly the
X_{2}
-derivatives are also bounded and therefore
\mathsf{P}_{0}(X_{2})^{-1}
belongs to the class

S(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C},B^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C})).

∎

3.3.
Approximate parametrix

We let
\mathscr{F}=\mathsf{Op}^{w}_{h}(\cdot\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}})
and
\mathscr{G}=\mathsf{Op}^{w}_{h}(\langle\cdot,\mathsf{G}_{{X_{2}}}\rangle)
, and we consider

\mathscr{P}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z&\mathscr{F}\\
\mathscr{G}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

which is a pseudodifferential operator with principal symbol
\mathsf{P}_{0,z}(X_{2})
. Using Proposition
3.5
, we will construct a parametrix for
\mathscr{P}
. The existence of this explicit approximate inverse will give coercivity properties of
\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}
, eventually leading to the bound stated in Proposition
3.2
. We define the subprincipal terms of
\mathscr{P}
by

\mathsf{P}_{j}(X_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}M_{j}(X_{2})&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathscr{P}_{j}=\mathsf{P}_{j}^{w}\,,

where the subprincipal terms
M_{j}(X_{2})
of
\mathscr{M}_{h}^{\Phi}
are defined in Proposition
2.5
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

(1)

When
\Phi\in S(\mathbb{R})
, for all
J\in\mathbb{N}
we have the expansion

\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathscr{P}_{1}+h\mathscr{P}_{2}+\ldots+h^{\frac{J}{2}}\mathscr{P}_{J}+h^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\mathscr{R}_{J}\,,

where
\|\mathscr{R}_{J}\|_{(B^{J+3}\otimes L^{2})\oplus L^{2}\to(L^{2}\otimes L^{2})\oplus L^{2}}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
h
.

(2)

When
\Phi^{\prime}\in S(\mathbb{R})+Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathscr{P}_{1}+h\mathscr{R}\,,

where
\|\mathscr{R}\|_{(B^{4}\otimes L^{2})\oplus L^{2}\to(L^{2}\otimes L^{2})\oplus L^{2}}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
h\in(0,1)
and
R\in[1,\infty)
.

Proof.

This result follows from Proposition
2.5
and Lemma
2.7
, using the Calderón-Vaillancourt theorem (see
[
28
, Théorème 2.1.16]
) to estimate the remainders. Note that, since the semi-norms of
\Phi^{\prime}
are uniformly bounded with respect to
R\in[1,\infty)
, the symbols involved belong to the class
S_{\frac{1}{2},0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
uniformly with respect to

. Thus the estimates are independent of
R
.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Assume
|z-b_{0}|<b_{0}
. Then there exists a sequence
(\mathsf{Q}_{j})_{j\geqslant 0}
of symbols in
S(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathcal{L}(B^{j}\oplus\mathbb{C},B^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}))
such that the following holds. Let
\mathscr{Q}_{j}=\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w,2}\mathsf{Q}_{j}
, and for
J\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}=\mathscr{Q}_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathscr{Q}_{1}+\ldots+h^{\frac{J}{2}}\mathscr{Q}_{J}.

(i)

When
\Phi\in S(\mathbb{R})
, we have for all
J\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.7)

\mathscr{P}\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}=\mathrm{Id}+h^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\mathscr{R}_{J}\,,

where
\mathscr{R}_{J}
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}((B^{J+1}\otimes L^{2})\oplus L^{2},(L^{2}\otimes L^{2})\oplus L^{2})
.

(ii)

When
\Phi^{\prime}\in S(\mathbb{R})+Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

(3.8)

\mathscr{Q}^{[1]}\mathscr{P}=\mathrm{Id}+h\mathscr{R}\,,

where
\mathscr{R}
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}((B^{4}\otimes L^{2})\oplus L^{2}),(B^{2}\otimes L^{2})\oplus L^{2})
.

Moreover, we can choose
\mathsf{Q}_{0}=\mathsf{P}^{-1}_{0}
,
\mathsf{Q}_{1}=-\mathsf{Q}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{1}\mathsf{Q}_{0}
, and

(3.9)

\mathsf{Q}_{2}=-\mathsf{Q}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{2}\mathsf{Q}_{0}+\mathsf{Q}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{1}\mathsf{Q}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{1}\mathsf{Q}_{0}-\frac{1}{2i}\{\mathsf{Q}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{0}\}\mathsf{Q}_{0}\,,

where we recall formula (
3.6
) for the inverse of
\mathsf{P}_{0}
.

Proof.

If
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
denote the symbol of
\mathscr{P}
and
\mathscr{Q}
respectively, then the symbol of
\mathscr{Q}\mathscr{P}
is given by the Moyal product (see the composition theorem in
[
28
, Theorem 2.1.12]
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}\star\mathsf{P}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{P}+\frac{h}{2i}\{\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P}\}+\mathscr{O}(h^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Q}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathsf{Q}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{1}+\mathsf{Q}_{1}\mathsf{P}_{0})+h\Big(\mathsf{Q}_{2}\mathsf{P}_{0}+\mathsf{Q}_{1}\mathsf{P}_{1}+\mathsf{Q}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{2}+\frac{1}{2i}\{\mathsf{Q}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{0}\}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{3}{2}})\,.

We obtain an expansion in powers of
h^{\frac{1}{2}}
. We want the first coefficient to equal the identity, and therefore
\mathsf{Q}_{0}=\mathsf{P}_{0}^{-1}
. We want all other coefficients to vanish. This leads to a sequence of equations that can be solved recursively, to find the values of
\mathsf{Q}_{j}
. The first two equations give the claimed formulas for
\mathsf{Q}_{1}
and
\mathsf{Q}_{2}
. Note that
\mathsf{Q}_{j}
belongs to the symbol class
S(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathcal{L}(B^{j}\oplus\mathbb{C},B^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}))
. The final error is controlled by using Lemma
3.6
. The estimate of
\mathscr{P}\mathscr{Q}
follows from the same arguments.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

As mentionned above, the expressions of the

are the same as in
[

]
. Note however that, in the context of

Then, we notice that
(\mathsf{P}_{0}-E)\partial_{x_{2}}\mathsf{F}=\partial_{x_{2}}E\mathsf{F}-\partial_{x_{2}}\mathsf{P}_{0}\mathsf{F}

By exchanging the roles of
x_{2}
and
\xi_{2}

(\{\mathsf{Q}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{0}\}\mathsf{Q}_{0})_{\pm}=-\langle(\mathsf{P}_{0}-z)\partial_{x_{2}}\mathsf{F},\partial_{\xi_{2}}\mathsf{G}\rangle+\langle(\mathsf{P}_{0}-z)\partial_{\xi_{2}}\mathsf{F},\partial_{x_{2}}\mathsf{G}\rangle\\
+2\partial_{x_{2}}E\langle\mathsf{F},\partial_{\xi_{2}}\mathsf{G}\rangle-2\partial_{\xi_{2}}E\langle\mathsf{F},\partial_{x_{2}}\mathsf{G}\rangle\,.

In
[

]
, the correct expression of the effective symbol is

\begin{split}\mu^{\mathsf{eff}}_{h}(X_{2})=&\accentset{\circ}{B}(X_{2})+\accentset{\circ}{V}(X_{2})\\
&+h\left(\langle\mathsf{P}_{2}(X_{2})f_{X_{2}},f_{X_{2}}\rangle-\langle\mathsf{P}_{1}(X_{2})(\mathsf{P}_{0}(X_{2})-z)^{-1}\Pi^{\perp}\mathsf{P}_{1}(X_{2})f_{X_{2}},f_{X_{2}}\rangle\right)\\
&+\frac{h}{2i}(\{\mathsf{Q}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{0}\}\mathsf{Q}_{0})_{\pm}\,.\end{split}

3.4.
Implications of the existence of the left inverse

We deduce from Proposition
3.7
a lower bound on
\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
C_{0}>0
and consider
\Phi^{\prime}\in S(\mathbb{R})+Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
. Then there is
C>0
such that, for
R\geqslant 1
,
h
small enough,
z\in D(b_{0},C_{0}h)
and for all
f\in B^{4}(\mathbb{R})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant C\|\mathscr{G}f\|_{L^{2}}+C\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}+Ch\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\,,

and

\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w,2}(\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})-b_{0})\mathscr{G}f,\mathscr{G}f\rangle\\
\leqslant C\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}+Ch\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\,.

Proof.

We use Proposition
3.7
, and denote

\mathscr{Q}^{[1]}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{Q}_{++}&\mathscr{Q}_{+}\\
\mathscr{Q}_{-}&\mathscr{Q}_{\pm}\end{pmatrix}.

Applying (
3.8
) to a vector
(f,0)
gives

(3.10)

\mathscr{Q}_{++}(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f+\mathscr{Q}_{+}\mathscr{G}f=f+h\mathscr{R}f\,,

and

(3.11)

\mathscr{Q}_{-}(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f+\mathscr{Q}_{\pm}\mathscr{G}f=h\tilde{\mathscr{R}}f\,,

where the remainders satisfy

\|\mathscr{R}f\|_{B^{2}\otimes L^{2}}\leqslant C\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\,,\quad\|\tilde{\mathscr{R}}f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant C\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\,.

The first estimate follows from (
3.10
) since
\mathscr{Q}_{++}
is bounded operator
B^{1}\otimes L^{2}\to L^{2}\otimes L^{2}
and
\mathscr{Q}_{+}
is bounded
L^{2}\to L^{2}\otimes L^{2}
. For the second estimate, we take the scalar product with
\mathscr{G}f
in (
3.11
) and obtain

\displaystyle-{\mathrm{Re}}\langle\mathscr{Q}_{\pm}\mathscr{G}f,\mathscr{G}f\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\mathscr{Q}_{-}(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{L^{2}}\|\mathscr{G}f\|_{L^{2}}+Ch\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}+Ch\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}},

where we used that
\mathscr{Q}_{-}
is a bounded operator
B^{1}\otimes L^{2}\to L^{2}
. Using the formula from Proposition
3.7
and equation (
3.6
), we find the symbol of
\mathscr{Q}_{\pm}
,

\mathsf{Q}_{\pm}=z-\mathcal{B}-\Sigma-h^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle M_{1}(X_{2})\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}},\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}\rangle.

The subprincipal term is vanishing because
M_{1}(X_{2})
is odd in
x_{1}
and
\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}
,
\mathsf{G}_{X_{2}}
are Gaussians. The result follows, replacing
z
by
b_{0}
up to additional
\mathscr{O}(h)
error terms.
∎

3.5.
Proof of Proposition
3.2

The second inequality in Corollary
3.9
implies that

\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w,2}(\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})-b_{0}+cR^{2}h\chi_{R,h})\mathscr{G}f,\mathscr{G}f\rangle\\
\leqslant C\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}+cR^{2}h\langle\chi_{R,h}\mathscr{G}f,\mathscr{G}f\rangle+Ch\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\,,

where we introduced the function
\chi_{R,h}
given in Assumption
3.1
, which satisfies

p_{h}(X_{2}):=\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})-b_{0}+cR^{2}h\chi_{R,h}(x_{2})-cR^{2}h\geqslant 0\,.

In order to exploit the positivity, one will need a Fefferman-Phong inequality in a slightly exotic class.

Lemma 3.10

(A Fefferman-Phong inequality)
.

There exists
C>0
such that, for all
R\geqslant 1
and
h
small enough,

\mathrm{Op}^{w,2}_{h}p_{h}\geqslant-Ch\,.

Proof.

By translation and rescaling (
x_{2}=c_{u}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}y
,
\xi_{2}=h^{-\frac{1}{2}}\eta
), we see that
\mathrm{Op}^{w}_{h}p_{h}
is unitarily equivalent to
\mathrm{Op}^{w}_{1}(q_{h})
with

q_{h}(y,\eta)=\\
\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal{B}(h^{\frac{1}{2}}\eta+i\Phi^{\prime}(c_{u}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}y),c_{u}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}y)+\Sigma(c_{u}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}y)-b_{0}+cR^{2}h\chi_{0}(y)-cR^{2}h\,.

By using that
\Phi^{\prime}\in S(\mathbb{R})+Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we see that
\partial^{\alpha}(h^{-1}q_{h})
is uniformly bounded as soon as
|\alpha|\geqslant 4
. Therefore, we can apply the standard Fefferman-Phong inequality (see, for instance,
[
7
, Théorème 3.2]
or
[
30
, Corollary 1.3.2]
) and the conclusion follows.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
3.10
, we deduce

(cR^{2}-C)h\left\langle\mathscr{G}f,\mathscr{G}f\right\rangle\leqslant C\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}+cR^{2}h\langle\chi_{R,h}\mathscr{G}f,\mathscr{G}f\rangle\\
+Ch\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\,.

Then, we notice that

[\mathscr{G},\chi_{R,h}]=\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})\,,

which implies, up to reducing
h_{0}
,

(cR^{2}-C)h\|\mathscr{G}f\|^{2}\leqslant C\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}+CR^{2}h\|\chi_{R,h}f\|_{L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\\
+Ch\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\,.

By using the first estimate in Corollary
3.9
, we get

(cR^{2}-C)h\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant C\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}-z)f\|_{B^{1}\otimes L^{2}}+CR^{2}h\|{\chi}_{R,h}f\|_{L^{2}}+Ch\|f\|_{B^{4}\otimes L^{2}}\,.

This ends the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

4.
Exponential decay

4.1.
Magnetic Agmon distance

In this section we prove an almost optimal result on the decay of eigenfunctions of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
, the single upper-well operator. More precisely, we prove decay in the variable
x_{2}
, which is the direction where tunneling occurs. The optimal decay rate is given by a function
\varphi_{u}(x_{2})
, which we call
Agmon distance
by analogy with electric wells. This function
\varphi_{u}
is solution to an eikonal equation where
\mathcal{B}+\Sigma
is the effective symbol. This is where Assumption
1.5
on the magnetic field is crucial.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume
\Sigma\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}_{+})
is such that
\Sigma(c_{d})>0
and
\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}
,
|{\rm{supp}}(\Sigma)|
are small enough. Then there exists a smooth non-negative function
\varphi_{u}
such that for all
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

(4.1)

\mathcal{B}\left(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2}\right)+\Sigma(x_{2})=b_{0}\,.

It satisfies
\varphi_{u}(c_{u})=0
,
\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(c_{u})=0
and
\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})=-b_{0}\gamma^{\prime}(c_{u})
. Moreover, outside the support of
\Sigma
it is given by

(4.2)

\varphi_{u}(x_{2})=\Big|\int_{c_{u}}^{x_{2}}|\Gamma(s)|\mathrm{d}s\Big|,\qquad x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus{\rm{supp}}(\Sigma),\quad x_{2}>c_{d}.

with

\Gamma(x_{2}):=\gamma(x_{2})\int_{[0,1]}B(it\gamma(x_{2}),x_{2})\mathrm{d}t\,.

Proof.

We recall that, for all
q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\mathcal{B}(\iota(q))=B(q)\,,

where
\iota
is the diffeomorphism defined in (
2.1
). Thus, by analyticity we have

b_{0}=B(i\gamma(q_{2}),q_{2})=\mathcal{B}(\iota(i\gamma(q_{2}),q_{2}))=\mathcal{B}\left(\int_{[0,i\gamma(q_{2})]}B(q_{1},q_{2})\mathrm{d}q_{1}\,,q_{2}\right)\,.

By symmetry, this is also true replacing
\gamma
by
-\gamma
, and we deduce that the smooth functions
\pm\Gamma
satisfy

(4.3)

\mathcal{B}(\pm i\Gamma(x_{2}),x_{2})=b_{0},\qquad\forall x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

We recall that
B(ix_{1},x_{2})>0
for
x_{1}
,
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, and therefore
\Gamma
has the same sign as
\gamma
,

\begin{cases}\Gamma(x_{2})<0\quad&{\rm{for}}\quad x_{2}\notin[c_{d},c_{u}],\\
\Gamma(x_{2})>0\quad&{\rm{for}}\quad x_{2}\in(c_{d},c_{u}).\end{cases}

For this reason the function

\psi(x_{2})=\Big|\int_{c_{u}}^{x_{2}}|\Gamma(s)|\mathrm{d}s\Big|=\begin{cases}\int_{x_{2}}^{c_{u}}\Gamma(s)\mathrm{d}s&\quad{\rm{if}}\quad x_{2}\geqslant c_{d},\\
-\int_{x_{2}}^{c_{d}}\Gamma(s)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{c_{d}}^{c_{u}}\Gamma(s)\mathrm{d}s&\quad{\rm{if}}\quad x_{2}\leqslant c_{d},\end{cases}

is non-negative, increasing on
[c_{u},\infty)
, decreasing on
(-\infty,c_{u}]
, and its global minimum is
\psi(c_{u})=0
. It is a solution to

\mathcal{B}(i\psi^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})=b_{0},

however it is not smooth at
c_{d}
. This is due to the double-well of
\mathcal{B}
on the real axis, and it is fixed using the non-negative cutoff
\Sigma(x_{2})
. We define
\varphi_{u}
to be equal to
\psi
outside the support of
\Sigma
. Equation (
4.1
) has locally a smooth solution, which cannot cross the axis
x_{1}=0
because
\mathcal{B}(0,x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})>b_{0}
away from
c_{u}
. Thus there must be a smooth function
\varphi_{u}^{\prime}
connecting the two branches of
\psi^{\prime}
on the support of
\Sigma
.
∎

4.2.
Weights satisfying Assumption
3.1

The proof of exponential decay is based on the functional inequality Corollary
3.3
derived in Section
3
. However, we cannot apply this inequality directly to the weight
\varphi_{u}
since it is not a bounded function. For this reason we introduce
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
which is constant at infinity. Let
A>c_{u}
, and consider
\chi_{1}\in\mathscr{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\chi_{1}=1
on
[-A,A]
,
\chi_{1}=0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-2A,2A]
and
0\leqslant\chi_{1}\leqslant 1
. We define

(4.4)

\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\quad{\tilde{\varphi}}_{u}(x)=\int_{c_{u}}^{x}\chi_{1}(s)\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s\,.

One easily checks the following properties of
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
.

—

{\tilde{\varphi}}_{u}=\varphi_{u}
on
[-A,A]
,

—

{\tilde{\varphi}}_{u}
is constant on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-2A,2A]
,

—

{\tilde{\varphi}}_{u}(\pm 2A)<\varphi_{u}(\pm 2A)
,

—

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
(x-c_{u})\tilde{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime}(x)\geqslant 0
,

—

|{\tilde{\varphi}}_{u}^{\prime}|\leqslant|\varphi_{u}^{\prime}|
and thus
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{u}\leqslant\varphi_{u}
.

Moreover, the function
(1-\eta)\tilde{\varphi}_{u}\in S(\mathbb{R})
satisfies Assumption
3.1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
\eta>0
is small enough, then the function
(1-\eta)\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
satisfies Assumption
3.1
.

Proof.

The proof is divided into two steps.

1. We first establish a couple of estimates for any weight
\Phi
. Thanks to the eikonal equation (
4.1
), we can write

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})-b_{0}\\
&=\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\right)\\
&=\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})+\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\right)\,.\end{split}

The function
\xi_{2}\mapsto\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})\right)
vanishes at
0
and its derivative is the function

\xi_{2}\mapsto\mathrm{Re}\,\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})=\mathrm{Re}\,\frac{\partial_{1}B}{B}(\iota^{-1}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2}))
, which is non-negative when
\xi_{2}\geqslant 0
and non-positive when
\xi_{2}\leqslant 0
(as a consequence of Assumption
1.7
). Thus,

(4.5)

\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})\right)\geqslant 0\,.

We deduce

(4.6)

\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})-b_{0}\geqslant\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\,,

where the right-hand side is real due to the symmetry assumptions on
\mathcal{B}
. Then, we only have to bound the right-hand side from below.

We use the Taylor formula to get

(4.7)

\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})=i\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})(\Phi^{\prime}-\tilde{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime})+\mathscr{O}\big(|\Phi^{\prime}-\varphi_{u}^{\prime}|^{2}\big),

and then

(4.8)

\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})=-\partial_{1}^{2}\mathcal{B}(0,x_{2})\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(\Phi^{\prime}-\varphi_{u}^{\prime})\\
+\mathscr{O}\big((\varphi_{u}^{\prime})^{2}|\Phi^{\prime}-\varphi_{u}^{\prime}|+|\Phi^{\prime}-\varphi_{u}^{\prime}|^{2}\big)\,.

Note that our assumptions on
B
imply that, for all
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\partial_{1}^{2}\mathcal{B}(0,x_{2})\geqslant c>0
. Moreover, thanks to Assumption
1.5
,

i\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(i\varphi^{\prime}_{u},x_{2})\frac{\varphi_{u}^{\prime}}{|\varphi_{u}^{\prime}|}<0,\quad x_{2}\neq c_{u}\,.

2. With
\Phi=(1-\eta)\varphi_{u}
, we deduce from (
4.8
) that,

\mathcal{B}(i(1-\eta)\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\geqslant\partial_{1}^{2}\mathcal{B}(0,x_{2})\eta(\varphi_{u}^{\prime})^{2}+\mathscr{O}\big(\eta(\varphi_{u}^{\prime})^{3}+\eta^{2}(\varphi_{u}^{\prime})^{2}\big)\,.

We know that
\varphi_{u}
is quadratic near
x_{2}=c_{u}
. Therefore, we can find a
c>0
such that, if
\delta
and
\eta
are small enough,

(4.9)

\mathcal{B}(i(1-\eta)\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\geqslant c(x_{2}-c_{u})^{2},\quad\forall x_{2}\in D(c_{u},\delta)\,.

Then, we use (
4.7
) so that, for all
x_{2}\in[-A,A]\setminus D(c_{u},\delta)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(i(1-\eta)\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})

\displaystyle=i\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})(\Phi^{\prime}-\varphi_{u}^{\prime})+\mathscr{O}\big(|\Phi^{\prime}-\varphi_{u}^{\prime}|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{\delta}\eta|\varphi_{u}^{\prime}|\,,

for some
c_{\delta}>0
, and thus, for all
x_{2}\in[-A,A]\setminus D(c_{u},\delta)
,

(4.10)

\mathcal{B}(i(1-\eta)\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\geqslant\tilde{c}_{\delta}>0\,.

Since
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}=\varphi_{u}
on
[-A,A]
, we get, from (
4.9
) and (
4.10
), that for
\Phi=(1-\eta)\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
, on
[-A,A]
,

\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\geqslant\tilde{c}_{\delta}\min(1,(x_{2}-c_{u})^{2})\,.

For all
x_{2}\notin[-A,A]
, we have

\mathcal{B}(i\Phi^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\geqslant c_{A}>0\,.

Thus,
\Phi=(1-\eta)\tilde{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime}
satisfies the Assumption
3.1
.
∎

In Proposition
5.4
about WKB constructions, we will also need the following almost optimal weight.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\rho
be a smooth function equal to
1
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1]
and supported in
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
. For all
R,\eta\geqslant 1
, define

\hat{\varphi}_{u}(x)=\tilde{\varphi}_{u}(x)-\eta R^{2}h\int_{c_{u}}^{x}\rho\left(\frac{s-c_{u}}{Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\frac{\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}_{u}(s)}{\tilde{\varphi}_{u}(s)}\mathrm{d}s.

Then if
\eta>0
is small enough,
\hat{\varphi}_{u}
satisfies Assumption
3.1
.

Note that
\hat{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime}\in S(\mathbb{R})+Rh^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
uniformly with respect to
R
.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of Lemma
4.2
. We can use the bounds (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) to
\Phi=\hat{\varphi}_{u}
. Note that we have

\hat{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime}=\left(1-\frac{\eta R^{2}h\rho_{h}}{\tilde{\varphi}_{u}}\right)\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}_{u}\,,

with
\rho_{h}(x)=\rho((x-c_{u})/Rh^{\frac{1}{2}})
. Thus, (
4.8
) gives, on
[-A,A]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(i\hat{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})

\displaystyle\geqslant\partial_{1}^{2}\mathcal{B}(0,x_{2})\eta R^{2}h\rho_{h}\frac{(\varphi_{u}^{\prime})^{2}}{\varphi_{u}}+\mathscr{O}\Big(\eta R^{2}h\rho_{h}\frac{(\varphi_{u}^{\prime})^{3}}{\varphi_{u}}+\eta^{2}R^{4}h^{2}\rho_{h}^{2}\frac{(\varphi_{u}^{\prime})^{2}}{\varphi_{u}^{2}}\Big).

For
x_{2}\in D(c_{u},\delta)
with
\delta
small enough, using that
\varphi_{u}
is quadratic near
c_{u}
, we deduce,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(i\hat{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})

\displaystyle\geqslant c\eta R^{2}h\rho_{h}+\mathscr{O}\Big(\eta R^{2}h\rho_{h}|x_{2}-c_{u}|+\frac{\eta^{2}R^{2}h^{2}\rho_{h}^{2}}{|x-c_{u}|^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\geqslant c\eta R^{2}h\rho_{h}(1-C\delta-C\eta).

When
\eta
and
\delta
are chosen small enough, we obtain, for all
x_{2}\in D(c_{u},\delta)
,

(4.11)

\mathcal{B}(i\hat{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\geqslant c\eta R^{2}h\rho_{h}\,.

When
x_{2}\in[-A,A]\setminus D(c_{u},\delta)
, then
\rho_{h}(x_{2})=1
and we use (
4.7
) to get

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(i\hat{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{\delta}\eta R^{2}h\frac{\varphi_{u}^{\prime}}{\varphi_{u}}+\mathscr{O}\left(\eta^{2}R^{4}h^{2}\frac{(\varphi_{u}^{\prime})^{2}}{\varphi_{u}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{\delta}\eta R^{2}h-C_{\delta}\eta^{2}R^{4}h^{2}.

We therefore obtain a
\tilde{c}_{\delta}>0
(depending on
\delta
) such that, for all
x_{2}\in[-A,A]\setminus D(c_{u},\delta)
,

(4.12)

\mathcal{B}(i\hat{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\geqslant\tilde{c}_{\delta}\eta R^{2}h\,.

Moreover, we have, for all
x_{2}\notin[-A,A]
,

\mathcal{B}(i\hat{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})-\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime},x_{2})\geqslant c_{A}>0\,.

We combine (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) to deduce that
\hat{\varphi}_{u}
satisfies Assumption
3.1
, as soon as
\eta
is chosen small enough.
∎

.

4.3.
Exponential decay of the one-well groundstate

We are now in position to prove an almost-optimal decay estimate on eigenfunctions of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\eta>0
small enough,
C_{0}>0
and
L\in\mathbb{N}
. There exist
C,h_{0}>0
such that, for all
h\in(0,h_{0})
and all eigenfunctions
\mathbf{f}
associated with an eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
such that
|\lambda-b_{0}|\leqslant C_{0}h
, we have

\|e^{(1-\eta)\,\tilde{\varphi}_{u}(x_{2})/h}\mathbf{f}\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}\leqslant C\|\mathbf{f}\|\,

where
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
is the weight defined in (
4.4
).

Proof.

We can assume
\eta
as small as we need, and we apply Corollary
3.3
to
\Phi=(1-\eta)\,\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
(which is allowed thanks to Lemma
4.2
). Applying the inequality to
f=e^{(1-\eta)\,\tilde{\varphi}_{u}(x_{2})/h}\mathbf{f}
with
z=\lambda
, we obtain

\|e^{(1-\eta)\,\tilde{\varphi}_{u}(x_{2})/h}\mathbf{f}\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}\leqslant C\|\chi_{R,h}(x_{2}-c_{u})e^{(1-\eta)\,\tilde{\varphi}_{u}(x_{2})/h}\mathbf{f}\|,

for some
R>0
and
C>0
. Since
\chi_{R,h}
is supported on a
h^{\frac{1}{2}}
-neighborhood of
c_{u}
, and
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
is quadratic near
c_{u}
, the exponential on the right-hand side is bounded on the support of
\chi_{R,h}
. The result follows.
∎

5.
A WKB-like construction

The parametrix constructed in Proposition
3.7
can also be used to contruct quasimodes for the operator
\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\Phi}
, when
\Phi=\varphi_{u}
is the
magnetic Agmon distance
defined in Lemma
4.1
. This yields a WKB-like construction for the upper-well operator
\mathscr{M}_{u}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There exist sequences of smooth functions
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and of real numbers
(z_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
N>0
such that the following holds for all
J\in\mathbb{N}
. Define

a^{[J]}=a_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}a_{1}+\ldots+h^{\frac{J}{2}}a_{J}\,,\quad z^{[J]}=z_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}z_{1}+\ldots+h^{\frac{J}{2}}z_{J}\,,

and let
\chi\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
\chi=1
on a neighborhood of
c_{u}
.

(1)

There exists an
h
-pseudodifferential operator
\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}
with principal symbol

\mathsf{Q}^{+}=\mathsf{F}_{(x_{2},\xi_{2})}(x_{1})+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})\quad{\rm in}\quad S(1),

such that

(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\varphi_{u}}-z^{[J]})\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}_{+}[\chi a^{[J]}]=\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{J+1}{2}})\quad{\rm{in}}\quad B^{L}(\mathbb{R})\otimes L^{2}(\{\chi=1\})\,,

for all
L\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

The quasimode

\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}(x_{1},x_{2})=\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})^{\frac{1}{4}}h^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\varphi_{u}(x_{2})/h}\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}_{+}\left[\chi a^{[J]}\right](x_{1},x_{2})

satisfies

e^{\varphi_{u}/h}(\mathbf{f}_{J,u}^{\rm{wkb}}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}_{J,u}^{\rm{wkb}})=\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{J}{2}-1-N})\quad\textrm{in}\quad B^{L}(\mathbb{R})\otimes L^{2}(\{\chi=1\})\,,

for all
L\in\mathbb{N}
, and where
\Pi_{u}
is the orthogonal projection on the ground state of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
.

Moreover
z_{0}=b_{0}
,
z_{1}=0
,
a_{0}(c_{u})=1
, and
\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|=1+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})
.

This results tells us that
\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}
is a good approximation of the true ground state
\mathbf{f}_{u}
of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
. This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
5.1
.

5.1.
A sequence of transport equations

We want to use the parametrix construction in Proposition
3.7
with weight
\varphi_{u}
. Since
\varphi_{u}
is not bounded, we use instead
\Phi=\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
defined in (
4.4
). We may choose the constant
A>0
defining
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
such that
{\rm{supp}}\,\chi\subset[-A,A]
. With this choice, and for any
z
of the form

z=b_{0}+z_{1}h^{\frac{1}{2}}+\cdots+z_{J}h^{\frac{J}{2}},

we obtain an
h
-pseudodifferential operator

\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{Q}_{++}^{[J]}&\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}\\
\mathscr{Q}_{-}^{[J]}&\mathscr{Q}_{\pm}^{[J]}\end{pmatrix},

such that

(5.1)

\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\tilde{\varphi}_{u}}-z&\mathscr{F}\\
\mathscr{G}&0\end{pmatrix}\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}=I+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{J+1}{2}}).

We recall that
\mathscr{F}=\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w}\mathsf{F}_{(x_{2},\xi_{2})}(x_{1})
, with the notation (
3.3
). Proposition
3.7
also tells us that
\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}=\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})
in the symbol class
S(1)
. Applying (
5.1
) to a function of the form
(0,\chi(x_{2})a(x_{2}))
, we deduce that

(5.2)

(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\tilde{\varphi}_{u}}-z)\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}_{+}(\chi a)=-\mathscr{F}\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}_{\pm}(\chi a)+\mathscr{O}\Big(h^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\|\chi a\|\Big)\,,

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and

(5.3)

\mathscr{G}\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}_{+}(\chi a)=\chi a+\mathscr{O}\Big(h^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\|\chi a\|\Big)\,,

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Our quasimode
\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}
should solve the eigenvalue equation for
\mathscr{M}_{u}
, which suggests to find
z
and
a
such that
\mathscr{Q}_{\pm}^{[J]}(\chi a)
is small (in (
5.2
)). This is the purpose of the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exist sequences of smooth functions
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and of numbers
(z_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\mathscr{Q}_{\pm}^{[J]}(\chi a^{[J]})=\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{J+1}{2}})\quad{\rm{in}}\quad L^{2}(\{\chi=1\}),

for all
J\in\mathbb{N}
, where
a^{[J]}=a_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}a_{1}+\cdots+h^{\frac{J}{2}}a_{J}
and
z=z_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}z_{1}+\cdots h^{\frac{J}{2}}z_{J}
.
Moreover,

z_{0}=b_{0},\quad z_{1}=0,\quad z_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{\partial_{1}^{2}B(C_{u})\partial_{2}^{2}B(C_{u})}}{2b_{0}}+\frac{\big(\sqrt{\partial_{1}^{2}B(C_{u})}+\sqrt{\partial_{2}^{2}B(C_{u})}\big)^{2}}{4b_{0}},

and
a_{0}
is explicitly given in equation (
5.10
) below.

Proof.

We recall that
\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}_{\pm}
is a pseudodifferential operator with symbol in
S(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of the form

(5.4)

\mathsf{Q}^{\pm}=\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\pm}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{Q}^{\pm}_{1}+h\mathsf{Q}^{\pm}_{2}+\cdots+h^{\frac{J}{2}}\mathsf{Q}^{\pm}_{J}.

with

(5.5)

\begin{cases}\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\pm}(x_{2},\xi_{2})=z-\mathcal{B}(\xi_{2}+i\tilde{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})-\Sigma(x_{2})\\
\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{\pm}(x_{2},\xi_{2})=0.\end{cases}

Now note that, given any symbol
\sigma\in S(1)
and any function
a\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

(5.6)

\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w}(\sigma(x,\xi))a=\mathsf{Op}_{1}^{w}(\sigma(x,h\xi))a=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{h^{n}}{n!}\mathsf{Op}_{1}^{w}(\partial_{\xi}^{n}\sigma(x,0)\xi^{n})a+\mathscr{O}(h^{N+1}).

Therefore, we can expand our quantity of interest

(5.7)

\mathscr{Q}_{\pm}^{[J]}(\chi a_{h})=\Big(\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w}(\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\pm})+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w}(\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{\pm})+\cdots\Big)(\chi a_{0}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\chi a_{1}+\cdots)

in powers of
h^{\frac{1}{2}}
. We want every term of the expansion to vanish, and this gives rise to a series of equation. We explain below how to find the sequence
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
that solves these equations, and the claimed result will follow. Note that, since the terms in (
5.6
) are differential operators, and since we only need to estimate (
5.6
) on
\{\chi=1\}
, we can remove
\chi
in the equations.

Equation of order

h^{0}
. The first equation arising from (
5.7
) is

\mathcal{B}(i\tilde{\varphi}_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})+\Sigma(x_{2})=z_{0}.

Since we want
z
to be close to
b_{0}
, we choose
z_{0}=b_{0}
. On
\{\chi=1\}
, we have
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}=\varphi_{u}
and the equation is solved by Lemma
4.1
. We then have
\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\pm}(x_{2},0)=z-z_{0}
.

Equation of order

h^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Due to the first equation, and since
\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{\pm}=0
, it only remains
z_{1}a_{0}=0
, which is solved by taking
z_{1}=0
.

Equation of order

h
. The terms of order
h
in equation (
5.7
) give a differential equation for
a_{0}
,

(5.8)

\mathcal{T}a_{0}=0,

with

\mathcal{T}=i\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dx_{2}}\Big(i\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})\Big)+\mathsf{Q}_{2}^{\pm}(x_{2},0)+z_{2}.

In comparison to standard WKB constructions,
\mathcal{T}
is our
transport
equation.
Note that, due to our assumptions on
\mathcal{B}
, the coefficient
i\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})
is real and vanishes only when
x_{2}=c_{u}
, and to first order only. If we choose

z_{2}=\frac{\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})}{2}\partial_{1}^{2}\mathcal{B}(0,c_{u})-\mathsf{Q}_{2}^{\pm}(c_{u},0),

then the function

(5.9)

D(x_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dx_{2}}\Big(i\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2}),x_{2})\Big)+\mathsf{Q}_{2}^{\pm}(x_{2},0)+z_{2}

vanishes to first order at
x_{2}=c_{u}
. Thus, equation (
5.8
) has a smooth solution,

(5.10)

a_{0}(x_{2})=\exp\Big(-\int_{c_{u}}^{x_{2}}\frac{D(s)}{i\partial_{1}\mathcal{B}(i\varphi_{u}^{\prime}(s),s)}\mathrm{d}s\Big).

Equation of order

h^{\frac{j+2}{2}}

for

j\geqslant 1
. We then follow the usual strategy, with details carried over in
[
8
]
for instance. For all
j\geqslant 1
, we obtain an equation for
a_{j}
of the form

(5.11)

\mathcal{T}a_{j}+z_{j+2}a_{0}=g_{j}(x_{2};a_{0},\cdots,a_{j-1},z_{0},\cdots z_{j+1}),

where
g_{j}
is some function of the quantities fixed at previous steps. We can always choose
z_{j+2}
such that (
5.11
) has as a smooth solution
a_{j}
.
∎

5.2.
Properties of the WKB-like state

Lemma
5.2
provides us with a sequence
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
defining the quasimode
\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}
. It then follows from (
5.2
) that

(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\tilde{\varphi}_{u}}-z^{[J]})\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}_{+}[\chi a^{[J]}]=\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{J+1}{2}})\quad{\rm{in}}\quad B^{N}(\mathbb{R})\otimes L^{2}(\{\chi=1\})\,,

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}=\varphi_{u}
on
{\rm{supp}}\,\chi
, and by definition of
\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}
we deduce

(5.12)

e^{\varphi_{u}/h}(\mathscr{M}_{u}-z^{[J]})\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}=\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{J}{2}+\frac{1}{4}})\quad{\rm{in}}\quad B^{N}(\mathbb{R})\otimes L^{2}(\{\chi=1\})\,.

We also get

Lemma 5.3
.

The state
\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}
, and the real number
z^{[J]}
constructed using Lemma
5.2
are such that:

(1)

For all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}-z^{[J]})\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}=\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\big).

(2)

The ground state energy
\mu
of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
satisfies

|z^{[J]}-\mu|=\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\big).

(3)

The WKB state is asymptotically normalized:
\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|=1+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})
.

Proof.

The first statement follows from (
5.12
) using
\varphi_{u}\geqslant 0
on
\{\chi=1\}
, and
\varphi_{u}>0
on
\overline{\{\chi\neq 1\}}
. Then, by the spectral theorem we deduce that

|\mu-z^{[J]}|=\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\big),

since
\mu
is the only eigenvalue of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
behaving like
b_{0}+hz_{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{3}{2}})
.
Finally, we can estimate the norm of
\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}
using that

\mathscr{Q}^{[J]}_{+}=\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})=\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w,2}\big(\mathsf{F}_{(x_{2},\xi_{2})}(x_{1})\big)+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Indeed, we then have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})}{h}}\|e^{-\varphi_{u}/h}\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w,2}\big(\mathsf{F}_{(x_{2},\xi_{2})}\big)\chi a_{0}\|^{2}(1+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}}))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})}{h}}\int e^{-2\varphi_{u}(x_{2})/h}|\mathsf{F}_{(x_{2},0)}(x_{1})\chi(x_{2})a_{0}(x_{2})|^{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\big(1+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})\big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})}{h}}\int e^{-2\varphi_{u}(x_{2})/h}|\chi(x_{2})a_{0}(x_{2})|^{2}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\big(1+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})\big),

because
x_{1}\mapsto\mathsf{F}_{X_{2}}(x_{1})
has norm
1
. We estimate this integral using the stationary phase method. We deduce
\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|=1+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})
since
\chi(c_{u})a_{0}(c_{u})=1
.
∎

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
5.1

Using the construction above, Theorem
5.1
is then a consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists
N>0
such that, for all
L,J\in\mathbb{N}
we have

e^{\varphi_{u}/h}(\mathbf{f}_{J,u}^{\rm{wkb}}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}_{J,u}^{\rm{wkb}})=\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{J}{2}-1-N})\quad\textrm{in}\quad B^{L}(\mathbb{R})\otimes L^{2}(\{\chi=1\})\,.

Proof.

We first notice that

(5.13)

\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}\big)\,,

which follows from

\displaystyle hb_{0}\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle\mathscr{M}_{u}(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}),\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\mathscr{M}_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\mu\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\big(\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}-z^{[J]})\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|+|z^{[J]}-\mu|\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|\big)\|\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|,

and Lemma
5.3
.

We then use Corollary
3.3
with
z=\mu
,
f=e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}\big(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\big)
, and the weight
\Phi=\hat{\varphi}_{u}
defined in Lemma
4.3
. We obtain

(5.14)

\|e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}\big(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\big)\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{h}\|e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u})\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}\\
+C\|\chi_{R,h}e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u})\|\,,

for some
C,R>0
.
On the support of
\chi_{R,h}
, the function
\hat{\varphi}_{u}
is of order
h
. Therefore, the last term can be estimated by using (
5.13
). We deduce that

(5.15)

\|e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}\big(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\big)\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{h}\|e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u})\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}\\
+\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}\big).

Since
\Pi_{u}
projects on the kernel of
\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu
, we get

\|e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}\big(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\big)\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{h}\|e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}+\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}\big).

We insert the weight
e^{\varphi_{u}/h}
and replace
\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}
by its expression to get

\|e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}\big(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\big)\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{h^{5/4}}\|e^{(\hat{\varphi}_{u}-\varphi_{u})/h}(\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\varphi_{u}}-\mu)\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}(\chi a^{[J]})\|_{B^{N}\otimes L^{2}}\\
+\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}\big)\,.

Since
\hat{\varphi}_{u}\leqslant\varphi_{u}
, we can use Lemma
5.3
to find

\|e^{\hat{\varphi}_{u}/h}\big(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\big)\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}=\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}-1}\big).

Finally, we use that
\hat{\varphi}_{u}\geqslant\tilde{\varphi}_{u}-CR^{2}h|\ln h|
. This gives an
N>0
such that

h^{N}\|e^{\tilde{\varphi}_{u}/h}\big(\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}-\Pi_{u}\mathbf{f}^{\rm{wkb}}_{J,u}\big)\|_{B^{L}\otimes L^{2}}=\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}-1}\big),

and the result follows because
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}=\varphi_{u}
on
\{\chi=1\}
.
∎

6.
Interaction matrix and proof of Theorem
1.8

6.1.
Interaction matrix

Once we have the exponential decay of the eigenfunctions given by Theorem
4.5
, we can adapt the Helffer-Sjöstrand theory
[
24
]
to obtain an effective interaction matrix describing the spectrum of the double-well operator
\mathscr{M}
.
Recall that by Proposition
2.3
the double-well magnetic Laplacian
\mathscr{L}
is unitarily equivalent to
h\mathscr{M}
, so it suffices to understand the eigenvalues of
\mathscr{M}
.
We consider the eigenspace

E=\ker(\mathscr{M}-\lambda_{1})\oplus\ker(\mathscr{M}-\lambda_{2})

and let
\Pi_{E}
be the associated orthogonal projection. We construct a basis for
E
using the ground states
\mathbf{f}_{u}
and
\mathbf{f}_{d}
of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
and
\mathscr{M}_{d}
respectively. Recall that the
up
and
down
operators

\mathscr{M}_{u}=\mathscr{M}+\Sigma(x_{2}),\qquad\mathscr{M}_{d}=\mathscr{M}+\Sigma(-x_{2})

are related by the symmetry
U\mathbf{f}(x)=\mathbf{f}(-x)
:

U\mathscr{M}_{u}U=\mathscr{M}_{d},\qquad\mathbf{f}_{d}=U\mathbf{f}_{u}.

We then define the truncated versions of
\mathbf{f}_{u}
and
\mathbf{f}_{d}
,

f_{{u}}=\chi_{{u}}\mathbf{f}_{{u}}\,,\qquad f_{{d}}=\chi_{{d}}\mathbf{f}_{{d}}=Uf_{u}\,,

where
\chi_{{d}}(x_{2})=U\chi_{{u}}(x_{2})=\chi_{{u}}(-x_{2})
and the cutoff
\chi_{u}
is such that

(6.1)

\chi_{u}(x_{2})=\begin{cases}0&{\textrm{on }}{\rm{supp}}(\Sigma),\\
1&{\textrm{away from a small neighborhood of }}{\rm{supp}}(\Sigma).\end{cases}

Using the decay of
\mathbf{f}_{u}
and
\mathbf{f}_{d}
, we deduce that they define good approximate eigenfunctions for
\mathscr{M}
. This is a corollary of Theorem
4.5
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\kappa>0
. Then, if
\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}
and
|{\rm{supp}}(\Sigma)|
are small enough, for
\star\in\{u,d\}
, we have

\|(\mathscr{M}-\mu)f_{\star}\|=\mathscr{O}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}})\|f_{\star}\|\,,

with
S=\int_{c_{d}}^{c_{u}}\Gamma(s)\mathrm{d}s
, and where
\mu
is the ground state energy of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
.
Moreover, the first eigenvalues of
\mathscr{M}
satisfy

\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}=\mathscr{O}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}})\,.

Proof.

Note that

(\mathscr{M}-\mu)f_{u}=(\mathscr{M}-\mu)(\chi_{u}\mathbf{f}_{u})=(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)(\chi_{u}\mathbf{f}_{u})\,,

where we used that
\Sigma(x_{2})\chi_{u}(x_{2})=0
. Then, we write

(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)(\chi_{u}\mathbf{f}_{u})=(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)\mathbf{f}_{u}+(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)((\chi_{u}-1)\mathbf{f}_{u})\,.

Since
\mathbf{f}_{u}
is an eigenfunction of
\mathscr{M}_{u}
, we deduce

\|(\mathscr{M}-\mu)f_{u}\|=\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)((\chi_{u}-1)\mathbf{f}_{u})\|\,.

Thanks to the Calderón-Vaillancourt theorem and Theorem
4.5
, we get that

\|(\mathscr{M}_{u}-\mu)((\chi_{u}-1)\mathbf{f}_{u})\|\leqslant C\|(\chi_{u}-1)\mathbf{f}_{u}\|_{B^{2}\otimes L^{2}}=\mathscr{O}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}})\|\mathbf{f}_{u}\|\,,

where we used that
\tilde{\varphi}_{u}(x_{2})>(1-\kappa)S
on the support of
1-\chi_{u}
, if this support is small enough around
c_{d}
(see (
4.2
)).
Therefore, by using Theorem
4.5
again,

\|(\mathscr{M}-\mu)f_{u}\|={\mathscr{O}}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}})\|f_{u}\|\,.

By symmetry we also have

\|(\mathscr{M}-\mu)f_{d}\|={\mathscr{O}}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}})\|f_{d}\|\,.

Then, the spectral theorem tells us that there are two eigenvalues of
\mathscr{M}
close to
\mu
, at a distance of order at most
\mathscr{O}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}})
. Due to the a priori estimate (
1.1
) of the spectrum of the double-well operator, these two eigenvalues are the only ones that close to
\mu
and they are the lowest two ones.
∎

We then construct a basis of the eigenspace
E
using
f_{d}
and
f_{u}
. Let us consider the projections of our one-well quasimodes
g_{\star}=\Pi_{E}f_{\star}
, with
\star\in\{u,d\}
. In order to orthonormalize this basis, we consider the Gram matrix,

G=\begin{pmatrix}\langle g_{u},g_{u}\rangle&\langle g_{u},g_{d}\rangle\\
\langle g_{d},g_{u}\rangle&\langle g_{d},g_{d}\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{u}\\
g_{d}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}g_{u}&g_{d}\end{pmatrix}\geqslant 0\,.

The matrix of the quadratic form associated with
\mathscr{M}
in the basis
(g_{u},g_{d})
is

M=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\mathscr{M}g_{u},g_{u}\rangle&\langle\mathscr{M}g_{u},g_{d}\rangle\\
\langle\mathscr{M}g_{d},g_{u}\rangle&\langle\mathscr{M}g_{d},g_{d}\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

The following proposition is a consequence of Proposition
6.1
. It tells us that
\mathrm{span}(f_{u},f_{d})
is an exponentially good approximation of
E
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\kappa>0
. We have, with
\star\in\{u,d\}
,

(6.2)

\|g_{\star}-f_{\star}\|={\mathscr{O}}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}})\,,\quad\langle\mathscr{M}(g_{\star}-f_{\star}),g_{\star}-f_{\star}\rangle={\mathscr{O}}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{2S}{h}})\,.

Moreover, the family
(g_{u},g_{d})
is asymptotically an orthonormal basis of
F
. More precisely, we have

(6.3)

G=\mathrm{Id}+\mathrm{T}+{\mathscr{O}}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{2S}{h}})\,,\quad\mathrm{T}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\langle f_{u},f_{d}\rangle\\
\langle f_{d},f_{u}\rangle&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

In addition, we have

(6.4)

M=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&w\\
\overline{w}&\mu\end{pmatrix}+\mu\mathrm{T}+{\mathscr{O}}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{2S}{h}})\,,\quad w=\langle(\mathscr{M}-\mu)f_{u},f_{d}\rangle\,.

Finally, the matrix of
\mathscr{M}
in the orthonormal basis

G^{-\frac{1}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}g_{u}\\
g_{d}\end{pmatrix}

is of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\mu&w\\
\overline{w}&\mu\end{pmatrix}+{\mathscr{O}}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{2S}{h}})+\mathscr{O}(|\rm T|^{2})\,.

The proof of Proposition
6.2
follows standard arguments going back at least to Helffer-Sjöstrand
[
24
]
. We refer to
[
13
, Section 4.1]
where identical estimates are obtained. Finally, the error term
|\rm T|^{2}
can be estimated using the decay of
f_{u}
and
f_{d}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\kappa>0
. If
\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}
and
|{\rm{supp}}\,\Sigma|
are small enough we have

|{\rm T}|=\mathscr{O}(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}}).

Proof.

We have to control the following scalar product,

\langle f_{u},f_{d}\rangle=\langle\chi_{u}\mathbf{f}_{u},\chi_{d}\mathbf{f}_{d}\rangle.

We use the weight
\varphi_{u}
defined in Lemma
4.1
and the corresponding version
\varphi_{d}
in the other well, defined by
\varphi_{d}:=U\varphi_{u}
.
We insert the exponential weight
(1-\kappa)\varphi_{u}
to get

\langle f_{u},f_{d}\rangle=\langle e^{-(1-\kappa)(\varphi_{u}+\varphi_{d})}e^{(1-\kappa)\varphi_{u}}\chi_{u}\mathbf{f}_{u},e^{(1-\kappa)\varphi_{d}}\chi_{d}\mathbf{f}_{d}\rangle.

On the support of
\chi_{u}\chi_{d}
, we have
\varphi_{u}(x)+\varphi_{d}(x)=\varphi_{u}(x)+\varphi_{u}(-x)=S
(see (
4.2
)), and
\varphi_{u}=\tilde{\varphi}_{u}
, with notation from (
4.4
). Thus, we can use the exponential decay from Theorem
4.5
and we find

|\langle f_{u},f_{d}\rangle|\leqslant Ce^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{S}{h}}\|\mathbf{f}_{u}\|^{2},

which concludes the proof.
∎

6.2.
Estimate of the interaction and conclusion

It follows from Proposition
6.2
(and Lemma
6.3
) that the first two eigenvalues of the double well operator
\mathscr{M}
satisfy

(6.5)

\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{M})=\mu-|w|+\mathscr{O}\big(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{2S}{h}}\big),\quad\lambda_{2}(\mathscr{M})=\mu+|w|+\mathscr{O}\big(e^{-(1-\kappa)\frac{2S}{h}}\big).

Therefore, the spectral gap is asymptotically given by
2|w|
. We now explain how to estimate
w
using the WKB-like construction, Theorem
5.1
. We introduce the conjugate operator
\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}
and we write

w=\langle(\mathscr{M}-\mu)f_{u},f_{d}\rangle=\langle e^{-(\varphi_{d}+\varphi_{u})/h}(\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}-\mu)\chi_{u}e^{\varphi_{u}/h}\mathbf{f}_{u},\chi_{d}e^{\varphi_{d}/h}\mathbf{f}_{d}\rangle\,,

where
\varphi_{d}(x_{2})=\varphi_{u}(-x_{2})
. Note that
\varphi_{u}+\varphi_{d}
is constant equal to
S
on
{\rm{supp}}(\chi_{u}\chi_{d})
, see (
4.2
) and (
6.1
). Therefore, if we introduce
\underline{\chi}_{u}
which is equal to
1
on
{\rm{supp}}(\chi_{u})
and supported on
\mathbb{R}\setminus{\rm{supp}}(\Sigma)
we have

w=e^{-S/h}\langle\underline{\chi}_{u}(\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}-\mu)\chi_{u}e^{\varphi_{u}/h}{\mathbf{f}}_{u},\chi_{d}e^{\varphi_{d}/h}\mathbf{f}_{d}\rangle+\mathcal{E},

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=\langle e^{-(\varphi_{d}+\varphi_{u})/h}(1-\underline{\chi}_{u})(\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}-\mu)\chi_{u}e^{\varphi_{u}/h}\mathbf{f}_{u},\chi_{d}e^{\varphi_{d}/h}\mathbf{f}_{d}\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-S/h}|\langle(1-\underline{\chi}_{u})(\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}-\mu)\chi_{u}e^{\varphi_{u}/h}\mathbf{f}_{u},\chi_{d}e^{\varphi_{d}/h}\mathbf{f}_{d}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-S/h}\mathscr{O}(h^{\infty}),

where we used
\varphi_{u}+\varphi_{d}\geqslant S
and the pseudo-locality of the pseudodifferential operator
\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}
.
Hence

(6.6)

w=e^{-S/h}\omega+\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\infty}e^{-S/h}\big),

with

\omega=\langle\underline{\chi}_{u}(\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}-\mu)\chi_{u}e^{\varphi_{u}/h}\mathbf{f}_{u},\chi_{d}e^{\varphi_{d}/h}\mathbf{f}_{d}\rangle\,.

Using Theorem
5.1
, and since the quadratic form is controlled by a
B^{2}\otimes L^{2}
-norm, we can replace
\mathbf{f}_{u}
and
\mathbf{f}_{d}
by their WKB approximations. Thus,

\omega=\sqrt{\frac{\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})}{h}}\big\langle\underline{\chi}_{u}(\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}-\mu)\chi_{u}\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}(\chi a^{[J]}),\chi_{d}U\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}(\chi a^{[J]})\big\rangle\big(1+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})\big)\\
+\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{J}{2}-1-N}\big)\,,

where
U
is the symmetry
Uf(x)=f(-x)
.
Note that for any fixed
N
, we can choose
J
as large as we want, so that the error is small. Since
\Sigma=0
on
{\rm{supp}}(\chi_{u})
, we can replace
\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}
by
\mathscr{M}^{\varphi_{u}}_{u}
. Commuting and using Theorem
5.1
again we deduce that

\omega=\sqrt{\frac{\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})}{h}}\big\langle\underline{\chi}_{u}[\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\varphi_{u}},\chi_{u}]\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}(\chi a^{[J]}),\chi_{d}U\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}(\chi a^{[J]})\big\rangle\big(1+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{1}{2}})\big)\\
+\mathscr{O}(h^{\frac{J}{2}-1-N})\,.

The above commutator is an
h
-pseudodifferential operator with respect to
x_{2}
, such that

[\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\varphi_{u}},\chi_{u}]\mathscr{Q}_{+}^{[J]}=\mathsf{Op}_{h}^{w,2}\Big(\frac{h}{i}\{M_{0},\chi_{u}\}\mathsf{F}_{(x_{2},\xi_{2})}+\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{3}{2}}\big)\Big),

where we recall that
M_{0}
is the principal operator-symbol of
\mathscr{M}_{u}^{\varphi_{u}}
. Therefore, the stationary phase method gives

(6.7)

\omega=\sqrt{\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})h}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2}){\mathcal{J}}(x_{2})a_{0}(x_{2})\overline{a_{0}}(-x_{2})\mathrm{d}x_{2}+\mathscr{O}(h),

where we introduced the function

(6.8)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\frac{1}{i}\partial_{\xi_{2}}M_{0}(x_{2},0)\mathsf{F}_{(x_{2},0)}\Big)(x_{1})\,\overline{\mathsf{F}_{(-x_{2},0)}}(-x_{1})\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

This shows, using (
6.5
) and (
6.6
), that

(6.9)

\lambda_{2}(\mathscr{M})-\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{M})=c_{0}h^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-S/h}\Big(1+\mathscr{O}\big(h^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)\Big),

with

(6.10)

c_{0}=2\sqrt{\varphi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(c_{u})}\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{u}^{\prime}(x_{2})\mathcal{J}(x_{2})a_{0}(x_{2})\overline{a_{0}}(-x_{2})\mathrm{d}x_{2}\Big|.

The double-well magnetic Laplacian
\mathscr{L}
is unitarily equivalent to
h\mathscr{M}
(Proposition
2.3
), meaning that
\lambda_{j}(\mathscr{L})=h\lambda_{j}(\mathscr{M})
, and thus Theorem
1.8
is a reformulation of (
6.9
).

Remark 6.4
.

Equation (
6.9
) gives

(6.11)

\lim_{h\to 0}e^{S/h}h^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})=c_{0}.

However, the left-hand side in (
6.11
) is independent of the choice of cutoff
\chi_{u}
, which appears in
c_{0}
. We infer that the function
x_{2}\mapsto{\mathcal{J}}(x_{2})a_{0}(x_{2})\overline{a}_{0}(-x_{2})
must be constant on a small interval of the form
[c_{d}+\eta_{0},c_{d}+2\eta_{0}]
.
Therefore, since
{\mathcal{J}}
and
a_{0}
do not vanish on
(c_{d},c_{u})
, we deduce that
c_{0}\neq 0
. In fact, we show in Appendix
A
that

is indeed constant everywhere, see Lemma
A.3
. This also gives an explicit formula for
c_{0}
, and finishes the proof of Theorem
1.8
.
