Beyond Smoothed Analysis: Analyzing the Simplex Method by the Book
Eleon Bach, Alexander E. Black, Sophie Huiberts, Sean Kafer

Abstract
Narrowing the gap between theory and practice is a longstanding goal of the algorithm analysis community. To further progress our understanding of how algorithms work in practice, we propose a new algorithm analysis framework that we call by the book analysis . In contrast to earlier frameworks, by the book analysis not only models an algorithm’s input data, but also the algorithm itself. Results from by the book analysis are meant to correspond well with established knowledge of an algorithm’s practical behavior, as they are meant to be grounded in observations from implementations, input modeling best practices, and measurements on practical benchmark instances. We apply our framework to the simplex method, an algorithm which is beloved for its excellent performance in practice and notorious for its high running time under worst-case analysis. The simplex method similarly showcased the state of the art framework smoothed analysis (Spielman and Teng, STOC’01). We explain how our framework overcomes several weaknesses of smoothed analysis and we prove that under input scaling assumptions, feasibility tolerances and other design principles used by simplex method implementations, the simplex method indeed attains a polynomial running time.

1.1
Smoothed Analysis and the Simplex Method

In this section we review the smoothed analysis of the simplex method.
We first recall the basic principles of the simplex method. The simplex method is best thought of as a class of algorithms. A simplex method first determines a basic feasible solution using a
Phase I
procedure and then iteratively moves to new basic feasible solutions until an optimal solution has been found. Such a move is called a
pivot step
, and the number of pivot steps is a proxy for the running time. The choice for what basic feasible solution to move to is governed by a
pivot rule
.
Some popular examples of pivot rules are the most negative reduced cost rule
[
Dan51
]
, the steepest edge rule and its approximations
[
Har73
,
Gol76
,
FG92
]
, and the shadow vertex rule, also known as the parametric rule
[
GS55
,
Bor77
]
.
It is this last pivot rule that is the foundational tool for most probabilistic analyses, including all results in smoothed analysis.

1.2
Limitations of Smoothed Analysis

First, modern linear programs seen in practice are very
sparse
; less than 1% of their entries are non-zero (see, e.g.,
[
Mar02
,
HM05
,
Gle+21
]
).
The same holds true for known worst-case inputs.
This stands in stark contrast with smoothed analysis, where 100% of the entries are non-zero since they are randomly perturbed according to a continuous probability distribution.
As such, in any smoothed probability model, the set of sparse linear programs has measure
0
.
Since both practical linear programs and theoretical worst-case inputs are sparse, it remains unclear whether smoothed analysis provides a viable synthetic model for describing the “brittleness” of worst-case inputs and whether smoothed analysis indeed helps to explain why the simplex method is usually fast on inputs observed in practice. In their foundational work introducing smoothed analysis
[
ST04
]
, Spielman and Teng highlighted the lack of sparsity as a weakness of their model and stated finding some analysis accounting for sparsity as a future direction.

Second, when LP constraint data is noisy or is rounded to low precision, this affects LP solver behavior negatively. For example, a nominally singular set of constraints might become a basis (potentially an ill-conditioned one) due to improper rounding, and this can lead to performance degradation.
For this reason it is recommended to specify all constraints with maximum numerical precision (see, e.g., Gurobi’s Guidelines for Numerical Issues
[
Gur
]
).
In contrast, smoothed analysis produces stronger performance guarantees if
more
noise is added, thereby seemingly predicting that lower numerical accuracy results in performance improvement.
Due to this disagreement between practical advice and theoretical prediction, we argue that smoothed analysis is not appropriate for accurately modeling the effects of round-off error or floating-point imprecision.

Third, a small perturbation to the constraint matrix can lead to a large change in the feasible region.
This fact has long been observed on practical LPs in the robust optimization literature (see, e.g.,
[
BGN09
]
).
We must assume that even these LPs which are sensitive to small perturbations were formulated appropriately by the modeler. We interpret this to imply that the constraint data is highly accurate, in the sense that the constraint matrix entries cannot be independently perturbed by as little as
\frac{1}{10000}
.
In this manner, we argue that smoothed analysis is not an appropriate model for measurement error or other forms of limited precision input.

The variable nature of
\sigma
leaves smoothed analysis agnostic as to what it models.
Although different interpretations have been given in the literature, they largely fall within one of three categories:
smoothed analysis models the “brittleness” or “isolation” of worst-case instances,
smoothed analysis models the effects of numerical imprecision in floating-point arithmetic,
or smoothed analysis models the effects of measurement error or other arbitrary influences.
As we have seen above, each of these three interpretations has notable weaknesses.

1.3
Introducing By The Book Analysis

In this paper we propose a new algorithm analysis framework that we call by the book analysis.
Unlike the above-mentioned agnosticism of smoothed analysis, by the book analysis is intentional about what effect it models.
As part of this, we open up the possibility of modeling not only the input data but also aspects of an algorithm’s implementation.
It is well-known that theoretical descriptions of algorithms differ from their software implementations.
These differences are often thought to be incidental and to be theoretically uninteresting, even though they have proven to be impactful repeatedly in practice.
We posit that these differences, if they are found in state of the art implementations, can be essential to a full theoretical understanding.

With by the book analysis, we strive to prove performance guarantees while using assumptions that are maximally grounded in computational experience.
For this reason, our research process has occurred in three phases as depicted in

In the initial phase we studied scientific literature, open-source code, and user manuals.
We also interviewed developers working on seven different LP software packages.
A primary role in this phase was played by the titular textbooks
[

Mar02

After observing the prominent role that numerical tolerances are given in both books (on which we will elaborate later), we verified their importance by way of inspecting both the source code of open-source LP solvers and the user manuals of closed-source LP solvers.
Because the simplex method is a popular and well-documented algorithm, we were able to draw on a diverse array of sources and identify points of broad agreement.
The outcomes of the first phase are described in
Section

2
.

The second phase was a more subjective process, during which we turned our observations into mathematically stated assumptions. We balanced verisimilitude with our observations against the tractability of the upcoming mathematical analysis.
Throughout this phase, we did our best to identify assumptions that would result in the strongest possible upper bound on the number of pivot steps.
That is, we aim for a traditional, theoretical performance guarantee which, when all parameters are filled in with plausible values, gives the strongest possible conclusion in terms of how well this bound is reflecting an algorithm’s behavior in practice.

In the third and final phase, we used our new mathematical assumptions on the data and the algorithm to prove running time bounds.
This phase was thus spent on more traditional theorem/proof work.
During this phase of the work, we found that the
mean width
(a type of mixed volume of a convex body, see, e.g.,
[
Sch13
]
) of the feasible set naturally appeared in our proofs.
For that reason, we went back and made one more observation that we had not thought to make during the first phase: we performed measurements on the MIPLIB 2017 dataset
[
Gle+21
]
to find typical values for the mean width.
Although we broke the intended by the book research flow with this step, we belief it to be justified nevertheless.
The mean width is a comparatively easy quantity to measure, which meant that there was no need to revisit the subjective work of the second phase.

Note that the order of the phases is important. In particular, the determination of parameters whose values are—in practical reality—constrained and the determination of those values occurs in each iteration of the proof process
before
the production of theoretical running time bounds. That is, it is contrary to by the book analysis to produce a theoretical bound (using whichever parameters prove to be mathematically fruitful) and to only then search for explanations as to why these parameters may be bounded in practice. By
basing
the theoretical approach on observed reality, we argue that this methodology is better equipped to produce accurate explanations for observed reality.

Different by the book analyses for the same algorithm are possible; varying in how the algorithm and the data are modeled, i.e., which assumptions and parameters were chosen.
We can distinguish at least two paths for improvements on a particular by the book analysis. The first occurs in the third phase and is one that we are used to in theoretical computer science: For the same set of mathematical assumptions, stronger mathematical analysis techniques can be developed, leading to stronger theorems.
The second is due to the fact that
different
mathematical assumptions can be reasonably derived from the same or different primary sources used in the first phase. One is unlikely to produce a singular, holistic explanation for the performance of an algorithm, parameterizing by all possible factors and perfectly distilling the impact of these parameters into a bound. This is obvious on its face, but it is all the more true because of the fact that the third phase necessitates that some aspects of reality be simplified into a mathematical model on which theoretical analyses can be performed. The goal is not to produce a perfect model of reality, but instead to produce a mathematical model which is better informed by and reflective of reality. As such, a by the book analysis can be improved in the first and second phases via a better interpretation of primary sources and/or a better synthesis of those primary sources into mathematical assumptions and models.
Some axes on which such improvement can be evaluated include, but are not limited to, evaluability and verisimilitude.
It is preferable to produce bounds in terms of parameters which can be effectively observed to be bounded.
It is preferable to use mathematical assumptions which more closely resemble computational practice, such as to have a theoretical description of an algorithm which comports with the way in which it is implemented in code.
To exemplify this last point, we can wield it against our present analysis. We still require the simplex method to use the shadow vertex pivot rule and the sequential Phase I procedure.
These are two of the ways in which our present mathematical model of the simplex method is dissimilar from common implementations.

1.4
Observations of Practical LP

Having described the framework of by the book analysis in general, we can now present a by the book analysis of the simplex method. We begin here with an overview of our findings about the properties held by LP in practice. As described in Section
1.3
, these findings will inform our mathematical assumptions. As noted already, two main contributing factors to our findings are the software implementations of the simplex method and the observed characteristics of (well-formulated) inputs.
A given problem can be modeled in various ways, and a given LP has a wide array of equivalent formulations. When LPs are solved in practice, they are typically modeled by domain experts who are constructing their formulations according to best practice recommendations explicitly detailed in software user manuals.

We review a number of software packages and describe our observations.
For the open-source solvers Glop and HiGHS, we report our observations from reading their respective source codes.
For the closed-source solvers Gurobi and MOSEK, we can only evaluate what their user manuals describe.
We run an experiment on the MIPLIB 2017 benchmark set to support our assumption that feasible sets of practical LPs have small mean width.
The features of solvers described here are mostly limited to what is directly relevant to concerns around scaling, tolerances, and bound/cost perturbations.
We first describe the general principles of what we observe, and in
Section

2
we describe what observations we have with regard to specific software packages.
There are many other features we do not remark upon, although we believe that some can be potentially of interest to theoretical computer scientists and may lead to improved by the book analyses of the simplex method.

Scaling

A well-formed LP ideally satisfies certain assumptions about the scale of the different quantities that appear among the input data, the output solution, and intermediate values.
These assumptions are important to the functioning of real-world software.
The primary way in which this scaling requirement has been explained is through the effects of floating-point arithmetic.
In an IEEE 754 double precision floating point number, there is 1 bit for the sign, 11 bits for the exponent, and 53 bits for the significand (of which 52 are explicitly stored).
This means that any such number can have a relative precision of at most
2^{-53}\approx 10^{-16}
.
Any arithmetic performed with numbers whose relative magnitudes differ by many orders of magnitude will yield imprecise results.
This effect is particularly visible when solving a system of linear equations.
Here, the backward error up to which it can be solved increases when the linear system has a high condition number. Typically in linear programming software, the condition number of linear systems is assumed to be no greater than
10^{10}
.

These numerical concerns are an important reason for why proper scaling of the input is explicitly recommended.
Users are advised to manually scale the rows and columns of the problem appropriately.
Most often, this advice takes the form of suggesting to choose the measurement units for the rows and columns in such a manner that no quantities will be too large or too small.
Beyond this advice, most LP solvers also include automatic scaling algorithms in case the user’s scaling is deemed insufficient.
Overall, the role of scaling for LP is poorly understood
[
Mar02
, p. 110]

[
Hua13
]
.

Perturbations

When feasibility tolerances are permitted, LP solvers can be made faster by intentionally changing the right-hand side
\textstyle\bf b
, bounds
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf 0$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf u$}}}
, or the objective
\textstyle\bf c

A number of mechanisms make use of this allowance.
We focus on one specific such mechanism, which is random perturbation before starting the simplex method.
Generally these perturbations are done in such a manner as to make the feasible region larger.
The perturbations are of a similar order of magnitude to the feasibility tolerance.
The primary use of this a priori perturbation is in perturbing the objective vector
\textstyle\bf c
before starting the dual simplex method.
In
Section

2
we will see that such perturbations are used in HiGHS.
During both the primal and dual simplex method, more perturbations and shifts can be performed at later iterations if needed. One consequence of bound perturbations is that we avoid degeneracy and consequently the simplex method cannot cycle.

\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf 0$}}},\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf u$}}}
and
\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}
.
Starting with the perturbed box constraints
\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf 0$}}}\leq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}\leq\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf u$}}}
, we solve for the vertex maximizing a random objective function
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}^{\top}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}
for a uniformly random objective vector
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
The Phase I procedure adds the inequality constraints from
A{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}\leq\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}
one-by-one, using the shadow vertex method to obtain bases that are feasible for the currently active set of constraints. In order to guarantee when the next to-be-added constraint can be added to the basis, we require that the square submatrices of
A
each have bounded condition number.
The dependence on this condition number is logarithmic.
Upper bounds on the condition numbers are also essential to the functioning of state of the art LP solvers as described in
Section

2
.

Apart from our choice of pivot rule, this Phase I procedure is nearly identical to the
sequential
algorithm described in
[
CW63
]
.
This specific Phase I method was chosen because it plays together well with the stochastic independence assumptions required in
Section

4
.
It is not used in practice, making this one way in which our analysis has qualitative room for improvement.

Phase II starts when a basic feasible solution is found for the linear program

\displaystyle\operatorname{maximize}\quad

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}^{\top}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}\quad

\displaystyle A{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}\leq\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf 0$}}}\leq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}\leq\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf u$}}}.

At this point, the algorithm will use the shadow vertex method with auxiliary objective
\textstyle\bf\theta
and the given objective
\textstyle\bf c
to find its solution.
We end
Section

4
with a proof that the resulting solution is feasible up to primal feasibility tolerances and satisfies the complementary slackness up to optimality tolerance, indicating that the returned solution satisfies all requirements.

Running time bound

Section

5
folds the variable bounds into the constraint matrix and considers linear programs of the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{maximize}\quad

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}^{\top}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}\quad

\displaystyle A{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}\leq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}},

where again every row of the matrix
A
is assumed to have norm equal to
1
.
Assuming that the right-hand side vector
\textstyle\bf b
is perturbed with certain exponentially distributed perturbations of size
\eta>0
, the expected number of pivot steps taken by the shadow vertex method to travel from the vertex maximizing a uniformly random objective vector
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
to the vertex maximizing an objective
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}+\varepsilon\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}
is, by
Theorem

24
, at most

O\left(d\sqrt{\frac{d\ln(n)M}{\eta}\ln\left(\frac{nN\kappa}{\eta\varepsilon}\right)}\right),

where
M>0
indicates the expected mean width of the feasible set and
N
is the maximum absolute objective value.
The condition number
\kappa\geq 1
is a measure of the matrix
A
and is assumed to be no more than
10^{10}
in practice as described in
Section

2
.
The ratio
N/\varepsilon
is essentially scale-invariant in
\|{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}\|
, but our analysis does require the linear program to be “well-scaled” in two ways:
the feasible set must be scaled such that the mean width
M
is bounded, and the constraint matrix must be scaled such that the rows of
A
each have norm equal to
1
. In addition, scaling assumptions are required for the feasibility tolerances to be well-defined, and are common for state of the art software implementations as well, as described in
Section

2
.
The perturbation size is chosen such that
\eta\cdot d\ln(n)\approx 10^{-6}
is the feasibility tolerance.
Our experiments on MIPLIB 2017 instances
[
Gle+21
]
suggest that many LPs have a value of
M
around
100
.
Filling in the parameters with these plausible values yields a running time for Phase II, when solving an LP up to tolerance
\text{feasTol}>0
, of

O\left(\frac{d^{2}\cdot\ln(n/\text{feasTol})^{1.5}}{\sqrt{\text{feasTol}}}\right)

pivot steps and a complete two phase simplex method of

O\left(\frac{d^{2}n\cdot\ln(n/\text{feasTol})^{1.5}}{\sqrt{\text{feasTol}}}\right)

pivot steps. The algorithmic details are found in
Section

4
. That is, after treating our chosen parameters (as identified during the first and second phases of by the book analysis) as having realistic and constant values, we obtain a polynomially bounded running time.

Importantly, we have produced a
theoretical
treatment of the simplex method’s running time that is principally grounded in observable traits of real-world codes and problem instances.

We note that the explanatory power of this result strongly exceeds that of smoothed analysis, since the result presented here follows from modeling practical implementations of the simplex method applied to real-world data. In particular, the constraint matrix is not perturbed, so our analysis applies to sparse matrices, and all parameters involved are based on measures computable in practice.
Our analysis is built to know ahead of time exactly what real-world properties our mathematical assumptions are modeling. In contrast (as discussed in Section
1.1
), there is not agreement on what, if anything, is actually modeled in smoothed analysis by perturbing the constraint matrix. We argue that the result presented here narrows the gap between what we can explain using theoretical frameworks and what is observed in practice.

Proof overview

The proof of our main technical result
Theorem

24
proceeds in three phases. First, in
Section

5.1
we describe the effect of the right-hand side perturbations.
We show that, conditional on a basis being feasible, the corresponding basic solution will have all its non-zero slack values on the inequalities be large.
Specifically, the smallest non-zero slack will be at least
\frac{\eta}{1240d\ln(n)}
with probability at least
0.9
.
As in the previous paragraph,
\eta
is a measure of the magnitude of the perturbations.
The lower bound on the slacks is derived using a Chernoff bound.
This argument is derived from a similar one used in smoothed analyses of the simplex method
[
HLZ23
,
BH25
]
.

Second, in
Section

5.2
we show a dual analog to the above, based on the shadow vertex simplex path from a random auxiliary objective
\textstyle\bf z
to a largely fixed objective
\varepsilon^{-1}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}
.
We show that, conditional on a basis lying on this shadow path, with probability at least
0.9
there exists an intermediate objective
t{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}
with
t\in[0,\varepsilon^{-1}]
for which this basis is optimized and for which the reduced costs on the tight constraints are large. Note that the existence of
t\in[0,\varepsilon^{-1}]
for which the basis is optimal for
t{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}
is guaranteed by virtue of being on the shadow path. The novelty is that we can guarantee a choice of
t
yielding large reduced costs on tight constraints.
In order to simplify this part of the analysis, we will throughout
Section

5
assume that the random objective
\textstyle\bf z
is sampled from an
L
-log-Lipschitz probability distribution.
This will not affect the final conclusion.

Finally, in
Section

5.3
we show our upper bound on the length of the semi-random shadow vertex path.
As a consequence of our results in
Section

5.1
and
Section

5.2
, we show that a large fraction of the feasible bases on this path must have both (in the above explained senses) large non-zero slacks and admit an intermediate objective for which all
d
reduced costs are large.
Afterwards, we argue that whenever two subsequent bases on this path both have both properties, then the pivot step leading from the first to the second of them must make large progress.
This progress is measured through four different potential functions.
Two of these potential functions measure the progress of the intermediate objective vector
t{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}
on the line segment from
\textstyle\bf z
to
\varepsilon^{-1}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}
.
The third and fourth potentials encode progress in the objective value and auxiliary objective value respectively.
A majority of pivot steps will consume a certain amount of at least one of these four potential functions.
The total amount of potential avaible is bounded, which leads to our upper bound on the expected number of pivot steps.

1.6
Related Work

The simplex method has been studied in the worst case complexity framework under various additional assumptions.
One line of work has assumed that the constraint matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times d}
is integer and has every square submatrix have a determinant which is bounded from above in absolute value.
Under this assumption, upper bounds on the running time of the simplex method have been proven
[
EV16
,
BR13
,
DH16
]
.
This extends earlier work on the combinatorial diameter of polyhedra with bounded subdeterminants
[
DF94
,
Bon+12
]
.
Approximating the maximum subdeterminant is known to be NP-hard
[
Sum+14
]
.
Similar results are available for a closely related condition measure, the circuit imbalance
[
ENV22
]
.
A study estimating the circuit imbalance on the instances in MIPLIB 2017
[
KK24
]
found it to require much higher numerical precision than is available with IEEE 754 double precision floating point arithmetic.

A parallel line of work investigates the worst-case performance of the simplex method when the slack values and reduced costs are bounded away from zero
[
KM11
,
KM12
,
TMK19
,
Kit25
]
.
These papers extend an earlier result about the policy iteration algorithm for Markov Decision Processes with bounded discount rate
[
Ye05
]
, and are similar to a calculation done by Dantzig
[
Dan90
]
.
The condition number underlying this line of work is NP-hard to compute as shown in
[
KST18
]
.
The same paper also estimated this condition number on the LP instances in the NETLIB test problem set
[
Gay85
]
, though the specific measurement methodology they used severely limits what conclusions can be drawn based on these estimates.

Another line of work focused on non-degenerate steps on polytopes with vertices in
\{0,1,\dots,k\}^{n}
called
(0,k)
-lattice polytopes. For
k=1
, Naddef showed their diameters were at most
n

[
Nad89
]
, and this bound was later shown to be achievable by the simplex method in
[
Bla+25
]
. More generally, Kleinschmidt and Onn showed a bound of
nk
on diameters of
(0,k)
-lattice polytopes with constant factor improvements in
[
DM16
]
and was shown to be algorithmically achievable in
[
DM22
,
Bla23
]
.

In our by the book analysis, we use a simplex method with bound perturbations to obtain provable running time guarantees.
Bound perturbations are often incorporated in codes with the stated purpose of preventing issues around degeneracy
[
Mar02
,
Kob05
]
.
Other methods to prevent stalling are possible, including the use of expanding tolerances or minimum step size
[
Gil+89
]
.
In a theoretical regime where bounds may not be changed, other approaches are available
[
Bla77
,
Mur08
,
KS24
]
.

Bound perturbations and random projections were previously used in
[
KS06
]
to find a weakly polynomial-time algorithm for linear programming.
For their algorithm, they construct a series of auxiliary linear programs for which boundedness must be proven.
Repeatedly their algorithm finds feasible solutions of large norm using a variant of the shadow vertex simplex method, which is then used to rescale the auxiliary linear program.
A volume decrease argument, similar to the analysis of the ellipsoid method, eventually allows them to prove whether the auxiliary linear program is bounded or unbounded.

Our main technical result

\displaystyle\max~

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}^{\top}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}

where
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is uniformly random distributed with respect to the Haar measure on the unit sphere.

In order to get an indication of what half the mean width of practical linear programs is, we performed a rudimentary experiment using the MIPLIB 2017 benchmark set
[
Gle+21
]
.

For every instance, we sampled an objective vector
\textstyle\bf z
and maximized them over the LP relaxation using
Gurobi 12.0.1
.
The entries of
\textstyle\bf z
were sampled independently at random from a Gaussian distribution with mean
0

1
.
After computing the optimum value for this objective, we divided the value by
\|{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}\|
so as to obtain the optimal value for
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}=\frac{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}}{\|{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}\|}
.
We repeated this 500 times for every instance, and every individual trial was given a time limit of
1
minute to solve the linear optimization problem.
We kept data on every instance for which all 500 trials returned with status
GRB.OPTIMAL
, indicating that an optimal feasible solution was found.

Out of 240 instances in the benchmark set, our code reported data on 169 of them.
Of these, 102 instances found an average of less than
100
for half the mean width among the 500 samples.
An average less than
1000
was obtained for 128 instances.
Twelve instances had an average less than
1
.
The code for this experiment can be found at
https://github.com/sophiehuiberts/miplib-meanwidth
.

We take these results as a preliminary indication that the mean width of the feasible set is bounded from above for well-scaled linear programs.

The optimal objective values for the LP relaxations, with their original objective, range between
-6\cdot 10^{8}
and
8\cdot 10^{8}
. When we normalize the objective vector to have norm equal to
1
, the optimal values range between
-5\cdot 10^{6}
and
4\cdot 10^{8}
.
These large ranges are caused by a small number of outlier instances.
For the majority of instances in the benchmark set, the LP relaxation’s optimal value is less than
10^{5}
in absolute value.
When normalizing the objective vector to have norm
1
, the vast majority of instances have an optimal value of at most
10
in absolute value.

We write
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\binom{[n]}{d}=\{B\subseteq[n]:\lvert B\rvert=d\}
.
We take
[0]=\emptyset
to be the empty set.
Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and a set
S\subseteq[n]
we write
A_{S}
to indicate the submatrix consisting of rows indicated by indexes in
S
, and its associated map
A_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{S}
.
A set

is called a

if the square matrix
A_{B}
has full rank.
For a vector
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we write
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
to indicate the restriction of
b
to the entries indexed in
S
.
When
A,{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}
are clear from context, we define the basic solution for a basis
B
as
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}^{B}=A_{B}^{-1}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}_{B}
.
Scalars are denoted by lowercase letters, vectors by bold lowercase letters, and sets and matrices by uppercase letters.
For two vectors
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
[{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}^{\prime}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the straight line segment connecting them.

3.1
The Shadow Vertex Simplex Method

Our analysis makes use of a particular simplex method called the shadow vertex simplex method originally introduced by Gass and Saaty in
[
GS55
]
in the context of parametric linear programming. A textbook introduction that proves the standard properties we rely on may be found in
[
DH21
]
.

The shadow vertex simplex method works as follows.
We fix a linear program

\displaystyle\operatorname{maximize}\quad

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}^{\top}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}\quad

\displaystyle A{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}\leq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}},

with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

as input and start with an initial feasible basis
B
. We obtain an auxiliary objective
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as follows. Let
\mathbf{z}
be generically chosen such that
\mathbf{z}A_{B}^{-1}>0
holds.
If
\mathbf{c}A_{B}^{-1}\geq 0
, already the current basis is optimal and we are done. Otherwise, there exists a coordinate
i
such that
(\mathbf{c}A_{B}^{-1})_{i}<0
. Over all those indices
i
for which
(\mathbf{c}A_{B}^{-1})_{i}<0
, we apply the following ratio test. We define

\lambda=\min_{i}\left(-\frac{(\mathbf{z}A_{B}^{-1})_{i}}{(\mathbf{c}A_{B}^{-1})_{i}}:(c\mathbf{A}_{B}^{-1})_{i}<0\right),

and let
p
be the argument minimizing the same function. Then, by the minimality of
\lambda
and positivity of
\mathbf{z}A_{B}^{-1}
,
((\mathbf{z}+\lambda\mathbf{c})A_{B}^{-1})_{i}>0
for all
i\neq p
and
((\mathbf{z}+\lambda\mathbf{c})A_{B}^{-1})_{p}=0
. By non-degeneracy, there is a unique basis
B^{1}\neq B
such that
B^{1}\supseteq B\setminus\{p\}
. We let
B^{1}
be the new basis and
\mathbf{z}+\lambda\mathbf{c}
as the new auxiliary objective and continue the process from
B^{1}
following a sequence of adjacent bases
B^{1},B^{2},\dots,B^{k}
until reaching an optimal basis. We note that the genericity of
\mathbf{z}
ensures that for each basis
B^{i}
on the path, there exists
t>0
such that
(\mathbf{z}+t\mathbf{c})A_{B^{i}}^{-1}>0
. The existence of such a
t
is what allows the loop to continue even though
({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf z$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf z$}}}+\lambda{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}})A_{B^{1}}^{-1}
is only positive in the coordinates of
B^{1}\cap B
and
0
otherwise.

There are two standard geometric interpretations of the shadow vertex simplex method. The first is in terms of a two dimensional linear projection. Let
\mathbf{v}=A_{B}^{-1}b_{B}
be the vertex for the initial basis
B
. Then
\mathbf{z}
is a generic vector optimized uniquely at
\mathbf{v}
. Then orthogonally projecting onto the
(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})
-plane gives a polygon. By construction,
\mathbf{v}
and the
\mathbf{c}
-maximizer are both projected onto the boundary of that polygon and are the vertices maximizing the first and second coordinate respectively. A path in the polygon between the projection of
\mathbf{v}
and the projection of the
\mathbf{c}
-maximizer corresponds uniquely to a path on the polytope of the same length. This path is precisely the path chosen by the shadow vertex simplex method with auxiliary vector
\mathbf{z}
.

The second geometric interpretation relies on a dual perspective on a polytope. Given any feasible basis
B
, the cone
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}A_{B}
is called the normal cone of
B
or equivalently the normal cone of the vertex
A_{B}^{-1}b_{B}
. It is precisely the cone of all objectives optimized at that vertex. Then the shadow vertex simplex method functions by choosing a generic vector
\mathbf{z}
in the normal cone of the initial vertex
\mathbf{v}
. Then the path traversed consists of all vertices with normal cones that intersect the line segment
[\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}]
. Equivalently, that is precisely the set of normal cones that intersect the ray
\{\mathbf{z}+\lambda\mathbf{c}:\lambda\geq 0\}
. We call the points on on
[\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}]
or the ray
\mathbf{z}+\lambda\mathbf{c}
intermediate objectives.

These geometric interpretations are useful for understanding the algorithm and explaining its efficiency in average case, smoothed analysis, and in the by the book analysis we undertake here.

For notational simplicity we assume that we are given a linear program of the form

\displaystyle\bf 0

\displaystyle\leq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}\leq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf u$}}}

We will solve this LP up to tolerances

To start, we perturb our bound vectors

The perturbation distribution is defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

Let
\eta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and let
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then define for each

Define a random vector
\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}
to be
({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}},\eta,\gamma)

.

We sample
(-\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf 0$}}},\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf u$}}},\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2d}

\left(({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf 0$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf u$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}),\frac{\text{feasTol}}{4\ln(n+2d)},2\ln(n+2d)\right)

Strong tail bounds will ensure that these perturbed bounds are suitable:

Lemma 2
.

Let
\eta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and let
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Let
\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}
be a
({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}},\eta,\gamma)
-exponentially distributed random vector. Then

\Pr\big[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}\in[{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}+2\gamma\eta]\text{ for all }i\in[k]\big]\geq 1-ke^{-\gamma}.

Proof.

By applying a union bound we know that

1-\Pr\big[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}\in[{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}+2\gamma\eta]\text{ for all }i\in[k]\big]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Pr[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}<{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}]+\Pr[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}>{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}+2\gamma\eta].

Note that by construction the latter probabilities are symmetrically of the same size, i.e.,
\Pr[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}<{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}]=\Pr[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}>{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}+2\gamma\eta]

for every
i\in[k]
.
Writing down their probability densities, we find

\displaystyle\Pr[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}<{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\eta}\int_{-\infty}^{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}}e^{-\lvert t-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}-\gamma\eta\rvert/\eta}\operatorname{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\eta}\int_{-\infty}^{-\gamma\eta}e^{-\lvert t\rvert/\eta}\operatorname{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{-\gamma}e^{-\lvert t\rvert}\operatorname{d}t=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\gamma}.

Summing over all
i\in[k]
,
we see that
1-\Pr\big[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}\in[{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}+2\gamma\eta]\text{ for all }i\in[k]\big]\leq ke^{-\gamma}
, and hence
\Pr\big[\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}\in[{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{i}+2\gamma\eta]\text{ for all }i\in[k]\big]\geq 1-ke^{-\gamma}.

∎

2

\displaystyle-\text{feasTol}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{d}

\displaystyle\leq\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf 0$}}}\leq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf 0$}}}

(1)

\displaystyle\bf u

(Slacks are large)

6
Discussion

In this paper we introduced the framework of by the book analysis and used it to study the performance of the simplex method.
We have presented a running time analysis for a simplex method with bound perturbations, obtaining an upper bound on the expected running time of
O\left(nd^{1.5}\ln(n)\sqrt{\frac{M\ln\left(\frac{nN}{\varepsilon\eta}\right)}{\eta}}\right)

pivot steps required to maximize the slightly-random linear objective
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf c$}}}+\varepsilon\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf\theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf\theta$}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},
with bound perturbations of order
\eta
.
We highlight the two most significant improvements over earlier work.
First, our analysis works for sparse constraint data, in contrast to smoothed and average-case analysis.
Since all practical LPs are sparse (see, e.g.,
[
Orc68
, p. 64]
and
[
Mar02
, p. 50]
) this is an important feature.
Second, the assumptions we make are grounded in observations from simplex method implementations, LP modeling best practices, and measurements from practical benchmark instances.
Our running time primarily depends on the LP being well scaled and the simplex method having bound perturbations.
Both of these properties are essential to any large-scale LP solver
[
Mar02
, p. 110, 241]
.
In this manner, the results of this by the book analysis correspond well with established knowledge on the simplex method as it is used in practice.

The exact role of scaling for linear programs has previously not been well understood, but is known to reduce the number of pivot steps
[
Mar02
, p. 110-111]

[
Pan16
, p. 98]
.
There is no broad agreement on how to define when an LP is well scaled
[
Tom75
]
.
We take our theorems and experiments to indicate that the mean width of the feasible set is bounded for “well-scaled” instances.
A more in-depth investigation is in order to substantiate or refute that belief.
Are the instances with small mean width indeed the ones that would be subjectively judged as well-scaled?
Are instances with large mean width typically harder to solve for a simplex method?
These are only some of the questions that need to be answered in order to confidently speak about whether small mean width is indeed a desirable property of linear programs.

We note that our analysis does not constitute a full or complete explanation of the real-world performance of the simplex method.
Part of the reason for this is quantitatively in the running time bound we find.
First, our running time bound contains a large constant factor.
Second, it scales faster than quadratically in the size of the problem, in contrast to the linear scaling observed in practice.
Third, the one over square root dependence on the feasibility tolerance would suggest that changing the feasibility tolerance by a factor
100
would change the running time by as much as a factor
10
.
Although more aggressive perturbations are said to improve performance, an effect of this magnitude has not been described in the literature.

Looking into the proofs, our analysis has strong requirements on the ratio test and pivot rule.
We require that bounds are perturbed once and never shifted again, which in particular rules out the use of infeasible step sizes as used with Harris’ ratio test.
Since any state of the art implementation of the simplex method benefits from Harris’ ratio test
[
Mar02
, p. 179]
, this is a notable limitation of the current analysis.
For the pivot rule, we require that the semi-random shadow vertex path is followed. For our analysis,
the randomness in the perturbations and pivot rule should furthermore be independent of each other and of the constraint matrix and fixed objective vectors.
This rules out a wide range of sophisticated pricing strategies, including steepest edge pivoting, partial pricing and multiple pricing (see
[
Orc68
, p. 112-114]
and
[
Mar02
, p. 186-188]
), as well as specific pivoting rules used in Phase I
[
Orc68
, p. 116-117]
.
The shadow vertex rule has also been criticized for the difficulty of following it with appropriate pivot tolerances in a numerical setting
[
Orc68
, p130]
.
We assume a primal simplex method which perturbs its bounds before starting to pivot, whereas in our observations we only found a priori perturbations to the cost vector in the dual simplex method.
Finally our Phase 1 procedure is not one commonly used in implementations.
For all these reasons, there is space to qualitatively improve over the analysis presented in this paper.

Despite these limitations, we believe that the present work is a significant step forward towards a theoretical understanding of the simplex method’s performance.
Moreover, we expect our proposed framework of by the book analysis to be applicable to many more algorithms.
Any algorithm could be the subject of a by the book analysis, provided that there is enough computational experience and/or high-quality open-source code available.

\displaystyle\Pr[s\leq t]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t}f(x)\operatorname{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t-2/L}f(x)\operatorname{d}x+\int_{t-2/L}^{t}f(x)\operatorname{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t-2/L}f(x)\operatorname{d}x+\frac{2}{3}\int_{t-2/L}^{t+1/L}f(T(y))\operatorname{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-\infty}^{t-2/L}f(x)\operatorname{d}x+\frac{2}{3e}\int_{t-2/L}^{t+1/L}f(y)\operatorname{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{3e}\int_{-\infty}^{t+1/L}f(y)\operatorname{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3e}\Pr[s\leq t+1/L]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{3e}\Pr[s\leq t+1/L].

(10)

Using the above two inequalities in order to bound the numerator and the denominator, we
can now prove the lemma as follows

\displaystyle\Pr[s\geq t-\varepsilon\mid s\leq t]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr\big[s\in[t-\varepsilon,t]\big]}{\Pr[s\leq t]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3e\Pr\big[s\in[t-\varepsilon,t]\big]}{2\Pr[s\leq t+1/L]}

(by (
10
))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3e^{3}\varepsilon L\cdot\Pr\big[s\in[t,t+1/L]\big]}{2\Pr[s\leq t+1/L]}

(by (
9
))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{31\varepsilon L\cdot\Pr\big[s\in[t,t+1/L]\big]}{\Pr[s\leq t+1/L]}

\displaystyle=31\varepsilon L\cdot\Pr[s\geq t\mid s\leq t+1/L].

In order to establish our final inequality, we use
\Pr[s\geq t\mid s\leq t+1/L]\leq 1
to find

\displaystyle\Pr[s\geq t]

\displaystyle=\Pr[s>t+1/L]+\Pr[s\geq t\mid s\leq t+1/L]\Pr[s\leq t+1/L]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr[s\geq t\mid s\leq t+1/L]\cdot\Big(\Pr[s>t+1/L]+\Pr[s\leq t+1/L]\Big)

\displaystyle=\Pr[s\geq t\mid s\leq t+1/L].

These two inequalities, then, prove the lemma as

\Pr[s\geq t-\varepsilon\mid s\leq t]\leq 31\varepsilon L\cdot\Pr[s\geq t\mid s\leq t+1/L]\leq 31\varepsilon\Pr[s\geq t].

∎
