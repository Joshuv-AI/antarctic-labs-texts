Metabolic status of grazing goat kids in the soil and climatic conditions of Santa Elena, Ecuador
Authors: Debbie Shirley Chávez-García, Juan Ramón García-Díaz, Néstor Vicente Acosta-Lozano, Raciel Lima-Orozco, Orlando Roberto Quinteros-Pozo, Joffre Javier Masaquiza-Aragón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Longitudinal monitoring provides new metabolic data on the kids from Santa Elena.•The Creoles and F1 Boer × Creole kids showed similar metabolic profiles.•This metabolic similarity supports the adaptation of the F1 kids to local grazing conditions.•Age was a significant source of variation in the metabolic profile of the growing kids.•The F1 Boer × Creole kids showed higher serum alkaline phosphatase activity.Keywords: Creoles, Boer, Hematological, Hemoglobin, Total proteins, Urea, Blood serum

Introduction

Metabolic status is evaluated by blood parameters and is an essential tool to know the nutrition and health of animals, and thus, develop strategies for the early diagnosis of metabolic disorders that affect the bioeconomic behavior of the animal, for the treatment and prevention of diseases, and to establish management practices that benefit bio productive behavior (Braun et al., 2010).

To properly assess the metabolic status of animals, it is necessary to compare the values obtained in each research with the population reference values and their interpretation is more correct when these are obtained in climatic zones and similar groups (Piccione et al., 2014).

The hematochemical parameters of animals, and of adult goats, are influenced by reproductive and productive status (Vitulli-Moya et al., 2020), management practices and environmental factors, the age of the animals (Arfuso et al., 2016), and breed (Habibu et al., 2017). These factors modify the body's homeostasis and metabolic response (Mazzuca et al., 2021).

Kids, being growing animals, are metabolically more unstable due to the change to the extrauterine environment, completely different from the intrauterine one, which can cause changes in their metabolic status (Piccione et al., 2010). In addition, their hematochemical profile is considerably influenced by race, sex, age, time and year of delivery (Mazzuca et al., 2021; Piccione et al., 2014) and the interaction between these factors (Habibu et al., 2017).

However, there is little scientific information on the metabolic profile of kids of the Creole and F1 Boer x Creole genotypes, and the influence of sex, age and production cycle on it. These studies have not been carried out in the goat production conditions of the province of Santa Elena, where the Creole breed predominates in extensive systems (Chávez-García et al., 2023, 2026).

Consequently, there are no scientific reports of the metabolic profile in Creole and F1 Boer x Creole kids in the Province of Santa Elena, Ecuador, nor of the impact of this crossing on metabolic indicators, with respect to the Creole genotype, under the same soil and climatic conditions of this region, which will allow the adoption of selection strategies for their genetic improvement. based on scientific bases.

Due to the above considerations, the objective of this research was to evaluate the metabolic status of goats in grazing under the soil and climatic conditions of the Santa Elena Peninsula, Ecuador.

Materials and methods

The research was carried out between June 2024 and January 2025, in the Santa Elena Peninsula, Ecuador, in an area located at 2° 11′ 45″ S and 80° 59′ 02″ W and has an average annual temperature ranging between 18 and 25 °C. Its highest values are between January and April, and March is the hottest month, with an average maximum temperature of 28 °C and a minimum of 24 °C. The coldest months are from July to November, with a minimum value of 15 °C in August, whose monthly average is 18 to 21 °C (Chávez-García et al., 2023, 2026).

The research scenario has a dry arid climate, with little rainfall, which has an annual average of 125 to 150 mm. The rainy season is from January 15 to April 24, in which approximately 90% of the rainfall of the year occurs, the rainiest month is February; it has an average of 13.1 days with rainfall and the month of the maximum average, March, with 122 mm in March. The little rainy season, from April 24 to January 15, which includes the least rainy month, August. It has a relative humidity of 86% and an altitude between 5 and 20 m.a.s.l. (Chávez-García et al., 2023, 2026).

The research involved 75 goats; 30 Creole and 45 F1 Boer x Creole; 49 females and 26 males. Animals of 2.5 months of age, 10 ± 1 kg of BW, clinically healthy, were selected, according to the clinical diagnosis made according to the procedures described by Gumasta et al. (2024) and with body condition (WC) between 3 and 4, which was evaluated according to the procedures described by Mendizabal et al. (2011).

The kids were weaned at three months of age and from that moment on, semi-extensive grazing began between 10:00 and 13:00 h in quarters (4 ha) with a predominance of muyuyo grass (Cordia lutea), star grass (Cynodon nlemfuensis) and cascol (Caesalpinia spinosa).

After grazing, they were housed and fed 250 g DM per animal per day of a mixture of forages of guinea grass (Megathyrsus maximus), elephant grass (Cenchrus purpureus), corn panca (Zea mays), and a mixture of protein forages (Moringa oleifera (moringa), 50:50 v/v, dry basis), 25:25:25:25.

The animals received the mini-vitamin supplement PQ-FOST® (Farbiopharma, Ecuador), from weaning until the end of the trial, at a rate of 5 g/animal/day, according to the manufacturer's recommendations. The animals were dewormed with Albendazole (Life, Ecuador), 5 mg/kg BW (1 mL/50 kg BW) and Ivermectin (Vecol, Colombia), 0.2 mg/kg BW) 28 days before starting the study.

A total of 30 goats kids, 15 from each genotype, were included in the study. Of these, 19 were females and 11 were males. Hematological evaluations were conducted, at 4, 5 and 6 months of age, resulting in 90 individual observations. The following hematological parameters were determined: Total Red Blood Cell Count (TRBC), Hemoglobin (Hgb), Hematocrit (HCT), Hemoglobin/Hematocrit Ratio (Hgb/HCT), Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW), Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV), Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH), Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration (MCHC), White Blood Cells (WBC), Lymphocytes (LYM), Neutrophils (NEU) and Monocytes (MON).

A total of 75 goat kids, at 3, 4, 5 and 6 months of age, resulting in 300 individual observations. the primcipal biochemical indicators were determined for each animal: Total proteins (PT), Albumin (ALB), Immunoglobulins (IGB), Albumin globulin ratio (A/G ratio), Urea, creatinine (CRE), Alanine amino transferase (ALT), Aspartate amino transferase (AST), Alkaline phosphatase (ALP), Calcium, Magnesium and Phosphorus.

Sampling

For hematologic analysis, blood samples were taken by venipuncture of the jugular (5 mL) and deposited in IDEXX VetTube™ (IDEXX LABORATORIES VetLab® USA) tubes with EDTA (1 mg/mL blood).

To determine the biochemical indicators, 5 mL of blood was also taken by venipuncture of the jugular and deposited in IDEXX VetTube™ tubes (IDEXX LABORATORIES VetLab® USA), without anticoagulant, previously sterilized and demineralized; it was centrifuged at 3 500 g for 15 min and blood serum was obtained, which was frozen at -10 °C until analysis.

Determination of hematochemical indicators

All hematochemical indicators were determined in the diagnostic laboratory of the Manglaralto Support Center of the Santa Elena Peninsula State University. It complies with the international standard ISO 15189 of 2022, which guarantees quality in Clinical Laboratories (Giannoli et al., 2025). The variables, methods, and equipment by which they were determined are shown in Table 1.

Standardized analytical methods were used for all indicators, which in internal and external quality control tests have achieved good accuracy (r ≥ 0.980) and repeatability and reproducibility of their methods (CV ≤ 5%).

Statistical processing

Hematochemical indicators were analyzed using a mixed linear model. Genotype, sex, and age were included and evaluated as fixed main effects, together with their potential interactions, due to their biological and clinical relevance. Since these interactions were not significant, they were excluded to simplify the final model. Mean comparisons between genotypes and sexes were performed using the LSD test, while comparisons among ages were conducted using the Sidak test (West et al., 2014).

Except for the NEU, the hematological indicators studied correspond to the values found by Piccione et al. (2014), Arfuso et al. (2016), Al-Bulushi and Shawaf (2017), Karaşahin et al. (2022) and Kotani et al. (2024). The higher NEU count may be the reason why the Creole breed and its crosses are very resistant to diseases (Chávez-García et al., 2019).

These results contrast with other authors who reported that genotype, sex, and age (Kotani et al. (2024) influence the hematological profile (Habibu et al., 2017; Karaşahin et al., 2022), probably due to the soil and climatic conditions, production, and breeds of each research.

TRBC, Hgb, LEU, LYM, MON and NEU are within the physiological reference parameters (PFR) for goats (Piccione et al., 2010, 2014). On the other hand, the HCT and MCV are lower than the PFRs published by the authors, while the MCHC is higher than these PFRs; which explains that the R Hgb/HT in both genotypes and sex, and in all ages, was < 2.5% (Table 2).

The Hgb/HCT ratio indicates that the kids exhibited small red blood cells with a high MCHC, even though Hb levels were within the reference ranges (Vasilatis et al., 2023). This may represent a distinctive characteristic of the evaluated genotypes, at least under those edaphoclimatic and production conditions, where, in order to compensate for the smaller erythrocyte size and optimize oxygen transport, the animals show higher Hb concentration and a relatively elevated RBC count (Chávez-García et al., 2019).

However, further research is required to reach conclusive results regarding the red blood cell size in kids of the studied genotypes under these production conditions, since the hematological profile is influenced by stress, age and its relationship with immune responses, nutritional status, altitude, season of the year, management system, diseases, sex, reproductive stage, and parasitic load in the animals (Arsenopoulos et al., 2025; Karaşahin et al., 2022).

RDW in both genotypes and sexes, and in all ages, was between 37 and 38%, with no differences (p > 0.05) between these factors (Table 2); these values are higher than the PFR (21 to 28% (Piccione et al., 2010) those published by Al-Bulushi and Shawaf (2017), Arfuso et al. (2016), Karaşahin et al. (2022) and Arsenopoulos et al. (2025), Piccione et al. (2014). This could be explained by the fact that in the present study, the values of hematological indicators, especially TRBC, HCT, and MCV, are like those published by the authors, while those of Hgb are higher.

Moreover, the increase in RDW observed in the present study and the differences compared with the consulted literature are related to the low Hb/Ht ratio and MCV (Table 2). These indicators denote that the kids had small erythrocytes and corroborate that RDW depends on these cells and on MCV. For these reasons, RDW represents the coefficient of variation of erythrocyte volume (Arsenopoulos et al., 2025; Vasilatis et al., 2023)

The results regarding the effect of age on RDW are like those published by Piccion et al. (2010); However, there is little scientific literature on RDW, due to limitations in the diagnosis of this indicator.

Table 3 shows the parameters of the protein profile, noting that TP, ALB, IGB and A/G ratio were not affected (p < 0.05) by genotype and sex. TP and IGB were higher (P < 0.05) in three-month-old animals. The A/G ratio were higher (P < 0.05) in children aged 4 and 6 months. These results indicate that age influences the blood serum concentrations of these indicators.

The TP, ALB, IGB, and CRE values are within the PFRs (Piccione et al., 2010) and are lower than those published by Al-Bulushi and Shawaf (2017) and Karaşahin et al. (2022), Piccione et al. (2014). These differences support the fact that race and soil and climatic conditions are factors that affect these indicators.

The concentrations of TP, ALB, and IGB in mammalian blood serum vary according to sex (Karaşahin et al., 2022) and age (Li et al., 2024). The higher TP concentrations observed at three months of age are attributed to the increased protein synthesis required to sustain muscle, bone, and other tissue development during this growth stage (Li et al., 2024). According to these authors, the elevated IGB values at three months (Table 3) are influenced by immune system stimulation, which is modulated by environmental conditions.

The concentrations of TP, ALB, IGB and A/G ratio in the blood serum of kids (Table 3) are higher than those published in young, native and Saanen goats with extensive and intensive production systems (Mazzuca et al., 2021) and in kids of the Pardo Alpina breed, at three and seven months of age (Widodo et al., 2019).

In adult goats, TP, ALB and IGB were higher in animals over five years of age, compared to those under that age (Piccione et al., 2014).The results of this study and those of the literature consulted are contradictory with respect to the effects of age on protein profile indicators, probably to the various factors and experimental conditions of the studies.

The R A/IGB (Table 3) is like that published by the authors and indicates that the kids investigated were healthy and adapted to the soil and climatic and production conditions of the present study (Li et al., 2026).

In the present study, ALB represents 58.12% of PTs overall, with values that fluctuated between 56.90 and 59.06% in the different groups according to genotype, sex and age; which is in line with what has been found by other authors (Mazzuca et al., 2021; Piccione et al., 2010, 2014).

Urea concentrations in blood serum in all groups were within 0,55 to 9,50 mmol/L, which are within the reference parameters for goats (Al-Rukibat et al., 2020) and were not affected (P > 0.05) by the genotype, sex of the animals and age of the animal (Table 3).

The animals exhibited higher values at six months of age, which may be attributed to the fact that, during this stage, the kids were consuming in the barn a mixture of Moringa oleifera forage, whose crude protein content ranges between 200 and 250 g/kg of dry matter (Achmadi et al., 2023). Moreover, the protein of Moringa oleifera shows high ruminal degradability, with effective values exceeding 70% and rapid degradation rates, thereby enhancing the efficiency of nitrogen metabolism (Yahya et al., 2025).

However, urea concentrations in blood serum are affected in grazing goats, depending on the availability and consumption of fertilized protein plants and grasses, and the available body reserves (Chikwanda & Muchenje, 2016). These were greater than 10.15 mmol/L, the upper limit of the PFRs established by Brem et al. (2011), in developing animals that grazed Prosopis glandulosa and Acacia farnesiana, in periods of maximum availability of these plants and lower than PFRs when these trees were less available (García-Sánchez et al., 2023).

In dairy and meat goats, the consumption of crude protein, particularly under conditions of negative energy balance, increases hepatic ammonium (NH₄⁺) metabolism, with the consequent energy expenditure and elevation of blood urea levels. A portion of this urea re-enters the rumen via saliva, maintaining a constant supply of non-protein nitrogen (NPN) for the ruminal microbiota. However, optimal dietary protein levels not only regulate this metabolic pathway but also enhance energy efficiency, nutrient digestibility, and productive performance in goats (Li et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026).

The results of this study confirm that both the age of the animals and the feeding strategies significantly influence blood urea concentrations. In this regard, protein sources (soybean meal vs. corn gluten meal) modify gastrointestinal microbial nitrogen utilization, thereby affecting blood urea levels and nutrient digestibility (Zhao et al., 2026). Likewise, variations in forage type influence feed intake and serum urea concentrations (Aldian et al., 2023; Msiza et al., 2025).

The concentrations of ALT and AST were higher (P < 0.05) at the fifth and sixth months of age; while those of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) were higher (P < 0.05) in F1 BxC kids, while those of Ca, P and Mg did not differ (P > 0.05) according to genotype, sex and age of the animals (Table 4).

The PFRs of ALT are 11–40 IU/L and those of AST are 78–32 IU/L (Piccione et al., 2010). All ALT values fall within those PFRs. However, AST in both sexes and genotypes are higher than these PFRs and at 4.5 and 6 months of age. Both enzymes have intracellular function and when increased in blood serum they indicate damage to liver tissue (Gwaze et al., 2012), physical stress (Piccione et al., 2010), or nutritional imbalances, such as excess Crude Protein (CP) (Karaşahin et al., 2022)

ALT and AST concentrations were higher (P < 0.05) at the fifth and sixth months of age, probably because at those ages the animals may have experienced greater muscular activity and physical stress (Li et al., 2024), particularly in the case of AST (Bishnoi et al., 2024). In addition, the consumption of Moringa oleifera forages, which contain high levels of crude protein, may have increased hepatic activity to metabolize excess NH4+, consequently affecting hepatocytes and elevating ALT and AST (Zaher et al., 2020).

However, contradictory results exist in the scientific literature regarding the effects of Moringa oleifera forage on serum concentrations of hepatic enzymes; its forage significantly increased ALT but not AST (Zaher et al., 2020), whereas in another study neither enzyme was elevated, which may suggest that this plant has hepatoprotective properties (Afzal et al., 2022). The results of this study, together with the consulted bibliography, indicate the need to clarify the effect of its consumption on liver function.

The increase in AF in F1 BxC kids (Table 4) could be since animals of this genotype have a higher growth rate and, therefore, greater activity of the bone FA isoenzyme (Al-Bulushi et al., 2017).

In this experiment, the concentrations of Ca, P and Mg in blood serum are within the PFR in goats; Ca, 2.25–3 mmol/L; P, 1.29–1.94 mmol/L and Mg, 0.73–1.43 mmol/L (NRC, 2007), so deficiencies of these macroelements were diagnosed. These do not differ (P > 0.05) between the factors evaluated, which suggests that they were subjected to similar nutritional requirements and that both genotypes and ages in these soil and production conditions responded similarly.

The results of this study contrast with other authors who have published differences in them, according to genotype, sex, and age, but in different races and ages and with different feeding conditions (Karaşahin et al., 2022).

The concentrations of Ca, P and Mg in blood serum in this study are like those diagnosed in adult Creole goats in Argentina (Kotani et al., 2024), but are lower than those obtained by Karaşahin et al. (2022), in adult haired goats, which live in mountainous areas of Turkey. According to these authors, this may be the cause of the differences, because, under these conditions, goats require higher levels of these macroelements, which facilitate more stable and stronger bones.

On the other hand, the values of Ca, P and Mg in blood serum were higher than those diagnosed in Argentina. In this study, those of Ca, P and Mg fluctuated from 1.85 to 1.97; 0.95 to 1.26 and 0.96 to 1.03 mmol/L, those of Ca, P and mg, respectively; with a high percentage of goats with hypocalcemia and hypophosphoremia (Vitulli-Moya et al., 2020). The differences reaffirm that these factors are influential on blood mineral concentrations and can be explained by the fact that these authors investigated adult Saanen goats at different stages of their production cycle and under other soil and climatic and management conditions.

In addition, the concentrations of Ca, P and Mg in blood serum also depend on the time of year, due to the content of these minerals in the soil, grass, forages, the maturity stage of the pastures and the climate (Kotani et al., 2024).

Conclusions

It is concluded that the indicators of the metabolic profile are within the physiological parameters of reference in goats. The genotype, sex and age of the kids did not influence their hematological parameters and Ca, P and Mg in blood serum; but according to their age, TP and IGB were higher at three months; the A/G, at the fourth and sixth month; urea, at six months and ALT and AST, at the fifth and sixth months, while AF was higher in F1 BxC kids.

Ethical management of animals

The animals were managed under regular production conditions, and data collection was carried out during routine health and reproductive control activities, minimizing additional handling. Blood samples were collected by trained personnel through jugular venipuncture, using sterile material and physiologically safe volumes for goats, reducing stress and ensuring animal welfare, in accordance with the principles of the “Three Rs” (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) established by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH).

The study was supported by the certification of the Ethics Committee for Animal Research of the State Peninsula University of Santa Elena (UPSE), through Resolution No. 007-CEIA-UPSE-2024 and the Certification of Compliance with Ethical Guidelines for the project “Productive, reproductive, and metabolic characterization of F1 Alpine-Creole and Boer-Creole goats in productive systems of Santa Elena,” CPU code: 91870000.0000.388938. This certification establishes that the procedures applied do not imply unnecessary suffering nor significantly affect animal welfare, in accordance with Sanitary Resolution No. 227 (2024) of AGROCALIDAD and current national regulations.

Highlights

A longitudinal study was conducted, representing an important methodological contribution, as it provides reference data that were previously unavailable for this region and demonstrates that the metabolic status does not differ between Creole goats and F1 Boer × Creole under extensive grazing and supplementation with Moringa oleifera. This finding offers valuable information on the metabolic adaptation of the F1 genotype. A limitation of the study is the absence of data from adult animals, which would have allowed a deeper understanding of the metabolic and biochemical changes in this age group.
