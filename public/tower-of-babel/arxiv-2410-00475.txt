Probabilistic Analysis of Copyright Disputes and Generative AI Safety CCS: Social and professional topics Copyrights CCS: Computing methodologies Probabilistic reasoning
Hiroaki Chiba-Okabe

Abstract
. This paper presents a probabilistic approach to analyzing copyright infringement disputes. Under this approach, evidentiary principles shaped by case law are formalized in probabilistic terms, allowing for a mathematical examination of issues arising in such disputes. The usefulness of this approach is showcased through its application to the “inverse ratio rule”—a controversial legal doctrine adopted by some courts. Although this rule has faced significant criticism, a formal proof demonstrates its validity, provided it is properly defined. Furthermore, the paper employs the probabilistic approach to study the copyright safety of generative AI. Specifically, the Near Access-Free (NAF) condition, previously proposed as a strategy for mitigating the heightened copyright infringement risks of generative AI, is evaluated. The analysis reveals that, while the NAF condition mitigates some infringement risks, its justifiability and efficacy are questionable in certain contexts. These findings illustrate how taking a probabilistic perspective can enhance our understanding of copyright jurisprudence and its interaction with generative AI technology.



1.
Introduction

Copyright law grants creators exclusive rights to their original works across diverse categories of creative expression, such as literature, music, and art, with the aim of incentivizing creation and enriching society
(
Gorman 2006
)
. The rise of new technologies has continuously challenged conventional copyright principles, and in response, copyright jurisprudence has evolved over time, becoming increasingly complex
(
Joyce et al. 2020
)
. Despite the ever-growing need to understand how copyright law interacts with emerging technologies—generative AI being a prominent recent example
(
Samuelson 2023
;
Lemley 2024
)
—the theoretical framework remains underdeveloped, leading to significant legal uncertainty
(
Depoorter 2009
)
.

This article employs a probabilistic approach to analyze copyright infringement disputes, seeking to bring clarity to its complexities. The analysis is carried out by distilling and formalizing the fundamental principles governing copyright disputes as established through case law in the United States, particularly focusing on the requirements a copyright owner must meet to make a prima facie case of copyright infringement in court. This body of jurisprudence encompasses not only the substantive elements of copyright law, but also the evidentiary principles that guide how evidence is presented and assessed
(
Loren and Reese 2019
)
.

The theoretical foundation of this work lies in the long-recognized link between probabilistic reasoning and judicial proof
(
Eggleston 1989
;
Cullison 1969
;
Broun and Kelly 1970
;
Finkelstein and Fairley 1970
;
Cohen 1977
;
Eggleston 1978
;
McCauliff 1982
)
. Specifically, the legal fact-finding process can be understood to involve assessment of the probabilities of allegations being true or false based on the available evidence
(
Stein 2005a
;
Cheng 2013
)
. Building on this link, core evidentiary principles in copyright jurisprudence can be formalized in probabilistic terms, allowing for a mathematical analysis of a controversial legal doctrine, the “inverse ratio rule.” This rule governs the relationship between two critical pieces of evidence in copyright infringement cases—access and similarity to the original work. Although the validity of this rule has been the subject of considerable debate
(
Aronoff 2007
;
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
, a formal proof is established here, demonstrating that it holds under natural assumptions when its content and scope are carefully delineated.

Furthermore, this approach facilitates the analysis of copyright infringement risks introduced by technological advancements and the identification of effective mitigation strategies. Specifically, the probabilistic approach is utilized to evaluate the efficacy of the Near Access-Free (NAF) condition
(
Vyas et al. 2023
)
, which has been proposed to mitigate the risk of copyright infringement posed by generative AI. This paper identifies extensive access to copyrighted materials by generative models as a significant source of heightened infringement risks. While the NAF condition addresses this issue to some extent, the analysis reveals that it does so partly on ethically questionable grounds—namely, by reducing transparency regarding the materials to which generative AI had access during training—and that its effectiveness in mitigating infringement risks remains limited under certain situations.

2.
Related Work

Existing research has investigated the relationship between copyright law and probabilistic thinking, specifically concerning how the probability of creation relates to concepts of creativity and originality within the context of copyright law
(
Byron 2006
;
Khong 2007
;
Vermont 2012
;
Hacohen and Elkin-Koren 2024
;
Chiba-Okabe and Su 2024
)
. However, there has been little discussion on the application of probabilistic approaches in analyzing the evidentiary rules of copyright infringement disputes. While a previous study has alluded to the connection between the probabilistic nature of legal fact-finding and the inverse ratio rule
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
, no formal probabilistic analysis of the rule has been undertaken, to the best of the author’s knowledge. This gap is addressed by employing a probabilistic approach to formalize the evidentiary principles governing copyright disputes and mathematically analyze the inverse ratio rule.

In response to rapid advancements in generative AI technology, a substantial body of literature has emerged, addressing potential copyright risks and defenses available to developers
(
Sag 2023
;
Henderson et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2024
)
. These works discuss various legal challenges, including the potential for copyright infringement arising from AI-generated content, with some studies analyzing the Near Access-Free (NAF) condition as a mitigation strategy
(
Lee et al. 2024
;
Elkin-Koren et al. 2024
)
. This paper contributes to the discourse by providing a probabilistic analysis of legal issues surrounding the NAF condition, particularly through the lens of inference based on evidence in copyright infringement disputes.

3.
Analysis of Copyright Disputes

3.1.
Essential Evidentiary Principles in Copyright Disputes

To prevail in a copyright infringement dispute, the copyright owner, as the plaintiff, must demonstrate two elements under the traditional
Arnstein
test: ‘‘(a) that defendant copied from plaintiff’s copyrighted work and (b) that the copying (assuming it to be proved) went so far as to constitute improper appropriation.’’
1
1

1

Arnstein v. Porter
, 154 F.2d 464, 468 (2d Cir. 1946).
These two requirements are often referred to simply as “factual copying” and “improper appropriation,” respectively
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
. Although several defenses, including fair use, may be available to the defendant in some cases even if these elements are established
(
Loren and Reese 2019
)
, these defenses are not considered in this paper to maintain focus on the essential structure of copyright infringement cases.

Direct evidence of factual copying includes admission by the defendant or an eyewitness account of the replication or appropriative behavior, but such evidence is hard to obtain.
2
2

2

See, e.g.,
Smith v. Jackson
, 84 F.3d 1213, 1218 (9th Cir. 1996), stating “direct evidence of copying is not available in most cases.”
Therefore, factual copying is almost invariably proved by circumstantial evidence
(
Gorman 2006
;
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
, and the evidence generally accepted by courts consists of that pertaining to access and similarity. Specifically, copying is proved based on (i) evidence showing that the defendant had access to the original work and (ii) the similarity between the original work and the defendant’s work
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
;
Loren and Reese 2019
)
. Courts generally hold that proof of access combined with a certain level of similarity, that is probative of copying, is sufficient to establish factual copying
(
Latman 1990
;
Loren and Reese 2019
)
. Although both access and similarity support the inference of factual copying, simply having proof of access does not suffice to establish factual copying when there is no similarity between the two works.
3
3

3

Arnstein
, 154 F.2d at 468.
Proof of access pertains to the antecedent of factual copying, in the sense that the defendant having had access to the original work is a necessary condition for engaging in copying
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
;
Latman 1990
)
. In contrast, similarity emerges as a result of copying
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
;
Latman 1990
)
, and when the similarity between the two works is so pronounced as to be deemed ‘‘striking,’’ this alone suffices to establish that copying has occurred.
4
4

4

Id.
at 468-69.
In such cases, it can be inferred that the defendant had access to the original work based on the high degree of similarity.
5
5

5

Ty, Inc. v. GMA Accessories, Inc.
, 132 F.3d 1167, 1170 (7th Cir. 1997), stating “a similarity that is so close as to be highly unlikely to have been an accident of independent creation is evidence of access.”

The second requirement, improper appropriation, is established by demonstrating that the extent of copying reaches the threshold at which the similarity between the original work and the defendant’s work can be deemed “substantial” in degree. Determination of this requirement involves a normative judgment, taking into account both quantitative and qualitative aspects of copying
(
Gorman 2006
;
Balganesh 2012
)
. Thus, similarity serves a dual role: it supports the finding of factual copying
and
acts as an independent criterion for establishing improper appropriation
(
Latman 1990
;
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
, though the sense of similarity differs in each context, even if there may be some overlap
(
Loren and Reese 2019
)
.

3.2.
Formalization of Evidentiary Principles

Legal fact-finding is a reasoning process that involves assessing and weighing available evidence to reach a decision amid uncertainty. This process may be appropriately characterized by probabilistic reasoning, wherein adjudicators employ common sense and logic, drawing on knowledge of the frequencies of occurrences of certain events and patterns derived from experience to evaluate the likelihood of the particular circumstances in question
(
Stein 2005b
)
. Although the absolute truth typically cannot be attained, the final decision in most civil cases is made based on the standard of “preponderance of the evidence.” This standard requires the party bearing the burden of proof to demonstrate that their claim is more likely true than not. From a probabilistic perspective, it is often understood as requiring a demonstration of greater than 50% likelihood that the claim is true
(
McCauliff 1982
;
Stein 2005a
)
, particularly in binary situations where the question is whether an assertion is true or false
(
Cheng 2013
)
. In criminal cases, the relevant standard is “proof beyond a reasonable doubt,” which is commonly understood to require a significantly higher probability
(
McCauliff 1982
)
. Probabilistic approaches to fact-finding have been extensively explored in the literature over many years
(
Eggleston 1989
;
Cullison 1969
;
Broun and Kelly 1970
;
Finkelstein and Fairley 1970
;
Cohen 1977
;
Eggleston 1978
;
McCauliff 1982
)
and, despite considerable criticisms
(
Tribe 1971
;
Allen and Stein 2013
)
, have remained influential, even if primarily at a conceptual level.

Building upon this foundation, the essential structure of copyright disputes can be formalized by representing adjudicators’ degree of belief in terms of subjective probabilities, utilizing a discrete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
and random variables defined on this space. The condition for copyright infringement to exist can be expressed using random variables as
Copy=1\wedge Substantial=1
. Here,
Copy
is a binary random variable representing the presence (
Copy=1
) or non-presence (
Copy=0
) of factual copying of the plaintiff’s copyrighted work during the creation of the defendant’s work. Similarly,
Substantial
represents the presence or non-presence of substantial similarity between the two works. Typically, adjudicators can determine whether substantial similarity exists, or the truth value of
Substantial
, with certainty by directly comparing the original work to the defendant’s work presented at trial. In contrast, the absolute truth about whether the defendant engaged in factual copying remains unknown during the trial, necessitating a probabilistic approach to account for this uncertainty. The plaintiff must present sufficient evidence for the adjudicators to conclude that the probability of
Copy=1
exceeds a certain threshold,
\lambda
, which could be 0.5, aligning with the preponderance of evidence standard.

The case law establishing that probative similarity combined with access typically suffices to prove copying can be represented in probabilistic terms as follows:

\begin{gathered}P(Copy=1|Access=1,\,Probative=1)=\eta\\
\textrm{such that}\,\,\eta>\lambda\end{gathered}

where
Access
and
Probative
are binary random variables representing whether access by the defendant to the plaintiff’s original work was present and whether the original and defendant’s works are probatively similar, respectively. Suppose the adjudicators learn from the evidence presented during the trial that the defendant indeed had access to the original work (
Access=1
) and that the defendant’s work is probatively similar to the original work (
Probative=1
). This results in the adjudicators’ degree of belief that copying occurred (
Copy=1
) surpassing the threshold
\lambda
(since
\eta>\lambda
), thereby the standard of proof being met, as desired.

This approach can accommodate the logical relationship between factual copying and access, specifically that the defendant must have had access to the original work in order to copy from it. This relationship can be represented as:

P(Copy=1|Access=0)=0.

As a result of imposing this restriction on
Copy
, if it is known with certainty that the defendant did
not
have access to the original work, the degree of belief that the defendant copied the original work must be zero, as expected.

A further step can be taken to represent the evidentiary rule that striking similarity is typically sufficient to establish copying as follows:

\begin{gathered}P(Copy=1|Access=1,\,Striking=1)=\rho\\
P(Access=1|Striking=1)=\sigma\\
\textrm{such that}\,\,\rho\sigma>\lambda\end{gathered}

where
Striking
is a binary random variable corresponding to the presence or non-presence of striking similarity. Assuming that the adjudicators learn during the trial that
Striking=1
with certainty, taking into account the logical relationship between access and copying, the degree of belief about copying can be computed as:

\begin{split}&P\left(Copy=1|Striking=1\right)\\
&=P\left(Copy=1|Striking=1,\,Access=1\right)P\left(Access=1|Striking=1\right)\\
&\,\,+P\left(Copy=1|Striking=1,\,Access=0\right)P\left(Access=0|Striking=1\right)\\
&=P\left(Copy=1|Striking=1,\,Access=1\right)P\left(Access=1|Striking=1\right)\\
&\,\,+0\cdot P\left(Access=0|Striking=1\right)\\
&=\rho\sigma>\lambda.\end{split}

Therefore, the degree of belief of the adjudicators reaches the threshold corresponding to the applicable standard of proof, allowing them to conclude that copying has occurred.

3.3.
The Inverse Ratio Rule

The so-called “inverse ratio rule,” adopted by some courts, dictates a specific relationship between proof of access and similarity: stronger proof of access lowers the level of similarity required to establish copyright infringement, while a higher level of similarity reduces the burden of proof regarding access
(
Aronoff 2007
;
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
. Although the rule has been applied for nearly a century, serious objections have been raised to its application
(
Aronoff 2007
)
. The controversial status of the inverse ratio rule gained renewed attention with its recent abrogation by the Ninth Circuit Court,
6
6

6

Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin
, 952 F.3d 1051, 1066-69 (9th Cir. 2020) (en banc).
while it remains in use in some other courts
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
.

The controversy surrounding the inverse ratio rule partly arises from a likely inadvertent divergence in the understanding of its scope and content. Some courts have applied the inverse ratio rule in a way that strong evidence of access lowers the level of similarity required to establish the improper appropriation prong necessary for proving infringement
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
;
Aronoff 2007
)
. However, there appears to be little justification for the inverse ratio rule operating in this manner
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
. Another, more promising formulation of the inverse ratio rule pertains to the factual copying prong, where the rule adjusts the degree of similarity or the strength of evidence of access required to establish factual copying. Specifically, when strong evidence of access is present, a lower degree of similarity is sufficient to prove factual copying, whereas weak evidence of access necessitates a higher level of similarity. Conversely, a high degree of similarity lessens the evidence of access required, and vice versa. Proponents argue that the inverse ratio rule of this form follows from general principles of evidential inference
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
. However, critics contend that even the inverse ration rule formulated in this way is flawed, as access is merely a necessary condition for copying, and better evidence of access does not increase the probability of copying
(
Aronoff 2007
)
. This formulation of the inverse ratio rule was also the one rejected by the Ninth Circuit Court.
7
7

7

Id. at 1066-68.

The analysis here precisely describes the content of the inverse ratio rule as it pertains to the factual copying element in formal language and
proves
that the rule, defined in this way, must hold. The proof relies solely on assumptions that are naturally deduced from well-established jurisprudence. In particular, we consider a set of random variables that encapsulates the evidentiary principles discussed in the preceding section in a more generalized form (Definition
3.1
).

Definition 3.1.

C,A\in\{0,1\}
,
S\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, and
EA\in\{1,2,\cdots,m\}
are random variables that satisfy:

(1)

\displaystyle P(C=1|A=0)=0

(2)

\displaystyle P(C=1|A=1,\,S=i)=\alpha_{i},\,\forall i

(3)

\displaystyle P(A=1|S=i,\,EA=j)=\beta_{i,j},\,\forall(i,j)

(4)

\displaystyle EA\perp\!\!\!\perp\{C,S\}|A

3.1

As reflected in the well-established evidentiary rule that copying can be established by demonstrating both access and probative similarity, similarity serves as significant evidence in proving factual copying (Definition
3.1
(
2

It is reasonable, at least in principle (assuming no other specific circumstances suggest otherwise), to think that there is a monotonic relationship among the inferential values of different degrees of similarity; that is, a higher degree of similarity more strongly infers factual copying, so that

3.1

(

\beta_{i+1,j}\geq\beta_{i,j}
for all

Variable
EA

3.1
(

Conversely, the fact that the original work was kept private, with little chance of the defendant encountering it, would imply a low likelihood of access. Such evidence is assumed to have direct impact only on the inference of access and is independent of other variables given
A
(Definition
3.1
(

\beta_{i,j+1}\geq\beta_{i,j}

During court proceedings, adjudicators learn both the level of similarity between the works and the strength of evidence of access presented, corresponding to a pair of values

•

The fact that access constitutes a necessary condition for copying does not imply that the strength of the evidence of access cannot affect the inference of copying, as critics of the inverse ratio rule argue. Stronger evidence of access can increase the likelihood of copying because the likelihood of copying is the product of two factors: (i) the likelihood of copying given the presence of access and the level of similarity, and (ii) the likelihood of access given the level of similarity and other evidence of access. Thus, stronger evidence of access leads to an increased likelihood of copying by elevating the value of the second factor in the product. Although Proposition
3.2
shows the inverse ratio rule only with weak inequalities, which can be proved with minimal assumptions, the interplay between these two factors allows the inverse ratio rule to hold in an even stronger sense, with strict inequalities, in certain specific yet highly plausible circumstances (see Appendix
A
for further details).

Furthermore, although the conclusion that the inverse ratio rule holds true is accepted, the analysis indicates that the reasoning posited by its proponents does not fully capture the intricacies involved. The argument previously put forward to support the inverse ratio rule contends that two pieces of relatively weak evidence—pertaining to access and similarity, respectively—possess strong inferential value when combined
(
Balganesh and Menell 2022
)
. However, the reasoning of the results concerning the inverse ratio rule demonstrates that the level of similarity and the strength of evidence on access interact also in a complementary manner, rather than merely cumulatively, due to their multiplicative effect on the probability of copying. Specifically, a higher degree of similarity increases factor (i), and even when combined with a relatively lower factor (ii) resulting from weaker evidence of access, the product of these probabilities can still surpass the necessary threshold for establishing factual copying, and vice versa. Since the product of two probabilities is always smaller than either individual factor, combining small factors—stemming from weak evidence in both similarity and access—cannot yield a stronger inference on its own. This demonstrates that the cumulative inference logic is insufficient to fully explain the interaction between evidence of access and similarity.

One might notice the product of the two probability terms is reminiscent of the so-called conjunction problem that has been a point of debate between legal scholars who support probabilistic views of legal fact-finding and those who are skeptical about these views. However, as elaborated in Appendix
B
, the situation at hand is different from typical scenarios that are considered in the context of the conjunction problem, and discussions surrounding the problem does not affect the analysis of the inverse ratio rule.

4.
Copyright Safety for Generative AI

4.1.
Evidentiary Considerations in Copyright Disputes Involving AI-Generated Works

When generative AI is used to create content, it is typically offered as a service by the AI developer and used by a user. In such cases, both the user and the AI developer may be considered direct or indirect infringers if the generated output is substantially similar to an original work, depending on the specific circumstances. Copyright infringement may occur even without the user or developer intending to replicate the copyrighted material or to create something similar
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
.

Due to the uninterpretability of generative models, it can be challenging to provide direct evidence of factual copying for AI-generated content. In most cases, access and similarity will likely serve as the primary evidence of infringement, much like in traditional copyright disputes
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
. While the creative processes of generative models differ markedly from human creativity, it appears that similar evidentiary principles are likely to apply even when generative AI is involved in the creative processes. At a minimum, it is generally recognized that copying, even for generative AI outputs, requires some form of access to the original work, which may be demonstrated by its inclusion in the AI model’s training data
(
Zirpoli 2023
;
Lee et al. 2024
;
Elkin-Koren et al. 2024
)
. Moreover, studies have shown that exposure to certain types of content during training leads to a higher likelihood of these models reproducing or closely imitating that content
(
Carlini et al. 2021
;
Carlini et al. 2023
;
Hacohen et al. 2024
)
, suggesting that inclusion of specific data correlates with a greater probability of producing similar outputs through copying. This echoes the evidentiary framework applicable to traditional copyright cases, where greater similarity between works strengthens the inference of access and copying.

A distinctive feature of generative models, setting them apart from human artists and traditional creative tools, is their capacity to be trained on vast and diverse datasets. Training datasets are often compiled by scraping content from the Internet, including copyrighted material
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, direct inclusion of copyrighted material in the training data is not even necessary for latent diffusion models to produce images similar to copyrighted works, as these models can reproduce content from other images that retain similar latent information
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
, which exacerbates the problem. These characteristics suggest a high likelihood that generative models have what can be construed as ‘‘access’’ to copyrighted works. As a result, knowing that generative AI was used to create the defendant’s work would likely lead to a stronger belief in the presence of access. For example, consider a scenario where the original work was posted online, and the defendant’s work was created by an AI model trained on data scraped from the Internet. Even in the Internet age, simply posting a work online does not automatically establish access by the defendant. Courts require more than just online availability; factors like the work’s visibility or significant dissemination are needed to strengthen the inference of access.
8
8

8

See generally
Cooley v. Target Corp.
, No. 20-2152 (D. Minn. Sept. 28, 2022).
The use of a generative model to produce the defendant’s work can alternatively support the finding of access through the possibility that the model accessed the original work, given the model’s extensive potential to access vast datasets and the common practice of training models on diverse web-scraped data.

4.2.
NAF as a Framework for Copyright Safety

The NAF condition
(
Vyas et al. 2023
)
ensures that the generation probability of a generative model is closely aligned with that of a version of the model that has not had access to the copyrighted material. In a canonical form
(
Vyas et al. 2023
;
Elkin-Koren et al. 2024
)
, the NAF condition can be understood to require the following:

P(Z=z|Access=1)\leq e^{\epsilon}P(Z=z|Access=0)

for any
z
, where
Z
is a random variable representing the output of the generative model,
Access
indicates whether the model had access to a copyrighted work, and parameter
\epsilon>0
determines the strength of theoretical guarantee (the smaller the value of
\epsilon
, the closer the output distribution of the model must be to that of a model trained without access). The output distribution of the AI model must satisfy this inequality with respect to any copyrighted work in the training data. The NAF condition with a specific parameter value
\epsilon
will be referred to as
\epsilon
-NAF herein. One should note that this formulation is idealized, and in reality, the existence or nonexistence of access may not be clear-cut, as copyright-protected elements can be widely distributed and repeated across training data in ways that make them inseparable from other data.

While the authors suggest that this criterion is related to the concept of substantial similarity in copyright law, they leave open the question of whether this approach could function as a valid defense in court
(
Vyas et al. 2023
)
. Although the NAF condition has been acknowledged as at least partially effective in reducing the risks of infringement
(
Elkin-Koren et al. 2024
)
, it has also faced skepticism for not fully aligning with the principles of copyright law
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
. Here, probabilistic formalism is employed as an aid for precise analysis to reconcile diverging views.

One straightforward way the NAF condition contributes to copyright safety is by addressing the
prospective
problem of how likely the model is to generate a copyright-infringing output. It can be concluded that this probability is low, given that (i) the model satisfies
\epsilon
-NAF with a small
\epsilon
and (ii) the model is unlikely to produce outputs highly similar to copyrighted works if it had been trained without access. This trivially follows from standard probabilistic reasoning by viewing the substantial similarity of a generated work to a fixed copyrighted work as a function of the generated work. Specifically, letting
Substantial(Z)
be a binary function of random variable
Z
that takes value 1 if the similarity of an output
z
to the copyrighted work reaches the threshold corresponding to substantial similarity,
\epsilon
-NAF ensures that:

\begin{split}&P(Substantial(Z)=1|Access=1)\\
&\leq e^{\epsilon}P(Substantial(Z)=1|Access=0).\end{split}

Due to this inequality, given that
P(Substantial(Z)=1|Access=0)
is small and
e^{\epsilon}
is close to 1, corresponding to a small value of
\epsilon
,
P(Substantial(Z)=1|Access=1)
must also be small. Consequently, the model is unlikely to generate content that is substantially similar to the copyrighted work, and thus unlikely to infringe on its copyright, as its generation would not satisfy the improper appropriation prong.

However, the
retrospective
problem of determining whether a specific generated output infringes copyright—the issue adjudicators face in court—is more nuanced. Drawing a conclusion based on broad statistical data that generative models satisfying the NAF condition produce substantially similar outputs with low probability, without assessing case-specific details pertaining to the substantial similarity between the specific output and the original work at hand, constitutes a fallacy that must be avoided
(
Tribe 1971
;
Allen and Stein 2013
)
. The mere rarity of substantially similar outputs generated by such models does not influence the evaluation of substantial similarity between the two specific works, which adjudicators can typically ascertain with certainty by directly examining both works presented in court. Therefore, linking the NAF condition to the improper appropriation prong in this context appears unfounded.

The focus now shifts to examining whether and how the NAF condition affects the determination of the other prong, factual copying, within the context of the retrospective problem. On one hand, there seems to be little justification for expecting the NAF condition to meaningfully influence the finding of copying,
given
both the presence of access and a fixed level of similarity in the probative sense. Specifically, the theoretical guarantee of the NAF condition do not directly impose constraints on factual copying; instead, it merely dictates a probabilistic relationship between access and generated outputs. Furthermore, as observed in
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
, the assertion that a similar output could have been produced by copying from another source does not constitute a valid defense under copyright law.

On the other hand, the NAF condition can impact the inference of access based on similarity, as it influences the observable distribution of similarity between generated content and copyrighted works. Random variables as defined in Definition
4.1
is considered to analyze the implications.

Definition 4.1.

A_{M}\in\{0,1\}
,
S\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, and
EA_{M}\in\{1,2,\cdots,m\}
are random variables that satisfy:

(1)

\displaystyle P(S=i|A_{M}=1)\leq e^{\epsilon}P(S=i|A_{M}=0),\,\forall i

(2)

\displaystyle P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)=\delta_{k},\forall k

(3)

\displaystyle EA_{M}\perp\!\!\!\perp S|A_{M}

where
\delta_{k+1}\geq\delta_{k}
.

In the context of AI generation, access can refer to two distinct concepts: access by the defendant (as in traditional cases) and access by the AI model. To clarify that access by the model is at issue, the variable for access,
A_{M}
, and the corresponding evidence,
EA_{M}
, are denoted with the subscript
M
. It is of interest to see how the NAF condition (Definition
4.1
(
1
)) affects the inference of access given similarity and other relevant evidence, or in other words, how it affects the value of
P(A_{M}=1|S=i,\,EA_{M}=k)
. To study this, some degree of belief,
\delta_{k}
, about the likelihood of access by the AI model given other evidence without the knowledge of the level of similarity is assumed (Definition
4.1
(
2
)). Analogous to
EA
in Definition
3.1
,
EA_{M}
directly impacts only the inference of access (Definition
4.1
(
3
)).

Given this setting, Proposition
4.2
on the effectiveness of the NAF condition can be obtained.

Proposition 4.2.

For any
\epsilon>0
and
(i,k)
,
P(A_{M}=1|S=i,\,EA_{M}=k)\leq\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta_{k})

where the random variables are defined as in Definition
4.1
and
\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta):=e^{\epsilon}\delta/(1+\delta(e^{\epsilon}-1))
.
\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta)
is increasing in
\epsilon
and
\delta
, and satisfies
\delta\leq\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta)
and
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta)=\delta
.

The proposition shows that the NAF condition indeed imposes an upper bound on the probability of finding the presence of access to the original work by the model. A lower value of
\epsilon
enhances the effectiveness of this upper bound, and, in the limit of
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, the evidentiary value of similarity in inferring access vanishes. In other words, similarity fails to serve as meaningful additional evidence of access, with the value of
P(A_{M}=1|S=i,\,EA_{M}=k)
converging to
\delta_{k}=P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)
for all
i
.

However, the way in which the NAF condition operates in this context is ethically debatable. The capacity to generate content with comparable likelihood—regardless of whether access occurred—impedes the determination of whether a work was produced with access based on the generation outcome. Intuitively, since a generative model satisfying the NAF condition is likely to produce a work with the same level of similarity even without access to the copyrighted work, the inferential value of similarity in establishing access by the AI model is reduced. It is questionable whether such an approach is justified, especially considering the demand for transparency in training data as advocated by researchers
(
Sag 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
and reflected in the EU AI Act,
9
9

9

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 June 2024 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence and amending Regulations (EC) No 300/2008, (EU) No 167/2013, (EU) No 168/2013, (EU) 2018/858, (EU) 2018/1139 and (EU) 2019/2144 and Directives 2014/90/EU, (EU) 2016/797 and (EU) 2020/1828 (Artificial Intelligence Act),
Official Journal of the European Union
, L 1689, 12 July 2024, pp. 1–144.
which imposes transparency requirements on developers of certain AI systems, mandating that they make available summaries of the training data used. Furthermore, since
\Gamma
is bounded below by the degree of belief
\delta_{k}
that the model had access to the specific original work in question, given available evidence of access other than similarity, the upper bound on the degree of belief about access becomes increasingly insignificant as stronger evidence of access to the specific original work by the model is presented (e.g., unequivocal evidence of the work’s inclusion in its training data) or as it becomes common practice to include broader sets of copyrighted materials in the training of AI models in general, both of which can lead to higher values of
\delta_{k}
.

5.
Discussion

Probabilistic analysis has been shown to enhance the understanding of copyright jurisprudence and its interaction with emerging technologies. In particular, a mathematical proof demonstrated that the controversial inverse ratio rule holds, provided it is appropriately defined. Furthermore, the NAF condition was examined as a mitigation strategy for the copyright infringement risks posed by generative AI, revealing ethical concerns and limitations in its efficacy when addressing the retrospective problem adjudicators face in court, where they must determine whether infringement has occurred based on available evidence.

There are certain limitations to the analysis presented in this paper. While the analysis is based on a probabilistic view of legal fact-finding, this perspective has faced criticisms
(
Tribe 1971
;
Allen and Stein 2013
)
, although it continues to receive support
(
Cheng 2013
;
Burtis et al. 2017
)
. The validity of probabilistic approaches in legal fact-finding in general is widely debated in legal literature and falls beyond the scope of this paper, except for the relation between the results concerning the inverse ratio rule and the conjunction problem. Furthermore, the analysis abstracts from certain details involved in AI generation, such as the content of user-provided prompts, which may influence the identification of direct or indirect infringers
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
and be important in actual cases.

Several related directions for future work remain to be explored. Although this paper has paid particular attention to the inverse ratio rule—especially compatible with probabilistic reasoning due to its direct connection to the assessment of evidence—the presented approach has broader potential applications within copyright jurisprudence, as many copyright principles can be understood through the lens of evidentiary rules
(
Lichtman 2003
)
. Furthermore, while the analysis has addressed the NAF condition, future research should focus on developing alternative copyright risk mitigation strategies beyond the NAF condition and conducting a more comprehensive analysis of how generative AI technology interacts with copyright law.

Appendix A
The Inverse Ratio Rule with Strict Inequality

Proposition
3.2
was proved by assuming only very general relationships among the
\alpha_{i}
’s as well as among the
\beta_{i,j}
’s. It does not specify, for example, how the value of
\beta_{i,j}
changes when one index is increased while the other is decreased simultaneously, nor does it address the relative magnitude of the changes in the values of
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i,j}
. Proposition
A.1
demonstrates that, under certain assumptions regarding these details, the inverse ratio rule holds with strict inequality.

The inequality
\alpha_{\mu^{\prime}}/\alpha_{\mu^{\prime}-1}\leq\beta_{\mu^{\prime}-1,j}/\beta_{\mu^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
assumed in the first part of Proposition
A.1
roughly states that the increase in the degree of belief in access resulting from stronger evidence of access (excluding similarity) is large relative to the changes in the degrees of belief in both access and copying that arise from different levels of similarity. On the other hand, the inequality
\beta_{i^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}}/\beta_{i,\kappa^{\prime}-1}\leq\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{i^{\prime}}
in the second part of the proposition roughly means that the increase in the degree of belief in copying due to a higher level of similarity is substantial relative to the possible increase (or decrease, in which case the inequality is trivially satisfied) in the degree of belief in access with stronger evidence of access paired with a showing of a lower level of similarity.

Importantly, these relations are local in the sense that they only have to hold for some specific indices for the proposition to apply, and it is not necessary for them to be universally true across all indices
i
and
j
. It is quite plausible, therefore, that the proposition apply in some actual instances.

Proposition A.1.

Assuming that the random variables are defined as in Definition
3.1
, the following strong inverse ratio rule holds for any
\lambda\in(0,1)
:

•

Suppose
j>j^{\prime}
and
\mu^{\prime}>1
where
\mu^{\prime}:=\min\{i:P(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j^{\prime})>\lambda\}
. Then,
\min\{i:P(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j)>\lambda\}<\mu^{\prime}
if
\alpha_{\mu^{\prime}}/\alpha_{\mu^{\prime}-1}\leq\beta_{\mu^{\prime}-1,j}/\beta_{\mu^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
.

•

Suppose
i>i^{\prime}
and
\kappa^{\prime}>1
where
\kappa^{\prime}:=\min\{j:P(C=1|S=i^{\prime},\,EA=j)>\lambda\}
. Then,
\min\{j:P(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j)>\lambda\}<\kappa^{\prime}
if
\beta_{i^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}}/\beta_{i,\kappa^{\prime}-1}\leq\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{i^{\prime}}
.

Appendix B
Relation to the Conjunction Problem

The conjunction problem is frequently cited to criticize probabilistic approaches to legal fact-finding
(
Pardo and Allen 2008
)
. It refers to the issue that when a claim consists of multiple elements that must each be established by evidence, the probability of the conjunction of these elements may not reach a required threshold
\lambda
, even if the probability of each individual element exceeds
\lambda
. More concretely, suppose there are two events,
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, that need to be established to make a certain claim. The probability that both occurs is the product of the probabilities of the two elements,
P(E_{1}\cap E_{2})=P(E_{1}|E_{2})P(E_{2})
, which is smaller than the probability of either individual element. Thus, even if both elements have been shown to have probabilities higher than
\lambda
individually,
P(E_{1}|E_{2}),P(E_{2})>\lambda
, it can be the case that the probability of both requirements being satisfied simultaneously is below the threshold,
P(E_{1}\cap E_{2})<\lambda
. A solution offered by the probabilistic camp is to require that the probability of the conjunction, in addition to the individual probability of each element, exceeds the threshold in order to make an affirmative finding
(
Nance 1986
;
Friedman 1992
)
. However, critics object to this solution for its counterintuitiveness
(
Pardo and Allen 2008
)
.

Such objection may appear to affect the results in this paper regarding the inverse ratio rule, which relies on how the probability of copying being a product of two terms: the probability of copying given the presence of access and the probability of access. However, this is not the case because the situation here differs from those considered in the context of the conjunction problem. Under the
Arnstein
test, it is not required to prove access to the original work as a separate element from copying. Instead, the plaintiff merely needs to show that the probability of copying reaches the threshold,
P(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j)>\lambda
. As shown in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, due to the logical relationship between copying and access (Definition
3.1
(
1
)), this probability can be expressed as the product of two terms, the probability of copying and that of access, leading to:

\begin{split}&P(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j)>\lambda\\
&\Leftrightarrow P\left(C=1|A=1,\,S=i\right)P\left(A=1|S=i,\,EA=j\right)>\lambda.\end{split}

This product arises merely as a consequence of the logical relationship between copying and access, rather than from adopting the controversial solution that necessitates the probability of the conjunction reaching the threshold, which can be formally described as:

P(C=1,\,A=1|S=i,\,EA=j)>\lambda.

Therefore, the argument presented here is not directly influenced by discussions surrounding the conjunction problem.

C.1.
Proof of Proposition

Using the logical relationship between copying and access (Definition

(

\begin{split}&P\left(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j\right)\\
&=P\left(C=1|A=1,\,S=i\right)P\left(A=1|S=i,\,EA=j\right)\\
&\quad+P\left(C=1|A=0,\,S=i\right)P\left(A=0|S=i,\,EA=j\right)\\
&=P\left(C=1|A=1,\,S=i\right)P\left(A=1|S=i,\,EA=j\right)\\
&\quad+0\cdot P\left(A=0|S=i,\,EA=j\right)\\

The first element of the inverse ratio rule is proved by showing contradiction. Assume
\mu>\mu^{\prime}
where:

\begin{gathered}\mu:=\min\{i:P(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j)>\lambda\}\\
\mu^{\prime}:=\min\{i:P(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j^{\prime})>\lambda\}.\end{gathered}

Then, by assumptions about the ordering of the terms of the form
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i,j}
,

\begin{split}\lambda<\alpha_{\mu^{\prime}}\beta_{\mu^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\leq\alpha_{\mu^{\prime}}\beta_{\mu^{\prime},j}\leq\alpha_{\mu}\beta_{\mu,j}\end{split}.

However, this implies that
\mu^{\prime}
, which is strictly smaller than
\mu
, would have achieved
P(C=1|S=\mu^{\prime},\,EA=j)>\lambda
, contradicting the assumption that
\mu
was the minimum that achieves this.

The proof for the second element of the inverse ratio rule is similar. Assume
\kappa>\kappa^{\prime}
where:

\begin{gathered}\kappa:=\min\{j:P(C=1|S=i,\,EA=j)>\lambda\}\\
\kappa^{\prime}:=\min\{j:P(C=1|S=i^{\prime},\,EA=j)>\lambda\}.\end{gathered}

Same as above, the following inequalities hold:

\lambda<\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\beta_{i^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}}\leq\alpha_{i}\beta_{i,\kappa^{\prime}}\leq\alpha_{i}\beta_{i,\kappa}

which results in contradiction with the assumption of the minimality of
\kappa
.

C.2.
Proof of Proposition
4.2

By the NAF condition (Definition
4.1
(
1
)) and conditional independence (Definition
4.1
(
3
)):

\begin{split}&P(A_{M}=1|S=i,\,EA_{M}=k)\\
&=\frac{P(S=i|A_{M}=1,\,EA_{M}=k)P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)}{P(S=i|EA_{M}=k)}\\
&\leq\frac{e^{\epsilon}P(S=i|A_{M}=0,\,EA_{M}=k)P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)}{P(S=i|EA_{M}=k)}\\
&=\frac{e^{\epsilon}\frac{P(A_{M}=0|S=i,\,EA_{M}=k)P(S=i|EA_{M}=k)}{P(A_{M}=0|EA_{M}=k)}P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)}{P(S=i|EA_{M}=k)}\\
&=\frac{e^{\epsilon}(1-P(A_{M}=1|S=i,\,EA_{M}=k))P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)}{1-P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)}.\end{split}

By rearranging terms,

\begin{split}P(A_{M}=1|S=i,\,EA_{M}=k)&\leq\frac{e^{\epsilon}P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)}{1+P(A_{M}=1|EA_{M}=k)(e^{\epsilon}-1)}\\
&=\frac{e^{\epsilon}\delta_{k}}{1+\delta_{k}(e^{\epsilon}-1)}\\
&=\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta_{k}).\end{split}

Taking the derivative of
\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta)
with respect to
\epsilon
and
\delta
, respectively,

\begin{split}&\frac{d\Gamma}{d\mathcal{\epsilon}}=\frac{e^{\epsilon}\delta(1-\delta)}{(1+\delta(e^{\epsilon}-1))^{2}}\geq 0\\
&\frac{d\Gamma}{d\delta}=\frac{e^{\epsilon}}{(1+\delta(e^{\epsilon}-1))^{2}}\geq 0.\end{split}

Therefore,
\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta)
is increasing in
\epsilon
and
\delta
.

The inequality
\delta\leq\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta)
follows from the observation that:

\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta)-\delta=\frac{\delta(e^{\epsilon}-1)(1-\delta)}{1+\delta(e^{\epsilon}-1)}\geq 0

where the inequality follows from the fact that each term in the numerator and denominator is non-negative because
1\geq\delta\geq 0
and
e^{\epsilon}>1
.

Finally, the limit as
\epsilon
approaches zero can be computed straightforwardly as:

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\Gamma(\epsilon,\delta)=\frac{1\cdot\delta}{1+\delta\cdot 0}=\delta.
