Study on Preparation and Thermal Insulation Properties of Silica Aerogel and Fiber-Based Thermal Insulation Materials
Authors: Chuan-Jun Jin, Chao Gao, Hong-Wei Mu, Xin-Long Liu, Su Liu, Ming-Ri Gan, Jian-Fei Sun, Yong-Liang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Deep underground mines commonly face the problem of high-temperature thermal damage, and using insulation materials on tunnel walls is an effective method to isolate heat sources from the source. In order to improve the comfort of underground operation and reduce energy consumption, this research aims to develop a pot process to prepare a green and efficient thermal insulation material. Porous silica aerogel and corn straw cellulose are used as raw materials. First, the influence of each component on the material performance is explored through a single-factor experiment. Then, the Box–Behnken experiment is designed using the response surface method. With dry density, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity as indicators, the interactions of three factors, fiber content, fiber length, and aerogel content, are systematically analyzed and optimized. The final thermal insulation test shows that the thermal insulation temperature difference of the optimal formula group is as high as 8.7 °C under heating conditions of 75 °C. With the synergistic porous structure constructed by cellulose and aerogel, the thermal conductivity of this material is significantly lower than those of similar materials, showing better thermal insulation performance and environmental adaptability.

Introduction

With the rapid depletion of mineral resources in China, coal mining has expanded at an accelerated pace toward deeper underground areas. However, this shift from shallow to deep mining has given rise to increasingly severe high-temperature environmental challenges in mines. , Currently, the mitigation of high-temperature thermal damage in deep mines primarily relies on mechanical cooling systems. In practical applications, these systems often suffer from high energy consumption, limited efficiency, structural complexity, and substantial maintenance costs.

Notably, the surrounding rock of tunnels itself acts as a massive heat source with significant heat release potential, making it one of the primary sources of thermal load in mines. Therefore, effectively controlling the rate of heat release from the surrounding rock to the tunnel space is a critical strategy for preventing and alleviating thermal damage. Installing a high-performance thermal insulation layer between the surface of the tunnel's surrounding rock and the airflow environment to form an “active thermal barrier” can significantly reduce the direct heat transfer rate from the surrounding rock to the tunnel. This, in turn, lowers the initial temperature of tunnel airflow and improves the underground working environment. This active thermal insulation technology offers an energy-efficient and cost-effective approach to addressing thermal hazards in deep mines while also aligning with the principles of sustainable mine environmental and resource management.

The cement industry is a major source of carbon emissions: the production of 1 ton of cement clinker generates approximately 0.98 tons of carbon dioxide, accounting for 7% of global anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions. To minimize environmental impact, researchers have conducted extensive studies and developed various measures, such as utilizing alternative raw materials and incorporating natural fibers. , Vitrified beads are inorganic glassy mineral materials produced through the specialized processing of glassy volcanic rocks (primarily pitchstone). As a new type of inorganic thermal insulation material, it features a porous interior, a closed surface, and a spherical fine-grained structure, as well as ideal properties such as low density and high strength, and it is widely used in thermal insulation mortars. Wu et al. successfully developed an ultralightweight cement composite (density: 1154–1471 kg/m3; 28 day compressive strength: 33.0–69.4 MPa) with low thermal conductivity (54 and 80% lower than those of traditional cement slurry and concrete, respectively) and high specific strength by using vitrified beads as lightweight aggregates. Ni et al. found through experimental research that lightweight cementitious composite sealing materials prepared with modified hollow glass microspheres exhibited an 8% increase in fluidity and a compressive strength of 60.191 MPa (compared to pure cement and solid microsphere systems) while also optimizing the pore structure. This provides a new direction for the development of high-performance, environmentally friendly cementitious materials. Wang et al. developed lightweight concrete by replacing sand in conventional concrete with glass microspheres, which significantly improved fluidity but slightly reduced mechanical properties.

In recent years, silica aerogels have garnered increasing attention due to their ultralow thermal conductivity and density, earning them the title of “the lightest solid in the world”. Owing to their ultralow thermal conductivity and highly porous structure, silica aerogels are frequently used as thermal insulation fillers. Wang et al. observed that the thermal conductivity of aerogel concrete decreases quadratically with increasing aerogel content (with a maximum reduction of 79.3%) and increases significantly with increasing temperature (a 15.5% increase between 20 and 90 °C) and humidity (19.51%/76.33% changes in AIC0/AIC60 at 0–100% relative humidity (RH)). Among these factors, humidity has a particularly pronounced impact on materials with a high aerogel content. Gao et al. successfully prepared adjustable lightweight thermal insulation concrete (AIC) with a density of 1.0 g/cm3, thermal conductivity of 0.26 W·m–1·K–1, and compressive strength of 8.3 MPa at 60% aerogel content. The stable combination of aerogel with the concrete matrix demonstrates promising application prospects in the construction industry. Strzalkowski and Garbalinsk fixed the aerogel content at 20% by volume and investigated its effects on the fly ash-cement matrix. Ng et al. incorporated aerogel into ultrahigh-performance concrete; however, no significant performance improvement was observed compared to ordinary mortar. When a specimen strength of over 20 MPa was ensured, the maximum aerogel content was 50%, resulting in a thermal conductivity of 0.55 W·m–1·K–1 for the specimens. Zeng et al. combined vitrified beads with silica aerogel to prepare a lightweight cement-based composite with a minimum thermal conductivity of 0.08 W·m–1·K–1.

Incorporating plant fibers into thermal insulation materials not only enhances performance and reduces costs but also contributes to environmental protection and sustainable development. Natural plant fibers have a long history of use as reinforcing substrates in China. Corn stalks, wheat straw, and rice husks are common agricultural wastes, and their conventional disposal method (incineration) causes environmental pollution and resource waste. Adding these fibers to thermal insulation materials achieves dual environmental benefits. Zhang et al. studied the development of tunnel thermal insulation materials based on crop straw fibers and found that concrete mixed with 7.5% straw fibers achieved the lowest thermal conductivity (0.158 W·m–1·K–1) while maintaining acceptable performance. COMSOL simulations confirmed that this material effectively reduces the tunnel airflow temperature, making it an excellent thermal insulation material for high-temperature tunnels. Korjenic et al. developed a new type of natural thermal insulation material using renewable resources, such as jute, flax, and hemp. By systematically adjusting raw material ratios and process parameters, the material’s building physical properties (thermal conductivity and moisture absorption) and mechanical properties (strength and elasticity) met the standards of commonly used petroleum-based/mineral-based thermal insulation materials. Experimental results showed that the optimized natural composite materials exhibited comprehensive performance compared to traditional materials. Ali et al. prepared a dry eucalyptus leaf-based thermal insulation material (thermal conductivity: 0.045–0.065 W·m–1·K–1) using corn starch as a binder. This material possesses excellent midfrequency sound absorption performance (500–1600 Hz) and thermal stability up to 210 °C (with a moisture content of 16%), providing a renewable and low-environmental-impact biobased solution for thermal insulation and sound absorption in building walls, and these studies indicate that corn stover fibers have low thermal conductivity, high specific heat capacity, and higher humidity sensitivity. At the same time, organic materials usually allow water vapor to permeate, and as the air humidity increases, moisture gradually releases into the surrounding environment to lower the ambient temperature. −

Importantly, porous silica gas gel and corn straw fiber were selected as raw materials for the preparation of thermal insulation materials. Silica gas gel has a nanoporous network structure, with a porosity of up to 80–99.8%, and the pore diameter is mostly 2–50 nm, which can greatly reduce the heat conduction of gas molecules and solid lattice. The surface of corn straw fiber is rich in hydroxyl, which easily forms a hydrogen bond with aerogel, which can effectively inhibit the agglomeration of aerogel. At the same time, the hollow tubular structure of the fiber itself can build a secondary heat insulation channel. After the combination of the two, a synergistic microstructure of the ″fiber support aerogel keyhole” is formed, giving consideration to structural stability and heat insulation efficiency. The thermal insulation performance of aerogel is outstanding, which is superior to traditional rock wool, polyurethane, and other thermal insulation materials. The resource utilization of straw fiber reduces the cost of raw materials, and both of them are free of harmful volatiles, especially to meet the safety requirements of low smoke and environmental protection, in line with the development trend of green materials.

Based on response surface analysis, this study aimed to reduce the air temperature in high-temperature tunnels by developing a lightweight thermal insulation material with low thermal conductivity, leveraging the low thermal conductivity of silica aerogel and vitrified beads. The research focused on incorporating straw fibers into the thermal insulation material and analyzing the dry density, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity of the material to determine the optimal formulation.

Experimental Materials and Methods

Experimental Materials

The straw fiber-based thermal insulation material comprises cement, sand (particle size <1 mm), gravel (particle size: 4–6 mm), closed-cell glass beads (particle size: 0.5–1.5 mm), silica aerogel (particle size: 0.5–3 mm), and a polycarboxylate superplasticizer. Corn straw fibers were sourced from farmers in Linyi City, Shandong Province. The cement used was P·O42.5 ordinary Portland cement (supplied by Tiger Eagle Cement Co., Ltd.). River sand was purchased from Shandong Linyi Quartz Sand Factory, and gravel was obtained from Nanjing Xinchi Yuhua Stone Pebble Factory. Closed-cell glass microspheres were provided by Henan Jieyang New Materials Co., Ltd., silica aerogel by Shenzhen Zhongning Technology Co., Ltd., and the polycarboxylate superplasticizer by Shandong Wanshan Chemical Co., Ltd.

Pretreatment of Straw Fiber

Corn straw was naturally dried for 2 months, and an intact, high-quality straw was selected as the experimental sample. The straw was soaked in a 4% sodium hydroxide solution for 48 h and then rinsed with deionized water until the effluent was colorless. It was subsequently dried in a 60 °C oven for 24 h to obtain dry corn straw. The long straw was manually shredded into fibers and kept at a length of about 3–4 cm (as shown in Figure ).

Sample Production

Prior to the experiment, molds for the specimens were prepared according to the experimental design. Cubic molds (100 × 100 × 100 mm) were used for compressive strength and tensile strength tests, while rectangular prism molds (40 × 40 × 160 mm) were used for thermal conductivity tests. The inner walls and bottoms of the molds were coated with a release agent to prevent direct adhesion of the thermal insulation material to the mold walls, facilitating demolding. Based on the relevant requirements of the “Code for Design of Mix Proportion of Ordinary Concrete (JGJ55-2011)” and the “Technical Specification for Application of Shocked Concrete (JGJT372-2016)”, and with reference to the design experience from relevant literature, the mix proportion of the thermal insulation material was determined as cement:sand:gravel:water = 1:1.15:2.5:0.5, with vitrified beads accounting for 40% of the total concrete volume.

Experimental Methods and Testing

The effects of the corn straw fiber content, fiber length, and silica aerogel content on the dry density, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity of the material were investigated. The corn straw content was controlled within the range of 0–2.5%, straw fiber length within 0–25 mm, and silica aerogel content within 0–25%. The dry density, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity of the specimens were tested to establish the relationship curves between these material properties and the three factors. To determine the optimal mix proportion, appropriate ranges were selected for each of the three factors.

However, single-factor experiments only preliminarily optimized parameters such as silica aerogel, vitrified beads, acrylamide, and superplasticizer content without identifying the optimal formulation. Therefore, a three-factor, three-level Box–Behnken response surface experiment was designed using Design Expert 13.0 software to optimize the parameter ranges obtained from single-factor optimization. The dry density test was conducted in accordance with the “Technical Standard for Application of Lightweight Aggregate Concrete (JGJ/T12-2019)”. Specimens cured for 28 days were placed in an oven until a constant weight was achieved. The mass (m) of the dried specimens was measured using an electronic balance, and the geometric dimensions of the specimens were measured with a ruler to calculate their volume (v). The dry density (ρ) of the specimens was calculated using eq : ρ=m/v 1

Among them, ρ is the dry density of the specimen, measured in kg/m3; m is the mass of the specimen after drying, in kg; and v is the volume of the specimen, in m3.

An electrohydraulic servo universal testing machine (manufactured by the Changchun Testing Machine Research Institute) with a maximum load of 2000 kN was used for compressive strength testing. When placing the specimen, the side with good flatness was selected as the pressure-bearing surface, and the axis of the specimen was aligned with the center of the upper and lower pressure plates of the testing machine. The loading rate was set at 0.5 MPa/s. During the loading process, close attention was paid to the specimen’s condition: loading was stopped immediately when the specimen failed, and the test results were recorded promptly. The appearance of the specimen before and after failure was photographed (as shown in Figure ) for visual comparison.

Appearance state diagram of the specimen before and after damage.

A plate thermal conductivity tester was used to measure the thermal conductivity of the concrete, based on the principle of one-dimensional steady-state heat transfer using Fourier’s law. In the steady state, the heat flow is defined as the amount of heat absorbed by the water flow passing through the central calorimeter per unit time after the heat has passed longitudinally through the cross-section of the specimen in a one-dimensional temperature field. The thermal conductivity (λ) was calculated using eq : λ=(C×w×Δt×δ)/(A×ΔT) 2

Among them, C is the specific heat capacity of water, measured in J/(g · K); w is the water flow rate, measured in g/s; Δt is the temperature at which water rises, measured in °C; δ is the thickness of the sample, measured in m; A is the area of the sample, measured in m2; and ΔT is the temperature difference between the hot and cold surfaces, measured in °C.

Results and Discussion

Effect of Straw Fiber Content on the Performance of Thermal Insulation Materials

As illustrated in Figure a, within the straw fiber dosage range of 0–2.5%, the dry density of the material decreases with the increase in fiber content. Compared to the control group (without corn stover fiber), the dry density of the material with 2.5% straw fiber dosage decreased by 4.9%. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that corn stover fiber is a lightweight porous material with a density much lower than that of traditional building material matrices. As the fiber content increases, fibers form voids within the matrix, which reduces the overall density of the material and increases its porosity. Additionally, fibers partially replace high-density matrix materials, directly decreasing the average density of the composite.

(a) Effect of straw fiber content on dry density; (b) effect of straw fiber content on compressive strength; and (c) effect of straw fiber content on thermal conductivity.

As shown in Figure b, the compressive strength of the material is 9.6 MPa when the straw fiber dosage is 0%. A peak compressive strength of 10.6 MPa is observed when the dosage reaches 1.5%, which is 10.4% higher than that of the control group. The improvement in compressive strength is due to the sodium hydroxide (NaOH) treatment, which enhances the interfacial bonding between fibers and the matrix: this treatment removes noncellulosic impurities, exposes the active hydroxyl groups (−OH) of cellulose, and strengthens the chemical bonding between fibers and the matrix. Furthermore, NaOH treatment increases the surface roughness of fibers, enhancing the mechanical interlocking effect between fibers and the matrix. However, excessive fiber dosage leads to difficulties in uniform dispersion, resulting in the formation of stress concentration points within the material. Meanwhile, excessive fibers damage the continuity of the matrix, increase the porosity, and ultimately weaken the overall strength of the material.

From Figure c, it can be seen that the straw fiber dosage has a significant impact on the thermal conductivity of the material. As the fiber dosage increases, the thermal conductivity decreases, reaching a minimum of 0.354 W·m–1·K–1 (a decrease of 7.4% compared to the control group). This is because straw fibers form closed micropores within the material, which block the heat conduction path. Moreover, the thermal conductivity of corn stover fiber (0.05–0.1 W·m–1·K–1) is much lower than that of cement, which directly reduces the overall thermal conductivity of the thermal insulation material.

Influence of Straw Fiber Length on the Performance of Thermal Insulation Materials

As presented in Figure a, when the straw fiber length is 10 mm, the thermal insulation material achieves the lowest dry density of 1433 kg/m3, which is 1.9% lower than that of the control group. The short fibers (after NaOH treatment) are uniformly dispersed and form micropores within the thermal insulation material, leading to a slight decrease in density. However, when the straw fiber length is excessively long, fibers tend to entangle and agglomerate, resulting in local accumulation (which reduces the number of pores) and a subsequent increase in density.

(a) Effect of straw fiber length on dry density; (b) influence of straw fiber length on compressive strength; and (c) influence of straw fiber length on thermal conductivity.

Regarding compressive strength (Figure b), a peak value of 10.2 MPa is observed when the straw fiber length is 10 mm, which is 6.3% higher than that of the control group. Nevertheless, when the fiber length exceeds 10 mm, the compressive strength begins to decrease. Moderate fiber length, combined with NaOH treatment (which enhances interfacial bonding), enables an effective stress transfer between fibers and the matrix. In contrast, excessively long fibers cause slight agglomeration, leading to an uneven stress distribution and the formation of weak areas within the material.

As shown in Figure c, the thermal conductivity of the thermal insulation material decreases with the increase in straw fiber length, although the impact of fiber length on thermal conductivity is less significant than that of fiber dosage. When the fiber length is 25 mm, the thermal conductivity reaches its minimum, which is 10.3% lower than that of the control group. Longer fibers create a more tortuous heat conduction path. Furthermore, long fibers form a continuous thermal insulation network within the matrix and generate larger closed pores, both of which effectively block heat transfer.

Influence of Silica Aerogel Content on the Performance of Thermal Insulation Materials

To investigate the effect of silica aerogel content, other material components were kept unchanged, and silica aerogel was used to replace 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25% of sand (by volume), respectively.

The dry density test results of the prepared thermal insulation materials are listed in Figure a. Within the silica aerogel content range of 0–10%, the dry density of the material decreases continuously. This is because silica aerogel itself has high porosity and low density; when incorporated into the thermal insulation material, it increases the material’s porosity and reduces its dry density. However, when the silica aerogel content reaches 15%, silica aerogel powder cannot be fully integrated into the material, and some silica aerogel remains at the bottom of the slurry, failing to form a uniform material system. This leads to an upward trend in the material’s dry density.

(a) Influence of silica aerogel content on dry density; (b) influence of silica aerogel content on compressive strength; and (c) influence of straw fiber length on thermal conductivity.

The compressive strength test results of the thermal insulation material samples are presented in Figure b. It can be observed that the compressive strength of the material decreases after the addition of the silica aerogel. Without silica aerogel, the compressive strength of the material reaches 9.6 MPa; when the silica aerogel content is 25%, the compressive strength drops to 5.3 MPa. This phenomenon is attributed to the porous structure of the silica aerogel, which is not conducive to the formation of a dense structure in the thermal insulation material. Thus, as the silica aerogel content increases, the mechanical properties of the material decrease sequentially, and the overall compressive strength gradually declines.

Figure c shows the thermal conductivity of thermal insulation materials with different silica aerogel contents. Within the silica aerogel content range of 0–10%, the thermal conductivity decreases gradually with the increase in silica aerogel content, reaching a minimum of 0.309 W·m–1·K–1. This is due to the low thermal conductivity of the silica aerogel: uniformly dispersed silica aerogel in the material contains a large number of tiny pores and trapped air. Since the pore diameter of silica aerogel is less than 50 nm, air within the pores cannot generate convective heat transfer, thereby blocking heat conduction. Heat can only be transferred through the complex skeleton of the aerogel, and this tortuous path effectively reduces the material’s thermal conductivity. When the silica aerogel content ranges from 10 to 25%, the material’s thermal conductivity begins to increase. This is because, with the increase in silica aerogel volume fraction, silica aerogel powder cannot be completely integrated into the material, and silica aerogels remain at the bottom of the slurry, unable to form a uniform material system, resulting in an upward trend in thermal conductivity.

Microscopic Morphology Analysis of Insulation Materials

Figure a shows the cement-based thermal insulation material without the addition of organic materials. It can be seen that the cement-based material is basically in granular shape, while Figure b shows the thermal insulation material of the silica aerogel. It is obvious to observe the structure of cellulose. The fibers arranged along the load direction can give full play to their high strength and high modulus characteristics, just like closely arranged “steel bars”, providing strong tensile support for the material. Figure c shows the porous microstructure of the local organic insulation material. The uniform distribution of pores usually affects the mechanical properties of the insulation material. The porous structure can bear the load uniformly under stress, avoiding excessive local stress and improving the strength and stability of the material. In terms of heat conduction, porosity is a key factor. When the porosity of the material increases, the thermal conductivity usually decreases. This is because the pores are filled with air or other low thermal conductivity media, which become obstacles to heat transfer. The microstructure of the organic insulation material after the compression test is shown in Figure d. It can be observed that after the fracturing test, the pore structure of the insulation material collapses, and cellulose becomes fine debris.

(a) Independent cement-based insulation material (without silica and cellulose); (b) thermal insulation material of silica aerogel; (c) porous microstructure of local organic insulation materials; and (d) microstructure of organic insulation materials after compression testing.

In general, the aerogel fiber interaction not only affects the mechanical properties of materials but also plays an important role in regulating the thermal properties of materials. The pore size in silica gas gel is far lower than the average free path of air molecules under normal pressure, which makes the air molecules in the pores nearly static, limiting the convection heat transfer of gas. At the same time, the bending path of the nano grid structure of the aerogel also effectively prevents the gaseous heat conduction. With the addition of cellulose fiber, a complex interface structure is formed between the fiber and the aerogel. These interfaces have become an important source of thermal resistance. In addition, cellulose fibers themselves have relatively low thermal conductivity, and their orientation also affects the direction of the heat transfer. When the fiber orientation is perpendicular to the direction of heat transfer, it can further enhance the thermal insulation performance of the material. The above dry density, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity demonstrate the advantages of organic thermal insulation materials.

Based on the significance analysis results, the primary term C (silica aerogel content) has a highly significant impact on dry density (P < 0.01), and the quadratic term A 2 (square of straw fiber content) also has a significant impact on dry density (P < 0.01). Additionally, the interaction terms AC (straw fiber content × silica aerogel content) and BC (straw fiber length × silica aerogel content) have significant effects on dry density (P < 0.05).

The relative importance of each factor can be determined by comparing the F-values of the factors in the model: a higher F-value indicates a greater impact of the factor on the test results. For the dry density of the thermal insulation material, the order of factor influence is C (silica aerogel content) > A (straw fiber content) > B (straw fiber length).

Three-dimensional coordinates can only represent the effects of two influencing factors at the same time; therefore, when analyzing the interaction between two factors, the third factor was set to the median value within its range and kept constant. The contour plots and response surface plots of dry density (showing the relationship between dry density and various influencing factors) are presented in Figure . The projection of the response surface plot onto the horizontal plane forms contour lines: elliptical contour lines indicate a significant interaction between the two factors, while circular contour lines indicate no significant interaction. The steepness of the response surface slope of each factor reflects the degree of its impact on dry density; usually, the steeper the slope, the greater the impact.

Based on the above analysis of contour lines and response surface plots, it can be concluded that the interaction terms AC and BC have significant impacts on dry density. Among the factors, silica aerogel content has the most significant impact on the dry density of the thermal insulation material, followed by straw fiber content, and straw fiber length has the smallest impact. The order of significance of the interaction effects is AC > BC > AB, which is consistent with the ANOVA results. Generally, the content of silicon aerogel has the most significant impact, and the core is derived from its unique microporous structure; low content aerogel particles are easy to disperse in the fiber matrix, forming a continuous closed pore structure, effectively blocking heat conduction. When the content reaches the critical value, the aerogel is easy to agglomerate and destroy the integrity of the porous system, leading to the decline of thermal insulation performance. The change in microstructure is directly related to the macroscopic insulation effect and is a key intrinsic mechanism that has a superior impact on performance compared with other factors.

Among the factors, silica aerogel content has the most significant impact on the compressive strength of the thermal insulation material, followed by straw fiber content, and straw fiber length has the smallest impact. The order of significance of the interaction effects is BC > AC > AB, which is consistent with the ANOVA results. As the reinforcing phase of the composite system, the spatial orientation of fibers has a significant anisotropic effect on the performance by changing the stress transfer path and heat conduction channel. As shown in Figure b, cellulose fibers are oriented along the load direction and can directly bear most of the stress. Through the “bridging effect”, crack propagation is suppressed, significantly improving the compressive strength and flexural toughness of the material. Meanwhile, fibers oriented along the direction of heat flow will form a continuous thermal conduction path, resulting in an increase in the material’s thermal conductivity.

Thermal Insulation Experiment

Based on the response surface test results, Design Expert 13 software was used to optimize the composition of the thermal insulation material. The optimal composition was determined as follows: straw fiber content = 1% (volume), straw fiber length = 11.3 mm, and silica aerogel content = 15% (volume). The theoretical dry density, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity of the thermal insulation material (calculated based on this optimal composition) are 1136 kg/m3, 4.77 MPa, and 0.214 W·m–1·K–1, respectively.

Three repeated experiments were conducted according to the above optimal ratio, and the average values of the tested properties are as follows: dry density = 1138 kg/m3, compressive strength = 4.32 MPa, and thermal conductivity = 0.205 W·m–1·K–1. The optimal conditions obtained from the comparative experiment RSM were compared with experimental verification under these conditions, and the results showed that the errors between the three were 0.18, 9.43, and 4.21%, respectively. These experimental values are close to the theoretical data calculated by Design Expert 13 software, with small errors, indicating a good fit between the predicted values and experimental values and confirming the important reference value of the model in guiding experiments.

To verify the thermal insulation performance of the optimally formulated material, we tested the temperature difference on both sides of the heated sample. A heating plate was used as the heat source, and a data acquisition device was used to record the temperatures. The specific operation steps were as follows: the heating plate was set to four temperature levels (45, 55, 65, and 75 °C); samples (from the response surface experiment) with the same bottom contact area and thickness were placed flat on the heating plate and heated for the same duration; temperature tests were conducted on both the control group and the optimal group; and differences in thermal insulation performance were reflected by comparing the temperature differences at different heating temperatures.

As the heating time increases, the temperature of the sample gradually increases, leading to an increasing temperature difference (a key indicator of thermal insulation performance). In the experiment, four temperature points (45, 55, 65, and 75 °C) were selected to evaluate the thermal insulation performance of the material. Conclusions

In this study, thermal insulation materials were prepared using organic components (cellulose, silica aerogel) and inorganic components (vitrified beads) as fine aggregates, with acrylamide and a poly­(carboxylate) superplasticizer added as modifiers. The effects of the straw fiber content, straw fiber length, and silica aerogel content on the apparent density, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity of the thermal insulation materials were systematically investigated.

The results showed that as the straw fiber content increased, the thermal conductivity of the material decreased by up to 7.4%. This was because fibers tended to form voids within the matrix, reducing the overall material density and increasing the porosity. As the length of straw fibers increases, the heat conduction path becomes more tortuous, and the long fibers form a continuous insulation network inside the material, thereby improving the insulation performance. When the silica aerogel content reached 15%, the dry density curve of the material showed an upward trend; within the silica aerogel content range of 0–10%, the thermal conductivity of the material gradually decreased, reaching a minimum of 0.309 W·m–1·K–1.

Design Expert 13 software was used to design response surface experiments, which were employed to study and analyze the interactions between various factors and determine the optimal composition of the thermal insulation material. Thermal insulation tests confirmed that the order of factor influence on the material’s performance (from largest to smallest) was silica aerogel content > straw fiber content > straw fiber length. For thermal conductivity, the silica aerogel content has the greatest impact, followed by the straw fiber content, and the straw fiber length has the smallest impact, which is consistent with the interaction effect analysis results.

Thermal insulation performance tests were conducted within the temperature range of 45–75 °C. The control group did not contain the three factors (straw fiber and silica aerogel) studied in this work; at a heating temperature of 75 °C, the thermal insulation temperature difference of the optimal group reached 8.7 °C (compared to the control group). Due to the synergistic porous structure of cellulose and silicon aerogel, the thermal insulation coefficient of the aerogel thermal insulation material is significantly lower than that of existing similar materials, and it is more suitable for complex service environments. These results indicate that the prepared insulation material can improve the comfort of deep underground mining workers while reducing cooling or heating loads, which is of great significance.
