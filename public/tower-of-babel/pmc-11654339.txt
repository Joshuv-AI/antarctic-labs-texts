The impact of using simulation-based learning to further develop communication skills of pharmacy students and pharmacists: a systematic review
Authors: Laura Foucault-Fruchard, Vanessa Michelet-Barbotin, Alison Leichnam, Martine Tching-Sin, Pierre Nizet, Sophie Tollec, Fabien Nativel, Elise Vene, Clémentine Fronteau, Jean-François Huon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective communication in healthcare, among professionals and between professionals and patients is crucial for delivering high-quality care. While simulation effectively translates technical skills to clinical practice, its impact on communication, particularly in pharmacies, is less well documented.AimThis systematic review examined the impact of simulation-based education on communication skills among pharmacy students and pharmacists. It aims to identify the types of simulations used, the outcomes obtained, and the effectiveness of these simulations in enhancing communication skills, perception and confidence.MethodWe conducted searches in MEDLINE, LISSA, EMBASE, and PsycINFO for articles on communication training through simulation for pharmacy students and pharmacists. The search covered the period from inception to August 31, 2022. To be included, studies had to meet the following criteria: (i) target pharmacy student or graduate pharmacists; (ii) use simulation in the field of clinical pharmacy; (iii) provide a complete description of the outcome measure. Studies for which it was impossible to extract data specific to pharmacy students or simulation were excluded. Three investigators independently evaluated each title and abstract. If each investigator considered the citation eligible, six authors progressed to full-text review. The outcomes related to communication were categorized into three groups: perception of the importance of communication in delivering high-quality patient care, confidence in communication skills, and assessed communication proficiency. The quality of studies included in this review was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).ResultsThe total number of participants was 3337. Among the 20 articles reviewed, which were primarily published in the United States over the last decade, only one focused on community pharmacists. Quasi experimental designs were predominant, and studies often relied on learners' self-assessments with non-validated questionnaires. Simulation-based learning aimed to enhance communication skills and attitudes across interprofessional contexts (involving nursing and medical students), patients, and families, frequently utilizing simulated patients. Studies evaluating outcomes have emphasized communication skills with patients and families, while those involving healthcare professionals often focused on perception of the importance of communication in delivering high-quality patient care.ConclusionDespite study heterogeneity, this systematic review concluded that simulation-based training significantly enhances perception, confidence, and communication skills. The development of clear guidelines and standardized assessment tools to evaluate communication would significantly improve the validity and reliability of future research efforts (PROSPERO registration: n°CRD42022371915).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06338-6.Keywords: Simulation, Communication, Clinical pharmacy, Students, Learning

Introduction

Communication skills are essential for healthcare professionals because they play a crucial role in patient care and safety [1]. Effective communication skills allow healthcare workers to understand their patients' needs, concerns, and expectations and to tailor care to each patient's unique circumstances [2, 3]. Among all professionals involved in patient care, pharmacists primarily serve as active communicators with patients, other health professionals, and the public [4]. Clinical pharmacy is a patient-centered healthcare discipline whose practice aims to optimize therapy at each stage of the care process [5]. It ensures the safe, appropriate, and efficient use of health products through collaboration with other healthcare professionals, patients, and caregivers [6]. Effective communication with healthcare teams is crucial for coordinating care, exchanging information, and making informed treatment decisions [4]. In contrast, poor communication can lead to medical errors, treatment delays, and reduced patient satisfaction [7]. Communication failures among healthcare professionals are reported to be the primary contributing factor to more than 50% of 'Never Events' involving patients [8, 9]. A 'Never Events' is defined by the health system as a serious, largely preventable, patient safety incident that should never be allowed to happen [10].

Simulation-based training, which has emerged as an effective method for improving healthcare professionals’ skills, and learning in clinical settings is crucial for developing and maintaining learners’ skills [11]. In healthcare education, simulation can be used to reproduce situations or environments of care, to teach diagnostic and therapeutic procedures and to allow repeat processes or decision-making by healthcare professionals [12]. Simulations facilitate healthcare workers’ learning to practice their skills in a safe and controlled environment where they can receive feedback and without patient consequences. Among all simulation activities, communication skills training provides healthcare workers the opportunity to practice communication techniques relevant to their specific field.

Different types of simulation exist, whether involving the use of simulated or standardized human patients, virtual patients, manikins, or serious (educational) games [13]. A simulated patient is a person specially trained to interpret the story of a real patient or to systematically reproduce the clinical signs, personality, body language and emotional reactions defined in a scenario. They can be amateur or professional actors or expert patients whose role is to exchange verbal or non-verbal information with learners (i.e., healthcare professionals or students) to symptoms or receive care-related information [14]. In simulation, the patient may also be 'standardized'. In this case, their role is predefined, and they must adhere strictly to it to facilitate summative assessment of the learners [14]. In short, standardized patients must behave in a consistent and predictable manner, whereas simulated patients should act flexibly and authentically to meet the needs of learners [15]. Finally, virtual patient software is used to simulate realistic clinical scenarios. Students and healthcare professionals interact with the virtual patient to gather medical history, conduct examinations, diagnose, and make therapeutic decisions [16].

Despite the growing use of simulation-based training in pharmacy curricula and in the continuing medical education of pharmacists, little is known about the impact of simulation on the communication skills of pharmacists and pharmacy students in the area of clinical pharmacy. Previous research has focused mainly on physicians, nurses, and other healthcare professionals. Indeed, Granheim et al. demonstrated in their review that communication skills training in nursing schools aimed to increase collaboration, teamwork, learning in practice, understanding of roles, and communication, particularly between nurses and doctors, by combining interprofessional learning and simulation [17]. Another scoping review in medical schools primarily discussed high-fidelity approaches with standardized patients for interprofessional communication, focusing on attitude and perception changes rather than comprehensive assessment [18]. Blackmore et al., in their systematic review, emphasized that communication skills learned through simulation could have benefits beyond the simulation center, although they critiqued the study designs of many reviewed studies, often using before-and-after designs instead of study and control groups [19]. Methodological biases have also been reported in other studies conducted on communication training through simulation [20]. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the effectiveness of simulation-based training to identify successful practice models for enhancing communication skills in clinical pharmacies.

Aim

This systematic review examined the impact of simulation-based education on communication skills among pharmacy students and graduate pharmacists in performing clinical pharmacy activities. In particular, it aims to identify the types of simulations used, the outcomes measured in these studies, and the effectiveness of these simulations in enhancing communication skills.

Method

This review was undertaken by pharmacists of the hospital pharmacy network (HUGOPharm) of university hospitals in Western France (Angers, Brest, Nantes, Orléans, Rennes, and Tours).

Protocol

This systematic review was registered into the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO n°CRD42022371915) and was conducted in adherence with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21].

Search strategy

The literature search was carried out in PubMed (Medline), Embase, Lissa and PsycInfo from inception to August 31, 2022. The search strategy was developed by the authors in consultation with two academic librarians. The following search strategy was used in PubMed (Medline): ((simulation training[MeSH Terms]) OR (active learning[Title/Abstract])) AND (pharmac*[Title/Abstract]). This search strategy was adapted to the syntax of the Embase, Lissa and PsycInfo databases. The search strategy is described in the supplementary material.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Only original research was considered. To be eligible for inclusion, the studies had to meet the following criteria: (i) they targeted pharmacy students or graduate pharmacists; (ii) they employed simulation (human simulation, virtual simulation, and manikin) in the field of clinical pharmacy, defined as “any action taken by the pharmacist directly with the patient, their caregivers, or their medication”; and (iii) they provided a full description of outcome measures. Reviews were excluded. Studies involving multiple professions where it was not possible to extract data specific to pharmacy students or graduate pharmacists and studies using multiple pedagogical methods where the impact of simulation in clinical pharmacy was not specifically identified were also excluded.

Three investigators (JFH, LFF, FN) independently evaluated each title and abstract. If all three studies were deemed eligible for inclusion, a full-text review was performed. Disputes were resolved through discussion.

Second, six authors conducted a full-text screening of the previously selected articles to retain only those reporting communication outcomes and written in French or English. Rayyan and Zotero referencing software were used for sorting articles, managing duplicates, and screening.

Data extraction

The full texts of the included articles were analyzed by six authors (VMB, AL, MTS, EV, CF, and JFH). A standardized electronic data-charting form was created in Microsoft Excel® software to collect data from the included studies. The following data were collected: first author, publication year, country, setting, population targeted by the intervention (i.e., patient and/or family, healthcare professionals), study design and number of study participants, academic level, simulation type (i.e., simulated or standardized patient, virtual patient, manikin), intervention, study objectives, communication assessment method, and key results. Reviewing the bibliographies of the included articles led to the inclusion of additional articles that were not identified by the search strategy.

Synthesis

The outcomes related to communication were categorized into three distinct groups: perception of the importance of communication in delivering high-quality patient care, confidence in communication skills, and assessed communication skills.

Quality assessment

The quality and risk of bias of each included study were assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [22]. Five authors evaluated all the articles (CF, FN, VM, LFF, PN). If there was any doubt about the evaluation of an article, two authors discussed the quality of the articles and a final decision was made by consensus (VM, LFF).

Results

Overall, 1727 articles were retrieved from the databases. After removing duplicates and excluding records that did not meet the selection criteria, 137 studies were assessed for full text, of which 18 were included. After screening the references, 20 articles were ultimately included for extraction (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

A total of 20 studies, which are listed in Table 1 [23–42], were included in this systematic review. Among the included studies, 15 were conducted in the United States. Notably, more than two-thirds of the studies (70%) were published since 2015. The sample sizes varied across studies, ranging from 15 to 195 participants per study. The total number of participants was 3337. These participants were mainly pharmacy students at different stages of their curriculum, with the third year being the most represented (n = 6). One study was conducted among community pharmacists [30].

In terms of study design, 80% of the studies (16 out of 20) were quasi experimental studies. Two studies were observational, one study was both observational and quasi experimental, and one study employed a randomized controlled design. In the studies selected, simulation-based learning aimed to enhance communication skills and attitudes, either within an interprofessional context involving nursing and medical students (n = 8) or with patients and their families (n = 5). Seven studies focused on these two populations.

Intervention characteristics and outcomes

The simulation types, intervention characteristics, study objectives, and evaluated outcomes, such perception of the importance of communication in delivering high-quality patient care, confidence in communication skills, and communication skills, are presented in Table 2. The most common simulation method involved simulating patients alone (n = 5), while others combined simulated patients with standardized patients (n = 4), virtual patients (n = 2), or manikins (n = 1). Some studies used virtual patients either with standardized patients (n = 1) or alone (n = 3), while others focused exclusively on either manikins (n = 1) or standardized patients (n = 3). The majority of studies evaluated communication skills outcomes with patients and families (4 out of 5 studies that focused on these groups), while the perception of communication was most common examined in studies involving healthcare professionals (6 out of 8 studies targeting this population type). Confidence in communication skills was a predominant outcome in studies encompassing both populations (6 out of 7 studies concerned).

Impact of simulation-based training on communication skills

Assessed communication skills

The methods used to assess communication skills and the main results are presented in Table 3 [25, 30, 33, 35, 38]. Communication skills were often assessed by self-assessment using questionnaires developed by the authors, either in their entirety (n = 10) or on the basis of existing questionnaires (n = 4). Six studies conducted their evaluation using published questionnaires that had not been modified by the investigating team. The questionnaires used were the Students' Perceptions of Interprofessional Clinical Education Revised (SPICE-R), TeamSTEPPS Teamwork Attitudes Questionnaire (T-TAQ), Interdisciplinary Education Perception Scale (IEPS) and Student Satisfaction and Self-Confidence in Learning (SSSCL). The Global Rating Scale (GRS) assessed by simulated patients and the National League for Nursing (NLN) Student Satisfaction and Confidence Scale were also mentioned. In addition, one study used a validated assessment tool created by the research team and published previously [25]. Finally, although outcomes were mainly assessed through self-assessments, some studies used assessments by instructors or assessors (n = 5) or by simulated patients (n = 1).

All articles provided results on changes in communication skills. The various aspects evaluated have been classified in Table 3 into two categories: ‘Patient care processes’ and ‘Specific communication behaviors and skills’. These two categories were combined in the evaluation of the effect of the intervention reported by the authors.

Three studies used only one type of simulation [25, 30, 35]. The combination of standardized patients and simulated patients or standardized and virtual patients was used in the two other studies [33, 38]. These studies showed significant improvements in communication skills and referred to verbal communication skills. Two articles also referred to non-verbal communication skills [30, 38]. Different types of non-verbal communication, including body language and gestures, were studied.

Assessed perception of the importance of communication

Seven articles provided results on changes in the perception of the importance of communication in delivering high-quality patient care [26, 28, 32, 34, 37, 40, 41] (Table 3). These seven articles used self-assessment questionnaires, and four used validated scores (NLN, T-TAQ, SPICE-R, SPICE-R2, IEPS). The results, based on a comparison of questionnaire responses before and after the intervention, indicated an increase in interest in interprofessional and patient communication after simulation-based training with simulated patients, standardized patients, virtual patients, or manikins. Non-significant results were obtained for questions that indicated an interest in communication before the intervention, such as 'It is important to ask patients and their families for feedback regarding patient care’. The SPICE-R and SPICE-R2 questionnaires included items on organizing sessions with students from different disciplines. The results confirmed the positive impact of these interprofessional sessions on identifying roles and facilitating subsequent communication. The study by Brennan et al. showed a significant improvement in the perception of the importance of communication in students who had received simulation training compared with those who had not [28]. However, there was no additional benefit to carrying out a second training session compared to carrying out only one training session.

Assessed of confidence in communication skills

Seven studies assessed confidence in communication skills [23, 24, 27, 29, 39, 40, 42] (Table 3). One study used validated scores (NLN score) [30]. A single virtual patient was used for the simulation method. Three studies used a combination of standardized patients and simulated patients. Two studies used only the standardized patient technique. All seven studies demonstrated a significant increase in confidence in communication skills. Students were more confident in their ability to communicate with patients and other healthcare professionals, allowing them to easily exchange information, formulate treatment recommendations, and disclose errors.

Among the included articles, three were observational studies that examined the satisfaction levels of pharmacy students who had participated in simulation-based training [26, 31, 36]. A study by Bodein et al. reported favorable feedback regarding simulation in the context of acquiring communication skills particularly in the context of interprofessional interactions [26]. They highlighted the difficulties of engaging with other healthcare professionals, but felt that simulation was beneficial. Fernandez et al.’s s demonstrated that students felt more comfortable interacting with patients after simulation sessions [31]. In contrast, the study by Monaghan et al. revealed that students were reluctant to use simulation to develop their communication skills, as it did not boost their self-confidence and left them feeling ill-prepared to counsel patients [36].

Finally, on the basis of qualitative responses to questions included in questionnaires from the studies reviewed [29, 39], students expressed a strong appreciation for the role-play aspects of these simulations, emphasizing that these encounters enriched their understanding of effective communication and interactions with patients. They particularly appreciated the feedback provided by the actors, believing that it enabled them to hone their communication skills. Importantly, qualitative data revealed that students felt that they had acquired a critical skill through simulation-based training: the ability to actively listen to and empathize with the concerns of others.

Study quality

The results of the MMAT quality assessment are detailed in the supplementary file. The overall score obtained was 40% for non-randomized quantitative studies and 52% for descriptive quantitative studies. The randomized study included in the review obtained an overall score of 40.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

This review synthesizes the results of 20 studies using a variety of study designs, such as observational studies, pre/post (non)controlled studies, and randomized controlled trials. Only one study focused on graduate pharmacists, while the student participants varied across different academic levels and workshop sizes. It should be noted that in some studies, the number of participants after the intervention was lower than that before the intervention. This could introduce bias due to missing data. No specific type of simulation predominated among the studies dealing with communication with patients, families, and/or interprofessional teams. The outcomes focused on learners' perceptions of communication, their confidence in communicating effectively, and their actual communication skills. Overall, the analysis consistently demonstrated a significant improvement in communication through simulation-based training across multiple stages of the patient care process, including patient assessment and history-taking, care plan development, monitoring and follow-up with patients and healthcare professionals, and documentation. Additionally, specific communication behaviors and skills, such as demonstrating empathy, active listening, non-verbal communication, verifying understanding, or even delivering clear instructions, adapting communication style to patient needs, and providing constructive feedback, showed marked enhancement.

Interpretation

The results of this systematic review suggest that simulation-based training has a positive impact on pharmacy students’ and pharmacists’ communication skills. However, evaluation methods often rely on self-developed questionnaires rather than validated questionnaires, raising questions about the accuracy and objectivity of the confidence ratings obtained. This methodological variability highlights the lack of standardized guidelines for simulation-based training and the assessment of communication skills in pharmacy education. A study by Huon et al. on the influence of simulation on the confidence of clinical pharmacy students also highlighted these methodological limitations [43].

Communication plays an essential role in the pharmacist profession, which interfaces with many other medical professions in patient care [44]. As demonstrated in this review, simulation workshops can be organized at each stage of the patient care process [45, 46], particularly to improve the acquisition of communication skills. First, there is information gathering. Simulation-based learning teaches students how to effectively gather information from patients, their families, and/or health professionals. Second, simulation reinforces students' knowledge and improves their ability to recognize errors. It also increases learners' confidence in sharing information about identified errors with nurses and doctors [24, 39]. Next, a care plan should be developed in collaboration with other healthcare professionals and the patient or caregiver. Learning to work as part of a team through simulation helps students understand each other's roles, gain confidence to interact effectively, and discuss treatment recommendations [28, 41]. Finally, simulation improves students' ability to implement and monitor care plans [30]. It has also improved their ability to inform patients or caregivers about medication details (efficacy, dosage, adverse effects, etc.) during pharmaceutical interviews [27, 33, 38].

Simulation training provides a controlled environment conducive to exchanges between professionals. This environment enables students to feel confident while they are exposed to potentially stressful practical situations, as explored in the systematic review of Ung et al. on simulation in mental health [47]. This enables them to develop communication strategies to deal with them in their real-life practice, through both verbal and non-verbal communication. This is an opportunity, as it can be adapted to a multitude of scenarios, such as managing frustrated patients, communicating difficult information or transmitting complex medical information.

There is a lack of clear guidelines for simulation courses in the pharmacy curriculum. Qualitative data revealed mixed results, with some students reporting limited improvement or even negative impacts on clinical abilities post-simulation, influenced by previous interprofessional team experiences and initial confidence in teamwork skills [39]. Advanced pharmacy students may find it difficult to change their attitudes and perceptions because of the ingrained habits they have developed as part of their more or less regular pharmacy practice. Consequently, improving their communication skills can be particularly challenging. They may need other methods (besides simulation-based learning) to enhance their communication skills. The early integration of simulations could effectively improve pharmacy students' communication skills.

Strengths and weaknesses

This review followed best practices, including protocol registration, rigorous research methodology, and meticulous data sorting, extraction, and analysis. However, several limitations affect the interpretability of the results. The variability of simulation techniques between studies introduces significant heterogeneity, limiting comparability and generalizability. Reviews on simulation-based training for medical students show predominantly positive impacts on patient communication and interprofessional skills, but they also highlight the difficulties in comparing studies due to the diversity of methodologies and the non-standardisation of data collection [18, 48]. In addition, some studies reporting improved communication skills assessed both stages of the patient care process and specific communication behaviors and skills. The patient care process is seen as an essential framework of clinical pharmacy practice, and its success is underpinned by effective communication skills (based on communication behaviours). Hence, these two elements should therefore have been considered and evaluated separately. Finally, new studies, including a standardized assessment design with the aim of measuring students' knowledge and communication skills rather than their perceptions [23], would make it possible to supplement the research data and propose simulation workshops adapted to contexts or levels of study.

Future research

To improve simulation-based pharmaceutical training, the development of clear guidelines and standardized assessment tools to evaluate communication would significantly improve the validity and reliability of future research efforts. Furthermore, they should aim to investigate the long-term impact of simulation-based training beyond the simulation center. Finally, a cost-effectiveness analysis of the use of simulation versus traditional classroom activities in pharmacy education could complement the research data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the use of robust methodologies with standardized guidelines and validated assessment tools would allow the impact of simulation on communication skills to be better measured. Despite study variations, this systematic review consistently demonstrated that simulation-based training significantly improves pharmacy students and pharmacists’ perceptions, confidence, and enhances their communication skills. Educators are encouraged to integrate simulation into curricula to enhance students’ learning experiences.
