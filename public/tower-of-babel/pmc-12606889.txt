Congenital oculomotor nerve palsy with aberrant eyelid movements and pupillary spasms: a case report
Authors: Donghun Lee, Geun Woo Lee, Sook Hyun Yoon, Jae Han Park, Sook Young Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe report a case of congenital oculomotor nerve palsy presenting with aberrant eyelid movements and an involuntary pupillary reaction.Case PresentationA 24-year-old woman presented with mild anisocoria with no significant medical history. Her ocular history included a history of strabismus and levator muscle surgery. Examination revealed exotropia, hypotropia, and ocular motility impairment, consistent with right oculomotor nerve palsy. Background

Anatomically, the nucleus of the oculomotor nerve is located at the superior colliculus in the dorsal midbrain. The oculomotor nerve innervates most of the extraocular muscles, as well as the levator palpebrae superioris and the sphincter pupillae via parasympathetic fibers [1, 2]. Therefore, oculomotor nerve palsy can present as ophthalmoplegia, which is characterized by limitations in supraduction, infraduction, and adduction, accompanied by ptosis and abnormal pupillary reactions.

Evaluating pupil size and pupillary reactions in cases of oculomotor nerve palsy is crucial, as this condition may result from compressive lesions, including life-threatening etiologies such as a posterior communicating artery aneurysm, which can lead to subarachnoid hemorrhage [2]. In acquired oculomotor nerve palsy, pupillary dilation and fixation usually indicate a compressive cause [3].

Therefore, a detailed assessment of pupillary function is essential for the early detection of potentially serious underlying causes.

Meanwhile, pupillary abnormalities observed in congenital oculomotor nerve palsy are uncommon and are likely related to aberrant regeneration [4–6]. Previous reports on aberrant regeneration of the pupillary reaction found that abnormal pupillary responses mostly manifested as miosis in certain ocular positions or cyclic pupillary spasms [7–9]. Based on previous reports in the PubMed database [10–12], irregular involvement of both miosis and mydriasis has not been reported. Therefore, we present a case involving involuntary pupillary spasms, along with a video for further reference. The patient has given consent to participate as well as consent to publish the data.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old woman presented to our clinic after noticing that her right pupil was smaller than her left. She had no significant medical or ocular history and reported no diplopia. Her ocular surgical history included two surgeries in the right eye performed at another hospital: strabismus surgery for correcting exotropia at 9 years and levator muscle surgery for correcting lid drooping at 20 years. Visual acuity was 20/20 in both eyes, with normal intraocular pressures. Slit-lamp examination was unremarkable. The right eye showed mildly decreased levator function of 8 mm compared with 14 mm in the left eye and exhibited 10 prism diopters of exotropia and 5 prism diopters of hypotropia at distance and near fixation. (Fig. 1A). Ocular motility testing showed limitations in adduction, elevation, and depression of the right eye (Fig. 1B), with upper eyelid retraction noted on downward gaze (Fig. 2A).

There was no relative afferent pupillary defect in either eye, with 2 mm in the right eye and 3 mm in the left under bright light. Under dim lighting, mydriasis promptly occurred in the left eye, whereas the pupil of the paralytic right eye did not dilate (Fig. 2B). The patient’s right eye exhibited intermittent pupillary miosis accompanied by spasmodic contraction of the right levator muscle, resulting in slight upper eyelid elevation coinciding with the miosis. Fine, twitch-like movements of the upper eyelid were also observed and there was slight elevation with widening of the fissure during the spasm phase. Subsequently, an involuntary pupillary dilation was noted in the right eye (Fig. 2C), which did not correlate with the position of the eye, including supraduction or infraduction (Supplemental Digital Content). The spastic phases were variable in both frequency and duration, lasting less than one minute but occurring with considerable variability.

Orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed to identify organic causes, which revealed no intracranial lesions. However, extensive atrophy of the extraocular muscles in the right eye was observed within the orbit (Fig. 3A), most prominently in the medial rectus (Fig. 3B). This suggests that ocular motility impairment had long been present. On the available orbital enhanced MRI, no abnormal findings were noted in the brain parenchyma or intracranial vasculature within the imaged field. Additionally, blood tests were unremarkable, excluding other potential causes of strabismus. Although the exact details of the childhood strabismus surgery were unavailable, orbital MRI findings suggest that the procedure involved horizontal muscle surgery rather than vertical transposition.

Orbital MRI findings and in the affected eye. (A) Orbital MRI revealed extensive atrophy of the extraocular muscles in the right eye (red box) in coronary T1-weighted fat-suppressed images. (B) Thinning of the right medial rectus muscle (yellow arrow) was prominently observed on axial T1-weighted fat-suppressed images

Considering the absence of diplopia, widespread extraocular muscle atrophy on orbital MRI, a history of strabismus and ptosis surgery in the right eye and abnormal retraction of the right eyelid on downward gaze, the patient was diagnosed with congenital oculomotor nerve palsy with aberrant regeneration of the levator palpebrae superioris and pupillary sphincter. The patient had no subjective symptoms related to the pupillary spasm and was managed conservatively. After six months of follow-up, no complications were observed.

Discussion and conclusions

Pupillary abnormalities associated with oculomotor nerve palsy are usually observed as anisocoria, with a larger pupil on the affected side, which is commonly observed in patients with acquired oculomotor nerve palsy. Pupillary involvement is more frequently associated with compressive lesions, whereas it is relatively uncommon in ischemic etiologies [1, 13]. Compressive lesions, such as neoplasms and aneurysms, are highly suspected because of the anatomical characteristics of the pupil fibers which reside in the most superficial and dorsomedial portions of the oculomotor nerve within the subarachnoid space [14]. Therefore, when acquired oculomotor nerve palsy with pupillary involvement is observed, careful clinical evaluation and appropriate imaging can identify the definitive cause in most cases [13, 14].

Regardless of whether the condition is congenital or acquired, aberrant regeneration following the recovery of the oculomotor nerve palsy has been reported to cause pupillary abnormalities, presenting in various forms, such as miosis, mydriasis, or cyclic spasm. Czarnecki and Thompson [4] studied the pupils of 14 patients with aberrant reinnervation of the oculomotor nerve and observed abnormal pupillary unrest with asynchronous random contractions of the iris sphincter, which is considered as fiber-sprouting collateral branches that reaches additional muscles of the oculomotor group other than those originally innervated. Hamed [5] reported two cases of congenital oculomotor nerve palsy with dilated fixed pupils that became miotic during growth. They concluded that pupillary miosis is a result of aberrant reinnervation, which appears to be much more frequent after congenital rather than acquired pupillary involvement in oculomotor palsy. In addition, Tian et al. [6] reported oculomotor nerve paresis with cyclic spasm, which is a rare congenital condition that is usually not associated with tumors.

The present case is similar to Tian et al.’s report in that both demonstrated a miotic pupil in the affected eye under room light, accompanied by alternating paralysis and spasm. In addition, as described by Tian et al., the patient’s eyelid retracted slightly and exhibited a subtle “twittering” movement occurring simultaneously with pupillary constriction. However, Tian et al.’s case was associated with the degree of exotropia along with ptosis and pupillary response, whereas the present case is characterized by intermittent pupil miosis that was accompanied by spasm of the right upper eyelid and slight elevation coincident with pupil miosis. Furthermore, during the paretic phase, the pupil dilates but may not reach the full mydriatic size of the fellow eye, likely due to aberrant regenerative components frequently observed in congenital oculomotor nerve palsy with cyclic spasms [10].

Furthermore, Loewenfeld and Thompson [10] emphasized that pupillary spasms are slower than normal pupillary movements and exhibit an abnormal movement pattern, which matches our patient’s pupillary movement. In our patient, the right pupil demonstrated twitch-like, spasmodic movements even when already miotic, which is inconsistent with simple sustained miosis or normal pupillary light reactions. For this reason, the term ‘pupillary spasm’ seems more appropriate than ‘miosis’.

Meanwhile, tadpole pupil, which is an episodic pupillary distortion, can be considered in the differential diagnosis, as it can occur unilaterally with variable and often long intervals between episodes [15]. In contrast, our case’s pupillary abnormalities were concurrent with exotropia and ptosis in the same eye. This indicates that they were associated with aberrant regeneration of oculomotor nerve palsy, which can serve as a distinguishing feature.

Previous reports of cyclic oculomotor palsy, including the seminal review by Loewenfeld and Thompson [10] and the case report by Yazici et al. [11], described relatively regular patterns of alternating paresis and spasm phases. In contrast, our patient presented with congenital oculomotor palsy, and the cyclic spasms were strikingly irregular in both frequency and duration. Moreover, the episodes involved not only pupillary constriction but also aberrant eyelid movements, underscoring the unique presentation of this case compared with previously documented reports.

In addition, the patient underwent strabismus surgery for exotropia in early childhood. Based on MRI findings, the procedure is presumed to have been a horizontal muscle recession and resection rather than a transposition. Strabismus surgery primarily serves to correct ocular alignment and is unlikely to significantly affect the mechanisms of oculomotor nerve regeneration. Therefore, the fundamental characteristics of aberrant regeneration observed in this case are considered intrinsic to the underlying congenital oculomotor nerve palsy.

Furthermore, the patient has maintained normal visual acuity in both eyes, which makes this case particularly interesting. She was able to alternate fixation with both eyes, without a clearly dominant eye, although she appears to use the non-paretic eye more comfortably. This is likely attributable to the correction of horizontal strabismus during early childhood, which may have positively influenced the development of fusional capacity.

The exact mechanism underlying the patient’s involuntary pupillary spasm with congenital oculomotor nerve palsy remains unclear, though several hypotheses have been proposed [12]. These include prenatal partial damage and impaired supranuclear connections, incomplete recovery of the peripheral oculomotor nerve, and aberrant nerve regeneration leading to intermittent conduction blocks. Oculomotor palsy with cyclic spasm should be differentiated from ocular neuromyotonia [16–18], which is typically associated with a significant medical history such as prior orbital radiation or thyroid eye disease—features that were absent in the present case. Based on these hypotheses, we propose that the pupillary spasms result from immaturity of the pupillary nerve fibers due to congenital oculomotor nerve palsy and aberrant regeneration.

This report is meaningful in that involuntary non-cyclic pupillary reaction observed in an eye with long-standing oculomotor nerve palsy. These abnormal pupillary movements were independent of the presence of light, near vision, or ocular position. Although there is no proven treatment for cyclic spasms in asymptomatic patients, the prognosis is considered favorable for asymptomatic patients without associated underlying causes.
