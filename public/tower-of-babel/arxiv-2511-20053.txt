Classification of Quaternionic Projective Transformations by Equicontinuity Regions
Sandipan Dutta, Krishnendu Gongopadhyay and Rahul Mondal

Abstract
. We describe the equicontinuity regions of cyclic subgroups of the quaternionic projective linear group PSL ⁡ ( n + 1 , ℍ ) \displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}) . We show that these regions depend solely on the dynamical type of the generator g \displaystyle g , i.e. whether g \displaystyle g is elliptic, parabolic, loxodromic or loxoparabolic. This yields an analytic interpretation of the dynamical classification of the elements. In particular, elliptic cyclic groups act equicontinuously on all of the quaternionic projective space, while for the parabolic, loxodromic and loxoparabolic elements the equicontinuity region is determined by explicit quaternionic projective subspaces arising from the generator’s Jordan form.

1.
Introduction

The dynamics of projective actions of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
has long constituted a central theme in the study of Kleinian groups and geometric group theory. For discrete groups acting on higher dimensional complex projective space, the
equicontinuity region
plays a fundamental role as an invariant that generalizes the classical domain of discontinuity (see, for instance, Cano–Seade
[
CS10
]
, Cano–Navarrete–Seade
[
CNS
]
, and Cano–Loeza–Ucan Puc
[
CLU
]
). It represents the largest open subset on which the group action induces a regular, well-behaved dynamics. It has proven to be a powerful data in understanding limit sets, orbit structures, and global geometric properties of complex projective transformations, cf.
[
CNS
]
.

Recently, the quaternionic analogues of complex Kleinian groups has gained some attention which is motivated by the rich interplay between quaternionic hyperbolic geometry and the dynamics of projective transformations over the quaternions. The quaternionic setting has a new layer of complexity due to the non-commutativity of the quaternionic multiplication. This alters the framework that is used in the complex case. Many of the tools and results in complex projective dynamics cannot be directly transferred, and must instead be reformulated to accommodate the subtleties of quaternionic analysis.

In our earlier works
[
DGM
]
and
[
DGL
]
, we initiated a systematic study of the dynamics of
quaternionic cyclic groups
and obtained a detailed description of their Kulkarni limit sets. This investigation extended the corresponding results established in the complex setting by Cano, Loeza, and Ucan-Puc
[
CLU
]
. It is worth recalling that the study of complex Kleinian groups was originally initiated by Seade and Verjovsky
[
SV99
,
SV01
]
, and subsequently developed in a series of works by Cano, Barrera, Navarrete, and others, see the comprehensive monograph
[
CNS
]
or the survey
[
BCNS
]
for an overview. The theory that has emerged in the complex case offers deep insights into the global dynamics of projective transformations, but its quaternionic counterpart remains relatively unexplored.

The notion of
equicontinuity
, which we aim to investigate in this paper, is rooted in the classical Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, providing a compactness criterion for families of functions. In
[
CS14
]
, Cano and Seade
introduced the concept of the equicontinuity region for
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(3,\mathbb{C})
action on the complex projective space
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
and established its relation with the Kulkarni limit set. This theory was subsequently generalized to higher-dimensional complex projective spaces
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
in
[
CLU
]
, where the authors provided a classification of the equicontinuity regions for cyclic subgroups of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{C})
. This gave a dynamical classification of the complex projective transformations.

The principal aim of the present article is to extend this work to the quaternionic setting in order to provide a complete classification of equicontinuity regions for cyclic subgroups of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
. To formulate our main results, we need the following notions.

Definition 1.1
.

A family
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
of maps from a topological space into
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is said to be
normal
if every sequence in
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
contains a subsequence that converges uniformly on compact subsets.

Using this concept, we define the equicontinuity region for projective actions over the quaternions.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a discrete subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
. The
region of equicontinuity
of
\displaystyle G
, denoted
\displaystyle\mathrm{Eq}(G)
, is the set of points
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
such that there exists an open neighborhood
\displaystyle U
of
\displaystyle z
for which the restricted family

G_{U}=\{g|_{U}:g\in G\}

is normal.

Intuitively,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Eq}(G)
consists of those points in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
where the group action behaves in a uniformly controlled manner, where orbits do not exhibit erratic divergence or collapse, and hence where local compactness and convergence properties are preserved.

In order to classify the equicontinuity regions of cyclic quaternionic projective groups, we analyze elements
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
via their quaternionic Jordan canonical forms, and determine the corresponding equicontinuity region
\displaystyle\mathrm{Eq}(G)
for the cyclic subgroup
\displaystyle G=\langle g\rangle
. The dynamical nature of
\displaystyle g
can be described by the following classification.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
. Then:

(i)

\displaystyle g
is called
elliptic
if it is semisimple and all its eigenvalue classes are represented by quaternions of unit modulus;

(ii)

\displaystyle g
is called
parabolic
if it is not semisimple and all its eigenvalues have unit modulus;

(iii)

\displaystyle g
is called
loxodromic
if it is semisimple but not all eigenvalue classes are represented by unit modulus quaternions;

(iv)

\displaystyle g
is called
loxoparabolic
if it is not semisimple and at least one eigenvalue has non-unit modulus.

Our approach builds upon the quaternionic Jordan canonical form together with the method of pseudo-projective limits. Our methods are inspired by the strategy developed in the complex setting by Cano and Seade in
[
CS14
]
. The quaternionic structure introduces new phenomena not present in the complex case, arising from the non-commutative nature of the underlying field and the resulting behavior of similarity classes of eigenvalues.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a projective transformation and let

\Gamma:=\langle\gamma\rangle\subset\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})

be the cyclic subgroup generated by
\displaystyle\gamma
. Denote by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
the equicontinuity set of the family
\displaystyle\Gamma
. Then the following holds.

(i)

The element
\displaystyle\gamma
is
elliptic
if and only if
\displaystyle\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
.

(ii)

The element
\displaystyle\gamma
is
parabolic
if and only if the complement
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)
is a single projective subspace
\displaystyle L
(see Theorem
4.5
for a precise description).

(iii)

The element
\displaystyle\gamma
is
loxodromic
if and only if it is diagonalizable and the complement
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)
can be described as the union of two distinct proper projective subspaces
\displaystyle L_{1},L_{2}
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
(see Theorem
5.1
for a precise description).

(iv)

The element
\displaystyle\gamma
is
loxoparabolic
if and only if it is not diagonalizable and the complement
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)
can be described as the union of two distinct proper projective subspaces
\displaystyle L_{1},L_{2}
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
(see Theorem
6.2
for a precise description).

Our results indicate that the description of the
dynamical type
of the generator
\displaystyle g
is classified by the equicontinuity region of
\displaystyle g
, and this provides an analytic classification of the elliptic, parabolic, loxodromic and loxoparabolic elements in
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n,\mathbb{H})
. It may be noted that there has been attempts to relate the classification of transformations to their algebraic data, for example, see
[
DGL2
]
,
[
KL
]
,
[
Go1
]
,

[
PS
]
. Specifically, we establish that elliptic cyclic groups act equicontinuously on the entire quaternionic projective space, whereas in the parabolic and loxodromic cases, the equicontinuity region is described by explicit quaternionic projective subspaces determined by the structure of the Jordan blocks associated with the generator. We hope that this work (along with
[
DGM
]
) lays the foundation for further investigations aimed at understanding the global dynamics of quaternionic projective groups and their invariants.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
denote the division ring of Hamilton’s quaternions. Every quaternion
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{H}
can be expressed as

a=a_{0}+a_{1}i+a_{2}j+a_{3}k,\qquad a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{R},

where the units satisfy
\displaystyle i^{2}=j^{2}=k^{2}=ijk=-1
. The conjugate of
\displaystyle a
is

\bar{a}=a_{0}-a_{1}i-a_{2}j-a_{3}k.

The real subspace
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}i
is identified with the complex plane
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
. For further background on quaternionic linear algebra and matrix theory, see
[
Ro
,
FZ
]
.

Eigenvalues of matrices in
\displaystyle\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
. A non-zero vector
\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a (right) eigenvector of
\displaystyle A
with (right) eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
if

Av=v\lambda.

Right eigenvalues occur in similarity classes: if
\displaystyle Av=v\lambda
, then for any
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}
, the vector
\displaystyle v\mu
is also an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\displaystyle\mu^{-1}\lambda\mu
. Each similarity class contains a unique complex number with non-negative imaginary part, which we take as its representative and refer to simply as an eigenvalue.

Complex Embedding and Quaternionic Determinant

Any
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
can be written as

A=A_{1}+A_{2}j,\qquad A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{C}).

The standard embedding

\Phi:\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})\longrightarrow\mathrm{M}(2n,\mathbb{C})

is given by

(2.1)

\Phi(A)=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
-\overline{A_{2}}&\overline{A_{1}}\end{bmatrix}.

Definition 2.2
.

For
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
, the quaternionic determinant of
\displaystyle A
is

{\det}_{\mathbb{H}}(A):=\det(\Phi(A)).

This definition is independent of the embedding
\displaystyle\Phi
by the Skolem–Noether theorem.

The corresponding Lie groups are

\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})=\{g\mid{\det}_{\mathbb{H}}(g)\neq 0\},\qquad\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{H})=\{g\mid{\det}_{\mathbb{H}}(g)=1\}.

Jordan Theory over Quaternions

Definition 2.3
.

[
Ro
]

A Jordan block
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}(\lambda,m)
is the
\displaystyle m\times m
matrix having
\displaystyle\lambda
on its diagonal,
\displaystyle 1
on its superdiagonal, and zeros elsewhere. A direct sum of Jordan blocks is called a Jordan form.

Lemma 2.4

(Quaternionic Jordan form, cf.
[
Ro
, Th. 5.5.3]
)
.

For every
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
, there exists
\displaystyle S\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})
such that

SAS^{-1}=\mathrm{J}(\lambda_{1},m_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{J}(\lambda_{k},m_{k}),

where each
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}
is a complex number with non-negative imaginary part. This decomposition is unique up to permutation of blocks.

Quaternionic Projective Space
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}

Consider
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
as a right
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
-vector space. The quaternionic projective space is defined by

\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}=\big(\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}\big)/\sim,\qquad z\sim w\iff z=w\alpha\ \text{for some }\alpha\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
denote the quotient map. A subset
\displaystyle W\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is a
\displaystyle k
-dimensional projective subspace if there exists a
\displaystyle(k+1)
-dimensional
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
-linear subspace
\displaystyle\widetilde{W}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
such that

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{W}\setminus\{0\})=W.

Projective
\displaystyle 1
-subspaces are called
lines
. For

p=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1},\quad[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n+1}]:=\mathbb{P}(p),

and for
\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0
,

[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n+1}]=[x_{1}\alpha:\cdots:x_{n+1}\alpha].

Given
\displaystyle S\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
,

\langle S\rangle=\bigcap\{H:H\ \text{is a projective subspace containing }S\}.

If
\displaystyle p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
are distinct, then
\displaystyle\langle p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\rangle
is the unique projective subspace containing them; for
\displaystyle m=2
it is the quaternionic projective line
\displaystyle L\{p,q\}
(cf.
[
DGL
]
).

Projective Transformations

For
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathrm{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
,

\gamma(\mathbb{P}(z))=\mathbb{P}(\gamma z),\qquad z\neq 0.

Scalar multiples act trivially, so

\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})=\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})/\mathcal{Z}(\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})),\qquad\mathcal{Z}=\{\pm I_{n+1}\}.

Each element has exactly two lifts
\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}
and
\displaystyle-\widetilde{\gamma}
. Using quaternionic Jordan theory, one obtains a well-defined classification of elements as elliptic, loxodromic, or parabolic (see Definition
1.3
).

Pseudo-Projective Transformations

Following Cano–Seade
[
CS10
]
, we extend the notion of pseudo-projective transformations to the quaternionic setting. Let
\displaystyle\widetilde{M}:\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
be a non-zero
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
-linear map. It induces

M:\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\ker(M)\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}},\qquad M(\mathbb{P}(v))=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{M}(v)),

where
\displaystyle\ker(M)
is the projectivization of
\displaystyle\ker(\widetilde{M})\setminus\{0\}
. We call
\displaystyle M
a
pseudo-projective transformation
and set

\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})=\{M\mid\widetilde{M}\ \text{is a non-zero }\mathbb{H}\text{-linear transformation}\}.

The group
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
embeds naturally into
\displaystyle\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
.

Pseudo-projective transformations, together with the classification of lifts in
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
, will be used to study of equicontinuty region .

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n,\mathbb{H})
. Then an element
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
is called a limit of the group
\displaystyle G
if there exists a sequence of distinct elements
\displaystyle(\gamma_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subset G
such that
\displaystyle\gamma_{m}\xrightarrow{m\rightarrow\infty}\gamma
.

If the Definition
2.5
holds, then the set of all the limits of
\displaystyle G
is denoted by
\displaystyle Lim(G).

Lemma 2.7

Let

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\displaystyle(\gamma_{n})\subset\mathrm{PSL}(n,\mathbb{H})
be a sequence that converges to a pseudo-projective transformation
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathrm{QP}(n,\mathbb{H})
uniformly on compact subsets of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\ker(\gamma)

\displaystyle\{\gamma_{n}\}
is given by

\mathrm{Eq}(\{\gamma_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\})=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\ker(\gamma).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}:\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
be a nonzero linear
representative of
\displaystyle\gamma
, and denote again by
\displaystyle\gamma
its projectivization

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\ker(\gamma)\to\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
.
By assumption
\displaystyle\gamma_{m}\to\gamma
uniformly on compact subsets of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\ker(\gamma)
.

Fix
\displaystyle x\notin\ker(\gamma)
.
Choose a neighborhood
\displaystyle U
of
\displaystyle x
such that
\displaystyle\overline{U}
is compact and

\displaystyle\overline{U}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\ker(\gamma)
.
Since
\displaystyle(\gamma_{m})
converges uniformly to
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle\overline{U}
, the family

\displaystyle\{\gamma_{m}\big|_{U}\}
is normal. Hence
\displaystyle x\in\mathrm{Eq}(\{\gamma_{m}\})
.

If possible, suppose,

\displaystyle x\in\ker(\gamma)\cap\mathrm{Eq}(\{\gamma_{m}\})
. We want to show that this leads to a contradiction.

Let
\displaystyle k=\dim(\mathrm{Im}(\gamma))
and choose a projective
\displaystyle k
–subspace

\displaystyle A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
such that

\displaystyle A\cap\ker(\gamma)\neq\varnothing
and
\displaystyle A\cap\mathrm{Im}(\gamma)\neq\varnothing
.
Let
\displaystyle L=\langle A,x\rangle
; then
\displaystyle\dim L=k
.

The Grassmannian of projective
\displaystyle k
–subspaces of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is compact.
Therefore, from the sequence
\displaystyle\gamma_{m}(L)
we may extract a subsequence

\displaystyle\gamma_{n_{j}}(L)
converging to a projective
\displaystyle k
–subspace

\displaystyle M\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Since
\displaystyle\gamma_{n_{j}}\to\gamma
uniformly on compact sets outside

\displaystyle\ker(\gamma)
and
\displaystyle A\cap\mathrm{Im}(\gamma)\neq\varnothing
,
we have
\displaystyle M\cap\mathrm{Im}(\gamma)\neq\varnothing
.

Because
\displaystyle x\in\mathrm{Eq}(\{\gamma_{m}\})
,
the sequence
\displaystyle(\gamma_{n_{j}})
is normal near
\displaystyle x
; hence,
after passing to a further subsequence, there exists a continuous map

\displaystyle g
defined on a neighbourhood of
\displaystyle x
such that

\displaystyle\gamma_{n_{j}}\to g
uniformly on that neighbourhood.
Let
\displaystyle(\xi_{j})
and
\displaystyle(\eta_{j})
be sequences in
\displaystyle L
with

\displaystyle\xi_{j}\to x
,
\displaystyle\eta_{j}\to x
and such that

\displaystyle\gamma_{n_{j}}(\xi_{j})\to\xi
,
\displaystyle\gamma_{n_{j}}(\eta_{j})\to\eta
for some distinct points
\displaystyle\xi,\eta\in M\setminus\mathrm{Im}(\gamma)
.
(These exist because
\displaystyle\dim M=\dim L=k
and
\displaystyle M\not\subset\mathrm{Im}(\gamma)
.)

Uniform convergence to
\displaystyle g
near
\displaystyle x
implies

\displaystyle\gamma_{n_{j}}(\xi_{j})\to g(x)
and
\displaystyle\gamma_{n_{j}}(\eta_{j})\to g(x)
,
hence
\displaystyle\xi=\eta
, a contradiction.
Therefore
\displaystyle x\notin\mathrm{Eq}(\{\gamma_{m}\})
.

Combining all these gives

\mathrm{Eq}(\{\gamma_{m}\})=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\ker(\gamma).

This completes the proof.
∎

From the previous Lemmas and Definition
2.5
we can deduce the following theorem.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
\displaystyle G\subset\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a discrete subgroup and
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a limit of
\displaystyle G
then

\mathrm{Eq}(G)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{g\in Lim(G)}\ker(g)}.

3.
Equicontinuity regions of cyclic subgroups generated by elliptic element

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be an elliptic transformation with lift

\gamma=\mathrm{D}\big[e^{2\pi i\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\alpha_{n+1}}\big]\in\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}).

Then the equicontinuity region of the cyclic group
\displaystyle\Gamma:=\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle
is given by

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}.

Proof.

We consider two cases depending on the rationality of the parameters
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
.

Case 1.
Suppose that all
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then there exists
\displaystyle n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

e^{2\pi in_{0}\alpha_{i}}=1\quad\text{for all }i=1,2,\ldots,n+1.

Hence,
\displaystyle\gamma
is of finite order, and so is
\displaystyle\Gamma
. That implies,

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}.

Case 2.
Suppose that at least one of the
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
, say
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}
, is irrational. Then there exists a subsequence
\displaystyle(\gamma^{n_{k}})
converging to some

g\in\mathrm{Lim}(\Gamma).

Since
\displaystyle|e^{2\pi i\alpha_{i}n_{k}}|=1
for all
\displaystyle i
, the limit map can be written as

g=\mathrm{D}[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1}],

where
\displaystyle|\alpha_{i}|=1
for every
\displaystyle i
. As
\displaystyle\ker(g)=\emptyset
, it follows from Lemma
2.8
that

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Equicontinuity regions of cyclic subgroups generated by parabolic element

In this section we shall compute the equicontinuity region for the cyclic subgroups generated by parabolic elements.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a parabolic transformation whose lift is given by

\gamma=\mathrm{J}(\lambda,n+1)\in\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}).

Then the equicontinuity region of the cyclic group
\displaystyle\Gamma:=\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle
is

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

Proof.

The proof is divided into two parts.

First, consider the powers of
\displaystyle\gamma
. By computing
\displaystyle\gamma^{k}
and examining the sequence

\frac{1}{{k\choose n}}\gamma^{k},

If a subsequence converges to a pseude projective limit then it must be of the form;

g=a\,\mathrm{E}_{1,n+1},

where
\displaystyle|a|=1
.

Next, we analyze the inverses
\displaystyle\gamma^{-k}
. Consider the sequence

\frac{1}{{k+n-1\choose n}}\gamma^{-k}.

Also , if a subsequence of
\displaystyle\frac{1}{{k+n-1\choose n}}\gamma^{-k}
converges to a pseudo projective limit then it must be of the type

g=a\,\mathrm{E}_{1,n+1},

where again
\displaystyle|a|=1
. Therefore, if
\displaystyle g\in Lim(\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle)

then

g=a\,\mathrm{E}_{1,n+1},

where
\displaystyle|a|=1
. Since

\ker(g)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

we conclude that the equicontinuity region of
\displaystyle\Gamma
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a parabolic transformation whose lift is given by

\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{l})&\\
&\mathrm{J}(\mu,k)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}).

Then the equicontinuity region of the cyclic group
\displaystyle\Gamma:=\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle
is

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle\gamma
is block diagonal, we have

\gamma^{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{l})^{m}&\\
&\mathrm{J}(\mu,k)^{m}\end{bmatrix}.

Consider the forward sequence

\frac{1}{{m\choose k-1}}\gamma^{m}.

then every convergent subsequence of this sequence converging to of the form;

g=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\\
&a\,\mathrm{E}_{1,k}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})

where
\displaystyle|a|=1
. Consequently,

\ker(g)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

Similarly, consider the backward sequence

\frac{1}{{m+k-2\choose k-1}}\gamma^{-m}.

Also every subsequence of this sequence converges to of the form;

g=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\\
&a\,\mathrm{E}_{k-1,k}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})

where again
\displaystyle|a|=1
. Hence,

\ker(g)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

Combining both cases, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a parabolic transformation whose lift is given by

\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{J}(\lambda,k)&\\
&\mathrm{J}(\lambda,k)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}),\qquad 2k=n+1.

Then the equicontinuity region of the cyclic group
\displaystyle\Gamma:=\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle
is

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k-1},e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{2k}\}.

Proof.

The
\displaystyle m
-th power of
\displaystyle\gamma
is given by

\gamma^{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{J}(\lambda,k)^{m}&\\
&\mathrm{J}(\lambda,k)^{m}\end{bmatrix}.

Consider the sequence

\frac{1}{{m\choose k-1}}\gamma^{m}.

It admits a subsequence converging to

g=\begin{bmatrix}a\,\mathrm{E}_{1,k}&\\
&a\mathrm{E}_{1,k}\end{bmatrix},

where
\displaystyle|a|=1
. Hence,

\ker(g)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k-1},e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{n+1}\}.

Similarly, for the inverse powers
\displaystyle\gamma^{-m}
, the corresponding normalized sequence

\frac{1}{{m+k-2\choose k-1}}\gamma^{-m}

has a subsequence converging to a matrix of the same form, yielding

\ker(g)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k-1},e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{n+1}\}.

Combining both cases, we conclude that

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k-1},e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{2k-1}\}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a parabolic transformation whose lift is given by

\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{J}(\lambda,k)&\\
&\mathrm{J}(\lambda,l)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}),\qquad k+l=n+1,\;k<l.

Then the equicontinuity region of the cyclic group
\displaystyle\Gamma:=\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle
is

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k-1},e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{n+1}\}.

Proof.

The
\displaystyle m
-th power of
\displaystyle\gamma
is given by

\gamma^{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{J}(\lambda,k)^{m}&\\
&\mathrm{J}(\lambda,l)^{m}\end{bmatrix}.

Consider the sequence

\frac{1}{{m\choose l-1}}\gamma^{m}.

Since
\displaystyle k<l
, the ratio of binomial coefficients satisfies;

\frac{\binom{m}{\,k-1\,}}{\binom{m}{\,l-1\,}}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }m\to\infty.

and

\frac{1}{{m\choose l-1}}\mathrm{J}(\lambda,l)^{m}\longrightarrow a\,\mathrm{E}_{1,l}

for some
\displaystyle|a|=1
, it follows that

\frac{1}{{m\choose l-1}}\gamma^{m}\longrightarrow g=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\\
&a\,\mathrm{E}_{1,l}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})

Hence,

\displaystyle\ker(g)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+l-1}\}.

Similarly, for the inverse powers
\displaystyle\gamma^{-m}
, the sequence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{m+l-2\choose l-1}}\gamma^{-m}

converges to the same limit

g=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\\
&a\,\mathrm{E}_{1,l}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})

and therefore,

\displaystyle\ker(g)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+l-1}\}.

Combining the two cases, we conclude that

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k-1},e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{n+1}\}.

This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Loxodromic Case

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a loxodromic element admitting a lift in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
of the form

\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}(|\lambda_{1}|,k_{1})&&&\\
&\mathrm{D}(|\lambda_{2}|,k_{2})&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\mathrm{D}(|\lambda_{p}|,k_{p})\end{bmatrix},

where
\displaystyle k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{p}=n
and

\displaystyle|\lambda_{1}|<|\lambda_{2}|<\cdots<|\lambda_{p}|
.
Then the equicontinuity region of the cyclic group

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle
is

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{p-1}}\}\,\cup\,L\{e_{k_{1}+1},e_{k_{1}+2},\ldots,e_{n}\},

where
\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(|\lambda_{i}|,k_{i})
denotes a diagonal block of size
\displaystyle k_{i}
with diagonal entries of modulus
\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}|
.

Proof.

The
\displaystyle k
-th power of
\displaystyle\gamma
is given by

\gamma^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}(|\lambda_{1}|^{k},k_{1})&&&\\
&\mathrm{D}(|\lambda_{2}|^{k},k_{2})&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\mathrm{D}(|\lambda_{p}|^{k},k_{p})\end{bmatrix}.

If a subsequence of
\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\lambda_{p}|^{k}}\gamma^{k}
, converges then it must be of the form

\gamma^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&\mathbf{0}&\\
&&&\mathrm{D}(|\alpha|,k_{p})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})

where
\displaystyle|\alpha|=1
. It follows that

\ker(\gamma^{\prime})=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{p-1}}\}.

Similarly, every convergent subsequence of
\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\lambda_{p}|^{-k}}\gamma^{-k}
converges to

\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}(|\alpha|,k_{p})&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&\mathbf{0}&\\
&&&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})

and

\ker(\gamma^{\prime\prime})=L\{e_{k_{1}+1},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

Combining these, we obtain

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{p-1}}\}\,\cup\,L\{e_{k_{1}+1},e_{k_{1}+2},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Equicontinuity regions of cyclic subgroups generated by loxoparabolic element

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be loxoparabolic, and let a lift in

\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
have the block form

\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}\,\mathrm{J}(1,k_{1})&0\\[5.69054pt]
0&\lambda_{2}\,\mathrm{J}(1,k_{2})\end{bmatrix},\qquad 0<|\lambda_{1}|<|\lambda_{2}|,\quad k_{1}+k_{2}=n+1.

Then the equicontinuity region of the cyclic group
\displaystyle\Gamma=\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle
is

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)={L}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}\ \cup\ {L}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k_{1}-1},\,e_{k_{1}+1},\dots,e_{n+1}\}.

Proof.

By taking power of the Jordan block,

\mathrm{J}(1,k)^{m}=\left[\binom{m}{\,j-i\,}\right]_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k},

the maximal entry of
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}(1,k_{2})^{m}
is the
\displaystyle(1,k_{2})
-th entry, which is
\displaystyle\binom{m}{k_{2}-1}
, and of
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}(1,k_{1})^{m}
is
\displaystyle(1,k_{1})
-th entry
\displaystyle\binom{m}{k_{1}-1}
. Since

\gamma^{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{m}\mathrm{J}(1,k_{1})^{m}&0\\[2.84526pt]
0&\lambda_{2}^{m}\mathrm{J}(1,k_{2})^{m}\end{bmatrix}.

Normalize
\displaystyle\gamma^{m}
by
\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{m}\binom{m}{k_{2}-1}
. Because
\displaystyle|\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}|<1
, the upper-left block tends to
\displaystyle 0
, and the lower-right block tends entrywise to
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{1,k_{2}}
. Therefore,

\gamma^{m}\longrightarrow\gamma^{+}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\[2.84526pt]
0&\mathrm{E}_{1,k_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H}),

so
\displaystyle\gamma^{+}\in Lim(\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle)
, with

\ker(\gamma^{+})=L\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}.

Similarly, using

\mathrm{J}(1,k)^{-m}=\left[(-1)^{j-i}\binom{m+j-i-1}{\,j-i\,}\right]_{i\leq j},

the dominant entry of the first block is
\displaystyle(1,k_{1})
. Normalizing
\displaystyle\gamma^{-m}
by
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{-m}\binom{m+k_{1}-2}{k_{1}-1}
gives

\gamma^{-m}\longrightarrow\gamma^{-}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{E}_{1,k_{1}}&0\\[2.84526pt]
0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H}),

so
\displaystyle\gamma^{-}\in Lim(\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle)
, with

\ker(\gamma^{-})={L}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k_{1}-1},e_{k_{1}+1},\dots,e_{n+1}\}.

Therefore the equicontinuity region is the complement of the union of the kernel hyperplanes of the forward and backward pseudo-projective limits:

\mathrm{Eq}(\Gamma)=\ker(\gamma^{+})\cup\ker(\gamma^{-})={L}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}\ \cup\ {L}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k_{1}-1},e_{k_{1}+1},\dots,e_{n+1}\},

as claimed.
∎

Let
