Incidence, timing, and risk factors of severe complications after percutaneous ultrasound-guided tumor biopsy: a proposal for safe aftercare based on 30 years´ experience
Authors: Hauke Weilert, Caspar-Carl Schröder, Peter Wohlmuth, Alexander König, Andreas H Mahnken, Axel Stang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous ultrasound (US)-guided core-needle biopsies (CNBs) is increasingly used to obtain tumor tissue across all anatomic regions to guide personalized cancer treatment; however, data with particular attention on serious adverse events (SAEs) and appropriate observation times to detect clinically-relevant SAEs are limited. We aimed to assess the incidence, influencing factors, and temporal manifestation of SAEs after US-guided CNB of tumors in the thorax and abdomen to identify patient safety measures with a focus on observation time that will allow safe post-biopsy patient discharge.MethodsThis retrospective review of a prospectively collected single-institutional database included 7.494 patients who underwent percutaneous US-guided automated CNBs of 522 thoracic and 6.972 abdominal tumors between 1992 and 2022. The database was reviewed to identify biopsy-related SAEs of Clavien-Dindo (CD) grade ≥ 2, which were classified as acute (< 6 h), subacute (6–24 h), or delayed (> 24 h). CD and CIRSE ≥ 2 scales were compared for counting biopsy-related SAEs.ResultsAmong a total of 29 (0.39%) SAEs CD grade ≥ 2, 28% (8/29) were acute, 62% (18/29) subacute, 10% (3/29) delayed, 90% (26/29) bleeding-related, and 10% (3/29) pneumothorax-related. Treatments were erythrocyte transfusion (12x), surgical bleeding control (10x), transarterial coiling (4x), and chest tube placement (3x). Incidences of ≥ 1% were observed for bleeding after spleen (1.6%) and kidney (1.3%) biopsy (other sites: 0-0.4%) and for pneumothorax after transpleural biopsy (1.6%) of intrapulmonal nodules. Bleeding risk associated significantly with INR, number of needle passes, double antiplatelet medication, and, in liver biopsy, with a hypervascular tumor type (p < 0.05). CIRSE classification added 7 prolonged observational stays without treatment (CIRSE grade 2/3), raising the SAE rate from 0.39% to 0.48%. No patient death occurred.ConclusionsOur results support the safety of the procedure but also highlight potential risks. SAE incidences vary by biopsy site and site-independent factors, and most SAEs occur within 6-24-hours after biopsy, suggesting need for risk-adjusted observation times, either for < 6-hours or 24-hours, to allow safe patient discharge. Further prospective studies are needed to validate our findings and to define reasonable thresholds for low and high SAE risks that could tailor aftercare time in a pragmatic and clinically-relevant sense.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02430-z.Keywords: Percutaneous tumor biopsy, Ultrasound guidance, Abdomen, Thorax, Complication

Background

Ongoing advances in precision oncology lead to greater demand of percutaneous ultrasound (US)-guided core-needle biopsies (CNBs) of tumors across all anatomic regions to identify actionable targets and guide personalized cancer treatment [1, 2]. The procedure is generally considered to be safe but is not without complication risks; large studies on percutaneous image-guided biopsy across multiple biopsy sites indicate risks for morbidity and mortality in the range of 0.3–0.5% and 0.02–0.05%, respectively [3–5]. However, studies assessing the US-guided approach with particular attention on severe adverse events (SAE) and appropriate observation time after US-guided biopsy to allow safe patient discharge are limited.

Meaningful analyses of biopsy-associated SAEs should be based on validated SAE criteria. The well-established surgical Clavien-Dindo (CD) classification focusses on SAEs requiring treatment (CD grade ≥ 2) and relates severity grades to treatment effort [6]. The more recent CIRSE classification, which focusses on quality assurance of interventional procedures, adds SAE categories for unplanned prolonged observational hospital stay and permanent adverse sequelae [7]. Surgical and interventional procedures differ in type of complications, and SAEs counted by CD or CIRSE classification may vary. However, at present, studies comparing biopsy-related SAEs quoted by CD and CIRSE in the same study population are lacking.

In the present study, we assess our experience regarding the incidence, influencing factors, and temporal manifestation of SAEs CD grade ≥ 2 after US-guided, automated 18 Gauge (G) Tru-Cut CNBs of tumors at multiple sites in the thorax and abdomen. We aimed to compare CD and CIRSE ≥ 2 scales for counting biopsy-related SAEs and to identify patient safety measures with a focus on appropriate observation time after biopsy that will allow safe patient discharge.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

A retrospective review of a prospectively maintained institutional database was performed on consecutive patients who underwent percutaneous US-guided automated 18G CNB of tumors in the thorax and abdomen at our tertial referral centre between January 1992 and December 2022 (start of pseudonymized data collection entering into the database: January 1992). The database documented patient demographics, tumor information, procedural data, coagulation parameters, anticoagulation/antiplatelet medication, and post-biopsy SAEs (presentation, timing, management, duration of hospital stay, and outcome). Information on delayed complications after discharge was accrued 7–30 days after biopsy (at appointments to discuss the biopsy result, or via telephone contact) and after 90 days (via telephone contact) to inquire about permanent adverse sequela at follow-up.

Preprocedural management

Prebiopsy laboratory assessments and informed consent were obtained ≤ 7 days prior to biopsy. Standard target parameters included an INR < 1.6 and platelet count > 50 × 109/L. Anticoagulation, single antiplatelet, and double antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) was discontinued 2–7 days before biopsy whenever possible [8–10]. Discontinuation of DAPT was considered in any case dependant on risks of DAPT withdrawal, namely the risk of coronary and cerebral ischemia and peripheral ischemia after stenting, based on consultation with the care team managing the DAPT (e.g., cardiovascular interventionists). DAPT was usually discontinued if consult advice allowed heparin bridging and/or withhold of one antiplatelet agent for 2–5 days or 2–4 doses before the procedure. In very exceptional cases, where consult advice allowed no heparin bridging and/or withhold of one antiplatelet agent (e.g., after acute coronary stenting due to acute myocardial infarction), and if emergent biopsy could not be delayed for clinical reasons (e.g. suspected aggressive, effectively treatable, but untreated prognostically poor malignant lymphoma), DAPT was continued based on multidisciplinary decision.

Biopsy procedure

US-guided biopsy was performed or supervised by five experienced operators (experience level: ≥10 years biopsy experience and ≥ 500 biopsies personally performed, respectively) using an automated biopsy gun device (Bard, Covington, GA, USA), 18G Tru-Cut needles (21 mm specimen notch) and a centrally channelled 3.5-MHz biopsy probe (EUP-B314; device: EUB 6500 or 8500 Hitachi, Japan). After planning the optimal patient position and biopsy pathway to the target lesion, skin disinfection and subcutaneous local anaesthesia (10 ml lidocaine 1%), the 18G Tru-Cut core needle was introduced through the perforated US probe and advanced along the electronically displayed path on the US monitor. If the needle tip reached the target lesion properly, the spring-loaded biopsy gun mechanism was triggered and the needle retrieved. All lesion punctures were performed through a direct access without coaxial needle (non-coaxial technique) and without puncture channel blocking. In anxious and uncooperative patients, midazolam or propofol was given as needed. General anaesthesia was not applied.

Postprocedure management

After bed rest for 2-hours and manual compression of the biopsy site for 10 min, patients were admitted to the hospital for an overnight observation. A 24-hours postprocedure patient observation was implemented as standard aftercare and means of ensuring immediate diagnosis, management and access to hospital ressources in case of life-threatening SAEs, ultimately to prevent progression in fatal outcomes. Patients were instructed to contact the available nursing staff during overnight stay if there was any new discomfort and/or symptom, such as complaints of dizziness, pain, dyspnoea, and cough. In addition, the experienced staff controlled whether patients were in stable clinical condition compared to the baseline. Any new symptom, either indicated by the patients or detected by the staff, was evaluated within 1 h for clinical relevance. Specific diagnostic measures performed (e.g. laboratory testing, imaging) depended on type and severity of symptoms and pathologic vital signs (e.g., tachycardia, hypotension). Diagnosis of SAE implied prompt access to management by medication, transfusion, endoscopic, interventional or surgical procedures, or intensified monitoring. No routine laboratory or imaging assessments were performed in asymptomatic patients except for chest radiographs 2-hours after biopsy of lung tumors. Patients were discharged from the hospital after 24-hours overnight stay after check by the nursing staff that patients were without complaints and that patient’s clinical condition was stable and without deviation compared to the baseline.

SAE identification and database review

For the period from January 1992 to December 2022, two authors independently reviewed the biopsy database to identify CD grade ≥ 2 SAEs (grade 2: blood transfusion; grade 3a: endoscopic or radiological intervention; grade 3b: surgery with general anaesthesia; grade 4: life-threatening, or grade 5: death) [6] and/or a CIRSE grade ≥ 2 SAEs (grade 2/3: observational stay > 24-hours without treatment; grade 4: permanent adverse sequala) [7]. Time from biopsy (0 h) to first SAE symptom was classified as acute (< 6-hours), subacute (6-24-hours), or delayed (> 24-hours). After reaching consensus decision for discrepancies arising from independent reviews, both authors performed a thorough chart review to assess the medical history of all identified CD and CIRSE grade ≥ 2 SAEs for each patient in detail.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as number (percentage), mean (standard deviation), or median (1st/3rd quartile). Due to the small number of events, a multiple parameter model was not used to associate variables with complications. Univariable logistic regression models were applied instead for correlation of demographic data (age, gender), tumor information (localization, size, histology), laboratory data (platelet counts, international normalized ratio [INR]), medication (platelet inhibitors within 7 days or heparin within 48 h prior to biopsy), procedural data (number of needle passes, distance skin-to-lesion) with complication rate. P values, odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported; the effect sizes were presented graphically. Potential risk factors that affected small subgroups (< 10%) of the entire cohort (e.g. anticoagulation) were assessed using chi-square tests. Two-sided p values were calculated for all analyses. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant. Calculations were performed using the statistical analysis software R (R Core Team, 2024).

Results

Study cohort

Figure 1 shows the flow of patient selection. The inclusion criterion for procedure selection from the database was the identification of a percutaneous US-guided biopsy performed between January 1992 and December 2022 at our institution. From a total of 7882 identified procedures, biopsies were then excluded for the following exclusion criteria: biopsy not targeting a solid lesion (n = 225), or repeated biopsies of the same lesion (n = 125). The latter exclusion criterion was applied to avoid clustering effects. For patients who underwent repeated biopsies of the same lesion (e.g., due to non-diagnostic prior biopsy), only the last biopsy was included into the dataset. After these exclusions, the final study cohort included 7494 patients (mean age, 59 years; 46% women) who underwent 7494 biopsies. A minority of 46 (0.6%) patients underwent the procedure under midazolam or propofol sedation. Of the 7494 lesions biopsied, 6972 (93%) were located in the abdomen, and 552 (7%) in the thorax. The 7494 lesions had a mean diameter of 3.67 ± 1.46 (SD) cm; the mean distance from skin to lesion was 4.67 ± 2.47 (SD) cm. It is of note that intrapulmonal lesions were typically located in the subpleural space (mean skin to 189 intrapulmonal lesions: 3.10 ± 0.9 cm). Further, although not directly related to the primary outcomes investigated in this study, it may be of interest that of all 7494 biopsies, 7305 (97.5%) were diagnostic. Out of 7494 patients, 29 patients (0.39%) experienced biopsy-related CD grade ≥ 2 SAEs, comprising 26 bleeding-related SAEs after 6972 abdominal tumor biopsies (0.37%), and 3 pneumothorax-related SAEs after a transpleural approach in 522 thoracic tumor biopsies (0.57%). Incidence and timing of SAEs by biopsy site

The incidence and timing of the 29 CD grade ≥ 2 SAEs is summarized by organ site in Table 3. A ≥ 1% incidence for bleeding was observed after spleen (1.6%) and kidney (1.3%) biopsy (other sites: 0-0.4%) and for pneumothorax after transpleural biopsy (1.6%) of intrapulmonal nodules. Among a total of 29 SAEs, 89.7% (26/29) occurred acute or subacute (≤ 24 h before discharge), and only 10.3% (3/29) delayed (> 24 h after discharge). Subacute SAEs within 6–24 h represented 62.1% (18/29) of SAEs and occurred after 0.24% (18/7494) of biopsies. SAEs presented with unspecific symptoms of hemodynamic instability indicative of bleeding (72%) or dyspnoea and cough (10%) indicative of a symptomatic pneumothorax (Supplementary Table 1). The time interval between earliest symptom suggestive of a SAE and treatment was ≤4 h for the 26 acute/subacute SAEs (during routine overnight observation) and 6–12 h for the 3 delayed SAEs (after patient discharge). Until treatment, none of the 29 patients experienced a worsening clinical condition, and all recovered fully after treatment.

Risk factors associated with SAEs

The univariate regression model outlined needle passes, INR, and for liver tumors a histology of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), neuroendocrine tumor (NET), and hemangioma as significant risk factors for a CD grade ≥ 2 SAE (Fig. 3; Table 4, p < 0.05, respectively). There was no significant association between heparin (410 patients) and aspirin use (135 patients) and post-biopsy bleeding. By contrast, 3 of 6 patients (50%) undergoing biopsy under DAPT experienced CD grade ≥ 2 bleeding complications (Supplementary Table 2, p < 0.05). All 3 pneumothorax-related SAEs requiring chest tube developed after transpleural biopsy (Fig. 2).

CD versus CIRSE classification

Hospital stay was prolonged in 7 patients (2x CIRSE grade 2; 5x CIRSE grade 3) due to 5 hematomas (3x liver, 1x spleen, 1x kidney) and 2 pneumothoraxes which presented 6–24 h after biopsy but were managed conservatively. Counting CD and CIRSE grades ≥ 2 resulted in a 24% increase from 29 to 36 SAEs, raising the overall SAE rate from 0.39% to 0.48%, and 69.4% (25/36) of SAEs managed either by treatment or prolonged observation presented 6–24 h post-biopsy. Notably, there were no SAEs of grade ≥ 4, neither in CD nor in CIRSE classification (Table 5).

Discussion

By analysing 7494 patients who underwent percutaneous US-guided CNB of 522 thoracic and 6972 abdominal tumors, we found 29 SAEs with CD grade ≥ 2 (0.39%), encompassing 26 abdominal bleeding events (0.37%) and 3 pneumothoraxes after transpleural biopsy (0.57%). Previous studies on image-guided tumor biopsy that included, like ours, multiple body sites reported rates for major complications and death of 0.3–0.5% and 0.02–0.05%, respectively [3–5]. The overall incidence of 0.39% SAEs in our US-guided approach is consistent with these reported data, but it is of note that we observed no life-threatening SAEs (CD grade 4) or biopsy-related deaths (CD grade 5).

A total of 89% SAEs (26/29) occurred within the first 24-hours, predominantly subacute within 6-24-hours after biopsy. Accordingly, SAEs occur more often delayed than expected, but > 24-hours observation contributes little to capturing SAEs. Further, all SAEs were detected by new symptoms after biopsy, supporting that routine US or laboratory testing is dispensable in asymptomatic patients. Many guidelines recommend to perform image-guided tumor biopsy as day-case procedure with a short 1-6-hours post-biopsy aftercare [11, 12]. However, if we extrapolate our SAE data on the temporal manifestation, one might expect a rate of 0.2% (half of ~ 0.4%) subacute SAEs within 6-24-hours after biopsy. Our standard practice of overnight observation ensured timely management of subacute SAEs within the 6-24-hour time period, which might have prevented fatal outcomes in one of 500 patients in our study population.

Bleeding-related SAEs requiring treatment represented 89% (26/29) of SAEs. Breaking them down by biopsy site, higher (≥ 1%) incidences were found after spleen (1.6%) and kidney (1.3%) biopsy compared to abdominal masses (0.4%) and the liver (0.3%). There were no bleeding-related SAEs involving other target sites. The risk distribution and ranking compares with large studies reporting on major haemorrhages across multiple abdominal biopsy sites, noting that spleen and kidney biopsies constitute a high-risk procedure [3–5]. Further, our site-specific bleeding-related SAE rates range within those quoted in large study populations, especially regarding the spleen (1.0-3.2%), kidney (1.1–4.3%), and the liver (0.1–4.6%) [3, 5, 13–19]. Notably, reflecting clinical practice, most of our tumor biopsies (71%) involve the liver, and HCC, metastatic NET, and hemangioma were indicative for increased post-biopsy bleeding. As the tumor types represent hypervascular liver lesions, future studies may evaluate whether lesion vascularity on imaging impacts on bleeding complications following liver tumor biopsy.

Beyond varying incidences by biopsy site, bleeding-related SAEs associated significantly with increased needle passes, elevated INR, DAPT, but not with aspirin or heparin use. These findings are supported by the literature [3, 5, 12, 18, 19] and validate guidelines for pre-biopsy coagulation/anticoagulation management [8–10]. However, they reflect cases needing to obtain tumor tissue despite deviation from normally accepted standards. In these cases, the best possible correction of clotting anomalies, discontinuation of anticoagulation medication in higher (≥ 1%) SAE risk biopsies (e.g. spleen, kidney, or multiple needle passes needed), and consult advice on pre-procedural heparin bridging alternatives for nonavoidable “highest-risk-bleeding-biopsies” in patients on DAPT (e.g. after coronary stenting) seems advisable.

A total of 11% (3/26) nonbleeding-related SAEs were pneumothoraxes requiring chest tube after transpleural biopsy of intrapulmonal nodules. No complications occurred after mediastinal and subpleural biopsies, suggesting that thoracic complications were related to the crossing of two separate pleural interfaces [20–22]. Our rate (1.6%) of pneumothoraxes requiring chest tube after US-guided 18G lung biopsy (3/189) is lower than those reported after CT-guided 18G lung biopsy (1%-14%) [20]. This difference is likely due to selection bias, as US limitations imply to target only easily accessible lung lesions in the subpleural space. Lung lesions are more commonly biopsied under CT-guidance, which involves to target more difficult and deep located lung lesions and carries a higher risk of post-biopsy pneumothorax [20–22].

CIRSE classification added 7 prolonged observational stays managed conservatively, or a 24% increase from 29 to 36 SAEs, raising the overall SAE rate from 0.39% to 0.48%. Thus, if we compare complication data across studies or procedures, we should be aware of the classification they are based on. However, the SAEs counted by CD and CIRSE include similar types (bleeding, pneumothorax), similar affected sites (liver, spleen, kidney, lung), and both classifications underline that more than half of SAEs occurred subacute 6-24-hours after biopsy. Notably, neither in CD nor in CIRSE classification, we found SAEs of grade 4 or 5 (life-threatening SAE or SAE-related death), which is likely attributable to the timely management of 26 of 29 SAEs (89%) occurring during routine post-biopsy overnight stay.

In reviewing our experience, a few peculiarities appear, which not the norm are across most studies and practices. Our biopsy technique and 24-hours aftercare has remained unchanged for 30 years for several reasons. First, regular quality controls showed no problems regarding diagnostic yield or patient safety. Second, there were no complaints from referring physicians or clinical oncologists regarding biopsy information. In particular, this did not change in the era of precision oncology, with higher demands on the quality of sample collections and a higher number of biopsies. Altogether, this gave us no reason to change our standard procedure. As an advantage, differences in imaging or biopsy techniques or uncertainties regarding complete coverage of clinically relevant SAEs within the first 24-hours may not confound the conclusions drawn with relevance to patient care. For clinical practice, we propose to differentiate low-risk (< 1%) and high-risk (≥ 1%) patients for experiencing SAE CD grade ≥ 2, which assigns patients to a risk-adapted observation after biopsy, either for < 6-hours or 24-hours. In our experience, a SAE risk threshold of ≥ 1% for overnight hospital stay would offer ≥ 1% subacute SAE in 200 biopsies immediate access to hospital resources.

Our study has limitations. First, there is nonavoidable selection bias given our retrospective, single-centre study design. Further, some selection bias may have been introduced as only visible and targetable lesions on US underwent the procedure. Target lesion visibility, location, biopsy path lengths, and imaging modality features affect complication rates, for example the pneumothorax rates after US- or CT-guided lung biopsies. Second, our results have been produced by experienced operators, which can affect the generalizability of results. However, experienced operators are often assigned more difficult cases, resulting in potentially higher SAE rates due to inherent obstacles by the case, which may outweigh effects on SAEs by lower operator experience level. Third, some information was not available in the database. This limited us to provide more detailed analyses on combinatorial site-specific risks such as liver cirrhosis in HCC, emphysema in transpleural biopsy, or lesion vascularity in organ biopsy. Fourth, due to historically limited availability of interventional radiology resources, surgical bleeding control predominates in our series. At present, most cases, especially those with coagulation problems, can be managed by arterial embolization. However, regardless of the method used, key to success remains quick access to resources that ensure timely treatment of SAEs. Finally, despite our large sample size, statistical assessments are limited by the small number of SAEs. In this context, we applied univariable logistic regression models to correlate variables with SAEs, as single-digit figures in subgroups would lead to overspecification in multiple parameter models.

Conclusions

By analysing 7494 cases of percutaneous US-guided 18G CNB of intraabdominal and thoracic tumors, we found an incidence of SAEs CD grade ≥ 2 of 0.39%. This result supports the safety of the procedure, but it also highlights potential risks. The majority of SAEs occurred within 6-24-hours after biopsy, and that some target sites, namely the spleen, kidney and intrapulmonal lesions, and coagulation/anticoagulation deviations, namely an elevated INR and DAPT, were associated with an increased (≥ 1%) SAE risk. For patient care, our experience supports that clinical awareness for new symptoms after biopsy and rapid access to hospital ressources may prevent morbidity and/or mortality. Further, our findings suggest that biopsied patients need different risk-adjusted aftercare times, either for < 6-hours or 24-hours. In clinical practice, a low-risk patient may qualify for a safe same-day discharge, whereas higher-risk patients may benefit from extended observation time with immediate management in case of delayed life-threatening SAEs. Further prospective multicentre studies are needed to validate our findings, and to generate sufficient evidence to define reasonable thresholds for low and high SAE risks that could tailor aftercare times in a pragmatic and clinically-relevant sense.
