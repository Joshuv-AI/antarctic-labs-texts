Serum metabolomics reveals distinctive signature of cancer staging in oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Wen Lu, Linjuan Liu, Jianhong Li, Haoyue Yang, Yongqin Yang, Qingang Hu, Xiaowei Song, Liu Liu, Xihu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most common form of head and neck cancer. It is of great significance to search for biomarkers associated with OSCC progression through minimally invasive methods.MethodsConductive polymer spray ionization mass spectrometry (CPSI-MS) was used to perform a rapid metabolic analysis of trace volume serum from OSCC and healthy controls. Key metabolites were identified by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and further validated by desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (DESI-MSI) and ultra–high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometer (UHPLC-MS/MS).ResultsThe study identified 125 metabolites that significantly differentiated early-stage OSCC from healthy controls. 47 metabolites showed an upward or downward trend in the OSCC staging (I-IV). Through ROC curve analysis and trend assessment of metabolite levels, 10 key metabolites associated with OSCC staging were pinpointed. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that glycerophospholipid metabolism is closely related to OSCC staging. Representative serum metabolites were validated by DESI-MS and UHPLC-MS/MS at tissue level. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis showed that PC (40:6) was an independent prognostic factor for OSCC.ConclusionWe identified 10 metabolites associated with OSCC staging, and found that glycerophospholipid metabolism may promote the progression of OSCC. Moreover, the key metabolite PC(40:6) is closely related to the prognosis of OSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07210-9.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most prevalent malignancy among head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC), comprising approximately 90% of all head and neck cancer cases [1]. Over 300,000 new OSCC cases are diagnosed globally each year, with this figure projected to rise annually [2]. Despite significant advancements in OSCC treatment modalities, including surgical intervention, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [3], the 5-year overall survival rate for OSCC has remained stagnant at approximately 50–60% in recent years [4]. Early diagnosis and timely intervention are crucial for optimizing clinical outcomes [5]. However, approximately half of all OSCC cases globally are identified at an advanced stage [6], underscoring the critical need for early detection methods and reliable biomarkers [7]. In addition, the molecular mechanisms that promote the clinical progression of OSCC have not been fully explored, and it is urgent to find reliable biomarkers associated with the clinical progression of OSCC to achieve risk stratification assessment of cancer patients.

Currently, the gold standard for diagnosing OSCC is still tissue biopsy and histological examination [8]. However, this approach has several limitations. The collection of tissue specimens is invasive, costly, and time-consuming, and biopsy samples may not be fully representative of the entire tumor [9]. In such scenarios, liquid biopsy emerges as a promising alternative. It is minimally invasive, easily collected, and capable of real-time monitoring [10]. The pathogenesis of OSCC is highly complex, encompassing not only alterations in gene and protein expression but also dynamic metabolic shifts [11]. Accumulating evidence indicates that the malignant progression of OSCC and changes in the tumor microenvironment are characterized by dysregulation of metabolic homeostasis [12, 13]. In addition to aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) and changes in amino acid metabolism [14], tumor cells also engage in lipid uptake, synthesis, and fatty acid oxidation to sustain their continuous proliferation [15].

Metabolomics has emerged as a critical approach for identifying metabolic alterations, uncovering novel metabolic biomarkers, and elucidating potential therapeutic targets across a range of diseases, by effectively detecting metabolites influenced by tumor progression in biological samples [16–18]. Unlike other omics approaches, metabolomics provides a direct assessment of phenotypes through the identification and quantification of metabolites and their concentrations [19]. Ambient ionization mass spectrometry (AIMS), a potentially advantageous mass spectrometry method in metabolomics, facilitates the identification of underutilized molecules as diagnostic biomarkers by directly detecting small molecules (including lipids and other metabolites) in the atmospheric environment [20]. Conductive polymer spray ionization mass spectrometry (CPSI-MS), a prominent AIMS technique, has been advanced for detecting various biological samples due to its advantages of minimal sample requirements, simplified pretreatment procedures, and rapid detection times. This method has demonstrated significant progress in clinical diagnosis [21–23].

We hypothesize that changes in energy metabolism will intensify as the disease progresses, leading to alterations in metabolite levels from healthy controls to OSCC (stage I-IV) patients. This study focuses on two issues: (1) whether metabolites exhibiting significant differences between healthy controls and OSCC patients can be identified; and (2) whether these differentially expressed metabolites in serum can not only serve to distinguish OSCC from HC, but also enable to differentiate OSCC at different stages (I-IV stages). Consequently, this study aims to identify metabolic markers associated with OSCC staging by CPSI-MS. The objective is to screen for reliable molecular metabolic markers that can assist in predicting the onset, development, and prognosis of OSCC, thereby facilitating early disease detection and supporting informed treatment decisions.

Methods

Study population

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Stomatology Hospital (2017NL-013(KS)) and Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University and adhered to the Helsinki Declaration. Serum samples from 254 patients were collected, cases of OSCC were officially diagnosed through histopathological examination and classified into four stages (I-IV), and healthy controls were visiting patients whose physical examination results are basically normal. Cancer staging (I-IV) are based on the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer Staging Manual [24]. OSCC patients who had previously undergone anti-cancer treatment were excluded from the study. Individuals with decompensated metabolic or systemic diseases, as well as those with a history of other malignant tumors, were also excluded. Inclusion criteria for the control group required participants to be in good general health, with no oral lesions, no chronic diseases, no history of major illnesses in the past, and no history of smoking. Additionally, participants were required to have refrained from taking any medications or nutritional supplements for at least 14 days prior to the examination. The CPSI-MS/ML procedure is similar to the reporting recommendations for tumor marker prognostic studies (REMAKRS).

Sample collection and pretreatment

All blood samples were collected on an empty stomach in the morning, and no drug administration was recorded within 24 h. Blood samples were drawn using BD Vacutainer EDTA tubes and treated for plasma separation as follows: The blood was centrifuged at 2000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. Collect upper-level serum samples. Serum samples are frozen at −80 °C for long-term storage until use. Before the formal CPSI-MS analysis, the frozen serum samples were thawed under room temperature and centrifuged again to precipitate the denatured proteins. Another 18 paired cases (each case including tumor and normal tissues) were also enrolled in this study for desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (DESI-MSI) and ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography - tandem mass spectrometer (UHPLC-MS/MS) targeted quantitative analysis. All fresh tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 30 min after operation and stored at −80 °C freezer until they were processed.

CPSI-MS data acquisition

A detailed overview of the CPSI-MS instrumentation is available in a previous publication [23]. The conductive polymer tip was composed of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) introduced by carbon nanotubes (CNTs). 3 µL of serum thawing at room temperature and 1 µL of 4-chloro-phenylalanine (internal standard, IS) were loaded onto the tip of the conductive polymer to form dry serum spots. Methanol water (7:3,v/v, 3 µL) was added as the spraying solvent, and a high voltage of 4.5 kV was applied to trigger the ionization process to extract the components. Subsequently, the charged microdroplet spray transported the dissolved metabolites into the LTQ Orbitrap Velos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) for further analysis. Set the full scan range to m/z 50–1000 for monitoring metabolomic characteristics. The temperature of the mass spectrometry capillary was adjusted to 275℃, and the voltage of the S-lens was calibrated to 55 V. Each sampling data acquisition cycle lasted 15 s. The intensities of metabolite ions were normalized, and serum samples were pooled to prepare quality control (QC) samples, which were used to monitor the stability and performance of the CPSI-MS system [25].

DESI-MSI data acquisition

Eight complete OSCC tissues (including normal areas and cancer areas) were collected during the operation to verify serum candidate metabolites. Parallel 12 μm frozen sections were prepared for each tissue sample. Two adjacent parallel sections were subjected to H&E staining and DESI-MSI analysis. A detailed overview of the DESI-MS instrumentation is available in a previous publication [26]. The tissue sections were scanned and imitated by the DESI system (Prosolia, Indianapolis, U.S). In this experiment, under-4 kV high voltage and 1.0 MPa nebuliser gas pressure, N, N-dimethyl formamideacetonitrile (1:1, v/v) was delivered as the DESI-MSI spray solvent at a flow rate of 2 µL/min. Scan the entire frozen section of the tissue line by line using a DESI sprayer. Set the total scanning speed (X-axis direction) to 300 mm/second and the line spacing (Y-direction) to 300 mm. Massimager (Chemmind Technologies Co., Ltd, China) was used to achieve the reconstruction of target ion images and discover the images of target metabolites.

Metabolomics data processing

The raw files were initially converted to CDF format using Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) and subsequently imported into MATLAB 2020a (MathWorks, USA) for further data processing. Each sample’s average mass spectrum was constructed from 10 continuous scans, with a mass tolerance of ± 0.005 m/z for peak intensity extraction. Peaks with intensities below a specified threshold (500 or 1E2) were discarded as background noise. The missing values were substituted with half of the minimum value found in the non-missing data. This matrix underwent IS normalization, natural log transform, zero-centering, and unit variance scaling before applying univariate analysis, multivariate analysis, and machine learning modeling.

Metabolite annotation and pathway enrichment analysis

The identification of metabolite ions was performed by querying the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, http://hmdb.ca) and Metlin database (https://metlin.scripps.edu), employing a mass tolerance of 5.0 ppm. The analysis specifically targeted adduct ions in positive ion mode, such as [M + H]+, [M + Na]+, [M + K]+, [M-H2O + H]+, [M + 2Na-H]+, [M + 2 K-H]+, and [M + NH4]+. In addition to evaluating exact m/z values, isotope peak distributions were analyzed to refine the list of candidate metabolites, with emphasis on compounds documented to be present in humans. MS/MS experiments were conducted to compare collision-induced dissociation (CID) fragmentation patterns against known standards, and spectra from HMDB and Metlin were recorded for unknown ions exhibiting significant changes. The significantly altered metabolites of interest were subsequently analyzed using the open-source platform, MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca), to identify the dysregulated metabolic pathways associated with them.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

The UHPLC-MS/MS system consists of a set of UHPLC System (Agilent Technologies) and a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column and electrospray ionization (ESI) interface source. Prepare water containing 0.1% formic acid as the aqueous phase and acetonitrile as the organic phase. The temperature was controlled at 40° C. The analytical run time was 4 min with a flow rate at 0.3 mL/min and the injection volume was 2 µL. MRM data acquisition and processing were accomplished by the Agilent MassHunter workstation software (B.08.00, Agilent Technologies).

Statistical analysis

The MetaboAnalystR package (version 2.0) [27] was used to conduct statistical analysis on 100 samples of healthy individuals and 154 samples of oral cancer metabolomics. Compounds with missing values exceeding 50% were removed, and the missing values were filled in using the minimum value method, that is, half of the minimum positive value in the original dataset. Use Autoscaling for data scaling. Standardize the data using the MetaboAnalystR package with Auto scaling. The student’s t-test was used for screening differential metabolites. The differential metabolites were screened with Fold change >1.5 or Fold change < 0.667 and p-value < 0.05. The error discovery rate (FDR) was estimated using the Benjamini and Hochberg methods, and the P-value was adjusted to evaluate statistical significance. For multivariate analysis, SIMCA-P (Umetrics, Umea, Sweden) was used for partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) of metabolic profiles. Statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.5, R version 3.6.0 (available at https://www.r-project.org/), and IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0. Student’s t-test was used to compare the means between two groups, whereas ANOVA test was used to compare the means among three or more groups. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Survival analysis

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause. Surviving patients were censored at the last known follow-up date. The prognostic value of PC(40:6) and other clinicopathological factors was evaluated using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.

Reprogrammed serum metabolic landscape in OSCC patients

To characterize plasma metabolic reprogramming in OSCC, we performed a metabolomic analysis of patients at different stages (I-IV) of OSCC. Specifically, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) distinguished the different stages of OSCC, indicating metabolic remodeling during OSCC progression (Fig. 2A). A total of 125 metabolites exhibited statistically significant differences between early-stage OSCC and HC (T-test, p < 0.05, fold change > 1.5 or < 0.667), including lipids, amino acids, organic acids, nucleotides, vitamins, amines, and carbohydrates (Table S1). Then 47 metabolites showed an upward or downward trend in the OSCC staging (I-IV) (Fig. 2B, Table S2). After overlapping two batches of metabolites, it was confirmed that 18 metabolites were not only statistically significant but also up-regulated or down-regulated (Fig. 2C). In addition, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of 47 increasing and decreasing differential metabolites revealed changes in several metabolic pathways, mainly including glycerophospholipid metabolism and arginine and proline metabolism (Fig. 2D).

The key differential metabolites associated with OSCC staging

To effectively differentiate OSCC at different stages from healthy controls, we conducted a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis on 18 characteristic metabolites. Ultimately, we identified 10 metabolites that exhibited area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.6 (Table 2). With the increase of OSCC staging, we observed two distinct trends among the 10 metabolites: levels of PG(36:2), PE(P-38:3), LPC(20:4), PC(40:6), PC(34:2), and PC(16:0/18:1) increased, while levels of Cer(44:1), proline, galactaric acid, and 1,5-anhydroglucitol (1,5-AG) decreased (Fig. 3, Fig. S1).

Validation of representative serum metabolite associated with OSCC staging

In order to further validate the reliability of CPSI-MS technology, we conducted tissue-level verification on representative differential metabolites identified from serum samples. Frozen sections from 8 OSCC tumor tissues were prepared for desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (DESI-MSI) analysis. The spatial distribution of the target metabolites within the excised OSCC tissues was mapped and compared. In situ validation of DESI-MSI revealed that proline was the differential metabolite shared between tissue and serum (Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, quantitative analysis of proline in 10 pairs of OSCC tissue samples was conducted using UHPLC-MS/MS, revealing that the expression level of proline in OSCC tumor tissues was significantly higher than in normal tissues (Fig. 4C). The quantitative verification of other amino acid metabolites is shown in Fig. S2.

Prognostic value of serum metabolites for OSCC

In order to further investigate the prognostic value of serum markers for OSCC, survival analysis was conducted to evaluate the prognostic significance of characteristic metabolites. The results showed that the overall survival (OS) rate of the PC (40:6) high-expression group was significantly higher than that of the PC (40:6) low-expression group (Fig. 5A). In addition, univariate Cox regression analysis was used to analyze the risk factors related to the survival of OSCC patients. The results showed that low expression of PC (40:6), TNM stage and lymph node metastasis (LNM) were risk factors for OS in patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). After adjusting for potential confounding factors through multivariate Cox analysis, LNM remained an independent prognostic factor for OS in patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5C). Based on these results, the elevated expression of PC(40:6) demonstrates a protective effect, suggesting its potential clinical value as a biomarker for favorable prognosis in OSCC.

Discussion

OSCC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, leading to diminished survival rates [4]. Understanding the complex mechanism of the progression of OSCC is crucial for enhancing our understanding of its pathogenesis. In recent years, several omics studies exploring OSCC characteristics have been reported, primarily focusing on the potential of DNA, RNA, and proteins as diagnostic indicators [28, 29]. Metabolic reprogramming, as an important feature of OSCC, cannot be ignored. Different from previous metabolomics studies of OSCC [11, 30, 31], our work mainly focuses on the metabolites related to OSCC staging and explores their prognostic value.

As a non-invasive/minimally invasive diagnostic technique, liquid biopsy can supplement information captured by tissue biopsies that cannot be continuously monitored. Cancer is detected and monitored by analyzing specific biomarkers present in body fluids (such as blood, urine or cerebrospinal fluid) and provides valuable information about the presence of cancer, its genetic characteristics [32]. CPSI-MS provides significant advantages in the fluid diagnosis of OSCC by enabling the rapid acquisition of high-dimensional metabolomics data from individual cases in just seconds, which meets the practical needs for large-scale clinical screening. Furthermore, CPSI-MS offers comprehensive coverage of multiple metabolite types, which not only helps identify potential biomarkers but also provides valuable insights into the metabolic pathways involved in tumorigenesis. Therefore, we aim to explore the correlation between serum metabolite alterations and the progression of OSCC using CPSI-MS, and to clarify the relationship between specific metabolic pathways and disease deterioration.

Dysregulation in lipid metabolism is among the most prominent metabolic alterations in cancer, tumor cells use lipid metabolism to obtain energy for proliferation, invasion and metastasis [33]. In this study, we observed that 10 metabolites changed with cancer staging and presented two changing trends. The majority of candidate metabolites belonged to lipids, and glycerophospholipids were identified as the most abundant lipid. PC and PE account for more than 50% of the total phospholipid species in eukaryotic membranes and play an important role in the structure and function of these membranes [34]. A lipid analysis study on OSCC found that the PCs and PEs levels of OSCC patients were lower compared with the control group [35], which was consistent with our results. However, with the deepening of the research, we found that three types of PCs (PC (16:0/18:1), PC (34:2), and PC (40:6) gradually increased with the progression of OSCC. It has been reported that PC (34:2) levels increase in colorectal cancer, gastric cancer and pancreatic cancer, and show an upward trend as the disease progresses [36]. Furthermore, as a key enzyme in the process of PC synthesis, CK-α is overexpressed in most cancers and is associated with the progression, invasion and metastasis of cancer [37–39]. This also verifies our discovery that PC expression increases with the progression of cancer. Phospholipids are the basic components of cell membranes. Changes in their composition (such as the ratio of PC to PE) can alter the viscosity and fluidity of the plasma membrane [40]. Membrane fluidity can directly affect the movement and behavior of tumor cells at different steps of metastasis and dissemination [41]. This might support the role that PC(40:6) plays in prognosis. PE is the second most abundant phospholipid in cell membranes and is crucial for the growth and stability of energy-generating organelles. Recent evidence indicates that plasmalogen phospholipids are particularly sensitive to oxidation and may possess antioxidative properties. A serum lipidomics study found that pPE in the serum of OSCC patients was lower compared with the control group, and suggested that most cancer cells may exhibit elevated oxidative stress [42]. This supports our finding that the expression of PE plasmalogen species (38:3) shows a downward trend in cancer patients.

The rapid proliferation of tumor cells necessitates a substantial supply of amino acids, leading to an aberrant amino acid metabolism, which is a critical manifestation of metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells. High intracellular proline levels are associated with the development of histological and clinical malignancies [43], and can protect cancer cells from oxidative stress, resulting in reduced uptake and production of reactive oxygen species [44]. This supports our finding that proline is highly expressed in cancer patients. In addition, proline catabolism also supports cancer cells’ survival and metastasis [45], which explains the down-regulation of proline expression in our results as OSCC progresses. 1,5-anhydroglucitol (1,5-AG) is a biomarker of glucose peak and has been reported to be associated with cancer mortality [46]. Meanwhile, 1,5-AG has also been reported to promote the development of precursor B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (pre-B ALL) cells by promoting glycolysis to activate the reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MAPK/ERK) pathway [47]. This supports our discovery of elevated 1,5-AG levels in cancer tissues and verifies that OSCC is involved in the process of glycolysis. Galactaric acid is the oxidation product of galactose and is mainly catalyzed by galactose dehydrogenase (GalDH). Galactose mainly enters energy metabolism in the human body through the Leloir metabolic pathway and participates in the development of cancer through glycolysis. Multiple pieces of evidence indicate that galactose kinase (GALK1), the main enzyme of galactose metabolism, is highly expressed in various cancers and is associated with poor prognosis [48, 49]. This also supports our finding of increased galactose and Galactaric acid levels in cancer cases.

LPC is a product of the phosphatidylcholine hydrolysis precipitated by phospholipase A activity; in tumor tissue, LPC is usually generated by saturated PC after the accumulation of liposomes [50]. It is reported that LPC is reduced in a variety of cancers, including colorectal cancer, lung cancer and esophageal cancer, and this reduction is associated with tumor progression [51–53]. Meanwhile, the plasma LPC level in patients with advanced metastatic cancer is decreased, which may be related to cell proliferation, migration and angiogenesis [54, 55]. Ceramide, as an important component of sphingolipids, its metabolic imbalance is closely related to the occurrence, development, treatment resistance and metastasis of cancer. At present, research on ceramides mainly focuses on C16 (highly expressed in cancer) and C18 (lowly expressed in cancer), and has discovered its bidirectional effects - it can not only promote apoptosis to achieve cancer suppression, but also support tumor survival through metabolic reprogramming [56, 57]. In our research, Cer(44:1) was expressed at a lower level in cancer patients and showed a continuous downward trend as the disease progressed. At present, there are no reports on ceramide C44, and further exploration of its mechanism is needed in the future.

The limitations of our research should also be pointed out. Owing to the difficulty in quantitative analysis of lipid metabolites, we were unable to perform comprehensive quantitative validation of the screened metabolites. Although CPSI-MS satisfies the requirements for timely detection, it also exhibits low metabolite abundance values due to an insufficient sample size for detection. Due to the uniformity of the race (for Chinese patients), the research results may lack universality. Potential confounding factors (such as the patient’s dietary habits) could not be further ruled out. Meanwhile, due to the limitation of sample size, our study lacks an external independent validation cohort. In subsequent studies, we plan to expand the sample collection, conduct further quantitative verification of the characteristic metabolites identified in this study, and simultaneously explore the underlying mechanisms of the representative metabolic pathways that were screened.

Collectively, we discovered the serum metabolic changes of OSCC patients at different stages, screened out metabolites related to OSCC progression, and ultimately discovered the prognostic markers. These results help to supplement the clinical progression information of OSCC and provide useful information for future cancer progression monitoring and treatment.
