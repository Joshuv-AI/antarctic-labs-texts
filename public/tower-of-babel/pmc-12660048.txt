Blunt Pancreatic Transection With Ductal Disruption in a Stable Patient: Rethinking Surgical Necessity Through a Case‐Based Review
Authors: Mehdi Didi, Belkacem Chagar, Ayoub Belhadj, Younes Aissaoui, Youssef Qamouss, Issam Serghini, Said Khallikane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBlunt pancreatic trauma is uncommon and difficult to diagnose early due to its retroperitoneal location and vague symptoms. While high‐grade injuries with ductal disruption often require surgery, selected hemodynamically stable cases may benefit from conservative management. We describe a 20‐year‐old woman who sustained a Grade III pancreatic transection with suspected ductal injury following blunt abdominal trauma from a horse kick. Despite a delayed diagnosis, she remained stable. Imaging with MRI and contrast‐enhanced CT confirmed complete distal pancreatic transection and a nonbleeding splenic contusion. A multidisciplinary team opted for nonoperative management, including antibiotics, bowel rest, parenteral nutrition, and close monitoring. The patient recovered without complications or need for surgery. Follow‐up imaging confirmed resolution without pseudocyst or abscess formation. This case supports the safety and effectiveness of conservative management in select high‐grade pancreatic injuries with ductal disruption, emphasizing the importance of early imaging, multidisciplinary coordination, and tailored decision‐making, especially in resource‐limited settings.Keywords: blunt abdominal injury, conservative treatment, emergency medicine, grade III pancreatic transection, horse kick trauma, multidisciplinary care, nonoperative management, pancreatic duct injury, pancreatic trauma, trauma imaging

Introduction

Pancreatic injuries are among the least common traumatic abdominal injuries, accounting for less than 5% of cases involving solid organ damage [1]. Due to its retroperitoneal location, the pancreas is often shielded from direct trauma, and early clinical signs may be subtle or nonspecific, leading to delayed diagnosis and increased risk of complications [2]. The diagnostic challenge is compounded by the frequent association of pancreatic trauma with other intra‐abdominal injuries, which can obscure clinical presentation and contribute to the organ's high morbidity and mortality profile. Blunt abdominal trauma—most often from motor vehicle collisions, bicycle handlebar injuries, or falls—is the predominant mechanism [3].

Over the past decade, evolving evidence from case series and trauma registries in high‐volume centers has advanced consensus on management strategies, yet significant variability persists, particularly regarding the role of nonoperative treatment in higher‐grade injuries [4]. This case report presents a rare and instructive scenario of an isolated Grade III pancreatic ductal injury in a young woman following a horse kick to the abdomen—an uncommon trauma mechanism rarely documented in the literature.

Notably, diagnosis was delayed for six days, yet the patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout. Instead of immediate surgical intervention, a conservative management strategy was adopted, consisting of intensive monitoring, bowel rest, and empirical antibiotics. The patient's favorable outcome supports the viability of nonoperative management in selected cases of high‐grade pancreatic trauma. This case is both clinically and academically significant—it challenges existing assumptions about the mandatory need for surgery in Grade III injuries and underscores the value of patient‐centered, context‐aware decision‐making in trauma care.

Case History and Examination

A 20‐year‐old previously healthy woman was referred to our tertiary care center five days after being initially hospitalized in a provincial facility in Essaouira. She had sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a fall from a horse, during which she received a direct kick to the left upper abdomen. At the time of referral, the patient exhibited signs of sepsis and severe abdominal pain with guarding. She was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for further evaluation and management.

Upon ICU admission, she was alert, hemodynamically stable, and had no respiratory or neurological compromise. Physical examination revealed marked tenderness in the left hypochondrium without abdominal distension, palpable mass, or rectal bleeding. Laboratory findings were notable for leukocytosis (WBC: 26,020/μL), slightly elevated serum lipase (54.62 U/L), and normal transaminases (AST: 35 U/L; ALT: 28 U/L). Liver function, electrolytes, and coagulation profiles were within normal limits (Table 1).

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Initial differential diagnoses included splenic injury, pancreatic trauma, bowel perforation, and retroperitoneal hematoma. Abdominal ultrasound detected free peritoneal fluid and a heterogeneous pancreatic head and isthmus but was limited by gas interference. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed fluid in all four quadrants, a pelvic collection in the Douglas pouch, and a grade II splenic contusion. (Figure 1).

MRI showing Grade II splenic injury with hemoperitoneum indicating splenic contusion (red arrow).

On day 6, a contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT scan showed a complete fracture of the pancreatic tail (2.53 × 3.59 cm), consistent with a Grade III injury per the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) classification. A disruption of the main pancreatic duct (Wirsung) was strongly suspected based on parenchymal discontinuity, devascularization, and adjacent fluid collections. The scan also confirmed a grade III splenic laceration with a subcapsular hematoma, without active bleeding (Figure 2). A second MRI corroborated the complete pancreatic tail transection with necrosis and suspected ductal injury, along with the splenic contusion (Figure 3).

Axial CT images showing pancreatic injury with complete tail detachment (blue arrow).

Coronal (A) and axial (B) MRI views demonstrating Grade III pancreatic injury with complete tail detachment, distal transection, suspected Wirsung duct damage, and associated Grade III splenic contusion.

Although MRCP and ERCP are ideal modalities for confirming pancreatic duct disruption, neither was performed due to the patient's clinical stability and multidisciplinary consensus. Given the absence of surgical indications, MRCP was deferred, with agreement that it would be reconsidered if clinical deterioration occurred. Additionally, financial constraints related to the patient's European travel insurance—which showed reluctance to approve high‐cost imaging—contributed to this decision. The presumptive nature of the ductal injury is acknowledged.

Despite the high‐grade pancreatic trauma, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and free of peritonitis or significant hyperamylasemia. A multidisciplinary team—including intensivists, radiologists, and surgeons—opted for conservative management.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient demonstrated steady clinical improvement under structured, multidisciplinary conservative management. Inflammatory markers normalized, abdominal pain resolved, and no signs of local complications—such as pseudocyst, necrosis, or abscess—were observed on serial examinations. A contrast‐enhanced CT scan performed prior to discharge confirmed stable post‐traumatic findings without evidence of progression or secondary pathology. After a 12‐day ICU stay, she was transitioned to the general surgical ward and discharged home in good condition.

Nonoperative management was selected based on established clinical criteria, including sustained hemodynamic stability, absence of peritonitis or enzymatic leakage, and preserved pancreatic function. These conditions align with current trauma guidelines, which support conservative strategies in carefully selected patients with high‐grade pancreatic injuries.

A key limitation of this case is the absence of structured long‐term follow‐up. The patient, a foreign national visiting temporarily, returned to her country of residence shortly after discharge. Despite multiple outreach attempts using the contact details provided, no postdischarge updates could be obtained. Consequently, potential late sequelae—such as endocrine or exocrine insufficiency—could not be formally assessed. This limitation, while not uncommon in real‐world trauma care involving transient populations, underscores the importance of coordinated longitudinal surveillance to better evaluate the durability of nonoperative treatment approaches.

Discussion

Pancreatic injuries are relatively uncommon but pose a significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenge in abdominal trauma. Their retroperitoneal location often masks early clinical signs, contributing to delays in diagnosis and increasing the risk of complications [1, 5]. The majority of these injuries occur due to motor vehicle collisions or direct epigastric trauma, with penetrating injuries accounting for up to 40% of cases, frequently accompanied by damage to adjacent organs [5, 6]. In this case, however, the trauma mechanism—a direct kick to the abdomen by a horse—represents a particularly rare etiology that is not commonly highlighted in existing trauma literature.

The pathophysiological impact of blunt pancreatic trauma often involves rotational shearing forces causing disruption at the pancreatic neck or body, particularly when associated with high‐impact force [7]. Clinical presentation can vary widely, from subtle epigastric discomfort to overt peritonitis and hemodynamic instability [8, 9]. Our patient initially presented with left hypochondrial pain and sepsis‐like symptoms, which were attributed to a concomitant splenic contusion. This overlap in presentation likely obscured the underlying pancreatic injury and contributed to the diagnostic delay, a common pitfall in similar cases [2].

Although serum lipase is a helpful marker, its sensitivity and specificity in trauma are limited. Indeed, in our patient, lipase levels were within normal limits, underscoring the limitations of relying on biochemical markers alone in acute settings [9, 10]. Imaging, therefore, remains the cornerstone of diagnosis. While CT is considered the first‐line modality for evaluating stable patients, its sensitivity in detecting main pancreatic duct injury is limited, particularly in early stages [1] [11]. MRI provides additional diagnostic detail, especially regarding ductal involvement. In this case, although the initial MRI was inconclusive, a follow‐up CT performed six days later revealed a complete transection of the pancreatic tail with suspected duct injury—classified as Grade III on the AAST scale [12, 13].

Ultrasound, despite its noninvasive nature, has a highly variable sensitivity for pancreatic lesions and is significantly operator‐dependent [11]. Moreover, in trauma patients with pain or agitation, the diagnostic utility of ultrasound is further reduced, as occurred in our case.

Management of pancreatic trauma hinges on injury severity and patient stability. Nonoperative treatment is generally reserved for low‐grade injuries (Grades I–II), while high‐grade lesions, especially those involving the pancreatic duct (Grades III–V), have traditionally been managed surgically [14, 15].

However, recent trends have supported a more selective approach, particularly in hemodynamically stable patients. In our patient, a multidisciplinary team opted for conservative management, with close monitoring, intravenous antibiotics, bowel rest, and parenteral nutrition. Her subsequent clinical improvement and imaging stability reaffirm the viability of this strategy in carefully selected cases.

Surgical options for severe pancreatic injuries include resectional procedures such as distal pancreatectomy and pancreaticoduodenectomy [16, 17]. These interventions, however, are not without long‐term sequelae, including endocrine and exocrine insufficiency.

While conservative management was successful in this case, it is important to acknowledge the role and implications of surgery. Distal pancreatectomy remains the standard operative approach for Grade III injuries with ductal disruption. The benefits include definitive removal of the injured segment, reduced risk of fistula, and prevention of delayed complications such as pseudocysts or abscesses. However, the risks are significant, including loss of pancreatic tissue with subsequent endocrine and exocrine insufficiency, increased perioperative morbidity, and potential for splenic injury if splenectomy is required. In contrast, nonoperative management, when feasible in stable patients, may help preserve pancreatic function and avoid surgical complications, but carries the risk of delayed ductal leakage, fistula formation, or need for secondary intervention. Balancing these risks requires individualized, multidisciplinary decision‐making tailored to patient stability, resources, and long‐term follow‐up availability.

The avoidance of resection in this case may have contributed to the preservation of pancreatic function and minimized long‐term morbidity.

While operative management remains essential in unstable patients or those with progressive clinical deterioration, nonresectional strategies, supported by advances in critical care and imaging, are increasingly recognized as effective alternatives in high‐grade injuries. Management should also prioritize adequate drainage, especially when ductal disruption or fluid collections are present [18].

In reflecting on this case, several clinically relevant points emerge. The unusual mechanism of injury, the presence of an isolated Grade III pancreatic lesion with normal lipase levels, the delayed diagnosis masked by splenic symptoms, and the patient's favorable response to conservative treatment despite ductal involvement—each of these elements reflects a complex clinical course that offers insight into evolving trauma care practices. Collectively, these features emphasize the importance of individualized, multidisciplinary decision‐making and suggest that nonoperative approaches may be both safe and effective in select cases of high‐grade pancreatic trauma.

Finally, this case underscores the potential challenges in resource‐limited or rural settings, where diagnostic delays may occur due to restricted imaging capabilities. The successful outcome observed here also reflects the growing capacity of intermediate trauma centers to manage complex injuries using evidence‐informed, patient‐tailored strategies.

Despite its limitations as a single‐patient report, this case contributes to the current literature by reinforcing key diagnostic and therapeutic considerations in pancreatic trauma. The insights offered may be of interest to clinicians and trauma specialists navigating similar diagnostic uncertainties in low‐incidence but high‐risk scenarios.

Conclusion

This case highlights the growing role of conservative management in high‐grade blunt pancreatic trauma, especially in stable young patients. The successful nonoperative approach challenges traditional surgical norms and underscores the importance of early imaging, multidisciplinary coordination, and individualized care. It also illustrates the practical challenges in nontertiary settings and emphasizes the need for future research to refine treatment indications and validate minimally invasive strategies.
