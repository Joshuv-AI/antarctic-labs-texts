Quantum circuits are just a phase
Chris Heunen, Louis Lemonnier, Christopher McNally, Alex Rice

Abstract
. Quantum programs today are written at a low level of abstraction—quantum circuits akin to assembly languages—and even advanced quantum programming languages essentially function as circuit description languages. This state of affairs impedes scalability, clarity, and support for higher-level reasoning. More abstract and expressive quantum programming constructs are needed. To this end, we introduce a novel yet simple quantum programming language for generating unitaries from “just a phase”; we combine a (global) phase operation that captures phase shifts with a quantum analogue of the “if let” construct that captures subspace selection via pattern matching. This minimal language lifts the focus from quantum gates to eigendecomposition, conjugation, and controlled unitaries; common building blocks in quantum algorithm design. We demonstrate several aspects of the expressive power of our language in several ways. Firstly, we establish that our representation is universal by deriving a universal quantum gate set. Secondly, we show that important quantum algorithms can be expressed naturally and concisely, including Grover’s search algorithm, Hamiltonian simulation, Quantum Fourier Transform, Quantum Signal Processing, and the Quantum Eigenvalue Transformation. Furthermore, we give clean denotational semantics grounded in categorical quantum mechanics. Finally, we implement a prototype compiler that efficiently translates terms of our language to quantum circuits, and prove that it is sound with respect to these semantics. Collectively, these contributions show that this construct offers a principled and practical step toward more abstract and structured quantum programming.

As a second argument, we show that this language captures a broad class of quantum algorithms. For example, Grover’s search algorithm (whose circuit representation is given in

and the diffusion operator is

\mathsf{Ph}(\pi)\otimes\mathsf{id}_{n};\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{+}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{+}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\pi)

In a similar way, we will show that our representation can elegantly express important quantum algorithms including Quantum Fourier Transform, Hamiltonian simulation, Quantum Signal Processing, and Quantum Eigenvalue Transformation.

Third, we validate the practicality of our approach through a prototype compiler that translates our higher-level constructs into standard quantum circuits efficiently, which we present as an evaluation function converting any term to a canonical “circuit-like” form. This may also be regarded as an operational semantics for the language.

Fourth, we equip the language with a categorical denotational semantics which naturally relates to established models of quantum theory
(
dimeglio:rstar
;
caretteetal:sqrtpi
)
.
More precisely, we build on rig dagger categories with independent coproducts. This gives a semantics-first approach to language design that fits in the larger programme of categorical quantum theory
(
heunenvicary:cqm
;
heunenkornell:hilb
)
. We employ these semantics to prove soundness of our compilation algorithm.

We believe that these four contributions show that the representation indeed captures a construct that is essential in quantum programming, at a higher level of abstraction than quantum circuits, and is simple and elegant to work with.

Quantum computing

We start with the essential background on quantum computing. We will only be concerned with unitary quantum computing, and have no need to consider the measurement readout at the end of the computation in detail. For more details we refer to textbooks such as
(

Qubits

The unit of quantum information is the
qubit
. The state of a qubit is a unit vector
\varphi=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)
in the Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, that is, a pair of complex numbers
x
and
y
such that
|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}=1
. This vector is often written in
ket notation

\ket{\varphi}
. Two special vectors are the
computational basis
states
\ket{0}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\ket{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
; a general state
\ket{\varphi}
is in a
superposition
of these two. Two such states that we will often use are
\ket{+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0}+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{1}
and
\ket{-}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{1}
.

Entanglement

In a system with multiple qubits, the state is a unit vector in the
tensor product
. For example, if the first qubit is in state
\ket{0}
, and the second qubit is in state
\ket{1}
, then the state of the compound system is the vector
\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, also written as
\ket{01}
. Not all states of a system with multiple qubits are of this form. For example, the state
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{00}+\ket{11}\right)
is
entangled
: it cannot be written in the form
\ket{\varphi}\otimes\ket{\psi}
. In general, the states of a system with
n
qubits can range over the unit vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{(2^{n})}
.

Unitaries

Qubits can undergo operations specified by unitary matrices. These are
2^{n}
-by-
2^{n}
matrices
U
with complex entries satisfying
U^{\dagger}U=1
(and hence also
UU^{\dagger}=1
), that is, the matrix is invertible and its inverse is its conjugate transpose. On single qubits, standard operations include:

\mathsf{H}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\mathsf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\qquad\mathsf{Y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\qquad\mathsf{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\mathsf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

The matrix
\mathsf{H}
is called the

transformation, and satisfies
\mathsf{H}\ket{0}=\ket{+}
and
\mathsf{H}\ket{1}=\ket{-}
.

Another way to combine two

Any

Phases

Two unitary matrices

Eigendecomposition

For a linear map

, its

are scalars

\{v:Uv=\lambda v\}

The maps

Syntax

We are now positioned to introduce the core language of this paper, a combinator-based language for describing unitary linear transformations, with two basic building blocks: a global phase unitary, and a quantum “if let” allowing a restricted form of pattern matching.

In this combinator-style language, terms represent unitary maps on a set number of qubits. The types for unitaries are therefore very simple, and are in direct correspondence with natural numbers: a term
t

\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
will represent a unitary map
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}

\vdash t:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n

for a term
t
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

\mathsf{S}

pattern
. Patterns are given types of the form
\mathsf{q}n<\mathsf{q}m
and correspond to isometries
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}}
. The “if let” expression then has the following typing rule:

\frac{\vdash p:\mathsf{q}n<\mathsf{q}m\vdash s:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n}{\vdash\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s:\mathsf{q}m\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}m}

One intuition for the action of the “if let” expression is the following: if
s
represents the unitary
U
, and
p
represents the isometry
i
, then the unitary represented by “
\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s
” sends
i(v)
to
i(U(v))
and acts as the identity on vectors orthogonal to the image of
i
, with the action on other inputs being determined by linearity. The “if let” construction can be viewed as a restricted form of symmetric pattern matching
(
sabryvalironvizzotto:control
)
.

Patterns are given by a separate but related syntax to terms, for which the rules are given below:

\frac{}{\vdash\ket{0}:\mathsf{q}0<\mathsf{q}1}\frac{}{\vdash\ket{1}:\mathsf{q}0<\mathsf{q}1}\frac{}{\vdash\ket{+}:\mathsf{q}0<\mathsf{q}1}\frac{}{\vdash\ket{-}:\mathsf{q}0<\mathsf{q}1}\frac{\vdash s:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n}{\vdash s:\mathsf{q}n<\mathsf{q}n}\frac{\vdash p:\mathsf{q}n<\mathsf{q}m\vdash q:\mathsf{q}l<\mathsf{q}n}{\vdash p\cdot q:\mathsf{q}l<\mathsf{q}m}\frac{\vdash p:\mathsf{q}n<\mathsf{q}m\vdash q:\mathsf{q}n^{\prime}<\mathsf{q}m^{\prime}}{\vdash p\otimes q:\mathsf{q}(n+n^{\prime})<\mathsf{q}(m+m^{\prime})}

The latter three rules observe that all unitary maps are also isometries, and that isometries are closed under composition and tensor products. Each pattern
\ket{x}
represents the isometry
z\mapsto z\ket{x}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
. We note that the composition for patterns is in function composition order, in contrast to the diagrammatic composition order for terms.

We highlight three important cases of this construction:

•

If
p:\mathsf{q}0<\mathsf{q}n
, then the term
\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\theta)
represents the unitary which maps
p(\alpha)+v
to
e^{i\theta}p(\alpha)+v
(where
\langle p(1),v\rangle=0
), which has eigenvalues
1
and
e^{i\theta}
.

•

Let
s:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
represent the unitary
U
and consider the term:

\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{1}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{n}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s

The unitary represented by this term sends anything input of the form
\ket{1}\otimes v
to
\ket{1}\otimes U(v)
, and leaves any input of the form
\ket{0}\otimes v
unchanged. This term therefore represents the controlled
U
operation.

•

Suppose
s,t:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
, representing unitaries
U
and
V
. Then the unitary represented by

\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}s\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}t

is the unitary
U\circ V\circ U^{\dagger}
, which sends
U(v)
to
U(V(v))
.

Example 1 (
\mathsf{X}
gate).

Our first example is the term:

\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{-}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\pi)

By the intuition above, this represents a unitary which maps
\ket{-}
to
e^{i\pi}\ket{-}=-\ket{-}
, and
\ket{+}
(which is orthogonal to
\ket{-}
) to
\ket{+}
. Its action on other vectors is determined by linearity;
\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{+}+\ket{-}\right)
is sent to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{+}-\ket{-}\right)=\ket{1}
and similarly
\ket{1}
is sent to
\ket{0}
, making this the quantum
\mathsf{X}
gate.

The syntax presented here allows the simple definition of two important meta-level operations: inversion and exponentiation. The ability to obtain the inverse of a quantum program is not uncommon, yet we highlight the simplicity of the definition below.

Definition 2 (Inversion).

Given a term
\vdash t:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
, we define its inverse
\vdash t^{\dagger}:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
by structural induction on the syntax:

\mathsf{Ph}(\theta)^{\dagger}=\mathsf{Ph}(-\theta)\qquad(\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s)^{\dagger}=\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s^{\dagger}\qquad(s\otimes t)^{\dagger}=s^{\dagger}\otimes t^{\dagger}\qquad(s;t)^{\dagger}=t^{\dagger};s^{\dagger}

A simple induction shows the resulting term is well-typed.

For terms which do not contain the composition constructor (in particular terms which consist of a single “if let” statement), we can perform the much more general operation of exponentiation. The ability to define exponentiation exemplifies the utility of our syntax.

Definition 3 (Exponentials).

Let
\vdash t:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
be a “composition-free” term, i.e. a term containing no instances of “
;
”. For a real number
\alpha
, define the exponentiation
\vdash t^{\alpha}:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
by structural induction:

\mathsf{Ph}(\theta)^{\alpha}=\mathsf{Ph}(\alpha\theta)\qquad(\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s)^{\alpha}=\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s^{\alpha}\qquad(s\otimes t)^{\alpha}=s^{\alpha}\otimes t^{\alpha}

Similar to above, a simple induction shows exponentiation is well-typed. We note that the case where
\alpha=-1
coincides with the inversion operation. We write
\sqrt{t}
for
t^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.

We highlight the use of the exponentiation operation by applying it to the
\mathsf{X}
gate.

Example 4.

The quantum
\mathsf{V}=\sqrt{\mathsf{X}}
gate, which satisfies
\mathsf{V}\circ\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{X}
is given by the matrix:

\mathsf{V}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+i&1-i\\
1-i&1+i\end{pmatrix}

Deriving this matrix from only the definition of
\mathsf{X}
(or indeed obtaining a quantum circuit for this gate) is non-trivial, yet its definition in our language is immediate from the definition of the
\mathsf{X}
and exponentiation:

\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{X}^{0.5}=\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{-}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}})

This term represents the unitary given by the matrix above.

Example 5.

The 5-qubit GHZ state (
=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}}(\ket{00000}+\ket{11111})

\mathsf{H}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{4};\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{1}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{4}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{X}\otimes\mathsf{X}\otimes\mathsf{X}\otimes\mathsf{X}

This highlights one of the flaws of the combinator style syntax, to apply a Hadamard gate to the first qubit, we must explicitly tensor it with the remaining qubits. Further, the pattern
\ket{1}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{4}
causes the body to be controlled by the first qubit, but also must explicitly tensored with the remaining qubits.

The combinator syntax also enforces a total ordering on the qubits, which may or may not be desirable. If we had decided to create the GHZ state with successive
\mathsf{CX}

Remark 6.

\ket{-}

Fig.

is more involved. The set up taken above, however, allows an arguably more minimal presentation by having the phase rotation be the only primitive unitary, and enables the exponentiation operation; it is unclear what the square root of a primitive Hadamard operation should be, yet its definition is immediate when presented as a single “if let” statement.

\mathsf{Swap}\coloneq\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\mathsf{CX}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{XC}

The unitary pattern
\mathsf{CX}

\mathsf{CX};\mathsf{XC};\mathsf{CX}

\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\mathsf{CX}\cdot(\ket{-}\otimes\ket{1})\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\pi)

This presents an alternative way of understanding the action of this gate. By observing that
\mathsf{CX}\ket{-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{01}-\ket{10})
, we notice that the term above multiplies this component of the input by

\ket{01}
and
\ket{10}
components.

4.
Algorithms

Many well-known quantum algorithms can be expressed naturally in terms of conditional phases.
Below, we give implementations of several of the most prominent algorithms and prove their correctness.
We begin with Grover’s search algorithm, whose
oracle
and
diffusion
operators are precisely conditional phases on the target subspace and the uniform subspace, respectively.
Next, quantum simulation algorithms are often based on finite-difference time-domain Hamiltonian evolution. In these algorithms, we take a number of time-steps, in each of which the state advances by applying conditional phases corresponding to the eigenspaces of the Hamiltonian.
After that, we show how the
quantum Fourier transform
(QFT) is naturally expressed as a sequence of conditional phases.
Finally, we present implementations of
quantum signal processing
and the
quantum eigenvalue transform
, which implement functions of black-box unitaries by applying conditional phases.

4.1.
Grover’s Algorithm

The celebrated quantum database search algorithm of Grover
(
grover
)
has a simple formulation in this language.
Assume we are searching a database
X=\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
of size
N=2^{n}
for elements
x
on which a function
f:X\rightarrow\{0,1\}
takes the value
1
. It is a convenient simplifying assumption that
f(x)=1
at only a single element
0\leq\omega<N
. Recall that the algorithm consists of three steps
(
nielsenchuang
)
:

(1)

Preparation of a uniform superposition
\ket{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|X|}}\sum_{x\in X}\ket{x}

(2)

Repeat
\lceil\pi\sqrt{N}/4\rceil
times:

(a)

Apply the
oracle operator

U_{f}=\sum_{x\in X}(-1)^{f(x)}\outerproduct{x}{x}=1-2\outerproduct{\omega}{\omega}
.

(b)

Apply the
diffusion operator

U_{s}=2\outerproduct{s}{s}-I
.

(3)

Measure the quantum state.

With high probability, the measurement result is
\ket{\omega}
.

The oracle operator
U_{f}
can be implemented by the following program:

\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{\omega_{0}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\omega_{n - 1}}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\pi)

where
\omega_{j}
is the
j
th
bit in the binary expansion of
\omega
. Similarly, the diffusion operator
U_{s}
is given by the program:

\mathsf{Ph}(\pi)\otimes\mathsf{id}_{n};\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{+}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{+}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\pi)

Grover’s algorithm is then simply an interleaving of these operators.

4.2.
Quantum Simulation

We can implement the Trotter simulation algorithm
(
lloyd1996universal
)
by applying a sequence of conditional phases.
Once the Hamiltonian decomposed into projections, we then construct a program that realises conditional phases on the subspace picked out by each projection.

Let
H\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a positive self-adjoint operator on a
2^{n}
-dimensional Hilbert space.
Then there is a (possibly empty) decomposition
H=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}\Pi_{i}
, where each
\Pi_{i}
is a projection. Write
\tilde{H}=((\lambda_{i},\Pi_{i}))_{i=1}^{K}
for the
K
-tuple of spectral components consisting of ordered pairs of eigenvalues and projectors. Now, the decomposition above is not unique, so neither is
\tilde{H}
. In fact, it need not be a
spectral
decomposition in the usual sense. We have two choices: impose uniqueness by requiring that the
\Pi_{i}
be orthonormal, that the range of
\Pi_{i}
coincides with the kernel of
H-\lambda_{i}I
, that
\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{K}
; or, take
\tilde{H}
to be primary and
H
to be derived. Let us adopt the latter approach.

Now suppose we are given a set of patterns
\{p_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq K}
, where without loss of generality
\vdash p_{i}:\mathsf{q}m_{i}<\mathsf{q}n
, which corresponding to the projections
\Pi_{i}
, that is
\Pi_{i}=\iota_{i}\iota_{i}^{\dagger}
where
\iota
is the isometry represented by
p_{i}
.

Then we define a program
U_{\tilde{H}}(t)
inductively, by

\displaystyle U_{\emptyset}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{id}_{n}

\displaystyle U_{((\lambda_{i},\Pi_{i}))_{i=1}^{k+1}}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p_{k+1}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(-\lambda_{i+1}t)\otimes\mathsf{id}_{m_{i}};U_{((\lambda_{i},\Pi_{i}))_{i=1}^{k}}(t).

If the
\Pi_{i}
are mutually orthogonal (or, more generally, mutually commute), then
U_{\tilde{H}}(t)
represents the unitary
e^{-iHt}
. This does not hold in general, for non-commuting projectors, but writing

U^{0}\coloneq\mathsf{id}_{n}\qquad U^{N+1}\coloneq U;U^{N},

one can use
U_{\tilde{H}}(t/N)^{N}
, which represents a unitary which converges to
e^{-iHt}
as
N\to\infty
by the well-known
Trotterization
formula
(
trotter1959product
)
.

4.2.1.
Interacting dipoles

Let us now consider a simple example of interacting spin-1/2 magnetic dipoles in an external magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}

(
sakurai2020modern
)
. The Hamiltonian is

H=H_{\mathrm{free}}+H_{\mathrm{int}}\qquad H_{\mathrm{free}}=-\frac{\hbar}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\gamma_{j}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\qquad H_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{\mu_{0}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\hbar^{2}}{16\pi r^{3}}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}-3(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1})(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})\right)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}
is the vector of Pauli operators on the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
spin,
\gamma_{j}
is the gyromagnetic moment of the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
spin,
\boldsymbol{r}
is the displacement between the two spins,
r=\lVert\boldsymbol{r}\rVert
, and
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}=\boldsymbol{r}/r
. We can simplify this expression to,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}\sigma^{z}_{1}+\omega_{2}\sigma^{z}_{2}+J\left(\sigma^{x}_{1}\sigma^{x}_{2}+\sigma^{y}_{1}\sigma^{y}_{2}-2\sigma^{z}_{1}\sigma^{z}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}\sigma^{z}\otimes I+\omega_{2}I\otimes\sigma^{z}+J\left(\sigma^{x}\otimes\sigma^{x}+\sigma^{y}\otimes\sigma^{y}-2\sigma^{z}\otimes\sigma^{z}\right)

Not having imposed uniqueness or even orthogonality on the decomposition
\tilde{H}
, we are free to work term-by-term. Writing:

\Pi_{+z}=\outerproduct{0}{0}\quad\Pi_{-z}=\outerproduct{1}{1}\quad\Pi_{+x}=\outerproduct{+}{+}\quad\Pi_{-x}=\outerproduct{-}{-}\quad\Pi_{+y}=\outerproduct{i}{i}\quad\Pi_{-y}=\outerproduct{-i}{-i}

We obtain:

\displaystyle H={}

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(\Pi_{+z}-\Pi_{-z})

\displaystyle\otimes(\Pi_{+z}+\Pi_{-z})

\displaystyle{}+{}

\displaystyle\omega_{2}(\Pi_{+z}+\Pi_{-z})

\displaystyle\otimes(\Pi_{+z}-\Pi_{-z})

\displaystyle{}+{}

\displaystyle J(\Pi_{+x}-\Pi_{-x})

\displaystyle\otimes(\Pi_{+x}-\Pi_{-x})

\displaystyle{}+{}

\displaystyle J(\Pi_{+y}-\Pi_{-y})

\displaystyle\otimes(\Pi_{+y}-\Pi_{-y})

\displaystyle{}-{}

\displaystyle 2J(\Pi_{+z}-\Pi_{-z})

\displaystyle\otimes(\Pi_{+z}-\Pi_{-z})

Finally, we can distribute the tensor products over the sums, obtaining the decomposition
\tilde{H}
into projectors. It remains to find the corresponding
patterns
. Recall that for each
\Pi_{i}
we must find a corresponding pattern
p_{i}
. The following patterns
p_{\pm x},p_{\pm y},p_{\pm z}
suffice:

p_{+z}=\ket{0}\quad p_{-z}=\ket{1}\quad p_{+x}=\ket{+}\quad p_{-x}=\ket{-}\quad p_{+y}=\mathsf{S}\cdot\ket{+}\quad p_{-y}=\mathsf{S}\cdot\ket{-}

These patterns allow us to compute a term representing
e^{-iHt}
, as required.

4.3.
Quantum Fourier Transform

The quantum Fourier transform over
\mathbb{Z}/2^{n}\mathbb{Z}
is the
n
-qubit unitary operator
F_{2^{n}}
such that
\bra{y}F_{2^{n}}\ket{x}=2^{-n/2}\omega^{xy}
, for
0\leq x,y<2^{n}
, where
\omega
is a primitive
2^{n}
th
root of unity
(
nielsenchuang
)
. If
x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n}
is the binary expansion of
0\leq x<2^{n}
, then

F_{2^{n}}\ket{x_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{x_{n}}=2^{-n/2}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\left(\ket{0}+e^{2\pi ix2^{j-1-n}}\ket{1}\right).

The textbook algorithm for implementing this operation consists of a sequence of controlled phases. Define the
dyadic rational phase gate
,

\mathsf{R}_{n}\coloneq\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{1}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{n}}),

To define
\vdash\mathsf{QFT}_{n}:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
, the term representing the
n
qubit quantum Fourier transform, we can leverage that chains of controlled gates with the same control qubit can be replaced by a single control block. Using this we obtain the following recursive definition:

\displaystyle\mathsf{QFT}_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{id}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathsf{QFT}_{n+1}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{H}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{n};\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{1}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{n}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{R}_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{R}_{n+1};\mathsf{id}_{1}\otimes\mathsf{QFT}_{n}

In order recover the original quantum Fourier transform
F_{2^{n}}
, the order of the output qubits must be inverted, which could be done with the addition of the appropriate
\mathsf{Swap}
gates.

4.4.
Quantum Signal Processing

Quantum signal processing
(QSP) is a procedure that transforms a parametric single-qubit rotation to modify its sensitivity to the parameter
(
low2016methodology
;
low2017optimal
)
. Following
(
martyn2021
)
, we write

W(a)=e^{i\theta X}=\begin{pmatrix}a&i\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\\
i\sqrt{1-a^{2}}&a\end{pmatrix}

for the parametric unitary to which the user has black-box access (a rotation about the
X
-axis by
\theta=-2\cos^{-1}a
). QSP makes a number of calls to
W(a)
, as well as a number of phases, to implement a modified unitary,

\tilde{W}(a)=\begin{pmatrix}P(a)&iQ(a)\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\\
iQ^{*}(a)\sqrt{1-a^{2}}&P^{*}(a)\end{pmatrix}

This is useful in, among other applications, quantum control of large ensembles of quantum systems subject to inhomogeneous coherent control
(
brown2004arbitrarily
)
.
We regard
a
as the amplitude of a some control field, which due to spatial gradients couples more strongly to some elements of an ensemble than to others.
When the amplitude of the control field varies over an interval
I=[a_{0},a_{1}]
within a sample, we may choose
P,Q
such that
\tilde{W}(I)
is approximately constant.
Dually, in sensing applications, we may wish to
enhance
sensitivity to the parameter
a
.

There turns out to be a construction of a composite control sequence that implements any
\tilde{W}
for any polynomials
P
(resp.
Q
) of degree
d
(resp.
d-1
) and parity
d\bmod 2
(resp.
(d-1)\bmod 2
), subject to the constraints imposed by unitarity of
\tilde{W}
, using
d
calls to
W
.
All such
\tilde{W}
can be realised as a product,

\tilde{W}(a)=W_{\vec{\phi}}(a)\coloneqq S(\phi_{0})W(a)S(\phi_{1})\cdots S(\phi_{d-1})W(a)S(\phi_{d}),

for some tuple
\vec{\phi}\in[0,2\pi)^{d+1}
, where
S(\phi)=e^{i\phi_{0}Z}
.

As a single-qubit protocol, the translation to a program is easy. Define

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{z}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{Ph}(-\alpha/2)\otimes\mathsf{id}_{1};\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{0}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{x}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{Ph}(-\alpha/2)\otimes\mathsf{id}_{1};\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{+}\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{Ph}(\alpha).

We then define a
quantum signal processing
program
\mathsf{QSP}(a;\vec{\phi})
inductively by

\displaystyle\mathsf{QSP}(a;(\phi_{0}))

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{R}_{z}(2\phi_{0})

\displaystyle\mathsf{QSP}(a;(\phi_{0},\dots,\phi_{k},\phi_{k+1}))

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{QSP}(a;(\phi_{0},\dots,\phi_{k}));

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\mathsf{R}_{x}(-2\cos^{-1}(a));\mathsf{R}_{z}(2\phi_{k+1})

(recalling that
\vec{\phi}
is by hypothesis of length
\geq 1
).

4.5.
Quantum Eigenvalue Transform

QSP generalises rather dramatically to the
quantum eigenvalue transform
(QET)
(
martyn2021
;
low2024quantum
)
.
Like QSP, the QET applies a polynomial transform to an operator, but in this instance an operator on a larger finite-dimensional quantum system, not merely a qubit.
Still following the presentation and conventions of
(
martyn2021
)
, we proceed to an implementation of the QET.

We are given a Hamiltonian
H
acting on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}}
, and unitary
U
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
such that
U
contains a copy of
H
in a block determined by a projector
\Pi
, which is also given to us. Then the QET produces a unitary
\tilde{U}
, such that (using the notation of
(
martyn2021

5.
Compilation

In this section we will describe a conversion from the combinator language in

Our strategy for the compilation will be to provide a normalisation algorithm, putting terms in a canonical “circuit-like” form. From this form, a circuit representation can be trivially extracted. The circuit representation we compile to here will consist of a fixed number of qubits (which can be referred to by index), a Hadamard gate, and arbitrary multi-controlled phase gates (both zero- and one- controlled with arbitrary angle). The problem of further compiling multi-qubit gates to a finite gate set is well studied

6.1.
Dagger categories

Except for measurement, quantum programming is intrinsically reversible,
following the rules of quantum mechanics. We capture this reversibility
mathematically by providing, for each morphism
f\colon X\to Y
, an
associated morphism
f^{\dagger}\colon Y\to X
in the converse direction.
Formally, a dagger category is a category equipped with an identity-on-objects
involutive contravariant functor
(-)^{\dagger}\colon\mathbf{C}^{\rm op}\to\mathbf{C}
, meaning that
X^{\dagger}=X
and
(fg)^{\dagger}=g^{\dagger}f^{\dagger}
.

Example 1.

Here are some examples of relevant dagger categories for reversible
programming.

The category with sets as objects and partial injective functions as
morphisms is a dagger category. We write
\mathbf{PInj}
for this category.
The dagger of a function
f
is its partial inverse, as in the following
example morphism
f\colon\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}
.

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if }x=0\\
\text{undefined}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\qquad\qquad f^{\dagger}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }x=1\\
\text{undefined}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

This category represents what we refer to as
classical

reversibility.

The category
\mathbf{Con}
of Hilbert spaces and contractive linear
maps—
i.e.
maps with norm at most
1
, also known as Kraus
operators—is a dagger category where the dagger is the adjoint of linear
maps. This category embodies
pure
quantum operations: it contains
both unitaries and isometries, and so can model pure quantum states as well
as programs.

Note that the dagger is not necessarily an inverse, but should rather be seen
as a
partial
inverse. This gives us the mathematical leverage to provide
a semantics to “if let” (see next subsection). In a dagger category, a
morphism
f
is a dagger monomorphism (resp. epimorphism) if
f^{\dagger}f=\mathrm{id}
(resp. if
ff^{\dagger}=\mathrm{id}
), and it is a dagger isomorphism if it is
both dagger monic and epic
(

)
.

Example 2.

In
\mathbf{PInj}

functions, and the dagger isomorphisms are the bijections. In
\mathbf{Con}
,
the dagger monomorphisms are the isometries and the dagger isomorphisms are

Later in this section, we interpret the programs in our languages as dagger
isomorphisms (

unitaries in a quantum setting), and patterns as
dagger monomorphisms (
e.g.

7.
Beyond Combinators

In this final section, we give two nominal variations of the language, exploring how the “if let” construction could manifest in different contexts.

•

A functional language:
here we allow qubits to be bound to linear variables, and unitaries are treated as functions which consume input qubits, producing new output qubits.
This allows a more concise syntax where a unitary can be applied to a subset of the available qubits without inserting explicit identity unitaries.
A consequence of this format is that there arises a native implementation of the “swap” gate on two qubits, which in turn allows swaps to appear within “if let” blocks.

•

An imperative language:
in the imperative model, variables are viewed as representing the
location
of a qubit (i.e. their denotation is given by an inclusion into some ambient space) instead of viewing variables as storing the state of a qubit at some fixed point in time.
In this model, variables need not be treated linearly, and unitaries are given by expressions rather than functions, and are viewed as mutating the variables they act on, rather than consuming them.

The following subsections briefly sketch these languages, exploring their differences to the core combinator language.

7.1.
Functional

In the functional variant, we introduce variables which intuitively store the quantum state at intermediate stages of the program.
Gates can then be explicitly applied to any specific qubits available, rather than requiring them to be tensored with appropriate identity operations to apply them to specific qubits.

With access to variables, the typing judgements must now be parameterised by a context. As unitaries are no longer the primitive constructions, we remove the unitary type
\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n
, and instead have the following typing judgement for terms:

\Gamma\vdash s:\mathsf{q}n

The context
\Gamma
is a list
x_{1}:\mathsf{q}n_{1},x_{2}:\mathsf{q}n_{2},\dots
where the
x_{i}
are variable names. We write
\cdot
for the empty context and
\Gamma,\Delta
for the concatenation of contexts
\Gamma
and
\Delta
. As is usual in the literature, we treat terms as equal up to
\alpha
-equivalence.

Instead of the composition operator present in the combinator language, we now have a “let” expression, allowing us to bind the (possibly multiple) outputs of a term. In general this will have the form:

\mathop{\mathsf{let}}x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}=s\mathrel{\mathsf{in}}t

We refer to expressions of the form
x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}
as
copatterns
, as they are also exactly the terms that can be matched to a pattern in an “if let” statement. To make this precise, we specify three classes of syntax:
\mathsf{Copattern}\subset\mathsf{Unitary}\subset\mathsf{Pattern}
. All three syntax classes are generated inductively, from the following grammars:

In principle, the typing rules could be modified to allow this, however doing so creates ambiguity in the semantics, as it can be unclear whether swaps occur inside the “if let” block (possibly being controlled) as opposed to happening before.

7.3.
Imperative

The final version of the syntax is an imperative syntax. By exploiting that unitaries always return the same number of qubits as they are input, we can instead view a unitary as a procedure that
mutates
its input qubits, and has no return value.

In this syntax, quantum computation is
effectful
. Under this view, our qubit variables now act as qubit “locations” rather than qubit states, and can be freely copied or unused, allowing the linearity condition to be dropped. We note that in this syntax, copying a variable is more akin to creating an alias, rather than duplicating a qubit state (which cannot be done by the no-cloning theorem).

Removing the need for return values comes with various advantages:

•

The overall syntax becomes more concise.

•

It is no longer possible to natively represent swaps as in the functional variant, yet we do not have a rigid qubit order as in the combinator variant.

•

It is much easier to give a typing rule to the “if let” clause which allows “variable capture”, the use of variables defined outside the pattern.

To demonstrate the last point, we consider the following program:

x:\mathsf{q}1,y:\mathsf{q}1\vdash(\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}\ket{1}=y\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}\mathsf{X}[x]);\mathsf{H}[x]

We are free here to use the variable
x
in the body of the “if let” clause, despite it not appearing in the pattern
\ket{1}
. Contrary to the functional variation of the syntax, the variable
x
is not treated linearly, and so we are free to continue using
x
after the “if let” clause. We also note that this program can be viewed as using the variables
x
and
y
“in the wrong order”. The translation of this program into the functional syntax would require multiple uses of the exchange typing rule, yet in this syntax the program can be naturally interpreted without any swap gates.

A disadvantage of the imperative approach is its treatment of patterns. Patterns naturally have a more functional treatment, causing the resulting language to contain functional and imperative components. For example, in the pattern
\mathsf{H}\cdot\ket{0}
, the term
\mathsf{H}
acts as a function which outputs a pattern, whereas in the expression
\mathsf{H}[x]
, the term
\mathsf{H}
acts on a qubit
x
which it is then viewed as mutating, returning nothing. To remedy this, we add a sort of gates, which bind the free variables in a unitary, and allow them to be embedded into a pattern.

The syntax for this language has the following grammar, where
\Gamma
is a context and
x
is a variable:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Copattern}\text{:}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\Coloneq

\displaystyle x\mid c_{1}\otimes c_{2}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pattern}\text{:}

\displaystyle p,q

\displaystyle\Coloneq

\displaystyle x\mid p\otimes q\mid g\cdot p\mid\ket{0}\mid\ket{1}\mid\ket{+}\mid\ket{-}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Unitary}\text{:}

\displaystyle s,t

\displaystyle\Coloneq

\displaystyle\mathsf{id}\mid s;t\mid\mathsf{Ph}(\theta)\mid\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p=c\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s

\displaystyle\mathsf{Gate}\text{:}

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\Coloneq

\displaystyle\Gamma\mapsto s

As in the functional syntax,
\mathsf{Copattern}\subset\mathsf{Pattern}
, but here the syntax for unitaries is separated.

We type this language with three judgements, one for pattern and copattern terms, one for unitary terms, and one for gates.

\Gamma\vdash p:\mathsf{q}n\qquad\Gamma\vdash s\qquad\vdash g:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n

The typing rules for patterns and copatterns are:

\frac{}{x:\mathsf{q}n\vdash x:\mathsf{q}n}\frac{\Gamma\vdash s:\mathsf{q}n\Delta\vdash t:\mathsf{q}m}{\Gamma,\Delta\vdash s\otimes t:\mathsf{q}(n+m)}\frac{\vdash g:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n\Gamma\vdash p:\mathsf{q}n}{\Gamma\vdash g\cdot p:\mathsf{q}n}\frac{}{\cdot\vdash\ket{0}:\mathsf{q}1}\frac{}{\cdot\vdash\ket{1}:\mathsf{q}1}\frac{}{\cdot\vdash\ket{+}:\mathsf{q}1}\frac{}{\cdot\vdash\ket{-}:\mathsf{q}1}

The typing rules for unitaries being:

\frac{}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{id}}\frac{\Gamma\vdash s\Gamma\vdash t}{\Gamma\vdash s;t}\frac{}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{Ph}(\theta)}\frac{\Delta\vdash c:\mathsf{q}m\Theta\vdash p:\mathsf{q}m\Gamma,\Theta\vdash s}{\Gamma,\Delta\vdash\mathop{\mathsf{if\ let}}p=c\mathbin{\mathsf{then}}s}

Lastly, the unique typing rule for gates is the following, where
\#\Gamma
is the number obtained by summing the indices of each type in
\Gamma
:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash s\#\Gamma=n}{\vdash\Gamma\mapsto s:\mathsf{q}n\leftrightarrow\mathsf{q}n}

In addition, we allow typing rules which permute the context, much like in the functional version of the syntax, though note that due to the lack of return values in this syntax, there is no way to create a “native” controlled swap, (i.e. without inserting a swap gate using quantum operations).

We further note that the typing rule here for the “if let” expression allows variable capture, without any of the complications that arised in the functional syntax.

8.
Future work

We conclude by discussing some future directions for the development of languages using the constructs introduced in this work. An immediate direction is to extend the compilation and denotational semantics to the functional and imperative variants of the language presented in
Section

7
.

In this paper we have focussed on using the “if let” construction as a device for writing quantum programs, yet it could also find use as a representation of quantum programs within an optimising compiler. At this higher level of abstraction than quantum circuits, more global optimisations become apparent.
Section

5
already contains one instance of this, simplifying a conjugation appearing in a controlled block.

Additionally, a study of more comprehensive equational systems on our language could extend the semantic equivalences between terms presented in
Section

6
. This could take the form of a complete representation of equality between quantum programs (as has been done for quantum circuits
(
clementetal:equationalcircuits
)
), or aim to characterise an equality relation which may not be complete (with respect to the semantics in unitary matrices) but could exhibit an efficient normalisation function, with potential applications for optimisation.

Finally, the language presented here describes programs which are purely quantum, with no classical components, omitting even allocation and measurement. A more fully-featured quantum programming language based on the “if let” construction requires the addition of these operations. In contrast to naively extending the language, these operations can reuse parts of the pattern infrastructure, with measurement bases specified by patterns, and patterns specified by subprograms with allocation but without measurement.

u_{1}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket^{\perp}=\left[\llbracket q\rrbracket^{\perp},\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\perp}\otimes\mathrm{id})\right]\circ{\cong}

which means that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket^{\perp}{\cong}i_{1}=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket^{\perp}\\
\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket^{\perp}{\cong}i_{2}=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\perp}\otimes\mathrm{id})\end{array}\right.

and we also know that:

u_{1}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi^{\prime}}^{\dagger}

and that
u_{2}
is the mediating morphism for the right internal square.
We need to prove that
u_{1}u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}
is indeed the mediating
morphism of the diagram, meaning that:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}u_{1}u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi u\xi^{\dagger}\\
u_{1}u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket^{\perp}=\llbracket q\rrbracket^{\perp}\end{array}\right.

The first equation is equivalent to:

u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi u

and since
(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\perp}\otimes\mathrm{id})
is jointly epic, it suffices to prove that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id})=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi u(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id})\\
u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\perp}\otimes\mathrm{id})=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi u(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\perp}\otimes\mathrm{id})\end{array}\right.

Now,

\displaystyle u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id})

\displaystyle=u_{2}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket\xi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket{\xi^{\prime}}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket s\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id})

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket s\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id})

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi u(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id})

\displaystyle u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\perp}\otimes\mathrm{id})

\displaystyle=u_{2}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket^{\perp}{\cong}i_{2}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket^{\perp}{\cong}i_{2}

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\perp}\otimes\mathrm{id})

and finally,

\displaystyle u_{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket^{\perp}

\displaystyle=u_{2}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket^{\perp}{\cong}i_{1}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket^{\perp}{\cong}i_{1}

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{\dagger}\llbracket q\rrbracket^{\perp}

which concludes.

Case
\mathsf{eval}^{\mathsf{p}}_{q,l,r}(s)
. Let
u
be the mediating morphism in the
induction hypothesis. We then have

\displaystyle u\llbracket q\rrbracket

•

Case
\mathsf{eval}^{\mathsf{p}}_{q,f}(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})
. By induction hypothesis, we
know that:

u_{1}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi^{\prime}}^{\dagger}

and

u_{2}\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket\xi^{\prime}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi^{\prime\prime}}^{\dagger}

Thus,

\displaystyle u_{1}u_{2}\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=u_{1}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket\xi^{\prime}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p\rrbracket_{2}\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi^{\prime\prime}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){x^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\xi^{\prime}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi^{\prime\prime}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\rrbracket\llbracket p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi^{\prime\prime}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\cdot p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi^{\prime\prime}}^{\dagger}

which concludes.

•

Case
\mathsf{eval}^{\mathsf{p}}_{q,l,r}(p_{1}\otimes p_{2})
. Without loss of
generality, we will work with

\begin{array}[]{l}\xi_{1}\colon\llbracket\mathsf{q}l\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}k_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}k_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}r\rrbracket\to\llbracket\mathsf{q}m\rrbracket\text{,}\\
\xi_{2}\colon\llbracket\mathsf{q}l\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}j_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}k_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}r\rrbracket\to\llbracket\mathsf{q}m\rrbracket^{\prime}\text{,}\\
\xi_{3}\colon\llbracket\mathsf{q}l\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}j_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}j_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket\mathsf{q}r\rrbracket\to\llbracket\mathsf{q}m\rrbracket\text{.}\end{array}

By induction hypothesis, we know that:

u_{1}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi_{1}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi_{2}}^{\dagger}

and

u_{2}\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket\xi_{2}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi_{3}}^{\dagger}

Thus,

\displaystyle u_{1}u_{2}\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=u_{1}\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket\xi_{2}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi_{3}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi_{1}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi_{2}}^{\dagger}\xi_{2}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi_{3}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi_{1}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi_{3}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi_{1}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi_{3}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\llbracket q\rrbracket\xi_{1}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\llbracket p_{1}\otimes p_{2}\rrbracket\otimes\mathrm{id}){\xi_{3}}^{\dagger}

which concludes.

∎

Soundness is then simply a corollary.
