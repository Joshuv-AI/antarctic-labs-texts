Pharmabiotics, Phocaeicola dorei, ameliorates cholestatic liver fibrosis by alleviating macrophage efferocytosis of neutrophils
Authors: Jung A Eom, In Gyu Park, Ji Ye Hyun, Na Young Lee, Goo Hyun Kwon, Sang Jun Yoon, Sung-Min Won, Young Lim Ham, Kyeong Jin Lee, Sang Hak Han, Dong-Hoon Yang, Dong Joon Kim, Ki Tae Suk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhocaeicola dorei has been reported to ameliorate metabolic diseases. Its role in liver fibrosis remains unclear. We evaluated the hepatoprotective effect of P. dorei in liver fibrosis. Fecal samples were collected from healthy controls and patients (n = 285) to assess the clinical relevance of P. dorei. In male mice models (3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1.4-dihydrocollidine [DDC] diet), P. dorei (109 CFU/g twice/week) was orally administered. Primary HSCs, LX-2, THP-1, and HL-60 cell lines were used for mechanical validation. The relative abundance of P. dorei increased with worsing liver disease in human. P. dorei administration significantly reduced neutrophil degranulation and efferocytosis pathways (Ly6g and F4/80). The dysregulated expression of neutrophil-associated chemokines (Cx3cl1 and Cx3cr1) was restored by P. dorei. P. dorei culture supernatant inhibited macrophage-mediated efferocytosis. P. dorei attenuates liver fibrosis by suppressing neutrophil and macrophage infiltration and disrupting efferocytosis. Our results identify P. dorei as a potential microbiome-based therapeutic candidate for cholestatic liver fibrosis.Subject terms: Liver fibrosis, Applied microbiology

Introduction

Liver fibrosis refers to scarring that occurs in the liver when inflammation persists over a long time1. In metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), inflammation and scarring can result in liver fibrosis, which is an abnormal wound-healing process of the liver in response to repeated and long-lasting injury from various etiologies such as infectious diseases, metabolic derangements, and autoimmune diseases2. Chronic liver diseases accompanied by liver fibrosis have caused significant morbidity and mortality in the world, showing an increasing prevalence rate. However, effective treatments for liver fibrosis remain unclear3.

The liver has direct contact with the intestinal tract via portal vein. Consequently, liver is exposed to translocated bacterial components and their metabolites4,5. Dysbiosis, an imbalance of the gut microbiome, is associated with a variety of liver diseases such as alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-related liver disease (MetALD), and primary sclerosing cholangitis6. Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes are major bacterial phyla in the gastrointestinal tract. Changes in phyla ratio can cause various pathologies, including obesity5.

Recently, strategies have been developed to treat liver fibrosis, especially MASLD and ALD by altering intestinal microbiome7,8. Supplementation of Lactobacillus or Pediococcus ameliorated the progression of MASLD by regulating gut microbiota and inflammatory pathways in Western diet mice model9,10. Phocaeicola (Bacteroides), a genus abundant in the mammalian intestine, is capable of protecting against pathogens and providing nutrients to other microbial inhabitants of the digestive tract11. Treatment with live Phocaeicola in vitro or in vivo can modulate lipopolysaccharide production by gut microbes and strengthen the intestinal barrier12. Phocaeicola bacteria have recently been considered as candidates for next-generation probiotics due to their potential role in promoting host health13.

Activation of innate immunity, including hepatic neutrophil infiltration, can serve as a potential mediator of inflammatory resolution. An abnormal accumulation of neutrophils in tissues and excessive release of bactericidal compounds can lead to unexpected damage to host organs14. The process of efferocytosis, in which phagocytes engulf apoptotic cells, is necessary for maintaining normal tissue homeostasis. It is a prerequisite for resolution of inflammation15. However, the efferocytosis process plays a dual role in stimulating hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) to induce hepatic fibrosis16. Macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils in liver injury promotes liver fibrosis17,18. As liver fibrosis progressed, it was confirmed that P. dorei increased in patient’s feces. In addition, neutrophil degranulation and phagocytosis pathways were reduced in the P. dorei group. In this study, we conducted experiments to elucidate the important role of P. dorei in liver fibrosis.

Results

Identification of gut microbiome differences among healthy, hepatitis, and cirrhosis patients

For the analysis of patients with cirrhosis, a total of 285 participants, including 156 in MetALD and 129 MASLD, were selected based on criteria as described in Materials and methods (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Patients with liver disease had higher levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), and total bilirubin (T-BIL) (Supplementary Fig. 1B, 1C).

To identify the effect of liver disease on gut microbiome alterations, we performed 16S rRNA analysis and shotgun metagenomic analysis of 219 and 66 fecal samples. The study population included patients with MASLD and MetALD, both classified as subtypes of steatotic liver disease. To characterize the alterations in gut microbial community structure, we analyzed alpha diversity using multiple indices. The Shannon diversity index and Chao1 estimator significantly decreased in the cirrhosis group compared to the normal control, indicating a substantial loss of both species evenness and richness (Fig. 1A). This dysbiotic trend was consistently corroborated by additional metrics, including observed operational taxonomic units (OTUs) and phylogenetic diversity (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Next, beta diversity was measured and visualized by clustering using the principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) to compare the similarity or distance among the three groups. Each group showed differential distributions in the plot (Fig. 1B). Taxonomic profiling based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed progressive phylum-level dysbiosis (Supplementary Fig. 2B–2F). Consistent with global dysbiosis, we observed a progressive depletion of Bacteroidetes and a marked expansion of Proteobacteria across disease stages. These compositional alterations were further corroborated by deep analysis using shotgun metagenomics, which confirmed consistent dysbiotic patterns at the phylum and genus levels (Fig. 1C).

To exclude potential confounding effects, we employed MaAsLin2 analysis adjusting for host covariates such as age, sex, and BMI. Notably, P. dorei demonstrated a consistent effect size and robust statistical significance (FDR q < 0.2) even after adjustment, confirming its specific link to disease progression independent of confounding factors (Fig. 1D). Consistent with the multivariate analysis, 16S rRNA-based species profiling identified Bacteroides and Phocaeicola as the genera most significantly altered by liver disease. P. dorei showed increased composition in the hepatitis and cirrhosis groups compared to the normal group. B. cellulosilyticus showed decreased composition in the hepatitis and cirrhosis groups compared to the normal group (Supplementary Fig. 2H). Owing to the low prevalence of MASLD-associated cirrhosis in Korea, MASLD cases were limited to the hepatitis stage and excluded from the primary analysis. Although not included in the primary analysis, MASLD patients exhibited an increase in the relative abundance of P. dorei up to the hepatitis stage (Supplementary Fig. 2G). Functional biomarkers were revealed in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Serum AST levels and AST/platelet ratio index (APRI) scores showed significant positive correlations with the log-transformed abundance of P. dorei (Supplementary Fig. 4A). The Furthermore, the diagnostic potential of P. dorei was confirmed through logistic regression analysis. The addition of P. dorei to the baseline clinical model significantly improved discriminatory performance, increasing the AUROC from 0.76 to 0.85 (DeLong test, P < 0.05; Fig. 1G). Notably, P. dorei abundance remained a robust independent predictor of advanced liver disease (Odds Ratio 3.78, P = 0.003; Fig. 1F).

Strain-level profiling using StrainPhlAn unveiled a distinct phylogenetic structure associated with liver disease. Further strain-level profiling confirmed that P. dorei was not only significantly more prevalent in patients (P = 0.0037) but also phylogenetically diverged into a tight, disease-specific clade (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Consistently, shotgun metagenomic analysis in human participants demonstrated that the relative abundance of P. dorei was significantly enriched in patients with hepatitis and cirrhosis and increased in parallel with disease severity (hepatitis, P = 0.002; cirrhosis, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 4C, 4D). In contrast, B. cellulosilyticus showed no significant association with disease status or stage (Supplementary Fig. 4E).

To characterize this divergence, read-based pangenome profiling demonstrated a clear separation between groups based on gene presence-absence patterns, indicating divergent functional repertoires (Fig. 1G). Functional profiling identified distinct gene signatures, where genes encoding tdh, podR, and bmpA were predominantly detected in the normal group, whereas rocF, praC, and utp showed significantly higher prevalence in the cirrhosis group (Fig. 1H). Longitudinal analysis of the disease cohort revealed that while P. dorei maintained a persistent relative abundance according to 16S rRNA profiling (P = 0.17), shotgun metagenomics of available follow-up samples demonstrated a significant temporal decline in its putatively beneficial functional pathways (P = 0.016; Fig. 1I). Examining the broader shotgun metagenomic cohort revealed a trend of functional decoupling (Fig. 1J and Supplementary Fig. 5A, 5B); the expansion of P. dorei in the cirrhosis group tended to uncouple from the tdh gene abundance, suggesting an overgrowth of functionally eroded variants.

Longitudinal analyses showed that P. dorei abundance alone does not exhibit a significant directional increase over time, indicating that species-level dynamics are insufficient to explain disease progression. Rather, given the limited longitudinal window relative to the timescale of MASLD progression, our data support a model in which disease severity is driven by a functional decoupling between P. dorei abundance and its symbiotic capacity, rather than by quantitative expansion alone.

To investigate the precise structural basis of this decoupling, we reconstructed representative P. dorei metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from the disease cohort (Supplementary Fig. 5C, 5D). Comparative structural genomics revealed a striking divergence: while the therapeutic strain largely conserved the symbiotic architecture of the healthy reference, the disease-associated MAGs exhibited massive physical deletions (Fig. 1K). Targeted profiling of these eroded regions confirmed the systematic absence of key host-microbe interaction modules, most notably the SusC/SusD-containing polysaccharide utilization loci (PULs) and cell surface proteins, which remained intact in the therapeutic strain (Fig. 1L). This physical loss was strictly validated by read-mapping analysis, demonstrating a near-complete depletion of normalized sequencing depth across the SusC/SusD loci in the disease MAGs (Supplementary Fig. 5E, 5F). Quantifying this structural erosion via a deletion signature score revealed significantly exacerbated genetic losses in the disease MAGs compared to the near-intact therapeutic strain (paired sign test, P = 0.0039; Fig. 1M).

The paradoxical expansion of P. dorei in cirrhosis despite its antifibrotic effects upon supplementation reflects pathoadaptive reductive evolution in a chronic inflammatory niche. Disease-associated P. dorei MAGs exhibit extensive, conserved genomic erosion, including physical deletion of SusC/SusD-containing polysaccharide utilization loci and cell surface proteins, indicating the expansion of functionally defective, dysbiotic variants rather than a restorative commensal response. In contrast, the therapeutic strain retains an intact symbiotic genomic architecture, demonstrating that our intervention functionally replenishes host-beneficial capacities that are structurally lost from the endogenous microbiome in cirrhosis. In previous studies, commensals frequently shed metabolically costly symbiotic genes (e.g., SusC/D) and surface antigens to evade immune surveillance19,20, prioritizing opportunistic growth to shift from a symbiotic cooperator to a dysbiotic scavenger.

P. dorei protects liver fibrosis in murine model

We identified that the number of P. dorei and B. cellulosilyticus in stool was changed by liver disease (Supplementary Fig. 2G). To confirm whether P. dorei and B. cellulosilyticus have a protective role in liver disease, we induced liver fibrosis in mice using two methods: 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) diet (Fig. 2A–2E) and injection of thioacetamide (TAA) (Fig. 2F–2H). Five-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were fed a normal diet for a week before induction of liver fibrosis. After one-week adaptation, mice were fed a DDC diet or got intraperitoneal injection of TAA three times per week for three weeks to induce liver fibrosis. For the P. dorei or B. cellulosilyticus group, the 109 CFU of P. dorei or B. cellulosilyticus was orally administered twice per week for three weeks, respectively, after 3 days adaptation period of DDC diet or TAA injection. The ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) was used as a positive control of treatment effect.

The mice in the P. dorei, B. cellulosilyticus, and UDCA groups showed significantly lower liver weight compared to the DDC group mice. The liver/body (L/B) ratio of mice in the P. dorei, B. cellulosilyticus, and UDCA groups was significantly lower than that of mice in the DDC group (Fig. 2A). The degree of liver fibrosis was histologically confirmed by liver staining with hematoxyline & eosin (H&E), masson’s trichrome (MT), and Sirius red (Fig. 2B). The fibrosis staging and fatty grading were calculated by the clinical research network scoring system. The P. dorei and B. cellulosilyticus groups showed significantly decreased grading, staging, and Sirius red-stained area. The expression levels of Col1a1, Timp1, and Tgf-β were significantly increased in the DDC group compared to the normal group and significantly decreased in the P. dorei group compared to the DDC group (Fig. 2C). The Il-10 expression was significantly increased only in the B. cellulosilyticus group compared to the normal group. To evaluate intestinal barrier integrity, we measured endotoxin levels in both serum and feces (Fig. 2D). In the serum, DDC significantly increased endotoxin levels (328.7 ± 22.4) compared to Normal controls (157.2 ± 19.4), whereas P. dorei supplementation effectively reduced this systemic translocation (230 ± 22.6, p < 0.0009). Conversely, fecal endotoxin levels were significantly higher in the P. dorei group (2118.8 ± 236.1) than in the DDC (444.7 ± 218.5) and Normal (1297 ± 246.5) groups. These data indicate that P. dorei strengthens the intestinal barrier, limiting the leakage of luminal endotoxins into the circulation and resulting in their increased fecal retention. The expression levels of markers for bile acid metabolism, including Bacs, Fxr, Shp, Cyp8b1, Hnf4a, and Mmp2 in the liver, were measured by qRT-PCR. The expression levels of markers for bile acid metabolism were significantly decreased in the DDC group compared to the normal group and significantly increased in the P. dorei group compared to the DDC group (Fig. 2E).

In TAA-induced fibrosis model, we found significantly decreased liver weight in the P. dorei and B. cellulosilyticus groups compared to the TAA group (Fig. 2F). We confirmed fibrosis by H&E, MT, and Sirius red staining. We observed significantly low grading and staging levels in the P. dorei group compared to the TAA group. We also found that the area stained with Sirius red was significantly decreased in the P. dorei and B. cellulosilyticus groups compared to the TAA group (Fig. 2G). The expression levels of Col1a1, Timp1, and Il-10 in the P. dorei group were significantly decreased compared to the TAA group (Fig. 2H).

In conclusion, oral gavage of P. dorei inhibits the progression of murine hepatic fibrosis induced by the DDC diet.

P. dorei modulates gut microbiotas in DDC diet-induced liver fibrosis model

At the phylum level, the composition of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Verrucomicrobia, and ETC is different for each group (NC [3%, 38%, 59%, 0%, 0%], DDC [9%, 39%, 51%, 0%, 0%], P. dorei [12%, 46%, 38%, 4%, 0%], and BC [6%, 50%, 41%, 3%, 0%]) (Fig. 3A). At the species level, species including Lactobacillus were analyzed. The ratio of Lactobacillus in DDC was reduced compared with NC, and it showed to be increased in P. dorei and B. cellulosilyticus. Interestingly, the ratio of L. murinus group, which is known to mediate anti-inflammatory effects in P. dorei (0.3%) and BC (0.2%), was high compared to NC (0.04%) and DDC (0.006%) (Fig. 3B). It was expressed as a heatmap to easily understand the changes in gut microbiome at the species level (Fig. 3C). Akkermansia muciniphila was increased in P. dorei (4.1%) and B. cellulosilyticus (2.8%) compared to DDC (0.06%).

P. dorei supplementation improves liver fibrosis by modulating intestinal environment

Liver α-SMA protein expression was significantly elevated in DDC-fed mice and was markedly attenuated by P. dorei treatment (Fig. 3F). This reduction was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining, which showed that the high density of α-SMA positive signals (indicated by yellow arrows) in the DDC group was substantially diminished following P. dorei administration (Fig. 3F). Intestinal permeability was evaluated by serum fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FD4) fluorescence and transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements. DDC-fed mice exhibited significantly increased serum FD4 levels and reduced TEER values compared with normal controls, indicating impaired intestinal barrier function. Treatment with P. dorei markedly decreased serum FD4 fluorescence and restored TEER, reflecting improved intestinal barrier integrity (Fig. 3G, 3H). P. dorei treatment restored tight junction protein expression and preserved intestinal mucosal thickness in DDC-fed mice (Fig. 3I). Species-specific qPCR analysis revealed that oral administration of P. dorei resulted in efficient fecal colonization, with relative abundance (2⁻ΔCt, normalized to total 16S rRNA) increasing after each gavage and remaining detectable over time (Fig. 3J). These findings indicate that repeated oral gavage (days 1, 3, and 5) enables stable intestinal persistence of P. dorei.

Although hepatic hydroxyproline levels tended to be lower in the P. dorei-treated group compared with the DDC group, the difference was not statistically significant, indicating a modest but non-significant reduction in collagen accumulation (Fig. 3K). Hepatic triglyceride levels tended to be lower in the treatment group compared with the control group; however, this reduction did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3L).

P. dorei culture supernatant attenuates LPS/TGF-β–induced activation of HSCs. To investigate whether soluble factors derived from P. dorei directly modulate HSCs activation, LX-2 cells were stimulated with LPS and TGF-β in the presence or absence of P. dorei culture supernatant (Fig. 3M). Cell viability is a measure of the proportion of live cells within a population. LX-2 cells were exposed to LPS at a concentration of 100 ng/mL, while P. dorei culture media were treated for 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, and 12 h. The viability of LX-2 cells exposed to LPS always decreased. However, treatment with P. dorei culture media recovered or increased viability (Fig. 3N). When LX-2 cell exposed to LPS and treated with P. dorei culture media, the expression of COL1A1 and TIMP1 was decreased (Fig. 3O). It showed that the P. dorei culture media increased viability and suppressed the fibrosis in LX-2 cells.

We analyzed the metabolite profile of P. dorei culture supernatant (Supplementary Fig. 7) and previously demonstrated that treatment with P. dorei culture medium effectively attenuates hepatic inflammation in a MASLD model11. Taken together, these findings suggest that P. dorei-derived metabolites or soluble factors play a critical role in modulating hepatic fibrogenesis.

P. dorei regulates fibrosis, neutrophil degradation, and phagocytosis pathway in HSCs

We observed that induction of Tgf-β, an HSC activation marker, in the DDC group was restored by oral gavage of P. dorei (Fig. 2C). To identify the effects of P. dorei on the activation of HSCs, we isolated RNA from HSCs of the normal, DDC, and P. dorei group mice and performed comparative transcriptome analysis by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Distinctive differences among the three groups were detected in the principal component analysis (PCA) of RNA-seq data (Fig. 4A). Global change of transcriptome was observed in the hierarchical clustering analysis between the normal and DDC group. A total of 321 genes and 457 genes were upregulated and downregulated in the DDC group compared to the normal group, respectively. In the P. dorei group, 382 genes were upregulated, and 140 genes were downregulated compared to the DDC group. There were 387 genes that showed oppositely regulated expression levels between the DDC to normal group and the P. dorei to DDC group (Fig. 4B). The expression levels of markers for fibrosis were significantly changed in the DDC group compared to the normal group and significantly improved in the P. dorei group compared to the DDC group (Fig. 4C, 4D).

Categorization of genes by reactome pathway and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway revealed that genes related to neutrophil degranulation, lysosome, and phagosome were DDC specifically induced (Fig. 4E). The DDC-specific genes related to neutrophil degranulation and phagosome showed decreased expression levels in the P. dorei group compared to the DDC group, resulting in similar gene expression profiles between the normal and P. dorei group. The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) also showed that genes related to neutrophil degranulation and phagocytosis were upregulated in the DDC group (Fig. 4F, 4G). The correlations of genes involved in neutrophil degranulation and phagosome pathways (Fig. 4G) were also determined. Based on these results, it was concluded that neutrophil degranulation and phagocytosis pathways are activated by DDC diet, and P. dorei might inhibit activation of corresponding pathways in HSCs.

Network-topology analysis was applied to distinguish primary regulatory drivers from downstream transcriptomic changes induced by P. dorei. Hub gene identification based on degree centrality revealed that genes involved in phagocytosis and efferocytosis formed a highly connected core module, suggesting that restoration of this clearance program represents a primary regulatory axis rather than a secondary inflammatory consequence. Consistently, transcription factor enrichment analysis of hub-centered genes identified SPI1 (PU.1) as a dominant upstream regulator (Figs. 4H and 4I).

Based on these results, it was concluded that neutrophil degranulation and phagocytosis pathways are activated by DDC diet and P. dorei might inhibit activation of corresponding pathways in HSCs.

P. dorei modulates the infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils in liver

To confirm the relationship between P. dorei and macrophages or neutrophils in the liver, immunofluorescence staining of the liver using two markers, F4/80 and Ly6G, was performed in the normal, DDC diet, and DDC diet with oral gavage of P. dorei group mice (Fig. 5A). The increased infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils was observed in the liver of the DDC diet group compared to the normal group. Interestingly, oral gavage of P. dorei reduced the infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils in the liver (Fig. 5B). We observed similar results in the expression of mRNA levels of the F4/80 and Ly6g, which indicated that P. dorei plays a role in the regulation of the infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils in the liver (Fig. 5C).

P. dorei regulates genes related to CC and CXC chemokines and neutrophil degranulation

The changes in the expression levels of CC and CXC chemokines, known to be involved in recruitment for injured tissues and activation of neutrophils, were observed in the comparative transcriptome analysis of HSCs. To confirm whether P. dorei regulates the neutrophil infiltration by the expression levels of CC and CXC chemokines, the expression levels of CC and CXC chemokines in the liver were measured by qRT-PCR. The gene expression levels of Ccl2 and Cxcl2 and their corresponding receptors Ccr2, Ccr5, and Cxcr2 were significantly increased in the DDC group compared to the normal group. The expression levels of those genes were significantly decreased in the P. dorei group. The Cxcl1 expression was significantly increased in the DDC and P. dorei groups compared to the normal group. The change in the Ccl3 expression levels among the three groups was not statistically significant (Fig. 5D).

We also checked the expression levels of granules released during neutrophil degranulation and their membrane receptors. There are at least four types of granules in neutrophils: azurophilic granules (primary), specific granules (secondary), gelatinase (tertiary), and secretory vesicles. The order of granules released during neutrophil degranulation is secretory vesicles, tertiary granules, secondary granules, and primary granules. The gene expression levels of the main components and receptors of granules belonging to secretory vesicles, gelatinase, and specific granules were significantly decreased in the P. dorei group. There was no significant difference in gene expression of Ne (belonging to azurophilic) known to be released lastly. However, the expression of Cd63, a membrane receptor expressed during the release of azurophilic granules, was increased in the DDC group compared to the normal group. Cd63 expression was decreased in P. dorei group (Fig. 5E). These results suggested that P. dorei modulates not only neutrophil infiltration but also neutrophil activation and the degranulation process.

In addition to the neutrophil degranulation, the phagosome pathway was affected by the DDC diet and P. dorei intake in comparative transcriptome analysis of HSCs. To confirm that the expression levels of genes involved in the phagosome pathway were changed by the DDC diet or P. dorei intake in the liver, the expression of several genes in the phagosome pathway was measured by qRT-PCR. The phagosome pathway-related genes, including Macro, Itgb2, Itgam, Clec7a, H2-M2, Ctss, and Cyba showed increased expression in the DDC group and similar expression in the P. dorei group compared to the normal group (Fig. 5F). These results indicated that the DDC diet activates the phagosome pathway, and P. dorei inhibits the activation of the phagosome pathway.

Ingestion of P. dorei inhibits the expression of efferocytosis-specific genes

The apoptosis pathway was affected by the DDC diet. Expression of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), which inhibits apoptosis, is regulated by the p38 MAPK pathway. Inhibition of the p38 pathway can reduce COX-2 expression, resulting in apoptosis. Protein expression levels of phospho-p38 (p-p38) and COX-2 were determined by Western blot. It was found that p38 phosphorylation and COX-2 expression were decreased in the DDC group compared to the normal group, but increased in the P. dorei group compared to the DDC group (Fig. 6A). This result suggested that apoptosis increased by the DDC diet might be reduced by P. dorei supplementation.

Efferocytosis is a clearance process of apoptotic cells by phagocytes. During cell dying, apoptotic cells release chemokines, lipids, and nucleotides, which cause rapid recruitment of efferocytic immune cells. The recruited efferocytic immune cells recognize apoptotic cells by signal molecules such as phosphatidylserine (PS), calreticulin, intercellular adhesion molecule 3 (ICAM3) and glycosylation moieties. PS is the most important apoptotic cell-associated signal. The PS direct receptors Stab1 and Timd4, bridge molecules Mfge8 and Pros1, and PS indirect receptors Axl and Cd36 are involved in the process of efferocytosis. We hypothesized that P. dorei might have a role in the protection of hepatic fibrosis by the regulation of efferocytosis based on the phagosome pathway-related gene expression and the apoptosis-related protein analysis results. To confirm whether P. dorei regulates efferocytosis, the expression levels of efferocytosis-related genes were measured in the normal, DDC diet, and P. dorei groups. The chemokine Cx3cl1 and its receptor Cx3cr1 gene expression levels were significantly increased in the DDC group and restored in the P. dorei group. The expression levels of PS direct receptors Stab1 and Timd4 were not changed significantly between the normal and DDC group. The bridge molecule genes Mfge8 and Pros1, and PS indirect receptor genes Axl and Cd36 showed increased expression levels in the DDC group compared to the normal group. The expression levels of those four genes were significantly decreased by supplementation of P. dorei. An internalization machinery gene Elmo1, expression was induced by the DDC diet, and this induction was inhibited by the P. dorei intake. Another internalization machinery gene Rac1 did not show significant change in expression among the groups (Fig. 6B). These results demonstrated that P. dorei intake might inhibit the process of efferocytosis via the regulation of signaling molecules and receptors.

Notably, the protective effects of P. dorei were also evident in the therapeutic DDC fibrosis model (Fig. 6C, 6D). Administration of P. dorei after fibrosis establishment significantly accelerated fibrosis resolution, as indicated by a decreased L/B ratio and a marked reduction in fibrotic area (Fig. 6D). Consistent with the histological improvements, P. dorei treatment led to a significant decrease in hepatic triglyceride (TG) levels, suggesting a reduction in lipid accumulation. Although the reduction in hydroxyproline levels, a key indicator of collagen content, did not reach statistical significance in this specific therapeutic model, it showed a downward trend consistent with the overall decrease in pro-fibrotic gene expression, such as Col1a1 and Mmp2 (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, P. dorei significantly improved intestinal permeability, evidenced by a reduction in serum FD4 fluorescence and the restoration of average crypt depth in the colon (Fig. 6E). In addition, P. dorei treatment markedly modulated efferocytosis-related pathways (Fig. 6F), as reflected by the downregulation of key efferocytosis-associated genes, indicating that P. dorei exerts therapeutic effects not only by preventing fibrosis progression but also by actively promoting recovery through regulation of efferocytosis-linked immune processes.

Model-dependent regulation of fibrosis-related pathways by P. dorei

Transcriptomic and pathway analyses following P. dorei supplementation revealed coordinated downregulation of neutrophil-related, chemokine signaling, and phagosome-associated pathways, raising the possibility that chemokine receptor–mediated signaling may contribute to the observed protective effects. In particular, CXCR2, a key receptor involved in neutrophil recruitment and inflammatory regulation, emerged as a candidate mediator linking host immune modulation to P. dorei administration.

To functionally evaluate whether CXCR2-dependent signaling represents a mechanistic axis underlying the effects of P. dorei, we therefore performed pharmacological CXCR2 inhibition using SB225002 in the DDC-induced liver injury model (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Although CXCR2 blockade alone modestly reduced fibrosis compared to the DDC control, P. dorei supplementation consistently exerted the strongest antifibrotic effect at the histological level. Notably, co-treatment with the CXCR2 antagonist SB225002 did not further enhance the protective effects of P. dorei. Integrated analysis of histopathological and transcriptional readouts did not reveal a uniform pattern supporting CXCR2-dependent signaling as a dominant mechanism mediating the effects of P. dorei.

We next examined whether the antifibrotic effects of P. dorei were conserved across distinct chronic liver disease models. P. dorei administration significantly ameliorated hepatic injury and fibrosis across chronic liver disease models, including Western diet–induced MASLD, streptozotocin–Western diet–induced steatohepatitis model (STAM), and choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined, high-fat diet (CDAHFD)-induced cirrhosis models, supporting its relevance beyond acute toxin-induced injury. However, pathway analyses revealed marked model-dependent differences. In the MASLD, STAM, and CDAHFD models, efferocytosis-associated pathways were not robustly or consistently regulated (Supplementary Fig. 8B–8D). In contrast, efferocytosis-related signatures were clearly regulated in the DDC treatment model (Fig. 6C–6F), where P. dorei significantly modulated these pathways during the resolution phase of toxin-induced fibrosis. Together, these results indicate that P. dorei exerts consistent antifibrotic effects across disease models, while engaging distinct molecular pathways depending on the injury context.

P. dorei attenuates macrophage-mediated efferocytosis and HSCs activation

In vitro experiment was conducted to determine whether efferocytosis of macrophages by neutrophil apoptosis could stimulate HSCs. First, after stimulating HL-60 cells with DMSO, the expression of CD11B expressed in neutrophil-differentiated cells was determined. After 5 days of treatment with 1.3% DMSO, it was confirmed that the expression of CdD11bB was increased (Fig. 7A). After that, differentiated neutrophils were irradiated with UV to prepare an apoptotic body. To determine whether apoptotic bodies could directly stimulate HSCs, HSCs were treated with apoptotic bodies, and mRNA expression was examined 24 hours later. TGF-β, COL1A1, and TIMP1 showed no difference in expression between treatment with and without apoptotic bodies (Fig. 7B). After co-culture of LX-2 cells and differentiated THP-1 cells, apoptotic bodies and P. dorei culture supernatant were used for treatment. As a result, it was confirmed that expression levels of TNF-α (an inflammatory marker) and ACTA2 and TGF-β (fibrosis markers) were increased compared to those in the control when treated with apoptotic bodies. However, they were decreased when cells were treated with P. dorei culture supernatant (Fig. 7C, 7D). These results show that efferocytosis of macrophages by apoptotic bodies of neutrophils can stimulate HSCs and that P. dorei can reduce the activity of HSCs.

To validate the relevance of our experimental findings in humans, we analyzed circulating efferocytosis-related factors across disease stages. Z-score standardized trajectories reveal a synchronized increase in P. dorei abundance (P_trend<0.01) and serum efferocytosis markers (Gas6, P_trend<0.001; MER,P_trend<0.05) across disease progression. Conversely, the putative homeostatic tdh gene exhibits a divergent downward trend P_trend=0.098), highlighting specific functional impairment despite the species’ clonal expansion. As liver disease progressed, serum levels of efferocytosis-associated markers were progressively increased, indicating systemic activation of efferocytosis-related pathways in advanced disease (Fig. 7E). Integrative analysis of microbial and host data revealed that although the relative abundance of P. dorei increased with disease severity, genomic analyses indicated a functional genetic deficiency in P. dorei-associated pathways (Fig. 7F) Thus, the observed increase in P. dorei appears to reflect a quantitative expansion with impaired functional capacity, rather than an effective protective response.

Discussion

Liver fibrosis results from excessive extracellular matrix accumulation during chronic liver disease and can progress to cirrhosis and liver failure21. Despite its clinical burden, no approved therapy for liver cirrhosis currently exists. Emerging evidence indicates that gut microbiota composition differs markedly between cirrhotic patients and healthy individuals7. Transplantation of gut microbiota in pre-cirrhotic or cirrhotic patients can also induce alterations in a patient’s gut microbiome, thus modifying the progression of liver fibrosis22,23.

Although P. dorei abundance increases in patients with hepatitis and cirrhosis, this observation does not contradict its protective role. Our strain-level genomic and functional analyses indicate that P. dorei enriched in advanced liver disease exhibits a marked loss of immune- and inflammation-related functions, with longitudinal data further revealing progressive functional decline despite stable or increased abundance. Consistent with prior reports that commensals undergo pathoadaptive reductive evolution by shedding metabolically costly symbiotic genes surveillance19,20, this expansion likely reflects a compensatory yet biologically ineffective state, underscoring that microbial abundance alone does not equate to protective capacity.

As a result of metascape analysis, expression levels of genes related to neutrophil degranulation and phagosome pathway were downregulated after ingestion of P. dorei. Genes related to phagocytosis were also downregulated through GSEA. These results suggest that HSCs can be activated by efferocytosis, which can remove apoptotic bodies. Our results suggest that P. dorei can protect the liver from the process of neutrophil degranulation and efferocytosis.

Based on previous results, P. dorei could have a hepatoprotective effect by regulating neutrophils and macrophages. Damage to liver tissues can lead to infiltration of leukocytes, monocytes, and macrophages into the tissue24. Excessive infiltration and activation of neutrophils can induce inflammation and limit organ repair25. One paper has shown that HSC activation is reduced when macrophage infiltration is blocked26. These results suggest that inflammatory cell infiltration is associated with liver damage and that preventing recruitment and infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages is important in resolving liver fibrosis.

CC and CXC chemokines produced by hepatocytes promote macrophage and neutrophil recruitment, driving neutrophil-dependent cytotoxicity and liver fibrosis. Receptors such as CCR2, CCR5, CXCR1, and CXCR2 play central roles in these inflammatory processes, and their inhibition has been shown to attenuate hepatic fibrosis27. In our study, P. dorei significantly reduced the expression of CC and CXC chemokine genes, suggesting a hepatoprotective effect mediated by suppression of neutrophil migration and activation.

Neutrophil degranulation involves the sequential release of four granule types, such as secretory, gelatinase, specific, and azurophilic. These granules collectively mediate inflammatory signaling, adhesion, tissue degradation, and microbial killing25. Dysregulated release of these granules contributes to inflammatory tissue injury. In the P. dorei group, expression of genes associated with all four granule types was reduced, suggesting that P. dorei suppresses neutrophil degranulation and thereby limits neutrophil-mediated liver damage.

Efferocytosis is a multistep process involving engulfment of apoptotic cells and phagolysosomal degradation and plays context-dependent roles in liver homeostasis and fibrosis28. While efficient efferocytosis can resolve inflammation via IL-10 and pro-resolving mediators29, dysregulated or excessive efferocytosis, particularly of apoptotic neutrophils, can activate TGF-β–dependent pathways, including MerTK–ERK–TGF-β1 signaling, leading to HSCs activation and fibrosis. In our study, the coordinated downregulation of efferocytosis-related signatures, together with reduced neutrophil-associated inflammatory programs and functional inhibition of efferocytosis by P. dorei-derived factors, is most consistent with attenuation of pathogenic neutrophil–macrophage efferocytosis.

Although P. dorei consistently attenuated liver injury and fibrosis across MASLD, STAM, CDAHFD, and TAA models, efferocytosis-associated pathways were not robustly engaged in these settings, particularly in the TAA model where toxin-induced injury occurs largely independent of the gut–liver axis. In contrast, efferocytosis-related signatures were clearly modulated in the gut-mediated DDC recovery model, indicating that macrophage efferocytosis of neutrophils represents a context-dependent mechanism specific to acute cholestatic injury. Accordingly, a key limitation of this study is that the efferocytosis-based mechanism was most prominently validated in the DDC model, and the therapeutic relevance of P. dorei should therefore be interpreted in an etiology-specific manner, warranting further validation in longer-term and diverse fibrosis models.

Activation of p38 and COX-2 signaling has been associated with reduced apoptosis through PGE2-mediated upregulation of anti-apoptotic pathways and suppression of HSC activation30–33. In line with this, increased p-p38 and COX-2 protein expression in the P. dorei group suggests a hepatoprotective effect accompanied by reduced apoptosis. Because efferocytosis is triggered by apoptotic cell–derived find-me and eat-me signals, a reduction in apoptosis is expected to attenuate subsequent efferocytotic processes. Consistently, P. dorei treatment decreased the expression of efferocytosis receptors and phagosome-related genes, indicating impaired apoptotic body–macrophage interactions and reduced efferocytosis.

Neutrophils contribute to host defense during liver injury but can also exacerbate fibrosis through excessive activation and interaction with HSCs34,35. Our findings indicate that P. dorei reduces neutrophil and macrophage infiltration, suggesting attenuation of inflammatory cell–driven fibrotic responses.

Cell experiments were conducted to prove that the efferocytosis process could stimulate HSCs. First, it was confirmed whether the apoptotic body of neutrophils could directly stimulate HSCs. As a result, it was found that the apoptotic body could not directly stimulate HSCs. When THP-1 cells differentiated into macrophages and LX-2 cells (HSCs) were co-cultured, and apoptotic bodies and P. dorei supernatant were used for treatment, the P. dorei supernatant protected the liver from the efferocytosis process. These results suggest that P. dorei can reduce HSCs activation by efferocytosis.

In the network-topology analysis, transcription factor enrichment analysis of hub-centered genes identified SPI1 (PU.1) as a dominant upstream regulator. SPI1 is a well-established master regulator of myeloid phagocyte differentiation and efferocytosis, governing transcriptional programs essential for macrophage homeostasis36,37. These findings support a model in which P. dorei normalizes a SPI1-centered phagocytic/efferocytic network, with coordinated downregulation of neutrophil degranulation and chemokine pathways emerging as downstream effects. This systems-level framework provides mechanistic prioritization that complements pathway enrichment analyses.

P. dorei could protect the liver from fibrosis. In vitro stimulation of LX-2 cells with LPS and TGF-β increased the expression of genes related to fibrogenesis38. Afterwards, when cells were treated with P. dorei supernatant, fibrosis markers were reduced. These results suggest that P. dorei supernatant might have a direct protective effect on HSCs.

The increase in fecal endotoxin observed following P. dorei supplementation does not contradict its barrier-protective effect. P. dorei administration enhanced intestinal tight junction integrity and significantly reduced circulating endotoxin levels, indicating decreased translocation of luminal endotoxin into the systemic circulation. Consequently, impaired absorption and increased luminal retention of endotoxin are reflected as higher fecal endotoxin levels. These findings support the interpretation that P. dorei strengthens intestinal barrier function by limiting endotoxin uptake rather than reducing total luminal endotoxin burden.

Although it is difficult to completely disentangle primary microbial effects from secondary changes driven by fibrosis regression, our data indicate that P. dorei-derived metabolites exert direct immunomodulatory effects. Immune-related gene expression was altered at early stages before overt fibrotic remodeling39, and P. dorei culture supernatant modulated efferocytosis-related pathways in the absence of extracellular matrix deposition. These findings support a dual-layer mechanism in which P. dorei-derived metabolites initiate primary immune modulation, which is subsequently reinforced by secondary immune normalization accompanying fibrosis attenuation.

In conclusion, P. dorei reduces the infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages in the liver and protects the liver by interfering with the connection between apoptotic bodies and macrophages, leading to a decrease in efferocytosis (Fig. 8). Our results suggest that P. dorei could be a promising pharmabiotic against liver fibrosis. More research on its mechanism is needed for effective prevention and treatment of liver fibrosis in the future.

Methods

Human study

The prospective observational study was carried out between April 2017 and March 2020 (Supplementary Fig. 1). A total of 1067 patients comprising of pariticipients with 16 s rRNA gene sequencing microbial community profiling (n = 219) and shotgun metagenomic deep strain level analysis (n = 66) were enrolled and analysed. The patients underwent standard treatment for their disease regardless of their enrollment in the study.

MASLD and MASH, and MetALD was diagnosed based on the definition described in the consensus statement on the new fatty liver disease nomenclature40,41. Cardiometabolic criteria including body mass index, fasting glucose, medication history, blood pressure, or cholesterol level, were used to diagnose MASLD. Healthy control group was collected from health promotion center. The hepatitis group comprised patients exhibiting elevated liver enzymes AST or ALT ≥ 50 IU/L or histological evidence of hepatitis. Cirrhosis was diagnosed based on the presence of complications (varix, ascites, and encephalopathy), blood tests, imaging findings, fibroscan, or pathological liver results42.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: consumption of probiotics (lactic acid bacteria, etc.), prebiotics (dietary fiber, fructooligosaccharide, etc.), synbiotics, and fermented milk in the month before the study; continuously taking antibiotics within the last 2 months or take antibiotics during the study period; continuously consumed medicines or health functional foods that affect liver function within the last 1 month; participation in other clinical trials within the last 1 month (however, this does not apply to medical device clinical trials); hereditary metabolic disease, autoimmune hepatitis; systemic inflammatory disease or immune disease; hepatocellular carcinoma or a history of malignant tumor diagnosis within the last 5 years; uncontrolled cardiopulmonary disease; other serious systemic disorders of the heart, lungs, blood, and endocrine system; pregnant or lactating women; hypersensitivity to the test drug/placebo or components contained in the test drug/placebo or experienced severe allergic reactions. Patients with a history of autoimmune hepatitis, pancreatitis, hemochromatosis, Wilson’s disease, cancer, and drug-induced liver injury. Informed agreement for enrollment was received from each participant.

Baseline evaluation was performed on complete blood count, liver function test, and viral markers. Patients with cirrhosis underwent abdominal ultrasound or computed tomography. AST, ALT, ALP, ALB, GGT, T-BIL, and Creatine were included serum biochemical parameters. Enrolled patients and control groups underwent stool sampling and clinical analysis. This project followed the ethics of the 1975 Helsinki Declaration, as reflected by a prior approval by the Chuncheon Sacred Heart Hospital Institutional Review Board for human research (2016-11-134 and 2022-01-027)(ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04339725). Informed consent was obtained from all the participants. All authors had access to the study data and reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and library quality control

Human fecal samples were immediately frozen at −20 °C upon collection and transferred to a −80 °C freezer within 24 h under a strictly maintained cold chain, whereas mouse whole intestines were directly stored at −80 °C post-euthanasia. For genomic DNA extraction, the SPINeasy DNA Pro Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA) was specifically used for human samples intended for shotgun metagenomics. Conversely, the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) was utilized for mouse samples and all 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing. Following the respective DNA extractions and library preparations, the constructed libraries were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA). The final libraries were quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and confirmed by qPCR with the KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Mix (Kapa Biosystems, USA). Library size and quality were further validated on a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, USA) equipped with a DNA 12000 chip.

16S rRNA amplicon and shotgun metagenomic sequencing

For 16S rRNA gene sequencing, the V3-V4 hypervariable region was amplified using the forward fusion primer 341 F and the reverse fusion primer 805 R, incorporating Nextera XT dual-index barcodes. Following AMPure XP bead purification and pooling, the amplicon libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform using reagent kit V3 (2 × 250 bp paired-end) at ChunLab, Inc. (Republic of Korea). Taxonomic profiling was conducted via the EZBioCloud platform and its comparative MTP analyzer using the PKSSU4.0 database. OTUs were clustered at a 97% similarity threshold using UCLUST and CD-HIT. Subsequently, rarefaction and alpha-diversity indices (Simpson, Jackknife, ACE, Chao1, Shannon, NPShannon, and Good’s coverage), as well as beta-diversity (PCoA and UPGMA), were evaluated.

For shotgun metagenomics, sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II FS DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, USA) following the low-input protocol. Sequencing was performed at CJ Bioscience, Inc. (Republic of Korea) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the NovaSeq 6000 Reagent Kit (300 cycles, 2 × 150 bp paired-end), targeting a raw data output of at least 10 Gb per sample.

Taxonomic and functional profiling of metagenomes

To decipher the taxonomic composition and functional potential of the microbiome, raw sequencing reads were analyzed using the BioBakery 4 meta-omics workflow. KneadData (v0.12.0) was employed for data pre-processing, invoking Trimmomatic (v0.39) to eliminate adapter contamination and trim low-quality regions based on a sliding window algorithm (SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20). Furthermore, reads falling below a length threshold of 50 bp were excluded (MINLEN:50) to guarantee high-quality input for downstream characterization.

Taxonomic classification was resolved via MetaPhlAn 4 (v4.0.6), mapping reads against the comprehensive marker gene database mpa_vJun23_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202403. The pangenomes of detected species were subsequently utilized to build sample-specific reference databases. Functional profiling was carried out using the HUMAnN pipeline (v3.9). For protein-level functional annotation, nucleotide sequences were aligned to the UniRef90 protein database (release 201901b) using the accelerated alignment tool DIAMOND (v2.0.15). To ensure robust annotations, we applied strict filtering criteria, requiring a minimum of 80% sequence identity and 90% query coverage. Finally, the abundances of identified gene families (UniRef90s) were stratified by their source species and aggregated into KEGG Orthologs (KOs) to elucidate metabolic pathways.

Strain-level phylogenetic and pangenome analysis

To characterize the strain-level evolutionary dynamics of specific species (P. dorei), we utilized StrainPhlAn 4 (v4.0.6). Consensus markers were extracted from metagenomic reads to reconstruct a phylogenetic tree, which was visualized using the ggtree package in R to illustrate disease-specific clustering patterns. Functional gene content was profiled using PanPhlAn 3 (v3.1). Reads were mapped against the species-specific pangenome database, and gene presence/absence profiles were determined using a minimum coverage threshold (1x) and stringent empirical detection cut-offs to minimize false positives. The resulting binary matrix was visualized via hierarchical clustering heatmaps. Furthermore, to identify functional markers associated with disease severity, we performed Fisher’s Exact Test to compare the prevalence of each gene family across groups. Genes showing statistically significant differences in prevalence (P < 0.05) were visualized using bar plots with 95% confidence intervals.

Metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) reconstruction and comparative structural genomics

Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were reconstructed from quality-filtered shotgun reads using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) with meta-sensitive parameters (minimum contig length: 1000 bp). Sequencing reads were mapped back to the assembled contigs using Bowtie2 (v2.2.3), followed by contig binning via MetaBAT2 (v2.12.1). Taxonomic classification and genome quality assessment were conducted using GTDB-Tk (v2.4.1) and CheckM (v1.2.4), respectively. For downstream comparative genomics, we strictly retained representative P. dorei MAGs from the disease cohort that met high-quality thresholds (completeness > 80% and contamination < 5%)

To evaluate structural divergence and physical genomic erosions, the reconstructed P. dorei MAGs and the therapeutic strain were aligned against the completely sequenced healthy reference genome (P. dorei DSM 17855). This comparative genomic architecture was visualized using the BLAST Ring Image Generator (BRIG, v0.95).

Bacterial culture and dosage determination

The evaluated Phocaeicola dorei strain (Supplementary Table 1), originally isolated from human feces and provided by CJ Bioscience, Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea), was identified based on its 16S rRNA gene sequence. The bacteria were cultivated anaerobically in Reinforced Clostridial Medium (RCM) at 35 °C for 24 h. To prepare the culture supernatant, the culture was centrifuged (3400 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and passed through a 0.45-μm filter. The P. dorei dose for mice (1.0 × 10⁹ CFU) was calculated using a body surface area-based conversion (Km factor = 12.33) from an established human equivalent dose (1.0 × 10¹¹ CFU/day) and administered via oral gavage twice a week.

Animal experiments

Specific-pathogen-free 5-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from Doo Yeol Biotech (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Only male mice were used to minimize the potential confounding effects of hormonal variations and to ensure consistency in the development of liver fibrosis and MASLD models, in accordance with SAGER guidelines. Animals were housed in a controlled facility (22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50% ± 10%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Prior to experimental interventions, all mice underwent a 1-week acclimatization period on a normal diet. All animal procedures were performed in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were officially approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the College of Medicine, Hallym University (approval no. 2021-25).

Animal models of liver fibrosis and injury

Following the acclimatization period, mice were randomly assigned to the following disease models. For all P. dorei-treated groups, the bacterial strain (1.0 × 10⁹ CFU) was administered via oral gavage twice a week. Control groups received an equal volume of PBS.

DDC and TAA fibrosis

To induce biliary fibrosis, mice were fed a diet (DY-DM-5700 2018; DooYeol Biotech, Seoul, Republic of Korea) containing 0.1% 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) from Day 7 to Day 30. For the recovery model, the DDC diet was replaced with a standard diet (DY-TD-2018; DooYeol Biotech) for a 12-day recovery phase (Day 30 to Day 42), during which mice received P. dorei or PBS twice weekly and were sacrificed on Day 42. For CXCR2 inhibition, DDC-fed mice received IP injections of SB225002 (10 mg/kg in 100 µL) twice weekly. In the TAA model, mice received IP injections of thioacetamide (TAA; Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in 100 µL of saline (JE IL Pharmaceutical, Daegu, Republic of Korea) three times a week with escalating doses (50–300 mg/kg).

WD and CDAHFD models

Mice were fed a Western diet (WD; DY-TD-8813, DooYeol Biotech) or a CDAHFD (custom-formulated by DooYeol Biotech based on Research Diets A06071302 with 1.2% cholesterol) for 20 weeks.

STAM model

Neonatal mice received a single subcutaneous injection of streptozotocin (STZ; 200 µg/mouse) 2 days after birth, followed by the transition to the WD (DY-TD-8813) starting at 4 weeks of age.

Intestinal permeability assay

To evaluate intestinal permeability, mice were administered 200 µl of fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FD4; 4 kDa; 120 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) via intragastric gavage 4 h prior to sacrifice. Following euthanization, blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 5,000 x g and 4 °C for 10 min to obtain serum. The concentration of FD4 in the serum was quantified by measuring fluorescence intensity (excitation: 485 nm, emission: 535 nm) using a Synergy H1 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Mice fed a standard normal chow were used as the control group to determine baseline permeability levels.

Trans-epithelial electrical resistance assay

For the trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements, Caco-2 cells were used as described previously43. Cells were inoculated into transwell-clear inserts (12-well clusters, 6.5-mm inserts with polyester membrane, pore diameter 0.4 μm, Corning NY) at a density of 105 cells/insert. Each insertion was located on top of a well in plates (1 ml-bottom and 200 μl media-top). Caco-2 cells were incubated for 5 days until confluence in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle with 20% fetal bovine serum without antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco, Carlsdad, CA, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% atmosphere.

TEER measurements were performed using a Millicell Electrical resistance system (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). When monolayer of cells reached the confluence, Caco-2 cells were co-incubated with 200 μl of OD600 0.3 bacterial suspension (7 × 107 CFU/ml) in MEM media for 8 h and the TEER was measured respectively. TEER values were compared with those of the control and expressed as the ratio of the TEER value of sample after 8 h co-incubation to the TEER value of control after 8 hr co-incubation.

Hepatic triglyceride assay

Hepatic TG levels were quantified from liver tissues (100 mg) using a colorimetric assay kit (ab65336; Abcam). Tissues were homogenized in 5% NP-40 and completely solubilized via two heat-cool cycles (80–100 °C). Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and results were normalized to tissue weight (nmol/mg).

Hepatic hydroxyproline assay

Hepatic collagen content was evaluated using the PicoSens™ Hydroxyproline Assay Kit (BIOMAX, Seoul, Korea). Liver tissues (10 mg) were homogenized and hydrolyzed with 12 N HCl at 120 °C for 3 h. Following evaporation at 70 °C, samples were colorimetrically reacted according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm using the Multiskan SkyHigh spectrophotometer, and concentrations were normalized to tissue weight (µg/mg).

Analysis of P. dorei colonization and persistence

To evaluate the colonization and persistence of P. dorei in the gastrointestinal tract, fecal samples were collected at baseline (day 0) and on Days 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20, and 25. Mice were orally administered with P. dorei (109 CFU) on Days 1, 3, and 5. Genomic DNA was extracted from the collected fecal pellets using a QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The abundance of P. dorei was quantified by quantitative PCR using strain-specific primers. The relative levels of P. dorei were calculated using the 2⁻ΔCt method, with the values normalized to total bacterial DNA or a reference gene.

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

Paraffin-embedded liver sections (4-µm thick) were stained with Masson’s Trichrome (cat. no. KH07013; Korea Bio, Korea) to evaluate collagen deposition. For immunofluorescence, tissue microarray cores (1.5-mm diameter) were subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in 10 mM sodium citrate. Following blocking (5% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100), sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-Ly6G (1:200; BioLegend, 127602), anti-F4/80 (1:100; Thermo Fisher, MA5-16363), and anti-α-SMA (1:200; Abcam, ab124964). Sections were then incubated with secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG, 1:500, A32731; anti-rat IgG, 1:500, A-21094; Thermo Fisher) and mounted with DAPI (Abcam, ab104139).

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer. Equal amounts of proteins were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were probed overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: phospho-p38, p38, and GAPDH (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, 4511S, 8690S, 5174S), α-SMA (1:10,000; Abcam, ab124964), COX-2 (1:1,000; Abcam, ab15191), Occludin (1:1,000; Abcam, ab167161), and Claudin-1 (1:1,000; Abcam, ab15098). Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000), bands were visualized using ECL substrate and an Amersham Imager 680. Uncropped scans are provided in the Source Data file.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent and reverse-transcribed using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 system using SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For Timp1, a Luna Universal Probe qPCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) and a target-specific probe (Applied Biosystems) were used. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Human ELISA assays

Circulating levels of human MER (MERTK) and Gas6 in patient serum or plasma were quantified using specific ELISA kits (BMS2285 and BMS2291; Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were pre-diluted (1:10 for MER and 1:200 for Gas6), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh spectrophotometer. Final concentrations were calculated using standard curves and adjusted by the respective dilution factors.

Cell culture and treatments

The LX-2 cell line was generated by immortalization of primary human HSCs. LX-2 cells were cultured in 5% CO2 at 37 °C in DMEM (Gibco-BRL, Rockville, MD, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% antibiotics (penicillin/streptomycin) (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA). Human monocytic cell line THP-1 cell and human leukemia cell line HL-60 cell were provided by KCLB (Korean Cell Line Bank). THP-1 cells and HL-60 cells were culture in 5% CO2 at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 (Gibco-BRL, Rockville, MD, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics (penicillin/streptomycin). THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages by incubation for 24 hours after treatment with 100 ng/ml phorbol myristate acetate44 and HL-60 cells were differentiated into neutrophils by incubation for 5 days with 1.3% DMSO45. LX-2 Cells (1.5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in a 6-well plate and incubated overnight. Then, cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/ml), TGF-β (10 ng/ml), and treated 10% of P. dorei culture supernatants and incubated for 24 h before total RNA isolation.

Preparation of apoptotic bodies and co-culture

HL-60 cells were differentiated into neutrophils and apoptosis was induced by UV irradiation. After 24 hours, apoptotic bodies were collected by centrifugation at 2000 × g, 4 min and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium46. The number of apoptotic bodies was determined by Trypan Blue staining. LX-2 cells (1.5 × 105 cells/well) were co-cultured with differentiated THP-1 cells (1 × 105 cells/well). Apoptotic bodies were added in a 3:1 ratio to THP-1 cells and exposed to 10% P. dorei supernatant for 24 hours.

Isolation of primary mouse hepatic stellate cells

Primary HSCs were isolated from male C57BL/6 J mice via in situ liver perfusion through the inferior vena cava. Livers were sequentially perfused with EGTA buffer, pronase (0.4 mg/mL; Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA), and collagenase (0.193 U/mg; Roche Diagnostics). The digested liver homogenate was filtered through a 70-µm mesh, and HSCs were purified using a density gradient of Cell-OptiPrep™ (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) prepared with Gey’s Balanced Salt Solution (GBSS; Sigma-Aldrich). Following centrifugation (1380 × g, 17 min, without braking), HSCs localized at the white interface were harvested and washed with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R software v4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For continuous variables, differences between two groups were assessed using the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, while comparisons among three groups (Normal, Hepatitis, Cirrhosis) were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc multiple comparison tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For the high-dimensional microbiome data, we employed MaAsLin2 (Multivariate Association with Linear Models 2) to identify microbial taxa associated with disease progression while strictly adjusting for potential confounding variables, including age, sex, and BMI. To control for false discoveries arising from multiple testing, raw P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure. For RNA-seq analysis, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the ExDEGA Analysis Tool (v.3.0.1; Ebiogen Inc., Korea), employing a rigorous combinatorial threshold (Fold Change ≥ 2, nominal P < 0.05, and Normalized log2 Expression Value ≥ 1) to exclude low-abundance noise and ensure robust biological relevance.

Model performance was quantified by the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve (AUC), and risk associations were reported as adjusted Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals.
