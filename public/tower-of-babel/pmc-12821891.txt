The correlation between the systemic immune-inflammatory index and the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events after transcatheter aortic valve replacement: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Rui Han, Zhenwei Wang, Hailong Zhang, Yujia Wu, Wanqian Pan, Hang Li, Xiaodong Qian, Haifeng Xu, Tingbo Jiang, Weixiang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study aimed to explore the association between the systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 138 patients who underwent TAVR surgery. SII was defined as an exposure variable, and MACE, including the occurrence of one or more of the following: all-cause death, cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke, was defined as an outcome variable. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, subgroup analyses, receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and restricted cubic spline plot (RCS) were used to explore the association between SII and MACE.ResultsDuring the median follow-up period of 12.5 months, 22 patients experienced MACE (15.94%). Compared with those without MACE, patients in the MACE group had higher levels of SII (P < 0.05). In multivariate Cox regression analysis, after adjusting for potential confounders, higher levels of SII were still statistically associated with a higher hazard of MACE. Each 1-standard deviation increase in SII was associated with a 29.7% higher hazard of MACE (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.297, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.054–1.597, P = 0.014). The subgroup analyses based on the Model 3 indicated that SII remained associated with MACE in most subgroups (P < 0.05). The ROC curve analysis indicated that SII showed a modest discriminatory ability for MACE (AUC = 0.697, 95% CI: 0.578–0.815, P = 0.004). The RCS analysis indicated a positive linear association between SII and MACE (P-nonlinear > 0.05).ConclusionIn patients undergoing TAVR, a higher level of SII was associated with a higher risk of MACE. However, these findings still require further validation in larger, multicenter prospective clinical trials.

Introduction

Calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) is the most prevalent form of valvular heart disease and the leading indication for valve replacement procedures worldwide. The pathological characteristics of CAVD include leaflet thickening, fibrosis, and calcification, which ultimately result in aortic valve stenosis [1]. Well-established risk factors for CAVD include advanced age, congenital bicuspid aortic valve, hypertension, lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), obesity, diabetes mellitus, and systemic inflammation [2, 3]. In addition, Mendelian randomization studies have further confirmed causal associations of blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), and Lp(a) levels with the risk of CAVD [4–6]. However, to date, no effective pharmacological therapy has been identified to halt the progression of CAVD. Traditional drugs targeting atherosclerosis, such as statins, have shown no clear benefit in prospective clinical trials. Although several novel targeted therapies have been investigated in recent years, the only proven effective treatment remains valve replacement, including surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) [1, 3]. At present, over 370,000 aortic valve replacement procedures are performed worldwide each year. By 2040, the prevalence of CAVD is projected to increase by 240%, with a particularly pronounced rise in developing countries, placing a considerable burden on healthcare systems [3]. Compared with SAVR, TAVR offers advantages such as shorter recovery time and lower perioperative risk, with more favorable outcomes in elderly and high-risk surgical patients. In addition, TAVR reduces the incidence of prosthesis-patient mismatch in patients with small annuli [7, 8]. As a result, TAVR has gradually become the mainstream treatment for severe aortic valve disease currently. Nevertheless, patients undergoing TAVR continue to experience a relatively high risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), underscoring the importance of identifying novel and potentially modifiable cardiovascular risk factors that may help inform future research on risk stratification and management for TAVR patients.

Inflammation plays an important role in the pathogenesis and progression of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [9, 10]. For instance, inflammation not only promotes the development of atherosclerotic plaques but also enhances oxidative stress and inflammatory activity in valvular interstitial cells, which induces their osteogenic differentiation, accelerates valvular calcification and fibrosis, thereby resulting in stenosis or regurgitation and increasing the risk of severe aortic valve disease [9–13]. However, inflammatory markers commonly used in clinical practice are relatively simple and may not comprehensively reflect the systemic immune-inflammatory state. To address this, researchers have proposed the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), which is calculated based on peripheral blood cell parameters and reflects the overall immune-inflammatory status of the body [14]. Since its introduction, SII has been widely applied in oncology and metabolic diseases for risk stratification and prognostic evaluation [14–18]. Previous studies have reported that SII not only predicted the risk of certain malignancies but also correlated with cancer-related mortality [14, 16, 17]. In addition, other investigations have suggested the predictive and prognostic value of SII in a variety of metabolic disorder-related conditions, including metabolic syndrome, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, insulin resistance, and CVD [18–22]. Nevertheless, the role of SII in prognostic assessment after TAVR has not been systematically explored. Given that chronic low-grade inflammation may continuously contribute to valvular interstitial cell injury, we hypothesize that SII may be associated with the risk of MACE after TAVR.

Based on this background and hypothesis, this study utilized data from a single center in China to explore the association between SII and MACE in patients undergoing TAVR. The objective was to provide preliminary insights into the prognostic value of SII in this population and to provide a reference point that may inform the development of prevention and management strategies tailored to population-specific risk profiles.

Methods

Study population

In this retrospective cohort study, a total of 138 patients who underwent TAVR at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 4, 2021, and April 30, 2025, were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; and (2) receipt of TAVR during hospitalization. The exclusion criteria were: (1) missing baseline data on platelet count, neutrophil count, or lymphocyte count; (2) presence of acute infection or severe chronic infection; (3) presence of severe autoimmune disease; (4) current treatment with anti-inflammatory medications; (5) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; (6) loss to follow-up; and (7) significant missing baseline information. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University.

Measurement of SII

The SII was calculated using the formula: SII = (platelet count × neutrophil count)/lymphocyte count, with all parameters derived from peripheral blood cell counts and expressed in units of ×109 cells/L [14]. To better fit its association with the risk of MACE, SII was standardized as follows: Standardized SII = (raw SII – mean)/standard deviation (SD).

Follow-up and outcomes

All patients included in this study were followed from the date of hospital discharge until the first occurrence of a MACE or until May 1, 2025, whichever came first. MACE was defined as the occurrence of one or more of the following: all-cause death, cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), or nonfatal stroke. All-cause death included cardiovascular death, which was defined as death due to cardiac rupture, malignant arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, heart failure (HF), stroke, sudden cardiac death, or other causes. Nonfatal MI was defined as clinical symptoms, signs, or imaging findings consistent with MI, without resulting in death. Nonfatal stroke was defined as symptoms, signs, or imaging consistent with a diagnosis of stroke, without fatal outcome [23–25].

Data collection and definitions

Demographic data collected in this study included age, sex, height, weight, BMI, blood pressure, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Smoking and alcohol use were defined as past or current use. Medical history variables included hypertension, diabetes, angina pectoris, syncope, cerebral infarction, previous percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification and atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter (AF/AFL). Among them, hypertension was defined as a prior diagnosis of hypertension or a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg during hospitalization [26]. Diabetes was defined as a known history of diabetes, fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, or current use of antidiabetic medications [27]. Echocardiographic data included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), expressed as a percentage.

TAVR-related complications included perioperative events, structural cardiac complications, and postoperative permanent pacemaker implantation (PPM). Among them, perioperative complications were defined as events occurring intraoperatively or within 7 days after the procedure, including vascular complications (e.g., puncture site hematoma, aortic dissection, arterial perforation), bleeding, cerebrovascular events (transient ischemic attack or stroke), and conduction disturbances (e.g., new-onset left bundle branch block or atrioventricular block) [7, 28]. Structural cardiac complications were defined as intraoperative or post-discharge prosthetic valve–related abnormalities, including structural valve deterioration (SVD; leaflet wear or tear, calcification, or fibrosis), postoperative angiographic paravalvular leak (PVL), patient-prosthesis mismatch, and coronary obstruction [28, 29].

Biomarkers included triglycerides, total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), Lp(a), FBG, HbA1c, white blood cell count (WBC), red cell distribution width (RDW), urea, uric acid, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Hs-CRP), and CRP. The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score was calculated using the official STS Short-term/Operative Risk Calculator (Adult Cardiac Surgery Database, version 2.0.6; https://acsdriskcalc.research.sts.org) based on patients’ baseline clinical and procedural characteristics.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test before analysis. Variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD, and differences between groups were assessed using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR), and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages), and differences between groups were evaluated using the chi-square test. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was first performed to identify factors associated with the risk of MACE, and the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for each variable. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate Cox regression models. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for sex only; and Model 3 was further adjusted for sex, NYHA class ≥ III, post-dilation, BMI, triglycerides, and uric acid, to examine the potential association between SII and MACE. Although the STS score showed no significant differences between groups and was not statistically associated with MACE in univariate Cox regression, given the important clinical relevance of the STS score in TAVR prognosis, we constructed an additional Model 4 by incorporating the STS score into Model 3. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess association between SII and MACE across different clinical subgroups. The predictive performance of SII for MACE was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A restricted cubic spline plot (RCS) was also applied to explore the potential nonlinear association between SII and MACE. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics according to MACE status

As shown in Table 1, during the median follow-up period of 12.5 months, a total of 138 patients were included, with 22 (15.94%) in the MACE group and 116 (84.06%) in the non-MACE group. Compared with the non-MACE group, the MACE group had a higher proportion of males, a greater proportion of patients with NYHA class ≥ 3, and higher levels of triglycerides, WBC, neutrophil count, monocyte count, cystatin C, creatinine, and SII (P < 0.05). Conversely, levels of HDL-C and ApoA1 were lower in the MACE group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in other baseline characteristics, including the STS score (P > 0.05). The detailed composition of MACE is summarized in Table 2.

Univariate Cox regression analysis for MACE

As presented in Table 3, univariate Cox regression analysis showed that male, post-dilation, NYHA class ≥ 3, BMI, heart rate, triglycerides, ApoA1, WBC, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, urea, uric acid, and SII were statistically associated with MACE (P < 0.05). In particular, each unit increase in SII was associated with a 0.1% higher hazard of MACE, and each SD increase was associated with a 41.4% higher hazard (HR = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.000–1.001, P < 0.001; HR = 1.414, 95% CI: 1.160–1.724, P < 0.001). In contrast, the remaining variables, such as the STS score, showed no significant associations with MACE (P > 0.05).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study based on real-world data, SII had a statistical association with MACE in TAVR patients, and this association was further observed in multiple subgroups. In addition, we also found that SII has a modest discriminatory ability for the occurrence of MACE and a positive linear association with the risk of MACE. These findings indicate that SII might have potential prognostic value in patients undergoing TAVR treatment, but its clinical application requires further evaluation.

Current evidence indicates that inflammation is closely linked to CVD. For example, macrophages serve as core regulators of the inflammatory response in CVD, participating in the injury and repair processes of diseases such as MI, cardiomyopathy, HF, and atherosclerosis [9]. They promote fibrosis, myocardial remodeling, and adverse outcomes by secreting inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, inflammation can cause structural and functional damage to coronary microvessels, leading to a decrease in coronary flow reserve, which in turn induces silent ischemia and triggers angina, HF, or even sudden death [10]. Inflammation and thrombosis can also promote each other: a chronic inflammatory state can induce platelet activation and coagulation cascade reactions, forming thromboinflammation and increasing the risk of MI [11]. Furthermore, chronic low-grade inflammation plays a significant role in CAVD. Several studies have shown that inflammation can cause valve endothelial injury, lipid deposition, and immune cell infiltration, thereby leading to osteoblastic transformation of interstitial cells, exacerbating valve fibrosis and calcification, and inducing aortic valve stenosis or regurgitation [12, 13, 30]. Inflammation also affects the prognosis of TAVR. For instance, Schwietz et al. found that postoperative systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) was an independent predictor of one-year mortality after TAVR (HR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.03–3.79, P = 0.04), with each unit increase in SIRS associated with a 98.0% increase in one-year mortality after TAVR [31]. Besides, Ertem et al. demonstrated that the SII was an independent predictor of contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN) after TAVR (OR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.001–1.003, P = 0.005), with each unit increase in SII associated with a 0.2% increase in CIN risk [32]. Furthermore, Tosu et al. found that SII was significantly correlated with the risk of MACE and 6-month mortality after TAVR (P < 0.01), and SII was an independent predictor of MACE (HR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.001–1.003, P < 0.01), with each unit increase in SII associated with a 0.2% increase in MACE risk [33]. However, unlike the above studies, the present analysis suggested an association between SII and postoperative cardiovascular events in patients undergoing TAVR and demonstrated consistent trends across multiple subgroups, while also exploring a potential linear relationship between SII and MACE. Taken together, these results indicate that SII could serve as a supplementary indicator for prognostic assessment, although its role in risk stratification requires further confirmation.

Nevertheless, the underlying biological mechanisms by which SII influences TAVR prognosis remain unclear. Based on a review of relevant literature, we hypothesize that the following biological mechanisms may be involved: First, SII may affect TAVR prognosis through oxidative stress and chronic inflammation. Contrast agents used during TAVR and various puncture procedures may induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. Inflammation and oxidative stress can promote vascular lesions, valve calcification, and thrombosis by damaging endothelial function, activating immune and coagulation pathways, and inducing cellular osteoblastic transdifferentiation, thereby contributing to adverse outcomes such as postoperative cardiovascular events and MACE [10, 11, 15, 34]. Second, SII may influence TAVR prognosis through mitochondrial autophagy. TAVR inevitably triggers an inflammatory response, through which SII may affect mitochondrial autophagy. Imbalances in mitochondrial autophagy can lead to a series of cardiovascular risks and subsequent poor TAVR outcomes. For example, sustained activation of mitochondrial autophagy can promote vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and lesions, myocardial cell damage, and exacerbation of HF, while autophagic defects can worsen ischemia-reperfusion injury [34, 35]. Third, SII may be related to vascular endothelial function impairment, hypertension, or aortic valve calcification. These factors are also risk factors for TAVR and its postoperative adverse outcomes [1, 11, 18]. Therefore, we hypothesize that SII may indirectly cause the occurrence of postoperative adverse events after TAVR by increasing the risk of aortic valve calcification and aortic valve stenosis/regression. Although the above biological mechanisms may be involved, more potential mechanisms likely remain undiscovered and unconfirmed. Future studies requiring cell and animal experiments, as well as prospective clinical trials, are needed to further explore the underlying biological associations.

Beyond the SII, emerging imaging-based parameters have also shown potential additional value in prognostic assessment for recipients of TAVR. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that in patients with aortic stenosis (AS) undergoing TAVR, impaired longitudinal strain measured by speckle tracking echocardiography (STE), including both left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LVGLS) and right ventricular longitudinal strain (RVLS), was associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality and composite adverse outcomes, irrespective of baseline LVEF [36]. This suggests that strain metrics may complement conventional pre-procedural risk stratification. In addition, exercise stress echocardiography (ESE) in mild-to-moderate AS can uncover hemodynamic disturbances not evident at rest, with an exercise-induced increase in the ratio of early diastolic transmitral flow velocity to early diastolic mitral annular velocity (E/e′) ≥ 15 reported as an independent predictor of short-term adverse events in multivariable analysis [37]. Given that chronic inflammation may contribute to endothelial dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and reduced diastolic compliance—mechanistic pathways that could connect elevated SII with strain impairment and abnormal filling pressure—future studies may consider exploring the combined prognostic implications of SII, LVGLS/RVLS, and exercise-induced ΔE/e′ in TAVR cohorts, including their potential to enhance risk reclassification and support more tailored management strategies.

In our study, the ROC curve analysis showed that SII alone yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.697, indicating a moderate discriminative ability. Although this suggests certain limitations in applying SII as a stand-alone tool for clinical decision-making, its advantages—being simple, inexpensive, and readily obtainable from routine blood tests—support its potential value as part of a broader prognostic framework. More importantly, when considered alongside other established prognostic indicators such as NYHA classification, STE-derived strain metrics (LVGLS/RVLS), and ESE-derived exercise-induced hemodynamic indices (such as ΔE/e′), SII may provide incremental value for overall prognostic risk stratification. Therefore, integrating SII into multidimensional predictive models that incorporate clinical, imaging, and hemodynamic parameters warrants further investigation, as such approaches could enhance predictive performance and applicability in the TAVR population.

Although this study yielded some findings, several unavoidable limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, as a retrospective cohort study, it was impossible to determine a causal relationship between SII and the risk of MACE. Secondly, observational studies of this type generally require larger datasets, ideally including several thousand patients. However, because our center initiated TAVR relatively late, the total number of eligible patients was limited, making it difficult to include a large cohort. And this study was a single-center retrospective analysis with a small sample size (n = 138) and only 22 MACE, which inevitably reduced the statistical power to some extent. In addition, we applied strict inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure the completeness and quality of clinical data, which further restricted the sample size. Despite these limitations, we attempted to use multiple statistical approaches, including multivariable Cox regression, subgroup analysis, and ROC curve analysis, to mitigate potential bias due to the small sample size; however, these methods cannot fully eliminate the inherent limitations of observational studies. Furthermore, our RCS analysis suggested a potential association between SII and MACE, but this result should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. We acknowledged that the overall strength of evidence in this study was relatively limited, and the findings might not be generalizable to other ethnic groups or populations. Future large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies are needed to further validate the relationship between SII and MACE after TAVR. Thirdly, as a hospital-based real-world study, certain epidemiological variables were inevitably not captured, including genetic factors, dietary habits, environmental exposures, and psychosocial or mental health variables. Fourthly, as shown in Table 4, the HR and 95% CI for SII per unit increase could not accurately reflect changes in MACE risk, which was attributable to the large numerical range of SII values (mean 596.30; minimum 77.38; maximum 6345.28). Because the change in MACE risk associated with a one-unit increase in SII was negligible, SII was standardized for analysis. However, during the analysis, the HR for standardized SII still exhibited a relatively wide 95% CI, which may be attributable to the limited sample size and the relatively low incidence of MACE. Furthermore, in the subgroup analyses, some subgroups had relatively small sample sizes, and the HRs, 95% CIs, and P values could not be reliably estimated through statistical modeling. Therefore, to minimize possible misinterpretation, we decided not to present these subgroup analysis results and added a clarifying note in the footnote of Table 5. Finally, given that this study was a single-center retrospective analysis with a small sample size, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future investigations should aim to enroll additional patients, improve study design, and expand sample size as much as possible to better examine the potential association between SII and MACE.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing TAVR, higher levels of SII were statistically associated with MACE, and this association showed similar trends across different subgroups. These findings indicate that SII could serve as a supplementary indicator for prognostic assessment, although its role in risk stratification requires further confirmation. However, as this study was a single-center, small-sample, retrospective analysis, the results should be interpreted with caution. Future multicenter, large-sample, prospective clinical trials are needed to further validate this association and to provide additional reference for risk stratification and postoperative management in patients undergoing TAVR.
