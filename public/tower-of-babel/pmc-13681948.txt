MFCAMNet: predicting miRNA-disease associations by multi-feature cascade attention mechanism network
Authors: Yuanxing Wang, Chen Yang, Yucheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrive by the rapid envolution of deep-learning techniques, a large body of biological experiments bas has uncovered extensive associations between microRNAs (miRNAs) and complex human diseases, hig- hlighting the pivotal roles of miRNAs in pathogenesis. Elucidating these associations is essential for understanding disease mechanisms and developing preventive strategies. Traditional wet-lab validati- on, however, is notoriously labor- and resource-intensive, creating an urgent demand for efficient computational tools that can prioritize the most promising miRNA–disease candidates. Existing predictors predominantly rely on a single category of handcrafted features, thereby overlooking the complementary information embedded in multiple, heterogeneous data sources. Although a few recent attempts integrate diverse features, they usually exploit only a limited subset and fail to capture the intricate, non-linear relationships among them. To address these limitations, we propose MFCAMNet, a Multi-Feature fusion and Cross-Self-Attention model for MiRNA–Disease association prediction. Firstly, we construct multiple similarity matrices and employ two independent autoencoders with multi-source feature attention to obtain deep features of miRNA and disease to extract the inherent relationships between multiple features. Secondly, the proposed model employs element-level addition, element-level multiplication, and concatenation operations to generate miRNA-disease pair features with rich information. Finally, we use the encoder structure of the transformer to fuse the three deep features and predict all potential miRNA disease associations. We conducted comprehensive evaluations on the public HMDD v2.0 and HMDD v3.2 benchmark datasets. MFCAMNet achieved average AUCs of 0.9455 and 0.9420 under 5-fold and 10-fold cross-validation on HMDD v2.0, respectively, and an AUC of 0.9578 under 5-fold cross-validation on HMDD v3.2, outperforming state-of-the-art competitors. Case studies on breast, esophageal, and lung cancers further corroborate the reliability and practical utility of the proposed method.Keywords: MiRNA-disease association prediction, MFCAMNet, Multi-source feature extraction, Multi-feature fusion, Self-attention mechanism

Introduction

Microrna(miRNA) is a class of endogenous small noncoding RNA with a length of approximately 22 nucleotides that plays essential regulatory roles in various biological processes, including inflammation, cell cycle regulation, stress response, differentiation, apoptosis, and migration [1, 2]. Since the discovery of the first miRNA these molecules have moved to the forefront of biomedical research; abundant evidence now links miRNA dysregulation to the initiation and progression of a wide spectrum of human disorders. The miR-17-92 cluster, for example, is implicated in colorectal carcinoma, B-cell lymphoma, gastric cancer and small-cell lung cancer [3–6]; miR-195 and miR-497 suppress proliferation and invasion of breast-cancer cells and their expression inversely correlates with tumour grade, positioning them as promising diagnostic and therapeutic targets [7]. In oesophageal malignancies miR-19b abundance positively correlates with tumour size, whereas miR-17a over-expression is associated with lymph-node metastasis and advanced stage [8], and miR-155 is frequently up-regulated in lung and other solid cancers [9]. Conventional experimental validation is costly and time-consuming; fortunately, the rapid expansion of bioinformatics and high-performance computing has been accompanied by the release of comprehensive public repositories that curate miRNA- and disease-related data [10–15]. Exploiting these resources, together with state-of-the-art algorithms, enables large-scale in-silico prediction and prioritisation of miRNA–disease associations. The increasing penetration of deep learning into computational biology [16, 17] has given rise to two main families of predictive models: (i) classical architectures such as fully connected, convolutional or recurrent neural networks, and (ii) graph-neural-network approaches built upon graph convolutional networks.

In the mining of miRNA and complex-disease features and association prediction, traditional machine-learning and deep-learning approaches have complementary emphases and are often used synergistically. Classical machine-learning models—such as support-vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF) and gradient-boosting decision trees (GBDT)—usually serve as the core predictor, frequently coupled with k-means clustering to balance positive and negative samples or to select salient features. Deep-learning methods exploit fully connected networks, convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, as well as special architectures like stacked auto-encoders and variational auto-encoders, to extract dense, high-dimensional representations from multi-source data (e.g., disease semantic similarity, miRNA functional similarity, heterogeneous interaction networks), thereby providing richer information for subsequent association inference. For example, Shen et al. [18] proposed CMFMDA, a collaborative-matrix-factorization model that learns a shared latent vector space for miRNAs and diseases and reconstructs the unknown association matrix via low-rank factorization for efficient prediction. Fu et al. [19] presented DeepMDA, a deep ensemble framework that uses stacked auto-encoders to capture high-order features from miRNA and disease similarity matrices and predicts association probabilities in an end-to-end manner. Zhang et al. [20] developed PCACFMDA, which fuses multi-dimensional similarity features, employs PCA to remove redundant dimensions, and feeds the compressed representations into an optimized cascade forest for accurate association scoring. Zhou et al. [21] introduced GBDT-LR: k-means clustering first filters reliable negative samples from unlabeled miRNA–disease pairs, GBDT then automatically extracts discriminative feature combinations, and finally logistic regression outputs the association score. Similarly, Zhao et al. [22] adopted k-means-based negative-sample sampling in the ABMDA framework to balance the training set and improve prediction performance.

Recently, deep-learning-based architectures have also been extensively employed to infer miRNA–complex-disease associations. For instance, Ha et al. [23] argued that conventional approaches inadequately model the non-linear interplay between miRNAs and diseases and suffer from low accuracy under data sparsity; they therefore proposed NCMD, a Node2vec-based neural collaborative filtering framework that first embeds miRNA and disease networks into low-dimensional vectors via Node2vec and then fuses generalized matrix factorization (GMF) and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to capture linear and non-linear interactions, respectively, for final association scoring. Building upon this line of research, Ha et al. [24] recently proposed GCNCF, a graph convolutional network with neural collaborative filtering that integrates GCN-based node embedding with NCF to simultaneously capture network structural information and complex miRNA-disease interactions. Subsequently, Ha et al. [25] proposed DWMDA, a DeepWalk-based graph embedding method that employs random walks and skip-gram models to extract low-dimensional node representations from miRNA and disease networks, followed by a deep neural network for association prediction. Lou et al. [26] noticed that standard graph convolutional networks (GCNs) exploit only first-order neighbourhood information and hence fail to encode high-order relationships; they presented MINIMDA, a hybrid-neighbourhood model that simultaneously aggregates 0–k-hop neighbourhood information within a unified multi-modal graph via refined graph convolutional layers to learn deep embeddings for miRNAs and diseases, followed by an MLP for association prediction. Echoing this, Ata et al.[27] proposed a collaborative multi-view network embedding framework, which can effectively fuse structural and attribute information in multiple views and enhance the embedding quality through collaborative learning among views. Ai et al. [28] devised an end-to-end framework named MHDMF that employs GCN to integrate multi-source information about miRNAs, genes and diseases, reconstructs the miRNA–disease scoring matrix, and subsequently applies deep matrix factorization (DMF) to uncover latent associations. Kim and Ha [?] recently proposed GMFLDA, which adapts this architecture for lncRNA-disease association prediction, achieving competitive performance under both leave-one-out and five-fold cross-validation frameworks. Prior to this, Ha [29] proposed EMFLDA, an lncRNA expression profile-based matrix factorization framework that incorporates expression data as implicit feedback to address the high false-negative rate problem in lncRNA-disease association prediction. Ji et al. [30] addressed the absence of reliable negative samples and the resulting training difficulties of traditional supervised methods by introducing AEMDA, an autoencoder-based model that first uses ridge regression to derive high-dimensional vector representations from integrated miRNA and disease similarity profiles and then feeds these vectors into a deep autoencoder whose reconstruction error serves as the association strength, enabling prediction without explicit negatives. Wang et al. [31] pre-trained a stacked autoencoder (SAE) in an unsupervised manner on all available miRNA–disease pairs, subsequently introduced positive and negative samples for supervised fine-tuning, and finally predicted novel associations by combining reconstruction error with a softmax classifier.Beyond RNA-disease association prediction, similar deep learning architectures have been successfully applied to drug-target interaction prediction. Ha [32] recently proposed the SVDTI framework, which employs a stacked variational autoencoder. This approach processes drug SMILES representations and protein sequences via LSTM networks, subsequently utilizing neural collaborative filtering for interaction prediction.

Despite the above-mentioned progress in miRNA–disease association prediction, two salient limitations remain. First, prevailing deep-learning pipelines still adopt a fixed fusion recipe—either simple concatenation or element-wise addition—without a systematic comparison among alternative strategies; for instance, AEMDA merely concatenates the high-dimensional miRNA and disease vectors before feeding them into the auto-encoder, leaving the optimal combination unexplored. Second, some studies rely on a single data source, whereas others [19, 31] incorporate multiple omics but exploit them only superficially or in isolation, failing to excavate their inherent complementarity and deep cross-modal correlations, thereby constraining further performance gains.

To remedy the aforementioned shortcomings, we present MFCAMNet, a novel computational framework that unites multi-feature processing with self-attention for miRNA–disease association prediction. The rationale is rooted in prior evidence: MINIMDA [33] and AMHMDA [34] improved accuracy by exploiting multi-source miRNA and disease representations; DDIMDL [35] achieved precise drug–drug interaction (DDI) event forecasting through integrating heterogeneous drug features; and MDFSA-DDI [36] further advanced DDI prediction by deepening this multi-source fusion philosophy. Building on these insights, MFCAMNet learns multimodal representations of miRNAs and diseases from diverse data sources and then infers their latent associations. Comparative experiments systematically analyse the performance impact of each fusion strategy, providing empirical guidance for choosing the optimal solution in miRNA-disease association prediction.

MFCAMNet

To reduce the noise and redundancy of the original input features, we utilize an autoencoder with multi-source feature attention to mine the intrinsic distribution of the multi-source features while reducing the dimensions of the multi-source features. Subsequently, the three feature fusion strategies, namely element-level addition, element-level multiplication, and concatenation operations, are used to fuse miRNA and disease features and send them into the classifier for prediction. As shown in Fig. 2, MFCAMNet consists of three mainly stages: miRNA and disease feature learning, miRNA and disease feature fusion, and miRNA-disease pair association prediction. Multiple sources of miRNA and disease features are first fed into autoencoders specific to each class. Both autoencoders receive multiple high-dimensional input features and simultaneously output low-dimensional latent features. The only difference is the type of input features, which allow the miRNA and disease features to be independently learned. It is essential to note that the autoencoder uses Multi-source Feature Attention to weigh and sum the features from multiple sources to help identify the portion of features that are helpful for prediction. Then, we fuse miRNA and disease features using three different methods to obtain features from different perspectives. The final predictions are made using the encoder structure from Transformer, which integrates multi-head attention, to learn the miRNA-disease pair features. Next, we will elaborate on these three stages.

The overall architecture of the proposed MFCAMNet

Multiple input feature

Multiple source features can provide deep learning models with diverse information from various perspectives to improve the accuracy of miRNA-disease association prediction compared to work using only a single feature. Other miRNA-disease association prediction models that used multiple sources of feature fusion strategy in a simple way and did not further examine the intrinsic distribution among multiple features. In our research, first construct multiple input similarity matrices, as depicted in Fig. 1.

Computing multiple similarity matrices based on multiple data sources about miRNAs and diseases

The construction of multiple similarity matrices is motivated by the complementary information each source provides. miRNA functional similarity captures shared biological pathways and regulatory roles; disease semantic similarity encodes hierarchical relationships from medical ontologies; Gaussian interaction profile kernel similarity reflects topological association patterns derived from known miRNA–disease networks; and integrated similarity synthesizes these aspects to mitigate data sparsity and provide a comprehensive representation. Together, these matrices enable a multi-faceted characterization of miRNAs and diseases, allowing the model to leverage both biological semantics and network topology for more robust association inference.

The constructed similarity matrices originated from different bioinformatics repositories and computational paradigms, with significant heterogeneity in the range of values, sparsity and distribution patterns. To ensure the fairness and validity of feature fusion, all input matrices are preprocessed with z-score normalisation to have zero mean and unit variance, thus eliminating scale differences. More importantly, the subsequent MSFA module, by learning dynamic weights for each data source, is able to assign higher weights to sources with more stable distributions and richer predictive information, and vice versa to suppress noise effects. This two-layer mechanism of static normalisation and dynamic attention enables the model to robustly handle heterogeneous inputs.

miRNA and disease feature prediction

During the miRNA–disease association-prediction stage, the fused miRNA–disease-pair features are further refined by a standard Transformer encoder layer. This layer is composed of four tightly coupled sub-modules—multi-head self-attention (MHSA), layer normalization (LN), residual connection, and a position-wise feed-forward network (FFN)—stacked in a way that guarantees gradient stability while capturing long-range dependencies and fine-grained feature interactions. Specifically, to quantify the heterogeneous contribution of each feature dimension to the final prediction, the input representation is first fed into MHSA. By computing h parallel scaled dot-product attention heads, the model evaluates pairwise feature relevance in multiple complementary subspaces and produces a context-aware representation that is adaptively re-weighted. The subsequent LN and residual connection jointly normalize and add the attention output to the original input, effectively mitigating training degradation in deep architectures. Finally, the FFN distills higher-order non-linear patterns through two successive linear transformations separated by a non-linear activation, yielding a more discriminative embedding for downstream classification. Results

Cross validation

On the HMDD v2.0 dataset, we conducted 5-fold cross-validation (5-CV) and 10-fold cross-validation (10-CV) experiments tocomprehensively evaluate the performance of MFCAMNet. Additionally, 5-fold cross-validation experiments are conducted on the HMDD v3.2 dataset to assure the model generalization ability. In the 5-fold cross-validation, all known miRNA-disease associations will be considered positive samples and randomly divided into five groups, with each group serving as a test set in turn and the remaining four groups serving as a train set. 10-fold cross-validation is similar to the above and our method also generates the same number of negative miRNA-disease samples from unknown associations as positive samples. For comparison with other methods, we measured performance primarily with six commonly employed evaluation metrics: area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), area under the precision/recall (PR) curve (AUPR), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. AUC describes the possibility that the model ranks positive samples higher than negative samples, whereas AUPR describes the relationship between precision and recall at different thresholds. AUC and AUPR are usually the two most useful metrics for evaluating the performance of a model. Greater AUC and AUPR indicate better model performance. Paraments analysis

Hyper-parameters in deep learning decisively influence predictive performance. To systematically assess their impact on our proposed model, we conducted five-fold cross-validation experiments on the HMDD v3.2 dataset, using the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPR) as primary evaluation metrics. The hyper-parameters examined include the auto-encoder output dimension, the number of heads in multi-head attention, dropout rate, training epochs, batch size, and learning rate. As shown in Fig. 10, the model is most sensitive to changes in learning rate and dropout, while adjustments to the remaining parameters yield only gradual improvements in AUC and AUPR. Guided by these observations, the optimal configuration was determined as follows: auto-encoder output dimension 64, multi-head attention heads 8, batch size 256, training epochs 100, dropout 0.3, and learning rate 1e-5.

Comparison of different feature fusion

In our experiments, we used multiple source features, including miRNA functional similarity, disease semantic similarity, miRNA GIP similarity, disease GIP similarity, integrated similarity for miRNA and integrated similarity for disease. In order to explore the most appropriate feature fusion approach, we attempted to fuse multiple source features using different methods. Thus, seven MFCAMNet models were constructed to explore and compare the performance differences between the different fusion methods. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 11. In previous studies, the feature fusion of miRNAs and diseases frequently used only one of addition, multiplication, or concatenation. In this experiment, we combined miRNA and disease features in three ways to obtain the best results. On the HMDD v2.0 dataset, as shown in Fig. 11, the combination of addition, multiplication, and concatenation yields the best results in the four performance metrics. The second-best results are obtained by combining multiplication and concatenation. Similarly, the experimental results on the HMDD v3.2 dataset show that the combination of addition, multiplication, and concatenation produces the best results in the accuracy, recall, and F1 metrics, with multiplication and concatenation producing the second-best results. Based on the results of the above experiments, the combination of addition, multiplication and concatenation seems to be the best strategy for miRNA-disease feature fusion and retains the most information. This also shows that the use of multi-source features and the fusion of multi-source features may provide deep learning models with diverse information from different perspectives, thereby improving the accuracy of miRNA-disease association prediction compared to previous work using single-feature information.

Ablation study

To rigorously quantify the contribution of the attention modules within MFCAMNet, we devised and compared three architectural variants: MFCAMNet-basic, MFCAMNet-MSFA, and MFCAMNet-MHA. MFCAMNet-basic completely removes all attention components; MFCAMNet-MSFA retains only the Multisource Feature Attention (MSFA) module in the miRNA and disease representation learning stage; MFCAMNet-MHA introduces Multi-Head Attention (MHA) exclusively in the association prediction stage via encoder layers, enabling the model to identify salient predictive signals by learning the weight distribution over miRNA–disease pair features. As illustrated in Fig. 12, the incorporation of attention mechanisms yields consistent and substantial improvements across all evaluation metrics, corroborating the indispensability and efficacy of the attention modules in enhancing the discriminative capacity of MFCAMNet.

Survival analysis

We obtain clinical data related to breast cancer from the Cancer Genome Atlas database and conduct Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to validate whether the top 6 predicted miRNAs can be confirmed by the clinical data. The top 6 miRNAs are miRNA-142, miRNA-150, miRNA-106a, miRNA-15b, miRNA-138, and miRNA-99a, respectively. Survival analysis is help to identify whether these miRNAs have prognostic value in breast cancer patients.

The results are displayed in Fig. 13a–f. Breast cancer patients with higher miRNA-142 expression levels exhibited a slightly higher overall survival rate compared to patients with lower expression levels. The expression level of miRNA-150 is positively correlated with the overall survival rate of patients, with a median survival period of 142.23 months for breast cancer patients with high expression levels, while patients with low expression levels had a median survival period of only 113.63 months, representing a difference of 28.6 months. Research indicates that the overexpression of miRNA-150 affects genes related to tumorigenesis , yet it does not increase the generation, migration, or invasion of breast cancer cells. Instead, this overexpression is directly associated with better patient prognosis. These findings suggest that miRNA-150 may have potential therapeutic and prognostic value in breast cancer patients. This discovery provides important insights for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms of breast cancer and the development of relevant treatment strategies, holding significant implications for improving the treatment and survival rates of breast cancer patients.

Survival analysis of top 6 predicted miRNA for breast cancer

The overexpression of miRNA-106a is positively correlated with the overall survival rate of patients. In contrast, the overexpression of miRNA-15b shows a negative correlation with the overall survival rate. The median survival period of breast cancer patients with miRNA-15b overexpression is only 115.73 months, while those with low expression levels have an extended median survival period of 131.37 months. Studies suggest that miRNA-15b is often significantly over expressed in breast cancer patients, while the expression level of PAQR3 is relatively low. However, PAQR3 has been demonstrated to have anti-cancer effects in various tumors. Therefore, miRNA-15b may be involved in the pathological process of breast cancer by suppressing PAQR3 expression, thereby promoting the deterioration of breast cancer. Further research may contribute to the development of treatment strategies targeting miRNA-15b and PAQR3 to slow down or inhibit the progression of breast cancer, providing more effective treatment options for breast cancer patients.

The overexpression of miRNA-138 leads to a sharp decrease in the overall survival rate of breast cancer patients, with a median survival period of 215.2 months for patients with low expression levels and only 115.4 months for those with overexpression, a difference of nearly two-fold. In contrast, breast cancer patients with overexpression of miRNA- 99a exhibit higher overall survival rates compared to patients with low miRNA-99a expression levels. Studies indicate that miRNA-99a acts as a suppressor in multiple tumors, including breast cancer. Overexpression of miRNA-99a exerts multiple inhibitory effects on breast cancer cells, reducing their vitality, inhibiting proliferation, and suppressing cell dissemination, thereby potentially slowing down tumor growth. On the other hand, miRNA-99a induces apoptosis in cancer cells, aiding in the clearance of abnormal cells, making miRNA-99a crucial for halting the development of breast cancer. MiRNA-99a may serve as a potential therapeutic target, contributing to improved patient survival rates.

The survival analysis conducted on breast cancer cases demonstrates that the miRNA predicted by the proposed model may play a significant role in early prevention, early diagnosis, and prognosis assessment of breast cancer. Additionally, this affirms the potential applicability of the proposed model in the biomedical field, holding promise for providing support in the research and treatment of breast cancer.

Conclusion

It is crucial to comprehend the association between miRNAs and diseases, as abnormal expression of miRNAs can result in the occurrence of specific diseases. To improve accuracy and efficacy in predicting potential miRNA-disease associations, we have developed a new method called MFCAMNet. MFCAMNet uses an attention mechanism to weigh the multiple similarity matrices and an autoencoder to effectively reduce the dimensionality of the input features and generate low-dimensional miRNA and disease feature expressions. MFCAMNet then employs multiple strategies to generate miRNA-disease pair features. Finally, the Encoder Layer in Transformer was utilized to predict the association between miRNA and disease. In addition, we analyze the effect of various means of fusing feature strategies on performance and experimentally show that multiple source features can provide diverse information for deep learning models from different perspectives. By applying 5-fold and 10-fold cross-validation, MFCAMNet obtained the highest AUC and AUPR scores in comparison to other methods. In addition, we demonstrated the reliability of MFCAMNet with the outcomes of case studies involving three diseases.

However, the limitations of the proposed model must still be addressed. We construct multiple similarity matrices from multi-source data as the model input and then generate miRNA and disease pair features via element-level addition, multiplication, and concatenation. The questions that need to be discussed are how to effectively determine the most effective strategy for integrating the features and which fusion strategies have not been considered. In addition, many studies have employed miRNA functional similarity, disease semantic similarity, and Gaussian interaction profile similarity as supplemental information to predict the association between miRNAs and diseases. In practice, however, we found it challenging to guarantee that a large number of miRNAs and diseases have this information, making the solution to this problem a formidable obstacle.
