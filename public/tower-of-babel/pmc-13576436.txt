Retrograde suction decompression (Dallas technique) and microsurgical clipping of large ruptured posterior communicating artery aneurysm associated with a fetal-type posterior cerebral artery: illustrative case
Authors: Eva M. Wu, Ho Jun Yun, Mustafa Motiwala, John E. Dugan, Luke A. Silveira, Nickalus R. Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDLarge aneurysms arising from a fetal-type posterior cerebral artery (PCA) configuration pose substantial technical challenges, especially when ruptured and involving critical perforating vessels. Endovascular treatment may be limited when the aneurysm is large and incorporates the fetal PCA outflow.OBSERVATIONSA 36-year-old woman presented with a ruptured large right posterior communicating artery aneurysm associated with a fetal-type PCA causing subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, and a symptomatic third nerve palsy. Because of the aneurysm’s size and morphology, microsurgical clip reconstruction with adjunctive suction decompression (Dallas technique) was performed.LESSONSThe Dallas technique can safely and effectively facilitate aneurysm collapse and clip application. This method of retrograde suction decompression can help preserve the fetal PCA and anterior choroidal artery. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26661

Large brain aneurysms are uncommon vascular lesions, comprising approximately 3%–5% of all intracranial aneurysms.1 Unlike smaller aneurysms, large aneurysms often present with neurological symptoms from mass effect, including compression of the brainstem or cranial nerves, rather than hemorrhage alone. They are associated with a significantly higher 5-year rupture risk and increased 2-year mortality if left untreated.2 Aneurysms associated with a fetal-type posterior cerebral artery (PCA) present unique technical challenges because preservation of critical branch vessels is essential during clip reconstruction. Compromise of these vessels during microsurgical clipping can result in ischemic stroke, while endovascular coiling may also carry a risk of cerebral ischemia, particularly when the fetal PCA origin is incorporated into the aneurysm. These concerns are amplified in large aneurysms, in which the aneurysm size and limited working space may hinder visualization of critical vessels and safe aneurysm reconstruction.3,4

Illustrative Cases

The patient is a 36-year-old female with a past medical history of breast cancer and hypertension who presented with 2–3 days of thunderclap headache. On examination, she was somnolent and had a right third cranial nerve palsy but was otherwise neurologically intact.

Noncontrast CT of the head demonstrated subarachnoid hemorrhage with intraventricular extension and a large mass in the medial temporal region (Fig. 1). CT angiography demonstrated a large right posterior communicating artery (PComA) aneurysm measuring 16.92 × 17.90 × 21.86 mm in height, width, and anteroposterior diameter, respectively, with a fetal-type PCA arising from the aneurysm (Fig. 2). Diagnostic cerebral angiography demonstrated a “Murphy’s teat,” which likely represented the rupture site. Three-dimensional reconstructed angiography further delineated the anatomy, including the fetal PCA outflow (Fig. 3). The rationale for surgery was the presence of a ruptured large right PComA associated with a fetal-type PCA. The risks of the procedure included bleeding, infection, seizures, CSF leak, pseudomeningocele, cranial neuropathy, stroke, vascular injury, and death. The expected benefits included definitive treatment of the ruptured aneurysm, prevention of rerupture, and preservation of the parent and branch vessels. Observation was not an appropriate option given the ruptured status of the aneurysm. Endovascular treatment was also considered; however, primary coiling was thought to be suboptimal because the large aneurysm size would likely result in persistent mass effect, and complete occlusion would be difficult due to the fetal PCA arising from the aneurysm. Microsurgical clipping alone was unsuitable given the large size of the lesion. Therefore, a microsurgical strategy incorporating suction decompression was selected.

The patient was positioned supine with the head extended to allow for two separate incisions: one for cervical carotid artery exposure and the other for the craniotomy (Fig. 4). Brain relaxation was facilitated by elevating the head above the level of the heart, administering mannitol at 1 g/kg, and maintaining a PaCO2 of approximately 28 mm Hg through controlled hyperventilation. These measures provided sufficient relaxation to perform the extradural anterior clinoidectomy and pretemporal peeling without opening the basal cisterns. Had the brain remained full, the opticocarotid cistern would have been opened to obtain additional CSF relaxation before proceeding. The key surgical steps included cervical carotid artery exposure, a right cranio-orbital craniotomy with orbital roof osteotomy, anterior clinoidectomy, proximal sylvian fissure dissection, temporary aneurysm trapping, retrograde suction decompression, and clip reconstruction (Video 1). Because of the large size of the aneurysm, a pretemporal corridor and anterior clinoidectomy were used to improve maneuverability and increase the degrees of freedom for clip placement. The dura was opened in a T-shaped fashion along the sylvian fissure toward the distal dural ring, minimizing brain exposure while combining extradural and intradural corridors to facilitate precise aneurysm reconstruction. Postoperative angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the aneurysm with preservation of the fetal PCA (Fig. 5). The patient tolerated the procedure well and remained neurologically intact, with significant improvement of the right third cranial nerve palsy by postoperative day 6. At the 1-month follow-up, she remained neurologically intact. Discussion

Observations

PComA aneurysms associated with a fetal PCA configuration have previously been treated successfully with microsurgical clipping. Takeda et al.5 reported a case of a ruptured distal PComA aneurysm arising from a fetal-type PComA that was clipped with preservation of the thalamoperforating artery. Dzhindzhikhadze et al.6 also described successful clipping of a PComA aneurysm involving a fetal PCA, with subsequent resolution of third nerve palsy. Large PComA aneurysms associated with a fetal PCA outflow from the aneurysm dome, however, present additional technical challenges because of their size, morphology, and involvement of critical branch vessels during clip reconstruction.

Xu et al.7 treated 8 patients with giant fetal PCA aneurysms using endovascular techniques, with 4 patients developing permanent paralysis and hemianopsia. Kikuta et al.8 utilized bypass with parent vessel occlusion; although major stroke was avoided, the risk of perforator infarction remained. The Dallas technique has been used effectively for giant aneurysms, particularly in the paraclinoid region. Nguyen et al.9 and Wu and Morcos10 have also described the use of retrograde suction decompression for giant paraclinoid and ophthalmic aneurysms. Our case further demonstrates that the Dallas technique can be successfully applied in the treatment of large PComA aneurysms with associated fetal PCA outflow from the aneurysm dome.

Lessons

To our knowledge, this is the first report in the literature to include an operative video demonstrating retrograde suction decompression and microsurgical clipping of a large PComA aneurysm in association with a fetal-type PCA. The patient had no procedural complications, supporting the safety and effectiveness of this technique for the treatment of these types of aneurysms. The limitations of this report include the absence of long-term follow-up, although no adverse outcomes were identified at the time of manuscript preparation.
