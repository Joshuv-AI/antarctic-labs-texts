Superconducting Diode Effect in Weak Localization Regime
Naratip Nunchot, Youichi Yanase

Abstract
We study a dirty two-dimensional superconductor with Rashba spin-orbit coupling and in-plane Zeeman fields described by the nonlinear sigma model that includes short-range electron-electron interactions from the Coulomb and Cooper channels. The renormalized Ginzburg-Landau theory, which includes the weak localization effects at the one-loop level, is constructed by using the Keldysh functional formalism. It is shown that the tricritical point appears in the phase diagram. The superconducting diode quality factor increases divergently as the system approaches the tricritical point. Near the superconducting phase transition lines, the absolute value of the diode quality factor decreases due to the cooperation of localization and interactions. The normal conductivity of the resistive state, in which the superconducting state is destroyed by the critical current, is calculated, and localization behaviors are demonstrated.

The role of Anderson localization in superconductors is an important topic of ongoing debate. Without considering strong mesoscopic correlations and electron-electron (e-e) interactions, the properties of dirty

In this Letter, we study the interplay of the e-e interaction and disorder on the SD effect and clarify the WL correction to conductivity when the superconducting (SC) state is broken by the critical current. We work with the NLSM for a dirty two-dimensional (2D) noncentrosymmetric superconductor, including the interactions from the Coulomb and Cooper channels in the short-range limit, and establish a renormalized Ginzburg-Landau (GL) theory at the one-loop level. Model:
We consider a dirty 2D superconductor aligned in the
xy
-plane with the Rashba SOC and a Zeeman field in the
x
-direction. In the Keldysh functional formalism, the partition function is given as
Z=\int D[Q,\Delta]\exp\left({iS[Q,\Delta]}\right)
with
Virtanen

\displaystyle S[\Delta,Q]

\displaystyle=S_{\rm HS}[\Delta]+S_{\rm N}[\Delta,Q]+S_{\rm T}[Q],

(1)

where
S_{\rm HS}

Sup

8\times 8

and

\gamma_{\rm c}=\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{\rm q}=\gamma_{1}

\gamma_{\rm c}\check{\Delta}_{\rm c}+\gamma_{\rm q}\check{\Delta}_{\rm q}

\check{\Delta}_{\rm c(q)}=\tau_{+}\Delta_{\rm c(q)}+\tau_{-}\Delta^{*}_{\rm c(q)}

\tau_{\pm}=(\tau_{1}\pm i\tau_{2})/2

is the classical (quantum) component of the SC gap. The check symbol
\check{(\cdot)}
highlights that the objects are
4\times 4
matrices. The terms in the form
\gamma_{\mu}\check{A}
stand for the expression
\gamma_{\mu}\otimes\check{A}
, where
\otimes
is a tensor product. We assume that the SC state is a helical state modulating in the
y
-direction and time-independent. In this case, the Cooper pair momentum corresponds to a uniform gauge field along the
y
-axis
A_{y}
, and we set
A_{x}=0
from now on.

In addition to Eq. (
1
), we take into account interaction terms of the Cooper and Coulomb channels. The former can be obtained directly by integrating the spatial fluctuation of the gap function from Eq. (
1
)
Konig
;
Burmistrov2
. The procedure results in two terms in action. The first term

has the same form as

[Eq. (S.1) in Supplemental Material

], but the spatial modulation in the gap functions is removed. Thus, in the following,
\Delta_{\rm c(q)}
denotes the spatially uniform gap function. The second term is

\displaystyle S^{\rm(c)}_{\rm int}[Q]=\tilde{\Gamma}_{\rm c}\!\!\sum\limits_{i=1,2}\!\int_{t,\bm{r}}\!\!\!\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{{\gamma_{\rm c}\tau_{i}\delta Q_{tt}(\bm{r})}\right\}\!\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{{\gamma_{\rm q}\tau_{i}\delta Q_{tt}(\bm{r})}\right\}\!,

(2)

where
\int_{t,\bm{r}}\equiv\int dtd\bm{r}
,
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\rm c}=-\pi^{2}\nu\Gamma_{\rm c}/16
,
\nu=m/(2\pi)

\Gamma_{\rm c}
is the SC coupling constant, and the trace
\mathrm{tr}\,\{\cdot\}
is performed only in the matrix elements. The spatial fluctuation is represented by
\delta Q=Q-\bar{Q}
, where
\bar{Q}
denotes the spatial average of
Q
. For the Coulomb channel in the short-range limit, following the procedure in Ref.

, we came to the action as follows:

\displaystyle S^{\rm(s)}_{\rm int}[Q]=\tilde{\Gamma}_{\rm s}\int_{t,\bm{r}}\mathrm{tr}\left\{{\gamma_{\rm c}\tau_{0}Q_{tt}(\bm{r})}\right\}\mathrm{tr}\left\{{\gamma_{\rm q}\tau_{0}Q_{tt}(\bm{r})}\right\},

(3)

where
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\rm s}=-\pi^{2}\nu\Gamma_{\rm s}/16
and

S_{\rm tot}=\bar{S}_{\rm HS}+S_{\rm N}+S_{\rm T}+S^{\rm(c)}_{\rm int}+S^{\rm(s)}_{\rm int}
.

Effective action:
In the ordinary mean-field theory, the system is described by saddle points of action, which are obtained by searching the matrix field
Q=\bar{Q}
that optimizes the action under the constraints
\bar{Q}^{2}=1
and
\mathrm{Tr}\,\{\bar{Q}\}=0
. Going beyond the mean-field approximation can be done by integrating out the fluctuations around the saddle points, resulting in the effective action of the gap function. In this work, we consider the Gaussian fluctuations around a SC saddle point
\bar{Q}_{\rm s}
. Since
\bar{Q}_{\rm s}
must be on the same manifold as a normal saddle point
\bar{Q}_{\rm N}
, there is a matrix
U
such that
\bar{Q}_{\rm s}=U\bar{Q}_{\rm N}U^{-1}
. The saddle point configuration may be taken as the fermionic action formulated by Keldysh path integration
Kamenev1
. That is
\bar{Q}=U_{\rm K}\Lambda U_{\rm K}
, where
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}\,\{\check{\Lambda}_{\rm R},\check{\Lambda}_{\rm A}\}
and
\rm R(A)
denotes the retarded (advanced) component. In the energy representation,
U_{\rm K}
is

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{K}\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\tau_{0}&F_{\epsilon}\tau_{0}\\
0&-\tau_{0}\end{pmatrix},

(4)

where distribution in thermal equilibrium leads to
F_{\epsilon}=\tanh\epsilon/(2T)
with energy
\epsilon
and temperature
T
. Using the above equations, we can rewrite
\bar{Q}_{\rm N(s)}=U_{\rm K}\Lambda_{\rm N(s)}U_{\rm K}
, where
\Lambda_{\rm N(s)}=\mathrm{diag}\,\{\check{\Lambda}_{\rm N(s),R},\check{\Lambda}_{\rm N(s),A}\}
. The form of
\Lambda_{\rm N}
in the energy representation is
\Lambda_{\mathrm{N}\epsilon}=\mathrm{diag}\left\{{\tau_{3},-\tau_{3}}\right\}

Kamenev1
;
Feigelman
. On the other hand,
\Lambda_{\rm s}
is generally a function of the
spectral angle

\theta
, which will be specified later. Note that
U
is also a function of
\theta
, just as
\Lambda_{\rm s}
is.

Next, we perform the transformation
Q=U\tilde{Q}U^{-1}
and then the transformation
\tilde{Q}=\exp\left({W/2}\right)\Lambda_{\rm N}\exp\left({-W/2}\right)
. Thus,
Q
can be expanded to the quadratic order of
W
as
Q=\Lambda_{\rm N}+\frac{1}{2}\,[W,\Lambda_{\rm N}]+\frac{1}{8}\,[W,[W,\Lambda_{\rm N}]]
. Expanding the total action to the quadratic order of
W
results in
S_{\mathrm{tot}}[\Delta,Q]\rightarrow S_{\mathrm{tot}}[\Delta,\theta,W]
, where

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{tot}}[\Delta,\theta,W]

\displaystyle=S_{0}[W]+S_{\rm MF}[\theta,\Delta]+\tilde{S}_{\rm int}[\theta,W]

\displaystyle\quad+S_{\rm I}[\theta,W]+S_{\rm II}[\theta,\Delta,W].

(5)

Here,
S_{0}[W]
is the action of the free cooperon and diffuson modes, which are determined from the condition
\{W,\Lambda_{\rm N}\}=0
. The explicit expressions for each term except the last term in Eq. (
5
) are listed in
Sup
. The last term
S_{\rm II}
contains the terms in the form
\sim\mathrm{Tr}\,\{(\dots)WW\}
and
\sim\mathrm{Tr}\,\{W(\dots)W\}
, which do not make any contribution in the first order of
\Delta_{\rm q}
and thus are neglected.

Now, we can find the effective action of the gap function,
S_{\rm eff}
. Regarding the terms other than
S_{0}[W]
as perturbative terms,
S_{\rm eff}
is given as
\exp\left({iS_{\rm eff}}\right)=\braket{\exp\left({i\left[{S_{\rm MF}+S_{\rm int}+S_{\rm I}}\right]}\right)}_{0}
, where
\braket{\cdots}_{0}
denotes the average with respect to
S_{0}
. Performing the cumulant expansion, at the one-loop level, the effective action is obtained from the following equation:

S_{\rm eff}=\braket{S_{\rm MF}+\tilde{S}_{\rm int}}_{0}+\frac{i}{2}\braket{S^{2}_{\rm I}}_{0}.

(6)

SC saddle point:
The SC saddle points
\bar{Q}_{\rm s}
are usually obtained by numerical techniques. However, since we aim to integrate out the fluctuations to obtain an effective action of the SC gap function, saddle points can be approximated so that all the fluctuations can be considered. Although taking into account all fluctuations is a difficult task, a method can give the familiar GL action for conventional dirty superconductors by calculations at the tree level
Yurkevich
;
Levchenko1
. Here, we choose the time-independent SC saddle point
\bar{Q}_{\rm s}
that includes the Zeeman coupling but excludes the interaction terms, the SOC, and the Cooper pair momentum. The saddle point
\bar{Q}_{\rm s}
is obtained by solving the equation
[\check{E}_{\epsilon}+\check{\Delta}_{\rm c}+\gamma_{\rm q}\check{\Delta}_{\rm q},\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{s},\epsilon\epsilon}]=0
, where
\check{E}_{\epsilon}=\tau_{3}(\epsilon+h\sigma_{3})
and
h
is a Zeeman field. The equation can be easily solved if
\Delta_{\rm q}=0
. However, in the absence of the quantum part
\Delta_{\rm q}
, the solution cannot include any effect of the terms other than the term associated with
\check{E}
on the effective action. We overcome this problem by instead using the solution of the following equation:

\displaystyle[\check{E}_{\epsilon}+\check{\Delta}_{\mathrm{R(A)},\epsilon}

\displaystyle,\check{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{sR(A)},\epsilon\epsilon}]=0,

(7)

\displaystyle\check{\Delta}_{\mathrm{R(A)},\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\check{\Delta}_{\rm c}\pm F_{\epsilon}\check{\Delta}_{\rm q}.

(8)

Solving Eq. (
7
), we obtain the saddle point
\bar{Q}_{\rm s}=U_{\rm K}\Lambda_{\rm s}U_{\rm K}
with
\Lambda_{\rm s}=\mathrm{diag}\,\{\check{\Lambda}_{\rm sR},\check{\Lambda}_{\rm sA}\}
, which can be written in the form
\check{\Lambda}_{\rm sR(A)}=\check{U}_{\rm sR(A)}\tau_{3}\check{U}^{-1}_{\rm sR(A)}
. By some calculations, we obtain
\check{U}_{\rm sR(A)}=1-\tau_{1}\hat{X}_{\rm R(A)}
, where

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{\rm R}=\mathrm{diag}\,

\displaystyle\Big\{\tanh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon+}}{2},\tanh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon-}}{2}\Big\},

(9)

\displaystyle\tanh\theta_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon\pm}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sgn}(\epsilon_{\pm})\Delta_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon}/|\epsilon_{\pm}\!+\!i0|,

(10)

and
\epsilon_{\pm}=\epsilon\pm h
.
\hat{X}_{\rm A}
has the same form as
\hat{X}_{\rm R}
but
\theta_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon\pm}
is replaced by
\theta_{\mathrm{A},\epsilon\pm}
, which satisfies
\tanh\theta_{\mathrm{A},\epsilon\pm}=\Delta_{\mathrm{A},\epsilon}/|\epsilon_{\pm}\!-\!i0|
. This is indeed a possible form of the
spectral angle
mentioned below Eq. (
4

U\equiv U_{\rm K}U_{\rm s}
and
U_{\rm s}\equiv\mathrm{diag}\left\{{\check{U}_{\rm sR},\check{U}_{\rm sA}}\right\}

as
\bar{Q}_{\rm s}=U\Lambda_{\rm N}U^{-1}

The solution of Eq. (
7

\theta_{\mathrm{R(A)},\epsilon\pm}
to be determined by optimizing the total effective action with respect to them. The outcome is the so-called

, first proposed in Ref.

by the replica formalism. To derive a similar equation in the Keldysh functional formalism, we have to take into account the quantum part of the spectral angle. Otherwise, for the same reason as described in the previous paragraph, the effective action becomes trivial. The problem is how the quantum part of the spectral angles should enter

8
) and (
10

9

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon\pm}\!

\displaystyle\rightarrow\theta_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon\pm}=\theta^{\rm c}_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon\pm}\!\!+F_{\epsilon}\theta^{\rm q}_{\mathrm{R},\epsilon\pm},

(11)

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{A},\epsilon\pm}\!

\displaystyle\rightarrow\theta_{\mathrm{A},\epsilon\pm}=\theta^{\rm c}_{\mathrm{A},\epsilon\pm}\!\!-F_{\epsilon}\theta^{\rm q}_{\mathrm{A},\epsilon\pm},

(12)

where
\theta^{\rm c}_{\mathrm{R(A)},\epsilon\pm}
and
\theta^{\rm q}_{\mathrm{R(A)},\epsilon\pm}
represent the classical and quantum parts of the generalized spectral angles, respectively. In the above equation,

plays an essential role. The effective action would become trivial if there were no term that couples

to
\theta^{\rm q}_{\mathrm{R(A)},\epsilon\pm}

\bar{Q}_{\rm s}
that is determined by Eq. (
9
) with Eqs. (
11
) and (
12

Modified Usadel equation:
Following the plan described in the previous paragraph, we determine the form of
\theta^{\rm c}_{\mathrm{R(A)},\epsilon\pm}

with respect to each spectral angle. We work within GL theory by expanding

or

\displaystyle f^{\rm R}_{j\epsilon\pm}=-f^{\rm A}_{j-\epsilon\mp},\quad\theta^{\rm Rq}_{j\epsilon\pm}=-\theta^{\rm Aq}_{j-\epsilon\mp}.

(14)

Using Eqs. (

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{kernel},j}[f_{1n\pm},f_{3n\pm}]=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{source},j}[f_{1n\pm},f_{3n\pm}],

where

Equation of state:
The gap equation is obtained from the stationary condition
[\partial S/\partial\Delta_{\rm q}]_{\Delta_{\rm q}\rightarrow 0}=0
. In GL theory, the equation has the form
\alpha_{2}\Delta_{\rm c}+\alpha_{4}\Delta_{\rm c}^{3}=0
, where

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=4\,\Gamma^{-1}_{\rm c}\!-4\pi T\!\sum_{n>0}\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\Big[f_{1n\lambda}\!+\!\frac{1}{\omega_{n}\!+\!i\lambda h}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{0}^{\omega_{\rm D}}\frac{d\epsilon}{\epsilon}\Big[\tanh\left({\frac{\epsilon_{+}}{2T}}\right)+\tanh\left({\frac{\epsilon_{-}}{2T}}\right)\Big],

(16)

\displaystyle\alpha_{4}

\displaystyle=-4\pi T\!\sum_{n>0}\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\Big(f_{3n\lambda}-\!\frac{1}{6}[f_{1n\lambda}]^{3}\Big).

(17)

Note that

is derived from Eq. (
15
), and

is regularized by introducing the ultraviolet cutoff, which is the Debye energy

, to the energy integration. The form of the gap equation in the GL level suggests that the free energy and the supercurrent are given as

and

is then determined by the latter equation. In particular, the second-order SC phase transition line in the absence of the supercurrent is calculated based on the conditions

and
\partial\alpha_{2}/\partial A_{y}=0
. From now on, let

be the

as

, which is denoted as

when we neglect the e-e interaction. At

, we describe

and
T_{\rm c}(0)=T_{\rm c}
, for simplicity. For later use, we define the reduced temperature
\tau_{\rm r}
as
\tau_{\rm r}=T/T_{\rm c0}
or
\tau_{\rm r}=T/T_{\rm c}
.

WL conductivity:
When the current reaches its critical value, the SC state changes to a resistive state. Here, we study the WL correction to the conductivity,
\sigma_{\rm WL}

;
Maekawa2
;
Bergmann
. For simplicity, we neglect the e-e interactions. We find that it is convenient to work with the alternative normal saddle point

The absolute values of the diode quality factor increase divergently as the tricritical point is approached. Summary:
We have formulated the renormalized GL theory including the Cooper and Coulomb interaction terms from the NLSM based on the Keldysh functional formalism. Our formalism extends the previous one and enables us to study dirty 2D Rashba superconductors under in-plane Zeeman fields, which are typical platforms of the SD effect. We find that the interplay between localization and interactions leads to the corrections that suppress the SC states, the first-order transition, and the SD effect. Furthermore, we have calculated the WL correction to conductivity that characterizes transport when the SC state is destroyed by an electric current exceeding the critical current. The WL correction can be suppressed or enhanced by interactions depending on the strength of SOC. We expect our results to pave the way for switching between superconducting, metallic, and insulating transport via an electric current.
