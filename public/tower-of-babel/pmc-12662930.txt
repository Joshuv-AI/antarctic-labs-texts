Chronic stress-related neuroendocrine mediators and ovarian cancer
Authors: Hengwei Du, Guoxing Yu, Ruiqi Hu, Jing Du, Cuilan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer (OC), as one of the three major gynecological tumors, has been known as the “silent killer”. It is characterized by high mortality, high recurrence rate, and frequent drug resistance. Chronic stress has been increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to ovarian cancer progression through dysregulation of neuroendocrine pathways, particularly via the prolonged activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system (SNS). Key mediators, including glucocorticoids (GCs), catecholamines (epinephrine (E), norepinephrine (NE), and dopamine (DA)), and other neuroendocrine factors, contribute to tumor progression by modulating cell proliferation, metastasis, angiogenesis, chemoresistance, and tumor immune microenvironment. This review summarizes current evidence on the role of neuroendocrine mediators in ovarian cancer progression, highlighting molecular mechanisms, clinical and preclinical findings, and potential therapeutic strategies. Understanding neuroendocrine-ovarian cancer crosstalk may provide novel insights into ovarian cancer progression and inform combinatorial therapies targeting stress-mediated tumorigenesis.Keywords: Chronic stress, Neuroendocrine mediators, Ovarian cancer, HPA, SNS

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the seventh most common cancer in women worldwide [1]. Due to its subtle early symptoms and common diagnosis at advanced stages, ovarian cancer is termed a “silent killer” [2]. Currently, the treatment for ovarian cancer combines surgery with radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Despite advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, ovarian cancer remains the deadliest gynecological malignancy primarily due to tumor heterogeneity and the development of drug resistance, and continues to face the challenge of ‘recurrence-chemotherapy-recurrence’ [3]. The occurrence and progression of ovarian cancer are influenced by multiple factors, including chronic stress, genetic predisposition, endocrine disorders, reproductive history factors, obesity, and lifestyle-related factors, etc. Notably, chronic stress has emerged as a significant risk factor. Patients with ovarian cancer with depression and anxiety exhibit a significantly higher prevalence compared to healthy women [4], with chronic stress exacerbating disease progression through neuroendocrine pathways [5].

Stress is a common psycho-physiological response triggered by challenging, undesirable, or difficult situations, often referred to as stressors [6]. When the body perceives a disruption in homeostasis, it produces stress [7]. Stress is generally categorized as either acute or chronic, depending on the duration of exposure. Acute stress is short-term and activates the “fight or flight” response, thereby enhancing alertness and physical readiness. Chronic stress refers to persistent stress or an exaggerated response to stressors, including bereavement, socioeconomic burdens, social isolation, and illness [8]. Chronic stress significantly alters the secretion of stress-related hormones, including catecholamines (epinephrine (E), norepinephrine (NE), and dopamine (DA)), glucocorticoids (GCs), along with other potential diagnostic biomarkers such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), prolactin, oxytocin, interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-8 [6]. These stress-related neuroendocrine mediators link tumors and the nervous system mainly through the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. These mediators are released into the bloodstream or act locally within the tumor microenvironment, thereby promoting tumor progression [5]. Despite increasing evidence in clinical studies and animal experiments demonstrating psychological stress is associated with cancer incidence and progression, the mechanisms underlying tumor modulation mediated by psychosocial stress remain highly complex [9–11]. In clinical studies, meta-analytic research indicated that the high incidence and poor survival of breast, lung, head, neck, hepatobiliary, lymphoid, and hematopoietic cancers are closely related to psychosocial factors [12]. Previous studies have shown that stress can exacerbate key cancer hallmarks, including genomic instability, tumor-promoting inflammation, immune resistance, proliferation signaling, resistance to cell death, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis [13–16], with ovarian cancer exhibiting similar vulnerability to these pathways.

This review systematically elaborates chronic stress-related neuroendocrine factors, including their definitions, receptors, and mechanisms of action, and demonstrates how these mediators promote ovarian cancer progression through multiple pathways. Furthermore, it explores potential therapeutic strategies targeting chronic stress-driven mechanisms, providing new insights for the prevention and treatment of ovarian cancer in the future.

Neuroendocrine mediators and ovarian cancer

Epinephrine and norepinephrine

Accumulating evidence shows that chronic stress accelerates tumor growth through sustained activation of the SNS (Fig. 1). The SNS releases catecholamines, primarily E, NE, and DA, which serve as key signaling molecules in stress response through sympathetic nerve terminals [17]. These hormones are predominantly secreted by the adrenal medulla into circulation and released into target tissues. This process is referred to as the sympathetic-adrenal medulla (SAM) axis. Sympathetic nerve fibers also secrete NE as a primary neurotransmitter. Both E and NE mainly bind to β-adrenergic receptors (ADRBs), activating adrenergic transduction signals that directly regulate cell behaviors. ADRBs belong to the G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) with three subtypes (ADRB1, ADRB2, and ADRB3). Its primary function is to transduce extracellular signals into intracellular responses [18]. Chronic stress-induced catecholamine elevation regulates multiple oncogenic processes, including tumor cell metastasis, invasion, tumor angiogenesis, resistance to cancer therapy, and anti-tumor immunity [19].

Chronic stress activates the HPA axis and the SNS. Chronic stress mediates its physiological effects through two pathways. Firstly, chronic stress activates the HPA axis or HPG axis. Stressors (such as pressure and anxiety) stimulate the hypothalamus to release CRH or GnRH, which then act on the pituitary to secrete ACTH, FSH, or LH, and ultimately inducing GC secretion via the adrenal glands, or estrogen production by the ovary. Secondly, chronic stress activates the SNS, resulting in sustained sympathetic nerve stimulation and the release of NE and E. These neuroendocrine mediators from these two pathways can enter the tumor microenvironment and interfere with tumorigenesis. E and NE induced by chronic stress can bind to the receptors on the surface of tumor cells, activating the intracellular cascade reaction to promote the invasion and metastasis of cells. (Fig. 2A) E and NE binding to ADRBs triggers protein kinase A (PKA)-dependent signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) phosphorylation, which promotes the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2)/MMP-9, enhancing the invasion of ovarian cancer cells [20, 21]. Alternatively, NE stimulates ADRB-PKA signaling cascade, then activates the human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) and snail family transcriptional repressor 2 (Slug) pathway, promoting ovarian cancer aggressiveness [22]. NE enhances prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) synthesis via the ADRB2-nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB)-prostaglandin endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2) axis, driving the growth and metastasis of ovarian cancer [23]. In addition, E and NE enhance the secretion of CXC-chemokines (CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, and CXCL8) in ovarian cancer cells, while blockade of these chemokine receptors effectively reverses catecholamine-driven tumor cells invasion [24]. In chronic stress-induced ovarian cancer mouse model, elevated serum NE and IL-10 levels, along with altered expression of a tumor-promoting protein (NM23) and N-Myc downstream-regulated gene 1 (NDRG1), promoted ovarian carcinoma growth [24]. E and NE protect ovarian cancer cells from anoikis via a focal adhesion kinase (FAK)-mediated signaling pathway initiated by ADRB2 [25].

In addition to directly acting on tumor cells, E and NE can also act on other cells in the tumor microenvironment to promote angiogenesis, thereby facilitating tumor progression and metastasis. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is one of the key factors that induce and promote angiogenesis. Clinical studies show that lower VEGF levels are linked to higher levels of social support in patients with ovarian carcinoma [26]. In addition, in vitro experiments in mouse brown fat cells and human ovarian cancer cells have shown that NE and isoproterenol (a β-adrenergic receptor agonist) can induce the expression of VEGF, which is mainly mediated by the ADRB/cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)/PKA signaling pathway, which process promotes tumor growth [27–29]. Isoproterenol can also enhance ovarian cancer cells motility and adhesion via the laminin-5/exchange protein directly activated by cAMP1 (EPAC1)-dependent pathway [30]. Chronic stress-induced NE upregulation promotes IL-8 expression via FosB proto-oncogene (an AP-1 transcription factor subunit) through activation of the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway, resulting in enhanced microvessel formation and accelerated ovarian cancer progression and metastasis [31].

E and NE also modulate immune responses in ovarian cancer. E and NE were detected in the ascites of patients and mice with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), and their levels were positively correlated with pro-inflammatory factors and immunosuppressive factors, which can drive tumor-associated inflammation, T-cell suppression, and cancer progression [5]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are critical immune cells in the tumor microenvironment and have received extensive attention in recent years. Chronic stress promotes the infiltration of TAMs into the ovarian tumor microenvironment. Chronic stress-induced NE exposure promotes monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) production via the cAMP/PKA pathway in ovarian cancer cells, which can promote macrophage infiltration and poor overall survival [32].

Acquired chemoresistance remains a therapeutic challenge in ovarian cancer management. Studying using a mouse model reveals that chronic stress induces platinum and niraparib resistance in ovarian cancer, which probably associates with DNA damage repair pathways [33]. E and NE exposure induce nuclear γ-H2AX lesions and DNA damage, which may modulate cisplatin resistance and reduce the effectiveness of chemotherapy in patients with ovarian cancer [34]. Additionally, NE activates the cAMP-protein kinase C (PKC)-cAMP response elementbinding (CREB) signaling pathway via ADRB2 to induce mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase-1 (MKP-1) gene expression, thereby inhibiting ovarian cancer cell responsiveness to paclitaxel chemotherapy [35].

Tumors can recruit peripheral nerves to the tumor microenvironment (TME) and lead to enhanced tumor growth through different mechanisms in a series of cancer models [36]. The study found that the overall survival of patients with perineural invasion (PNI)-positive symptoms was significantly shorter than that of patients with PNI-negative symptoms. Multivariate analysis indicated that PNI was a significant independent prognostic factor for patients with ovarian cancer [37]. Autonomic nerve-derived NE activates the ADRB3-BDNF axis of ovarian cancer cells, then elevated BDNF levels in the tumor microenvironment increase innervation by nerve tyrosine kinase receptor B (TrkB) receptor [38]. A team has established a murine model of HGSOC and utilized image-guided microinjections of retrograde neurotracer to label tumor-infiltrating peripheral neurons, mapping their source and circuitry. The study found that regional sensory neurons innervate HGSOC tumors, and interestingly, the axons within the tumor trace back to local dorsal root ganglia as well as jugular-nodose ganglia [39].

In conclusion, as shown in Fig. 2A, E and NE mainly regulate the activity of transcription factors (e.g., STAT3, NF-κB, and Myc) via the cAMP/PKA pathway, then increase the levels of effector proteins (e.g., MMPs, VEGF) to drive malignant behaviors in ovarian cancer. However, the differential selectivity of receptors for E and NE may vary and require further investigation. Their effects on other cancer hallmarks, such as metabolic reprogramming, cellular stemness, and senescence, also warrant further exploration. Besides, their direct impacts on tumor microenvironment components (particularly immune cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts) need deeper investigation.

Glucocorticoid

The HPA axis, a negative feedback neuroendocrine system, is another central neuroendocrine mechanism activated by chronic stress (Fig. 1). In stressful conditions, the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) in the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) to trigger the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) from the anterior pituitary, then the ACTH stimulates the release of GCs from the adrenal cortex, which exert negative feedback regulation through the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary [40]. GCs consist mainly of cortisol, along with a small amount of corticosterone. The glucocorticoid receptor (GR), a nuclear hormone receptor activated by endogenous cortisol and synthetic glucocorticoids, is a ligand-dependent transcription factor that plays a critical role in stress response, energy homeostasis, and immune regulation [41]. Dysfunction of the HPA axis, characterized by decreased diurnal cortisol variability, a flat diurnal cortisol slope, and elevated nocturnal cortisol levels, is associated with low survival rates in patients with ovarian cancer [42]. GCs are important stress hormones commonly used clinically as anti-inflammatory agents, immunosuppressants, and adjuncts in cancer treatment to alleviate the adverse side effects of chemotherapy. However, evidence indicates that GR may be involved in tumor progression and affect the treatment response.

A clinical study involving 481 patients with ovarian cancer demonstrated that the median progression-free survival was significantly shorter in the GR-high expression group than in the GR-low expression group, suggesting that GR activity may promote tumor progression [43]. Another ovarian cancer clinical study found that elevated cortisol levels were associated with reduced survival; patients in the high nocturnal cortisol group had shorter overall survival than those in the low cortisol group [42].

Preclinical experiments revealed that cortisol can increase the aggressiveness of the ovarian cancer SKOV3 cell line [21]. However, another study has found that GC-GR complex may upregulate miR-708 expression, thereby inhibiting RAS-like small GTP-binding protein 1B (Rap1B), reducing integrin-mediated adhesion plaque formation, and suppressing ovarian cancer cell migration and invasion as well as abdominal metastasis in orthotopic xenograft mouse models [44] (Fig. 2B).

Dexamethasone (DEX), an exogenous synthetic glucocorticoid that activates GRs, exhibits pharmacological effects including anti-inflammatory, anti-endotoxic, immunosuppressive, anti-shock, and stress-enhancing properties. As a result, it is widely used across multiple clinical departments to treat a variety of diseases. DEX promotes ovarian cancer cells adhesion and chemoprotection through upregulation of integrin β1/α4/α5, fibronectin (FN), and mucin antigen 1 (MUC1, a transmembrane glycoprotein) [45–47]. When DEX is combined with cisplatin and/or paclitaxel (PTX), it increases the activity and adhesion of ovarian cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner [48], rendering them resistant to chemotherapy. DEX upregulates downstream ROR1 signaling mediators, upregulates Ras homolog gene family member A (RhoA) GTPase, YAP/TAZ, and B-cell-specific moloney murine leukemia virus insertion site 1 (BMI-1), suggesting that GCs can induce a ROR1-associated stemness phenotype in ovarian cancer cells [49]. In addition, knockdown of ROR1 and ROR2 significantly impaired the adhesion and invasion capabilities of ovarian cancer cells to retinal tissue, consequently suppressing retinal metastasis in ovarian cancer [50]. Furthermore, in a study investigating the apoptotic mechanism in epithelial ovarian cancer, GR activation was found to upregulate cellular inhibitor of apoptosis protein 2 (cIAP2) expression. These results indicate that cIAP2 serves as the primary anti-apoptotic factor regulated by DEX, and GCs exert anti-apoptotic effects through cIAP2-mediated pathways in ovarian cancer cells [51].

These findings demonstrate that elevated glucocorticoid levels or GR overexpression can promote drug resistance, recurrence, and metastasis through multiple pathways, including suppression of anti-tumor immunity, enhancement of cancer stemness, and increased invasiveness and anti-apoptotic capacity in ovarian cancer (Fig. 2B). This is highly correlated with poor prognosis in ovarian cancer. The biological effects of GCs may exhibit dose-dependent variations, and their impacts on stromal and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment require further investigation.

Dopamine

DA, an inhibitory neurotransmitter, is the precursor of NE and the most abundant catecholamine neurotransmitter in the brain. DA acts on dopamine receptors (DRs), which are classified into D1-class (DRD1/DRD5) or D2-class (DRD2/DRD3/DRD4) based on structural, pharmacological, and biochemical differences. Among these, D1-class receptors can increase intracellular cAMP levels after activation. Conversely, D2-class receptors reduce intracellular cAMP levels after activation. Both D1-like and D2-like receptors are highly expressed in ovarian cancer tissues [35]. DA deficiency causes depressive symptoms, and its interaction with DRs can have a range of physiological effects on tumors and stromal cells [35]. The pro-tumorigenic or anti-tumorigenic effects of DA may be related to different cancer species or DRs.

DA, an antiangiogenic neurotransmitter, stimulates perivascular coverage of the tumor vascular system through the DRD1-cAMP-PKA signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting chronic stress-mediated tumor growth. However, research has predominantly focused on DRD2 for ovarian cancer. In DRD2-knockout mouse models of ovarian cancer, tumor burden was increased, which may be related to enhanced tumor angiogenesis [30]. This effect may be attributed to dopamine’s ability to attenuate VEGF-induced angiogenesis by decreasing VEGFR-2-mediated downstream activity. In addition, DA acts on its specific DRD2 to inhibit tumor growth by counteracting the effects of vascular permeability factor(VPF)/VEGF-A on the tumor endothelium and bone marrow-derived endothelial progenitor cells [52]. Another study demonstrated that DA reduces cAMP levels and inhibits both norepinephrine(NE)- and VEGF-induced Src kinase activation through the DRD2-cAMP pathway in vitro, thereby blocking stress-induced ovarian cancer growth, suppressing cell invasion, promoting apoptosis, and attenuating stress-stimulated tumor angiogenesis [53]. DA and its agonists may also enhance the therapeutic efficacy of cisplatin therapy [54]. However, other studies have shown that DA may promote the growth of malignant tumors. For instance, DRD2 may contribute to the progression of psychologically stress-induced malignancies, such as melanoma and breast cancer, by activating the oxygen-independent hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) pathway [55]. Accordingly, DA exerts pro-/anti-tumor effects through different mechanisms across various cancer types. In ovarian cancer, it mainly exerts a tumor suppressor effect by regulating angiogenesis (Fig. 2C).

Other stress-related neuroendocrine mediators

Besides acting on the SNS or the HPA axis to produce catecholamines or glucocorticoids, chronic stress also activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis to produce sex hormones. Moreover, it can stimulate nerve fibers to secrete various neurotrophic factors and serotonin, thereby exacerbating the progression of ovarian cancer (Fig. 1).

Sex hormones

The HPG axis is an endocrine regulatory system that controls the physiological cycle in women. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) is produced and secreted by GnRH neurons in the hypothalamus. The anterior pituitary gland produces luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), while the gonads produce estrogen and testosterone (Fig. 1). Studies have shown that sex hormones potentially regulate the psychosocial stress response of the HPA axis and the SNS. In the hypothalamus system, the regulation of testosterone, estrogen, and progesterone can reduce the influence of stress on the cortisol response [56].

Estrogen regulation modulates a brain network that influences stress responses and contributes to cognitive and emotional dysregulation [57]. In vivo experimental studies found that increasing estrogen levels significantly accelerated the growth of estrogen receptor alpha (ERα)-negative HGSOC xenografts. Tumors in estradiol-treated mice exhibited significantly high rates of proliferation, angiogenesis, and TAMs density [58]. Clinical data suggest that menopausal estrogen replacement therapy increases the risk of ovarian cancer [58].

Androgen, also known as testosterone, is mainly secreted by the adrenal cortex, followed by the ovary in women is primarily synthesized by the Leydig cells of the testis, with additional secretion from the adrenalcortex and the ovaries. Available data suggest that androgens, androgen receptors (ARs), and associated signaling pathways are involved in the occurrence and development of ovarian cancer [59]. At present, the expression of ARs in ovarian cancer has been confirmed. Androgen-mediated ARs signal transduction can enhance the proliferation and invasion abilities of ovarian cancer cells. Approximately 1% of ovarian tumors composed of testicular cells can cause hyperandrogenism, followed by characteristic masculinization [56]. Among them, patients with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) have the characteristic of hyperandrogenism, which is one of the risk factors for ovarian cancer.

GnRH, secreted by the hypothalamus, stimulates or inhibits gonadotropin secretion by the pituitary gland. GnRH analogs can act directly on tumor tissue. Relevant studies have revealed that GnRH agonists enhance the motility and invasiveness of ovarian cancer cell lines Caov-3 and OVCAR-3. GnRH analogs upregulate metastasis-related proteases MMP-2 and MMP-9, suggesting that GnRH may promote ovarian cancer metastasis via activating MMPs to promote cancer cells migration and invasion [60].

Serotonin

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT), a stress-modulating neurotransmitter, is colloquially termed the "happy hormone" due to its association with mood regulation. It is widely distributed in the central nervous system and peripheral tissues, including female reproductive organs. Serotonin enhances the function of the body’s autonomic nervous system and is involved in psychiatric disorders, such as depression and generalized anxiety. It also regulates the sleep/wake cycle, mood, oocyte maturation, and stress-related dysregulation [61]. Through genome-wide CRISPR screening, serotonin receptor 1E (HTR1E) was identified as a key gene regulating the peritoneal spread of ovarian cancer. In patients with ovarian cancer, high HTR1E expression is positively correlated with improved clinical prognosis. Serotonin/HTR1E signaling inhibits chronic stress-induced ovarian cancer progression [61].

Neurotrophic factors

Neurotrophic factors are a family of small polypeptide growth factors , consisting of five members: nerve growth factor (NGF), BDNF, neurotrophin (NT)-3, NT-4/5, and NT-6. Among these, NGF mediates stress and plays a critical role in translating environmental stimuli into physiological and pathological feedback [62].

NGF is critically involved in stimulating epithelial ovarian cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis, and its functionality is modified by stress exposure [63]. Catecholamines can upregulate the levels of neurotrophic factors, increasing sympathetic innervation and local NE accumulation, thus forming a feedforward loop [64]. Additionally, BDNF promotes cancer cell growth, survival, proliferation, mutation, and migration through TrkB and p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR) death receptor [65]. A previous study in vitro and in vivo found that BDNF/TrkB might also mediate the inhibition of anoikis apoptosis via activating the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT/PKB) pathway in ovarian cancer cells [66]. TrkB can serve as a prognostic marker for ovarian cancer, and its overexpression is associated with tumor size, metastasis, and advanced disease [67].

Potential therapeutic strategies targeting neuroendocrine mediators

Despite the rapid development of medical technology and advancements in surgery and chemotherapy, ovarian cancer remains a threat to women’s health worldwide. Based on the previous mechanistic elucidation, chronic stress-related mediators can promote or suppress the development of ovarian cancer. The development of targeted drugs benefits from research on the underlying mechanisms linking chronic stress-related mediators to ovarian cancer. These drugs may serve as potential therapeutic strategies for clinically treating or alleviating chronic stress-induced ovarian cancer (Table 1).

Drugs associated with β-adrenergic receptors

Regarding the pro-tumor effects of E and NE on ovarian cancer, the pharmacological antagonism of β-adrenergic signal transduction suggests a potential therapeutic effect on cancer progression, the tumor microenvironment, and systemic mechanisms [17]. In preclinical studies, propranolol, a non-selective β1 and β2 adrenergic receptor blocker, inhibited tumorous pro-invasive effects by both direct and indirect function [21]. Propranolol also suppresses STAT3 activation, and significantly reduces stress-induced focal metastasis and tumor weight [20]. When combined with programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, propranolol reduced tumor burden and metastatic nodules while enhancing anti-tumor immunity in an orthotopic ovarian cancer mouse model [19]. This mechanism may involve propranolol’s regulation of the cancer immune response by decreasing interferon (IFN)-γ production and IFN-γ-mediated PD-L1 overexpression [19]. Additionally, in a clinical retrospective study examining the association between beta-blockers and epithelial ovarian cancer progression and survival, patients using beta-blockers had a 54% lower risk of death from ovarian cancer and prolonged survival compared to non-users [68, 69].

Drugs related to glucocorticoids or their receptors

GCs act as a double-edged sword in ovarian cancer progression, either promoting or inhibiting, depending on their binding mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment and their effects on cancer cells and stromal cells. Therefore, in the clinical treatment of patients with ovarian cancer, GCs should be used with caution.

Building on the first-generation GR antagonist mifepristone, researchers developed ORIC-101 as a highly potent steroidal glucocorticoid receptor antagonist. It has reduced androgen receptor agonist activity, making it suitable for administration in ARs-positive tumors, and it features improved cytochrome P450 2C8 (CYP2C8) and cytochrome P450 2C9 (CYP2C9) inhibition profiles, minimizing the potential for drug interactions [70]. In ovarian cancer xenotransplantation models, ORIC-101 enhanced the ovarian cancer response to chemotherapy and demonstrated anti-tumor activity in vivo [70].

β-arrestin, a scaffold protein involved in negative signaling of GPCRs, influences cell proliferation, cytoskeletal rearrangement, and cell motility [71]. Studies have found that patients with ovarian cancer with high expression of β-arrestin 1 and 2 exhibit improved survival rates, whereas patients with low GR expression had high survival rates [71]. Notably, patients with high-expression β-arrestin 1 and low-expression GR had the best prognosis across all groups. The combination of β-arrestin and GR may provide a stronger ability to predict prognosis.

Drugs related to dopamine and its receptors

The previous section demonstrated that DA exerts receptor-specific effects in ovarian carcinogenesis via modulating distinct pathways. These findings suggest that targeting DA pathways could offer effective strategies to alleviate, treat, and prevent ovarian cancer.

DRD2 blockers, such as thioridazine, are commonly used clinically to treat anxiety and stress caused by psychosomatic disorders. In vitro studies have demonstrated that thioridazine induces apoptosis and autophagy in ovarian cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner, and exerts chemo-sensitization effects via inhibiting the cell cycle [72]. Another experiments in vivo found that thioridazine inhibited the growth of SKOV3 xenografted tumors in nude mice [73]. Similarly, another DRD2 inhibitor, trifluoperazine (TFP), also exhibits potential anti-tumor effects. Liu et al. found that trifluoperazine suppresses the progression of malignant tumors under psychological stress [55]. The anti-tumor effects of thioridazine and trifluoperazine suggest that these drugs are promising candidates for the treatment of patients with ovarian cancer.

Other interventions

Sex hormones

Ovarian cancer is an endocrine-related tumor whose progression may be either promoted or inhibited by hormones released from the HPG axis [71]. Gonadotropins, estrogens, and androgens are known to promote ovarian cancer progression, whereas GnRH and progesterone may help prevent it [71].

Targeting estrogen receptors (ERs) is a promising treatment option for patients with ERs-positive ovarian cancers. Progesterone, a natural hormone, is generally regarded as a protective factor against ovarian cancer. However, it is also a key endogenous factor that induces the development of primary tumors in metastatic ovarian cancer. In contrast, inhibition of progesterone signaling-achieved through mifepristone treatment or progesterone receptor (PR) gene knockout- effectively prevents progression and peritoneal metastasis in HGSOC, indicating progesterone’s potential tumor-promoting role in ovarian cancer [74]. Therefore, targeting progesterone signaling with anti-progesterone agents may serve as an effective nonsurgical option for the prevention of ovarian and breast cancer [74].

The contraceptive effect of combined oral contraceptive pills is primarily mediated through suppression of pituitary gonadotropin (LH and FSH) release, thereby preventing ovulation and suppressing the synthesis of ovarianprogesterone and estrogen. Consequently, women who use oral contraceptives maintain consistently lower levels of menstrual progesterone during their reproductive years, which significantly reduces their risk of ovarian cancer after menopause [74]. Additionally, GnRH analogs represent a potential treatment for ovarian cancer. These analogs inhibit pituitary gonadotropin secretion and may target GnRH receptors using either GnRH agonists or antagonists as therapeutic agents [75].

Psychosocial interventions

Patients with ovarian cancer often endure the emotional burden of diagnosis, the physical challenges of surgery, the pain of long-term chemotherapy, and the financial strain of treatment. These factors can lead to feelings of detachment from people or activities, making it difficult to adapt to daily life, work, and education. Such challenges may impair psychological functioning and create a vicious cycle between negative emotions and disease progression. In addition to pharmacological interventions, it is crucial to support patients with ovarian cancer in improving their cognitive, emotional, and behavioral functions [76]. This involves restoring or rebuilding damaged psychological well-being through appropriate psychosocial interventions tailored to the unique needs of each patient.

Treatment with traditional Chinese medicine (TCM)

TCM has long been known for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and other health-promoting effects. In recent years, with the improvement of living standards, TCM has gained increasing attention for its potential in healthcare. Numerous studies have demonstrated that TCM also has substantial anti-tumor effects, including inhibiting promotion ovarian cancer cell apoptosis, inhibiting cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and drug resistance. These findings offer novel insights into the clinical management of ovarian cancer and lay the foundation for further research on the pharmacological mechanisms of TCM.

For example, salidroside exerts anti-tumor effects via inhibiting glycolysis mediated by the STAT3/c-Myc pathway, thereby interfering with malignant biological processes in ovarian cancer cells [77]. Ginkgetin, an active bi-flavonoid isolated from leaves of Ginkgo biloba L., inhibits ovarian cancer growth by targeting the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) /STAT3 and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) signaling pathways [78]. Additionally, combining quercetin—an active ingredient in Astragalus—with poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) enhances the antiproliferative activity of breast cancer susceptibility gene (BRCA) wild-type ovarian cancer cells [79]. In recent years, TCM has also been used to treat depression. For instance, Zhi-zi-chi Decoction has demonstrated efficacy in alleviating depressive symptoms [80]. Furthermore, TCM has been reported to delay the progression of tumors induced by chronic stress [81]. Tonglaxative prescriptions inhibit the growth of stress-induced tumors in colorectal cancer mouse models by stimulating the immune response [81]. This effect may be mediated by the inhibition of the HPA axis, promotion of dendritic cell maturation, and activation of T cells, thereby enhancing the antitumor immune response. Ultimately, this halts colorectal cancer progression. Similarly, Xiaoyao Powder has been shown to inhibit chronic stress-induced liver metastasis in colon cancer [82]. Notably, the potential therapeutic effect of TCM on chronic stress-related ovarian cancer remains unexplored. Focusing on TCM with anti-cancer and anti-depressive activities may provide new insights for the management or adjuvant therapy of stress-related ovarian cancer.

Exercise intervention

It is well known that appropriate exercise interventions can effectively reduce anxiety and depression, and improve physical fitness and function. Exercise therapy has been widely used in the rehabilitation of various diseases and has been recognized as an effective adjuvant therapy for improving cardiopulmonary function [83]. Studies have shown that exercise can significantly reduce tumor size and weight, potentially inhibiting cancer progression by upregulating thrombospondin type 1 domain containing 7B (THSD7B) [84]. Aerobic exercise or high-intensity interval training can slow lung cancer progression and reduce incidence/mortality. These benefits may be mediated by enhanced macrophage anti-tumor activity [85]. Chemotherapy is a primary treatment for ovarian cancer, but it often causes debilitating side effects, such as peripheral neuropathy. Exercise may help counteract chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy in patients with ovarian cancer. Cao et al. found that 6 months of aerobic exercise combined with weekly health education calls (that is attention control) significantly improved peripheral neuropathy symptoms and enhanced the quality of life in patients undergoing ovarian cancer chemotherapy [86, 87].

To summarize, in this section, we discussed potential treatment strategies for patients with ovarian cancer encountering chronic stress. Among them, social psychology and traditional Chinese medicine theories are valuable exploration directions, but the current evidence is mainly observational and animal experiments, and clinical translation requires caution. Additionally, the therapeutic potential of antagonists or agonists targeting chronic stress-related neuroendocrine mediators requires further preclinical validation. In clinical practice, while these factors may complement standard therapy (e.g., by improving quality of life), they are not recommended as alternatives [87]. Therefore, in the future, we need to strengthen mechanism research and accumulate high-level clinical evidence to avoid over-interpreting previous results.

Discussion

This review systematically elaborates on the multi-dimensional roles of chronic stress-related mediators in the progression of ovarian cancer. Existing evidence suggests that the hormones and neurotransmitters released under long-term stress are not merely by-products of psychological states but serve as key regulatory factors actively involved in tumor biological behavior. They mediate the entire process, from tumor growth and metabolic adaptation to distant metastasis, by activating specific cellular signaling networks, thereby offering a new perspective for explaining the high invasiveness and recurrence rate of ovarian cancer. Different stress mediators exhibit cross-interaction and integration in regulating downstream signaling pathways and biological processes, indicating that their effects on ovarian cancer are not isolated but form a complex regulatory network. In addition to evidence from basic research, clinical studies also suggest a potential association between levels of stress mediators and the prognosis, survival duration, quality of life, or treatment resistance in patients with ovarian cancer [68, 69].

Gaps and challenges

The interrelationship between neuroendocrinology and cancer is extremely complex, and several research gaps remain as outlined below. (1) Most previous studies have focused on investigating single factors (e.g., those mediated by ADRBs, GRs, or DRs). The effect of a single intervention measure may be limited. Whether different stress mediators exert synergistic or antagonistic effects in ovarian cancer remains to be explored. (2) Research on the cross-talk between stress mediators and other factors in the tumor microenvironment (such as cytokines and chemokines) is still in its infancy. (3) Current studies predominantly rely on cell lines and mouse models, which are difficult to replicate the heterogeneous, long-term, and low-level chronic stress conditions in humans. There is a need to develop more advanced models that can integrate the complex interactions among the nervous, endocrine, and immune systems.

Given the close relationship between stress and cancer, the importance and effectiveness of stress management have been proven [10]. However, there are technological and methodological challenges in preclinical and clinical research. First, stress exposure of different intensities (e.g., restraint stress, electric shock, and social defeat) and duration of stress (e.g., acute stress and chronic stress) may have different, even contrary effects on ovarian cancer progression [88]. Second, as the clinical patient population presents greater complexity, anti-stress treatment targeting neuroendocrine mediators may generate poor or adverse clinical outcomes. Studies showed that beta-blockers have the effect of prolonging the survival rate of patients with ovarian cancer [68, 69]. However, another retrospective study using multifactor analysis found that patients with ovarian cancer treated with beta-blockers at the time of surgery had lower overall survival rates and a greater risk of death than those who were not treated with beta-blockers [89]. Third, due to the dynamic nature of stress-related mediators, it is difficult to accurately assess the stress level in patients through a single measurement of mediators at a specific time. Additionally, the use of medications such as beta-blockers or glucocorticoids for other conditions can perturb endogenous hormone levels. Therefore, additional biomarkers are needed to precisely identify patients with stress-related conditions and to carry out targeted anti-stress interventions.

Future outlook

Current research on the role of the neuroendocrine system in ovarian cancer has focused on isolated pathways (such as the HPA axis or the SNS), while overlooking the dynamic crosstalk between different axes. Employing systems biology approaches (such as multi-omics integration analysis and single-cell sequencing) to reveal the synergistic or antagonistic effects of different stress mediators in the TME, as well as their dynamic regulation of immune cell functions, will help identify “synergistic nodes”. Future therapeutic strategies may involve multi-target interventions, such as simultaneously blocking ADRB and GR (e.g., using a combination of propranolol and mifepristone), to counteract potential compensatory pathway activation.

Additionally, developing non-invasive and dynamic monitoring of chronic stress-related neuroendocrine mediators will evaluate their potential as predictive biomarkers for therapeutic efficacy or prognosis, and provide a basis for staged treatment and dynamic adjustment of intervention strategies. Furthermore, additional preclinical studies should assess if combining stress-reducing interventions with targeted therapies improves overall survival in chronic stress-associated ovarian cancer. Exploring the mechanisms of TCM in regulating the HPA axis and sympathetic activity may also provide adjuvant therapies to mitigate side effects of surgery and chemotherapy. To advance the clinical translation of research on chronic stress-induced ovarian cancer, we need to break through the “single-pathway mindset”. Integrating multi-omics approaches, innovative clinical trials, and interdisciplinary collaboration will bridge the gap between basic research and clinical application, providing a new dimension for treating patients with ovarian cancer.

Limitations

Although this review has summarized extensive research on the impact of stress on ovarian cancer, it primarily focuses on experimental studies, and the inclusion and discussion of clinical observational studies may not be sufficiently comprehensive. Due to limitations in length and scope, some recently discovered stress-related molecules might have been omitted. Moreover, as most of the cited mechanistic studies are based on preclinical evidence, extrapolating these findings to clinical applications requires extreme caution.

Conclusion

In conclusion, abundant evidence has established that chronic stress-related mediators play a crucial role as key promoters of the malignant progression of ovarian cancer. These mediators regulate tumor cells and their microenvironment through a complex molecular network, forming a bridge connecting psychological and neural activities with tumor biology. Although challenges remain in deepening the understanding of the underlying mechanisms and translating these findings into clinical applications, targeting stress-related signaling pathways undoubtedly represents a highly promising new therapeutic direction. In the future, through close collaboration between basic researchers and clinicians, as well as the integration of psychosocial care with biological treatment strategies, a more comprehensive and effective “mind-body” integrated management model is expected to be developed for patients with ovarian cancer.
