Bloodborne Infections Relevant to Dental Practice
Authors: Suhail H. Al-Amad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The surgical nature of dental practice, which frequently includes a combination of using sharp tools and the presence of blood, puts dental practitioners at a significant risk for infection with bloodborne pathogens, mainly hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV. Several seroprevalence studies and case reports traced back newly diagnosed cases of hepatitis B and C to recent episodes of dental treatments. This paper provides an update on the prevalence of hepatitis B and C and HIV, the likelihood of acquiring an infection following an occupational injury, the manner in which serologic tests should be interpreted, and practical advice on ways to reduce the risk of occupational exposure.

Introduction

Bloodborne infections are defined as the transmission of a bloodborne microorganism from one person to another. This transmission occurs when the microorganism from the source (ie, the person who has the infectious microorganism) is able to enter the bloodstream of a recipient(s) (ie, the person who does not have the microorganism at the time of microbial transmission). For this transmission to take place, microorganisms need to be carried by a vehicle and enter the recipient's body through a route. A vehicle represents the medium on which a microorganism is carried from the source to the recipient. Media include air, blood, and exocrine secretions (eg, saliva). The route—on the other hand—represents the portal through which that entry occurs (Figure 1).Fig. 1Diagram showing the manner in which bloodborne infections occur.Fig 1

Depending on the nature of their clinical work, health care providers are at risk of acquiring bloodborne infections and can themselves be a source of infection to their patients directly by transmitting microorganisms from themselves to their patients or indirectly by neglecting to properly disinfect clinical surfaces or sterilise contaminated tools and equipment.

Contrary to other health care providers, dental health care workers, during the provision of dental treatments, are simultaneously exposed to a number of potential vehicles that carry microorganisms to them from their patients. Those vehicles include blood, air, and saliva. Westermann et al1 estimated that dental health care workers are 3.5 times more likely to be infected with hepatitis C virus (HCV) compared with the general population, whilst the odds ratio for the same risk is 2.2 for both medical and laboratory personnel. Within the dental profession, oral surgeons were 3.2 times more likely to have laboratory findings that were positive for antibodies against HCV compared with general dentists.2 This occupational risk is ameliorated by the fact that the infection status of source patients is unknown at the time of transmission, which can result in a delay in seeking early intervention on the part of health care providers sustaining the injury.3 It is therefore crucial for health care providers to know the infection status of source patients immediately after each injury.

This review paper aims to provide an update on the prevalence of bloodborne viral infections, particularly hepatitis B virus (HBV), HBC, and HIV, in addition to discussing recent data on the likelihood of acquiring an infection following an occupational injury. The paper also aims to enhance dental health care providers’ knowledge on interpretation of serologic tests of bloodborne infections.

Microorganisms causing bloodborne infections

Fred et al4 listed more than 30 microorganism species that can be transmitted by blood from source to recipient. The list includes viruses, bacteria, protozoa, and prions. Despite each of those microorganisms having the ability to cause human disease, the greatest risk of morbidity and mortality amongst health care professionals has been attributed to 3 viral species. Those are HBV, HCV, and HIV, which will be the focus of this paper.

Prevalence rates and outcomes

HBV

HBV infection is a significant health problem with potentially serious consequences. It is believed that around 30% of the world population is or has been infected with HBV,5 with an estimated 350 million persons chronically infected with HBV worldwide.6 The prevalence of HBV varies widely amongst regions and amongst nations within the same region, with rates ranging from less than 0.5% in North America, Scandinavia, and parts of western Europe to more than 8% in sub-Saharan Africa and East and South-East Asia.6 In addition to this marked geographic diversity, there is an observed diversity in the route of transmission between low- and high-endemicity countries. Low-endemicity countries show a horizontal mode of transmission that is mainly attributed to high-risk behaviours and noncompliance with infection-control measures, whilst high-endemicity countries show a vertical mode of transmission from infected mother to neonates during delivery.6

HBV infection can result in acute or chronic liver disease. Progression to a chronic HBV infection is inversely related to age.7 It is estimated that in 90% of perinatal babies, 25% to 50% of preschool children, and 4% to 5% of adults, acute infection progresses to chronic HBV infection.6,8 HBV, if not remitted via natural or therapeutic measures, can result in mortality rates of around 40% and 15% in infected men and women, respectively. Death from HBV is attributed to liver cirrhosis, liver failure, or hepatocellular carcinoma.5 Overall, 70% of all hepatocellular carcinoma is caused by hepatitis infection.7 Fortunately, the advent of the HBV vaccine in early 1980s9 resulted in a reduction in HBV infections in many parts of the world, particularly amongst newborns and children.10

HCV

Worldwide, an estimated 58 million persons have chronic HCV infection, 3.2 million of whom are children and adolescents.11 Similar to HBV, prevalence rates of HCV show remarkable regional diversity,12 with the highest overall prevalence rate exceeding 3.5% in Central and East Asia and in North Africa/the Middle East.13

In nearly 1 in 4 persons infected with HCV, the disease will be self-limiting with no serum HCV-RNA detectable 6 months after the initial infection. The remaining 75% of patients will experience chronic hepatitis, which can later progress to liver cirrhosis, end-stage liver disease, or hepatocellular carcinoma.14,15 Over a 20-year period of chronic HCV infection, 10% to 30% of patients will experience liver cirrhosis and 1% to 5% will experience hepatocellular carcinoma, both of which result in an annual mortality rate of almost 400,000.14 The overall prognosis of HCV infection varies depending on patients’ age, sex, ethnicity, and comorbidities that impact the patient's immune response.15 Mortality rates have also been associated with the patients’ serologic state. Patients with both HCV antibodies and HCV-RNA are almost 8 times more likely to die from liver-related disease than those with only HCV antibodies.16

In addition to its detrimental hepatic consequences, HCV infection has been linked to several extrahepatic diseases and conditions, including diabetes mellitus; vasculitis; diseases affecting renal, cardiovascular, and cerebrovascular systems; autoimmune and immune-mediated diseases such as Sjogren's syndrome; rheumatoid-like artheritis; and lichen planus.16,17 The exact role of HCV in causing those diseases remains unclear.

HIV

The number of people currently living with HIV worldwide is estimated to be 39 million.18,19 In 2022, 1.3 million new infections were reported amongst individuals, the majority of whom live in sub-Saharan Africa.18 Despite preventive measures and early screening, HIV cases have increased in several regions in the world since 2010, particularly in South Africa, Nigeria, Mozambique, India, Kenya, and the United States.20 Remarkable diversity in the prevalence of HIV has been reported at a the level of individual countries.21 The prognosis of HIV dramatically worsens as infection progresses from the HIV carrier state to an active AIDS state. Progression to AIDS occurs in approximately one-third of HIV-infected persons.22 A number of factors have been associated with this progression, including patient's age (greater rates in older persons), mode of infection (greater rates in homosexuals), and CD-4 cell count (greater rate associated with lower counts).22 Mortality rates from AIDS have declined in many countries since 2005. In 2022, 630,000 patients died of AIDS-related illnesses, which is less than one-third of AIDS-related deaths reported in 2004.19 Reductions in mortality rates have been attributed to enhanced methods of early intervention, adoption of universal precautions, and the development of antiretroviral therapy, which was particularly impactful in preventing mother-to-child transmission during delivery.20

Dentistry as a risk factor for transmission of bloodborne infections

Likelihood of transmission of bloodborne viruses, particularly HCV and HBV, has been associated with a number of variables, including patients’ age, use of glass syringes, multiple sexual exposures, provocative abortion, and delivering children at home using traditional methods.23, 24, 25 Several seroprevalence studies have shown significant associations between occurrences of HBV and HCV infections and history of receiving dental treatment.23, 24, 25, 26, 27 In the United States, a number of cases of newly diagnosed HBV infection were traced back to recent dental treatment, during which infection control measures were reported as being suboptimal.28, 29, 30, 31

Despite the associations reported in observational studies and case reports, large cohort studies that included health care providers actually exposed to HBV, HCV, and HIV during occupational activities did not show concerning rates of transmission between infected patients and providers. For example, in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland, amongst a series of 4830 cases of occupational exposure to HBV, HCV, and HIV that occurred between 2004 and 2013, 9 health care providers were infected with HCV (at a rate of 1 in 285) and none were infected with HBV or HIV.32 Interestingly, a very similar outcome was reported by Lee et al,33 who reviewed 284 cases of occupational exposure to samples infected with HBV, HCV, and HIV and reported a single case of transmission of HCV, with no transmission HBV or HIV from infected patients to occupationally exposed health care providers.33 Eskandarani et al,34 on the other hand, reported no transmission of HBV, HCV, or HIV between infected patients and occupationally exposed health care providers in a cohort with 2274 incidents of exposure to blood and body fluids. The very low rate of transmission can be attributed to a number of factors, including immunisation against HBV and adherence to postexposure prophylaxis policies. For example, in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland, amongst health care providers who were exposed to HBV following occupational injury, 96% were already immune to the virus and almost all providers exposed to HCV and HIV promptly received postexposure prophylactic antiviral therapy.32

Percutaneous injuries amongst dentists

Kane et al35 reported high estimates of HBV, HCV, and HIV infection in the developing world attributed to unsafe injections. Dentists are amongst the health care professionals who are at significant risk for acquiring bloodborne infections following percutaneous injuries.36 Prevalence of percutaneous injuries amongst dentists is relatively high. It is estimated that around 7.7% of dentists have had a percutaneous injury during their professional career,37 which puts dentists and their allied clinical teams at a great risk for acquiring bloodborne infections. The risk of acquiring a bloodborne infection from an infected person after a percutaneous injury depends on a number of factors, such as the amount of blood carried to the bloodstream of the recipient, the gauge of hollow needle that caused the injury, and the viral load of the source at the time of injury. Amongst the many sharp tools used in dentistry, needles, dental burs, and metal matrices were the instruments associated with most injuries, whilst those associated with the fewest injuries were surgical instruments, scalers, explorers, and curettes.37,38 Injuries have also been associated with dentists’ juniority and the fewer years of clinical experience.39,40

Measures to reduce exposure to bloodborne pathogens

Development of policies on the proper handling of sharp instruments, conducting regular trainings of health care workers on those policies, monitoring compliance, and reviewing the circumstances of each injury occurrence are some of the important measures needed to reduce occupational injuries.

Most occupational percutaneous injuries are considered preventable by avoiding unsafe practices and utilising safety-engineered devices, particularly when dealing with blades, suture needles, local anaesthesia needles, irrigation needles, burs, and scaler tips.

The following are suggested measures to reduce the likelihood of percutaneous injuries:-Keep the operating tray (where sharp instruments are placed) well illuminated throughout the procedure and placed at a suitable height and distance from the operator, so that instruments can be reached with ease and without the dentist having to bend or overextend their arms. Instruments on the tray should be ordered in a practical manner and aligned parallel to each other. Instead, dispose of them in the sharps container immediately after use, even if you think you might need to use them again. For optimal safety, your operating tray should never hold used surgical blades or local anaesthesia needles. Actions to take in case of percutaneous or mucocutaneous injury

Regardless of their respective medical histories, patients and health care providers should be serologically assessed after any percutaneous (sharp) or mucocutaneous (splash) injuries that occur during or after treatment. A percutaneous injury is considered to occur when a used sharp instrument penetrates the epidermis. This is normally evidenced by the visible presence of blood on the skin of the injured individual. A mucocutaneous injury occurs when the patient's blood or body fluids (ie, saliva in the case of dentistry) contact the health care provider's mucosal surfaces of the eyes, nose, or mouth.42Fig. 3Summary of the steps and serology tests that should be undertaken after percutaneous and mucocutaneous injuries.Fig 3

Interpretation of serologic assessments

Following percutaneous or mucocutaneous injuries, serologic assessment of the source and the recipient are mandatory to discover potential transmission and start treatment early. Serologic assays for HBV, HCV, and HIV are discussed below, along with the interpretation of each test result.

Interpretation of HBV

Multiple tests have been developed to assess HBV infectivity and immunity. Those include the following43:

HBs-Ag (HBV surface antigen): This test assesses a surface protein antigen of HBV. A positive result indicates an active HBV infection, during which time the infected person can spread the disease. This surface antigen is the viral component used to produce the HBV vaccine. HBsAg can be detected within the first 30 days of receiving the HBV vaccine and may be misinterpreted as an active infection. Therefore, vaccination history should be obtained from the source patient prior to interpretation of results.

Anti-HBs (antibodies against HBV surface antigen): Because the HBV vaccine is based on HBs-Ag, the detection of antibodies against HBV surface antigen is considered evidence of immunity against HBV following either successful vaccination or remission from an infection.

Anti-HBc (antibodies against HBV core antigen): Detection of antibodies against the core antigen indicates recent or old viral damage to hepatocytes. This is the main test to diagnose HBV infection. Because the HBV vaccine does not cause hepatocellular damage, anti-HBc results will be negative. Anti-HBc is thus useful to distinguish between those with immunity against HBV as a result of a previous infection from those who are immune as a result of the HBV vaccine. Anti-HBc persists throughout life, and two classes of antibodies are assessed to interpret the stage of infection: IgM (indicating an acute infection within less than 6 months) and IgG (indicating a chronic infection).

Vaccination

Health care providers are at risk for exposure to bloodborne pathogens, and a main precaution for preventing infection is vaccination. To date, there are no effective vaccines against HCV or HIV, although several novel methods to develop an effective vaccine against those 2 viruses are being undertaken.47,48 An effective vaccine against HBV exists, and the earliest version of this vaccine was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 1981, followed by a more effective recombinant vaccine in 1986.49 The vaccine consists of a viral subunit that sensitises the immune system to produce antibodies against the viral outer protein coat and surface antigen. After receiving 3 consecutive doses at 0, 1, and 6 months, recipients develop lifelong immunity. However, seroconversion does not occur in all vaccinated persons. To verify seroconversion, antibody titre testing against surface antigen (ie, anti-HBs) must be performed 1 to 4 months after the last dose. In cases in which seroconversion does not occur, a single booster dose should be given, followed by another confirmatory anti-HBs test. A lack of response after the booster dose necessitates revaccination with 3 doses. Persons in whom immunity against HBV still does not develop must receive HBV immunoglobulin after each potential exposure to this virus.49

Postexposure prophylaxis (PEP)

Despite taking all precautionary measures to prevent percutaneous and mucocutaneous injuries, health care providers, particularly dentists, continue to be prone to infection with bloodborne viruses. PEP is a term used to describe the immediate medical measures aimed at reducing the chances of a recipient's seroconversion following an exposure to a confirmed case of HBV, HCV, or HIV. Although the efficiency of PEP regimes in preventing bloodborne infections has been extensively studied, their application in various parts of the world remains low because of either lack of awareness or concerns over their safety and tolerability.50

Amongst the 3 main bloodborne viruses, HIV was most studied in terms of PEP regimes. Following an exposure to a confirmed case of HIV, recipients normally receive a combination of antiretroviral drugs, in what is known as highly active antiretroviral therapy. The standard regime for PEP consists of an integrase strand transfer inhibitor given in combination with tenofovir and either lamivudine or emtricitabine.50 To be successful, this regime should be initiated within 7 days of exposure to HIV.51

PEP regimes to prevent HBV infection largely depend on the immunisation status of the recipient. Persons with protective antibody titres do not need postexposure prophylactic measures. For the those who have not been immunised, PEP measures might include hepatitis B immunoglobulins or an initiation of HBV vaccination.52 PEP for HCV remains controversial and consists of either early initiation of antiviral medications or deferring intervention and monitoring the recipient with regular anti-HCV antibody screenings, starting antiviral treatment if transmission is confirmed.52 Due to diversity of guidelines and policies amongst nations, it is imperative for health care providers to familiarise themselves with institutional and national guidelines where they practice.

Conclusions

Bloodborne infections can occur following percutaneous or mucocutaneous injury. Amongst all health care professionals, dental health care workers are at the greatest risk for acquiring bloodborne infections, mainly HBV, HCV, and HIV. Receiving a successful HBV vaccine and handling sharp instruments properly during dental treatments are essential steps to minimise the likelihood of an occupational exposure. Correct interpretation of the source's blood test findings is important in assessing the exposure risk and starting treatment early.
