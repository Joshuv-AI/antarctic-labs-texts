A Case of Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension and Pregnancy, Managed With Pulmonary Endarterectomy: Clinical Challenges, Case Report, and Literature Review
Authors: Hassan Ahangar, Mohammad Ghasemi Palangi, Sepehr Gohari, Payman Yasami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a rare, potentially curable form of pulmonary hypertension, with limited data in pregnancy. We describe a 37‐year‐old woman with progressive dyspnea, back pain, and cough, whose evaluation revealed severe right ventricular enlargement, high pulmonary pressures, and extensive V/Q mismatches, confirming CTEPH. She underwent successful pulmonary thromboendarterectomy with marked hemodynamic recovery. An unrecognized early pregnancy, later confirmed after spontaneous miscarriage at 14 weeks, complicated the course. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges of CTEPH in women of reproductive age and highlights the impact of pregnancy‐related hemodynamic stress on disease presentation.Keywords: chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, maternal mortality, pregnancy, pulmonary endarterectomy, right ventricular dysfunction, ventilation–perfusion scan

Introduction

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), a WHO Group IV pulmonary hypertension, arises from unresolved pulmonary embolism leading to organized thrombotic obstruction of the pulmonary arteries, elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, and eventual right ventricular failure [1]. Although it often follows acute pulmonary embolism, up to 25% of patients develop CTEPH without a prior symptomatic event, delaying diagnosis and contributing to under‐recognition [2].

The standard of care for operable CTEPH is Pulmonary Endarterectomy (PEA), a potentially curative surgical procedure that significantly improves hemodynamics and survival [1, 2]. For patients who are inoperable or have persistent disease, Balloon Pulmonary Angioplasty (BPA) and targeted medical therapies such as riociguat and phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitors have emerged as effective alternatives [1, 2, 3].

Pregnancy imposes profound hemodynamic and coagulative changes including a 45% increase in cardiac output and blood volume, reduced systemic vascular resistance, and a hypercoagulable state that can overwhelm the compromised right ventricle in CTEPH [4, 5]. Accordingly, pulmonary hypertension has historically been considered a contraindication to pregnancy due to high rates of maternal morbidity and mortality [5, 6]. A recent systematic review encompassing 4583 pregnant patients with pulmonary hypertension reported a maternal mortality rate of 6%, with substantially higher risks among those with severe disease or Eisenmenger physiology [6].

Specific experience in CTEPH during pregnancy remains limited but informative. A recently published case report describes two successful pregnancies in a woman with Antiphospholipid syndrome who had undergone PEA, with relatively stable cardiopulmonary status throughout gestation [7]. Additional literature suggests that when hemodynamic status is optimized either via BPA or postoperative recovery from PEA, careful pregnancy planning and multidisciplinary management may lead to favorable outcomes [7, 8].

Taken together, these data highlight that, although pregnancy in the context of severe CTEPH carries considerable risk, contemporary advances in surgical and interventional techniques, rigorous anticoagulation, and PH‐specific medical therapies, coupled with multidisciplinary care, may allow for successful pregnancies in select patients.

In this report, we describe the case of a pregnant woman diagnosed with CTEPH, outlining her clinical course, therapeutic interventions, and maternal fetal outcomes, alongside a literature review to contextualize current evidence and best practices.

Case Presentation

A 37‐year‐old woman presented complaining of progressive exertional dyspnea, exertional back pain, and a dry non‐productive cough, which had gradually worsened over the course of 1 year. Past medical history was remarkable for gestational hypertension. She denied any prior history of venous thromboembolism, cardiovascular disease, or chronic pulmonary conditions. Upon presentation, her vital signs were as follows: heart rate 95 bpm, blood pressure 135/80 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 95% on room air. Physical examination was unremarkable. Transthoracic echocardiography performed on first visit revealed the following: left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 48%, and the left ventricular cavity was notably small (Left Ventricular End‐Diastolic Volume Index (LVEDVI) = 27 cc/m2). The right atrial area (RAA) was enlarged (21 cm2), and there was evidence of septal flattening. The right ventricle (RV) was severely dilated with moderate systolic dysfunction (Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic Excursion (TAPSE) = 10 mm; RV sm = 8.8 cm/s), and mild RV hypertrophy (7 mm) was noted. Systolic Pulmonary Artery Pressure (sPAP) = 95 mmHg, (Tricuspid Regurgitation Gradient (TRG) = 80 mmHg). The inferior vena cava (IVC) measured 2.2 cm with < 50% inspiratory collapse. The TAPSE/sPAP ratio was reduced (0.175 mm/mmHg), suggesting impaired RV‐pulmonary artery coupling.

A ventilation‐perfusion (V/Q) lung scan showed complete absence of radiotracer uptake in the upper and middle lobes of the right lung, extending into the upper segment of the right lower lobe. Perfusion was preserved in the lower half of the right lung, and two additional segmental perfusion defects were identified in the lower lobe of the left lung, findings consistent with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH).

Thoracic CT scan without contrast revealed cardiomegaly and mild thickening of the interlobular septa, without evidence of parenchymal masses or focal lesions. Bronchoscopy showed no abnormalities. Pulmonary function testing demonstrated preserved ventilatory function: Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 s (FEV1) = 84%, Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) = 81%, and FEV1/FVC ratio = 109%, excluding significant obstructive or restrictive lung disease.

During the course of diagnosis, the patient experienced an unplanned pregnancy, which was not disclosed to the medical team. At approximately 14 weeks of gestation, she suffered a spontaneous miscarriage. The pregnancy and its outcome were only recognized retrospectively, as the patient had not reported her condition to the healthcare providers.

Based on clinical findings and imaging, a diagnosis of CTEPH was established. The patient was referred for surgical intervention and underwent successful pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PEA) (Figure 2).

Postoperative echocardiography after 8 weeks demonstrated marked improvement: LVEF was 51%, RV size was normalized with preserved systolic function (TAPSE/sPAP = 0.66 mm/mmHg), TRG decreased to 20 mmHg, and sPAP reduced to 25 mmHg. RAA had decreased to 17 cm2, LVEDVI had increased to 44 cc/m2, and IVC was 1.7 cm with > 50% inspiratory collapse. Vital signs at this stage were stable: heart rate 80 bpm, blood pressure 120/80 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 96% on room air. A postoperative ECG was obtained (Figure 3).

The patient's symptoms improved significantly following surgery, and she was placed on continued follow‐up with the pulmonary hypertension team.

Discussion

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a progressive and under‐recognized cause of pulmonary hypertension (PH), resulting from persistent obstruction of the pulmonary arteries due to organized thrombi. Although rare, it remains a potentially curable disease through pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PEA), especially when diagnosed and managed in a timely manner [9, 10].

Our case a 37‐year‐old woman with progressive exertional dyspnea and back pain, ultimately diagnosed with severe CTEPH, demonstrates several notable differences compared to the existing literature. Unlike most patients, she had no prior history of pulmonary embolism (PE), thrombophilia, or major cardiovascular disease, aligning with reports that nearly 25%–50% of CTEPH patients present without known thromboembolic events [11, 12].

Comparison With Published Cases

In the case reported by Kulkarni et al. [13], a 35‐year‐old man also presented with no documented history of PE but had significant extrahepatic portal vein thrombosis, leading to systemic‐pulmonary collaterals and right heart overload. Unlike our patient, in whom an unplanned pregnancy ended in spontaneous miscarriage at 14 weeks of gestation, the diagnosis in their case was suspected relatively early due to portal hypertension. This comparison highlights the importance of timely recognition and management of pulmonary hypertension, as delayed diagnosis or unawareness of pregnancy in such patients may further increase maternal and fetal risks.

The adolescent case presented by Aristizabal et al. [12] described a 17‐year‐old male with progressive dyspnea, hemoptysis, and high pulmonary pressures. Imaging revealed thrombi in the main pulmonary artery extending into branches, and cardiac catheterization confirmed severe PH (PVR = 15 WU). Surgical PEA resulted in functional improvement. Similarly, our patient underwent successful PEA with dramatic hemodynamic recovery, sPAP reduced from 95 mmHg to 25 mmHg, and RV function normalized. However, in contrast to the adolescent case, our patient's surgical intervention occurred post‐fetal loss, suggesting the critical importance of early risk stratification in pregnancy.

Arant et al. [14] reported a case misdiagnosed as asthma for several years, only to be later diagnosed with CTEPH after detailed hemodynamic and imaging evaluation. Their patient also underwent PEA with excellent clinical outcomes. This parallels our case in terms of misattribution of symptoms to benign respiratory conditions and delayed diagnosis. However, our case is unique due to the pregnancy context, which amplified the diagnostic complexity and clinical risk.

Clinical Implications

This case reinforces the diagnostic challenge posed by CTEPH, especially in women of reproductive age, where symptoms such as dyspnea, fatigue, or lower oxygen saturation may be misattributed to normal gestational physiology. In our patient, the occurrence of an unplanned pregnancy, which was not disclosed to the medical team, resulted in a spontaneous miscarriage at 14 weeks of gestation, highlighting how the lack of pregnancy‐related information can hinder timely clinical decision‐making. Given the high maternal mortality associated with pulmonary hypertension in pregnancy, current guidelines recommend aggressive assessment of unexplained cardiopulmonary symptoms in this population [3, 15, 16], along with effective patient counseling regarding the risks of pregnancy in women with CTEPH. Our findings support the integration of echocardiography and V/Q scanning as first‐line tools, even in pregnant patients, due to their safety and diagnostic yield.

Moreover, the successful outcome after PEA in our patient, despite late diagnosis and pregnancy‐related complications, mirrors findings from published literature regarding the efficacy and safety of PEA in experienced centers [17]. Nevertheless, this case uniquely emphasizes that delayed recognition in pregnancy may result in irreversible fetal consequences, making early suspicion and diagnosis paramount.

Conclusion

In summary, this case highlights a rare but life‐threatening presentation of CTEPH in a woman of reproductive age who experienced an unplanned and undisclosed pregnancy that ended in spontaneous miscarriage at 14 weeks of gestation without prior PE. Compared to the existing literature, it illustrates the added complexity of pregnancy, the importance of V/Q imaging, and the critical impact of timely surgical intervention. This underscores the need for heightened awareness and early multidisciplinary evaluation of dyspnea in women of childbearing age, particularly when accompanied by signs of right heart strain such as an improvement in TAPSE/SPAP ratio following targeted therapy.
