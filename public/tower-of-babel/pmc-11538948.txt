Institutionalising social innovation in the Global South: Lessons learned☆
Authors: Saray Bucio-Mendoza, José Alberto Solis-Navarrete
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost social innovation research in scientific literature focuses on cases in the Global North, where basic needs have already been met. However, examining the Global South, where institutional failures and gaps pose a more complex challenge and require innovative solutions to promote territorial development, is crucial. Our study analyses how social innovation with territorial impact has been institutionalised in Global South countries, contrasting these experiences with Global North countries. The most pressing needs addressed by social innovation in the Global South are food, poverty and health, with a higher emphasis on service and process innovations. These innovations create socio-environmental value through governance processes and attention to basic needs, particularly in rural and peri-urban areas. The various experiences in institutionalising social innovation in the Global South have given rise to multi-faceted innovation referred to as territorial–institutional innovations in contrast to the Global North, combining bottom-up and top-down approaches to institutionalising social innovation through governance structures and policy support to promoting territorial development.Keywords: Institutional innovation, Developing countries, Global North, Territorial innovation, Rural and peri-urban areas

Introduction

The scientific literature documents diverse experiences with social innovation, mainly from countries in the Global North [1]or those with their basic needs met. However, there needs to be more references that reflect diverse contexts, such as those observed in the Global South or the countries of Latin America, Asia, and Africa, which denote regions outside Europe and North America, primarily low-income countries [2]. This complicates reaching a consensus on the conceptualisation of social innovation and how it can contribute to a territory.

The territorial diversity observed in urban, peri-urban, and rural areas [3] and the emergence of new approaches to innovation [4] go beyond economic or mercantile purposes, such as social innovation, which can be defined as new ideas that work to satisfy social goals [5] or innovative activities and services that aim to fulfil social needs [6]. It is even more complex to analyse the characteristics that allow identification and recognition of social innovation and its impacts in Global South countries [1].

A large part of social innovation attempts to address basic needs and urgent problems and is motivated by events such as institutional failures and gaps [7], socio-environmental conflicts [8] government failures [9] and poverty [10]. Global integration processes have generated territorial transformations by diffusing and appropriating social innovations, as evidenced in the spatial and socio-environmental spheres [[11], [12], [13]].

How innovations are adopted, recognised and socially appropriated in diverse territories of the Global South could contribute to territorial development; hence, the institutionalisation of social innovation is a critical element of territorial development [14]. This idea stems from the broad concept of social innovation mentioned earlier, which enables the integration of grassroots initiatives [15,16] and those associated with top-down approaches of policies and formal institutions that support social innovation [[17], [18], [19]].

Although this research does not attempt to generate a debate on territorial development, innovation can sometimes refer to isolated efforts that have yet to become institutionalised in replicable practices at the governmental or civil society level and, therefore, cease to be sustainable or are lost with time.

Social innovations in different forms and initiatives have also reached a territorial anchorage by evidencing positive social change [20] facilitating governance processes, reducing uncertainty, and improving relations between citizens and the government itself [21] thereby generating processes of institutionalisation of social innovation towards sustainable development.

Thus, this paper analyses experiences with institutionalisation of social innovation with territorial impacts in countries of the Global South contrasting these experiences with countries of the Global North through a qualitative approach of content analysis and semantics focused on the scientific literature.

Conceptual background

In this section, different innovation approaches are developed. These include traditional or business-focused innovation (subsection 2.1), which has various offshoots such as frugal innovation [22], inclusive innovation [23], and cost innovation [24]. These offshoots are geared towards meeting the needs of emerging markets, low-income sectors, vulnerable groups, or those with limited access to specific products and services. They are more economically motivated rather than socially, and for this analysis, we will not be discussing these concepts.

The primary definition of social innovation guiding this research is based on innovations initially driven by the goal of meeting social needs more effectively than existing solutions [25]. This involves systematically applying knowledge innovations to solve social problems without profit motives, ultimately aiming to improve living conditions [6,21].

This section discusses the concepts of innovation (subsection 2.1) and social innovation (subsection 2.2), as well as the different innovation approaches primarily associated with social innovation and territory (subsections 2.3 to 2.7). Finally, it provides a more detailed explanation of the connection between social innovation and territorial development in subsection 2.8.

Innovation

The concept of innovation has evolved through distinct phases. It associates the idea with new knowledge or its combination with other resources in search of profit [26,27], and [28] conceived as the commercial application of a new or improved product or the action of endowing resources with a unique capacity to produce wealth. In this sense, innovation emerges from studying economic progress by applying new forms of production that are increasingly based on the use and application of knowledge.

According to the Oslo Manual [29] the introduction to the market of a new or significantly improved product (good or service) or process, a new marketing method, or a new organizational method for the company's internal practices, workplace, or external relations.

The concept of innovation is often associated with knowledge creation. However, while there have been proposals for broader and more integrative concepts of innovation, the need for social innovation has been mainly discussed in the Global North, particularly in the European Union. It has not been as established in the Global South, according to much of the academic literature [30].

The Oslo Manual [29] identified several different types of innovation, all of which introduce something new or improved to the market: technological product innovation (including internal characteristics); process innovation; marketing innovation as new marketing methods and delivering the value proposition; and organisational innovation. The latter two can be grouped into service innovation.

By taking up the idea of the value proposition [31], it is also possible to identify the objective and motivation of social innovation [21] distinguishing its impact, whether economic, social or environmental. Thus, primarily referring to the value generated, innovation can be successful in different contexts according to a pluralistic approach [26] to environmental innovation with economic consequences. These can form part of a new approach to innovation, although with some overlap with other types of innovation, such as technological and process innovation.

On the other hand, the emergence of multiple approaches to innovation has led to broadened the discussion on the still diffuse scope and boundaries of what can be considered as social innovation; thus, the concept of frugal innovation is not considered part of this analysis, due to its objective and motivation, being primarily oriented to generate economic value [21] through the development and marketing of products and services at a lower cost, oriented to the satisfaction of needs in lower-income markets [32] and can also make inclusive innovations affordable, as evidenced in formal and informal economic settings [22].

Social innovation

Social innovation could be associated with the Oslo Manual definition, as was done by Ref. [33], that referred to it as new solutions considering the different types of innovation mentioned in section 2.1 that primarily satisfy social needs more effectively than existing solutions; these can come from both bottom-up initiatives and are therefore closely linked to grassroots innovation [15], as can be seen in Section 2.3. However, they can also include social processes and practices [34], public policies, and institutional changes, i.e., top-down initiatives [35,36]. Thus, social innovations must first satisfy social needs and secondly arise from the systematic application of knowledge to be considered as innovations [30], the latter being what distinguishes them from social entrepreneurship and multiple forms of social interactions [37], thus being non-innovative social processes [21].

Therefore, social innovation includes essentially the same activities and possibilities mentioned in the Oslo Manual but for a different purpose. In this case, its objective is to generate social change [18] understood as improving the social conditions of life and achieving a better society. This favoured a shift in the paradigm of innovation towards a much broader concept that is reflected in the latest edition of the Oslo Manual [25] which considers new or improved products or processes that differ from previous products or processes of the unit that are placed at the disposal of potential users.

Social innovation is also a collective learning process involving the distinctive participation of civil society actors aimed at solving a social need through a change in social practices that produce changes in social relations, systems and structures, thereby contributing to a significant socio-technical change [38]. Finally, social innovation is associated with novelty, which is derived from generating and applying knowledge of knowledge and seeks to address social problems and needs (social value) and improve living conditions [21].

Grassroots innovation

Although papers on the conceptualisation of grassroots innovations are emerging, they consider the articulation of networks to provide customised and incremental solutions to localised problems [39,]. Thus, grassroots innovation is a community-led initiative to solve problems related to adopting a socially inclusive approach with different actors participating [40]. In this sense, multiple approaches to innovation may address social needs, such as inclusive innovation, frugal innovation and jugaad innovation, however, these may be linked to a more economic profit-driven orientation rather than necessarily responding to social motivations [21], thus outside the analysis of this paper on social innovation.

On the other hand, grassroots innovation is highly related to social innovation, as both are located within the collective power to innovate and promote social change at the local level [41]. Additionally, from an endogenous point of view, it involves a ‘bottom-up’ perspective since it refers to networks with a local approach with shared interests and values among a community [16]. Therefore, it concerns initiatives from organised civil society [42] or new citizen participation with different actors in each territory [43]. Grassroots innovation values traditional knowledge and recognises vulnerable or excluded groups [4]. Similarly, grassroots innovations can focus on more just and ecological societies [44].

Transformative innovation

Like grassroots innovation, transformative innovation is also linked to the territory. However, it involves more systemic and profound changes by considering capabilities, infrastructure and institutions with crucial public policy participation to favour innovation with different effects and scales [45]. It is an integrative and transversal process of public policy to trigger governance processes that invert political dirigisme towards the empowerment of citizens in making their own decisions. Therefore, it is about transformation in systemic aspects of the territory, from the socio-technical to radical changes oriented towards sustainability [46].

This type of innovation can be hybridised with social innovation by considering systemic changes and an institutional perspective through change narratives and the development of social innovation ecosystems [47]. Thus, it is possible to observe transformative innovation in policymaking by considering the agenda, design, implementation, monitoring, evaluation and learning aspects [48].

Institutional innovation

The analysis of the institutional framework is related to the new institutionalism, and it is a determinant factor in the development of a society in which the quality and type of existing institutions are essential for the proper performance of the economy and the social and educational sectors. The main idea is that nations produce within their borders not what available resources allow but what the institutions and public policies allow [49]. [50] initially proposed the term ‘institution’ as a set of habits that shape the relationships and functions of individuals and the community. According to Ref. [51], institutions are the formal and informal boundaries that limit individual behaviours; they are the boundaries created to restrict individuals' performance in a community.

Institutions emerge to reduce uncertainty; as ‘rules of the game’, they constrain human interaction, correcting market failures in which agents can enhance their development [52]. Institutions can be formal, such as laws, rights and codes, or informal, such as the family, community and religion. In due time, they coexist with organisations with a physical infrastructure, which are also governed by institutions that define roles and functions for trusted performance. Organisations, learning and institutional change depend on technological change, market size, incentives through laws, pure and applied knowledge and the scaling of technological development that favours evolution and social change for better economic performance [51].

According to the pluralistic model [26]that can generate institutional value derived from such institutional changes based on the application of knowledge can generate certainty through changes in policies and institutional frameworks [53].

As a result, public innovations can be included, such as new, non-written forms of institutions or changes in informal institutions that facilitate governance processes by achieving social consensus. Thus, institutional change can be treated as endogenous to the economic system to promote development through new forms of property rights and more efficient market institutions [54]. Thus, institutional innovation may provide the enabling framework conditions and create policies for innovation, especially social innovation [20].

These innovations refer not only to governmental innovations in terms of efficiency, transparency, cutting red tape or the attention to public problems but also to the involvement of different actors and new forms of participation [55]. Therefore, these innovations are linked to social innovations, initially by providing new and better public policies. In the long term, they can generate positive social changes by enhancing the government's and its citizens' relationship [21].

Territory and territorial innovation

Multiple types of territory analysis exist, for which it is possible to identify a series of classifications. From a geographical perspective, we can divide the territory according to its area or environment in a multidimensional sense associated with population and land use, thus creating urban, rural, and peri-urban areas [3].

The urban environment is a space with high concentrations of people organised around different activities, other than agricultural activities [56], with characteristics such as high population density (above 1500 inhabitants per square kilometre) and infrastructure that does not allow high spatial fragmentation [57]. In contrast, rural areas have low population agglomeration, low population density (100–700 inhabitants per square kilometre) and high agricultural dynamics [58]. However, they may transcend agricultural activity to production decisions, climate, natural resource use, customs and culture, local knowledge and land tenure [59].

Peri-urban areas are not entirely urban or rural; they could be considered urbanised rural areas [3]. As they are present around large and medium-sized cities, they also present more systemic changes or transitions [60]. Therefore, they can assume a form of heterogeneous spaces or processes susceptible to change [61].

In addition to the urban, rural, and peri-urban areas or environments, there may be conditions and capabilities for innovation to emerge in a suitable territorial environment promoting sustainable development [62]. This assumption has been widely discussed from the perspective of regional innovation systems [63] with flows of knowledge and institutions and the generation of networks. Such networks facilitate the formation of human capital, entrepreneurship, governance [64] and social inclusion [65].

Like sustainable development, territorial innovation can simultaneously adopt several types of innovation (social, environmental and economic). We refer to an environment that favours the emergence of innovation with the generation and articulation of capabilities through knowledge flows influencing the improvements in living conditions and sustainability at the territorial level, like the territorial innovation systems approaches [21,66].

Although our research does not attempt to generate a debate on the conceptualisation of territorial development, we can refer to the vision of [67] as a multidimensional concept based on dimensions (economic competitiveness, social cohesion, environmental sustainability, social governance and spatial planning), pillars (create, retain and distribute wealth) and scales (from local to continental). For this purpose, it is necessary to consider social networks or commonly shared values and the interaction between coordination, institutional factors and resources [68].

Social Innovation to territorial development

Social innovation can be directly linked to development and have implications at the territorial level. This includes placed-based innovation that involves co-governance between public policy and civil society [11], which is a form of institutional innovation. It also encompasses the development of social innovation ecosystems through the formation of networks and knowledge flows to address social needs and improve living conditions [69,70]. Furthermore, it involves local innovations in specific contexts that engage local people and resources [6], as well as the articulation of global innovation networks [13] at a territorial level rather than sectoral, creating territorial links. In addition to improving the well-being of those involved in social innovation, it can also positively affect the well-being of third parties [21].

Social innovation and territorial development are understood as a multifaceted form of innovation within a spatial context (urban, peri-urban, or rural). This serves as a transformative mechanism for spatial relationships through new governance and organizational structures involving actors and space within an institutional context [62,71]. Institutions, and thus institutional innovation, play a crucial role in linking territory and actors, resulting in institutional changes that transform social relations within space, perpetuating place-bound and spatially exchanged identities and culture [67,71]. This encompasses bottom-up processes within non-formal institutions through grassroots innovations and top-down processes within formal institutions, which are associated with transformative innovation.

Materials and methods

In this research, we used a qualitative approach. Data analysis was done using the content analysis method [74,75] with a semantic revision of documentary sources from scientific information databases, such as Web of Science, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar. We considered scientific journals, books and book chapters published in English and Spanish on the different cases and experiences in different countries and did not limit the research to a particular area or discipline of knowledge. The search period covered the last 22 years—from 2002 to 2023—in the indicated databases and has the highest emergence of definitions of social innovation in the scientific literature [38].

We used keywords as categories of analysis according to the proposed conceptual background. The categorisation and validity of the research protocol were performed following the criteria on social innovation and new innovation approaches presented by Refs. [4,21] prioritising research that analysed the impact on the territory. According to the conceptual background of this paper, particul arly section 2.8 the categories we analysed were.

Using FoxIt Reader and Atlas.ti, the search string was configured by combining the category of social innovation with the rest of the categories, as shown in Fig. 1. To do this, we first identified over 138,000 documents on social innovation. Subsequently, the results were combined with grassroots innovation (155 documents), transformative innovation (1024 documents) and institutional innovation (2840 documents), obtaining three combined results; thus, the innovation change narratives were framed as a combination of transformative innovation and social innovation. In our third step, we excluded the duplicate documents. Then we combined the categories related to the territory, including territorial innovation and development, so that the territory was the main criteria for inclusion. Another inclusion criterion for the articles retrieved was that they mentioned or referred to results through which value generation could be analysed using the pluralistic approach [26].

Research procedure.

As previously explained and shown in Fig. 1, the qualitative analysis involved content analysis. First, we identified documents using the category 'social innovation' (SI). Then, we added the categories 'grassroots innovation' (GI), 'transformative innovation' (TI), and 'institutional innovation' (II) to the analysis, resulting in three coded results: SI + GI, SI + TI, and SI + II. These three results were then combined with the territory (T) categories, which included the keywords: Territory, territorial innovation, and territorial development. This process yielded three combinations: SI + GI + T, SI + TI + T, and SI + II + T, totalling 304 documents. Finally, we deeply analysed these 304 documents to identify experiences in both the Global North and the Global South.

Results

From 2002 to May 2024, we found 304 documents, including research articles (239), books (19) and chapters (49) on social innovation related to the territory in both the Global North and the Global South. Only a quarter of these referred to experiences in Global South countries; these geographical distribution criteria was based on the location of each identified experience. It denotes higher visibility to the needs of more developed countries, showing that social innovation is closely linked to the social economy because economic motivations are considered in its emergence.

Most documents provided evidence of activities related to fair trade, social and solidarity economy, alternative economic practices or where sectoral activities become frequent. These activities mainly contribute to improving economic conditions in a territory through employment, the elimination of intermediaries or access to fair trade networks [76,77].

As shown in Fig. 2, the examples in the scientific literature are abundant in the cases of social innovation for territorial development analysed in the Global North, with the most prevalent in the United Kingdom, the United States, Sweden, Northern Ireland and Spain, followed by Australia, Canada, France and Poland; thus, facilitating institutionalisation processes through the establishment of regulatory frameworks and public policies as well as organisational schemes aimed at governance.

The product innovations identified were implemented in an urban context, addressing mobility solutions for the visually impaired in Europe (European Commission, 2011) and developing financial solutions with patterns of social change and ecological impact in Brazil [82,95]. Process innovations have been presented in different contexts and territories, with most innovations in countries of the Global North. At the same time, in the Global South, cases were identified in rural areas, including governmental platforms in Colombia [81]; agricultural production and consumption networks in Africa and Brazil [83,94]; family healthcare strategies [80] in Brazil; policy space and transformative social learning in India [85,86]; grassroots policies in South Asia [39] and emancipatory processes of territorial planning with nature–society interaction in Turkey [84].

Service innovations were primarily identified in countries of the Global South, mainly in Latin America, and occurred in different sectors and contexts, either with or without government participation.

In this sense, we find social, environmental, economic, and institutional innovations, and there may also be combinations. Although the articles we identified are directly related to social innovation, we found not only the generation of social value but also an essential contribution in the environmental and institutional areas and hybrids formed from them, socio-environmental and socio-institutional. In the institutional sphere, processes were aimed at governance, regulatory improvement, public policies (and co-creation), the generation of platforms and support networks, observatories, crowdsourcing in the legislative agenda and mechanisms that articulate innovation systems. However, most of this evidence was found in the urban areas of countries in the Global North (34 documents).

In the socio-institutional component, cases from the Global North were also more frequent. In contrast, in the Global South, different experiences were identified, such as the creation of digital platforms by the government based on co-creation to reduce poverty in Colombia as the result of organisations and laboratories [81]. They contributed to the formation of B companies and the articulation of actors in the innovation system. Other cases include the social impact bonds taken from the United Kingdom and applied in Mexico [80] and the initiative ‘Turkish Institutional Context’ to generate interrelationships between institutional gaps and social innovation processes by using actors as catalysts and agents of change [91].

In the environmental and socio-environmental field, countries of the Global South stand out (26 documents) with initiatives related to ecology, dialogues of knowledge for environmental sustainability or, specifically, the cases of the development of financial solutions with social and ecological impact in Brazil [93] and the generation of grassroots initiatives between the government and society to detonate socio-ecological regional development in Costa Rica [89].

According to the type of environment (see Fig. 6), most of the experiences can be identified in urban areas mainly in European cities, urban and rural areas in Canada and the United States, and the previously mentioned case of social impact bonds in Mexico.

In the rural environment, countries in the Global South participated more, with experiences in the education sector in Paraguay (63), the development of an Indigenous telecommunications network in mountainous areas [4], family health care strategies to reduce infant mortality in Brazil [80], inclusive innovation in the coffee sector in Mexico [96], and regional development through a dialogue between actors in Turkey [84].

According to the literature review, social innovation experiences can also be identified through the inclusion and participation of women in the reconstruction of rural areas, as highlighted by Refs. [97,98].

The institutionalisation of Social Innovation in the Global South is characterized by two different approaches. The first approach focuses on grassroots innovations and involves changes in non-formal institutions. This includes collective behaviours linked to social capital that aim to create bottom-up governance processes, facilitating collaboration between various stakeholders at the local level to address social needs stemming from institutional gaps and lack of public services. These needs may include poverty, food security, education, and the development of new products through collaborative efforts.

The second approach is centered on transformative innovations and driven by innovation policies. This top-down approach leads to changes in formal institutions, with a focus on promoting entrepreneurship, improving health services, addressing employment challenges, providing financial inclusion, and developing technological platforms.

Both approaches are present in different regions of the Global South, and some regions, such as Brazil, Mexico, Turkey, and Southeast Asian countries, share similar experiences in social innovation.

Discussion and conclusions

The literature shows more studies on experiences in countries of the Global North, with 72 % of the total number of documents identified (213). These studies primarily focus on entrepreneurship-related activities, with impacts mostly linked to the market and environmentally sustainable practices.

In the Global South, examples from the literature related to the cases of transformative innovation (35 documents) include the generation of new urban living arrangements promoting sustainability in the peri-urban areas of some Asian cities [99] as well as a couple of experiences in the peri-urban territories in Mexico related to the health and education categories, such as social innovation for education and mental health in marginalised communities [100] and the creation of a territory/model that rethinks economic and social principles with social justice and multiple environmental, technological innovations [87].

This research did not seek to measure sustainable development [101] by analysing the trends and trajectories that conceptual categories of plural valuation have taken in the Global South, as [102] did; instead, it seeks to make visible the efforts made in the Global South sensu [103] linking innovation for sustainability at academical framework, in our research we find that the high shortages are linked to basic needs could generate a more complex context for successful social innovation towards sustainability, particularly in the long term, without necessarily having a public or private financing structure, maybe more easily abandoned [5]. It seeks to address the most pressing basic needs in more rapid ways than a social innovation may require in terms of time, resources and articulation of actors.

The institutionalisation of social innovation in countries of the Global South is associated with initiatives linked to territorial development with different approaches and scopes. The impacts on the territory are evidenced by improvements in the population's living conditions due to the provision of services and infrastructure that were not available before and by the generation of social capital and governance processes that have facilitated institutional change based on bottom-up social innovations. This is evident in Latin American cases such as the indigenous telecommunications networks in Mexico [4], the Four-Leaf Clover program for access to health in Brazil [68], and Sitawi for financial solutions in Brazil [64]. Additionally, there are examples of experiential learning educational infrastructure in Paraguay [64], the Association for the Development of the Northern Zone in Costa Rica [72], and the Turkish Institutional Context generating institutional support and actor training in Turkey [70]. Moreover, there is public policy support to grassroots innovations inMalaysia, the Philippines, and Thailand [81]; the development of responsive, transformative, and inclusive practices in India [82]; and honey bee networks in various North African countries [83], among others.

In areas with higher marginalisation, such as the peri-urban and rural areas of the Global South, innovations have detonated a bivalent change: social and institutional. They generate social value in the first instance and institutional value in the second, by addressing social needs and then by filling institutional gaps and governmental failures resulting from the lack of public service, thereby contributing to an improvement from an environmental sustainability perspective [79,87,94,99,[104], [105], [106], [107], [108]].

In countries of the Global South, various instances of institutionalisation go beyond social innovation. These instances refer to non-formal transformative innovations or territorial-institutional innovations in contrast to Global North countries. They involve institutional changes in behavioural or unwritten forms and have a strong sense of belonging to the territory. This broadens the scope of transformative innovation, which is linked to innovation policy [39,85,86]. Different innovation approaches emerge with multiple values being generated, primarily focusing on social values in the Global South, unlike the Global North, followed by institutional and economic values mentioned above. Civil society also participates broadly, linking social innovation with grassroots innovations. These facilitate the development of governance processes in the territory to address multiple problems, such as socio-environmental issues.

This methodology was used because it was considered relevant to the research objective. We were interested in identifying the knowledge published in the academic literature over the last twenty years about the subject and the specific correlations of concepts linked to the new approaches to innovation and the impacts on the territory that we were interested in making visible. Although many review papers focused on the Global South adopt an inclusion/exclusion criterion that removes studies conducted in the Global North context. We have decided to include both groups of countries, firstly to contrast the proportion of documents identified, 72 % for the Global North and 28 % for the Global South, and secondly to show the differences in the processes of institutionalisation of social innovation, and finally to highlight the main gaps identified in the countries with lower levels of development.

This research differs from other bibliometric studies on social innovation, grassroots innovation, and transformative innovation. It combines the type of value generated by innovation with institutional and territorial aspects. This allows us to visualise experiences in countries of the Global South with more complex social needs and problems addressing sustainability than those of the Global North, linked to economic and social initiatives. From a territorial and sustainable perspective, this research allows us to disaggregate the initiatives according to the different types of areas, identifying a direct relationship between the Global North linked to the urban areas and the Global South more linked the rural and peri-urban areas.

The relevance of this research is its focus on the recognition, integration, implementation and adoption of specific characteristics of social innovation in the Global South. These characteristics can allow the development of institutionalisation processes through bottom-up initiatives that enable the generation of different innovations in the territory addressing sustainable development. Thus, the proposal suggests that innovation is a multi-faceted phenomenon driven by social, economic, environmental, and institutional factors within different geographic contexts, where they can intersect in various ways as the idea of place-based innovation [11].

This paper focuses on bringing attention to social innovation experiences in the Global South, often overlooked in academic literature in contrast to the Global North countries. It aims to highlight social innovation's key characteristics and context at the territory level. The findings can be used to shape public policies that support various forms of innovation with a substantial impact on the local community. It emphasizes combining bottom-up and top-down approaches to institutionalising social innovation through governance structures and policy support, only the latter key aspect being mainly present in the Global North. The final goal is to create collective agendas for addressing social needs and promoting sustainable development.

The limitations of this work are related to the level of depth in the analysis of the different documents identified, allowing for a broader categorisation. Future research can further explore the various types of territories in both the Global North and the Global South. The evaluation and scope of institutions significantly change in informal institutions over time. It is also necessary to propose research that would make it possible to determine the impact these social innovations have had on territorial-sustainable development by correlating them with economic, social, and environmental indicators.
