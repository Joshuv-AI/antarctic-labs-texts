Enhancing leadership transitions in student-run clinics: a quality improvement initiative to standardise onboarding
Authors: Hunter Cohn, Anne Patterson, Dhwani Krishnan, Rahul Babu, Lukas Biel, Camden Gardner, Diane Levine

Introduction

The healthcare industry faces significant challenges in workforce management, particularly in talent acquisition, training and retention.1 2 Among these challenges, inefficient onboarding processes can lead to prolonged adjustment periods, miscommunications and errors, ultimately creating operational inefficiencies and negatively impacting patient outcomes.3

Medical student-run organisations, such as free clinics, exemplify these challenges.4 With high turnover and short leadership cycles, which are more common with shortened preclinical curricula, many organisations experience recurring inefficiencies, particularly in onboarding.5 6 Even a brief onboarding period can disrupt organisational continuity, mirroring broader issues in healthcare institutions that medical students will face after their training.

In this context, onboarding structure is critical for workforce readiness and as a foundational skill for future physicians and health system leaders. To address these challenges, medical students must be trained in structured problem solving, quality improvement (QI) and systems-based thinking, to prepare them to navigate future operational and care delivery complexities in their careers.7 8 Training medical students in QI principles through real-world applications can provide experiential learning opportunities with direct applicability to their future careers.9,11

The medical school chapter of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI), an organisation that seeks to engage medical students and faculty in QI education and research, collaborated with Street Medicine Detroit (SMD), a student-run free clinic providing care to individuals experiencing homelessness. SMD faced significant onboarding challenges due to its large membership (100+ volunteers) and frequent leadership turnover (30+ annual leadership positions) that led to inefficiencies, prolonged learning curves and lapses in operational effectiveness that ultimately affected patient care, in combination with SMD’s many moving parts (informal clinic settings, decentralised care teams and unpredictable patient locations). As such, increasing operational efficiency was essential to improve continuity and minimise disruptions.12 To address these issues, IHI helped implement a structured QI project that followed a series of Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles.13

This project aimed to improve SMD’s functionality and efficiency by educating student leaders in QI methodologies. By engaging in structured problem solving and process optimisation, participants gained hands-on experience, enhancing their leadership skills and providing a foundation for addressing operational challenges in healthcare. While this initiative benefited from a collaboration with a medical school IHI chapter, similar efforts can be implemented at other institutions through locally or publicly available resources. This paper describes the rationale, implementation and impact of the standardised operating procedure (SOP) initiative, demonstrating how structured QI interventions can enhance both organisational efficiency and medical education, regardless of institutional affiliation.

Methods

SMD is a student-run free clinic dedicated to providing healthcare services to individuals experiencing homelessness in Detroit. The organisation is led by more than 30 first-year and second-year medical students, with leadership transitioning more than once a year—in alignment with two 9-month preclinical segments. Within SMD, leaders are medical students who direct individual clinic shifts, coordinating volunteers, managing supplies and serving as liaisons to supervising physicians, while board members oversee broader organisational responsibilities such as governance, scheduling and budgeting. These frequent leadership transitions create challenges in the onboarding process, inefficiencies in knowledge transfer and disruptions in operational continuity. To address these issues, a medical school’s IHI chapter partnered with SMD leadership to develop and implement an SOP document aimed at streamlining onboarding, improving organisational effectiveness and ensuring the sustainability of institutional knowledge within SMD. The latest version of the SOP is available in online supplemental file 2.

Project methodology

This initiative employed a QI approach, with a PDSA cycle associated with each of the three phases of the project to ensure systematic, responsive and iterative improvements.

Each PDSA cycle pursued a distinct objective, but all were part of a progressive, structured improvement effort. Within each cycle, iterative refinements were made in response to feedback. In Discovery, preliminary interview themes were reviewed with SMD leaders (member checking), prompting adjustments to the interview guide to better assess the accuracy and relevance of initial findings. In Design, the SOP underwent multiple rounds of review and revision to refine content, clarify role descriptions and improve accessibility. In Educate, postorientation feedback from new leaders led to immediate edits to the SOP, updated training emphasis and formal assignment of ongoing SOP maintenance to the vice president. As shown in online supplemental figure 1, this combination of within-cycle iteration and cross-cycle progression exemplifies a responsive and rigorous QI approach.14

All quantitative surveys were measured using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1—strongly disagree, 2—disagree, 3—neither agree nor disagree, 4—agree, 5—strongly agree.

Intervention

The intervention centred around the development of SOPs tailored to SMD’s needs. The SOP aimed to streamline onboarding by providing a comprehensive resource that included organisational structure and governance, roles and responsibilities of leadership, standard clinic event workflows, key documents, frequently asked questions and troubleshooting guides. The SOP was iteratively developed with input from SMD leadership and members to ensure its relevance and usability.

The SOP development process involved several steps:

Needs assessment: initial qualitative interviews with past leaders and a survey of current and past leaders to identify organisational pain points, including unclear role expectations, inefficient knowledge transfer and onboarding challenges.

The intervention’s effectiveness was assessed through a mixed-methods approach to incorporate both qualitative and quantitative feedback. Surveys were administered at three stages:

Post implementation: a survey of 26 new leaders who used the SOP during onboarding, assessing perceived improvements in efficiency, clarity and independence.

Quantitative data were measured using Likert scale surveys (1=strongly disagree, 5=strongly agree) to assess factors such as clarity of role expectations, effectiveness of training materials and reduction in errors or reliance on informal knowledge transfer.

Sustainability

As part of the ‘Educate’ phase, a brief curriculum was introduced to enhance understanding of QI principles. This initiative provided training on the meaning, methodology and value of QI, using the SOP project as a case study to illustrate the process. Similarly, the SMD vice president role was expanded to oversee and maintain the SOP in the future.

To promote long-term sustainability, the board position ‘Vice President of Education and Training’ (VP of Education and Training) was formally expanded to include responsibility for maintaining and updating the SOP. A structured governance process was introduced, including biannual refresh meetings during which the VP of Education and Training reviews the SOP with board members and current leaders to identify needed updates. A feedback intake form is distributed after each new leader training session, allowing immediate collection of suggestions for SOP improvements. Any proposed changes undergo a formal review and approval process, overseen by the VP of Education and Training, to ensure consistency and quality. These systems aim to institutionalise the SOP as a living document embedded in the organisation’s leadership structure.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this QI initiative. The project focused exclusively on medical student leaders within the organisation and did not include patient or public engagement.

Discussion

This QI initiative successfully addressed long-standing onboarding inefficiencies within SMD through the development and implementation of an SOP document. Findings from retrospective surveys of past leaders highlighted key organisational pain points, including a lack of structured onboarding, unclear role expectations and inefficient knowledge transfer. The implementation of the SOP significantly improved new leader onboarding, reducing reliance on board members for information, increasing clarity of training materials and improving perceived preparedness. Additionally, the initiative integrated QI education into leadership training, fostering a culture of continuous improvement.

This study’s findings suggest that SOPs are an effective intervention for streamlining onboarding and improving leadership transitions. Notably, new leaders trained with the SOP reported higher clarity and comprehension scores during their orientation and reported fewer unanswered questions compared with past leaders. In support of these findings, leaders onboarded with the SOP also reported a significantly shorter time to feel comfortable in their roles, a reduction from 8 weeks to 4 weeks on average (p<0.05), highlighting a tangible gain in onboarding efficiency. This improvement suggests that beyond perceived clarity, the SOP may have directly accelerated new leaders’ ability to contribute independently and effectively. Understandably, relying on senior members for training and guidance is an unsustainable and inefficient model. As such, the shift from informal knowledge transfer to structured self-reliance using the SOP represents an important improvement in organisational efficiency. Establishing a centralised SOP fosters a culture of independent problem solving, reducing disruptions and improving long-term organisational resilience. These findings align with broader literature on onboarding best practices in medicine, emphasising the importance of structured documentation in improving role clarity, lessening learning curves, enhancing organisational continuity and improving overall team performance.13 15

Beyond onboarding, this intervention also demonstrated how integrating QI principles into leadership training can enhance operational efficiency while fostering skills applicable to future medical practice.16 17 While learning QI principles was not the primary objective of the SOP, survey responses indicated that leaders valued the exposure to structured problem solving and systems-based thinking. This appreciation was reflected in the design of the onboarding process itself, where QI principles were intentionally embedded to improve both structure and delivery. The SOP itself was a product of QI methodology that resulted in a tool that cut average onboarding time in half (from 8 weeks to 4 weeks, p<0.05). Additionally, the SOP was shared as a pre-read prior to the 2-hour training session, which allowed participants to arrive with a stronger working knowledge of their roles, more targeted questions and greater readiness to absorb new information. This change, rooted in QI principles of systems-level planning and workflow optimisation, helped shift the training session from an introductory overview to a more nuanced, higher yield discussion.

These findings suggest that integrating QI education into student-led healthcare organisations can serve as a powerful tool for both operational improvement and leadership development. Research supports that practice-based education and experiential learning enhance knowledge retention, aligning with experiential adult learning theory, which emphasises the importance of self-directed, experience-based learning for adult learners.17 18 By embedding QI principles within student-run free clinics or other organisations that align with students’ interests and passions, this approach strengthens organisational efficiency and fosters long-term engagement with QI methodologies. Providing students with hands-on opportunities to apply structured problem solving and systems-based thinking within meaningful, real-world contexts may encourage sustained use of these skills throughout their medical careers.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study which should be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of past leader surveys introduces recall bias, as respondents may have overestimated or underestimated their onboarding challenges. The number of leaders who participated in the post implementation qualitative interviews (n=6) was also limited, which may not fully capture the diversity of perspectives among new leadership. In addition, the study relied primarily on self-reported survey data without time series analysis or objective performance metrics, limiting our ability to evaluate long-term impact or verify outcomes beyond participant perception. A Hawthorne effect is also possible, in which leaders aware of participating in a QI initiative may have reported more favourable impressions.

Although formal processes were implemented to promote long-term SOP usage and adaptation as outlined in the Methods section, sustainability is a common challenge in healthcare organisations and will require continued evaluation over time.19 In practice, it is possible that, over time, leaders may revert to informal mechanisms for knowledge transfer, necessitating ongoing reinforcement of SOP usage. To mitigate this risk, responsibility for SOP governance was formally assigned to the VP of Education and Training, who now oversees regular biannual review meetings and a structured feedback intake and approval process following each training cycle. These mechanisms aim to preserve institutional knowledge, support continuous improvement and maintain the SOP as a living document aligned with the clinic’s evolving needs. Finally, the study was conducted within a single student-run organisation, and while findings may be generalisable to similar volunteer-based healthcare settings, broader applicability requires further study.

Implications for practice and future directions

This initiative demonstrates that student-run healthcare organisations can benefit from structured onboarding processes that improve efficiency and leadership transitions. Future work should explore strategies for reinforcing SOP utilisation, such as periodic refreshers during training periods, leadership transition workshops or alternative onboarding formats. Additionally, the integration of QI education into leadership training may serve as a scalable model for medical student leadership development, equipping future physicians with the tools to address systemic inefficiencies in clinical practice.

While this initiative was successful due to a collaboration with a medical school IHI chapter, similar efforts can be implemented at other institutions even without direct access to IHI resources. Several online QI resources, such as those provided by the National Association for Healthcare Quality, the IHI, the Health Research & Educational Trust and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, can serve as valuable tools for student leadership development. Institutions can also identify local resources, such as faculty or community partners, who can provide basic QI training to students, ensuring that QI education remains accessible and impactful regardless of institutional affiliations or external partnerships.

Given the inherent challenges of managing decentralised settings like SMD—where variability in clinic locations and patient encounters complicates standardisation—the SOP has proven effective in improving onboarding and leadership transitions. As well, it is worth considering that, given its success in this decentralised context, it could be similarly effective in less complex, more traditional, single-site clinical environments.

Further research could examine the impact of SOPs across different student-led healthcare initiatives, evaluating their role in enhancing operational effectiveness and leadership preparedness. Additionally, tracking long-term outcomes—such as sustained SOP usage and improvements in clinic performance—would provide deeper insights into the durability of this intervention. Similarly, future research could compare the effectiveness of QI education delivered through workshops versus experiential, practice-based learning models to gauge how real-world application in QI training informs best practices for medical education.

Conclusion

The development and implementation of an SOP within SMD effectively addressed long-standing onboarding inefficiencies, improving clarity, reducing reliance on board members and streamlining leadership transitions. This initiative enhanced operational efficiency and demonstrated the value of integrating QI education into student-led healthcare organisations. By equipping students with essential skills for addressing systemic inefficiencies, the initiative fosters competencies that are crucial for future medical practice. These findings underscore the significance of structured onboarding in student-run clinical environments and suggest that similar interventions could be effectively applied in more traditional clinical settings, where operational challenges may be less complex.
