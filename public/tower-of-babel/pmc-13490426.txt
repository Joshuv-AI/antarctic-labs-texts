FFRLS-MPC: An adaptive model predictive control method for robust ship course keeping
Authors: Yanyu Huang, Jinlai Liu, Jianwei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate course control is essential for safe and efficient maritime navigation. Conventional controllers often degrade under time-varying vessel dynamics and persistent environmental disturbances. We propose an adaptive course-control strategy that couples online system identification with model predictive control (MPC). The resulting FFRLS-MPC controller uses recursive least squares with a forgetting factor (FFRLS). It identifies key manoeuvring-model parameters in real time. The updated model is supplied to MPC for state prediction and constrained rudder optimisation. This loop compensates for model-plant mismatch and unmodelled disturbances. We validated the method in simulations under calm water and wind-wave-current disturbance scenarios. Performance was compared with PID and robust adaptive MPC baselines. FFRLS-MPC produced faster responses, lower steady-state errors and more accurate dynamic course tracking. The identification module captured disturbance effects as adaptive parameter variation. These results indicate a practical route to robust, high-precision ship motion control.Keywords: Model predictive control, Course control, Online parameter identificationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Background

Waterborne transport carries more than 90% of global cargo volume and remains central to international trade1. Safe and efficient navigation has therefore become a major maritime research priority2. Recent autonomous-navigation research has focused on planning and control. Path-planning studies address route generation and collision avoidance3. Related control work regulates vessel attitude, course and position under operational constraints4–6.

Course keeping is a core layer of these control pipelines. Heading errors propagate into trajectory-tracking and station-keeping errors. They can also increase lateral drift and collision risk. Wind, waves and currents amplify these errors during routine navigation. Robust course control is therefore essential for safe and efficient vessel operation.

Related work

Proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control remains a practical baseline for ship-course regulation7. Many modern autopilots still build on this structure. More advanced designs have also been explored for ship course control. Sliding-mode and adaptive controllers have been investigated for disturbance rejection8,9. Fuzzy control provides another nonlinear control route10. Model predictive control (MPC) adds prediction and constraint handling11. Neural-network controllers have also been introduced for data-driven tracking12.

Many algorithms perform well under ideal calm-sea conditions. Their behaviour in realistic marine environments remains less fully addressed. Zhou et al. developed adaptive fuzzy back-propagation for trajectory tracking under model uncertainty and actuator saturation13. Li et al. proposed output-error-based dynamic event triggering for robust path tracking14. That design depends on environmental perception and can increase instantaneous computational load. Wu et al. introduced input event triggering into self-tuning iterative learning control for autonomous berthing15. However, boundedness of all closed-loop signals was not guaranteed in that setting.

Ship navigation is affected by external disturbances and uncertain vessel dynamics. Loading condition and speed can also alter the yaw response. Disturbance-compensating MPC improved heading control under unknown disturbances16. Residual disturbance rejection nevertheless remained limited. Backstepping and nonlinear feedback improved robustness and energy efficiency17,18. They also increased settling time. Liu et al. used a reduced-order extended-state observer to estimate disturbance-induced yaw variation19. The acceptance circle still ignored manoeuvrability and relied on empirical parameters. Comparisons between linear and nonlinear MPC indicate accuracy advantages for nonlinear prediction20. Such advantages depend on accurate models and efficient optimisation. Gao et al. combined robust adaptive MPC with variable line-of-sight guidance28. The method simplified path tracking into course keeping. It also neglected turning-radius constraints and showed adjustment oscillations. Adaptive backstepping with a third-order prefilter can smooth tracking without prior disturbance information29.

Recent MPC and predictive-control studies have increasingly moved beyond standard fixed-model receding-horizon control. One direction improves the prediction model itself. Linear and nonlinear MPC have been compared for underactuated USV path following, showing that nonlinear prediction can improve tracking accuracy when the model captures vessel dynamics more faithfully20. In process-control applications, explicit machine-learning-based MPC and neural-network-based MPC have been introduced to represent nonlinear dynamics while reducing online solution cost21,22. Physics-informed neural networks have also been embedded into explicit MPC frameworks to combine data-driven approximation with physical consistency23. These studies indicate that predictive performance depends strongly on the fidelity and computational tractability of the internal model.

Another line of recent work strengthens adaptation, robustness and constraint handling. Robust adaptive MPC combined with variable line-of-sight guidance has been used to improve path-following performance under uncertainty28. Event-triggered and globally constrained MPC schemes have also been introduced to reduce unnecessary updates while preserving course and path constraints11,14. For nonlinear process systems, online machine learning has been used with MPC to handle switched nonlinear dynamics24, while adaptive model-based predictive control has been studied for systems with changing operation points25. Data-enabled economic predictive control has further explored convex optimisation formulations for nonlinear systems26. Computationally efficient predictive control is another active direction; recent enhanced predictive functional control shows that reformulating the predictive optimisation problem can reduce online computational burden while improving tracking quality27. Together, these studies show a common trend: recent enhanced MPC methods seek better prediction, stronger robustness, more explicit constraint handling, online adaptation and lower computation.

However, these advances do not fully resolve the ship-course-control problem considered here. Some enhanced MPC methods improve nonlinear prediction or optimisation efficiency, but their prediction models are not necessarily updated using physical manoeuvrability parameters during operation. Others improve robustness through constraint tightening, observer design, event triggering or learning-based prediction, but they do not explicitly refresh the Nomoto indices used by the course-prediction model. In practical navigation, loading condition, speed and wind-wave-current disturbance can alter the effective yaw response. A controller that only optimises over a fixed or weakly updated model can therefore retain model-plant mismatch. The proposed FFRLS-MPC addresses this gap by identifying the Nomoto parameters K and T online and feeding them back into MPC prediction and rudder optimisation. Its merit is not only predictive optimisation, but also continuous adaptation of the physical course-response model used inside MPC.

Uncertain motion-model parameters motivate identification methods for ship control. Full-scale trials and correction studies have used filtering for manoeuvrability-index estimation30. Data-driven MPC has also entered ship-control research31. Song et al. identified hydrodynamic coefficients in a four-degree-of-freedom manoeuvring model32. Chen et al. used least-squares support vector machines for grey-box ship modelling33. Kalman and Gaussian filters provide further routes for parameter estimation34,35. Many of these methods rely on offline data. Offline calibration cannot track rapid changes in loading, speed or environmental forcing. Cui et al. addressed online adaptation through MPC and Gaussian-process reinforcement learning36. That framework was validated on an autonomous ship in uncertain marine conditions.

Despite this progress, two limitations remain important for ship-course control. First, practical manoeuvrability constraints are often simplified or omitted. Second, many controllers assume fixed or accurately known model parameters. In service, both assumptions can fail. Loading condition, speed and disturbances alter vessel response. Fixed-model controllers can then retain bias and prediction error. Even computationally efficient predictive controllers require an internal model that remains representative of the current plant. An effective course controller should therefore learn online while respecting actuator constraints.

This study asks whether online manoeuvrability identification can improve constrained predictive course control. We address this question by coupling forgetting-factor recursive least squares (FFRLS) with MPC. The main contributions are as follows.

MPC then updates its prediction model before solving the constrained rudder-optimisation problem. This design reduces dependence on a fixed offline model. It also improves prediction under varying dynamics or unmodelled disturbances.

FFRLS-MPC adaptive course-control strategy

This section presents an adaptive course-control strategy termed FFRLS-MPC. The method integrates online system identification with model predictive control. It addresses a central limitation of conventional MPC. A fixed offline model often degrades under time-varying ship dynamics and unmodelled environmental disturbances. As shown in Fig. 2, FFRLS-MPC forms an adaptive control loop. Real-time vessel-response data continuously update the predictive model. This update improves adaptability and prediction accuracy.

Overall schematic of the FFRLS-MPC controller.

Overall architecture of the FFRLS-MPC controller

The FFRLS-MPC architecture contains two coupled modules. The first is an online identification module based on FFRLS. The second is a dynamic MPC optimisation module. These modules are connected through the updated Nomoto manoeuvrability indices and through the measured heading feedback, as shown in Fig. 2.

The MMG model represents the controlled vessel and generates the measured course response ψ (t) under the applied rudder angle δ (t) and environmental loads. The online parameter-identification block receives the measured course response and rudder input. It constructs the regression vector from recent input-output data and recursively estimates the manoeuvrability indices K and T. These updated parameters are passed to the heading-prediction model, which refreshes the internal Nomoto model used by MPC and produces the uncorrected predicted course . The feedback-correction block compares the measured course with the predicted course and compensates the future prediction. The corrected prediction and the reference course are then sent to the MPC controller. The MPC controller solves the constrained rolling optimisation problem and computes an optimal rudder-command sequence. Only the first element of this sequence is applied to the steering gear, which implements the rudder command subject to the physical rudder-angle limit.

The two modules operate jointly at each control step. The identification module updates the predictive model, while the MPC module closes the tracking loop and generates the rudder command. The main advantage of this architecture is therefore its dual feedback mechanism. The controller corrects both course-tracking error and model-plant mismatch. It can adapt continuously to vessel-dynamic changes caused by speed variation, loading condition or external disturbances.

The ship manoeuvrability index is critical for accurate course prediction. It is usually determined by theoretical calculation, tank towing tests or empirical formulae. Dynamic MPC optimisation module

The MPC module computes the optimal rudder angle by solving a constrained optimisation problem at each sampling instant. Unlike standard MPC, the proposed controller uses a dynamically updated internal prediction model. At each time step k, this model receives K(k) and T(k) from the FFRLS module. The basic MPC principle is reviewed before the formulation is introduced.

Principle of model predictive control

MPC uses a mathematical system model to predict and regulate future behaviour. By forecasting future states, it selects control inputs that optimise a prescribed objective function. MPC is well suited to nonlinear and strongly coupled systems. It can also incorporate linear and nonlinear constraints systematically. This capability explains its broad use in industrial process control.

The operating principle of MPC is shown in Fig. 3. At each sampling instant k, MPC solves an optimisation problem. The current system state and reference trajectory define the problem. The solution gives an optimal control-input sequence over a finite prediction horizon. Only the first control action is applied to the plant. The optimisation is then repeated at k+1 with updated measurements. This rolling mechanism is the basis of receding horizon control. In Fig. 3, denotes the control horizon. The variable denotes the prediction horizon. Predicted outputs and control inputs are updated iteratively to track the reference while satisfying constraints.

Implicit disturbance observation and adaptive compensation mechanism

A key feature of FFRLS-MPC is compensation for environmental disturbances without explicit disturbance modelling. The method treats wind, wave and current effects as apparent variations in K and T. A persistent disturbance torque changes the vessel input-output dynamics. The FFRLS module captures this changed behaviour continuously. It adjusts K and T to match the observed system response.

In this role, the identification module functions as an implicit disturbance observer. Explicit observers estimate disturbance forces directly. By contrast, this module represents the integrated disturbance effect through parameter variation.

This mechanism benefits predictive control. MPC optimises with a model whose parameters reflect ongoing disturbances. It can therefore predict future states more accurately under disturbed conditions. The controller then computes compensatory rudder commands before large course errors accumulate. This anticipatory compensation helps remove steady-state errors under persistent environmental loads. The mechanism is most appropriate for persistent or slowly varying disturbances. If the external disturbance changes rapidly, the equivalent-parameter interpretation may lag behind the actual disturbed dynamics and prediction accuracy may temporarily decrease. Figure 7 shows close agreement between the FFRLS and FEKF prediction curves and the MMG reference. The Nomoto model is first-order and linear. Some error from model simplification is therefore unavoidable. FEKF also suffers from state-parameter coupling in state-space formulations. FFRLS avoids this issue by recursively estimating the relationship between rudder input and course response. It therefore achieves slightly higher accuracy than FEKF. The prediction curves converge well to the reference values in later simulation stages. These results confirm the reliability of the identification results. Discussion

This work shows that combining online identification of Nomoto indices K and T with MPC improves ship course control. The gains are evident in course-keeping and course-tracking under combined wind, wave and current disturbances. Updating the prediction model at each receding-horizon step reduces model-plant mismatch in situ. It shortens rise and settling times, suppresses overshoot and removes steady-state bias. Mechanistically, adaptation converts persistent external loads into apparent parameter shifts. The optimiser internalises these shifts, making rudder sequences anticipatory rather than purely corrective. This interpretation agrees with identification studies. Those studies emphasise well-conditioned regressors and lightweight real-time estimators. Relevant examples include Xie34, Wang et al.35, Chen et al.33 and Song et al.32. The proposed scheme converges faster under sustained bias than RA-MPC28. It also outperforms constraint-aware MPC in this setting11. It actively removes prediction error rather than only attenuating it. The sinusoidal tracking experiments also show reduced phase lag in high-curvature segments. This behaviour is consistent with an updated forward model that captures time-varying yaw response. Disturbance-aware observers can improve path following by estimating sideslip or bias states19. However, they do not directly re-identify plant parameters. The proposed method complements such observers by embedding parameter learning into predictive optimisation. Its computational footprint also remains modest. First-order prediction physics, short horizons and recursive closed-form updates support high-rate shipboard implementation. The method retains MPC constraint handling and actuator-friendly operation20.

The results clarify a practical trade-off among robustness, adaptation and actuation cost. RA-MPC buffers uncertainty through tightened constraints and rate penalties. However, residual bias can persist under sustained disturbances. Adding online identification reallocates control effort over time. The controller first applies bounded front-loaded actuation to cancel bias. It then settles into a low-variance steady regime. This behaviour improves tracking quality and rudder economy relative to PID, backstepping and SMC-style designs. Such methods lack lookahead and often require conservative tuning in seaways8,17. The method also has limitations. First, the Nomoto-I model limits representation under strong nonlinearities, large drift angles and near-saturation hydrodynamics. Grey-box MMG surrogates could expand bandwidth. Higher-order Nomoto models could offer a similar extension while preserving identifiable structure32,33. Second, reliable online estimation requires persistent excitation. Long plateaus with weak input variation can inflate covariance and slow convergence. In practice, small scheduled rudder dithers within safety margins may preserve identifiability. Adaptive horizons or weights could also be used when estimator confidence degrades. Third, folding disturbances into parameter updates can transiently misalign predictions under rapidly varying loads. If wind, wave or current changes faster than the identification window can represent, the estimated K and T may lag or become noisy. This may reduce prediction accuracy and lead to more oscillatory or conservative rudder action. Tube-based tightening for adaptive predictors, explicit disturbance observers and adaptive forgetting factors may help ensure recursive feasibility under bounded identification error20. Finally, long-horizon deployments require bias-aware identification to limit drift from residual sensor bias. Despite these caveats, the data indicate consistent tracking-error reduction with restrained control energy. This supports the central hypothesis that prediction accuracy is critical to closed-loop performance.

The implications are practical for surface vessels with variable manoeuvrability across speed, loading and sea state. Embedding online K and T estimation into MPC provides a middle path between model-free learning and non-predictive robust control. Model-free learning can be sample intensive and may offer weaker guarantees. Non-predictive robust control provides limited anticipation. The present framework is compact and interpretable. This remains true relative to learning-augmented tracking12. It also compares favourably with linear and nonlinear path-following MPC20. It is also fast enough to reject realistic biasing loads. The method is compatible with gyrocompass, GNSS/INS and log measurements. It could be layered onto existing autopilots as a supervisory module. Such deployment may improve track keeping, reduce cross-track excursions and limit unnecessary ruddering. These benefits could reduce fuel use and maintenance demand in traffic-constrained waterways. Future work should combine adaptation with structured robustness, such as tube MPC or feedback-linearisation hybrids. This step would support formal feasibility and stability guarantees. The predictive core should also be upgraded to grey-box MMG or higher-order Nomoto models. Estimator tractability must be retained. Finally, the method should be validated in hardware-in-the-loop experiments and controlled sea trials. Collectively, the results indicate that real-time parameter learning and predictive optimisation are synergistic in maritime heading control.

Conclusion

This paper proposed an adaptive FFRLS-MPC framework for ship course control. The main contribution is the integration of forgetting-factor recursive least-squares identification with constrained model predictive control. FFRLS estimates the Nomoto manoeuvrability indices K and T from recent rudder-course data, and MPC uses the refreshed model to predict future heading response and optimise rudder commands under actuator constraints. This structure reduces dependence on a fixed offline model and improves the consistency between the prediction model and the current vessel response.

The simulation results show that the proposed controller achieves faster convergence, smaller steady-state error and more accurate course tracking than PID and RA-MPC baselines under both calm-water and wind-wave-current disturbance conditions. The online parameter trajectories further indicate that persistent environmental loads can be represented as apparent variation in K and T. This implicit disturbance-compensation mechanism allows MPC to compute anticipatory rudder commands without introducing a separate disturbance-force observer.

The method also has limitations that guide future MPC research. The first-order Nomoto model cannot fully represent strong nonlinear hydrodynamics, large drift angles or near-saturation behaviour. Rapidly varying disturbances may also weaken the equivalent-parameter interpretation and temporarily degrade prediction accuracy. Future work should combine online identification with robust or tube-based MPC, explicit disturbance observers and adaptive forgetting-factor mechanisms. Higher-order Nomoto models or grey-box MMG prediction models should also be investigated while preserving real-time tractability. Hardware-in-the-loop experiments and controlled sea trials will be necessary to evaluate feasibility, stability and robustness in operational marine environments.
