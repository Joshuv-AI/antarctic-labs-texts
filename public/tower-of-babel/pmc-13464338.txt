Circumventing IFN-γ induced resistance in ovarian cancer with a double-hit: NKG2A knockout of PRAME-TCR expressing NK cells
Authors: Els P van Hees, Olivia W Rensing, Renate S Hagedoorn, Cilia R Pothast, Anne K Wouters, Rosa A van Amerongen, J H Frederik Falkenburg, Rosa de Groot, Mirjam HM Heemskerk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe success of NK cell therapies against solid tumors remains limited, possibly due to tumor resistance mechanisms associated with the upregulation of inhibitory ligands. Previous studies have demonstrated that expanded NK cells can lyse ovarian cancer cells and produce IFN-γ. However, secretion of IFN-γ within the tumor microenvironment, leads to the upregulation of both classical HLA class I and the non-classical HLA-E on bystander tumor cells, thereby contributing to resistance against NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. To overcome this IFN-γ induced resistance, we developed NKG2A-knockout NK: TCR cells targeting PRAME (NK: PRAMENKG2A KO), a tumor-associated antigen expressed in ovarian cancer.MethodsPrimary NK cells were isolated from PBMCs, stimulated with cytokines, and genetically modified using CRISPR-Cas9 to knockout the KLRC1 gene, which encodes NKG2A. After stimulation, the NK cells were further engineered to express the PRAME-specific TCR. NK: PRAMENKG2A KO were compared with control NK: PRAMENKG2A WT and NK: MOCKNKG2A KO cells for effector function against PRAME-positive ovarian cancer cell lines and primary ovarian cancer cells. To mimic the pro-inflammatory tumor environment, ovarian cancer cell lines were pre-treated with IFN-γ.ResultsFirst we observed that NK: PRAME cells without a KLRC1 knockout were not effective to lyse IFN-γ treated ovarian cancer cells, irrespective of upregulated HLA class I expression. To overcome HLA-E mediated inhibition, the CRISPR-Cas9 induced KLRC1 knockout was successfully achieved without negatively impacting NK: TCR cell engineering, expansion and further alterations in phenotype. As a result, the NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells exhibited increased cytotoxicity against these IFN-γ treated tumor cells.ConclusionsThis dual-targeting strategy offers a unique advantage by enabling the targeting of both HLA-positive and HLA-negative tumor cells, promoting a pro-inflammatory environment and enhancing the efficacy of TCR-based immunotherapy for ovarian cancer and other solid tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02156-0.Keywords: Cancer, NK cell therapy, TCR, Interferon-γ, Ovarian

Background

Over the last decade, adoptive cell therapy (ACT) has shown significant clinical potential, especially since the rise of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR-T) and T cell receptor (TCR-T) therapy [1, 2]. Nevertheless, there are still limitations associated with these therapies. Besides adverse events, such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity, therapeutic resistance can arise, most commonly through downregulation of the target antigen on tumor cells [3–5]. For TCR-T therapies, this escape mechanism can involve reduced expression of both the target protein and the presenting HLA molecule [5]. To overcome HLA-dependent immune escape, natural killer (NK) cells could be used as an alternative source for TCR therapy, since NK cells are capable of recognizing and eliminating tumor cells with minimal HLA expression [6–10]. By introducing tumor-specific TCRs into NK cells (NK: TCR), a dual-targeting approach can be achieved: TCR-medicated recognition of intracellular tumor-associated antigens presented on HLA molecules, combined with NK-mediated recognition of HLA-deficient tumor cells [11, 12].

NK: TCR therapy could also be an interesting strategy for the treatment of solid tumors, such as ovarian cancer, as they often exhibit heterogenous HLA expression, which makes them not always suitable for TCR-T therapy [13]. Intrinsically, NK cells have shown promising results in vitro for treatment of ovarian cancer. However, their clinical efficacy remains limited [14–16]. One possible reason for this could be the interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) production of NK cells upon tumor interaction, inducing HLA class I and HLA-E expression on tumor cells, which could eventually provoke resistance to NK-mediated cytotoxicity [13, 17–20]. However, this upregulation of HLA class I offers possibilities for NK: TCR therapies, since ovarian cancer cells express a variety of intracellular tumor-associated antigens recognized by tumor-specific TCRs [21, 22]. Over the years, we have identified several high-affinity TCRs specific to various antigens expressed in ovarian cancer and presented in different HLA class I alleles [21–25]. One of these TCRs targets a peptide derived from the preferentially expressed antigen in melanoma (PRAME) presented in HLA-A*02:01 [22]. Given the widespread expression of PRAME across ovarian cancer subtypes, it makes a strong candidate for NK: PRAME-TCR therapy [26].

Unfortunately, IFN-γ-induced upregulation of HLA class I is often accompanied by increased expression of the non-classical HLA molecule HLA-E [19]. This molecule can suppress NK cell function through interaction with the inhibitory NKG2A/CD94 receptor complex [27–30]. Since engineered NK cells frequently express high levels of NKG2A, elevated expression of HLA-E on the tumor cells might pose a challenge for NK: PRAME-TCR therapies [11, 31].

To overcome HLA-E-mediated inhibition, CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing has been used to knockout NKG2A in NK cells [32–34]. Our study aims to further improve this method in order to create a dual-targeting NK cell product by engineering them with a tumor specific PRAME-TCR and a knockout for NKG2A (NK: PRAMENKG2A KO), in order to improve NK-mediated cytotoxicity by preventing suppression by HLA-E and to enhance TCR-mediated recognition of HLA-positive ovarian cancer cells expressing PRAME.

Materials and methods

NK cell isolation

Peripheral blood was obtained from healthy donors after informed consent. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated using ficoll separation. CD56+CD3− NK cells were isolated from cryopreserved PBMCs using an untouched NK cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). 1 × 106 NK cells were cocultured in a 24-well plate with NK medium (NK-M) consisting of IMDM (Lonza, Switzerland) supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated FBS (Gibco, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), 5% human serum (Sanquin, the Netherlands), 1.5% L-Glutamine (Lonza), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza) and 5 ng/mL IL-15 (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and 100 IU/mL IL-2 (Novartis, Switzerland).

CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing

The KLRC1 gene sequence (NM #002259), encoding for NKG2A, was obtained from the NCBI gene database and guide RNA sequences were designed using the IDT Custom Alt-R™ CRISPR-Cas9 guide RNA webtool (Integrated DNA Technologies, US). Three crRNA sequences were designed: #1 5’-GGTCTGAGTAGATTACTCCT-3’, #2 5’-TTGAAGGTTTAATTCCGCAT-3’, and #3 5’-GCCTTTTTATGAGACTAACC-3’ [33]. Target ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) were generated by annealing the crRNA with a standard tracrRNA (Integrated DNA Technologies, USA) at 95 °C for 5 min in the PCR thermocycler and slowly cooling to room temperature. Subsequently, Alt-R S.p. Cas9 Nuclease V3 (Integrated DNA Technologies, USA) was added to the tracrRNA: crRNA duplex together with Cas9 Electroporation enhancer (Integrated DNA Technologies, USA) to form the RNP complex. RNPs were stored at -80 °C. At day 1 after isolation, NK cells were electroporated with the Invitrogen Neon Transfection System (settings: Pulse Voltage = 1950, Pulse Width = 10ms, Pulse Number = 2). 1 × 106 NK cells were centrifuged and washed with PBS once before electroporation. Pellets were taken up in 9 µl T buffer (Invitrogen, USA) and 1 µl RNP, regardless the number of gRNAs combined. The electroporation was conducted in 10 uL Neon tip in E buffer (Invitrogen, USA). Immediately after electroporation NK cells were transferred to a 24-well plate with 1 ml NK-M and stimulated with 0.25 × 106 irradiated (100 Gy) K562 cells expressing membrane-bound interleukin-21 and 4-1BB-ligand (K562F).

Retroviral modification

On day 3 post stimulation, NK cells were retrovirally transduced as previously described [12], with retroviral vector encoding for the HLA-A*02:01 restricted murinized PRAME-TCR (HSS1) linked via 2 A self-cleaving sites to CD8αβ [22]. On day 7, cells were re-stimulated with irradiated K562F in an effector to stimulator ratio of 2:1 in NK-M (1 × 106 NK cells/ml). On day 10, NK cells were transduced with codon optimized CD3 invariant chains linked via 2 A self-cleaving sites in the following order: CD3ζ, CD3δ, CD3ε, CD3γ or a truncated NGFR (tNGFR) construct as mock control. On day 14, NK cells were MACS enriched for transgene expression (either murine TCRβ or tNGFR) and subsequently re-stimulated. NK cells were expanded for 7 days and frozen down in liquid nitrogen or used for functional assays.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometric analysis of CRISPR-Cas9 edited NK cells was performed on either a 3- or 5-laser Aurora (Cytek Biosciences, USA). Cells were stained 15 min at RT with the following Mouse anti-Human antibodies: CD56-BV510 (BD, USA), CD159a-PE (Beckman Coulter, USA), CD159a-BUV650 (BD, USA) CD94-RB744 (BD, USA), CD94-PE (BD, USA), CD3-APC-H7 (BD, USA), CD8beta-PE (BD, USA), CD8beta-eFluor-660 (Invitrogen, USA), HLA-E-BV421 (Biolegend, USA) and HLA class I – FITC (Invitrogen, USA), IFN-γ – BV711 (BD, USA). Murinized TCR was stained using Hamster anti-Mouse TCR-Cbeta-APC (BD, USA) or Hamster anti-Mouse TCR-Cbeta-BV711 (BD, USA). For each stain, 30,000 NK cells were aliquoted into a 96-well V-bottom or U-bottom plate. To stain intracellular cytokines a FOXP3 buffer kit (eBiosciences, USA) was used following manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry data was unmixed using Spectroflo (Cytek Biosciences) and analysis was done using FlowJo v10.10 software.

High dimensional reduction analysis

To perform a final analysis of the phenotype of the cellular products, 30,000 NK cells were stained with a panel of 21 fluorochrome tagged antibodies as described in Suppl Table 1. Flow cytometry data was unmixed using Spectroflo (Cytek Biosciences) and analysis was done using the OMIQ software from Dotmatics (www.omiq.ai, www.dotmatics.com). For each acquired flow cytometry data file, quality control of the samples was performed based on CD56 + NKp46+ marker expression. 1 sample did not meet the selection criteria and was for further analysis gated on CD56 + NKp46+ expression (Suppl Fig. 1). To generate Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding of multiparameter FACS, the NK cell number between samples were normalized using randomized downsampling, resulting in a number of 3,109 cells per sample (count table; Suppl Table 2). After quality control, an UMAP dimensional reduction analysis could be performed based on the expression of 17 markers (Fig. 3). Frequencies of marker expression depicted in Fig. 4D were based on manual gating of the CD56 + NKp46+ lymphocyte population prior to downsampling.

Target cell culture

Primary OVCA-L23 cells derived from an HLA-A*02:01 positive OVCA patient were previously isolated from ascites fluid by centrifugation (> 70% EpCAM positive cells and > 95% CD45 negative cells) and immediately frozen [21]. Ovarian cancer (OVCA) cell lines, A2780 (ECACC), COV641 (kindly provided by dr. E. Verdegaal, Medical Oncology, LUMC [35]) and primary OVCA-L23 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, high glucose 4.5 g/L, NEAA) (Gibco), 8% FBS, 2% 200 mM L-glutamine and 1% 10,000 U/mL Pen/Strep. All three OVCA cell lines were transduced with a retroviral vector encoding for HLA-A*02:01. Additionally, the OVCA cell lines and the primary patient-derived OVCA-L23 cells were transduced with a retroviral vector encoding for luciferase and dTomato-Red.

Bioluminescence cytotoxicity assay

Target cells were plated in an opaque 96-well flat-bottom plate in a concentration of 10,000 target cells/well. Subsequently, if indicated, the target cells were pretreated with 1000 IU/mL human IFN-γ for 24 h and loaded with 250nM PRAME peptide (SLLQHLIGL), which was washed away thereafter. Next, 20,000 NK cells were added in NK-cell medium containing luciferin (Cayman Chemical) and co-cultured for 4 h. Subsequently, bioluminescence signal was measured by SpectraMax. Longitudinal live-cell analysis cytotoxicity assay

Target cells were plated in a 96-well flat-bottom plate in a concentration of 10,000 target cells/well. Subsequently, if indicated, the target cells were pretreated with 1000 IU/mL human IFN-γ for 24 h and loaded with 250nM PRAME peptide (SLLQHLIGL), which was washed away thereafter. Next, 20,000 NK cells were added in phenol red-free NK-M and co-cultured in the Incucyte® Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius, Germany). The dTomato-Red signal by the OVCA cells was measured to quantify living cells (Fig. 6). Images were acquired and fluorescence signal was measured every 2–4 h. Integrated fluorescence signal was calculated and normalized to t = 0. Analysis was performed using Incucyte Live-Cell Analysis Software 2020B. Each experiment was performed in duplo.

Results

Upregulation of HLA class I and HLA-E in ovarian cancer cells after IFN-γ exposure hampers NK-mediated cytotoxicity in vitro

To assess the effect of cytokines produced by K562F-expanded primary NK cells on the HLA expression of ovarian cancer (OVCA) cell lines, these OVCA cells were co-incubated with supernatant harvested from a 24-hour coculture of the respective OVCA cell line with K562F-expanded NK cells derived from five different donors (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, we measured HLA-E and HLA class I expression on the OVCA cell lines using flow cytometry. Consistent with current literature, our data show that exposure to the supernatant increases the cell surface expression of both HLA-E as well as HLA class I on SK-OV-3 OVCA cells (Fig. 1BC), as well as A2780 and COV641 OVCA cell lines (Fig. 1DE). Furthermore, this co-culture supernatant contained high levels of IFN-γ, and treatment of the cell lines with IFN-γ resulted in a similar increase of HLA class I and HLA-E expression on the respective OVCA cell lines, mimicking the pro-inflammatory condition of the supernatant. (Fig. 1DE,Suppl Fig. 2) [25, 36]. From this point, in further experiments, IFN-γ pre-treatment was used to imitate the pro-inflammatory effect of NK cell exposure in the tumor microenvironment.

Secondly, we investigated the effect of IFN-γ exposure on the susceptibility of ovarian cancer cells to NK cell cytotoxicity. dTomato-luciferase transduced OVCA cell lines were pre-treated with or without IFN-γ for 24 h and subsequently co-cultured with K562F-expanded NK cells for 16 h. We observed that all three OVCA cell lines became more resistant to NK-mediated cytotoxicity after IFN-γ exposure (Fig. 1FGH). These findings confirm the decreased reactivity of K562F-expanded NK cells to IFN-γ exposed ovarian cancer cells.

Introducing a tumor-specific PRAME-TCR does not overcome IFN-γ induced NK cell resistance

Although NK-mediated cytotoxicity reduces when HLA class I is upregulated upon IFN-γ exposure, these tumors may become more susceptible to TCR-mediated cytotoxicity. As shown previously, NK cells modified to express a TCR (TCR: NK) are capable to efficiently kill HLA class I positive tumor cells that express the specific target antigen [11, 12]. Therefore, we harnessed K562F-expanded NK cells with a TCR targeting a peptide derived from PRAME, presented in HLA-A*02:01, following a previously described protocol [11]. Subsequently, NK: PRAME and NK: MOCK cells were cocultured with IFN-γ exposed A2780 ovarian cancer cells, endogenously expressing the TCR target antigen PRAME and transduced with HLA-A*02:01 and dTomato-Red (Suppl Fig. 3B). The cytotoxic activity of the NK: PRAME and NK: MOCK cells was compared in an Incucyte longitudinal live-cell analysis. In line with previous experiments we found that both NK: PRAME and NK: MOCK cells were able to efficiently kill A2780 cells that were not exposed to IFN-γ, whereby the NK: PRAME cells show a minimal increase in tumor lysis (Fig. 1I, dark grey). However, upon IFN-γ exposure the cytotoxic activity of both NK: PRAME and NK: MOCK toward OVCA cell line A2780 was reduced, whereby no difference could be observed between the tumor-specific NK: PRAME cells and the NK: MOCK cells (Fig. 1I, orange). Nevertheless, when OVCA cells were loaded with supraphysiological amounts of PRAME peptide, the IFN-γ pre-treated OVCA cells were killed by NK: PRAME, although IFN-γ exposed OVCA remained more resistant to lysis compared to the IFN-γ naïve OVCA cells. These findings indicate that the NK: PRAME cells require an artificially high exposure to HLA-peptide complexes to override the inhibitory effect of IFN-γ and illustrates that introduction of a tumor-specific TCR in NK cells alone does not overcome IFN-γ induced resistance in a physiological context.

Efficient KLRC1 knockout achieved using a combination of multiple gRNAs

To investigate whether IFN-γ induced resistance of OVCA cells to NK-mediated cytotoxicity is driven by upregulation of HLA-E, which interacts with the inhibitory receptor NKG2A, we performed CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of KLRC1, the gene encoding NKG2A, in primary NK cells. NK cells were MACS isolated from cryopreserved PBMCs and stimulated with IL-2 and IL-15. The following day, NK cells were electroporated with CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes targeting KLRC1 followed by stimulation with irradiated K562F cells. To increase the knockout efficiency, we tested three different single-guide RNAs (gRNAs) and combinations thereof. Evaluation of knockout efficiency at day 7 revealed that gRNA #1 yielded the best result (Suppl Fig. 4A). Electroporation of primary NK cells using gRNA #1 from four different donors reduced the NKG2A expression to 30–40%. However, by combining multiple gRNAs, a further reduction in NKG2A could be achieved, resulting in 10–20% NKG2A expression (Fig. 2B), while number of viable cells at day 7 was not inferior to that of the single gRNA group (Fig. 2C, Suppl Fig. 4B). Moreover, the primary NK cells were electroporated prior to the first stimulation with K562F cells, resulting in a decreased expansion in the first week of the culture protocol. After re-stimulation at day 7, the electroporated NK cells from all donors could be further expanded (Fig. 2D). These results demonstrate that combining multiple gRNA complexes targeting KLRC1 achieves robust knockout of NKG2A.

Dual-modification of NK cells to combine NKG2A knockout with TCR expression

To investigate whether NK cells expressing a TCR and lacking NKG2A can recognize IFN-γ exposed OVCA cells more efficiently, we generated NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells. In this protocol, the KLRC1 knockout was followed by retroviral transduction of TCR-CD8αβ on day 3 and the full CD3 complex, on day 10 post-isolation (Fig. 3A). An interim analysis on day 7 revealed that this protocol resulted in a highly pure CD56 + NK cell population (Fig. 3B). KLRC1 knockout remained successful after transduction, resulting in over a 70% reduction in NKG2A + NK cells (Fig. 3CD). Furthermore, transduction of the TCR-CD8αβ construct resulted 60% CD8αβ expression in the electroporated NK cells (MOCK EP/KLRC1 KO) (Fig. 3EF). To induce cell surface expression of the TCR, both KLRC1 KO and MOCK EP NK cells were transduced on day 10 with a retroviral vector encoding all four CD3 subunits. Cells were subsequently enriched for TCR expression, resulting in a highly pure NK: PRAMENKG2A KO product by day 14 (Fig. 3GHI). Importantly, NKG2A expression remained consistently low over time, indicating no selective proliferative advantage of residual NKG2A + NK cells (Fig. 3J). These results demonstrate the feasibility of combining KLRC1 knockout with sequential retroviral transduction of TCR-CD8αβ and CD3, leading to a highly pure dual-modified NK cell product within 14 days.

Multicolor flow cytometry analysis indicates no alterations in NK phenotype of NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells

To study whether the introduction of the TCR-CD3 complex and KLRC1 knockout changed the phenotype of primary NK cells, we determined the phenotype of different NK cell products by multicolor flow cytometry. Primary NK cells of 5 donors with or without NKG2A knockout and transduced with either PRAME TCR (NK: PRAME) or mock receptor (NK: MOCK) were examined 7 days after the last restimulation with K562F cells. Data were analyzed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for unsupervised dimensional reduction (gating strategy shown in Suppl Fig. 1). The UMAP revealed that the four different NK cell products clustered uniformly based on TCR/CD3 and NKG2A/CD94 expression, confirming purity of the samples (Fig. 4A). Moreover, NK cells of all 5 donors were equally divided over all four products (Fig. 4B).

Phenotypic analysis revealed no differences in expression of activation markers, such as KLRG-1, HLA-DR, CD57, CD38, NKG2D, NKG2C and NKp30, as well as co-inhibitory receptors, including PD-1, TIM-3, TIGIT and LAG-3, between NKG2A knockout and wildtype NK cells, nor between PRAME-TCR and MOCK-transduced NK cells (Fig. 4C). As shown in Fig. 4C, some markers were not uniformly expressed by all NK cells. However, manual gating of these markers did not show any significant difference in expression between the 4 products but rather highlighted a similar degree of heterogeneity across the four NK cell populations (Fig. 4D). Overall, phenotypic analysis revealed no significant differences in activation or inhibition markers following TCR-CD3 introduction or KLRC1 knockout.

KLRC1 knockout improves effector function of NK: PRAME cells against ovarian cancer cell lines

To assess whether a KLRC1 knockout improves the cytotoxic effector function of NK: PRAME cells against tumor targets exposed to IFN-γ, we performed 4-hour bioluminescence cytotoxicity assays with the four different NK cell products co-incubated with different OVCA cell lines expressing HLA-A*02:01 and the TCR antigen PRAME (Fig. 5, Suppl Fig. 3, Suppl Fig. 6B). As a control, all NK cells recognized HLA-negative K562 cells, confirming intact NK-mediated cytotoxicity (Suppl Fig. 5A). Consistent with previous results, all NK cell products were able to lyse the OVCA cell line SK-OV-3 in the absence of IFN-γ pre-treatment, with no clear difference in cytotoxicity between NK: PRAME and NK: mock cells (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, co-incubation of NK cell products with the PRAME-expressing OVCA cell lines SK-OV-3 and COV641 resulted in an increased percentage of intracellular IFN-γ positive NK: PRAME cells, irrespective of NKG2A knockout status. In contrast, co-incubation with the PRAME-negative cervical cancer cell line Caski did not induce IFN-γ expression, demonstrating a PRAME-specific cytokine response by the NK: PRAME cell products (Suppl Fig. 6).

Following IFN-γ pre-treatment of SK-OV-3 cells, clear differences in cytotoxicity between the engineered NK cell products became apparent (Fig. 5B). NK: MOCKNKG2A WT cells showed reduced cytotoxicity towards IFN-γ pre-treated SK-OV-3 cells compared with untreated cells. In contrast, this reduction in cytotoxicity after IFN-γ pre-treatment was not observed for the NK: MOCKNKG2A KO cells. In line with previous results, the NK: PRAMENKG2A WT cells were not able to override IFN-γ induced inhibition, despite the expression of the PRAME-TCR. However, when the SK-OV-3 cells were loaded with supraphysiological amounts of PRAME peptide, the NK: PRAMENKG2A WT were able to counteract this inhibition and specifically lyse these cells (Fig. 5C). Finally, the NK: PRAMENKG2A KO were able to successfully lyse the IFN-γ pre-treated SK-OV-3 cells, and the percentage of cytotoxicity was even higher than that observed against SK-OV-3 cells not treated with IFN-γ (Fig. 5AB). Interestingly, the cytotoxicity of the NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells was equally high against IFN-γ pre-treated SK-OV-3 with or without additional PRAME peptide loading, in contrast to the NK: PRAMENKG2A WT cells. This suggests that NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells already reach their maximum killing capacity against targets endogenously expressing the PRAME antigen, even when PRAME expression is less than 10% of housekeeping gene expression (Suppl Fig. 3B). Overall, the NK: PRAMENKG2A KO showed a significant better performance in comparison to both NK: MOCK and NK: PRAMENKG2A WT cells against IFN-γ pre-treated SK-OV-3 cells, a pattern also observed against the OVCA cell line COV641 (Suppl Fig. 5B).

The increased killing capacity of NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells was confirmed in a 16-hour IncuCyte assay, which tracked dTomato-expressing SK-OV-3 cells in co-culture with the engineered NK cell products (Fig. 6AB). Without IFN-γ, all products showed similar cytolytic capacity towards SK-OV-3 (Fig. 6A). However, upon IFN-γ pre-treatment both NK: MOCK cells as well as the NK: PRAMENKG2A WT cells showed reduced cytotoxicity against SK-OV-3 cells (Fig. 6B). In line with the short-term assays, only the NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells were able to lyse the IFN-γ pre-treated cells as effective as the untreated SK-OV-3, resulting in a performance advantage compared to the other engineered cell products. These findings underscore the need for combining TCR expression and KLRC1 knockout to successfully unlock the cytotoxic potential of NK cells to target IFN-γ exposed tumor cells.

NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells are effective against primary ovarian cancer cells

To further address the therapeutic potential of NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells, we tested their cytotoxic capacity against PRAME-positive primary ovarian cancer cells isolated from ascites fluid of an HLA-A*02:01 positive OVCA patient (OVCA-L23). In an Incucyte assay, tumor cells were co-cultured for 8 h with the engineered NK cell products generated from different healthy donors (Fig. 6CD). Similar to the results of the OVCA cell lines, no significance difference in killing capacity between the engineered NK cell products was observed against the primary OVCA cells, not treated with IFN-γ (Fig. 6C). However, when the primary OVCA cells were exposed to IFN-γ differences between the efficiency of cytotoxic capacity were observed. In here, NK: PRAMENKG2A WT were able to induce a cytolytic response, however, again, the NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells performed better against the primary OVCA, outperforming the NK: MOCK as well as the NK: PRAMENKG2A WT cells (Fig. 6D). These results validate the enhancement of NK: PRAMENKG2A KO in the lysis of IFN-γ exposed ovarian cancer cells.

Discussion

In this study, we describe a dual-modification strategy for NK cells in which CRISPR-Cas9 mediated KLRC1 gene disruption is combined with transduction of a TCR-CD3 complex and CD8 coreceptor. This approach achieved both high knockout efficiencies and effective transduction, resulting in highly pure NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cell products. Functionally, these engineered NK cells significantly increased lysis of OVCA cell lines and primary OVCA cells, particularly following IFN-γ pre-treatment, to mimic a pro-inflammatory environment.

Previous work by our group and others has demonstrated that primary NK cells can be engineered to express a TCR and IL-15, enabling effective TCR-mediated targeting of B-cell malignancies [11, 12, 37, 38]. However, unlike in hematological malignancies, cellular therapies have yet to achieve a significant breakthrough in the treatment of solid tumors. NK cell-based therapies are considered promising in this context, since solid tumors exhibit lower HLA class I expression compared to hematological malignancies. However, NK cells often fail to effectively control solid tumors, likely due to immunosuppressive chemokines and interferons secreted within the tumor microenvironment (TME), a phenomenon also addressed in the context of ovarian cancer [19, 39, 40].

To address this in our study, we investigated the direct role of IFN-γ produced by primary NK cells in promoting immune evasion in ovarian cancer, while acknowledging that other cytokines and chemokines also play a non-negligible role in immune escape mechanisms. As shown by others, primary NK cells initially elicit a strong cytotoxic response against ovarian cancer cells [14, 39]. However, cytokines, such as IFN-γ, released during this response contributes to resistance against NK cell therapy by upregulating HLA-E and HLA class I on the tumor cells [20, 36, 41, 42]. Notably, although HLA class I expression is upregulated on tumor cells following IFN-γ exposure, our findings indicate that expression of a tumor-specific TCR in NK cells alone is insufficient to restore an effective anti-tumor response. Therefore, we proposed that inhibition via HLA-E may limit the efficiency of NK: TCR therapy. Previous studies have shown that HLA-E-mediated inhibition can be prevented by blocking or knocking out NKG2A [20, 34, 43–45]. To test this, we combined NK: TCR therapy with CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of NKG2A via disruption of the KLRC1 gene. We observed that NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells reinstated TCR-mediated lysis against IFN-γ exposed ovarian cancer cells. In contrast, wild-type NK: PRAMENKG2A WT cells required supraphysiological concentrations of PRAME peptide to achieve comparable target cell lysis as NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells. This NKG2A-dependent inhibition of TCR-signaling has also been observed for NKG2A-expressing T cells targeting IFN-γ pretreated ovarian cancer cells [46]. Furthermore, IFN-γ secretion within the TME can exert effects over a considerable distance. Hoekstra et al. demonstrated that IFN-γ influenced not only neighboring cells but also bystander cells over distances exceeding 800 μm, creating a widespread immunosuppressive environment in an in vivo ovarian cancer model [47, 48]. These findings underscore the critical need of NKG2A knockout to enhance the therapeutic potential of NK: TCR and NK: CAR approached in solid tumors. Our results suggest that NK: PRAMENKG2A KO cells hold potential for clinical translation as a cellular therapeutic for ovarian cancer. Moreover, this approach may be applicable to other PRAME expressing solid tumors, such as uveal and cutaneous melanoma, synovial sarcoma and neuroblastoma, many of which are characterized by low HLA class I expression [23–25, 49, 50](NCT06383572, Suppl Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the NK: TCRNKG2A KO platform could be broadened by introducing high-affinity TCRs targeting alternative tumor-specific antigens, such as CTCFL [21].

Our results also offer broader implications for NK cell immunotherapies that combine CRISPR/Cas9 gene disruption with viral transduction, since our protocol differs from existing methods in several key aspects [34, 51–55]. For example, to enhance the efficiency of CRISPR/Cas9-medicated NKG2A knockout, we combined multiple gRNAs targeting two exons of the KLRC1 gene, which resulted in higher knockout efficiencies compared to previous reported methods using only gRNA 1, which was previously used by Bexte et al., who managed to reduce NKG2A expression to 40% [33, 34, 44, 56]. While multiplex gene editing has been associated with chromosomal instability, we do not expect such risks in our approach, as only a single gene on one chromosome is targeted [57]. Nevertheless, further investigation regarding potential off-target effects is needed prior to clinical translation of this protocol. Additionally, we demonstrate that overnight cytokine stimulation renders NK cells sufficiently receptive to CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene modification, achieving knockout efficiencies comparable to, or exceeding, protocols using pre-expanded NK cells. Although CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing is known to reduce cell viability, the sequence of modifications in our protocol, from cytokine stimulation, electroporation, transduction and expansion, resulted in a cellular product highly susceptible to retroviral transduction, and with normal expansion rates after day 7. We consider this revised workflow to be a more cost-effective method for generating NK: TCR or NK: CAR products, as it reduces the consumption of guide RNAs, Cas9 protein, and viral supernatant since fewer cells need to be electroporated and transduced compared to methods that modify already expanded cells. Conceptually, incorporating these adjustments into a -single construct- manufacturing protocol would result in ready-to-use NK: CARNKG2A KO or NK: TCRNKG2A KO product within 8 days. However, before clinical application, the feasibility of our production protocol has to be validated in large-scale productions, along with comprehensive evaluations of safety, efficacy and toxicity.

No in vivo evaluation of the NK: PRAMENKG2A KO product has yet been performed, due to limitations in recapitulating the pro-inflammatory conditions described in our manuscript within a murine model. To assess the effectivity of human cell therapies against human tumors, we are restricted to the use of NSG mice. However, inoculation of SKOV-3 cells into these mice did not result in upregulation of HLA-E, likely due to the absence of an immune response, whereas HLA-E upregulation has previously been described ex vivo in the tumor microenvironment of patients [19]. Nevertheless, we have previously demonstrated effective tumor eradication by PRAME TCR-engineered T cells [21], as well as antigen-specific tumor eradication of previously generated NK: TCR products in vivo [11, 12].

In summary, we propose a highly efficient and cost-effective dual-modification protocol for NK cells combining NKG2A knockout with tumor-antigen-specific TCR expression. Our data highlight the increased NK-mediated and TCR-mediated cytotoxicity of NK: PRAMENKG2A KO against ovarian cancer cells under immunosuppressive conditions. These findings emphasize the potential of a NKG2A knockout to prevent HLA-E mediation inhibition and improve the efficacy of NK cell-based therapies for solid tumors.
