Chemometric Methods Applied to Infrared and Raman Spectroscopy for Arboviruses Diagnosis: A Systematic Review With Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Karime Zeraik Abdalla Domingues, Raul Edison Luna Lazo, Laís Salvador do Amaral, Alexandre de Fátima Cobre, Luana Mota Ferreira, Roberto Pontarolo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArboviruses such as dengue, Zika, chikungunya and yellow fever share similar clinical manifestations, making differential diagnosis challenging, particularly in endemic regions with viral co‐circulation. Conventional laboratory methods present important limitations, including cross‐reactivity and reliance on specialized infrastructure. In this scenario, spectroscopic techniques such as Fourier‐transform attenuated total reflectance infrared spectroscopy (ATR–FTIR) and Raman, when combined with artificial intelligence (AI), have shown promise by enabling rapid, low‐cost analyses. This systematic review (PROSPERO CRD420251006929) aimed to qualitatively and quantitatively synthesize studies that applied infrared and Raman spectroscopy to clinical samples, supported by chemometric models. All 23 included studies investigated dengue patients, with only one also assessing Zika and chikungunya. Most studies employed Raman spectroscopy and multivariate analysis methods, such as principal component analysis with linear discriminant analysis (PCA‐LDA, 39.1%) and partial least squares with discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA, 21.7%), with an overall sensitivity of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.91–0.96) and overall specificity of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–0.98) for Raman spectroscopy. The risk of bias across all studies was high, according to PROBAST‐AI development and evaluation assessment. These findings highlight the potential of spectroscopic approaches combined with AI for diagnosing arboviral infections, although further robust studies are required to support broader clinical validation.Keywords: artificial intelligence, chemometrics, dengue, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy

Introduction

Arboviruses are a group of viral diseases transmitted by arthropods, with the female Aedes aegypti mosquito being the main vector for dengue, Zika, chikungunya and the urban cycle of yellow fever [1, 2]. The spread of these infections has been intensified by the expansion of urban areas, climate change and the increasing resistance of vectors to traditional control methods [3, 4, 5]. Dengue, in particular, is one of the most prevalent arboviruses in tropical and subtropical regions, representing a significant global public health concern. Estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicate between 100 and 400 million cases annually worldwide, with nearly half of the global population at risk for this infectious disease [6, 7]. Zika virus, initially confined to Africa and Asia, emerged globally after the 2015–2016 outbreak in Brazil; since then, transmission has been confirmed in more than 92 countries in all continents [8, 9]. Similarly, chikungunya has rapidly expanded, with major outbreaks in more than 100 countries worldwide [10, 11]. Yellow fever, although preventable by vaccination, remains a public health concern in endemic regions of Africa and South America, with recent outbreaks in Brazil raising alarms due to the virus reaching areas near large urban centres, and where vaccination coverage may be insufficient [5, 12, 13].

The most commonly used laboratory diagnostic methods for detecting arboviruses are polymerase chain reaction (PCR), for direct detection of viral genetic material, and serological tests, such as the Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA), which identify antibodies or antigens related to the infection. Despite the high sensitivity of PCR, this technique has important limitations as it requires complex laboratory infrastructure and trained professionals and has a short diagnostic window, being most effective only in the early days of infection. ELISA, while more accessible and simpler, suffers from cross‐reactions among arboviruses, which compromises its specificity, and is only useful from the fifth day of symptoms onward, when antibodies begin to be produced [14, 15, 16].

In light of these limitations, Raman spectroscopy, surface‐enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) and infrared spectroscopy, especially the Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR–FTIR) technique, have emerged as promising tools for the detection and characterization of various diseases. These techniques provide vibrational information about the chemical bonds present in biological samples, enabling detailed molecular characterization. ATR–FTIR primarily operates in the mid‐infrared (MIR) range (4000–400 cm−1) and is sensitive to changes in biochemical components such as lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. Raman spectroscopy, in turn, relies on the inelastic scattering of light to generate spectra that reflect the chemical composition of complex samples [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. When nanostructured metallic substrates such as silver or gold are employed, the technique known as surface‐enhanced Raman scattering enables a substantial amplification of the Raman signal, both chemically and electromagnetically. This signal enhancement is mainly due to localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), a phenomenon when incident light induces collective oscillations of conduction electrons on the surface of the metallic nanostructures, significantly boosting the sensitivity of detection, even at extremely low analyte concentrations [22, 23, 24].

In recent years, significant advances in instrumentation, combined with the development of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning algorithms, have further enhanced the use of chemometrics in healthcare [25, 26, 27, 28]. Spectroscopic techniques such as Raman and ATR–FTIR offer the advantages of rapid analyses, independence from specific reagents or complex instrumentation and compatibility with chemometric approaches for multivariate data analysis. When combined with these methods, they enhance the extraction of detailed chemical information, improving the ability to discriminate between infected and non‐infected samples [29, 30, 31, 32]. In this context, the present work aims to synthesize the available evidence on the application of chemometric methods combined with AI models for the analysis of spectroscopic data obtained from biological samples, with emphasis on MIR and Raman spectroscopy. These complementary techniques are explored as potential diagnostic and screening strategies for arboviral infections, including dengue, Zika, chikungunya and yellow fever.

Methodology

Protocol and Registration

This study was conducted and documented following the guidelines of the Cochrane Collaboration and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The study protocol (CRD420251006929) is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The selection of studies and data extraction were carried out independently by two reviewers, with a third reviewer consulted to resolve any discrepancies.

Study Selection and Eligibility Criteria

Searches were conducted without time or language restrictions (last update in October 2025) to identify studies published in PubMed, Embase and Web of Science databases. Keywords related to arboviruses, such as dengue, chikungunya, Zika virus and yellow fever, and Raman and infrared spectroscopy techniques, were combined using the Boolean operators AND and OR (Table S1). The records retrieved from the databases were imported into the Rayyan web app, free version, Rayyan Systems Inc. (https://new.rayyan.ai/) [33] where duplicate entries were removed using the SR Accelerator software (https://sr‐accelerator.com) [34].

This systematic review included articles with the following eligibility criteria: (i) studies with biological samples (e.g., saliva, blood, plasma and serum) from human patients infected with dengue, chikungunya, Zika virus or yellow fever; (ii) healthy individuals and patients without arboviral infections or with another infectious disease as the control group; (iii) use of spectroscopic techniques, such as Raman (e.g., micro‐Raman, SERS, etc.) or infrared (e.g., MIR–FTIR and ATR–FTIR); (iv) application of predictive machine learning models (e.g., partial least squares discriminant analysis—PLS‐DA and principal component analysis—linear discriminant analysis—PCA‐LDA); and (v) studies with true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), false negative (FN), sensitivity and specificity data. Studies such as case reports, conference papers and reviews, as well as articles with non‐Roman characters, were excluded from the systematic review. Two reviewers independently carried out all stages of study selection and data extraction, with a third author consulted in case of discrepancies.

Data Extraction and Risk of Bias Assessment

Data from studies that met the eligibility criteria were independently extracted by two reviewers. The following information was collected: general study details (authors’ names, number of participants, period of sample collection, publication year, country of publication, potential conflicts of interest, study design, sample type), characteristics of the diagnostic methods (such as the spectral region analysed, chemometric methods, cross‐validation and spectral biomarkers), chemometric methods and diagnostic performance metrics (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, FPs, FNs, TPs, true negatives, etc.).

The assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were carried out using the PROBAST‐AI (Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool for AI), created for studies that develop or validate predictive models based on AI and machine learning techniques [35].

PROBAST‐AI, an extension of the original PROBAST tool [36], is structured into four main domains designed to identify potential sources of bias: (i) the first domain, related to participants and data sources, evaluates the adequacy of the datasets used, the study design and the representativeness of the sample; (ii) the second domain, concerning predictors, analyses the definition, measurement and processing of input variables, ensuring that preprocessing and feature selection were appropriately conducted without data leakage; (iii) the third domain, focused on outcomes, examines whether the predicted result was defined and measured in an objective and predictor‐independent manner. Finally, (iv) the analysis domain considers the adequacy of modelling methods, validation strategies, control of overfitting, handling of missing data, class balance and the clarity with which performance metrics are reported. In addition to assessing the risk of bias, PROBAST‐AI also evaluates the applicability of the studies, that is, whether the participants, predictors and outcomes investigated are sufficiently aligned with the research question or intended use of the model. Applicability is particularly examined in the first three domains, where potential discrepancies between the study context and the target population or setting are identified.

Furthermore, PROBAST‐AI is organized into two complementary instruments: PROBAST‐AI development and PROBAST‐AI evaluation, designed to address different stages of prediction model research. The PROBAST‐AI development version focuses on studies that aim to construct new AI‐based prediction models and accesses aspects such as dataset representativeness, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, internal validation procedures and transparency in model reporting. The PROBAST‐AI evaluation version, on the other hand, is tailored for studies that externally validate or compare existing AI prediction models, emphasizing the independence of validation data, consistency in data preprocessing, adequacy of reference standards and reproducibility of performance metrics.

Each PROBAST‐AI signalling question is answered using standardized response options, such as ‘Yes (Y)’, ‘Probably Yes (PY)’, ‘Probably No (PN)’, ‘No (N)’, ‘No information (NI)’ or ‘Not applicable (NA)’, depending on the level of clarity and completeness of the information reported in the study. These responses guide the judgement for each domain in terms of risk of bias and concerns regarding applicability. For risk of bias, a domain is rated as low when all signalling questions are answered ‘Yes’ or ‘Probably Yes’, indicating that bias is unlikely to influence the study results. Conversely, if one or more questions are answered ‘No’ or ‘Probably No’, the domain is judged to have high risk of bias. When the available information is insufficient to make a judgement, it is classified as unclear. Similarly, the applicability assessment examines whether the study's population, predictors and outcomes are consistent with the intended review question or clinical context. Concerns regarding applicability are rated as low, high or unclear, following the same logic.

Finally, an overall judgement for each study is derived by integrating the ratings across domains. A study is considered to have an overall low risk of bias only if all domains are rated as low, and if one or more domains are judged to have a high or unclear risk, the study is considered to have a high or uncertain overall risk of bias, respectively. This structured approach ensures a transparent and reproducible evaluation of methodological quality, highlighting both strengths and weaknesses that may influence the validity and generalizability of AI‐based prediction models.

Meta‐Analysis

Meta‐analyses were conducted according to the sample type, spectroscopic method and machine learning algorithms used in each study.

Sensitivity represents the model's ability to detect positive cases accurately. It is determined by the proportion of TP among all real positive cases, meaning that it reflects how many positive instances the model successfully identifies compared to the total number of positives. Specificity, on the other hand, expresses the model's ability to correctly recognize negative cases. It is obtained by calculating the proportion of true negatives among all actual negative cases, indicating how well the model avoids false alarms. Sensitivity is therefore associated with the detection of positives, whereas specificity relates to the correct rejection of negatives [37, 38]. Overall specificity and sensitivity were calculated using TP, TN, FN, FP values, along with 95% confidence intervals. Summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC‐ROC) were also generated.

The chi‐square test was applied to assess statistical heterogeneity among the included studies in the meta‐analysis. Heterogeneity was considered moderate when the I 2 statistic ranged from 50% to 75% and high when I 2 exceeded 75%. Sensitivity analyses were carried out in cases where I 2 was above 50%, either by hypothetically excluding studies one at a time or by performing subgroup analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using the Meta‐Disc software version 1.4.7 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/metadisc/) [39].

Results

The systematic search in the databases, last performed in October 2025, resulted in the identification of 3289 articles retrieved from the PubMed, Embase and Web of Science platforms, of which 1222 were removed as duplicates. Title and abstract screening were conducted on 2067 studies, leading to selection of 68 articles for full‐text review and the exclusion of 45 studies (Table S2). After this stage, 23 chemometric studies were deemed eligible for inclusion in the systematic review (Figure 1).

In total, the 23 included articles reported data from 2116 individuals, with 931 dengue patients (44% of the total, 85.3% of patients with some arbovirus), 30 Zika patients (1.4% of total), 130 chikungunya patients (6.1% of total) and 1025 patients from control groups (48.4%). In most studies (n = 17, 73.9% of studies), serum samples were used, whereas four (17.4%) used plasma samples, and two (8.7%) used whole blood. All studies evaluated samples from individuals infected with the dengue virus, with two of them also including chikungunya patients [44, 59], whereas Santos et al. [59] conducted a comparative assessment of dengue, Zika and chikungunya samples using chemometric techniques combined with ATR–FTIR spectroscopy. In general, the studies did not provide detailed information about the infection stage and evaluated population, such as gender, age and presence of other comorbidities, nor whether the cases were primary or secondary infections.

As for reference diagnostic tests, 14 studies (60.9%) applied ELISA for the detection of IgM and IgG antibodies and the DENV NS1 protein, whereas PCR was used in five studies (21.7%), with three studies reporting the use of both methods to evaluate patients prior to applying the models [40, 44, 60]. In contrast, seven studies (30.4%) did not specify which reference test was employed for diagnostic confirmation. ELISA was primarily used for the detection of IgM and IgG antibodies and the DENV NS1 protein, a non‐structural glycoprotein essential for viral replication that is not present at all stages of infection, as its levels decrease after the acute phase in both primary and secondary cases, whereas RT‐PCR detects viral RNA and is therefore useful for identifying active infection, particularly in the early stages [61].

Regarding the spectroscopic techniques used for analysing patients’ biological samples, most studies (n = 18, 78.3%) employed Raman spectroscopy, with five of them applying SERS to enhance the Raman signal. One study by Das et al. [44] used both Raman and FTIR, whereas five studies (21.7%) applied only ATR–FTIR to acquire spectra prior to the application of predictive models.

Regarding the chemometric methods applied, principal component analysis (PCA) was used in 17 studies (73.9%). PCA is an unsupervised multivariate technique commonly employed as a preprocessing step to reduce data dimensionality and capture the main sources of variability in the spectral data. Among the studies that applied PCA, only one used it as the sole multivariate analysis method. However, as PCA is not a classification algorithm, it cannot independently build a diagnostic model, as it lacks predictive capability for classifying new samples. In contrast, the remaining studies combined PCA with supervised classification techniques, such as linear discriminant analysis (PCA‐LDA) (n = 9 studies; 39.1%), which enables both dimensionality reduction and discrimination by identifying linear combinations of features that maximize the separation between classes.

Five studies (21.7%) [40, 47, 48, 56, 58] applied partial least squares‐discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA) to predict sample group classification. In chemometric applications, PLS‐DA is a supervised model, based on the PLS algorithm for regression tasks, and is particularly useful for handling highly collinear or noisy spectral variables, as it projects the data into a latent space that maximizes the covariance between predictors and class labels, enhancing class separation while preserving relevant chemical information. Thus, PLS‐DA allows not only dimensionality reduction but also the identification of discriminant spectral regions among sample groups. Conversely, four studies used partial least squares regression (PLS‐R) [43, 53, 57, 58]. Although PLS‐R can effectively handle multicollinearity and extract latent variables that capture the main variance in the predictors, it is less suitable for classification or diagnostic purposes because it does not produce a categorical decision for classifying individuals. Instead, these regression‐based approaches compute a spectral similarity score relative to the training datasets. This score can then be interpreted using a predetermined decision threshold to assign class labels. Notably, two of these studies relied solely on PLS‐R for their analysis [43, 57], whereas the other two applied PLS‐R in conjunction with additional supervised models better suited for diagnostic methods.

In terms of cross‐validation, six studies conducted 10‐fold cross‐validation (26.1%), of which three reported the jackknife approach with 10‐fold, similar to leave‐one‐out (LOO) method, two studies (8.7%) applied 5‐fold validation, and six employed the LOO method. Only one study, conducted by Das et al. [44], explicitly reported the use of a stratified k‐fold cross‐validation strategy, aiming to preserve the proportional distribution of classes within each fold and ensure a more balanced evaluation of model performance. K‐fold cross‐validation, such as the 10‐ and 5‐fold methods mentioned, randomly divides the dataset into ‘k’ subsets or folds. The process is then repeated ‘k’ times, where in each iteration, a different subset is used for testing, whereas the remaining (k − 1) subsets are used for training. In the same way, the LOO method is a special case of k‐fold cross‐validation where k equals the total number of samples in the dataset. In each iteration, a single sample is used as the test set, and all the remaining samples are used for training. This method is particularly suitable for small datasets, as it allows the model to be trained on nearly the entire dataset in each iteration, providing a more reliable estimate of model performance when data are limited. Furthermore, 12 studies (52.2%) did not report any data splitting strategy, and 8 studies (34.8%) did not mention the use of any cross‐validation approach, with 3 of these overlapping between both groups [45, 47, 62].

Almost all studies that employed classification models reported good sensitivity and specificity results for dengue diagnosis. According to the meta‐analysis of 11 studies evaluating dengue virus in blood, plasma or serum samples by Raman spectroscopy, combined with chemometric classification models, the overall sensitivity was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.91–0.96), whereas the overall specificity was 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–0.98), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.9849 (Figures S1–S3).

In subgroup analysis (Figures S4–S6), including the nine studies that used only serum as the sample for Raman spectral analysis, the results remained similar, with an overall sensitivity of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.90–0.96), overall specificity of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.98), with an AUC of 0.9851 (Figures S4 and S5). Across the analyses, most subgroups presented moderate heterogeneity (I 2 > 25%). PLS‐DA and PC‐LDA subgroups (Figures S7–S12, respectively) showed no statistical heterogeneity, though their wide confidence intervals suggest limited precision due to fewer data points. Therefore, although all models demonstrated high pooled diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) values, indicating strong discriminative potential, the variability and uncertainty reflected by both I 2 values and broad confidence intervals imply that results should be interpreted with caution until more standardized and externally validated studies are available.

It was not possible to conduct a subgroup analysis according to the reference tests, as five of the studies included in the meta‐analysis confirmed positive samples using ELISA, whereas two studies did not report which specific reference test was used.

Regarding the risk of bias assessment using the PROBAST‐AI tool (see Table S3 for the complete evaluation), all studies demonstrated a high risk of bias (Figure 2) across both development and validation steps (apparent performance, internal and external validation). This classification primarily reflects the limited number of patients and samples analysed, the imbalance between them and corresponding spectra, and the lack of population representativeness, as most datasets originated from a single centre, reducing the robustness and generalizability of the predictive models to broader clinical contexts. In addition, the absence of cross‐validation in several studies and the lack of external validation further increase the risk of overfitting and may lead to overestimated model performance. Other contributing sources of bias include insufficient reporting of data partitioning and control for data leakage between training and testing sets. Collectively, these issues underscore the need for more rigorous methodological standards, better data transparency and adherence to current AI reporting frameworks to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of machine learning–based spectroscopic diagnostic models.

In this review, concerns regarding applicability were generally low for predictors (Domain 2, see Figure 2), reflecting the consistent use of accessible and measurable spectral variables in spectroscopic diagnostic models. However, applicability concerns were more evident for outcomes, as the adequacy of the time interval between predictor and outcome assessment was often unclear, and inconsistencies in the use of reference standards across cases and controls were common. In the context of infectious disease diagnostics, such methodological discrepancies, including variations in sample processing, may compromise both the reliability of spectral biomarker quantification and the validity of outcome classification. Moreover, to enhance clinical translation and practical deployment, further comparative studies between traditional machine learning and deep learning (DL) models, as well as between spectroscopic modalities such as ATR–FTIR, Raman and SERS, are needed to assess not only diagnostic accuracy but also operational feasibility and cost‐effectiveness in real‐world healthcare settings.

Discussion

In diagnostic methods, AI acts as a powerful tool within chemometrics for modelling, prediction and interpretation of complex datasets, enabling the extraction of meaningful patterns from large and multivariable data.

One of the most widely used unsupervised methods in this context and among the studies in this review is PCA, which is a multivariate statistical technique that transforms a set of potentially correlated variables into a new set of uncorrelated variables called principal components. This transformation is performed in such a way that the first principal component captures the largest possible variability of the data, allowing dimensionality reduction with minimal loss of information [64]. PCA is widely used in exploratory data analysis, especially to simplify complex datasets and facilitate the visualization of relevant patterns and clusters. Many models combine PCA with supervised classification algorithms, such as in PCA‐LDA, PCA‐FDA and PCA‐DA, where this dimensionality reduction is followed by discriminant methods that maximize the separation between groups. These combinations aim to construct models capable of interpreting the data, especially when the data exhibit collinearity and noise, common characteristics in spectra from biological samples [65, 66, 67].

In this review, five studies used PLS‐DA (21.7%). Considered the gold standard for binary data analysis in metabolomic studies, PLS‐DA is one of the most widely used machine learning classification algorithms. In a comparative study involving eight different machine learning algorithms—including PLS‐DA, logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), principal component regression analysis (PCRA), radial basis function kernel, support vector machines (SVM), artificial neural networks (ANN) and an alternative neural network model, evaluated using publicly available omics datasets—PLS‐DA outperformed the other methods [68]. This superior performance is largely attributed to its ability to handle datasets in which the number of variables exceeds the number of samples, eliminating the need for prior variable filtering. In contrast, more complex models, such as ANNs and especially those based on DL, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), generally require large datasets [69, 70, 71].

Although PLS‐DA has been widely adopted and often performs well in high‐dimensional datasets, the use of this algorithm should not be applied blindly to all datasets. Despite its strong capacity to identify features that separate classes, datasets with a large number of variables can often be separated by chance, producing models that capture noise rather than meaningful biological variation. Therefore, cross‐validation is essential to identify when the model is being used ineffectively. Moreover, PLS‐DA should ideally be used as both a feature selection and classification tool only when the underlying data relationships are understood or hypothesized. When this is not possible, researchers should interpret PLS‐DA results cautiously, relying on cross‐validation and complementary modelling approaches [72].

In studies employing regression‐based chemometric approaches—such as partial least squares regression (PLS‐R) or principal component regression analysis (PCRA) [41, 43, 57]—the models should be assessed using some performance parameters, including the coefficient of determination (R 2), root mean square error (RMSE), RMSE for cross‐validation (RMSECV) and RMSE of prediction (RMSEP) on external data. PLS‐R is particularly useful when predictor variables are highly collinear or when the number of predictors exceeds the number of observations, as it projects predictors into a lower dimensional space while maximizing the covariance with the response variable. However, as regression‐based approaches generate continuous outputs, they are inherently limited for classification tasks. To overcome this challenge, studies commonly adapt the method by assigning numerical ‘dummy’ variables to the classes (e.g., ‘1’ for infected and ‘0’ for healthy). Converting these continuous predictions into discrete diagnostic categories requires applying an arbitrary decision threshold, which can introduce bias and reduce model interpretability. Furthermore, this method is highly susceptible to overfitting, which can generate unrealistically optimistic performance metrics. Moreover, it is essential to report validation parameters such as analytical accuracy and linearity, typically assessed with calibration curves incorporating cross‐validation [67, 73, 74, 75]. The absence or misuse of these parameters and practices may reflect methodological inconsistencies and compromise both the interpretability and the reliability of the reported findings.

In DL approaches, Hassan et al. [46] used a pretrained deep CNN originally designed as a feature selection for Raman spectra. This architecture learns hierarchical representations of data, detecting complex spectral patterns that might be missed by manual feature selection. In comparison, more traditional machine learning models like SVM, k‐NN and RF lagged behind, especially in sensitivity. In this context, the advantage of transfer learning is that the model exploits prior knowledge from unrelated domains, helping overcome the small‐sample problem [76, 77]. The authors also highlight that interpretability is a limitation, since the CNN, despite its accuracy, works as a black box, making it hard to understand which chemical features drive predictions. In contrast, Das et al. [44] and Waiwijit et al. [60] evaluated MLPs (multi‐layer perceptrons) that are basic neural networks with one or two hidden layers, alongside standard classifiers like SVM and LDA. In both studies, MLPs were technically ‘DL’ but implemented as shallow networks. In Waiwijit et al. [60], MLPs, SVMs and decision trees were compared on the same SERS dataset, but LDA and LR models ultimately outperformed the neural networks. This highlights that, unless paired with a large and diverse training set, simple deep neural networks may not add much beyond what classic models can achieve in this field, while also being considerably more computationally demanding in terms of training time, processing power and data requirements.

Thankappan et al. [40] applied a pipeline that combines a GAN (generative adversarial network) with a ResNet‐based classifier. GANs help address the data imbalance and augmentation problem by generating synthetic yet realistic spectral samples for classes with fewer cases, such as dengue. GANs are powerful for small or imbalanced spectroscopic datasets, but direct clinical translation requires further work to ensure that generated spectra do not introduce bias or artefacts that could influence decisions [78, 79].

Six studies reported that external validation was performed with independent datasets after model development, which is recommended to enhance the robustness and predictive performance of models in real‐world clinical scenarios. Four of these [42, 43, 55, 58] used blinded testing, preventing bias in data interpretation by ensuring that the analyst or algorithm is unaware of the true class labels during evaluation, thereby better simulating how the model would perform when exposed to unseen patient data [67, 70]. However, despite the methodological advantage of applying external validation, the sample sizes of these independent test sets were still limited and not representative of broader populations. Moreover, all studies used patient samples collected from the same geographic location or institution, which restricts the generalizability of their findings.

Among the most frequently used cross‐validation techniques in chemometric methods, k‐fold cross‐validation stands out, where the dataset is divided into k subsets (or folds), with one subset held out for testing, whereas the remaining folds are used for training. This process is repeated k times, each time using a different subset for testing. The 10‐fold method, for instance, is widely used due to its balanced trade‐off between bias and variance. The LOO approach is a variation of cross‐validation, where each observation is individually used as a test set and is especially useful for very small sample sizes. Furthermore, the jackknife method is similar to LOO but is mainly aimed at assessing the statistical variability of the models. In this context, the absence of cross‐validation in eight studies (34.8%) can compromise the robustness of results, as it prevents the detection of potential overfitting, where the model fits the training data too closely and performs poorly on unseen data [80, 81, 82, 83].

Spectroscopic analyses revealed distinct biochemical signatures capable of discriminating samples from dengue‐infected patients, healthy controls and other infectious diseases. Early infection stages were characterized by alterations in spectral regions associated with cytokine activity, lectins, aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine and tryptophan), DNA and α‐ and β‐protein structures, consistent with reports of increased plasma cell‐free DNA due to apoptosis and cell lysis [43, 84]. Additional spectral features were linked to immune response components, including IgM and IgG production, and to viral proteins such as NS1. Among the altered bands, features corresponding to N‐acetylglucosamine, deoxyribose ring bending, amide III structures and ceramide suggested specific biochemical changes related to viral infection and replication. This compound, found in the dengue virus envelope protein E, is associated with viral attachment and host cell entry, given its two N‐linked glycosylation sites (Asn‐67 and Asn‐153), with Asn‐67 being dengue‐specific and Asn‐153 conserved across flaviviruses [85, 86, 87].

It is important to note that the assignment of these spectral bands to specific antibodies or viral proteins remains putative, as spectroscopic techniques primarily reflect general molecular structures and functional groups rather than individual biomolecules. Consequently, some of these signals may represent immunoglobulins or immune‐related molecules more broadly, rather than being exclusive to dengue infection. To validate these findings, further studies are needed, including additional comparisons with samples from patients with other infectious diseases and the use of complementary immunological or biochemical assays, which would help determine whether these spectral features can reliably serve as disease‐specific biomarkers.

Five studies [45, 48, 56, 60, 62] employed SERS technique through a portable platform to acquire the spectral data. This technique offers several advantages, including high sensitivity, molecular specificity, rapid analysis and the potential for device miniaturization, which supports its use in point‐of‐care diagnostics. Furthermore, when combined with nanotechnology, SERS enables the development of biosensors for highly sensitive and specific molecular detection in complex biological and environmental samples [22, 23, 88, 89, 90, 91]. In this context, Yang et al. [92] developed a label‐free diagnostic platform combining SERS with DL for rapid and quantitative detection of respiratory virus coinfections. The DL model developed achieved high accuracy (98.6%) in virus identification and precise concentration prediction, completing the detection process of 11 viruses in just 15 min [92]. However, the real‐world applicability and scalability of such approaches still require further assessment, as both SERS and DL involve higher operational costs due to the need for specialized nanomaterials and substrates, and because of the intensive computational and data processing demands of DL approaches.

The studies included in this review demonstrated that both attenuated total reflectance ATR–FTIR and, most notably, Raman spectroscopy, when combined with chemometric methods, are capable of detecting specific biochemical changes associated with viral infections such as dengue, allowing for the differentiation between samples from infected dengue patients, other infectious diseases and healthy controls with high accuracy. These techniques have been applied to the analysis of biological samples such as blood, saliva, urine and tissues, enabling the identification of biochemical variations associated with cancer, infections, neurodegenerative conditions and metabolic disorders [31, 89, 93, 94]. Our recent work showed the application of Fourier transform MIR spectrophotometry (MIR–FTIR) spectroscopy combined with chemometric methods, such as PCA in the preprocessing stage and PLS‐DA, for the analysis of clinical samples from individuals with infectious diseases such as leprosy, as well as metabolic conditions such as diabetes and dyslipidaemia [93, 95]. All initial studies reported high diagnostic accuracy, supporting the feasibility of this approach for distinguishing between condition and non‐condition states. Additionally, this methodology has shown potential for monitoring therapeutic response, as observed in the leprosy study by Novack et al. [95], where spectral variations reflected changes related to treatment progression.

The findings of this work align with studies conducted on other viral diseases such as COVID‐19. In particular, Cobre et al. [96] reported in a meta‐analysis an overall sensitivity of 0.912 and specificity of 0.886 for COVID‐19 diagnosis using FTIR, with an AUC of 0.97, which values are comparable to those obtained for dengue in the present review (sensitivity and specificity of 0.93; AUC of 0.9722). Despite the similarities in diagnostic performance, important methodological differences were observed. Although COVID‐19 studies included a broader range of biological matrices, and more detailed participant characterization, several arboviruses’ studies included in the review lacked such methodological rigour, limiting reproducibility and bias assessment.

In this systematic review, key limitations were identified in the included studies. Detailed participant characterization and consistent model information were lacking in several arbovirus studies. Furthermore, most of these studies can be considered proof‐of‐concept investigations, often involving a relatively small number of patients, which increases the risk of overfitting, particularly in models using multiple spectra per individual. Although cross‐validation techniques were applied in most of the studies, the presence of repeated spectra from the same patient without proper handling can artificially inflate model performance, potentially compromising the generalizability of the findings.

The overall assessment of risk of bias using the PROBAST‐AI tool revealed a high risk across most domains for both model development and validation (apparent, internal and external validation). This classification primarily reflects the small and homogeneous patient populations, insufficient methodological transparency and the lack of external validation or representative and adequate sample size, which undermine the robustness and transferability of the models to broader clinical settings. However, for Domain 2 (Predictors), most studies were judged as low risk of bias, as predictors were clearly defined and preprocessed in a similar way across all participants. The spectral data obtained from ATR–FTIR and Raman techniques represent objective and reproducible measurements, reducing the likelihood of bias in predictor assessment. Moreover, the predictors acquisition after model development should not depend on knowledge of the outcome, which further minimizes potential bias, indicating that the models could, in principle, be applied prospectively.

To minimize risks such as overfitting and data leakage, future studies should consider employing nested cross‐validation, which separates hyperparameter tuning from model evaluation and provides a more unbiased estimate of performance. Data leakage, which occurs when information from the test set inadvertently influences preprocessing or model training steps (e.g., normalization, feature selection or dimensionality reduction), can lead to overly optimistic performance estimates and compromise model reliability. To prevent this, all preprocessing and feature selection procedures should be applied exclusively to the training data, whereas the test set must remain completely independent until the final evaluation of the developed model.

Moreover, ensuring a clear separation between training and testing datasets throughout all analytical steps and adopting systematic data splitting strategies, such as stratified sampling when dealing with imbalanced classes, can further enhance the robustness of model validation. These methodological precautions should be complemented by transparent and detailed reporting of data partitioning, preprocessing workflows, feature selection steps and performance metrics, in accordance with current AI reporting standards such as TRIPOD‐AI and PROBAST‐AI [35, 97].

The methodological variations identified across studies, along with poor‐quality reporting, underscore the need for further research involving larger sample sizes, more rigorous validation strategies and comprehensive external evaluations to confirm the applicability of these models. Additionally, standardizing reporting frameworks and incorporating multi‐centre datasets could strengthen reproducibility and generalizability, ultimately facilitating the translation of these predictive models.

Conclusions

On the basis of our findings, the application of ATR–FTIR and Raman spectroscopy combined with chemometric models (e.g., PLS‐DA and PCA‐LDA algorithms) represents a promising approach for the detection of arboviral infections. These techniques offer several advantages, including low operational cost, rapid and non‐destructive analysis, minimal sample preparation and potential for point‐of‐care implementation. Nevertheless, most studies to date involved small patient cohorts, which may increase the risk of overfitting and limit generalizability. Therefore, further research with larger and more diverse patient populations, standardized protocols, rigorous cross‐validation and multicentre external validation is essential to confirm the reliability, reproducibility and broader clinical applicability of these spectroscopic‐chemometric frameworks.
