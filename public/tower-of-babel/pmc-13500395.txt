Spatial epidemiology and socio-economics of bovine brucellosis in selected districts of the Northern Province of Sri Lanka
Authors: Yoshiharu SUGINO, Sivagurunathan VASEEHARAN, Kasthuri Arachchige Chandrika Hemali Abeyratne KOTHALAWALA, Madalagama Appuhamilage Roshan PRIYANTHA, Palika FERNANDO, Shyamali DE ALWIS, Renuka GOWRITHILAGAN, Kohei MAKITA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted to characterize the spatial epidemiology and socio-economics of bovine brucellosis on dairy farms in the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts of Northern Province, Sri Lanka. A cross-sectional study involving bulk milk sampling and interviews using a structured questionnaire was conducted at 214 dairy farms. Milk samples were tested for Brucella antibodies using an indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Global spatial clustering statistics and SaTScan were also performed. The association between milk sero-positivity and socio-economic variables was evaluated using univariable analysis. Multi-variable spatial risk factor analysis was conducted using the integrated nested Laplace approximation selecting principal components (PCs) of land use and socio-economic and ecological variables with P<0.2 in the univariable analyses as explanatory variables. The crude and true herd-level prevalence was estimated at 12.1% (26/214; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 8.4–17.2%), and 11.2% (95% CI: 7.3% to 16.3%), respectively. The Cuzick and Edward’s k-nearest neighbor test found significant clustering at 8, 10, and 11 nearest neighbors; however, no local cluster was suggested by SaTScan. In the final multivariable model, the PC representing agricultural land as positive loading and forest land as negative was positively associated with brucellosis sero-positivity, whereas barn cleaning was negatively associated, suggesting good farm hygiene is important for preventing bovine brucellosis.Keywords: bovine brucellosis, hygiene practices, integrated nested Laplace approximations, Northern Province of Sri Lanka, spatial analysis

INTRODUCTION

Brucellosis is one of the most widely prevalent neglected zoonotic diseases in the world, including in high-income countries [18, 37]. Brucellosis is caused by gram-negative bacteria of the genus Brucella, and the disease adversely impacts both human health and livestock productivity. A variety of brucellosis transmission routes in livestock are known, including contact with abortion products, vaginal secretions and/or semen, milk feeding, and vertical transmission [46]. Brucellosis leads to significant losses in animal production, manifesting as spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, infertility, reduced milk production, and longer calving intervals [23, 46].

In humans, brucellosis is transmitted via the consumption of unpasteurized milk and other dairy products, inhalation of contaminated aerosols, and contact with infected animals and contaminated materials [45]. Bovine brucellosis has been eliminated in several developed countries such as Australia, New Zealand, and Japan through long-term control program [49]. However, many developing countries lack active surveillance systems for brucellosis, which complicates the accurate assessment of disease prevalence [47, 48]. In Sri Lanka, the consumption of livestock products has surged over the past two decades, driven by increased per capita income, urbanization, and socio-cultural shifts [12]. Agricultural activities, including livestock production, account for 6.9% of the gross domestic product [6]. Brucellosis was first reported in Sri Lanka in 1956. Since then, the disease has remained a persistent problem, although its prevalence varies across herds, regions, and locations [24]. The disease predominantly affects the dry zone of Sri Lanka, particularly the Eastern, North Central, and Northern Provinces [10], and annual economic losses total approximately 141 million Sri Lankan Rupees [16].

Veterinary services in Sri Lanka regularly visit milk collection centers to test bulk milk for brucellosis using the milk ring test [11, personal communications]. However, due to the limited number of veterinarians, ensuring that all milk collection centers are regularly visited can be challenging. As an alternative to bulk milk testing, veterinarians rely on farm owner reports of spontaneous abortions. Animals on farms with a history of spontaneous abortions undergo serological testing for anti-Brucella antibodies using the Rose Bengal test, and positive results are confirmed using the complement fixation test. In Sri Lanka, there is no compensation scheme for culling infected animals with brucellosis. In addition, due to religious slaughter restrictions, brucellosis–sero-positive cattle are generally not culled. Instead, with farmer consent, veterinary authorities vaccinate all female cattle, excepting calves under 6 months of age and pregnant animals, at affected farms using the live vaccine of the S-19 strain. In 2021, a total of 3,298 heads were vaccinated [10].

Previous studies in the Ampara District, Eastern Province, Sri Lanka, identified various key socio-economic determinants such as Muslim religious faith, and risk factors including the use of grazing, introduction of new animals into herds, and a history of spontaneous abortions for brucellosis [25]. The findings of previous studies have highlighted the necessity of integrating socio-economic and behavioral considerations into brucellosis prevention and control strategies [1, 25, 38].

The Northern Province is located in the northernmost part of Sri Lanka, within the dry zone, and brucellosis is considered endemic in the area [25]. Most of the population in each district of the Northern Province is Tamil and predominantly Hindu [7]. Cattle hold religious significance in the area, and dairy farming is an integral part of the local culture, making this region one of the leading livestock production areas of Sri Lanka. The government actively promotes dairy development in the province in order to increase milk production self-sufficiency [30]. However, due to post–civil war changes, such as increased poverty, the socio-economic landscape of the area has precluded collection of up-to-date epidemiologic data on brucellosis in the Northern Province [26].

In this context, the present study attempted to elucidate the spatial epidemiologic characteristics of brucellosis at the farm level in selected districts of the Northern Province of Sri Lanka [15, 19]. We also attempted to identify the risk factors of brucellosis on dairy farms in order to generate evidence useful in the planning of disease control strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area

This study was conducted in the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts of the Northern Province of Sri Lanka (Fig. 1). Geographically, the Northern Province is located at around 9°20′ N latitude and 80°20′-80°50′ E longitude, and the distance to India is approximately 22 miles (35 km). The province is administratively divided into five districts (Jaffna, Mullaitivu, Kilinochchi, Mannar, and Vavuniya) and covers a total area of 8,848.58 km2.

Map of the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts showing the locations of studied farms.

The Northern Province is located as within the dry zone of Sri Lanka and thus vulnerable to the effects of climate change, such as flooding and cyclones. Over 30 years of intense internal conflict has significantly impacted the region and hindered social and economic progress [35]. The majority of the population in the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts of the Northern Province is of Tamil ethnicity.

The prevalence of brucellosis in the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts of the Northern Province is reportedly high [1, 25, 38]. Three primary livestock management systems are employed in these districts: (i) intensive (characterized by limited grazing and greater reliance on stall feeding and purchased feed), (ii) semi-intensive (characterized by herds conducting both grazing and stall feeding), and (iii) extensive (characterized by a significant dependence on grazing) [26]. Throughout Sri Lanka, animal grazing in rice fields after the rice harvest is a part of the traditional scenery during the dry season. In the rainy seasons, animals are grazed in communal lands where grass becomes abundant, according to the internal reports of a development project in the Northern Province by the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA). The human population in the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts is 148,448 and 139,744, respectively in 2022 [42], whereas the reported cattle population in each district is 36,606 and 56,113, respectively in 2019 [12].

The jurisdictional range of the governmental veterinary offices in the Northern Province of Sri Lanka is established by each division. These offices offer veterinary services and engage in breeding, improvement, and dissemination activities. In the present study, we examined four divisions in the Kilinochchi District (Karachchi, Poonakaly, Pachchipalai, and Kandawalai) and six divisions in the Mullaitivu District (Manthai East, Maritimepattu, Oddusuddan, Puthukkudiyiruppu, Thunukkai, and Welioa).

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was selected for dairy farms in the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts. Farm sample size was calculated using Equation 1 in order to estimate the farm-level prevalence of brucellosis [40]:

Equation 1, where Pexp represents the expected prevalence in the herd, d represents the desired absolute precision, and n indicates the required sample size. Based on a previous study, an expected herd prevalence of 9.6% at a 95% confidence level (CI) and 5% precision was considered [43]. The farm sample size was thus calculated as 133. The calculated design effect and expected response rate were 1.5 and 95%, respectively, and the final sample size was set at 214.

A two-stage random sampling method was applied, with district division as the first stage and management type (i.e., intensive, semi-intensive, or extensive) as the second stage. The number of farms to be sampled was proportionally allocated to the divisions according to the number of registered farms. Within each division, the number of farms was again proportionally allocated to the operation type according to the number of farms undertaking the respective management type.

Information regarding the number of registered farms in the study areas and their management styles was acquired from the statistical data of the Department of Animal Production and Health (DAPH) of Sri Lanka [12, 31]. The total number of registered farms in the study areas was 16,337, for a sampling fraction of 1.31%.

The study farms were randomly selected according to allocated sample sizes from lists of registered farms at veterinary offices. To minimize false positives, farms at which cattle had been vaccinated with the S19 vaccine were excluded, given that the proportion of vaccinated farms was low (personal communications with veterinary officers). Additionally, farms with poor access conditions, such as challenging road infrastructure, were also excluded based on the practicality of the study.

The study was conducted in accordance with an agreement between the DAPH, Sri Lanka, and Rakuno Gakuen University. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Rakuno Gakuen University (approval no. 21-3, 2021). Informed consent for inclusion was obtained from all participating farms.

Field survey

A field survey involving bulk milk sampling, recording of geographic locations using a mobile phone, and interviews using a structured questionnaire was conducted in November and December 2021. The questionnaire included basic information regarding the owner of the farm, dairy hygiene management practices, and animal health–related information focused particularly on brucellosis (Table 1). The questionnaire was drafted in English, and a pilot study was conducted involving four Sinhala- and Tamil-speaking farmers in the target area. The pilot study was conducted by enumerators fluent in English, Sinhala, and Tamil using the respective languages according to each farmer’s ethnicity. The questionnaire was modified as necessary so that the questions could be easily understood by the farmers.

Questionnaire contents

Prior to data collection, the enumerators received training on the procedures for collecting milk samples and conducting interviews based on the questionnaire. The survey involved one or two visits to each selected farm for milk sampling and completion of the questionnaire. At each farm, the owner or person in charge of dairy farming was interviewed. After collection, the data were quality-checked, and any discrepancies or errors were identified and corrected as necessary.

Regarding milk sampling, approximately 5 mL of milk per farm was collected aseptically from bulk milk containers using sterile tubes. The collected samples were subsequently labeled and transported to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions.

Laboratory diagnosis

An indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (i-ELISA) was used to detect anti-Brucella antibodies in bulk milk samples using a SVANOVIR Brucella-Ab-i-ELISA kit (Svanova Biotech, Uppsala, Sweden) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Assays were carried out at the Veterinary Research Institute in Kandy, Sri Lanka. To ensure accuracy, all samples were tested in duplicate, and the average optical density (OD) value was used; in cases involving ambiguous results, the assay was repeated. The OD readings were then converted to ELISA units using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Office 365, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). The OD values of both the control and test samples were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate photometer. According to the kit protocol, a cutoff OD of 1.0 was adopted for the identification of a positive reaction. Additionally, as recommended by the manufacturer, the percent positivity value was calculated by dividing the OD of the sample or negative control by the OD of the positive control, then multiplying the result by 100. For milk samples, an OD cutoff of 7.0 was used (i.e., OD values ≥7.0 were considered indicative of a positive result).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive epidemiology: Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted for the farm data. Knowledge and hygiene practices scores were calculated by summing up the correct or positive responses in the questionnaire. The true prevalence of brucellosis at the farm level was estimated based on the sensitivity and specificity of the i-ELISA (98.6% and 98.7%, respectively [39]) using Equation 2 [36]:

Equation 2,where Se and Sp represent the assay sensitivity and specificity, respectively, and crude prevalence is the observed proportion of herds positive for brucellosis. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the true prevalence was calculated by adjusting the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of the beta distribution of the crude prevalence using Equation 2.

A map showing the spatial distributions of positive and negative herds was generated using ArcGIS software (ESRI Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Land use data were sourced from the Sri Lanka Land Use Policy and Planning Department. Across Mullaitivu and Kilinochchi Districts combined, forest land accounted for the largest proportion of land use (53.2%), followed by agricultural land (19.1%), built-up land (10.5%), and water bodies (10.0%), while the remaining categories each accounted for less than 5% of the total area. The distances from each farm to the nearest major road, nearest forest, and nearest water source were calculated using ArcGIS.

Spatial clustering: The Cuzick and Edward’s k-nearest neighbor global spatial statistic test was performed to determine whether geographic clustering occurred in the study areas [8]. The Cuzick and Edward’s k-nearest neighbor test calculates the test statistic Tkusing Equation 3:

Equation 3, where dik represents the number of cases among the k-nearest neighbor farms of the ith farm, and δi serves as an identifier of brucellosis sero-positivity in milk (1 for positive, and 0 for negative). To evaluate global clustering, a Monte Carlo simulation, in which a value of 0 or 1 was assigned for the identifier δi while the number of positive results remained the same, was performed to calculate the p-value as the proportion of the simulation results that produced a value greater than or equal to Tk. The Monte Carlo simulation was performed for k values between 1 and 25 using the ceTk function of the nnspat package in R statistical software [5].

To assess global clustering in terms of the distance between farms, Ripley’s K-function was determined [44]. At an intensity of λ points per unit area, the K-function at distance s was defined as λK(s), the expected number of events within a distance s of an arbitrary chosen event, as described by Equation 4:

Equation 4,where R equals the area of a region of interest, dij the distance between the ith and jth farms in R, and Is(dij) represents an indicator function that equals 1 if dij≤s and 0 otherwise. The K(s) value was calculated using the Ripley function in R in the REAT package [5] for both sero-positive and sero-negative farms for anti-Brucella antibodies in bulk milk. As the maximum value of K(s) differed between sero-positive and sero-negative farms, each K(s) value was divided by the respective maximum value to generate a cumulative proportion curve. At each s value, the difference between the cumulative proportion of K(s) was calculated to evaluate the pattern of global clustering.

Local special clustering was assessed using a spatial scan statistic with SaTScan software, version 10.1.2 [28]. A spatial Bernoulli model was selected with a maximum cluster size equal to 25% of the total population and a significance level of 0.05. SaTScan calculates the relative risk (RR) of a cluster, as shown in Equation 5:

Equation 5,where c represents the number of observed cases within the cluster, C represents the total number of cases in the dataset, and E[c] represents the expected number of cases within the cluster. Therefore, the RR is the estimated risk within the cluster divided by the estimated risk outside of the cluster.

Socio-economic and spatial risk factor analysis: To characterize the ecological features of the studied farms, principal component (PC) analysis was conducted using the mutually exclusive proportion variables of eight land use types (built-up area, agricultural land, forest land, wetland, water bodies, rocky area, sandy area, and barren lands) within a 1-km radius of each farm. A 1-km radius was selected because (1) some farms are neighbors but it allows farm-specific ecological characteristics (only 2.3% of the between-farm distances was less than 1 km) and (2) grazing areas were generally within 1 km from the farm according to the informal interview with farmers and local veterinary officers. PCs were used instead of land use categories where the farms were located, because cattle grazing was common in the study areas and cattle were exposed to the surrounding environment. The R function prcomp was employed to derive the principal component (PC) scores and loadings. Buffer data from within the 1-km radius from each farm were generated, and the area associated with each of the eight land use types was calculated using ArcGIS.

For the geographical data, a univariable analysis of sero-positivity was performed using the distances from each farm to the nearest major road, nearest forest, and nearest water source, in addition to PC scores. The chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used for binary and categorical data. For non-spatial univariable analysis of socio-economic variables collected using the questionnaire, values for count variables and ordinal data on a 5-point Likert scale were compared between sero-positive and negative farms using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. For continuous variables, normality was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For continuous variables following a normal distribution, t-tests were performed, and for those not following a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used. The proportions of small- (1−10 cattle), medium-(11–20), and large-scale (21=<) farms were compared between sero-positive and sero-negative farms using Fisher’s exact test.

Multivariable socio-economic and spatial risk factor analysis of brucellosis milk sero-positivity was conducted using the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) with binomial errors, taking spatial autocorrelation into account [27, 33]. An INLA model is expressed as latent Gaussian Markov random fields, and for a multivariable binomial model, hyperparameters of Gaussian distributions (means and standard deviations) of fixed- and random-effects variables at logit scale are estimated [27]. In the INLA multivariable binomial model, milk i-ELISA test results were selected as an outcome variable, and ecological variables, such as the shortest distance from each farm to the main road and from the farm to a water source, PC scores and socio-economic variables with a p-value less than 0.2 in the non-spatial univariable analyses were selected as fixed-effects explanatory variables. Spatial autocorrelation was included as a random effect using a spatial field stochastic differential equation (SPDE) model. Continuous fixed-effect variables were standardized by dividing by their standard deviations. Collinearity between variables was assessed based on the variance inflated factor (VIF), and no pairs exhibited a VIF >2. The INLA was performed using the R package “INLA” [33], with a mesh created based on farm location data using the inla.mesh.2d function. The SPDE model of the spatial field was defined using this mesh and the Matérn covariance function, and smoothing parameters were estimated using the inla.spde2.matern function. Based on the observation of between-farm distances, the minimum and maximum sizes of edges were set at 20 m and 100 m, respectively. Models were selected based on the widely applicable information criterion (WAIC). All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software, version 4.2.1 [32].

RESULTS

Descriptive epidemiology

Regarding demographic characteristics, 62.1% (133/214) of the respondents (the owner or person in charge of the dairy farm) were male, 37.4% (80/214) were female, and one was uncategorized. The mean, median, inter-quartile range and range of family size were 4.1, 4, 3–5, and 1–10 individuals, respectively. Tamil ethnicity was predominant (93.5%, 200/214), with the remainder Sinhalese (6.5%, 14/214). The mean, median, inter-quartile range and range of age of respondents was 46.4 years, 46, 40–54 and 18–73, respectively. The age composition was 21–30 years 7.5%, 31–40 years 15.9%, 41–50 years 40.2%, 51–60 years 25.2%, 61–70 years 9.3%, and 71–80 years 1.9%. With regard to religious practice, the vast majority of respondents were Hindu (92.0%, 197), followed by Buddhists (6.5%, 14). In terms of level of education, the majority of farmers had attained a primary (12.6%), junior secondary (35.1%), or senior secondary (41.6%) level education. Only one farmer was illiterate.

The mean, median, inter-quartile range, and range of herd size were 20.4, 12, 12–18, and 3–144, respectively. The proportion of farms with less than 30 cattle was 90.2% (193/214 farms). In terms of dairy management type, 12 of the 214 farms were categorized as intensive (5.6%), 84 as semi-intensive (39.3%), and 118 as extensive (55.2%). The mean numbers of cattle according to the management type were 38.9, 18.5 and 19.8 for intensive, semi-intensive, and extensive farms, respectively. The cattle herd size was significantly larger in intensive farms than the other farms (mean 19.3 in semi-intensive and extensive farms, P=0.015). The majority (94.4%, 202 farms) conducted free grazing. Cattle were kept in the backyard of the home on 120 farms (56.1%) and otherwise separate from the home. Goats were kept at 63 farms (29.4%). Approximately half of the farmers (102, 47.7%) had a work experience exceeding 10 years. The proportion of the farms with dairy as the primary income was 36.9% (79 farms). A small minority of farmers were aware of brucellosis (5.1%) and contagious spontaneous abortion (8.4%) on their farms.

Among the 214 participants who completed the questionnaire, 26 samples of bulk milk were tested positive, and the crude prevalence of brucellosis at the farm level was 12.1% (95% CI: 8.4–17.2%). The true prevalence was calculated to be 11.2% (95% CI: 7.3–16.3%). The Kandawalai veterinary range had the highest true prevalence, at 27.4% (95% CI: 11.1–47.4%). By contrast, the Pachchipalli and Manthai East veterinary ranges had the lowest herd-level prevalence, at 0% (95% CI: 0–32.0% and 0%-22.1%, respectively; Table 2).

Farms that had not introduced new cows from the outside over the past 5 years tended to have a higher farm-level prevalence of brucellosis (11.7%) than those that did introduce new cows (2.9%, P=0.088). The introduced cows were primarily obtained from other farmers (91.4%, data not shown in Table 5), and these cows were typically introduced after a health examination and quarantine period. Additionally, no significant associations were observed between brucellosis sero-positivity in milk and foot and mouth disease vaccination or encounters between cattle and small ruminants during grazing. A discernible trend indicated the potential benefit of following veterinary advice, as indicated by the answers to Likert-scale questions. Farms that were more open to veterinary guidance tended to have a lower prevalence of brucellosis, although this association was not statistically significant (P=0.103). Farmers at both brucellosis-positive and -negative farms in the area tended to be confident in their own knowledge and responses to livestock and animal health management issues, with higher scores observed among farmers at brucellosis-positive farms; however, this association was not statistically significant (P=0.098).

No significant associations were observed between brucellosis sero-positivity in milk and a history of breeding disorders, including abortion (P=0.645), stillbirth (P=0.350), or retained placenta (P=0.775). A total of 13.1% of the respondents (28/214) were familiar with either the term “brucellosis” (11 farms) or “contagious abortion” (17 farms). Regarding knowledge of transmission routes in cattle, 10.3% of respondents (22/214) recognized aborted fetuses as a potential source of transmission, whereas 8.9% (19/214) identified the placenta from live births as a potential transmission route. Regarding zoonotic characteristics, 11.2% of respondents (24/214) knew that brucellosis can be transmitted from cattle to humans. Of these respondents, 9.8% (21/214) knew that brucellosis can be transmitted to humans through the consumption of raw (non-boiled) milk. Only 4.2% of respondents (9/214) had knowledge of other transmission routes, such as handling the results of an abortion. However, the knowledge score did not differ between farmers at brucellosis–sero-positive farms (mean=1.05) versus brucellosis–sero-negative farms (mean=1.17, P=0.795). Furthermore, there was no significant association between brucellosis sero-positivity in milk and having received animal health training (χ2=0.778, df=1, P=0.378).

Regarding cleaning, basic hygiene practices were common, as 85.0% of all farms (182/214) engaged in removal of feces or changing of bedding, and 43.0% of all farms (92/214) cleaned with water. However, the use of disinfectants was rare (1.9%, 4/214 farms). No cleaning practices were significantly associated with brucellosis sero-positivity in milk; however, the total score for cleaning habits was significantly higher among sero-negative farms (mean=1.47) than sero-positive farms (mean=1.19, P=0.040).

The distances to the nearest major road, forest, or water source did not differ significantly between the brucellosis–sero-positive and sero-negative farms (2,953 m vs. 2,438 m, P=0.344; 861 m vs. 686 m, P=0.529; and 625 m vs. 544 m, P=0.769, respectively). The mean PC1 and PC2 loadings had opposite signs between the milk–sero-positive (PC1: −0.388 and PC2: 0.487) and milk–sero-negative (PC1: 0.054 and PC2: −0.067) farms, respectively, but neither was significantly different (PC1: P=0.116; PC2: P=0.070). The mean PC3 loadings in milk-sero-positive (0.002) and sero-negative farms (0.015), and the mean PC4 loadings in milk-sero-positive (−0.001) and sero-negative farms (0.005) were also not significantly different, respectively (PC3: P=0.843; PC4: P=0.542).

DISCUSSION

This study elucidated the spatial epidemiologic and socio-economic characteristics of bovine brucellosis at the farm level in selected districts within the Northern Province of Sri Lanka and evaluated the risk factors associated with bovine brucellosis sero-positivity in milk at dairy farms. Focusing on the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts, which have the highest density of cattle in Sri Lanka [13], this research addressed the critical gap in understanding of brucellosis in the post–civil war context.

The true herd-level prevalence of brucellosis in the study area was 11.2%. Brucellosis milk i-ELISA has a high sensitivity and is capable of detecting one infected animal in a herd from bulk milk testing [41]. This result was consistent with the prevalence of 9.6% observed in another arid region of Sri Lanka, the Eastern Province [25]. Previous studies conducted in various countries, including Sri Lanka, identified certain management characteristics (e.g., grazing-dependent type and large herd size in an area) as significant risk factors for brucellosis infection [2, 9, 29]. In this study, the majority of dairy herds were small- (1−10 cattle, 51/214 farms, 23.8%) or medium-scale (11−20 cattle, 125/214 farms, 58.4%). Large herd size (21=<cattle, 38/214 farms, 17.8%) was not associated with brucellosis sero-positivity in milk in this region. A history of spontaneous abortion in cattle is a very common indicator of brucellosis [29, 38]; spontaneous abortions are caused by infection with Brucella spp., and these abortions can also serve as a source of new infection in other animals in the herd [29]. In the present study, however, spontaneous abortion was not identified as a statistically significant factor. With regard to grazing, which is a common practice in the study region regardless of herd size, all intensive farms that did not practice grazing were sero-negative for brucellosis. This suggests that grazing may be actually a risk factor for brucellosis transmission, but spontaneous abortions in semi-intensive and extensive herds might be overlooked during grazing. Moreover, it is known that several pathogens cause abortion in cattle such as Leptospira spp. [4] and Neospora caninum [21], and such abortions might mask the statistical association with brucellosis.

Previous studies have shown that the risk of brucellosis increases when cattle are introduced from outside markets or farms [9]. However, in the present study, bought-in cattle was not a significant factor for brucellosis milk sero-positivity in the univariable analysis. This may suggest that the disease may be transmitted internally within the community due to the closed environment and conditions specific to the region, where the majority of the population is Tamil, and the area has been closed off due to the past civil war and other cultural influences. These observations indicate that an intra-regional control strategy based on understanding the current situation of the region is important for controlling the spread of brucellosis, rather than focusing solely on the movement of cattle from the outside.

Global spatial statistical analyses revealed brucellosis disease clustering. Bovine brucellosis has spread widely throughout northern Sri Lanka, and in each locality, clustering of infected herds was observed, with global aggregation at a k of 8 to 11 nearest neighbor farms and disease cluster 1 had a size of 0.45 km radius with a P-value of 0.061. While the distribution of sero-positive farms was not completely random, these farms do not form clear statistically significant local clusters. The identified global aggregation suggested clustering at the community level. The results of the INLA analysis suggested that the distribution of brucellosis–sero-positive farms was characterized by higher agricultural land use, as indicated by PC2. In northern Sri Lanka, brucellosis transmission is more likely to occur on agricultural land particularly after rice harvest rather than in forested areas, as our field observations indicate that agricultural land is commonly used for communal grazing to feed cattle with rice straw. In the rainy seasons, animals are grazed in communal lands in the study areas. The exact geographical locations of such communal lands were not collected in this study, but considering the abundance of grass in the rainy seasons, they may be located within or nearby agricultural lands. The significant negative relationships between brucellosis milk-sero-positivity and goat raising, and between goat raising and PC1, as well as the Fig. 6, suggested that cattle farms in remote villages who rely on goat farming as an additional economic strategy tended to be free from brucellosis. In such remote areas, chance of contact between cattle through communal grazing may be limited.

The INLA final model indicated the importance of hygiene practices. Univariable analyses revealed that no single hygiene practice can help prevent brucellosis; however, the aggregated score indicated that cleaning practices exert a significant preventive effect on the transmission of bovine brucellosis at the herd level.

Brucellosis management practices in developed countries include an effective combination of vaccination and test-and-slaughter strategies [34]. Although surveillance leading to the culling of sero-positive cattle may eradicate the disease, social and religious constraints in the Northern Province of Sri Lanka, where cattle hold significant religious importance among the Hindu majority, may render such strategies unacceptable [3]. In similar religious cultures, such as India, alternative approaches emphasizing vaccination and enhanced biosecurity measures (such as visitor restrictions) at the farm level have been suggested as effective [14]. Given the current veterinary service capacity, interventions that minimize disease transmission and encourage farmer participation appear useful. Although the level of knowledge regarding brucellosis was not associated with herd-level brucellosis positivity in the present study, development of a comprehensive animal health education package, including information regarding the disease, risk factors, and herd management, could facilitate a change in the perception of management and communication between farmers and veterinary services for dealing with signs of brucellosis [17, 20]. Strengthening veterinarian-farmer relationships through education and engagement is essential for the success of future surveillance and vaccination efforts.

Previous research has established a correlation between poverty rates and the incidence of brucellosis [25], suggesting that the higher poverty rates in the Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu Districts, which have been exacerbated by the civil war, may contribute to the neglect of brucellosis risks in this region. With 15.0% of households in Kilinochchi and 11.2% households in Mullaitivu living in poverty, compared with a 2016 national average of 3.1% [13], the economic challenges faced by these communities underscore the need for disease control strategies that are both effective and culturally sensitive. In this context, the current lack of adequate veterinary services further emphasizes the desperate need for strengthening existing surveillance and vaccination programs. Addressing these challenges will require a comprehensive approach that integrates effective disease management with improving socio-economic conditions to enhance both the understanding and control of brucellosis.

Although the findings of the present study shed light on the prevalence and risk factors of bovine brucellosis in northern Sri Lanka, the following limitations may affect the generalizability of the findings. First, some factors associated with the prevalence of brucellosis may not have been well captured, for example, abortions which were not noticed, or contents of guidance by veterinary officers. Second, our focus on the farm-level prevalence based on testing of bulk milk samples did not account for individual animal-level data pertaining to cattle. Previous studies have indicated that the prevalence of brucellosis increases with animal age, which may also be related to the risk of infection of farm owners and other small ruminants [22]. For a more comprehensive understanding of brucellosis and to facilitate the development of more effective management strategies for the disease, individual animal-level data and human diagnostic results are needed [22]. Comparisons of behaviors and infection status in humans of different ethnic groups and regions may increase our understanding of human brucellosis. Our present approach, which focused on land use for assessing grazing-related infection risks, could be adapted for examining other environments, and may provide a versatile framework for future studies [25]. Understanding these limitations is vital for correctly interpreting the findings of the present study and for guiding future research directions. Our results emphasize the need for a comprehensive approach to increase our understanding of brucellosis in order to develop effective control measures.

In conclusion, bovine brucellosis in the Northern Province of Sri Lanka was characterized by a wide disease distribution throughout the study area. The spatial distribution of brucellosis sero-positive farms is not completely random, but significant local disease clusters were not found. Bovine brucellosis transmission may be occurring in agricultural lands through grazing. Intensive farming, zero-grazing, and implementation of cleaning practices could aid in the prevention of bovine brucellosis.
