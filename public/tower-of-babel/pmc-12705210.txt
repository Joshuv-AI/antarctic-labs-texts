Quebec’s Bill 2: a health system wrecking ball
Authors: Shannon Charlebois, Ken Flegel, Matthew B Stanbrook
Published: 2025

During his 2018 election campaign, recognizing health care access as a top priority for his electorate, Quebec Premier François Legault promised that all residents of Quebec would be rostered with a family doctor.1 Now facing another provincial election but having failed to deliver on the promise, the Quebec government has not invested in new doctors, nurses, staff, or systems; rather, it has rushed through a health reform bill that will do nothing except shift responsibility for system failure to physicians. Bill 2, an Act purported to establish collective responsibility for improving and ensuring continuity of access to medical services, instead blames the province’s already burned-out physicians for not working hard enough and mandates that they work harder, yet offers them no additional support or funding. CMAJ editors stand unequivocally in support of our colleagues in Quebec in challenging this law. We call on the Quebec government to engage in sober reconsideration of the bill in collaboration with physicians, not in opposition to them.

Currently, nearly 2 million Quebec residents are not registered with a primary care provider, despite the province having one of the highest ratios of physicians per capita.2 This speaks to a much larger problem within the health system. For example, low hospital capacity, staffing shortages beyond physicians, and limited operating room time for surgeons contribute to problems that see patients and procedures being diverted to private clinics, paid for by the public purse.3–5

The government could have worked on innovative primary care solutions, such as recruitment, training, and reform, to increase the total primary care workforce, using team-based approaches. It could have sought to address other problems that are crippling the health care system. Instead, along with setting an arbitrary and unrealistic target that all patients presenting to an emergency department be seen by a physician within 90 minutes, it has demanded that existing primary care physicians merely take on additional patients and workload, with implications for earnings. Capitation is applied, and if a practice does not register the number of patients set by the government, the entire physician group would see their pay cut, regardless of individual performance.5

The legislation also forces the redistribution of existing resources within the system by mandating that primary care physicians be remunerated based on a patient’s bureaucratically assessed degree of vulnerability. Incentivizing care delivery to, and therefore prioritizing, sicker and more complex patients could be considered wise strategy within a whole system that is adequately resourced. However, without complementary levers, the bill’s provisions will serve to reduce family physicians’ ability to deliver necessary and effective preventive care by disincentivizing engagement with patients who are healthier, which includes newborns and children. Consequently, optimization of longer-term health for Quebec’s population will be sacrificed in pursuit of short-term political goals.

Bill 2 was passed in the early hours of Oct. 25, 2025, after being forced through the National Assembly using an exceptional parliamentary procedure to limit the time for debate and compel its rapid adoption, suggesting that the government knew it would never withstand thoughtful examination. Most egregiously of all, the new law forces physicians into silence by imposing fines of up to $20 000 a day if they protest it or even openly discuss alternative subsequent career plans on the basis it would interfere with future medical services in the province. The Canadian Civil Liberties Association called Oct. 25 “a dark day for fundamental freedoms in Quebec.”6 Violating physicians’ freedom to speak to the problems of the system in which they work will also stifle much-needed system innovation.

Even before taking effect, the bill has yielded potentially disastrous consequences by demoralizing already exhausted physicians. Hundreds of Quebec physicians have now applied to work in other provinces that will welcome their bilingualism and expertise; between Oct. 23 and Dec. 5 of this year, the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario received 382 applications for licensure from Quebec doctors and granted 197.7,8 Family practice clinics in Quebec are shutting down.9,10 Many physicians will seek to retire early or reinvent their careers in a private system.

In the 2025 Canadian resident match, Quebec had the highest proportion of unmatched family medicine positions of all Canadian jurisdictions, with 35.5% unfilled.11 Bill 2 will ensure that medical graduates selecting family medicine training are even more unlikely to choose Quebec, which will affect the family physician workforce in Quebec for many years.

Decades of bad health policy will not be fixed with yet more bad health policy. The Quebec government, having failed for the last 7 years to deliver on its promise of ensuring access to primary care for all Quebecers, has now enacted a poorly conceived, controlling, and intrusive policy. The people of Quebec will likely not be fooled by their politicians’ political expediency. But the legacy of the current Quebec government will be destruction of the province’s health care system if Bill 2 is not reconsidered.
