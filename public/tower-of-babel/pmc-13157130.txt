Effects of a High‐Fat Meal on the Pharmacokinetics of Olverembatinib in Patients With Chronic Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Hengbang Wang, Yun Yang, Zi Chen, Min Yu, Lixin Jiang, Lichuang Men, Hua Yan, Dajun Yang, Yifan Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOlverembatinib is approved in China to treat patients with chronic‐ or accelerated‐phase chronic myeloid leukemia (CML‐CP or CML‐AP) with T315I mutations and CML‐CP that is resistant and/or intolerant to first‐ and second‐generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors. The objective of this study was to determine the effects of a high‐fat meal on the pharmacokinetics of olverembatinib in patients with CML. Twelve participants were enrolled and received olverembatinib treatment over two periods, with a washout of 7 days between consecutive doses. Pharmacokinetics and safety were assessed in all participants. When olverembatinib was administered with a high‐fat meal, the maximum plasma concentration (C max) and area under the plasma concentration versus time curve from time 0 to infinity (AUC0–∞) of olverembatinib increased by 105.74% and 69.74%, respectively, compared to fasting conditions. The median time to maximum plasma concentration (T max) of olverembatinib was not affected when it was administered with a high‐fat meal, and the geometric mean terminal plasma half‐life (t 1/2) was comparable between high‐fat meal and preprandial conditions (26.8 vs. 31.1 h). The safety profile of olverembatinib was consistent with observations from previous studies; all treatment‐related adverse events were mild, and no serious adverse event was reported. These results indicate that consumption of a high‐fat meal 30 min before administering olverembatinib can increase its plasma exposure to some extent.

Introduction

Olverembatinib is a novel, potent, orally active, third‐generation BCR::ABL1 tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) [1, 2] that is the first and only third‐generation BCR::ABL1 TKI approved by China's National Medical Products Administration for the treatment of patients with chronic‐ or accelerated‐phase chronic myeloid leukemia (CML‐CP or CML‐AP) with T315I mutations and CML‐CP that is resistant and/or intolerant to first‐ and second‐generation TKIs [3, 4]. It is also one of the third‐generation BCR::ABL1 TKIs that has been developed around the world.

The pharmacokinetic profile of olverembatinib in patients with CML has been characterized. Olverembatinib exhibits relatively slow absorption (time to maximum concentration [T max] of approximately 4–8 h). Systemic exposure to olverembatinib increases in an approximately dose‐proportional manner from 1 to 60 mg. The mean terminal elimination half‐life (t 1/2) of olverembatinib is approximately 25 h, with minimal accumulation observed at steady state [5]. This pharmacokinetic profile is compatible with alternate‐day (QOD) dosing.

Olverembatinib and other approved BCR::ABL1 TKIs, including imatinib [6], dasatinib [7], nilotinib [8], bosutinib [9, 10], and ponatinib [11], are administered orally. Drug absorption after oral administration can be impacted by food or other factors that may alter the gastrointestinal milieu. Because olverembatinib is orally administered, it is critical to determine the impact of food on its pharmacokinetic profile to accurately inform any recommendations regarding the meal conditions for optimal clinical use of this medication. Here, we report results from a Phase 1 clinical study investigating the effect of a high‐fat meal on olverembatinib pharmacokinetics in patients with CML (Clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT03882281). Data from this study were used in the drug administration guidance related to food intake.

Other inclusion criteria comprised an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance score ≤ 2, life expectancy of ≥ 3 months, and no evidence of significant hepatic, renal, pulmonary, pancreatic, or cardiovascular disease. Eligibility criteria are tabulated (Table S1).

Participants were excluded for the following reasons: conditions affecting drug absorption; consumption of food or beverages (e.g., coffee, tea, cola/soda, or chocolate) containing xanthine (e.g., caffeine) within 48 h before the first dose; participation in any other clinical trial of an investigational product within 14 days before the first dose; coadministration with either strong CYP2C9 inducers or inhibitors or moderate and strong CYP3A4 inducers or inhibitors; previous treatment with ponatinib or olverembatinib (or drugs with similar components); resting supine blood pressure > 140 mmHg (systolic) or > 90 mmHg (diastolic); mean pulmonary arterial pressure > 25 mmHg on cardiac ultrasound; or previous autologous or allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

Study Design

This was a Phase 1, randomized, open‐label, two‐period crossover, single‐dose, single‐center study. Twelve patients with CML‐CP were randomly allocated to one of two sequences of regimens: “AB” or “BA.” Patients received a single dose of olverembatinib (30 mg) under fasting conditions (Treatment A; after an approximately 10‐h fast and at least 4 h before lunch) and after a high‐fat meal (Treatment B, 30 min after starting a high‐fat meal) in two different periods. In both Treatments A and B, each dose of olverembatinib was taken orally with approximately 250 mL of water. The study periods were separated by a washout interval of 7 days.

Drug Administration and Treatments

The study had a two‐period, two‐sequence design. The treatments were: Treatment A: a single dose of olverembatinib 30 mg (3 × 10‐mg tablets) administered in a fasting state; Treatment B: a single dose of olverembatinib 30 mg (3 × 10‐mg tablets) administered after a high‐fat meal.

A standard high‐fat meal is 800–1000 kcal, with fat providing > 50% of the caloric requirement as specified in the FDA guidance for industry Food‐Effect Bioavailability and Fed Bioequivalence Studies [12]. This includes two slices of bread (100 g), three slices of classic bacon (72 g), two slices of plain cheese (41.7 g), two boxes of unsalted butter (20 g), lettuce leaves (31 g), and one hard‐boiled egg (65 g or 55 g if shelled).

Pharmacokinetic Assessments

Blood samples for olverembatinib pharmacokinetic assessments were collected by direct venipuncture at various timepoints, including 0 h (within 1 h before dosing) and at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after olverembatinib administration in each treatment period. Blood samples were centrifuged at approximately 1500 x g and 4°C for 10 min within 30 min of blood collection, after which plasma was obtained and stored at −70°C for subsequent liquid‐chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis at Shanghai Center for Drug Metabolism and Pharmacokinetics Research (Shanghai, China).

Standard noncompartmental analysis was performed to obtain the following olverembatinib plasma pharmacokinetic parameters: area under the plasma concentration versus time curve from time 0 to infinity (AUC0–∞), AUC from time 0 to the time of the last quantifiable concentration (AUC0–t ), AUC from time 0 to 48 h (AUC0–48h), maximum plasma concentration (C max), time to maximum plasma concentration (T max), terminal half‐life (t 1/2), apparent oral plasma clearance (CL/F), apparent volume of distribution (V z/F), and mean residence time (MRTinf). Pharmacokinetic parameter values were calculated using internally validated WinNonlin software (WinNonlin 7.0, Pharsight). Below‐limit‐of‐quantitation (BLOQ) values before C max were set to 0 ng/mL and to missing at the end of the concentration curve for the pharmacokinetic analysis.

Safety Assessments

Throughout the study, all participants were assessed for safety, which included physical examinations, laboratory tests, electrocardiograms (ECGs), and frequency and severity of adverse events (AEs). The number and percentage of patients reporting treatment‐emergent and treatment‐related adverse events (TEAEs and TRAEs) were summarized for fasting conditions and after a high‐fat meal. Adverse‐event severity was classified according to National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.03.

Statistical Analysis

Multifactor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on natural log‐transformed (Ln‐transformed) pharmacokinetic parameters AUC0–∞, AUC0–t , and C max. The ANOVA included sequence, treatment, and period as fixed effects and subject nested within sequence as a random effect. The effect of food on pharmacokinetic parameters was assessed by calculating geometric mean ratios and corresponding 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–∞ in patients administered olverembatinib after a high‐fat meal compared to fasting conditions. The absence of a food effect was concluded if the 90% CIs for the ratio of geometric means fell entirely within the bioequivalence criteria of 80%–125%. T max was analyzed using a two‐tailed nonparametric test, with the significance level set at p < 0.05. All other pharmacokinetic parameters were summarized descriptively.

It is noteworthy that a dramatically lower olverembatinib plasma concentration was observed in three of the 12 patients who received olverembatinib under fasting conditions, with C max values of 0.682, 1.36, and 1.83 ng/mL, respectively. However, normal olverembatinib plasma concentrations were observed in these three patients who received olverembatinib under fed conditions (high‐fat meal), with C max values of 9.74, 12.8, and 14.6 ng/mL, respectively. Results of a sensitivity analysis performed after excluding the data obtained from these three patients showed that olverembatinib's C max increased by 23.9%, and AUC0–∞ by 19.9% under fed conditions (high‐fat meal) compared with fasting conditions (Table S2). These findings suggest that administration of olverembatinib with food, rather than under fasting conditions, results in optimal and more consistent bioavailability.

Safety and Tolerability

Olverembatinib was well tolerated. A total of seven of 12 patients (58.3%) experienced AEs when treated with olverembatinib. TEAEs reported in at least two participants were hypertriglyceridemia (2/12, 16.7%) and anemia (2/12, 16.7%), and the events were grade 1 or 2. Proportions of patients with TRAEs during fasting (4/12, 33.3%) and postprandial (5/12, 41.7%) conditions were similar. The most frequently reported TRAE (occurring in at least two participants in any treatment group) was anemia (Table 4). No deaths were reported, and no interruptions, reductions, or discontinuations of olverembatinib because of AEs were observed. There were no clinically meaningful abnormalities in clinical laboratory parameters, vital signs, or ECG parameters. Adverse events by deidentified study participant are tabulated (Table S3).

Discussion

This is the first study to examine the effect of food on olverembatinib pharmacokinetics in patients with CML. We selected a high‐fat meal to evaluate the greatest potential effect of food on the pharmacokinetic profile of olverembatinib, as recommended by regulatory agencies [12]. This study demonstrated that coadministration of olverembatinib (30 mg) with a high‐fat meal increased olverembatinib C max by 105.74% and AUC0–∞ by 69.74% compared with values observed in the fasting state in patients with CML, indicating that absorption of olverembatinib is improved and oral bioavailability of olverembatinib is increased. However, the food effect on olverembatinib might have been overestimated due to the three patients with abnormally lower exposure under fasting conditions. Because the 90% CIs of the geometric mean ratio of both C max and AUC0–∞ were outside the bioequivalence range of 0.8–1.25, bioequivalence between the fasting and the fed conditions (with a high‐fat meal) could not be established. Preclinical toxicology studies did not exclude involvement of healthy volunteers; however, patients with CML were chosen to reflect food‐drug interactions in this patient population and to avoid potential gastrointestinal irritation to healthy subjects. Olverembatinib 30 mg, administered as a single dose after a high‐fat meal or in the fasting state, was well tolerated, with no new safety concerns in patients with CML.

There is substantial evidence that a complex combination of factors, including stimulation of bile flow, modification of gastric motility patterns, gastrointestinal pH changes, alterations in splanchnic blood flow or presystemic metabolism, and physical/chemical interactions with the drug can alter the bioavailability of drugs in the presence of food within the gastrointestinal tract [13, 14, 15]. As a weak base, olverembatinib (pKa = 2.90, 8.09, and 10.89) exhibits pH‐dependent solubility across the physiologic pH range, with a higher solubility at lower pH. Olverembatinib is classified as a BCS (Biopharmaceutics Classification System) class IV drug (low solubility/low permeability). For a BCS class IV drug, an increase in bioavailability in the presence of a high‐fat meal is more likely due to increased solubilization of the drug in the small intestine [16].

With dissolution‐limited absorption, the bioavailability of olverembatinib was increased after intake of a high‐fat meal, and this effect might be attributed to the fact that the fat content of food and postprandial bile secretion enhance the solubility of olverembatinib. Meanwhile, this study also showed that olverembatinib's plasma exposure (C max and AUC0–∞) was much more variable, with an intersubject coefficient of variation (CV%) of 121.6% in C max and 82.2% in AUC0–∞ under fasting conditions versus 38.6% in C max and 40.8% in AUC0–∞ after a high‐fat meal. Therefore, to ensure consistent exposure, it is more appropriate for patients to take olverembatinib with food.

Nevertheless, the high‐fat meal did not evidently alter olverembatinib elimination, with comparable mean terminal plasma elimination half‐life (t 1/2) values observed in both fasting and fed conditions. The olverembatinib T max was also not delayed after a high‐fat meal. It has been reported that food intake may improve the gastrointestinal tolerability of TKIs (e.g., imatinib, bosutinib) indicated for the treatment of CML [10, 17, 18, 19]. It is recommended that both imatinib and bosutinib be taken with food to minimize gastrointestinal irritation and optimize tolerability [10, 17, 18, 19]. Although no gastrointestinal toxicity was observed in the 12 patients who participated in our food effect study, this may be due to the short treatment duration (single dose in each period) and limited sample size. In previous studies including several hundred patients (data on file; investigator's brochure version 7.0), the incidence rate of treatment‐related gastrointestinal disorders of any grade after a median treatment duration of 25.4 months was about 20%. Consequently, taking olverembatinib with food may reduce the possibility of gastrointestinal side effects in patients with CML.

Limitations of this study are that food effects on the pharmacokinetics of olverembatinib were investigated only with a high‐fat meal; consequently, the impacts of a low‐fat meal on the pharmacokinetics of olverembatinib are unknown. However, these limitations do not have an impact on the overall interpretation of this study.

Food ingestion was considered to have little effect on the pharmacokinetics of olverembatinib in most patients with good absorption under fasting conditions. However, for some patients with poor absorption under fasting conditions, food can promote drug absorption and greatly improve the bioavailability of olverembatinib.

During early (Phase 1) clinical development of olverembatinib (Clinical protocol No. SJ‐0002), enrolled patients were all dosed with food, without specific restrictions on fat or caloric content. After the first dose of olverembatinib, the resulting C max and AUC0–48h values of 8.43 ng/mL and 174 ng*hr/mL were 24.7% and 20.9% lower than the corresponding values from this present food effect study [20]. Essentially, the systemic exposure (C max and AUC0–48h) of olverembatinib between these two studies largely overlapped. The exposure difference is within the range of interindividual variation and does not have a clinically meaningful impact. This finding suggests that there appear to be no significant differences in plasma exposure following olverembatinib's administration with either a high‐fat meal or food without specific restrictions on fat or caloric content. This finding is important because adhering to specific meal types (e.g., high‐fat meals) for drug dosing could be challenging for most patients with cancer. Overall, results from this study and early clinical trials support the recommendation that olverembatinib should be administered orally with food, and specific instructions on fat or calorie composition are not needed in order to achieve adequate and consistent bioavailability.
