Clinical observation and correlation between the different degrees of myopia and choroidal thickness in children and adolescents
Authors: Yan Wang, Hongxia Chang, Xiaolong Li, Jingjing Wang, Jie Zhang, Jie Li, Yunxian Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myopia is a common visual problem, and the relationship between the subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) and the different degrees of myopia has always been a hotspot in myopia research. This was a prospective longitudinal cohort study. From July 2022 to December 2023,102 (204 eyes) myopic patients from Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital were collected, and 3 groups were low, moderate and high myopia according to spherical equivalent (SE). All patients underwent an ocular basic examination, and the macular subfoveal and peripheral choroidal thickness was measured by enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography. Follow-up after 1 year, record relevant data and statistical analysis. There were significant differences in choroidal thickness, SE, and axial length (AL) in the different degrees of myopia at baseline (P < .05), and no differences in corneal curvature (P > .05). At follow-up, the choroidal thickness was significantly different from the low and moderate groups (P < .05), and some loci in the low and moderate groups were significant (P < .05). Choroidal thickness was different between the 3 groups at baseline and at follow-up (P < .05). Overall, SFCT was positively correlated with SE (r = 0.63, P < .05) and negatively with AL (r = −0.62, P < .05). There are different associations between different degrees of myopia. The low myopia group had the thickest choroid in the macular area, the high myopia group the thinnest, and the thinnest nasal side. Myopia increases, and the choroidal thickness becomes thinner. SFCT was negatively correlated with AL, positively correlated with SE in high myopia, and no such relationship between moderate and low myopia. Choroidal thickness is associated with AL and refractive error, suggesting it may serve as a biomarker for monitoring myopic changes in children and adolescents. By comparing the changes of SFCT of different degrees of myopia, analyzing the correlation between AL and choroidal thickness, it provides a basis for exploring the relationship between choroidal thickness and myopia.

1. Introduction

The problem of myopia among adolescents has attracted widespread attention from doctors, experts and scholars around the world. It is estimated that by 2050, the world’s myopia population will reach 5.8 billion, and one-tenth of the population will become highly myopia.[1] According to relevant research statistics, compared with the prevalence of myopia in other regions (such as Europe and North America), the prevalence of myopia among children and adolescents and adolescents in East Asia has increased significantly.[2] In the next few decades, the proportion of myopia people will inevitably increase, and it is foreseeable that more and more young myopia individuals will develop pathological myopia in later life. Pathological myopia can lead to pathological changes in the eye. Its complications such as retinal detachment, macular holes, choroidal neovascularization, choroidal atrophy, etc, seriously damage the patient’s visual function, cause irreversible visual damage, and cause the patient’s quality of life. Serious decline.[3] Therefore, it is necessary to conduct in-depth research on its occurrence and development process from multiple angles.

In the past, the focus of myopia was on retinal changes and sclera remodeling, but in recent years, more and more scholars have turned their attention to the choroid. Some scholars have found that many signals originating from the retina are likely to be transmitted by the retinal pigment epithelium layer. Different retinal signals can be summarized on the retinal pigment epithelium layer to affect the choroidal thickness in a hierarchical manner,[4] so the choroid plays an important role in the signal cascade. Many clinical studies at home and abroad have shown that the degree of choroidal thinning in myopia is related to the degree of refractive error.[5] The younger the myopic children and adolescents, the faster the axis elongation and myopia progress, and the more significant the choroid thinning, indicating a close association between choroidal thickness and myopia development, which warrants attention in prevention and control strategies. Therefore, regular observation and monitoring of the changes in choroidal thickness in myopic eyes is of great significance for exploring the progress of myopia and identifying high-risk groups of myopia in a timely manner.

Previous studies have mostly been limited to high myopia and emmetropia, and few studies have been conducted on low to moderate myopia. Nowadays, with the rapid development of medical technology, optical coherence tomography (OCT) is widely used in ophthalmology, making choroid visualization possible and the measurement of the choroid becomes clearer and more accurate. The emergence of enhanced depth imaging (EDI) technique has greatly improved the imaging quality of choroidal cross-sections. Through average imaging, eye tracking, high-speed scanning, low noise and other in vivo tests on the choroid, it can provide a reliable basis for quantitative analysis of the choroid.[6] Based on this, this paper will conduct research according to 3 groups of low, moderate and high myopia. Enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography technology will be used to measure the choroidal thickness in the macular area of myopic adolescents aged 6 to 17 years old, compare the changes in choroidal thickness in the macular area of patients with low, moderate and high myopia, and analyze the correlation between choroidal thickness and ocular axis and diopter.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Research objects

From July 2022 to December 2023, 102 teenagers (204 eyes) who met the inclusion criteria who visited the ophthalmology clinic of the Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region were collected. There were 42 males and 60 females, aged 6 to 17. According to the spherical equivalent (SE) degree, myopia can be divided into 3 different degrees: low, moderate, and high. Low myopia group: −3.00 D < SE ≤ −0.50 D, moderate myopia: −6.00 D < SE ≤ −3.00 D, high myopia group: SE < −6.00 D. This study follows the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the purpose of the study is explained to the subjects and their families (No. 2023XE0132).

All subjects have informed and agreed. Inclusion criteria: myopic teenagers aged 6 to 17 years old have high degree of cooperation in examination; the best corrected visual acuity in low myopia group and moderate myopia group was ≥ 1.0; the best corrected visual acuity in high myopia group was ≥ 0.8; the intraocular pressure was normal (10–21 mm Hg, 1 mm Hg = 0.133 kPa), the anterior segment was normal on slit lamp examination, and the refractive matrix was clear. Fundus examination under front lens or direct ophthalmoscope showed no other abnormalities except for leopard-like changes and near-optic disc myopia arc. Exclusion criteria: history of ocular refractive surgery, intraocular surgery, and trauma; patients with amblyopia, dominant strabismus, congenital cataracts, aphakic eyes, glaucoma; diseases such as uveitis, macular holes, choroidal neovascularization, and retinal detachment that have a serious impact on the thickness and shape of the choroid; systemic diseases such as autoimmune diseases, craniocerebral trauma or lesions, hypertension, diabetes, and thyroid-related diseases can cause systemic diseases that change the eyes. (Flow diagram show in Fig. 1). This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies.

2.2. Examinations of the eye

Routine examination: an examination group consisted of 1 doctor, 1 optometrist, and 2 experienced nurses. All examinations were performed according to the proposed protocol. All examinations were completed in the same environment by designated experienced specialist ophthalmology technicians using the same examination equipment and in accordance with standard operating procedures. Ask for and record the subject’s name, gender, age, ethnicity, family history of myopia among immediate family members within 3 generations, and other medical history.

Choroidal thickness examination: all subjects were examined using Cirrus high definition enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography scanning technology produced by Zeiss, Germany. During the examination, the subject takes a comfortable position, with their forehead pressed against the forehead support and their jaw pressed against the jaw support, and moves with the detector back and forth, left and right. The green “+” fluorescence in the eye fixation instrument is observed, and the lens position is adjusted until the screen presents a clear and complete choroid in the macular area, the scan line is centered, and eye fixation is maintained for 3 seconds. After obtaining the choroid image, the scan line is set to a high-definition 5-line raster, and the macular area is scanned at 0° and 90° respectively with a 6 mm scan line. The vertical distance between the outer boundary of the retinal pigment epithelium and the choroid-sclera junction is the measurement value of choroidal thickness. Using the Zeiss OCT scanner’s own caliper, using the thinnest point of the retina in the fovea as the symbol of the fovea, the choroidal thickness values below the fovea and 500 μm, 1000 μm, and 1500 μm away from the fovea temporal (T), nasal (N), superior (S), and inferior (I) were manually measured. There were 13 site values in each eye, which were subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT), T1, T2, T3, N1, N2, N3, S1, S2, S3, I1, I2, and I3. The average of 13 measurement points was recorded as the average foveal choroidal thickness (A). The average value of each eye was taken 3 times, and the scanning and measurement work was completed independently by the same experienced and skilled physician. Excluding the effect of circadian rhythm on choroidal thickness, the examination time for all subjects was between 10 am and 4 pm. Choroidal thickness measurements were performed under small pupils, and compound tropicamide eye drops were no longer applied to the eyes to mydriate (Fig. 2).

(A) Horizontal and (B) vertical measurement of SFCT in the macular area. SFCT = subfoveal choroidal thickness.

Equivalent spherical power inspection: use cycloplegic compound tropicamide eye drops, applying 1 to 2 drops to each eye of the subject for a total of 5 times, with a 5-minute interval each time. After the last application, the eye is rested for about 30 minutes. After ensuring that the pupil’s light reflection disappears, the subject is asked to sit in front of the fully automatic computer optometry instrument. Adjust the operating table to an appropriate height, place the lower jaw in the mandibular support, and ensure the upper forehead is close to the upper forehead support. The subject should open both eyes naturally at the same time. After observing the machine’s internal vision, the subject is asked to make 3 consecutive measurements and take the average value. Then, ophthalmoscopy is performed to confirm the objective power, and then the lens is inserted based on the refraction results to obtain the subjective refraction. Based on the subjective refraction results, the equivalent spherical power (spherical power + 1/2 cylinder power) is calculated and recorded as the refractive power of the eye. All measurements are completed by the same experienced doctor. SE power examination is performed after other ophthalmic examinations are completed.

2.3. Outcome measures

Primary outcome measure: SFCT. Secondary outcome measures: axial length (AL), SE. T1 is at 500 μm from the temporal side to the fovea; T2 at 1000 μm from the temporal side to the fovea; T3 at 1500 μm from the temporal side to the fovea; and 3 sites on the nasal side with the same naming order as the temporal side: N1, N2, N3; the upper 3 sites are S1, S2, S3; and the lower 3 sites are I1, I2, I3. The average foveal choroidal thickness is denoted as A.

2.4. Methods for statistics

Statistical Package for the Sciences 26.0 and R4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) were used for data collation and statistical analysis. Data on age, intraocular pressure, AL, SE, corneal curvature, and choroidal thickness that complied with the normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation. An independent sample t-test was used for comparisons between the 2 groups; 1-way analysis of variance was used for 3 groups and above; and a paired t-test was used for comparisons between the front and back groups. If the measurement data did not conform to a normal distribution, it was expressed in the form of median [25th quantiles, 75th quantiles]. An independent sample nonparametric test was used for comparison between 2 groups; the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test was used for 3 groups and above; and a paired sample Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparison of differences before and after. The counting data were described using absolute numbers and constituent ratios, and the chi-square test was used to compare differences between groups. Linear correlation analysis was used to explore the relationship between SFCT and AL and SE. The test level was P < .05, and the difference was considered to be statistically significant .Given the exploratory nature of this study, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. However, a post hoc power analysis was conducted for the primary outcome measure, SCFT. Based on the baseline SFCT means and standard deviations observed in the 3 groups (low: 287.10 ± 58.3 μm; moderate: 260.54 ± 55.1 μm; high: 216.27 ± 62.5 μm), with an alpha of 0.05, the study had > 99% power to detect significant differences among the 3 groups using a 1-way analysis fo variance, and approximately 85% power for the pairwise comparison between the moderate and high myopia groups.

3.4. Analysis of choroidal thickness in the macular area

Total samples were measured in the fovea, temporal, nasal, 500 μm, 1000 μm, and 1500 μm, denoted as T1, T2, T3, N1, N2, N3, S1, S2, S3, I1, I2 and I3. Mean macular foveal choroidal thickness is denoted as A.

3.4.1. Analysis of the choroidal thickness in the internal macular area in the 3 groups

After the normality test by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov method and the Shapiro–Wilk method, N2 (nasal side 1000 μm), N3 (nasal side 1500 μm), S2 (superior side 1000 μm), and S3 (superior side 1500 μm) complied with a normal distribution, while other variables did not conform to a normal distribution. Among the 3 groups, the choroidal thickness in the low myopia group was thicker at all sites than the other 2 groups, and the high myopia group was the thinnest. In the low myopia group, choroidal thickness was greatest under the fovea, followed by the temporal, superior, and inferior sides, and the thinnest on the nasal side. In contrast, the choroidal thickness in the moderate and high myopia groups was the thickest on the temporal side, followed by the fovea, superior, and inferior, and the nasal side was the thinnest. Among them, the average choroidal thickness in the low myopia group was 287.10 μm, the moderate myopia group was 260.54 μm, and the high myopia group was the thinnest, at 216.27 μm. There were statistically significant differences in choroidal thickness at all sites in the macular area among the 3 groups at baseline (P < .05) as detailed in Table 4.

3.6. Linear correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was used to analyze SFCT with AL and SE in all myopic children and adolescents. The AL was negatively correlated with the SFCT (r = −0.62, P < .05), and the SE was positively correlated with the SFCT (r = 0.63, P < .05). Correlation analysis was performed on SE, AL, and SFCT in the high myopia group, moderate myopia group, and low myopia group. In the high myopia group, SFCT was highly positively correlated with SE (r = 0.657, P < .05) and negatively correlated with AL (r = −0.470, P < .05). In the moderate myopia group, SFCT was moderately negatively correlated with AL (r = −0.373, P < .05), but had no correlation with SE (P > .05). In the low myopia group, SFCT was moderately negatively correlated with AL (r = −0.310, P < .05), but not correlated with SE (P > .05). See Figure 4 and Table 8 for details.

4. Discussion

Adolescent myopia shows a high incidence in East and Southeast Asia, which is significantly related to education intensity and insufficient outdoor activities.[7] Although current prevention and control measures (orthokeratology lenses, defocus glasses, etc) are effective in the short term, their long-term safety still needs to be verified. Studies have confirmed that choroidal thinning is a key indication of early myopia, and research on its mechanism provides a new path for breakthroughs in prevention and control.[8] Advances in OCT technology have revealed a structure-functional connection of the choroid: this vascular layer regulates retinal position through thickness changes and secretes growth factors to mediate scleral remodeling.[9,10] Animal experiments have shown that the development of myopia is accompanied by a decrease in choroidal thickness and changes in sclera glycosaminoglycan metabolism, which may delay axial growth by regulating the composition of suprachoroidal fluid.[11] Sclera hypoxia caused by reduced blood flow can induce fibroblast transdifferentiation and accelerate extracellular matrix remodeling.[12] Clinical evidence shows that the growth of the AL directly leads to a decrease in the choroidal blood vessel density, forming a vicious cycle of “mechanical stretching-decreasing blood supply-thinning thickness.”[13,14] Primate studies further confirmed that this process is related to retinal functional damage.[15] The correlation between SFCT and SE is strongest (r = −0.82),[16,17] and the surrounding area is affected as myopia progresses.[18] The choroidal thickness around the optic disc in patients with high myopia is 32 to 47% lower than that in emmetropic eyes, and is linearly negatively correlated with the degree of myopia (β = −0.36, P < .001),[19] confirming that choroid parameters are strongly associated with myopia severity and may serve as potential biomarkers for monitoring disease status. These findings laid a theoretical foundation for establishing a dynamic monitoring system based on choroidal characteristics.

Numerous studies have shown that choroidal thickness is closely related to the degree of refractive error. The choroid in farsightedness is thicker than that in emmetropic eyes, which is thicker than that in myopic eyes. The deeper the myopia, the thinner the choroid. However, the characteristics of changes in choroidal thickness in myopic children and adolescents have rarely been described before. This study conducted longitudinal observations of choroidal thickness of myopic children and adolescents and made 3 important new findings. First, during the 1-year follow-up period in myopic children and adolescents, the choroidal thickness in the macular area became thinner, most significantly in the central fovea, and also significantly in the paramacular area. Second, longitudinal changes in choroidal thickness are related to changes in SE and axial axis, especially in children and adolescents with rapid progression of myopia. However, when analyzing children and adolescents with different diopters, only the SFCT in children and adolescents with high myopia was highly correlated with SE. Third, it was determined that the main influencing factors of the SFCT in children and adolescents with myopia were AL and SE, and the formula for calculating SFCT was obtained: SFCT = 649.142–13.294 × AL (mm) + 8.168 × SE (D).

Baseline data showed that the average macular choroidal thickness in the low myopia group was 287.10 μm, which was thicker than that in the moderate and high myopia groups. Within 1 year, the AL of the myopic children and adolescents included in the study increased by an average of 0.2 mm, and the SE increased by an average of −0.52 D, which was significantly higher than the baseline (P < .05), indicating that the faster increase in AL and the increase in SE were related to more thinning of SFCT. Previous studies have shown that choroidal thickness varies in different refractive states. Bao Li et al[20] found that there was a significant change in the choroidal thickness in the macular area only in moderate and high myopia groups. When Emmanuel Kobia et al[21] studied the choroidal thickness in children and adolescents with different myopia degrees in Europe, they found that there were differences in the average choroidal thickness in different myopia groups, but the distribution overlapped greatly. A larger choroidal thickness can prevent or delay myopia, while a lower thickness can easily cause myopia, which may be related to dysfunction of the choroid vascular system. With age, the choroid becomes thinner, making myopic children and adolescents more prone to chororetinopathy. This study also found that after 1 year of follow-up, the choroidal thickness in the macular area became thinner in low, moderate, and high myopia groups, with the choroidal thickness becoming the thinnest under the fovea. The thickness of the choroid in the macular area differs in different quadrants. Baseline data showed that the choroid under the fovea was thickest and the nasal side was thinnest in the low myopia group; the temporal side was thickest and the nasal side was thinnest in the moderate and high myopia group. Longitudinal study found that the choroid thinning rate in adolescents with low myopia was faster than that with moderate and high myopia, and the changes in SE and AL in the low myopia group were greater than those in the moderate and high myopia group.

The AL growth rate is closely related to changes in choroidal thickness. In this study, there was a negative correlation between the increase in AL and the thinning of SFCT (r = −0.62). Read et al[22] follow-up found that children and adolescents with rapid AL growth had less choroidal thickening. Studies have also shown that the baseline SFCT is an important factor in determining its change and is independent of the AL at baseline. Studies of chick eyes found that eyes with thin choroids grow faster. Some studies have found through multiple regression analysis that the choroidal thickness in mildly myopic children and adolescents with SE > −2.00 D decreases with age. A retrospective study in highly myopic eyes has shown that AL is an important predictor of SFCT.[23] Saeedi et al[24] research has shown that the central foveal choroidal thickness is negatively correlated with the AL of the eye. For every 1 mm increase in the AL, the SFCT decreases by 23μm.

As the SE increases, the choroidal thickness gradually becomes thinner.[18] In this study, myopic children and adolescents were grouped according to SE. It was found that SE and SFCT were positively correlated in all myopic children and adolescents, but only highly myopic children and adolescents showed a high correlation between SFCT and SE. Different studies have different conclusions on the relationship between choroidal thickness and SE. Shu Benliang et al[25]believe that increased SE affects the AL and density of blood vessels, leading to changes in blood flow density. Some studies[26] measured choroidal thickness in children and adolescents and found that the low myopia group was larger than that in the moderate and high myopia group, but there was no significant difference in choroidal thickness between the moderate and high myopia groups. Saeedi et al[24] found that for every 1 D increase in myopia, the thickness of SFCT decreased by 13 μm, and SE was most correlated with the thickness of the choroid under the fovea. In this study, an equation was established after regression analysis of SE, AL, and SFCT: SFCT (μm) = 649.142–13.294*AL (mm) + 8.168*SE (D). If SE remains unchanged,SFCT decreases by 13.29 μm for every 1mm increase in AL.

The findings of this study have several potential clinical implications. First, the observed negative correlation between SFCT and AL (r = −0.62) suggests that choroidal thickness measurements could serve as a noninvasive indicator of axial elongation, which is the primary structural change in myopia progression. In clinical practice, regular monitoring of choroidal thickness, particularly SFCT, may help identify children and adolescents experiencing rapid myopic changes who may benefit from more intensive intervention. Second, our data suggest potential threshold values that warrant attention. Based on the distribution observed in our cohort, an SFCT below 220 μm in children and adolescents may indicate high myopia or risk of rapid progression, as this value approximates the mean SFCT in our high myopia group (216.27 μm). Conversely, an SFCT above 280 μm was typically associated with low myopia in our sample. However, these thresholds should be interpreted with caution given the significant inter-individual variability and overlap between groups.Third, the quadrant-specific differences observed (particularly the pronounced thinning on the nasal side in all myopia groups) suggest that clinicians should not rely solely on SFCT but should evaluate the full macular choroidal profile. The nasal quadrant, being the thinnest and most susceptible to change, may be particularly sensitive for detecting early myopic alterations.Finally,by specifically focusing on the understudied population of children and adolescents with low to moderate myopia, we have filled an important gap in the literature, as most previous work has concentrated on adults or on high myopia. This is particularly relevant given the rising prevalence of childhood myopia worldwide and the critical window for intervention in this age group.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings.First, the sample sizes across the 3 myopia groups were unequal, with a relatively smaller cohort in the high myopia group (17 patients,32 eyes). This distribution reflects the lower prevalence of high myopia in the general pediatric population. While this limits the statistical power for some subgroup analyses and the generalizability of conclusions specific to high myopia, the observed significant differences even with this smaller group underscore the robustness of the main findings.Second, both eyes from each participant were included in the analysis. Although we accounted for inter-eye correlation using a linear mixed model in a sensitivity analysis (which confirmed our main findings) the potential for bias from intra-subject correlation cannot be completely excluded. Future studies may consider selecting 1 eye per participant or using clustered analysis as the primary analytical approach. Third, manual measurement of choroidal thickness, despite being performed by a single experienced physician, may contain inherent measurement errors. Additionally, automated segmentation algorithms were not employed, which could introduce variability. Fourth, the observational design of this study precludes any causal inferences. While we observed significant associations between choroidal thinning and axial elongation, we cannot determine whether choroidal thinning is a cause or a consequence of myopia progression. Fifth, potential confounders such as the use of myopia control interventions (e.g., orthokeratology lenses, atropine eye drops) and the physiological effects of adolescent growth and development were not fully controlled for in the analysis. These factors may influence both choroidal thickness and refractive error progression. Finally, the 1-year follow-up period, while sufficient to observe significant changes, may not capture longer-term patterns of choroidal remodeling and myopia progression. Extended follow-up studies are needed to establish the predictive value of baseline choroidal thickness for future myopia progression. Future research should address these limitations by recruiting larger, more balanced cohorts, particularly for high myopia; employing automated choroidal segmentation techniques; controlling for myopia control interventions; extending follow-up duration; and incorporating multimodal imaging including choroidal vascularity assessment to provide a more comprehensive understanding of choroidal changes in myopia development.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study observed significant differences in macular choroidal thickness among children and adolescents with different degrees of myopia. The choroid was thickest in the low myopia group and thinnest in the high myopia group, with the nasal quadrant being the thinnest overall. During the 1-year follow-up, choroidal thinning was observed alongside increases in AL and myopic refraction. Choroidal thickness showed negative correlations with AL and positive correlations with SE, with variations by myopia severity. These findings highlight associations between choroidal thickness and myopia-related ocular parameters. However, given the modest sample size, 1-year follow-up duration, and limited adjustment for potential confounders, further large-scale, long-term studies are required to determine whether choroidal thickness measurements could have clinical utility in monitoring myopia progression.
