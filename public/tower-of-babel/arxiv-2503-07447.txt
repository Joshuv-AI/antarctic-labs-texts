1
Introduction

1.1
Majority Dynamics

Consider a parliamentary election with two parties.
The election season begins with Day 0, where each voter supports one side.
At the end of every day, each voter polls their friends, then supports the majority side the next morning, or keeps their affiliation in case of a tie.
On election day, everyone votes for the party they currently supports and the parliament will be divided proportionally with each side’s number of votes.

This process can be modeled with a graph process, where vertices represent voters and edges represent friendships.
We let Red and Blue, or R and B, stand for the two parties.
The state of affiliations at any time is simply a coloring on the graph.
This process on a graph is called
Majority Dynamics
in literature, due to the majority-based updating rule.

There is a vast literature of more general
opinion exchange
processes, with applications in many fields.
We refer to the influential survey by Mossel and Tamuz
[
10
]
for an overview.
In this paper, we focus on Majority Dyanmics and study its final states.

While previous works
[
7
,
5
]
used
\pm 1
assignments in place of Red and Blue, we adopt the Red/Blue notation to be consistent with a recent paper by the second author and Vu
[
13
]
, which uses the following formal definition

Definition 1.1

(Majority Dynamics)
.

Given simple undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, and a partition
V=R\cup B
, the
majority dynamics
on
G
with initial sides
(R,B)
is a process that begins with Day 0, where vertices in
R
and
B
are respectively colored Red and Blue.

For each
t\geq 0
, on day
t+1
,
each vertex adopts the color the majority of its neighbors hold on day
t
, or remains unchanged in case of a tie.

On theory, the process goes on forever, but a beautiful argument, independently discovered by Goles and Olivos
[
8
]
and Poljak and Sura
[
11
]
, show that
after at most
|V|
days,
the process either stabilizes or alternates forever between two colorings, so we can view them as the
de facto
final states.
In the settings in this paper, the process will almost always ends in a special stable position called
unanimity
, where every vertex has the same color.
There is a large and active research area dedicated to finding the conditions under which unanimity occur.
In the next section we will introduce two main models in the literature, their relationship, and the main questions in each.

Let us first introduce extra notations to aid the presentation.

•

Given a coloring
(R,B)
of
V
, there is a corresponding map
\ell(R,B):V\to\{\pm 1\}
such that
\ell(R,B)(v)=1
if
v\in R
and
-1
if
v\in B
.
When the coloring in the context is clear, we simply use
\ell
.
We will abuse the notation and treat
\ell
and
(R,B)
as the same object.

•

Given
G=(V,E)
, and a coloring
(R,B)
of
V
, we denote the coloring on day
t
by
(R_{t},B_{t})
and denote
\ell_{t}=\ell(R_{t},B_{t})
.
We will state clearly when we do not follow this convention.

•

Given a coloring map
\ell
, we define the reverse notations
R(\ell):=\ell^{-1}(1)
and
B(\ell):=\ell^{-1}(-1)
.
This makes
R_{t}=R(\ell_{t})
and
B_{t}=B(\ell_{t})
.

•

If a Red unanimity occurs, we say Red wins, and analogously for Blue.

1.2
Main models and questions

The graph
G
and the initial coloring
\ell_{0}
may be deterministically given or randomly generated from some joint distribution.
We use the following model:

•

G
is a random graph generated from the Erdös-Rényi
G(n,p)
distribution: there are
n
vertices, each pair of which is connected independently with probability
p
.
The vast majority of works on majority dynamics use this graph model.

•

\ell_{0}
is chosen randomly and independently of
G
by sampling
R_{0}
uniformly among all subsets of
V
with
n/2+\Delta
elements.
This is called the
fixed advantage coloring scheme
in
[
13
]
.

The numbers
n
,
p
and
\Delta
are model parameters.
The independence between
G
and
\ell_{0}
allows them to be generated in any order, simultaneously or not.
In fact, the discussion in
[
13
]
shows that the following models are equivalent (in terms of the joint distribution of
(G,\ell_{0})
)

1.

Fix
n
vertices and their colors such that
|R_{0}|=n/2+\Delta
, then draw
G\sim G(n,p)
over them.

2.

Fix
n
vertices and their colors such that
|R_{0}|=|B_{0}|=n/2
, then draw
G\sim G(n,p)
over them, then sample a set of “defectors” uniformly among subsets of
B_{0}
of size
\Delta
and flip their colors.

Tran and Vu were among the first to consider the fixed advantage coloring scheme
[
14
]
.
They discovered the “power of few” phenomenon:
when
p
is
\Theta(1)
, for any
\varepsilon
, one can choose
\Delta
to be a sufficiently large constant to ensure Red wins with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.
In other words, bribing a constant number of people is enough to win in a landslide, no matter how large the population is.
This constant is not large, as it is proven in
[
14
]
that for
p=1/2
,
\Delta=6
is enough for Red to win with probability
.99
.
Devlin and Berkowitz
[
4
]
later showed that
\Delta=3
is enough for the winning odd to be at least
.6
.
Sah and Sawhney
[
12
]
later lowered this to
\Delta=1
, the smallest possible gap.

In real life, the average person knows only a tiny fraction of the population.

How many people need to be bribed for unanimous victory in this general case?

Clearly, the density
p
cannot be lower than the
connectivity threshold

n^{-1}\log n
, otherwise there will be, with high probability, small connected components with the same initial color.
These “echo chambers” will maintain their affiliation forever, no matter who the rest vote for.
Tran and Vu
[
13
]
formally posed the question

Question 1.2
.

Given
n
and
p
such that
(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n\leq p<1
for some constant
\lambda
, what is the smallest
\Delta=\Delta_{\min}(n,p,\varepsilon)
for which Red wins with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
?

It was shown in
[
13
]
that
\Delta=\max\{C_{\varepsilon}p^{-1/2},10p^{-1}\}
for a sufficient constant
C_{\varepsilon}
is enough, which interestingly does not depend directly on
n
.
The authors also conjectured
[
13
, Conjecture 1.11]
that
\Delta=C_{\varepsilon}p^{-1/2}
is the optimal “power of few” value.
This has been confirmed for the dense regime, where
p>\log^{-1/16}n
in
[
12
]
.
The case for lower densities remains open.

Another popular coloring scheme, considered by most papers before
[
14
]
, is the
random 1/2 coloring scheme

[
13
]
:
\{\ell_{0}(v)\}_{v\in V}
are i.i.d. random variables taking value in
\{\pm 1\}
with equal probabilities.
(This is also equivalent to sampling
(R_{0},B_{0})
uniformly among all possible partitions of
V
.)
Consider majority dynamics on
G\sim G(n,p)
with
\ell_{0}
being the random 1/2 coloring.
The main question in this model is

Question 1.3
.

Given
n
and
\varepsilon
, what is the minimum
p=p_{\min}(n,\varepsilon)
for which the side with the initial majority wins with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
?

This question is related to Question
1.2
in the following way.
For any
\varepsilon
, there is a constant
c_{\varepsilon}
such that an initial coloring from the random 1/2 scheme satisfies
|R_{1}|\geq(n+c_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{n})/2
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon/2
, by the central limit theorem.
Therefore, one can replace a random 1/2 coloring with a fixed coloring with
\Delta=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
to analyze any high-probability event.
Question
1.3
is then equivalent to:

What is the minimum
p
such that
\Delta_{\min}(n,p,\varepsilon)=c_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}
?

If the optimal “powef of few” value is indeed
C_{\varepsilon}p^{-1/2}
, it is tempting to deduce that the optimal
p_{\min}(n,\varepsilon)
is
(C_{\varepsilon}/c_{\varepsilon})n^{-1}
.
However, the correct answer would be the connectivity threshold, more precisely
(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
for any
\lambda>0
, to avoid the “echo chambers” effect discussed above.

A very similar prediction was made in the pioneering paper by Benjamini, Chan, O’Donnell, Tamuz and Tan
[
3
]
.
They conjectured that
p=C_{\varepsilon}n^{-1}
is enough for
\varepsilon
-unanimity:
the initially larger side having at least
(1-\varepsilon)n
vertices in the final state.
It is likely that resolving Question
1.3
will also advance the progress towards Benjamini et al’s conjecture.

1.3
Current progresses and new results

Fountoulakis, Kang and Makai
[
7
]
made the first progress towards resolving Question
1.3
.
They proved that the initial majority side wins in 4 days with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
in the range
C_{\varepsilon}n^{-1/2}\leq p<1
for a sufficient constant
C_{\varepsilon}
.
Interestingly, their proof first translates the context into the fixed advantage coloring scheme with
\Delta=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, without explicitly defining this scheme.

Tran and Vu independently defined the fixed advantage coloring scheme and discovered the “power of few” phenomenon for
p=\Theta(1)
in
[
14
]
.
Their second paper
[
13
]
united progresses in both models, extending “power of few” for
(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n\leq p<1
(the best possible range) and reproducing the main result of
[
7
]
(with an extra day), and posed Question
1.2
explicitly.

In a recent breakthrough on Question
1.3
, Chakraborti, Kim, Lee and Tran
[
5
]
proved that the initial majority wins in 5 days with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
if
p\geq C_{\varepsilon}n^{-3/5}\log n
for a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
, and conjectured that their approach can be improved to work even for
p\geq C_{\varepsilon}n^{-2/3}\log n
.

Our main result pushes the boundaries towards both questions and resolves the conjecture in
[
5
]
, even achieving a slightly better logarithmic factor.

Theorem 1.4
.

Consider majority dynamics on a
G(n,p)
random graph
G
and a fixed initial coloring
\ell_{0}
with
|R_{0}|\geq n/2+\Delta
.
For every
\varepsilon
, there is a universal constant
C
and constants
C_{\varepsilon}
and
N_{\varepsilon}
only depending on
\varepsilon
such that if
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
,
n^{-1/2}\log^{1/4}n\geq p\geq Cn^{-1}\log^{2}n
, and
\Delta\geq C_{\varepsilon}p^{-3/2}n^{-1/2}\log n
, Red wins within
O(\log_{pn}n)
days with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

In other words, in the range
n^{-1/2}\log^{1/4}n\geq p\geq Cn^{-1}\log^{2}n
, the theorem above gives the answer
\Delta=C_{\varepsilon}p^{-3/2}n^{-1/2}\log n
for Question

.
This is better than the answer

Theorem

also gives the answer

, improving on the answers

in
[

]
and

in
[

and is the best known so far.
This is also the best (in terms of power of

1.4
Theoretical limits for “power of few”

For convenience, let
\Delta_{t}:=\frac{1}{2}(|R_{t}|-|B_{t}|)
.
In their seminal paper
[
3
]
, Benjamini et al predicted that for
p\geq(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
, the dynamics progresses in two phases (assuming
\Delta_{0}
is large enough):

1.

Slow expansion:
The Red advantage grows by a
\Theta(\sqrt{pn})
factor every day, until it is more than
2\varepsilon n
, for some small constant
\varepsilon
(meaning Red has more than
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)n
members).

2.

Landslide:
Once the threshold
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)n
is passed, the Red camp rapidly converts everyone.
Blue will shrink by a
\Theta(pn)
factor every day, leading to a Red win after
O(\log_{pn}n)
days.

All papers in majority dynamics that we know of so far follow this roadmap.

The landslide phase has been proven to happen regardless of the members in each side, independently in
[
7
]
and
[
13
]
, the latter for the whole range
p\geq(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
.

In fact, it is shown in
[
13
]
that once
\Delta_{k}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}
for some sufficient constant
C
(the day before the landslide), the graph is so well-connected that Red wins no matter who its members are.

The slow expansion phase is tricky because the advantage has not reached a point where one does not need to care about the members of each camp.

Most arguments so far deal with this phase as a whole, meaning they choose some
k
and prove directly that
\Delta_{k}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}
, rather than showing
\Delta_{t}\geq c\sqrt{pn}\Delta_{t-1}
for all
1\leq t\leq k
.
The latter is very hard to prove since for
t\geq 2
, one does not have the independence between the Red and Blue neighborhoods of a vertex
v
to analyze
\ell_{t}(v)
anymore.

The approaches by Tran and Vu
[
14
,
13
]
, Fountoulakis et al
[
7
]
, and Devlin and Berkowitz
[
4
]
and Sah and Sawhney
[
12
]
are
first-day approaches
, since they avoid this loss of independence by proving
\Delta_{1}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}
under the right conditions.
One needs
\Delta_{0}=\Delta
to be large enough for this to hold, and since
\Delta_{1}=\Theta(\Delta_{0}\sqrt{pn})
, one needs
\Delta=\Omega(Cp^{-1})
for this aproach to work.

The approach by Chakraborti et al
[
5
]
and this paper is a
second-day approach
.
We do not make any statement about
\Delta_{1}
and prove directly that
\Delta_{2}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}
.
Since
\Delta_{2}=\Theta(\Delta_{0}pn)
, the best one can get out of this approach is
\Delta=\Omega(p^{-3/2}n^{-1/2})
.
This means Theorem
1.4
is optimal for any second-day approach, except for the log factor.

What does an optimal value for
\Delta
look like?

To kick off the first phase, we need
\Delta_{0}
to not be too small.
The analysis in
[
13
]
shows that
\Delta_{1}
(as a function of the random graph
G
) has expectation
\Theta(\Delta\sqrt{pn})
and standard deviation
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.
Informally, we need
\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{1}\right]\geq C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\mathbf{Var}\left[\Delta_{1}\right]}
for a sufficiently large
C_{\varepsilon}
to assert that
\Delta_{1}=\Omega(\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{1}\right])
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.
This implies
\Delta\geq C_{\varepsilon}p^{-1/2}
is the lowest possible advantage.
This has been reached by Sah and Sawhney
[
12
]
in their breakthrough paper for the dense regime.
To extend it to the lower densities, one likely needs to find a general
k-day approach
to deal with the slow expansion phase.

When translating to the model with the random 1/2 coloring scheme to find
p_{\min}(n,\varepsilon)
, it is not hard to see that the results of Tran and Vu
[
13
]
and Fountoulakis et al
[
7
]
, namely
p=C_{\varepsilon}n^{-1/2}
are optimal for any first-day approach;
while Corollary
1.5
, which gives
p=C_{\varepsilon}n^{-2/3}\log^{2/3}n
, is optimal up to the log factor for any second-day approach.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows:
In section
2
, we prove Theorem
1.4
after introducing two technical lemmas.
These lemmas will be proven in section
3
.
Section
4
is reserved for utility estimates on binomial random variables used throughout the proofs.

2.1

For the proof, we will add many new notations.

, at the cost of an error

C_{0}\frac{p(1-p)[p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}]|W|}{[p(1-p)|W|]^{3/2}}\leq\frac{2C_{0}}{\sqrt{pn}}.

We have, by regularity of
U
,

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbf{P}}(\widehat{\ell}_{1}(u)=1)={\mathbf{P}}\left(X^{+}-X^{-}+m_{u}\geq 0\right)=\Phi\left(\frac{m_{u}+p(|W^{+}|-|W^{-}|)}{\sqrt{p|W|}}\right)\pm\frac{2C_{0}}{\sqrt{pn}}

(6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\frac{m_{u}+p(|W^{+}|-|W^{-}|)}{\sqrt{p|W|}}\right)\pm\frac{2C_{0}}{\sqrt{pn}}=\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\frac{\pm\log n\pm p\sqrt{pn\log n}}{\sqrt{pn}}\right)\pm\frac{2C_{0}}{\sqrt{pn}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\pm O\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{pn}}\right)\quad\implies\ |\varepsilon_{u}|=O\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{pn}}\right).

This crude bound allows us to estimate
\mathbf{Var}\left[\widehat{\ell}_{1}(v)\right]
and the Berry-Esseen error in Eq. (
5
).
We have

\mathbf{Var}\left[\widehat{\ell}_{1}(v)\right]=|U|-\sum_{u\in U}\varepsilon_{u}^{2}=|U|\left(1-O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{pn}\right)\right).

(7)

Recall that we are allowed
pn\geq C\log^{2}n
for a large enough
C
, so this bound is still meaningful.
For the error term, temporarily let
\delta=(pn)^{-1/2}\log n
, we have

\frac{|U|-\sum_{u\in U}\varepsilon_{u}^{4}}{\left(|U|-\sum_{u\in U}\varepsilon_{u}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}=\frac{|U|(1-O(\delta^{4}))}{|U|^{3/2}(1-O(\delta^{2}))^{3/2}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pn}}\right).

(8)

We keep these bounds in mind for later use.

Now let us estimate
\sum_{u\in U}\varepsilon_{u}
more carefully.
We expect more cancellations than simply adding the crude bounds in Eq. (
6
).
By
[
5
, Lemma 2.5]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in U}{\mathbf{P}}(\widehat{\ell}_{1}(u)=1)=\sum_{u\in U}{\mathbf{P}}\left(X^{+}-X^{-}+m_{u}\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in U}\left[{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}\geq X^{-})+m_{u}{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}=X^{-})\pm O\left(\frac{m_{u}^{2}}{p|W|}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=|U|{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}\geq X^{-})+{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}=X^{-})\sum_{u\in U}m_{u}\pm O\left(\frac{1}{pn}\sum_{u\in U}m_{u}^{2}\right).

Let us simplify the above expression.
We have

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in U}m_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in U}\left(d_{U^{+}}(u)-d_{U^{-}}(u)\right)+\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v)|U|-|U^{-}|=\mathbf{1}_{|U|}^{T}\mathbf{d}_{U}+\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v)|U|-|U^{-}|

\displaystyle=\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v)|U|-\frac{|U|}{2}\pm 4\sqrt{pn\log n},

where the last estimate is due to
U
being regular.
Next, also by regularity,

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in U}m_{u}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{u\in U}\left[\left(d_{U^{+}}(u)-d_{U^{-}}(u)\right)^{2}+4\right]=\|\mathbf{d}_{U}\|_{2}^{2}+8|U|\leq 10pn\log n.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in U}{\mathbf{P}}(\widehat{\ell}_{1}(u)=1)

\displaystyle=|U|{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}\geq X^{-})+{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}=X^{-})\left(\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v)|U|-\frac{|U|}{2}\pm 4\sqrt{pn\log n}\right)\pm O(\log n)

(9)

\displaystyle=|U|\left({\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}\geq X^{-})-\frac{{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}=X^{-})}{2}+\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v){\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}=X^{-})\right)\pm O(\log n).

Ideally, we want the first term to simplify
|U|/2
, so that the sum on the left-hand side is close to
|U|/2
, meaning
\sum_{u\in U}\varepsilon_{u}
is close to
0
.
This will work out if
X^{+}
and
X^{-}
have identical distributions.
While this is not the case, we can replace them with i.i.d. random variables at some cost.

Let
Z^{+}
and
Z^{-}
be two independent copies of
\operatorname{Bin}(n/2-\lceil pn\rceil,p)
.
By
[
5
, Corollary 2.8]
, we can replace
X^{\pm}
respectively with
Z^{\pm}
in
{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}\geq X^{-})
and
{\mathbf{P}}(X^{+}=X^{-})
for an error of at most

O\left(\frac{p\bigl(||W^{+}|-(n/2-\lceil pn\rceil)|\vee||W^{-}|-(n/2-\lceil pn\rceil)|\bigr)}{\sqrt{pn}}\right)=O\left(\frac{p\sqrt{pn\log n}}{\sqrt{pn}}\right)=O(p\sqrt{\log n}).

Now by symmetry,

\penalty\ {\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}\geq Z^{-})-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})=\frac{1}{2},

so Eq. (
9
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in U}{\mathbf{P}}(\widehat{\ell}_{1}(u)=1)

\displaystyle=|U|\left[\frac{1}{2}\pm O(p\sqrt{\log n})+\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v){\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\right]\pm O(\log n)

\displaystyle=\frac{|U|}{2}+\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v)|U|{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\pm O(p|U|\sqrt{\log n})\pm O(\log n)

\displaystyle=\frac{|U|}{2}+\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v)|U|{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\pm O\bigl(\log n\bigr),

where we use the fact
p|U|=O(p^{2}n)=O(\sqrt{\log n})
.
Since
\varepsilon_{u}=2{\mathbf{P}}(\widehat{\ell}_{1}(u)=1)-1
, we have

\sum_{u\in U}\varepsilon_{u}=2\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v)|U|{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\pm O\bigl(\log n\bigr).

(10)

Note that the first term on the right-hand side is
\Theta(\sqrt{pn})
, so it is indeed the main term.

Now we can finally estimate
{\mathbf{P}}(A_{v})
.
Plugging Eqs. (
7
), (
8
), (
10
) into Eq. (
5
), we get

{\mathbf{P}}(A_{v})=\Phi\left(\frac{D+2\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v)|U|{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\pm O\bigl(\log n\bigr)}{\sqrt{|U|(1-O(\delta^{2}))}}\right)\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pn}}\right),

where
\delta=(pn)^{-1/2}\log n
as previously defined.
We analyze the term inside
\Phi(\cdot)
.
By the regularity of
U
,
|U|=pn(1\pm O(\delta))
, so we can write this term as

\displaystyle\frac{D}{\sqrt{|U|(1-O(\delta^{2}))}}+\frac{2\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v){\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\sqrt{|U|}}{\sqrt{1-O(\delta^{2})}}\pm\frac{O(\sqrt{\log n})}{\sqrt{|U|(1-O(\delta^{2}))}}

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{\sqrt{pn(1\pm O(\delta))}}+2\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v){\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\sqrt{pn(1\pm O(\delta))}\pm O\bigl(\delta^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{\sqrt{pn}}+2\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v){\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\sqrt{pn}\pm O\left(\delta\left(\frac{D}{\sqrt{pn}}+{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\sqrt{pn}+\delta\right)\right).

Consider the error term.
By the assumption
D\leq 10\sqrt{pn}
, and the facts that
{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\leq 4(pn)^{-1/2}
and
\delta<.1
, this error term is just
O(\delta)=O((pn)^{-1/2}\log n)
.
We get

{\mathbf{P}}(A_{v})=\Phi\left(\frac{D\pm O(\log n)}{\sqrt{pn}}+2\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v){\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\sqrt{pn}\right)\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pn}}\right).

(12)

By allowing
D\geq C\log n
for a sufficient constant
C
, we have

D\pm O(\log n)\geq D/2
.
Eq. (
12
) then implies

P(A_{v})=\Phi\left(\frac{D}{2\sqrt{pn}}+2\widehat{\ell}_{0}(v){\mathbf{P}}(Z^{+}=Z^{-})\sqrt{pn}\right)\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pn}}\right),

which is the desired expression.
We are not done yet, since this is actually
{\mathbf{P}}(A_{v}\mid U=N(v),H=G(U))
for the fixed regular pair
(U,H)
that we have been using.
However, since the total probability of irregular pairs is
O(n^{-2})
, which gets absorbed by the error term
O((pn)^{-1/2})
above, this does not affect
{\mathbf{P}}(A_{v})
in the general case.
The proof is complete.
∎

3.2.2
Step 2: Upper bounding
\mathbf{Var}\left[|A|\right]

Below is the main claim

Claim 3.3
.

Let
A
be the set of
D
-almost positive vertices.
Let
B>0
be an arbitrary constant.
There are universal constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
(large enough) such that if
pn\geq C_{1}\log^{2}n
and
B\sqrt{pn}\geq D\geq C_{2}\log n
, then

\mathbf{Var}\left[|A|\right]=O\left(\frac{n\log^{2}n}{p}\right).

The key idea is to write

\mathbf{Var}\left[|A|\right]=\sum_{v\in V}\mathbf{Var}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v}}\right]+\sum_{v_{1}\neq v_{2}}\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{1}}},\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{2}}}\right]

Since the first part is at most
n/4
, it suffices to consider the covariances.
Fix a distinct pair
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
, we have the claim

Claim 3.4
.

Under the settings of Claim
3.3
, we have

\left|\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{1}}},\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{2}}}\right]\right|=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{pn}\right).

Suppose this holds, we can prove Claim
3.3
.

Proof of Claim
3.3
.

By the decomposition above, we have

\mathbf{Var}\left[|A|\right]\leq\frac{n}{4}+\binom{n}{2}\cdot O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{pn}\right)=O\left(\frac{n\log^{2}n}{p}\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

Now we move to the main technical challenge.
Similarly to the proof of Claim
3.2
, we want to prove Claim
3.4
in an “ideal” environment, namely where the statistics of
N(v_{1})
and
N(v_{2})
are close to their expectations.

This time we want to expose everything inside both

and
N(v_{2})

Fix

and a subgraph

over

, and condition

on the events
U_{1}=N(v_{1})\setminus N(v_{2})
,
U_{2}=N(v_{2})\setminus N(v_{2})
,
U_{3}=N(v_{1})\cap N(v_{2})
(all excluding
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
) and

.

We introduce some extra notation:

•

U_{13}:=U_{1}\cup U_{3}=N(v_{1})\setminus\{v_{2}\}
and
U_{23}:=U_{2}\cup U_{3}=N(v_{2})\setminus\{v_{2}\}
in our conditioned space.

•

and

.
Define the same “Red” and “Blue” versions for the sets defined above.

•

W=(V\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2}\})\setminus U
.
Define

•

.

•

Again, simple Chernoff bounds show that

.
Now we can proceed to the formal proof.

Proof of Claim
3.4
.

Let
h_{i}=\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{i}}}\right]
without any conditioning.
Fix disjoint
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}
and subgraph

over

Condition

on the events
(N(v_{1})\setminus\{v_{2}\})\setminus N(v_{2})=U_{1}
,

(N(v_{2})\setminus\{v_{1}\})\setminus N(v_{1})=U_{2}
,

(N(v_{1})\setminus\{v_{2}\})\cap(N(v_{2})\setminus\{v_{1}\})=U_{3}
,
and
H=G(U)
.
The covariance on this conditioned probability is off from the real covariance by an error of just
O(n^{-2})
.
Until noted otherwise, we omit the events from the probability.

We first rewrite the covariance as

\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{1}}},\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{2}}}\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[(\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{1}}}-h_{1})(\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{2}}}-h_{2})\right]

(13)

We want to split this into a product, but
A_{v_{1}}
and

are not independent.
However, their only shared dependency is on the first day colors of their joint neighbors,

.

Fix a set of first day colors

for these vertices.
Condition the space further on the event
\mathcal{J}:=\{\forall u\in U_{3},\ \widehat{\ell}_{1}(u)=j_{u}\}

This does not affect

for the other

We can rewrite the conditioned Eq. (

\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{1}}},\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{2}}}\mid\mathcal{J}\right]=\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{1}}}\mid\mathcal{J}\right]-h_{1}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{2}}}\mid\mathcal{J}\right]-h_{2}\right).

Consider the factor
\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{1}}}\mid\mathcal{J}\right]-h_{1}
.
Let
H_{1}(\mathcal{J})=\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{v_{1}}}\mid\mathcal{J}\right]

Let

.
Reuse the computation leading Eq. (

H_{1}(\mathcal{J})=\Phi\left(\frac{D+\widehat{\ell}_{1}(v_{2})\mathbf{1}_{v_{1}\sim v_{2}}+j+\sum_{u\in U_{1}}\varepsilon_{u}}{(|U_{1}|-\sum_{u\in U_{1}}\varepsilon_{u}^{2})^{1/2}}\right)\pm O\left(\frac{|U_{1}|-\sum_{u\in U_{1}}\varepsilon_{u}^{4}}{(|U_{1}|-\sum_{u\in U_{1}}\varepsilon_{u}^{2})^{3/2}}\right).

In this space conditioned on

, the variables
\varepsilon_{u}=\mathbf{E}\left[\widehat{\ell}_{1}(u)\right]
for
u\in U_{1}
are not affected since they are independent from
\{\widehat{\ell}_{1}(u)\}_{u\in U_{3}}
.
Note that this expression only depends on

Fix another set of colors

This is an innovation from the proof in

.

By showing that this expression does not change much when

Let
H_{1}:={\mathbf{P}}(A_{v_{1}}\mid U_{1},U_{2},U_{3},H)

\left|H_{1}(\mathcal{J})-H_{1}\right|=O\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{pn}}\right),

The idea is not too different from the previous step.

4
Preliminaries bounds on binomial random variables

In this section we describe several useful inequalities to bound binomial random variables.
The first one is the usual Chernoff bound
[
9
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
, and
\varepsilon>0
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(X\geq(p+\varepsilon)n\right)\leq e^{-D(p+\varepsilon\|p)n}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\varepsilon<1-p,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(X\leq(p-\varepsilon)n\right)\leq e^{-D(p-\varepsilon\|p)n}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\varepsilon<p,

where
D(x\|y):=x\log\dfrac{x}{y}+(1-x)\log\dfrac{1-x}{1-y}
for
x,y\in(0,1)
.

In this paper, we mainly use the following corollary of the Chernoff bound:

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
and
t>0
. We have

{\mathbf{P}}\left(X=pn\pm t\sqrt{pn}\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2(1+\frac{t}{3\sqrt{pn}})}\right).

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon=tp^{1/2}n^{-1/2}
, so that
pn+t\sqrt{pn}=(p+\varepsilon)n
.
If
\varepsilon\geq 1-p
, the inequality trivially holds.
If
\varepsilon<1-p
, we have by Theorem
4.1
:

{\mathbf{P}}(X>pn+t\sqrt{pn})\leq\exp\left[-D\Bigl(p+t\frac{\sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\penalty\ \Big\|\penalty\ p\Bigr)n\right].

(18)

It is a well-known inequality that
D(x\|y)\geq 3(x-y)^{2}(2x+4y)^{-1}
for each
x,y\geq 0
.
Thus

D\Bigl(p+t\frac{\sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\penalty\ \Big\|\penalty\ p\Bigr)n\geq\frac{3\bigl(tp^{1/2}n^{-1/2}\bigr)^{2}}{2(3p+tp^{1/2}n^{-1/2})}n=\frac{t^{2}}{2(1+\frac{t}{3\sqrt{pn}})}.

Plugging in Eq. (
18
), we get the desired bound.

Consider the event
X<pn-t\sqrt{pn}=(p-\varepsilon)n
.
If
\varepsilon\geq p
, the inequality trivially holds.
If
\varepsilon<p
, i.e.
t<\sqrt{pn}
, we have by Theorem
4.1
:

{\mathbf{P}}(X<pn-t\sqrt{pn})\leq\exp\left[-D\Bigl(p-t\frac{\sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\penalty\ \Big\|\penalty\ p\Bigr)n\right].

(19)

We have

D\Bigl(p-t\frac{\sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\penalty\ \Big\|\penalty\ p\Bigr)n\geq\frac{3\bigl(tp^{1/2}n^{-1/2}\bigr)^{2}}{2(3p-tp^{1/2}n^{-1/2})}n=\frac{t^{2}}{2(1-\frac{t}{3\sqrt{pn}})}\geq\frac{t^{2}}{2(1+\frac{t}{3\sqrt{pn}})},

where the last inequality is due to
0<t<\sqrt{pn}
.
Plugging in Eq. (
19
), we get the desired bound.
∎

We quickly record another bound useful for binomial variables with small expectations:

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
and
t\geq epn
, we have:

{\mathbf{P}}(X>t)\leq 2\left(\frac{epn}{t}\right)^{t}
.

Proof.

For each integer
k\in[0,n]
,

{\mathbf{P}}(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}\leq\frac{n^{k}}{k!}p^{k}
.
Let
k=\lceil t\rceil
