Examining the role of social determinants of health in alcohol-exposed pregnancy risk among American Indian women
Authors: Jessica D. Hanson, Carolyn Noonan, Makenzie Clough, Robert Rosenman, Kyra Oziel, Karen Little Wounded, Michelle Sarche, Dedra Buchwald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Context and purposeAlcohol-exposed pregnancy (AEP) is a public health issue that impacts women from all backgrounds and nationalities. In particular, little is known about American Indian women at risk for AEP during the preconceptual period. The purpose of this paper is to characterize the social determinants of health among American Indian women at risk for AEP in the Great Plains.MethodsBaseline data with American Indian women enrolled in a randomized controlled trial of a culturally modified version of CHOICES, an AEP risk reduction intervention, were analyzed. Eligibility included age (18–44), self-identification as American Indian, current risky drinking, and the ability to get pregnant but not currently pregnant. Social determinants of health metrics comprised the broad categories of economic and housing stability, health care access, and social and community context.ResultsA total of 404 women with a mean age of 31.9 were enrolled. Most were unemployed (77%) and half reported a total annual household income of less than $5,000. Most (78%) considered their housing situation secure, although 43% had experienced homelessness in the past 6 months. Participants placed high importance on Native values and practices and reported strong social connections and social support.ConclusionsThis descriptive, cross-sectional study of American Indian women at risk for AEP highlighted the importance of queries on housing, economic factors, social support, and culture. Despite adverse social determinants of health, the fabric of social support and the importance of local cultural values was strong, factors that could be used in future AEP prevention efforts.

Background

Three factors influence risk for an alcohol-exposed pregnancy (AEP) during the preconceptual period: risky alcohol use, sexual activity, and birth control use [1]. Vulnerability to AEP is high when women engage in risky drinking while being at risk for an unintended pregnancy, meaning being sexually active and able to get pregnant but using ineffective, inconsistent, or no birth control [2]. Risky drinking is conceptualized as binge drinking, defined as consuming 4 or more drinks in 2 h for women [3]; or heavy drinking, defined as consuming 8 or more drinks per week [4]. Unintended pregnancies are problematic because women may not realize they are pregnant and thus unknowingly expose the fetus to alcohol during an especially vulnerable first trimester developmental period [1].

Data on the risk for AEP are inconsistent. Nationally representative studies conducted by the CDC, one in 2002–2003 and another in 2011–2013, found that the weighted prevalence of AEP risk among U.S. women aged 15–44 years ranged from 3.4% to 7.3% [5, 6], respectively. In contrast, a study from South Africa observed over 20% of sexually active women were at risk for AEP [7]. In 2004, data from Russia showed that between 32% and 54% of women would be classified as at risk for AEP depending on their residence, with the higher rates found in rural communities than urban locations [8]. More recent research concluded that surveillance on AEP risk is flawed, and that previous studies overestimated the prevalence of AEP [9].

One way to understand AEP is by examining social determinants of health (SDOH) to identify specific risk factors. According to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, SDOH are “the conditions in the environments where people are born, live, learn, work, play, worship, and age” that affect a wide range of health and quality of life outcomes and risks [10]. SDOH are often described as socioeconomic factors that impact health outcomes, such as stable incomes, wealth, educational attainment, and rank in an occupational hierarchy [11]. Other SDOH that can influence health in both positive and negative ways include housing, social inclusion, and access to affordable and quality health care [12, 13]. SDOH appear to be more important than lifestyle choices in influencing health [11–13], accounting for 30 to 55% of health outcomes [13].

The goal of this paper is to characterize SDOH and AEP risk among a sample of 404 American Indian (AI) women who participated in a randomized controlled trial of the Native CHOICES intervention. Native CHOICES, a cultural adaptation of the evidence-based CHOICES intervention, aimed to reduce risky drinking and/or increase effective contraceptive use among AI women at risk for AEP. In the trial, eligibility required that participants be non-pregnant AI women aged 18–44, sexually active with a male partner in the past three months, not using effective contraception, and engaging in risky drinking in the past three months [14]. Because data were collected at baseline prior to intervention exposure, the survey responses provide a unique opportunity to elucidate SDOH among this high-risk subset of AI women. Indeed, by focusing on women already identified as at risk for AEP, analyses of their baseline profiles allow for deeper insight into how economic stability, educational attainment, housing or transportation concerns, health care access, and other social-contextual factors may intersect with risky alcohol use and contraceptive practices in tribal and urban AI communities. Understanding SDOH—beyond traditional behavioral risk markers—is important because upstream social and structural factors may moderate both risk and responsiveness to interventions in AI populations.

Methods

Ethics and community engagement

Our tribal partner was involved in both the initial design of the study and its implementation. The study had an active Community Action Board, which included tribal elders, community leaders, and Indian Health Service providers, that offered advice and helped refine the intervention. The regional Indian Health Service Institutional Review Board and the lead investigator’s institution approved the study protocol and informed consent forms. This manuscript was also reviewed and approved by the regional Indian Health Service IRB. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolling in the study.

Recruitment and eligibility

Participants were recruited at both a reservation site and an urban site in the North Central United States (U.S.). To recruit participants, the study was advertised through social media and local media outlets. Participants were also recruited in community settings and events, including Women, Infants, and Children program offices, health fairs, powwows, the local community college, and word of mouth. Interested women completed a pre-screening survey to establish eligibility, including self-reported inclusion criteria for age (18–44) and race (AI). In addition, potential participants had to: (1) be able to get pregnant, defined as being sexually active with a male partner in the past 3 months, (2) not currently be using effective contraception defined by standard methods [15], (3) self-report heavy drinking (8 or more standard drinks in a week) or binge drinking (4 or more standard drinks in a two hour period) in the past 3 months, (4) live in one of 8 counties that encompass both the reservation and urban sites, and (5) not live in a household with a participant enrolled in the trial.

Data collection

After establishing eligibility, participants were invited to schedule the baseline data collection within a week either in-person or by telephone, depending on the tribal public health guidelines during the pandemic. For both the in-person and telephone surveys, survey questions were read to participants whose verbal responses were then entered into a REDCap electronic database system [16]. All participants received a $30 check after completing their baseline data collection.

Measures

Demographic questions included categorial measures of age, race, employment, household income, education, marital status, current residence, housing security, and smoking status. Participants also completed questions on knowledge and beliefs about alcohol use during pregnancy and fetal alcohol syndrome (Table 1) [17]. Depression was assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CESD) Scale, 10-item version [18]. Scoring included reverse coding positive items and summing all items, where higher scores reflect more depressive symptoms and scores of 10 or above are indicative of depression [18]. For the CESD, n = 264 (65%) of the participants responded to all the items; one question was inadvertently skipped for the rest of participants thereby impacting the ability to score the screening tool.

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, a 10-item screening tool, was used to identify risky drinking patterns or active alcohol use disorders; a score of 15 or more indicates the likelihood of alcohol dependence [19]. Table 2 shows additional questions on risky drinking taken from the original CHOICES intervention [20]. Participants were asked about vaginal intercourse and birth control, if any, they used over the past 3 months. Participants who did not use birth control at any sexual encounter were not asked subsequent questions about contraception. Those who used at least one method were asked questions developed by the CDC to determine if contraception was used effectively [15].

Participants were also asked about their current form of health insurance (e.g., Medicaid, Medicare, private insurance, Indian Health Services). Next, the EQ-5D-5 L was used to compute a quality-of-life index using the U.S. value set, with values ranging from 1 to 5 and higher scores indicating worse health. The EQ-5D-5 L also asked respondents to assess their overall health on a 0 to 100 scale with 100 being “the best health you can imagine” and 0 being “the worst health you can imagine” [21]. Finally, social and cultural context measures included questions on identity (see Table 3), spirituality and religion, instrumental social support [22], and social connectedness, with higher scores indicating more social connectedness [23].

Data analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics for demographic characteristics, health behaviors, and SDOH. Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviation for continuous variables. We performed an available-case analysis; therefore, the number of missing values differs for some variables. Summing percentages for all categories of a variable may not equal 100% due to rounding error.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 408 individuals completed the baseline data collection; 4 were later discovered to be ineligible, leaving 404 for data analysis. All participants self-identified as AI; 5% also identified ethnically as Hispanic or Latina. The mean age of participants was 31.9 years old (SD = 6.6 years). For marital status, 57% were single and never married, 17% had a partner or a significant other, 13% were divorced, separated, or widowed, and 12% were currently married. The highest level of education completed by participants included less than a high school diploma (36%), a high school diploma or GED (36%), some college or vocational training (21%), and graduating college or a higher degree (5%). A total of 245 (61%) women lived on the reservation most of the time, 107 (26%) lived in a small town or rural area, and 52 (13%) lived in an urban area. Most (65%) women were current smokers. For those that answered all CESD questions, the mean depression score was 12.3 (SD = 7.2); 59% scored 10 or higher, indicating depression. Knowledge and beliefs about AEP are detailed in Table 1.

SDOH: economic stability

Many participants were unemployed and receiving government or tribal assistance (46%) or were unemployed without government assistance (31%); only 20% were employed full- or part-time. Half of participants had a total annual household income of less than $5,000; 17% reported household incomes between $5,000 and $20,000; and 4% had household incomes over $20,001. Most (78%) considered their housing situation secure; 57% lived in a permanent residence. However, 46% stated it was likely where they were currently living would change in the next 1–3 months, and 43% had experienced homelessness in the past 6 months.

SDOH: health care

Most (65%) women received health care through the Indian Health Service, through Medicaid or Medicare (52%), or private insurance (3%). Only 7% had no form of health care coverage. The averages of the individual EQ-5D-5 L components, where higher scores indicate worse health, include mobility (1.21, SD = 0.58), self-care (1.06, SD = 0.31), usual activities (1.22, SD = 0.55), pain/discomfort (1.60, SD = 0.92), and anxiety/depression (1.88, SD = 1.05). The mean quality of life index value, created from the individual EQ-5D-5 L responses, was 0.86 (SD = 0.19) while the mean response for the assessment of health was 71.4 (SD = 21.4).

SDOH: social context and identity

Table 3 highlights the reported identity of participants. In addition, the mean score on the Social Connectedness Scale – Revised was 81.2 (SD = 15.0), with a range of 42 to 115. For instrumental social support, 95% of women had someone to go to a community event with; lend them money if needed in an emergency (82%); lend them a car or drive them somewhere (74%); and check in on them regularly (85%). Only 46% of participants could call someone to post bail if they were arrested or in jail. Religious identity included traditional Native beliefs (25%), the Native American Church (17%); Protestant (16%); Catholic (12%), other religious beliefs (15%) and no religious identity (21%). Only 25% spent time every day or almost every day on spiritual or religious activities.

Discussion

We described the SDOH among a sample of rural and urban AI women living in the North Central U.S. who were at risk for AEP. Most were unemployed, compared to a 1.6% unemployment rate in women of similar age in this geographic area [24]. Likewise, the sample’s reported annual household income – with half having $5,000 or less annual income - compares to the U.S. median household income of $70,784 [25], a median household income of $58,275 in this geographic area (all races), and a median household income of $27,902 for AI/AN people living in this geographic area [26]. These socioeconomic disparities are concerning, as health and illness follow a “social gradient”: the lower the socioeconomic position, the worse a person’s health [13]. In other studies, compared to individuals with family incomes at or above 400% of the federal poverty level, those with lower incomes were almost 5 times more likely to report being in fair or poor health and more than 3 times as likely to have activity limitations due to chronic illness [27]. Given the relatively young age of Native CHOICES participants, a striking finding was the high quality of life indicated by the EQ-5D-5 L index in the context of relatively low assessments of their current health.

Income can impact access to quality education. Often seen as a “driver of opportunity but also as a reproducer of inequality”, adults with higher educational attainment tend to live healthier and longer lives [28]. A stable income affects the ability to purchase quality food, neighborhood and living conditions, and access to reliable health care, all of which directly influence health status [29]. Of note, our sample experienced low educational attainment and lower income levels, compared to 79.7% of AI women in this geographic area of the U.S. who had a high school degree or higher [30]. Lastly, our housing disparities reflect the overrepresentation of AI people among the homeless population. Although they represent approximately 8% of the population in different states in the North Central U.S., AIs make up nearly 60% of the unhoused population [31].

Despite an abundance of socioeconomic disparities highlighted in this sample of women at risk for AEP, cultural identity, social support, and social connectedness were high. While not clearly measured in this study, enculturation has been found to protect against alcohol use among AIs [32]. Although a previous study found that enculturation was positively associated with anxiety, this association became non-significant when accounting for cultural efficacy, or one’s confidence to learn about and engage in cultural activities and traditional knowledge [33]. Community connections, social support, and cultural connectedness have also been observed to be a protective factors for substance use, depression, and mental health in AI youth and older adults [34–36]. Likewise, AI individuals who reported high instrumental social support were less likely to report obstacles when seeking substance use treatment [37]. Finally, other studies have found increased risk for AEP in Hispanic women with higher acculturation and less social support [38].

There are limitations to this study. First, the study sample may not be representative of all AI women, particularly because participants were enrolled in the context of an AEP prevention intervention, which may have introduced selection bias. Second, data were self-reported, which may be subject to recall and social desirability biases, particularly for sensitive topics such as income, alcohol use, and housing stability. Third, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits our ability to infer causal relationships between SDOH and the other variables. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insight into the interplay of SDOH, resilience, and risk among AI women at risk for AEP, underscoring the importance of culturally grounded approaches to prevention and health promotion.

Conclusion

In a sample of AI women living in the North Central U.S. who were at risk for AEP, SDOH measures help us understand AEP risk. While these data do not represent all AIAN women at risk for AEP, our findings give a snapshot of SDOH that can be leveraged to inform AEP interventions. In particular, the strong social and cultural connectedness found in this sample appears essential for prevention efforts. As noted recently by the United States Surgeon General (2023), an epidemic of isolation and loneliness has engulfed the nation, and building upon strong social support, connection, and Native identity holds promise to improve health outcomes. Incorporating strategies to encourage social and cultural connections and social support into Native CHOICES or other AEP prevention programs holds promise to improve health outcomes on multiple levels.
