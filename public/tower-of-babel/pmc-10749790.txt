RNet: a network strategy to predict RNA binding preferences
Authors: Haoquan Liu, Yiren Jian, Jinxuan Hou, Chen Zeng, Yunjie Zhao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetermining the RNA binding preferences remains challenging because of the bottleneck of the binding interactions accompanied by subtle RNA flexibility. Typically, designing RNA inhibitors involves screening thousands of potential candidates for binding. Accurate binding site information can increase the number of successful hits even with few candidates. There are two main issues regarding RNA binding preference: binding site prediction and binding dynamical behavior prediction. Here, we propose one interpretable network-based approach, RNet, to acquire precise binding site and binding dynamical behavior information. RNetsite employs a machine learning-based network decomposition algorithm to predict RNA binding sites by analyzing the local and global network properties. Our research focuses on large RNAs with 3D structures without considering smaller regulatory RNAs, which are too small and dynamic. Our study shows that RNetsite outperforms existing methods, achieving precision values as high as 0.701 on TE18 and 0.788 on RB9 tests. In addition, RNetsite demonstrates remarkable robustness regarding perturbations in RNA structures. We also developed RNetdyn, a distance-based dynamical graph algorithm, to characterize the interface dynamical behavior consequences upon inhibitor binding. The simulation testing of competitive inhibitors indicates that RNetdyn outperforms the traditional method by 30%. The benchmark testing results demonstrate that RNet is highly accurate and robust. Our interpretable network algorithms can assist in predicting RNA binding preferences and accelerating RNA inhibitor design, providing valuable insights to the RNA research community.Keywords: RNA binding site prediction, interface binding dynamical behavior, local and global network properties, distance-based graph algorithm

INTRODUCTION

Many RNA molecules interact with proteins or small molecules to perform various biological processes. RNA binding preferences play a significant role in biological processes, including gene transcription, protein translation and disease occurrence [1–4]. For example, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a devastating retrovirus that attacks human T cells [5, 6]. The HIV viral protein Tat interacts with the cellular positive transcription elongation factor b to initiate the viral transcription [7]. To overcome the elongation pause, P-TEFb (CDK9/CyclinT1 complex) is recruited by Tat onto the viral transactivation response element (TAR) RNA, suggesting that the binding interface between TAR and P-TEFb is crucial in the HIV transcription elongation [8–11]. The thymidylate synthase (TS) binding to the mRNA hairpin motif prevents the production of TS through a translation-repression feedback mechanism. Stabilizing the mRNA hairpin by ligands can reduce TS levels in the anticancer therapy [12]. The RNA genome of the hepatitis C virus (HCV) binds the 40S ribosomal subunit to initiate protein translation. Recruitment of the ribosomal subunit to the HCV RNA is driven by the high affinity of the IRES-40S interaction [13, 14]. The inhibition of the RNA-binding protein YTHDF2 triggered apoptosis in human triple-negative breast cancer cell lines and hindered the growth of xenografted tumors in vivo [15, 16].

Accurately predicting the binding preferences of RNA and biomolecules is crucial for understanding the regulatory mechanisms and developing inhibitors [17–19]. The demand for understanding RNA binding preferences is constantly increasing. However, the experiment cost of determining the RNA binding preferences is expensive. There is an urgent need for computational techniques to predict RNA binding preferences accurately. The massive growth of systematic and comprehensive mapping of the physical interactions between RNA and other molecules provides an opportunity to utilize the network approach to predict RNA binding preferences [20].

The prediction of RNA binding preferences can be divided into two problems: predicting binding sites and dynamical binding behavior. Currently, there are several computational methods to predict RNA binding sites [21]. For example, Rsite and Rsite2 are computational methods based on RNA structure (Rsite) or RNA secondary structure (Rsite2) [22, 23]. Rsite identifies binding sites for each nucleotide by calculating the extreme Euclidean distances from other sites. Rsite2 replaces the tertiary Euclidean distance with a secondary structure Euclidean distance. Both models only tested on small data sets and showed poor accuracy. RBind transforms RNA structure into a network and utilizes the network properties [degree (DG) and closeness] to identify the binding sites [24]. However, the strict thresholds of network features prevent RBind from identifying binding sites with highly accurate predictions. RNAsite uses a machine learning-based method with two network properties [DG, closeness centrality (CL)], two structural features (accessible surface area, Laplacian norm) and evolutionary information for binding site prediction [25]. However, the method requires too many geometrical features from experimental structures, leading to a significant error if some feature perturbations exist. For the dynamical binding behavior prediction, Anurag Sethi et al. [26] proposed the dynamical networks from the simulation trajectory to calculate the correlation coefficient values for the allosteric interaction prediction. For example, Anurag Sethi et al. successfully identified the allostery communications in the aaRS:tRNA complex. However, this dynamical network model shows poor accuracy when it comes to interface dynamical behavior applications. Therefore, there is an urgent need for new computational methods to predict RNA binding preferences.

Here, we propose a network strategy called RNet, which aims to predict RNA binding preferences. We use tertiary structures to generate networks, with nucleotides/residues as nodes and non-covalent interactions as edges. RNetsite predicts RNA binding sites using a machine learning-based model that considers global and local network features. The model has been extensively evaluated on published testing sets, which include large RNAs with tertiary structures. The smaller and dynamically regulatory RNA structures are not considered as they are small and constantly changing. RNetdyn utilizes a distance-based dynamical network for interface dynamical binding behavior prediction, which can help researchers see the consequences of inhibitor binding. The results consistently outperform existing methods in predicting binding site and interface dynamical binding behavior. RNet employs straightforward pure network analysis and machine learning algorithms to provide an interpretable model. This allows us to understand the innate network properties of RNA and gain insight into its binding preferences. We expect the technique described here will be useful for RNA mechanism study and inhibitor design.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data sets for RNA-ligand binding site prediction

We utilized three published tertiary structure data sets for training and evaluating the binding sites prediction model RNetsite [25]. Previously, Su et al. [27] collected RNA-ligand structures with RNA chain lengths between 20 and 1500 nt from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). They considered the low molecular weight small molecules (1000 daltons) as ligands that consist of metal ions, natural metabolites or other drug-like molecules (excluding water molecules and crystallization additives). Thus, 712 RNAs were obtained for further redundancy calculation. TM-scoreRNA was used to distinguish the RNA structure similarity of RNA chains with a pairwise structure similarity cutoff of 0.3 [28]. The structure with the most binding site residues is retained when the TM-scoreRNA is higher than 0.3. The remaining 78 RNAs were divided into 60 RNA structures as the training set (denoted by TR60) and 18 RNAs as the testing set (denoted by TE18) based on sequence similarity clustering according to the RNAsite description. The third testing data set is obtained from a published benchmark of 19 RNA-ligand complex structures previously used in RBind and RNAsite methods. After removing the redundant RNAs between the training set TR60 and 19 testing RNAs, we identified the nine nonredundant RNAs as RB9. The experimental binding sites are the nucleotides within 4 Å distance from the ligand. In contrast to RNAsite, which uses a lot of structural geometric information, RNetsite exclusively utilizes network properties extracted from tertiary structures to provide higher-order coarse-grained topological information. Details about the data sets are summarized in Tables S1–S3. The training and testing data sets can be downloaded from http://zhaoserver.com.cn/RNet/RNet.html. It is worth noting that TE18 and RB9 encompass diverse types of experimentally well-characterized RNAs, including aptamers, viral RNAs, ribozymes, rRNAs and riboswitches (Table S4). Such broad types of experimentally verified RNA structures are instrumental in showcasing the strong predictive capability of RNetsite for RNA-related applications.

Network properties used in RNet

The RNA/protein structure is transformed into a network. The main components of a network are nodes and edges. In the network, RNet denotes a single nucleotide/residue as a node and the non-covalent interactions as edges. We introduce local and global network properties to identify the node’s importance. The local properties are DG and neighborhood connectivity (NC). The global properties are betweenness centrality (BC), CL and eccentricity (EC).

DG and NC describe the connection ability to neighbors and secondary neighbors. The DG refers to the number of edges directly connected to a node. Performance metrics for binding site prediction

We utilized precision, recall and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) to evaluate the performance of the binding sites prediction model RNetsite. We calculated the evaluation metrics as follows:

The TP (FP) represents true (false) positive, i.e. the number of true (false) binding sites among the predicted binding sites. The TN represents the true negative, i.e. the number of true nonbinding sites among the predicted nonbinding sites. The FN represents false negative, i.e. the number of true binding sites the method fails to predict. Precision is the proportion of the correctly predicted binding sites out of the total number of predicted binding sites. Recall gives the fraction of true binding sites that can be predicted. MCC is a correlation coefficient between the true and predicted classes.

We performed statistical tests to determine if the performance improvements in RNetsite are significant, following the procedure outlined in reference [34]. We conducted a statistical analysis to compare the performance (precision, recall and MCC) of RNetsite and other existing methods. We randomly selected half of the RNA from the testing data sets and repeated the process 10 times to ensure accuracy. To analyze the results, we evaluated the normality of the measurements using the Anderson–Darling test. We used a paired t-test to analyze if the measurements were normally distributed. Otherwise, we utilized the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. We also used a criterion of P < 0.05 for our analysis.

RESULTS

Modeling RNA binding propensity in networks

The network approach provides an opportunity to predict RNA binding preferences. RNet includes two algorithms: RNetsite for predicting RNA binding sites using machine learning-based network decomposition and RNetdyn for characterizing interface binding behavior through a distance-based dynamical graph algorithm.

We introduced two local and three global network properties to calculate the impact of the network on RNA binding propensity prediction. Local properties are DG and NC to describe the importance of nodes in the neighborhood. The global properties are CL, BC and EC to describe the importance of nodes in the whole network.

In RNet, RNA native structures or simulation trajectories are transformed into networks (Figure 2A and B). In Figure 2A, we convert an RNA tertiary structure into a network for binding site prediction. The nucleotide units are represented as yellow nodes. The non-covalent interactions within an 8 Å cutoff are depicted as edges (gray lines) between the nodes according to RBind and RNAsite [24, 25]. Figure 2B illustrates the conversion of an RNA–protein complex into a network to determine whether the interface of the complex is disintegrating. The nucleotide/residue units are shown as yellow/pink nodes. The non-covalent interactions within a 4.5 Å cutoff are indicated as edges between the nodes according to DCC [26]. The gray lines are the RNA or protein structure interactions, and the red-dashed lines are the RNA–protein interface interactions. RNetsite decomposes the local and global network properties for the machine learning algorithm to identify binding sites (Figure 2C). Research has shown that RNA is an attractive target for binding small molecules [41–43]. We utilized three published data sets of RNA-small molecule binding for training and testing purposes [25]. RNetdyn utilizes a distance-based dynamical graph algorithm to characterize the coupled binding motions in the simulation trajectories (Figure 2D). The local and global perspectives (Figure 2E and F) can be used to describe the binding dynamical behaviors. We introduced the DDNC (Figure 2G) to characterize the coupled binding motions at the interface. We applied RNetdyn in simulations of the TAR/Tat/P-TEFb complex upon different inhibitors (competitive inhibitor JB181 and allosteric inhibitor F07#13) to study the interface dynamical binding behavior between TAR and P-TEFb.

Application to RNA binding site prediction

Model construction

We trained the network topological properties using three machine learning algorithms: random forest (RF), light gradient boosting machine (LGBM) and extreme gradient boosting (XGB), to predict RNA binding sites (Figure 3). First, we transformed the RNA tertiary structure into a network with nodes representing nucleotides and edges denoting the noncovalent interactions [24]. According to RBind and RNAsite, nonconsecutive nucleotides are considered connected if the distance between any pair of heavy atoms is <8 Å (Figure 3A and B). Second, we calculated the local and global properties of the network feature descriptions (Figure 3C). Since the nucleotide in RNA is affected by its surrounding nucleotides, we encoded each nucleotide’s training features by combining the neighbors’ network properties within the adjacent windows (size ). Thus, each nucleotide possessed training features. As the minimum RNA length in the training set TR60 is 20, the parameter optimization process selected a range from 0 to 9 to ensure that < 20. The encoded 1D features were used for machine learning algorithms training (Figure 3D and E). The proposed method, RNetsite, utilizes a voting process to combine prediction results from RNetsite-RF, RNetsite-LGBM and RNetsite-XGB. If the prediction is consistent across more than two models for each sample, it will be chosen as the result for the RNetsite model.

Optimizing the parameters of machine learning algorithms

To maximize the performance of the MCC value, we employed 5-fold cross-validation to tune the machine learning algorithms parameters on the training set TR60. For each parameter, we repeated 50 times the 5-fold cross-validation and reported the average MCC values.

To optimize the performance of RNetsite-RF, we focused on tuning two parameters: the sliding window size (w) and the number of trees (n). First, we fixed the number of trees at n = 100 and explored various window sizes w ranging from 0 to 9. The performances of the MCC values with different window sizes w are shown in Figure S1A. The MCC value peaks at 0.228 with w = 3 and declines rapidly. Therefore, the optimal w for RNetsite-RF was determined to be 3. Subsequently, we fixed window size w = 3 to optimize the number of trees with n = 10, 50, 100, 200 and 500. The performance of the model with different numbers of trees n is presented in Figure S1B. The lowest MCC value of 0.202 is observed when n = 10, followed by an increase in MCC from 10 to 200. Therefore, the optimal number of trees for RNetsite-RF was 200.

For RNetsite-LGBM, we need to tune three parameters: the sliding window size (w), max_bin (controls the maximum number of bins that features will be bucketed into) and num_leaves (controls the complexity of the tree model). We fixed max_bin = 20 and num_leaves = 5 to optimize the window size w. The performances of the MCC values with different window sizes w are shown in Figure S2A. The optimal w for RNetsite-LGBM was determined to be 7. We also employed a grid search using the GridSearchCV program from scikit-learn to explore max_bin values from 10 to 100 (in increments of 10) and num_leaves values from 5 to 50 (in increments of 5). The performance of the model with various pairs of (max_bin, num_leaves) is shown in Figure S2B. The highest MCC is 0.295 when (max_bin, num_leaves) = (60, 20), which were determined as the optimal parameters.

For RNetsite-XGB, we need to tune three parameters: the sliding window size (w), learning rate (LR) and gamma (minimum loss reduction required to make a further partition on a leaf node of the tree). We fixed LR = 0.01 and 0 to optimize the window size w ranging from 0 to 9. The performances of the MCC values with different window sizes w are shown in Figure S3A. The optimal w for RNetsite-XGB was determined to be 8. Same as LGBM, a grid search was employed to explore values of LR from 0.01 to 0.1 (in increments of 0.01) and gamma from 0 to 1 (in increments of 0.1). The performance of the model with various pairs of (LR, gamma) is shown in Figure S3B. The highest MCC is 0.292 when (LR, gamma) = (0.07, 1), which were determined as the optimal parameters.

Comparison with existing methods

We compared our machine learning-based methods with the existing widely used RNA binding site prediction methods (Rsite, Rsite2, RBind and RNAsite) on the TE18 and RB9 data set. Rsite, Rsite2 and RBind predict the RNA binding sites using information from tertiary structure (Rsite and RBind) or secondary structure (Rsite2). RNAsite uses tertiary structure, accessible surface area and conservation-based information to identify the RNA binding sites.

To assess the performance of our method, we calculated the precision, recall and MCC values. For testing data set TE18, RNetsite, RNetsite-RF, RNetsite-LGBM and RNetsite-XGB perform with precision of 0.701, 0.699, 0.651 and 0.642, compared with 0.449 for Rsite, 0.370 for Rsite2, 0.655 for RBind and 0.675 for RNA site (Figure 4A). The results suggest that the binding sites identified by RNetsite are more precise. RNetsite and RNetsite-RF have higher precision compared with other existing methods. RNetsite-LGBM and RNetsite-XGB exhibit comparable precision to other established methods. Similarly, the recall values of RNetsite, RNetsite-RF, RNetsite-LGBM and RNetsite-XGB are 0.357, 0.326, 0.396 and 0.325 (Figure 4B), compared with 0.288 for Rsite, 0.214 for Rsite2, 0.173 for RBind and 0.263 for RNAsite. Our methods have superior recall values compared with existing ones. Based on the results, it seems that RNetsite-LGBM can detect more binding sites. Besides, the MCC value of RNetsite, RNetsite-RF, RNetsite-LGBM and RNetsite-XGB are 0.307, 0.273, 0.301 and 0.265 (Figure 4C), compared with 0.071 for Rsite, 0.010 for Rsite2, 0.187 for Rbind and 0.253 for RNAsite. For testing data set RB9, RNetsite, RNetsite-RF, RNetsite-LGBM and RNetsite-XGB achieved precision scores of 0.788, 0.756, 0.737 and 0.778, respectively. In comparison, Rsite and Rsite2 achieved precision scores of 0.430 and 0.382, whereas RBind and RNAsite achieved scores of 0.681 and 0.750 (Figure S4A). The results indicate that RNetsite achieves better accuracy on the RB9 data set than other methods. Similarly, the recall values for RNetsite, RNetsite-RF, RNetsite-LGBM and RNetsite-XGB are 0.458, 0.374, 0.516 and 0.471, compared with 0.353 for Rsite, 0.187 for Rsite2, 0.230 for RBind and 0.378 for RNAsite (Figure S4B). RNetsite exhibits better recall performance compared with existing methods. Notably, The RNetsite-LGBM model performs exceptionally well in identifying more binding sites. Additionally, when we consider the MCC values, RNetsite, RNetsite-RF, RNetsite-LGBM and RNetsite-XGB achieve scores of 0.465, 0.385, 0.476 and 0.477 (Figure S4C), in contrast to 0.159 for Rsite, 0.072 for Rsite2, 0.278 for Rbind and 0.426 for RNAsite.

Analyzing the contributions of local and global features in RNetsite-RF

The RF repeats the sample data to train each decision tree. Out-of-bag (OOB) data refers to data that has not been used. The OOB data can be used to calculate the prediction error rate of the model, which is called the OOB error rate [44]. The contributions of local and global features to the RF algorithm were calculated by the OOB error rate. The importance of a feature is the OOB error rate after randomly permuting the values of the feature. If a feature influences the model, then permuting its values should affect the model error. Figure S5 shows that local and global features are crucial in predicting RNA-ligand binding sites, with CL and NC having the highest and second-highest importance values, respectively.

Application to RNA interface binding behavior prediction

Model construction

We used a distance-based network model to predict RNA–protein binding behavior dynamically. First, we converted each frame of the TAR/Tat/P-TEFb simulations into a network. The residue/nucleotide units are defined as nodes, and the edges between nodes are represented with a cutoff of 4.5 Å. Second, we build a distance-based dynamical network model to characterize the interface binding dynamical behavior. While the traditional method DCC studies the consistency of motion direction to determine whether the interface is disintegrating, it may not accurately account for the impact of different velocities on the interface. To address this issue, we have introduced the DDNC analysis, which considers direction and velocity to describe interface binding behavior precisely. We hypothesize that the native complex is equilibrium. Thus, the motion correlation coefficient of the native complex is set to 0. The equilibrium reference position for each pair of nodes satisfies the following requirements: (i) the inner product of the position vector formed with two nodes is 0. (ii) Closest to the average position of the binding interface. Third, we analyze the local and global network properties described in the dynamical network features. The distance-based dynamical network approach RNetdyn can dynamically characterize the interface binding preference for the RNA complex.

We tested the RNetdyn using two kinds of simulations in the TAR/Tat/P-TEFb system. One example is the competitive inhibitor JB181 binding to TAR RNA, which breaks up the TAR/Cyclin T1 interface in the TAR/Tat/P-TEFb complex according to previous experiments [45]. The other example is the allosteric inhibitor F07#13 binding to CDK9, which can pull down TAR RNA from P-TEFb, leading to Tat degradation through long-range interactions [46]. However, the details of the breaking-up process are unknown, and obtaining precise information about the binding dynamical behaviors at the interface can be challenging, requiring complicated experimental techniques such as Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM). Therefore, we aim to use short-term simulations to study the binding dynamical behavior at the interface during the breaking-up process. These results can be compared with extensive computational simulations.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the DDNC accurately captured the interface binding behavior.

Comparison with existing method

We evaluated and compared our method with the widely used network model proposed to characterize the DCC. Our method defines equilibrium reference position as the interface in the native complex for the position vectors of the node pair and to integrate direction and velocity to describe interface binding behavior. However, DCC utilizes the average position of the node in each frame during the simulations as the reference point to construct the vector . Thus, DCC may not accurately account for the impact of different velocities on the interface, and is difficult to characterize the interface stability between pairs of nodes.

To assess the performance of our method, we focused on the breaking-up process of the TAR/CyclinT1 interface and calculated the precision values for 101–700 frames of the JB181 simulation and 901–1200 frames of the F07#13 simulation. For the JB181 simulation, the analysis results of DDNC show that the interface breaks up in Figure 5A. Then, the interface shows fluctuations (Figure 5B–E) and finally breaks up (Figure 5F). The stable and unstable interaction numbers determined by DCC are displayed in Figure S7G. The average accuracy of the interface dynamical behavior is 0.833 (Figure 6A and Table S17). However, the analysis results of DCC show a different breaking-up process. The interface becomes breaking up in Figure S7A. Then, the interface returns to a stable state (Figure S7B–E) and finally breaks up (Figure S7F). The average accuracy of the interface dynamical behavior is 0.528 (Figure 6B and Table S17). DDNC shows significant improvement over the traditional DCC for interface binding behavior analysis. The computational analysis of the DDNC model indicates that DDNC can accurately capture the interface binding behavior, which is consistent with simulation results. For the F07#13 simulation, Figure S6A–C (Figure S6E–G) shows the transition of DDNC (DCC) in 901–1200 frames. The DDNC performs an accuracy of 0.694 compared with 0.556 for the widely used DCC model (Figure S6D and H and Table S18). The results demonstrate that the DDNC accurately captured the interface binding behavior, with a 13.8% improvement over the traditional DCC method, even for the challenging allosteric inhibitor binding simulations.

Binding dynamical behavior analysis

We aimed to develop a network model to elucidate the binding dynamical behavior of biomolecular complexes. From the TAR/Tat/P-TEFb simulations upon JB181 binding, we divided 700 frames into three periods: unstable period I (frames 101–200), stable period (frames 201–500) and unstable period II (frames 501–700). Since CDK9/CyclinT1 remained stable during the simulation, we systematically analyzed and investigated five local and global network properties for the binding nucleotides/residues located on TAR/CyclinT1 and CDK9/CyclinT1 interfaces in each period. For each period, we calculated the average values of all the features. As depicted in Figure S8, DG, NC, CL and EC values can distinguish the interface binding dynamical behaviors. The BC property remained relatively stable. The weakening of the binding interactions of nodes in the interface may lead to decreased edges connected to those nodes. Moreover, nodes in the interface acquire large EC and minor CL values when the binding interface is pulled apart.

Figure 7B, D and F shows that the DG, NC and CL values of interface nucleotides decrease, whereas the EC values of interface nucleotides increase during the breaking-up process. The changes in such properties primarily result from reduced connections associated with the interface nucleotides. Consequently, the decreased interactions at the interface break up the complex into two independent networks.

The process of TAR pulling down from Tat/P-TEFb. (A-B) Unstable period I. (C-D) Stable period. (E-F) Unstable period II. (A, C, E) The interaction states in each period. (B, D, F) Network properties DG, NC, CL, and EC in each period.

It is noted that the CL values of nucleotides C8, C13 and C14 decrease rapidly, whereas U15, G16 and G17 increase slowly (Figure 7B, D and F). To explain the fluctuations of CL values, we analyzed the interaction breaking-up process in detail. We define an interaction as stable if it exists in more than 50% of the frames within the period. During the simulation, the nucleotides C8, C13 and C14 break up, and then nucleotides U15, G16 and G17 become unstable (Figure 7A, C and E). The initial nucleotides (C8, C13 and C14) break up, reducing the communication between those nucleotides and P-TEFb, resulting in a fast decrease in the CL value. Conversely, the remaining connecting nucleotides (U15, G16 and G17) undertake more communication of the complex, leading to a slighter decrease in the CL value.

Together with DG, NC, CL and EC properties, the breaking-up nucleotides lead to decreased DG, NC, CL, and increased EC. The nucleotides with low binding energy possess faster decreased CL. The nucleotides with high binding energy possess slower decreased CL. Our method accurately characterizes the behavior interface binding behavior. With more simulation trajectory data, these network properties can facilitate RNA binding site prediction with binding strength information.

DISCUSSIONS AND CONCLUSION

RNA binding preference prediction is still a relatively unexplored research field. We aim to utilize straightforward pure network and machine learning models to provide an interpretable RNA binding preference prediction strategy. This can be extremely beneficial for gaining insights and improving our knowledge of RNA binding preferences. Accurate binding preference prediction information can facilitate the RNA inhibitor design with only a tiny pool of candidates.

Previous efforts focused on RNA binding site prediction using sensitive geometrical features, such as the laplacian norm, accessible surface area, etc. The bottleneck of prior methods is significantly affected by RNA flexibility, especially the structure perturbations. RNetsite utilizes only network input information to provide high prediction accuracy and robustness for structure perturbations. We examined the model tolerance by perturbing structure data, which reflects realistic scenarios where RNA structure exhibits flexibility. To evaluate the extent of structure perturbation, we calculated the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the native structure and the perturbing structure. The native structures (RMSD = 0) were obtained from the PDB data set. We perturbed the RNA structure by using RNAComposer, 3DRNA and Vfold3D [47–49]. For each RNA in the test data set TE18, we assessed the precision, recall and MCC of our RNetsite model on a set of predictive structures with various RMSDs (within 3–6, 30 Å). As shown in Figure S9A–C, the performance of RNetsite is exceptional even when there are structural perturbations. Even with RMSD up to 6 Å, RNetsite achieves a precision of 0.615. The results indicate that RNetsite is robust and not significantly affected by structure perturbation. We conducted an in-depth analysis of the correlation between RMSD and precision, recall and MCC (Figure S9D–F). Here, precision, recall and MCC represent the difference of precision, recall and MCC between the predicted structure and the native structure. Our analysis yielded correlation coefficients of −0.232, −0.053 and −0.144, respectively. The results further demonstrate that 3D structural perturbations have a limited impact on the accuracy of RNetsite. RNetsite outperforms other methods, offering ease of implementation, fast processing speed and strong robustness.

The RNA interface binding dynamical behavior is still a challenging problem. The previous dynamical network model shows poor accuracy for interface binding behavior applications. We utilized a geometrically intuitive physical model to define the interface binding correlation. We assume that the interface binding dynamical correlation of the crystal complex structure is zero. The model aims to ensure that the inner product of position vectors of node pairs is 0 at the initial time by finding an appropriate reference point for each pair of nodes. RNetdyn enables precise analysis of individual residues and nucleotides’ binding status and dynamical behavior at the interface. Compared with the current method, RNetdyn significantly improves the precision of binding interface analysis.

We introduced the TAR RNA in the TAR/Tat/P-TEFb complex system for RNet testing to provide one concrete example. First, we conducted the RNetsite to predict the inhibitor binding site to TAR RNA, achieving a high precision of 0.800 (Figure S10). Then, we performed MD simulations to analyze the interface binding dynamical behavior consequences of the TAR/Tat/P-TEFb upon inhibitor JB181 binding. The competitive inhibitor JB181 breaks up the TAR/Cyclin T1 interface during the simulations, consistent with previous experiments [45]. The study revealed that RNA hairpin motifs, such as TAR RNA, are promising drug targets to regulate cellular functions.

We recently successfully designed two competitive inhibitors to RNA hairpin motifs, SL3 and RBE RNA, with the help of our proposed network strategy model [50]. The SL3 RNA interacts with the NC protein to recognize and package the HIV-1 genome. One of the inhibitors, L22#15, has shown promising results with a low micromolar binding affinity of ~5 M in NMR experiments, surpassing the efficacy of other SL3 RNA inhibitors. The other example is the RBE RNA, which regulates viral gene expression by interacting with the HIV-1 Rev protein. The inhibitor L22 + 16, also achieves around 11 M binding affinity. These results indicate that our RNet model can effectively aid in developing RNA inhibitors for RNA hairpin motifs. In the future, we aim to explore more RNA motifs to facilitate engineering for biological or medical purposes.

In this work, we used the available published training and testing data sets to evaluate the performance for fair comparison. It can be regarded as the most comprehensive and representative RNA-ligand data set available. However, the smaller regulatory RNAs, such as snRNAs and IRE-containing mRNAs, cannot form 3D folds because of their small size and dynamic nature. As a result, the existing benchmarks for training and testing based on structures extracted from PDB do not include the smaller regulatory RNAs. Recently, deep learning has demonstrated remarkable success in various fields, including protein structure prediction problems. For example, AlphaFold, a neural network-based model, demonstrated competitive accuracy with experimental structures in most cases and outperformed other methods in CASP14 [51]. However, deep learning heavily relies on extensive data sets. The available experimentally determined RNA structures and simulation trajectories are very limited, which is insufficient for our needs. In the future, as more experimental resources become available, we aim to develop physics-based deep learning algorithms that can precisely predict RNA binding sites and interface binding dynamical behaviors.

In summary, we developed an efficient and interpretable bioinformatics tool for RNA binding preference prediction using a network strategy. RNet has been extensively assessed on its ability to identify RNA binding preference on different diverse testing sets. RNet achieved a success rate of 70.1% (78.8%) on TE18 (RB9) in RNA binding site prediction tests. Additionally, it achieved an 83.3% success rate in predicting RNA interface dynamical behavior upon competitive inhibitor binding. The significant improvements suggest that RNet can accurately evaluate RNA binding preferences by resolving structural flexibility. We expect the method to promote RNA-related prediction and inhibitor design applications.
