Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a valuable imaging modality for many conditions. For patients with congenital heart disease (CHD), MRIs for cardiac diagnosis and management may be routine or for non-cardiac reasons such as brain and spine, liver, or orthopedic concerns. MRIs on patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED) have been considered to carry risk which may include arrhythmia, heating at the tip of the lead and device malfunction or reset.1 These risks may be higher in MRI non-conditional devices, defined as those devices that are not designed by the manufacturer to be MRI compatible or in patients with lead fragments. Non-conditional devices can also include “mixed systems” defined as CIEDs with different manufacturers for lead(s) and generator. Epicardial devices are MRI non-conditional and are frequently used in pediatric patients owing to size or congenital abnormalities.

Multiple research studies have shown minimal risk for pediatric patients with non-conditional MRIs and abandoned leads.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 In fact, in a recent multicenter pediatric study, 74% of patients had MRI non-conditional devices.2 The 2021 PACES Expert Consensus Statement on the Indications and Management of Cardiovascular Implantable Electronic Devices in Pediatric Patients lists class of recommendation for pediatric patients with non-conditional transvenous CIEDs as IIa and IIb for those with epicardial or abandoned leads.7 Despite published research and consensus statements, institutional policies may limit pediatric patients with CIED in MRI. This can have negative effects on the healthcare of children by limiting access to MRI, especially in vulnerable populations like those with CHD who may require MRI for management of their disease.

A recent Heart Rhythm Society Call-to-Action statement recommends eliminating barriers for clinically indicated MRIs in patients with CIEDs.8 A multicenter pediatric study recommends that an electrophysiologist review pertinent data to approve the MRI and an electrophysiologist or “qualified” advanced practice provider monitor these patients during MRI.2 Electrophysiology (EP) resources can be limited and device management during an MRI can be time consuming for providers. Pathways to reimbursement for the time spent in MRI are limited to non-existent.

Our institutional practice to provide MRI support for pediatric and adult patients with CHD is for an EP-specialized advanced practice provider with International Board of Heart Rhythm Examiners (IBHRE) device certification review patient information and determine recommendations for CIED management during MRI based on center specific protocols. We report our experience with peri-MRI CIED care provided primarily by pediatric EP nurse practitioner(s) (NP).

Methods

This is a single center retrospective study of all pediatric and adult patients with CHD with CIEDs undergoing MRI between November 2022 and February 2025. The research reported in this paper adhered to Declaration of Helsinki. No patient consent was necessary as this was an exempt study and approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board. Data is presented descriptively with continuous variable presented as mean and standard deviation or mean and interquartile range. Categorical variables are counts or percentages. Data collected from the medical record included age at the time of MRI, rhythm diagnosis and reason for CIED, presence and type of CHD, evidence of device dependency (defined as absent intrinsic rhythm when programmed at VVI 30 bpm), and presence of fragmented or abandoned leads (verified with chest x-ray). Data collected from CIED included baseline testing (lead threshold and impedance, device battery life) and MRI compatibility (per manufacturer recommendations as conditional or non-conditional).

Clinical process

An IBHRE device-certified NP led the service and assessed the current pacing system for non-conditional components and risk based on a center-specific developed algorithm approved by pediatric radiology, cardiology imaging, and electrophysiology services. No patient undergoing MRI with CIED was excluded. The IBHRE certified EP NP also determined if programming changes were necessary and which mode was optimal during the MRI. These recommendations were documented via a dedicated form (Figure 1) and placed in the patient chart for review on the day of the MRI. EP NPs provided pre and post-MRI CIED evaluation and programming of conditional devices; a collaborating electrophysiology physician was available for consultation if needed. A full interrogation was completed prior to and immediately after MRI; assessing for battery and lead measurement changes or arrhythmias. Clinically significant change to lead measurements was defined as any change that required a change to programming (ie, increase in output after MRI) or a change in impedance outside expected normal day-to-day variation. Patient reports of symptoms and concerns during MRI were recorded. For MRIs on patients with non-conditional CIEDs and those who required programming changes, the IBHRE certified NP remained in the MRI scanner to monitor rhythm and provide programming changes during the scan if needed. For all conditional devices, MRI mode was utilized and the IBHRE certified NP determined the correct pacing mode for the patient based on clinical history.Figure 1EP review form for MRI in patients with CIED. Results

There were 28 MRI scans completed on 27 patients included in the study between November 2022 and February 2025. Median patient age was 21.7 years (interquartile range [IQR] 18–23; range 0.5– 37). There was presence of CHD in 21 patients (84%). Rhythm diagnoses include heart block in 15 patients (52%), sinus node dysfunction in 8 (30%), and ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation in 5 (18%). Of the 7 with abandoned and fragmented epicardial leads, 2 had additional transvenous systems. There were 2 (8%) pacemaker dependent patients and 7 (25%) with higher lead threshold of ≥ 2 V. For 22 (78%) MRI scans, the IBHRE certified EP NP remained in MRI throughout the scan to monitor rhythm or make changes to programming as needed. Discussion

CIEDs in MRI continue to spark much debate. Historical concerns regarding risk have been a barrier to patients with CIED receiving MRIs; continued multicenter studies have shown minimal risk to patients and the CIED in MRIs.2,6 Despite this, many centers continue to struggle with developing protocols collaborating with radiology. This is especially true in pediatric centers since many times children’s devices or patients with CHD’s devices are not MRI-conditional. In addition, the pediatric population with CIEDs may have structural abnormality which may require cardiac MRIs for diagnosis, management, and outcome measurement.

This study supports prior results from both pediatric and adult research showing minimal risk of CIEDs in MRI.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 This is a complex group of patients as most MRI scans were done on those with MRI non-conditional devices. In addition, 2 patients were pacemaker-dependent and 7 others had high lead threshold (defined as >2V at baseline) included. Most patients were undergoing a cardiac MRI, which is thought to result in more risk compared to other types of MRI scans as the leads and the devices are both located within the magnetic field. This holds true for epicardial and transvenous devices. Despite this complex group of patients, there were no adverse events, including arrhythmia or patient reported symptoms. The primary NP-led service successfully navigated these complexities and there were no clinically significant changes to pre- and post-MRI interrogation values.

Another barrier to CIED evaluation and management in MRI is billing and reimbursement. Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 93286 and 93287 are used for peri-procedural device evaluation (in-person) and programming of device system parameters before or after surgery, procedure, or test with analysis, review and report by a physician or other qualified health care professional for pacemaker and ICDs. According to the National Medicare data and Michigan-specific guidelines, this is reimbursed at a rate of $14.00–$20.00 and associated with 0.3 work relative value unit (RVUs) per procedure. After 2025, additional CPT codes were added which can supplement reimbursement for the preparation time for these patients. Although these codes may increase billing and reimbursement for MRI procedure, it still does not cover the resource costs for the time it takes for many pediatric patients with MRI non-conditional CIEDs. In this study, the average time evaluating and managing the device and patient for MRI by the EP NP was 100 minutes. The new codes do not take into account the “in MRI-time” which can be extensive. In addition, one-quarter of patients in this study had abandoned or fragmented leads. Although research supports that MRIs with abandoned or fragmented leads are low risk for arrhythmias or patient symptoms, Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) reimbursement remains limited for EP services for CIEDs in MRI with these configurations.8 MRIs continue to be completed for these patients because of medical necessity, therefore, reimbursement and billing ought to follow current evidence. The Heart Rhythm Society call-to-action publication for improved MRI is advocating for better reimbursement for CIEDs in MRI8 which can help support access to MRIs.

NPs who are specialized in device management and possess IBHRE certification are well-positioned to care for pediatric patients who have CIEDs for MRI testing. In fact, the additional MRI CPT billing codes added in 2025 support qualified health care professionals to provide these services and receive reimbursement if there is a written report. IBHRE device certification provides a distinct pathway for the EP NP to demonstrate the knowledge and experience to manage CIEDs in MRI and support efficient clinical work. The device-specialized EP NP can make decisions on programming and the needs of the patient during the MRI scan. In addition, EP NPs frequently possess advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) (pediatric or adult) certification which is recommended to manage these patients.8 Using an IBHRE-certified EP NP in this capacity eliminates the challenges with alternative options. Industry-based clinical representatives should not have a significant role in CIEDs in MRI as they are unlikely to have sufficient clinical knowledge of the patient.9 EP attendings can focus on other clinical needs such as performing procedures in the EP lab. Our experience supports an NP-led process developed in collaboration with the EP attending team to improve efficiency and patient access to care. With increasing billing for these procedures, NPs will be able to continue to provide independent care for patients with CIEDs. This can be especially important in centers that focus on productivity of the NP by work relative value units (wRVU).

Automated features already present in some CIEDs will continue to improve the safety of MRI in patients with CIED. These features can automatically detect the MRI and switch into and out of an MR safe mode.9,10 In other devices, MRI settings will revert back to original settings after a programmed amount of time. This can minimize errors of discharging patients without restoring original settings. This is especially important for patients with ICD when shocks need to be enabled after MRI. One manufacturer has developed a tablet-based application that will assess the patient’s pacing requirements and device history and based on patients’ needs, will program the MRI mode settings. This could allow non-cardiology providers to program devices for MRI.10 When coupled with remote monitoring, it can decrease the resources needed at the beginning and end of the scan for an MRI-conditional device. In fact, a recent study showed that these features eliminated the need for post-MRI interrogation in 91% of patients.9 In pediatrics, conditional devices are the minority, but this technology could help to decrease resources and time commitment for NPs who are responsible for this type of work for certain patients and devices.

Limitations

This is a single center study with a protocol that was developed and utilized at the University of Michigan. The small study sample size and homogeneity of the sample may reduce generalizability. This center has an IBHRE certified dedicated EP NP which may not be possible at all centers. This is a retrospective study which is limited based on reliance on existing data which can be incomplete, or inconsistent.

Conclusion

Specialized EP NP provided peri-MRI CIED management can be a successful physician-adjacent service. There were no adverse outcomes including lead measurement changes, patient-reported symptoms or arrhythmias for patients with conditional or non-conditional CIEDs consistent with prior studies. The NP-led service was able to safely support complex patients with CIED undergoing an MRI. Time spent in MRI does not include time for preliminary assessment and determination of risk and NP time commitment to peri-MRI care was significant on a per patient basis. Pathways to provide increased reimbursement for these important services in a growing patient population are essential.
