A numerical method for the fractional Zakharov-Kuznetsov equation
Mukul Dwivedi and Andreas Rupp

Abstract
. This paper develops a fully discrete Fourier spectral Galerkin (FSG) method for the fractional Zakharov-Kuznetsov (fZK) equation posed on a two-dimensional periodic domain. The equation generalizes the classical ZK model to incorporate nonlocal dispersion through a fractional Laplacian of order α ∈ [ 1 , 2 ] \alpha\in[1,2] . We first propose a semi-discrete FSG scheme in space that preserves the discrete analogs of mass, momentum, and energy. The existence and uniqueness of semi-discrete solutions are established. Using compactness arguments, we prove the uniform convergence of the semi-discrete approximations to the unique solution of the fZK equation for the periodic initial data in H per 1 + α ​ ( Ω ) H^{1+\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega) . The method achieves spectral convergence of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( N − r ) \mathcal{O}(N^{-r}) for initial data in H per r H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}} with r ≥ α + 1 r\geq\alpha+1 , and exponential convergence for analytic solutions utilizing a modified projection. An efficient integrating-factor Runge-Kutta time discretization is designed to handle the stiff fractional term, and an error analysis is presented. Numerical experiments validate the theoretical results and demonstrate the method’s effectiveness across various fractional orders.

1.
Introduction

The study of nonlinear dispersive equations has been a cornerstone of mathematical physics since the 19th century, with the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation
[
25
]
serving as a fundamental model for unidirectional wave propagation in shallow water channels. The KdV equation exhibits a remarkable balance between nonlinear steepening and dispersive spreading, leading to the existence of stable solitary wave solutions
[
43
]
. Its mathematical theory was firmly established through the pioneering work of
[
2
]
on the initial value problem and the discovery of its complete integrability in
[
12
]
.

While the KdV equation successfully describes one-dimensional wave phenomena, many physical systems require multidimensional models to capture transverse effects. The Zakharov-Kuznetsov (ZK) equation, originally derived in
[
44
]
to describe ion-acoustic waves in magnetized plasma, provides a multidimensional generalization of the
1+1
dimensional KdV equation:

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x_{1}}\Delta u+u\partial_{x_{1}}u=0,

(1.1)

where
u=u(\mathbf{x},t)
,
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
. The physical relevance of the ZK equation extends beyond plasma physics to include applications in solid state physics
[
24
]
and fluid dynamics
[
29
]
. A rigorous derivation from the Euler-Poisson system was provided in
[
26
]
, cementing its importance as a canonical multidimensional dispersive equation.

To incorporate nonlocal dispersion and to model sub-dispersive phenomena, one generalizes the classical ZK equation by replacing the Laplacian
\Delta
with the fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
of order
\alpha\in(0,2]
. The resulting fractional Zakharov-Kuznetsov (fZK) equation reads

\partial_{t}u-\partial_{x_{1}}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u+u\,\partial_{x_{1}}u=0.

In this work, we consider the initial value problem for the fZK equation with
\alpha\in[1,2]
posed on the two-dimensional torus, i.e.,
\Omega=[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\partial_{x_{1}}(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u+u\partial_{x_{1}}u=0,&\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega,\;\;t\in(0,T],\\
u(\mathbf{x},0)=u_{0}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
T>0
is a given final time. The fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
is defined spectrally for periodic functions through the Fourier series expansion:

(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|\mathbf{k}|^{\alpha}\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}},

(1.3)

where
\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})=(2\pi)^{-2}\int_{\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}d\mathbf{x}
are the Fourier coefficients of
f
. The fZK equation (
1.2
) possesses several fundamental conserved quantities that play a crucial role in its analytical and numerical analysis. For sufficiently smooth solutions, the following invariants are preserved
[
30
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle(\text{mass}),

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle(\text{momentum}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{4}}u(\mathbf{x},t)\right|^{2}d\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{6}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u^{3}(\mathbf{x},t)d\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle(\text{Hamiltonian energy}).

These conservation laws are essential for understanding the long-time behavior of solutions and play a fundamental role in establishing well-posedness results
[
10
,
30
]
. In the periodic setting considered in this work, analogous conserved quantities exist with integration over the torus
\Omega
instead of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

The well-posedness theory for the classical ZK equation (
\alpha=2
) has been extensively developed. Faminski
[
10
]
established global well-posedness in the energy space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, while Linares et al.
[
28
]
proved local well-posedness in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
s>3/4
using the Fourier restriction method. Subsequent refinements in
[
31
]
and
[
15
]
extended this to
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
s>1/2
. In the periodic setting,
[
31
]
established local well-posedness in
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for
s>1
. The three-dimensional case was treated in
[
34
]
, who proved local well-posedness in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for
s>1
. Recent breakthroughs in
[
16
]
and
[
23
]
established well-posedness in the optimal Sobolev ranges
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
s>-1/4
and
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
s>(n-4)/2
when
n\geq 3
.

The case
\alpha=1
reveals deep connections with completely integrable systems. The fZK equation with
\alpha=1
can be reformulated as

\partial_{t}u-\mathcal{R}_{1}\Delta u+u\partial_{x_{1}}u=0,

(1.5)

where
\mathcal{R}_{1}
denotes the Riesz transform in the
x_{1}
-direction, defined by

\mathcal{R}_{1}f(x)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{x_{1}-y_{1}}{|x-y|^{n+1}}f(y)dy,

(1.6)

where
p.v.
stands for the principal value.
This equation was first derived in
[
36
]
to model bi-dimensional long-wave perturbations in boundary-layer shear flows and represents a higher-dimensional generalization of the Benjamin-Ono equation
[
1
,
32
]

\partial_{t}u-\mathcal{H}\partial_{x}^{2}u+u\partial_{x}u=0,

(1.7)

where
\mathcal{H}
is the Hilbert transform
[
40
]
. The well-posedness theory for the
\alpha=1
case was initiated in
[
18
]
, who established local well-posedness in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
s>3/5
in two dimensions and
s>n/2+1/2
for
n\geq 3
. Schippa
[
35
]
improved these results to
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
s>(n+3)/2-\alpha
with
n\geq 2
and
\alpha\in[1,2)
. The periodic case was also addressed in
[
35
]
, establishing local well-posedness in
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
.

For the Benjamin-Ono equation itself, the well-posedness theory has seen remarkable progress. Iorio
[
19
]
established local well-posedness in
H^{3/2}(\mathbb{R})
, while Tao
[
40
]
proved global well-posedness in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. The groundbreaking work
[
22
]
established sharp global well-posedness in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
for
s>-\frac{1}{2}
, resolving a long-standing open problem. These developments have significantly influenced the study of related nonlocal dispersive equations.

For intermediate values
\alpha\in(1,2)
, the analytical theory remains less developed. Mendez
[
30
]
made significant progress by establishing a Kato-type smoothing effect, showing that solutions gain
\alpha/2
derivatives locally in both space and time. This smoothing property, first discovered in
[
20
]
for the KdV equation and later generalized in
[
6
]
, plays a crucial role in the analysis of dispersive equations. Additional regularity properties and smoothing effects for the solution of the related nonlocal equations were studied in
[
13
,
14
,
21
,
11
,
9
]
.

Despite substantial analytical progress, numerical methods for the fZK equation (
1.2
) remain largely unexplored. For the classical ZK equation (
1.1
), various numerical methods have been proposed, including multi-symplectic spectral discretizations in
[
3
,
33
,
27
]
and stable discontinuous Galerkin methods in
[
42
,
38
]
. However, these works lack rigorous error analysis with nonlinear flux, convergence of approximate solutions to the unique solution of equation (
1.1
), and spectral convergence. To the best of our knowledge, no numerical schemes with complete convergence analysis exist for the fZK equation (
1.2
).

This work presents a novel Fourier spectral Galerkin framework for the fZK equation (
1.2
), providing the first complete numerical analysis for this model and thereby bridging a critical gap in the existing literature. The state-of-the-art methodology capitalizes on the inherent affinity between spectral techniques and periodic boundary conditions, combined with the computationally efficient representation of fractional operators within a Fourier basis
[
4
,
5
]
. The main contributions of this paper are:

•

Formulation of a semi-discrete spectral scheme that exactly preserves the discrete analogs of the continuous mass, momentum, and energy invariants. This structure-preserving property ensures the long-term stability and physical fidelity of numerical simulations.

•

Rigorous proof of existence and uniqueness for the semi-discrete solution. This is established through the application of standard ordinary differential equation theory to the Fourier coefficient system.

•

Strong convergence analysis of the spectral approximations to the exact solution. Using compactness arguments based on uniform bounds in appropriate Sobolev spaces, we demonstrate convergence to the unique solution of the fZK equation (
1.2
). As a byproduct, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the periodic fZK equation (
1.2
) in
H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
,
r\geq 1+\alpha
.

•

Derivation of optimal error estimates through the introduction of a carefully designed modified projection operator. This approach yields algebraic convergence of order
\mathcal{O}(N^{-r})
for initial data in the periodic Sobolev space
H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
with
r\geq\alpha+1
, and exponential convergence for analytic initial data.

•

Development of an efficient integrating-factor Runge-Kutta time discretization that effectively handles the stiffness introduced by the fractional dispersive term. This method avoids the severe time-step restrictions of explicit schemes while maintaining the explicit treatment of nonlinear terms.

•

Comprehensive numerical investigation validating the theoretical results and demonstrating the method’s performance across various fractional orders. The experiments include convergence verification, solitary wave propagation, and long-time behavior analysis.

The paper is organized as follows: Section
2
introduces the functional framework and fractional operators. Section
3
presents the Fourier-Galerkin scheme and establishes well-posedness. Section
4
develops an optimal error analysis. Section
5
describes the time discretization, and Section
6
presents numerical experiments validating our theoretical results.

2.
Preliminaries: Function Spaces and Fractional Operators

This section establishes the functional framework and analytical tools required for our analysis of the fZK equation (
1.2
). We consider the problem on the two-dimensional torus
\Omega=[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions. The space of square-integrable periodic functions is denoted by
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
, equipped with the inner product and norm

(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}u(\mathbf{x})\overline{v(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x},\quad\|u\|=(u,u)^{1/2}.

For any real number
s\geq 0
, the periodic Sobolev space
H^{s}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
consists of all functions
u
whose Fourier coefficients
\hat{u}(\mathbf{k})
satisfy

\|u\|_{s}^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(1+|\mathbf{k}|^{2})^{s}|\hat{u}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}<\infty,

where
|\mathbf{k}|^{2}=k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}
and every function
u\in H^{s}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
admits a Fourier series expansion

u(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\hat{u}(\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}.

Note that
H^{0}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
coincides with
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
. For numerical approximation, we introduce the finite-dimensional space of trigonometric polynomials

\mathbb{X}_{N}=\mathrm{span}\left\{e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}:|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}=\max\{|k_{1}|,|k_{2}|\}\leq N\right\}.

The orthogonal projection
\Pi_{N}:L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{X}_{N}
is defined by

\Pi_{N}u(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N}\hat{u}(\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}},

which satisfies the orthogonality condition

(\Pi_{N}u-u,\psi)=0\quad\text{for all }\psi\in\mathbb{X}_{N}.

(2.1)

In addition, every piecewise continuous function
u
permits a Fourier series
\Pi_{N}u
such
that
\|\Pi_{N}u-u\|\to 0
as
N\to\infty

[
17
, Theorem 13.2]
, and every periodic function
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
permits a Fourier series
\Pi_{N}u
such
that
\|\Pi_{N}u-u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\to 0
as
N\to\infty

[
17
, Theorem 13.1]
.
We now establish several fundamental properties of the fractional Laplacian that will be essential for our analysis.

Lemma 2.1
.

The fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

For
f,g\in H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
,
\alpha\geq 0
, we have

\left((-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f,g\right)=\left(f,(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}g\right),

(2.2)

and for real-valued
f\in H^{1+\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
,

\left((-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}(\partial_{x_{1}}f),f\right)=0.

(2.3)

(ii)

For
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0
and
f,g\in H^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
, we have

\left((-\Delta)^{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})/2}f,g\right)=\left((-\Delta)^{\alpha_{1}/2}f,(-\Delta)^{\alpha_{2}/2}g\right),

(2.4)

and equivalently,

(-\Delta)^{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})/2}f=(-\Delta)^{\alpha_{1}/2}(-\Delta)^{\alpha_{2}/2}f=(-\Delta)^{\alpha_{2}/2}(-\Delta)^{\alpha_{1}/2}f.

(2.5)

(iii)

For the orthogonal projection
\Pi_{N}
and
f\in H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
with
r\geq\alpha\geq 0
, the fractional Laplacian commutes with the projection:

(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}(\Pi_{N}f)=\Pi_{N}((-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f).

(2.6)

Proof.

Property (
2.2
) follows directly from Parseval’s identity and from the fact that
f
and
g
are real valued

\left((-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f,g\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|\mathbf{k}|^{\alpha}\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})\overline{\hat{g}(\mathbf{k})}=\left(f,(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}g\right).

Periodicity of
f
, integration by parts, the commutative property of
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
with partial derivatives, and (
2.2
) imply (
2.3
).
Property (
2.4
) follows from the identity

\left((-\Delta)^{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})/2}f,g\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|\mathbf{k}|^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})\overline{\hat{g}(\mathbf{k})}=\left((-\Delta)^{\alpha_{1}/2}f,(-\Delta)^{\alpha_{2}/2}g\right).

The commutative property (
2.5
) is immediate from the commutativity of the Fourier multipliers
|\mathbf{k}|^{\alpha_{1}}
and
|\mathbf{k}|^{\alpha_{2}}
. Finally, property (
2.6
) holds because both operators act as Fourier multipliers, and
\Pi_{N}
truncates high frequencies

(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}(\Pi_{N}f)(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N}|\mathbf{k}|^{\alpha}\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=\Pi_{N}((-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f)(\mathbf{x}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we establish a product estimate for the fractional Laplacian, which will be crucial for handling nonlinear terms.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f,g\in H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
with
\alpha\geq 0
. Then, for
C(\alpha)=\max\{1,2^{\alpha-1}\}
, we have

(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}(fg)(\mathbf{x})\leq C(\alpha)\left(f(\mathbf{x})(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}g(\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{x})(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f(\mathbf{x})\right)

(2.7)

for almost every
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
. Consequently,

\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}(fg)\|\leq C(\alpha)\left(\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}g\|+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f\|\right).

(2.8)

Proof.

The proof follows
[
8
, Lemma 2.4]
and adapts it to the two-dimensional setting.
∎

Finally, we state some useful inequalities for trigonometric polynomials in
\mathbb{X}_{N}
.

Lemma 2.3

(Inverse and Sobolev Inequalities)
.

For any
v_{N}\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
, the following estimates hold:

(i)

Inverse inequality:

\|\partial_{x_{j}}v_{N}\|\leq N\|v_{N}\|,\quad j=1,2.

(ii)

Sobolev inequality:

\|v_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 3N\|v_{N}\|.

(iii)

For any
r\geq 0
, we have the embedding

\|v_{N}\|\leq\|v_{N}\|_{r}.

Proof.

The inverse inequality follows from Parseval’s identity and the fact that
|k_{j}|\leq N
for
|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N
:

\|\partial_{x_{j}}v_{N}\|^{2}=\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N}|k_{j}|^{2}|\hat{v}_{N}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\leq N^{2}\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N}|\hat{v}_{N}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}=N^{2}\|v_{N}\|^{2}.

The Sobolev inequality is proved using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that there are
O(N^{2})
Fourier modes:

|v_{N}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N}|\hat{v}_{N}(\mathbf{k})|\leq\left(\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N}1\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N}|\hat{v}_{N}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq 3N\|v_{N}\|.

The embedding result is immediate from the definition of the Sobolev norm.
∎

3.
Fourier-Galerkin scheme and well-posedness

We consider the fractional Zakharov-Kuznetsov (fZK) equation (
1.2
) on the periodic domain
\Omega
. Multiplying by a test function
v\in H^{\alpha}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
and integrating by parts yields the variational form. Find
u\in H^{\alpha}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
such that

(\partial_{t}u,v)+((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u,\partial_{x_{1}}v)-\frac{1}{2}(u^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}v)=0,\quad\forall\,v\in H^{\alpha}_{\text{per}}(\Omega).

(3.1)

The Fourier spectral-Galerkin approximation consists of finding
u_{N}\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
such that

(\partial_{t}u_{N},v)+((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N},\partial_{x_{1}}v)-\frac{1}{2}(u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}v)=0\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{X}_{N},

(3.2)

with the initial condition
(u_{N}(0),v)=(u_{0},v)
.

Let
u_{N}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N}\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{k},t)e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}

and taking test functions
v(\mathbf{x})=e^{i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x}}
for
|\mathbf{m}|_{\infty}\leq N
, we obtain the following system of ordinary differential equations for the Fourier coefficients:

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{m},t)=-im_{1}|\mathbf{m}|^{\alpha}\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{m},t)+\frac{1}{2}im_{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N\\
|\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N\end{subarray}}\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{k},t)\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{k},t),\quad|\mathbf{m}|_{\infty}\leq N.

(3.3)

Let
M:=(2N+1)^{2}
and let
U(t)
denote the vector of all Fourier coefficients
\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{m},t)
for
|\mathbf{m}|_{\infty}\leq N
. Then the above system can be written as

\frac{dU}{dt}(t)=G(U(t)),

(3.4)

where
G:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
is defined by

G(U(t))=\{G_{j}(U(t))\}_{1\leq j\leq M},

and each component function

G_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
(
1\leq j\leq M
) are smooth and locally Lipschitz continuous.
The initial condition
u_{N}(0)=\Pi_{N}u_{0}
implies

U(0)=\{U_{j}(0)\}_{1\leq j\leq M}=\left\{\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{m},0)\right\}_{|\mathbf{m}|_{\infty}\leq N}=\left\{(u_{0},e^{i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x}})\right\}_{|\mathbf{m}|_{\infty}\leq N}.

(3.5)

By the Picard-Lindelöf theorem, the system (
3.4
) with initial condition (
3.5
) has a unique solution
U(t)\in C^{1}([0,T_{\text{max}});\mathbb{R}^{M})
for some
T_{\text{max}}>0
. To uniquely extend the solution for all
t>0
, we will show that the
L^{2}
norm of
u_{N}
is conserved.

Theorem 3.1
.

The scheme (
3.2
) conserves mass, momentum, and the Hamiltonian specified by (
1.4
). In particular, the approximation
u_{N}\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
obtained by the scheme (
3.2
) preserves the following invariants

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(u_{N})=0,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{M}(u_{N})=0,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(u_{N})=0.

(3.8)

Proof.

Mass conservation (
3.6
) follows from taking
v=1
in (
3.2
). Now take
v=u_{N}
in (
3.2
), we have

(\partial_{t}u_{N},u_{N})+((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N},\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N})-\frac{1}{2}(u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N})=0.

The second term vanishes due to the property (
2.3
) and the third term vanishes due to periodicity:

(u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N})=\frac{1}{3}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{1}}(u_{N}^{3})d\mathbf{x}=0.

This implies (
3.7
).
Now we take
v=\Pi_{N}\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{2}\right)\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
in (
3.2
), we have

\left(\partial_{t}u_{N},\Pi_{N}\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{2}\right)\right)+\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}\Pi_{N}\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{2}\right)\right)=0.

Utilizing the projection properties, we have

\left(\partial_{t}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{2}\right)+\left(\Pi_{N}\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{2}\right),\partial_{x_{1}}\Pi_{N}\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{2}\right)\right)=0.

The second term above vanishes due to the periodicity of
u_{N}
and property (
2.2
) implies

\left(\partial_{t}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{2}\right)=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{4}}u_{N})^{2}-\frac{1}{6}u_{N}^{3},1\right)=0,

which implies (
3.8
).
∎

Now we provide the existence and uniqueness result
for the fZK equation (
1.2
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
u_{0}\in H^{1+\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
, where
\alpha\in[1,2]
. Then there exists a unique solution
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1+\alpha}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
of the fZK equation (
1.2
) with
\partial_{t}\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
for all
T\geq 0
.

To prove the above theorem, we first state and prove the following convergence lemma, which is the crucial result of this paper. Let us first introduce the definition of a weak solution for the fZK equation (
1.2
). A function
u\in L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
is said to be a weak solution of (
1.2
) with initial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
if, for every test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\Omega)
, the following variational formulation holds:

\int_{0}^{T}\left[\left(u,\partial_{t}\varphi\right)+\left(u,(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{x_{1}}\varphi\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(u^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}\varphi\right)\right]dt+\left(u_{0},\varphi(0)\right)=0.

(3.9)

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
u_{N}
be an approximate solution of the fractional ZK equation (
1.2
) obtained via the scheme (
3.2
). Then there exists a finite time
\bar{T}
such that the following bounds hold

\displaystyle\norm{u_{N}(\cdot,t)}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\norm{\partial_tu_{N}(\cdot,t)}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\norm{u_{N}(\cdot,t)}_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.12)

for all
t\leq\bar{T}
, where the constant
C=C(\norm{u_0}_{1+\alpha},\alpha,\bar{T})
is independent of
N
. Moreover, the approximation
u_{N}
converges uniformly to the unique solution of the fZK equation (
1.2
) as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

Choosing a test function
v=(-\Delta)^{1+\alpha}u_{N}
in the scheme (
3.2
), we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}u_{N})\|^{2}+\left(u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{1+\alpha}u_{N}\right)-\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{1+\alpha}u_{N}\right)=0,

(3.13)

Note that the last term

\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{1+\alpha}u_{N}\right)=\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{2+3\alpha}{4}}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{\frac{2+3\alpha}{4}}u_{N}\right)=0,

by the property (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). Now we estimate the term
\left(u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{1+\alpha}u_{N}\right)
.
First, employing the triangle inequality and the properties of the fractional Laplacian from Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
, and then utilizing the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality repeatedly alongside the Sobolev embedding results (Lemma
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\left(u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{1+\alpha}u_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}(u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N}),(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}u_{N}\right)\right|

(3.14)

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha)\left|\left(u_{N}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}u_{N}\right)\right|

\displaystyle+C(\alpha)\left|\left(\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}u_{N},(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}u_{N}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}C(\alpha)\norm{\partial_{x_1}u_N}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\norm{(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}u_N}^{2}

\displaystyle+C(\alpha)\norm{\partial_{x_1}u_N}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\norm{(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}u_N}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\norm{u_N}_{1+\alpha}^{3}.

Now, identity (
3.13
) and estimate (
3.14
) imply

\frac{d}{dt}\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}u_{N})\|^{2}\leq C\norm{u_N}_{1+\alpha}^{3}.

(3.15)

A similar estimate yields the following

\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u_N}_{1+\alpha}^{2}\leq C\norm{u_N}_{1+\alpha}^{3}.

(3.16)

Define
D(t):=\|u_{N}(t)\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}
. From the differential inequality (
3.16
), we get

\frac{dD}{dt}\leq CD^{3/2},

with initial condition
D(0)=D_{0}:=\|u_{0}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}
. Consider the equation

\frac{de}{dt}=Ce^{3/2},\quad e(0)=D_{0},

where
C
is independent of
t
. Solving by separation of variables yields

e(t)=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D_{0}}}-\frac{Ct}{2}\right)^{-2},

which is increasing and convex for
t<T_{b}:=\frac{2}{C\sqrt{D_{0}}}
. Define

\bar{T}:=\frac{T_{b}}{2}=\frac{1}{C\sqrt{D_{0}}},

which gives the bound

e(t)\leq e(\bar{T})=4D_{0},\quad\text{for all }t\leq\bar{T}.

By standard comparison theory for differential inequalities, we conclude

D(t)\leq e(t)\leq 4D_{0}\quad\text{for }t\leq\bar{T},

and consequently

\|u_{N}(t)\|_{1+\alpha}\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{1+\alpha}\leq C,

where the constant
C
depends on the initial data. This proves (
3.12
) for all
t\leq\bar{T}
. Now take the test function
v=\partial_{t}u_{N}
, which yields

\norm{\partial_t u_N}^{2}=((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},\partial_{t}u_{N})-(u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},\partial_{t}u_{N}).

Utilizing the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the bounds (
3.10
) and (
3.12
) with Sobolev embedding result (Lemma
2.3
), we have

\norm{\partial_t u_N}\leq\norm{(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}} \partial_{x_1}u_N}+\norm{u_N}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\norm{u_N}_{1+\alpha}\leq C,\qquad\forall\,t\leq\bar{T}.

This proves (
3.11
) for all time
t\leq\bar{T}
.

The bound (
3.11
) implies that the sequence
\{u_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is equicontinuous in
C([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
, since for any
0\leq s<t\leq\bar{T}
:

\|u_{N}(t)-u_{N}(s)\|\leq\int_{s}^{t}\|\partial_{t}u_{N}(\tau)\|\,d\tau\leq C|t-s|.

Combined with the uniform boundedness in
L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
from (
3.10
), the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem guaranties the existence of a subsequence
\{u_{N_{k}}\}
that converges uniformly to some function
\bar{u}
in
C([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
.

We now demonstrate that
\bar{u}
is a weak solution of the fractional ZK equation (
1.2
) in a sense of (
3.9
). Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,\bar{T})\times\Omega)
be a test function. The variational formulation (
3.2
) for
u_{N}
reads:

(\partial_{t}u_{N},v)+((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N},\partial_{x_{1}}v)-\frac{1}{2}(u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}v)=0,\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{X}_{N}.

Choosing
v=\Pi_{N}\varphi\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
and integrating over
[0,\bar{T}]
yields:

\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\left[(\partial_{t}u_{N},\Pi_{N}\varphi)+((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N},\partial_{x_{1}}\Pi_{N}\varphi)-\frac{1}{2}(u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}\Pi_{N}\varphi)\right]dt=0.

(3.17)

We analyze the convergence of each term as
N_{k}\to\infty
. The first term can be estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(\partial_{t}u_{N},\Pi_{N}\varphi)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(\partial_{t}u_{N},\varphi)dt+\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(\partial_{t}u_{N},\Pi_{N}\varphi-\varphi)dt.

Integration by parts in time gives

\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(\partial_{t}u_{N},\varphi)dt=-\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(u_{N},\partial_{t}\varphi)dt-(u_{N}(0),\varphi(0)).

By uniform convergence
u_{N_{k}}\to\bar{u}
in
C([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
, we have

\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(u_{N_{k}},\partial_{t}\varphi)dt\to\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(\bar{u},\partial_{t}\varphi)dt,\quad(u_{N_{k}}(0),\varphi(0))\to(\bar{u}(0),\varphi(0)).

The error term vanishes since

\left|\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(\partial_{t}u_{N_{k}},\Pi_{N_{k}}\varphi-\varphi)dt\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\|\Pi_{N_{k}}\varphi-\varphi\|dt\to 0,

using the boundedness of
\partial_{t}u_{N_{k}}
from (
3.11
) and the projection error estimate. Now we estimate the second term of (
3.17
) as follows

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N},\partial_{x_{1}}\Pi_{N}\varphi)dt=\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N},\partial_{x_{1}}\varphi)dt+\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N},\partial_{x_{1}}(\Pi_{N}\varphi-\varphi))dt.

The first term above converges to
\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\bar{u},\partial_{x_{1}}\varphi)dt
by the weak convergence of
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N_{k}}
in
L^{2}(0,\bar{T};L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
, which follows from the bound (
3.12
). The second term tends to zero due to the
L^{2}
-convergence of the projection and the boundedness of
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}
.
Finally, we estimate the third term of (
3.17
) as follows

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}\Pi_{N}\varphi)dt=\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}\varphi)dt+\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(u_{N}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}(\Pi_{N}\varphi-\varphi))dt.

The uniform convergence
u_{N_{k}}\to\bar{u}
in
C([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
and the boundedness in
L^{\infty}(0,\bar{T};H^{1+\alpha}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
imply
u_{N_{k}}^{2}\to\bar{u}^{2}
strongly in
L^{2}(0,\bar{T};L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
. Thus, the first term converges to
\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}(\bar{u}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}\varphi)dt
. The second term vanishes due to the
L^{2}
convergence of the projection
\Pi_{N}
and the boundedness of
u_{N}^{2}
.
Passing to the limit in (
3.17
) and combining the above estimate with property (
2.2
), limit
\bar{u}
satisfies the weak formulation (
3.9
).
Furthermore, the bounds (
3.10
)-(
3.12
) imply that
\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}([0,\bar{T}];H^{1+\alpha}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
, establish that
\bar{u}
is a strong solution.

To prove uniqueness, suppose
\bar{u}
and
\bar{w}
are two solutions of (
1.2
) with
\bar{u}(0)=\bar{w}(0)=u_{0}
. Let
v=\bar{u}-\bar{w}
. Then
v
satisfies

\partial_{t}v-\partial_{x_{1}}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}v+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x_{1}}(\bar{u}^{2}-\bar{w}^{2})=0,\quad v(0)=0.

Taking the inner product with
v
and using the skew-symmetry property (
2.3
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|v\|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}(\bar{u}^{2}-\bar{w}^{2},\partial_{x_{1}}v)=-\frac{1}{2}(v(\bar{u}+\bar{w}),\partial_{x_{1}}v).

Integration by parts and periodicity yield

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(v(\bar{u}+\bar{w}),\partial_{x_{1}}v)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{1}}(\bar{u}+\bar{w})v^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}.

Therefore,

\frac{d}{dt}\|v\|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{1}}(\bar{u}+\bar{w})v^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Sobolev embedding
H^{1+\alpha}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
:

\frac{d}{dt}\|v\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\partial_{x_{1}}(\bar{u}+\bar{w})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|v\|^{2}\leq C\|v\|^{2},

where
C
depends on
\|\bar{u}\|_{1+\alpha}
and
\|\bar{w}\|_{1+\alpha}
. Therefore, Grönwall’s inequality with
v(0)=0
implies
\|v(t)\|=0
for all
t\in[0,\bar{T}]
, proving uniqueness. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The local existence and uniqueness result established in Lemma
3.3
can be extended to arbitrary finite time intervals through an iterative continuation procedure, following the technique from
[
37
]
. The crucial observation is that the existence time
\bar{T}
depends on
\|u_{0}\|_{1+\alpha}
. Since the semi-discrete approximation is bounded on the
H^{1+\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}
-norm uniformly in time (
3.12
), we may repeatedly apply the local existence and uniqueness Lemma
3.3
. At each iteration step
t=k\bar{T}
, we take
u_{N}(k\bar{T})
as the new initial data and solve forward on
[k\bar{T},(k+1)\bar{T}]
. This bootstrap argument yields a solution that exists uniquely on any given time interval
[0,T]
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The Lemma
3.3
and Remark
3.4
together proves that there exists a unique solution
u\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{1+\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))
of the fZK equation (
1.2
) with
\partial_{t}\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
for all
T\geq 0
.
∎

4.
Optimal Error Analysis

In this section, we develop an optimal error estimate for the FSG method (
3.2
) applied to the fZK equation (
1.2
). The key insight, inspired by the work of Deng
[
7
]
on the one-dimensional Kawahara equation, is to introduce a carefully designed projection operator that captures the structure of the differential operator in our equation. This allows us to overcome the suboptimal error estimates that would result from using standard
L^{2}
-projections.

Let
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
be a smooth function. For any
w\in H^{r}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
with
r\geq 1+\alpha
, we define the modified projection
\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
as follows

(K(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w-w)+u\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w-w)-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w-w),v)=0,\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{X}_{N},

(4.1)

where
K>0
is a constant that will be chosen sufficiently large to ensure coercivity. We now establish the existence and uniqueness of
\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w
using the Lax-Milgram theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and
w\in H^{r}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
with
r\geq\alpha+1
. There exists a constant
K_{0}>0
depending on
u
and
\alpha
, such that for any
K\geq K_{0}
, the variational problem (
4.1
) has a unique solution
\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi=\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
be the unknown. Equation (
4.1
) is equivalent to finding
\phi\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
such that for all
v\in\mathbb{X}_{N}

(K\phi+u\partial_{x_{1}}\phi-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}\phi,v)=(Kw+u\partial_{x_{1}}w-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}w,v).

Define the bilinear form
B:\mathbb{X}_{N}\times\mathbb{X}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
by

B(\phi,\psi)=(K\phi+u\partial_{x_{1}}\phi-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}\phi,\psi),

and the bounded linear functional
F:\mathbb{X}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
by

F(\psi)=(Kw+u\partial_{x_{1}}w-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}w,\psi).

We need to show that
B
is coercive and bounded on
\mathbb{X}_{N}
with the
L^{2}
norm. For any
\phi\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
, we have

\displaystyle B(\phi,\phi)

\displaystyle=(K\phi+u\partial_{x_{1}}\phi-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}\phi,\phi)

\displaystyle=K\|\phi\|^{2}+(u\partial_{x_{1}}\phi,\phi)-((-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}\phi,\phi).

By integration by parts and periodicity

(u\partial_{x_{1}}\phi,\phi)=-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x_{1}}u,\phi^{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{x_{1}}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|^{2}.

and
((-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}\phi,\phi)=0.
by Lemma
2.1
. Therefore

B(\phi,\phi)\geq\left(K-\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{x_{1}}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\right)\|\phi\|^{2}.

Thus, if we choose
K>K_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{x_{1}}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, then
B
is coercive with constant
\alpha=K-K_{0}>0
. For any
\phi,\psi\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
, we have:

\displaystyle|B(\phi,\psi)|

\displaystyle\leq K\|\phi\|\|\psi\|+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\partial_{x_{1}}\phi\|\|\psi\|+\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}\phi\|\|\psi\|.

Using the inverse inequalities for trigonometric polynomials

\|\partial_{x_{1}}\phi\|\leq CN\|\phi\|,\quad\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}\phi\|\leq CN^{\alpha+1}\|\phi\|,

we obtain

|B(\phi,\psi)|\leq\left(K+C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}N+CN^{\alpha+1}\right)\|\phi\|\|\psi\|.

Thus,
B
is bounded by the constant
M=K+C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}N+CN^{\alpha+1}
.

Since
\mathbb{X}_{N}
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space with the
L^{2}
inner product, and
B
is a coercive and bounded bilinear form on this space, the Lax-Milgram theorem guaranties the existence of a unique
\phi\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
such that
B(\phi,\psi)=F(\psi)
for all
\psi\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
. Therefore,
\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w=\phi
is well-defined and unique for any
w\in H^{r}_{\text{per}}(\omega)
,
r\geq 1+\alpha
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
r\geq 0
,
0\leq l\leq\min\{r,\alpha+1\}
,
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
,
w\in H^{r}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
, and
\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}
be defined by (
4.1
). There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
N
such that:

\|w-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w\|_{l}\leq CN^{l-r}|w|_{r}.

(4.2)

If
u
and
w
are analytic on
\Omega
, then there exist positive constants
C,\sigma
independent of
N
such that

\|w-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w\|\leq Ce^{-\sigma N}\|w\|.

(4.3)

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
r\geq 0
,
0\leq l\leq\min\{r,\alpha+1\}
,
u\in C^{1}([0,T];C^{1}(\Omega))
,
w\in C^{1}([0,T];H^{r}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
, and
\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}
is defined by (
4.1
). Then the projection error satisfies

\|\partial_{t}(w-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}w)(t)\|_{l}\leq CN^{l-r}(|\partial_{t}w(t)|_{r}+|w(t)|_{r}).

(4.4)

The proofs of Theorem
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
follow the approach developed by Deng
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
for the Kawahara equation. The methodology involves establishing coercivity estimates for the operator
\mathcal{L}=K+u\partial_{x_{1}}-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}
using modified test functions, followed by a duality argument to obtain optimal convergence rates. While the adaptation from the one-dimensional Kawahara setting to our multidimensional fractional ZK equation requires careful handling of the fractional Laplacian and the additional spatial dimension, the core analytical techniques remain applicable. For brevity and to maintain focus on the novel aspects of our spectral discretization for the fZK equation (
1.2
), we omit the detailed proofs and refer readers to
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
for the complete methodology. Moreover, using (
4.2
) and Stirling’s formula
[
39
]
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{w - \widetilde P_N w}\leq CN^{-r}|w|_{r}\leq C\frac{r!}{N^{r}}\|w\|\leq C\frac{r^{r}e^{-r}}{N^{r}}\norm{w}\leq Ce^{-cN}\norm{w},

where we assume that
r
is proportional to
N
. This implies the proof of (
4.3
).

Theorem 4.4

(Spectral convergence)
.

Assume that
u
is the exact solution of (
1.2
) satisfying
u\in C^{1}([0,T];H^{r}_{\text{per}}(\Omega))
with
r\geq\alpha+1
, and let
u_{N}
be the semi-discrete approximation obtained via (
3.2
). There exists a constant
C=C(T,r,\|u_{0}\|_{1+\alpha})
, independent of the discretization parameter
N
, such that

\|u(t)-u_{N}(t)\|\leq CN^{-r},

(4.5)

for all
t<T
. If, in addition, the initial data
u_{0}
is real-analytic, then there exist positive constants
C
and
\sigma
, independent of
N
, such that

\|u(t)-u_{N}(t)\|\leq C\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma N},

for all
t<T
.

Proof.

From the equation (
1.2
), scheme (
3.2
), and (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}u_{N}-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N}+u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},v)+(\partial_{t}u-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{x_{1}}u+u\partial_{x_{1}}u,v)

(4.6)

\displaystyle-(K(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u)+u\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u)-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),v)

\displaystyle-(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),v)+(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),v)-(\partial_{t}\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u,v)+(\partial_{t}\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u,v)=0,

which implies the following error equation

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}_{N}-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{x_{1}}\mathcal{E}_{N}+u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N}-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),v)

(4.7)

\displaystyle-(K(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),v)-((u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u)\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),v)+(\partial_{t}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),v)=0,

where
\mathcal{E}_{N}=u_{N}-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u
. We take
v=\mathcal{E}_{N}
in (
4.7
), and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N}-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),\mathcal{E}_{N})+(K(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),\mathcal{E}_{N})

(4.8)

\displaystyle+((u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u)\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),\mathcal{E}_{N})-(\partial_{t}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),\mathcal{E}_{N}).

We estimate each term on the right-hand side by utilizing (
4.2
), (
4.4
), Sobolev embedding, the inverse inequality (Lemma
2.3
), Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequality, and (
3.12
).

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u)-u_{N}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},\mathcal{E}_{N})

\displaystyle=((\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u_{N})\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},\mathcal{E}_{N})+(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u_{N}),\mathcal{E}_{N})

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(\mathcal{E}_{N})^{2})+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),(\mathcal{E}_{N})^{2})

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(\mathcal{E}_{N})^{2})+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x_{1}}(u_{N}-\mathcal{E}_{N}),(\mathcal{E}_{N})^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x_{1}}u_{N},(\mathcal{E}_{N})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

\displaystyle((u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u)\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),\mathcal{E}_{N})

\displaystyle\leq(\|\partial_{x_{1}}(u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|\partial_{x_{1}}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)})\|u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|

\displaystyle\leq C(|u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u|_{2}+|u|_{2})\|u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|

\displaystyle\leq(N^{2-r}|u|_{r}+|u|_{2})\|u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-2r}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle(K(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),\mathcal{E}_{N})-(\partial_{t}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),\mathcal{E}_{N})\leq C_{K}N^{-2r}+\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

Combining all the above estimates, equation (
4.8
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}\leq CN^{-2r}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

(4.9)

Applying Grönwall’s inequality and (
4.2
), we have

\|u(t)-u_{N}(t)\|\leq CN^{-r},\qquad\forall t\leq T.

(4.10)

Now, let
w
be an analytic function on
\Omega
. The estimate (
4.3
) implies
((u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u)\partial_{x_{1}}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u),\mathcal{E}_{N})\leq Ce^{-\sigma N}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}
and
(K(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),\mathcal{E}_{N})-(\partial_{t}(\widetilde{\Pi}_{N}u-u),\mathcal{E}_{N})\leq C_{K}e^{-\sigma N}+\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}
. Therefore, instead of (
4.9
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}\leq Ce^{-\sigma N}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality, we have

\|u(t)-u_{N}(t)\|\leq C\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma N},

for all
t<T
.
This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Time Discretization by an Integrating-Factor RK4 Scheme

The fZK equation (
1.2
) contains a higher order stiff dispersive term
\partial_{x_{1}}(\!-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u
that produces high‑frequency oscillations when discretized. If one were to apply a standard explicit Runge–Kutta method to the semi‑discrete system, these oscillations would impose a restrictive time‑step constraint of the form
{\Delta t}\,N^{1+\alpha}\ll 1
. Implicit methods can overcome this restriction but require the solution of nonlinear systems at each time step. An integrating‑factor approach avoids both drawbacks by solving the linear part exactly while applying an explicit RK4 scheme only to the remaining nonlinear terms. This technique, introduced for one‑dimensional stiff equations in
[
4
,
41
]
, is well suited to multidimensional dispersive problems such as the fZK equation (
1.2
).

The Fourier spectral Galerkin formulation in Section
3
yields a system of ordinary differential equations of the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=G\bigl(U(t)\bigr),

(5.1)

where the nonlinear operator
G
can be decomposed into linear and nonlinear parts:

G(U)=LU+\mathcal{N}(U).

Here,
L
is a diagonal matrix of order
(2N+1)^{2}\times(2N+1)^{2}
with diagonal entries

L_{\mathbf{m}}=-im_{1}|\mathbf{m}|^{\alpha}\quad\text{for}\quad|\mathbf{m}|_{\infty}\leq N,

and
\mathcal{N}(U)
is the nonlinear operator with components

\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{m}}(U)=\frac{1}{2}im_{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N\\
|\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}\leq N\end{subarray}}\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{k},t)\hat{u}_{N}(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{k},t).

To integrate (
5.1
) in time, define the integrating factor variable
V(t)
by

V(t)=e^{-Lt}U(t).

Differentiating
V(t)
with respect to
t
yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(e^{-Lt}\right)U(t)+e^{-Lt}\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}.

Since
L
is constant and diagonal,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(e^{-Lt}\right)=-Le^{-Lt}
. Substituting
\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}=LU+\mathcal{N}(U)
gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t}=-Le^{-Lt}U(t)+e^{-Lt}\left(LU(t)+\mathcal{N}(U(t))\right)=e^{-Lt}\mathcal{N}(U(t)).

Expressing
U(t)=e^{Lt}V(t)
, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t}=e^{-Lt}\mathcal{N}\left(e^{Lt}V(t)\right)=:\mathcal{F}(t,V(t)).

(5.2)

The transformed system (
5.2
) is integrated using the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method. Let
\Delta t
be the time step, and denote
V^{n}
and
U^{n}
as approximations of
V(t_{n})
and
U(t_{n})
respectively, where
t_{n}=n\Delta t
. The RK4 steps are as follows:

V^{n+1}=V^{n}+\frac{1}{6}(k_{1}+2k_{2}+2k_{3}+k_{4}),

(5.3)

where

\displaystyle k_{1}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{F}(t_{n},V^{n}),

\displaystyle k_{2}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{F}(t_{n}+\Delta t/2,V^{n}+k_{1}/2),

\displaystyle k_{3}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{F}(t_{n}+\Delta t/2,V^{n}+k_{2}/2),

\displaystyle k_{4}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{F}(t_{n}+\Delta t,V^{n}+k_{3}).

The solution in the original variable is recovered by

U^{n+1}=e^{Lt_{n+1}}V^{n+1}.

In terms of Fourier coefficients, this gives

\hat{u}_{N}^{n+1}(\mathbf{m})=e^{L_{\mathbf{m}}t_{n+1}}\hat{v}^{n+1}(\mathbf{m}),\quad|\mathbf{m}|_{\infty}\leq N,

(5.4)

where
\hat{v}^{n+1}(\mathbf{m})
is the
\mathbf{m}
-th component of
V^{n+1}
.

Stability Analysis for the Linearized Scheme

To analyze the stability of the method, consider the linearized version of the fZK equation where the nonlinear term is replaced by
\lambda\partial_{x_{1}}u
, with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The semi-discrete system becomes

\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}=LU+\lambda DU,

where
D
is the diagonal operator with entries
D_{\mathbf{m}}=im_{1}
. Applying the integrating factor transformation
V(t)=e^{-Lt}U(t)
yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t}=e^{-Lt}(\lambda D)e^{Lt}V(t).

Since
L
and
D
are diagonal, this simplifies to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{v}(\mathbf{m})}{\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda im_{1}\hat{v}(\mathbf{m}),\quad|\mathbf{m}|_{\infty}\leq N.

The RK4 method applied to this equation gives

\hat{v}^{n+1}(\mathbf{m})=R(\lambda im_{1}\Delta t)\hat{v}^{n}(\mathbf{m}),

where
R(z)
is the stability function of RK4:

R(z)=1+z+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\frac{z^{3}}{6}+\frac{z^{4}}{24}.

The stability condition is
|R(\lambda im_{1}\Delta t)|\leq 1
. For real
\lambda
, set
z=iy
with
y=\lambda m_{1}\Delta t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

R(iy)=\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{y^{4}}{24}\right)+i\left(y-\frac{y^{3}}{6}\right),

and

|R(iy)|^{2}=\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{y^{4}}{24}\right)^{2}+\left(y-\frac{y^{3}}{6}\right)^{2}.

The method is stable when
|R(iy)|\leq 1
. Numerical evaluation shows that this holds for
|y|\leq 2\sqrt{2}\approx 2.785294
. Thus, the stability condition is

|\lambda m_{1}\Delta t|\leq 2\sqrt{2}.

Since
|m_{1}|\leq N
, the most restrictive condition is

\Delta t\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{|\lambda|N}.

(5.5)

This is a mild constraint compared to the original stiffness, as it does not depend on
\alpha
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For the linearized fZK equation with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the integrating-factor RK4 scheme is stable under the condition

\Delta t\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{|\lambda|N}.

Proof.

The stability function
R(iy)
for RK4 satisfies
|R(iy)|\leq 1
for
|y|\leq 2\sqrt{2}
. Since
y=\lambda m_{1}\Delta t
and
|m_{1}|\leq N
, the condition follows.
∎

We now analyze the temporal truncation error of the integrating-factor RK4 scheme (
5.3
) applied to the transformed system (
5.2
). The temporal local truncation error
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
at time step
n+1
is defined by

\mathcal{T}_{n+1}=V(t_{n+1})-V^{n+1},

(5.6)

where
V(t)
denotes the exact solution of (
5.2
) with the initial condition
V(t_{n})=V^{n}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
u
be the exact solution of the fZK equation (
1.2
) with initial data
u_{0}\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{r}(\Omega)
,
r\geq\alpha+5
, and assume the nonlinear term
\mathcal{N}
is sufficiently smooth. Then the temporal local truncation error of the integrating-factor RK4 scheme satisfies

\|\mathcal{T}_{n+1}\|\leq C\Delta t^{5},

where the constant
C
depends on
\|u_{0}\|_{r}
,
\alpha
, and the final time
T
, but is independent of
\Delta t
and
N
.

Proof.

By the Taylor expansion of the exact solution
V(t_{n+1})
about
t_{n}
in (
5.2
), we have

\displaystyle V(t_{n+1})=V(t_{n})

\displaystyle+\Delta t\mathcal{F}(t_{n},V(t_{n}))+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t_{n},V(t_{n}))

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{6}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}(t_{n},V(t_{n}))+\frac{\Delta t^{4}}{24}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t^{3}}(t_{n},V(t_{n}))+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{5}).

(5.7)

Using Taylor expansions for each stage in the integrating-factor RK4 scheme (
5.3
), we have

\displaystyle k_{1}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{F},

\displaystyle k_{2}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{F}\left(t_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2},V^{n}+\frac{k_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\left[\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{8}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{4}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{8}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{48}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{3}}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{16}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{16}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{48}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{3}}\mathcal{F}^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4})\right],

\displaystyle k_{3}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{F}\left(t_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2},V^{n}+\frac{k_{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\left[\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{8}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{4}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{8}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{48}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{3}}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{16}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{16}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{48}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{3}}\mathcal{F}^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{16}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{16}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4})\Bigg],

\displaystyle k_{4}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\mathcal{F}(t_{n}+\Delta t,V^{n}+k_{3})

\displaystyle=\Delta t\left[\mathcal{F}+\Delta t\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\Delta t\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}}+\Delta t^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{6}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{3}}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{2}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{2}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{6}\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{3}}\mathcal{F}^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4})\Bigg],

where all derivatives be evaluated at
(t_{n},V^{n})
.
Computing the weighted combination gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}(k_{1}+2k_{2}+2k_{3}+k_{4})=

\displaystyle\Delta t\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{6}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}}+2\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta t^{4}}{24}\left(\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{3}}+3\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}\partial V}\mathcal{F}+3\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}+\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{3}}\mathcal{F}^{3}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+4\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}}+4\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right)+2\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+2\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right)\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{5}).

Note that the total derivatives satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F},

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}}+2\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V}\mathcal{F}+\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{3}}+3\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}\partial V}\mathcal{F}+3\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{2}+\frac{\partial^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\partial V^{3}}\mathcal{F}^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+4\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t^{2}}+4\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial t\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right)+2\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial t}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial V}\mathcal{F}\right)\right),

we can write the numerical update as

V^{n+1}=V^{n}+\Delta t\mathcal{F}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{6}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}+\frac{\Delta t^{4}}{24}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t^{3}}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{5}).

Comparing the above identity with the exact Taylor expansion (
5.7
) and the initial condition
V^{n}=V(t_{n})
, we find that the numerical method matches the exact solution up to the
\Delta t^{4}
term. The local truncation error is therefore:

\mathcal{T}_{n+1}=V(t_{n+1})-V^{n+1}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{5}).

Under the regularity assumption, the constant in the
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{5})
term is bounded uniformly in
N
, completing the proof of the local error estimate.
∎

The global error accumulates as
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4})
over
\mathcal{O}(1/\Delta t)
time steps. Combining with the spatial error analysis from Theorem
4.4
, the overall error of the fully discrete scheme satisfies

\|u(t_{n})-u_{N}^{n}\|\leq C_{1}N^{-r}+C_{2}\Delta t^{4},

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are constants independent of
N
and
\Delta t
.

The integrating-factor RK4 scheme effectively handles the stiffness introduced by the fractional dispersive term while maintaining an explicit treatment of nonlinearities. The method achieves optimal convergence rates in both space and time, providing an efficient computational framework for long-time simulations of fractional nonlinear dispersive equations.

7.
Conclusion

This work introduced a novel Fourier spectral Galerkin (FSG) method for the fZK equation (
1.2
) on the periodic domain, providing the first complete numerical analysis of this model. The proposed scheme preserves discrete analogs of mass, momentum, and Hamiltonian energy, ensuring long-term stability and physical fidelity. We established the well-posedness of the semi-discrete problem, proved the uniform convergence of the semi-discrete solution to the unique solution of (
1.2
), and derived optimal error estimates, including exponential convergence for analytic data. An efficient integrating-factor Runge–Kutta time discretization was developed to handle stiffness from the fractional dispersive term.

Future work will extend this framework to more general boundary conditions, such as Dirichlet, and investigate the Kato-type smoothing effect numerically using weighted or modified Galerkin methods. Such studies would bridge the gap between the analytical smoothing results, as in
[
30
]
, and computational practice, offering deeper insight into the regularity of fZK solutions in bounded domains. This paper lays the groundwork for such advanced numerical explorations.
