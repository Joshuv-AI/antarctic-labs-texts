Inversion by Partial Evaluation: A Reversible Interpreter Experiment
Robert Glück Louis Marott Normann

Abstract
A computational limit of combining partial evaluation and program inversion is investigated. Using a reversible Turing machine interpreter, we show that the first Futamura and inversion projections can produce not only functionally but also textually equivalent programs. The construction of the interpreter in a reversible flowchart language is shown in full. Insights are provided on the practical interplay between reversible interpreters, program inverters, and partial evaluators. We conclude that both projections must be included in the program transformation toolbox.

The
Futamura projections
are a cornerstone of program transformation. They show, among other things, that programs can be translated by specializing interpreters
[
14

.
Partial evaluation
is a method for program specialization for which all three Futamura projections have been constructively realized
[
29
]
.
Inspired by this success,

Reversible computing
is an unconventional computing paradigm that,
like partial evaluation,
emerged
in the 1970s
[
6
]
and has attracted renewed interest through applications in quantum-based and energy-efficient computing (e.g.,
[
11

).
The defining features
are backward deterministic computation without information erasure and the
invertibility of reversible programs
[
24
]
.
By contrast, mainstream languages are typically
backward nondeterministic,
and

is a challenging

(e.g., program calculation

Reversible

RTMs
are forward and backward deterministic Turing machines. They operate over a finite set of tape symbols (including the blank symbol
b
) by reading/writing symbols and moving the tape head on a two-way infinite tape.
An example is the RTM
inc
, which increments a binary
n
-bit number by 1 (modulo
2^{n}
). For simplicity, we assume that the least significant bit comes first. Initially, the tape head is on the left of the first digit. The tape head then shifts to the right, flipping bits until it
flips a “0” to a “1” or encounters a blank and then returns to
its original position (e.g., 1101

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{inc}}}}{{\rightarrow}}

0011, 1111

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{inc}}}}{{\rightarrow}}

0000)
[
43
]
.
The transition diagram of
inc
is shown in Fig.
2
(a), and the transition rules are shown in Fig.
3
(a).

An RTM is defined by a finite set of symbol and shift
rules that
transition from state
q_{1}
to state
q_{2}

of the machine
when reading/writing
on the tape
or moving the tape head:

•

the

symbol rule

(q_{1},(s_{1},s_{2}),q_{2})

reads symbol
s_{1}
and writes symbol
s_{2}
on the tape;

•

the
shift rules

(q_{1},\mbox{{--}},q_{2})
and
(q_{1},\mbox{{+}},q_{2})

move the tape head to the left and right,
respectively.

An RTM starts and halts at unique start and final states in a
standard position
,
namely, with the tape head on the first blank to the left of a finite blank-free string of tape symbols (the input and output string).
There must be no transition leading to the start state or out of the final state.
The remaining tape is assumed to contain an infinite number of blanks.

A machine is locally
forward
and
backward deterministic
if every state has either (i) one outgoing or incoming shift rule or (ii) one or more outgoing (incoming) symbol rules that read (write) different symbols.
The binary increment machine
inc
is such an RTM. It is easy to syntactically check whether it is locally forward and backward deterministic by inspecting the rules (either in the diagram or the rule set).
For example, state 4 has exactly one outgoing shift rule and three incoming symbol rules that write three different symbols (0, 1,
b
).
A Turing machine is usually only forward deterministic, whereas an RTM is always forward and backward deterministic.
RTMs are a proper subset of the Turing machines.

The RTMs were introduced by Bennett
[
6
]
; we use the modern
triple transition rules

of publication
[
5
]
, where a complete formalization
is presented. RTMs are the gold standard for reversible programming languages because they compute the
injective computable functions
(for every Turing machine that implements an injective function, there exists an RTM that implements the same function, and vice versa). A reversible language that is computationally as powerful as the RTMs is
r-Turing complete

[
4
,
5
]
.

Bennett gave a method
[
6
]
that can embed any deterministic
Turing machine in an RTM
at the cost of computing additional output.
It is remarkable that every
irreversible computation can be embedded in a reversible (injective) computation: this is the theoretical basis for
the possibility of universal, reversible computing devices.
In addition, there are several subclasses, such as the self-inverse RTMs
[
34
]
.

Machine Inversion

Unlike a conventional Turing machine, the inversion of an RTM into an inverse machine is straightforward:

1.

swap the unique start and final states, and

2.

locally invert each rule as follows:

(q_{1},

(s_{1},s_{2}),

q_{2})
to

(q_{2},

(s_{2},s_{1}),

q_{1})
,

(q_{1},

\mbox{{+}},

q_{2})
to

(q_{2},

\mbox{{--}},

q_{1})
,

(q_{1},

\mbox{{--}},

q_{2})
to

(q_{2},

\mbox{{+}},

q_{1})
.

The result of inverting an RTM is again an RTM.
An example of this is the inversion of the binary increment machine
inc
illustrated in Fig.
2
(a).
The inverse machine
inc
-1
that decrements a binary number is shown in
Fig.
2
(b) and its transition rules in
Fig.
3
(b).

The inversion of transition diagram (a) into transition diagram (b) in Fig.
2

can also be performed graphically by swapping the unique start and final states, changing the direction of all arrows, swapping the read/write symbols, and changing the shift direction (
+

\leftrightarrow

–
).
In both cases, the formal and graphical inversion, the result is
a binary decrement machine.
Note that inversion neither adds nor deletes rules.
Inverting an RTM twice returns the original RTM.
For example, inverting
inc
-1

gives:
({\textsf{inc}^{-1}})^{-1}={\textsf{inc}}
.

Machine Inversion - Revisited

Although the theoretical
description
of an RTM
contains
an unordered set of rules, this abstraction cannot be represented in ARL.
These rules are represented by an ordered list, as shown in Fig.
3
.
The order in which the rules are listed
affects the performance of the interpreter
because it determines the order in which the main loop checks them.

Therefore, the order
of rules
after inversion is important.
There are several ways an RTM inverter can list inverted rules,
all of which are
functionally equivalent
but influence the performance of the
inverse machine,
such as leaving the original order unchanged or sorting the rules according to their states.
However, neither
preserves the performance of the original machine.
A closer examination of the structure of the interpreter leads to the conclusion that the inverter should

reverse
the original order of the rules instead.
The inversion shown in Fig.
3
(b) uses this approach.

The partial evaluator for ARL (

) was introduced in a previous work
[

]
.
PEARL is fully implemented in Haskell and is publicly available as part of the complete

1

https://github.com/Yakokse/PEARL
.
It is an offline partial evaluator
inspired by a three-step procedure from previous partial evaluators of irreversible flowchart languages
[
26
]
. However, further action must be taken to ensure that the final residual program is a well-formed reversible program.
PEARL
is an offline partial evaluator
that performs a uniform

For more information on partial evaluation for traditional flowchart languages, see previous publications

,

The key factors for ensuring well-formed reversible programs have been discussed in detail in previous publications
[
33
,
35
]
.
Similar to how ARL-programs must always have a single entry, they must also have only a single exit, which means that the exit block must
be specialized only once.
In some cases, assertion statements are introduced to preserve the exact semantics of source programs.
Static store updates by the partial evaluator must be injective to generate backward deterministic residual programs.

Reasons for the Successful Projections

There are six degrees of freedom in the experimental setup
in Fig.
1
, which allow for many subtle variations that can result in the residual programs not being textually equivalent.
Given that we have the partial evaluator PEARL, the only publicly available specializer for a reversible language, its source language ARL is an obvious choice for
{\mathit{R}}
, fixing
{\mathit{R}}
-
{\it{spec}}
and
{\mathit{R}}
-
{\it{inv}}
.
This determines the first three degrees of freedom:
{\mathit{R}}
,
{\mathit{R}}
-
{\it{spec}}
, and
{\mathit{R}}
-
{\it{inv}}
.

For the remaining three degrees of freedom,
{\mathit{L}}
,
{\mathit{L}}
-
{\it{int}}
, and
{\mathit{L}}
-
{\it{inv}}
, we chose the RTM language for
{\mathit{L}}
owing to its computational power.
However, the
exact
design and implementation details of
{\mathit{L}}
-
{\it{int}}
and
{\mathit{L}}
-
{\it{inv}}
remained open to experimentation.
This
resulted in several experiments
that produced residual programs with minor performance and textual differences
[
35
, Sec. 5.2]
.
We have identified many requirements that lead to the successful
application of the
projections.
These are summarized as follows.

1.

Alignment of

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}

w.r.t. PEARL and

{ARL}
-
{\it{inv}}
: The dynamic parts of

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}

must be textually equivalent to the appropriate dynamic parts of

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}
.
The parts of

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
that PEARL
determines as
dynamic must be symmetric (e.g., the blocks for
updating and moving the tape head)
because

{ARL}
-
{\it{inv}}

[
35
, Appdx. A]

performs inversion purely structurally.
However, asymmetric parts
are acceptable as long as they are static
(e.g., block
reload
at the end of the main loop)
because they are evaluated
by the partial evaluator
and are not part of the residual program.

2.

Alignment of

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{inv}}

w.r.t.

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
:
Some freedom in

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{inv}}
may be restricted by other components. For example, the
reverse
order of the inverted rules was determined by how

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}

traverses the rule list, as explained in Sec.
2.1
.
If another order had been used, the order of matching symbol rules
in the residual program
could have been different.

3.

{ARL}
-
{\it{inv}}
and
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{inv}}
:
The inverters for the two languages
were effective in preserving the structure and quality of their respective programs. If they instead generated some artifacts in the code (e.g., additional rules), it would
also possibly
affect the residual programs.
The machine
artifacts are directly compiled into the residual program via the Futamura projection. Interpreter artifacts also appear in the residual program if deemed dynamic by the specializer.

4.

PEARL
:
A key characteristics of the uniform BTA used by PEARL is that it is flow-insensitive (i.e., a variable has the same binding time at every point in a program).
This is a desirable characteristic
when we want the
binding-time annotations of
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
to match those of

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}
.

Generality of Results

The above discussion shows how interconnected the different parts of the experiment were. An obvious question is whether the
two residual programs
generated by (i) and (ii)
are textually equivalent
for all RTMs and not just for
inc
and some other machines.
Although we have no formal proof,
let the following argument suffice.

The inversion of

{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
to
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}

by
{ARL}
-
{\it{inv}}

is
performed once
for all RTMs, and
we can examine
the two programs
independently of a particular RTM.

The specialization of
an
{ARL}
-program w.r.t. static input
follows the program annotations of PEARL’s BTA,
and the outcome of this specialization can be predicted.
This is an advantage of using an offline partial evaluator.
The BTA only uses the
classification
of
the machine description as
static
(
Start
,
Final
,
Rules
) and the input tape as
dynamic
(
S_right
).
It
guarantees that all operations that depend only on the rules (e.g.,
Rule
,
Rules
, and
RulesRev
)
and the state (i.e.,
Q
,
Q1
, and
Q2
) are annotated as static (colored blue in Fig.
5
) and fully
evaluated at specialization time.
Static operations in
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
and
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}
are
thus never part of the residual programs
(e.g., the entire blocks
act1
to
act3
of the main loop).
Only the operations that depend on the tape (
S
,
S_right
, and
S_left
) are annotated as dynamic.
These operations occur in blocks related to moving the tape head and updating the symbol
S
.
Notably, the annotation of
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
and
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}
by the BTA is independent of a particular RTM.

Because the same
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{inv}}
is used for all RTMs, we know how each rule
is inverted (Sec.
2
) and that the inverted rules are listed in reverse order in the description of the inverse machine
(Sec.
2.1
).
It remains to be examined in which order symbol rules are matched, and how the blocks that execute symbol and shift
rules in
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}
and
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
are specialized by PEARL w.r.t. a rule
r
and its inverse
r^{-1}
.

•

The net effect of the opposite rule orders of an RTM and its inverse is that
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}
and
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
traverse the
rule lists
in the
same order
.
Thus, if a state has multiple outgoing or incoming symbol rules, they are matched with the dynamic symbol
S
in the same order in both residual programs.

•

By specializing
the blocks that execute a symbol rule in
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
w.r.t.
r^{-1}=(q_{2},(s_{2},s_{1}),q_{1})
and by specializing the corresponding inverted blocks in
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}
w.r.t.
r=(q_{1},(s_{1},s_{2}),q_{2})
, textually equivalent residual blocks are generated, regardless of the
concrete
states
and symbols.

•

A
left shift

(q_{2},\mbox{{--}},q_{1})
in an inverse RTM is implemented in
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}
using blocks that move the
head to the
left
.
A
right shift

(q_{1},\mbox{{+}},q_{2})
in the original RTM is implemented in
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{int}}^{-1}
using blocks that move the head to the
left
.
In both cases, the
residual blocks shift left and are textually equivalent.
The same applies to a right shift
(q_{2},\mbox{{+}},q_{1})
and a left shift
(q_{1},\mbox{{--}},q_{2})
.

Thus, the order in which the symbol rules are matched is the same in both residual programs, and the residual blocks implementing the
symbol and shift rules are textually equivalent. This completes the argument for why we conjecture that
the two residual programs
are textually equivalent for all RTMs.

Alternative Experiment

One of the tantalizing
questions is whether the inversion is neutralized across two levels, i.e., whether specializing an inverse interpreter
{\it{int}}^{-1}
w.r.t. an inverse program
{\it{q}}^{-1}

is as if no inversion was performed at all, neither of
{\it{int}}

nor

{\it{q}}
.
That is, whether the
residual
program is textually equivalent to the residual program generated by specializing
{\it{int}}
w.r.t.
{\it{q}}
according to the first Futamura projection
[
14
]
, which corresponds to the translation of
{\it{q}}_{\mathit{L}}
into
{\it{q}}_{\mathit{R}}
.

Similar to (
1
), this question can be briefly stated:

\displaystyle\llbracket{\it{spec}}\rrbracket({\it{int}},{\it{q}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\footnotesize?}}}{{\cong}}

\displaystyle\llbracket{\it{spec}}\rrbracket({\it{int}}^{-1},{\it{q}}^{-1})~.

(2)

Again, the residual programs generated by
{\it{spec}}
on both sides of (
2
)
are
guaranteed to be
functionally equivalent
[
3
,
14
]
, but can they be textually equivalent?
In our case, where

{ARL}
-
{\it{inv}}
and
{\mathit{RTM}}
-
{\it{inv}}

perform a structural inversion,
this experiment
is an instance of the original setup in Fig.
1
.
Everything said
above
also applies to the experimental setup (
2
),
since
{\it{p}}

can be any RTM and in particular the inverse of an RTM.
For example,
inc
-1
in Fig.
2
is just another RTM
of the same
quality as
inc
,
and

({\textsf{inc${}^{-1}$}})^{-1}={\textsf{inc}}
.

Let
{\it{p}}
be the inverse of an RTM
{\it{q}}

{\it{p}}={\it{q}}^{-1}
,
then
{\it{p}}^{-1}=({\it{q}}^{-1})^{-1}={\it{q}}

With the textual equivalence (

the inversion of both levels is always neutralized:

\displaystyle\llbracket{PEARL}\rrbracket({\mathit{RTM}}\text{-}{\it{int}},{\it{q}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\llbracket{PEARL}\rrbracket({\mathit{RTM}}\text{-}{\it{int}},{\it{p}}^{-1})

(3)

\displaystyle\cong

4
Conclusion and Future Work

Our goal
was to explore the
interplay
between partial evaluation and
program
inversion in reversible computing. We focused on the computational limit of
program inversion by interpreter specialization, a possibility
that has been known for some time
[
2
,
3
]
.
For this purpose, we used an RTM-interpreter because RTMs are as computationally powerful as any reversible programming language
[
5
]
.

We confirmed that the offline partial evaluator for a reversible flowchart language
[
35
]
can completely eliminate the interpretive overhead of a reversible interpreter and
that an RTM-interpreter exists for which the first Futamura and inversion projections can produce
textually equivalent programs.
We found that both methods are equally powerful and that neither projection is fundamentally preferable.
The method that is more effective
depends, among other things, on the particular interpreter and its source language.
We conclude that both projections must be included in our
transformation toolbox.

For irreversible languages, it seems more difficult to achieve the same
results, regardless of whether inversion is performed at the source or meta-level because the effective inversion of irreversible Turing machines and programming languages remains a challenging problem (first attempted in the 1950s
[
32
]
).
On the other hand, any deterministic Turing machine can be embedded in an RTM
at the cost of computing additional output
[
6
]
.

Programming languages, in general, and unconventional
program
transformations, in particular, require
constructive validations.
Although the projections used in this study confirm the theoretical possibility and semantic correctness of
the transformations,
only actual
program constructions can demonstrate the
real possibility of achieving non-trivial
results.
The above results
show the potential
of combining a small number of basic meta-operations, including specialization and inversion of programs
[
20
]
.

The mission continues: to explore strange new worlds of computing!
