From Malaise to Oral Pain: A Case Report on Oral Mucosal Melanoma

1. Introduction

Oral melanoma is a rare form of melanoma, accounting for 0.2%–0.8% of all melanomas [1, 2] and 1%–2% of all oral malignancies [1, 3]. Unlike cutaneous melanoma, it often lacks early visible signs and can mimic benign pigmented lesions or other intraoral conditions, making timely diagnosis particularly challenging. The 5‐year survival rate remains low, ranging from 15% to 38% [4], largely due to delayed detection and early metastasis.

Oral mucosal melanoma (OMM) most commonly affects older men, with a mean age of approximately 60 years, although cases have been reported across a wide age range, including adolescents and the elderly [5, 6]. Oral melanoma is more common in non‐Hispanic White individuals.

Due to the neural crest origin of melanocytes, melanomas may arise in noncutaneous locations containing neural crest derivatives, including the central nervous system and gastrointestinal tract [7]. Unlike cutaneous melanoma, oral melanoma is not associated with ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure. Its pathogenesis is thought to involve malignant transformation of melanocytes in the basal layer of the oral epithelium, influenced by genetic and epigenetic factors [8].

These tumors typically arise on the hard palate or maxillary gingiva and may be asymptomatic in early stages. By the time they are clinically recognized—often due to swelling, bleeding, or tooth mobility—they may have already invaded local tissues or metastasized [9]. Common metastatic sites include the regional lymph nodes, lungs, and liver [7].

This case is particularly notable because metastatic visceral melanoma was identified prior to recognition of the oral primary lesion, underscoring the diagnostic challenges of OMM and the critical role of dental evaluation in patients with melanoma of unknown primary.

2. Clinical Case

A 61‐year‐old Bengali male presented to the emergency department with groin pain. His past medical history included hypertension, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). His medications included amlodipine, metformin, glipizide, nicotine polacrilex, and omeprazole. He had no known drug allergies.

The patient was a former smoker with a 40 pack‐year history (1 pack per day for 40 years), having quit 1 year prior and was currently using nicotine replacement therapy. On presentation, it was noted that he had unintentionally lost 20 pounds over the past year.

Although the groin pain was ultimately determined to be insignificant, CT imaging revealed masses in the liver. Medical oncology was consulted for further evaluation. Additional imaging revealed retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, pulmonary masses, and trace pelvic ascites. Given the concern for metastatic disease, the patient was referred for outpatient biopsy and oncology follow‐up.

Biopsy of a liver metastasis confirmed metastatic melanoma, and the patient was subsequently started on combination immunotherapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab. Further imaging showed metastatic involvement of the lungs, kidneys, spleen, splenic hilum, rectus muscle, and chest wall. At this stage, the primary site of melanoma remained unclear, as no cutaneous lesions were identified. Three weeks later, the patient returned to the hospital with complaints of oral pain. Examination in the emergency department revealed a dark gingival pigmentation in the region of Teeth #6–9, and he was referred to the dental clinic for evaluation and extraction of nonrestorable teeth.

On presentation to dental clinic, the patient appeared frail and complained of fatigue, poor appetite, weight loss, and progressive loosening of his upper front teeth. He reported only receiving sporadic dental care in the past and had noticed the change in the color of his gingiva months prior to his dental visit. Intraoral exam revealed black discoloration of upper labial vestibule and incisor teeth gingiva as well as an exophytic mass of similar color palatal to Teeth #7–9 (Figure 1). The affected teeth had Grade 3 mobility with no or minimal bone support on imaging (Figures 2 and 3).

An incisional biopsy of the maxillary anterior gingiva and vestibule was subsequently performed. Histopathological analysis revealed malignant melanoma. Gingival mucosa was infiltrated by sheets of heavily pigmented cells with atypical nuclei and numerous mitotic figures (Figure 4a). Lesional cells stained diffusely positive for SOX‐10, HMB‐45, and Melan‐A (Figure 4b–d) and showed a high proliferative index with Ki‐67 staining, consistent with malignant melanoma. Following three cycles of ipilimumab and nivolumab, imaging demonstrated disease progression. The patient′s treatment was subsequently changed to pembrolizumab and lenvatinib. Nearly a year later, the patient was admitted to the hospital once again, this time due to poor appetite. The dental team was consulted to evaluate a lesion on the hard palate. The patient reported intermittent pain localized to the left side of the hard palate, particularly during eating, and experienced tenderness upon palpation of the lesion.

Extraoral examination revealed bilateral, fixed, firm nodules approximately 1 cm in diameter at the angles of the mandible (Figure 5). Follow up computed tomography of the facial bones disclosed multiple pathologic lymph nodes in the right submandibular region and right posterior triangle (Figure 6). Intraorally, edentulous alveolar mucosa corresponding to Tooth #9 appeared blue‐black and a 3 × 1 cm growth with rolled borders and central perforation was evident on the left hard palate (Figure 7) likely representing another site of disease spread.

The patient reported limited oral intake and continued to experience progressive weight loss and physical decline. A PET CT was ordered and demonstrated ongoing progression of metastatic disease involving the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. He was referred to palliative care for pain management and was prescribed oxycodone 2.5–5 mg every 4 h, 2% viscous lidocaine, and acetaminophen, all as needed.

The patient elected to enroll in Phase I clinical trial of immune‐based therapy for patients with advanced or metastatic solid tumors but unfortunately passed away.

3. Discussion

Melanoma is a malignant neoplasm arising from melanocytes, most commonly affecting sun‐exposed areas of the skin. However, melanocytes are also present in mucosal tissues, including the oral cavity.

Non‐Hispanic White individuals have the highest absolute incidence rates of oral melanoma, primarily due to their higher overall melanoma rates. However, Asian/Pacific Islander, Black, and Hispanic populations have a higher proportion of mucosal melanomas—including oral melanoma—relative to their total melanoma cases [10]. A study by Altieri et al. [10] reported that mucosal melanomas constitute approximately 15% of all melanomas in Asian/Pacific Islanders, 9% in non‐Hispanic Blacks, and 4% in Hispanics, compared to only about 1% in non‐Hispanic Whites.

Importantly, increased melanin levels—which offer protection against UV radiation—do not influence the risk of developing oral melanoma [9]. This is because oral melanoma arises in mucosal tissues that are not exposed to UV light [9]. Although benign intraoral melanocytic proliferations (nevi) do occur and may serve as precursors in rare cases, the progression from nevus to melanoma in the oral cavity remains poorly understood. Currently, most oral melanomas are believed to arise de novo, without an identifiable precursor lesion [9]. In rare instances, malignant transformation involves the clonal expansion of melanocytes—either within an existing nevus or among isolated basal layer melanocytes—that acquire a selective growth advantage and begin uncontrolled proliferation [9]. The etiology of oral melanoma remains largely unknown, and the mechanisms underlying malignant transformation in mucosal melanocytes continue to be an area of active research.

Clinically, oral melanoma often presents as an asymptomatic macule with variable pigmentation. Classic features include a dark brown to black lesion, frequently involving the maxillary gingiva or hard palate. These lesions typically have irregular and uneven borders and may exhibit loss of pigmentation, ulceration, or bleeding. In some cases, approximately 37%, OMM arises from a pre‐existing pigmented lesion [11]. Differential diagnoses for isolated pigmented lesions include benign entities such as amalgam tattoo, melanotic macule, melanocytic nevus, and melanoacanthoma. After a thorough clinical history taking to determine if this lesion is isolated or part of a systemic condition, dentists should evaluate the size, color, border, and induration of these lesions. Lesions that are isolated with irregular borders and color variation should be referred for biopsy. The American Academy of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathology recommends biopsy of any persistent, solitary pigmented lesion—particularly in high‐risk sites such as the palate and gingiva [12, 13].

As OMM progresses, the lesion may become nodular and exhibit invasive characteristics, including erosion of underlying bone [14]. In the present case, the patient exhibited diffuse blue‐black pigmentation in the anterior maxilla (Teeth #6–9), along with a 3 × 1 cm endophytic lesion with rolled borders and associated bone perforation.

Determining whether the oral lesion represented a primary OMM or an oral metastasis from an unknown primary site was challenging. Diagnostic criteria proposed for primary oral melanoma include evidence of malignant melanoma in the oral mucosa, intraepithelial or junctional activity when present, and exclusion of another primary melanoma site [14]. In the present case, malignant melanoma was confirmed within the oral mucosa; however, significant junctional activity or an unequivocal melanoma in situ component was not identified histologically; therefore, clinicopathologic correlation and exclusion of another primary site are essential. The oral cavity cannot be designated as the definitive primary site with absolute certainty, and the possibility of metastatic melanoma to the oral cavity or melanoma of unknown primary cannot be completely excluded.

Nevertheless, several clinicopathologic features support the oral cavity as the presumptive primary site. First, no cutaneous, ocular, or other mucosal primary lesion was identified despite clinical and radiographic evaluation. Second, the patient reported noticing gingival discoloration months before the dental visit, suggesting that the oral lesion may have been present prior to it being formally evaluated and biopsied. Third, the lesion involved a common site for OMM, namely the maxillary gingiva/anterior palate, and demonstrated locally destructive behavior with severe alveolar bone loss and marked tooth mobility. Finally, OMMs are often diagnosed late and may present after regional or distant metastasis has already occurred. For these reasons, the oral lesion was interpreted as the most likely, though not definitively proven, primary site.

Immunohistochemical diagnosis of OMM can be challenging, as no single marker reliably detects all cases. Commonly used markers include S‐100, Melan‐A, MITF, tyrosinase, and HMB‐45, each showing variable expression [15]. SOX‐10 has emerged as a useful marker due to its high sensitivity in OMM, though it has moderate specificity in broader mucosal melanoma [15]. In our case, the tumor demonstrated positive staining for S‐100, SOX‐10, Melan‐A, and HMB‐45.

Due to the rarity and heterogeneity of OMM, there is no standardized treatment protocol. Surgical excision is the mainstay for localized disease. However, advanced and metastatic cases require systemic therapy. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown promise in prolonging survival in patients with metastatic melanoma [16, 17]. Despite receiving combination immunotherapy, our patient experienced disease progression, prompting a transition to investigational therapy on a clinical trial and then palliative‐focused care.

Prognosis for oral melanoma remains poor, with reported 5‐year survival rates ranging from 15% to 38% [4]. In their 35‐case series, Lourenço et al. [18] similarly reported that most patients presented with advanced‐stage disease, contributing to unfavorable clinical outcomes. This is primarily due to its aggressive nature and early hematogenous and lymphatic spread to the lungs, liver, and regional lymph nodes [18, 19]. In this case, the patient′s disease was already advanced at initial presentation, with widespread metastases to the liver, lungs, spleen, and retroperitoneum, and later spread to the brain.

4. Conclusion

Oral melanoma is a rare form of cancer with poor prognosis and challenging to treat. Increased awareness for practitioners, especially general dentists, could improve detection, expedite care, and aid in better outcomes.

This case illustrates the impact of irregular dental checkups and patient′s lack of knowledge on timely diagnosis of a readily visible oral malignancy. Oral health care providers should recognize, appropriately assess, and accordingly monitor pigmented lesions of the oral cavity. Furthermore, this case highlights the importance of oral cavity inspection by medical providers, particularly when the primary source of visceral melanoma is unknown and detection of lesions in additional sites could potentially impact the therapeutic efficacy. Collaboration between medical and dental professionals could facilitate best case practices for meeting the needs of patients with oral melanoma.
