Prediction of major arrhythmic outcomes in ischaemic cardiomyopathy: value of hibernating myocardium in positron emission tomography/computed tomography
Authors: Boldizsar Kovacs, Valon Gllareva, Frank Ruschitzka, Firat Duru, Philipp A Kaufmann, Ronny R Buechel, Dominik C Benz, Ardan M Saguner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsKnown predictors of major arrhythmic events (MAEs) in patients with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (ICM) include previous MAE and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 35%. Myocardial scars detected by perfusion imaging in ICM have been linked to MAE, but the prognostic significance of hibernating myocardium (HM) is unclear. The objective was to predict MAEs from combined 13N-ammonia (NH3) and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) in ICM.Methods and resultsConsecutive patients with ICM undergoing combined NH3- and FDG-PET/CT were included. HM was quantified in relation to total left ventricular myocardium (i.e. ≥7% is large). The primary outcome was MAEs [sudden cardiac death, implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) therapy, and sustained ventricular tachycardia/fibrillation].Among 254 patients, median baseline LVEF was 35% [interquartile range (IQR) 28–45] and 10% had an ICD. PET/CT identified ischaemia in 94 (37%), scar in 229 (90%), and HM in 195 (77%) patients. Over a median follow-up of 5.4 (IQR 2.2–9.5) years, MAE occurred in 34 patients (13%). Large HM was associated with a lower incidence of MAE (hazard ratio 0.31, 95% confidence interval 0.1–0.8, P = 0.001). After multivariate adjustment for history of MAE, LVEF ≤35%, and scar ≥10%, large HM remained significantly associated with a lower incidence of MAE (P = 0.016). LVEF improved over time among patients with large HM (P = 0.006) but did not change in those without (P = 0.610) or small HM (P = 0.240).ConclusionHM conveys a lower risk of MAE in patients with ICM. This may be explained by an increase in LVEF when a large extent of HM is present.

Introduction

Sudden cardiac death (SCD) due to ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation (VT/VF) is a major cause of mortality in patients with structural heart disease, particularly ischaemic heart disease with left ventricular dysfunction [ischaemic cardiomyopathy (ICM)].1 Ischaemia leads to a gradual functional loss of cardiomyocytes and eventually to the loss of structural integrity, thus necrosis with subsequent scarring.2 These scars may be substrate for ventricular arrhythmias/major arrhythmic events (MAEs) due to the changes in electrophysiological properties and pathological remodelling.3–5 Strong predictors of MAE such as SCD, survived SCD, or appropriate implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) therapies in ICM include prior MAE, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 35%, and functional class [New York Heart Association (NYHA)].6–8 Current guidelines recommend ICD implantation based on these parameters.9,10 LVEF, however, is a single cross-sectional assessment of cardiac function and has important limitations to predict MAE.11

Viability testing of myocardium allows for differentiation between scar and hibernating myocardium (HM), which may predict improvement of LVEF.10,12,13 The detection of perfusion/metabolism mismatch using the combination of 13N-ammonia (NH3) and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) allows for the quantification of areas that have sustained a metabolism even though the coronary blood flow is reduced, thus having entered in a sort of dedifferentiated but not yet degenerated state.5 In an animal model, HM has been associated with SCD due to regional remodelling on a cellular level with reduction of Connexin43 and inhomogeneous sympathetic innervation.14,15 This association, however, could not be demonstrated in a cohort of patients with ICM undergoing revascularization,14,16,17 although the identification of HM is often performed for clinical decision-making regarding revascularization in daily clinical practice.

This retrospective study aims to predict MAE from combined NH3/FDG-PET/CT in patients with ICM and to specifically address the association between HM and sustained ventricular arrhythmias.

Methods

Study population

The study population consists of consecutive patients included in the Zurich quantitative PET registry.18–20 Briefly, this is a retrospective single-centre registry including patients referred for perfusion and viability testing by NH3- and FDG-PET/CT.19,21,22 The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (BASEC 2016-00177).

PET imaging acquisition and analysis

The study participants underwent NH3-PET imaging both at rest and during adenosine stress using a standard infusion rate of 0.14 mg/min/kg for 7 min, during which 700–900 MBq of NH3 was intravenously administered into a peripheral vein 3 min into stress. After a 20-min interval, a glucose load was given and blood glucose levels measured at 5- to 10-min intervals. After blood glucose levels had peaked, insulin was injected based on blood glucose levels (i.e. sliding scale). Subsequently, 250 MBq of FDG was injected. Following an hour of rest, images were acquired using either a Discovery (LS/RX) or Advance PET/CT scanner (both GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI), in the same manner as previously reported.18–20 The imaging data were reconstructed to obtain static, dynamic, and electrocardiographically gated images for NH3 perfusion imaging and static images for FDG viability imaging.

Image analysis was performed as previously reported.19 Visual scoring was used to assess regional NH3 uptake in reoriented myocardial slices and polar maps using the standardized 17-segment model.23 Regions with perfusion defects on rest images were considered scar, while ischaemia was diagnosed in regions with stress-induced perfusion defect and normal rest perfusion. Defect extent was categorized semi-quantitatively as small to moderate (1–9%), large (≥10%), and very large (≥20%) based on the number of segments involved. HM was identified visually as regions with reduced rest perfusion and normal/enhanced FDG uptake (i.e. perfusion–viability mismatch). If HM was identified, the extent was calculated quantitatively using commercially available software (CSI software, Cedars-Sinai Cardiac Suite, Version 2017.7, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center Artificial Intelligence in Medicine Program, Los Angeles, CA, USA). In some patients (18, 7%), the software failed to quantify HM, and the extent of mismatch was assessed visually. The extent of HM was expressed in % of total LV myocardium and additionally as % of scar. The extent of HM was categorized by the previously established threshold from PARR-2 trial (i.e. 7%) into small (<7%) and large HM (≥7%).24 Image interpretation was performed by two readers, and any disagreement was resolved by consensus. Management recommendations by nuclear cardiologists in the initial clinical report were collected for the present analysis.

Echocardiography

The majority of patients (n = 215, 85%) underwent baseline and follow-up transthoracic echocardiography. Measurement of LVEF was performed by the biplane summation-of-discs method.25

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome of this analysis is time to MAE defined as any of the following events occurring first after index PET scan:

SCD: death within 1 h of symptom occurrence or within 24 h last being seen alive when unwitnessed.

Defibrillation by an external defibrillator for VT/VF.

Appropriate ICD therapy [shock or antitachycardia pacing (ATP)].

Sustained VT/VF (≥30 s) either documented on electrocardiogram, Holter, telemetry, or ICD.

All follow-up data were collected after inclusion into the registry using electronic health records and by contacting the patients’ primary physician, as appropriate. Mortality and cause of death were independently reviewed by two study personnel. ICD programming, in particular detection zones, was reviewed for all patients with an implanted ICD. Cardiac electrograms in patients with ICD therapies were reviewed by a cardiac electrophysiologist.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as medians [interquartile range (IQR)] or mean (±SD) and categorical variables as n (%), as appropriate. Statistical analysis was performed using independent and paired t-test after assessing for normal distribution. Results of the uni- and multivariate Cox proportional hazards model are shown as hazard ratio (HR) and the 95% confidence interval (CI). Patients undergoing revascularization [percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)] within 90 days of PET scan were censored at the time of revascularization. Annualized event rates were calculated from the number of events divided by the sum of follow-up years. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method was done when appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 29, IBM, USA) and Prism (version 10, Dotmatics, USA).

Results

Twenty-six patients (10%) had an ICD implanted at baseline, 18 (69%) for primary, and 8 (31%) for secondary prevention. Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) was implanted in 22 patients (9%). A history of MAE before index PET scan was reported for 33 patients (13%): 16 patients (6%) with a history of survived SCD, 8 (3%) with an appropriate ICD shock, 4 (2%) with appropriate ATP, and 6 (2%) with sustained VT/VF without ICD therapy. After the index PET/CT scan, 30 patients (12%) were implanted with an ICD during follow-up: 21 (70%) for primary and 9 (30%) for secondary prevention. During follow-up, nine patients (4%) received an upgrade to CRT. The ICD programming (VT/VF zones) is shown in the Supplementary data online, Table S1.

Analysis of NH3/FDG-PET/CT scans revealed ischaemia in 94 (37%), myocardial scar in 229 (90%), and HM in 195 (77%) patients. The median extent of HM was 5% (IQR 1–10%) of the total LV myocardium, and median extent of HM for patients with any HM was 7% (IQR 4–10%) (see Supplementary data online, Table S2). Only 35 patients (14%) had significant ischaemia (≥10%), but 179 patients (71%) had large scar (≥10%). The extent of HM was significantly smaller in patients with a scar size 0–9% compared to scar size 10–19% or ≥20% (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1).

Major arrhythmic outcome events

Median follow-up time was 5.4 years (IQR 2.2–9.5). MAE occurred in 34 patients (13%); 18 patients (7%) suffered (survived) SCD, 19 (8%) had at least one sustained VT/VF episode treated with either ICD shocks (4 patients, 2%), ATP (12 patients, 5%), or external shocks (4 patients, 2%). In 2/19 patients (11%), the sustained VT/VF episodes terminated spontaneously. Median time to MAE was 2.5 years (IQR 1–3.9).

Univariate Cox proportional hazards model

History of MAE, LVEF ≤ 35%, and digoxin use were associated with MAE in univariate Cox proportional hazards model (P < 0.05), with digoxin use reported in only 10 patients (2%) (Figure 1). Ischaemia ≥10% of LV myocardium was not associated with MAE (HR 2.0, 95% CI 0.8–5.2; P = 0.16). Conversely, scar ≥10% of LV myocardium was associated with MAE (HR 3.7, 95% CI 1.1–12.2; P = 0.03).

The extent of HM was associated with MAE (P = 0.04). Patients with large HM (≥7%) had less MAE compared to patients without HM (HR 0.3, 95% CI 0.1–0.8, P = 0.018). There was a numerical but not statistically significant difference in MAE between patients with large and small HM (i.e. 1–6%; HR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2–1.2, P = 0.1) and no difference between small and no HM (HR 0.7, 95% CI 0.3–1.6, P = 0.41). Multivariate Cox proportional hazards model

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were created after adjusting for known predictors of MAE including a history of MAE, NYHA functional class ≥2, and LVEF ≤35%. Model 1 included only the known predictors and was strongly associated with MAE during follow-up (P < 0.001). Model 2 included, in addition to Model 1, large LV myocardial scars of ≥10% and did not show an improvement (P = 0.98 compared to Model 1). Model 3 additionally incorporated the extent of HM and significantly improved the model (P = 0.03, compared to Model 2; Figure 3). The extent of HM adjusted only for history of MAE yielded comparable results (see Supplementary data online, Table S3).

Impact of HM on changes in LVEF over time

Among the 215 patients (85%) with baseline and follow-up LVEF assessment (at 4.5 ± 3.4 years of follow-up), both baseline (30 ± 10% vs. 37 ± 12%; P = 0.003) and follow-up LVEF (28 ± 9% vs. 39 ± 14%, P < 0.001) were lower in patients with vs. those without MAE. In the overall cohort, there was a significant increase in LVEF from 36 ± 12% at baseline to 38 ± 14% at follow-up (P = 0.005). Stratified by the extent of HM, only patients with an HM extent of ≥7% had a significant increase in LVEF (P = 0.006, Graphical Abstract).

Interaction of HM with treatment strategy

Of the total study population, 49 patients (19%) underwent revascularization within 90 days of index PET scan (=early revascularization). Twenty-five (10%) patients had PCI and 24 (9%) patients had CABG. Patients with early revascularization were more likely to have ≥10% ischaemia (51% vs. 14%, P < 0.001) and less likely to have ≥10% scar (16% vs. 28%, P = 0.023), but the prevalence of ≥7% HM (45% vs. 40%, P = 0.53) and the extent of HM (6.5 ± 7.1% vs. 6.7 ± 6.4%, P = 0.88) were similar compared to those without early revascularization. In a two-way interaction (early revascularization*HM ≥ 7%), there was no interaction with MAE (HR 0.04, 95% CI 0–8.5, P = 0.24). Outcomes are illustrated for each group in Supplementary data online, Figure S2.

The nuclear cardiologist reading the index PET/CT scan recommended revascularization based on the imaging findings in 18 patients (7%). Patients with treatment recommendation were more likely to have ≥10% ischaemia (33% vs. 12%, P = 0.013). They were equally likely to have ≥7% vs. <7% HM (39% vs. 41%, P = 0.85) and ≥10% vs. <10% scar (66% vs. 70%, P = 0.71) than patients without treatment recommendation. These patients with treatment recommendations were more likely to undergo early revascularization [9/18 patients (50%), with all 9 patients undergoing CABG] than patients without treatment recommendation [40/236 patients (17%) including 25 patients with PCI and 15 patients with CABG] (P = 0.002). MAE rate did not differ between patients with adherence to treatment recommendation (i.e. 0%) and patients who did not adhere (i.e. 11%, P = 0.303).

Discussion

This study investigates the value of nuclear cardiology to predict major ventricular arrhythmic events in ICM. In a large contemporary population with ICM undergoing combined perfusion and viability testing by NH3/FDG-PET/CT, this study demonstrates that a large extent of HM (≥7%) is associated with a lower risk of MAE, even after adjusting for known risk factors of MAE such as a history of previous MAE and LVEF ≤35%. The prognostic benefit of large HM, furthermore, was independent of early revascularization. More importantly, we demonstrated that large HM extent at baseline is linked to an increase in LVEF over long-term follow-up and could serve as an explanation for the favourable arrhythmic outcome in this cohort (Graphical Abstract).

Hibernating myocardial cells, per definition, remain viable and have the potential of regaining their function if perfusion is restored.26 It has been hypothesized that the tissue composition of HM may lead to heterogenous conduction properties serving as the functional basis for re-entrant tachycardia circuits necessary for the occurrence of VT/VF.14,27 While previous studies consistently found an association between myocardial scar and arrhythmic outcomes, HM was also hypothesized to be arrhythmogenic based on animal studies.3,14 Moreover, one clinical study investigated 167 patients with ICM and HM with medical treatment only. They found a significant association between the extent of HM and cardiac death but did not report on arrhythmic outcomes.28

Previous studies have shown conflicting associations between HM and ventricular arrhythmias. In a cohort of SCD survivors and ICM during a 3-year follow-up, 37% of the study population had either SCD or appropriate ICD therapies. These events were associated with lower LVEF and larger scars as detected on imaging studies. However, the presence of HM detected by single-photon emission CT was associated with a lower risk of arrhythmic events when revascularization was performed.17

Myocardial scar was significantly associated with adverse cardiac outcomes in a study investigating the predictive value of 82Rb PET/CT for SCD and ICD shocks in 439 patients. However, HM did not show a significant association with SCD or ICD shocks.3 Of note, this trial included a more heterogenous cohort of ischaemic and non-ICM. Moreover, that study relied on semiquantitative image analyses from 82Rb PET/CT only, which has inferior sensitivity for HM compared to FDG-PET/CT.23 Similar to our study, they did not show an association between ischaemia and the incidence of MAE. With an increase in scar extent, the prognostic and therapeutic benefit of revascularization vanishes.29

Another study investigating the association of sympathetic myocardial denervation and MAE in 204 ICM patients found a significant association between the size of the denervated viable myocardium but not the presence or size of HM and MAE (P = 0.001 and 0.5, respectively). In comparison to the present study, the authors reported a mean age of 67 years (vs. 66 years), a mean LVEF of 27% (vs. 35%), and 33 outcome events over a median of 4.1 years (vs. 34 over 5.4 years). Interestingly, despite a higher LVEF, they reported a considerably smaller scar and HM compared to our study providing a possible explanation for the difference in findings.30

In the recently published REVIVED-BCIS2 trial, revascularization in ICM patients with viable myocardium (as defined in most patients by cardiac magnetic resonance) was neither associated with lower all-cause mortality nor with less sustained VT/VF episodes or ICD treatments.31,32 Notably, the authors reported a higher rate of MAE than our study (41% vs. 13%), likely due to a different (i.e. sicker) study population (older, lower LVEF). Their results suggest that HM per se may not lead to adverse arrhythmic outcomes. This corresponds well to our findings, where viable myocardium (i.e. HM) was associated with less MAE only if >50% of the myocardial scar was viable or if the size of HM exceeded 7% of the LV myocardium. Importantly, this effect was independent of revascularization similar to what was seen in the REVIVED-BCIS2 trial.

The lower rate of MAE in patients with large HM may be explained (i) by the significantly larger amount of viable myocardium with associated improvement in LVEF during follow-up. It is likely that HM has an increased potential of favourable reverse remodelling when appropriate heart failure therapy is established. Considering LVEF is a strong predictor for MAE, this improvement may explain the different outcomes in the groups. Another reason for the lower rate of MAE may be (ii) the higher arrhythmogenicity of non-viable (i.e. transmural) scars than HM. It has been demonstrated in patients with ICM that the majority of critical sites for VTs are found in non-viable rather than viable myocardium.33 The current investigation adds to this finding by demonstrating that HM may indeed be a favourable prognostic marker for arrhythmic outcomes irrespective of revascularization, LVEF, and previous MAE. A possible explanation may be the major improvement of medical therapy for LV dysfunction over the years. The currently reported cohort had a high administration of beta-blocker and ACE-inhibitor/ARB therapy (83 and 89%, respectively). In contrast, when HM was associated with worse prognosis, only 41% of patients received beta-blocker therapy.28

Limitations

The limitations of this study are its retrospective, single-centre design and relatively low number of outcome events, despite the large cohort size. The generally low number of MAE likely demonstrates the increasing efficacy of coronary revascularization and optimized therapy over the years, leading to a lower rate of cardiovascular and arrhythmic endpoints.

Conclusion

In conclusion, HM conveys a lower risk of MAEs in patients with ICM, independent of early revascularization. This may be explained by an increase in ejection fraction when a large extent of HM is present.
