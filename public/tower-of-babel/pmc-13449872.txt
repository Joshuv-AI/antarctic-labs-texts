Poly-γ-glutamic acid with moderate nitrogen mitigates cadmium accumulation in pepper fruits
Authors: Yu Yang, Xuantong Li, Ziqi Dai, Hailan Peng, Zhenzhu Xu, Jianjian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCadmium (Cd) contamination in agricultural soils threatens crop productivity and food safety worldwide. Pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) readily accumulates Cd in edible tissues, which increases potential health risks. Nitrogen (N) fertilization can improve chlorophyll content and yield in pepper, but it may also increase Cd uptake. Poly-γ-glutamic acid (PGA), a microbial biopolymer with metal-chelating ability, may help reduce this negative effect.MethodsA pot experiment was conducted with two soil Cd levels, three urea application rates, and two foliar PGA concentrations. Chlorophyll content, yield and yield components, N and P concentrations, micronutrient concentrations, and Cd concentrations were measured.ResultsUnder Cd stress, nitrogen was the main factor improving yield. PGA provided an additional benefit, especially at the moderate urea level. Compared with the Cd treatment alone, Cd × N1 increased total yield by 121.36%, and Cd × N1 × PGA further increased total yield by 179.55%. The Cd × N1 × PGA treatment also reduced fruit Cd concentration by 58.44% and decreased the root-to-fruit Cd translocation factor by 60.19%. These results show that the effect of PGA on Cd mitigation depended on urea rate. Within the tested range, PGA was more effective at limiting root-to-fruit Cd translocation at the moderate urea level, whereas the higher urea level gave a greater yield increase.ConclusionsThe effect of PGA on reducing fruit Cd concentration and root-to-fruit Cd translocation depended on the N input level. Within the tested range, foliar PGA combined with a moderate urea rate was more effective for limiting root-to-fruit Cd translocation, while a higher urea rate was more favorable for yield improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09130-4.

Introduction

Pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) is an important edible vegetable and industrial crop in China, with Guizhou Province ranking among the top regions in both cultivated area and yield [1, 2]. However, pepper exhibits a strong capacity for cadmium (Cd) accumulation, with bioaccumulation factors reported up to 3.04 [3]. When cultivated in Cd-contaminated soils, fruit Cd concentrations can readily exceed food-safety thresholds [3]. This issue is particularly critical in Guizhou, where the extensive karst landscapes generate a geochemically elevated Cd background. The mean soil Cd concentration in this region (0.659 mg kg⁻1) is more than twice the national average of 0.27 mg kg⁻1 in agricultural soils [4, 5]. Recent surveys have revealed a high frequency of Cd contamination in Guizhou peppers; more than 50% of fruit samples exceeded permissible limits [2]. Overall, these findings highlight the urgent need for effective strategies to mitigate Cd uptake in crops while maintaining yield and quality, particularly in highly Cd-contaminated regions [3, 5, 6].

The absorption and accumulation of Cd in plants are determined not only by their genetic background but also by environmental factors [7], among which nitrogen (N) fertilization exerts a critical influence [8, 9]. As an essential macronutrient, N constitutes proteins, chlorophyll, and nucleic acids, and is indispensable for plant growth and metabolic regulation [8, 10]. Depending on their chemical form, N fertilizers are generally categorized as ammonium-based (e.g., ammonium sulfate), nitrate-based (e.g., calcium nitrate), or amide-based (e.g., urea). Among these, urea is the most widely applied in modern agriculture due to its high N concentration and ease of handling [11]. Nevertheless, the influence of urea on Cd dynamics remains controversial. Some studies have shown that urea application can increase soluble Cd concentrations in soil and enhance Cd uptake in crops such as tobacco [12], ryegrass [13], and willow [14]. Conversely, other reports indicate that urea application markedly reduces Cd concentrations in brown rice by 2–66% [15]. Such discrepancies suggest that the effects of nutrient fertilization on Cd accumulation are species-dependent and modulated by application rate and environmental conditions [12, 16].

Given the complex interactions between nutrient acquisition and environmental stressors, understanding the role of biostimulants in regulating micronutrient dynamics becomes crucial. Plants acquire essential micronutrients such as Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn from the growing medium to maintain cellular ion homeostasis, support physiological functions, and ensure proper growth and development [17, 18]. The uptake and distribution of these micronutrients are influenced by fertilization regimes, including nitrogen and phosphorus supply, as well as soil properties [17, 19]. Heavy metal stress, such as cadmium exposure, can disrupt micronutrient acquisition because divalent cations including Fe, Mn, and Zn compete for the same uptake sites, impairing the absorption and utilization of essential ions [20]. Moreover, biostimulants such as poly-γ-glutamic acid (PGA) have been reported to modulate micronutrient dynamics under stress conditions, enhancing Fe accumulation in shoots and Mn/Zn accumulation in roots while restricting Cu uptake under low-fertility conditions [21]. Despite extensive research on nutrient acquisition and heavy metal interactions, the mechanisms by which biostimulants such as PGA influence micronutrient dynamics under cadmium stress remain poorly understood.

PGA is a high–molecular-weight biopolymer produced via microbial fermentation, consisting of D-and L-glutamic acid residues linked through γ-amide bonds. It is highly water soluble, exhibits strong metal-chelating capacity, and serves as a biodegradable and environmentally benign material [22, 23]. Owing to its ability to retain soil moisture and chelate nutrient ions, PGA has been reported to enhance nutrient acquisition and promote plant growth and yield, particularly under nutrient-deficient conditions [24, 25]. Moreover, its chelating properties may help reduce Cd bioavailability in the growth medium, thereby alleviating Cd-induced stress and lowering Cd uptake in cucumber seedlings [26]. Despite these promising findings, the interactive effects of foliar-applied PGA and N fertilization on pepper growth, nutrient dynamics, and Cd accumulation remain poorly understood. Recent studies have shown that nutrient regulation can markedly influence Cd uptake and translocation in pepper, and have begun to provide insight into the underlying processes. Xin et al. [27] reported that boron supply reduced Cd uptake and translocation in Capsicum annuum L, thereby decreasing Cd accumulation in edible tissues. Huang et al. [28] further showed that boron can restrict Cd entry into pepper roots through the regulation of root apoplastic barriers and related cellular processes. In addition, Shen et al. [29] identified key genes associated with Cd transport and distribution in pepper roots, highlighting the complexity of Cd regulation at the molecular level. However, whether foliar-applied PGA interacts with urea-N supply to regulate nutrient acquisition, micronutrient distribution, and Cd accumulation in pepper fruits under Cd-contaminated conditions remains insufficiently understood.

To address this knowledge gap, we investigated how foliar-applied PGA and urea fertilization jointly affected leaf chlorophyll content, yield performance, nutrient status, and Cd behavior in pepper plants grown in Cd-contaminated soil. Specifically, this study aimed to (i) assess the effects of PGA and urea fertilization on leaf chlorophyll content, yield, and yield components, and (ii) examine their associations with nutrient acquisition as well as Cd uptake and translocation within plant tissues. In particular, we sought to determine whether the effects of PGA were consistent across different urea levels, or whether distinct urea inputs would differentially influence the balance between yield improvement and fruit Cd risk under Cd stress. We hypothesized that (H1) co-application of PGA and urea fertilization would further enhance chlorophyll content and yield-related performance compared with urea fertilization alone; (H2) PGA, through its metal-chelating properties, would mitigate Cd accumulation in fruits, particularly under the moderate urea level tested in this study; and (H3) combined PGA and urea fertilization would improve fruit micronutrient status, supporting both yield and nutritional quality. Our findings provide useful insight into how foliar-applied PGA interacts with contrasting urea supply levels to influence pepper productivity and fruit Cd risk in Cd-affected environments.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

The experiment was conducted at the experimental base of the College of Life Sciences, Guizhou University, China (26°11′–26°34′ N, 106°27′–106°52′ E; 1100 m a.s.l.), with a mean annual temperature of 14.9 °C and mean annual precipitation of 1178.3 mm. The pepper cultivar used was ‘Yijiao 1807’ (Capsicum annuum L.; Registration No. GPD Pepper [2024] 520,288; Certificate No. 2024–18–1137). Seeds were purchased from Shandong Changyi Shangzhu Agricultural Development Co., Ltd. (China). The growth substrate consisted of yellow soil, perlite, and coconut husk at a ratio of 7:2:1. Urea (N ≥ 46.4%) was used as the nitrogen (N) source, potassium dihydrogen phosphate (P₂O₅ ≥ 52%) as the phosphorus (P) source, and potassium sulfate (K₂O ≥ 50%) as the potassium (K) source. A water-soluble fertilizer containing 0.05% Cu, 0.05% Zn, 0.05% Fe, and 0.02% Mn was purchased from the Aowei Agricultural Supplies Official Flagship Store. The air-dried soil was passed through a 2-mm sieve and uniformly amended with an aqueous CdCl₂·2.5H₂O solution in a single application. The treated soil was thoroughly mixed, equilibrated for one week under a rain shelter, and then placed into non-woven planting bags, with 9.0 kg soil per bag.

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted from May to August 2024 under a completely randomized design (CRD). Uniform pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) seedlings at the six-leaf stage were selected and transplanted into non-woven planting bags (base diameter × height = 30 cm × 25 cm) for outdoor pot culture, with one seedling per bag.

The treatments consisted of two cadmium (Cd) levels, three urea application rates, and two foliar PGA concentrations. Specifically, two Cd levels were established: (i) no Cd addition (− Cd), and (ii) Cd addition at 10 mg CdCl₂·2.5H₂O kg⁻1 dry soil (+ Cd), corresponding to 4.92 mg Cd kg⁻1 dry soil (This Cd level was selected to simulate a relatively severe Cd-contamination scenario relevant to high-risk agricultural soils in Guizhou karst regions). Nitrogen fertilization was applied at three levels using urea as the N source: 0, 3.5, and 5.0 g urea plant⁻1. Foliar PGA was applied at two concentrations: 0 mg L⁻1 (water control) and 450 mg L⁻1. The pots were randomly arranged at the outdoor experimental site and repositioned every 7 days to minimize positional effects. Potassium dihydrogen phosphate and potassium sulfate were uniformly applied to all treatments at a total rate of 2 g plant⁻1 each, divided into three applications during the growth period. A water-soluble micronutrient fertilizer was also uniformly supplied to all treatments. It was prepared as a 1‰ solution and soil-applied at 100 mL per plant per application, for a total of three applications during the growth period. Urea was applied in three split applications beginning 15 days after transplanting. PGA was foliar-applied at 200 mL per plant per application, for a total of four applications at 5-day intervals. All treatments were subjected to the same irrigation regime under the rain shelter. Water was supplied uniformly according to soil moisture status to maintain suitable soil moisture and avoid obvious drought stress.

Measurement methods

Chlorophyll content

Chlorophyll content was determined at 30, 45, 60, and 75 days after transplanting, representing the seedling, initial flowering, initial fruiting, and peak fruiting stages, respectively. Relative chlorophyll content was measured on clear mornings using a portable chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502, Konica Minolta, Japan), and expressed as SPAD values. For each treatment, four plants were randomly selected, and three fully expanded leaves were measured per plant. Each leaf was measured three times, and the mean SPAD value for each leaf was calculated before statistical analysis.

Yield components and yield

Pepper fruits were harvested in successive batches as they matured, and total fruit yield was calculated accordingly. Fruit number per plant was recorded, and mean fruit weight was calculated accordingly. Fruit yield was extrapolated to a planting density of 45,000 plants ha⁻1 and expressed as kg ha⁻1.

Determination of Cd and nutrient element concentrations

Plant samples were oven-dried, weighed, finely ground, and passed through a 100-mesh sieve for subsequent nutrient analysis.

Total nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) concentrations (g kg−1) were determined following H2SO4–H2O2 digestion [30, 31]. Approximately 0.10 g of dried and ground plant material was weighed into a digestion tube, moistened with 3–5 drops of distilled water, and mixed with 3 mLH2O2 and 5 mL concentrated H2SO4. After a 20 min pre-reaction, the samples were heated in a block digestion furnace for 30–40 min. If digestion was incomplete, the tubes were cooled for 10–20 min, an additional 10 drops of H₂O₂ were added, and heating was repeated until the solution became clear and free of precipitate. After cooling, the digest was brought to 100 mL with deionized water. Three reagent blanks were included in each batch to correct for background values. Total N was determined using the Kjeldahl method, and total P was determined by the molybdenum–antimony colorimetric method, separately for root, stem, leaf, and fruit tissues.

Micronutrient (Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn) and Cd concentrations were determined after HNO₃–HClO₄ digestion according to Cooper and Doran [32]. Approximately 0.20 g of dried and ground plant material was weighed into a digestion tube, moistened with 3–5 drops of distilled water, and then mixed with 15 mL of HNO3–HClO4 (5:1, v/v). The tubes were left to stand overnight in a fume hood and then digested in a block digestion furnace. Samples were first heated at 100–180 °C until the evolution of brown fumes ceased. The temperature was then increased to 200 °C and maintained until dense white fumes disappeared and the solution became clear or pale yellow without precipitate. After cooling, the digests were brought to 10 mL with deionized water and further diluted, when necessary, to meet the detection range of the instrument. Three reagent blanks were included in each batch to correct for background values. Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, and Cd concentrations were determined by flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, all measured variables, including chlorophyll content, yield and its components, Cd concentration, Cd accumulation, translocation factor, nutrient (N and P) concentrations, and micronutrient (Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn) concentrations, were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. When necessary, data were log- or square-root transformed to meet the assumptions of ANOVA. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the 12 treatment combinations for each measured variable, and treatment means were separated using Duncan’s multiple range test at P < 0.05. A multifactor ANOVA was subsequently performed to examine the main and interactive effects of Cd, PGA, and nitrogen level. Pearson correlation analysis was performed in R (version 4.5.1; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) to explore relationships among Cd-related traits, nutrient variables, and chlorophyll-related indices. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to visualize treatment separation and identify the major variables contributing to overall variation.

All figures, including bar charts, correlation heatmaps, and PCA biplots, were prepared using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA). Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of four replicates.

Results

Leaf chlorophyll content at various pepper phenophases

As shown in Fig. 1a–d, under non-Cd conditions, PGA alone significantly reduced leaf chlorophyll content at the seedling, initial flowering, and early fruiting stages by 10.91%, 18.29%, and 19.26%, respectively, but had no significant effect at the peak fruiting stage (P < 0.05). In contrast, nitrogen fertilization (N1 and N2) significantly increased chlorophyll content throughout the whole growth period, with a stronger effect under N2 than under N1. Combined N × γ-PGA treatments further increased chlorophyll content in some cases, but the effect was not consistently stronger than nitrogen alone at all stages. Specifically, N2 alone resulted in the highest chlorophyll content at the initial flowering and peak fruiting stages, whereas the N2 × γ-PGA treatment resulted in the highest chlorophyll content at the seedling and early fruiting stages.

Cadmium treatment reduced leaf chlorophyll content at the seedling, early fruiting, and peak fruiting stages (Fig. 1a–d), with significant decreases of 13.73%, 31.54%, and 4.16%, respectively (P < 0.05). Under Cd treatment, γ-PGA alone increased chlorophyll content by 34.99% and 7.82% at the early fruiting and peak fruiting stages, respectively, but decreased it by 7.47% at the initial flowering stage (P < 0.05). Nitrogen fertilization under Cd treatment significantly enhanced chlorophyll content at all growth stages, with N2 generally showing a greater increase than N1. Under Cd treatment, nitrogen consistently increased leaf chlorophyll content across growth stages, whereas the effect of γ-PGA varied with growth stage and treatment combination. Specifically, N2 alone resulted in the highest chlorophyll content at the seedling stage, whereas the N2 × γ-PGA treatment resulted in the highest chlorophyll content at the initial flowering, early fruiting, and peak fruiting stages.

Across all Cd, nitrogen, and PGA treatments, three-way ANOVA indicated that SPAD values were significantly affected by nitrogen and cadmium at all growth stages, whereas the significant effect of PGA appeared from the initial flowering stage onward. Among the interaction terms, PGA × Cd, N × Cd, and PGA × N × Cd were significant at all growth stages, while the PGA × N interaction was significant at the seedling, initial flowering, and peak fruiting stages, but not at the early fruiting stage (Tables S1–S2).

As shown in Fig. 2a–b, under non-Cd conditions, γ-PGA alone significantly increased fruit number per plant by 27.45% relative to the CK treatment, while mean fruit weight was not significantly affected. Nitrogen fertilization (N1 and N2) markedly increased both mean fruit weight and fruit number per plant, with greater increases under N2 than under N1. Combined N and γ-PGA treatments further promoted fruit growth, with mean fruit weight reaching the highest value under N1 × γ-PGA and fruit number per plant peaking under N2 × γ-PGA. Under Cd treatment, cadmium contamination alone had no significant effect on either mean fruit weight or fruit number per plant, and γ-PGA alone did not significantly enhance these parameters. Nitrogen fertilization significantly increased both mean fruit weight and fruit number per plant, with greater increases under Cd × N2 than under Cd × N1. For example, Cd × N2 increased mean fruit weight and fruit number per plant by 59.77% and 118.87%, respectively, relative to Cd alone (P < 0.05). As shown in Fig. 2c, under non-Cd conditions, nitrogen fertilization markedly increased total pepper yield, whereas the effect of PGA alone on yield was not evident from the treatment-wise comparison. Discussion

Leaf chlorophyll content at various growth stages

Chlorophyll content is a reliable indicator of photosynthetic capacity and nitrogen metabolism, and its temporal dynamics reflect plant health and adaptability [33, 34] In the present study, systematic monitoring of leaf chlorophyll content (i.e., SPAD values) across four growth stages revealed distinct regulatory effects of N fertilization and PGA application under contrasting environments (Fig. 1). Under Cd-untreated conditions, urea application consistently promoted chlorophyll accumulation, with the higher urea level (N2) showing stronger effects than the moderate urea level (N1). This is consistent with established principles that nitrogen, as a structural component of chlorophyll, photosystem proteins, and Rubisco, directly supports the assembly and function of the photosynthetic apparatus [35, 36]. Indeed, adequate N thereby enhances carbon assimilation capacity to sustain growth and fruiting [8, 37, 38]. At the initial and peak flowering stages, the effect of N2 alone on SPAD exceeded that of N2 combined with PGA, suggesting that during reproductive stages of peak N demand, direct nutrient supply may have been the primary limiting factor. By contrast, foliar PGA application alone exhibited stage-dependent responses, significantly reducing chlorophyll at the seedling, flowering, and early fruiting stages. This counterintuitive effect likely reflects resource reallocation, as PGA is known to stimulate root growth [39], secondary metabolism [23, 24], and systemic resistance [40], transiently diverting resources away from chlorophyll biosynthesis.

Under Cd stress, chlorophyll declined markedly throughout development, consistent with heavy metal toxicity directly impairing photosynthesis by displacing Mg in chlorophyll, disrupting thylakoid membranes, and inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts [41, 42]. Nevertheless, nitrogen remained the main factor alleviating the Cd-induced decline in chlorophyll content, whereas the contribution of PGA was additional and depended on growth stage and urea level. In particular, the Cd × N2 × PGA treatment generally showed relatively high chlorophyll values during the mid-to-late growth stages. This response may be related to the role of nitrogen in supporting chlorophyll maintenance and stress tolerance, as well as to the potential of PGA to influence Cd distribution and nutrient balance under stress conditions [43, 44]. However, because Cd concentrations in aboveground vegetative tissues were not determined in the present study, the relationship between chlorophyll responses and Cd accumulation in green tissues could not be directly assessed. In addition, because the present study did not directly examine the underlying physiological or biochemical mechanisms, these interpretations should be regarded as possible explanations rather than direct evidence [45–47].

Composition and yield of peppers

Crop yield is the ultimate manifestation of photosynthetic assimilation and dry matter partitioning [48]. Our results demonstrate that both nitrogen fertilization and PGA application significantly enhanced pepper productivity, albeit through distinct yet complementary mechanisms. Under Cd-untreated conditions, nitrogen was the primary driver of yield increase, exerting a dose-dependent effect on both mean fruit weight and fruit yield per plant (Fig. 2). For total yield, nitrogen was the dominant factor, whereas the contribution of PGA was conditional rather than uniformly evident across all treatment combinations [49]. The application of PGA alone also significantly increased fruit yield, indicating its intrinsic growth-promoting properties, potentially linked to improved nutrient use efficiency [50].

A critical finding was the differential effect of the N-PGA combinations on yield components: the N1 × PGA treatment produced the highest mean fruit weight, whereas the N2 × PGA treatment achieved the maximum fruit yield per plant and consequently the highest total yield (Fig. 2), these results suggest that the contribution of PGA to yield formation depended on urea level. Its positive effect was more evident at the moderate urea level than at the higher urea level. This complex interaction highlights the importance of matching nitrogen and biostimulant application to target specific yield components [51, 52].

Under cadmium stress, the paradigm for yield formation shifted notably [53]. While cadmium application itself did not cause a significant yield reduction compared to the non-stressed control—indicating a degree of pepper tolerance—it fundamentally altered the efficacy of the amendments. In this context, PGA alone (Cd × PGA) failed to enhance fruit yield, revealing that the mitigating effect of the biostimulant is contingent upon an adequate nutrient base [46]. The most significant insight from the Cd-contaminated soil was the consistent and superior performance of the combined treatments, particularly Cd × N2 × PGA. This triple interaction not only recovered yield but pushed it to levels surpassing those achieved by nitrogen alone under Cd stress. The mechanism is likely twofold: First, nitrogen facilitates a "growth dilution" of cadmium and supports the synthesis of defense metabolites, mitigating toxicity. Second, PGA complements this by potentially reducing Cd uptake and translocation, improving micronutrient homeostasis (e.g., Fe, Zn), and alleviating oxidative stress [54], thereby ensuring that the carbon and nutrient resources mobilized by nitrogen are effectively channeled into fruit development rather than being diverted to stress mitigation [55, 56]. Ultimately, the combined application of PGA and urea under Cd stress may help improve yield performance while reducing fruit Cd risk, indicating its potential as a management strategy for pepper production in Cd-affected soils.

Total phosphorus and nitrogen concentrations

Nitrogen and phosphorus are essential nutrients for plant growth and are fundamental constituents of key macromolecules, including proteins and phosphate-containing compounds [57]. The total N and P concentration in plant tissues reflects not only nutritional status but also the influence of environmental stress on nutrient assimilation capacity [57, 58]. Plant uptake and utilization of these nutrients vary under different environmental conditions. Previous studies have shown that, within a certain range, increasing N application rates can enhance nutrient acquisition in plants, and hence promoting plant growth and development [58, 59]. In the present study, under cadmium-untreated conditions, N application significantly increased both total N and P concentration in all organs of pepper plants. This response may be related to improved nutrient uptake and assimilation under greater N supply. PGA further increased N in the fruits and elevated P in the roots, stems, and leaves. This suggests that PGA facilitates P accumulation in most organs in pepper plants, which aligns with the results reported by Guo in pepper, tomato, and maize plants [60]. However, in the present study, this response was more evident for P than for N under Cd-free conditions, and the pattern also varied among organs. Moreover, the combined application of N and PGA further enhanced both N and P across all plant organs, indicating a synergistic effect between PGA foliar spraying and nitrogen fertilization on nutrient uptake and translocation.

Under cadmium stress, the absorption of cadmium ions by roots impairs the plant's ability to utilize essential nutrients such as N and P, disrupting macromolecular biosynthesis and potentially leading to nutrient deficiency symptoms [41]. Previous research has demonstrated that N application under cadmium stress can increase N concentration in soybeans [61]and P concentration in the rice [62]. In this experiment, N application similarly increased both N and P in both aboveground and belowground parts of cadmium-stressed pepper plants. This improvement may be attributed to enhanced root growth and nutrient uptake capacity, thereby improving cadmium tolerance. Similar results were reported by Ikhajiagbe et al. [61], who found that N addition in Cd-contaminated soil increased P concentrations in soybean roots and leaves. In the present study, N application also increased N and P concentrations in pepper organs under Cd stress. Compared with the non-Cd treatment, PGA application significantly increased N and P in most organs of cadmium-exposed plants. This effect may be due to the ability of PGA to inhibit cadmium uptake and translocation to fruits, thereby mitigating cadmium toxicity and improving nutrient assimilation. Furthermore, the co-application of PGA and N under cadmium stress further enhance both nutrients in almost all organs in pepper plants, indicating a synergistic interaction between PGA foliar spraying and N fertilization in promoting nutrient uptake and translocation even under heavy metal stress [16, 63].

Iron, manganese, copper, and zinc concentrations

Plants acquire micronutrients from the growing medium to maintain cellular ion homeostasis and support normal physiological functions, as well as plant growth and development [17, 18]. Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn are involved in numerous plant metabolic pathways, and their uptake is influenced by fertilization practices and soil properties [17, 19]. In this study, under Cd-untreated conditions, N application significantly increased Cu and Fe in pepper plants and markedly enhanced Fe and Zn concentration in roots (Fig. 5). Similar results were reported by Wyszkowski [64], who found increased Zn and Fe concentrations in maize grains after N application. In the present study, N application also increased Fe and Zn concentrations in pepper roots and affected Cu and Fe concentrations in fruits. PGA application significantly increased Cu and Zn concentration in roots and elevated Cu, Fe, Mn, and Zn concentration in fruits of Cd-untreated plants, while reducing Mn in roots (Fig. 5). The polyanionic nature of PGA may account for these effects, as it can chelate cations such as calcium, magnesium, and trace elements, thereby preventing the formation of insoluble precipitates and improving micronutrient bioavailability and uptake [23, 24, 65]. Co-application of N and PGA significantly increased Zn in roots and Cu and Fe in fruits, indicating a synergistic effect of PGA and nitrogen on Zn uptake in roots and the translocation of Cu and Fe to fruits.

Under cadmium stress, cadmium competes with divalent micronutrient cations such as Fe, Mn, and Zn for uptake sites, thereby disrupting the absorption and utilization of essential metal ions [20]. In this experiment, cadmium exposure significantly reduced Cu, Fe, and Mn concentration in roots and fruits, while increasing Zn concentration, consistent with previous findings [66, 67]. Nitrogen application under cadmium stress significantly increased Fe, Mn, and Zn concentration in roots, as well as Cu and Fe concentration in fruits, compared to the cadmium-untreated treatment. This response may be associated with improved nutrient acquisition under nitrogen application, although the underlying physiological mechanisms were not directly examined in the present study [68]. Moreover, PGA treatment under cadmium stress significantly increased Fe and Zn concentration in roots and Fe, Mn, and Zn concentration in fruits, while reducing Cu and Mn concentration in roots. Similar effects of PGA on micronutrient dynamics were reported by Chen et al. [21], In the present study, PGA also altered Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn concentrations in pepper roots and fruits under combined Cd and N treatments. The combined application of PGA and N under cadmium stress significantly increased Cu, Fe, and Zn concentrations in both roots and fruits, while reducing Mn concentration in fruits. This pattern may reflect improved nutrient acquisition under combined treatment, although the underlying processes were not directly investigated in this study [69]. Notably, the responses of different metal elements to PGA were not unidirectional, suggesting that the role of PGA in metal regulation may be element-specific. One possible explanation is that the chelating property of PGA does not affect all metals equally, but depends on differences in metal affinity, competition among ions for uptake and transport pathways, and the nutritional adjustments triggered under Cd stress. In this context, PGA may contribute to the stabilization or retention of some metals, while favoring the availability, redistribution, or accumulation of others. Such divergence may help explain why Cd and essential micronutrients did not respond in the same manner in the present study. However, because metal speciation, complex formation, and transporter-level processes were not directly examined, these interpretations should be considered tentative rather than mechanistically confirmed.

Cadmium uptake and translocation

Cd concentration, Cd accumulation, and the root-to-fruit Cd translocation factor are important indicators for evaluating Cd uptake, accumulation, and translocation within plants [70, 71]. Research indicates that N application can increase the water-soluble and exchangeable cadmium fractions in soil, enhancing its bioavailability and thereby promoting plant uptake and accumulation [72, 73]. According to a study on wheat [74], nitrogen application and cadmium uptake show a positive relationship. In the present study, this stimulatory effect of urea application on Cd uptake was also observed, but it differed between urea levels, with the moderate and higher urea levels showing distinct patterns of Cd distribution between roots and fruits. As expected, cadmium treatment increased Cd concentration in both roots and fruits of pepper plants. This pattern is consistent with previous reports showing that Cd can accumulate in plant tissues under Cd exposure [75]. The moderate urea level increased Cd concentration in roots but reduced it in fruits, and this pattern was accompanied by a lower root-to-fruit Cd translocation factor (Fig. 6). This pattern indicates that the moderate urea level was associated with lower root-to-fruit Cd translocation [76]. In our study, PGA application reduced Cd concentrations in both roots and fruits, decreased cadmium accumulation in fruits, and lowered the root-to-fruit translocation factor. This mitigation may be associated with changes in micronutrient status under PGA treatment, which could influence cadmium distribution within the plant. When PGA was co-applied with the moderate urea level, the root-to-fruit Cd translocation factor was further reduced, accompanied by a lower Cd concentration in fruits. Similarly, a recent study indicated that nitrogen-containing phosphate-functionalized biochars can simultaneously reduce Cd availability in soil and promote wheat growth [77]. Likewise, NP-enriched peel biochars effectively immobilized Cd and enhanced mung bean growth [78]. However, unlike these soil-applied amendments, the present study focused on foliar-applied PGA combined with urea fertilization, and the effect on reducing fruit Cd accumulation varied with urea level, being more evident under the moderate urea level than under the higher urea level. In the present study, under high nitrogen fertilization, the combined treatment with PGA significantly increased cadmium concentration and accumulation in both roots and fruits. These results suggest that, although nitrogen may enhance Cd uptake as reported previously, the addition of PGA partly mitigated Cd accumulation under the moderate urea level, whereas this effect was less evident under the higher urea level [72–74]. Nevertheless, these findings provide new insights into nutrient management strategies for improving crop growth and reducing food-chain risks in Cd-contaminated soils [77–80]. To further explore these nutrient interactions, correlation and PCA analyses were used to reveal trait relationships and the effects of experimental factors [81–84]. In this study, these approaches allowed us to examine how Cd stress reshapes nutrient networks in pepper plants. Nutrients and micronutrients in roots and fruits generally showed positive associations, with the same element strongly correlated across organs, indicating coordinated root–fruit translocation. Under Cd-untreated conditions, the network was compact, especially among fruit N, P, Fe, Mn, and Cu, reflecting cooperative nutrient regulation. Minor antagonistic interactions, such as root Cu versus fruit Mn or Zn, were also observed. Cd exposure disrupted these patterns, weakening or reversing several root–fruit associations, particularly for Cu, Mn, and Zn, indicating impaired translocation and disturbed homeostasis (Fig. 7) [85]. PCA again highlighted that Cd stress intensified separation between fruits and vegetative organs, whereas Cd-unstressed plants maintained cohesive nutrient relationships (Fig. 8) [86, 87]. Notably, co-application of PGA and the moderate urea level was associated with a more coordinated nutrient profile under Cd stress [59]. These findings indicate that Cd contamination altered nutrient relationships in pepper plants, whereas the combined application of PGA and the moderate urea level was associated with a more coordinated nutrient profile under the present experimental conditions.

Conclusion

Nitrogen was the main factor driving chlorophyll and yield responses in pepper, whereas the effect of PGA was conditional and depended on urea level and Cd condition. Under non-Cd conditions, nitrogen was the main driver of chlorophyll formation and yield improvement, whereas PGA showed a supplementary effect. Under Cd stress, yield formation patterns shifted markedly. While Cd exposure alone did not cause severe yield losses, it altered the effectiveness of amendments. Under Cd stress, PGA combined with the moderate urea level (3.5 g urea plant⁻1) was associated with a lower root-to-fruit Cd translocation factor and reduced Cd accumulation in pepper fruits, while helping maintain fruit nutrient status. Overall, γ-PGA combined with the moderate urea level was more favorable for reducing fruit Cd risk, whereas the higher urea level contributed more strongly to yield improvement. Therefore, within the tested urea range, the choice of urea level should balance yield improvement with fruit Cd risk under Cd stress.
