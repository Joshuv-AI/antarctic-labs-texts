Artificial intelligence in public health education: current evidence, competencies, and a framework for responsible integration
Authors: Hisham Arab, Ghazal Assaad Mirdad, Maha Abulfetoh Mohamed Abd-Allah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Public health practice and education are being profoundly transformed by artificial intelligence (AI), yet formal AI literacy training in academic programs remains lacking. This narrative review synthesizes literature identified through a structured search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar for publications issued between January 2020 and June 2026. The narrative synthesis draws on 21 publications, including empirical studies, reviews, perspectives, educational frameworks, and authoritative guidance documents. The review underscores a significant gap between the widespread use of informal AI and the limited availability of formal curricula, with most evidence drawn from medical education rather than public health-specific settings. Key applications include adaptive learning platforms, AI-enhanced simulation, and research training support. We propose the Public Health AI–Education Integration Framework comprising nine domains: learner and curriculum needs assessment; AI literacy foundation; AI tool selection and educational alignment; public health dataset and case integration; adaptive learning and simulation delivery; human-in-the-loop supervision; bias, equity, and ethical auditing; assessment and academic integrity; and feedback, monitoring, and curriculum refinement. The framework is proposed as a conceptual model requiring prospective validation, intended to move beyond ad hoc AI use toward structured, competency-based training that strengthens analytical reasoning, ethical judgment, and equity-oriented practice in AI-enabled public health systems.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence is transforming nearly every facet of public health, from disease surveillance to policy development. Large language models such as ChatGPT, Claude, DeepSeek, Llama, Copilot, and Gemini are now employed in epidemiological modeling, crisis response, and health communication, offering powerful new tools for data-driven decision-making. In public health, the use of artificial intelligence (AI) is being explored for disease surveillance, forecasting disease outbreaks, risk stratification, health communication and misinformation detection, resource allocation, evidence synthesis, and policy modeling (1–3). These developments are occurring in parallel with a second transition in higher education, where generative AI tools are being used for writing support, tutoring, assessment preparation, literature summarization, coding support, and feedback generation (4–6). Public health education sits at the nexus of these two transitions. Public health graduates will need to work in environments increasingly populated by digital platforms, large datasets, predictive models, dashboards, and AI-supported decision systems (1–3). But many students and educators are still learning about AI through informal experimentation instead of through a structured curriculum. This creates a gap between the technological realities of public health practice and the competencies formally taught in public health programs. Recent literature has emphasized that AI literacy should become a core component of public health education (1). AI literacy in this context does not mean training every student to become a machine-learning engineer. Rather, it refers to the ability to understand what AI systems can and cannot do, to critically evaluate AI outputs, to recognize bias and uncertainty, to protect privacy, to communicate limitations, and to use AI responsibly in public health decision-making (1–3, 7). Recognizing the significance of AI in advancing global health objectives, the World Health Organization has highlighted the need for AI literacy as a core competency for the next generation of healthcare professionals (7). Despite growing interest, existing reviews have predominantly focused on medical education or general health professions education, with limited attention to the population-level, policy-oriented, and equity-focused dimensions that distinguish public health practice. This review therefore addresses a specific gap by synthesizing evidence specifically relevant to public health education, identifying competencies unique to the discipline, and proposing a structured framework for responsible AI integration. This review therefore aims to: (1) synthesize current trends in AI use in public health teaching and education; (2) summarize comparative evidence from recent studies and frameworks; and (3) propose a structured model for responsible integration of AI into public health curricula.

Literature search and study selection

Given the narrative nature of this review, a structured literature search was conducted to identify relevant publications. PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar were searched for English-language publications from January 2020 to June 2026. The search was last conducted on 30 June 2026. This period was selected to capture recent developments in AI-assisted education, including those following the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 and the accelerated digital transformation of education during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible publications included empirical studies, systematic and scoping reviews, educational frameworks, perspectives, commentaries, and authoritative guidance documents addressing AI applications, AI-related competencies, governance, assessment, or curriculum development in public health education or closely related health-professions education.

Publications were eligible if they: (1) addressed the use or integration of AI in an educational context; (2) focused on public health education or provided findings or recommendations transferable to public health from health informatics, medical education, or broader health-professions education; (3) examined AI-related teaching, learning, assessment, competencies, curriculum development, governance, ethics, or equity; and (4) were published in English between January 2020 and 30 June 2026.

Publications were excluded if they: (1) did not address an educational context; (2) focused exclusively on technical AI development or clinical performance without educational relevance; (3) did not provide findings, frameworks, or recommendations applicable to public health or health-professions education; or (4) did not have an accessible full text.

Screening and publication selection

The database searches identified 133 records: 11 from PubMed, 85 from Google Scholar, 22 from Web of Science, and 15 from Scopus. After 21 duplicate records were removed, 112 unique records remained for screening.

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the 112 records against the eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Following title and abstract screening, 78 records were excluded, and the full texts of the remaining 34 records were sought. Fourteen full texts could not be retrieved, leaving 20 publications for full-text eligibility assessment.

The two reviewers independently assessed the 20 full-text publications against the eligibility criteria. Sixteen publications were excluded: nine because their focus was not relevant to the review objectives and seven because their publication type or design did not meet the eligibility criteria. Consequently, four publications identified through the database searches were included.

Backward and forward citation searching of the eligible publications and relevant reviews was subsequently undertaken to identify additional sources. This process identified 17 additional publications, which were assessed against the same eligibility criteria. In total, 21 publications were included in the narrative synthesis.

The included literature comprised empirical educational studies, systematic and scoping reviews, perspectives, educational and competency frameworks, and policy or governance documents. Given the narrative nature of the review and the heterogeneity of the included publications, no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative synthesis was undertaken.

Narrative synthesis of current evidence

Overview of the selected literature

The narrative synthesis was conducted using 21 selected publications on AI in public health and health professions education. The literature selected comprised empirical educational studies, systematic and scoping reviews, perspective and framework papers, and policy or governance documents. Importantly, the evidence base for public health education is relatively small, and a significant proportion of it is drawn from medical education, health informatics, and general health professions education. Key themes discussed were the development of teaching and learning approaches with AI-powered simulations and customized learning environments, the expansion of AI and online literacy education, varying levels of institutional readiness, and urgent ethical concerns about algorithmic fairness and inclusion. Interdisciplinary collaboration and curriculum reform were identified as critical to maintaining AI integration.

Current trends in AI use in public health teaching

AI is being incorporated into public health and health professions education through several primary mechanisms. The most visible trend is the use of generative AI tools as learning assistants, where students use these tools to summarize readings, clarify concepts, generate quiz questions, draft outlines, translate technical information, and receive immediate explanations, while faculty use similar tools to create teaching materials, case scenarios, rubrics, formative assessment items, and feedback prompts (8). Generative AI models are increasingly used to answer student queries, create personalized lessons, provide tailored feedback, and even simulate virtual patients based on aggregated data (9). A second notable trend involves the move toward adaptive and personalized learning environments, in which AI-driven systems use student performance data to pinpoint knowledge gaps, modulate content difficulty, suggest supplementary resources, and deliver real-time feedback (2). Wang and colleagues highlighted opportunities for AI in individualized instruction, behavioral modeling, and public health policy applications, capabilities especially valuable for quantitatively demanding disciplines such as epidemiology, biostatistics, disease modeling, and health informatics (2). A third emerging trend is the integration of AI into scenario-based and simulation-based learning, enabling dynamic scenarios in which students respond to evolving outbreak conditions, communication challenges, policy constraints, or resource allocation problems (2, 3). These AI-enhanced simulations adapt in real time to learner inputs, offering a personalized experience that mirrors the complexity of real-world public health practice (10). They include simulations of disease outbreaks, exercises in vaccine hesitancy, emergency preparedness drills, assessments of occupational hazards, and evaluations of community needs (3). Patil et al. (11) reported high levels of engagement with an AI-augmented simulation activity among undergraduate public health students, while Howard (10) described AI-driven outbreak simulations that immerse students in realistic, high-pressure situations to develop critical crisis communication skills. However, these findings are primarily based on student-reported measures rather than objective learning outcomes, and evidence of sustained educational benefit remains limited. Comulada et al. (12) found that incorporating AI-based digital health interventions into graduate curricula elicited positive student responses, although participants raised concerns about their relevance to more traditional public health practice. A fourth trend is AI-assisted research training, where AI can support literature reviews, evidence synthesis, research proposals, data analysis, and scientific writing, but only when students are trained to verify outputs, detect hallucinated references, evaluate source quality, and document AI assistance transparently (1, 5, 7, 9). Finally, there is growing attention to institutional governance, as universities and schools of public health are beginning to develop policies on acceptable AI use, academic integrity, privacy, assessment design, and disclosure, though guidance remains variable and public health-specific frameworks are still emerging (1, 13).

A critical observation across these trends is that while AI applications are proliferating, the empirical evidence evaluating their educational effectiveness in public health contexts remains limited. A 2026 systematic review restricted to randomized controlled trials found insufficient evidence to establish a consistent educational benefit. Heterogeneity, imprecision, and a lack of higher-level outcomes limited the conclusions (14). This suggests that current trends should be characterized as experimental and promising rather than established and validated.

Required AI competencies for public health students

Public health students require a progressive, level-appropriate set of AI competencies. At the foundational level, they should understand basic AI terminology, common tool types, the generative-predictive distinction, and the limitations of AI outputs, including recognizing hallucinations, unsupported claims, and fabricated references (1, 7, 15). Medical education frameworks similarly emphasize foundational knowledge of AI and data (16). At the applied level, students should use AI for public health tasks like data exploration, communication drafting, literature searching, code support, and scenario analysis (2, 3), while also verifying outputs, explaining assumptions, and documenting AI use (7, 15). The blended pedagogical intervention described by Parmar et al. demonstrated that students can effectively use ChatGPT to explore occupational hazards and prevention strategies and to critically analyze AI-generated content (3). At the critical appraisal level, students should be able to evaluate AI tools and AI-based studies by asking whether a model was validated, which population was used for training, whether performance differs across subgroups, whether the model is interpretable, and whether the tool is appropriate for the intended public health context (1, 7, 17). Critical appraisal of AI output has been identified as a recurring area of emphasis in competency frameworks (16).

AI Literacy for Epidemiological Reasoning.

A critical gap in existing competency frameworks is insufficient attention to the statistical and data literacy needed for responsible AI use in public health. Students must understand fundamental concepts in data science and epidemiology to evaluate AI systems critically. These include training, validation, and test data; overfitting; calibration; discrimination; sensitivity and specificity; predictive values; dataset shift; external validation; missing data; representativeness; and the distinction between causal and predictive modeling.

In addition, public health students should be educated that AI-generated summaries easily blur the line between association and causation. Students should understand confounding, selection bias, collider bias, ecological fallacy, reverse causality, and the distinction between causal and predictive models.

A 2025 narrative review on AI (18) found that students could critically evaluate AI-generated language but not the model itself unless they received explicit training in data quality and model evaluation, highlighting the need for statistical literacy in AI education.

Ethically and in terms of governance, students need to know about concepts like privacy, consent, data protection, accountability, transparency, bias, health equity, and digital exclusion (7, 15), and be taught to see AI not just as a technical tool but as a sociotechnical intervention that may affect population health, institutional trust and resource allocation, with ethical and legal oversight being acknowledged as a core domain in AI competency frameworks (16). Finally, students need to be trained to work with AI, including prompt writing, critical verification, transparent disclosure, and responsible use, all without sacrificing independent reasoning (1, 8, 15).

The competency domains proposed are adapted from existing AI competency frameworks in the health professions education literature. Specifically, the seven domains proposed by Hunt et al. (2026) (16) are: AI ethics, law and regulation, professionalism, clinical applications, critical appraisal, research and innovation, and theory and foundations.

Ethical, legal, and equity considerations

Because public health decisions affect entire communities rather than just individuals, public health education has a distinct obligation to address the ethical dimensions of AI. AI systems can replicate or worsen inequalities if they are built on biased data, used without local validation, or deployed in environments with unequal digital access (7, 15). This is why AI integration must be grounded in a commitment to equity, institutional capacity, and ethical responsibility (19). Privacy and data protection are non-negotiable; public health datasets often include sensitive health, demographic, or geographic information, so students must learn not to upload identifiable data into unsecured platforms (7, 15), and institutions need to provide clear guidance on approved tools and data governance (5, 13). Transparency should also be a core expectation: any use of AI in coursework, teaching, analysis, or writing ought to be disclosed as a matter of responsible practice, not as a punitive control (5, 7, 15). Equity should be embedded throughout, with students gaining an understanding of how algorithms may vary in performance across different age groups, languages, ethnicities, socioeconomic backgrounds, geographic regions, and healthcare settings (1, 7). In short, ethical and equitable AI implementation requires a comprehensive approach that integrates data governance, ethical reflection, community involvement, education, and continuous monitoring (19).

A more critical discussion of governance challenges is warranted. Key implementation challenges include accountability when AI systems produce harmful outputs; the tension between transparency and intellectual property protection; algorithmic bias that may disproportionately affect marginalized populations; and the digital divide that may exacerbate existing health inequities across diverse healthcare and educational settings. These governance challenges require institutional policies that are regularly updated to keep pace with rapidly evolving AI capabilities.

Challenges in integrating AI into public health education

Several barriers hinder the responsible integration of AI into public health education. The most pressing of these is faculty readiness: many educators will be expected to guide students in using AI without having received much, if any, formal training themselves (1, 4, 20). This speaks to the need for faculty development that emphasizes ethical considerations, practical skills, and the development of critical and reflective thinking among teachers and students alike (21). At the same time, curricula are already dense, and programs will need to incorporate AI into existing courses without displacing core topics like epidemiology, biostatistics, health policy, social determinants, ethics, and community engagement (1). Institutional policies provide another source of inconsistency: some institutions actively promote experimentation and innovation, while others clamp down on the use of AI tools for academic integrity reasons (5, 13). The challenge is in striking a balance that allows for responsible use without undermining the educational process. There is also the risk that AI tools will exacerbate inequities if students are required to pay for premium access or if institutions are unable to provide secure, reliable platforms (7, 15). Finally, deskilling is a real concern; students may become too reliant on AI for writing, coding, analysis, or even reasoning (5, 15). This is of particular concern in public health, where professionals need to manage uncertainty, weigh evidence critically, and communicate effectively in high-pressure situations.

Particular attention should be paid to practical barriers in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as infrastructure limitations (e.g., unreliable internet connectivity, insufficient computing resources), faculty preparedness (e.g., limited training opportunities, language barriers), resource constraints (e.g., inability to purchase premium AI tools, lack of technical support), and implementation challenges (e.g., cultural resistance, limited institutional capacity). If not specifically tackled by capacity-building efforts and international cooperation, these barriers could exacerbate the global health equity divide.

Assessment and academic integrity in the AI era

AI disrupts traditional assessment methods like unsupervised essays, take-home assignments, and generic literature summaries. Rather than relying mainly on detection tools, public health educators should redesign assessment around applied reasoning, transparency, and demonstration of competence (5, 15). Suggested approaches include oral defenses, in-class tasks, reflective AI logs, local data-based policy briefs, group simulations, critical appraisal of AI outputs, and comparisons of AI-generated versus evidence-based answers. Students could submit an AI-use statement detailing the tool used, prompts, verification process, and any modifications made (5, 15). Assessment should focus on what AI cannot replace: judgment, interpretation, ethical reasoning, communication, contextualization, community awareness, and defensible decision-making (7, 15). Practical frameworks should guide responsible use of generative AI in health professions education (8).

It is important to distinguish evidence-based recommendations from expert opinion. While assessment redesign is widely recommended in conceptual literature, empirical evidence specifically evaluating these alternative assessment approaches in public health education remains limited. Longitudinal studies comparing traditional versus AI-resistant assessments are needed to validate these recommendations.

Proposed framework: the public health AI-education integration framework

The nine framework domains were developed through an iterative process of author discussion and consensus. Recurring themes identified across the selected literature were grouped into preliminary domains and compared with existing AI competency frameworks and established educational models. The domains were subsequently refined to reduce conceptual overlap, improve clarity, and ensure relevance to the distinctive population-level, epidemiological, ethical, and equity-oriented requirements of public health education. This process represents a narrative and concept-development approach rather than a formal Delphi procedure or an empirical framework-validation exercise. Accordingly, the resulting framework should be regarded as a proposed conceptual model requiring external review and prospective validation.

Building upon recent calls for AI literacy, existing practices in the field, the wider health professions education literature, and guidance on ethics and governance, this narrative review draws upon recurring themes identified across the selected literature to propose a structured framework for the responsible integration of AI into public health education. The proposed framework is based on nine domains, is designed as a conceptual and practical model, and requires future evaluation and validation in different educational contexts.

The framework is grounded in existing educational models, such as competency-based education and experiential learning theory. The framework embeds AI literacy within competency-based education, requiring students to develop specific AI competencies rather than simply perform AI activities. This combination of adaptive learning and simulation delivery aligns with the theory of experiential learning, which emphasizes learning through experience and reflection, and is particularly applicable to AI-augmented simulations that provide immediate, contextual feedback (Figure 1).

Nine-domain framework for integrating artificial intelligence into public health education. The circular framework comprises nine interrelated domains around the core goal of Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Public Health Education, which include Learner and Curriculum Needs Assessment, Artificial Intelligence Literacy Foundation, Artificial Intelligence Tool Selection and Educational Alignment, Public Health Dataset and Case Integration, Adaptive Learning and Simulation Delivery, Human-in-the-Loop Supervision, Bias, Equity, and Ethical Auditing, Assessment and Academic Integrity, and Feedback, Monitoring, and Curriculum Refinement. The circular design underscores that implementation is iterative, with feedback and monitoring driving the ongoing refinement of the curriculum.

Domain 1, Learner and Curriculum Needs Assessment, recommends that programs begin by assessing students’ current literacy in AI, quantitative skills, core public health competencies, and professional goals, and then map the curriculum to determine where AI content is most appropriate. Domain 2, AI Literacy Foundation, is designed to ensure that all students establish a solid foundation in essential AI concepts, including generative models, predictive algorithms, data quality, hallucination, bias, privacy, and ethics (1, 7, 15). This will equip learners to comprehend the limitations and strengths of AI, critically assess outputs, recognize bias and uncertainty, protect privacy, and responsibly use AI in public health work. This domain should also explicitly include components of data literacy and statistical literacy, such as training, validation, and test data; overfitting; calibration; sensitivity and specificity; predictive values; dataset shift; missing data; and the difference between causal and predictive models. Domain 3, AI Tool Selection and Educational Alignment, emphasizes the need for secure, validated, accessible, and pedagogically appropriate platforms (5, 7, 15). As Conrad and Hall (20) emphasized, capacity building in generative AI is critical for improving curriculum design, teaching approaches, assessment, and inclusivity.

Domain 4, Public Health Dataset and Case Integration, grounds learning with AI in real-world materials on surveillance data, outbreak scenarios, health promotion campaigns, policy briefs, and program evaluations (2, 3). This domain should explicitly emphasize training in causal inference, including confounding, selection bias, collider bias, ecological fallacy, reverse causality, and the distinction between prediction and explanation. Students should be trained to recognize when AI-generated summaries confuse association with causation. Domain 5, Adaptive Learning and Simulation Delivery, leverages AI to deliver personalized tutoring, immediate feedback, dynamic simulations, and scenario-based exercises tied to specific competencies (2–5, 11, 12). Domain 6, Human-in-the-Loop Supervision, preserves faculty oversight of all educational decisions, with AI supporting practice and feedback while instructors guide interpretation, assessment, and ethical reflection (7, 15).

Domain 7, Bias, Equity, and Ethical Auditing, weaves attention to bias, fairness, privacy, transparency, and population-level impacts into every AI-supported learning activity (1, 7, 15), acknowledging the interconnected nature of data governance, ethics, community engagement, education, and ongoing evaluation (19). Domain 8, Assessment and Academic Integrity, advocates for transparent AI disclosure, applied tasks, oral defenses, reflective assignments, and competency-based assessment (5, 8, 15). Finally, Domain 9, Feedback, Monitoring, and Curriculum Refinement, calls for continuous review of AI integration using student performance data, learner and faculty feedback, equity indicators, academic integrity outcomes, and relevance to evolving public health practice (15, 17). Ultimately, the framework treats AI integration as an ongoing, adaptive process rather than a one-off implementation.

Future validation should include prospective implementation studies, mixed-methods evaluations, external expert review, and longitudinal assessment of student competency development across diverse educational contexts.

Discussion

The literature synthesized in this narrative review suggests that AI is increasingly influencing public health education, although structured curricular integration and evidence of educational effectiveness remain limited. However, the evidence bases specifically evaluating AI’s educational effectiveness in public health remain much thinner than the enthusiasm surrounding the topic suggests. A considerable proportion of available literature is conceptual, opinion-based, or derived from medical education rather than public health-specific contexts. The reviewed literature consistently highlights a gap between the widespread informal use of generative AI by students and educators and the limited availability of structured curricula that guide its responsible use. Most AI applications in health professions education are currently focused on evaluating learners and programs, with additional opportunities in curriculum design, identifying learning needs, and personalizing instruction (9).

Looking across the comparative evidence, a few notable patterns stand out. Studies specifically focused on public health are relatively rare compared with the broader health professions education literature, with most of the available work coming from medical education, which may not adequately reflect the population-level, policy-oriented, and equity-focused dimensions that define public health practice. The evidence base is also quite varied, with few randomized controlled trials and limited longitudinal data (17). A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis restricted to randomized trials concluded that the evidence remains insufficient to establish a consistent educational benefit, with heterogeneity, imprecision, and a lack of higher-level outcomes limiting the conclusions. This finding should moderate expectations about current AI integration in public health education. At the same time, ethical and governance issues are consistently highlighted across studies, signaling broad agreement that AI integration needs to be handled with care (7, 15, 19).

When the literature is classified as direct, adjacent, or indirect evidence, the analysis shows that direct evidence from public health education remains limited. Evidence from medical education and health informatics may offer transferable insights but should be applied cautiously. However, the findings from clinical education should be applied to public health at the population level with caution. The proposed framework aims to address this gap by emphasizing population-level reasoning, equity, and epidemiological thinking areas that differentiate public health from clinical education.

These observations have several important implications for health education: First, we must understand AI literacy as an emerging core competency rather than an add-on digital skill. This requires reforming the public health curriculum to embed AI throughout, rather than as a single course. Competency frameworks for health professions education have identified seven domains: AI ethics, law and regulation, professionalism, clinical applications, critical appraisal, research and innovation, and theory and foundations (16). Public health extends these by adding population-level reasoning, the distinction between causal and predictive models, and equity-oriented evaluation of AI systems. Faculty development is another priority. Many instructors lack formal training in AI and may not feel they are equipped to help students use it responsibly. Institutions should concentrate on programs that teach the technical and pedagogical aspects of AI. As Conrad and Hall (20) have argued, building capacity around generative AI is a prerequisite for improving the quality of public health and health promotion curricula. Another urgent area is assessment reform. Traditional take-home assignments are becoming vulnerable to AI abuse. Educators need to move toward assignments that emphasize applied reasoning, transparency and skills that cannot be easily replicated by AI. Finally, equity must be embedded in the integration of AI: institutions must ensure equal access to AI tools and incorporate discussions of algorithmic bias, digital exclusion and population-level equity into the educational program. While the promise of AI for public health education is great, this promise will need to be realized through careful attention to equity, institutional readiness, and ethical accountability.

However, some discrepancies are evident in the literature, as this review shows. Student satisfaction and engagement with AI-enhanced educational interventions are often reported positively, but objective learning outcomes are rarely measured, and the evidence is mixed when they are. Few studies in the systematic review by Feigerlova et al. (17) examined objective learning outcomes or used rigorous comparative designs. This implies that the current enthusiasm for AI in education is getting ahead of the evidence base. Future research should involve controlled evaluations with validated outcome measures.

AI-enabled public health education is particularly relevant to regions undergoing digital health transformation, such as the Middle East, where ongoing investments in health data systems, population health management, and workforce development create a strong need for professionals who can work with digital surveillance, predictive analytics, and AI-supported decision systems. Arabic-language AI tools and culturally adapted health communication deserve specific attention. Public health students should learn how to assess whether AI-generated Arabic health content is accurate, culturally appropriate, readable, and suitable for different literacy levels. Although AI models have been shown to improve the readability of patient education materials, further optimization is needed for completeness and linguistic diversity (22).

This review has several limitations. The narrative review approach is useful for synthesizing different types of evidence but is less systematic than a full systematic review and may be more vulnerable to selection bias. Although a structured literature search was undertaken, the narrative methodology may not have identified all relevant publications. Study selection and interpretation involved author judgment and may therefore have introduced subjectivity. The restriction to English language publications could have led to the exclusion of relevant evidence from other settings. The literature was heterogeneous in terms of populations, educational contexts, AI applications and reported outcomes, which limited direct comparison between publications. In addition, some findings and recommendations may need to be updated frequently as artificial intelligence (AI) technologies are evolving rapidly. The proposed framework is conceptual in nature and has not yet been prospectively validated.

Important research gaps remain. Rigorous empirical studies with well-defined objectives, comparators, validated outcomes, long-term follow-up, and transparent reporting are needed to assess AI-based interventions in public health education (17). Research should also include issues of equity, access, algorithmic bias, and impacts on workforce diversity. An inter-disciplinary comparison can reveal specific opportunities and challenges. Longitudinal studies are needed to explore the transfer of AI competencies learned in education to the professional work environment. Another urgent problem is the absence of standard evaluation frameworks for AI-based educational tools (17). Future work should include randomized controlled trials of AI-enhanced educational interventions in public health, longitudinal studies to track competency development over time, and implementation science research to examine barriers and facilitators in varied institutional and geographic settings.

AI is increasingly influencing public health practice and is beginning to enter public health education. Its influence extends beyond technical tasks to the way students learn, analyze data, synthesize evidence, communicate risk, and prepare for population-level decision-making. However, AI will only strengthen public health education if it is integrated responsibly. The evidence base for AI’s educational effectiveness in public health remains limited, and current enthusiasm should be tempered with realistic expectations about what AI can and cannot achieve in educational settings. Public health programs should move beyond ad hoc AI use toward structured curricula, competency-based training, faculty development, ethical governance, assessment redesign, and continuous evaluation. The proposed Public Health AI-Education Integration Framework provides a practical model for aligning AI literacy, public health applications, adaptive learning, simulation, human oversight, equity, and academic integrity. However, the framework is proposed as a conceptual model requiring prospective validation through implementation studies, mixed-methods evaluations, and longitudinal tracking of student competency development. Without such validation, it should be viewed as an organizing structure for curriculum development rather than an evidence-based standard. The purpose of AI in public health education is not to replace educators, professional judgment, or community engagement, but to prepare future public health professionals to use AI critically, ethically, and effectively to improve population health. As the World Health Organization has emphasized, AI literacy as a core competency for the next generation of healthcare professionals is essential for navigating the AI-driven public health landscape responsibly (7). The integration of AI into public health education should proceed with caution, guided by evidence, attentive to equity, and grounded in the core values of public health practice.
