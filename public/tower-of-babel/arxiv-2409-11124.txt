Comparison principle for general nonlocal Hamilton-Jacobi equations with superlinear gradient
Adina Ciomaga, Trí Minh Lê, Olivier Ley, Erwin Topp

Abstract




Abstract
.
We obtain the comparison principle for discontinuous viscosity sub- and supersolutions of nonlocal Hamilton-Jacobi equations, with superlinear and coercive gradient terms. The nonlocal terms are integro-differential operators in Lévy form, with general measures:
x
-dependent, possibly degenerate and without any restriction on the order.
The measures must satisfy a combined Wasserstein/Total Variation-continuity assumption, which is one of the weakest conditions used in the context of viscosity approach for this type of integro-differential PDEs. The proof relies on a regularizing effect due to the gradient growth. We present several examples of applications to PDEs with different types of nonlocal operators (measures with density, operators of variable order, Lévy-Itô operators).

Keywords:
nonlinear integro-differential equations, comparison principle, viscosity solutions

AMS Subject Classification:
35R09, 35B51, 35D40, 35J60, 35F21

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the well-posedness of degenerate elliptic nonlocal Hamilton-Jacobi equations with superlinear gradient growth. The model equation takes the form

\lambda u-\mathcal{I}_{x}u(x)+H(x,Du)=0\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N},

(1.1)

where
\lambda>0
is a constant,
H:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous Hamiltonian, superlinear and coercive with respect to the gradient variable, and
\mathcal{I}_{x}
is an integro-differential operator of Lévy type, that is, for
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
measurable, and
\xi,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it writes

\mathcal{I}_{\xi}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(u(x+z)-u(x)-\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)Du(x)\cdot z\right)\nu_{\xi}(dz),

(1.2)

whenever the integral makes sense. Throughout the paper we assume
\nu_{\xi}
is a measure in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\nu_{\xi}(\{0\})=0
, and such that the family
\left(\nu_{\xi}\right)_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
satisfies the uniform Lévy condition

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min(1,|z|^{2})\nu_{\xi}(dz)<\infty.

Under this assumption,
\mathcal{I}_{\xi}u(x)
makes sense if
u
is bounded and sufficiently regular at
x
, say
C^{2}
.

The types of Lévy measures we consider include, but are not restricted to, Carathéodory density measures, i.e. measures for which there exists
K:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\nu_{\xi}(dz)=K(\xi,z)dz.

(1.3)

Within this case, of particular interest are kernels comparable to the one of the fractional Laplacian of order
\sigma\in(0,2)
, in the sense that there exist
0\leq c\leq C
, such that,

\frac{c}{|z|^{N+\sigma}}\leq K(\xi,z)\leq\frac{C}{|z|^{N+\sigma}}\quad\mbox{for all}\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\ z\neq 0.

(1.4)

We prove existence and uniqueness of bounded, continuous solutions of (
1.1
) within the framework of viscosity solutions’ theory. Our main result, Theorem
2.8
, establishes a comparison principle between bounded, discontinuous viscosity sub- and supersolutions of (
1.1
). This, coupled with Perron’s method, further allows us to obtain well-posedness for problem (
1.1
), which is the content of Theorem
2.9
.

The main novelty of the results presented in this article are related to the generality of the Lévy measures involved in the equation. At this respect, we shall mention that comparison results for general partial integro-differential equations (PIDEs) involving
\xi-
dependent operators of the form (
1.2
) remain one the most important open questions in the field, even though progress has been made in this direction. Several results have been obtained, according to the
nature of the integral operator
- general Lévy or Lévy-Itô, or to
the order of the operator
- often reflected in the singularity at the origin of the Lévy measure against which they are integrated.

In the first case, we recall that a Lévy-Itô operator takes the form

\mathcal{J}_{\xi}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(u(x+j(x,z))-u(x)-\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)Du(x)\cdot j(x,z)\right)\nu(dz),

where, for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
j(\xi,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a measurable function known as the
jump function
, and
\nu
is a Lévy measure. Under adequate assumptions on
j
, the Lévy-Itô case is relatively well understood, either by means of optimal stochastic control or by direct viscosity methods. Some of the first existence and comparison results were established by Soner in
[
32
]
. The viscosity theory for general PIDEs has been revisited by Barles and Imbert in
[
10
]
. Therein, the authors provide a general variant of Jensen Ishii’s Lemma for a large class of PIDEs. However, comparison principles are only obtained for
nonlocal operators in Lévy-Itô form
. Their results generalize the ones obtained by Imbert in
[
23
]
for first-order Hamilton Jacobi equations, and by Jakobsen and Karlsen
[
26
,
27
]
and Arisawa in
[
4
]
for second order PIDEs. Strong comparison results were later given by Ciomaga in
[
17
]
. Barles, Ley and Topp extended in
[
13
]
the comparison results for PIDEs where the nonlocal diffusion is coupled with a dominant, coercive Hamiltonian. Chasseigne and Jakobsen in
[
19
]
provided comparison results for quasilinear PIDEs with Lévy-Itô structure.

The general case of
\xi
-dependent Lévy operators is by far less understood. General existence and comparison results for semi-continuous (and yet unbounded) viscosity solutions were found by Alvarez and Tourin in
[
1
]
when the kernels involved are finite. In the case of space-time, bounded measures, results were provided by Amadori
[
3
]
and Alibaud
[
2
]
, for continuous viscosity solutions. For PIDEs involving
first order operators
, meaning that

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min(1,|z|)\nu_{\xi}(dz)<\infty,

(1.5)

results were established by Soner in
[
33
]
, followed by the works of Sayah in
[
31
]
.

In the attempt of dealing with general nonlocal operators
up to the second order
, most of the times comparison results rely on extra assumptions. For instance, for equations “in divergence form”, uniqueness results for weak (or distributional) solutions can be obtained through Maximum Principles, see for instance
[
20
]
. In terms of the nonlocal operator, some symmetry assumption on the kernel is required.
Coming back to the “non-divergence” setting, Mou and Świȩch
[
29
]
proved comparison principle for a class of nonlinear PIDEs including Bellman-Isaacs equations when
a priori regularity
of the involved sub and/or supersolution is known. This is a rather standard principle in the viscosity formulation, in the sense that the more regularity of the functions to compare we have, the better the control of the terms arising from the penalization introduced in the doubling variables method is.

An innovative step forward can be found in the paper of Guillen, Mou and Świȩch
[
22
]
, where new comparison principles among continuous solutions of PIDEs are obtained, for a wide class of nonlocal operators, by means of Optimal Transport techniques. In particular, they impose a Lipschitz continuity condition with respect to the
L^{p}
transport metric, with the exponent
p\in[1,2]
related to the order of the singularity at the origin. Though this approach still validates comparison principles for nonlocal equations of lower order (i.e., (
1.5
) holds), or when one of the functions to compare has higher a priori regularity, the methods introduced in
[
22
]
allow to consider far more general family of measures which do not fit into the assumptions previously addressed.

The current work continues the research in the direction introduced in
[
22
]
. Our main contribution is a comparison principle between discontinuous viscosity sub- and supersolutions, for
general
\xi
- dependent Lévy operators, without any restriction on the order
. The interest of the result is twofold: (i) there is no restriction on the order of the operator, and (ii) we do not need to assume any a priori regularity neither of the subsolution nor of the supersolution, aside from the upper and the lower semicontinuity. This last point is of particular interest since this implies that no uniform ellipticity (in the sense of Caffarelli and Silvestre
[
16
]
, that is
c>0
in (
1.4
)), or even weak ellipticity (as in
[
8
]
) of the nonlocal operator is required. In fact, the nonlocal diffusion could be very degenerate in nature, and could be of mixed type, (that is, it can be combined with a local diffusion), or with variable order, see Sections §
4
and §
5
for a more accurate exposition of these settings.

At this point, we mention that such level of generality for the nonlocal operator is possible by the presence of
H
in the equation, and we are still far from the most general comparison result among discontinuous viscosity sub and supersolutions. In fact, we get the result when we combine the nonlocal diffusion with a superlinear coercive Hamiltonian in the gradient variable. To fix ideas, the reader could bear in mind the superlinear eikonal model, where the Hamiltonian takes the form

H(x,p)=b(x)|Du|^{m}-f(x)

(1.6)

where
b,f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
are bounded, continuous functions, with
b(\cdot)\geq b_{0}>0
, and
m>1
.

Roughly speaking, the degenerate elliptic nature of the nonlocality is controlled by the action of the Hamiltonian in the doubling variables procedure: if
u,v
are respectively a viscosity sub- and super-solution to the problem, we double variables and penalize as usual, arguing over the function

(x,y)\mapsto u(x)-v(y)-\epsilon^{-2}|x-y|^{2},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Up to a localization, we have the existence of a maximum point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
of the above function, and we employ the viscosity inequalities for
u
and
v
at
\bar{x},\bar{y}
respectively. Then, under the assumptions of the data, the leading contribution of the Hamiltonian allows us to prove that the “viscosity gradient”

\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{\epsilon^{2}}

is bounded, uniformly in
\epsilon
(see Lemma
3.6
). This is exactly the type of conclusion we get, for instance, if one of the functions to compare is smooth.

Of course, the continuity of the coefficients with respect to the state variable is a structural assumption of the method. As such, the hypotheses on the map
\xi\mapsto\nu_{\xi}
are adapted to the singularity of the measures around the origin. Namely, we assume continuity in the total variation distance away from the origin, i.e. for
0<r<R
there exists a modulus of continuity
\omega_{r,R}
such that

|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(B_{R}\setminus B_{r})\leq\omega_{R,r}(|x-y|),\quad\mbox{for all}\ x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

On the other hand, we assume a 1/2-Hölder continuity on the Wasserstein distance (
L^{2}
transport metric), localized around the origin, i.e. for
0<r<1
we have

W_{2}(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})(B_{r})\leq o_{r}(1)|x-y|^{1/2},\quad\mbox{for all}\ x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

We refer the reader to Section §
2.3
for precise definitions and hypotheses. These are sufficient conditions to conclude the result, as they lead to a “regularizing effect” of the Hamiltonian into the equation, even though we neither use nor we prove any regularity property on
u
or
v
. We point out that, one could invoke the Hölder estimates for
subsolutions
to (
1.1
) when the gradient term dominates the diffusion. In terms of (
1.4
) and (
1.6
), this means that
m>\sigma
, see
[
11
,
14
]
. On the other hand, if the nonlocality is uniformly elliptic and the diffusion rules the equation (that is,
c>0
in (
1.4
) and
m\leq\sigma
), it is possible to employ elliptic estimates to get Hölder estimates for continuous solutions, see
[
9
]
. Nonetheless, our results cover the intriguing scenario where neither the ellipticity nor the coercivity rules the equation, namely, when
1<m\leq\sigma
, and
\mathcal{I}_{x}
is degenerate elliptic.

We finish this introduction by mentioning that these types of equations occur in the study of stochastic optimal control problems with jump-diffusion processes. In this setting,
\mathcal{I}_{\xi}
is the infinitesimal generator of a jump process either in Lévy-Itô form, or in Courrège form, see
[
5
]
. In the local case (namely, when the diffusion is governed by a continuous process like the Brownian motion), a superlinear Hamiltonian with the form (
1.6
) is related to trajectories controlled with unbounded drift, and the value function of the control problem is the viscosity solution of the associated Hamilton Jacobi equation, see
[
28
,
15
]
. We expect to have similar verification results in the nonlocal context, see for instance
[
30
]
. Since we do not assume convexity on
H
, this type of result has potential application to differential games.

The paper is organized as follows. We introduce the notations and definitions, make precise the set of assumptions and state the main results in Section §
2
. In Section §
3
we prove the main comparison result. We then provide a couple of extensions of the Comparison Principle to the second order and time dependent case in Section §
4
. Finally, we present a set of examples that illustrate the scope of the assumptions, which include interesting measures without density or measures with intricate elliptic properties in Section §
5
.

.

. We denote by

USC(A)

.

\mathbb{S}^{N}

2.2.
Distances between Lévy measures

We use in this paper two notions of metrics between Lévy measures: the Wasserstein distance and the total variation.

Following the notion of boundary Wasserstein metric between finite measures on bounded sets, introduced by Figalli and Gigli in
[
21
]
, and later developed for Lévy measures by Guillen, Mou and Świeçh in
[
22
]
, we consider the following notion of Wasserstein distance. For a given measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let
\mathcal{M}(A)
be the set of Lévy measures on
A
– possibly singular at the origin, that is

\mathcal{M}(A):=\left\{\nu\text{ positive Borel measure on }A\;:\;\nu(\{0\})=0,\;\int_{A\setminus\{0\}}\min(1,|z|^{2})\nu(dz)<\infty\right\}.

Let
A
be a subset of the unit ball
B
such that
0\in A
. Given
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(A)
, we define the set of admissible couplings for
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
as the set of positive measures on
A\times A
whose marginals on
A\setminus\{0\}
are
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
respectively, that is

\displaystyle{\text{Adm}_{A}}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{\gamma\text{ positive measure on }A\times A:\gamma(\{(0,0)\})=0,\;\pi^{i}_{\#}\gamma\mid_{A\setminus\{0\}}=\nu_{i},\text{ for }i=1,2\right\},

where
\pi^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{2N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
\pi^{i}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{i}
, is the canonical projection in the
i
-th coordinate.

Definition 1

(Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
p\in[1,2]
and
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(B)
. For any
\,0<r<1
, we define the
Wasserstein distance between
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
, restricted on
B_{r}
,
as

W_{p}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})(B_{r}):=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in{\mathrm{Adm}_{B_{r}}(\nu_{1}^{r},\nu_{2}^{r})}}\int\limits_{B_{r}\times B_{r}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{p}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})\right)^{1/p},

where
\nu_{i}^{r}:=\nu_{i}\mid_{B_{r}}
is the restriction of the measure
\nu_{i}
to
B_{r}
.

Remark 1
.

Similarly to
[
22
]
, the admissible set
\mathrm{Adm}_{A}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
considers measures defined on
A\times A
, despite the fact that marginals are restricted to
A\setminus\{0\}
. This is due to the fact that Lévy measures are singular at the origin, therefore the origin can be used as an infinite reservoir: we can take mass and redistribute within
B
or, conversely, take infinitely large mass from
B
and send it to the origin, provided we pay an adequate transportation cost. This plays a substantial role in the definition. On the contrary, Lévy measures being finite elsewhere, the boundary of the unit ball can be treated as having zero mass. Therefore, working with measures defined on the open ball
B
or the closed ball
\overline{B}
shall not matter, but what matters is that mass is concentrated at the origin. Additionally, as pointed out in
[
21
]
, the form of the cost function
c(z_{1},z_{2})=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{p}
in the definition of Wasserstein distance allows us to assume without loss of generality that
\gamma(\{(0,0)\})=0
for every
\gamma
admissible. This justifies the choice in the definition of admissible couplings.

We also employ the total variation between two Lévy measures, that we recall here for simplicity.

Definition 2

(Total variation)
.

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. We call
the total variation between
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
the positive measure

|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}|=(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{+}+(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{-},

where
(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{\pm}
are given by the Jordan decomposition
\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}=(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{+}-(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{-}
on the signed measure
(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})
, that is,
for any measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{+}(A)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\;\;\sup\big\{\nu_{1}(E)-\nu_{2}(E):E\subseteq A,\ E\ \mbox{ measurable }\big\}

\displaystyle(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{-}(A)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\inf\big\{\nu_{1}(E)-\nu_{2}(E):E\subseteq A,\ E\ \mbox{ measurable }\big\}.

We list below a series of basic properties of the Wasserstein distance between Lévy measures, used throughout the paper. We concentrate on Lévy measures supported on the unit ball
B
, but all the results can be readily adapted to other local domains of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(B)
such that
{\rm supp}(\nu_{1})=A_{1},{\rm supp}(\nu_{2})=A_{2}
. Then, for each admissible coupling
\gamma\in\text{Adm}_{B}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
we have that

\displaystyle\;{\rm supp}(\gamma)\subset(A_{1}\times A_{2})\cup(A_{1}\times\{0\})\cup(\{0\}\times A_{2}).

Proof.

Since
\gamma(\{(0,0)\})=0
, without any loss of generality, we may assume
0\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
. We show that

{\rm supp}(\gamma)\subset A_{1}\times A_{2}
. We argue by contradiction and assume there exists a set
E\subset(B\times B)\setminus(A_{1}\times A_{2})
such that
\gamma(E)>0
.
We decompose the set into the disjoint union
E=E_{1}\cup E_{2}\cup F
, with

E_{1}=E\cap A_{1}\times B,\quad E_{2}=E\cap B\times A_{2},\quad F\subset A_{1}^{c}\times A_{2}^{c}.

Since
\gamma
is an admissible coupling with marginals
\nu^{i}
,
\pi^{1}(E_{2})\subset B^{*}\setminus A_{1}

and
(\pi^{1})^{-1}(\pi^{1}(E_{2}))\supset E_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\nu_{1}(\pi^{1}(E_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma\mid_{B^{*}}(\pi^{1}(E_{2}))=\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma(\pi^{1}(E_{2})\cap B^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma((\pi^{1})^{-1}(\pi^{1}(E_{2}))\cap(B^{*}\times B))=\gamma(E_{2}\cap(B^{*}\times B))\geq\gamma(E_{2}).

Similarly, we can check that
\gamma(E_{1})=0
and
\gamma(F)=0
. Summing up, we reach a contradiction with the assumption
\gamma(E)>0
.
∎

An important property employed in our arguments is related to the integration of functions of one variable against admissible couplings between two Lévy measures.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(B)
and
\gamma\in\mathrm{Adm}_{B}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
an admissible coupling.
If
f
is a measurable function with support away from the origin (there exists
\delta>0
so that
{\rm supp}(f)\subset B\setminus B_{\delta}
), then

\displaystyle\int_{B\times B}f(z_{i})d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B}f(z)\nu_{i}(dz)\quad i=1,2.

Proof.

For simple functions
f=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\chi_{A_{k}}
with
c_{k}\geq 0
and
A_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
measurable, we can always assume that
A_{k}\cap B_{\delta}=\emptyset
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{B\times B}f(z_{1})d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\int_{B\times B}\chi_{A_{k}}(z_{1})d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\gamma(A_{k}\times B).

Taking into account that
\gamma
has marginals
\nu_{i}
, i=1,2, and
A_{k}\cap B_{\delta}=\emptyset
, we get

\gamma(A_{k}\times B)=\gamma((A_{k}\cap B^{*})\times B)=\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma\mid_{B^{*}}(A_{k})=\nu_{1}(A_{k}).
It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{B\times B}f(z_{1})d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\nu_{1}(A_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\int_{B}\chi_{A_{k}}(z_{1})\nu_{1}(dz_{1})=\int_{B}f(z_{1})\nu_{1}(dz_{1}).

For general nonnegative measurable functions, it is possible to conclude by the previous argument and the Monotone Convergence Theorem. From here, the result follows for a general measurable function with support away the origin, by writing
f=f^{+}-f^{-}
and using the previous results.
∎

An immediate corollary of the above lemma is the following result.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(B)
and
\gamma\in\mathrm{Adm}_{B}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
an admissible coupling.
If
f,g:B\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are measurable functions with support away the origin, then

\int_{B\times B}[f(z_{1})+g(z_{2})]d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{B}f(z)\nu_{1}(dz)+\int_{B}g(z)\nu_{2}(dz).

If instead, we are integrating functions supported in a ball –such as a quadratics, the equality fails, but we still have a control of the integral against the admissible coupling, by the integral of one variable only. More precisely, the following holds.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(B)
and
\gamma\in\mathrm{Adm}_{B}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
an admissible coupling. Then

\int_{B\times B}|z_{1}|^{2}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})\leq 4\int_{B}|z|^{2}\nu_{1}(dz).

Proof.

Decompose the integration domain as

\displaystyle B\times B=\bigcup\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{k}\times B\quad\text{with }A_{k}=B_{2^{-k}}\setminus B_{2^{-(k+1)}}.

Then, taking into account that
\gamma
is an admissible coupling and

\gamma(A_{k}\times B)=\gamma((A_{k}\cap B^{*})\times B)=\nu_{1}(A_{k})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{B\times B}|z_{1}|^{2}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{A_{k}\times B}|z_{1}|^{2}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-2k}\int_{A_{k}\times B}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-2k}\int_{A_{k}}\nu_{1}(dz_{1})\leq 4\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{A_{k}}|z_{1}|^{2}\nu_{1}(dz_{1}).

∎

Finally, the Wasserstein distance is always controlled by the second moment of the total variation.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(B)
. Then

W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})(B)\leq\int_{B}|z|^{2}|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}|(dz).

(2.1)

Proof.

We aim at constructing a particular admissible coupling between the measures
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
that yields the above bound. To this end, we keep the common mass between the two measures via the identity map
Id
and transport mass from and onto the origin, via the projection function
P:B\to B
given by
P(x)=0
, for all
x\in B
. Given the Lévy measures
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(B)
, we consider the
the minimal measure
\nu_{1}\wedge\nu_{2}
, defined by

\nu_{1}\wedge\nu_{2}=\nu_{1}-(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{+}.

Consider then the coupling transport plan
\gamma:B\times B\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by

\gamma=(Id\times Id)_{\#}(\nu_{1}\wedge\nu_{2})+(Id\times P)_{\#}(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{+}+(P\times Id)_{\#}(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{-},

which is admissible for the pair
(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
.
Indeed, for each measurable subset
A\subseteq B^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma\mid_{B^{*}}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma((\pi^{1})^{-1}(A)\cap(B^{*}\times B))=\gamma(A\times B)=(\nu_{1}\wedge\nu_{2})((Id\times Id)^{-1}(A\times B))

\displaystyle+(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{+}((Id\times P)^{-1}(A\times B))+(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{-}((P\times Id)^{-1}(A\times B))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\nu_{1}\wedge\nu_{2})(A)+(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{+}(A)+(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{-}(\emptyset)=\nu_{1}(A).

Similarly, we get
\pi^{2}_{\#}\gamma\mid_{B^{*}}(A)=\nu_{2}(A)
and
\gamma(\{(0,0)\})=0
.
Thus, the transport plan
\gamma
above is admissible.
Computing with changes of variables, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B\times B}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{B}|z|^{2}(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{+}(dz)+\int_{B}|z|^{2}(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})^{-}(dz)=\int_{B}|z|^{2}|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}|(dz).

Taking the infimum over all admissible couplings
\gamma
, the conclusion follows.
∎

2.3.
Assumptions

We now state the assumptions on the family of Lévy measures and on the Hamiltonian.
We consider a family of Lévy measures
\big(\nu_{\xi}\big)_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, satisfying the following conditions:

(M1)

There exist a constant
C_{\nu}>0
such that

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min(1,|z|^{2})\nu_{\xi}(dz)\leq C_{\nu}.

(M2)

There exists
R_{0}>1
such that, for all
R\geq R_{0}
,

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\nu_{\xi}(dz)\leq o_{1/R}(1).

(M3)

For all
0<r<R
, there exists a modulus of continuity

\omega_{R,r}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
so that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|\left(B_{R}\setminus B_{r}\right)\leq\omega_{R,r}(|x-y|).

(M4)

There exists
0<r_{0}<1
such that, for all
0<r<r_{0}
and for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

W_{2}(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})(B_{r})=o_{r}(1)|x-y|^{1/2}.

Assumption (M1) is a uniform Lévy condition, whereas (M2) is a uniform decay at infinity, which is a piece of interest when we deal with problems in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
The next two assumptions are the key points in this research and they are assumed to hold
without any restriction on the order of the nonlocal operator.
Due to the singularity at the origin of the Lévy measure, we are often led to split the nonlocal operator into sets around the origin and away from the origin.
Assumption (M3) deals with the bounded part of the Lévy measures and it involves the total variation. Assumption (M4) deals with the singular part of the Lévy measures and it involves the Wasserstein distance. A common assumption would have been a unified continuity condition, expressed in terms of the total variation only:

(M)

For all
r>0
, and for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\int_{B_{r}}\min(1,|z|^{2})|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)\leq o_{r}(1)|x-y|.

The continuity condition (M4) with respect to the data has already appeared in the study of the regularity of solutions, see for example
[
9
,
8
,
12
]
. Recently, Guillen, Mou and Swieçh showed in
[
22
]
that, within the study of comparison principles for integro-differential equation, by means of doubling of variables technique, the quadratic optimal transport distance naturally arises as a nonlocal Jensen-Ishii variant. In addition, the Wasserstein distance is to be considered only on small balls around the origin. This explains the coupling (M3)-(M4).

It is easy to see that a family of measures satisfying assumption (M) will satisfy (M3) and (M4).

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\big(\nu_{\xi}\big)_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a family of Lévy measures which satisfies
(M)
.
Then
(M3)
and
(M4)
hold.

Proof.

Writing (M) for
r=1
, we have for all
0<r\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{B}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)=\int_{B_{r}}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)+\int_{B\setminus B_{r}}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)\leq o_{1}(1)|x-y|.

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{B\setminus B_{r}}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)\leq\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B\setminus B_{r}}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)\leq\frac{o_{1}(1)}{r^{2}}|x-y|.

Therefore, for
0<r\leq 1\leq R
,

\displaystyle|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(B_{R}\setminus B_{r})=\int_{B\setminus B_{r}}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)+\int_{B_{R}\setminus B}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)\leq\left(o_{R}(1)+\frac{o_{1}(1)}{r^{2}}\right)|x-y|,

where we have used (M) for
R>1
to estimate the total variation on
B_{R}\setminus B
. This gives (M3).
Assumption (M4) follows from Proposition
2.5
together with (M) for
r<1
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Assumptions (M3)-(M4) are, at least localy, weaker than assumption (M). To illustrate this fact, we construct a family of measures for which (M3)-(M4) hold for
|x-y|
small, but (M) does not.

Let

\nu(dz)=\mathbf{1}_{Q}(z)|z|^{-(2+\sigma)}dz,\quad\mbox{with}\ Q=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x,y>0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

For any point on the unit sphere
\mathrm{S}
,
x=e^{i\theta_{x}}
with
\theta_{x}\in(-\pi,\pi]
consider the rotation
R_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by
R_{x}(z)=e^{i\sqrt{|\theta_{x}|}}z
.
Define the family of Lévy measures indexed with respect to points on the unit sphere,
\left(\nu_{x}\right)_{x\in S}
given by
\nu_{x}=\left(R_{x}\right)_{\#}\nu
. Note that

\nu_{x}(dz)=\mathbf{1}_{Q_{x}}(z)|z|^{-(2+\sigma)}dz,\quad\mbox{with}\ Q_{x}=\{R_{x}(z):z\in Q\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

In order to check
(M4)
and evaluate the Wasserstein distance, we construct

\gamma_{x,y}:=(R_{x}\times R_{y})_{\#}\nu^{r}
which is an admissible coupling in
\textrm{Adm}_{B_{r}}(\nu_{x}^{r},\nu_{y}^{r}).

Indeed, for
A\subseteq B_{r}\setminus\{0\}
, we see that

\gamma_{x,y}(B_{r}\times A)=\nu^{r}((R_{x}\times R_{y})^{-1}(B_{r}\times A))=\nu^{r}(R_{x}^{-1}(B_{r})\cap R_{y}^{-1}A)=\nu^{r}(R_{y}^{-1}A)=(R_{y})_{\#}\nu(A)=\nu_{y}^{r}(A)

and similarly
\gamma_{x,y}(A\times B_{r})=\nu_{x}^{r}(A)
. Hence, we have, for
|x-y|
small,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})(B_{r})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}\times B_{r}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}d\gamma_{x,y}(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{B_{r}}|R_{x}(z)-R_{y}(z)|^{2}d\nu(z)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2(1-\cos\left(|\theta_{x}|^{1/2}-|\theta_{y}|^{1/2}\right)\int_{Q\cap B_{r}}|z|^{-\sigma}dz

\displaystyle\lessapprox

\displaystyle Cr^{2-\sigma}\left||\theta_{x}|^{1/2}-|\theta_{y}|^{1/2}\right|^{2}\leq Cr^{2-\sigma}\left||\theta_{x}|-|\theta_{y}|\right|,

where we have used that
1-\cos(\alpha)\sim\alpha^{2}/2
for
\alpha
small. On the other hand, we have that

|x-y|=\sqrt{2(1-\cos\left(|\theta_{x}|-|\theta_{y}|\right)}\approx\left||\theta_{x}|-|\theta_{y}|\right|.

We conclude the existence of a constant
C>0
such that

W_{2}(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})(B_{r})\leq Cr^{(2-\sigma)/2}|x-y|^{1/2}.

We now check the total variation condition (M3). Let
x,y\in S
and assume, without loss of generality,
that
\theta_{x}>\theta_{y}>0
. Then, it holds by similar arguments, that

|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(B_{R}\setminus B_{r})=\int_{(B_{R}\setminus B_{r})\cap(Q_{x}\Delta Q_{y})}|z|^{-(2+\sigma)}dz=2C\int_{r}^{R}\rho d\rho\int_{\sqrt{\theta}_{y}}^{\sqrt{\theta}_{x}}d\theta\lessapprox C\left(r^{-\sigma}-R^{-\sigma}\right)|x-y|^{1/2}.

On the other hand, we see that

\int_{B_{r}}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)=\int_{B_{r}\cap Q_{x}\Delta Q_{y}}|z|^{-\sigma}dz=\frac{r^{2-\sigma}}{2-\sigma}\left|\sqrt{\theta_{x}}-\sqrt{\theta}_{y}\right|\gtrapprox Cr^{2-\sigma}|x-y|^{1/2},

for some
C>0
small enough, but independent of
x
or
y
. Thus, we do not have the Lipschitz condition (with respect to
|x-y|
) on the second momentum of the total variation.

We finally make precise the assumptions on the Hamiltonian. We emphasize that the key assumption is a superlinear growth in the gradient variable - which will dominate all of the other terms in the equation.
Let
H:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy the following:

(H0)

H\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
and it satisfies

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm sup}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}|H(x,0)|<+\infty.

(H1)

There exists
m>1
and two moduli of continuity

\omega^{1}_{H},\omega^{2}_{H}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

such that, for all
x,y,p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
|q|\leq 1
,

H(y,p+q)-H(x,p)\leq\omega_{H}^{1}(|x-y|)(1+|p|^{m})+\omega_{H}^{2}(|q|)(1+|p|^{m-1}).

(H2)

There exists
m>1
, some constants
b_{m},b_{0}>0
,
r_{0}>0

and
\mu_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for all
\mu\in[\mu_{0},1]

and
x,p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
|p|\geq r_{0}
,

\mu H(x,\mu^{-1}p)-H(x,p)\geq(1-\mu)(b_{m}|p|^{m}-b_{0}).

The first condition is typical in establishing the existence of solutions, by means of Perron’s method.
Assumption (H1) is a classical continuity condition on the data in this context, and (H2) expresses
the superlinear coercivity property of
H
. The constant
m\in\mathbb{R}
in assumptions (H1)-(H2) plays the role of the power of the gradient nonlinearity, and
m>1
means that we consider superlinear Hamiltonians. More precisely, we have the following estimate, whose proof can be found in
[
6
]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian satisfying assumptions (H0)-(H1).
Then, for every
R>0
,

\mathop{\rm sup}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},|p|\leq R}|H(x,p)|=:C_{R}<+\infty.

If, in addition, (H2) holds, then there exists a constant
C>0
(depending on the data from the assumptions) and
r_{0}>0
such that, for all
x,p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
|p|\geq r_{0}
,

H(x,p)\geq C|p|^{m}-\frac{1}{C}|p|.

2.4.
Main results

The main contribution of this paper is a comparison result between
discontinuous viscosity sub- and supersolutions
, for elliptic integro-differential equations coupled with a superlinear coercive Hamiltonian of the form (
1.1
). We state below the definitions for equation (
1.1
), used throughout the paper (see
[
10
]
, for several equivalent definitions).

Definition 3

(Viscosity solution)
.

(i)

A function
u\in USC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is
a
viscosity subsolution of (
1.1
) iff, for any test function

\phi\in C^{2}(B)\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, if
\overline{x}
is a local maximum
of
u-\phi
in
B_{\delta}
, with
\delta>0
, then

\lambda u(\overline{x})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\phi)-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{x},D\phi(\overline{x}),u)+H(\overline{x},D\phi(\overline{x}))\leq 0.

(i)

A function
u\in LSC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is
a
viscosity supersolution of (
1.1
) iff, for any test function

\phi\in C^{2}(B)\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, if
\overline{x}
is a local minimum
of
u-\phi
in
B_{\delta}
, with
\delta>0
, then

\lambda u(\overline{x})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\phi)-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{x},D\phi(\overline{x}),u)+H(\overline{x},D\phi(\overline{x}))\geq 0.

(iii)

A function
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is
a
viscosity solution of (
1.1
) iff it is both a viscosity subsolution and a viscosity supersolution.

The main result of this paper is the following comparison principle.

Theorem 2.8

(Comparison Principle)
.

Let
\lambda>0
,
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a family of Lévy measures associated with the nonlocal operators
\left(\mathcal{I}_{\xi}(\cdot)\right)_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
satisfying assumptions (M1) - (M4), and let
H
be a Hamiltonian with superlinear growth, satisfying assumptions (H1)-(H2).

Then, the comparison principle holds: if
u\in USC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a viscosity subsolution of (
1.1
) and
v\in LSC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a viscosity supersolution of (
1.1
),
then
u\leq v\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Following Perron’s method introduced for viscosity solutions by Ishii in
[
25
]
, one can establish the existence of discontinuous solutions for problem (
1.1
), where integro-differential terms are linearly coupled with coercive Hamiltonians. However, in order to start Perron’s iteration it is necessary to assume the boundedness assumption (H0). This, coupled with the comparison result above leads to the following well-possedness result for problem (
1.1
).

Theorem 2.9

(Well-possedness)
.

Let
\lambda>0
,
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a family of Lévy measures satisfying assumptions (M1) - (M4), and
H
be a Hamiltonian with superlinear growth, satisfying assumptions (H0)-(H2). Then, there exists a bounded continuous viscosity solution of (
1.1
).

3.
Proof of the comparison principle (Theorem
2.8
)

The proof is divided into several technical lemmas, which correspond to the main steps. It relies, as usual, on the doubling of variables technique, and fine estimates of the terms coming from the viscosity inequalities. We focus on the most difficult and new part, which concerns fine estimates on the nonlocal terms.

Let
u\in USC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a bounded viscosity subsolution of (
1.1
) and
v\in LSC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a bounded viscosity supersolution of (
1.1
). In order to show that
u\leq v
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we argue by contraction and assume that

M:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(u(x)-v(x)\big)>0.

In order to enhance the superlinear growth of the Hamiltonian, given by assumption (H2), we multiply the subsolution
u
by a scaling parameter
\mu\in(0,1)
and define, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\overline{u}(x)=\mu u(x)
. Then, for
\mu
sufficiently close to
1
, we will have that

\overline{M}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(\overline{u}(x)-v(x)\big)>0.

To ensure that a close supremum is to be attained, it is necessary to add a localization function
\psi_{\beta}
, defined as in Lemma
A.1
, with a proper choice of constant
c>0
, so that, for
\beta
sufficiently small,

M_{\beta}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\overline{u}(x)-v(x)-\psi_{\beta}(x)\right)>0.

We finally double the variables and take
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small in order that

M_{\varepsilon,\beta}:=\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\overline{u}(x)-v(y)-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\psi_{\beta}(y)\right)>0.

In the sequel, we denote the function over which the supremum is taken by

\phi_{\varepsilon,\beta}(x,y):=\overline{u}(x)-v(y)-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\psi_{\beta}(y).

Several technical and useful results about the behaviour of the maxima points and maxima values used throughout the proof, are summed up in Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under the notations above, assume
M>0
. Then the following assertions hold.

(i)

For any
\mu\in(0,1)
, the function
\overline{u}=\mu u\in USC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is a bounded viscosity subsolution of the following partial integro-differential equation

\lambda\overline{u}-\mathcal{I}_{x}\overline{u}(x)+\mu H(x,\mu^{-1}D\overline{u})=0\quad\mbox{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(ii)

Let
c=2(||u||_{\infty}+||v||_{\infty})
in equation (
A.1
)
defining the localization function
\psi
.
If
\mu\in(0,1)
is sufficiently close to
1
, and
\beta\in(0,1)
is small enough, then

M_{\varepsilon,\beta}\geq M_{\beta}\geq\frac{\overline{M}}{2}\geq\frac{M}{4}>0.

(iii)

The supremum
M_{\varepsilon,\beta}
is achieved at some

(x_{\varepsilon,\beta},y_{\varepsilon,\beta})\in{\overline{B}_{C\varepsilon+2/\beta}\times\overline{B}_{2/\beta}}

with
C>0
a constant depending only on
||u||_{\infty}
and
||v||_{\infty}
.

(iv)

For fixed
\beta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\frac{|x_{\varepsilon,\beta}-y_{\varepsilon,\beta}|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}=o_{\varepsilon}^{\beta}(1),

and uniformly in
\beta>0

\displaystyle|{x}_{\varepsilon,\beta}-y_{\varepsilon,\beta}|=o_{\varepsilon}(1).

(v)

For fixed
\beta
,
M_{\varepsilon,\beta}\rightarrow M_{\beta}
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, and in turn,

M_{\beta}\rightarrow\overline{M}
as
\beta\searrow 0
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

(i) In view of assumption (H2), the proof is immediate and identical to the local case,
due to the fact that the nonlocal term is linear with respect to
u
.

(ii) Note that
M\leq||u||_{\infty}+||v||_{\infty}
, and

\overline{u}(x)-v(x)=(u-v)(x)+(\mu-1)u(x)\geq u(x)-v(x)-(1-\mu)||u||_{\infty}.

Thus, taking supremum over all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it follows that, for
\mu
sufficiently close to
1
,

\overline{M}\geq M-(1-\mu)|u|_{\infty}\geq\frac{M}{2}.

The localization function
\psi_{\beta}
, defined as in Lemma
A.1

with
c=2(||u||_{\infty}+||v||_{\infty})
, satisfies

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta}(x)>||u||_{\infty}+||v||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\text{ when }|x|\geq 2/\beta,

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta}(x)=0

\displaystyle\text{ when }|x|\leq 1/\beta.

Let
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be such that
\overline{u}(\hat{x})-v(\hat{x})\geq\overline{M}/2
.
Then, for
\beta
sufficiently small,
\psi_{\beta}(\hat{x})=0
and

\overline{M}\geq M_{\beta}\geq\overline{u}(\hat{x})-v(\hat{x})\geq\frac{\overline{M}}{2}.

The supremum
M_{\varepsilon,\beta}
, satisfies, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

M_{\varepsilon,\beta}\geq\phi_{\varepsilon,\beta}(x,x)=\overline{u}(x)-v(x)-\psi_{\beta}(x),

which implies, taking the supremum over all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
that

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon,\beta}\geq M_{\beta}\geq\frac{\overline{M}}{2}.

(iii) Note first that, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
|y|\geq 2/\beta
, we have

\psi_{\beta}(y)>||u||_{\infty}+||v||_{\infty}
, and hence

\phi_{\varepsilon,\beta}(x,y)=\overline{u}(x)-v(y)-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\psi_{\beta}(y)\leq||u||_{\infty}+||v||_{\infty}-\psi_{\beta}(y)<0.

Therefore the supremum is taken over
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\overline{B}_{2/\beta}
.
On the other hand, by the positivity of
M_{\varepsilon,\beta}
, it follows that,
for any sequence
(x^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta},y^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\overline{B}_{2/\beta}

for which the sequence of values is converging to the maxima, i.e.

\phi_{\varepsilon,\beta}(x^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta},y^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta})\to M_{\varepsilon,\beta}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
we have

\frac{|x^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta}-y^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta}|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\leq\overline{u}(x^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta})-v(y^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta})-\psi_{\beta}(y^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta})\leq||u||_{\infty}+||v||_{\infty}=:C^{2}.

This further implies that the sequence is bounded

|x^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta}|\ \leq|x^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta}-y^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta}|+|y^{n}_{\varepsilon,\beta}|\leq C\varepsilon+2/\beta,

and hence there exists a subsequence converging to a point

(x_{\varepsilon,\beta},y_{\varepsilon,\beta})\in{\overline{B}_{C\varepsilon+2/\beta}\times\overline{B}_{2/\beta}}
,
as
n\to\infty
. In view of the upper semi-continuity of the function
\phi_{\varepsilon,\beta}
,
it follows that the supremum
M_{\varepsilon,\beta}
is attained at
(x_{\varepsilon,\beta},y_{\varepsilon,\beta})
.

(iv) It is immediate to see that, for fixed
\beta>0
, the sequence
M_{\varepsilon,\beta}
is monotone
with respect to
\varepsilon>0
. Indeed, if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
, then

\phi_{\varepsilon,\beta}(x,y)\leq\phi_{\varepsilon^{\prime},\beta}(x,y)
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which in turn implies

M_{\beta}\leq M_{\varepsilon,\beta}\leq M_{\varepsilon^{\prime},\beta}.

Therefore the sequence
\left(M_{\varepsilon,\beta}\right)_{\varepsilon}
is decreasing as
\varepsilon\searrow 0

and bounded from below, so there exists
\widetilde{M}_{\beta}\geq M_{\beta}
such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}M_{\varepsilon,\beta}=\widetilde{M}_{\beta}.

On the other hand, note that

(v) Note that, in view of (iii), the sequence

is bounded,
and, in view of point (iv), there exists
x_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that,
up to a subsequence,
{x}_{\varepsilon,\beta},y_{\varepsilon,\beta}\rightarrow x_{\beta}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Since
u\in USC(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
v\in LSC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\overline{u}(x_{\varepsilon,\beta})-v(y_{\varepsilon,\beta})\right)\leq\overline{u}(x_{\beta})-v(x_{\beta}).

This leads to

\displaystyle M_{\beta}\leq\widetilde{M}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}M_{\varepsilon,\beta}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\phi_{\varepsilon,\beta}({x}_{\varepsilon,\beta},y_{\varepsilon,\beta})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\overline{u}(x_{\varepsilon,\beta})-v(y_{\varepsilon,\beta})-\psi_{\beta}(y_{\varepsilon,\beta})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{u}(x_{\beta})-v(x_{\beta})-\psi_{\beta}(x_{\beta})\leq M_{\beta}.

In conclusion,
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}M_{\varepsilon,\beta}=M_{\beta}
.
In order to pass now
\beta\to 0
, recall that
M_{\beta}\leq\overline{M}
for every
\beta>0
.
From the definition of
\overline{M}
, it follows that, for any
\eta>0
, there exists
x_{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

\overline{u}(x_{\eta})-v(x_{\eta})>\overline{M}-\eta.

From the definition of
M_{\beta}
, we infer that

M_{\beta}\geq\overline{u}(x_{\eta})-v(x_{\eta})-\psi_{\beta}(x_{\eta})\geq\overline{M}-\eta-\psi_{\beta}(x_{\eta}).

Choosing
\beta
sufficiently small, we have

, so that

\psi_{\beta}(x_{\eta})=0
.
Therefore, we have

This implies precisely that
\lim_{\beta\rightarrow 0}M_{\beta}=\overline{M}
.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int\limits_{B_{\delta}\times A_{\rho,\delta}}|z_{2}|^{2}d\gamma_{(\overline{x},\overline{y})}(z_{1},z_{2})+\int\limits_{B_{\delta}\times A_{\rho,\delta}}\left(\psi_{\beta}(\overline{y}+z_{2})-\psi_{\beta}(\overline{y})+\overline{q}\cdot z_{2}\right)d\gamma_{(\overline{x},\overline{y})}(z_{1},z_{2})

Taking infimum among all admissible couplings, we are led to the estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\overline{x},\overline{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{5}{\varepsilon^{2}}\inf_{\gamma_{(\overline{x},\overline{y})}}\int\limits_{B_{\rho}\times B_{\rho}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}d\gamma_{(\overline{x},\overline{y})}(z_{1},z_{2})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\beta}(1)

Finally, when assumptions (M4) is employed, we obtain (

).
∎

Lemma 3.7

Proof of Lemma

.

, with small

.
The goal is to refine the above estimate by considering an intermediate radius

and control the nonlocal difference by the total variation distance of the Lévy measures on the exterior crown

– taking profit of the boundedness of

As such, the nonlocal difference on

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}[B\setminus B_{\rho}](\overline{x},\overline{y})

\displaystyle\left(2\|u\|_{\infty}+|\overline{p}|\right)\int_{B\setminus B_{\rho}}|\nu_{\overline{x}}-\nu_{\overline{y}}|(dz).

\displaystyle\int_{B\setminus B_{\rho}}\big(\overline{u}(\overline{x}+z)-\overline{u}(\overline{x})-(v(\overline{y}+z)-v(\overline{y}))+\overline{q}\cdot z\big)\nu_{\overline{y}}(dz)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}[B\setminus B_{\rho}](\overline{x},\overline{y})

\displaystyle\left(2\|u\|_{\infty}+|\overline{p}|\right)|\nu_{\overline{x}}-\nu_{\overline{y}}|(B\setminus B_{\rho})+o_{\beta}(1).

This, coupled with the estimate of the nonlocal difference on

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}[B\setminus B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\overline{y})

\displaystyle\left(2\|u\|_{\infty}+|\overline{p}|\right)\omega_{1,\rho}(|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|)+|\overline{p}|o_{\rho}(1)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\beta}(1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\omega_{1,\rho}(|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|)+o_{\rho}(1)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\beta}(1).

∎

Finally, it follows from the viscosity inequality (
3.2
),
and the estimates provided in (

), (
3.4
),
(
3.5
), (
3.7
), (
3.13
)
for the terms therein, that

\displaystyle\lambda\frac{M}{16}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\mu)b_{m}|\overline{p}|^{m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\omega_{1,\rho}(|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|)+o_{\rho}(1)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\varepsilon}(1)+o_{\beta}(1).

Let first
\delta\searrow 0

\displaystyle\lambda\frac{M}{16}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\mu)b_{m}|\overline{p}|^{m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\omega_{1,\rho}(|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|)+o_{\rho}(1)+o_{\varepsilon}(1)+o_{\beta}(1).

Fix now

4.
Extensions

The above comparison result can be extended to elliptic partial integro-differential equations with degenerate diffusion, as well as to parabolic problems where the time dependence only appears in the coefficients of the Hamiltonian. We discuss the two extensions below.

4.1.
Elliptic PIDEs with second order terms

Consider an elliptic integro–differential equation with possibly degenerate second–order diffusion, which takes the following form

\lambda u-F(x,Du,D^{2}u)-\mathcal{I}_{x}u(x)+H(x,Du)=0\quad\mbox{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N},

(4.1)

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times S^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is nondecreasing with respect to the matrix variable
X\in\mathbb{S}^{N}
.

For the second order case, we employ the definition of viscosity sub- and supersolution involving sub- and super-jets instead of the derivatives of the test function
(D\phi,D^{2}\phi)
. This is necessary when proving the comparison result to deal with the doubling of variables technique, resolved by the local Jensen-Ishii lemma (see
[
10
]
). For completeness, we recall the notions of semi-jets within the nonlocal framework.

Definition 4
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
v:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be respectively an upper–semicontinuous and a lower-semicontinuous function.

(i)

We call
the superjet of
u
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and we denote by
\mathcal{J}^{2,+}u(x)
the set

\mathcal{J}^{2,+}u(x)=\left\{(p,X)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{S}^{N};\;u(x+z)\leq u(x)+p\cdot z+\frac{1}{2}Xz\cdot z+o(|z|^{2})\right\}.

We say
(p,X)
is a limiting superjet of
u
at
x
if there exists a sequence of points
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a family of superjets
(p_{n},X_{n})\in\mathcal{J}^{2,+}u(x_{n})
such that
(x_{n},p_{n},X_{n})\to(x,p,X)
and
u(x_{n})\to u(x)
. We denote this closure by
\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,+}}u(x)
.

(ii)

We call
the subjet of
v
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and we denote by
\mathcal{J}^{2,+}v(x)
the set

\mathcal{J}^{2,-}v(x)=\left\{(p,X)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{S}^{N};\;v(x+z)\geq v(x)+p\cdot z+\frac{1}{2}Xz\cdot z+o(|z|^{2})\right\}.

We say
(p,X)
is a limiting subjet of
v
at
x
if there exists a sequence of points
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a family of subjets
(p_{n},X_{n})\in\mathcal{J}^{2,-}v(x_{n})
such that
(x_{n},p_{n},X_{n})\to(x,p,X)
and
v(x_{n})\to v(x)
. We denote this closure by
\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,-}}v(x)
.

We are now in place to state the definition of viscosity solutions for the second-order case PIDEs.

Definition 5

(Viscosity solution – second order nonlocal)
.

(i)

A function

is

) iff, for any test function

\overline{x}
is a global maximum of

on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
(p,X)\in\mathcal{J}^{2,+}u(\overline{x})
with

and

, then for any

,

\lambda u(\overline{x})-F(\overline{x},p,X)-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\phi)-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{x},p,u)+H(\overline{x},p)\leq 0.

(ii)

A function
u\in LSC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is
a
viscosity supersolution of (
4.1
) iff, for any test function
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, if
\overline{x}
is a global minimum of
u-\phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
(p,Y)\in\mathcal{J}^{2,-}u(\overline{x})
with

and

(iii)

A function

a

In order to overcome the difficulties imposed by the presence of the

dependent datum both in the second-order terms and in the nonlocal terms, we need a stronger assumption than (M4). Namely, we make a continuity assumption on the second-order moment for the total variation.

(M4)’

There exists

such that, for all

, the second moment for the total variation distance between Lévy measures satisfies, for all

\int_{B_{r}}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)\leq o_{r}(1)|x-y|.

In view of Proposition

In addition to the set of assumptions (M1)–(M4)’ on the family of Lévy measures, and (H1)-(H2) on the Hamiltonian, we shall assume the following on the local diffusion – see
[

(E)

For any
R>0

\omega^{R}_{F}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

, any

(4.3)

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume (M1) and (M4)’ hold.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\varphi\in C^{2}(B(x))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. If
(x_{n})_{n}
is a sequence of points in

converging to

as

(\varphi_{n})_{n}
is a family of functions in

converging in

to

\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](x_{n},\varphi_{n})=\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](x,\varphi)+o_{1/n}(1).

Proof.

In view of the regularity of the functions
\varphi_{n}
and
\varphi

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](x_{n},\varphi_{n})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](x,\varphi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\delta}}\big(\varphi_{n}(x_{n}+z)-\varphi_{n}(x_{n})-D\varphi_{n}(x_{n})\big)\nu_{x_{n}}(dz)

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{\delta}}\big(\varphi(x+z)-\varphi(x)-D\varphi(x)\big)\nu_{x}(dz)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{|z|\leq\delta}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(D^{2}\varphi_{n}(x_{n}+\theta z)-D^{2}\varphi(x+\theta z)\Big)z\cdot z\;d\theta\;\nu_{x_{n}}(dz)

From here, in view of assumptions (M1) and (M4)’, we obtain that, for any

, and for

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](x_{n},\varphi_{n})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](x,\varphi)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\varphi_{n}-\varphi|_{C^{2}(\overline{B_{2\delta}(x)})}\int_{B_{\delta}}|z|^{2}\nu_{x_{n}}(dz)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}|D^{2}\varphi|_{L^{\infty}(\overline{B(x)})}\int_{B_{\delta}}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x_{n}}-\nu_{x}|(dz)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}C_{\nu}|\varphi_{n}-\varphi|_{C^{2}(\overline{B_{2\delta}(x)})}+|D^{2}\varphi|_{L^{\infty}(B(x))}o_{\delta}(1)|x_{n}-x|,

Theorem 4.2

(Comparison Principle for Elliptic PIDEs with local diffusion)
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
F
be a diffusion nonlinearity satisfying the ellipticity assumption (E). Let
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a family of Lévy measures associated to the nonlocal operators
\left(\mathcal{I}_{\xi}(\cdot)\right)_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
satisfying assumptions (M1)–(M4)’, and let
H
be a Hamiltonian with superlinear growth, satisfying assumptions (H1)-(H2).

Then, the comparison principle holds: if
u\in USC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a viscosity subsolution of (
4.1
) and
v\in LSC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a viscosity supersolution of (
4.1
),
then
u\leq v\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Remark 3
.

The consideration of second-order terms requires the use of a nonlocal analog of Ishii-Jensen lemma. This is a key point, that we would like to discuss here briefly, providing references for the details that we omit.
The argument behind Ishii-Jensen lemma is the use use of inf- and sup-convolutions of
u
and
v
respectively, and then exploit the semiconvexity properties through Aleksandrov Theorem, see
[
18
]
. Following this, if
\varphi\in C^{2}
is a function for which
(x,y)\mapsto u(x)-v(y)-\varphi(x,y)
has a maximum point at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, as in Proposition 3 in
[
10
]
, we define

\displaystyle u^{\alpha}(x,p)

\displaystyle=\sup_{|x-z|\leq 1}\Big\{u(z)+p(x-z)-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}|x-z|^{2}\Big\},

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(y,q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{|y-z|\leq 1}\Big\{v(z)-q(y-z)+\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}|y-z|^{2}\Big\},

where
p=D_{x}\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
,
q=-D_{y}\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
\alpha>0
. In order to use the viscosity inequalities for the convoluted functions, we need to consider the inf- and sup-convolution of the test function
\varphi
, which is already smooth. Then, in order to conclude Ishii-Jensen Lemma, we shall invoke Lemma
4.1
and for this, we need to prove that, if
\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then

\varphi^{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}\to\varphi\text{ in }C^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Though it seems to be part of the folklore of viscosity solutions and convolutions, we discuss it briefly for completeness.
In general, if
\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, taking
p=D\varphi(x)
and
\alpha
very small in terms of
\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B(x))}
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\varphi^{\alpha}(x,p)
is attained at a unique point

x+z^{\alpha}(x)
with
|z^{\alpha}(x)|\in B_{1/2}
. This immediately implies that
\varphi^{\alpha}
is
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and

D\varphi^{\alpha}(x)=D\varphi(x+z^{\alpha}(x)),

see the Introduction in
[
7
]
. This, together with the first-order criteria that allows us to find
z^{\alpha}(x)
and the Implicit Function Theorem, allows us to prove the map
x\mapsto z^{\alpha}(x)
is in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and from here that
\varphi^{\alpha}
is
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Moreover, since we know that
z^{\alpha}(x)\to x
as
\alpha\to 0
, from which we conclude that
\varphi^{\alpha}\to\varphi
in
C^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

We proceed as before and assume
M=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u-v)>0

\varepsilon,\beta>0
and
\mu\in(0,1)

M_{\varepsilon,\beta}=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{R}}\big(\overline{u}(x)-v(x)-\varphi(x,y)\big),

\varphi(x,y)=\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\psi_{\beta}(y).

We next employ the nonlocal Jensen-Ishii lemma
[
10

.

It follows that, for any
\delta>0

\tilde{\alpha}>0
such that, for

,
(p+q,Y_{\alpha})\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,-}}v(\overline{y})

and a family of functions (given by sup-convolutions)

\varphi^{\alpha}(x,y):=\sup_{|(x,y)-(x^{*},y^{*})|\leq 1}\left(\varphi(x^{*},y^{*})+p\cdot(x-x^{*})+(p+q)\cdot(y-y^{*})-\frac{1}{2\alpha}|(x,y)-(x^{*},y^{*})|^{2}\right)

satisfying

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{x}\varphi^{\alpha}(\overline{x},\overline{y})=D_{x}\varphi(\overline{x},\overline{y})=\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\overline{x}-\overline{y})

such that the following viscosity inequalities hold

\displaystyle 0,

Subtracting the two inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda(\overline{u}(\overline{x})-v(\overline{y}))

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle F(\overline{y},p+q,Y_{\alpha})-F(\overline{x},p,X_{\alpha})+\mu H(\overline{x},\mu^{-1}\overline{p})-H(\overline{y},\overline{p}+\overline{q})

(4.4)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\varphi^{\alpha}(\cdot,{\overline{y}}))-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{y},-\varphi^{\alpha}({\overline{x}},\cdot))+\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{x},\overline{p},\overline{u})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{y},\overline{p}+\overline{q},v).

From assumption (E), for

F(\overline{y},p+q,Y_{\alpha})-F(\overline{x},p,X_{\alpha})\geq-\omega_{F}^{R}(|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|^{2})+o_{\beta}(1)+o_{\alpha}(1).

\varphi^{\alpha}\to\varphi

(4.6)

\displaystyle\lambda(\overline{u}(\overline{x})-v(\overline{y}))

\displaystyle-

4.2.
Parabolic PIDEs

We now discuss the case of time-dependent problems involving integro-differential operators and superlinear and coercive Hamiltonians, of the form

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(x,t)-\mathcal{I}_{x}u(x,t)+H(x,t,Du(x,t))=0&\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.\end{cases}

(4.7)

We consider a nonlocal operator of Lévy type, with the Lévy measures
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
acting on the space-variable

\mathcal{I}_{\xi}u(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(u(x+z,t)-u(x,t)-\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)Du(x,t)\cdot z\big)\nu_{\xi}(dz).

The case of time dependent Lévy measures
(\nu_{(\xi,t)})_{(\xi,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T]}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T))
could be partially addressed in this framework, by accordingly adapting the assumptions with t-dependence. However, to ensure completeness of this framework, we prefer to address this problem in a future work.

We assume the family of measures satisfies exactly the same set of assumptions as in the stationary case and, since we allow a time-dependent in the Hamiltonian, we upgrade to assumptions on
H
to the case of space-time coefficients.

(H1-t)

H:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T]
and
there exists
m>1
and two moduli of continuity

\omega^{1}_{H},\omega^{2}_{H}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

such that, for all
t\in[0,T]
, and
x,y,p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
|q|\leq 1
,

H(y,t,p+q)-H(x,t,p)\leq\omega_{H}^{1}(|x-y|)(1+|p|^{m})+\omega_{H}^{2}(|q|)(1+|p|^{m-1}).

(H2-t)

There exists
m>1
, some constants
b_{m},b_{0}>0
,
r_{0}>0

and
\mu_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for all
\mu\in[\mu_{0},1]
,
t\in[0,T]

and
x,p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
|p|\geq r_{0}
,

\mu H(x,t,\mu^{-1}p)-H(x,t,p)\geq(1-\mu)(b_{m}|p|^{m}-b_{0}).

Theorem 4.3

(Comparison principle for time-dependent problems)
.

Let
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a family of Lévy measures associated with the nonlocal operators
\left(\mathcal{I}_{\xi}(\cdot)\right)_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
satisfying assumptions (M1)–(M4), and let
H
be a Hamiltonian satisfying assumptions
(H1-t)
-
(H2-t)
.

Then, the parabolic comparison principle holds: if
u\in USC(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T])\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T])
is a viscosity subsolution of (
4.7
) and
v\in LSC(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T])\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T])
is a viscosity supersolution of (
4.7
), so that

u(\cdot,0)\leq u_{0}\leq v(\cdot,0)\;\text{ in }\;\mathbb{R}^{N},

with
u_{0}\in BUC(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
then

u\leq v\;\text{ in }\;\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T].

Remark 4
.

The notions of viscosity sub and supersolutions are defined in a similar manner to the stationary case. We require the sub- and supersolution to satisfy the equation in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T)
only, and not at the final time
T
. However, it can be checked, similarly to the first order case, that the notions of subsolutions and supersolutions can be considered to the extended set
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T]
.

Proof.

The key difference with the proof from the stationary case is that we need to double the variables both in space in time. In addition to this, we need to subtract a linear term in time, in order to overcome the absence of
u
in the equation (replaced by the time derivative). Hence, we assume again that

\displaystyle M=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T]}(u-v)>0

and note that, for
\varepsilon,\eta,\lambda,\beta>0
sufficiently small, and
\mu
close to
1
, we have that

\overline{M}:=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}\times[0,T]^{2}}\left(\overline{u}(x,t)-v(y,s)-\phi(x,y,t,s)\right)>0,

with

\varphi(x,y,t,s)=\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{(t-s)^{2}}{\eta^{2}}+\psi_{\beta}(y)+\lambda t.

The maxima will be attained at some
(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{s})
with
\overline{t},\overline{s}>0
, and as
\eta\to 0
, they will converge to some
(\hat{x},\hat{y},t^{*})
and correspondingly we will have
\overline{p}\to\hat{p}
, and
\overline{q}\to\hat{q}
.
With the same notations as in the main comparison proof, we are led to the following viscosity inequalities at the maxima points
(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{s})
:

\displaystyle\lambda+\frac{2(\overline{t}-\overline{s})}{\eta^{2}}-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\overline{t},\varphi_{\overline{y},\overline{s}})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{x},\overline{t},\overline{p},\overline{u})+\mu H(\overline{x},\overline{t},\mu^{-1}\overline{p})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\frac{2(\overline{t}-\overline{s})}{\eta^{2}}-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{y},\overline{s},\varphi_{\overline{x},\overline{t}})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{y},\overline{s},\overline{p}+\overline{q},v)+H(\overline{y},\overline{s},\overline{p}+\overline{q})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0.

Subtracting the two inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mu H(\overline{x},\overline{t},\mu^{-1}\overline{p})-H(\overline{y},\overline{s},\overline{p}+\overline{q})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\overline{t},\varphi_{\overline{y},\overline{s}})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\overline{y},\overline{s},\varphi_{\overline{x},\overline{t}})+\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{x},\overline{t},\overline{p},\overline{u})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\overline{y},\overline{s},\overline{p}+\overline{q},v).

Sending
\eta\to 0
, we have, in view of the continuity of
H
, that

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mu H(\hat{x},t^{*},\mu^{-1}\hat{p})-H(\hat{y},t^{*},\hat{p}+\hat{q})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\hat{x},t^{*},\varphi_{\hat{y},t^{*}})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\hat{y},t^{*},\varphi_{\hat{x},t^{*}})+\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\hat{x},t^{*},\hat{p},\overline{u})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\hat{y},t^{*},\hat{p}+\hat{q},v).

In view of (H1-t) and (H2-t) the estimate obtained in Lemma
3.2
still holds. The above inequality now takes a similar form to the one in the stationary case

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1-\mu)b_{m}|\hat{p}|^{m}-o_{\beta}(1)-o_{\varepsilon}(1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\hat{x},t^{*},\varphi_{\hat{y},t^{*}})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}](\hat{y},t^{*},\varphi_{\hat{x},t^{*}})+\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\hat{x},t^{*},\hat{p},\overline{u})-\mathcal{I}[B_{\delta}^{c}](\hat{y},t^{*},\hat{p}+\hat{q},v).

From here on, we can employ all the nonlocal estimates provided in the previous section and reach the conclusion.
∎

4.3.
Guillen-Mou-Świȩch case

In
[
22
]
the authors introduced optimal transport techniques to obtain comparison results for nonlocal equations with, a priori, no restriction on the order of the operators. They impose a Lipschitz condition with respect to the
p
–Wasserstein metric, i.e.,

(M4)”

There exists
p\in[1,2]
and
C_{p}>0
such that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

W_{p}(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})(B)\leq C_{p}|x-y|.

The exponent
p\in[1,2]
is related to the singularity at
z=0
for the Lévy measures: for a nonlocal operator of order
\sigma
, one has
p>\sigma
.
However, it is rather difficult to construct measures for which (M4)” holds in the case
p>1
,
which in fact limits the use of such a hypothesis.
For example, in the case of measures with density

\nu_{\xi}(z)=K(\xi,z)dz,

it has only been shown that condition (M4)” is satisfied when
p=1
, restricting the nonlocal operator to order
\sigma\in(0,1)
, see
[
22
, Corollary 4.5]
. As a matter of fact, it is natural to expect
W_{p}
to be only
1/p-
Hölder continuous, provided the kernel
K
is Lipschitz in
\xi
. See
[
22
, Example 5.12]
and Section §
5.1
for details. In addition, the authors show that the comparison principle still holds if the Wasserstein distance is
1/2-
Hölder continuous, provided the subsolution or the supersolution is
C^{1}
.
Within our approach, we establish the comparison principle for
1/2-
Hölder continuous
p
-Wasserstein distances, without having to assume a priori regularity on either the sub/supersolution.

Theorem 4.4

(Comparison Principle –
W_{p}
)
.

Let
\lambda>0
,
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a family of Lévy measures associated with the nonlocal operators
\left(\mathcal{I}_{\xi}(\cdot)\right)_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
satisfying assumptions (M1), (M2), (M3) with
r=1
and (M4)” with
p>1
, and let the Hamiltonian
H
satisfy assumptions (H1)-(H2).

Then, the comparison principle holds: if
u\in USC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a viscosity subsolution of (
1.1
) and
v\in LSC(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a viscosity supersolution of (
1.1
),
then
u\leq v\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proof.

The proof is literally the same, at a sole modification in Lemma
3.5
.
More precisely, on
B\setminus B_{\delta}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[B\setminus B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\overline{p},\overline{u})-\mathcal{I}[B\setminus B_{\delta}](\overline{y},\overline{p}+\overline{q},v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{5}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B\times B}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\beta}(1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{5}{\varepsilon^{2}}2^{2-p}\int_{B\times B}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{p}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\beta}(1).

Taking infimum in
\gamma
and employing the new assumption (M4)”, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[B\setminus B_{\delta}](\overline{x},\overline{p},\overline{u})-\mathcal{I}[B\setminus B_{\delta}](\overline{y},\overline{p}+\overline{q},v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{5}{\varepsilon^{2}}2^{2-p}W_{p}^{p}(\nu_{\overline{x}},\nu_{\overline{y}})(B)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\beta}(1)

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{p}\frac{|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|^{p}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\beta}(1).

Moreover, we still have the boundedness of
\bar{p}
as in Lemma
3.6
. Indeed, it follows from the viscosity inequality (
3.2
), and the estimates provided in (
3.3
), (
3.4
), (
3.5
), (
3.7
), (
4.8
), that

\displaystyle\lambda\frac{M}{16}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\mu)b_{m}|\overline{p}|^{m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{p}|\overline{p}|\;|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|^{p-1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\varepsilon}(1)+o_{\beta}(1).

Using that
|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|=o_{\varepsilon}(1)
and
p\geq 1
, the conclusion of Lemma
3.6
follows.

We then reiterate the estimate above, and use that
\bar{p}
is bounded, to obtain

\displaystyle\lambda\frac{M}{16}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C|\overline{x}-\overline{y}|^{p-1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}o_{\delta}^{\overline{x},\overline{y}}(1)+o_{\varepsilon}(1)+o_{\beta}(1).

Letting
\delta\to 0
, then
\varepsilon,\beta\to 0
, and recalling that

, we arrive again at a contradiction.
∎

5.
Examples

In this section we give examples of nonlocal operators and PIDEs that fit into our framework, and for which comparison results hold.
We first detail different forms that Lévy operators can take in order to satisfy assumptions (M1)–(M4). We then present a series of the PIDEs involving these operators for which we have uniqueness (and existence) results.

5.1.
Measures with density

Let
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
be a family of Lévy measures with density kernels:

\nu_{x}(dz)=K(x,z)dz,

where

K:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a Carathéodory function. The nonlocal Lévy operator then writes

\mathcal{I}_{\xi}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(u(x+z)-u(x)-\boldsymbol{1}_{B}(z)Du(x)\cdot z\right)K(\xi,z)dz.

We assume the kernel is degenerate elliptic of order
\sigma
, and Lipschitz continuous with respect to
\xi
. More precisely, let
K
satisfy the following:

(K1)

(Degenerate ellipticity)
There exist
\Lambda>0
and
\sigma\in(0,2)
such that, for all
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

0\leq K(x,z)\leq\frac{\Lambda}{|z|^{N+\sigma}}.

(K2)

(Uniform Regularity)
There exist
C_{K}>0
and
\sigma\in(0,2)
such that, for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

|K(x,z)-K(y,z)|\leq C_{K}\frac{|x-y|}{|z|^{N+\sigma}}.

In particular, if
K(x,z)=C(N,\sigma)k(x)|z|^{-(N+\sigma)}
,
with
k
a Lipschitz continuous positive function, and
C(N,\sigma)
the constant appearing in (K2), then

\mathcal{I}_{x}u(x)=k(x)\left((-\Delta)^{\sigma/2}u\right)(x)

is the
k-
weighted fractional Laplacian of order
\sigma\in(0,2)
.

It is straightforward to check, in view of assumption (K1), that the family
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi}
satisfies the uniform Lévy condition (M1), as well as the uniform decay at infinity condition (M2).

(M1)

The constant bounding uniformly the integrals is expressed in terms of
C(N,\sigma)
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min(1,|z|^{2})\nu_{\xi}(dz)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min(1,|z|^{2})\frac{\Lambda}{|z|^{N+\sigma}}dz\;=\;\Lambda{\rm vol}(\partial B)\left(\frac{1}{2-\sigma}+\frac{1}{\sigma}\right).

(M2)

Similarly, the uniform decay at infinity is related to that of the fractional Laplacian:

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\nu_{\xi}(dz)=\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}K(\xi,z)dz\leq\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\frac{\Lambda}{|z|^{N+\sigma}}dz\;=\;\frac{\Lambda}{\sigma}{\rm vol}(\partial B)R^{-\sigma}=\Lambda o_{1/R}(1).

We show below that, in view of condition (K2), assumptions (M3) and (M4) are satisfied.

(M3)

Let
0<r<R
. Then, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\displaystyle|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|\left(B_{R}\setminus B_{r}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}|K(x,z)-K(y,z)|dz\;\leq\;C_{K}|x-y|\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{C_{K}}{\sigma}{\rm vol}(\partial B)\big(r^{-\sigma}-R^{-\sigma}\big)|x-y|=\omega_{R,r}(|x-y|).

(M4)

Let
0<r<1
. For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we estimate the Wasserstein distance
between Lévy measures
\nu_{x}
and
\nu_{y}
.
Using Proposition
2.5
and (K2), for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})(B_{r})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int\limits_{B_{r}}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)=\int_{B_{r}}|z|^{2}|K(x,z)-K(y,z)|dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{K}|x-y|\int_{B_{r}}\frac{|z|^{2}}{|z|^{N+\sigma}}dz=\frac{C_{K}}{2-\sigma}{\rm vol}(\partial B)r^{2-\sigma}|x-y|=o_{r}(1)|x-y|.

5.2.
Nonlocal operators of variable order

Let
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
be a family of Lévy measures with density kernels
(K(\xi,\cdot))_{\xi}

of variable order, given by

K(\xi,z)=\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma(\xi)}},

where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow(0,2)
is a function satisfying:

(S1)

(Uniform ellipticity)
There exist
0<\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}<2
such that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma(x)\leq\sigma_{2}.

(S2)

(Lipschitz Regularity)
There exists
C_{\sigma}>0
such that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|\leq C_{\sigma}|x-y|.

The nonlocal Lévy operator then writes

\mathcal{I}_{\xi}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[u(x+z)-u(x)-\boldsymbol{1}_{B}(z)Du(x)\cdot z\right]\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma(\xi)}}dz.

Note that the kernel satisfies, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\min\left(\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{1}}},\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{2}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma(\xi)}}\leq\max\left(\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{1}}},\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{2}}}\right).

In view of this estimate, we can easily check that assumptions (M1) and (M2) are satisfied.

(M1)

The constant bounding uniformly the integrals is expressed in terms of
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min(1,|z|^{2})\nu_{\xi}(dz)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B}|z|^{2}\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma(\xi)}}dz+\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B^{c}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma(\xi)}}dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B}|z|^{2}\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{2}}}dz+\int_{B^{c}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{1}}}dz={\rm vol}(\partial B)\left(\frac{1}{2-\sigma_{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}}\right).

(M2)

Similarly, the uniform decay at infinity can be expressed in terms of a function
associated with the fractional Laplacian of order
\sigma_{1}
. Indeed, for any
R>1
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\nu_{\xi}(dz)=\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma(\xi)}}dz\leq\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{1}}}dz=\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}}{\rm vol}(\partial B)R^{-\sigma}\;=\;o_{1/R}(1).

In order to show that (S2) implies (M3) and (M4), we rely on the following remark. Fix
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and without the loss of generality, we assume
\sigma(x)<\sigma(y)
. Then, by the mean value theorem applied to the real function
t\mapsto|z|^{t}
, it follows that there exists

\xi=\xi(x,y)\in(\sigma(x),\sigma(y))\subset(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
such that

\displaystyle\left||z|^{\sigma(x)}-|z|^{\sigma(y)}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|z|^{\xi}\ln(|z|)\left|\sigma(y)-\sigma(x)\right|\leq C_{\sigma}|z|^{\xi}|\ln(|z|)||x-y|.

(5.1)

This is sufficient to get the estimates required by (M3) and (M4), as follows.

(M3)

Let
0<r<1<R
. Then, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|\left(B_{R}\setminus B_{r}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}\left|\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma(x)}}-\frac{1}{|z|^{N+\sigma(y)}}\right|dz=\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{\left||z|^{\sigma(x)}-|z|^{\sigma(y)}\right|}{|z|^{N+\sigma(x)+\sigma(y)}}dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}|x-y|\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{|z|^{\xi}\big|\ln(|z|)\big|}{|z|^{N+\sigma(x)+\sigma(y)}}dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}|x-y|\left(\int_{B_{R}\setminus B}\frac{\big|\ln(|z|)\big|}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{1}}}dz+\int_{B\setminus B_{r}}\frac{\big|\ln(|z|)\big|}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{2}}}dz\right).

We now evaluate the last integral terms in the sum and get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\setminus B}\frac{\big|\ln(|z|)\big|}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{1}}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm vol}(\partial B)\int_{1}^{R}\frac{\ln(s)}{s^{1+\sigma_{1}}}ds={\rm vol}(\partial B)\left(-\dfrac{R^{-\sigma_{1}}}{\sigma_{1}}\left(\ln(R)+\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{1}}\right)+\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\int_{B\setminus B_{r}}\frac{\big|\ln(|z|)\big|}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{2}}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm vol}(\partial B)\int_{r}^{1}\frac{-\ln(s)}{s^{1+\sigma_{1}}}ds={\rm vol}(\partial B)\left(-\dfrac{r^{-\sigma_{2}}}{\sigma_{2}}\left(\ln(r)+\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{2}}\right)+\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right).

Summing up we obtain a Lipschitz bound for the total variation between the two Lévy measures, on the circular crown
B_{R}\setminus B_{r}
.

(M4)

Let
0<r<1
. In view of Proposition
2.5

and of inequality (
5.1
), for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it holds

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})(B_{r})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int\limits_{B_{r}}|z|^{2}|\nu_{x}-\nu_{y}|(dz)=\int_{B_{r}}\frac{|z|^{2}\left||z|^{\sigma(x)}-|z|^{\sigma(y)}\right|}{|z|^{N+\sigma(x)+\sigma(y)}}dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}|x-y|\int_{B_{r}}\frac{|z|^{2+\xi}\big|\ln(|z|)\big|}{|z|^{N+\sigma(x)+\sigma(y)}}dz\leq C_{\sigma}|x-y|\int_{B_{r}}\frac{\big|\ln(|z|)\big|}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{2}-2}}dz=:\omega(r)|x-y|,

where
\omega:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is given by

\displaystyle\omega(r)=C_{\sigma}\int_{B_{r}}\frac{\big|\ln(|z|)\big|}{|z|^{N+\sigma_{2}-2}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}{\rm vol}(\partial B)\dfrac{r^{2-\sigma_{2}}}{2-\sigma_{2}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2-\sigma_{2}}-\ln(r)\right).

5.3.
Lévy-Itô operators

We focus next on Lévy-Itô operators, which take the form

\mathcal{J}_{\xi}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(u(x+{j(\xi,z)})-u(x)-\boldsymbol{1}_{B}(z)Du(x)\cdot{j(\xi,z)}\right)\nu(dz),

(5.2)

where
\nu
is a Lévy measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and the jump function
j:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies the following set of assumptions:

(J1)

For any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the jump function at
\xi
, given by
j(\xi,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, is invertible.

(J2)

There exist
0<c_{0}\leq c_{1}
such that, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

c_{0}|z|\leq|j(\xi,z)|\leq c_{1}|z|.

(J3)

For any
r>0
, there exists a modulus of continuity

\omega_{r}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
and for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}
,

|j(x,z)-j(y,z)|\leq\omega_{r}(|x-y|).

(J4)

There exists
r_{0}>0
such that, for all
r\in(0,r_{0})
, and for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\int_{B_{r}}|j(x,z)-j(y,z)|^{2}\nu(dz)\leq o_{r}(1)|x-y|.

These operators naturally appear as infinitesimal generators of non-symmetric stochastic jump processes, see
[
30
]
. Comparison principles for nonlocal Lévy operators have been first established in Lévy-Itô form, by Barles and Imbert in
[
10
]
(see also
[
27
]
). In their paper, no invertibility assumption is required, due to the fact that comparison principles are proved directly for nonlocal operators in Lévy-Itô form, and the proof takes into account their special structure. In our case, the passage from (
5.2
) to the above operator requires, at this level, the invertibility of the jump function. We prove that under assumptions (J1)-(J4), Lévy-Itô operators can be reformulated as Lévy operators, for which the comparison result stated in Theorem
2.8
holds.

The results of Barles and Imbert in
[
10
]
are more general than the ones we present here.
In particular, there is no constraint with respect to the invertibibility of the jump function.
The interest of our approach is that it gives a comparison result for nonlocal operators with a slightly less general assumption (J4) than the corresponding part in
[
10
]
.

5.3.1.
Lévy-Itô operators through the lens of general Lévy operators

We start by making precise how Lévy-Itô operators can be written in the general Lévy form, through an associated
push-forward measure
. To this end, consider, for any point
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the jump function at
\xi
, given by
j_{\xi}:=j(\xi,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
and let the push forward measure of the Lévy measure
\nu
through
{j_{\xi}}
be given by

\nu_{\xi}^{j}:=({j_{\xi}})_{\#}\nu.

The above measure can also be defined by duality, through integration against measurable functions, as follows. For each measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(z)\leq C\min(1,|z|^{2})
, let

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(z)\nu_{\xi}^{j}(dz)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f({j_{\xi}(z)})\nu(dz).

In view of (J2), the measure
\nu_{\xi}^{j}
is well defined and it satisfies the Lévy condition.

In view of assumption
(J1)
, Lévy-Itô operators given by (
5.2
) can be re-written, through a change of variables, as

\mathcal{J}_{\xi}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(u(x+z)-u(x)-\mathbf{1}_{j_{\xi}(B)}(z)Du(x)\cdot z\big)\nu_{\xi}^{j}(dz).

The term
\mathbf{1}_{j_{x}(B)}(z)Du(x)\cdot z
may fail, a priori, to compensate the first-order finite difference of
u
at
x
if the origin is not an interior point of
{j_{\xi}}(B)
. This is, however, ensured by the uniform linear control given by assumption (J2), which ensures that
B_{rc_{0}}\subset{j_{\xi}}(B_{r})
, for all
r>0
. Therefore, we can write the nonlocal Lévy-Itô operator as

\mathcal{J}_{\xi}u(x)=\mathcal{I}_{\xi}u(x)+b^{j}(\xi)\cdot Du(x),

where
\mathcal{I}_{\xi}
is a nonlocal operator taking the Lévy form

\mathcal{I}_{\xi}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(u(x+z)-u(x)-\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)Du(x)\cdot z\big)\nu_{\xi}^{j}(dz),

and
b^{j}(\xi)
is the vector field given by

b^{j}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)-\mathbf{1}_{{j_{\xi}}(B)}(z)\right)\;z\;\nu_{\xi}^{j}(dz)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{j_{\xi}^{-1}(B)}(z)-\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)\right)\;j_{\xi}(z)\;\nu(dz).

We show below the vector field is well defined and bounded, and the family of measures
(\nu_{\xi}^{j})_{\xi}
satisfies assumptions (M1)-(M4).

Remark 5

(
Symmetric Lévy-Itô operators)
.

In the case of symmetric Lévy measure, i.e., when
\nu(-A)=\nu(A)
, for all measurable sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
assumption (J1) is not necessary anymore, as we assume instead the following:

(J1)’

For any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the jump function
{j_{\xi}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is symmetric, i.e.,
{j_{\xi}}(-z)={j_{\xi}(z)}
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Then, the Lévy-Itô operators can be written as

\mathcal{J}_{\xi}u(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(u(x+j(x,z))-u(x)\big)\nu(dz)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(u(x+z)-u(x)\big)\nu_{\xi}^{j}(dz).

It is easy to see that the operator is well-defined, and we shall check below that assumptions (M1)-(M4) are satisfied.

5.3.2.
Boundedness and regularity of the vector field
b^{j}(\cdot)

The extra drift term will be considered as part of the Hamiltonian, posing

\widetilde{H}(x,p)=H(x,p)+b^{j}(x)\cdot p.

Thus, running the comparison proof with
\widetilde{H}
instead of
H
, it requires for
\widetilde{H}
to satisfy the assumptions (H0)-(H2). The map
p\mapsto b^{j}(x)\cdot p
is linear, and therefore it will not affect the boundedness assumption (H0). However, the superlinear coercivity condition (H2) relies on the boundedness of
b^{j}_{\xi}
, whereas the regularity assumption (H1) requires a modulus of continuity for
b^{j}_{\xi}
. Note first that, in view of the invertibility assumption (J1) and of the uniform bounds (J2), we have, for any
r>0
, and for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

B_{rc_{0}}\subseteq j_{\xi}(B_{r})\subseteq B_{rc_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{r/c_{1}}\subseteq j_{\xi}^{-1}(B_{r})\subseteq B_{r/c_{0}}.

(5.3)

On one hand, in view of assumptions (J1) and (J2), it follows that the vector field is well-defined and bounded:

\displaystyle|b^{j}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\mathbf{1}_{j_{\xi}^{-1}B}(z)-\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)\right|\;|j_{\xi}(z)|\ \;\nu(dz)\;\leq\;c_{1}\int_{B\Delta j_{\xi}^{-1}(B)}|z|\nu(dz)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}\max(1,\frac{1}{c_{0}})\int_{B\Delta j_{\xi}^{-1}(B)}\nu(dz)\;\leq\;c_{1}\max(1,\frac{1}{c_{0}})\int_{B_{\max\{c_{0}^{-1},1\}}\setminus B_{\min\{c_{1}^{-1},1\}}}\nu(dz)=:C_{c_{0},c_{1},\nu},

with
C_{c_{0},c_{1},\nu}
a constant depending only on
c_{0},c_{1}
and
C_{\nu}
.

On the other hand, we obtain a modulus of continuity for the vector field. Taking into account the ball inclusion (
5.3
), there exists
r_{0}\leq\min(1,c_{0})
and
r_{1}=\min(1,r_{0}/c_{1})
such that, uniformly in
\xi

B\setminus j_{\xi}(B)=(B\setminus B_{r_{0}})\ \setminus j_{\xi}(B)=(B\setminus B_{r_{0}})\setminus j_{\xi}(B\setminus B_{r_{0}}).

Hence, the following estimate holds

\displaystyle|b^{j}(x)-b^{j}(y)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B\setminus j_{x}(B)}z\nu_{x}^{j}(dz)-\int_{B\setminus j_{y}(B)}z\nu_{y}^{j}(dz)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B\setminus B_{r_{0}}}z\left(\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}\right)(dz)+\int_{B\setminus B_{r_{1}}}\left(j(x,z)-j(y,z)\right)\nu(dz)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B\setminus B_{r_{0}}}|z||\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}|(dz)+\int_{B\setminus B_{r_{1}}}\left|j(x,z)-j(y,z)\right|\nu(dz)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r_{0}|\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}|(B\setminus B_{r_{0}})+\int_{B\setminus B_{r_{1}}}\left|j(x,z)-j(y,z)\right|\nu(dz).

Similarly to the computations performed below for
(M3)
, it follows that

\displaystyle|b^{j}(x)-b^{j}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{\omega}_{1,r_{0}}(|x-y|)+\omega_{1,r_{1}}(|x-y|).

5.3.3.
Assumptions (M1)-(M4) for the family of push-foward Lévy measures

We show below that the family of Lévy measures
(\nu^{j}_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
is well-defined, and that assumptions (M1)-(M4) are satisfied, provided (J1)-(J4) hold.

(M1)

It follows immediately from (J2) and the definition of the push forward measure that

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min(1,|z|^{2})\nu_{\xi}^{j}(dz)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min(1,|{j_{\xi}(z)}|^{2})\nu(dz)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}^{2}\int_{B_{1/c_{1}}}|z|^{2}\nu(dz)+\int_{B_{1/c_{1}}^{c}}\nu(dz)<\infty.

(M2)

Let
R>1
. It follows from the definition of the push forward measure, assumption (J2) and the definition of the Lévy measure, that

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\nu_{\xi}^{j}(dz)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{({j_{\xi}})^{-1}(B_{R}^{c})}\nu(dz)\leq\int_{B_{R/c_{1}}^{c}}\nu(dz)=o_{1/R}(1).

(M3)

Let
0<r<R
. Notice that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

|\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}|=\left(\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}\right)^{+}+\left(\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}\right)^{-}

and

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}\left(\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}\right)^{+}(dz)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{A\subset B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}\left(\int_{A}\nu_{x}^{j}(dz)-\int_{A}\nu_{y}^{j}(dz)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{A\subset B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}\left(\int_{j_{x}^{-1}(A)}\nu(dz)-\int_{j_{y}^{-1}(A)}\nu(dz)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{A\subset B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}\nu\left(j_{x}^{-1}(A)\setminus j_{y}^{-1}(A)\right),

and we have a similar estimate for
\left(\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}\right)^{-}
.

In view of (J2), we have

\displaystyle j_{x}^{-1}(A)\setminus j_{y}^{-1}(A)\subseteq j_{x}^{-1}(A)\subseteq j_{x}^{-1}(B_{R}\setminus B_{r})\subseteq B_{R/c_{0}}\setminus B_{r/c_{1}}.

It follows, in the first place, that the total variation is finite:

\displaystyle|\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}|\left(B_{R}\setminus B_{r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{B_{R/c_{0}}\setminus B_{r/c_{1}}}\nu(dz)<\infty.

In view of assumption (J3),
j_{y}(z)=j_{x}(z)+\omega_{r/c_{1}}(|x-y|)e
,
with
e\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
a unit vector, depending on
z,x,y
and
j
. In the worst case scenario, the set shrinks by a factor of
\omega_{r/c_{1}}(|x-y|)
, and we have a rough estimate

\displaystyle|j_{x}^{-1}(A)\setminus j_{y}^{-1}(A)|\leq|A|\,\tilde{\omega}_{r}(|x-y|),

with
\widetilde{\omega}_{r}(s)=\left(\omega_{r/c_{1}}(s)\right)^{N}
. Finally, if the measure
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, this amounts to

\displaystyle|\nu_{x}^{j}-\nu_{y}^{j}|\left(B_{R}\setminus B_{r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{R,r}(|x-y|).

(5.4)

(M4)

Let
r_{0}=\min(1,1/c_{1})
and
r\in(0,r_{0})
. Then, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the Wasserstein distance
between the Lévy measures
\nu_{x}^{j}
and
\nu_{y}^{j}
restricted on
B_{r}
is given by

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{x}^{j},\nu_{y}^{j})(B_{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\gamma}\int\limits_{B_{r}\times B_{r}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2}).

Let
\gamma=(j_{x}\times j_{y})_{\#}\nu\mid_{B_{r}}
.
We claim that
\gamma
is an admissible coupling plan for
\nu^{j}_{x}\mid_{B_{r}}
and
\nu^{j}_{y}\mid_{B_{r}}
.
Indeed, in view of Lemma
2.1
,
\text{supp}(\gamma)\subset B_{r}\times B_{r}
,
and hence, for all
A\subset B_{r}\setminus\{0\}
measurable, we have

\displaystyle\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma((\pi^{1})^{-1}(A))=\gamma(A\times B)=(j_{x}\times j_{y})_{\#}\nu\mid_{B_{r}}(A\times B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\mid_{B_{r}}\Big(\big(j_{x}\times j_{y}\big)^{-1}(A\times B)\Big)=\nu\mid_{B_{r}}\Big(\big(j_{x}\big)^{-1}(A)\cap\big(j_{y}\big)^{-1}(B)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\Big(\big(j_{x}\big)^{-1}(A)\Big)=(j_{x})_{\#}\nu(A)=\nu_{x}^{j}\mid_{B_{r}}(A),

using assumption (J2), which ensures that

.
Similarly,

Now, for each

\displaystyle\int\limits_{B_{r}\times B_{r}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}d\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})

Appendix A
Nonlocal estimate for a localization function

We present some fine estimates of nonlocal operators acting on smooth localization functions, extending some results previously obtained in
[
12
]
. These are used for PIDEs posed on unbounded domains, where we employ a localization function, which can take the following form.
Let
c>0
be a fixed constant. Consider
\psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that

\begin{cases}\psi=0&\mbox{in }B,\\
\psi=c&\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{2},\\

(A.1)

Notice that there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that

||\psi||_{\infty},||D\psi||_{\infty},||D^{2}\psi||_{\infty}\leq C_{0}.

(A.2)

The following lemma is an extension of
[
12
, Lemma 2.3]
to the case of a family of point-dependent Lévy measures
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
, satisfying the uniform integrability assumption (M1).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(\nu_{\xi})_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
be a family of Lévy measures satisfying assumption (M1).
Let
\psi

). Then, for any
\beta\in(0,1)
,

satisfies

\displaystyle||D\psi_{\beta}||{\infty}\leq C_{0}\beta,\,||D^{2}\psi_{\beta}||_{\infty}\leq C_{0}\beta^{2},

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{\delta}](x,\psi_{\beta})\leq\min\left(\frac{1}{2}C_{0}C_{\nu}\beta^{2},\beta^{2}o_{\delta}^{\xi}(1)\right),

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{\delta}^{c}](x,\psi_{\beta})\leq o_{\beta}^{\xi}(1),

(A.3)

where

) and

If, in addition (M2) holds, then estimate (

Proof.

The estimates for

and

are obvious.
In order to estimate the nonlocal term on
B_{\delta}
, we take into account the

regularity of
\psi_{\beta}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{\delta}](x,\psi_{\beta})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{|z|\leq\delta}\int_{0}^{1}D^{2}\psi_{\beta}(x+\theta z)z\cdot zd\theta\,\nu_{\xi}(dz).

A direct estimate gives, in view of assumption (M1),

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{\delta}](x,\psi_{\beta})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|D^{2}\psi_{\beta}||_{\infty}\int_{|z|\leq\delta}|z|^{2}\nu_{\xi}(dz)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}C_{0}\beta^{2}\int_{|z|\leq\delta}|z|^{2}\nu_{\xi}(dz)\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{0}C_{\nu}\beta^{2}.

Moreover, for a fixed

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\int_{|z|\leq\delta}|z|^{2}\nu_{\xi}(dz)=0,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{\delta}](x,\psi_{\beta})|\leq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}o_{\delta}^{\xi}(1).

In order to estimate the nonlocal term on

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{\delta}^{c}](x,\psi_{\beta})=~\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B](x,\psi_{\beta})-\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{\delta}](x,\psi_{\beta})+\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B^{c}](x,\psi_{\beta}).

From the previous step, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B](x,\psi_{\beta})\right|+\left|\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{\delta}](x,\psi_{\beta})\right|\leq C\beta^{2}.

To conclude, it remains to prove that
\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B^{c}](x,D\psi_{\beta}(x),\psi_{\beta})\to 0

, uniformly with respect to

. For any

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B^{c}](x,\psi_{\beta})=\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{R}\setminus B](x,\psi_{\beta})+\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{R}^{c}](x,\psi_{\beta}).

In order to estimate the first term, we rely on the regularity of
\psi_{\beta}
and (M1), whereas for the second one, on the regularity of the measure. Thus, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\xi}[B_{R}\setminus B](x,\psi_{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1\leq|z|\leq R}[\psi_{\beta}(x+z)-\psi_{\beta}(x)]\nu_{\xi}(dz)\;=\;\int_{1\leq|z|\leq R}\int_{0}^{1}D\psi_{\beta}(x+\theta z)\cdot zd\theta\nu_{\xi}(dz)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|D\psi_{\beta}|_{\infty}\int_{1\leq|z|\leq R}|z|\nu_{\xi}(dz)\;\leq\;\beta C_{0}\int_{1\leq|z|\leq R}|z|\nu_{\xi}(dz)\;\leq\;\beta C_{0}C_{\nu}R,

\displaystyle\int_{|z|>R}[\psi_{\beta}(x+z)-\psi_{\beta}(x)]\nu_{\xi}(dz)\;\leq\;2|\psi_{\beta}|_{\infty}\int_{|z|>R}\nu_{\xi}(dz)\;\leq\;2C_{0}o_{1/R}^{\xi}(1).

In view of Lemma

Lemma A.2
.

Let
g:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that
g(R)\to 0
as
R\to+\infty
. Define

\displaystyle f(\beta):=\inf_{R\geq 1}\left(g(R)+R\beta\right).

Then
f(\beta)\to 0
as
\beta\to 0
.

Sketch of proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
g
is a smooth, decreasing, convex function (otherwise one can replace
g
with a larger function, which still tends to
0
at
+\infty
). In this case, the infimum in
R
is achieved at some
R_{\beta}
satisfying
g^{\prime}(R_{\beta})+\beta=0.
Therefore

R_{\beta}=(g^{\prime})^{-1}(-\beta)\mathop{\to}_{\beta\to 0}+\infty.

In view of the convexity of
g
, we have, for all
R\geq 1
,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(R_{\beta})(R-R_{\beta})\leq g(R)-g(R_{\beta}).

Writing the previous inequality for
R=R_{\beta}/2
and using the expression for
R_{\beta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta R_{\beta}\leq 2\left(g(R_{\beta}/2)-g(R_{\beta})\right)\mathop{\to}_{\beta\to 0}0.

We conclude that
f(\beta)=g(R_{\beta})+\beta R_{\beta}\to 0

as
\beta\to 0
.
∎
