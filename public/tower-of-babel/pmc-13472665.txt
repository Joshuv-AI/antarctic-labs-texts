A Pancreas Out of Place: Acute Interstitial Pancreatitis Caused by Pancreatic Herniation in a Type IV Paraesophageal Hernia
Authors: Arman Manjikian, Victoria Diaz, Mark Hsu, Christian Stone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPancreatic involvement in paraesophageal hernias is rare and can precipitate acute pancreatitis through mechanical compression or ischemia. We report an 82-year-old woman who presented with epigastric pain and an elevated lipase level. Imaging demonstrated a large type IV paraesophageal hernia containing the stomach, transverse colon, and pancreatic body and tail, with inflammatory changes localized to the pancreatic tail. Common causes of pancreatitis were excluded. The patient improved with conservative management and was referred for elective hernia repair after resolution of pancreatitis. This case highlights an uncommon mechanical cause of acute pancreatitis and the importance of anatomic evaluation in atypical presentations.

INTRODUCTION

Acute pancreatitis is most often caused by gallstones and alcohol use; however, less common anatomic abnormalities may result in pancreatic inflammation through mechanical distortion, ischemia, or obstruction of pancreatic drainage.1,2 Paraesophageal hernias typically involve herniation of the stomach, while herniation of the pancreas is exceedingly uncommon and infrequently recognized as a cause of pancreatitis.2,3 Notably, transient pancreatic displacement may be sufficient to cause pancreatitis even after spontaneous reduction, underscoring the importance of anatomic evaluation in atypical presentations. We describe a case of acute interstitial pancreatitis resulting from pancreatic herniation within a type IV paraesophageal hernia.

CASE REPORT

An 82-year-old woman with a medical history significant for hypertension, dyslipidemia, hypothyroidism, and factor V Leiden deficiency presented with 2 days of constant epigastric pain radiating to the back, associated with nausea. She denied vomiting, alcohol use, trauma, dysphagia, heartburn, or prior abdominal surgeries.

Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast demonstrated a large type IV paraesophageal hernia containing both the stomach and pancreatic body and tail, without evidence of biliary dilation, gallstones, or choledocholithiasis (Figure 1).

Computed tomography in coronal view, blue arrow pointing to herniated portion of pancreas.

Laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated lipase level of 1,012 units per liter (reference range: 12–52 units per liter). Triglyceride and calcium levels were within normal limits, and liver enzymes were unremarkable. Repeat CT of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast was obtained given the severity of symptoms and for surgical planning. Imaging demonstrated that the type IV paraesophageal hernia now contained only the stomach, indicating that the pancreas had reduced back into the abdominal cavity (Figure 2). Despite this interval change, inflammatory changes surrounding the pancreatic tail were present, consistent with acute interstitial pancreatitis.

Computed tomography in coronal view, blue arrow pointing to nonherniated pancreas with evidence of pancreatitis.

The patient remained hemodynamically stable without evidence of sepsis or bowel ischemia. Gastroenterology and cardiothoracic surgery services were consulted. The patient was deemed a surgical candidate; however, given the presence of active pancreatitis, operative intervention was deferred. She was managed conservatively with intravenous fluid resuscitation, supportive care, and early oral refeeding in accordance with guideline-directed management.6 Her abdominal pain gradually improved, and she was discharged with plans for outpatient cardiothoracic surgery follow-up to discuss elective hernia repair after resolution of pancreatitis. Informed patient consent was obtained for publication of the case details.

DISCUSSION

Pancreatic herniation through a paraesophageal defect is a rare clinical entity, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature.2–4 Paraesophageal hernias themselves represent a minority of hiatal hernias, accounting for approximately 5% to 15% of cases, and herniation of organs other than the stomach occurs even less frequently.1,5 Pancreatic involvement is particularly unusual because the pancreas is normally fixed in the retroperitoneum by surrounding ligamentous and vascular attachments, making migration through the esophageal hiatus uncommon.2,4 Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain pancreatitis in this setting, including mechanical compression of the pancreas, traction on the pancreatic tail, vascular compromise, and transient obstruction of the pancreatic duct.2,4 In the present case, inflammatory changes were confined to the pancreatic tail, corresponding anatomically to the portion of the pancreas involved in the hernia. This finding is consistent with prior reports in which the pancreatic body or tail was the portion most frequently involved in herniation, likely due to its relative mobility compared with the pancreatic head.2–4

Prior reports describe pancreatitis occurring with herniation of the pancreatic body or tail into large paraesophageal hernias, demonstrated on CT imaging.2–4 In contrast, our case showed interval reduction of the pancreas on repeat imaging, with the stomach remaining herniated and inflammatory changes persisting in the pancreatic tail. This suggests that transient pancreatic displacement and traction alone may be sufficient to precipitate pancreatitis even after the pancreas returns to its normal intra-abdominal position.

This case highlights the importance of considering anatomic causes of pancreatitis when common etiologies have been excluded, particularly in elderly patients or in those with atypical imaging findings. immunoglobulin G4 levels were not obtained, as the clear mechanical etiology and absence of features suggestive of autoimmune pancreatitis made this diagnosis unlikely, particularly in an elderly patient with a single acute presentation. Advanced age and associated comorbidities also independently influence surgical decision making; in elderly patients, operative risk warrants careful multidisciplinary discussion even after pancreatitis has resolved. In this case, the patient was considered a surgical candidate, and elective hernia repair was recommended following recovery from pancreatitis, in accordance with a shared multidisciplinary decision between gastroenterology and cardiothoracic surgery. Imaging of both the chest and abdomen was essential for establishing the diagnosis and understanding the underlying mechanism.4 Management requires a multidisciplinary approach, with initial conservative treatment of pancreatitis followed by consideration of elective surgical repair once pancreatic inflammation has resolved.3,6
