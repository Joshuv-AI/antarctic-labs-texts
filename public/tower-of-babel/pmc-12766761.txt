High-impact technical and patient management tips from the 2024 AAST Continuous Certification Course: preperitoneal pelvic packing, trauma video review, video-assisted thoracic surgery for retained hemothorax, and laparoscopic common bile duct exploration
Authors: Clay Cothren Burlew, Whitney Renee Jenson, Katherine Cironi, Emma Gilman Burke, Ryan Peter Dumas, Rachel D Appelbaum, Matthew J Martin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTrauma and acute care surgeons are constantly balancing the need for rapid, decisive intervention with the ongoing evolution of surgical technique and cutting-edge technology. This article presents a collection of practical, experience-driven insights across four topics that reflect the modern breadth of trauma and acute care surgery presented during a lunch session at the American Association for the Surgery for Trauma Annual Meeting 2024: preperitoneal pelvic packing, trauma video review, video-assisted thoracic surgery for retained hemothorax, and laparoscopic common bile duct exploration. Although seemingly disparate, these topics are united by a common goal: enhancing technical precision, team performance, and patient outcomes through deliberate, refined practice.Keywords: thoracic surgical procedures; shock, hemorrhagic; quality improvement; biliary tract

Pack like a pro: preperitoneal pelvic packing and control of pelvic fracture-associated hemorrhage

While the majority of pelvic fractures result from low energy mechanisms and do not result in hemodynamic instability, a subgroup of patients will experience significant pelvic fracture-related hemorrhage resulting in mortality rates as high as 32%–41%.1,3 There are multiple options for the management of patients with serious bleeding secondary to pelvic fractures, primarily consisting of preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP), angioembolization (AE), and resuscitative balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA). PPP has repeatedly been demonstrated to have a shorter time-to-intervention as well as time required for intervention compared with AE24,7 and, as such, should be in the armamentarium of all surgeons.

Technique

PPP is performed by first externally stabilizing the pelvis to both reduce the pelvic volume and provide a stable surface against which tamponade can be created. Pelvic stabilization is ideally performed with an external fixator or a c-clamp. The specific conformation of the external fixator should be discussed with the orthopedic team prior to placement, particularly if an exploratory laparotomy is to be performed so that the crossbar does not obscure the operative field. If external fixators or c-clamps are not available, the pelvic binder should be repositioned so that the superior margin is below the pubic symphysis and out of the operative field. When extrapelvic sources of hemorrhage exist, these may be addressed while the pelvis is stabilized by the orthopedic team.

A midline incision is made from the pubic symphysis superiorly extending approximately 6–8 cm. The subcutaneous tissue and the midline fascia are divided, leaving the underlying peritoneum intact. At this point, the pelvic hematoma is usually encountered. If the hematoma has not dissected completely around the bladder anteriorly in the preperitoneal space, blunt dissection is used to sweep the tissue away from the posterior surface of the pubic symphysis continuing laterally into the paravesicle space until the hematoma is encountered. Once the hematoma is unroofed, laparotomy pads are inserted on one side of the pelvis at a time. Using ring forceps, the first laparotomy pad is placed posteriorly in the presacral space, followed by two more laparotomy pads moving anteriorly around the bladder (figure 1). To facilitate placement, the bladder is gently retracted to the contralateral side. Packing is then repeated on the other side of the pelvis. Generally, three laparotomy pads are required per side to create tamponade in an adult, whereas two laparotomy pads per side are required in children. If a suprapubic catheter is required, it should be placed at this point. The midline fascia is closed using heavy weight running monofilament suture and the skin is closed with staples.

(A) Placement of laparotomy pads in the preperitoneal space using ring forceps. (B) Location of laparotomy pad placement for preperitoneal packing.

The major pitfall of PPP is allowing decompression of the pelvic hematoma either externally or into the peritoneal cavity. To avoid this, a separate, more lateral stab incision should be used if a suprapubic catheter is required, and a separate midline incision should be made cranial (above the umbilicus) to the PPP incision if a laparotomy is required. While there may be hemorrhage externally from an open pelvic fracture, particularly those with perineal wounds, PPP may be performed and is effective in this patient population.

The patient returns to the operating room once coagulopathy has been corrected and physiological derangements have been treated, generally at 24–48 hours. The laparotomy pads are removed from one side of the pelvis at a time taking care to control any ongoing bleeding with clips, sutures, electrocautery, or topical hemostatic agents. Repacking the pelvis should be avoided due to the significantly increased risk of pelvic infection with this maneuver (3.7% for one episode of PPP vs 45%–47% for multiple episodes of packing).8,12

Indications

The key to treating pelvic fracture-related hemorrhage is to develop an algorithm incorporating all available treatment options (figure 2). PPP, AE, and REBOA should be viewed as complementary, not competing techniques. The ideal patient for PPP has pelvic fractures with persistent shock despite transfusion of two units of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) and placement of a pelvic binder.4 9 Although research regarding whole blood transfusion thresholds for PPP has not been performed, we recommend the same two-unit threshold when patients are resuscitated with whole blood.

Prior to transportation to the operating room, the patient should be evaluated for other sources of hemorrhage that may require intervention (eg, focused assessment with sonography in trauma exam to evaluate for intraperitoneal bleeding and chest radiograph to evaluate for intrathoracic bleeding). About half of all patients with pelvic fractures and hemodynamic instability have significant extrapelvic sources of bleeding and over 85% of patients in one series required at least one operative intervention in addition to PPP.2 As compared with AE, PPP allows multiple operative interventions to occur concurrently or in rapid succession.

If the patient’s systolic blood pressure remains below 70–80 mm Hg after initial resuscitation, REBOA should be considered for temporary hemorrhage control.13 14 In a study by Werner et al, mortality rates of patients with unstable pelvic fracture managed with or without REBOA in conjunction with PPP were examined. Despite significantly worse injury severity scores and physiological derangements in the group treated with PPP and REBOA, there was no difference in mortality between the two groups. This suggests a potential mortality benefit for the combined use of PPP and REBOA for severely injured patients.14

After PPP, if the patient remains unstable or requires four or more units PRBCs in 12 hours after coagulopathy has been corrected, angiography should be performed. Around 13% of patients who undergo PPP will ultimately require AE as well. After implementation of a PPP clinical pathway, the Denver group found that the average time to AE was over 10 hours.8 The primary benefit of performing PPP prior to AE is that it allows AE to be performed in a delayed manner and thus allows other time-sensitive aspects of patient care to be addressed, such as control of extrapelvic hemorrhage or interfacility transfer.

PPP is an ideal approach for management of pelvic fracture-related hemorrhage in unstable patients because it can be performed rapidly with minimal resources, allows for completion of multiple procedures concurrently, and is compatible with REBOA and AE. Mastery of this technique is pivotal for all surgeons who care for traumatically injured patients.

Let’s go to the tape! Running a trauma video review program

Trauma video review (TVR) was first described as a tool for education and quality improvement in the 1980s.15 After the success by Hoyt et al in decreasing resuscitation times and efficiency following TVR implementation, interest in developing similar programs grew.15 After a brief decline in popularity due to misinterpretation of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act,15,17 TVR use has been increasing at trauma centers across the USA. By capturing audiovisual recordings of trauma resuscitations, film can be reviewed for educational, research, or quality improvement-related endeavors.18

The ability of TVR to track both technical and non-technical skills makes it an invaluable tool for trauma centers and research. Before the advent of TVR, assessment of trauma bay resuscitation quality relied on review of the electronic medical record (EMR), or data collection performed by trained observers. Chart reviews rely on data that may be inaccurately or incompletely captured. While this can be due to human error while charting, it is also due to the inadequacies of the EMR to assess the intangibles of patient care.18 19 While having specialized observers can circumvent some of these issues, many projects would be at the mercy of institutions to coordinate research assistants to perform manual observations. This can prove to be logistically difficult, costly, and data have shown that it is less accurate than data collected with TVR.20 21 In addition to facilitating quality improvement projects, associations between interventions and non-technical skills can be linked to EMR data to answer clinical questions.19 22 The ability of TVR to assess all aspects of care by trauma teams creates a multitude of opportunities to measure and improve patient outcomes.

Before a trauma center can begin using film to answer important clinical questions, a significant amount of time and energy must be devoted to implementing a TVR program. Development of a TVR program requires collaboration with surgical, legal, and administrative personnel. Once an advisory team is assembled, a project timeline should be created with identification of critical steps in implementation. Feasibility and medicolegal analysis, stakeholder review, identification of funding sources and equipment, and logistical planning must occur before the first patient can be recorded. Initial plans for TVR require an understanding of consent processes, hospital policies, and laws governing audiovisual recording.23 While laws regarding consent for recording vary by state, many TVR programs are able to consent for taping as part of the Conditions of Admission.24 25 Early consultation with legal and privacy offices can facilitate navigation of national and local policies.9 Importantly, a recent review of medicolegal case law did not find that TVR has ever been used successfully in litigation.24

Once feasibility and legal framework have been established, discussions with employees impacted by the program can begin. Obtaining buy-in from trainees, faculty, nurses, advanced practice providers, and ancillary staff is critical for lasting success. Despite the known benefits associated with TVR programs, staff are often anxious about professional privacy, patient consent, and potential punitive action resulting from film review.26 Creating space for discussion early in the planning process can mitigate concerns as they arise.23 Despite initial concerns, staff perceptions of TVR generally become more favorable as a program is implemented.27 As stakeholders become involved and program champions are identified, the necessary equipment can be obtained. Program cost can vary widely based on the number of trauma bays equipped, recording quality, and processing software functionality.23 While TVR program maintenance costs are significantly less than upfront costs, a lack of resources is frequently cited as a reason for program discontinuation and must be budgeted for appropriately.16

Lastly, guidelines and policies for video review should be authored to identify which clips are viewed and how associated educational activities are managed. Program maintenance hinges on the culture surrounding TVR review practices. Ensuring that data will not be used in a punitive manner, but rather to improve patient care and safety, is paramount.9 Multidisciplinary conferences can be used to review an array of topics from leadership qualities to procedural techniques.25 28 The structure of and audience for TVR conferences is determined by the individual needs and resources of each institution. Large group sessions focused on team performance and communication may include all resuscitation team members, whereas conferences focused on procedural skills may be more appropriate for surgical faculty and trainees. In addition, individual coaching sessions focused on non-technical skills have been successfully employed.9 It can often feel like the trauma bay is not an ideal location for providing and receiving feedback; use of TVR-based educational programming can create the necessary opportunities for these interactions. However, as with any feedback process, TVR should be performed in close temporal proximity to the captured resuscitation, as feasible, to maximize learning.

In modern trauma care and performance improvement, TVR provides unparalleled ability to measure critical benchmarks in resuscitation and identify opportunities during the continuum of acute trauma care. Creating a program is a lengthy process that requires collaboration to generate a positive, educational culture that ensures TVR program longevity.

Clean out that chest: how to perform video-assisted thoracic surgery like a pro

Similar to any intervention, with video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS), we want to identify our ideal operative candidate. VATS should be strongly considered in patients with retained hemothorax (rHTX) within the first 24–72 hours following tube thoracostomy.29 30 In the setting of comorbidities, the risk of operative intervention must be weighed against the increased risk of respiratory failure and sepsis from the rHTX itself. At a minimum, patients must be able to tolerate single lung ventilation and lateral decubitus position. There is often debate if end-stage chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, unstable spine injury, or traumatic brain injury with refractory intracranial pressures is prohibitory. It ultimately depends on whether optimization can be achieved during a short interim prior to intervention or if operative intervention is ultimately too high-risk. Generally, VATS washout should be performed early, as intervention after the first 5 days risks the presence of early adhesions. The development of adhesions can convert the routine thoracoscopy into a complex one and increase the risk of parenchymal injury and empyema.31 The Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma Practice Management Guidelines recommends VATS within 4 days of admission32 33 and the Western Trauma Association recommends VATS within 3 days for rHTX.34

Once the decision is made to proceed, we must consider lung isolation, positioning, and port placement. Single-lung ventilation involves letting the operative side collapse while ventilating the opposite side. This can be performed emergently by mainstem intubation of the endotracheal tube (ETT) or in a more controlled setting by placing a double-lumen ETT. If the patient is unable to tolerate this due to underlying lung pathology or significant contusion, double lung ventilation with intermittent apnea is an option. Patients are typically positioned in lateral decubitus with the involved side up. The operating table is flexed at a 30-degree angle to expand the intercostal spaces. The patient’s padded arms are placed in front of their face in a ‘prayer’ position. An axillary roll is placed under the dependent arm to prevent compression of the brachial plexus and axillary vascular structures. The patient’s legs should be positioned together and flexed, with pillow padding.21

For port placement, there are several techniques, including the ‘baseball diamond,’ the two-port, and the single-port approaches. The standard VATS procedure typically involves making three to four incisions arranged in a triangular configuration. The area of interest is often dependent and posterior towards the diaphragm. If the patient has a chest tube, you can use this thoracostomy site as the first port since you already know it is in the chest (fourth-fifth intercostal space). The camera is inserted through this incision to create additional entry ports safely. One port is created in the auscultatory triangle to accommodate the camera, while a third port is made in the midaxillary line at the level of the utility port incision (seventh intercostal space).35 Once the ports are established, the procedure progresses under the guidance of the video thoracoscope. Enlarging the anterior trocar to accommodate a small Alexis wound protector is a useful option, allowing for clot removal or suturing if needed. Additionally, it is important to avoid severely fractured regions of the chest wall for port placement whenever possible, as this can cause tenting of the pleura, making port placement more difficult and potentially obscuring the view. Regarding debridement, your technique will need to adjust based on the extent of fibrinous exudate present. The goal of the procedure is to break up loculations and remove clot and fibrinous exudate to allow for the lung to re-expand. Debridement will use a combination of blunt dissection and peeling of the fibrinous tissues. A Yankauer suction tip and a sponge stick are two great options. The key is knowing when to stop as excessive debridement will put the patient at increased risk for parenchymal damage and ongoing air leak.

Ultimately, earlier intervention is better as there is less inflammation and less fibrinous exudate. Later intervention is not impossible, but trauma surgery versus thoracic surgery intervening depends on your comfort level.36 Do not be disheartened by your postop film in the setting of significant decortication, as it will lag behind clinical improvement. The key is seeing physiological improvement. Pain control and aggressive pulmonary toilet are important to maintain this progress. These basic VATS principles can be expanded to other adjunct thoracic procedures including intercostal blocks, cryoablation, and rib plating; however, these topics are beyond the scope of this manuscript.

Laparoscopic common bile duct exploration for the acute care surgeon

Choledocholithiasis is a relatively common condition, affecting up to 10%–15% of patients undergoing a cholecystectomy.37,41 Managing choledocholithiasis includes various stone extraction approaches, such as pre-operative, intraoperative, or postoperative endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), intraoperative cholangiography with flushing of the duct, or laparoscopic common bile duct exploration (LCBDE).42 Among these options, LCBDE offers the advantage of allowing a single surgical team to perform both stone extraction and laparoscopic cholecystectomy under one anesthetic episode, eliminating the need for separate interventions and reducing reliance on additional healthcare teams. The combined LCBDE and cholecystectomy approach has demonstrated several advantages, including lower overall costs, reduced hospital stays, decreased rates of postoperative bleeding, and lower incidence of pancreatitis.43,45 However, the use of LCBDE remains relatively uncommon due to multiple factors including lack of training and expertise, the longer required operative times, and limitations of the available technology and equipment. Here, we present a brief summary of the techniques and approaches for LCBDE that can be incorporated into the practice of any practicing acute care surgeon.

Transcystic versus trans-choledochal common bile duct exploration

Typically, a CBDE starts with obtaining an intraoperative cholangiogram (IOC) to assess the presence, number, location, and size of any common bile duct (CBD) stones in addition to the cystic and CBD anatomy. These findings will help inform the subsequent approach and optimal techniques for clearance of the CBD. In select cases of small, non-obstructing stones, flushing via the IOC catheter may be the only intervention that is required. In the case of larger and/or obstructing stones that are not larger than the diameter of the dilated cystic duct, transcystic CBDE and retrograde stone extraction can be performed. In cases where the CBD stone(s) is larger than the cystic duct or is impacted at the ampulla and unable to be grasped with a wire basket, then additional techniques including forward pushing the stone with the choledochoscope, lithotripsy of the stone into smaller fragments, or balloon sphincteroplasty and then forward pushing or flushing the stones may be required. This will also vary by the surgeon’s personal preference, available equipment, and experience and expertise in these techniques.

In general, there are two distinct approaches or main technique categories for LCBDE, although they can be thought of as options along a continuum that are applied in specific situations. Transcystic CBDE is the standard default approach used in most patients, as it can be readily performed in combination with intraoperative transcystic cholangiography and does not require direct exposure or instrumentation of the CBD. This typically involves establishing wire access through the cystic duct and into the CBD and duodenum (if possible) and then exploration and removal of any CBD stones using either choledochoscopy (direct visualization) or fluoroscopic-guided CBD clearance via flushing and passage of instruments such as wire baskets or Fogarty balloon catheters to remove the obstructing stones. Our preferred approach is via direct visualization with transcystic choledochoscopy, but an understanding of the alternative techniques is important to optimize the chances of a successful CBDE.

Trans-choledochal CBDE is an important but less commonly performed technique that requires a higher level of minimally invasive surgery technique and that is only used in several specific situations. It is typically performed by surgeons with advanced minimally invasive surgery or hepatobiliary training, but can be safely adopted by any acute care surgeon with appropriate training and laparoscopic suturing skills. The transcholedochal approach is typically used as a ‘step-up’ procedure if a transcystic approach cannot be performed or fails to remove the CBD stones, in cases where the CBD stones are too large for transcystic extraction, or in patients who have had a prior cholecystectomy and the cystic duct cannot be safely identified and used for access. As opposed to the transcystic CBDE where there is no dissection or direct instrumentation of the CBD, transcholedochal CBDE requires dissection and exposure of at least the anterior surface of the CBD and making a choledochotomy for direct CBD access and instrumentation. This does add the additional risks of injury to the CBD or the right hepatic artery and bile leaks or strictures related to the choledochotomy closure. However, several series have reported low rates of additional complications with this approach versus transcystic techniques.38 42 43

Although preoperative or postoperative ERCP has become increasingly popular, it exposes patients to an additional procedure with its own risks and potential complications.

LCBDE offers a viable alternative, allowing for definitive management in a single stage while reducing the need for multiple interventions, ultimately leading to comparable outcomes and shorter hospital stays. Given that acute care surgeons frequently encounter choledocholithiasis, it is essential for them to be well-versed in its management, including the single-staged approach of LCBDE combined with laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Conclusion

Excellence in trauma and acute care surgery is built on a foundation of reproducible technique, critical thinking, and relentless self-assessment. Techniques such as PPP, TVR, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery for rHTX, and laparoscopic common bile duct exploration exemplify the vast spectrum of often disparate topics that fall into the purview of surgeons in this field. By sharing practical tips and lessons learned from the Continuous Certification Course at the AAST Annual Meeting 2024, we hope to contribute to a broader culture of surgical precision, innovation, and continuous improvement.
