Effects of argipressin on intraoperative fluid administration in cytoreductive surgery with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (CRS-HIPEC): study protocol of a randomised clinical trial (HiPress Trial)
Authors: Judith van Zwol, Arend G J Aalbers, Charissa E van den Brom, Markus W Hollmann, Abraham H Hulst, Wytze Lameris, Pieter R Tuinman, Alexander P J Vlaar, Denise P Veelo, Sabrine N T Hemmes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCytoreductive surgery (CRS) with heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) is a treatment for peritonitis carcinomatosa. These procedures often involve significant blood and fluid loss, leading to hyperdynamic circulation and vasodilation, necessitating intraoperative fluids and vasoconstrictors such as catecholamines. Excessive fluid administration to counteract vasodilation can cause intraoperative fluid overload, which is linked to increased postoperative complications. Vasopressin has emerged as a potential alternative to catecholamines, restoring vascular tone via non-adrenergic pathways and supporting perfusion pressure, potentially reducing the need for compensatory fluids solely administered to compensate for vasodilation. We hypothesise that compared with norepinephrine, vasopressin reduces cumulative intraoperative fluid administration during CRS-HIPEC within a goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT) protocol, ultimately leading to a lowering of postoperative complications.Methods and analysisHiPress is a two-centre, two-arm randomised clinical trial with blinding of both patients and outcome assessors. A total of 70 adult patients undergoing CRS-HIPEC will be included. Patients will be randomised to receive either continuous low-dose argipressin or continuous low-dose norepinephrine. Both groups will receive standardised GDFT during the procedure. The primary endpoint is cumulative intraoperative fluid administration (mL). Secondary endpoints include direct fluid-related outcomes (eg, cumulative intraoperative fluid (ml/kg/hour), postoperative fluid balance until day five and ultrasound-assessed pulmonary oedema and venous congestion) and indirect fluid-associated outcomes (eg, quality of recovery, surgical and abdominal complications, acute kidney injury (AKI), pulmonary complications, length of ICU and hospital stay and 30-day mortality).Ethics and disseminationThe study is enrolling patients since February 2025. The trial is approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee (hereinafter: MREC) NedMec, The Netherlands (Ref: D-25-500202). Introduction

Cytoreductive surgery with heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy (CRS-HIPEC) has emerged as a promising, yet aggressive, treatment for patients with peritonitis carcinomatosa from various primary tumours. During CRS-HIPEC, the primary tumour and visible metastasis are first resected and heated chemotherapy is circulated through the peritoneal cavity, targeting microscopic peritoneal metastasis.1 Compared with palliative chemotherapy, CRS-HIPEC increases survival of patients presenting with disseminated cancer.2,4 However, the procedure is associated with high perioperative morbidity, with reported rates ranging from 30%–50%.5 6 Multiple factors contribute to the risk of these complications, such as patient-specific characteristics, tumour biology, and the extent of the procedure.

Moreover, perioperative morbidity is substantially impacted by intraoperative fluid management.7 8 Excessive fluid administration can lead to tissue oedema, impairing cardiac- and pulmonary function, gastrointestinal recovery, tissue oxygenation, anastomotic integrity and wound healing.7,9 As a result, the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery guidelines emphasise the importance of an individualised fluid therapy approach to avoid excessive intravenous fluids during CRS-HIPEC procedures.10

CRS-HIPEC patients experience haemodynamic instability due to various reasons: drainage of ascites leading to intravascular fluid loss, vasodilatory effects of epidural analgesia and the vasodilatory effect of heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy.11 Patients typically develop a hyperdynamic circulatory state characterised by an increased cardiac output and decreased systemic vascular resistance.12 Despite the use of catecholamines to achieve haemodynamic stability, there is substantial intraoperative fluid administration.

Intravenous fluids are administered in response to intraoperative haemodynamic instability, often based on the assumption of hypovolaemia. However, haemodynamic instability during CRS-HIPEC is frequently multifactorial and may be driven by vasodilation rather than fluid loss. Over-reliance on fluids in this situation may lead to fluid overload, which is associated with adverse postoperative outcomes.

In recent years, argipressin, a synthetic exogenous vasopressin, emerged as a potential alternative vasoconstrictive agent to catecholamines. Argipressin counteracts hypotension caused by vasodilation by restoring vascular tone through non-adrenergic V1 receptor activation independent of adrenergic pathways. These pathways may be less effective perioperatively. In addition, it preserves endothelial barrier integrity.13 These effects contribute to haemodynamic stability and may prevent excessive intravenous fluid administration.14

At present, argipressin is not routinely administered during general surgery. However, in critical care medicine, argipressin is routinely used in settings of norepinephrine-refractory vasoplegic shock. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 guideline recommends early argipressin addition to norepinephrine to maintain haemodynamic stability.15 Although studies suggest that argipressin is superior compared with catecholamines in reducing mortality16 and organ dysfunction,17,19 a recent Cochrane review found no conclusive evidence favouring any vasoconstrictor above the other in terms of survival.20 However, argipressin may offer potential benefits compared with norepinephrine, including improved renal function. In addition, argipressin appears to have favourable effects on immune function and defence response.21

In general surgery, data on argipressin is scarce. A retrospective study suggests that low-dose vasopressin, combined with goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT) during CRS-HIPEC, may reduce intraoperative fluid administration and postoperative morbidity.14 Furthermore, a current study assesses if argipressin reduces intraoperative blood loss by decreasing portal and hepatic blood flow in patients undergoing liver surgery.22

The HiPress trial will assess whether low-dose argipressin, compared with norepinephrine, leads to a reduction in cumulative intraoperative fluid administration when combined with standardised GDFT during CRS-HIPEC. Furthermore, we will collect data on postoperative fluid balance, occurrence of renal, pulmonary, abdominal and major complications (Clavien-Dindo ≥2), third day quality of recovery and 30-day outcome.

Moreover, this trial will provide valuable insights into the role of argipressin in intraoperative haemodynamic management during extensive abdominal surgery and provide data to determine whether a larger trial is warranted to primarily examine the effects on clinical outcomes.

Methods and analyses

This is a randomised, controlled, outcome assessor and patient blinded trial in two centres (Dutch Cancer Institute – Antoni van Leeuwenhoek and Amsterdam UMC) with two parallel groups. Inclusion was started in February 2025. This trial will include 70 patients undergoing CRS-HIPEC for disseminated cancer within an estimated 2 years.

The study is designed in adherence with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines (online supplemental file 1a).

Screening and recruitment

All adult patients (defined as ≥18 years of age) undergoing CRS-HIPEC for disseminated cancer are screened for inclusion. During preoperative consultation, verbal and written information on the study is provided to all patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria (table 1). Patients are given the opportunity to ask questions and have a minimum of 5 days to consider participation before providing oral and written consent. Following patient informed consent, patients are assigned a patient identification number by electronic case report form (eCRF) system, Castor EDC.

Randomisation, stratification and blinding

Randomisation is performed as block randomisation with block sizes of maximum six with a 1:1 allocation, stratified by study centre. Randomisation is performed via Castor EDC.23 Blinding is maintained for the participants, the outcome assessor, and clinical staff involved in perioperative and postoperative care, except for the anaesthesia team’s anaesthetist and anaesthesia nurse assigned to the procedure. Participants remain blinded to their group assignment throughout the duration of the trial and are informed of their allocation after postoperative day 30 on request. Additionally, data analysis will be performed by researchers blinded to the randomisation allocation. Figure 1 provides an overview of the study design.

Study intervention

On the day of surgery participants are randomised to the treatment (argipressin) or control (norepinephrine) group. Both groups receive GDFT according to a standardised GDFT protocol (figure 2), guided by continuous measurement of stroke volume variation and cardiac index with the FloTrac/HemoSphere monitoring system. A baseline crystalloid solution infusion is administered throughout the procedure at 1 mL/kg/hour to all patients. Anaesthesia, perioperative monitoring, other treatments and postoperative care are performed according to clinical routine.

Haemodynamic management during procedure. (A) Haemodynamic management during the use of argipressin (treatment group). (B) Haemodynamic management during the use of norepinephrine (control group). MAP, Mean Arterial Pressure; SVV, Stroke volume variation.

Treatment group

Participants assigned to the treatment group receive continuous argipressin (Empressin 0.8 IU/mL) throughout the procedure, starting at 0.02 IU/min at induction. If MAP decreases below 65 mm Hg, the steps in flow-chart of figure 2A are followed. The maximum rate of argipressin infusion is 0.03 IU/min. The dosage of argipressin infusion is based on the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines15 and previous literature.14 If MAP increases above 100 mm Hg, argipressin is decreased in steps of 0.01 IU/min until stop.

Control group

Patients assigned to the control group receive continuous norepinephrine throughout the procedure, starting at 200 mcg/hour at induction, as standard of care in both participating centres and supported by clinical guidelines10 and expert consensus.24

If MAP decreases below 65 mm Hg, the steps in the flowchart of figure 2B are followed. If MAP increases above 100 mm Hg, norepinephrine is decreased in steps of 50 mcg/hour until stop.

Patients with hypertension classified are managed according to standard perioperative care without protocol-specific modifications. Intraoperative vasopressor use and fluid therapy in this subgroup follow the same GDFT algorithm applied to all participants.

Perioperative inflammatory response

Blood samples will be drawn before the procedure, immediately after the procedure and on postoperative day 1 to compare the potential effect of argipressin versus norepinephrine on the inflammatory response. Circulating cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns are measured and include tumour necrosis factor alpha, interleukin−6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-1 receptor antagonist, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha and beta, heat shock protein 70, S100A8/S100A9, S100A12, lactate dehydrogenase, high mobility group box 1 protein and human leucocyte antigen-DR mRNA expression as a marker of immune competence. No dexamethasone will be administered during induction and surgery in this study.

A complete overview of all outcomes can be found in the supplement, (online supplemental table S1).

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS)

Fluid overload will be clinically evaluated POCUS. Lung ultrasound is carried out at six specific regions in the chest (see online supplemental figure S1). These regions can be divided into lower and upper parts of the anterior, lateral and posterior walls. The lungs will be studied using the Bedside Lung Ultrasound in Emergency protocol.27 In this trial we will use this protocol to assess A-lines, B-lines, lung sliding, pleural effusion and consolidation in the defined lung zones to detect pulmonary congestion.

Consequently, systemic venous congestion will be determined using the Venous Excess Ultrasound (VExUS) score. The VExUS protocol involves ultrasound examination of the inferior vena cava, hepatic vein, portal vein and intrarenal veins.28 The total VExUS score will be calculated using the VExUS C grading system. A VExUS score ≥grade 2 is considered abnormal.29

Fluid overload is defined as an increase in body weight of >10% and/or signs of fluid overload by ultrasound: presence of bilateral B-profile and/or pleural fluid. Details regarding the training of the ultrasound examiner can be found in online supplemental file 1c).

Monitoring

Trial monitoring

A qualified clinical monitor is assigned to conduct study monitoring in accordance with the pre-defined monitoring plan. Monitoring activities include two on-site visits while the study is actively recruiting and one on-site close-out visit. During the visit, the presence and completeness of the Trial Master File and participant Informed Consent Forms is verified. Additionally, source data verification is carried out as outlined in the monitoring plan. Site-specific aspects, including documentation practices and data entry procedures, will be reviewed to ensure compliance with the protocol, GCP guidelines and applicable regulatory requirements. This low-risk phase IV study does not have a Data Safety Monitoring Board. Safety will be monitored after five and 10 patients have been included in the intervention arm by the safety analysis, described in the statistical analysis plan.

(Serious) adverse event report

The safety desk of the Dutch Cancer Institute – Antoni van Leeuwenhoek manages all SAEs and serious unexpected serious adverse reactions (SUSARs). SAEs deemed unrelated to the study intervention will be included as line listings in the annual safety report, while SAEs that are related or potentially related to the investigational product will be reported as SUSAR. In the event of a SUSAR, the safety desk will submit the report to EudraVigilance, in accordance with regulatory timelines,30 and as defined in the protocol. If a SUSAR necessitates a change in study protocol or presents a risk to participant safety, all investigators and, where appropriate, affected patients will be informed as soon as possible. The safety desk will continue to follow-up on all SAE cases, including SUSARs, until each case is fully documented, resolved or deemed stable.

Finally, the Clinical Research Associate will prepare an annual safety update for submission via the Clinical Trials Information System, including an aggregated overview of all reported SAEs in the Annual Safety Report.

Statistical analysis plan

Sample size

Based on unpublished retrospective data from the local registry, a consensus meeting with senior clinicians and researchers, informed by local experience and feasibility considerations, concluded that a reduction of 1500 mL was both clinically relevant and feasible. A sample size of 35 patients per group will achieve 83% power to detect a ratio of mean cumulative fluid administration of 0.77 (5000 mL/6500 mL), at a 2-sided 5% significance level, and assuming a coefficient variation of 0.38 (2500 mL/6500 mL) in the original scale.

Patients withdrawn before randomisation will be replaced. In case of an ‘open-close procedure’ due to extensive disease, the patient will be replaced. Other patient withdrawal after randomisation will not be replaced.

Statistical methods

All analyses will be performed in an intention-to-treat population, including all randomised patients who completed CRS-HIPEC surgery. Per protocol, analysis will be performed to check robustness of results.

Primary outcome

The effect of the intervention on the primary outcome, cumulative total intraoperative fluid administration, will be tested using a t-test after log transformation.

Secondary outcomes

Postoperative fluid administration, postoperative fluid balance and fluid balance at D1 will be calculated in the same way as the primary outcome. Ultrasound measurements will be compared between groups using each of the clinical reference classes (fluid overload present vs absent) using two-sample tests for binomial proportions (χ2, Fisher’s exact test) or continuous variables (t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests). Missing ultrasound parameters can be imputed from the measurement the day before or after.

Major complications are rated as Clavien-Dindo score ≥2 up to postoperative day 30. Patients who develop at least one major complication, as well as the cumulative number of major complications per patient, will be scored (for definitions, see online supplemental file 1d).

Postoperative pulmonary complications occurring in the first 5 days after surgery are presented as a collapsed composite outcome (for definitions, see online supplemental file 1d). Patients who develop at least one of the abovementioned complications are considered as meeting the secondary outcome.

Major complications and postoperative abdominal- and pulmonary complications will be compared between the two intervention groups, and the OR and relative risks with corresponding 95% CIs will be calculated using logistic regression analysis. The effect of the intervention on length of hospitalisation and ICU stay will be estimated with Poisson models or negative binomial models, depending on the presence of overdispersion.

Subgroup analysis

Planned subgroup analyses will explore heterogeneity of treatment effects of the primary endpoint across patient age groups (≤50 vs >50), ASA physical status (<3 vs ≥3), peritoneal cancer index score (≤10 vs >10) and extent of organ resection (≤2 vs >2 organs resected).

Other study variables

Descriptive data will be summarised using frequencies and percentages, means with standard deviations (±SD), or medians with IQRs, based on the distribution of the data. The effect of the intervention on other binary outcomes will be assessed with risk ratio and 95% CIs calculated with Wald’s likelihood ratio approximation test and with χ2 tests for hypothesis testing. For continuous variables we will run a two-sample equal-variance t-test for the effect. Linear regression models with the intervention as the independent variable and continuous parameters as the dependent variable will be developed.

Missing values

Concerning both primary and secondary outcomes on postoperative fluids, we anticipated less than 5% of missing values. Sensitivity analyses replacing missing data by best-case and worst-case values to check the robustness of results will be performed. In addition, in case there are more than 5% missing values, the missing data mechanism will need to be assessed and multiple imputation techniques will be considered if applicable.

Missing data on secondary endpoints on complications, outcome and recovery will not be imputed.

Safety analysis

We have incorporated Bayesian safety monitoring into the protocol for early detection of potential harm in the intervention arm. We will monitor safety, defined as any AE leading to reduction or stopping of argipressin infusion in the intervention arm (‘safety event’), with probability of occurrence θ. Safety will be monitored after five and ten patients have been included in the intervention arm. In case two out of five or three out of ten patients experience any AE leading to reduction or stopping of the argipressin infusion, the trial will be stopped for safety reasons.

Ethics and dissemination

This trial is conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, in accordance with the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO), and is consistent with the international Conference on harmonisation of Good Clinical Practice. The study protocol, including the patient information sheet and informed consent form, has been reviewed and approved by the Dutch Medical Ethics Review Committee. All participants will be asked to provide written informed consent (online supplemental file 2) before any study-specific procedures are initiated.

Strengths and limitations

This trial is the first randomised clinical trial to assess the effects of vasopressin as a primary vasopressor, compared with norepinephrine, on intraoperative fluid administration during CRS-HIPEC. The intervention is low-risk, as vasopressin has been extensively studied across diverse patient populations and is routinely used in clinical practice at low doses, with a low incidence of adverse effects. The study is conducted in two centres, enhancing the generalisability of the findings. Monitoring of organ ischaemia, renal function, microcirculation, and immune response, alongside patient-reported outcomes using the QoR-15 questionnaire, to evaluate safety and perioperative impact.

The study is not placebo-controlled, as withholding vasoactive therapy would be unethical given the need to maintain a minimum mean arterial pressure throughout the procedure. Furthermore, the open-label nature of the intervention for treating clinicians may introduce performance bias; however, blinding of outcome assessors is implemented to mitigate this risk.

Overall, this study is designed to ensure patient safety while addressing a knowledge gap in perioperative fluid management during CRS-HIPEC, generating evidence that may guide future clinical practice.
