Impact of COVID-19 Pandemic on Testing for Hepatitis B in British Columbia, Canada: An Interrupted Time Series Analysis
Authors: Richard L Morrow, Jean D Makuza, Dahn Jeong, Mike Irvine, Beate Sander, William W L Wong, Yeva Sahakyan, Zoë Greenwald, Hin Hin Ko, Héctor A Velásquez García, Sofia R Bartlett, Jason Wong, Amanda Yu, Mel Krajden, Alnoor Ramji, Ji Hyun Choi, Julia Li, Stanley Wong, Naveed Zafar Janjua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious research suggests the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with reductions in HBV testing early in the pandemic. However, impacts of the pandemic on HBV testing in the longer-term and among people who inject drugs (PWID) are unclear. We investigated the impact of the pandemic and related policies on HBV testing from 2020 to 2022, including among PWID, in British Columbia (BC), Canada.MethodsUsing population data from the BC COVID-19 Cohort, we conducted interrupted time series analyses of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg), HBV DNA, and HBV e-antigen (HBeAg) testing. The study included a prepandemic period (January 2017–February 2020), a transition period (March–May 2020), and pandemic periods in 2020 (June–December), 2021, and 2022.ResultsHBsAg testing decreased by 16.5% (95% CI 13.9–18.9) and HBV DNA testing decreased by 11.6% (95% CI 9.5–13.6) in June–December 2020 relative to predicted levels, and testing remained lower than predicted throughout 2021 and 2022. Percentage reductions in HBV DNA testing were greater for PWID compared with non-PWID in 2020 (30.0% vs 11.2%) and thereafter. Changes in HBeAg testing overall were less pronounced but varied by sex and age.ConclusionsThe pandemic and related policies were associated with decreases in HBsAg and HBV DNA testing in 2020, and testing remained lower than predicted throughout 2021 and 2022. Additional efforts to increase HBV testing are needed, including strategies to ensure linkage to care for PWID.

Hepatitis B (HBV) infection affects an estimated 257.5 million people globally and 216 000 people in Canada [1]. While vaccines and effective treatments for achieving viral suppression are available, chronic HBV infection remains a cause of serious morbidity and mortality. Canada has endorsed targets of the World Health Organization campaign to eliminate HBV as a public health threat by 2030. As the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted health care services in Canada and other countries, it added to the challenges of meeting HBV elimination targets [2, 3].

Previous research suggests the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with reductions in HBV testing early in the pandemic [4–8]. A survey of clinical centers, which compared HBV testing in January–December 2020 to testing in the previous year, suggests HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) testing was reduced by 39% in European centers and 21% in non-European centers, while HBV DNA testing was reduced by 30% in European centers and 22% in non-European centers [6]. A study from the Canadian province of Ontario found HBsAg testing was reduced by 33% in the first wave of the pandemic (March–August 2020), 18% in the second wave (November 2020–February 2021), and 15% in the third wave (March–May 2021) and that HBV DNA testing was reduced by 37%, 27%, and 20% in each consecutive wave [8].

In BC, universal HBV testing is recommended during pregnancy and one-time HBV testing is recommended for immigrants from HBV-endemic countries [9]. Testing for HBV is also recommended for other groups with risk factors for HBV, such as people who currently use or ever used illicit drugs, persons born between 1945 and 1965, persons who are or were incarcerated, Indigenous people, people who received injections where infection control practices may have been poor, and gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men [9].

While previous studies indicate the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with reductions in HBV testing in Canada and other countries early in the pandemic, the long-term impact of the pandemic on HBV testing is unclear. Additionally, the impact of the pandemic on HBV testing among people who inject drugs (PWID), who represent an important population at high risk of HBV [10] and who have been disproportionately affected by the pandemic [11–13], has not been adequately investigated. In this study, we investigated the impact of the pandemic and related policies on HBV testing—including HBsAg, HBV DNA, and HBV e-antigen (HBeAg) testing—from March 2020 to December 2022 among the full population of BC and by sex and age, prenatal status, and injection drug use status.

METHODS

Data Sources

We used province wide data from the BC COVID-19 Cohort, a platform integrating and linking person-level surveillance and health administrative data and provincial disease registries [14] (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Digital Content). This included data on enrollment in the province's universal public health insurance plan, physician billings, hospital admissions, emergency department visits, laboratory data, and deaths.

Study Population and Design

We used a retrospective cohort study design to investigate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and related policies on HBV testing. Individuals were included in a given month of follow-up during the study period if they had provincial public health coverage on the first day of the month and a 3-year window preceding that month.

The overall study period included a prepandemic period from January 2017 to February 2020, a transition period from March to May 2020, and three pandemic periods: June to December 2020, January to Dec 2021, and January to December 2022. In mid-March 2020, a public health emergency and provincial state of emergency were declared by public officials in BC, which marked the commencement of a range of policies over time to reduce transmission of SARS-CoV-2, such as school closures, a suspension of in-person dining in restaurants, and a recommendation to adopt work-from-home policies [15]. Restrictions on nonessential health services were introduced on 23 March 2020, and later eased on 20 May 2020 [15]. As this period was considerably more restrictive of health services including HBV testing compared with other periods, we designated March to May 2020 as a transition period.

We used interrupted time series analysis with monthly measures of HBV testing to evaluate the association of the pandemic and related policies with changes in HBsAg, HBV DNA, and HBeAg testing in each pandemic period [16, 17]. We conducted analyses of the full population and stratified analyses, including analyses for male and female populations stratified by age group and analyses stratified by injection drug use status, where injection drug use was defined with administrative health data using a validated algorithm (Supplementary Box 1) [18]. Our study also included analyses stratified by prenatal status, where HBV testing outcomes were assessed among women 15 to 49 years of age and classified as prenatal if followed by a hospital delivery within 40 weeks (and otherwise classified as nonprenatal) (Supplementary Table 2) [19, 20].

Outcome Measures

Outcome measures were the monthly number of individuals with an HBsAg test per 100 000 population, the monthly number of individuals with an HBV DNA test, and the monthly number of individuals with an HBeAg test. We identified individuals who had received HBV tests as those with a medical visit where the health provider billed with a BC Medical Services Plan fee item for the relevant test (91765 for HBsAg test, P90831 for HBV DNA test, or T90674 for HBeAg test) [21]. When we compared use of these fee items to identify HBV testing to laboratory data from the provincial laboratory information solution database as a gold standard (which were available for a portion of our study period), we found that the fee item for HBsAg testing had a specificity of 99.6% and sensitivity of 78.3%, the fee item for HBV DNA testing had a specificity of 100.0% and sensitivity of 96.1%, and the fee item for HBeAg testing had a specificity of 100.0% and sensitivity of 80.2%.

Our data sources for this study allowed us to identify when HBV tests were ordered but did not include test results. As we were not able to identify members of the study population who were HBsAg-positive, we could not calculate HBV DNA and HBeAg testing rates based on the number of individuals eligible for these tests. We therefore used monthly counts of the number of individuals who received each test in our analyses of HBV DNA or HBeAg testing. Similarly, we used counts of HBsAg testing when evaluating HBsAg testing among prenatal and nonprenatal women, because our classification of HBV tests based on subsequent hospital delivery did not account for pregnancies that did not come to term and some deliveries (eg, 2.5% of deliveries occurred at home with a registered midwife in 2021/2022) [22].

Statistical Analysis

We used interrupted time series analysis to estimate predicted versus actual change in each outcome measure for each pandemic period, ie, 2020 (June–December), 2021, and 2022. We estimated predicted values using models with a linear time trend to adjust for secular trends, adjustment for seasonality with monthly dummy variables, and adjustment for autocorrelation as necessary [23]. We calculated both the mean absolute difference and the mean percentage difference between monthly predicted and actual outcome measures for each pandemic period and estimated 95% confidence intervals using bootstrapping [24, 25]. Predicted values reflect unobservable outcome values that might have occurred in the absence of the pandemic and related policies (ie, counterfactual outcomes), in contrast to the actual values observed. Statistical analyses were conducted with SAS, version 9.4.

The study was approved by the University of British Columbia Behavioural Research Ethics Board (H20-02097; H21-03595).

Public Involvement Statement

As immigrants from HBV-endemic countries have a higher burden of HBV compared with nonimmigrants and represent the largest risk group for HBV in BC, we sought input on our findings through S.U.C.C.E.S.S., an organization serving immigrants and newcomers in the Lower Mainland of BC. We received feedback on interpreting our findings from S.U.C.C.E.S.S. staff members and through a community circle focus group facilitated by the organization in November 2024. Participants in the community circle (n = 9) were immigrants from China (BC's largest immigrant community), who had been in Canada >10 years and were primarily women and 60 years of age and older. Written consent was obtained from all participants in the Community Circle.

RESULTS

During the prepandemic period, the median monthly number of individuals who received an HBsAg test was 20 136 (interquartile range [IQR] 2318) and the median HBsAg testing rate was 412 (IQR 42) individuals per 100 000 population (Table 1 and 2). During the same period, the median monthly number of individuals with a HBV DNA test was 2564 (IQR 258), and the median monthly number of individuals with an HBeAg test was 487 (IQR 47) (Tables 3 and 4). Individuals under 19 years of age had the lowest rate of HBsAg testing, while PWID had the highest rate of HBsAg testing (Table 1). The median monthly number of individuals 45 to 64 years of age who received an HBV DNA test was higher, compared with those in other age groups; the median monthly number of PWID who received an HBV DNA test was 37 (IQR 12) (Table 3). Similarly, the median monthly number of individuals 45 to 64 years of age who received an HBeAg test was higher, relative to other age groups (Table 4).

The rate of HBsAg testing was gradually increasing during the prepandemic period (Figure 1). HBsAg testing dropped to much lower rates during March to May 2020. This was followed by a partial recovery of HBsAg testing to rates below levels predicted based on prepandemic data during June to December 2020, and the trend in HBsAg testing remained lower than predicted throughout 2021 and 2022. This was reflected in an estimated percentage decrease of 16.5% (95% confidence interval [CI] 13.9% to 18.9%) in HBsAg testing during June to December 2020, which represented 72 (95% CI 59 to 85) fewer individuals per 100 000 population tested each month, relative to predicted HBsAg testing (Table 1). The rate of HBsAg testing was 19.8% lower than predicted in 2021% and 25.7% lower than predicted in 2022. Reductions in the rate of HBsAg testing followed an age gradient for females and males, where percentage reductions were lower for older age groups; the exception to this pattern was HBsAg testing for women 19 to 44 years, which was likely due to prenatal screening for HBV. Percentage reductions in HBsAg testing were similar among PWID and non-PWID during June to December 2020 but slightly greater for non-PWID in 2021 and 2022 (Table 1; Supplementary Figure 1). Among women 15 to 49 years, nonprenatal HBsAg testing decreased relative to predicted values in all pandemic periods; in contrast, prenatal HBsAg testing did not decrease during any pandemic period, instead showing increases of 6.5% (95% CI 3.4% to 9.8%) in June to December 2020 and 3.5% (95% CI 1.1% to 6.0%) in 2021 and no statistically significant difference from predicted values in 2022 (Table 2; Supplementary Figure 2).

The monthly number of individuals with an HBV DNA test followed a gradually increasing trend during the prepandemic period (Figure 2). HBV DNA testing abruptly decreased to much lower levels during March to May 2020, and then recovered to a gradually increasing trend but at a lower level than predicted from June 2020 to December 2022. Monthly HBV DNA testing was reduced by 11.6% (95% CI 9.5% to 13.6%) during June to December 2020, which represented 321 (95% CI 257 to 385) fewer individuals tested each month, relative to predicted HBV DNA testing (Table 3). Similarly, HBV DNA testing was 9.1% lower than predicted in 2021 and 11.0% lower than predicted in 2022. Reductions in HBV DNA testing were observed across age groups for females and males during 2020 to 2022. Percentage reductions in HBV DNA testing were larger for PWID compared with non-PWID in June to December 2020 (30.0% vs 11.2%) through to 2022 (52.1% vs 10.2%), although in absolute terms the number of individuals tested was much higher among non-PWID prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (Table 3; Supplementary Figure 3). Among women 15 to 49 years of age, nonprenatal HBV DNA testing was lower than predicted in all pandemic periods, whereas prenatal HBV DNA testing was not lower than predicted in any pandemic period (Table 3). In 2021 and 2022, point estimates of prenatal HBV DNA testing were almost one-third above predicted values, but 95% confidence intervals were wide and the estimated difference from predicted values was only significant in 2021 (representing a potential increase in 1 to 9 women receiving an HBV DNA test each month) (Table 3).

The monthly number of individuals with an HBeAg test was stable during the prepandemic period (Supplementary Figure 4). HBeAg testing decreased abruptly to lower levels during March to May 2020, and then recovered to close to prepandemic levels from June 2020 through to December 2022. Overall, no statistically significant changes in HBeAg testing in June to December 2020 or in 2021 were observed, but HBeAg testing was 6.2% (95% CI 2.2% to 9.7%) lower than predicted during 2022 (Table 4). Stratified analyses suggested that the pandemic was associated with changes in HBeAg testing that varied by sex and age. Among females 45 years and above, HBeAg testing showed a statistically significant decrease of ∼10% in 2021 and decreased further in 2022. Among males 45 to 64 years of age, HBeAg testing decreased by 11.9% in 2022. In contrast, monthly HBeAg testing increased by 18.5% during June to December 2020 among males 65 years and above and remained above predicted levels during 2021 and 2022 (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This population-based study showed that the COVID-19 pandemic and related policies were associated with a reduction in both HBsAg and HBV DNA testing during June to December 2020 relative to predicted levels and remained lower than predicted throughout 2021 and 2022. Changes in HBsAg testing tended to follow an age gradient where percentage reductions were lower for older age groups. Percentage decreases in HBV DNA testing were consistently greater for PWID compared with non-PWID for all pandemic periods. The pandemic and related policies were not associated with decreases in prenatal HBsAg testing or HBV DNA testing. In summary, HBsAg and HBV DNA testing decreased during 2020 and did not recover to prepandemic levels in 2021 or 2022, whereas changes in HBeAg testing were overall less pronounced but varied considerably by sex and age.

Findings from this study provide information about the association between the COVID-19 pandemic and changes to HBV testing used for screening and care. The decrease in HBsAg and HBV DNA testing might have been related to a greater focus on COVID-19-related care and caution to avoid straining testing resources by health professionals, as well as reduced health-seeking behavior by patients. The decrease in HBV DNA testing could in part reflect decreased HBsAg testing and consequently decreased identification of new HBsAg-positive cases. In addition, health professionals might have felt that monitoring through HBV DNA testing could reasonably be deferred for some patients during the pandemic, as reflected in guidance published by the Canadian Association for the Study of the Liver advising that “especially with respect to untreated individuals without advanced liver disease, it would be prudent to defer routine viral load testing” and that “[i]t is reasonable to defer HBV DNA testing for patients who are adherent to long-term potent nucleos(t)ide analog therapy with a low risk of resistance.” [26]

Our study showed that HBsAg and HBV DNA testing remained lower than predicted throughout 2021 and 2022, although the reasons for this are unclear. While the province of BC ended its state of emergency due to COVID-19 in mid-2021 and the College of Physicians and Surgeons of BC encouraged health practitioners to resume in-person visits in early September 2021 [15], it is possible that the continued strain on primary health care services in BC and the shift to more virtual care among general practitioners and specialists impeded a recovery in HBV screening and testing [27]. When asked about our study's findings about the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on hepatitis B testing, immigrants from the Chinese community in BC's Lower Mainland who participated in a Community Circle focus group spoke about the increased pressure on the health care system during the pandemic. Participants also spoke about the increasing difficulty of finding a family doctor in recent years and reported they had to wait longer to get appointments with a family doctor, which was exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic. While the difficulty in finding a family doctor or making timely appointments did not begin with the COVID-19 pandemic, the doctor shortage and difficulties in accessing primary care may have been a barrier to hepatitis B testing returning to expected levels even by late 2022.

Our study also revealed some variation in the association of the pandemic with changes in HBV testing among different population groups. Percentage reductions in HBsAg testing were lower among older age groups during all pandemic periods. While percentage reductions in HBsAg testing were similar among PWID and non-PWID, relative reductions in HBV DNA testing were consistently greater among PWID. This suggests PWID who are HBsAg-positive faced barriers to continued monitoring and linkage to care, such as challenges in accessing care through telemedicine or the need to schedule appointments for lab testing during the pandemic.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that found the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with decreases in HBsAg and HBV DNA testing early in the pandemic, including an international survey of European and non-European centers and a descriptive study using data from Ontario [6, 8]. The latter study also reported that prenatal HBsAg testing was stable and reductions in HBsAg testing were lower for people over 65 years [8]. Similarly, we found prenatal HBsAg testing did not decrease and reductions in HBsAg testing were attenuated for those 65 years and above; additionally, our study was able to go a step further and show that impacts on HBV screening followed an age gradient, where increasing age was associated with lower reductions in screening. A key contribution of our study was that we investigated longer-term impacts of the pandemic on HBV testing and found that both HBsAg and HBV DNA testing remained at lower levels than predicted throughout 2021 and 2022. These findings highlight that additional efforts are needed to increase HBV testing in BC. We also showed PWID experienced greater relative decreases in HBV DNA testing, suggesting they faced barriers to continued monitoring and linkage to care.

Limitations of our study merit consideration. The use of physician fee item codes to identify HBV testing showed high specificity for all tests but only moderate sensitivity for HBsAg and HBeAg tests, leading to some outcome misclassification. Similarly, our classification of HBV tests as prenatal based on subsequent hospital delivery did not account for all pregnancies, leading to some misclassification of prenatal status. As we lacked HBsAg test results, we were unable to calculate HBV DNA and HBeAg testing rates based on the number of individuals eligible for these tests. Consequently, we used monthly counts of the number of individuals who received each test in our analyses of HBV DNA or HBeAg testing, and our estimates of the impact of the pandemic on these tests may in part reflect the decreased identifiation of new HBsAg-positive cases. While we included analyses of important subpopulations, we were unable to disaggregate analyses for other priority populations, such as individuals who were born in or resided in regions where HBV is more common and people who with experience of incarceration [10]. Also, we were unable to evaluate the impact of the pandemic in rural compared with urban areas due to varied capture of testing data from rural areas; however, most (>90%) testing is conducted in the urban population.

CONCLUSIONS

The COVID-19 pandemic and related policies were associated with decreases in HBsAg and DNA testing during June to December 2020 in BC, and testing remained lower than predicted throughout 2021 and 2022. Percentage reductions in HBsAg testing were attenuated for older age groups and among prenatal women, while percentage reductions in HBV DNA testing were greater among PWID. Meeting HBV elimination goals in BC will require additional efforts to increase HBV testing, including strategies to ensure linkage to care for PWID. Findings of this study provide valuable data for future modeling related to ongoing monitoring of progress toward HBV elimination.
