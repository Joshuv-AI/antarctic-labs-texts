A case study on entropy-aware block-based linear transforms for lossless image compression
Authors: Borut Žalik, David Podgorelec, Ivana Kolingerová, Damjan Strnad, Štefan Kohek
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractData compression algorithms tend to reduce information entropy, which is crucial, especially in the case of images, as they are data intensive. In this regard, lossless image data compression is especially challenging. Many popular lossless compression methods incorporate predictions and various types of pixel transformations, in order to reduce the information entropy of an image. In this paper, a block optimisation programming framework Φ is introduced to support various experiments on raster images, divided into blocks of pixels. Eleven methods were implemented within Φ, including prediction methods, string transformation methods, and inverse distance weighting, as a representative of interpolation methods. Thirty-two different greyscale raster images with varying resolutions and contents were used in the experiments. It was shown that Φ reduces information entropy better than the popular JPEG LS and CALIC predictors. The additional information associated with each block in Φ is then evaluated. It was confirmed that, despite this additional cost, the estimated size in bytes is smaller in comparison to the sizes achieved by the JPEG LS and CALIC predictors.Keywords: Computer science, Information entropy, Prediction, Inverse distance transform, String transformationsSubject terms: Computer science, Software

Introduction

Storing knowledge and data for future generations is among the most important responsibilities of society. This task has been made easier by today’s advancements in information and communication technologies. However, the amount of data is increasing rapidly due to massive digitalisation and omnipresent sensors. Because the technological advance cannot keep pace with the explosion of modern data generating devices, it remains crucial to store such vast amounts of data in the most efficient way possible, which is why data compression is employed frequently.

Data compression is one of the pioneering disciplines in Computer Science, starting with the works of Shannon1, Fano2 and Huffman3. Initially, it was motivated by the limited computer memory, both on-board and in peripheral storage devices. Today, however, the compression is indispensable for data archiving and various computer-supported applications in telecommunications, geographic information systems, environment observation, medicine, wireless sensor networks, engineering, entertainment and other fields. In general, data compression methods can be divided roughly according to two criteria: they can be domain-dependent or independent, and they may be lossless or lossy. Numerous approaches from all categories have been collected in various books4,5 and surveys6–8.

Compression of data is achieved by removing redundancies from the input data, which are, typically, considered as raw data. The redundancy in data is related strongly to entropy when the compression is lossless. Entropy is a well-established concept in science, related to disorder, randomness and uncertainty9. The concept of entropy in Computer Science was introduced by Shannon1, who named it information entropy.

Let X be the sequence of elements from the input (or raw) data, i.e., X=⟨xi⟩, 0≤i<|X|, where |X| denotes the length of the sequence. X consists of elements from the alphabet Σ={xj}, 0≤j<|Σ|. Each element xj∈Σ can be assigned a probability pj of its appearance in X. The information entropy H(X) is then obtained by Eq. 1.

H(X) determines the minimal number of bits required to encode each element xj∈Σ. When applying these codes on the symbols from X, the output sequence Y is obtained, and its length can be calculated by Eq. 2.

|Y| can be achieved asymptotically by the classical entropy encoders, such as Huffman or arithmetic coding4.

The concept is general, regardless of the type of data being compressed. In contrast to text, some other data, such as images or sounds, may be compressed as either lossy or lossless. Lossy compression typically involves the transformations Tf:xi↦fi, which map the input elements xi∈Σ to the coefficients fi∈R in the frequency space. That enables an elimination of high frequency coefficients, to introduce losses that are difficult for users to notice. Discrete cosine/sine transform10 or various types of wavelet transforms11 are typical examples of Tf. The remaining coefficients fi are then encoded losslessly.

On the other hand, the lossless compression, which is the focus of this paper, often applies the concept of predictions. The predictions are also transformations; however, this time they transform the elements xi∈Σ to the domain of integer numbers, i.e., T:xi↦yi, xi∈Σ, yi∈N. The predicted values x^i are subtracted from the actual xi

to get the sequence of errors E=⟨ei⟩. If the predictions are adequate, E tends to have many small integer values. In this way, the statistical properties of E differ from those of X, resulting, hopefully, in a smaller information entropy, i.e., H(E)<H(X). As a consequence, |YE|=H(E)·|E|<|YX|=H(X)·|X| where, of course, |E|=|X|.

Lossless compression of images is particularly challenging, because they are typically data intensive, and contain noise and other artifacts that lossless compression must not discard. Therefore, lossless greyscale raster image compression is considered in this paper. Characteristics of raster images differ according to the captured scenes. It can be expected that different scenes are predicted better with various methods. Furthermore, this is true, even for the individual parts, and even pixels in the same image. Therefore, different predictors should be used for different parts of the image. In this case, the information about the selected predictor has to be stored near each part of the image. As accompanying information of which predictor is the best for each pixel would increase the space requirement significantly, the image can be organised into blocks, where the best predictor is determined for each block individually.

In this paper, various predictors are utilised at the block level. The best predictors are then applied to individual blocks of the image, and the obtained information entropy is calculated. This information, together with the associated information of the blocks, is used to estimate the actual size of the compressed image. The main contributions of this paper are summarised as follows:

Background

Compression of continuous-tone raster images (i.e., photographs) has a long tradition, and, therefore, there are a vast number of solutions, which are covered well in the literature4,5. In this comprehensive overview, traditional linear prediction methods will be considered, followed by two string transformations techniques (Move-To-Front and Move-with-Interleaving) and an interpolation method (Inverse Distance Weighting).

Let X represent a greyscale raster image with pixels xi,j, where 0≤i<M, 0≤j<N, and M and N correspond to the image resolution. The value of pixel xi,j∈Σ, Σ=[0,2c-1], determines its intensity, where c corresponds to the number of bit-planes used. To reduce the information entropy of an image, the original pixel values can be replaced by prediction residuals, transformed values obtained from string transformations, or interpolated values. In the following sections, we provide a brief overview of popular approaches within these three categories.

String transformations

String transformation methods may also reduce the information entropy of X. Various string transformation techniques have been proposed, including Move-To-Front (MTF)22, Inversion Frequencies (IF)24, Burrows-Wheeler Transform (BWT)25, and others26,27. However, not all string transformation techniques are suitable for the study discussed in Section 3. For example, the Burrows-Wheeler Transform (BWT), which may rearrange the pixel values such that the same values tend to be close together, would not be efficient for two reasons:

the sequences in blocks of pixels would be too short to obtain the desired grouping, and

the sequences in blocks of pixels would be too short to obtain the desired grouping, and

inverse BWT requires additional information (the so-called BWT index), which would represent an additional overhead.

inverse BWT requires additional information (the so-called BWT index), which would represent an additional overhead.

The Inversion Frequencies transform is also unsuitable. IF calculates the distance between the identical pixels, starting from 0 and ending at 255. However, it is not expected that all pixels are presented in each block. Specifying which values actually exist in the considered block would represent an unacceptable overhead. Move-To-Front and Move-with-Interleaving do not have such limitations, and are, therefore, considered briefly in the continuation.

Move-To-Front Move-To-Front22,23 is a well-known string transformation technique, which transforms the input sequence X=⟨xi⟩, where xi∈Σ, to the output sequence Y=⟨yi⟩, where yi∈{0,1,2,⋯|X|-1}, i.e., to positive integer values. A list L is applied to perform a transformation. L is filled by all the elements Σ. The X=⟨xi⟩, 0≤i<|X|-1 is then scanned element by element. The location of each xi is found in L, its position is sent to Y, and xi is moved to the front of L.

Move-with-Interleaving Move-with-Interleaving (MwI)21 is similar to MTF, but when xi is located more than a user-defined parameter t from the beginning of L, 2t+1 interleaved values around xi, i.e., xi,xi+1,xi-1,xi+2,xi-2,⋯,xi+t,xi-t, are moved to the front of L. MwI has proven to be an efficient alternative for BWT, followed by MTF in reducing the information entropy in raster images, i.e., H(MTF(BWT(X)))≈H(MwI(X)). It also does not need any additional information for performing the inverse transform.

Interpolation

Interpolations have not been used in lossless image compression, as they do not cope well with possible sudden changes in the values that may appear in raster images. Indeed, in geometric modelling and CAD systems32, computer graphics34, and geographic information systems29,30,33, for example, they have been designed to assure smoothness of the interpolated values with respect to the known values. Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation is applied in this paper. IDW is a well-known interpolation technique proposed by Shepard35. IDW gives more influence to nearby pixels, which is a desired property in pixel prediction. However, it needs to be adapted slightly to be used as a predictor in raster images. Specifically, only pixels closer than the user-specified distance D∈N to the predicted pixel xi,j, as well as pixels that have already been visited in the raster scan order, are used for this purpose. Materials and methods

This Section presents a block optimisation-based framework Φ36 given in the pseudocode of the Algorithm 2. It evaluates the effects of various transformations Tt, 0≤t<NoOfT, on the information entropy H(Y) in greyscale raster images, where Y is a matrix with transformed coefficients. Each image X is divided into equally-sized blocks, where the number of blocks Q is given by Eq. 11, where M and N correspond to the image resolution, while BSize is the size of the block. The rightmost or bottom-most blocks may be smaller and unequally sized.

All transformations except MTF and MwI do predict/interpolate the values, and they are subtracted from the actual pixel values according to Eq. 3. This results in positive or negative values of ei. They are transformed into only positive values by Eq. 12, as done, for example, in JPEG LS.

After the transformation ti is performed and a matrix E+ has been obtained, E+ is evaluated in Line 10. There are various possibilities for how to perform this evaluation. The most straightforward method would be to calculate the information entropy of the block. However, as there are considerably fewer coefficients in E+ than in X, the local statistical property of E+ may not contribute optimally to the global one. For example, if there were just one value in E+, say 200, the information entropy would be the smallest possible, i.e., 0. However, it is expected that the majority of the transformed values in the whole X tend to be small. The value 200 would be rare globally, and, therefore, this block could contribute to an increase in the final information entropy. Another option would be to calculate the Standard Deviation of E+. However, even if the coefficients of E+ are large, its Standard Deviation could still be small. This is why the sum of E+ coefficients is used in Φ. The index of the best transform and the best E+ is then stored temporarily in Lines 11 – 15.

When all transforms are executed, the best matrix of errors EBest are incorporated into the matrix Y, while the index of the transform tBest is stored in an auxiliary list in Line 17. The new block coordinates are then determined in Line 18, and the process is repeated until all the blocks have been examined.

Conclusions

In this paper, a block optimisation-based programming framework Φ has been presented, with the aim of studying various transformation methods for lossless image compression when the image is divided into blocks. Eleven transformation methods were applied to each block. These transforms achieved improvements compared to the JPEG LS and CALIC predictors on all the benchmark photographs. After that, the three most frequent transformations were selected for each image. The number of bytes used for transformed values and the associated block control bits is calculated. Φ requires fewer bytes than when the whole image is transformed with just one method; the JPEG LS and CALIC predictors were used as the reference. Although the gain is not dramatic, it is consistent, and offers new possibilities for experiments and development. Therefore, we can conclude that dynamically selected predictors per block outperform statically selected predictors, even when space requirements are considered for the selected predictor per block. The sensitivity of the results with regard to the block size were performed finally. The experiments showed that there is no significant difference between block sizes of 6, 8, 12, or 16.

In the proposed framework, different but predefined predictors have been used at the block level for lossless compression. The first obvious extension of this paper is adding support for lossy and near-lossless compression by introducing a quantization step. However, in this context, it is more appropriate to introduce additional transformations that have been used mainly in the past in the context of lossy compression, such as DFT, DCT, and DWT. Thus, different combinations of performing predictions on the DCT coefficients could reduce the entropy of the residuals further. Another interesting possible research direction is the use of spatial statistical analysis, where established statistical models, such as those based on Markov Random Fields39–41, could be integrated into the predictors. By treating pixels or even groups of pixels as nodes in an undirected graph when modelling predictions, there is a new opportunity to eliminate the need to use rectangular blocks, as they can have any shape or be eliminated altogether.
