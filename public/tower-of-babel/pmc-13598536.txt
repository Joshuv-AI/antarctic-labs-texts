An exploratory study on tick control in the field
Authors: Yan-Dong Wang, Shun-Shuai Liu, Yi-Chuan Yang, Jun Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A field test for tick control was carried out using a 10% lambda-cyhalothrin microcapsule suspension (Weibao®10% lambda-cyhalothrin CS, Jiangsu Gongcheng Bio-tech Co., Ltd.) to kill ticks and prevent diseases during field crowd-gathering activities or temporary resettlement sites after disasters. In this study, three field test sites were selected, and each experimental site was set up with a test plot and a control plot. Before spraying, the density of ticks in three test plots and three control plots was investigated by the flagging method, and then the insecticide at a concentration of 0.03% was sprayed using a motor mist sprayer and an electric constant sprayer. After spraying, the effect on tick abundance was continuously monitored. On days 1, 7, 14, 21 and 28 after spraying, the treatment efficacy in three test plots was high (80–100%) in most cases. Wild animals, sheep and dogs were not prohibited from entering test sites in the test and control areas. Spraying 0.03% lambda-cyhalothrin microcapsule suspension could maintain a low-density tick state for about three weeks. This study also provides an approach for controlling the epidemic situation by killing ticks during high incidence of tick-borne diseases.

Introduction

Ticks are blood-sucking arthropods that are widely distributed around the world. From 2005 to 2020, the number of reported cases of vector-borne infectious diseases in China showed a fluctuating downward trend, while the number of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) cases showed a significant upward trend (Liu Qi-yong, 2022). Ticks are the main vector of SFTS (Yu et al., 2011), and Haemaphysalis longicornis is a major vector of SFTSV (Peng et al., 2025). Ticks possess a wide range of host selectivity and the ability to efficiently transmit pathogens, making them an important vector group after mosquitoes (Dantas-Torres et al., 2012). Ticks can transmit bacteria, viruses, protozoans, and other pathogens (Fang et al., 2015), posing a serious threat to human and animal health and animal husbandry; ticks parasitize livestock and suck blood, causing problems such as anemia, weight loss, and physical weakness, especially in young animals (Butler and Trout Fryxell, 2023). Ticks occur in a wide range of environments, such as forests, grasslands, shrubs, and urban parks (Arz et al., 2023). Although current research on ticks, tick-borne diseases, and the pathogens they carry has become more in-depth, more people are bitten by ticks in the wild, especially in vast rural areas. Compared with a series of pathogen screening and diagnosis after tick bites, reducing tick density and preventing tick bites is also a disease prevention approach.

Zibo City is located in the central region of Shandong Province, with many mountainous hills in the south (Fig. 1). During 2010–2024, a total of 459 SFTS cases were reported in Zibo City, including 32 deaths, with a mortality rate of 6.97% (Zhao et al., 2026). Materials and methods

Experimental sites

Three vegetation-rich hillside wastelands were selected as experimental sites in Zichuan County, Zibo City, Shandong Province (Site 1: 36°32′45.50″N; 118°06′50.26″E; Site 2: 36°32′53.61″N, 118°06′43.92″E; Site 3: 36°32′52.09″N, 118°06′39.05″E; see map in Supplementary Fig. S1). The vegetation was dominated by weeds and low shrubs (< 60 cm in height). In the past three years, the species of questing ticks monitored in this area were all identified as H. longicornis. A tick density peak occurred from July to August, with nymphs and adult ticks as the main life stages present (Tufts et al., 2019; Jung and Lee, 2023), which was convenient for observing the control effect on ticks. The surrounding villagers often keep sheep in this area. Three sites, Site 1 (700 m2), Site 2 (1000 m2) and Site 3 (1000 m2) were used as test plots (spraying areas), respectively, and the control plots were set up in roughly the same area around them. Under the premise of meeting the experimental requirements, according to the local actual topography, the selected area (mountain) was different. The experimental area and the control area were separated by more than 10 m (arranged according to the terrain); the three test plots were separated by more than 300 m. The test plots met the basic requirements of a background tick density ≥ 10 ticks/flag per hour. The insecticide was applied in the test area and not applied in the control area. The vegetation and light (on the same side of the mountain) of all six test plots were similar. Supplementary Table S1 provides weather data over the 28-day experimental observation period.

Acaricide application

A 10% lambda-cyhalothrin microcapsule suspension (Weibao®10% lambda-cyhalothrin CS, Jiangsu Gongcheng Bio-tech Co., Ltd.) was used. The application equipment consisted of an ordinary electric constant sprayer and a motorised mist sprayer. The spray direction was oblique downward, and the spray distance was less than 5 m.

The experiment began in mid-July. The weather was sunny on the day of application. An electric constant sprayer was used in Site 1, and a mobile mist sprayer was used in Sites 2 and 3. The average dosage was 60 ml/m2, and the reagent concentration was 0.03% (10% lambda-cyhalothrin microcapsule suspension diluted 300 times). Tick monitoring

The density of questing ticks was monitored before and after the test. Dragging was conducted for 0.5 h in the test area (test plot) by the cloth flag method, and the collected ticks were identified using Baolin and Houyong (2003). The ticks were not placed back into the site, and when monitoring questing ticks with the flag method, the routes of flag towing were monitored multiple times. The monitoring routes did not overlap as much as possible, and the density was calculated after counting using the formula D = N/T, where D is tick density, N is the total number of ticks collected, and T is the time of collecting ticks, in hours (h).

Verification of the control effect

After spraying, the density of questing ticks in the test (spraying) plot and the control plot was monitored by the cloth flag method after 1, 7, 14, 21 and 28 days, respectively. The changes in tick density before and after spraying were compared, and the treatment efficacy was calculated using the formula E (%) = (C–T)/C × 100, where C is the mean density of ticks in the control plot, and T is the mean density of ticks in the test plot. The effect was considered significant at a treatment efficacy of ≥ 80% on the first day after application, and monitoring of the follow-up time was used to assess the duration of spraying efficacy.

The average density of ticks at Site 2 was 28/flag per h in the test plot, and 24/flag per h in the control plot. The activity range of ticks is very small unless they attach to the host and are transferred over a large distance, so the distribution of ticks is assumed to be random. Tick density monitoring after pesticide application in the experimental area

The density of questing ticks was monitored on Days 1, 7, 14, 21 and 28 after spraying in the three test plots. The ticks captured in the test and the control plots were classified and identified. All of them were identified as H. longicornis, most of them were adult ticks; a small number of nymphs were recovered, and there were no larval ticks. On Days 1–28 after spraying, the number of ticks in the test plot of Site 1 ranged between 0 and 38/flag per h, whereas the range in the control plot was 42–140/flag per h. No ticks were captured in the test plot of Site 2, and the number of ticks in the control plot ranged between 4 and 54 ticks/flag per h. The number of ticks in the test plot of Site 3 ranged between 0 and 2/flag per h, whereas that in the control plot ranged between 65 and 119 ticks/flag per h, respectively, in the control plot (Fig. 2, Table 2).Fig. 2Monitoring of tick density in the test and control plots of the three experimental sites on Days 1, 7, 14, 21, and 28.

The treatment efficacy was 100% on the first day after application at all three sites. After 7, 14, 21 and 28 days, tick density in the test plots was distinctly lower than in the control plots, overall and at each site. The treatment efficacy ranged between 32.1% and 100%. Only on Day 28, the treatment efficacy at Site 1 was 32.1%, i.e. much lower than the set target value of 80%, whereas the treatment efficacy was 100% at Site 2 and 98.5% at Site 3.

Discussion

Our study showed that the effect of spraying a 10% lambda-cyhalothrin microcapsule suspension at a concentration of 0.03% is characterised by high treatment efficacy and persistence, and the advantages are significant. First, the high efficacy and short action time resulted in a quick reduction of tick density after a single application. Secondly, the residual activity is lasting and can be maintained for three to four weeks.

The use of 10% lambda-cyhalothrin microcapsule suspension in the present natural environment tick control test resulted in an excellent immediate treatment efficacy rate (100% on Day 1) against H. longicornis. After one month of continuous observation, tick density in the three test plots remained low (efficacy range: 97.2–100%), indicating that the use of the insecticide for a single tick-control operation can suppress the density of ticks in the area for about three weeks. Notably, in two of the test plots, the control efficacy was high on Day 28 (98.5–100%). This actual control effect can serve as a reference for crowd-gathering activities in the field and for the prevention and control of ticks in temporary resettlement sites in the field after a disaster, and also provides basic data for broadening the application scenarios of similar insecticides. Our results thus provide a new means for carrying out tick eradication work in major venues during high incidence of tick-borne diseases, reducing the risk of tick bites to humans, thereby reducing the risk of disease occurrence.

Field studies on tick control with lambda-cyhalothrin are few (Jurisic et al., 2010; Jurišić et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2015; Park et al., 2019; Bickerton et al., 2021, 2022). Jurisic et al. (2010) studied the efficacy of lambda-cyhalothrin against ixodid ticks (Ixodes ricinus and Dermacentor marginatus) in areas with high epidemiological risk caused by tick bites. These authors reported treatment efficacy for up to 28 days during 3 years at four sites in Serbia, ranging between 38.4% and 95.6% on Day 1 post-treatment and between 61.9% and 100% on Day 28 post-treatment. Jurisic et al. (2010) suggested that high efficacy in tick control is observed in localities that were improved before acaricide application, e.g. mowed, with litter removed and the abundance of stray cats and dogs controlled. Jurišić et al. (2023) assessed the efficacy of lambda-cyhalothrin against I. ricinus, D. marginatus and Haemaphysalis punctata in a range of natural habitats in Serbia. These authors reported efficacy ranges as follows: Day 1 post-treatment: 74.3–76.4%; Day 7 post-treatment: 78.2–88.6%; Day 14 post-treatment: 81.5–94.7; Day 21 post-treatment: 79.7–91.3%. Our results reveal a similar residual effect (21–28 days), a strong immediate toxic effect (efficacy of 100% on Day 1 post-treatment) and an overall greater efficacy thereafter (88.9–100%) except for the test plot of Site 1 on Day 28 (Table 2).

To the best of our knowledge, four field (semi-field) studies examined specifically the effect of lambda-cyhalothrin on H. longicornis (Lee et al., 2015; Park et al., 2019; Bickerton et al., 2021, 2022). Lee et al. (2015) revealed a short residual effect in a semi-field test (acaricide sprayed on field plots and ticks added to cut leaves in the laboratory), i.e. mortality of 60% on Day 1 post-treatment reduced to 13% on Day 7 post-treatment. In a similar semi-field test, Park et al. (2019) recorded adult tick mortality of 100%, 33.3% and 16.7% and nymph tick mortality of 100%, 70% and 46.7% on Days 1, 7 and 10 post-treatment, respectively. The data from the two semi-field studies align well with the treatment efficacy recorded by us on Day 1 (100%), but show poor residual effect (efficacy of 88.9–100% and 100% on Days 1 and 7, respectively, in the present study, Table 2) that was also examined within a shorter time frame (7–10 days post-treatment).

Our results are consistent with the results for the tick-eradication effect of lambda-cyhalothrin applied in the field against H. longicornis, i.e. 100% control in single or sequential applications on Day 1 and that a single application provided 100% control for up to seven weeks (Bickerton et al., 2021). A second field study by Bickerton et al. (2022) confirmed that a single application of lambda-cyhalothrin is highly effective at controlling adults, nymphs and larvae of H. longicornis (an average of 99.1% control).

Lambda-cyhalothrin has the effect of driving out, knocking down and poisoning insects (Gao et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). Microcapsule suspension is an advanced dosage form that encapsulates active ingredients in polymer capsule walls and disperses in water. It has the advantages of slow release and improved stability (Wang et al., 2018). The surrounding areas of the test sites are mostly mountainous terraces used for corn, peanut, sweet potato and soybean cultivation. Local farmers often use pyrethroid pesticides to control pests in the field; thus, the present experiment did not bring new ecological threats to the local environment. Lambda-cyhalothrin may cause harm to fish, bees, silkworms and other insects. This has been considered in the selection of the experimental sites, and we have verified that there are no fishponds, beehives, silkworm rooms or mulberry gardens near the sites. On-site staff have also taken appropriate protective measures (medical protective masks, gloves, hats, protective clothing, boot sets, etc.).

In order to positively evaluate the effect of killing ticks, during the selection of the test and control plots before the insecticide application, plots with high initial tick density were used as the application (test) areas, and plots with lower density were used as the control areas. If the ticks with higher density can be killed, it is reasonable to infer that the ticks with lower density can also be killed. The background survey of tick density was a single survey, and there were variations in the data of questing ticks monitored by the flag method. Nevertheless, tick density was much higher than the preset basic requirements for the experimental plots (10 ticks/flag per h); tick density in the experimental area has been greater than this basic requirement for many years. The distance between the experimental and the control plot was more than 10 m, and the plots were selected according to the terrain to avoid the influence of drug dispersion. The three test plots are separated by more than 300 m, and judging from the terrain, they are relatively independent, so that insecticide spraying in each plot would not affect the others.

The limitations of this study include the following. First, as time progresses, collecting questing ticks using the flag method and bringing them to the laboratory for identification may reduce tick density in the area, leading to statistical bias in the results. If the tick density in the selected experimental plots is low, this may affect the interpretation of the results. However, based on the experimental process, the number of ticks detected in the control area far exceeded that in the treated area, which only serves to mitigate the assessment of tick control efficacy, a finding that does not conflict with the positive confirmation of the treatment’s effectiveness. Secondly, the test area is an open environment where flocks of sheep regularly pass through, and wild animals such as rodents are present. The introduction of new ticks is unavoidable and may influence the test results, potentially underestimating the duration of the tick control effect. Thirdly, the trial was conducted during the peak tick density period, which coincided with summer and high precipitation. Heavy rainfall in the mountainous terrain may have affected tick density. Rainfall occurred on the third day after application in the test plot, and over the 28-day observation period, rain was recorded on 13 days in total. Prior to density monitoring on Day 21, heavy rain had just fallen, and there were signs of soil erosion in both the experimental and control areas (more pronounced in Plots 1 and 2). The observed tick density in the control areas was also relatively low at that time; the rainwater runoff may also have led to an underestimation of the duration of the tick control effect.

In summary, for the prevention of tick-borne infectious diseases, the reduction of tick density and thus prevention of tick bites is key. In high-tick-density seasons, targeted tick-control interventions can be carried out in local areas to rapidly reduce tick density and maintain a low density for a long time, thereby reducing tick bites and the probability of disease occurrence. This approach needs further experimental support from field studies.

Conclusions

This study was conducted during the summer, when ticks were active, and tick density was high. By spraying a highly effective, low-toxicity insecticide, tick density was rapidly reduced and maintained at low levels for an extended period. The novelty of this study lies in conducting field tick control trials under natural outdoor conditions, without restricting animal access to the area. This provides a feasible strategy for controlling tick-borne disease outbreaks through tick eradication during high-incidence periods and offers new insights for tick control and disease prevention at outdoor gathering sites. It is of great significance to control epidemic situations during high incidence of tick-borne diseases.
