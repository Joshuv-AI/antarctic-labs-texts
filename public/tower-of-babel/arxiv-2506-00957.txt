A celestial kinematical interpretation for an extended BMS 4 Preprint: APS/123-QED
Carles Batlle a), Roberto Casalbuoni b), Daniele Dominici b), José Figueroa-O’Farrill c), Joaquim Gomis d)

Abstract
Motivated by the work of Longhi and Materassi, who constructed a realisation of the (centreless) BMS 4 algebra for the massive Klein-Gordon field in 3 + 1 3+1 , we build a realisation of the (centreless) BMS 4 algebra including super-rotations by using celestial coordinates. This realisation depends only on the momenta in the lightcone in the celestial coordinates without any reference to the Klein–Gordon field. The quadratic Casimir of the Lorentz algebra written in terms of a second order differential operator and the volume form plays an essential role in this construction. The BMS algebra in terms of vector fields shows its kinematical nature, like the Poincaré algebra. We also construct a dynamical realisation of BMS from the symplectic structure on the solutions of the massless four-dimensional Klein–Gordon field in terms of quadratic expressions of the Fourier modes.



I
Introduction

The BMS algebra was introduced in
Bondi et al. 1962
;
Sachs 1962
as
the asymptotic symmetry algebra of a four-dimensional flat spacetime
at null infinity. An extended BMS algebra which includes the Poincaré,
super-translations and super-rotations was proposed by

Barnich and Troessaert 2010
. A comprehensive presentation of recent
applications of BMS symmetries can be found in
Strominger 2017
.
Longhi and Materassi
Longhi and Materassi 1999
found a canonical
realisation of the BMS algebra in terms of the natural symplectic
structure on the Fourier modes of a free massive Klein–Gordon (KG)
field in four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime. These authors extend
the translation algebra to include super-translations. In this
construction a fundamental role is played by one of the two Casimir
operators of the Lorentz algebra, through the more general solution of
the eigenfunctions of the Casimir operator.

In this letter we extend this approach, by constructing a realisation
of super-translations and super-rotations, which depend only on the
momenta in the lightcone in the celestial coordinates and therefore
showing its kinematic character. We obtain a realization of this
algebra in terms of the vector fields of degrees zero and one. This
construction should be valid for any free relativistic massless
theory. As an example we derive the BMS generators in the case of the
KG field.

III
Lorentz Casimir and extended BMS algebra

III.1
BMS algebra

To build an extension of the Poincaré algebra containing super-translations, we start, as shown in
Longhi and Materassi 1999
, from the Casimir operator

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(M_{0i}^{2})-M_{12}^{2}-M_{23}^{2}-M_{31}^{2}

\displaystyle=-{\omega}(3\partial_{\omega}+{\omega}\partial^{2}_{\omega})

(12)

where we have used the explicit expression of the Lorentz generators
in celestial coordinates given in eqs. (
3
,
4
). The
second Casimir operator turns out to be zero,
\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}M^{\mu\nu}M^{\rho\sigma}=0
. Since we know
that a general four-vector is eigenfunction of the
C_{2}
operator with
eigenvalue -3, we consider the regular eigenfunctions of the Casimir

C_{2}
corresponding to the same eigenvalue:

\displaystyle C_{2}f({\omega},z,{\bar{z}})

\displaystyle=-{\omega}(3\partial_{\omega}+{\omega}\partial^{2}_{\omega})f({\omega},z,{\bar{z}})

\displaystyle=-3f({\omega},z,{\bar{z}}).

(13)

The Casimir operator does not contain differentiation operators with
respect to
z,{\bar{z}}
. The regular solution is given by

f({\omega},z)={\omega}g(z,{\bar{z}})

(14)

with
g(z,{\bar{z}})
arbitrary.

Since the solutions given in eq. (
14
) are degenerate and since the commutator of
C_{2}
with the helicity
M_{12}
is zero, we now require that the solution be also eigenfunction of the helicity as in
Longhi and Materassi 1999
, or

M_{12}g(z,{\bar{z}})(z\partial_{z}-{\bar{z}}\partial_{\bar{z}})g(z,{\bar{z}})=\lambda g(z,{\bar{z}}).

(15)

The solution, if we factorize and expand it in Laurent series, is given in terms of
g(z,{\bar{z}})=z^{p}{\bar{z}}^{q}
with
\lambda=p-q
with
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Let us define the super-translations as

P_{-1/2+p,-1/2+q}({\omega},z,{\bar{z}})=2{\omega}z^{p}{\bar{z}}^{q},\quad p,q\in\mathbb{Z},

(16)

or equivalently

P_{k,l}({\omega},z,{\bar{z}})=2{\omega}z^{k+1/2}{\bar{z}}^{l+1/2},\quad k,l\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}.

(17)

The
P
’s in eqs. (
10
) correspond to the choices
p,q=(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)
.
We can check that the BMS algebra given in eq. (
8
) and
eq. (
11
) is satisfied for
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}
.
The role of the Lorentz Casimir was also enphazised in the massive case in a different approach
Iacobacci and Nguyen 2024
.

III.2
Extended BMS algebra

Since the Lorentz generators are first-order differential operators we
consider the general holomorphic first-order differential operator

\xi=\xi^{z}({\omega},z)\partial_{z}+\xi^{\omega}({\omega},z)\partial_{\omega}.

(18)

The condition
[C_{2},\xi]=0
implies

\xi^{z}({\omega},z)=\xi^{z}(z),\quad\xi^{\omega}({\omega},z)={\omega}C(z).

(19)

A second condition comes by requiring that the Lorentz-invariant
volume form

\Omega={\omega}\ d{\omega}\wedge dz\wedge d{\bar{z}}

(20)

be invariant also under super-rotations,
{\cal L}_{\xi}\Omega=0
, or
equivalently, due to

{\cal L}_{\xi}\Omega=(\text{div}\ \xi)\,\,\Omega,

(21)

that
\xi
be divergenceless. Using Cartan’s identity for the Lie
derivative
{\cal L}_{\xi}
with respect to the vector field
\xi
we
get

(\partial_{\omega}+\frac{1}{{\omega}})\xi^{\omega}+\partial_{z}\xi^{z}+\partial_{\bar{z}}\xi^{{\bar{z}}}=0.

(22)

Taking into account only the holomorphic part and plugging (
19
) into (
22
) one gets the relation

2C(z)=-\partial_{z}\xi^{z}(z).

(23)

If we consider the functions
\xi^{z}
and
C
to be expressed in Laurent series

\xi^{z}(z)=\sum_{n}a_{n}z^{n},\quad C(z)=\sum_{n}c_{n}z^{n},

(24)

we obtain the general relation

c_{n}=-\frac{n+1}{2}a_{n}.

(25)

Using this, the generalization of the Lorentz generators
L_{n},n=0,\pm 1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
is given by

L_{n}=-z^{n+1}\partial_{z}+\frac{n+1}{2}z^{n}{\omega}\partial_{\omega},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(26)

and similarly, for the anti-holomorphic part,

\bar{L}_{n}=-{\bar{z}}^{n+1}\partial_{\bar{z}}+\frac{n+1}{2}{\bar{z}}^{n}{\omega}\partial_{\omega},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(27)

Note that the first term in the super-rotations is the standard
representation of the Witt algebra (centerless Virasoro algebra) in
two-dimensional conformal field theory.

The super-translation and super-rotations operators satisfy the
algebra given by eqs. (
11
) for
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
i,j,k,l\in\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}
.

We have then shown that the Poincaré algebra has an
infinite-dimensional extension that we call extended BMS algebra.
This algebra has two interesting infinite-dimensional subalgebras: the
first one obtained extending the Poincaré algebra to BMS and the
second one extending the Lorentz algebra to the Witt algebra. The
extended BMS algebra is then the semi-direct sum of BMS and Witt.

Similar results were obtained by different methods by considering
Carrollian conformal scalar field theories in
2+1
dimensions

Bekaert et al. 2023
;
Bekaert et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Despontin et al. 2025
.

IV
Klein–Gordon representation of extended BMS generators

Let us consider the expansion of a real Klein–Gordon field in celestial coordinates:

\phi(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int{\omega}d{\omega}d^{2}z\left[a({\omega},z,{\bar{z}})e^{i{\omega}q^{\mu}x_{\mu}}+h.c.\right].

(28)

Since
\phi

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int{\omega}^{\prime}d{\omega}^{\prime}d^{2}z^{\prime}\left[a^{\prime}({\omega}^{\prime},z^{\prime},{\bar{z}}^{\prime})e^{i{\omega}^{\prime}q^{\mu^{\prime}}x_{\mu}^{\prime}}+h.c.\right]

.

(29)

In the previous equation
{\omega}^{\prime},z^{\prime}
and
{\bar{z}}^{\prime}

{\omega},z
and
{\bar{z}}

Since the integration measure is invariant under these transformations

{\omega}^{\prime}d{\omega}^{\prime}d^{2}z^{\prime}={\omega}d{\omega}d^{2}z,

we get

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int{\omega}d{\omega}d^{2}z\left[a({\omega},z,{\bar{z}})e^{i{\omega}q^{\mu}x_{\mu}}+h.c.\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int{\omega}d{\omega}d^{2}z\Big[a^{\prime}({\omega}^{\prime},z^{\prime},{\bar{z}}^{\prime})e^{i{\omega}^{\prime}q^{\prime\mu}x_{\mu}^{\prime}}+h.c.\Big]

Then we obtain,

By making use of eqs. (

V
Conclusions

In this letter we have seen that the extended BMS algebra can be realised in terms of vector fields of zero and one degree in celestial coordinates and in the massless case, showing its kinematical nature, like the Poincaré group. As an example, we have built the realisation of
the extended BMS algebra for the Klein–Gordon field. It would be interesting to understand the role of the Lorentz Casimir, which in our derivation is crucial, in the analysis of celestial amplitudes. Due to the isomorphism between extended BMS and Conformal Carroll symmetries, a better understanding of the relation between our approach and Carrollian field theories in 2+1 dimensions deserves further studies.

We plan to extend this approach to include
w_{1+\infty}
algebra
Strominger 2021
where the
{\omega}
independent part is already known:

\omega_{n,m}=-z^{n+1}\,\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{m+1},

(39)

as it is suggested, for example, in
Cappelli et al. 1993
. To complete the construction we need to compute the
{\omega}
dependent part.

Furthermore we plan to try to extend this derivation to the massive case where the celestial coordinates are parameterized as

p^{\mu}=\frac{m}{2y}[1+y^{2}+z\bar{z};z+{\bar{z}},-i(z-{\bar{z}}),1-y^{2}-z{\bar{z}}].

(40)

Appendix A
Lorentz and
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
transformations

The generic matrix
M\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is written as

M=\begin{pmatrix}a&-c\\
-b&d\\
\end{pmatrix},

(41)

with the condition

\det M=ad-bc=1
.
For an infinitesimal transformation we parametrize
M
as

M=\begin{pmatrix}1+\alpha&-\gamma\\
-\beta&1-\alpha\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
acts on the space of momenta (identified with the
space of hermitian
2\times 2
matrices) via Lorentz transformations

X\mapsto MXM^{\dagger}
. Since
\pm I
act trivially, (the identity
component of) the Lorentz group is isomorphic to

\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})/\{\pm I\}
.

Using that isomorphism, we can compute the infinitesimal Lorentz transformations of
{\omega},z,{\bar{z}}
:

\delta{\omega}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta p^{0}+\delta p^{3})={\omega}[-({\alpha}+\bar{\alpha})+{\gamma}z+\bar{\gamma}\bar{z}]

(43)

and

\delta z=\delta\left(\frac{p^{1}+ip^{2}}{p^{0}+p^{3}}\right)\sim 2{\alpha}z-{\gamma}z^{2}+{\beta},

(44)

that coincide with the results of
Donnay et al. 2020
;
Pasterski 2021
when expanding the finite transformations for infinitesimal parameters.

In an analogous way we can obtain

\delta{\bar{z}}=2\bar{\alpha}{\bar{z}}-\bar{\gamma}{\bar{z}}^{2}+\bar{\beta}.

(45)
