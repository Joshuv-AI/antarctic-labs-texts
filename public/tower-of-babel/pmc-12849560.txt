Overexpression of the lncRNA TUG1 regulates high-glucose-induced endocytic clearance dysfunction in HLSECs via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway
Authors: Yingying Li, Weiwen Ding, Qi Zhang, Rangbao Cheng, Jing Ju, Junyi Zhu, Lirong Wei, Qianqian Tian, Shuyue Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) are linked to endocytic clearance dysfunction in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), which accelerates their synergistic progression. Long noncoding RNA TUG1 (lncRNA TUG1) bridges these two diseases, but its mechanism in alleviating high glucose-induced endocytic impairment in human LSECs (HLSECs) remains unclear.MethodsHLSECs were cultured under standard conditions. A lentiviral lncRNA TUG1 overexpression vector (LV-lncRNA TUG1) was generated via genetic recombination and transduced into HLSECs. Six experimental groups were established: negative control (NC), high glucose (HG), HG + LV-lncRNA TUG1, HG + empty vector control (LV-CON), HG + LV-lncRNA TUG1 + XAV939, HG + LV-CON + XAV939. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) detected lncRNA TUG1 expression levels. β-Catenin and other relevant molecules were assayed by qRT-PCR and Western blot. Immunofluorescence staining detected β-catenin localization to assess Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation. Dil-labeled low-density lipoprotein (Dil-LDL) and fluorescence microscopy evaluated cellular LDL uptake. We further explored TUG1’s regulatory effects on downstream molecules and mechanisms under high glucose.ResultsHigh glucose downregulates lncRNA TUG1 expression in HLSECs, which in turn induces endocytic clearance dysfunction—characterized by elevated CAV-1 expression, reduced PLVAP levels, and impaired DiI-LDL endocytic activity—and concurrently activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (enhanced β-catenin phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, as well as upregulated Cyclin D1 expression). TUG1 overexpression significantly attenuates these pathological changes, while the Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitor XAV939 further potentiates TUG1’s protective effect.DiscussionThis study identifies a novel mechanism by which high glucose-induced TUG1 downregulation activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, contributing to HLSEC endocytic impairment. TUG1 overexpression mitigates these pathological processes, with XAV939 enhancing its protective role, providing new insights into MAFLD’s mechanistic basis. However, the study is limited to in vitro experiments, requiring in vivo validation to confirm TUG1’s therapeutic potential.ConclusionsLncRNA TUG1 attenuates high glucose-induced endocytic clearance dysfunction in HLSECs, potentially via regulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. As a key regulatory target for HLSEC endocytic function, TUG1 offers novel perspectives for understanding MAFLD’s pathogenesis and developing targeted therapies.

Introduction

The number of patients with T2DM globally has now exceeded 400 million [1]. Glycolipotoxicity and insulin resistance (IR) are the shared pathological underpinnings of T2DM and MAFLD [2].Specifically, glycolipotoxicity synergistically accelerates T2DM progression by exacerbating endothelial inflammation and vascular injury [3]. Structural and functional abnormalities of LSECs serve a central role in MAFLD progression. LSEC structural changes—such as defenestration and capillarization—alongside functional deficits, including scavenger receptor downregulation, contribute to impaired endocytic clearance function [4]. This reduces lipoprotein clearance and promotes the oxidation of low-density lipoproteins (LDL) and inflammatory factors, thereby triggering lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses; inducing insulin resistance; and forming a vicious cycle. This pathological cascade exacerbates hepatic steatosis [5] and drives the progression of simple fatty liver to MAFLD, further to liver cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma [6].

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), as key components of gene expression regulatory networks, play pivotal roles in regulating cellular physiology and mediating disease pathogenesis—roles that have been well-documented in extensive research [7]. LncRNAs—the most abundant and functionally diverse subtype of ncRNAs—regulate cellular biological behaviors by modulating canonical signaling pathways, with their underlying mechanisms becoming a major research focus in the field [8].Taurine upregulated gene 1 (TUG1), a lncRNA that is conservatively expressed in vascular endothelial cells from different sources, is closely associated with metabolic diseases [9]. The lncRNA TUG1 can participate in the regulation of pancreatic β-cell function, and its overexpression can significantly improve the pathological morphology of renal tissues in rats with diabetic nephropathy [10]. In the cirrhotic microenvironment simulated by starvation and lipopolysaccharide treatment, the expression of TUG1 in LSECs is upregulated, which promotes autophagy and endothelial‒mesenchymal transition, thereby participating in the progression of liver cirrhosis. TUG1 significantly promotes tumor angiogenesis in human hepatoblastoma and glioblastoma by enhancing the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells [11] and positively regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [12]. Inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway can significantly ameliorate cardiac structural remodeling and functional impairments induced by type 2 diabetes mellitus [13]. After activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, β-catenin stably activates target genes, affecting caveolin-mediated endocytic clearance function [14]. Despite TUG1’s established expression in vascular endothelial cells and implications in metabolic diseases and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, its role in high glucose-induced HLSEC dysfunction—particularly in endocytic clearance—remains unexplored.

Therefore, we hypothesize that high glucose may impair the endocytic clearance function of HLSECs, with this impairment linked to lncRNA TUG1 downregulation and aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

GAPDH (bsm-33033 M), antibodies against β-catenin (bsm-55526R), and p-β-catenin (bs-12854R), antibodies against Cyclin D1 (bs-0623R), antibodies against CAV-1 (bsm-60809R), and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were purchased from Bioss Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Antibodies against PLVAP (67158–1-Ig) were purchased from Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) was purchased from APExBio (USA). Lentiviral vectors for lncRNA TUG1 overexpression (LV-lncRNA TUG1) and empty vector control (LV-CON) were purchased from Shanghai Gene Chem Co., Ltd. XAV939 (HY-15147) was purchased from Med Chem Express (USA).

Cell culture

HLSECs were purchased from Wuhan Saios Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The cells were maintained in low-glucose (5.5 mmol/L) primary endothelial cell medium at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The culture medium was renewed every 48 h. To establish a physiologically relevant high‑glucose model, HLSECs were treated with a gradient of glucose concentrations. The concentration of 25 mmol/L was selected for subsequent experiments, because it provided a sustained pathological stimulus while maintaining cell viability above 85% (Fig. 1c), thereby minimizing confounding effects from overt cytotoxicity. Under this condition, qRT‑PCR analysis confirmed that 25 mmol/L glucose significantly reduced the mRNA expression of lncRNA TUG1 in HLSECs (Fig. 1e). Cell viability assay

HLSEC viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. The cells were digested with trypsin–EDTA, counted, and resuspended in culture medium to prepare a cell suspension at a density of 1 × 104 cells/mL. Aliquots of 100 μL cell suspension were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate, with 5 replicate wells per group to ensure experimental precision. Peripheral wells of the plate were filled with 200 μL PBS to prevent medium evaporation during incubation. The plate was then incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator to allow cell adhesion.

Complete culture medium was prepared with glucose gradients (5.5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, and 35 mmol/L) and the Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor XAV939 at concentrations of 0, 1, 5, 10, 15, and 20 μmol/L, taking care to avoid bubble formation during preparation. HLSECs were cultured for 12, 24, 48, or 72 h under these conditions. At each timepoint, 10 μL CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C on a shaking platform (Shanghai Hengke Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Lentiviral transduction

The full-length spliced transcript sequence of lncRNA TUG1 (RefSeq accession: NR_110785.1; NCBI Gene ID: 138648) was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, corresponding to human-derived lncRNA TUG1. The genetic material cloned into the lentiviral vector was confirmed to be the full-length spliced transcript of lncRNA TUG1 via Sanger sequencing performed by the vector supplier.

LV-lncRNA TUG1 and LV-CON were purchased from Shanghai Gene Chem Co., Ltd. The core lentiviral transfer plasmid backbone of the purchased vectors is pLVX-Puro, a standard backbone for overexpression vectors provided by the company. Prior to delivery, the vectors were pre-validated by the supplier to ensure correct insertion orientation of the target sequence and absence of sequence mutations or deletions. Viral titers provided by the supplier were further verified in our laboratory using a fluorescence-based limiting dilution assay: the titer of LV-lncRNA TUG1 was 3.0 × 10⁹ TU/mL, and that of LV-CON was 7.1 × 10⁸ TU/mL.

To determine the optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) for HLSECs, pre-experiments were conducted with an MOI gradient of 10, 30, 50, and 80. HLSECs were seeded in 6-well plates and subjected to lentiviral transduction in complete medium supplemented with infection enhancer solution A under different MOI conditions. At 72 h post-transduction, total RNA was extracted to validate the overexpression efficiency of lncRNA TUG1 by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). The selection criteria for optimal transduction conditions were as follows: ① minimal lentivirus dosage without compromising cell viability and ② transduction efficiency of approximately 80% [15]. Based on these criteria, the optimal transduction conditions for LV-lncRNA TUG1 in HLSECs were established as complete medium supplemented with infection enhancer solution A at an MOI of 50 (Fig. 1a, b).

DiI-LDL fluorescence microscopy observation

Under aseptic conditions, 1,1'-dioctadecyl-3,3,3',3'-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate-labeled low-density lipoprotein (DiI-LDL; Nanjing Wobio Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was diluted to a concentration of 25–50 μg/mL in cell culture medium. The prepared solution was added to live cell cultures, and cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 4–5 h.

After incubation, DiI-LDL-containing medium was aspirated, and cells were washed three times with probe-free medium. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beijing Biosan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) at room temperature for 15–20 min to preserve morphology, followed by three PBS washes to remove residual paraformaldehyde. Nuclei were stained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd.) to enable nuclear localization. Antifluorescence quenching mounting medium (Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd.) was used for slide mounting to avoid air bubbles. Images were acquired and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining protocol

After 48 h of culture, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were washed three times with PBS (5 min per wash), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd.) for 20 min, and washed again with PBS. Cells were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (Wuhan Procell Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd.) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with a β-catenin primary antibody (1:200 dilution) at 4 °C. The samples were incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) for 2 h in the dark, followed by DAPI staining (5 min) and three additional PBS washes. Antifade mounting medium was used for slide mounting to prevent air bubble formation. Images were subsequently acquired and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan), and the RNA concentration was determined via a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA quality was assessed by absorbance ratios (A260/A280: 1.8–2.0; A260/A230 > 2.0). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT‒PCR) was performed via the Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Mix Kit (with gDNA Eraser) and Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (SYBR Green) (Hunan Aikerui Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Each sample was amplified in triplicate on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The target gene mRNA levels were normalized to those of the endogenous control GAPDH and calculated via the 2⁻ΔΔCT method. The sequences of primers used for qRT‒PCR analysis are listed in Table 1. Western blot

Total proteins were extracted via radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beijing Coolaber Technology Co., Ltd.) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.). The protein concentration was determined via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% or 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) (Wuhan Boster Biological Engineering Co., Ltd.) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck KGaA, Germany). After being blocked with 5% nonfat milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by a 2-h incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Beijing Biosan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and exposed via a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Densitometry of each band was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The integrated optical density (IOD) of each target protein band was measured and normalized to the IOD of the corresponding GAPDH band.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 and SPSS 26.0 software. All samples represented independent biological replicates, with n ≥ 3. Quantitative data are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs). For comparisons of paired samples (e.g., pre- vs. post-treatment of the same batch of HLSECs, or parallel detections of the same experimental unit), a paired Student’s t test was employed following verification of the normal distribution of paired differences using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons of repeated measurements across multiple conditions, repeated-measures one-way analysis of variance (repeated-measures ANOVA) was applied. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was performed to assess the assumption of sphericity; if this assumption was not met, Greenhouse–Geisser correction was implemented, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test to adjust for multiple comparisons. All experiments were replicated at least three times to ensure the reproducibility of the results. Statistical significance was defined as follows: P < 0.05 (statistically significant), P < 0.01 (highly significant), and P < 0.001 (extremely significant).

Observations under an inverted phase-contrast microscope revealed that HLSECs were organized in an orderly fashion and exhibited a spindle morphology with centrally positioned, prominent nuclei. The phosphorylation of serine 675 (Ser675) on the β-catenin subunit acts as a "molecular switch" for Wnt pathway activation, and changes in its level directly regulate the stability and nuclear translocation of β-catenin [17]. The expression of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway can be detected by measuring the expression of β-catenin and CyclinD1, which are downstream target proteins of this pathway. After HG treatment, the protein level of phosphorylated β-catenin (p-β-catenin) in HLSECs was reduced (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05), and the mRNA and protein expression levels of β-catenin (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05 for mRNA, P < 0.01 for protein) and CyclinD1 (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05 for mRNA, P < 0.05 for protein) were also significantly elevated compared with the NC group (Fig. 3a–g).

Further immunofluorescence analysis revealed that under normal conditions, β-catenin mainly accumulated in the cytoplasm, with a small amount localizing to the cell membrane; under high-glucose conditions, β-catenin significantly accumulated in the nucleus, and the fluorescence intensity in the cytoplasm was weakened (Fig. 3h). Quantitative analysis of β-catenin nuclear translocation (N/C ratio) showed a significant increase in the HG group compared with the NC group (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05; Fig. 3i), confirming the aberrant nuclear translocation of β-catenin induced by high glucose. Consequently, our results demonstrate that high glucose induces aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in HLSECs, which is associated with the downregulation of lncRNA TUG1.

The lentiviral LncRNA TUG1 overexpression vector (LV-LncRNA TUG1) successfully upregulated the expression of LncRNA TUG1 in HLSECs

We constructed a lentiviral vector for LncRNA TUG1 overexpression to explore the potential association between LncRNA TUG1 and high glucose-induced endocytic clearance dysfunction. After HLSECs were transfected with LV-lncRNA TUG1 or LV-CON lentivirus for 72 h, they were induced with high glucose for 24 h to establish the HG + LV-lncRNA TUG1 and HG + LV-CON groups. The transfection efficiency was verified via qRT‒PCR. The results revealed that the relative mRNA expression of the lncRNA TUG1 in the HG + LV-lncRNA TUG1 group was significantly greater than that in the HG group, and was 4.92-fold greater than that in the HG + LV-CON group (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05; Fig. 1a, b).

The overexpression of lncRNA TUG1 attenuates the endocytic clearance dysfunction of HLSECs induced by high glucose

Compared with the HG + LV-CON group, lncRNA TUG1 overexpression significantly decreased the protein expression of p-β-catenin (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05), reduced the mRNA and protein expression levels of β-catenin (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05 for both), and significantly decreased the mRNA and protein expression levels of Cyclin D1 (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05 for both), indicating that the overexpression of lncRNA TUG1 can reverse the aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway induced by high glucose (Fig. 3a–g). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that after lncRNA TUG1 overexpression, β-catenin translocated back from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, with a uniform distribution in the cytoplasm. In contrast, there was no significant difference between the HG + LV-CON group and the HG group, where β-catenin remained predominantly in the nucleus (Fig. 3h). Quantitative analysis of β-catenin nuclear translocation (N/C ratio) confirmed a significant decrease in the HG + LV-lncRNA TUG1 group compared with the HG + LV-CON group (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05; Fig. 3i), further validating the reversal effect of TUG1 overexpression on β-catenin nuclear translocation.

Effect of XAV939 on HLSECs viability

We investigated the relationship between lncRNA TUG1 and the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. XAV939, an inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, has significant inhibitory effects and cytotoxicity [18]. The cells (HG + LV-lncRNA TUG1 group) were treated with 0, 1, 5, 10, 15, or 20 μM XAV939 for 12, 24, 48, or 72 h to determine the optimal treatment concentration, followed by evaluation via the CCK-8 assay. In this study, cell viability decreased with increasing dose and time (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05; Fig. 1d). Compared with the control treatment, treatment with 10 μM XAV939 for 48 h significantly reduced HLSEC viability by 49.4% (paired Student’s t test, n = 3, P < 0.05).

Thus, HLSECs were incubated with 10 μM XAV939 for 48 h in subsequent intervention experiments. β-Catenin and Cyclin D1, which are downstream target proteins of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, were detected. Compared with those in the HG + LV-lncRNA TUG1 group, the protein levels of p-β-catenin in the HLSECs in the HG + LV-lncRNA TUG1 + XAV939 group were significantly increased, (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05), and the mRNA and protein expression of Cyclin D1 showed no significant differences (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P > 0.05; Fig. 3a–g).

High glucose induces endocytic clearance dysfunction in HLSECs

The cellular endocytosis mechanism of DiI-LDL is essentially consistent with that of native LDL, which occurs primarily through the clathrin-mediated endocytosis pathway [16]. Therefore, we evaluated the endocytic clearance ability of HLSECs by measuring the expression level of CAV-1 and the changes in the intracellular and extracellular fluorescence intensities of DiI-LDL via fluorescence microscopy. The results from real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR and Western blot analysis revealed that, compared with those in the NC group, the relative expression level of CAV-1 (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05 for both) in the HG group was significantly increased, whereas the relative mRNA and protein expression levels of PLVAP (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05 for both) were decreased (Fig. 4a–e). Fluorescence microscopic observations revealed that following HG intervention, the cellular uptake of Dil-LDL was significantly reduced (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05), with a concurrent increasing trend in the extracellular fluorescence intensity (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05; Fig. 4f). In addition, after treatment with high-dose glucose (25 mM) for 24 h, the relative mRNA expression level of lncRNA TUG1 in the HG group was lower than that in the NC group (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05; Fig. 3a). The overexpression of lncRNA TUG1 can reverse the endocytic clearance dysfunction of HLSECs induced by high glucose

Compared with those in the HG + LV-CON group, the relative mRNA and protein expression levels of CAV-1 (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05 for both) tended to decrease, whereas those of PLVAP (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05 for both) tended to increase (Fig. 4a–e). Fluorescence microscopic observations demonstrated that following lncRNA TUG1 overexpression, the cellular uptake of Dil-LDL tended to increase (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05), whereas the extracellular fluorescence intensity tended to decrease (repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, n = 3, P < 0.05; Fig. 4f, g). Notably, the downregulation of lncRNA TUG1 under high glucose conditions was accompanied by a significant increase in CAV-1 and a decrease in PLVAP at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figs. 3a, 4a, b), suggesting a potential inverse relationship between TUG1 expression and these critical endocytic regulators. Thus, the aforementioned results demonstrate that the overexpression of lncRNA TUG1 can reverse both the aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and the endocytic clearance dysfunction in HLSECs.

Discussion

As the central hub of glucose metabolism, the liver plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of T2DM. Insulin resistance, coupled with dysregulated gluconeogenesis and glycogen synthesis, forms a key mechanistic underpinning; in addition, T2DM can further drive hepatic steatosis and metabolic perturbations, exacerbating liver injury. [19]. A growing body of clinical and preclinical evidence underscores a strong bidirectional association between MAFLD and T2DM, with a well-characterized directional effect: T2DM not only accelerates the pathological progression of MAFLD but also worsens adverse outcomes in both hepatic and extrahepatic tissues. Such extrahepatic complications include cardiovascular events, chronic kidney disease, and diabetic retinopathy, all of which elevate morbidity and mortality rates in patients with this dual condition [20]. Conversely, MAFLD also increases the risk of T2DM onset and worsens impairments in glucose metabolism in patients with T2DM [21].

Vascular endothelial cell dysfunction induced by a high-glucose environment serves as a key pathological link in a variety of metabolic-related diseases, and abnormal endocytic clearance function is one of its important manifestations [22].

LSECs are specialized endothelial cells that line hepatic sinusoids and are characterized by the lack of a basement membrane and abundant open fenestrae. Beyond maintaining hepatic vascular tone and regulating immune responses, impaired endocytic clearance capacity in LSECs promotes the accumulation of harmful substances. This accumulation, in turn, drives the inflammatory and fibrotic pathways underlying acute and chronic liver injury, liver fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [23]. The pathogenesis of MAFLD is complex, and emerging evidence suggests that LSEC-derived endocytic clearance dysfunction contributes to the progression of this disease. The fenestrae of LSECs (with a diameter of 100–200 nm) exhibit a sieve-like arrangement (sieve plates), forming a specialized filtering structure in the sinusoidal wall that mediate efficient substance exchange between blood and hepatocytes. [24]. Structural and functional abnormalities in LSECs (such as fenestral loss and capillarization) drive the progression of T2DM and MAFLD via exacerbating insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and intrahepatic inflammation. Conversely, shared metabolic perturbations of MAFLD and T2DM—such as dyslipidemia and elevated proinflammatory factors—can, in turn, trigger LSEC structural and functional dysfunction, thereby establishing a pathological feedback loop that exacerbates the progression of both diseases. [25]. LSECs develop endocytic clearance impairment due to fenestral loss (defenestration) and aberrant expression or dysfunction of endocytic receptors. This impairment, in turn, promotes intrahepatic lipid accumulation, hepatic inflammation, and hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, ultimately contributing to the pathogenesis and progression of MAFLD [4].

In endothelial cells, lncRNAs participate in the regulation of endocytic clearance through diverse and complex mechanisms. For instance, MALAT1 and MIAT, which are highly expressed in cardiovascular diseases, modulate endothelial cell metabolism and endocytic processes [26]. LncRNA BM450697 enhances the endocytic clearance of LDL by endothelial cells through the regulation of the LDL receptor (LDLR). Meanwhile, LINC08148 facilitates caveolae-mediated endocytosis of Zika virus (ZIKV) in vascular endothelial cells by binding to the transcription factor SP1 and upregulating Src expression [27].

To our knowledge, this study is the first to report the role of lncRNA TUG1 in regulating endocytic clearance dysfunction in HLSECs under a high-glucose microenvironment. While other lncRNAs have been implicated in endothelial endocytic clearance or MAFLD. However, this study is the first to associate TUG1 with the Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis and highlight its unique potential as a therapeutic target for HLSEC dysfunction in the context of T2DM and MAFLD comorbidity. In the present study, we found that a high-glucose environment can induce endocytic clearance dysfunction in LSECs, while the overexpression of LncRNA TUG1 can significantly attenuate this phenomenon. The lncRNA TUG1 regulates downstream gene expression by sponging miRNAs—a mechanism that confers oncogenic properties to TUG1. Specifically, it promotes cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance in multiple cancer types, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Furthermore, TUG1 participates in the pathological progression of non-malignant disorders, such as diabetes mellitus DM [28]. In MAFLD, the lncRNA TUG1 functions as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by sponging miRNAs, thereby inhibiting glycolysis and fatty acid synthesis, promoting glycogen synthesis and lipolysis, and ultimately alleviating hepatic steatosis in hepatocytes [29]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, TUG1 upregulates CD47 expression by sponging miR-340, impairing macrophage endocytic clearance capacity and facilitating tumor immune evasion [30].

In LSECs, high glucose exposure upregulates CAV-1 expression and concomitantly reduces LDL endocytosis. This finding demonstrates that high glucose impairs endocytic clearance capacity in LSECs. Endocytosis—a fundamental cellular process mediating extracellular substance internalization—occurs primarily through three distinct pathways: receptor-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, and phagocytosis. It plays pivotal roles in diverse physiological processes, including nutrient metabolism, signal transduction, plasma membrane turnover, and extracellular waste clearance. Dysregulated endocytosis drives the initiation and progression of metabolic disorders and promotes the invasion and survival of pathogenic micro-organisms [31]. LSECs mediate the endocytosis of macromolecular substances primarily via two canonical pathways: clathrin-mediated endocytosis and caveolin-mediated endocytosis [32]. Bhandari et al. demonstrated that endocytic clearance impairment in LSECs is closely linked to hepatic steatosis [33]. As one of the cell types with the strongest endocytic capacity in mammalian systems, LSECs are unique in that their endocytic vesicles retain the clathrin coat longer than any other cell type [34]. PLVAP is a newly identified type II integral transmembrane glycoprotein [35]. PLVAP is expressed in normal microvascular endothelial cells but is nearly undetectable in large blood vessels. As a downstream effector of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, PLVAP participates in regulating hepatic lipid metabolism and is also critical for preserving LSEC structure and function [36]. In PLVAP-deficient mice, LSECs exhibit a marked reduction in fenestral number along with impaired sinusoidal permeability, and the mice spontaneously develop severe steatosis [37]. Plasma membrane invaginations are ubiquitous in mammalian cells. CAV-1, the major structural protein of caveolae, is not only a key membrane protein mediating endocytic clearance—regulating systemic lipid homeostasis and cellular endocytic processes—but also plays a critical role in preserving the fenestrated phenotype of LSECs [38]. Dysregulated expression of CAV-1 may disrupt the interaction between clathrin and caveolin, thereby leading to dysfunction of the endocytic mechanism [39]. Thus, targeting the amelioration of the high-glucose microenvironment as an entry point to reverse the aberrant endocytic clearance function of LSECs may serve as an innovative therapeutic strategy for MAFLD.

To further investigate the effect of TUG1 on endocytic clearance dysfunction in HLSECs, we constructed LV-LncRNA TUG1 and LV-CON. Following transfection, the cells were subjected to high glucose intervention to establish two experimental groups: the high glucose + lentivirus-LncRNA TUG1 group (abbreviated as the HG + LV-LncRNA TUG1 group) and the high glucose + lentivirus-control group (abbreviated as the HG + LV-CON group). We found that the expression of CAV-1 was lower and the endocytosis of LDL was greater in the HG + LV-LncRNA TUG1 group than in the HG + LV-CON group. These data demonstrate that the overexpression of LncRNA TUG1 can mitigate high glucose-induced endocytic clearance dysfunction in LSECs. Together, these data suggest a negative correlation between LncRNA TUG1 expression levels and the severity of high glucose-induced endocytic clearance dysfunction.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a pivotal regulatory role in the pathogenesis and progression of MAFLD by orchestrating multiple key pathological processes, including hepatic lipid metabolism, intrahepatic inflammatory responses, hepatic fibrogenesis, and liver regenerative capacity [40]. As a highly conserved evolutionary signaling pathway, aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway serves as a crucial driver in the progression of MAFLD from simple hepatic steatosis to NASH, hepatic fibrosis, and even HCC. Furthermore, therapeutic intervention strategies targeting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway present a novel and promising direction for the clinical management of MAFLD, particularly in addressing advanced pathological stages, such as NASH and hepatic fibrosis [41]. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway modulates the endocytic clearance of lipids in LSECs—a key process for maintaining hepatic lipid homeostasis. Aberrant activation of this pathway impairs LSEC-mediated endocytic function, which in turn exacerbates intrahepatic lipid accumulation, propagates intrahepatic inflammatory responses, and accelerates hepatic fibrogenesis, thereby driving the progression of MAFLD [42]. The overexpression of lncRNA TUG1 is associated with attenuated activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway under high-glucose conditions, whereas its downregulation is correlated with enhanced pathway activation. High glucose induces the expression of TUG1, relieves its inhibition of serine kinases, promotes the phosphorylation of β-catenin at the Ser675 site, and activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [17]. On the basis of the aforementioned findings and mechanistic deductions, we propose the following working hypothesis: a high-glucose microenvironment can induce aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway; this pathway activation, in turn, impairs the endocytic clearance function of LSECs, thereby contributing to the progression of MAFLD. The experimental data demonstrated that high glucose exposure can significantly increase the protein expression level of p-β-catenin. Concurrently, this high-glucose treatment results in elevated mRNA transcription and protein expression of total β-catenin. Notably, this change in expression is accompanied by perturbations in the subcellular localization of β-catenin—specifically, the nuclear translocation of β-catenin, a hallmark event indicative of Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway activation. Moreover, the mRNA and protein expression levels of Cyclin D1, a well-characterized downstream effector of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, consistently increased, further confirming the aberrant activation of this pathway under high-glucose conditions. Notably, the overexpression of the lncRNA TUG1 can significantly abrogate the aforementioned high glucose-induced events—including the upregulation of p-β-catenin and total β-catenin (mRNA and protein) levels, β-catenin nuclear translocation, and increased Cyclin D1 expression. Moreover, treatment with XAV939, a well-characterized small-molecule inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, further mitigated high glucose-induced endocytic clearance dysfunction in LSECs. In summary, our data establish that the protective effect of TUG1 overexpression is associated with the inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. While this study does not delineate the direct or indirect nature of this interaction, the correlative evidence presented here provides a solid foundation for future mechanistic investigations.

The lncRNA TUG1 holds promise as a potential therapeutic target for T2DM, particularly in patients with comorbid MAFLD. This study demonstrated that endocytic clearance dysfunction in LSECs represents an early pathological event in T2DM patients with MAFLD comorbidity. Furthermore, the lncRNA TUG1/Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis plays a critical role in sustaining the functional integrity of LSECs. This regulatory effect highlights the axis’s importance in orchestrating two core LSEC-mediated physiological processes in the context of T2DM–MAFLD comorbidity: the maintenance of hepatic lipid homeostasis and the preservation of hepatic microvascular barrier function. This finding not only delineates the potential mechanism by which lncRNA TUG1 reverses endocytic dysfunction in LSECs—specifically, by antagonizing high glucose-induced aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway—but also provides a critical theoretical foundation and therapeutic target for the development of LSEC-protective strategies in patients with T2DM comorbid with MAFLD.

This study has several limitations. First, it is important to note that our findings primarily establish a significant association among high glucose-induced downregulation of TUG1, aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and endocytic dysfunction in HLSECs. While these results support a working model in which TUG1 acts via this pathway, direct causal evidence—such as rescue experiments (e.g., reactivating the Wnt pathway in TUG1-overexpressing cells to reverse its protective effects)—is required to definitively establish the mechanistic link. Second, our work is restricted to in vitro cell models. It should be noted that the high-glucose monoculture model, while allowing for mechanistic dissection, cannot fully replicate the complex in vivo milieu of T2DM–MAFLD comorbidity characterized by glucolipotoxicity, multicellular crosstalk, and hemodynamic alterations. Future animal experiments are essential to validate the pathophysiological relevance of this TUG1/Wnt/β-catenin axis in MAFLD progression. Third, the specific upstream molecular target through which TUG1 exerts its regulatory effect (e.g., as a ceRNA for specific miRNAs or via protein interactions) remains unidentified and warrants further investigation using techniques, such as RNA interactome analysis. Finally, our characterization of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway focused on core components (β-catenin and Cyclin D1), and a systematic evaluation of upstream regulators (e.g., GSK-3β) is needed to construct a more complete mechanistic model. In addition, this study did not include control groups treated with XAV939 or TUG1 overexpression under normal glucose conditions. While these controls could further clarify the specificity of the interventions, the present experimental design remains sufficient to support our primary conclusion regarding the role of the TUG1/Wnt/β-catenin axis in high glucose-induced dysfunction. Nevertheless, these limitations do not diminish the novelty of the associations reported here. In the future, we will focus on addressing these issues through the outlined experimental strategies, which we anticipate will transform the correlative insights of this study into validated mechanistic understanding.

Conclusion

This study revealed that high-glucose conditions reduce the expression of the lncRNA TUG1, leading to abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. We also observed that the lncRNA TUG1 regulates the endocytic clearance function of HLSECs through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. In addition, this study revealed the lncRNA TUG1-mediated mechanism involved in HG-induced endocytic clearance dysfunction in HLSECs, laying a foundation for elucidating the pathogenesis of MAFLD. We will subsequently further validate these findings in animal models and clinical settings to advance the depth of related research.
