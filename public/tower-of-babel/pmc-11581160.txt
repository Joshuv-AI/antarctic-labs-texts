Complete resection of a giant epidermoid cyst of the occipital bone: case report and systematic review
Authors: Ishwarya Nair, Adrian Zammit, Timothy Williamson, Ananthababu Pattavilakom Sadasivan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpidermoid cysts are uncommon accounting for less than 1% of all intracranial tumors. Among them, giant intradiploic epidermoid cysts of the posterior fossa i.e. those exceeding 5 cm are an even rarer entity. Here we report on the complete resection of a 7 cm posterior fossa epidermoid cyst involving the occipital bone in a 32-year-old female presenting with an acute on chronic exacerbation of headaches and vertigo. Complete resection including the cyst wall is crucial in preventing recurrence and malignant transformation. We add to limited existing data by reporting on the first systematic review to date assessing 25 cases of giant epidermoid cysts of the posterior fossa to discuss presentation, clinical and radiographic features and definitive management strategies.Keywords: : epidermoid cysts, epidermoid tumors, giant, intradiploic, occipital bone, posterior fossa, systematic review

Introduction

Epidermoid cysts are rare slow-growing, benign, lesions that represent around 0.2–1.0% of all intracranial tumors [1]. They are generally thought to be congenital in nature originating from aberrant migration of ectodermal elements between the third and fifth weeks of intrauterine life. This results in cutaneous epithelial tissue being trapped within the neural tube thereby forming an ectodermal inclusion cyst [2]. Rare case reports have also been published on iatrogenic [3] and post-traumatic [4] causes of intradiploic epidermoid cysts. Epidermoid cysts tend to frequently occur in the subarachnoid space with the most common location being the cerebellopontine angle, followed by the parasellar region and the middle cranial fossa and are usually less than 2 cm in diameter [5]. Epidermoid cysts of the cranium can be divided into intra and extradural lesions, the latter often occupying the diploe of skull bones resulting in them being termed intradiploic epidermoid cysts. Few epidermoid cysts reach or exceed the size of 5 cm classifying them as giant epidermoid cysts (GECs) making them an even rarer subgroup of epidermoid cysts [6]. Here we present a case of gross total resection of a rare 7 cm posterior fossa GEC involving the left occipital bone. We also present the first published systematic review on the definitive management of posterior fossa GECs involving 25 reported cases.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old female presented to our neurosurgical unit with a history of acute on chronic exacerbation of headaches and vertigo over 1 month on a background of 12 months of symptoms. She did not have any neurological deficits on presentation. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) brain revealed a large 6.7 × 5.0 × 7.0 cm extra-axial left posterior fossa mass with markedly heterogenous FLAIR signal characteristics. This lesion had internal irregular and serpiginous areas of T1 hyperintensity as well as mixed T2 hypo and hyperintensity. There was no discernible contrast enhancement or perilesional edema. Restricted diffusion was uniformly demonstrated throughout the lesion. Computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed smooth scalloping and erosion of the inner table of the left occipital bone through the diploe and marked thinning of the outer table. There was significant midline shift causing compression of the 4th ventricle and distal aqueduct stenosis resulting in chronic hydrocephalus involving the 3rd and lateral ventricles (Figures 1 & 2). A CT venogram also demonstrated compression of the left transverse sinus without occlusion.

A left sided occipital craniotomy was performed in the left lateral Janetta position. Erosion of the occipital bone was observed at several areas. Waxy, cheesy material was seen emerging through a small portion of the bone that was fully eroded through close to the midline. On extending the bony margin of the craniotomy to the level of the foramen magnum, a large thick waxy pearly lesion came into view. This was removed in piecemeal fashion (Figures 3 & 4). The dura was compressed but was intact. The cyst wall was then very carefully dissected off the dura achieving overall gross-total resection whilst preserving dural integrity.

Macroscopic appearance demonstrates a yellowish-white pearly waxy GEC.

The hematoxylin-eosin-stained sections show predominantly keratin with a small amount of cyst wall lined by stratified squamous epithelium in keeping with an epidermoid cyst.

Postoperative MRI (Figure 5) confirmed complete resection of the lesion as well as significant reduction in mass effect on the brainstem and basal cisterns. Her chronic hydrocephalus resolved as well. The left transverse sinus remained severely compressed but the rest of the cerebral venous sinuses were patent. Histopathology confirmed a cyst lined by keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium in keeping with an epidermoid cyst. The patient made an excellent recovery and was discharged day 3 post-op with no neurological deficits. Eight months post-op, the patient remains well and recurrence-free.

Materials & methods: systematic review

All information pertaining to the present case was obtained from the patient's electronic medical record.

A systematic review of the literature was conducted in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations [7]. We sought to answer the following research questions:

Based on a comprehensive review of several databases (Pubmed, Cochrane, Web of Science, Embase and Medline) from inception to 20 April 2024, we analysed all case reports and case series in the English language. Reference lists were also manually scoped for additional cases from eligible studies.

Studies were included if they involved patients who had GECs of the posterior fossa i.e. from the petrous temporal bone to the occipital bone. Exclusion criteria involved epidermoid cysts that were not giant, were intradural epidermoid cysts, did not involve the posterior fossa or were not in the English language. Duplicate case reports or series were only reported once in this review.

Systematic review of literature

Utilizing dedicated search terms such as “giant” OR “huge” OR “large” AND “epidermal cyst” OR “epidermoid cyst” OR “epidermal tumor” OR “epidermoid tumor” AND “intradiploic”, 316 articles were identified across the 5 databases. After eliminating duplicate articles (n = 27), irrelevant articles (n = 3), articles based on title and abstract (n = 107) and articles based on full text reviews (n = 156), 24 studies were selected for analysis (Supplementary Figure S1). The 3 articles were excluded for irrelevance as one article was conducted on dogs, another was not in English and the third was part of a quiz.

We extracted the following descriptive data from each study: demographics (age and sex), clinical features of GECs (size, location), symptoms and duration that led to presentation, pre-op imaging investigations (e.g. XR, CT, MR and/or additional vascular imaging), surgical management (gross total resection versus partial excision) and/or any other interventions such as adjuvant radiation therapy as well as post-operative complications or recurrence. Data on size and location of GECs were also extracted from patient photographs included in a few articles where this was not made explicit.

Evaluation of publication bias was not feasible because all included studies were case reports and case series.

Results

A total of 24 publications [1,3,4,8–28] reported on 25 patients with posterior fossa intradiploic GECs between 1978 and 2024 (Table 1). The mean age of patients was 51.2 ± 16.4 years and 52% of patients were females (Table 2). Of the 19 patients for whom size was documented, the mean size of GEC at diagnosis was 7.11 ± 2.20 cm. The remaining 6 patients were included in this systematic review based on imaging findings that suggested that they had GECs. The most common symptom at presentation was headaches (76%), cerebellar ataxia (36%), localized swelling (24%), vomiting (16%), nausea/vomiting/vertigo (12% each), diplopia/papilledema/nystagmus (12%), weakness (12%) and dysmetria (4%) (Table 2 & Graph 1). Of the 16 patients for whom duration of symptoms to presentation was recorded, this ranged from 1 month to several decades (Table 1).

Often thought to have a congenital basis for origin, Prat Acin et al., 2008 [3] reported the first intradiploic GEC in a 44-year-old female associated with iatrogenic epithelial seeding on the cranial vault after anesthetic infiltration through to the periosteum during the resection of a small, intradermal melanocytic nevus involving the right occipital scalp under local anesthesia three years prior. She then presented with a 6 cm painless mass in the same region which was confirmed to be a non-infiltrative intradiploic epidermoid cyst after en bloc resection.

Similarly, Enchev et al., 2011 [4] reported on a GEC involving the occipital region in a 54-year-old man. It is suspected that inclusion of ectodermal cells occurred during his open scalp injury at 5 years of age when he also had an underlying linear skull fracture in the left occipital region for which he underwent a surgical washout at the time.

Maiuri et al. [13] report on the first posterior fossa GEC studied by MRI. Prior to this, patients who had GECs underwent X-ray and CT scan as part of their pre-operative workup due to the lack of availability of MRI scanners.

GTR was defined as complete removal of the cyst and capsule. Partial excision was defined as partial removal of the cyst with capsular remnants visualized intraoperatively or on postoperative imaging. GTR was achieved in 22 of 25 cases and partial excision in 3 cases. Bretschneider et al. [11] report the case of a 71-year-old female who presented with decades of headaches, vertigo, ataxia and nystagmus to the left. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI demonstrated a solid enhancing component of the tumor within the right cerebellar hemisphere broadly attached to the cyst wall raising suspicion for malignant degeneration given that epidermoid cysts generally do not demonstrate contrast enhancement. Intra-op, the GEC had invaded through dura into the right cerebellar hemisphere. As such, residual tumor was left at the sigmoid-transverse sinus junction. Histological examination revealed a cystic tumor lined by keratinised squamous epithelium filled with keratinous and sebaceous material with adjacent areas of mature skin appendages and solid foci of squamous cell carcinoma invading the underlying cerebellar parenchyma confirming diagnosis of a moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma resulting from malignant degeneration of an epidermoid cyst. This patient underwent adjuvant radiation therapy but nine months later, was noted to have a large tumor recurrence with caudal extension and was deemed no longer suitable for further surgical debulking or chemotherapy. She died 12 months following surgery. In Trivedi et al.'s case, near total excision of a 6.25 × 7.56 × 6.8 occipital GEC was achieved except at the cerebellopontine angle where the capsule was extremely adherent to dura. Over 2 years of follow-up, no recurrence was noted but this patient certainly is at risk of recurrence as capsule was left behind [26]. In Ahuja et al.'s case, partial excision was achieved in a 24-year-old female with a posterior fossa GEC extending into the 4th ventricle. It is unclear as to why gross total resection was not achieved in this case. However, there was no recurrence reported over the 3-month follow-up for this patient [28]. Of the data available for 16 patients, the mean follow-up data in this systematic review was 18.7 ± 18.5 months. None of these patients experienced post-op complications (Table 2).

Of available data for 12 patients in this review, 42% of patients underwent paramedian suboccipital craniotomy, 33% underwent midline suboccipital craniotomy, 17% underwent retrosigmoid craniotomy and 8% underwent temporoparietal craniotomy (Table 1). Of available data for 12 patients, dura was intact and preserved during surgery for 92% of patients and only had to be excised in one case including the transverse and part of the sigmoid sinus to achieve gross total resection as the GEC was highly adherent to it [8] (Table 2). 20% of patients underwent cranioplasty with cement, titanium mesh or acrylic material as the overlying bone had been completely eroded by the GEC (Table 2).

All patients in this systematic review reported improvement and/or resolution of their symptoms within days post-operatively.

Discussion

Epidermoid cysts are typically benign tumors of epithelial origin and must be differentiated from other lesions such as dermoids and teratomas that also contain epithelial tissue. Epidermoid cysts contain a mass of desquamated cells containing keratohyaline within a well-defined stratified squamous epithelium cyst wall which is the most crucial component for resection to prevent recurrence [29].

Epithelial tumors occur due to the misplacement of ectodermal cells during embryonic development. They then grow if adequate blood supply is available. The differentiation of epithelial tumors into epidermoids and dermoids depends on the age, depth and position of misplaced cell-rests during development [2] as well as the influence of metablastic tissues containing an organizer substance [30].

Epidermoid cysts of the cranium can be divided into intra and extradural lesions. Earlier ectopic ectodermal cells are more prone to form intradural epidermoid cysts whereas later ectopic cells are more prone to form extradural lesions [27]. First described by Muller [21,31] in 1838, of extradural epidermoid cysts, approximately 25% are intradiploic [16,32]. Intradiploic epidermoid cysts often have a supratentorial predilection for the frontal and parietal bones of the skull, making cysts of the posterior fossa uncommon [12]. Few epidermoid cysts reach or exceed the size of 5 cm classifying them as giant epidermoid cysts (GECs) making them an even rarer subgroup of epidermoid cysts [33]. GECs only tend to be discovered in the third to fifth decades of life due to the slow growth of the cyst. When these cysts grow intracranially, they can reach a large size within the cranium before decompensation occurs resulting in slowly progressive neurological deficits secondary to compression of vascular or neurological structures becoming apparent later. With extracranial growth, patients tend to present earlier with a visible or palpable lump. This explains the wide range of symptoms from months to years noted amongst patients in this systematic review.

According to our systematic review, the first GEC of the posterior fossa measuring 13 cm was reported by Rengachary et al., in 1978 [8]. The mean age of presentation in this review was 51.2 ± 16.4 years which is in keeping with literature that suggests that GECs are typically diagnosed in the third to fifth decade of life due to headaches or symptoms arising from compression of neighboring vascular and neurological structures [6]. In this review, GECs were almost equally distributed between males (48%) and females (52%) which again is in keeping with literature that suggests that there is no specific gender predilection.

The most common symptom was headaches experienced by 19 patients (76%). 9 patients (36%) presented with ipsilateral cerebellar ataxia owing to mass effect. 6 patients (24%) presented with localized swelling. 3 patients (12%) presented with nausea and 4 patients (16%) presented with vomiting. Only 4 patients (16%) presented with signs of raised intracranial pressure such as diplopia, papilledema and nystagmus. Interestingly, the patient who presented earliest in our systematic review i.e. within one month of symptoms only reported painless subcutaneous swelling in the right occipital region. He did not have headaches or other neurological symptoms and imaging did not reveal any concerning features such as hydrocephalus [10]. Similarly, a patient who waited nine years prior to presentation also only reported progressive painless localized swelling with no associated neurological symptoms [15]. In our case, despite clear intradiploic expansion and disruption of both tables of the occipital bone, our patient did not present with localised swelling probably because there was greater intracranial extension of the epidermoid tumor.

In terms of etiology, majority of the cases in this review were congenital in nature whereas only two cases [3,4] were related to trauma. The time from initial trauma to diagnosis of the intradiploic GEC ranged from three years in one case [21] to forty-nine years in another case [4]. Acquired epidermoid cysts have generally been reported in the lumbar spine secondary to iatrogenic seeding of epidermal cells following lumbar punctures or anesthetic infiltration for back pain management. However, both these cases suggest that the inclusion of epidermal cells at the time of trauma could have resulted in a tissue inflammatory reaction resulting in the formation of the epidermoid cyst [4,34].

The key concern with GECs of the posterior fossa is related to deterioration secondary to raised intracranial pressure which can be due to two factors. One is due to obstructive chronic hydrocephalus that results from direct compression of the fourth ventricle and kinking of the cerebral aqueduct due to the large size of the lesion as seen in our case and the rest of the literature. Another reason could be due to occlusion of major venous sinuses resulting in venous congestion as seen in several patients who underwent CT venous imaging as part of their pre-operative workup. Excision of the GEC did not restore patency through the compressed venous sinus(es) post-op. However, given the slow growth of epidermoid cysts, collateral venous circulation that develops appears adequate for maintaining drainage of the central nervous system as demonstrated by our study as well as Rengachary et al. [8], Dias et al. [9], Guridi et al. [10] and Yokota et al. [23].

Imaging findings

Diagnosis of an epidermoid cyst is guided by pre-operative imaging including CT and MRI. All patients in this literature review underwent CT imaging as part of their workup. CT typically demonstrated a lytic well-circumscribed lobulated hypodense lesion involving the inner table in all cases as well as the outer table in some cases. Occasionally these lesions were also associated with calcification in the cyst wall. Patients in earlier studies also underwent X-ray imaging that revealed lytic bony defects but this has since been replaced by CT imaging that displays such bony changes far more clearly. The epidermoid cyst in our case presented similarly with a well-circumscribed hypodense lesion eroding through the inner and outer tables of the left occipital bone associated with some peripheral calcification. Significant stenosis of the left transverse sinus pushed anterior to the lesion was noted but there was no occlusive filling defect.

Patients in earlier studies in the literature did not undergo MRI imaging prior to surgery. However, if available, MRI imaging should be an obligatory part of pre-operative work-up as it provides valuable anatomical information and helps exclude differentials. This systematic review suggests that on MRI, epidermoid cysts appear T1 iso or hypointense with areas of hyperintensity indicating calcification depending on the contents of the cyst. They appear T2 and FLAIR hyperintense. The DWI sequence is the most helpful sequence given that epidermoid cysts display restricted diffusion on DWI/ADC due to their cholesterol content thereby differentiating it from mimics such as dermoid cysts, hemangiomas, arachnoid cysts and/or osseous tumors including aneurysmal bone cysts, fibrous dysplasia and eosinophilic granulomas. Epidermoid cysts do not enhance with contrast. In keeping with this, in our case, the GEC generally appeared T1 hypointense with serpiginous areas of hyperintensity and T2 hyperintense with areas of hypointensity representing calcification. Nonetheless, rare, atypical “white epidermoids” present with reversed signal intensity and no diffusion restriction on MRI due to their high protein content [35].

Macroscopic & microscopic findings

Macroscopically, GECs tend to be slightly fluctuant round avascular masses with a smooth whitish capsule. Intradiploic GECs tend to invade through the outer and inner table of the bone creating a moth-eaten appearance. Diagnosis of epidermoid cysts can subsequently be confirmed on histology which shows that they tend to be covered externally by keratinized stratified squamous epithelium which grows due to the desquamation of epithelium. They are known as “pearly tumors” because the tumor capsule is filled with a soft, waxy, white keratin and cholesterol formed from the desquamation and deterioration of keratin from the epithelial internal layer of the cysts [33].

Surgical approach

Despite its large size, complete surgical resection of a GEC including its capsule whilst preserving the integrity of the underlying dura and major venous sinuses is gold standard. Except one case in the literature, this was achieved for all cases as well as for our patient. There is a 8.3–25% risk of recurrence if the cyst wall is not completely removed [35]. Unlike intradural epidermoid cysts, extradural epidermoid cysts tend to grow in a regular fashion enabling them to be completely removed with the tumor capsule. GECs are often accompanied by severe brain tissue deformation and extensive lytic skull erosion sometimes requiring cranioplasties for the large resulting skull defect. On the other hand, intradural epidermoid cysts, particularly multicompartmental lesions tend to grow in an irregular fashion often associated with capsule adherence to many neurovascular structures thereby making gross total resection challenging [29]. Spillage of cyst content into the subarachnoid space whilst suctioning can cause postoperative aseptic meningitis (2–50%) thereby leading to dense adhesions between residual capsule, cranial nerves and vasculature [26,33]. Repeated irrigation of the surgical cavity with normal saline prevents aseptic meningitis and recurrence [11]. Malignant transformation of epidermoid cysts to squamous cell carcinoma is rare however has been reported in one case involving a posterior fossa GEC [11]. Incomplete removal, repeated infections and recurrence are the most common risk factors for malignant transformation [30]. It is worth noting that in the case reported by Bretschneider et al., portions of the tumor had already demonstrated malignant degeneration from epidermoid cyst into squamous cell carcinoma [11].

Intra-operatively, we utilized neuronavigation to define the limits of the craniectomy and protect dural and sinus integrity. On day 1 post-op, our patient reported resolution of headaches and dizziness. This is in keeping with the literature review where patients typically reported resolution of symptoms within days after surgery. Apart from one case of malignancy in the systematic review [6], there have been no other cases of recurrence or malignant transformation of the epidermoid tumor over a follow-up period of 18.7 ± 18.5 months. Similarly, our patient remains well and free from recurrence eight months post-op.

Limitations

One limitation in this study is that all included literature in this systematic review is based on case reports and case series due to the rarity of posterior fossa GECs. The small size of this systematic review also precluded more rigorous statistical analysis. Included studies are published across multiple decades which may limit direct comparisons as advances in imaging workup and surgical techniques affect outcomes. A longer follow-up period in our case report as well as systematic review would have strengthened the paper. Furthermore, there are several key components that are inconsistently reported in the cases reviewed such as tumor sizes, duration of symptoms prior to presentation, operative approach, status of dura integrity and preservation as well as whether patients underwent a cranioplasty. We are unable to account for the effects of these potential confounders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we present a case of successful gross total resection of a giant posterior fossa epidermoid cyst, a rare entity amongst these tumors. CT and MRI are helpful in distinguishing this tumor from close mimics as well as guiding surgical planning. This systematic review including our study shows that GECs of the posterior fossa tend to present later in the fifth decade of life owing to their slow growth. They generally do not penetrate through the dura. Therefore, definitive management can be achieved by careful and complete surgical resection of a GEC, guided by neuronavigation, whilst preserving underlying dura and preventing cyst spillage in order to mitigate post-operative complications, recurrence and malignant transformation.

Further research could focus on evaluating the natural history of giant epidermoid cysts. Given their rarity and long latency to discovery, such study is somewhat limited at present. Furthermore, what happens after the 18 months of follow-up as in this systematic review? Questions prevail such as whether the size of the GEC increases risk of malignant transformation or whether complete cyst resection is sufficient to prevent this? How soon can one expect to encounter malignant transformation of a GEC of the posterior fossa? Does this timeframe differ between intracranial and intradiploic cysts?
