Hepatic necrosis and dysfunction following angioembolization in a trauma-related mortality
Authors: Ali Cadili, Jonathan Gates
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAngiographic embolization is a key tool in managing hemorrhage from blunt traumatic injuries, particularly hepatic trauma, but it carries risks such as hepatic necrosis. The incidence of clinically significant hepatic necrosis post-embolization is around 6%, with non-selective embolization increasing this risk compared to selective techniques.ObjectiveTo report a case of a patient with high-grade blunt hepatic injury who developed hepatic necrosis and dysfunction leading to death following non-selective angiographic embolization.MethodsA 46-year-old male involved in a high-speed motor vehicle collision underwent emergency laparotomy for multiple liver lacerations, followed by non-selective coil embolization of the right and middle hepatic artery trunks due to suspected ongoing hemorrhage. The case was retrospectively analyzed, including operative findings, imaging (CT and angiography), and clinical outcomes over 25 days.ResultsDespite no active contrast blush on CT or angiography, non-selective embolization was performed. The patient developed patchy hepatic necrosis, requiring multiple laparotomies, and progressed to liver failure, ARDS, and cerebral edema. Death occurred 25 days post-admission due to severe encephalopathy, with hepatic necrosis likely exacerbated by the embolization.ConclusionThis case suggests that non-selective angiographic embolization may have contributed significantly to hepatic necrosis and mortality in a patient with high-grade hepatic trauma, highlighting the need for careful consideration of embolization indications and techniques.

Introduction

Angiographic embolization is a useful adjunct in the management of hemorrhage due to blunt traumatic injuries. Its greatest utility lies in helping to avert the need for operative intervention in patients with radiological evidence of active bleeding but who otherwise remain hemodynamically stable and lack any other indication for surgery [1]. The determination of active bleeding is typically made by detecting a blush (or active contrast extravasation) on contrast-enhanced CT scanning. Another possible indication for angiographic embolization would be its post-operative use for either bleeding that was not controlled during the operation or for de novo or recurrent postoperative hemorrhage. In the trauma setting, angiographic embolization is most commonly performed for blunt splenic injury, hepatic injury, as well as pelvic arterial bleeding [2]. This tool has made a valuable addition to the armamentarium available to trauma clinicians and has undoubtedly solidified the trend of non-operative management in trauma. As with all other therapeutic interventions, however, angioembolization is not without iatrogenic risks.

Given the complexity of hepatic anatomy and function, combined with the degree of injury as well as the systemic sequelae of massive resuscitation and hepatic dysfunction, the additive complications from angioembolization may confer a significant impact in hepatic trauma. Examples of possible complications following angioembolization for hepatic injury include arterial access site complications, hepatic necrosis, infection, as well as bile leaks. While these complications can vary in severity, incidence, and outcome, it is generally accepted that hepatic necrosis represents the most impactful, serious, and feared complication following angioembolization. The rate of clinically significant hepatic necrosis has been reported to be in the range of 6% in the literature [3]. Among the strategies employed to reduce the incidence of hepatic necrosis, the use of selective or super selective embolization, as opposed to non-selective embolization, is thought to be the most important factor in minimizing the rate of adverse events following angioembolization. More selective vessel embolization is meant to minimize the disruption of vascular supply to healthy surrounding liver tissue and hence reduce the chance of post-procedure liver dysfunction and necrosis. Necrosis can lead to multiple repeat operative interventions, hepatic dysfunction and even failure, and in some cases ultimately lead to death. We report here a case of high grade blunt hepatic injury patient who underwent angiographic embolization and ended up developing hepatic dysfunction and necrosis that led to death.

Case

A 46-year-old male in a high-speed motor vehicle collision was brought into the emergency department and found to be hypotensive and tachycardic with a distended abdomen. A FAST ultrasound examination was performed and this showed free fluid in the right upper quadrant. Pelvic and chest radiographs did not demonstrate any significant abnormalities other than bilateral non-displaced rib fractures. At this point the patient was hemodynamically unstable but responsive to resuscitation. The patient was taken emergently to the operating room and upon laparotomy was found to have multiple liver lacerations. The bleeding was noted to slow down, but not entirely stop, with a Pringle maneuver with a total clamp time of 20 min. Mobilization of the liver with retroperitoneal exploration did not reveal a retrohepatic veous injury. The liver lacerations were closed with sutures, and the abdomen was tightly packed with laparotomy sponges before a temporary wound vac closure was performed. There was no injury to the caudate lobe and no deep intraparenchymal venous injuries identified. After activating the suction on the wound vac device, and while still in the operating room, brisk bright red blood was noted from the wound vac. The patient’s hemodynamic status had improved with ongoing resuscitation throughout the operation and did not worsen with the new blood seen on activating the wound vac suction. The plan had already been made to send the patient for a CT scan of the head, spine, chest, abdomen and pelvis immediately postoperatively as this was not done preoperatively. An angiographic embolization procedure was also arranged to take place immediately following the CT scan given suspected ongoing intraabdominal hemorrhage. The CT scan revealed bilateral rib fractures (with no displacements or flail segments) and a non-displaced manubrial fracture. No lung contusions or hemo/pneumothoraces were detected. A grade 4 liver injury to the right hepatic lobe was also seen but no area of active contrast blush was noted. After a discussion between the Trauma Surgery and Interventional Radiology teams, the decision was made to proceed to angiography anyway based on a strong clinical suspicion of ongoing hemorrhage from the liver. The visceral angiography procedure did not reveal any active extravasation and there no replaced or accessory hepatic arterial branches identified. The interventional radiologist then decided to proceed with a non-selective coil embolization of the main right and middle hepatic artery trunks. The decision was taken given the evidence for continued hepatic arterial bleeding combined with ongoing hemodynamic instability and the failure to completely control the bleeding with laparotomy and packing. The decision to embolize both the right and middle hepatic main arterial trunks was again based on the patient’s degree of injury complexity, critical clinical /hemodynamic status, failed initial operative management to control the bleeding, and the lack of blush to help guide a selective approach to embolization. The patient was transferred to the Intensive Care Unit thereafter on vasopressors with a plan for repeat laparotomy the next day. The patient was indeed taken to the operating room the next day; no major bleeding was noted but a small area of segment 4b of the liver appeared to be ischemic and was resected. No intestinal ischemia or other abnormalities were noted. A definitive closure of the abdomen was attempted but abandoned half-way through due to a significant intraoperative increase in peak airway pressures and a decrease in blood pressure. A wound vac was again used for temporary abdominal wall closure and the patient tolerated this well without raising concern for increased intra-abdominal pressure as with the attempted primary abdominal wall closure. The patient was then sent back to the Intensive Care Unit. Of note, the patient was not known to have prior liver disease or any other significant chronic illness but his alcohol consumption status was unknown.

The next day, being hemodynamically stable and off vasopressors, the patient was sent down to Interventional Radiology for an Inferior Vena Cava filter placement; prophylactic anticoagulation was still being held given his bleeding risk and it was uncertain as to when it could be restarted. Over the next couple of days, the patient developed Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) and then recurrent hypotension necessitating the reinstitution of Vasopressor therapy. On repeat laparotomy, two days following the second operation, multiple areas of patchy necrosis were noted in the right lobe of the liver. Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound showed adequate blood flow and given that there was no frank necrosis, the decision was made to forgo any resection on that operation and bring the patient in for a repeat laparotomy to re-evaluate the liver at a later date. Three days later, there were signs of clear necrosis in segments IVa and V of the liver which were resected along with the gallbladder. Multiple other areas of questionable patchy necrosis throughout the liver surface were also seen but were left alone for observation. Two days after that, additional areas of necrosis compared to the last operation were seen and the non-necrotic areas of the liver appeared somewhat dusky and gray in color. Two further laparotomies were performed which showed increase in number and size of necrotic-appearing areas. During this time, period the patient also developed a ventilator-associated pneumonia and liver enzymes and ammonia continued to rise congruent with worsening mental status changes and overall weakness. 25 days after admission to the hospital, the patient developed seizures and a CT of the head showed severe diffuse cerebral edema and absence of cerebrospinal fluid in the basilar cisterns. The Neurology and Neurosurgery services were consulted and deemed the prognosis to be very poor in the setting of severe encephalopathy and cerebral edema likely secondary to liver failure. The next day, the patient’s goals of care are changed to Comfort Measures Only by the family and the patient passed away later that afternoon.

Discussion

A thorough review of this case highlights several important questions regarding the optimal management of high-grade hepatic injuries: When is post-operative angiographic embolization indicated or necessary in blunt hepatic trauma? When should non-selective embolization of main hepatic arterial trunks be done, if ever, as opposed to selective or super selective angioembolization? Is there sufficient evidence to conclude in this case that the hepatic necrosis and dysfunction that led to this patient’s death resulted from the angiographic embolization? The first lesson from this case is the reaffirmation of the principle that there is no substitute for excellent operative technique and strategy when the patient is treated operatively. Adjunctive tools such as angioembolization and hemostatic blood products may supplement operative care but cannot fully make up for an inadequate or poorly executed operation. In this case, it was in fact not clear as to whether adequate operative control of the hemorrhage was achieved in the first operation. Immediately following the application and initiation of the wound vac, brisk bright red blood output was noted. The patient was taken directly to the Radiology Department from the operating room with the intention of performing a CT scan followed by an embolization procedure at the angiography suite. The first step to controlling the bleeding is to identify its source. Hepatic trauma is no different from other solid organs other than the dual blood supply and variety of possible bleeding sources making identification somewhat less straightforward. The source of hemorrhage in liver trauma needs to be pinpointed to the hepatic arterial, portal venous, hepatic venous systems or some combination of the three. A correct application of the Pringle maneuver is one of the first and most useful steps in achieving this goal. If the Pringle maneuver during the operation fails to completely stop the bleeding, as had occurred in this case, then a hepatic venous etiology is probable. Hemorrhage from a hepatic venous source can, in most, cases be stopped with proper packing. If packing does not succeed in controlling the hemorrhage, then liver mobilization to search for a retrohepatic venous injury should be undertaken.

Identification of the source of bleeding is important not only for initial operative control but also for determining what procedures and steps are available as either post-operative adjuncts or if bleeding recurs. Angiographic embolization can be expected to be effective only against hepatic arterial bleeding. The presence of a hepatic arterial source of bleeding implies that a Pringle maneuver would cause a visible cessation of the bleeding in the operating room. An exception to this may be the presence of a concomitant hepatic venous injury (in which case one may still see bleeding intraoperatively despite an adequate Pringle maneuver). In this case, the bleeding slowed down but did not stop with the application of a Pringle maneuver. This argues against hepatic arterial bleeding, at least as the sole cause of hemorrhage. The fact that the post-operative CT scan did not show an arterial phase blush further diminishes the likelihood that the bleeding was of a predominantly hepatic arterial source. One may be inclined to think, therefore, that the post-operative angiographic procedure in this case may have not been indicated. However, this would only be true if a negative CT scan effectively rules out with reasonably great certainty the presence of a hepatic arterial bleed. Studies to date have found the sensitivity of CT scan in detecting a source of arterial bleed in trauma (by displaying a blush) to be 75–90% [4]. This would suggest, therefore, that blunt hepatic trauma patients with no blush on CT may still have up to a 25% chance of hepatic arterial bleeding; an injury that could be amenable to angiographic embolization. One study, in fact, noted that out of 36 blunt trauma patients with no blush on initial CT, 12 were found to have evidence of active extravasation on angiography. One factor that could contribute to CT scans missing active arterial extravasation is delay of arterial contrast opacification due to hypotension in trauma. Given the imperfect sensitivity of CT in detecting active hepatic arterial bleeding, we would suggest that such patients still be considered for angiography (to detect a missed hepatic arterial bleed) if clinical suspicion of such bleeding is high. An example scenario would include clinical signs of ongoing hemorrhage despite adequate packing on laparotomy. This may especially be the case with known hepatic venous injuries; such injuries have been shown to raise the likelihood of concomitant arterial injury by a factor of 3.5 compared to hepatic trauma not involving the hepatic veins [5].

It is also possible, on the other hand, for a patient with a blush on CT to have an angiography that does not reveal active arterial extravasation. Retrospective data has shown that this may occur in up to 33% of hepatic injury cases [6]. Indeed one may be inclined in such instances to forgo embolization since the angiogram is negative. Unfortunately, studies have shown that patients with CT blush but no angiographic blush who do not get embolized are at a higher risk of rebleeding compared to those who do get embolized. If embolization is to be performed under such circumstances, however, it would need to be done in a non-selective fashion given that no specific source is available to target. Non-selective embolization is thought to incur a higher risk of complications, including hepatic necrosis. Therefore, the decision as to whether to proceed with embolization in such cases should be weighed carefully. Factors that should be considered include the patient’s hemodynamic status, degree of suspicion of ongoing arterial hemorrhage, extent and severity of injury, pre-injury health and functioning of the liver, type and severity of concomitant injuries, as well as overall health and physiological derangements amongst many others. It is certainly recommended that this decision be discussed between the Trauma Surgery team and Interventional Radiology as was done in our case. Significant functional liver injury as well as bleeding may result from the ischemia-reperfusion causing microcirculatory disturbances and bleeding [7]. In our case, both the CT scan and angiography showed no blush yet the embolization was carried out anyway given the very high degree of clinical suspicion of an ongoing arterial hemorrhage. This was based on bright red bleeding despite adequate laparotomy packing and the ongoing hemodynamic instability of the patient. Since the embolization was being performed without evidence of a blush, it had to be performed in a non-selective manner. The main trunks of both the right and middle hepatic arteries were embolized given that the liver injury in our case was right-sided.

Our patient ultimately succumbed to the sequelae of advanced hepatic dysfunction. Major guidelines recommend using angioembolization, selective and super-selective, as an adjunct rather than a mainstay in the management of blunt hepatic injuries [8–10]. A detailed review of his hospital course raises the spectre that the angiographic embolization event was a significant contributor to his severe and progressive hepatic dysfunction leading to death. This embolization compounded the original traumatic liver injury, circulatory shock, as well as multiple other serious events including the development of ARDS. We do know that the embolization likely had a direct impact on the development of hepatic necrosis (especially when it compounded the aforementioned factors). Necrosis itself is known to be associated with worse clinical outcomes, mainly due to release of inflammatory cytokines as well as the risk of infection. In addition, the necrosis here resulted in multiple operative interventions which likely worsened the outcomes given the repeat surgical and anesthetic insults. Interestingly, no angiography-related deaths have been reported thus far in the blunt liver trauma literature. This is likely a function of the complexity and intertwining of factors leading to death in severe hepatic injuries making it difficult to draw a straight line from an angioembolization procedure to death days or weeks later. Thus, the lack of reported deaths in the literature likely applies only to early or immediate post-angiographic deaths. Deaths that result from angioembolization may, in reality, be manifesting much later (as a consequence of the sequelae of necrosis, thrombosis, or renal failure) without practical means of unequivocally attributing the death to the procedure. In our case, one can argue that the mortality was most likely a result of the necrosis given the fact that the patient ultimately succumbed to progressive hepatic failure. Multiple factors other than the angiographic embolization, however, may have contributed to the liver failure in this case. Such factors include the physical traumatic injury itself, recurrent hypotension, sepsis, as well as medications and prolonged parenteral nutrition. The cause may indeed be an aggregate or interaction of these factors. We hypothesize, however, that the non-selective angioembolization was the seminal underlying event that that led to the liver necrosis, dysfunction, and ultimately patient mortality. Retrospective series have shown that while non-selective angiographic embolization for hepatic injuries is successful in achieving hemostasis in up to 97% of cases, it is also fraught with a significant risk of morbidity and mortality compared to selective embolization [11–14]. As mentioned, the necrosis also led to multiple liver debridement episodes and repeat operations. While it is certainly true that multiple factors played into this patient’s outcome, we believe there is a reasonable chance this previously healthy patient (with a previously healthy liver) may have been able to overcome his injury had the angiography not compounded the picture. The timing and pattern of the progressive hepatic necrosis also points to the likelihood of the embolization being the critical cornerstone step that led to this outcome. As such, we recommend strict adherence to guidelines when making crucial decisions at key junctures in the management of complex blunt hepatic injuries.

In conclusion, the non-selective nature of the angioembolization performed in this case most likely played a major role in the patient’s hepatic failure and ultimate death. Angiographic embolization should be reserved for patients with radiological or angiographic evidence of arterial bleeding, and whenever possible, should be performed in a selective or super-selective manner so as to minimize ischemic complications. In the setting of persistent bleeding after initial packing and a negative angiogram, a safer alternative would have been a planned re-laparotomy with re-packing after correction of coagulopathy, acidosis, and hypothermia, rather than empiric non-selective embolization. Key takeaway points

Angiographic embolization should ideally be limited to selective or supe-selective embolization for bleeding control, and only when there is evidence of active contrast extravasation. This minimizes ischemia and necrosis compared to non-selective main arterial trunk occlusion.
