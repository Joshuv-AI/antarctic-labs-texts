Holistic approach and Advanced Color Singlet Identification for physics measurements at high energy frontier
Yongfeng Zhu, Hao Liang, Yuexin Wang, Yuzhi Che, Hengyu Wang, Jianfeng Jiang, Chen Zhou, Huilin Qu, Manqi Ruan

Abstract
To enhance the discovery power of high-energy colliders, we propose a holistic approach and Advanced Color Singlet Identification (ACSI), both of which utilize inclusive reconstructed information as input. The holistic approach is designed to simultaneously classify physics events, while ACSI focuses on associating final-state particles with their parent massive bosons. Applied to the physics benchmarks of ν ​ ν ¯ ​ H \nu\bar{\nu}H and q ​ q ¯ ​ H q\bar{q}H processes with H → b ​ b ¯ H\to b\bar{b} , c ​ c ¯ c\bar{c} , g ​ g gg , and s ​ s ¯ s\bar{s} decays, these concepts improve the expected precisions by factors of two to six, and enable the potential observation of the rare H → s ​ s ¯ H\to s\bar{s} decay at future electron-positron Higgs factories.

Detector concept, Samples, and Tools.—

The AURORA detector concept
Wang et al. 2025
, evolved from the CEPC Conceptual Design Report (CDR) baseline
The CEPC Study Group 2018
, is used as the reference detector in this study.
It features high-granularity electromagnetic and hadronic calorimeters, a low-material high-precision tracker, a high-resolution vertex detector, and a large solenoid magnet.
A key design of AURORA is the usage of 5-dimensional (5D) calorimetry, where each calorimeter cell provides time measurement with a resolution of roughly 100 ps.
Using AI-enhanced reconstruction, the AURORA concept provides a proof of principle for one-to-one reconstruction, a highly efficient reconstruction and identification of all the final state particles in a physics event
Wang et al. 2025
.
In this study, we model the AURORA performance using a fast simulation tool that has been validated by the full simulation performance studies.

The event samples used in this study are generated using Whizard-1.95

Sjostrand et al. 2006

To estimate the uncertainties introduced by hadronization, we also compare to Herwig-7.2.2
Bahr et al. 2008

Bellm et al. 2016

Bierlich et al. 2022

Discussion and Summary.—

Efficient classification of physics events is critical for the scientific exploration at the high-energy frontier.
We propose and realize the concepts of the holistic approach and ACSI, where both concepts take all reconstructed particle information as input using SOTA AI technologies.
The holistic approach serves as a framework for general event classification, while the ACSI characterizes the complex hadronic events by identifying final-state particles originating from individual massive bosons.
Tested on simulated data samples of CEPC, these two concepts boost the relative precisions of physics benchmarks by two to six times, see Table
2
and Fig.
3
.

The relative statistical precision of the
H\to b\bar{b}
,
H\to c\bar{c}
, and
H\to gg
measurements in the
\nu\bar{\nu}H
channel using the holistic approach is close to the statistical limit.
In contrast, the precision of the
H\to s\bar{s}
measurement is currently limited by contamination from
H\to c\bar{c}
and
H\to gg
events.
In the
q\bar{q}H
channel, we observe that the ACSI method yields a significant improvement on top of the holistic approach, despite both using the inclusive information of all reconstructed particles.
This improvement can be attributed to the inductive knowledge introduced by ACSI during training—it learns that all reconstructed particles should be partitioned adequately into two groups.
Nonetheless, the performance achieved with ideal CSI remains two to three times better than that of ACSI.
While we cannot yet pinpoint the ultimate achievable performance of ACSI, further development in this direction would be highly valuable.

It should be noted that the benchmark analyses presented in this manuscript adopt a simplified approach.
In real experiments, the final measurement precision will be influenced by reducible backgrounds, systematic uncertainties arising from detector imperfections and instabilities, and theoretical uncertainties stemming from discrepancies between real-world physics and the relevant theoretical models, etc.
Explicitly, we would like to point out:

•

Backgrounds:
The current analysis considers only irreducible backgrounds.
However, reducible backgrounds—such as two-fermion processes, di-photon events, beam-induced backgrounds, and even detector noise—are also relevant to those measurements.
At a Higgs factory like CEPC, key measurements like
\ell^{+}\ell^{-}H
(Higgs recoil) and
\nu\bar{\nu}H
with
H\to b\bar{b}/c\bar{c}/gg
typically achieve signal efficiencies times purities ranging from 25% to 80%
Chen et al. 2017
;
Zhu et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2020
, even with conventional cut-based and BDT approaches.
In addition, the holistic and ACSI proposed in this study are expected to further enhance the separation between signal and reducible background.
Therefore, we expect the impact of reducible backgrounds to be moderate and controllable.

•

Experimental systematic uncertainties:
Experimental systematic uncertainties arise from limited understanding of the detector, with imperfections and instabilities in the detector system potentially playing a critical—and even dominant—role.
Systematic control is thus a key requirement in detector research and design for future Higgs factories.
In this context, the development of detectors capable of one-to-one correspondence between reconstructed particles and final-state particles, as proposed in Ref.
Wang et al. 2025
, could be essential to control the relative systematics, by providing the inclusive information of reconstructed particles in the continuous data stream.

•

Theoretical uncertainties:
Both the holistic and ACSI approaches are based on supervised learning, which relies on high-quality and well-validated simulations.
Since these methods use inclusively reconstructed particles as input, the modeling of hadronization
Webber 1994
, which governs the distribution of final-state particles, becomes critical.
To assess the impact of hadronization models, we extract results under several scenarios.
Each scenario involves a pair of hadronization models—one for training and one for inference.
The results are summarized in Fig.
4
.
For the
H\to b\bar{b}
and
H\to c\bar{c}
channels, different combinations yield comparable performance, demonstrating the robustness of the holistic approach.
For
H\to gg
and
H\to s\bar{s}
, variations between model combinations can result in relative differences of up to a factor of two—likely due to the less well-understood hadronization processes of gluon and strange jets in current models.
This discrepancy underscores the need for more accurate and data-driven hadronization modeling, which can be improved iteratively through validation with real experimental data.
Combinations involving Herwig-7.2.2 and Pythia-8.313 consistently outperform those with Pythia-6.4, as the former two predict higher yields of charged kaons, enhancing the identification of strange jets.
It is also notable that the results with same model in training and inferring always leads to slight better results comparing to the case by changing one model to alternative.

The difference between our estimation and other studies could arise from different facility specifications, different background estimation, while the different analysis technologies also played a critical role.
In this sense, our study set up an optimistic benchmark, which could be approached through advancements in detector design, the development of more sophisticated AI tools, and the refinement of analysis strategies.

The holistic approach and ACSI can substantially enhance discovery power, particularly for measurements involving small signals that are crucial for the discoveries.
The observed improvements are rooted in the rapid advancements of AI technologies, which enable efficient processing of large data sets in high-dimensional phase spaces.
With further development and optimization, these concepts could be scaled to complex event topology, such as high multiplicity jet events and physics events at hadron colliders with significant pile-ups.
Series of follow-up studies—spanning detector development, theoretical investigations, and AI research—are necessary to probe the underlying mechanisms and guiding principles of these AI-driven approaches.
These studies aim not only to optimize performance but also to provide references and benchmarks for research on AI interpretability.
It’s worth mention that, after specify the signal process, the holistic approach is in principle free of human intervene.
With future advancements, the holistic approach points to a novel paradigm of task sharing between humans and AI: human define the goal, AI serves as the means.

Appendix B
Appendix B: Particle Transformer for ACSI

The Particle Transformer was originally developed for jet tagging tasks.
It consists of eight encoder blocks, each with eight attention heads, followed by two additional attention layers that aggregate the encoded information into a single representation for jet-level classification (e.g., to predict the type of quark that initiated the jet).
However, in the case of ACSI, where the goal is to predict the origin of each final-state particle individually, such global aggregation is not required.
Instead, we retain the per-particle representations after the eight encoder blocks and directly perform classification at the token (particle) level.

In this study, ACSI is trained and applied to fully hadronic
WW
,
ZZ
, and
ZH
events, where each event contains exactly two bosons.
The objective is to assign the final-state particles to two groups, each corresponding to one of the bosons.
Since this is a supervised learning task, we assign a binary label (0 or 1) to each final-state particle, indicating its boson of origin.
Importantly, we do not distinguish between the specific identities of the two bosons (e.g., which is
W
or
Z
, or
H
).
Instead, the boson that appears first in the event record is arbitrarily labeled as 0, and the second as 1.
These labels are symmetric and interchangeable.

During training, the loss function is designed to group particles into two sets.
Because the labels are symmetric (0 and 1 can be swapped), we compute the loss twice: once using the original labels and once using the flipped labels.
The final loss for each event is taken as the minimum of the two.
This strategy ensures that the model focuses on grouping structure rather than absolute label identity.
