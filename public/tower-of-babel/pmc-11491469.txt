Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and cycling performance on the 3-minute aerobic test (3mAT): placebo and nocebo effects
Authors: Nicholas J Hanson, Rachel M Maceri, Panagiotis Koutakis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranscranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has been used extensively but research on its efficacy within the sport and exercise science realm has been inconsistent. There may be placebo and nocebo effects present with its use. Our objective was to determine if subjects can be influenced to believe that tDCS will improve cycling performance. Subjects were separated into a belief group (B; 5 women, 6 men) and a disbelief group (DB; 9 women, 3 men). The B group was told that the stimulation would improve performance on a subsequent cycling test. In the DB group, subjects were told that it was not effective and would hinder performance. The cycling test was a 3-minute aerobic test (3mAT) where subjects maintained the highest power output possible for three minutes, after completing a full 20 min warmup. During the warmup, they were given either no stimulation (control) or 2 mA bilateral stimulation over the M1 region. There was a very slight increase in maximal minute power for the B group (0.22%) and a small decrease for the DB group (-1.00%); however, these differences were not significant. No significant differences were found for any of the cycling variables. In conclustion, tDCS was unable to improve performance on the 3mAT. These findings, in conjunction with others, suggest that the acute effect of tDCS is still questionable when aiming to enhance endurance performance.Subject terms: Neurophysiology, Motivation, Human behaviour, Musculoskeletal system

Introduction

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a non-invasive technique where a specific area of the cerebral cortex is stimulated with a low-level current of 1-2 mA with at least two electrodes1. By enhancing the excitability of neurons within the brain, an action potential threshold is lowered, which can lead to decreased muscular fatigue and increased power output2. tDCS has a range of uses, from treating neurological and psychiatric disorders to chronic pain relief3. It can also be used to improve physical performance in sports through stimulation of the primary motor cortex, or M1 4,5. Many studies and anecdotal reports have shown positive findings and has led to the term “neurodoping”4, or using brain stimulation as a performance-enhancing measure.

Recent research regarding the effectiveness of tDCS on performance variables in sport and exercise has provided inconsistent results. A meta-analysis using 19 studies on the effect of single-session anodal tDCS on sport-specific performance in athletes showed that stimulation of the M1 brain region does show a significant moderate effect, or standardized mean difference (SMD)5. There are studies have shown positive results with tDCS; for example, Huang et al.6 investigated the effects of tDCS on power output during repeated sprint cycling and Okano et al.7 explored this relationship during an incremental exercise test. Both studies determined that tDCS was effective in improving performance. However, other studies have deemed tDCS to be an ineffective intervention8–11. With such conflicting results, it is difficult to determine how influential tDCS is on physiological responses surrounding exercise.

It is possible that the perceived benefits from tDCS are from the placebo effect, a psychobiological phenomenon that occurs after an individual has been influenced to believe a treatment to be effective12,13. The nocebo effect has the opposite influence, and is a response to an ineffective or even harmful treatment14. For decades, use of the placebo effect was mostly limited to medical settings (e.g., clinical trials), but has received some attention in the field of exercise and sport science12,13. Recently, there have been numerous reviews on the topic of placebos and nocebos in sports14,15,16. In exercise and sport science research, placebo and nocebo effects have been implicated with respiratory training devices, ergogenic aids, and various forms of supplementation17, but have yet to be investigated with transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS).

There are many factors to consider when determining whether or not tDCS is an adequate method for enhancing performance. One such factor is the belief in the effectiveness of this intervention. Previous research has not yet investigated the placebo effect and how it elucidates psychobiological outcomes after receiving tDCS. Because of this, research is warranted to evaluate these perceptions of tDCS and how they influence exercise performance. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine if subjects can be influenced to believe that tDCS will improve performance on a cycling test. We hypothesized that subjects led to believe in the efficacy of tDCS would show improved performance on an exercise test, thus displaying a placebo effect. Conversely, we theorized that subjects led to believe in the inefficacy of tDCS would lead to a decrement in performance, showing a nocebo effect.

Methods

Subjects

The study included recreationally active subjects (N = 23; 14 women, 9 men; mean ± SD age: 25.0 ± 6.2 years). Subjects were recruited from the university and surrounding community. Individuals who reported a musculoskeletal injury within the last six months were excluded from participation. Other exclusion criteria included history of epilepsy or seizures, cochlear implants, pacemakers, metal implants in the head, and more than one cardiovascular risk factor (using ACSM guidelines). The study was approved by the Western Michigan University Human Subjects Institutional Review Board (IRB-2021-36). If subjects met all required inclusion criteria, they were asked to read and sign an informed consent document. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Subjects were placed in one of two groups using a counterbalanced order: belief (B): persuaded to believe that tDCS is an effective technique that will enhance performance, or disbelief (DB): persuaded to believe the tDCS is not an effective technique and it will not enhance performance. Subjects were blind to this portion of the study. It was explained that the purpose of the was simply to determine whether or not the administration of tDCS affects performance.

Experimental procedures

Subjects were asked to come to the lab for three total visits, each separated by a minimum of 24 h. All visits were completed within a two-week period. The first visit was an informational session where the subject was asked to read the informed consent, and sign if they agreed to participate. Subjects then completed a Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire, or PAR-Q + form, and were taken through a health screening document to if they should seek medical clearance before participation in the study. The researchers then read from one of two oral scripts depending on which group the subject was placed; these scripts were used in hopes to persuade subjects towards the belief or disbelief in the effectiveness of tDCS. The script for the B group began by telling subjects that the method was safe and effective, and easy to use. Subjects were told that it is used by many professional athletes and sports teams, and has been shown to improve performance. Published peer-reviewed manuscripts on the efficacy of the equipment, along with a short video from the manufacturer’s website of a testimonial from an athlete were also shown. The DB group was told that although the equipment is safe and easy to use, it does not seem to work and would likely hinder performance on the cycling test. They were told that independent studies need to be run to show that some tDCS equipment does not enhance performance. Subjects were also presented with peer-reviewed studies with findings that showed tDCS as not effective for athletic performance.

After the oral script was read, body composition analysis was performed using an InBody 570 Body Composition Analyzer (InBody, Cerritos, California). This is a full-body bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) device used to estimate body fat percentage. This is a quick analysis in which a subject is asked to take off their shoes and socks, and step on to a scale. While the unit is analyzing body composition, they are required to stand still, while also holding on to the attached handgrips. The test takes approximately 2–3 min. If body fat percentage was determined to be greater than 20% for males or 33% for females, subjects were not allowed to continue in the study. Body fat percentages greater than or equal to these values are considered “overfat”18 and the goal was to have a homogenous sample of relatively fit individuals.

Subjects were then fully familiarized with all laboratory equipment that would be used. A Wattbike Pro cycle ergometer (Woodway, Waukesha, Wisconsin) was used for all exercise testing. The Wattbike includes software that has preprogrammed tests, such as the 3-minute aerobic test (3mAT), which can measure maximal minute power, or MMP, and provides a reliable and valid VO2max prediction19. Data from the Wattbike was exported and provided the following: peak power (W), average power (W), peak cadence (rpm), average cadence (rpm), metabolic equivalents (METs), and heart rate (bpm). Once fitted to the Wattbike, they performed a full 20-minute warm up, followed by the 3mAT. Warm up: Subjects were asked to cycle on the Wattbike for 20 min, keeping their cadence between 70 and 90 rpm. At the 8- and 12-minute mark of the warm up, subjects completed 6s, all-out sprints. 3mAT: Subjects were asked to cycle as hard as they could for the full duration of the test (3 min), with the goal of maintaining the highest power output possible.

Once the familiarization trial was completed, the researchers recorded the VO2max estimate that was generated by the Wattbike. To minimize variability in fitness level and limit outliers within the dataset (those with low cardiorespiratory fitness), a VO2max threshold for continued participation in this study was used. Male subjects with an estimated VO2max below 40.1 ml·kg−1·min−1 and female subjects with an estimated VO2max below 30.5 ml·kg−1·min−1were excluded. These are the values for the 25th percentile for men and women, according to the American College of Sports Medicine18.

The following two visits, which were counterbalanced, included experimental and control conditions. The details of each are as follows: Experimental condition: tDCS treatment was administered through the Halo Sport (Halo Neuroscience, San Francisco, California): This is a commercially available headphone set that has electrodes built into the top strap. After being fully saturated in water, the three 6.4 × 4.4 cm electrodes were placed in the headset, which was then positioned on the subject’s head, directly across the vertex. According to the manufacturer, during bilateral stimulation, the anode is centered on Cz and the two cathodes are over C5 and C6 (using international 10–20 EEG placement guidelines). The headset connects via Bluetooth to a mobile application where in which the investigators were able to adjust intensity and check for an appropriate connection. The treatment was approximately 2.0 mA, which corresponds to level “9” intensity on the mobile application. This stimulation was provided for the entire duration of the warm up (20-minutes); after which, it was removed and the subject immediately performed the 3mAT. Control condition: No treatment was administered during this session. Subjects completed the 20-minute warm up without tDCS, followed by the 3mAT.

For both conditions, subjects were asked their rating of perceived exertion (RPE) every 30s of the 3mAT using the Borg 6–20 scale20. The scale ranges from 6 to 20 with verbal anchors such as no exertion (6), somewhat hard (13), or maximal exertion (20). Increases in RPE are based on physiological changes that occur during exertion (e.g., increased heart rate or breathing rate). Upon completion of the last visit, subjects were asked to complete a supplemental question form in order for the investigators to analyze whether or not the deception technique used was effective. Once the data collection for the entire study was complete, subjects were debriefed on the true purpose.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27, Armonk, NY). Mixed model ANOVAs were used to compare the within-subjects (tDCS condition: experimental vs. control), and the between-subjects (group: belief or disbelief) factors. Normality of the data was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were used. Effect sizes are shown with partial eta squared (ηp2), Cohen’s d, and Pearson’s r correlation coefficients. Significance was set a priori at < 0.05.

Results

Cycling performance variables: As shown in Fig. 1, there was a small increase in MMP for the B group (0.22%) and a small decrease for the DB group (-1.00%); however, these differences were not significant. The mixed-model ANOVA showed that there was no significant main effect of tDCS (F(1,21) = 0.039, p = 0.845, ηp2 = 0.002) and no interaction present (F(1,21) = 0.102, p = 0.752, ηp2 = 0.005) for MMP. Similar results were found for all other cycling variables (all p > 0.05). Group comparison for the dependent variables of interest are presented in Table 1. For all subjects, average RPE during the tests increased significantly over time (F(2,40) = 268.5, p = < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.931) at a similar rate (Fig. 2). At the first RPE assessment (1 min; Fig. 3), there was a significant group*condition interaction (p = 0.031, ηp2 = 0.213); the B group increased their first reported RPE in the experimental condition, compared to the control (13.2 ± 0.44 vs. 14.0 ± 0.47), whereas the DB group had similar values (12.8 ± 0.44 vs. 12.8 ± 0.47) between conditions.

Subjects were questioned after their final session about whether or not they believed the tDCS to work effectively and improve performance. In the B group, 6 subjects (55%) thought that it was effective, and 5 (45%) did not. In the DB group, more subjects believed it to be effective than not [8(67%) vs. 4(33%)]. Overall, 61% were confident that it helped them, and 39% were not convinced that it was beneficial. There was no relationship between this belief and their actual cycling performance, as measured by the increase or decrease in their MMP (r = 0.162, p = 0.230). Although not significant, the DB group did have a stronger correlation between their belief and cycling performance compared to the B group (r = 0.312, p = 0.162 vs. r = 0.091, p = 0.389, respectively).

Discussion

Previous studies on ergogenic aids and other products purported to enhance performance have suggested that an athlete must truly believe that a product is beneficial for it to make a significant positive impact. Brophy-Williams et al21. recorded subjects’ perception of the efficacy of compression socks for recovery. They had subjects perform two 5 km time trials on a treadmill, one with the compression socks and one without. They found that when subjects were grouped according to their belief in the efficacy of the socks, only those with a strong belief had significantly better running performance while wearing the socks. Our attempt to persuade subjects to either believe or disbelieve in the product may have been unsuccessful; it may be necessary to assess their belief firsthand, and then separate them into groups based on this factor. It is possible that although some subjects were placed in the belief group, they still were not convinced that it would improve performance, and may even have thought that the stimulation could be harmful. Therefore, the inherent belief in a product’s efficacy may not be able to be changed.

The main finding in the current study was that even when the subjects in the belief condition were led to believe that the tDCS treatment would be beneficial, it did not lead to significantly improved performance. A similar finding was discovered with the disbelief group; performance was not significantly worse after administering tDCS. This was shown by no significant interactions between the two factors on the dependent variables of interest. There are several possible reasons for this outcome, such as the general difficulty in elucidating placebo/nocebo effects, the chosen performance test, exercise preference, and tDCS protocol.

Placebo/nocebo effects

Beedie & Foad22published a review paper on the placebo effect in sport, and found that psychological variables such as motivation, expectancy and conditioning might drive both positive and negative outcomes. Additionally, it is likely that these variables interact with each other, and with physiological variables. In a previous study, Beedie23provided athletes with a placebo ergogenic aid capsule before a repeated-sprint protocol. Some subjects were told that the capsule would enhance both sprint and endurance performance, and others were told that it would have a negative impact on performance. The subjects in the latter condition did have a decrease in performance, showing a nocebo effect, while the subjects in the belief condition had a slight increase. This is similar to our findings, in which the subjects in the disbelief group had nearly 2x percent decrease in MMP compared to the belief group (-1.36% vs. + 0.77%). As suggested by Beedie23, a negative belief may simply be a more powerful influence on behavior than a positive belief; when an athlete is told that something will hinder performance, it is likely that a simple drop in motivation leads to slower times or lower power output. In an earlier study, Beedie et al24. told subjects that they were receiving a placebo and two dosages of caffeine, when in reality all conditions used a placebo; performance dropped as the supposed dosage of caffeine increased. Also, one subject reported severe caffeine-related symptoms and could not complete the study. These finding suggest that the placebo and nocebo effects are very strong in exercise science research, but sometimes difficult to elucidate.

Performance test

Another possible reason for the lack of significant findings was possibly the performance test that was chosen for the study. The 3mAT was implemented because it is a test that cyclists and non-cyclists can both perform with relative ease, as long as a familiarization trial is completed. Also, the 20 min warmup was an ideal time to administer the tDCS treatment. However, a recent review25on the effects of tDCS on cyclists and runners showed that it helps with overall performance on a time to exhaustion (TTE) but not performance-based tests. There have been several studies showing differences between open-loop tests (e.g., TTE, when the endpoint is unknonwn) and closed-loop tests (where there is a clear endpoint, based upon time or distance)26–29. Future studies should carefully choose their performance test based upon the present study as well as previous literature.

Exercise test preference

Promising results concerning the relationship between performance and exercise preference (i.e., exercise modality and/or intensity) have been reported. Specifically, positive affective responses such as mood satisfaction have been reported when exercise intensity is matched with subjects’ typical intensity selection30,31. Correspondingly, positive affective responses have been shown to promote physiological benefits. Parfit et al32. observed that subjects exercised significantly harder, indicated by exercising at a greater percentage of their VO2max, when implementing exercise at a preferred (versus non-preferred) intensity. Moreover, subjects’ work rate increased over the duration of exercise, in comparison to the non-preferred intensity selection. Additionally, due to exercise modality preference, individuals have been shown to report a significantly lower perception of effort33. Participant history with specific exercise modalities, as well as experience and familiarity with the expected physiological responses, creates greater resistance to fatigue. In the current study, subjects’ history of exercise preference was not obtained. Therefore, it is plausible that the chosen test did not align with all subjects’ preferences, leading to more negative affective responses (i.e., dissatisfaction), which in return could hinder performance. Exercise preference is possibly a moderating variable that should be considered in future studies.

tDCS application

Previous research has denoted a large variability in the effectiveness of tDCS, which may be attributed to the specific tDCS protocol being used. Specifically, methodology for electrode placement can alter the strength of cortical excitability. In a recent meta-analysis5, it was documented that just over 50% of studies used conventional tDCS (one pair of sponge electrodes), while the remainder of the studies used varying electrode montages such as bilateral, conventional tDCS (two pairs of sponge electrodes), Halo-Sport devices (two implemented electrodes), and HD-tDCS (an array of small gel-based electrodes). Additionally, cortical target areas varied between M1, temporal cortex (TC), prefrontal cortex (PFC), and cerebellum (CB). In studies looking at endurance performance, M1 stimulation demonstrated no effects on performance markers5,34but has shown improvements in skill-related components such as reaction time35and motor learning36. Conversely, studies using PFC stimulation had more promising results for endurance performance37,38and reducing the negative impact of mental fatigue39. Due to the design of the Halo device, the current study used a bilateral design with focus on M1, but perhaps traditional anodal stimulation over the PFC would produce more favorable results.

While findings within single session tDCS seem inconsistent, studies using multiple sessions of tDCS seem more consistent. In several studies using a combination of multiple tDCS sessions (ranging from 3 to 10 sessions) and training, there seem to be greater effects on the excitability of the motor cortex and consequently enhanced performance40,41. Hendy & Kidgell40implemented nine sessions of tDCS during a 6-week training session, resulting in a 22.6% increase in motor-evoked potential (MEP) amplitude, concluding that multi-session tDCS significantly improved maximal voluntary strength. However, it seems that improvements with the use of tDCS relate to resistance training and sport-specific skills. da Silva Machado et al34. completed a systematic review that suggesting there is weak evidence that tDCS over M1 improves cycling performance. These findings, again, suggest that improvements in markers of cardiorespiratory endurance may require investigating the effect of tDCS in other brain areas like the PFC.

Limitations

The biggest possible limitation of this study was the ability of the researchers to fully convince the subjects to either believe or disbelieve in the efficacy of tDCS to alter performance. Every attempt was made to persuade the subjects to believe that tDCS would improve performance, or would hinder it, depending on the group assignment. Sometimes this may not have worked, and in some subjects they may have come into the study with preconceived beliefs about the product. However, when questioned about any previous opinions about tDCS and athletic performance, only one subject had heard of the device and all subjects seemed to be open to the researchers’ educated opinions about it. Another limitation is the subject group assignments; a coordinated effort was made in an attempt to have equal numbers of men and women in each of the groups, but due to subject dropout and self-imposed thresholds for cardiorespiratory fitness and body composition, numbers were slightly unequal. The B group consisted of 5 women and 6 men; DB had 9 women and 3 men. However, when comparing the groups on various demographic variables (Table 2) there were no significant differences found.

Conclusion

In conclusion, tDCS stimulation of primary motor cortex was unable to improve performance on a 3mAT cycling test. These findings, in conjunction with others, suggest that the use of tDCS is still questionable when aiming to enhance endurance performance. tDCS continues to be proposed as a potential ergogenic aid and its use continues to grow. However, these devices should be used seldomly with endurance athletes, especially using M1 as the target region of interest, as the results are not favorable regarding markers of performance they look to enhance. Future research surrounding tDCS should investigate whether longer periods of use (i.e., multiple training sessions) and different stimulation sites (e.g., the prefrontal cortex) can improve endurance performance prior to implementing this ergogenic aid.
