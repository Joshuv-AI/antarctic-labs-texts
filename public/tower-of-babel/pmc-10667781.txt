Surgical debridement and continuous irrigation for early surgical site infection caused by Staphylococcus aureus with a closed patella fracture surgery: A case report
Authors: Xiong Wang, Qing Gu, Wenqiang Wei, Shuming Zi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Postoperative surgical site infection (SSI) of fracture internal fixation is a huge problem [1,2]. It commonly occurs after proximal tibial fracture surgery, ankle fracture surgery, elbow fracture surgery, and open fracture surgery [[3], [4], [5]]. Infection related to orthopedic devices was the most serious and disastrous result for surgeons and patients [6]. Control of the infection after internal fixation commonly costs a long time, causes a great economic burden and affects the patient's quality of life. External factors and host factors are the main causes of SSI [7]. The external factors included the nature of the injury, the damage extent of soft tissue, fracture type, aseptic operation, and perioperative management, while the host factors included immune status, nutritional status, and basic disease.

The patella is the largest sesamoid bone in the human body and plays an important role in stabilizing knee joint function, supporting walking, and strengthening quadriceps strength [8]. Surgical intervention is required when patella fracture affects knee function, it can provide a better prognosis than conservative treatment [9]. The surgical indications include articular surface fracture, comminuted fracture, fracture displacement greater than 2–4 mm, and extensor mechanism disruption [10,11]. However, the postoperative complications of patella fracture were high [12]. The infection was one of the severe complications, ranging from 2 to 10 % [13]. It usually results in knee dysfunction, walking difficulty, and somatopsychic disturbance [14]. There is no uniform standard treatment method for SSI of fracture surgery [15]. To the best of our knowledge, there are few studies to research the treatment method for early SSI caused by S. aureus after patella fracture surgery. In this study, we explored whether aggressive surgical debridement, continuous articular irrigation, sensitive antibiotics, and active rehabilitation training can control infection and promote functional recovery in the early SSI with a closed patella fracture surgery, and to provide clinicians with some alternative treatment recommendations in this case.

Presentation of case

A 68-year-old female patient had a closed right patella fracture due to a fall to the ground. The patient was sent to our hospital as an emergency case. A comminuted patella fracture was confirmed through X-ray and computed tomography (CT) scans. Remarkably, the patient did not present any comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, peripheral arterial diseases, or other chronic conditions. Additionally, she had no known drug, food, or other allergen allergies, and no history of tuberculosis. Her nutritional status was within normal ranges, with a Body Mass Index (BMI) of 20.54 kg/m2. Routine laboratory test results were unremarkable—total protein was 63 g/L, albumin 36 g/L, and blood glucose level at 5.3 mmol/L. Subsequently, she underwent open reduction, followed by internal fixation using tension band wiring and circle wire. To prevent infection, cefazolin sodium pentahydrate (1 g) was administered intravenously 30 min before surgery and maintained for 24 h post-operation. Postoperative X-rays demonstrated successful fracture fixation. Both the surgical incision and postoperative indicators appeared normal. Consequently, the patient was discharged three days post-operation (Fig. 1).

Preoperative X-ray and computed tomography scan (CT), postoperative X-ray.

One week after discharge, the patient reported symptoms of wound infection: redness, swelling, and noticeable secretions. Outpatient dressing changes and oral antibiotics were prescribed to control the infection, but these measures proved ineffective. The patient was consequently readmitted to our hospital. Preoperative examination of the wound revealed redness, swelling, elevated skin temperature, and purulent secretions (Fig. 2A). A culture was taken from the infected area before surgery to identify the causative bacteria. Laboratory results indicated a white blood cell count (WBC) of 8.7 × 109/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 127.5 mg/h, a neutrophilic granulocyte percentage of 80.7 %, and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 121 mm/h. Based on the aforementioned findings, the diagnosis was early postoperative surgical site infection (SSI) after internal fixation of a patellar fracture.

The second admission performance of the patient and the treatment process.

Upon completion of all preoperative examinations, aggressive surgical debridement along with continuous intra-articular irrigation was performed, while retaining the internal fixation system. A profusion of purulent secretion and inflammatory tissues were encountered, all of which were completely removed. Portions of these purulent secretions and inflammatory tissues were preserved for culture. Thereafter, large amounts of hydrogen peroxide, povidone‑iodine, and saline were used for irrigation until clear effluent was observed. We kept the knee in the extension position and placed two drain tubes under the muscle flap and cavum articular respectively for irrigation (Fig. 2B).

Gentamicin 0.4 g was injected into 500 ml saline for continuous irrigation within 24 h (Fig. 2C, D). We chose levofloxacin combined with the third-generation cephalosporin anti-infection treatment according to clinical experience. Povidone‑iodine was used for disinfecting wounds. The irrigation was unobstructed and the drainage liquid was clear. The bacterial culture of secretions was Staphylococcus aureus. Drug sensitivity tests suggested that it was sensitive to levofloxacin, rifampicin, and so on, but is not sensitive to cephalosporin. So we changed cephalosporin to rifampicin for anti-infective therapy. Levofloxacin (200 mg, QD) was used intravenously, and rifampicin (450 mg, BID)was used orally to prevent infection until discharge. However, the knee joint appeared with serious swelling, bubble, pain, pruritus, and skin irritative reaction on the 4th day (Fig. 2E). Therefore, we removed the drainage tubes to avoid continued irrigation aggravating the skin irritative symptoms. We also applied an anti-allergy drug and normal saline disinfection instead of povidone‑iodine (Fig. 2F).

However, the wound still showed swelling, skin irritative reaction, and obvious aches. Additional MRI showed a large amount of effusion, and there may be residual infection (Fig. 2G). Laboratory results revealed that WBC was 5.0 × 109/L, C reactive protein was 10.9 mg/h, neutrophilic granulocyte percentage was 71.3 %, and ESR was 131 mm/h. We recommended the patient take one more surgical debridement, but the patient rejected any further surgical treatment and hoped to be discharged strongly. The current treatment was continued until the skin irritative symptom was relieved, and the patient was discharged on the 14th day (Fig. 2H).

Levofloxacin (0.5 g, QD) and Rifampicin (450 mg, BID) were utilized to control possible infection for 1 month. In addition, regular follow-up rehabilitation exercises were also provided, such as isometric quadriceps, ankle pump exercises, straight-leg raising, and knee flexion-extension exercises. At 1 month, laboratory results revealed that WBC was 4.9 × 109/L, C reactive protein was 5 mg/h, neutrophilic granulocyte percentage was 62.0 %, and ESR was 123 mm/h. The X-ray revealed good internal fixation, and no sign of wound infection was observed at 1.5 months (Fig. 3A). All laboratory inflammation markers were normal at 3 months, WBC was 4.8 × 109/L, C reactive protein was 3 mg/h, neutrophilic granulocyte- percentage was 56.7 %, and ESR was 15 mm/h. The X-ray showed fracture union and knee function recovered well at 6 months (Fig. 3B). In the final follow-up, the patient returned to normal life, walked normally, and the knee joint function recovered well. The knee joint ROM was about 100° (Fig. 3C). This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [16].

The X-ray and knee function recovery at 1.5 months, 6 months, and 1.5 years.

Discussion

A comprehensive understanding of the post-fracture surgical site infection (SSI) mechanism is crucial for effective treatment and prognosis. The Association for the Study of Internal Fixation (AO/ASIF) has reported that the diagnostic criteria for SSI include surgical wound dehiscence, purulent secretion, and a positive bacterial culture [5]. Early SSI is defined as occurring within 2 weeks following fracture fixation [17]. It was divided into superficial and deep infections based on the different tissue layers involved in the infection [18]. The common etiology factors that induce infection include the elderly, malnutrition, poor immunity, associated diseases, and improper wound care. In this case, the preoperative diagnosis was superficial infection due to subcutaneous pus, but the intraoperative discovery of a large amount of pus in the joint was corrected to deep infection. Whether superficial or deep infection, removal of infected and necrotic tissue is the key to treatment, avoiding secondary infection and triggering dysfunction [19].

In this comminuted patella fracture, we adopt tension band wiring with cannulated screws combined with wire cerclage, because it can provide stable fixation and promote early functional rehabilitation exercise. Unfortunately, complications of postoperative wound infection happened. Although postoperative complications of patella fracture are not uncommon, but the early SSI of patella fracture surgery is rare [20]. At present, the common treatment methods include debridement, retention or removal of internal fixation, continuous irrigation or vacuum sealing drainage, antibiotic bone cement, sensitive antibiotics, and rehabilitation exercises [21]. However, internal fixation removal or not remains a great controversy. Removing the internal fixation too early may increase the instability of the fracture and increase pain. Retaining internal fixation can maintain stability, but bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation on the implant surface could hide the bacteria and increase the risk of persistent infection [22]. It is reported that the internal fixation should be retained as much as possible under the premise of good fracture reduction, stable internal fixation, and effective control of infection. If repeated debridement is ineffective, the scope of infection is wide, the symptom is severe, and the inflammation is not well controlled, the internal fixation should be removed [23]. After comprehensive consideration of various factors and infection conditions, aggressive surgical debridement, retention of internal devices, continuous articular irrigation, sensitive antibiotics, and rehabilitation exercises were adopted. Aggressive debridement and continuous articular irrigation can decrease the infection tissue and prompt wound healing. A high level of sensitive anti-infective antibiotic can inhibit and kill bacteria. Stable fixation of fracture and active rehabilitation training can promote bone healing and function recovery [24].

Povidone‑iodine solution has been widely and routinely used for surgical field disinfection and prevention of wound infection [25]. However, the surrounding of wound occurred severe povidone‑iodine irritative reaction in this case. We thought the possible reason may be attributed to the obvious tissue edema caused by a large amount of flushing and the high skin brittleness, which caused skin adverse reactions with povidone‑iodine. The patient's discomfort symptoms were alleviated and disappeared after the removal of the irrigation tubes, anti-allergy treatment, and dressing wound with saline [26]. Whereas, there is insufficient clinical evidence for the response of povidone‑iodine to skin irritation reactions [27]. We should note that povidone‑iodine could cause skin irritation reactions, especially for patients with brittleness skin, and serious soft tissue damage. Anti-allergy drugs, replaced povidone‑iodine with saline, and symptomatic treatment can help to relieve uncomfortable symptoms. The limitation of study is a case report of patella fracture surgery infection, it can provide some treatment experience but is not suit for all fracture surgery infections. In the future, more research is needed to compare the effectiveness of treatment for fracture postoperative infection.

Conclusions

Sufficient surgical debridement is vital to control infection of early SSI caused by Staphylococcus aureus with a closed patella fracture surgery. It can effectively remove necrotic tissue, pus and suspected infected tissue. Continuous articular irrigation and sensitive antibiotics help control infection, and active rehabilitation training improves knee function recovery.
