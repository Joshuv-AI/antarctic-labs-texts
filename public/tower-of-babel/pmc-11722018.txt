Disentangling the phenotypic patterns of hypertension and chronic hypotension
Authors: William W. Stead, Adam Lewis, Nunzia B. Giuse, Annette M. Williams, Italo Biaggioni, Lisa Bastarache
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective:2017 blood pressure (BP) categories focus on cardiac risk. We hypothesize that studying the balance between mechanisms that increase or decrease BP across the medical phenome will lead to new insights. We devised a classifier that uses BP measures to assign individuals to mutually exclusive categories centered in the upper (Htn), lower (Hotn) and middle (Naf) zones of the BP spectrum; and examined the epidemiologic and phenotypic patterns of these BP-categories.Methods:We classified a cohort of 832,560 deidentified electronic health records by BP-category; compared the frequency of BP-categories and four subtypes of Htn and Hotn by sex and age-decade; visualized the distributions of systolic, diastolic, mean arterial and pulse pressures stratified by BP-category; and ran Phenome-wide Association Studies (PheWAS) for Htn and Hotn. We paired knowledgebases for hypertension and hypotension and computed aggregate knowledgebase status (KB-status) indicating known associations. We assessed alignment of PheWAS results with KB-status for phecodes in the knowledgebase, and paired PheWAS correlations with KB-status to surface phenotypic patterns.Results:BP-categories represent distinct distributions within the multimodal distributions of systolic and diastolic pressure. They are centered in the upper, lower, and middle zones of mean arterial pressure and provide a different signal than pulse pressure. For phecodes in the knowledgebase, 85% of positive correlations align with KB-status. Phenotypic patterns for Htn and Hotn overlap for several phecodes and are separate for others. Our analysis suggests five candidates for hypothesis testing research, two where the prevalence of the association with Htn or Hotn may be under appreciated, three where mechanisms that increase and decrease blood pressure may be affecting one another’s expression.Conclusion:PairedPheWAS methods may open a phenome-wide path to disentangling hypertension and chronic hypotension. Our classifier provides a starting point for assigning individuals to BP-categories representing the upper, lower, and middle zones of the BP spectrum. 4.7 % of individuals matching 2017 BP categories for normal, elevated BP or isolated hypertension, have diastolic pressure < 60. Research is needed to fine-tune the classifier, provide external validation, evaluate the clinical significance of diastolic pressure < 60, and test the candidate hypotheses.

Introduction

Population studies have been key to our understanding of blood pressure (BP). Indeed, hypertension was first established as a risk factor in studies that repurposed life insurance data to study the relationship between hypertension and mortality [1]. Another population study, the Framingham Heart study, further clarified hypertension as a risk factor for heart disease [2]. However, these groundbreaking studies and the many that followed tended to focus on hypertensive states, which were both more common and appeared to have a more substantial influence on serious outcomes like mortality than hypotension. As a result, knowledge regarding the pathophysiology, epidemiology, and management of chronic hypotension is lacking.

Hypertension and hypotension are not merely opposites but rather two ends of a spectrum that reflect a complex biological control system. BP is the product of cardiac output and vascular resistance. These two physiologic variables reflect a web of central nervous system, cardiac, renal, and vascular mechanisms [3]. Receptors sense changes in one and effect changes in others to maintain homeostasis. They are affected by genetic, environmental, and behavioral factors and modified by many pathological mechanisms.

Systolic pressure (SBP) and diastolic pressure (DBP) are two measurements used in the clinic to assess BP. SBP is the peak pressure achieved when the heart is contracting, DBP is the trough when the heart is relaxing. SBP and DBP reflect different control and pathologic mechanisms. For example, SBP reflects the resistance in large conductance vessels and DBP reflects peripheral resistance during run off. In this paper, we hypothesize that studying SBP and DBP, both in terms of epidemiology and phenotypic correlates, will lead to new insights into the balance between mechanisms that increase and decrease blood pressure across diagnoses in the medical phenome.

To test this hypothesis, we used repurposed BP measures from electronic health records (EHR) of an academic medical center. We summarized these measures as medians for an individual and compared the distribution of these medians by age and sex with past epidemiology studies to assess similarity. We then assigned individuals to three mutually exclusive BP-categories that are centered in different zones of the BP spectrum: hypertensive (Htn), hypotensive (Hotn) and not affected (Naf). Importantly, these BP-categories are designed to signal differences in the balance between mechanisms that increase and decrease BP, they are not intended as clinical labels. Accordingly, we further stratified them into subtypes aligned with the 2017 BP categories [4] and compared the distribution of the subtypes to past epidemiology studies.

To study the phenotypic patterns of our BP-categories, we used PheWAS [5], a high throughput logistic modeling method that agnostically scans for correlation between a single variable (in this case BP categories) and thousands of phenotypes. These phenotypes, called phecodes [6], define case/control status, the dependent variable in the logistic regression. PheWAS allows researchers to observe correlations of a single classifier with a wide array of phenotypes sampled from the medical phenome. PheWAS was originally designed to study pleiotropy for genetic variants but has since been applied to a variety of non-genetic exposures and clinical measurements. When used in this manner, PheWAS represents a departure from the traditional epidemiology studies that seek to examine the relationships among a discrete set of predefined variables to test specific hypotheses. By widening the aperture, PheWAS can uncover unexpected relationships, but when applied to classifiers like BP-categories, which are associated with hundreds of phenotypes (as causes, risk factors and complications), the volume of associations poses a significant interpretive challenge.

We compared our PheWAS results with those reported in the literature using a knowledgebase to encode disease associations as phecodes, allowing for easy comparison between a PheWAS result set and associations reported in the literature. We previously reported PhecodeKbHtn, our knowledgebase of associations with hypertension [7]. For this study, we created a second knowledgebase, PhecodeKbHotn, with disease associations with hypotension. By pairing PhecodeKbHtn and PhecodeKbHotn, knowledgebases for conditions at opposite ends of the BP spectrum, we also address the concept of negative associations. We then paired PheWAS results for Htn and Hotn, annotated the results with these paired knowledgebases, and filtered paired correlations and associations to surface unexpected relationships. We include cross walks between this manuscript and the related previous publication [7] for methodological contributions (Table A.1) and scientific contributions (Table A.2).

Related work

Reference ranges

Reference ranges for biologic variables enable the transformation of continuous measures into discrete categories which can be used for diagnosis or research. In most cases reference ranges are calculated to exclude the upper and lower 2.5 % of values from a cross section of a population that is presumed to be healthy. While recent more sophisticated methods have been developed to create personalize reference intervals that reflect an individual’s characteristics [8,9], in general approaches to calculating reference ranges focus on single variables and do not consider how a change in a set point for one may affect the others. Moreover, simple calculations relying on upper and lower percentiles may not be appropriate for skewed distributions, which may be induced due to feedback loops controlling the variable [10].

Blood pressure ranges in the absence of disease

The definitive study of the range of BP in the absence of disease reported a statistical analysis of a random sample of persons who had previously applied for life insurance [1]. Robinson created a delimited group by arbitrarily separating individuals with BP >=140/90. Mode for the delimited group was SBP 112+/−10 DBP 70+/−8 for females and SBP 115+/−10 DBP 72+/−8 for males. Medians for the delimited group were like medians for the whole group, but means for the delimited group were considerably lower than the whole group. All prior studies reported an average for a population of individuals with normal and elevated BPs.

Chronic hypotension

The next year Robinson reported secondary analysis of the subgroup of his original cohort with SBP < 110 or DBP < 70 and declared that hypotension is the ideal BP because that subgroup had the lowest cardiovascular mortality [11]. More, recent data, however, indicate that hypotension is not a benign condition. Pemberton uses a definition of constitutional hypertension found in German texts as SBP constantly less than 110 for males and 100 for females with DBP under 60[12]. Constitutional hypotension is described as a condition with symptoms of fainting, fatigue, anxiety or depression; prevalence is estimated at 1–4 % with a 2–3:1 female:male ratio [13]. Although pressures less than 90/60 are generally considered hypotensive [14], and generally coincide with 5 % of normal subjects [15], guidelines for chronic hypotension do not exist, despite association with symptoms that affect quality of life [16].

Blood pressure categories

Since Framingham reported a linear relationship between the probability of cardiovascular disease and SBP [2], the focus of BP categories and management has been on SBP. The SPRINT trial’s report that treatment to SBP < 120 reduced deaths in high-risk adults without diabetes [17] led to the 2017 BP categories [4]. These categories are designed to detect and manage hypertension to reduce cardiovascular risk. Individuals are classified as normal if their SBP and DBP are less than 120 and 80 respectively. The categories do not specify a lower limit below which an individual would be classified as hypotensive.

Cardiac perfusion occurs predominantly during diastole. Studies suggest a “J-curve” where the drop in cardiovascular events reverses at DBP > 80 [18], or higher DBPs in presence of coronary artery obstruction [19]. An analysis of the relationship between DBP and high sensitivity cardiac troponin suggests myocardial injury begins to occur at DBP < 70 in patients with SBP >=120, accompanied by an increase in cardiac events but not stroke [20]. Stroke was included as a negative control since cerebral perfusion is not limited to diastole.

Materials and methods

Our PairedPheWAS study pipeline (Fig. 1) anneals parallel data analysis and knowledge management workstreams. They progress from extraction of raw sources, through abstraction of concepts and relationships, to analysis, before joining to support pattern visualization for hypothesis generation.

Data analysis

Data resource

We obtained data for this study from Vanderbilt University Medical Center’s (VUMC) Synthetic Derivative [21], a de-identified image of EHR data. VUMC’s Institutional Review Board #211951 designated this study as not qualifying as human subject research. This study utilized the following data elements: demographic information (age, sex, date of birth), blood pressure and heart rate (HR) measurements, visit type, and ICD-CM codes (version 9 and 10). Our cohort included individuals with greater than two viable measurement triplets on distinct days between ages 18 and 81. We defined viable measurements as SBP > 50 and < 270, DBP > 10 and < 170, SBP > DBP, and HR > 30 and < 200 and calculated age in years by dividing days by 365.25. We labeled each measurement triplet with visit type (emergency department, inpatient, outpatient, or unknown) by matching the measure date to start and end dates in the visit table; labeled each member of the cohort as E, I, O or U if all of their measures had the same visit type, or mixed E,I,O if measures came from 2 or 3 of the visit types. We excluded individuals with no ICDs and unknown sex. The resulting cohort comprised 832,560 individuals. Supplemental Figure A.1 presents the cohort extraction cascade and summarizes the data analysis process.

BP-categories and subtypes

We calculated medians of SBP and DBP measurements over the course of EHR follow-up without adjustment for hypertensive medication. Distribution of sex, median measurements, and duration of EHR follow-up are summarized by 10-year age increment in Supplemental Table A.3. Using linear regression, we modeled relationship of each measure – SBP, DBP and HR (as medians) – with sex, the first measurement age with a cubic spline, and the interaction between these two variables.

We classified individuals into three mutually exclusive BP-categories by delimiting the middle of the BP spectrum (Naf), as both SBP and DBP being between upper and lower limits (SBP 119–90, DBP 79–60). The upper cutoffs align with the 2017 BP categories [4]. Since blood pressures less than 90/60 are commonly recognized as hypotensive, we used lower limits based on those values. We classified Htn as either SBP or DBP being above its upper limit, and the other being above its lower limit. We classified Hotn as either SBP or DBP being below its lower limit. Our classification rules are presented in Supplemental Table A.4. 550,522 individuals were classified as Htn, 254,597 as Naf, 27,441 as Hotn. Supplemental Table A.5 presents the mix of measurement visit types for the cohort by BP-category.

We further defined subtypes for Htn and Hotn using cutoffs aligned with the 2017 BP categories as shown in Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table A.6 to support comparison of prevalence to published epidemiologic studies; stratified our cohort by first measurement age (10-year increments) and sex; calculated the percentage of each subtype for each stratum.

We tested the coherence of our BP-categories using one dimensional density plots [22] to visualize the shape of the distribution of each BP-category within the distributions of median SBP, DBP, mean arterial pressure and pulse pressure. We also visualized the distributions by sex within the distributions of median SBP and DBP before and after stratifying by BP-category.

Cases and controls

ICD codes were mapped to phecodeX [6]. For each phecode, we defined cases as individuals with two or more target phecodes on unique billing dates. We defined controls as individuals without the target phecode. The prevalence of each phecode for our cohort is presented in Appendix D.1. Phecodes with fewer than 400 cases were excluded from subsequent analysis.

PheWAS analysis

We used PheWAS to explore correlations between phecodes and the case/control status for Htn and Hotn. The PheWAS used logistic regression to test each phecode as the dependent variable. We included age at last encounter and sex as covariates. We used a Bonferroni correction to define “phenome-wide significant” associations (p-value < 0.05/total number of tests). To assess the effect of multicollinearity in logistic regression tests, we computed the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) using the vif() function available in the ‘car’ R package.

PhecodeKbHtnHotn

PhecodeKbHtnHotn is a knowledgebase that pairs chapters containing known association with two conditions, hypertension and hypotension. Each chapter includes positive associations between phecodes and the condition; with sections for three sources: literature review, VDDxKB [23] and phecodeX taxonomy [6]. The abstraction of a source includes positive associations between the condition and phecodes, the direction of the association (condition-to-phecode or phecode-to-condition), and the type of association (co-occurrence, part of a system, risk factor or cause).

We previously reported design principles for the structure of the knowledgebase, population of PhecodeKbHtn (the chapter on hypertension) and the structure and provenance of VDDxKB and phecodeX [7]. Here, we enhance the knowledgebase structure to pair chapters for conditions centered at opposite ends of a clinical spectrum and report population of PhecodeKbHotn (the chapter on hypotension).

Extension of the knowledgebase structure to accommodate paired chapters

We limited each chapter to positive associations between phecodes and a condition. Accordingly, PhecodeKbHtn contains positive associations between phecodes and hypertension and PhecodeKbHotn contains positive associations for hypotension. Negative associations, where one condition down modulates the other, are not represented except as they occur as positive associations in the chapter for the paired condition. For example, PhecodeKbHtn includes hypertension as a risk factor for myocardial infarction. PhecodeKbHotn includes myocardial infarction as a cause for cardiogenic shock. PhecodeKbHtn does not represent the negative association between myocardial infarction and hypertension that occurs when an individual progresses from hypertension through infarction to shock.

Since the associations in the two chapters are linked by phecodes, we are able to compute the aggregate PhecodeKbHtnHotn status by assigning phecodes KB-status = 1 if they have associations in the hypotension chapter and no association in the hypertension chapter, KB-status = 3 for the reverse, and KB-status = 4 if they have associations in both chapters.

Documentation for PhecodeKbHtnHotn, PhecodeKbHtn, and PhecodeKbHotn are presented in Appendix B with data dictionaries in Appendix B.1; phecode coverage of diagnoses by source in Table B.2.1; specificity of maps by source in Table B.2.2, one-to-many and many-to-one relationships by source in Table B.2.3.; Euler diagrams of contribution by source in Figure B.2.1; and number of phecodes by KB-status in Table B.2.4. PhecodeKbHtnHotn and related files are accessible at https://github.com/PheWAS/PhecodeKb.

Populating PhecodeKbHotn

Supplemental Table A.7 presents the process we used to abstract, assemble, and verify the literature section for PhecodeKbHotn. We organized the search around classifications of hypotension; conducted an initial search [24] to identify associations; mapped associations to phecodes; and conducted a second search to verify citations post-mapping. Appendix C.1 presents literature associations by phecode with citations for orthostatic hypotension [25–80], distributive shock [81–138], cardiac shock [88,99,120,139–197], hypovolemic shock [88,99,100,198–254], and obstructive shock [99,120,250,255–284]; see Appendix C.2 for the literature flowsheets and PubMed searches.

VDDxKB includes 4 diagnoses representing hypotension and 5 manifestations of a positive association with hypotension. The diagnoses are hypovolemic shock, cardiogenic shock, pyogenic shock, and heat exhaustion. The manifestations include 5 physical findings, SBP 90–110, SBP < 90, DBP < 60, narrow pulse pressure, and orthostatic hypotension. We extracted diagnoses linked to these diagnoses or manifestations and represented them in the VDDxKB section of PhecodeKbHotn as shown in Appendix B.1.4.

ICD includes codes for varieties of hypotension. Phecodes mapped to these ICDs are encoded in the phecode taxonomy section as PT = 1 to indicate they are positive controls and to limit circular logic. Supplemental Tables A.8 and A.9 present the phecode-ICD maps that define positive controls for hypertension and hypotension. Similarly, an ICD representing a concept in the literature or VDDX may map or multi-map to non-specific phecodes. These maps are encoded as PT = 4.

Analysis of PairedPheWAS correlations with PhecodeKbHtnHotn associations

We assessed PheWAS results for phecodes encoded in the knowledgebase as positive controls for hypertension with a Forest plot [285] pairing Htn and Hotn correlations and KB status for each phecode in the subset, showing correlates not reaching phenome-wide significance as filled circles at odds ratio (OR) = 1. We did the same for the positive controls for hypotension.

We also tested the hypothesis that the effect size of PheWAS results would be correlated with PhecodeKbHtnHotn KB-status with a linear model of betas from both Htn and Hotn PheWAS. We compared the resulting betas with a Kruskal-Wallis test, a nonparametric method testing whether samples come from the same distribution [286], and with pairwise Wilcox tests.

Further, a domain expert (WWS) compared paired PheWAS correlations for Htn and Hotn to PhecodeKbHtnHotn associations for each phecode in PhecodeKbHtnHotn to identify potentially novel research hypotheses and/or potential additions to the literature sections of the knowledgebase. Candidates were chosen based on the pattern of the paired correlations and KB-status. No changes were made to the knowledgebase during this analysis to avoid introduction of bias.

Visualization of phenotypic patterns

We surfaced phenotypic patterns for Htn and Hotn by selecting their positive correlations; family pruning (filtering parent phecodes that lost significance when children were excluded); and filtering phecodes to ones with a correlate OR in the upper quartile. We split these phecodes into a subset where the phenotypic patterns for Htn and Hotn overlap (both correlate ORs > 1) and subsets where they are distinct (one correlate OR > 1 and the other < 1 or no signal). We visualized the resulting subsets of phecodes with Forest plots as described in section 2.3.

The PheWAS analysis was powered for 1857 phecodes. For Htn, 50.2 % were “phenome-wide significant”, 41.7 % for Hotn. Of phenome-wide significant correlations, 33 % were positive (OR > 1) for Htn, 49 % were positive for Hotn. VIF was within acceptable limits, and never exceeded 1.15. Of the 446 phecodes in PhecodeKbHtnHotn, 324 (72.6 %) had adequate case counts to include in the PheWAS analysis.

13 of the 15 phecodes encoded in the knowledgebase as positive controls for hypertension were powered for the PheWAS. For example, CV_402 (elevated blood pressure without diagnosis of hypertension) and CV_401.7 (hypertensive crisis) have similarly high odds ratios for Htn. While CV_402 has no signal for Hotn, CV_401.7′s odds ratio for Hotn is almost as high as for Htn. This contrast is expected because CV_401.7 is frequently associated with cardiovascular complications that cause hypotension [287] and CV_402 is not

This pattern suggests a negative correlation with Htn may be the first signal that mechanisms that decrease BP are beginning to dominate ones that increase BP; with addition of a positive correlation with Hotn as the imbalance becomes more severe

Correlation of effect size of PheWAS results to KB-status

Estimated effect size (betas) in PheWAS were significantly different for phecodes in PhecodeKbHtnHotn versus those that were absent from the knowledgebase for both Htn and Hotn as shown in Supplemental Figure A.8. For Htn, median betas were negative for KB-status of 1, positive for 3, with 4 in between. Comparison of individual PheWAS correlations to knowledgebase associations

The correlation of a phecode with either Htn or Hotn may be positive, negative or no signal. Pairing correlations for Htn & Hotn creates 9 combinations, 8 of which provide signal. Supplemental Table A.11 compares paired correlations to KB-status for the 274 phecodes that are represented in the knowledgebase and provide signal. Five pairs signal expression in a positive direction for Htn, Hotn, or both. The alignment of these correlations with associations in PhecodeKbHtnHotn is summarized in Table 1. “Yes” indicates the direction of the pair align with KB-status and “No” that it does not. “Compatible” indicates a positive association with either hypertension or hypotension, while not expressed, may be down modulating the other.

Supplemental Table A.12 presents this information for the three pairs that signal suppression of one or both BP-categories without expressing either one. These pairs cannot align with the knowledgebase since it only includes positive associations.

Analysis of each pair of correlations for phecodes represented in PhecodeKbHtnHotn suggests five candidates for hypothesis testing research. Correlations of two phecodes, (B12 deficiency anemia and systemic sclerosis) suggest mechanisms that increase or decrease BP may mask ones that do the reverse. Correlations of three phecodes (thiamine deficiency, polycystic kidney disease and hyperparathyroidism) suggest prevalence of hypertension or hypotension may be under appreciated.

Fig. 5 presents a Forest Plot of all paired correlations for the anemia phecodes. Iron deficiency anemia (blue oblong), both expresses hypotension and suppresses hypertension. This pattern is consistent with mild anemia presenting as ISH and progressing to hypotension with increasing severity. B12 deficiency anemia (orange oblong) stands out as the only anemia phecode where Hotn is suppressed and Htn provides no signal. This pattern is also surprising because B12 deficiency can cause reversible autonomic neuropathy [288]. It may reflect B12′s role as cofactor in metabolism of homocysteine and methyl melonic acid. In vitro animal and basic science studies suggest elevated homocysteine damages vascular smooth muscle and endothelial cells leading to increased blood pressure [289]. Cohort studies of association between homocysteine and hypertension are mixed [290], a case control study found an association [291]. Increase in homocysteine due to deficiency of B12 may be increasing BP sufficiently to suppress hypotension while the hemodynamic effects of the anemia prevent expression of hypertension.

Supplemental Figures A.8–A.11 present Forest plots and analysis for the four other candidates for hypothesis testing research [292–296]. Supplemental Table A.13 presents associations between 13 phecodes and hypertension and/or hypotension that were identified as candidates for addition to the literature sections of the knowledgebase.

Phenotypic patterns

Fig. 6 presents the filtered view of the correlations for Htn and Hotn across the medical phenome. The patterns overlap for 27 phecodes with ORs > 1 for both Htn and Hotn. They are distinct for 37 phecodes with OR > 1 for Htn and < 1 (4) or no signal (33) for Hotn, and 59 phecodes with OR > 1 for Hotn and OR < 1 (35) or no signal (24) for Htn.

Discussion

Objective

Our study of BP seeks to visualize variations in the balance between mechanisms that increase and decrease BP across the medical phenome to surface research hypotheses. This objective requires a departure from epidemiologic studies that are designed to examine relationships among a discrete set of predefined variables to test specific hypotheses. Accordingly, we cast a broad net for data to assess BP in adults, used median SBP and median DBP as summative indicators reflecting different mechanisms in the BP control system, and devised a classifier to assign individuals to the upper, lower, and middle zones of the BP spectrum.

Together, SBP and DBP characterize a two-dimensional blood pressure spectrum. Drawing these dimensions as a square, with the cutoffs for SBP and DBP as the four corners, defines a central middle zone, which we are labeling Naf (not affected).We intend for this middle zone to reflect the variation in SBP and DBP around their physiologic set points as they interact with one another in a complex control system in the absence of disease. The four cutoffs are proxies for thresholds that indicate the balance of BP control mechanisms is beginning to shift in one direction or another toward the upper (Htn) or lower (Hotn) zones. We intend for Htn and Hotn to capture the presence of a shift and its direction, not its magnitude. Our cutoff definitions are a departure from reference ranges, which attempt to identify individuals outside of population or individual averages; 2017 BP categories, which are designed to manage cardiac risk; and continuous traits which are used to model the contribution of one or more genetic variants to BP [297].

Interpretation of results

Investigators derive indicators of BP from real world data in multiple ways (e.g., mean [297] or median [298] of all measurements, mean of 2–3 consecutive measurements [299], ICD codes used alone or in combination with reported BP treatment and/or BP measurements). Our linear regression models of median SBP, DBP, and HR with age and sex as independent variables are similar to the results of cohort studies reporting trends over the life course [300,301]. The distribution of hypertensive subtypes by age decade aligns with previous cohort studies [301,302]. These results replicate ones we previously reported using means [7] and suggest median measurements over the course of EHR follow-up provide meaningful signal.

Our study results indicate that Htn, Hotn, Naf are mutually exclusive BP-categories with distributions centered in the upper, lower, and middle zones of mean arterial pressure. They identify distinct subpopulations within the multimodal distributions of SBP and DBP and account for most differences in the distributions of SBP and DBP by sex. The distributions of Htn and Hotn are centered at about the same place within the distribution of pulse pressure, separate from Naf, suggesting Htn & Hotn are providing a different signal than pulse pressure.

Of the 27,441 individuals in our cohort classified as Hotn, all but 187 have DBP < 60. 4.7 % of individuals matching 2017 BP categories for normal, EBP or ISH have DBP <60. Distribution of hypotensive subtypes by age-decade, stratified by sex, suggests prevalence is greater for females than males across the lifespan.

Individuals with DPB < 60, ages 18–49 without elevated SBP may include individuals with constitutional hypotension, ones over age 50 with elevated SBP may represent cooccurrence of age-related arterial stiffening and conditions associated with decreased systemic vascular resistance such as high output heart failure, autonomic neuropathy or other conditions associated with distributive shock. For example, autonomic neuropathy can present as autonomic hypotension and supine hypertension [303].

PheWAS is a disease agnostic method that allows researchers to observe correlations of a single classifier across the broad spectrum of phecodes. When applied to BP-categories, about half of the correlations from the Htn and Hotn PheWAS reach phenome-wide significance. These correlations represent a mixture of noise, causes, risk factors and complications. By pairing the correlations with known associations in the PhecodeKbHtnHotn knowledgebase, we create a snapshot to assess alignment of correlations for BP-categories with known associations and an enriched sample for further analysis.

Each BP-categories’ correlation with a phecode reflects the balance of mechanisms that increase and decrease BP in cases and controls labeled with that BP-category. The correlations for Htn and Hotn reflect distinct perturbations of this balance. Individually, the correlations may be expressed, suppressed, or provide no signal. Paired, they reveal where similar sets of perturbation occur across the phenome. For example, when Htn is expressed and Hotn is either suppressed or no signal, mechanisms that increase BP dominate. When both Htn and Hotn are expressed, mechanisms that increase and decrease BP dominate in different subgroups labeled the phecode.

All but one of the phecodes encoded in the knowledgebase as positive controls for hypertension expressed Htn, with several also expressing Hotn. The latter may include subgroups with hypotension due to cardiovascular complications of hypertension. Of positive controls for hypotension, half expressed Hotn, and all but 1 suppressed Htn, a pattern that indicates mechanisms that decrease BP are dominating ones that increase BP to varying degrees in this subset of phecodes.

For phecodes in the knowledgebase, when one or both paired correlations have a OR > 1 that reaches phenome-wide significance, 85 % align with associations in the knowledgebase. The degree of alignment is surprising given the differences in the purpose and definition of our BP-categories and the definitions used in the literature represented in the knowledgebase, particularly for hypotension, which we scoped to include orthostatic hypotension and four classifications of shock. Estimated effect sizes (betas) for phecodes with associations in the knowledgebase are significantly different for both Htn and Hotn from those for phecodes absent from the knowledgebase. The relationship between Htn’s median betas by KB-status are what we would expect (negative for KB-status of 1 indicating associations with hypotension, positive for KB-status of 3 indicating associations with hypertension). Surprisingly, Hotn’s median betas for both KB-status 1 and 3 are positive.

The filtered view of the correlations of Htn and Hotn across the medical phenome reveal distinct phenotypic patterns. Phecodes that express Htn, rarely suppress Hotn, while phecodes that express Hotn, frequently suppress Htn. The subset where both are expressed includes phecodes where complications of hypertension may cause hypotension, as in the positive controls, and others associated with hypertension and a condition that decreases systemic vascular resistance, for example diabetes, which is a risk factor for hypertension and a cause of autonomic neuropathy. This subset where both are expressed may explain why median Hotn betas are positive for phecodes with KB-status 3 as well as 1.

Pairing phecode correlations with Htn and Hotn and KB-status for a group of related phecodes surfaced five candidates for hypothesis testing research by showing where correlates for a subset of the group reflect a different balance of the mechanisms that increase and decrease BP than the rest of the group.

Potential applicability, limitations, and next steps

Our PairedPheWAS methods pair correlations for phecodes with Htn and Hotn to provide signal about the interplay of mechanisms that increase and decrease BP and link paired correlations to KB-status to provide an enriched set for further exploration. The information about causes, risk factors, and complications in the knowledgebase chapters on hypertension and hypotension provides a quick explanation or indication of potential novelty. The knowledgebase is freely accessible as a research resource and can be directly applied to EHR-based projects that use phecodes.

The choice of EHR-derived indicator and the design of the classifier depend on the purpose of the study. Our choices reflect our desire to detect subtle differences in the balance of mechanisms that increase and decrease BP across the phenome. A different indicator and classifier would be required to study the temporal characteristics of an individual’s pressure that correlate with complications, such as percent of time above or below a threshold level. The knowledgebase may be used in analysis of results for a series of studies along these lines to reduce effort with the potential of increasing reproducibility.

This retrospective cross-sectional EHR study has many limitations. The cohort comprises adults who seek care at a tertiary academic center in Tennessee, USA and is not representative of the general population. EHR datasets are subject to ascertainment bias and other sources of noise [304]. Median SBP and DBP over the course of EHR follow-up are summative indicators and do not consider medications affecting blood pressure, postural, or temporal variations. For example, individuals with orthostatic hypotension may by hypertensive supine and normal seated [303], and visit-to-visit variability is sufficient to obscure treatment effect [305]. Conditions associated with hypertension and aging overlap. The mappings from knowledge source to ICDs and phecodes and the comparison of PheWAS correlations to knowledgebase associations were performed by a single investigator (WWS) with expertise in nephrology and biomedical informatics.

Next steps include external validation of the classifier and PairedPheWAS results using data from a different population such as the All of Us data set; repeating the PheWAS including pulse pressure as a covariate; exploring different methods for controlling for age in the PheWAS model, testing machine learning approaches to optimizing the classifier; working with clinical research colleagues to evaluate the clinical significance of DBP < 60 and to test the candidate hypotheses. The latter will require reductionist methods that consider factors such as blood pressure trajectory over time, measurement visit type, body mass index, medications, etc.

If those results are promising, our long-term goal is to test whether the PairedPheWAS hypothesis generation methods are applicable to other complex conditions such as hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism. Conclusion

PairedPheWAS methods may open a phenome-wide path to disentangling hypertension and chronic hypotension. Our classifier provides a starting point for assigning individuals to BP-categories representing the upper, lower, and middle zones of the BP spectrum. 4.7 % of individuals in our academic medical center cohort matching the 2017 BP categories for normal, EBP or ISH have DBP < 60, indicating they may have an element of chronic hypotension.

Pairing PheWAS correlations for BP-categories and annotating them with KB-status to indicate known associations in paired knowledgebase chapters for hypertension and hypotension, surfaces distinct phenotypic patterns and candidates for hypotheses testing research.

Research is needed to fine-tune the classifier, provide external validation, evaluate the clinical significance of diastolic pressure < 60, and test the candidate hypotheses.
