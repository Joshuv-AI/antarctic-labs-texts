Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease and Vascular Complications: Associations with Serum Testosterone Levels in a Korean Multicenter Male Cohort
Authors: Hyo Jung Cho, Seong Hee Kang, Hye Yeon Chon, SungA Bae, Eunju Kim, Seong Kyun Na, Su Jin Kim, Dooyeon Kim, Hye Ri Ahn, Huigyeong Kim, Soon Sun Kim, Jae Youn Cheong, Soon Koo Baik, Young Kul Jung, Hyung Joon Yim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsWe investigated the associations of testosterone levels with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and vascular complications in a large Korean male cohort. Despite the high global prevalence of MASLD and significant morbidity from cardiovascular disease, the interplay among testosterone levels, MASLD, and vascular outcomes remains unclear, particularly in Asian populations.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed 1,167 men from the Korean Health Screening-Based MASLD Registry. MASLD was defined as imaging-confirmed steatosis with metabolic dysfunction. Log2-transformed testosterone levels were analyzed using logistic regression adjusted for metabolic risk factors to evaluate associations with MASLD, advanced fibrosis (Fibrosis-4 index [FIB-4] ≥1.3), carotid plaques, and cerebrovascular accidents (CVAs).ResultsAmong the 1,167 men (mean age 57.0 years), 730 (62.6%) had MASLD. These patients had lower testosterone levels than non-MASLD men did (223.5 ng/dL vs 347.5 ng/dL; p<0.001) and showed adverse metabolic features. Higher testosterone levels were independently associated with reduced risk of MASLD (odds ratio [OR], 0.89; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.85 to 0.94; p<0.001). Among the MASLD patients, higher testosterone levels were linked to a significantly lower prevalence of carotid plaques (OR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.73 to 0.81) and CVAs (OR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.67 to 0.82). The associations remained robust across sensitivity analyses with adjustments for obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. No significant association was observed with advanced fibrosis (FIB-4 ≥1.3).ConclusionsLow testosterone levels are independently associated with MASLD and its vascular complications but not with advanced fibrosis. Serum testosterone levels may serve as a practical biomarker for metabolic and vascular risk stratification in MASLD patients, warranting validation in prospective and interventional studies. (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06843421)

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide, affecting roughly one-quarter of the global population.1,2 Its clinical significance lies not only in its potential to progress to advanced liver fibrosis and cirrhosis but also in its strong association with systemic metabolic comorbidities, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease (CVD).3 Indeed, the burden of MASLD-related morbidity and mortality is largely driven by extrahepatic complications, with CVD representing the leading cause of death among affected patients.4

Testosterone, a key sex hormone in men, plays a crucial role in regulating metabolic function, body composition, and vascular health.5 Low serum testosterone, or hypogonadism, has been linked to components of metabolic syndrome, such as insulin resistance, central obesity, and dyslipidemia.6,7 A growing body of evidence suggests an inverse relationship between testosterone levels and the risk of MASLD, although the precise mechanisms are still being elucidated.8,9 Furthermore, testosterone has direct effects on the cardiovascular system, influencing endothelial function, blood pressure, and inflammatory markers, which are all implicated in the development of atherosclerosis and cerebrovascular events.10

While the individual associations between low testosterone and MASLD, and between MASLD and CVD, are well-documented, the synergistic interplay between these three conditions is not fully understood, especially in Asian populations. The metabolic and genetic profiles of Asian individuals may differ from those of Western populations, potentially influencing the clinical presentation and progression of these diseases.

In this context, we conducted a large-scale, multicenter, cross-sectional study within a Korean male health-screening cohort to investigate the independent associations of serum testosterone levels with the presence of MASLD and its vascular complications. We hypothesized that low testosterone would be a significant risk factor for MASLD and that this association would extend to a higher prevalence of carotid plaque and cerebrovascular accidents (CVAs) among men with MASLD, even after accounting for conventional metabolic risk factors. This study aimed to provide novel insights into the systemic role of testosterone in metabolic and vascular health, highlighting its potential utility as a biomarker for risk stratification in male MASLD patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study population

This study was conducted using data from the Korean Health Screening-Based Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease Registry (K-MASLD Registry; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06843421). The registry was designed as a multicenter retrospective cohort to evaluate the association of hepatic steatosis with CVD incidence and other chronic metabolic diseases. Study participants were recruited from university-affiliated health screening centers. Although data collection was initially planned across six hospitals, complete data were ultimately obtained from four centers, yielding a total of 3,466 participants between January 2020 and December 2023.

Recruitment was based on abdominal ultrasonography: (1) individuals with imaging-confirmed hepatic steatosis who met the criteria for metabolic dysfunction were classified as the MASLD group, whereas (2) participants without ultrasonographic evidence of hepatic steatosis were classified as the non-MASLD control group, irrespective of the presence of metabolic risk factors.

To enhance statistical power for group comparisons, MASLD and non-MASLD participants were enrolled in approximately a 1:1 ratio; therefore, the prevalence of MASLD in this registry does not reflect its true prevalence in the general population. For the present analysis, we excluded women (n=1,401), individuals with excessive alcohol consumption (n=70), and those without available serum testosterone data (n=828). The final study population consisted of 1,167 men, of whom 730 had MASLD and 437 did not (Fig. 1).

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ajou University Hospital (AJOUIRB-DB-2024-310) and by the respective IRBs of the participating institutions. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified health screening data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

2. Data collection and definitions

Baseline demographic and clinical variables, including age, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, liver function tests, lipid profile, platelet count, hemoglobin A1c, thyroid function, and sex hormones, were obtained from the health screening database. MASLD was defined according to current international consensus criteria, based on evidence of hepatic steatosis on ultrasonography in the presence of metabolic dysfunction, and after exclusion of other chronic liver diseases and excessive alcohol intake.11 Fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) was calculated as an estimate of advanced fibrosis, with a cutoff of 1.3 used to stratify risk groups.12 Carotid plaque was assessed by carotid ultrasonography performed as part of the screening program. CVA events were defined as the presence of relevant ICD-10 codes recorded in clinical encounters up to January 2025. Testosterone was analyzed as a continuous variable after log2 transformation. In addition to analyses using log2-transformed testosterone as a continuous variable, we performed supplementary categorical analyses using a clinically established cutoff for low testosterone (<300 ng/dL), commonly used to define biochemical hypogonadism.13

3. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Differences between groups were compared using the Student t-test or chi-square test, as appropriate. Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate associations between clinical variables and outcomes (MASLD, FIB-4 ≥1.3, carotid plaque, and CVAs). To reduce multicollinearity, variables representing overlapping clinical domains were screened, and only the most representative or statistically significant variable was included in the multivariable logistic regression models. For instance, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was selected as the representative marker among liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase [AST], ALT, γ-glutamyltransferase [GGT], and alkaline phosphatase), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was included instead of diabetes status to represent glycemic control. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

1. Baseline characteristics

A total of 1,167 male participants with available testosterone measurements were included in the analysis, comprising 730 patients with MASLD (62.6%) and 437 without MASLD (37.4%). The overall mean age was 56.97±9.71 years, and there was no significant difference between groups (56.6 years vs 57.6 years; p=0.083). Patients with MASLD showed significantly higher BMI compared to those without MASLD (26.61±3.20 kg/m2 vs 23.81±2.59 kg/m2; p<0.001). The prevalence of hypertension (43.1% vs 29.1%; p<0.001), diabetes mellitus (20.4% vs 14.7%; p=0.017), and hyperlipidemia (41.4% vs 28.9%; p<0.001) was also higher in the MASLD group. Alcohol consumption also differed significantly between groups (p=0.015); the MASLD group had a higher proportion of mild drinkers (58.6% vs 56.5%), whereas moderate-to-severe drinking was more prevalent in the non-MASLD group (19.0% vs 12.9%). Regarding liver-related parameters, MASLD patients had higher AST (34.28±16.45 U/L vs 31.07±15.60 U/L; p=0.001), ALT (39.37±23.68 U/L vs 27.57±14.45 U/L; p<0.001), and GGT levels (52.58±46.82 U/L vs 39.35±46.14 U/L; p<0.001). Alkaline phosphatase was also slightly higher in the MASLD group (71.15±18.26 U/L vs 68.78±18.54 U/L; p=0.034). No differences were observed in total bilirubin levels between groups. Albumin was marginally higher in MASLD patients (4.82±0.29 g/dL vs 4.74±0.28 g/dL; p<0.001), while platelet counts were also significantly higher (248.14±54.18×109/L vs 238.83±57.09×109/L; p=0.005) (Table 1).

Metabolic parameters demonstrated adverse profiles in MASLD patients, including higher HbA1c (5.99%±0.86% vs 5.73%±0.77%; p<0.001), triglycerides (156.52±91.52 mg/dL vs 104.08±67.11 mg/dL; p<0.001), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol (123.56±42.35 mg/dL vs 116.94±41.09 mg/dL; p=0.009). Conversely, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels were significantly lower in the MASLD group (48.41±11.19 mg/dL vs 57.27±15.65 mg/dL; p<0.001). Total cholesterol levels did not differ significantly between groups. Endocrine parameters revealed that serum testosterone levels were significantly lower in patients with MASLD (223.52±238.87 ng/dL vs 347.46±284.43 ng/dL; p<0.001). Thyroid function parameters showed no differences in thyroid-stimulating hormone between groups, while Free T4 was slightly lower in MASLD patients (1.30±0.19 ng/dL vs 1.33±0.26 ng/dL; p=0.015) (Table 1).

2. Association between testosterone and MASLD

Univariable logistic regression analysis (Table 2) showed that an increase in BMI (odds ratio [OR], 1.45; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.37 to 1.54; p<0.001), hypertension (OR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.43 to 2.38; p<0.001), diabetes (OR, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.09 to 2.07; p=0.014), and hyperlipidemia (OR, 1.74; 95% CI, 1.35 to 2.25; p<0.001) were associated with higher risk of MASLD. In addition, increases in AST, ALT, GGT, HbA1c, triglycerides, and LDL-cholesterol were associated with higher risk, whereas an increase in HDL-cholesterol was associated with lower risk (OR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.94 to 0.96; p<0.001). Notably, an increase in log2-transformed testosterone was associated with lower risk of MASLD (OR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.85 to 0.92; p<0.001).

In the multivariable model, independent predictors of MASLD included older age, higher BMI, ALT, albumin, HbA1c, and triglycerides, whereas HDL-cholesterol remained protective. Importantly, log2-transformed testosterone remained independently associated with lower risk of MASLD (OR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.85 to 0.94; p<0.001). These findings are illustrated in Fig. 2A. In categorical analyses, low testosterone level in the hypogonadal range was independently associated with a higher risk of MASLD (adjusted OR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.47 to 2.68; p<0.001), consistent with the primary continuous analyses (Supplementary Table 1).

Consistent associations were observed in age-stratified analyses (<60 years vs ≥60 years), in which lower testosterone levels were associated with higher odds of MASLD in both age groups (Supplementary Table 2).

3. Factors associated with FIB-4 in MASLD patients

In MASLD patients, factors associated with FIB-4 were assessed (Table 3). Univariable analysis showed that older age and higher AST were associated with higher risk of FIB-4 ≥1.3, while higher ALT and platelet counts were associated with lower risk. Other metabolic parameters were not significantly associated.

In the multivariable model, older age (OR, 1.82; 95% CI, 1.58 to 2.17; p<0.001) and higher AST (OR, 2.65; 95% CI, 2.13 to 3.51; p<0.001) remained independent predictors of FIB-4 ≥1.3. Conversely, higher ALT (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.58 to 0.72; p<0.001) and higher platelet counts (OR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.84 to 0.90; p<0.001) were associated with lower risk. By contrast, log2-transformed testosterone was not significantly associated with FIB-4 in either model. These results are summarized in Fig. 2B. Similarly, in categorical analyses, testosterone level (<300 ng/dL) was not independently associated with FIB-4 ≥1.3, consistent with the findings of the continuous analyses (Supplementary Table 3).

In age-stratified analyses (<60 years vs ≥60 years), log2-transformed testosterone was not significantly associated with FIB-4 ≥1.3 in multivariable models in either age group, indicating no consistent age-dependent association between testosterone levels and fibrosis risk (Supplementary Table 4).

4. Association between testosterone and carotid plaque in MASLD patients

In MASLD patients, the prevalence of carotid plaque differed significantly by testosterone status (Fig. 3A). Carotid plaque was observed in 26.0% of the high testosterone group compared with 60.5% of the low testosterone group (p<0.001). Univariable regression (Table 4) showed that older age, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, higher HbA1c, and LDL-cholesterol were associated with carotid plaque. Importantly, log2-transformed testosterone was strongly associated with a lower risk (OR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.75 to 0.83; p<0.001). In the multivariable model, older age (OR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.04 to 1.09; p<0.001) and diabetes (OR, 2.41; 95% CI, 1.37 to 4.32; p=0.003) remained independent risk factors, while log2-transformed testosterone remained an independent protective factor (OR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.73 to 0.81; p<0.001). These associations are depicted in Fig. 3C. In categorical analyses, low testosterone level (<300 ng/dL) was independently associated with a higher risk of carotid plaque (adjusted OR, 3.70; 95% CI, 2.62 to 5.26; p<0.001), consistent with the primary continuous analyses (Supplementary Table 5).

5. Association between testosterone and CVA in MASLD patients

The incidence of CVA also differed according to testosterone level (Fig. 3B). CVA occurred in 2.7% of the high testosterone group and in 15.3% of the low testosterone group (p<0.001). Univariable analysis (Table 5) showed that older age and some liver enzyme levels were associated with CVA. However, these associations did not remain significant after adjustment. By contrast, log2-transformed testosterone consistently showed an independent inverse association with CVA (OR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.67 to 0.82; p<0.001). These findings are summarized in Fig. 3D. In categorical analyses, low testosterone level (<300 ng/dL) was independently associated with a higher risk of CVA (adjusted OR, 7.53; 95% CI, 3.55 to 18.60; p<0.001), consistent with the primary continuous analyses (Supplementary Table 6).

DISCUSSION

In this large multicenter study of Asian men, we demonstrated that lower serum testosterone levels were independently associated with an increased risk of MASLD, as well as a higher prevalence of carotid plaque and CVA. By contrast, testosterone levels were not significantly associated with FIB-4, a surrogate marker of liver fibrosis. These findings suggest that lower serum testosterone levels are associated with MASLD and vascular complications, while being less clearly associated with fibrosis progression. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to evaluate these associations simultaneously in a well-characterized Asian cohort.

Our results extend prior evidence linking testosterone deficiency to metabolic dysfunction and fatty liver disease. Several population-based studies, including analyses from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cohort, have shown that low testosterone is associated with a higher prevalence of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease independent of obesity and insulin resistance.14-16 Our findings are consistent with these observations and confirm that lower testosterone is inversely associated with MASLD in Asian populations, supporting the notion that lower testosterone levels are linked to hepatic steatosis through shared metabolic pathways involving visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia.

In contrast, the relationship between testosterone and liver fibrosis has remained controversial. Experimental studies in animal models have indicated that androgen deprivation can exacerbate hepatic stellate cell activation and fibrogenesis.17 However, clinical studies have been inconsistent, with some reporting inverse associations between testosterone and fibrosis18 and others showing null results.15 In our study, testosterone was not significantly associated with FIB-4 in either univariable or multivariable models, suggesting that testosterone may have a more prominent effect on metabolic drivers of MASLD rather than fibrosis progression itself. These discrepancies may also reflect differences in study design, fibrosis assessment methods, and underlying population characteristics.

Perhaps the most notable finding of our analysis is the strong and independent inverse association between testosterone levels and vascular complications, including carotid plaque and CVA, in men with MASLD. This aligns with prior epidemiologic studies and meta-analyses showing that low testosterone has been consistently associated with atherosclerosis, stroke, and CVD.10,19,20 Several mechanisms may underlie these associations. Testosterone has been shown to improve insulin sensitivity, increase muscle mass, reduce visceral fat deposition, and exert anti-inflammatory effects.21-23 Moreover, experimental data indicate that testosterone enhances endothelial function and reduces arterial stiffness.24,25 These mechanisms may partly account for the observed associations between testosterone levels and vascular outcomes.

From a clinical perspective, our findings highlight the potential role of testosterone as a biomarker for identifying MASLD patients at higher risk of vascular complications, in addition to established metabolic risk factors. However, whether correction of testosterone deficiency has a causal impact on these outcomes remains uncertain. Large-scale longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials are needed to clarify the temporal relationship and to assess whether interventions addressing testosterone deficiency could improve metabolic or vascular outcomes in MASLD populations.

This study has important strengths, including its large sample size, multicenter design, and the use of log-transformed testosterone to evaluate quantitative and linear associations. Furthermore, we simultaneously examined hepatic and vascular outcomes, providing a comprehensive assessment of testosterone-related risks in MASLD. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, testosterone was measured at a single time point. Although this approach is commonly used in large-scale epidemiologic studies, it does not account for diurnal variation or within-individual variability over time, which may limit clinical interpretability and lead to measurement error. Consequently, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution, and future studies incorporating repeated morning measurements are warranted. In addition, information on specific endocrine disorders or testosterone-related therapies was not systematically available, and residual confounding by unmeasured endocrine factors cannot be excluded. Finally, fibrosis was assessed using FIB-4 rather than histology or elastography, which may have limited sensitivity for detecting early-stage fibrosis.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that low testosterone is independently associated with increased risk of MASLD, carotid plaque, and CVA, but not with liver fibrosis as assessed by FIB-4, in a large cohort of Asian men. These results suggest that testosterone may serve as a useful biomarker of metabolic and vascular risk in MASLD populations. Future prospective and interventional studies are warranted to validate these associations and to explore whether therapeutic strategies targeting testosterone deficiency could improve long-term outcomes.
