Breast cancer metastasis mimicking pacemaker pocket infection: a diagnostic pitfall and long-term preservation of the cardiac implantable electronic device—a case report

Introduction

Cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) implantation represents the standard treatment for patients with symptomatic bradyarrhythmia and advanced atrioventricular conduction disorders. Although implantation techniques and peri-procedural management have substantially improved over recent decades, CIED pocket infection remains the most frequent infectious complication following device implantation or generator replacement and is generally managed by complete device and lead extraction according to current international recommendations.1–5

However, local inflammatory changes involving the device pocket are not specific for infection. Haematoma, foreign body reactions,6 inflammatory disorders, and, more rarely, primary or metastatic neoplasms may closely mimic CIED pocket infection, making the differential diagnosis particularly challenging.7–11

The largest available hybrid study published in 2026 further confirmed the exceptional rarity of CIED pocket neoplasms while emphasizing the importance of early histopathological diagnosis in patients with atypical pocket lesions.11

The present case is unique in demonstrating that a pacemaker pocket metastasis represented the first clinical manifestation of an otherwise clinically occult disseminated breast carcinoma. Furthermore, careful integration of clinical findings, microbiological investigations, multimodality imaging, and histopathology allowed long-term preservation of a normally functioning CIED, avoiding unnecessary system extraction and permitting timely oncological management.

Case presentation

An 81-year-old woman with hypertension and hypothyroidism underwent implantation of a single-lead VDD pacemaker in December 2005 because of symptomatic complete atrioventricular block presenting with recurrent syncope. Elective generator replacements were performed in 2012 and again in January 2020 because of battery depletion, without immediate procedural complications. The new generator was implanted within the existing left prepectoral pocket, and subsequent device interrogations demonstrated stable electrical parameters and normal pacemaker function.

Several months after the second generator replacement, the patient developed progressive pain, erythema, and swelling over the left infraclavicular pacemaker pocket (Figure 1A and B). Physical examination revealed a tense, tender prepectoral swelling with overlying erythema, highly suggestive of CIED pocket infection. She remained afebrile, with no clinical or laboratory evidence of systemic infection.12 Repeated blood and tissue cultures remained consistently negative.

Clinical appearance of the cardiac implantable electronic device pocket. (A) Initial erythema and swelling over the left prepectoral pocket. (B) Progressive enlargement with marked skin tension and erythema mimicking cardiac implantable electronic device pocket infection.

Shortly before symptom onset, the patient reported a tick bite in the same anatomical region. Positive Borrelia burgdorferi IgM serology prompted empirical doxycycline therapy; however, subsequent infectious disease assessment considered active Lyme disease unlikely. Despite antimicrobial therapy, the lesion continued to enlarge.

Because CIED pocket infection remained the leading clinical diagnosis, complete system extraction was initially recommended in accordance with current international recommendations.1–5 The patient declined lead extraction; therefore, surgical pocket revision was undertaken. No purulent material or macroscopic evidence of infection was identified. The generator was replaced with a smaller device and relocated cranially, while multiple tissue samples were obtained for microbiological examination. All cultures remained sterile.

Progressive enlargement despite surgery prompted multimodality imaging. Ultrasonography demonstrated a highly vascularized solid lesion rather than a fluid collection. Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a 50 × 44 × 36 mm lobulated prepectoral mass adjacent to the generator together with bilateral pulmonary nodules, bilateral breast nodules, a small pericardial effusion, and multiple osteolytic lesions involving the sternum, thoracic vertebrae, and L1, highly suggestive of disseminated metastatic disease (Figures 2A–D and 3A–C).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrating a highly vascularized solid pocket mass. (A–D) Axial, sagittal, and coronal reconstructions showing the lesion adjacent to the generator; maximum diameter ∼50 × 44 × 36 mm.

Systemic staging. (A and B) Whole-body bone scintigraphy showing multiple skeletal metastases. (C) Computed tomography demonstrating vertebral osteolytic metastasis.

Complete surgical excision of the pocket mass was performed on 31 March 2021 while preserving the pacing system. Instead of purulent material, exploration revealed a firm, highly vascularized tumour firmly adherent to the surrounding tissues (Figure 4). Histopathology demonstrated well-differentiated metastatic invasive ductal carcinoma of breast origin (grade 1) with oestrogen receptor 95%, progesterone receptor 95%, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 score 0, and Ki-67 7%. Importantly, the pacemaker pocket metastasis represented the first clinical manifestation leading to the diagnosis of an otherwise clinically occult disseminated breast carcinoma. The primary breast lesion and the pulmonary and skeletal metastases had remained clinically silent until staging investigations.

Gross specimen after complete excision of the pocket mass. Histopathology confirmed metastatic well-differentiated invasive ductal carcinoma of breast origin.

Endocrine therapy with letrozole followed by a CDK4/6 inhibitor achieved prolonged disease control. At the first radiological reassessment, metastatic disease remained stable with a marked reduction in tumour markers. At the latest follow-up in 2026, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated partial regression of the metastatic disease with no evidence of local recurrence at the pacemaker pocket. The patient remained clinically asymptomatic, maintained an excellent quality of life, and required only a single morning dose of Depalgos for analgesia, and the CIED continued to function normally without evidence of device-related infection.

Discussion

Cardiac implantable electronic device pocket infection remains the most frequent complication after pacemaker implantation or generator replacement and is generally considered an indication for complete device and lead extraction according to current ESC and EHRA recommendations. However, local inflammatory signs such as erythema, swelling, tenderness, and pain are not pathognomonic of infection. A broad spectrum of non-infectious conditions—including haematoma, foreign body reactions, inflammatory disorders, and exceptionally primary or metastatic malignancies—may closely mimic CIED pocket infection, creating a challenging diagnostic scenario.

The exceptional rarity of neoplastic involvement of the CIED pocket has recently been confirmed by Fortunato et al., who published the largest study currently available on this topic, combining an original case report, a scoping review, and a multicentre clinical survey. Among 56 reported CIED pocket neoplasms, only 16 represented metastatic disease, while breast carcinoma was the most frequent metastatic tumour in women. The authors concluded that persistent or atypical pocket lesions should prompt early histopathological assessment and oncological evaluation rather than being managed solely as presumed infection. These findings strongly support the diagnostic strategy adopted in our patient and reinforce the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis whenever microbiological investigations, imaging findings, and clinical evolution are discordant. Our case extends these observations by documenting one of the longest reported follow-ups after preservation of a functioning CIED in a patient with metastatic pocket involvement, demonstrating sustained device function, absence of local recurrence, and partial radiological regression of metastatic disease following systemic therapy. This observation is consistent with the landmark report by Kojodjojo et al., who described two patients referred for presumed CIED pocket infection in whom histopathology revealed metastatic malignancy, highlighting the importance of considering neoplastic disease whenever microbiological cultures are negative and pocket findings are atypical.13

Our case adds several clinically relevant observations to the currently available literature. First, the pacemaker pocket lesion represented the first clinical manifestation leading to the diagnosis of disseminated metastatic breast carcinoma. Although the patient had a previous history of surgically treated breast cancer, metastatic disease had not previously been recognized, and the rapidly enlarging pocket mass was the event that prompted complete oncological staging. Second, the metastatic lesion involving the pacemaker pocket was markedly larger than all other metastatic deposits identified during systemic evaluation, suggesting a peculiar biological behaviour at the site of previous device intervention. Third, unlike most published cases, our patient achieved an excellent long-term outcome following preservation of the pacing system, with more than 5 years of follow-up demonstrating normal CIED function, absence of local recurrence, partial radiological regression of metastatic disease under endocrine therapy, and an excellent clinical condition.

An intriguing aspect of this case is the temporal relationship between elective generator replacement and the subsequent development of the rapidly enlarging metastatic lesion. Although a causal association cannot be established from a single clinical observation, this sequence raises a biologically plausible hypothesis. Surgical injury initiates a complex wound healing response characterized by activation of inflammatory pathways, release of cytokines and chemokines, extracellular matrix remodelling, angiogenesis, and recruitment of fibroblasts and immune cells. These mechanisms are essential for tissue repair but may also transiently generate a microenvironment favourable to tumour-cell survival, proliferation, and angiogenesis. Experimental and translational oncology studies have demonstrated that inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-6, tumour necrosis factor-α, vascular endothelial growth factor, and activation of the NF-κB pathway, play central roles in tumour progression and metastatic growth.14 Although direct evidence linking CIED procedures to metastatic expansion is currently lacking, the disproportionate size of the pocket metastasis compared with the relatively limited tumour burden elsewhere suggests that local wound healing mechanisms may have facilitated expansion of a previously occult metastatic focus.

Another important lesson provided by this case concerns clinical decision-making. Once repeated microbiological cultures remained negative, inflammatory markers failed to support infection, and imaging demonstrated a highly vascularized solid lesion rather than a fluid collection, continuation of the diagnostic work-up proved essential. Histopathological examination ultimately established the correct diagnosis and avoided unnecessary complete extraction of a normally functioning pacing system. In retrospect, preservation of the CIED represented the most appropriate strategy because the underlying pathology was neoplastic rather than infectious.

Current international guidelines appropriately recommend complete system extraction for definite CIED infection because delayed removal is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. However, our experience underlines that these recommendations should only be applied after infection has been confidently established. In selected patients presenting with atypical clinical evolution, negative microbiological investigations, discordant imaging findings, or an unusually large solid pocket mass, tissue diagnosis should be actively pursued before irreversible therapeutic decisions are undertaken.

Finally, our case emphasizes the value of multidisciplinary collaboration involving electrophysiologists, cardiac surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, infectious disease specialists, and oncologists. Integration of clinical findings, multimodality imaging, microbiological investigations, surgical exploration, and histopathology allowed accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment while avoiding unnecessary device extraction. The favourable long-term outcome observed in our patient further supports the safety of preserving a normally functioning CIED15 once infection has been reliably excluded and appropriate oncological management has been initiated.

Although causality cannot be inferred from a single case, our observations generate a clinically relevant hypothesis that merits further investigation in larger clinical series and experimental studies exploring the interaction between surgical wound healing, inflammation, and metastatic tumour biology.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that not every enlarging CIED pocket lesion represents infection. Histopathological confirmation should be actively pursued whenever microbiology, imaging, and clinical evolution are discordant. Long-term follow-up extending beyond 5 years demonstrated sustained device function, absence of local recurrence, partial radiological regression of metastatic disease, and excellent clinical status, supporting the safety of a CIED-preserving strategy once infection had been confidently excluded.
