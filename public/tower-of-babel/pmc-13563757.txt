Association of the Naples prognostic score with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in U.S. patients with heart failure: a retrospective cohort study using NHANES 2001–2018
Authors: Ke Wang, Jinzhen Gong, Ziting Yuan, Ruiyan Wang, Mengyu He, Yuli Huang, Jing Zhou, Feng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNaples Prognostic Score (NPS) predicts mortality risk in heart failure (HF).Methods and ResultsThis retrospective cohort study utilized data from the 2001–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), including 1,111 adults with HF. The NPS was calculated based on serum albumin (SA), total cholesterol (TC), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR). Mortality outcomes were ascertained through linkage with the National Death Index (NDI). Associations between NPS and all-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) were evaluated using survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan-Meier (KM) analyses. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. Over a median follow-up of 70 months (interquartile range [IQR], 36–114 months), 545 ACM and 202 CVM cases were documented. Compared with participants with an NPS of 0, those in the high-risk group (NPS: 3–4) had significantly increased risks of mortality, with adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) of 2.53 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.38–4.63; P < 0.01) for ACM and 2.62 (95% CI, 1.20–5.74; P < 0.05) for CVM. These associations remained consistent across subgroup and sensitivity analyses, supporting the robustness of the results.ConclusionA higher NPS was independently associated with increased risks of ACM and CVM among U.S. adults with HF. Background

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome characterized by impaired ventricular filling and/or ejection due to structural or functional cardiac abnormalities [1]. As the end stage of various cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), HF affects more than 64.3 million individuals worldwide and remains a major contributor to global mortality [2, 3]. Despite substantial therapeutic advances, HF patients continue to experience high rates of clinical decompensation, recurrent hospitalization, and unfavorable long-term outcomes [4–8].

Malnutrition and chronic inflammation are highly prevalent in HF and frequently coexist, forming a self-perpetuating “nutrition-inflammation” phenotype that accelerates disease progression and worsens prognosis [9, 10]. A composite index derived from routinely available laboratory parameters that captures both nutritional and inflammatory status may therefore improve early risk stratification.

The Naples Prognostic Score (NPS) incorporates four readily available biomarkers, serum albumin (SA), total cholesterol (TC), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), which collectively reflect nutritional status, metabolic reserve, and systemic inflammation [11]. Given the central role of the nutrition-inflammation axis in HF pathophysiology, NPS may serve as a concise surrogate marker of this high-risk biological phenotype. Importantly, NPS offers practical advantages as an objective, low-cost, and easily reproducible composite measure based exclusively on routine laboratory data, in contrast to prognostic models that depend on specialized investigations (e.g., echocardiography or natriuretic peptides) or semi-subjective clinical assessments.

NPS has demonstrated prognostic value across a range of conditions, including colorectal cancer, myocardial infarction (MI), and rheumatoid arthritis [11–16]. Nevertheless, its utility for mortality risk stratification in HF, particularly in large, nationally representative populations, remains insufficiently investigated. Existing HF risk models often incorporate costly, technically demanding, or partially subjective indicators [17–19], highlighting the need for a simple, objective tool based on routine labs.

The prognostic relevance of the nutrition-inflammation axis reflects a fundamental pathophysiological mechanism underlying HF progression, suggesting that an index such as NPS may have broad applicability across diverse populations. Although the present study focuses on a nationally representative U.S. cohort to establish initial evidence, the underlying biological rationale supports further validation in other settings. Prospective studies in diverse populations, including Chinese cohorts, are warranted to confirm its generalizability and clinical utility.

Therefore, using data from the 2001–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this study aimed to evaluate whether NPS is independently associated with all-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) among U.S. adults with HF.

Methods

Study population

NHANES is a nationally representative, biennial survey designed to collect comprehensive data on laboratory measurements, physical examinations, demographic characteristics, and health-related questionnaires, thereby enabling an integrated assessment of the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population. By linking detailed laboratory data, questionnaire information, and long-term mortality follow-up, NHANES provides a robust platform for evaluating the associations of NPS with ACM and CVM among HF patients. Ethical approval for NHANES was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Data from NHANES cycles spanning 2001–2018 were included. Participants were excluded if they were younger than 20; lacked a documented diagnosis of HF; or had missing data on essential laboratory measurements, covariates, mortality follow-up, or sampling weights. In addition, participants were required to have complete data for all four NPS components: SA, TC, NLR, and LMR. Individuals with missing data for any NPS component were excluded. NPS assessment

The NPS was calculated among participants with complete data for all four components, following the method proposed by Galizia et al. [11]. A score of 0 was assigned for SA ≥ 40 g/L (4.0 g/dL), TC > 180 mg/dL (4.65 mmol/L), NLR < 2.96, and LMR > 4.44. Conversely, a score of 1 was assigned for SA < 40 g/L, TC ≤ 180 mg/dL, NLR ≥ 2.96, and LMR ≤ 4.44. Each participant received one point for each abnormal parameter, yielding a total NPS ranging from 0 to 4. Participants were subsequently categorized into three groups: Group 1 (NPS = 0), Group 2 (NPS = 1–2), and Group 3 (NPS = 3–4) [20]. This categorization was adopted from prior studies and was selected to ensure adequate sample sizes across risk strata.

HF assessment

In NHANES, HF status was ascertained using a standardized, self-reported questionnaire. Participants were asked, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had congestive heart failure (CHF)?” (variable MCQ160B). Response options included “Yes,” “No,” “Don’t know,” and “Refused.” Individuals who responded “Yes” were classified as having HF. Although self-reported diagnoses are subject to potential misclassification, this approach is widely accepted and routinely employed for disease ascertainment in large-scale, population-based surveys such as NHANES, in which systematic clinical adjudication of all participants is not logistically feasible. The questionnaire can be found at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx. Because NHANES is de-identified, no chart review could be performed, as individual medical records are not available. NHANES employs intricate, multistage probability sampling to guarantee representation, thereby precluding detailed assessments of narrowly defined patient subgroups.

Survival status and ACM were determined through linkage of NHANES Mobile Examination Center (MEC) data with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2019 Public-Use Linked Mortality File, with follow-up through December 31, 2019. CVM was defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes I00-I09, I11, I13, and I20-I51.

Covariates

A comprehensive set of covariates was included, encompassing demographic characteristics, socioeconomic indicators, lifestyle factors, clinical comorbidities, and laboratory indices. Demographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), and body mass index (BMI). Race/ethnicity was categorized as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or other. Educational attainment was classified as below high school, high school or equivalent, and above high school. Marital status was grouped as married or cohabiting, widowed/divorced/separated, or never married. Lifestyle factors included smoking (never, former, or current) and alcohol consumption. Clinical comorbidities were identified based on self-reported physician diagnoses of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and CVD. Hypertension and diabetes were defined using standardized questionnaire items BPQ020 and DIQ010, respectively. CVD was defined as a composite of CHF, MI, angina pectoris, coronary artery disease (CAD), or stroke (MCQ160C-F). This composite variable was used for descriptive purposes only and was not included in regression models. In multivariable Cox regression (Model 3), individual CVD components (CAD, angina pectoris, MI, and stroke) were adjusted separately. CHF was not included as a covariate because all participants had HF by definition. Laboratory variables included hemoglobin, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), hematocrit, lymphocyte count (LC), monocyte count (MC), segmented neutrophil count, uric acid (UA), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), as well as derived indices (NLR and LMR). Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, prior literature, and data availability in NHANES. Demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol consumption), clinical comorbidities, and laboratory variables (including UA) were included to account for potential confounding. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., medication use, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and natriuretic peptides) cannot be excluded.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, all reported percentages were survey-weighted to account for the complex sampling design of NHANES. All analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach. Participants with missing data on NPS components, mortality outcomes, or any covariates were excluded from the analytical cohort (Fig. 1). The statistical analyses followed NHANES analytical guidelines, incorporating the complex survey design and applying sampling weights to ensure nationally representative estimates. Since both the household interview and MEC data were utilized, MEC-specific weights were applied. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and weighted percentages, with intergroup differences assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for ACM and CVM among HF patients. Model 1 was crude. Model 2 involved adjustments of age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, and household income. Model 3 further adjusted for BMI, creatinine, BUN, WBC, RBC, hemoglobin, HbA1c, HDL-CC, hypertension, diabetes, CAD, angina, MI, stroke, UA, alcohol consumption, and smoking, providing a fully adjusted and conservative estimate. Multicollinearity was assessed using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIFs), with GVIF ≥ 10 defined as the threshold for severe multicollinearity. Notably, the inclusion of NPS components (NLR and LMR) and related hematologic parameters allows evaluation of whether the composite NPS provides prognostic information independent of these individual routine measures. All analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach; participants with missing data on NPS components, mortality outcomes, or covariates were excluded (Fig. 1). Effect modification was assessed by incorporating interaction terms between NPS categories and prespecified subgroup variables into the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards models. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption for the Cox models was tested using Schoenfeld residual-based tests, implemented via the cox.zph function in R. A global test and individual tests for each covariate were performed; a non-significant p-value (≥ 0.05) indicated that the PH assumption was not violated. Graphical assessment of Schoenfeld residuals against transformed time was also conducted to support the statistical tests. All weighted analyses accounted for the complex survey design of NHANES using appropriately scaled examination weights for combined survey cycles, without additional recalibration. Both Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves and log-rank tests were performed using survey weights, consistent with the weighted Cox regression models, to account for the NHANES complex sampling design and generate nationally representative estimates. Potential heterogeneity across clinically relevant strata was examined via subgroup analyses. Sensitivity analyses were conducted following the exclusion of participants who died within the first two years of baseline assessment, thereby mitigating the influence of reverse causality. All analyses were enabled by R 4.5.1. Two-tailed p-value < 0.05 denotes statistical significance.

The median age was 68 (IQR, 58–77), and approximately three-quarters of participants were non-Hispanic White. Hypertension was present in 75% of the cohort. During follow-up, nearly half of the participants (46%, weighted percentage) died from all causes, whereas CVM accounted for 16% of the total cohort (weighted percentage), representing 37.1% of all deaths. The median survival duration was 70 months (IQR, 36–114). 81 participants were allocated to Group 1 (NPS = 0), 657 to Group 2 (NPS = 1–2), and 373 to Group 3 (NPS = 3–4). Intergroup disparities were significant in age, hemoglobin, HbA1c, BUN, RBC, hematocrit, LC, MC, WBC, segmented neutrophil count, HDL-CC, NLR, LMR, and diabetes. During follow-up, ACM rates increased progressively from 24% in Group 1 to 56% in Group 3. Correspondingly, median survival time decreased markedly from 125 months (IQR, 75–167) in Group 1 to 47 months (IQR, 26–85) in Group 3, indicating a clear dose-response relationship between higher NPS and poorer survival outcomes.

Relationship between NPS and ACM/CVM in HF Patients

KM survival analysis

KM survival curves demonstrated clear and progressive separation across NPS categories, with higher NPS associated with substantially poorer survival (Fig. 2A and B). Log-rank tests confirmed statistically significant differences in survival distributions among the three groups for both ACM (P < 0.001) and CVM (P < 0.001). Consistent with these findings, mortality proportions were lowest in Group 1 and highest in Group 3 (ACM: 24% vs. 56%; CVM: 7.4% vs. 22%), underscoring a clinically meaningful gradient in risk across NPS strata.

(A) KM plots for ACM in individuals with HF; (B) KM plots for CVM in individuals with HF

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis

Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated that hemoglobin (GVIF = 37.4) and hematocrit (GVIF = 51.9) exceeded the predefined threshold (GVIF ≥ 10) in the initial model (Supplementary Table S1). After removal of hematocrit, all retained covariates had GVIF values below 10 (Supplementary Table S2), confirming the absence of severe multicollinearity. Survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards regression analyses demonstrated a strong and independent association between higher NPS and increased risks of ACM and CVM. Compared with participants in Group 1 (NPS = 0), those in Group 3 (NPS = 3–4) exhibited significantly elevated risks of both ACM and CVM. After full adjustment for demographic characteristics, socioeconomic factors, comorbidities, lifestyle behaviors, and laboratory variables, higher NPS remained independently associated with mortality, corresponding to approximately a 2.5-fold increased risk of ACM and a 2.6-fold increased risk of CVM (Table 2). These associations were consistent across sequential regression models, confirming the robustness of NPS as an independent prognostic indicator in HF. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. The global test (p = 0.9996) and all individual covariate tests (all P > 0.05) indicated no violation of the proportional hazards assumption (Supplementary Figure S1).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses indicated that the association between elevated NPS and increased mortality risk was generally consistent across most prespecified demographic and clinical subgroups (Fig. 3A and B). Formal interaction testing revealed no significant effect modification by sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, diabetes status, or smoking (all P for interaction > 0.05). A nominally significant interaction was observed for educational attainment (P for interaction < 0.05 for both ACM and CVM), suggesting that the association between higher NPS and mortality may be more pronounced among individuals with lower educational levels. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the exploratory nature of the analyses, the potential for multiple comparisons, and the relatively small sample sizes in certain subgroups (as reflected by wide CIs in Fig. 3). Detailed subgroup-specific HRs and interaction P-values are presented in Fig. 3A and B.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the primary findings and address concerns regarding reverse causality and potential overadjustment, we performed four sensitivity analyses. First, we sequentially excluded participants who died within the first two years and those who died within the first year of follow-up to mitigate reverse causality (Table 3). After full adjustment, higher NPS remained significantly associated with increased risks of both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality across both exclusion windows. Second, we conducted two additional sensitivity analyses using the fully adjusted Cox model: one additionally adjusting for alcohol consumption and UA, and another further excluding hemoglobin, WBC, and RBC to avoid potential overadjustment due to biological overlap with NPS components (Table 4). In both analyses, the dose–response associations between elevated NPS and mortality persisted and remained statistically significant, with effect estimates consistent with the primary analysis. Collectively, these sensitivity analyses confirm that the independent prognostic value of NPS for mortality in patients with heart failure is robust across different exclusion windows for early deaths and alternative adjustment strategies for hematological parameters (Figure 3B).

Discussion

Principal findings and novelty

In this large, nationally representative cohort of U.S. adults with HF, a higher NPS was independently associated with increased risks of both ACM and CVM. After multivariable adjustment, individuals in the highest NPS category (score 3–4) exhibited approximately a 2.5-fold higher hazard of ACM and a 2.6-fold higher hazard of CVM compared with those in the lowest category (score 0). These associations remained consistent across sensitivity analyses and prespecified subgroups, confirming the robustness of the findings. This study extends the existing literature on NPS, which has primarily focused on oncology, MI, and rheumatoid arthritis populations [11–16] by providing generalizable evidence of its prognostic utility in HF using a large, nationally representative dataset. In doing so, this work addresses a key evidence gap and overcomes limitations related to small sample sizes and restricted generalizability in prior investigations.

Pathophysiological plausibility: the nutrition-inflammation axis in HF

The prognostic relevance of NPS in HF is biologically plausible, given the central role of malnutrition and chronic inflammation in HF pathogenesis and progression [21–23]. NPS integrates SA and TC as indicators of nutritional and metabolic reserve and systemic illness burden, together with NLR and LMR as markers of immune dysregulation and systemic inflammation [24–26]. By capturing this combined nutrition-inflammation phenotype, NPS may reflect a higher-risk clinical profile in HF, consistent with prior evidence linking hypoalbuminemia, adverse lipid profiles, and inflammatory cell ratios to worse HF outcomes [27–29].

Comparison with established HF risk scores

Several well-validated risk scores, including the Seattle Heart Failure Model (SHFM) and the Meta-Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure (MAGGIC) score, demonstrate strong prognostic performance by integrating a broad spectrum of clinical, therapeutic, and hemodynamic variables [30, 31]. In contrast, the NPS is derived exclusively from four routinely available laboratory parameters. Its principal advantages include simplicity, objectivity, low cost, and immediate availability from standard blood panels. Although the NPS is not intended to replace comprehensive prognostic models in specialized clinical settings, it may serve as a practical complementary tool for initial risk stratification when detailed phenotyping or advanced clinical data are unavailable. Future studies should quantify the incremental prognostic value of NPS beyond established HF risk scores and directly compare discrimination and calibration in clinically adjudicated HF cohorts.

Interaction with socioeconomic factors

Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested a potential interaction by educational attainment, with stronger associations between elevated NPS and mortality observed among individuals with lower levels of education. However, given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the possibility of type I error due to multiple testing, this finding should be interpreted with caution and requires confirmation in independent cohorts. This finding may reflect broader social determinants of health, as lower educational attainment is frequently associated with limited health literacy, reduced access to continuous medical care, suboptimal management of comorbidities, and poorer nutritional status, factors that may contribute to persistent inflammation and metabolic dysregulation [32]. Nevertheless, this finding should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted cautiously, given the potential for residual confounding and multiple comparisons. Confirmation in independent cohorts is warranted.

Clinical implications and future directions

As a readily calculable composite derived from routine laboratory tests, NPS may help identify HF patients with a high-risk nutrition-inflammation phenotype. A potential clinical application could involve using NPS as an initial screening tool, whereby patients with high scores (e.g., 3–4) are flagged for early and intensified multidisciplinary evaluation. Such evaluation may include formal nutritional assessment, investigation of modifiable inflammatory contributors, and optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy.

Regarding generalizability, although the underlying pathophysiological basis of NPS, namely the nutrition-inflammation axis, is broadly relevant in HF, direct extrapolation of these risk estimates to other populations (e.g., Chinese patients) or healthcare systems requires formal external validation. Score calibration and prognostic performance may vary across geographic regions and ethnic groups.

Importantly, whether NPS-guided management translates into improved clinical outcomes cannot be inferred from this observational analysis and requires prospective evaluation. Beyond its roles in nutrition and inflammation, NPS may also reflect systemic oxidative stress and redox imbalance. Serum albumin, a key component of NPS, possesses intrinsic antioxidant properties [33]. Other endogenous antioxidants have also been linked to cardiovascular outcomes: bilirubin may improve endothelial function [34]. UA exhibits a biphasic effect, whereby hyperuricemia promotes oxidative stress and is associated with increased mortality in HF [35]. Creatinine, traditionally considered a marker of renal function, has been reported to show inverse associations with oxidative stress-related cancer risk [36]. Composite indices like the albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) index further support integrating antioxidant biomarkers into risk assessment [37]. Although the present study did not directly examine these specific biomarkers (e.g., bilirubin, UA beyond covariate adjustment, creatinine, or ALBI), their potential interplay with NPS in heart failure outcomes warrants further investigation. Accordingly, we consider the oxidative stress pathway as a hypothesis-generating biological rationale rather than a definitive conclusion. Future research should: (1) validate NPS in prospective, clinically adjudicated HF cohorts across diverse geographic and ethnic settings (including Chinese populations), incorporating echocardiographic and biomarker data; (2) compare its prognostic performance and incremental value against established risk models such as SHFM and MAGGIC; (3) assess whether the prognostic utility of NPS differs across HF phenotypes (e.g., stratified by LVEF) and whether targeted interventions addressing nutrition-inflammation pathways improve outcomes in patients with elevated NPS; and (4) explore integration of NPS into digital health platforms to enable dynamic risk monitoring.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, HF diagnosis relied solely on a single self-reported questionnaire item without clinical adjudication or objective confirmation (e.g., echocardiography or elevated natriuretic peptides). This approach may introduce misclassification bias, potentially including individuals with milder or misdiagnosed conditions while excluding undiagnosed cases, which could attenuate the observed associations.

Second, the NHANES dataset lacks key variables critical for comprehensive HF phenotyping and prognostication. Data on LVEF, natriuretic peptide levels (e.g., N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide), detailed medication history, particularly guideline-directed therapy, and HF acuity, chronicity, or prior decompensations were unavailable. This limitation restricts the ability to evaluate the prognostic utility of NPS across distinct HF phenotypes (e.g., HFrEF versus HFpEF) and to fully account for disease severity.

Third, all laboratory measurements, including the four NPS components, were obtained only at baseline. Therefore, longitudinal changes in nutritional or inflammatory status, which may be dynamic and prognostically relevant, could not be assessed.

Fourth, although a wide range of covariates was adjusted for, Model 3 included several hematological parameters (hemoglobin, RBC, and WBC) that share biological pathways with components of NPS (e.g., NLR and LMR). Adjustment for such closely related variables may result in overadjustment, potentially attenuating the true association between NPS and mortality. To address this concern, a sensitivity analysis excluding these hematological variables was performed (Table 4, Sensitivity analysis B). The results remained consistent, supporting the robustness of the primary findings. Nevertheless, residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors cannot be fully excluded, and the possibility of overadjustment should be considered when interpreting the adjusted effect estimates.

Fifth, a complete-case analysis was applied, excluding participants with missing data on any covariate or NPS component. Although this approach is widely used and provides a straightforward analytical framework, it may introduce selection bias if missingness is not completely at random and may limit generalizability to the broader HF population. However, the proportion of excluded participants due to missing covariates was relatively small (n = 352, 3.2% of the post-NPS sample), and survey sampling weights designed to account for non-response and the complex NHANES sampling design were applied. These factors help mitigate potential impacts on validity. Nonetheless, future studies using multiple imputation or other missing data approaches are warranted to further confirm the robustness of these findings.

Conclusion

Higher NPS values were independently associated with increased risks of ACM and CVM among U.S. adults with HF. Although NPS shows potential as a simple and cost-effective prognostic marker, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the observational design and inherent limitations (e.g., self-reported HF, single baseline measurement, and residual confounding). Further validation in prospective clinical cohorts is warranted.
