Analysis of risk factors and establishment of a predictive model for brain injury in neonates with surgical necrotizing enterocolitis: a retrospective study
Authors: Xinyin Zhang, Huan Wei, Qi Tan, Zhengli Wang, Hanbin Zhao, Zhenhua Guo, Wei Liu, Jian Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is prevalent among preterm neonates and is associated with high morbidity and mortality. While surgical intervention remains essential for advanced NEC, postoperative neonates exhibit an elevated risk of brain injury. However, which surgery-related factors exacerbate neonatal brain damage remains insufficiently investigated. This study aimed to identify risk factors for brain injury in neonates with surgical NEC and establish a predictive model to facilitate early identification and intervention, which may ultimately decrease neurodevelopmental impairment rates.MethodsThis study analyzed 181 consecutive NEC surgical cases at our tertiary referral center (2017–2023). Brain injury was confirmed by cranial MRI. Primary analysis was used to compare groups via Student's t test for normally distributed data and the Mann‒Whitney U/χ2 test for nonparametric variables. Significant predictors (p < 0.05) were incorporated into multivariate logistic regression, with model performance validated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsMultivariate analysis revealed six independent risk factors (all p < 0.05): lower gestational age (GA) (OR 0.85 [95% CI: 0.73–0.98]; p = 0.028), higher procalcitonin (PCT) levels at surgery (OR 1.03 [95% CI: 1.01–1.06]; p = 0.017), postoperative sepsis (OR 3.46 [95% CI: 1.36–8.76]; p = 0.009), transmural necrosis with perforation (OR 3.03 [95% CI: 1.18–7.78]; p = 0.021), longer diagnosis-to-surgery intervals (> 24 h) (OR 3.52 [95% CI: 1.23–10.08]; p = 0.019), and retention of the necrotic bowel (OR 4.88 [95% CI: 1.50–15.91]; p = 0.009). The ROC analysis revealed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86 for the prediction model.ConclusionThe incidence of brain injury in neonates with surgical NEC is independently associated with elevated PCT levels at surgery, the presence of sepsis, and the occurrence of transmural necrosis with perforation. Conversely, increased GA, early surgical recognition and intervention, and complete resection of the necrotic bowel may serve as potential protective factors against brain injury. Background

Neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), a devastating gastrointestinal emergency predominantly affecting preterm infants, remains a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality [1]. While advances in surgical management and critical care have modestly improved survival rates [2], emerging evidence reveals a sobering reality: over 40% of NEC survivors experience significant neurodevelopmental impairments, including cognitive deficits, motor dysfunction, cerebral palsy, and visual disturbances [3, 4], which not only compromise quality of life but also impose profound lifelong socioeconomic burdens on affected individuals and healthcare systems.

Research has shown that surgical NEC infants exhibit significantly higher rates of neurodevelopmental impairment than medically managed patients do, with surgery itself being an independent risk factor for adverse neurological outcomes [5, 6]. However, whether surgery-related factors, including perioperative management, surgical approach, postoperative therapeutic regimens and surgical complications, may further exacerbate neurodevelopmental impairment risk has not been sufficiently addressed in the current literature.

Early neonatal brain injury is recognized as a pivotal etiological determinant and pathophysiological foundation for neurodevelopmental impairment in neonates. Timely detection and therapeutic intervention for such cerebral lesions may attenuate adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes [7]. Therefore, this study focuses on neonates with surgical NEC as a distinct clinical subgroup, with the following objectives: (1) to systematically investigate risk factors for brain injury as the primary outcome, (2) to develop a predictive model for early detection, and (3) to establish an intervention framework aimed at reducing the incidence of neurodevelopmental impairment.

Materials and methods

This single-center, retrospective cohort study was conducted in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of the Children's Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, a tertiary referral center, between June 2017 and August 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution (Approval No. CHMU2023-244) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Study population

Our study enrolled 181 neonates with surgically treated NEC who had undergone cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) prior to discharge at our center from June 2017 to August 2023. After cases of early mortality, treatment withdrawal, incomplete data and preoperative intraventricular hemorrhage were excluded, 148 patients were ultimately included in the analysis. On the basis of the MRI findings, the patients were categorized into two groups: those with brain injury and those without.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records and classified into the following six categories: (1) demographic characteristics: sex, gestational age (GA), weight-for-GA Z score, delivery mode, APGAR score, and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) status; (2) maternal and prenatal information: maternal age, conception method, pregnancy status, pregnancy complications (including gestational hypertension, anemia, cholestasis, meconium/kidney function tests, albumin, blood glucose, and electrolyte levels); (3) pre-NEC clinical information: preoperative transfusion history, pulmonary hemorrhage, congenital heart defects, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia; (4) NEC clinical features: clinical manifestations, physical signs, and laboratory findings (including white blood cell count, platelet count, procalcitonin, liver/kidney function tests, albumin, blood glucose, and electrolyte levels); and (5) NEC surgical information: diagnosis-to-surgery interval, surgical approach, operative duration, length of resected intestine, presence of transmural necrosis with perforation, retention of necrotic bowel, and unplanned reoperation; and (6) postoperative information: coagulation function, liver/kidney function, postoperative sepsis, duration of invasive mechanical ventilation, postoperative antibiotic use, and time.

Definition

Brain injury was defined as the presence of white matter injury (WMI), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), or cerebellar hemorrhage (CBH) on MRI findings [8]. All MRI examinations were completed prior to discharge. The images were reviewed by an experienced radiologist and a neonatologist who was blinded to the infant's clinical information.

Surgical intervention was defined as either exploratory laparotomy or peritoneal drain placement. Indications included: (1) radiographically confirmed pneumoperitoneum; (2) clinical deterioration following maximal medical management (manifested by persistent abdominal tenderness/erythema/discoloration, refractory metabolic acidosis, or hemodynamic instability) [9–11]. All cases required consensus evaluation by at least two board-certified pediatric surgeons and consulting neonatologists.

Retention of necrotic intestine was defined as preserving intraoperatively identified nonviable intestinal segments meeting these criteria: (1) Discoloration from normal pink to dark red/purplish-black or grayish-white; (2) Absence of both spontaneous and stimulated peristalsis; (3) Disappearance of terminal arterial pulsation in the mesentery. ALL decisions were made by attending surgeons (> 5 years’ experience) after evaluating necrosis extent, location, short bowel risk, and patient stability, with all cases documented.

The diagnosis-to-surgery interval was strictly measured from the initial definitive diagnosis of NEC meeting Bell's stage IIB or III criteria (confirmed by both clinical and radiographic evidence) to the time of surgical intervention commencement. Based on the distribution characteristics and diagnostic performance metrics, we established 24 h as the optimal cutoff value. (see Supplementary Table 4).

Statistics

All the statistical data were analyzed via both SPSS software (Version 26; IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (Version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were normally distributed and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or nonnormally distributed and are reported as the median (interquartile range [IQR]), with comparisons made via Student’s t test or the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages) and were analyzed via either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. All variables (those with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis or considered clinically relevant) underwent collinearity diagnostics prior to inclusion in the multivariate logistic regression analysis for identifying independent risk factors, and a predictive model was constructed accordingly. The predictive value was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Assisted reproductive technology was utilized in 11.49% (17/148) of conceptions, and 77.70% (115/148) were delivered via cesarean section. All 148 neonates underwent standardized cranial MRI evaluation prior to discharge. MRI revealed that the incidence of brain injury in these neonates was 39.86% (59/148). There were no significant differences in corrected gestational age (P = 0.992) or body weight (P = 0.351) between the two groups at the time of MRI. Comparative analysis indicated that neonates with brain injury had a lower GA (p < 0.001) and a higher incidence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (p = 0.003). The brain injury group presented higher PCT levels (P = 0.034) and sepsis rates (P < 0.001). In addition, neonates with brain injury had longer intervals from diagnosis to surgical intervention (> 24 h) (P = 0.001), increased rates of transmural intestinal necrosis and perforation (P < 0.001), higher frequencies of preservation of the necrotic bowel (P < 0.001), a greater chance of unplanned reoperations (p = 0.003), and extended durations of postoperative invasive mechanical ventilation (P < 0.001). A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the independent risk factors for brain injury in neonates with surgical NEC. Variables were selected on the basis of univariate significance (P < 0.05) or clinical relevance. Six independent risk factors were found in the analysis: lower GA (OR 0.85 [95% CI: 0.73–0.98]; p = 0.028), higher PCT level at surgery (OR 1.03 [95% CI: 1.01–1.06]; p = 0.017), postoperative sepsis (OR 3.46 [95% CI: 1.36–8.76]; p = 0.009), transmural necrosis with perforation (OR 3.03 [95% CI: 1.18–7.78]; p = 0.021), longer diagnosis-to-surgery interval (> 24 h) (OR 3.52 [95% CI: 1.23–10.08]; p = 0.019), and retention of the necrotic bowel (OR 4.88 [95% CI: 1.50–15.91]; p = 0.009) (Table 3).

To increase the predictive accuracy of R software for brain injury in neonates with surgical NEC, we developed a nomogram model using the six risk factors (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test revealed no significant difference between the predicted and observed probabilities (χ2 = 5.89, p = 0.66), indicating that the model was well calibrated (Fig. 3). In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86 (95% CL: 0.80–0.92), indicating robust diagnostic performance for NEC-associated brain injury in neonates (Fig. 4). The Youden index identified 0.53 as the optimal cutoff, achieving a sensitivity of 87% and specificity of 75% for predicting brain injury. Decision curve analysis (DCA) further confirmed the model’s clinical utility, demonstrating favorable predictive value and net benefit, suggesting its applicability in guiding clinical decision-making (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Neonatal brain injury is recognized as a significant risk factor and fundamental pathological substrate for neurodevelopmental impairment [7], and is closely associated with the subsequent occurrence of cognitive deficits, motor dysfunction, and visual impairments [12]. Contemporary evidence confirms these relationships: Selvanathan et al. (2023, 2024) identified MRI-detected brain injuries (WMI by volume/location, IVH, CBH) as predictors of motor deficits in preterms [13, 14]. Drommelschmidt et al. (2025) differentiated these associations, demonstrating IVH’s impact on both cognitive-motor functions versus CBH’s specific cognitive effects [15], while Axford et al. (2025) associated IVH with moderate to severe neurodevelopmental abnormalities [16]. These evidences not only confirm the predictive value of MRI (WMI, IVH or CBH) for brain injury, but more highlights the importance of early identification and timely intervention.

Currently, surgical intervention for NEC is known to increase the risk of brain injury, the specific intraoperative factors contributing to this complication remain insufficiently explored [17]. In this study, we focused on a surgical NEC cohort to investigate the impact of perioperative factors—including preoperative assessment, surgical decision-making, operative techniques, and postoperative management—on the development of brain injury. Our findings aim to optimize perioperative care strategies, thereby reducing the incidence of brain injury in this high-risk population. Ultimately, such improvements may enhance long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes, improve quality of life, and alleviate the socioeconomic burden on affected families.

Our study revealed that the incidence of brain injury in neonates following surgical intervention for NEC is relatively high, approximately 39.86%, which is largely consistent with other published studies [18]. Multivariate regression analysis revealed that higher PCT at surgery, postoperative sepsis, transmural necrosis with perforation, longer diagnosis-to-surgery intervals (> 24 h), and retention of the necrotic bowel were independently associated with an increased risk of brain injury, whereas higher GA may serve as an independent protective factor against brain injury.

Surgery is a crucial treatment modality for neonates with NEC complicated by intestinal necrosis. However, the optimal intraoperative management of necrotic bowel segments remains a subject of ongoing debate. Proponents of radical resection argue that complete excision of the necrotic intestine minimizes residual diseased tissue, thereby reducing potential sources of persistent inflammation [19]. Conversely, conservative surgeons highlight the challenges in accurately assessing bowel viability intraoperatively, noting that marginally perfused segments may recover with time. They caution against overresection, which could precipitate short bowel syndrome—a devastating complication in this vulnerable population [20–23]. However, there is limited research on preservation versus resection of the necrotic bowel, with existing studies constrained by confounding factors and small sample sizes. To our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively investigate the potential association between retaining the necrotic bowel and postoperative brain injury in NEC patients. The findings revealed that infants with preserved necrotic bowels face twice the risk of brain injury compared with those who undergo complete resection and that necrotic bowel retention independently correlates with increased neurological damage. This may be attributed to persistent inflammation and cascade reactions triggered by retained necrotic tissue [24]. These data provide new evidence to guide surgeons in deciding whether to resect or preserve the necrotic bowel during NEC operations.

Determining the optimal surgical timing is a crucial yet challenging aspect of decision-making in NEC management, which is largely dependent on surgeons' subjective clinical experience [10]. Some studies have shown that delayed surgical intervention increases the risk of feeding intolerance and mortality in neonates [25–28]. However, whether such delays contribute to brain injury remains incompletely understood [29]. To address this, we added the variable interval from diagnosis to surgery (> 24 h) to our analysis. We found that the proportion of infants with a diagnosis-to-surgery interval exceeding 24 h was significantly greater in the brain injury group than in the nonbrain injury group, and this delay was independently associated with brain injury. These findings suggest that early recognition and timely surgical intervention for NEC may help reduce the incidence of postoperative brain injury.

Intraoperative evaluation of intestinal pathology has been confirmed to be closely associated with poor prognosis in neonates with NEC [30–32]. Our findings indicate that transmural necrosis with perforation is an independent risk factor for brain injury, and the probability of brain injury in NEC neonates with intestinal necrosis complicated by perforation is approximately to 3 times greater than that in the nonfull-thickness necrosis group. This heightened risk may stem from bacterial translocation and systemic toxin release secondary to intestinal perforation. Our data suggest that the greater the pathological extent of intestinal necrosis, rather than milder necrosis, the greater the likelihood of brain injury, which appears to differ somewhat from the important findings reported by Garg PM et al. [19, 32]. Further large-scale prospective studies are needed to clarify this relationship.

Both PCT and sepsis are important indicators for assessing the severity of systemic infection in pediatric patients. Most research findings suggest that severe systemic infection may further exacerbate systemic inflammatory storms, hemodynamic instability, local microcirculatory dysfunction, and immune dysregulation, thereby leading to brain tissue damage [33–35]. Our study revealed a similar pattern—elevated PCT levels and the occurrence of sepsis significantly increased the risk of postoperative brain injury in NEC patients. This highlights the importance of early and prompt removal of inflammatory tissues and a reduction in inflammatory levels.

In addition, Our multivariate analysis revealed a significant protective effect of increasing GA, with each additional week associated with a 15% reduction in brain injury risk (OR = 0.85). These findings aligns with previous studies demonstrating that GA is an independent predictor of neurological outcomes in neonates with NEC, while providing novel quantitative evidence of its protective effect in surgical cases. This may be attributed to both the maturation of cerebrovascular autoregulation and blood‒brain barrier in preterm infants integrity with advancing GA [36–39]. However, the higher median gestational age (34.00 weeks) in our study compared to typical NEC populations may reflect referral patterns and MRI inclusion criteria. Although GA remained a significant risk factor, its generalizability to extremely preterm infants requires further validation due to their different pathophysiology. Future studies should conduct GA-stratified analyses.

Finally, we verified the predictive value and reliability of the model. All the data indicated that the prediction model we constructed had a high net benefit and a strong discrimination ability for clinlical application. Notably, unlike immediately available parameters such as GA and PCT levels at surgery, the diagnosis of postoperative sepsis requires clinical assessment. This characteristic determines the optimal 24–48 h postoperative window for model application, which allows all necessary data to become available while still enabling timely neuroprotective interventions. In addition, we recommend applying the model at this postoperative juncture using the optimized threshold of 0.53, which balances predictive performance with decision-making practicality. This implementation framework will undergo further evaluation in our forthcoming multicenter validation study.

This study has several limitations: (1) potential information biases due to a single-center retrospective analysis; (2) reduced statistical power from a modest sample size; (3) lack of long-termneurodevelopmental outcome data; and (4) the absence of external validation which represents an urgent gap that must be addressed before clinical translation. On the basis of these limitations, particularly the critical need for validation, We will initiate collaborations with multiple neonatal surgery centers to conduct a multicenter prospective validation study for surgical NEC cases (targetN = 200) as soon as possible. This validation work will employ standardized MRI and clinical assessment protocols, combined with a 2-year neurodevelopmental follow-up program. Preliminary results on brain injury are expected within 1 year, while those on neurodevelopment within 2.5 years. The rapid implementation of this validation will generate the robust evidence required for clinical adoption of this prediction model.

Summary

In summary, the incidence of brain injury in neonates with surgical NEC is relatively high and is independently associated with elevated PCT levels at surgery, the presence of sepsis, and the occurrence of transmural necrosis with perforation. Conversely, increased GA, early surgical recognition and intervention, and complete resection of the necrotic bowel may serve as potential protective factors against brain injury. The predictive model constructed on the basis of these findings demonstrated strong discriminative ability and we propose immediate neuroprotective intervention when model-predicted risk exceeds the 53% threshold.

Our study provides evidence-based insights for the prevention and management of brain injury in surgical NEC patients, highlighting the importance of optimizing surgical timing, controlling infections, and ensuring thorough debridement of necrotic tissue.
