Favorable Cardiovascular Health Among Hispanic and Non‐Hispanic Adults in the United States: Results From the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, 2008 to 2024
Authors: Wenyan Ma, Ryung S. Kim, Carmen R. Isasi, Linda C. Gallo, Krista M. Perreira, Gabriela Trifan, Martha L. Daviglus, Amber Pirzada, Daniela Sotres‐Alvarez, Christina Cordero, Frank J. Penedo, Robert C. Kaplan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe long‐term trends of favorable cardiovascular health among US Hispanic and Latino adults are not well understood. Using recent data spanning 2008 to 2024, this study examines contemporary changes in cardiovascular health within a diverse Hispanic and Latino population and compares them with national patterns.MethodsWe applied the American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 metrics of cardiovascular risk factor status to HCHS/SOL (Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos) participants aged 28 to 74 years who were free of cardiovascular disease. Across 3 study visits (2008–2024), we examined the prevalence of achieving favorable metrics (Life's Essential 8 score ≥80) for nicotine exposure, body mass index, blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure. Comparisons were made with Hispanic and Latino and non‐Hispanic White adults in the NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey).ResultsOver time, Hispanic and Latino adults had increasing prevalence of meeting the favorable Life's Essential 8 metric for blood lipids (HCHS/SOL, 29.4%–45.3%; NHANES, 30.6%–38.9%) and nicotine exposure (HCHS/SOL, 56.3%–63.4%; NHANES, 60.8%–67.1%), along with declining prevalence of meeting the favorable blood glucose metric (HCHS/SOL, 44.6%–37.5%; NHANES, 33.6%–30.0%). When comparing the Hispanic and Latino populations versus NHANES non‐Hispanic White adults, the most marked and persistent differences were more favorable nicotine exposure and less favorable body mass index and glucose levels among the Hispanic and Latino groups.ConclusionsHispanic and Latino adults exhibit both advantages and disadvantages in cardiovascular health compared with non‐Hispanic White adults. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions and culturally tailored policies to address cardiovascular health disparities.

Hispanic and Latino adults living in the United States face a high burden of risk factors for developing cardiovascular diseases (CVD) such as hypertension, and as in other US racial and ethnic groups, heart disease is their leading cause of death. 1 , 2 , 3 Across the first 2 decades of the 2000s, the US population experienced a consistent decreasing trend in mortality rate from CVD, although the CVD mortality rates increased among adults in 2020 and remained high in 2022. 4 , 5 At odds with the US‐wide trend in CVD mortality, a study applying the American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 (LE8) criteria to the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) suggested that between 2005 and 2018, the overall cardiovascular health (CVH) of Hispanic adults worsened. 6 Across the United States, the arrest of the favorable pre‐2020 trends meant that an additional 228 000 more CVD deaths occurred between 2020 and 2022 than would have been expected had the trends before 2020 persisted, which is equivalent to almost a decade of lost progress. 5 The COVID‐19 pandemic is known to have affected Hispanic and Latino adults disproportionately. It reduced Hispanic and Latino populations' life expectancy at birth by an estimated 3.05 years. 7 , 8 , 9 Thus, understanding recent changes in CVH among Hispanic and Latino adults is important. Furthermore, existing studies examining CVH in the Hispanic and Latino population did not consider potential variation by birthplace or national background. Prior work from the HCHS/SOL (Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos) has used the LE8 indicators to study the relationship of CVH with incident hypertension and life‐course socioeconomic position, 10 , 11 whereas analyses of CVH changes over time in the population remain limited.

This study from the HCHS/SOL was designed to enhance our knowledge about CVH in people with different Hispanic and Latino backgrounds. Initiated in 2008 as a population‐based cohort drawn from 4 US regions, HCHS/SOL completed its third in‐person visit in 2024. This provides longitudinal data on CVH metrics for over a decade. We used contemporaneous data on Hispanic and Latino and non‐Hispanic White (NHW) adults from the NHANES to test the generalizability of HCHS/SOL data and provide a race‐ and ethnicity‐based comparator. Understanding the secular change of CVH in the diverse Hispanic population provides helpful information to further evaluate and guide related policy to better promote health in this population.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The data from the HCHS/SOL cohort study that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. NHANES data are publicly available and can be accessed at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/default.aspx.

Study Population

The HCHS/SOL cohort study enrolled 16 415 participants aged 18 to 74 years from 4 centers (Bronx, Miami, Chicago, and San Diego). Through 2024, the study completed 3 clinical examination visits (baseline visit 1: 2008–2011; visit 2: 2014–2017, which was completed by 11 623 people; and visit 3: 2020–2024, which was completed by 9090 participants). 12 HCHS/SOL participants were selected using a probability sample design within these 4 areas to provide a representative sample of the target population. 13 HCHS/SOL recruited a diverse group of participants with Hispanic and Latino heritage backgrounds including Cuban, Central American, South American, Dominican, Puerto Rican, or Mexican. 13

To permit comparisons across the 3 HCHS/SOL visits, we only used data collected when participants were within the range of age 28 to 74 years, which was the age range represented at all 3 visits (the youngest participant at visit 3 was aged 28 years, and the oldest participant at the baseline visit was aged 74 years). Including participants aged >74 years would have introduced a nonoverlapping age group at the follow‐up visits, making it impossible to compare across visits while adjusting or stratifying by age in a consistent manner.

We studied individuals free of major cardiovascular diagnoses. This is because the CVH concept quantifies success at staying free of current and future CVD, and the inclusion of individuals with prevalent CVD could lead to the interpretation that an individual with an established CVD diagnosis could have favorable CVH. The exclusion criterion of prevalent CVD in the HCHS/SOL was defined as meeting 1 of the following criteria: (1) had a history of provider‐diagnosed heart attack; (2) had a procedure such as balloon angioplasty, a stent, or bypass surgery to the arteries in the heart; (3) had ECG findings consistent with myocardial infarction; (4) had a history of stroke (not including transient ischemic attack); or (5) had a history of heart failure. Participants were excluded at each visit if they had prevalent CVD defined based on data collected at the visit or from prior visit(s). Exclusion criteria were applied consistently at each visit and included: (1) missing key variables such as measures of CVH, US‐born status (born in the 50 states or Washington, DC), and prevalent CVD; (2) the presence of CVD reported at or before the time of the particular study visit; and (3) active or recent pregnancy. Figure S1 presents the population flow chart. These exclusions resulted in an analytic file with 12 725 HCHS/SOL participants at baseline, 9246 at visit 2, and 7090 at visit 3. The study was reviewed and approved by each participating institution's review board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Multiple survey cycles of the NHANES were combined to create a US‐wide comparison population of adults aged 28 to 74 years. NHANES aims to study the health and nutritional status of individuals in the United States through interviews and physical examinations. 14 The NHANES program began in the early 1960s, and NHANES has been conducted annually since 1999. NHANES uses a multistage probability sampling design to ensure the selected samples are nationally representative of the noninstitutionalized US population. 14 It uses a complex survey design that includes stratification, clustering, and oversampling of specific subgroups to improve the precision of estimates. 14 Three survey cycles of NHANES, specifically 2007 to 2008, 2009 to 2010, and 2011 to 2012, were combined to correspond to the HCHS/SOL visit 1 period (2008–2011). NHANES cycles 2013 to 2014, 2015 to 2016, and 2017 to 2018 were combined to correspond to the HCHS/SOL visit 2. NHANES cycle 2021 to 2023 was used to correspond to the HCHS/SOL visit 3. Adjacent NHANES survey cycles were combined to improve precision, and the current study followed NHANES guidance on combining survey weights across cycles to ensure the results remain nationally representative. 14 , 15 We excluded individuals with prevalent CVD in NHANES according to the following conditions as diagnosed by a doctor or other health professional: congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, heart attack, or stroke. The current study combined those who identified their ethnicity as Mexican American or other Hispanic into a group of all Hispanics. NHW individuals were also included. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to both HCHS/SOL and NHANES.

Life's Essential 8

Selected metrics of the AHA's LE8 were used to assess CVH. 16 HCHS/SOL and NHANES measured 5 metrics across all visits, including 1 health behavior (nicotine exposure) and 4 health factors (body mass index [BMI], blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure). The other 3 LE8 metrics (ie, diet, sleep, and physical activity) were measured at the HCHS/SOL baseline visit but not at each HCHS/SOL follow‐up visit, so these variables were not considered here. The definitions were based on clinical measurements, medical history, and medication use (Table S1). BMI was calculated using weight and height obtained at study visits. Fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c, and lipid fractions were measured from collected fasting blood samples. Non‐high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol was calculated by subtracting high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol from total cholesterol. Three systolic and diastolic blood pressures were measured and averaged at each clinic visit. Nonresponses to survey items (eg, nicotine use) were treated as missing values rather than as a “no” response. Scores for each metric were calculated for each participant based on the American Heart Association's recommendation (Table S2). 16 Each metric has a score ranging from 0 to 100 points, and for each of the 5 LE8 factors, a score of ≥80 points was defined to mean meeting the metric of favorable CVH. 16 In addition, we used 2 approaches to analyze CVH across multiple risk factors. To define individuals with overall favorable CVH status, we considered those who met or exceeded a score of 80 for at least 4 of the 5 measured LE8 metrics to have favorable CVH. This approach of counting favorable risk factors was modeled upon prior studies that used a similar algorithm to characterize CVH profiles associated with lower future CVD risk based on clinical factor control. 17 In addition, we analyzed the continuous CVH mean score, which was calculated by averaging across the 5 CVH metric scores.

Demographic and Health Care Variables

Demographic and health care‐related variables in HCHS/SOL and NHANES were selected to describe participant level characteristics that were anticipated to have an association with the achievement of CVH metrics. Across both cohorts, we included fundamental demographic factors, socioeconomic status variables, and indicators of health care access. These variables were HCHS/SOL field center, sex, age, self‐reported Hispanic and Latino background (Mexican, Cuban, Puerto Rican, Dominican, Central American, South American, and other/>1 heritage in HCHS/SOL; Mexican American or other Hispanic in NHANES), annual household income (collapsed from the original collected categories to <$20 000 and ≥$20 000 groups), education (less than high school, high school or equivalent, greater than high school or equivalent), health insurance status, and health care visit in the past year. An additional key variable of interest was whether participants were born in the United States, defined as US born (including those born in the 50 US States or Washington, DC) and non‐US born (including those not born in the 50 US States or Washington, DC).

Statistical Analysis

The goal of the analysis was to describe CVH in the longitudinal HCHS/SOL cohort as well as NHANES data. We used the 5 individual LE8 metrics, our composite measure of overall favorable CVH, and the continuous CVH mean score to describe the CVH of each cohort at each time point. To account for the repeated‐measures design of HCHS/SOL, we estimated trends in CVH metrics across study visits using weighted linear mixed models, with regression coefficients representing the average change per visit, adjusting for age categories (percentage‐point change for CVH metrics and 1‐point change for the continuous CVH mean score). Subsequently, we analyzed subgroups of the HCHS/SOL population, defined by sex, age, born in the 50 US states or Washington, DC, Hispanic and Latino background, and health insurance coverage. Visit‐specific sampling weights were applied for each HCHS/SOL examination to account for the complex survey design and nonresponse across follow‐up visits, ensuring weighted estimates remain representative of the target population. 13 For HCHS/SOL analyses, data from visit 2 and visit 3 were internally standardized to the HCHS/SOL age distribution at the baseline visit. Furthermore, for HCHS/SOL and NHANES comparisons, data were survey‐weighted and externally standardized to the age and sex distribution from the 2020 US Census to make these estimates comparable across cohorts. 18 Based on the 2020 US Census, we created 3 age categories in 2 sex groups (a total of 6 categories) to calculate each category's proportion among the total population. Calculated percentage distributions were then applied for standardization of HCHS/SOL and NHANES data. Analysis was done in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), R/RStudio version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), SUDAAN version 11.0.4 (RTI International, Research Triangle Park, NC), and Stata statistical software version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Although 49.0% had health insurance initially, the proportion that was insured increased markedly by visit 2, reaching 79.8% at visit 3.

Over time, increasing proportions met the favorable blood lipid CVH metric (30.0% at visit 1, 41.4% at visit 2, and 45.1% at visit 3), and concurrently, we observed improvement in nicotine exposure metrics of CVH (nonsmoking) (Table 2). The percentage meeting the blood glucose CVH metric declined across visits, particularly between the first and second period (47.6%, 41.1%, and 40.2%). The proportion of having favorable BMI and blood pressure CVH metrics did not have a discernable pattern of change between visits. Across the 3 study periods, the proportion achieving only 0 or 1 of the CVH metrics decreased, whereas the CVH mean score improved, especially between the 2008 to 2011 and 2014 to 2017 periods. Overall, trend estimates from mixed models were largely directionally consistent with changes in weighted prevalence estimates. Similar patterns were observed in analyses restricted to participants who attended all 3 HCHS/SOL visits (Table S3).

Defining overall favorable CVH as meeting 4 or 5 metrics with a score ≥80, we found that women and adults aged <50 years had more overall favorable CVH than men and older adults (Table 3). Comparisons between those born within or outside the United States (50 states or Washington, DC) did not show a consistent advantage for 1 group over the other in overall favorable CVH. Examining each group separately, among the US born, the proportion with overall favorable CVH was similar across time; at each of the 3 time points, the proportion with favorable CVH had overlapping 95% CIs. Among the non‐US born, the proportion with favorable CVH increased from 12.7% in 2008 to 2011 (95% CI, 11.6%–13.8%) to 16.6% in 2014 to 2017 (95% CI, 15.1%–18.3%). Subsequently, during the post‐2020 period, the proportion with favorable CVH declined among the non‐US born to 14.1% (95% CI, 12.2%–16.1%).

We observed differences in overall favorable CVH by Hispanic and Latino background (Table 3 and Figure 1[A]). South American individuals consistently had a relatively high percentage of favorable CVH, in the range of 18% to 21%. People of Dominican, Cuban, and Puerto Rican background had the lowest percentage with favorable CVH at visit 1 (ranging from 9% to 14%), but these groups experienced the most improvement across visits in favorable CVH. Health insurance coverage varied markedly across Hispanic and Latino background groups, and the proportion with coverage improved over time in all groups. However, temporal changes related to health insurance did not appear to explain the group‐specific patterns in favorable CVH (Tables S4–S6).

Calculated percentages were then applied to SUDAAN procedures for standardization for HCHS/SOL and NHANES data, respectively. Sample size for NHANES non‐Hispanic White: timeframe 2008 to 2011 N=4475; timeframe 2014 to 2017 N=3503; timeframe 2020 to 2024 N=2265. BMI indicates body mass index; CVH, cardiovascular health; HCHS/SOL, Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos; NH, non‐Hispanic; and NHANES, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

At visit 1, overall favorable CVH was equally common among those with and without health insurance (Table 3). However, over time our measure of favorable CVH diverged between insured and uninsured groups, such that at visit 3, 16.5% of insured and 8.5% of uninsured people had overall favorable CVH.

Comparison With NHANES Participants

Within NHANES, the most pronounced and consistent findings were improvements in nicotine and lipid metrics. In NHANES Hispanic and Latino adults, the prevalence of meeting the blood lipid metric increased from 30.6% in 2008 to 2011 (95% CI, 28.9%–32.3%) to 38.9% in 2020 to 2024 (95% CI, 34.1%–43.9%), and a similar improvement in the blood lipid metric was observed among NHANES NHW adults (34.3% in 2008–2011 to 41.3% in 2020–2024) (Figure 1[B]). Across the study period, the proportion meeting favorable nicotine exposure metrics improved by 6.3% among NHANES Hispanic adults and by 7.2% among NHANES NHW adults.

As compared with HCHS/SOL participants, Hispanic and Latino people in NHANES were more likely US born (>30%), and also had higher incomes (Table S7). The changes in CVH metrics across time were similar across Hispanic and Latino groups in both the NHANES and HCHS/SOL; this was seen in the overall Hispanic and Latino groups and also among individuals of Mexican background (Figure 1[B] and Table S8). In both the HCHS/SOL and NHANES Hispanic and Latino populations over time, smoking and blood lipid metrics improved and blood glucose CVH metrics worsened, whereas blood pressure and BMI metrics changed little.

NHANES NHW adults had higher incomes than both the NHANES Hispanic and Latino and HCHS/SOL groups (Table S7). In contrast to Hispanic and Latino people, who had a relatively low level of education and who were less often insured, over two‐thirds of the NHANES NHW group were college graduates, and >85% had health insurance even during the initial 2008 to 2011 time period.

Both NHANES Hispanic and Latino adults and HCHS/SOL Hispanic and Latino adults were consistently more likely than NHANES NHW adults to meet the smoking CVH metric, which improved over time among all groups (Figure 1[B]). The BMI CVH metrics were consistently more favorable among NHANES NHW adults than among the Hispanic and Latino adults in the HCHS/SOL and NHANES, without consistent direction of change in any group. Blood lipid CVH improved over time in NHANES NHW adults, but improved even more among HCHS/SOL and NHANES Hispanic and Latino adults, such that a disadvantage among Hispanic and Latino adults in the 2008 to 2011 period became less apparent over time. In all 3 groups, including both Hispanic and Latino, and NHW adults, improvements in blood lipid CVH metrics were especially apparent between the 2008 to 2011 and 2014 to 2017 periods. In terms of blood glucose CVH, NHANES NHW adults had an advantage over NHANES and HCHS/SOL Hispanic and Latino adults, especially in the later study years, because the blood glucose metric declined in Hispanic and Latino but not in NHW adults. Finally, among NHANES NHW adults, the blood pressure CVH metric neither changed over time nor differed when compared with the Hispanic and Latino adults in both studies.

DISCUSSION

Using longitudinal data from the HCHS/SOL cohort study, we observed an increase between 2008 and 2024 among US Hispanic and Latino adults in proportions having favorable blood lipids, which occurred concurrently with a decline in nicotine exposure. Among Hispanic and Latino adults, the prevalence of achieving a highly favorable state of CVH was higher, as expected, among women and younger adults. We also found differences in achievement of favorable CVH by Hispanic and Latino national background; some background groups enjoyed improvements over the study period in overall favorable CVH, whereas others did not. This emphasizes the importance of disaggregated analyses, which can reveal variation in cardiovascular risk factor control within the diverse US Hispanic and Latino communities. Drawing comparisons between data from the HCHS/SOL and the nationally representative data from the NHANES, when compared with NHW adults, Hispanic and Latino adults tended to have more favorable levels of nicotine exposure but less favorable BMI. Over the 15‐year study period, improvement in blood lipids was seen among both Hispanic and Latino and NHW adults, whereas worsening of blood glucose status was particular to Hispanic and Latino and not NHW adults. In summary, through confirmatory analysis across 2 large population data sets, this study points to particular threats to favorable CVH in the Hispanic and Latino population, as they have evolved over time.

Among Hispanic and Latino adults as characterized in the 4‐city HCHS/SOL cohort study, the observed differences in CVH between time points were consistent with previous studies of Hispanic and Latino adults including NHANES. The prevalence of favorable nicotine exposure metrics as defined by smoking status and secondhand smoke exposure increased over the 3 HCHS/SOL study visit periods. Concurrently, data from the National Health Interview Survey showed that the prevalence of current smokers among Hispanic and Latino adults decreased from 12.9% to 8.0% between 2011 and 2020. 19 The improvement in meeting the blood lipid metric over time in our study corresponds to the decrease in average total cholesterol in NHANES Hispanic and Latino adults from 198.6 mg/dL in 2007 to 2008 to 188.6 mg/dL in 2017 to 2018. 20 Among NHANES Hispanic and Latino adults, hemoglobin A1c rose from 5.71% to 5.80% between 2007 and 2018, 20 consistent with our observed decrease in meeting the blood glucose metrics over time. Several previous studies using the NHANES also suggested a worsening of blood glucose levels and an improvement in blood lipids among the general US population. 6 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 The prevalence of meeting the blood pressure CVH metric in Hispanic and Latino adults has remained relatively consistent over the years. 21

The observed worsening of blood glucose metrics among US Hispanic and Latino adults has important clinical and population health implications. Given the key role of diabetes and prediabetes in the development of CVD, 26 unfavorable glycemic trends may attenuate improvements seen in other CVH components, such as blood lipids. 27 , 28 Culturally tailored initiatives from the National Diabetes Prevention Program promoting early screening of diabetes‐related biomarkers and lifestyle modification such as improvements in dietary quality, physical activity, and weight management may be particularly relevant for US Hispanic and Latino communities. 29 Integrating such approaches within primary care and community‐based settings may help address the growing burden of diabetes in this population.

The current study leveraged 5 available metrics of the LE8 framework (nicotine exposure, BMI, blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure), which represent core clinical risk factors for CVH, having well‐established associations with CVD. 16 These components represent a profile of clinical risk factor control rather than a comprehensive assessment of all LE8 behaviors. These clinical risk factors also account for a high fraction of CVD‐specific mortality, with high population‐attributable risk for blood pressure (16.2%) and blood glucose (10.2%). 30 Although some health behavior metrics (diet, physical activity, and sleep) were not captured, they often operate along the causal pathway leading to the health factor metrics included here. 31 , 32 , 33 Importantly, data from 6 contemporary cohorts indicate that among middle‐aged US adults, the health factor subscore maintained a robust protective association with incident CVD (hazard ratio [HR] per 10‐point increase in the score, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.76–0.79]), which shows lower hazard for events than the health behavior subscore (HR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.89–0.93]) and remain largely consistent with the protective effect of complete LE8 score (HR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.68–0.71]). 31 Accordingly, the LE8 profiles examined in this study should be interpreted as indicators of relative CVH and CVD risk associated with clinical factor control, rather than as full representations of the complete LE8 framework.

Overall, between the first and second visit cycles, members of the HCHS/SOL cohort became increasingly likely to meet multiple individual CVH metrics and had an improvement in the overall LE8 CVH score. During the latter period of follow‐up, which spanned the COVID‐19 pandemic, these favorable changes leveled off. Moreover, we gleaned some insights about the health of Hispanic and Latino populations in further analyses that examined population subgroups. The healthy immigrant effect would suggest that those born outside of the United States exhibit better health status than US‐born populations, and past studies demonstrate that foreign‐born Hispanic individuals have a lower probability of being current smokers, having poor health, or having obesity than those who were US born. 34 , 35 , 36 The HCHS/SOL cohort study has previously demonstrated the immigrant health advantage when studying the presence of individual adverse risk factors for CVD such as smoking. 37 Here, when examining the most favorable end of the spectrum of overall CVH, the results suggest a complex pattern. Among those born in the United States (defined as the 50 states or Washington, DC), the proportion with overall favorable CVH appeared to be unchanged over time. Among the non‐US born, the proportion achieving favorable CVH was higher during the 2014 to 2017 period than during the initial 2008 to 2011 period. Then, we observed that the proportion with favorable CVH subsided among the non‐US born group during the 2020 to 2024 period. This may reflect that the non‐US born Hispanic and Latino population was particularly susceptible to postpandemic interruption to health care services delivery, economic hardship, and declining engagement in health‐promoting behavior. 38 Most previous studies about nativity and Hispanic health focused on Mexican‐origin individuals and had a limited geographic range, 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 whereas HCHS/SOL included Hispanic and Latino people from multiple different cultural groups and spanned 4 US regions. More studies that account for the heterogeneity within the Hispanic and Latino population will be needed to further elucidate the relationship between birthplace and health, and how this association might be changing with time.

The HCHS/SOL highlights additional communities within the Hispanic and Latino population that experienced distinctive alterations in CVH status across the 15+ years of the study. South American‐background adults had a relatively high proportion of favorable CVH, which was observed during the entirety of the study period. A pattern of CVH that was low in the initial period but that improved over time was observed among people of Dominican background (mainly from the Bronx, NY center), people of Cuban background who were predominantly from Miami, and people of Puerto Rican background who were mostly located in the Bronx and Chicago. Thus, these favorable changes for 3 Latin American background groups were not specific to a particular geographic location. Nor did the Hispanic and Latino groups with the most improvement in CVH over time have the greatest increases in health care insurance coverage, which suggests the importance of considering nonmedical lifestyle or sociocultural factors in efforts to promote optimal CVH.

In terms of health insurance coverage, HCHS/SOL participants with and without health insurance had a similar prevalence of overall favorable CVH at visits 1 and 2. Later, during the postpandemic 2020 to 2024 phase, insured people were more likely to achieve favorable CVH than were uninsured people. Since the expansion of health insurance subsequent to the Affordable Care Act in 2010, along with federal support for maintaining Medicaid enrollment despite COVID‐19 pandemic‐era interruptions, 43 those who remained uninsured at the end of the study period may be more likely to be unemployed people, low‐wage workers, or undocumented immigrants beyond the reach of these programs. 44 As a 4‐city study, the results may also be influenced by the policies of states such as Florida that did not adopt Medicaid expansion. The Commonwealth Fund Biennial Health Insurance Survey in 2018 found that more than one‐third of the US uninsured population was Hispanic and Latino, and 25% of the uninsured comprised foreign‐born Latino people. 44 In the early stage of the COVID‐19 pandemic, the percentage of the uninsured Hispanic population increased, and the decline in employer‐sponsored insurance was larger than the increase in other insurance sources for Hispanic adults. 45 Thus, uninsured Hispanic and Latino adults as well as Latin American‐born individuals living in the United States could face more challenges with access to health care, especially in the aftermath of the COVID‐19 pandemic, thus having more difficulties with maintaining CVH.

The study has some limitations. We only considered 5 of the LE8 metrics when examining CVH, because information on diet, physical activity, and sleep was not collected at all HCHS/SOL study visits. Information on CVD diagnoses and nicotine exposure was self‐reported, which is prone to recall bias. Future studies will be necessary to extend our conclusions to incident CVD events. However, prior studies that have used similar criteria as we did for defining favorable CVH, have found that if individuals were able to reach this ideal state of CVH, then their relative risk of developing CVD would be only a fraction of that among higher‐risk individuals. 17 , 46 Although we intended only to include individuals free of CVD diagnoses, those with undiagnosed CVD might be included in the analysis and thus bias the results. HCHS/SOL engaged a closed cohort in repeated visits, which may have led to enhanced medical surveillance, especially after the baseline phase. Data were combined into multiyear cycles, and the potential year‐to‐year changes in CVH factors prevalence were beyond the scope of the present study. Furthermore, although HCHS/SOL provides detailed Hispanic and Latino background group data enabling subgroup analyses, NHANES categorizes Hispanic and Latino participants more broadly as Mexican American or other Hispanic, precluding cross‐cohort comparisons stratified by detailed Hispanic and Latino background groups. Last, our study period included the COVID‐19 pandemic, but the study could not adjust for COVID‐19‐related morbidity, mortality, or socioeconomic disruption effects across the HCHS/SOL and NHANES because comparable data were not available across both data sets. 38 , 47 Despite differences in study design, we observed relatively consistent results between the HCHS/SOL cohort and NHANES, which drew 3 independent nationwide samples of Hispanic adults. HCHS/SOL trends reflect longitudinal change within a closed cohort, whereas NHANES reflects national population‐level secular trends. Thus, consistency was interpreted as comparable standardized patterns rather than direct equivalence. The consistency in results suggests that HCHS/SOL data are informative for understanding CVH among US Hispanic and Latino adults and may complement national surveillance efforts. Strengths include the availability of data across >15 years of follow‐up to study CVH in a diverse group of Hispanic and Latino people living in the 4 US metropolitan areas. We also compared the HCHS/SOL data with NHANES by presenting weighted and standardized results, which allowed us to understand the differences and similarities in meeting CVH metrics between different populations.

In summary, using the risk factor goals set by the American Heart Association's LE8 metrics to characterize CVH profiles associated with future risk of CVD, we found that Hispanic and Latino adults have both advantages and disadvantages relative to NHW adults. Across the United States, among both Hispanic and Latino, and NHW adults, the proportion of adults with favorable blood lipids has improved over time. Our findings highlight the need for targeted public health interventions to address worsening diabetes and prediabetes risks, especially among the Hispanic and Latino population. Culturally tailored strategies promoting healthy dietary patterns, physical activity, and equitable access to preventive care may help improve blood glucose status, maintain recent success at blood lipid management, and mitigate disparities in CVH for Hispanic and Latino adults living in the United States. Additionally, the persistently low prevalence of favorable BMI among Hispanic and Latino adults underscores the importance of obesity prevention and management efforts specific to this population. Meanwhile, the higher proportion of nonsmokers among Hispanic and Latino adults presents an opportunity to reinforce and expand tobacco prevention strategies that have been effective in this community.
