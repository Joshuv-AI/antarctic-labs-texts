Bilateral giant pulmonary hydatid cysts in a toddler presenting as severe pneumonia: a risk-adapted staged surgical approach
Authors: Davood Salehi, Negin Rostamzadeh, Samin Rostamzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Pulmonary hydatid disease is common in endemic regions, yet bilateral giant cysts in toddlers are exceptionally rare and may mimic severe pneumonia. Their management is challenging, particularly in physiologically vulnerable children.Case presentation:We report a 32-month-old girl presenting with fever, progressive cough, and severe respiratory distress. Imaging revealed four large pulmonary hydatid cysts-three in the left lung and one in the right-complicated by superimposed bacterial infection and hypoxemia. Albendazole therapy (15 mg/kg/day) was initiated, and a risk-adapted, staged surgical plan was selected due to low body weight, poor nutritional status, and limited respiratory reserve. The patient underwent left thoracotomy with cyst excision and capitonnage, followed by right thoracotomy 3 months later, after clinical improvement and weight gain. Recovery was uneventful, and no recurrence was observed during follow-up.Conclusion:This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of bilateral giant pulmonary hydatid cysts in toddlers and supports staged thoracotomy as a physiologically tailored strategy to minimize perioperative respiratory risk in compromised pediatric patients.

Introduction

Hydatidosis, or cystic echinococcosis, caused by Echinococcus granulosus, remains a major public health concern in endemic regions such as Iran[1–3]. While all age groups can be affected, adults are more frequently diagnosed due to the disease’s slow progression[4,5]. Although the liver is the most common site, pulmonary involvement predominates in children, in which cysts may enlarge rapidly because of the lung parenchyma’s elasticity[6]. We report a 32-month-old girl with bilateral giant pulmonary hydatid cysts, illustrating the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of such rare pediatric presentations.

Case presentation

During the winter of 2024, a 32-month-old girl, a resident of a rural area near Urmia, northwestern Iran, was brought to the pediatric emergency ward with complaints of coryza, fever, poor appetite, wheezing, and a worsening wet cough over the last 4 days. Her past medical history was unremarkable, and her medication history consisted of intermittent oral acetaminophen to control fever. There was no history of a similar case, recent travel, livestock contact, or domestic pets.

Upon physical examination, the patient was ill-appearing but awake and had respiratory distress (subcostal retraction). Vital signs were HR = 171/min, RR = 36/min, BT = 38.9°C, BP = 90/60 mmHg, and O2 sat = 67% on room air and 88% with a simple mask. Chest examination revealed normal heart sounds and bilateral crackles with end-expiratory wheezing and diminished basilar lung sounds. The rest of the examination was unremarkable. The patient was transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit after immediate interventions to facilitate breathing, i.e., suctioning of upper airway secretions and nebulized Ventolin, achieving an O2 sat of 91% with supplemental oxygen.

Due to the patient’s fever and progressive respiratory distress, pneumonia was the probable diagnosis. To rule out COVID-19 and influenza, we obtained a nasopharyngeal swab for polymerase chain reaction testing and started the patient on broad-spectrum antibiotics and oseltamivir. The blood analysis showed elevated acute-phase reactants; the erythrocyte sedimentation rate = 70 and C-reactive protein = 26.

The chest X-ray showed multiple round and mass-like opacities in both lungs with consolidation in the left lower lobe (LLL) (Fig. 1). Abdominal ultrasound was normal. Oral albendazole (15 mg/kg/day in two divided doses) was initiated. Considering the number and size of cysts and their complication (infection), thoracic surgery was consulted for surgical resection. A two-stage surgery was recommended as a result of the child being underweight (weight = 10 kg; height = 90 cm). The first surgery was performed 3 days after admission, with left thoracotomy and cyst excision (2 cysts from LLL and 1 cyst from LUL) and capitonnage. Two intercostal drainage tubes were inserted into the left hemithorax. The pathologic examination of the specimen confirmed a hydatid cyst. The patient was discharged after 12 days in stable condition, on oral albendazole, and scheduled for outpatient follow-up. The second surgery was performed after 3 months and adequate weight gain (weight 12 kg), with right thoracotomy and resection of the single cyst from RLL with decortication, capitonnage, and insertion of two intercostal drainage tubes. The patient was discharged after 7 days on oral albendazole for 6 months. No recurrence was observed during the 6-month follow-up.

Discussion

Pulmonary hydatid disease remains a significant surgical problem in endemic regions such as Iran[1,2]. Although pediatric patients account for a smaller proportion of total cases[4,5], lung involvement is more common in children than hepatic disease, likely due to the compressible and elastic nature of pulmonary parenchyma that permits rapid cyst expansion[6–8].

The most common symptom of pulmonary hydatidosis is cough, although other symptoms such as dyspnea, hemoptysis, chest pain, and fever can also be present. Cyst size and location, and whether the cyst is complicated, can affect the symptoms, which means that small, uncomplicated cysts may remain asymptomatic[9–12].

Pulmonary hydatid cysts can be complicated by cyst rupture, secondary infection, suppuration, and pneumothorax. Cyst rupture can lead to hypersensitivity and anaphylactic reactions in severe cases, which can be fatal[13]. Additionally, compression of adjacent lung tissue may precipitate acute respiratory compromise. In toddlers, limited respiratory reserve may amplify the physiologic impact of bilateral disease.

Although the mean age at diagnosis in pediatric pulmonary hydatidosis is reported to be approximately 10–11 years[9,14], occasional cases in significantly younger children have been described[15], underscoring the rarity of presentation in early childhood.

In addition, most pediatric series report a male predominance[12,14] and predominantly unilateral lung involvement[16]. Bilateral pulmonary hydatid cysts are considerably less common and are mainly described in case reports or small series[17,18]. The present case is therefore notable for bilateral distribution, large cyst size, and presentation at 32 months of age.

Although cystic echinococcosis affects the right lung more than the left, and cysts predominantly affect the lower lobes rather than the upper[14,15], in our case one cyst was in the RLL, but three cysts were present in the left lung, one in the upper lobe and two in the lower lobe.

Surgery is the treatment of choice in pulmonary hydatidosis[18]. From a surgical perspective, complete cyst removal with maximal preservation of lung parenchyma remains the cornerstone of treatment[6,7,17]. Cystotomy with capitonnage is widely performed in pediatric patients and has demonstrated favorable outcomes in large series[6,7].

When bilateral disease is present, two principal strategies are described: single-stage surgery (via median sternotomy or bilateral thoracotomy) and staged sequential thoracotomies[7,15,17]. Single-stage procedures offer the advantage of shorter total hospitalization and avoidance of a second anesthesia exposure. However, median sternotomy carries theoretical risks of mediastinal contamination in the event of cyst rupture, as well as increased operative stress in small children[7,15]. Conversely, staged thoracotomies permit physiologic optimization between procedures. Interval nutritional rehabilitation, infection control, and pulmonary recovery may reduce perioperative respiratory complications – particularly in underweight or hypoxemic toddlers. Despite these theoretical considerations, no comparative randomized studies exist to define the optimal strategy, and current practice relies primarily on institutional preference and individual risk assessment[15,17].

As summarized in Table 1, previously reported pediatric bilateral pulmonary hydatid cysts have predominantly involved male patients and were most often managed using single-stage bilateral thoracotomy or minimally invasive bilateral approaches. Staged surgery has been less frequently described. In addition, most reported patients were older than 3 years at presentation. In contrast, our patient represents one of the youngest documented cases and the only female toddler within this comparative series.

The decision to perform staged thoracotomy in our patient was guided primarily by physiological considerations, including low body weight, active superinfection predominantly involving the left lung, and significant hypoxemia with concern for postoperative respiratory compromise. The favorable postoperative course and absence of respiratory decompensation support the rationale for a staged approach in physiologically vulnerable pediatric patients.

Despite the increasing number of case reports, no standardized surgical guideline currently exists for the management of bilateral giant pulmonary hydatid cysts in toddlers. Some surgical reviews suggest that, in bilateral disease with rupture on one side, addressing the intact contralateral cyst first may reduce the risk of intraoperative contamination and help preserve postoperative respiratory reserve[20,21]. However, in the absence of rupture, the operative strategy largely depends on institutional experience and individual patient characteristics rather than comparative evidence.

In this context, a risk-adapted, physiology-driven surgical strategy may provide a practical framework for surgical planning in the absence of standardized guidelines. Based on the available literature and our clinical experience, we propose a practical decision framework for surgical planning in pediatric bilateral pulmonary hydatid disease, as illustrated in Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A159.

The role of preoperative albendazole in large pulmonary cysts is controversial. Some authors discourage its routine use because of the theoretical risk of cyst wall weakening and rupture[22]. Others advocate short-term administration to decrease parasite viability and reduce the risk of recurrence[23,24]. In our case, albendazole was administered under close monitoring without evidence of rupture or hemoptysis. These findings suggest that medical therapy should be tailored rather than universally avoided.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this report include the detailed description of a rare presentation of bilateral giant pulmonary hydatid cysts in a toddler and the integration of available evidence into a practical decision framework for staged surgical management. However, the proposed algorithm is derived from a single case and a limited number of previously reported cases and, therefore, should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than prescriptive. Its applicability requires validation in larger case series and future studies.

Conclusion

Bilateral giant pulmonary hydatid cysts in toddlers may present with acute respiratory compromise and can initially mimic severe pneumonia, particularly in endemic regions. In physiologically vulnerable patients – such as those with hypoxemia, superinfection, or poor nutritional status – a staged surgical approach may reduce perioperative respiratory risk while preserving lung parenchyma. Surgical decision-making in bilateral disease should therefore be guided not only by anatomical distribution but also by the patient’s physiological reserve. Further accumulation of comparative pediatric data is needed to support standardized, risk-adapted management strategies.

Patient perspective

The patient’s parents reported that the rapid progression from what initially appeared to be a simple respiratory infection to severe breathing difficulty was highly distressing. They expressed relief once a definitive diagnosis and treatment plan were established. Following staged surgical treatment, they observed marked improvement in their daughter’s respiratory symptoms, appetite, and activity level. The family appreciated the stepwise approach, which allowed recovery between procedures and increased their confidence in the treatment process.
