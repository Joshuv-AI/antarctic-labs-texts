Minimal surfaces and comparison geometry
Otis Chodosh

Abstract




1.
Introduction.

A basic idea in Riemannian geometry is to minimize the length of a curve to get a stable geodesic. By analyzing the second variation of length, it is possible to relate curvature to global properties. A natural generalization is to minimize the area of a submanifold. We discuss comparison results that have been obtained via this method, as well as some recent progress on regularity issues.

2.
Minimization of area.

Foundational work in geometric measure theory by Almgren, De Giorgi, Federer, Fleming, and Simons in the 1960s gives the following existence and regularity for codimension-one minimizers:

Theorem 1

(cf.
[
117
]
)
.

Let
(M^{n+1},g)
be a closed Riemannian manifold and
\sigma\in H_{n}(M;\mathbb{Z})
be a codimension-one homology class. If
n+1\leq 7
then there’s a smooth representative
\Sigma\in\sigma
of least area.

In general,
\Sigma
could have a singular set of dimension
\leq n-7
.

3.
First and second variation.

Consider now
\Sigma^{n}\subset(M^{n+1},g)
of least area. As with curves, we consider the first and second derivatives of the area of a variation of
\Sigma
through hypersurfaces
t\mapsto\Sigma_{t}
. Assume that
\Sigma
is
two-sided
(i.e. admits a unit-normal
\nu
) and the variation has initial speed
\varphi
. The
first variation of area
gives

(1)

0=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\area_{g}(\Sigma_{t})=\int_{\Sigma}H\varphi

where
H
is the scalar mean curvature of
\Sigma\subset(M,g)
. We recall that
H=\tr A
is the trace of the second fundamental form
A(X,Y)=g(\nabla_{X}\nu,Y)
. Since (
1
) holds for any
\varphi
, we find that

, i.e.
\Sigma
is a
minimal surface
. As with geodesics, we should also consider the second derivative. The
second variation of area
yields

0\leq\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Big|_{t=0}\area_{g}(\Sigma_{t})=\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}-(|A|^{2}+\Ric(\nu,\nu))\varphi^{2}.

Here,
|A|
is the norm of the second fundamental form and
\Ric(\nu,\nu)
is the ambient normal Ricci curvature.

4.
The Geroch conjecture

We call a two-sided
\Sigma^{n}\subset(M^{n+1},g)
a
stable minimal hypersurface
if

(2)

\int_{\Sigma}(|A|^{2}+\Ric(\nu,\nu))\varphi^{2}\leq\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}

for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma)
. For applications, we must know that certain curvature properties of
(M,g)
will restrict the geometry/topology of

. The first such result was obtained in 1979 by Schoen and Yau who showed:

Theorem 2

(
[
113
]
)
.

If
(M^{3},g)
has positive scalar curvature
R>0
then each component of a closed two-sided stable minimal surface
\Sigma\subset(M,g)
has genus zero.

Proof.

The Gauss equations relate the curvature of
g
to the curvature of the induced metric on
\Sigma
. In particular, using
H=0
and the (doubly traced) Gauss equations we obtain

(3)

\Ric(\nu,\nu)+|A|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(R+|A|^{2}-2K)

where
R
is the ambient scalar curvature and
K
is the intrinsic Gaussian curvature. Using this in (
2

(4)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(R+|A|^{2})\varphi^{2}\leq\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+K\varphi^{2}.

Taking
\varphi=1
on
\Sigma
in (
4
), the Gauss–Bonnet theorem and
R+|A|^{2}>0
yields

∎

As a consequence, Schoen and Yau were able to resolve the

Theorem 3

(

The

Assume that

yields
\Sigma\in\sigma

\Sigma
is a sphere. On the other hand,

5.
Inductive descent.

Theorem
3
actually holds in all dimensions as proven by Schoen and Yau (using minimal surfaces) and Gromov and Lawson (using the Dirac operator).

Theorem 4

(
[
108
,
67
]
)
.

The

-torus does not admit a metric of positive scalar curvature.

Sketch of the proof.

Suppose that
(T^{n+1},g)
has positive scalar curvature. Let
\Sigma_{n}
minimize area in the homology class
[T^{n}\times\{0\}]\in H_{n}(T^{n+1};\mathbb{Z})

then
\Sigma_{n}
is smooth by Theorem

\int_{\Sigma_{n}}(R+|A|^{2})\varphi^{2}\leq\int_{\Sigma_{n}}2|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+R_{\Sigma_{n}}\varphi^{2}

where
R

Because

The inductive descent method has been recently refined by Gromov and Hanke

6.
Spectral positivity of curvature.

In the proof of Theorem
4
sketched above, we glazed over the inductive analysis of the stability inequality (
5
). We explain this briefly here. Observe that (
5
) implies that (writing
\Sigma=\Sigma_{n}
) the operator
-2\Delta_{\Sigma}+R_{\Sigma}
is positive. We call this condition
spectral positivity of scalar curvature
. Since
-\Delta_{\Sigma}
is nonnegative, this is a weaker condition than positivity of scalar curvature.

A key principle is that spectral positivity of curvature has—in many cases—the same geometric and topological implications as pointwise positivity of curvature. For example, consider a closed surface
(\Sigma^{2},h)
with strictly positive Gaussian curvature in the spectral sense,
-\alpha\Delta_{\Sigma}+K_{\Sigma}\geq 1
. This is equivalent to

(6)

\int_{\Sigma}\varphi^{2}\leq\int_{\Sigma}\alpha|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+K_{\Sigma}\varphi^{2}

for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
. Then, this principle says that
(\Sigma,h)
will behave as if it has Gaussian curvature
\geq 1
. For example, if
\Sigma
is connected we can take
\varphi=1
in (
6
) and use Gauss–Bonnet to find
\area(\Sigma)\leq 2\pi\chi(\Sigma)
. Thus
\Sigma
is a sphere and has area
\leq 4\pi
(the same thing as implied by
K_{\Sigma}\geq 1
). In the sequel, we will often pretend (as in the proof of Theorem
4
) that spectral positivity of curvature is the same thing as pointwise positivity of curvature. For details on how to make such arguments correct and rigorous one may refer to e.g.
[
73
,
32
]
.

While we didn’t specify the coefficient
\alpha
, this can be extremely important. For example, Schoen and Yau proved
[
112
]
that if a complete
(\Sigma^{2},h)
satisfies (
6
) with
\alpha\in(0,4)
, then
\Sigma
is compact and
\diam(\Sigma)\leq\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{4-\alpha}}
(cf.
[
80
]
). No such estimate is possible for
\alpha\geq 4
since the hyperbolic plane has
K_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}=-1
and
-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\geq-\frac{1}{4}
.

7.
Localization via prescribed mean curvature.

The minimal surface (and Dirac) techniques have the downside that area-minimization (or the construction of a harmonic spinor) is a global operation. Gromov introduced a “
\mu
-bubble” localization of the minimal surface technique and used it to prove:

Theorem 6

(
[
71
,
69
,
73
,
141
]
)
.

For
n\leq 6
, if
(T^{n}\times[-1,1],g)
has scalar curvature
R\geq n(n+1)
, then

(7)

d(T^{n}\times\{-1\},T^{n}\times\{1\})<\frac{2\pi}{n+1}.

Note that no assumptions are made at the boundary and the theorem relates scalar curvature and distance.

Sketch of the proof for
n=2
.

Fix a function
h
on the “band”
W:=T^{2}\times[-1,1]
and write
\partial_{\pm}W=T^{2}\times\{\pm 1\}
. Then, for
\Omega\subset W
containing a neighborhood of
\partial_{-}W
we consider the functional

\mu(\Omega):=\area(\partial\Omega)-\int_{\Omega}h

If a critical point of
\mu(\cdot)
exists with
\partial\Omega=\partial_{-}W\cup\Sigma
,
\Sigma
contained in the interior of
W
, the first variation of
\mu(\cdot)
implies that the mean curvature of
\Sigma
satisfies the prescribed mean curvature equation
H=h
.

The second variation of
\mu(\cdot)
becomes

\int_{\Sigma}\left(3+\frac{3}{4}h^{2}+\nabla_{\nu}h\right)\varphi^{2}\leq\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+K\varphi^{2}

for
K
the Gaussian curvature of the induced metric on
\Sigma
. Thus, as long as the inequality

(8)

3+\frac{3}{4}h^{2}+\nabla_{\nu}h>0

holds, we may take
\varphi=1
as in the proof of Theorem
2
to find that each component of
\Sigma
is a sphere. This is a contradiction, since the projection
W\to T^{2}
restricts to a degree
1
map
\Sigma\to T^{2}
.

Take
h=h(\rho)
where
\rho=d(\cdot,\partial_{-}W)
(or an appropriate smoothing). Then, since
|\nabla\rho|=1
a sufficient condition for (
8
) to hold is

3+\frac{3}{4}h^{2}-|h^{\prime}|=\delta>0.

Set
\delta=0
for simplicity (one should actually take
\delta>0
sufficiently small) and observe the following function is a solution to the ODE:

h(\rho)=-2\tan\left(\tfrac{3\rho}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right).

Note that
h(\rho)\to\infty
as
\rho\to 0
and
-\infty
as
\rho\to\frac{2\pi}{3}
.

For contradiction we now assume that
d(\partial_{-}W,\partial_{+}W)>\frac{2\pi}{3}
. Now, the “effective mean curvature”
H-h
of the boundary of the region
W^{\prime}:=\rho^{-1}[0,\frac{2\pi}{3}]\subset W
has the correct sign for us to construct a minimizer. This gives a contradiction using the second variation argument above.
∎

The

We now discuss some recent applications of the minimal surface and

9.
The geometry of positive scalar curvature.

Observing that for any closed

Other curvature conditions

Scalar curvature is not the only curvature condition that may be studied via minimal surfaces. For example Y. Shen and R. Ye introduced the bi-Ricci curvature, defined as

\biRic(u,v)=\Ric(u,u)+\Ric(v,v)-\sec(u,v)

for all orthonormal vectors
u,v
. One may check that in three-dimensions the biRicci curvature equals the scalar curvature (up to a multiplicative constant) but in higher dimensions positivity of biRicci implies positivity of scalar curvature (but not vice versa). The following is a key observation of Shen and Ye:

Theorem 28

(
[
115
,
116
]
)
.

Suppose that
\Sigma^{n}\subset(M^{n+1},g)
is a two-sided stable minimal hypersurface in a manifold with
\biRic\geq 1
. Write
\lambda_{\Ric,\Sigma}
for the smallest eigenvalue of the Ricci curvature of the induced metric on
\Sigma
. Then the Ricci curvature of the induced metric
\Sigma
is strictly positive in the spectral sense.

Proof.

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n},\nu
denote an orthonormal basis for
T_{p}M
with
\lambda_{\Ric,\Sigma}=\Ric_{\Sigma}(e_{1},e_{1})
. Using the Gauss equations we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{\Ric,\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\sec_{\Sigma}(e_{1},e_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{n}(\sec_{M}(e_{1},e_{j})+A(e_{1},e_{1})A(e_{j},e_{j})-A(e_{1},e_{j})^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\biRic(e_{1},\nu)-\Ric_{M}(\nu,\nu)-|A|^{2}

where we used
\tr A=0
in the last step. Using
\biRic(e_{1},\nu)\geq 1
, stability of
\Sigma
as in (
2
) may be rewritten as

\int_{\Sigma}\varphi^{2}\leq\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\lambda_{\Ric,\Sigma}\varphi^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

13.
The (stable) Bernstein problem.

We now discuss the Bernstein problem and its modern variants. As we’ll explain, major progress in this area came from the discovery of links with the previous sections.

We will say that
M^{n}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
is an
entire minimal graph
if
M
is a minimal hypersurface and a graph of
u:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}
. One may easily check that this is equivalent to requiring that
u
satisfies the minimal surface equation

(10)

\textrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)=0.

This may be regarded as a nonlinear analogue of the Laplace equation
\Delta u=0
. In 1914, Bernstein proved
[
8
]
that an entire solution to (
10
) on
\mathbf{R}^{2}
is affine. This can be regarded as a non-linear version of Liouville’s theorem and is particularly remarkable since no boundedness assumption on
u
is required. It is natural to ask about higher dimensions. A remarkable series of works in geometric measure theory resolved this in the 1960s:

Theorem 31
.

For
n\leq 7
, a minimal graph
u:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}
is affine. On the other hand, for
n\geq 8
, there are non-flat minimal graphs on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

For later reference, we briefly outline the steps of the proof:

(1)

If
M\subset\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
is a minimal graph then a calibration argument proves that it’s area-minimizing (and thus stable).

(2)

Using “monotonicity of area ratios” for minimal surfaces and compactness for least area hypersurfaces, Flemming
[
63
]
proved that if
u:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}
is a non-affine solution to the minimal surface equation then there’s a non-flat area-minimizing hypercone
C\subset\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
.

(3)

De Giorgi proved
[
56
]
that the hypercone actually splits
C=C^{\prime}\times\mathbf{R}
and thus there’s a non-flat area-minimizing hypercone
C\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

(4)

Generalizing work
[
2
]
of Almgren, Simons used the stability inequality to prove
[
121
]
that there are no non-flat area-minimizing hypercones in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
for
n\leq 7
.

(5)

Bombieri, De Giorgi, and Giusti
[
12
]
constructed a non-affine graph over
\mathbf{R}^{8}
.

We note that the non-existence non-flat of area-minimizing hypercones (as in the fourth point above) is precisely the reason for the regularity of area minimizers in low dimensions (Theorem
1
).

A natural generalization of the Bernstein problem is to drop the graphical condition. If we retain the area-minimizing assumption then the problem was completely resolved by the previously mentioned work:

Theorem 32
.

If
\Sigma^{n}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
is a complete area-minimizing hypersurface and
n+1\leq 7
then
\Sigma
is flat. For
n+1\geq 8
there are non-flat examples.

The shift in dimensions is because De Giorgi’s splitting result does not hold without graphicality.

By replacing “area-minimizing” with “stable” we obtain the
stable Bernstein problem
: If
\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a complete two-sided stable minimal immersion, is it flat? One important reason to study the stable Bernstein problem is that it is equivalent to
a priori
curvature estimates for stable minimal surfaces in Riemannian manifolds.

In
\mathbf{R}^{3}
this was resolved in 1980 by Fischer-Colbrie and Schoen
[
62
]
, do Carmo and Peng
[
58
]
, and Pogorelov
[
99
]
. These proofs leverage the two-dimensionality of
M
in various strong ways (uniformization, negative curvature of a minimal surface in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
).

In higher dimensions, well-known work of Schoen, Simon, and Yau, Schoen and Simon, and a recent breakthrough by Bellettini resolves the stable Bernstein problem assuming volume growth bounds. (See also the recent works
[
26
,
64
]
for stable Bernstein results for other geometric problems.)

Theorem 33

(
[
107
,
111
,
7
]
)
.

Suppose that
\varphi:\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
is a complete two-sided stable minimal immersion that satisfies extrinsic volume growth estimates
\area(\varphi(\Sigma)\cap B_{R})\leq O(R^{n})
for all
R>0
. If
n+1\leq 7
then
\Sigma
is flat. With only intrinsic volume growth estimates
\area(B_{R}^{\Sigma})\leq O(R^{n})
, the same conclusion holds for
n+1\leq 6
.

Thus, the resolution of the stable Bernstein problem becomes a question of a more geometric nature: Can the stability inequality can be used to derive volume growth bounds for
\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
? This geometric problem has recently seen considerable activity and is currently almost completely resolved (only the case
M^{6}\to\mathbf{R}^{7}
remains unsolved). With C. Li, we solved the
M^{3}\to\mathbf{R}^{4}
case
[
43
]
. Later we discovered a second proof
[
40
]
and shortly after a third proof was discovered by Catino, Mastrolia, and Roncoroni
[
21
]
. Our first proof was based on techniques related to nonnegative scalar curvature (cf.
[
95
,
123
]
) while our second proof was based on techniques related to strictly positive scalar curvature. Finally, the Catino–Mastrolia–Roncoroni proof was based on Bakry–Émery Ricci curvature. We describe here the proof from
[
40
]
based on strict positivity of scalar curvature.

Theorem 34
.

If
\Sigma^{3}\to\mathbf{R}^{4}
is a complete two-sided stable minimal immersion then it’s flat.

Sketch of the proof.

The stability inequality (
2
) in Euclidean space reads

\int_{\Sigma}|A|^{2}\varphi^{2}\leq\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}.

Using the Gauss equations we have
|A|^{2}=-R_{\Sigma}
. Thus, the stability inequality is the same thing as spectral nonnegativity of
-\Delta_{\Sigma}+R_{\Sigma}
. To promote this to spectral positivity we use an old observation due to Gulliver and Lawson
[
75
]
. Letting
g
denote the induced metric on
\Sigma
and
r:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
the restriction of the ambient distance
r(x)=|x|
. Then, we consider the conformally equivalent metric
\tilde{g}=r^{-2}g
. (Motivation for considering
\tilde{g}
may be seen from the example of
\mathbf{R}^{n}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
so that
\tilde{g}
becomes the product metric on
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{S}^{n-1}
; note that for
n\geq 3
this metric has strictly positive scalar curvature.)

Conformally changing the nonnegative operator
-\Delta_{\Sigma}+R_{\Sigma}
into
\tilde{g}
-quantities gives that
(\Sigma,\tilde{g})
satisfies

-2\tilde{\Delta}+\tilde{R}\geq c(n)>0

for
n\geq 3
. As such, we find that
(\Sigma,\tilde{g})
has
uniformly positive
scalar curvature in a spectral sense. As such, we may apply a variant of the
\mu
-bubble exhaustion technique to prove that for any
\rho>0
, there’s a compact region
\Omega
so that
B_{\rho}^{\Sigma}(p)\subset\Omega
,
\partial\Omega\subset B_{C\rho}^{\Sigma}(p)
, and
\partial\Omega
admits strictly positive scalar curvature in a spectral sense.

A well-known result
[
18
]
(discussed further in the sequel) of H.-D. Cao, Y. Shen, and S. Zhu gives that
\Sigma
has only one end. As such, it’s possible to modify
\Omega
so that
\partial\Omega
is connected. Since
n=3
,
\partial\Omega
is two-dimensional. Thus, we have (spectral positivity) of Gaussian curvature which implies that
\partial\Omega
(with the metric induced by
\tilde{g}
) is a sphere of bounded diameter and bounded volume. After undoing the conformal change, we thus find that
\area(\partial\Omega)=O(\rho^{2})
(with the metric
g
induced on
\Sigma
by the immersion). Using that minimal surfaces satisfy the Euclidean isoperimetric inequality by work of Michael and Simon
[
94
]
(see also the recent breakthrough by Brendle
[
15
]
) this yields the intrinsic volume growth estimate
\vol(\Omega)=O(\rho^{3})
. By Theorem
33
, this completes the proof.
∎

Subsequently, with Li, Minter, and Stryker we discovered
[
45
]
that one may generalize this argument to
\Sigma^{4}\to\mathbf{R}^{5}
by using bi-Ricci curvature in place of scalar curvature. In particular, a key observation is that although
|A|^{2}=-R_{\Sigma}
by the doubly traced Gauss equations,
|A|^{2}
actually controls the full curvature tensor of
(\Sigma,g)
. For example, the Gauss equations give

\displaystyle\Ric_{\Sigma}(e_{1},e_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sec_{\Sigma}(e_{1},e_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(e_{1},e_{1})A(e_{i},e_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(e_{1},e_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{n-1}{n}|A|^{2}

where we used the AM-GM inequality and
\tr A=0
in the last step (this is closely related to the well-known “improved Kato inequality”). Thus, stability of
\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
implies that
(\Sigma,g)
has nonnegative spectral
Ricci
curvature in some sense. In hindsight, one may view the Cao–Shen–Zhu one-ended result used in the proof of Theorem
34
as the combination of this observation with a result that (roughly stated) proves that a complete Riemannian manifold with spectrally nonnegative Ricci curvature that admits a Euclidean Sobolev inequality must have only one end of infinite area.

One may repeat this kind of calculation for bi-Ricci curvature and then combine it with the Gulliver–Lawson conformal change to deduce that if
\Sigma^{4}\to\mathbf{R}^{5}
is a complete two-sided stable minimal immersion then the conformally changed metric
(\Sigma,\tilde{g})
has strictly positive bi-Ricci curvature in the spectral sense. In particular, the
\mu
-bubble technique in the proof of Theorem
34
yields
\partial\Omega
with spectrally positive Ricci curvature. The final step was then to prove that
\partial\Omega
has uniformly bounded volume. This was achieved by proving a spectral Bishop–Gromov style volume comparison theorem using the isoperimetric surface approach introduced by Bray in his thesis
[
13
]
. Combining these elements, the proof of the stable Bernstein theorem for
\Sigma^{4}\to\mathbf{R}^{5}
then follows in a similar way as in
\mathbf{R}^{4}
.

The spectral Bishop–Gromov volume comparison result (which we could only prove for
3
-manifolds) was subsequently generalized to all dimensions by Antonelli and Xu who proved:

Theorem 35

(
[
4
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Suppose that
(M^{n},g)
has spectrally positive Ricci curvature
-\frac{n-1}{n-2}\Delta+\lambda_{\Ric}\geq(n-1)
. Then
\vol(M)\leq\vol(\mathbf{S}^{n})
.

Shortly after this result appeared, Mazet was able to generalize the techniques described above by using Theorem
35
, with a weighted notion of bi-Ricci curvature (originally introduced by Shen and Ye), along with a delicate choice of coefficients to resolve the stable Bernstein problem for
\Sigma^{5}\to\mathbf{R}^{6}
. In sum, this yields

Theorem 36

(
[

,
58
,
99
,
43
,
40
,

,
45
,
92
]
)
.

For
n+1\leq 6
, if
\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
is a complete two-sided stable minimal immersion then it’s flat.

This leaves only the case of
\Sigma^{6}\to\mathbf{R}^{7}
unsolved. We briefly mention several other open problems:

(1)

Removable singularities: If
\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbf{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
is two-sided stable minimal and complete away from

(2)

(3)

Classify low Morse index

Finally, we briefly mention that the rigidity of (non-compact) stable minimal hypersurfaces has also been extensively studied in other ambient manifolds. For example, Fischer-Colbrie and Schoen proved

14.
Generic regularity for the Plateau problem.

We’ve shown that area-minimizing hypersurfaces are effective tools for comparison geometry. The possibility of singularities in high dimensions is a major issue with the technique. We close this survey by discussing generic regularity, which provides one possible approach to this problem based on the following (the name is taken from the recent work
[
55
]
but the conjecture has been around since at least the 1980s in various forms):

Conjecture 38

(Generic regularity hypothesis)
.

For a closed smooth manifold
M^{n+1}
, there’s a Baire generic set of metrics
\mathcal{G}
so that if
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
\sigma\in H_{n}(M;\mathbb{Z})
is a codimension-one homology class then there’s a smooth representative
\Sigma\in\sigma
of least area.

One may reasonably extend this conjecture include
\mu
-bubbles since they behave very similarly (the integral constraint scales away near a singularity).

We now explain how the generic regularity hypothesis can be used in minimal surface comparison results by describing the proof of the following result generalizing Theorem
4
.

Theorem 39
.

Assume the generic regularity hypothesis is known for all closed manifolds
X^{k}
with
k\leq n+1
. If
M^{n+1}
is a closed manifold then
T^{n+1}\#M^{n+1}
does not admit a Riemannian metric of positive scalar curvature.

Theorem
39
is known to imply the positive mass theorem.

Sketch of the proof.

We mimic the proof of Theorem
4
. Assume that
(T\#M,g)
has positive scalar curvature. Since positivity of scalar curvature is an open condition and the set of metrics
\mathcal{G}
in the generic regularity hypothesis is dense (by assumption), we may assume without loss of generality that
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Thus, the area minimizer
\Sigma_{n}
in the homology class
T^{n}\times\{0\}\subset T^{n+1}\#M
will be smooth. As in Theorem
4
,
\Sigma_{n}
has strictly positive scalar curvature in the spectral sense. Schoen and Yau showed that this implies
[
108
]
that the induced metric on
\Sigma_{n}
is conformal to a metric of strictly positive scalar curvature
g_{n}
.

Since we assumed the generic regularity hypothesis is applicable to
\Sigma_{n}
, we can replace
g_{n}
by a nearby metric and thus assume that
g_{n}\in\mathcal{G}
(again, this does not change the condition of positivity of scalar curvature). Now, using the topological argument from Theorem
4
, we may repeat this process (minimize, conformally change the induced metric, perturb to an element of
\mathcal{G}
) until we reach a contradiction in
n=2
.
∎

Progress on Conjecture
38
was made by Hardt and Simon
[
76
]
and Smale
[
122
]
who proved it for
8
-dimensional ambient manifolds (the first time singularities can appear). Their approach has two essential features:

(1)

Perturb the metric
g
slightly to start. Then, for a (singular) area-minimizer
\Sigma^{7}\subset(M^{8},g)
, we may perturb
g
slightly to
\tilde{g}
so that the new minimizer
\tilde{\Sigma}
moves to “one side” relative to
\Sigma
, but remains close to
\Sigma
.

(2)

The singularities of
\Sigma
are modeled on area-minimizing hypercones
\mathcal{C}
. Hardt and Simon proved that if
\mathcal{C}
is singular only at the origin (this always holds in
8
-dimensions) then
\tilde{\Sigma}
will be modeled on a
completely smooth
area-minimizing hypersurface
S
, lying on one side of
\mathcal{C}
. By
\varepsilon
-regularity for area-minimizers, this shows that
\tilde{\Sigma}
is completely smooth.

The first step works in all dimensions but (seemingly) not the second step. If
\mathcal{C}
has a non-trivial singular set, then the classification of area-minimizing hypersurfaces lying to one side is not solved (existence was proven by Z. Wang
[
133
]
). Even worse, a proof of such a uniqueness result would not substitute for step (2) above, as it might be possible that
\tilde{\Sigma}
is modeled on an
iterated tangent cone
of
\mathcal{C}
(i.e. a singularity of
\tilde{\Sigma}
is very close to a singularity of
\mathcal{C}
that does not lie at the origin; this is not prohibited by
\varepsilon
-regularity).

With Mantoulidis and Schulze, we have been able to resolve this issue in
9
and
10
ambient dimensions (see also
[
49
,
86
]
) and recently in addition with Z. Wang we have extended this to
11
-dimensions as well.

Theorem 40

(
[
48
, Theorem 1.2]
,
[
51
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

The generic regularity hypothesis holds in manifolds of dimension
\leq 11
.

The proof of Theorem
40
is too complicated to fully describe here (one may refer to the lecture notes
[
33
]
). Instead we show how a key ingredient (cf.
[
48
, Proposition 3.3]
) is intimately related to De Giorgi’s splitting theorem (see step 3 in the outline of the proof of Theorem
31
).

Proposition 41
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}^{n}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
is an area-minimizing hypercone and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is a non-zero vector so that
\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathcal{C}+\mathbf{x})=\emptyset
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is a flat hyperplane.

Idea of the proof.

Since
\mathcal{C}
is dilation invariant (this is the definition of cone) we find that
\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathcal{C}+\lambda\mathbf{x})=\emptyset
for all
\lambda>0
. This proves that the regular part of
\mathcal{C}
is graphical in the
\mathbf{x}
-direction and thus we can find a solution to the minimal surface equation (
10
). Removable singularity results for the minimal surface equation (and estimates for the singular set of an area-minimizer) imply that this solution extends to all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbf{x}^{\perp}
. A non-flat
\mathcal{C}
cannot be graphical near the tip of the cone at
\mathbf{0}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 42
.

We cannot help but indicate how this implies De Giorgi’s splitting theorem. Suppose that
M
is a minimal graph in
\mathbf{R}^{n+1}
and
\mathcal{C}
is a tangent cone at infinity obtained by blowing down
M
. The graphical condition implies that
M+\lambda\mathbf{e}_{n+1}
is disjoint from
M
for all
\lambda>0
, we find that
\mathcal{C}
is (weakly) disjoint from
\mathcal{C}+\mathbf{e}_{n+1}
. The strong maximum principle then implies that either
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}+\mathbf{e}_{n+1}
are strictly disjoint (impossible by Proposition
41
) or else they coincide. This implies that
\mathcal{C}
is translation invariant and thus splits a
\mathbf{R}
factor.

Proposition
41
may be combined with a geometric argument to show that if
\Sigma
is a minimizer locally modeled on
\mathcal{C}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}
is a new minimizer lying “to one side” then
\tilde{\Sigma}
either improves near
\mathcal{C}
(is less singular in a certain sense) or else it is closely modeled on some singularity of
\mathcal{C}
. This does not directly prove that
\tilde{\Sigma}
is less singular than
\Sigma
, but what it does imply is that if we form a “foliation”
\Sigma_{s}
that moves in a monotone way then the singular set
\mathcal{S}
of the entire foliation has the (Hausdorff) dimension bound
\leq n-7
(this is what one would obtain for a single leaf!). When combined with an estimate on the rate that (disjoint) minimal hypersurfaces “pull apart” at a singularity (going back to Simons
[
121
]
classification of non-flat stable cones) this proves that some leaf of the foliation must be non-singular, proving Theorem
40
(in
9
and
10
dimensions).

To extend this proof to
11
dimensions, one must analyze one specific case, namely that of quadratic cylindrical cones, where the argument is borderline. Using Jacobi field analysis and nonconcentration estimates (pioneered by Simon
[
118
,
119
,
120
]
, cf.
[
128
,
127
,
59
]
) we prove that either the dimension estimate improves or else the separation estimate improves near such a cone. Results of this form had previously been proven by Figalli, Ros-Oton and Serra for the obstacle problem
[
61
,
60
]
.

Extending Theorem
40
to higher dimensions currently presents a major challenge. It seems possible that one must choose the “foliation”
\{\Sigma_{s}\}_{s}
more carefully, although it is yet unclear how such a choice can be transmitted to the small scale analysis of singularities. Some other problems related to Theorem
40
are as follows:

(1)

Classify or otherwise improve understanding of minimizing hypercones
\mathcal{C}^{7}\subset\mathbf{R}^{8}
. Must they be “strictly minimizing” and/or “strictly stable” in the sense of
[
76
]
?

(2)

Simons proved the non-existence of non-flat stable cones in low enough dimensions by proving that quadratic cones have the smallest first eigenvalue of the stability operator among minimal hypersurfaces in the sphere
[
121
]
. Is it possible to estimate the gap to the non-quadratic cones (assuming they exist)?
