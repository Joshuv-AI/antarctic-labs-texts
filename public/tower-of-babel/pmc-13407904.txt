Automated orbital wall fracture detection and severity classification of ocular injuries using deep learning
Authors: Farhad Salari, Zahra Meskar, Farhad Fallah, Maryam Fazeli, Reza Samiee, Hossein Arabalibeik, Seyed Mohsen Rafizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Orbital fractures are frequently encountered in maxillofacial trauma and can be associated with severe globe injuries (SGI). Accurate and timely diagnosis is critical to prevent long-term complications, yet subtle fractures and soft tissue injuries may be overlooked. Recent advances in artificial intelligence offer promising tools to support clinical decision-making in acute settings. We collected CT images from 250 eyes with orbital wall fractures treated at a single center between May 2020 and May 2023. From CT scans of included subjects, a dataset of 18,000 annotated orbital CT scan slices was used, with segmentation preprocessing based on a U-Net framework. Two supervised convolutional neural network (CNN) classifiers were trained on axial and coronal CT images of the orbit. The first model (FxDetectCNN) was trained to detect orbital fractures, while the second model (SGIDetectCNN) was trained to classify injury severity as non-severe or severe. The models’ performance was compared with annotations by two experienced ophthalmologists. The fracture detection model achieved an overall accuracy of 81%, with 86% sensitivity and 76% specificity. The SGI estimation model achieved 64% accuracy, with a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 48%, compared with expert annotation, which reached a maximum accuracy of 59.5% for SGI estimation. Our study demonstrates the feasibility of CNN models in detecting fractures and classifying the severity of ocular injuries from CT scans. These tools can assist emergency physicians in triaging patients and may serve as valuable decision-support systems in emergency settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54114-x.

Introduction

Traffic collisions and sports-related injuries have led to a growing incidence of orbital fractures. Orbital fractures are one of the most frequently encountered facial fractures in maxillofacial surgery, accounting for up to 90% of such cases and approximately 3% of emergency room consultations1,2. Accurate diagnosis of orbital fractures relies on orbital computed tomography imaging (CT)3,4. While larger fractures are typically easy to identify using CT, more subtle fractures may be missed by non-experts. Moreover, orbital fractures may result in severe globe injuries (SGI), which can be sight-threatening and require urgent ophthalmic intervention5.

Many studies have investigated clinical scoring systems that combine clinical and demographic data with radiologic findings to enhance the detection and assessment of ocular injuries in patients with orbital wall fractures5–14; however, none of these approaches have gained widespread use due to the challenges of applying them in real-world clinical settings. Recent advancements in deep learning methods have resulted in the development of models that can improve the detection of orbital blowout fractures15,16. For instance, Li et al. utilized convolutional neural networks (CNNs) combined with an XGBoost model to detect orbital blowout fractures, reporting a single-image accuracy of 92% and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.957417. Umapathy et al. focused on volumetric analysis by using a modified U-Net model to quantify globe volumes, achieving a high F1 score of 0.9518. Also, Bao et al. employed DenseNet-169 along with U-Net for the identification and segmentation of orbital fractures19. One of the limitations of these previous works is the lack of models trained and validated on complex, multi-wall orbital fractures.

Despite advances demonstrating the value of artificial intelligence (AI) in orbital trauma diagnostics, AI-based prediction of severe globe injuries (SGI) in orbital fractures remains unexplored. Timely access to ophthalmologists in emergency settings remains limited, hindering the diagnosis and management of ocular injuries associated with orbital fractures. Severe ocular injuries are at risk of being overlooked by non-ophthalmologists, as specialized examination instruments, such as slit lamps and indirect ophthalmoscopes, are not always readily available in emergency departments and require specific training to use. Failure to promptly recognize ocular injuries may lead to serious long-term complications. To bridge this care gap, we introduce the development of two separate AI models: one for fracture detection and the second for predicting SGI in subjects with orbital wall fractures. This model aims to improve triage by non-ophthalmologists while serving as a decision-support system for ophthalmologists, enabling more precise and timely diagnosis20.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.FARABIH.REC.1401.026). The requirement for informed consent was waived by Tehran University of Medical Sciences’ ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data (IR.TUMS.FARABIH.REC.1401.026).

Dataset and annotations

Imaging from subjects diagnosed with orbital fractures at Farabi Eye Hospital between May 2020 and May 2023 were included in this study. Patients were eligible for inclusion if the interval from trauma to their first ophthalmologic examination was less than one week and if complete ophthalmologic evaluations, along with high-resolution fine-cut orbital CT imaging (2 mm slice thickness and 1 mm slice increment) without contrast, were available. Subjects were excluded if visual acuity data were missing, whether due to patient non-cooperation (e.g., young age or inability to complete the Snellen chart) or incomplete documentation. Additionally, patients with a history of ocular surgery (e.g., corneoscleral repair, canthotomy, or cantholysis) were excluded. Summary statistics for the final analyzed cohort, including counts of unilateral and bilateral cases as well as positive and negative labels for each predictive task, are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Eyes with orbital fractures were labeled based on medical records documented by an experienced oculoplastic surgeon (SMR). The severity of ocular injury was determined from clinical records using a modification of Rossin’s classification6(Supplementary Table 2)21. In this classification, ocular injuries associated with orbital fractures are categorized into four grades according to the urgency of ophthalmologic intervention. Injuries classified as grades 2 and 3 were considered severe globe injuries (SGI).

To establish a baseline for evaluating model performance against expert clinical judgment, two expert ophthalmologists (SMR and FS), blinded to the clinical information, independently annotated the validation dataset using CT imaging alone. Both experts reviewed 50 randomly selected samples and had access to all available imaging planes, including sagittal, coronal, and axial views.

Preprocessing

The dataset used in this study consists of high-resolution orbital CT images with corresponding annotations indicating whether a fracture is present and, for fractured cases, the severity (mild or severe). 18,000 orbital CT slices were obtained from 250 eyes with orbital fractures. All images were preprocessed to ensure uniform input sizes and consistent normalization for effective model training.

The axial dataset was extracted from raw DICOM files, with pixel intensities converted to Hounsfield Units (HU) using the DICOM rescale slope and intercept. To standardize the input, 40 slices were selected per case using a fully automatic, rule-based procedure. The orbital region was first localized along the axial (superior–inferior) axis using anatomical landmarks derived from the orbit, including its maximum extent and central position within the scan. Based on these references, a contiguous slice window was adaptively shifted or expanded along the stack according to predefined rules until exactly 40 slices encompassing the orbital region were retained. This approach ensured consistent anatomical coverage while providing a uniform input depth across all cases.

To focus on the region of interest (ROI), specifically the eye globe and its surrounding orbital bones, we used the proposed U-Net network [1] to segment the mask. For each case, we began by defining an all-zero tensor. Next, we passed each axial slice through the model, added the output mask to the zero tensor, and repeated these steps for all slices. Finally, we applied a nonzero filter to identify the largest connected area, which was assumed to contain the eye globe. The processing area was then refined by applying dilation operations to the extracted mask.

To enhance relevant anatomical detail and ensure consistent feature representation, the volumes were clipped to a Hounsfield Unit (HU) window of − 50 to 50. Following windowing, intensities were scaled to the [0,1] range using linear min–max normalization (subtracting the minimum and dividing the range). This entire preprocessing pipeline, including HU rescaling, windowing, and min–max scaling, was applied consistently across the training, validation, and testing sets to prevent data leakage and ensure numerical stability. Finally, both eye globes were extracted and concatenated into a single image (Fig. 1).

At the next stage, each patient’s CT volume was processed to create two eye-specific samples (right and left eyes). Rather than splitting the image by fixed pixel ranges, we isolated the orbital region by centering a crop on the midline of each axial or coronal slice. Specifically, the image was cropped around the middle and then symmetrically padded by 128 pixels on both the left and right sides to preserve the central field of view and ensure a consistent input size focused on the mid-orbital anatomy. Horizontal flipping was applied deterministically to standardize anatomical orientation between left- and right-eye samples. Each sample was aligned such that the lateral (outer) orbital wall appeared on the left side of the image and the medial (inner) wall on the right. This canonical left–right mapping ensured that both eyes shared the same spatial layout (corner-to-corner and inside-to-inside alignment) and removed confounding laterality differences. The same left–right rule was applied consistently across axial and coronal stacks to preserve slice-to-slice correspondence and ensure coherent orientation within each volume.

For patients with unilateral orbital fractures, each eye was treated as an independent sample, with the affected eye labeled as fracture (1) and the unaffected eye labeled as normal (0). Both samples were included in the dataset, using the contralateral eye as within-patient control. For patients with bilateral orbital fractures, both eyes were included as separate positive samples, each labeled as fracture (1). Labels for right and left eyes were extracted from the ground truth annotations and concatenated to form the final eye-level label set. This strategy increased the number of training instances while preserving the true fracture status of each eye and treating each orbit as an independent anatomical unit for classification.

The final post-preprocessing sample dimensions and input tensor configurations are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. For the severity (SGI) estimation and fracture detection of axial branches, images were resized to 120 × 150 and provided to the network as tensors of shape (batch, 40, 150, 120), treating the 40 axial slices as input channels. For the coronal fracture detection branch, images were resized to 256 × 256 × 32 and provided as (batch, 32, 256, 256), where the 32 coronal slices served as input channels.

Here, we aim to conduct two primary tasks: binary classification to detect the presence of a fracture (fracture vs. non-fracture) and binary classification to determine severity (severe vs. non-severe).

Model architectures

We developed two convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures using 18,000 cropped CT images, focusing on the orbital region from the included cases. The first, FxDetectCNN, integrates axial and coronal CT views via a dual-branch architecture to detect orbital fractures. The second, SGIDetectCNN, is a single-branch CNN that processes axial slices to classify fracture severity as severe or non-severe. This design allows the detection model to benefit from multi-view structural information, while the severity model emphasizes detailed axial features relevant to soft-tissue and optic nerve involvement.

We initially evaluated 3D CNN architectures on the same region of interest using identical train–validation splits. However, in our dataset, 3D models were computationally less efficient and demonstrated a greater tendency toward overfitting, with improved training performance but no corresponding improvement in validation performance. This behavior is likely related to the relatively limited cohort size and the substantially larger parameter space of volumetric models. For this reason, we adopted a 2D multi-channel design, in which adjacent slices were treated as input channels. This approach provided better data efficiency, lower memory requirements, and more stable optimization. In addition, CT volumes commonly exhibit anisotropic voxel spacing, where through-plane resolution differs from in-plane resolution; therefore, direct 3D convolutions require careful resampling or anisotropic kernels. From a practical perspective, the dual-branch axial and coronal 2D architecture also better reflects routine radiological interpretation, in which clinicians review image series sequentially across multiple planes.

The FxCNN_Model is designed to classify orbital CT images as fracture or non-fracture. It consists of two main branches, one processing the axial slice and the other the coronal slice. Both branches are equipped with convolutional layers, batch normalization, and max-pooling operations to extract spatial features from the input images. The two branches are then concatenated to create a joint representation, which is passed through a fully connected layer for final classification. The model structure is displayed in Fig. 2. The SGIDetectCNN is intended to classify fractures as non-severe or severe. This model uses a similar structure to the fracture classification model (Fig. 3).

The models were trained with an 80–20 train-test split, using the training set for optimization and the validation set for evaluation. Training employed Binary Cross Entropy Loss and Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10⁻³, reduced by a factor of 10 every 5 epochs if validation loss did not improve. A batch size of eight was chosen, with a maximum of 50 epochs and early stopping if validation loss did not improve for ten consecutive epochs. L1 and L2 regularization, and dropout layers were applied to prevent overfitting. Model performance was tracked after each epoch, with the best model based on validation accuracy saved.

To contextualize model performance, we compared our architectures to several widely used convolutional and transformer-based models initialized with ImageNet pretrained weights and fine-tuned on the same dataset, including EfficientNet22, GoogLeNet23, VGG1624, ResNet1825, Vision Transformer (TinyViT)26, DINOv2 ViT S/1427, Swin Tiny28, and ConvNeXt Tiny29. All models were trained using identical preprocessing, train–validation splits, and hyperparameter tuning procedures.

To assess the contribution of key architectural components, we performed ablation experiments by systematically modifying the network design and input configuration. Specifically, for the fracture detection model, we evaluated axial-only, coronal-only, and dual-branch multi-planar configurations to determine the contribution of each imaging plane and the effect of feature fusion. For the severe globe injury prediction model, we compared architectures with and without the DepthwiseConv2D stage to evaluate its impact on model performance and parameter efficiency. All ablation models were retrained and evaluated under identical preprocessing, training, and validation conditions using the same evaluation metrics.

All experiments were conducted on a machine equipped with an NVIDIA GTX 1660 GPU, and total training time per model ranged from 5 to 10 min, depending on the number of epochs and model complexity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the open-source software PyTorch (version 2.3.1) and Python 3.12.4. The data were presented as averages with standard deviations for continuous variables and as counts for categorical variables. Classification accuracy was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score (harmonic mean of recall and precision).

Results

250 orbital wall fractures met the eligibility criteria, with a mean age of 32.9 ± 14.42 years (range: 5–79 years). Details of demographic and radiologic features of included subjects are summarized in Supplementary Table 4.

The dataset comprised 200 training and 50 internal validation volumetric orbital CT scans, each annotated with the affected eye’s laterality. Each CT scan consisted of an axial volumetric series centered on the orbital region. Laterality information was used to standardize anatomical orientation during preprocessing, as described in the Methods section.

Fracture detection

The FxDetectCNN achieved an overall accuracy of 81% in detecting orbital bone fractures, with a sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 76%. In addition, the F1 score was 0.82, indicating substantial agreement between model predictions and ground-truth annotations. These results demonstrate that the model performs particularly well at detecting severe or positive injury cases, a critical capability in clinical applications where the cost of false negatives can lead to significant diagnostic and therapeutic delays and critical diagnostic oversights. Figure 4 presents the confusion matrix for orbital fracture detection, and Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the training and validation performance across 20 epochs. For orbital fracture detection, the FxDetectCNN achieved an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.79. The performance of FxDetectCNN was compared with benchmark models in Table 1. Several baseline architectures achieved comparable AUROC values, including GoogLeNet (0.813) and ResNet18 (0.845).

SGI estimation

A separate network, SGIDetectCNN, was trained to classify orbital injury severity as severe or non-severe. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 64%, with a sensitivity of 80%, a specificity of 48%, and an F1 score of 0.69. This indicates a strong ability to detect severe cases, although the model showed limitations in differentiating non-severe injuries. We compared the SGIDetectCNN model’s performance with other classification models, demonstrating its superiority with an F1 score of 0.69. Ablation study

Ablation studies were conducted to evaluate the contribution of key architectural components. For fracture detection, models trained using axial‑only and coronal‑only inputs achieved validation AUROC values of 0.575 and 0.565, respectively. A dual‑branch configuration integrating both planes improved performance, reaching AUROC = 0.79 in the ablation experiments. The final FxDetectCNN model, which incorporates dual‑branch multi‑planar inputs and a depthwise convolution stage, achieved an F1‑score of 0.82 and an AUROC of 0.79 (95% CI 0.682–0.868). For the severity estimation network, we evaluated the effect of the depthwise convolution stage. The configuration including this component achieved AUROC = 0.82, whereas removing it resulted in AUROC = 0.5625. The final SGIDetectCNN model using this architecture achieved an accuracy of 0.64, sensitivity of 0.80, specificity of 0.48, F1‑score of 0.692, and AUROC of 0.817 (95% CI 0.780–0.942). These results indicate that lightweight convolutional components can modestly improve performance while maintaining compact and computationally efficient architectures suitable for limited medical imaging datasets.

Comparison with experts’ performance

The first expert (FS) achieved 94.0% accuracy in binary injury detection and 56.5% in injury severity estimation. The second expert (SMR) demonstrated comparable accuracy for fracture detection (94.0%) and slightly higher accuracy for severity estimation (59.5%). Discussion

Fine-cut orbital CT scans are the gold standard for diagnosing orbital wall fractures and for planning their management30. Although a comprehensive ophthalmic examination is the most reliable method for diagnosing severe ocular injuries associated with orbital wall fractures, factors such as decreased level of alertness or coexisting conditions, including severe periorbital edema, may limit emergency physicians’ ability to accurately assess and manage ocular injuries31. Additionally, the intraconal and extraconal fatty pads around the globe provide excellent contrast, revealing details of eye structures. Recent studies also provided clues that certain aspects of CT imaging can be used to predict SGIs, including the location of the fracture6 and the muscle involvement21.

The present study demonstrates the potential of an interpretable CNN framework for the dual tasks of orbital fracture detection and injury severity estimation from CT scans. Our model achieved 81% accuracy for fracture detection and 64% for severity estimation. The implementation of Grad-CAM provided crucial model interpretability, confirming that its diagnostic focus aligns with clinically relevant anatomical structures. The severity estimation model demonstrated similar or better accuracy than the diagnostic performance of human experts (56.5–59.5%), suggesting that such AI systems can be used to reduce inter-observer variability and enhance diagnostic reliability in orbital trauma assessment.

Most applications of artificial intelligence in orbital fractures have focused on detecting and segmenting blowout fractures. Kim et al. (2025) achieved an accuracy of 99.47% for blowout fracture detection32, while studies by Bao et al. (2023) and Li et al. (2020) also reported accuracies exceeding 92% using various deep learning architectures17,19. The work by Li et al. (2024), which utilized a dual-network system (YOLOv7 for detection and an enhanced UNet for segmentation), achieved a detection F1-score of approximately 76.8% and a high segmentation Dice coefficient of 90.2%33. The hierarchical system developed by Oku et al. (2025) achieved excellent performance in classifying fracture morphology, with patient-level sensitivity of 94.7% for detection and 100% for differentiating clinically urgent trap-door fractures34. While these models demonstrated excellent performance, their scope was often confined to specific fracture subtypes. A significant contribution of this research is its departure from the current focus on isolated blowout fractures in existing literature. By developing a model trained on an inclusive dataset encompassing all fracture morphologies, including complex multi-wall patterns, our framework possesses enhanced ecological validity. The FxDetectCNN model showed an acceptable performance for the detection of fractures in a dataset of 250 cases with mixed orbital wall fractures; however, further model adjustment on a larger dataset is required to improve the model’s accuracy.

We compared our architectures to several widely used convolutional and transformer-based models. The FxDetectCNN demonstrated moderate discriminative ability for fracture detection (AUROC 0.792, 95% CI 0.682–0.868), with balanced sensitivity (0.86) and specificity (0.76). Several baseline architectures achieved comparable AUROC values, including GoogLeNet (0.813) and ResNet18 (0.845), indicating that the detection task is learnable across diverse model families. Within this context, FxDetectCNN provides favorable sensitivity, supporting its utility as a task-specific architecture for this dataset. For severity estimation, SGIDetectCNN achieved an AUROC of 0.817 (95% CI 0.780–0.942), with a sensitivity of 0.80 and a specificity of 0.48. Although several transformer-based models produced higher AUROC values, many did so with highly imbalanced operating characteristics (e.g., specificity near 0.96 but markedly lower sensitivity). By contrast, SGIDetectCNN offered a more clinically appropriate sensitivity-oriented trade-off, which is relevant for identifying potentially vision-threatening globe injury. The relatively wide confidence intervals across models reflect the limited dataset and difficulty of severity estimation from CT alone. Although the proposed models demonstrate meaningful predictive capability, their performance should be interpreted with appropriate caution. FxDetectCNN’s AUROC of 0.79 remains lower than some very high values reported in prior studies, which may reflect differences in dataset size, imaging heterogeneity, case difficulty, and labeling criteria. For severity estimation, the low specificity (0.48) indicates that many non-severe cases may be flagged as severe at the selected threshold, suggesting that the model is more suitable as a screening or triage mechanism rather than a standalone diagnostic tool.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to apply deep learning to improve SGI prediction in patients with orbital fractures. Estimating injury severity using only orbital CT scans has inherent limitations; for example, mild anterior segment hemorrhage or small full-thickness corneal lacerations cannot be detected with imaging alone. However, the widespread availability of CT in emergency departments and the difficulty in performing full ocular examinations by non-ophthalmologists in acute settings justifies efforts to enhance its utility by incorporating deep learning techniques. The SGIDetectCNN model achieved 64% accuracy on this complex task, surpassing the rates of expert clinicians. Although the specificity for severity estimation was moderate (48%), the model’s high sensitivity (80%) underscores its potential as a screening tool for the early identification of severe cases where timely intervention is paramount. Future studies are suggested to evaluate model performance in a prospective cohort to demonstrate its potential to improve clinical outcomes.

This study has several limitations. The model was developed using a modest sample size of 250 cases from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. We suggest that further studies on models’ performance be conducted in a multicenter setting to externally validate this work. The observed variability in expert annotations for severity grading highlights the intrinsic subjectivity of the SGI diagnostic task. Finally, the model input in this paper was only orbital CT scans, which have inherent limitations in the diagnosis of subtle ocular injuries. Future work could focus on building models that integrate optical imaging, such as fundus photography and optical coherence tomography, with CT scans.

This study introduces a novel deep learning framework that can detect orbital fractures and estimate injury severity. These models achieved clinically relevant performance, with the severity estimation model performing at least as well as expert ophthalmologists. This work is a first step toward applying AI as a decision-support tool in the diagnosis and management of orbital wall fractures in emergency settings where specialized expertise may not be immediately available.
