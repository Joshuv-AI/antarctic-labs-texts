Hypoxia‐preconditioned adipose‐derived mesenchymal stem cells‐derived exosomes transferring H19 obstruct neutrophil extracellular traps formation via HOXA5‐mediated inactivation of TLR4/NF‐κB/NLRP3 inflammatory signaling
Authors: Li Qian, Xianxi Meng, Bairong Fang, Li Pi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHypoxia‐stimulated adipose‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs)‐derived exosomes (Hypo‐Exo) have a positive impact on diabetic wound healing. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) can delay wound healing under diabetic hyperglycemia. This study aimed to investigate the mechanisms by which Hypo‐Exo influence NETs formation.MethodsThe dorsal excisional wound model was performed using streptozotocin‐induced diabetic mice. Neutrophils were treated with phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate and Hypo‐Exo or ADSCs overexpressing H19 Exo, which were prepared for subsequent exploration. NETs formation was analyzed employing Sytox Green staining and PicoGreen dsDNA assay. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were exposed to the culture medium of neutrophils with Hypo‐Exo treatment. CD31 and Alpha‐Smooth Muscle Actin protein expression were detected by immunofluorescence staining. Long non‐coding RNA H19 (H19) expression was evaluated by RNA‐FISH analysis. The luciferase reporter gene and RNA immunoprecipitation analysis verified the interactions between miRNA‐130a/b‐3p (miR‐130a/b‐3p) and H19 or Homeobox A5 (HOXA5).ResultsHypo‐Exo promoted diabetic wound healing by repressing excessive NETs formation. Furthermore, Hypo‐Exo inhibited Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4)/Nuclear factor κB (NF‐κB) pathway and inactivated NOD‐like receptor pyrin domain‐containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. Moreover, Hypo‐Exo‐mediated inhibition of NETs formation promoted the proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis of HUVECs. H19 could interact with miR‐130a/b‐3p to generate a competing endogenous RNA regulatory network, thereby positively modulating HOXA5. Additionally, ADSCs overexpressing H19‐derived exosomes promoted diabetic wound healing by regulating the miR‐130a/b‐3p/HOXA5 pathway in vivo.ConclusionHypo‐Exo transferring H19 promoted diabetic wound healing by repressing NETs formation via the miR‐130a/b‐3p/HOXA5 pathway.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes is a well‐recognized chronic metabolic disease characterized by hyperglycemia, which can lead to various complications 1 . Approximately 25% of individuals with diabetes suffer from delayed wound healing, a debilitating complication that often progresses to diabetic foot disease and may ultimately necessitate limb amputation 2 . Mechanistically, diabetic hyperglycemia disrupts multiple biological processes—including dysregulated inflammatory responses, impaired angiogenesis, and defective fibroblast function—which collectively drive the pathogenesis of delayed wound healing 3 . Standard therapeutic interventions aimed at facilitating diabetic wound healing, including glycemic control, surgical debridement, and systemic pharmacotherapy, have shown limited efficacy 4 . Although peptides and peptide‐derived agents have attracted significant attention as novel candidates for the restoration of skin and mucosal barriers, with cyclic heptapeptide FZ1, Cy RL‐QN15, and RL‐QN15 serving as significant examples 5 , 6 , 7 . However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of diabetic delayed wound healing remain to be fully elucidated.

From an immunological perspective, neutrophils play a crucial role in the early stages of the healing processes 8 . Neutrophils can form and release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which are extracellular networks containing bactericidal proteins 9 . NETs function to entrap and eliminate invading pathogens, but also contribute to pathological processes, including tumor metastasis and organ tissue injury 10 , 11 . In terms of mechanism, NETs formation is a heterogeneous process encompassing both lytic (suicidal) and non‐lytic (vital) pathways, which correspond to slow and rapid NETs formation, respectively 12 . High‐glucose conditions commonly trigger excessive, detrimental inflammatory responses that converge on the lytic (suicidal) NETs formation pathway 13 . It follows a defined molecular sequence: Toll‐like Receptor 4 (TLR4) activation initiates signaling, Peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) mediates histone citrullination, and Citrullinated Histone H3 (CitH3) formation drives the assembly and release of destructive suicidal NETs 14 . Moreover, previous studies have also clarified that NETs formation is enhanced under diabetic hyperglycemia, and this aberrant NETs formation is negatively correlated with wound‐healing outcomes in patients with diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) 15 , 16 , 17 . Nevertheless, the upstream mechanisms governing suicidal NETs formation in diabetic wound healing remain incomplete. Unraveling these mechanisms could identify novel cellular targets for the treatment of delayed diabetic wound healing and its relevant diseases.

Adipose‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs), a subset of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), play a vital role in skin regeneration and the amelioration of morphological or structural deficits intrinsic to skin aging 18 . Moreover, ADSCs promote wound healing via rapid proliferation and differentiation into cutaneous lineage cells, including dermal fibroblasts and keratinocytes 19 . In the context of diabetic wound healing, ADSCs have been shown to enhance the closure of diabetic wounds 20 . ADSCs‐derived exosomes function as paracrine factors, facilitating communication during ADSCs‐mediated diabetic wound healing by delivering signaling molecules, including proteins and non‐coding RNAs (e.g., microRNAs and long non‐coding RNAs) 21 , 22 . Furthermore, the exosomes derived from MSCs restrain NETs formation, thereby controlling tissue injury 23 . At the same time, the role of ADSCs‐derived exosomes in the regulation of NETs formation during diabetic wound healing remains unclear. Moreover, hypoxia‐stimulated ADSCs‐derived exosomes (Hypo‐Exo) exhibit a significantly enhanced effect on wound healing compared with normoxia‐treated ADSCs‐derived exosomes (Normo‐Exo) in diabetic wounds 24 . Given the pathological role of dysregulated NETs formation in diabetic wound healing, elucidating the precise mechanisms by which Hypo‐Exo regulate NETs formation will reveal its contribution to disease pathogenesis and identify potential therapeutic targets for diabetic wound‐related complications.

Long non‐coding RNA (lncRNA) H19 (H19) can modulate the inflammation and angiogenesis processes 25 . For diabetic wound healing, H19 expression is down‐regulated; however, its overexpression significantly accelerates wound healing by promoting skin fibroblast proliferation and angiogenesis in the DFU model 26 . Consistent with the previous report, MSCs‐derived exosomes that transfer H19 accelerate diabetic wound healing by enhancing fibroblast function 27 . Our prior work also demonstrated that Normo‐Exo exosomal H19 improved the functional capacities of skin fibroblasts to promote cutaneous wound healing 28 . In addition, our previous data showed that Hypo‐Exo facilitated wound healing by up‐regulating H19 in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) 29 . Additionally, H19 expression level is negatively associated with the increased population of neutrophils in the sepsis‐induced acute lung injury model 30 . While the regulatory effects of Hypo‐Exo exosomal H19 on the NETs formation of neutrophils and their interaction mechanism in diabetic wound healing remain unclear, they warrant further exploration.

MicroRNA (miR)‐130 exerts important anti‐tumor effects in various types of tumors 31 . Notably, miR‐130a‐3p expression is down‐regulated in patients with diabetes and its complications, and its overexpression attenuates glucose lipid accumulation and oxidative injury in a mouse model of diabetic retinopathy 32 . Moreover, ADSCs‐derived extracellular vesicles that deliver miR‐130a‐3p enhance the function of high‐glucose (HG)‐treated Schwann cells 33 . This suggests the potentially protective effect of miR‐130a‐3p on fibroblast function against diabetic hyperglycemia‐induced injury. The modulatory mechanism of miR‐130a/b‐3p or Hypo‐Exo exosomal miR‐130a/b‐3p in diabetic wound healing remains unknown. Moreover, the regulatory effects of miR‐130a/b‐3p on NETs formation under diabetic hyperglycemia conditions have not yet been published. According to the Starbase database, H19 was predicted to bind with miR‐130a/b‐3p. This interaction may modulate dysregulated NETs formation in neutrophils and thereby regulate the diabetic wound‐healing process, which warrants further validation.

Homeobox A5 (HOXA5), a member of the HOX transcription factor family, plays a crucial role in skeletal and organ development 34 . Abnormal expression of HOXA5 is closely associated with aberrant cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis in both normal and tumor cells 35 . As for wound healing, HOXA5 has been shown to exert inhibitory effects on angiogenesis and healing; however, its role in diabetic or chronic wound healing remains elusive 36 . MSCs‐derived exosomes have been found to promote autophagy and proliferation through HOXA5 in breast cancer cells 37 . Whether Hypo‐Exo can exert regulatory effects via HOXA5 on diabetic wound healing also remains unknown. Furthermore, NETs formation can affect HOXA5 protein stability, which influences the therapeutic efficacy of anti‐fibroblast growth factor‐inducible molecule 14 (Fn14) therapy for septic acute kidney injury 38 . This mechanism may also operate in diabetic wound healing and warrants further investigation. In addition, HOXA5 may interact with miR‐130a/b‐3p, and the mechanisms of their interaction in NETs formation during diabetic wound‐healing progression remain to be elucidated.

In this study, we utilized a diabetic mouse model with dorsal excisional wounds, Hypo‐Exo or H19‐overexpressing ADSCs‐derived exosomes‐stimulated neutrophils, and HUVECs treated with the culture medium of neutrophils with the pretreatment of Hypo‐Exo to explore the mechanisms underlying Hypo‐Exo‐mediated regulation of NETs formation and diabetic wound healing. We speculated that Hypo‐Exo that transfer H19 may restrain NETs formation to promote diabetic wound healing by regulating the miR‐130a/b‐3p/HOXA5 axis. Clarifying this mechanism will identify potential cellular targets for accelerating wound healing in patients with delayed diabetic wound healing.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Human neutrophil isolation

Human neutrophil isolation was performed using a Human peripheral blood neutrophil isolation solution kit (#P9040, Solarbio Biotech, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, human peripheral blood was supplemented with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) solution (#C500623, 1×, 30%, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and erythrocyte sedimentation solution (30%, #P9040, Solarbio Biotech). The mixture was incubated for 30 min, after which the supernatant layer was carefully collected. The sample was then centrifuged at 800 g at room temperature for 30 min, and the lower white membrane containing the neutrophil was collected. Subsequently, the white membrane was transferred into a new centrifuge tube and washed twice with a cell cleaning solution (10 mL, #P9040, Solarbio Biotech). After centrifugation at 250 g for 10 min, the peripheral blood neutrophils were isolated and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium (#11875119, Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, #10099141, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, #15140122, Gibco), under conditions of 37°C and 5% CO2.

NETs isolation and purification

NETs were induced and isolated from neutrophils, following published protocols 39 . In brief, neutrophils were stimulated with phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA, 20 nM, #HY‐18739, MCE, Shanghai, China) for 3 h. After that, NETs were resuspended in the prechilled D‐Hanks solution without calcium and magnesium but containing phenol red solution (0.5 mL, #H1040, Solarbio Biotech), followed by centrifugation at 400 g (5 min, 4°C). The NETs‐enriched supernatant was collected as the purified fraction.

Cell culture and treatment

Adipose‐derived stem cells (ADSCs) were obtained from mouse inguinal subcutaneous adipose tissues, according to the relevant study 40 . In brief, after the removal of vessels and washing with PBS (#C500623, Sangon Biotech), the fatty tissues were digested with collagenase type I (1%, #SCR103, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 1 h at 37°C. The digested product was then centrifuged at 1,000 g for 10 min, and the non‐adherent cells were acquired after a 48‐h incubation. ADSCs were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium F12 (DMEM/F12, #11320033, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (#10099141, Gibco) and 1% P/S (Gibco) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. HUVECs were purchased from Procell Life Biotech (Wuhan, China) and cultured in DMEM (#11965092, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (#10099141, Gibco) and 1% P/S (#15140122, Gibco). The cells were maintained under conditions of 37°C and 5% CO2.

ADSCs‐exosomes isolation and treatment

In our previous study, we successfully isolated and validated ADSCs‐derived exosomes in our lab 29 . Briefly, ADSCs, which reached 70–90% confluence, were cultured in microvascular endothelial cell growth medium‐2 (without serum, #11111044, Gibco) for 24 h. For the hypoxia treatment, ADSCs were cultured under conditions of 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2 for 24 h, and then, the cell supernatant was collected to isolate the hypoxic ADSCs‐derived exosomes (Hypo‐Exo). Moreover, exosomes extracted from ADSCs under normoxic conditions (5% CO2 and 95% O2) were designated as normal ADSCs‐derived exosomes (Normo‐Exo). We utilized the Hieff Quick exosome isolation kit for Cell Culture Media (#41201ES50, Yeasen Biotech, Shanghai, China) to isolate exosomes, and then, the acquired exosomes were stored at −80°C, based on the manufacturer's instructions. As previously described, we conducted a comprehensive physicochemical characterization and validation of exosomal surface marker proteins for both Normo‐Exo and Hypo‐Exo. This included transmission electron microscopy for structural observation, nanoparticle tracking analysis for size distribution, and the determination of positive and negative surface marker proteins, specifically Tumor Susceptibility Gene 101 (TSG101), Heat Shock Protein 70 (HSP70), CD63, and Calnexin 28 , 29 .

To explore the effects of Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo on NETs formation of neutrophils, the PMA‐stimulated neutrophils were treated with Hypo‐Exo (100 μg/mL), Normo‐Exo (100 μg/mL), or PBS (#C500623, Sangon Biotech) for 3 h. Moreover, to evaluate the effects of Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo‐mediated regulation of NETs formation in neutrophilson, the cell reactions of HUVECs, the culture medium (CM) of neutrophils with Hypo‐Exo (NET (Hypo‐Exo)‐CM), Normo‐Exo (NET (Hypo‐Exo)‐CM), or PBS (NET (PBS)‐CM) was collected to subsequently incubate HUVECs. Then, the HUVECs were obtained to conduct relevant molecular determination. Furthermore, to clarify the role of H19, ADSCs were transfected with H19 overexpression (OE, Genechem, Shanghai, China) or an empty vector (negative control, NC, Genechem), and then their exosomes (ExoH19 or ExoNC) were isolated to treat neutrophils.

Cell transfection

For the overexpression of miR‐130a/b‐3p, the matching miR‐130a/b‐3p mimics and NC (mimics‐NC) were acquired from Genechem. To overexpress H19 and HOXA5, the relevant full‐length cDNAs were also obtained from Genechem and subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 vectors (#V79020, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to construct the pcDNA3.1‐H19 and pcDNA3.1‐HOXA5 vectors. Isolated neutrophils and ADSCs were transfected with these plasmids using a Lipofectamine 3000 (#L3000075, Invitrogen) kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. After transfection, the expression levels of miR‐130a/b‐3p, H19, and HOXA5 in the neutrophils and ADSCs transfected with NC and OE vectors were examined and compared to validate the transfection efficiency.

Diabetic mice model establishment

Male C57BL/6J mice (aged 5–6 weeks, 20 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The mice were provided with unlimited access to water and pellet feed in a temperature‐controlled animal laboratory maintained at 22°C, 55% relative humidity, and a 12‐h light/dark cycle. To induce type 2 diabetes, the mice in the treatment group were fed a high fat diet (HFD) consisting of 60% fat (#H10060, HFK Biotech, Beijing, China) for 4 weeks 41 . Then, the mice were administered intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ, 35 mg/kg, in 0.01 M citrate buffer, #S0130, Merck, Shanghai, China) over a period of 5 days, following established experimental protocols 42 , 43 . The mice in the control group were injected with an equivalent volume of citrate buffer. Blood glucose levels were measured using a glucose meter (Accu‐chek, Roche, Lyon, France) at 72 h post‐STZ injection. The mice were judged as diabetic if their blood glucose levels were ≥250 mg/dL for 3 consecutive days and subsequently employed for the formal experiment. All animal experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University.

Dorsal excisional wound model establishment

Dorsal excisional wounds, measuring 6 mm in diameter, were created on the dorsal skin of the diabetic mice that were administered pentobarbital sodium anesthetization (1%, 30 mg/kg, #P3761, Sigma‐Aldrich, Shanghai, China), in accordance with previous studies 43 , 44 . Photographic documentation of the wound conditions was performed on days 0, 3, 7, and 14 post‐wounding to facilitate subsequent assessments of wound gross appearance and calculations of closure length (Image J, NIH, Bethesda, MA, USA). Moreover, on the 7th and 14th day after the wounding, wound tissues of each group were obtained with an 8‐mm biopsy punch for further determination. On day 3 post‐wounding, during the treatment of Exo, either miR‐130a/b/−3p agomir (2 nmol/50 μL PBS, Genechem) or ExoH19 (2 mg/100 μL PBS) combined with miR‐130a/b‐3p agomir (2 nmol/50 μL PBS, Genechem) were injected subcutaneously at four central points along the wound edge 43 .

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Dorsal wound tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde (4%, #E672002, Sangon Biotech), dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin (#A601890, Sangon Biotech) following standard procedures to acquire paraffin sections (4 μm thickness). Then, H&E staining was performed, where sections were immersed in hematoxylin for 1 min and eosin resolution for 30 s, using a Hematoxylin–Eosin staining assay (#MB9898, Meilunbio, Dalian, China) per the manufacturer's guidelines to evaluate diabetic wound recovery. More than five images were randomly selected for each sample to monitor the change in diabetic wounds under the MZ10F microscope (Leica, Solms, Germany, ×40 magnification).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

IF staining was employed to observe NETs formation in the diabetic wound bed beneath the scab and human peripheral neutrophils. Briefly, the neutrophils were fixed in paraformaldehyde (4%, #E672002, Sangon Biotech) and then permeabilized with diluted Triton X‐100 (0.1%, #IR9069, Solarbio Biotech) for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were blocked by 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, #A419063, Sangon Biotech) solution for 30 min at 25°C. The paraffin sections of dorsal wound tissues were subjected to deparaffinization and antigen retrieval 45 . Tissue sections were also permeabilized with diluted Triton X‐100 (0.2%, #IR9069, Solarbio Biotech) for 10 min and blocked by 5% BSA solution (#A419063, Sangon Biotech) for 30 min at 25°C. After, the fixed neutrophils and paraffin sections were incubated with the marker antibodies for NETs, including anti‐CitH3 (1:50, #ab281584, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti‐Myeloperoxidase (MPO, 1:50, #ab45977, Abcam), as well as the marker antibodies for angiogenesis including anti‐CD31 (1:200, #ab222783, Abcam) and the marker for myofibroblasts including anti‐Alpha‐Smooth Muscle Action (α‐SMA; 1:200, ab7817, Abcam) for 12 h at 4°C following a prior washing with PBS (#C500623, Sangon Biotech). The slides were further incubated with the corresponding second antibody (1:20, #ab2761, Abcam) for 2 h and then incubated with the 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) staining solution (#ab228549, Abcam) for 5 min. Fluorescence images (at ×400 magnification) were observed under a SP8 LIGHTNING confocal microscope (Leica). More than five fields were randomly selected per section. ImageJ software (NIH) was employed to analyze the fluorescence intensity of each group.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of circulating MPO‐DNA complexes were measured using a capture ELISA. Briefly, a 96‐well microtiter plate was coated with an anti‐MPO monoclonal antibody (5 μg/mL, 50 μL, #ab9535, Abcam) for 12 h at 4°C. After washing, mouse plasma samples (50 μL) and incubation buffer containing peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐DNA monoclonal antibody (80 μL, #11920685001, Cell Death Detection ELISA‐PLUS Kit, Roche, Mannheim, Germany) were supplemented to each well and incubated for 2 h at 25°C. Following another washing step, peroxidase substrate solution (100 μL, #15169, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, and the plates were subsequently incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 405 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax ABS Plus, Molecular Devices, Shanghai, China).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for lncRNA H19

In situ hybridization for lncRNA H19 was performed utilizing a FISH kit (#C10910, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China), based on the manufacturer's instructions and the established protocol 46 . The lncRNA H19 probe was also designed and obtained from RiboBio, and it was labeled with Cy3 fluorescent dye. Following fixation and permeabilization, paraffin sections of dorsal wound tissues were blocked in the pre‐hybridization buffer, which was derived from the kit, for 0.5 h at 37°C. Subsequently, they were incubated in the hybridization buffer containing the lncRNA H19 probe (0.5 μM) at 37°C for 12 h. Moreover, to confirm the interaction between H19 and Transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP43), the paraffin sections were further incubated with TDP43 primary antibody (1:50, #ab109535, Abcam) for 12 h at 4°C. After washing with PBS (#C500623, Sangon Biotech), the sections were incubated with the second antibody (1:20, #ab2761, Abcam) for 2 h. Then, the sections were washed with saline sodium citrate buffer and stained with DAPI solution (#ab228549, Abcam) for 10 min at 25°C. Images were captured at a magnification of ×400, with more than five fields randomly selected per section. A laser confocal microscope (TCS SP8, Leica) was utilized for the imaging process, and the analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (NIH).

Cell counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay

Cell viability was estimated using a CCK‐8 assay (#C0037, Beyotime Biotech). HUVECs (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96‐well plates for 24 h. Next, the CCK‐8 working solution (100 μL) was supplemented and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. The absorbance at 450 nm was read on a microplate reader (SpectraMax ABS plus, Molecular Devices).

5‐Ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine (EdU) staining

For the EdU incorporation assay, HUVECs (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in a 96‐well and labeled with EdU (#ST067, Beyotime Biotech) and Hoechst 33258 (#C1017, Beyotime Biotech), based on the manufacturer's protocol. Following washing and permeabilization, DAPI (#ab228549, Abcam) was employed to stain the cell nuclei. Fluorescence was visualized at a magnification of ×400, with more than five fields randomly selected for each sample. The EdU‐positive cells were counted under an MZ10F fluorescence microscope (Leica). EdU‐positive cells were quantified as a percentage of total cells.

SYTOX green staining assay

The PMA‐stimulated human neutrophils (6 × 105 cells) were cultured on the Poly‐L‐lysine (0.01%, #60716ES, Yeasen Biotech)‐coated 6‐well plate. Then, the neutrophils were incubated with SYTOX green solution (5 μM, #C1186, Beyotime Biotech) for 15 min at room temperature to stain extracellular DNA. Images were captured at ×400 magnification, with more than five fields randomly selected per section. A fluorescence microscope (Leica) was utilized for the imaging, and the analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (NIH). NETs were defined as the regions where the individual SYTOX Green‐positive areas exceeded a threshold of 500 μm2. To quantify the area of NETs, we calculated the ratio of all regions surpassing this threshold to the total area of all SYTOX Green‐positive regions 47 , 48 .

PicoGreen double‐stranded (dsDNA) assay

A Quant‐iT PicoGreen dsDNA kit (#P7589, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was utilized to quantify the NET‐DNA levels of neutrophils, following the manufacturer's protocol and previous reports 48 , 49 . In brief, after the stimulation of PMA and the removal of cell supernatant, the neutrophils (5 × 105 cells) were digested with micrococcal nuclease (500 mU/mL, #EN0181, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 20 min, which was terminated by ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (5 mM, #HY‐Y0682, MCE). Following this, the cells were centrifuged (370 g, 5 min) to obtain the supernatant, which contained NET‐DNA, and the supernatant was quantified using the PicoGreen dsDNA Quantitation Reagent on a fluorescence spectrometer (Molecular Devices) with an excitation wavelength set at 480 nm.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production evaluation

The ROS level of neutrophils was examined using 2,7‐dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA, #50101ES01, Yeasen Biotech). The PMA‐induced neutrophils (2 × 106 cells) were incubated with DCFH‐DA (5 μM) at 37°C for 1.5 h in the dark, and then, the cells were washed with PBS (#C500623, Sangon Biotech). After that, the ROS level was determined on a flow cytometer (BD Science, Shanghai, China) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. Data analysis was performed using the FlowJo software (BD Science).

Tube formation assay

The tube formation of HUVECs was studied using an Angiogenesis assay (#ab204726, Abcam). HUVECs were cultured in the medium without serum for 48 h, in a 96‐well plate, then the diluted ECMatrix (50 μL, containing Matrigel, #08110, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) was added to each well and further incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Furthermore, HUVECs (3 × 104/well) and NETs (100 ng/mL) or NETs (1,000 ng/mL) were supplemented into the wells and incubated for another 48 h at 37°C. Afterward, tube formation was visualized, and the number of branches was quantified using an MZ10 F microscope (Leica, ×400 magnification), with more than five fields randomly selected per sample. The Image‐Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA) was employed to calculate the tube length.

Scratch assay

When the HUVECs (1 × 105/well) reached about 95% confluency, the scratch was conducted using a pipette tip (200 μL). Moreover, an optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to observe the scratch width after 24 h. The wound closure rate was calculated using ImageJ software (NIH).

PAD4 activity assay

The wound tissues were homogenized on ice, after which the tissue supernatant was collected to examine the activity of PAD4 employing a commercial kit (#700560, Cayman Chemicals, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). The absorbance at 470 nm was read on a microplate reader (Molecular Device). The PAD4 activity was calculated according to the instructions from the kit.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using a protein extraction kit (#20127ES50, Yeasen Biotech). Protein concentration was estimated using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (#PC0020, Solarbio Biotech). Protein samples (15 μg) were then separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and electrotransferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) at 4°C. Then the bands were incubated with the specific primary antibodies for 12 h at 4°C. After that, the bands were further incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 25°C. Chemiluminescence signals were detected on an ImageQuant LAS system (GE Healthcare, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) using a BeyoECL Star Ultra‐sensitive ECL chemiluminescence kit (#P0018AFT, Beyotime Biotech). The primary antibodies included anti‐HOXA5 (1:1,000, #ab140636), anti‐TLR4 (1:1,000, #ab13556), anti‐p65 (1:1,000, #ab32536), anti‐p‐p65 (1:1,000, #ab76302), anti‐CitH3 (1:1,000, #ab281584), anti‐histone H3 (H3, 1:1,000, #ab1791), and anti‐PAD4 (1:1,000, #ab96758), all of which were acquired from Abcam. GAPDH (1:500, #ab8245, Abcam) was chosen as the internal loading control.

Luciferase reporter assay

The potential binding sites of miR‐130a/b‐3p on H19 (WT‐H19) or HOXA5 (WT‐HOXA5) and the mutated site (MUT‐H19 or MUT‐HOXA5) were cloned into the firefly luciferase gene in PGL3 vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Neutrophils were maintained in 24‐well plates for 12 h, and then, the cells were co‐transfected with the WT‐H19/MUT‐H19 or WT‐HOXA5/MUT‐HOXA5 reporter gene plasmids and miR‐130a/b‐3p mimics or NC. After a 48‐h incubation, a Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (#E1910, Promega) was employed to monitor the luciferase activities.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) analysis

RIP analysis was conducted to investigate the interaction between H19 and miR‐130a/b‐3p, utilizing a RIP Kit (#17‐700, Merck). Neutrophils were lysed using RIP lysis buffer, and the resulting cellular extract protein (15 μg) was incubated with protein A/G magnetic beads in conjunction with anti‐immunoglobulin G (IgG, 4 μg, #ab172730, Abcam), anti‐Argonaute2 (Ago2, 4 μg, #ab156870, Abcam), and anti‐TDP43 (#ab109535, Abcam) antibodies for 1 h at 4°C. Subsequently, the co‐precipitated RNA was extracted, and the relative levels of H19 and miR‐130a/b‐3p were examined through qRT‐PCR analysis.

RNA pull‐down assay

Biotinylated TDP43 and H19 (both sense and antisense strands) were obtained from Genechem. The Biotin RNA Labeling Mix (#11685597910, Roche) was utilized to transcribe and label the TDP43 and H19 RNAs. Subsequently, the RNAs were treated with DNase I (#B618252, Sangon Biotech) and purified using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (#74135, Qiagen Biotech, Shanghai, China). Following purification, streptavidin beads (#S1638, Sigma‐Aldrich) were employed to capture the biotinylated TDP43 and H19 RNAs for a duration of 12 h at 4°C. The RNA‐protein complex adhered to the beads was collected to assess TDP43 protein levels (#ab109535, Abcam) via western blot analysis.

RNA decay assay

Neutrophils were transfected with the OE‐H19 + si‐TDP43 vector (Genechem) and subsequently stimulated with Actinomycin D (Act D, 5 μg/mL, #A606804, Sangon Biotech) for durations of 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. Following stimulation, neutrophils were harvested to examine the mRNA levels of HOXA5 using RT‐qPCR analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical tests are justified as proper for each figure, and the data satisfy the assumptions of these tests, including normal distribution. The estimated variance is comparable between the groups being statistically compared. A two‐tailed Student's t‐test was employed to assess the significance of the differences 51 . The one‐way anova with Tukey's post‐hoc tests was used for multiple‐group comparisons. Experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

RESULTS

Hypoxia‐preconditioned ADSCs‐derived exosomes accelerated diabetic wound healing and reduced excessive NETs formation in vivo

To explore the mechanisms of diabetic wound healing, we conducted a HFD and STZ‐induced diabetic wound model in mice, in which the diabetic group had an up‐regulated blood glucose level (Figure S1A). The diabetic group had significantly slower wound closure kinetics than the vehicle group during the 0–21 post‐wounding days (Figure S1B,C). Moreover, the dorsal wound tissues of the diabetic group exhibited delayed healing compared with the control mice, according to the H&E staining of skin tissues on the 7 post‐wounding day (Figure S1D). Specifically, the protein levels of markers associated with NETs formation, including CitH3 and PAD4, were significantly elevated in the diabetic group as the duration increased (Figure S1E). Furthermore, IF staining revealed that the levels of NETs formation markers CitH3 and MPO in the wound bed beneath the scab were also up‐regulated in the diabetic mice compared with the control group, which also exhibited robust and sustained intravascular release of NETs (Figure S1F,G). Therefore, we found that diabetic mice exhibited a significant delay in wound healing and an enhancement in the formation of NETs compared with non‐diabetic mice.

Subsequently, ADSCs were maintained under normoxic or hypoxic conditions, and their respective cell supernatant was collected for exosome isolation for subsequent examinations. Results from nanoparticle tracking analysis indicated that no statistically significant differences were observed between exosomes isolated under hypoxic conditions and those isolated under normoxic conditions, with respect to particle size distribution, particle concentration, or the particle‐to‐total‐protein ratio (i.e., particles per μg protein) (Figure S2A–C). Moreover, the protein levels of exosomal positive surface markers, including Tumor Susceptibility Gene 101 (TSG101), Heat Shock Protein 70 (HSP70), and CD63, have indicated that the Hypo‐Exo exhibit higher expression levels compared with the Normo‐Exo (Figure S2D). Additionally, both the Normo‐Exo and Hypo‐Exo have not expressed the exosomal negative marker protein, Calnexin, which is an endoplasmic reticulum protein (Figure S2D). Whereas, hypoxic preconditioning has remarkably increased H19 expression pattern in the group of Hypo‐Exo (Figure S2E). Collectively, these data might imply the functional superiority of Hypo‐Exo stems from a qualitative change in their molecular cargo, instead of a higher quantity of secreted vesicles.

Accordingly, either Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo was injected subcutaneously at four central points along the wound edge. As shown in Figure 1a, compared with PBS control, Normo‐Exo or Hypo‐Exo treatment significantly promoted diabetic wound healing during the post‐wounding 0–14 days, while the Hypo‐Exo group had a greater effect on wound healing than the Normo‐Exo group. In the diabetic mouse model, the wound area and length in the Hypo‐Exo and Normo‐Exo injection groups were consistently lower than those observed in the PBS group; moreover, Hypo‐Exo demonstrated a more pronounced effect compared with the Normo‐Exo treatment (Figure 1b,c). Furthermore, according to H&E staining, Hypo‐Exo treatment significantly enhanced diabetic wound healing as there was less re‐epithelialization impairment and inflammatory cells infiltration, mainly neutrophils, in the stained sections during post‐wounding 7–14 days, compared with the PBS or Normo‐Exo groups (Figure 1d). In addition, Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo treatment markedly up‐regulated angiogenesis in the wound sections of diabetic mice, characterized as increased CD31 protein level (CD31 was used as the endothelial cell marker to quantify angiogenesis during wound healing) as well as α‐SMA protein level, in contrast with the PBS group (Figure 1e). Moreover, in comparison with PBS, both Hypo‐Exo and Normo‐Exo significantly inhibited NETs formation in the diabetic wound skin tissues, which was evidenced by a greater enrichment of CitH3 and MPO as observed through IF staining, with Hypo‐Exo demonstrating a superior capacity to suppress NETs formation (Figure 1f). In addition, Normo‐Exo and Hypo‐Exo decreased the circulating MPO/DNA‐NET concentrations in the plasma of diabetic mice and the Hypo‐Exo‐mediated effect was the most significant (Figure 1g). The Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo treatment reduced the PAD4 enzymatic activity, a vital enzyme involved in NETs formation, in the skin tissue lysate of diabetic wounds, and the Hypo‐Exo exerted a more significant effect (Figure 1h). Notably, compared with PBS, RNA‐FISH analysis of the expression levels of H19 indicated that H19 was also up‐regulated by Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo treatment in the diabetic wound sections, with Hypo‐Exo demonstrating the most significant effect on the increase of H19 expression (Figure 1i). Collectively, this part data indicated that both Normo‐Exo and Hypo‐Exo could promote wound healing, enhance angiogenesis, repress NETs formation, down‐regulate plasma circulating MPO/DNA‐NET concentrations, decrease PAD4 enzymatic activity, and up‐regulate H19 expression in diabetic wound mice models; at the same time, Hypo‐Exo exhibited more significant effects than Normo‐Exo.

ADSCs‐derived exosomes, especially under hypoxic preconditioning, inhibited NETs formation in vitro

To further investigate the modulatory effects of ADSCs‐derived Exo on NETs formation in neutrophils, the isolated neutrophils were stimulated with PMA to induce NETs formation and subsequently treated with the ADSCs‐derived Exo under normoxic or hypoxic preconditioning. As displayed in Figure 2a, compared with control, NETs formation was enhanced in neutrophils under PMA stimulation, while the co‐treatment of Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo alleviated the PMA‐mediated enhancement of NETs formation, with Hypo‐Exo exerting a more significant effect. Subsequently, PMA stimulation also significantly up‐regulated the NET‐DNA levels of neutrophils, whereas Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo co‐treatment ameliorated that up‐regulation, and the effect of Hypo‐Exo was more potent than Normo‐Exo (Figure 2b). Furthermore, according to DCFH‐DA staining, PMA treatment increased the ROS levels, while the co‐treated Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo reversed the increase, and Hypo‐Exo had the most significant effect (Figure 2c). The protein level of NETs formation marker, CitH3, was up‐regulated by PMA; however, the additional treatment with Hypo‐Exo or Normo‐Exo abolished the PMA‐mediated up‐regulation in neutrophils, and Hypo‐Exo engaged with a more pronounced amelioration (Figure 2d). In conclusion, these data demonstrated that both Hypo‐Exo and Normo‐Exo could alleviate the PMA‐induced NETs formation in neutrophils, with Hypo‐Exo exhibiting the most significant effect.

In summary, our data indicated that both Hypo‐Exo and Normo‐Exo inhibited NETs formation and suppressed the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway as well as NLRP3 inflammasome activation in PMA‐induced neutrophils, with Hypo‐Exo demonstrating a more pronounced effect.

H19 directly combined with miR‐130a/b‐3p to generate a competing endogenous RNA regulatory network to positively regulate HOXA5

As shown in Figure 5a, according to the subcellular localization assays for H19 using RT‐qPCR, H19 was primarily detected in the cytoplasmic fraction of neutrophils. Moreover, from the online prediction evidence (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/), there were potential binding sites of miR‐130a/b‐3p on H19 (Figure 5b). And luciferase reporter assay further validated the interactions between miR‐130a/b‐3p and H19, as the luciferase activities were decreased by miR‐130a/b‐3p mimics in the H19‐WT transfected cells, whereas there was no change in the neutrophils transfected with H19‐MUT (Figure 5c). Consistently, AGO2‐RIP analysis indicated that the expression levels of H19 and miR‐130a/b‐3p in AGO2 pellets were higher than those in IgG pellets, suggesting that H19 may function as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) that interacted with miR‐130a/b‐3p, thereby forming an RNA‐induced silencing complex (RISC) in neutrophils (Figure 5d). In addition, overexpression of H19 significantly down‐regulated the miR‐130a/b‐3p expression in neutrophils (Figure 5e). Furthermore, under PMA, Hypo‐Exo treatment also repressed the miR‐130a/b‐3p expressions in neutrophils, compared with the Normo‐Exo and PBS groups (Figure 5f). Subsequently, after the overexpression of miR‐130a/b‐3p, AGO2‐RIP analysis revealed that the H19 enrichment in the anti‐AGO2 group was significantly higher than in the anti‐IgG group (Figure 5g). Next, the potential binding sites of miR‐130a/b/‐3p on HOXA5 were displayed, using the Starbase Database (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) (Figure 5h). And the interactions between miR‐130a/b‐3p and HOXA5 were verified by luciferase reporter assays (Figure 5i). The overexpression of miR‐130a/b/‐3p significantly down‐regulated the gene and protein expressions of HOXA5 in neutrophils (Figure 5j,k). Moreover, RIP analysis further showed that in H19‐overexpressing cells, the enrichment of AGO2 on H19 increased, while its enrichment on HOXA5 decreased, which indicated that H19 could capture endogenous miR‐130a/b‐3p, thus releasing HOXA5 (Figure 5l). And the overexpression of H19 significantly enhanced the mRNA and protein expressions of HOXA5, while the co‐overexpression of miR‐130a/b‐3p reversed that change (Figure 5m,n). The above data demonstrated that H19 could sequester miR‐130a/b‐3p through a “sponge” mechanism, which reduced their inhibitory effect on the downstream mRNA target, HOXA5, thereby alleviating the silencing of HOXA5. Additionally, based on the subcellular localization, H19 was also distributed within the nucleus of neutrophils. Therefore, we explored the potential impact of its binding to RNA‐binding proteins (RBP) on the stability of downstream mRNA. As shown in Figure S3A, there was a binding between H19 and TDP43, a classic nucleic acid‐binding protein, in the nucleus of neutrophils, through FISH analysis. And RNA pull‐down analysis further validated the interaction between H19 and TDP43 (Figure S3B). Subsequently, RIP analysis confirmed that HOXA5 mRNA bound to TDP43, and the overexpression of H19 significantly enhanced the interaction between TDP43 and HOXA5 mRNA (Figure S3C). The overexpression of H19 also up‐regulated the mRNA and protein levels of TDP43 and HOXA5, while the co‐treatment of TDP43 silencing reversed the up‐regulation (Figure S3D,E). The overexpression of H19 enhanced the mRNA stability of HOXA5, while the additional silencing of TDP43 resulted in a decreased mRNA stability of HOXA5 in neutrophils (Figure S3F). These data identified that H19 could combine with miR‐130a/b‐3p to generate a competing endogenous RNA regulatory network to positively regulate HOXA5 in neutrophils.

DISCUSSION

Delayed wound healing in diabetes is contended as the primary pathological driver of DFU, accounting for substantial morbidity and mortality 52 . Diabetic hyperglycemia disrupts multiple aspects of wound healing, including inflammation, angiogenesis, and the function of keratinocytes and fibroblasts 1 , 53 . Notably, neutrophils play a crucial yet detrimental role in the pathogenesis of non‐healing wounds: Under hyperglycemic conditions, NETs formation is significantly up‐regulated, which further impairs reparative processes 8 . NETs formation in neutrophils primed by diabetes has been extensively documented as a factor that impedes wound healing, and the inhibition of NETs formation facilitates the closure of diabetic wounds 54 , 55 . Moreover, exosomes that carry signaling molecules, such as the platelet‐rich plasma‐derived exosomal miR‐26b‐5p, have been evidenced to negatively regulate NETs formation, thereby enhancing the recovery of diabetic wounds 43 . In this study, we elucidated the upstream mechanism of NETs formation in diabetic wound healing: Hypo‐Exo that transfer H19 inhibited NETs formation via the miR‐130a/b‐3p/HOXA5 axis, thereby enhancing the cellular functions of HUVECs, including proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, ultimately promoting diabetic wound closure in vivo. These elucidated mechanisms provided potential therapeutic pathways and molecular targets for diseases associated with delayed diabetic wound healing.

ADSCs are widely recognized for their beneficial effects on diabetic wound healing 56 . A previous study has demonstrated that ADSCs can suppress inflammation, enhance angiogenesis, and promote re‐epithelialization in foot wound tissues, thereby facilitating the repair of DFU 20 . Additionally, the positive impact of ADSC‐derived exosomes on diabetic wound healing has been extensively validated, particularly under hypoxic conditions, where Hypo‐Exo exhibit enhanced efficacy in accelerating wound healing 24 , 57 . In this study, we also demonstrated that Hypo‐Exo promoted wound closure and angiogenesis, showing a more pronounced effect compared with Normo‐Exo, which aligned with existing studies. Importantly, we confirmed equivalent purities of Normo‐Exo and Hypo‐Exo through two approaches: quantifying the particle number‐to‐total protein ratio and analyzing the protein expression of exosomal surface markers, both positive and negative. This critical validation reinforced the conclusion that the observed modulatory effects were specifically mediated by exosomes. Furthermore, MSCs‐derived exosomes have been shown to repress NETs formation, thereby mitigating tissue damage in a spinal cord injury model 23 . Subsequently, the hypoxic MSCs‐extracted exosomes have down‐regulated the NETs formation to accelerate the healing of wounds in diabetic mice 58 . Notably, we identified that Hypo‐Exo treatment markedly reduced NETs formation in the wound sections of diabetic mice and in the PMA‐stimulated neutrophils, which represented a novel finding. Moreover, the inhibition of TLR4/NF‐κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome was revealed in the Hypo‐Exo‐treated neutrophils under PMA stimulation, accompanied by the inhibition of NETs formation. The ultra‐short cyclic peptide Cy RL‐QN15 and the peptide (RADA)4‐FZ1 also exert similar inhibition of the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway, which alleviates inflammation and accelerates oral ulcer and diabetic wound healing 59 , 60 . Consistently, the NF‐κB signaling inhibitor, Ro 106‐9920, suppresses the formation of NETs and enhances diabetic wound healing 61 . And Disulfiram can promote diabetic wound healing by inhibiting NETs formation through suppressing the NLRP3 signaling 54 . Therefore, our data were in accordance with the above published results. In addition, MSCs‐derived extracellular vesicles have accelerated diabetic wound healing by enhancing angiogenesis of endothelial cells via the inhibition of NETs‐induced ferroptosis 62 . Originally, we also revealed that Hypo‐Exo‐mediated inhibition of NETs formation (NET(Hypo‐Exo)‐CM) could up‐regulate the proliferation, migration, tube formation, and angiogenesis capacities of HUVECs, beneficially affecting cellular functions related to wound healing. This finding was partly consistent with a previous study that found high levels of NETs hindered the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes, thereby diminishing their wound‐healing potential 63 . We provided novel evidence that Hypo‐Exo promoted wound healing by repressing NETs formation under both hyperglycemic and normoglycemic conditions, along with the downregulation of TLR4/NF‐κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Collectively, these results establish Hypo‐Exo as a potential therapeutic strategy for the treatment of impaired diabetic wound healing.

Our previous data clarified the mechanism that hypoxia could increase the H19 expression levels in ADSCs and ADSCs‐derived exosomes through the ubiquitin‐specific protease 22 (USP22)‐mediated stabilization of hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1α) protein via a deubiquitination mechanism 29 . In the present study, the expression level of H19 was also significantly elevated in ADSCs‐derived exosomes under hypoxic stimulation. A limitation of this study is whether hypoxia drives the selective cargo sorting of H19 into ADSCs‐derived exosomes, or simply upregulates H19 expression in parental ADSCs, leading to an increase in exosomal H19 levels. This mechanistic distinction remains to be elucidated in our future studies and will be critical for fully understanding the regulatory mechanism underlying hypoxia‐induced exosomal H19 enrichment. In diabetic wound models, the overexpressed H19 has enhanced cell proliferation and angiogenesis of skin fibroblasts to promote DFU repair 26 . In addition, our study indicated that exosomal H19 from Normo‐Exo and Hypo‐Exo could increase the diabetic wound‐healing efficacy 29 , 64 . There, we found that Hypo‐Exo significantly inhibited NETs formation and elevated H19 levels in recipient neutrophils under PMA stimulation, marking the first investigation into the relationship between exosomal H19 and NETs formation. Furthermore, in line with the previous findings, our current study demonstrated that ExoH19 promoted healing in diabetic wound sections. In addition, miR‐130a/b‐3p has been implicated in the progression of diabetic peripheral neuropathy, and ADSCs‐derived exosomal miR‐130a‐3p can promote angiogenesis in a DPN rat model 33 . In the present study, we validated that H19 could directly interact with miR‐130a/b‐3p and negatively regulate its expression, consistent with the previous validation 64 , 65 . Mechanistically, H19 may function as a competing ceRNA that sponges miR‐130a/b‐3p, thereby preventing its incorporation into the RISC in neutrophils. Furthermore, miR‐130a/b‐3p was down‐regulated alongside a reduced NETs formation in the Hypo‐Exo‐treated neutrophils under PMA stimulation. The overexpression of miR‐130a/b‐3p reversed the decrease in NETs levels induced by ExoH19 in neutrophils treated with PMA and in wound sections of diabetic mice. Therefore, we elucidated the interaction mechanism of the Hypo‐Exo exosomal H19/miR‐130a/b‐3p axis in inhibiting NETs formation, providing underlying regulatory targets for NETs formation in diabetic wound healing.

As a transcriptional factor, HOXA5 is a crucial regeneration protein in modulating embryo development, hemopoiesis, and tumorigenesis 34 . We verified the interaction between miR‐130a/b‐3p and HOXA5, and functionally demonstrated that miR‐130a/b‐3p repressed HOXA5 expression in neutrophils. This finding mirrored the previously reported miR‐130a/b‐3p‐HOXA5 regulatory axis observed in hepatocellular carcinoma cells and vascular endothelial cells 66 , 67 . Hypo‐Exo treatment significantly up‐regulated HOXA5 expression in neutrophils, accompanying a reduced NETs formation under PMA. Subsequently, HOXA5 overexpression reversed the miR‐130a/b/‐3p's promotive effects on NETs formation in PMA and ExoH19 co‐treated neutrophils, which was first revealed. Our results aligned with previous studies indicating a negative correlation between NETs formation and HOXA5 stability in human kidney proximal tubular epithelial cells 38 . That suggested the positive role of HOXA5 in the Hypo‐Exo‐mediated inhibition of diabetic NETs formation. In terms of mechanism, we initially confirmed that H19 could capture endogenous miR‐130a/b‐3p, thereby releasing HOXA5, and the overexpression of miR‐130a/b‐3p abolished the H19 overexpression‐mediated increase of HOXA5 expression levels in neutrophils. Concerning the profound underlying mechanism, lncRNAs typically interact with DNA/RNA‐binding proteins to influence the stability of downstream target mRNAs. For instance, lncRNA HOST2 can enhance the mRNA stability of NOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase 2 in breast cancer cells through its interaction with ELAV Like RNA‐Binding Protein 1, a crucial RBP 68 . In the present study, H19 was found in both nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions and could directly interact with TDP43, a vital DNA/RNA‐binding protein, within the nucleus of neutrophils. Our findings were consistent with results from porcine satellite cells, which also demonstrated that H19 interacted with TDP43 69 . We identified that H19 may maintain the mRNA stability of HOXA5 through its interaction with TDP43. However, it required further validation and exploration of other regulatory pathways in the future, which was also a limitation of the present study. Collectively, this study uncovered a novel modulatory mechanism in which H19 directly interacted with miR‐130a/b‐3p to form a ceRNA regulatory network that positively regulated HOXA5, thereby modulating the formation of NETs in neutrophils. Furthermore, our data indicated that TLR4/NF‐κB signaling was activated by the overexpression of miR‐130a/b‐3p in neutrophils with PMA and ExoH19 co‐treatment and diabetic mice's wound tissues, while the overexpressed HOXA5 significantly reversed that activation. It has been evidenced that HOXA5 acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting the TLR4/NF‐κB inflammatory pathway, and loss of HOXA5 leads to elevated NF‐κB signaling, driving inflammation and cancer progression, particularly in breast tissues 70 . The precise role of HOXA5 in regulating TLR4/NF‐κB signaling during PMA‐induced NETs formation in both isolated neutrophils and diabetic wound tissue remains to be fully elucidated, which is a priority for our future studies.

In conclusion, our exploration revealed a previously unrecognized mechanism by which Hypo‐Exo‐derived exosomal H19 modulated NETs formation through the miR‐130a/b‐3p/HOXA5 axis in diabetic wound healing. The present study still has limitations, including a limited sample size of clinical specimens, the precise role of TDP43 in regulating NETs formation, and the mechanism requires validation in spontaneous diabetes animal models, such as the db/db mouse model. In addition, regarding neutrophil stimulation, we established an in vitro NETs formation model using PMA, a widely accepted inducer, in isolated human peripheral blood neutrophils 71 . This approach delivers high induction efficiency and consistent experimental reproducibility, facilitating the elucidation of the underlying mechanisms 72 . However, a limitation of the present study is that we exclusively employed PMA for neutrophil stimulation. To better mimic the in vivo microenvironment of NETs formation, it is necessary to utilize more physiologically relevant stimuli, such as lipopolysaccharide. We will explore the Hypo‐Exo‐mediated modulatory effects on NETs formation in LPS‐stimulated neutrophils and their role in diabetic wound healing in our future study. Overall, these findings enriched the precise function and mechanism of Hypo‐Exo H19/miR‐130a/b‐3p/HOXA5 in the NETs formation control for diabetic wound healing. This axis may hold significance for diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in diseases associated with delayed diabetic wound healing.
