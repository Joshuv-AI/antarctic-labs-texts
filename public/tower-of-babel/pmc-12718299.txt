We need transparency standards for social media research that involves companies
Authors: Georgia Turner, Ian Anderson, Luisa Fassi
Published: 2025

Concerns about social media’s impact on individuals and society are increasingly widespread (1). In response, policymakers and institutions have launched a range of initiatives to protect users. For example, the US Surgeon General has recommended parental warning labels for social media platforms (2), while Australia has announced a social media ban for children under age 16 (3). Regulations have passed in the United States (4), India (5) and the European Union (EU) (6), and numerous groups have proposed legal frameworks or regulatory fixes (7–9). Yet, these actions are being taken in the absence of robust scientific evidence (10). The mismatch between growing societal concerns and a scientific knowledge base stems from a shared obstacle facing governments and technology researchers alike: a lack of high-quality data about the benefits and harms of social media (11, 12).

Internal industry-run research has only been released to the public via leaks from whistleblowers and self-selected publications (13). Under pressure to provide more evidence, technology companies have therefore begun publishing research on the effects of social media in collaboration with external academic researchers. Here, we argue, with the help of a case study, that such industry–academia collaborations should be subject to independent regulation to ensure transparency. Regulating industry-involved research does not constitute a reduction in research freedom, but rather a way of addressing existing scientific resource and power imbalances.

Case in Point

The “2020 Election Project” was an industry–academia collaboration of unprecedented scale. The research team included researchers employed by the platform being studied—we call these internal industry researchers—and researchers who were not employed by the platform, whom we label external academic researchers. This team set out to study the impact of social media on political attitudes and behaviors during the 2020 US election. Studies took advantage of Meta’s unique research resources to conduct large field experiments on Facebook users, altering newsfeed algorithms and measuring outcome variables such as ideological segregation (14) and political polarization (14). The project culminated in a set of studies published from 2023 onward, across leading scientific journals, including Science, Nature, and PNAS (14–17).

The reaction of the scientific community to the project has been mixed, with lively debates about whether industry–academia collaborations of this kind are ethical (18–20). This debate often hinges on whether efforts should be devoted instead to facilitating independent research by external academic researchers (7, 21). For example, many have advocated for free access to social media data, citing new laws both in Europe and certain US states that grant users the right to request and donate their own social media data for research (21).

Although debates continue over whether and when industry–academia collaborations should occur at all, there has been less discussion on how to regulate and improve such collaborations when they do happen.

Although debates continue over whether and when industry–academia collaborations should occur at all, there has been less discussion on how to regulate and improve such collaborations when they do happen. As a result, research teams often set their own standards, despite clear conflicts of interest. In collaborations of this kind, internal industry researchers are financially incentivized to validate their company and its products. In parallel, external academic researchers have incentives to maximize chances of continued collaboration—their only way to access invaluable research resources such as company-owned datasets or opportunities to conduct field experiments. Such conflicts of interest require a high degree of transparency.

We argue that the 2020 Election Project fell short in transparency vis-à-vis study design, data collection, and reporting of results.* The solution: consensus-based transparency guidelines for industry–academia social media research collaborations. Academic organizations, and ideally policymakers and legal bodies, should implement and enforce these via independent regulatory frameworks.

Election Project Transparency

When designing an experiment, internal industry researchers have greater knowledge than external academic researchers about user privacy, such as platform-specific policies around user data-sharing consent. Such knowledge asymmetries can give internal researchers greater influence over study design. In the 2020 Election Project, an independent rapporteur, selected by the joint research team, observed and documented the interactions between internal Meta researchers and external academics. The rapporteur summarized his findings in a publicly accessible report entitled “Independence by Permission” (22).

Although this report marked an important step toward transparency, it also highlighted instances where Meta limited data access. Specifically, internal Meta researchers cited “privacy concerns” to preclude the use of any data pertaining to follower networks. This, despite explicit requests from the external academic researchers, one of whom argued: “If you want to understand how social media works, you need to study who follows who” (22).

Indeed, excluding follower networks can undermine study validity, as accounting for them could help to determine whether these projects violated assumptions for Randomized Controlled Trial research (23). Still, the published empirical research provided no further rationale for excluding such data. By declining the requests of external academic researchers without detailed explanation, internal Meta researchers could leverage asymmetric knowledge of privacy considerations to unilaterally shape study design without clear justification.

Comprehensive transparency about the nature of privacy and commercial constraints would enable the wider research community to assess the legitimacy of such concerns. This transparency would then empower future researchers to collaboratively devise strategies that balance privacy protection and other commercial considerations with research needs.

To maximize transparency about how privacy constraints affect study design, we suggest that publications arising from future industry–academia collaborations include a dedicated “Privacy and Commercial Constraints” section. This section should elaborate on all privacy considerations that affect either study design or any other aspect of the research process (e.g., data availability), whether due to industry policies or external regulatory frameworks such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). In particular, the section should detail any data restrictions and impacts due to such constraints, what exactly those constraints were, why they could not be overcome, and when they were communicated. For example, in the case of the 2020 Election Project, the section might detail precisely what privacy concerns arose from the inclusion of follower networks with reference to user consent agreements, if necessary. The section should also consider what impact this limitation might have, such as the inability to test assumptions, and report when they were communicated between internal Meta and external academic researchers.

To facilitate transparency, external academic researchers should preregister all the variables they hope to use, as well as the known privacy and commercial constraints at that time. However, the independent rapporteur notes: “On occasion, [the external researchers modified] what it is they sought to study, or how they sought to study it, as they learned details about what data were collected and structured by Meta for analysis” (22). Therefore, to accommodate the dynamic nature of study design, the preregistration should act as a minimum list. The final paper would then refer to, at least, all variables and constraints that were originally preregistered, as well as any that are raised during the collaboration.

Experimental Conditions and Data Collection

Exclusive industry control over research resources can affect not just study design, but also how studies are carried out. Studies in the 2020 Election Project preregistered methodology (i.e., publicly declared their analysis plan before beginning the study) and then documented deviations from this preregistration in the final paper, which follows open science best practices from organizations like the Open Science Framework. However, a letter published in Science subsequently noted that during the experimental period, Meta implemented a set of 63 emergency alterations to the Facebook algorithm (24). These alterations were designed to counteract political instability during the election, such as circulation of false claims about voting reliability, which Meta was concerned could spiral into offline political violence. These changes altered the content delivered to all US-based users (and thus all participants in the election experiments) during data collection, with the stated goal of rendering Facebook’s newsfeed both less polarizing and more reliable. In practice, this meant that content such as claims of voting machine fraud, even if organically gaining traction, was no longer boosted by the algorithm, thereby reducing its visibility.

These interventions fundamentally changed the experimental environment. Given that the changes were directly intended to impact users’ political attitudes—the study’s key outcome variable—the external validity of the experimental manipulation is called into question. In other words, the Election Project’s intervention could not affect polarization as it would have under usual platform conditions; the emergency alterations were simultaneously attempting to shape the very polarization under study. Such a change has been likened to “installing temporary air filters on factories only while measuring their pollution levels” (25). A Meta research director insisted that Meta was forthcoming about these changes to the 2020 Election Project researchers, yet these specific changes were not documented or discussed in the 2020 Election Project papers (24). This prompted Science to point readers to an editorial warning future researchers about the potential impact of the algorithmic alterations (26).

Transparency about any changes to experimental conditions during the data collection period is crucial. We recognize that such changes may be inevitable as companies negotiate competing considerations alongside research. Further, platforms may be concerned about sharing sensitive information related to the planned changes with external academic researchers. However, transparency guidelines to maintain research integrity should require explicit reporting of all changes during data collection, including those to algorithms, interface design, and any other aspects of the social media platform that could affect treatment or control conditions. Such reporting should be published either in the final paper in a “Data Collection Alterations” section or as a critical update issued by the journal within a year of the authors becoming aware of the problem—even if the changes were not preregistered (24).

Control Rights and Interpreting Results

Research results can allow for differing or even opposing interpretations. This is often the case for social media research in particular, as inferences depend on interactions between platform design and highly complex socio-political ecosystems (27). This ambiguity can create opportunities for conflicts of interest to guide how findings are interpreted—especially in the absence of a clear framework to prevent such opportunities. In the case of the 2020 Election Project, external academics initially obtained final decision-making authority (“control rights”) over how the authors interpreted and presented findings (22). However, the independent rapporteur noted that Meta “sought to reopen policies and procedures related to control rights” as articles approached publication. Specifically, he wrote, “Meta researchers wanted to be able to expressly disagree with lead author interpretations of findings or other matters in articles they co-authored” (22). In this case, academic collaborators did prevent this effort. But attempts of this kind to renegotiate terms late in the publication process underscore the importance of a clear, predetermined framework on control rights.

To enhance transparency in the reporting and interpretation of study findings, all future studies should follow the 2020 Election Project’s example in explicitly clarifying control rights at the start of the collaboration, such as through a public preregistration (28). However, unlike in the 2020 Election Project, upholding this commitment should not depend on ad hoc negotiation within the research team. Instead, there should be an independently enforced transparency framework to robustly and preemptively prevent attempts to renege on control right agreements at a later point. This framework should be established by agreement between academics, industry researchers, and policymakers, rather than decided by research teams on a per-study basis.

Implementation

Our recommendations for enhancing transparency are far from exhaustive. We hope this case study will initiate a broader conversation across multiple stakeholders (e.g., industry researchers, policymakers, and academic researchers) to establish and enforce comprehensive, consensus-based transparency standards for future industry–academia collaborations.

In the short term, academic journals or ethics review boards could play a key role in enforcement by, for example, requiring compliance before considering studies for publication. However, we also anticipate potential unintended consequences of implementing transparency guidelines. For example, vaguely specified regulation could enable superficial forms of “transparency-washing” akin to “AI audit-washing,” in which companies use claims of transparent research to bolster their reputation (29). Further, transparency regulation could discourage companies from carrying out certain studies with potentially reputation-threatening outcomes (29).

To minimize the risk of “transparency-washing,” transparency standards could ultimately be enforced by government agencies and legal bodies, similar to other technology regulations such as the EU Digital Services Act. Although it may need to contend with ongoing compliance issues (30), legal regulation will help ensure that guidelines are a minimum ethical requirement for industry–academia collaborations rather than a superficial badge that companies can use to enhance their reputation.

Models for such regulation already exist in other high-stakes industries. For example, EU regulations ensure the transparency and sustainability of food safety testing (31). These regulations mandate that food companies notify the European Food Safety Authority of all studies they intend to carry out and require data access as well as independent audits to ensure compliance with standards (31). In the case of social media research, such a framework could specify, among other requirements, that privacy concerns and changes to experimental conditions be transparently communicated, as well as publicly solidifying control rights.

However, there is a key difference between food and social media safety testing. It is hard to define transparency in technology research due to the complexity of the systems underlying the products. Even if all the code behind social media content delivery algorithms were published, it would remain difficult to interpret how these systems function in practice, given their reliance on machine learning models that resist straightforward explanation (32). It will therefore be essential to devise transparency regulations in social media research that maximize algorithm interpretability and understandability.

Counteracting all conflicts of interest in industry-involved research may well be impossible. Indeed, even the most transparent research study can only address the priorities and hypotheses of the researchers who design it. Furthermore, compulsory transparency for industry-involved research may deter companies from conducting such research. Therefore, no type of industry-involved research should be viewed as a substitute for fully independent academic research --i.e., research without the oversight or funding of industry. Independent, open, expert-reviewed research must remain the gold standard for generating robust, unbiased evidence on the safety and societal impact of social media products. We call on academics and publishers of scientific journals, including editors and reviewers, to recognize this and to evaluate, publish, and promote future research accordingly.
