Spectral representations of interpolation spaces of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
Michael Bitzer, Ingo Steinwart

Abstract
In statistical learning theory, interpolation spaces of the form [ L 2 , H ] θ , r [\mathrm{L}^{2},H]_{\theta,r} , where H H is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, are in widespread use. So far, however, they are only well understood for fine index r = 2 r=2 . We generalise existing results from r = 2 r=2 to all possible values of r r . In particular, we present a spectral decomposition of such spaces, analyse their embedding properties, and describe connections to the theory of Banach spaces of functions. We additionally present exemplary applications of our results to regularisation error estimation in statistical learning.



1
Introduction

Since their introduction, reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) have turned out as fundamental objects in various branches of mathematical theory as well as in applications.
As they combine the geometry of a Hilbert space with the continuous evaluation functionals of a function space, they posses many desirable properties.
Apart from applications in functional analysis and numerics, statistical learning theory takes advantage of this situation by considering a large variety of kernel-based algorithms.

Recent years showed that in the statistical analysis of such learning algorithms, certain interpolation spaces play a key role. To be more precise, for an RKHS
H
, spaces of the form

\displaystyle[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu),H]_{\theta,2}

(1)

are important for the analysis of kernel-based least squares regression, see e.g.
[
5
]
, for estimations of the conditional mean embedding, see e.g.
[
11
]
, and the analysis of learning curves, see e.g.
[
10
]
.
Similarly, these spaces occur naturally for certain questions related to Gaussian processes, see e.g.
[
15
]
,
[
9
]
.

The main reason for choosing
2
as the fine index in (
1
) is the spectral representation of these interpolation spaces. More precisely, the spaces (
1
) are given as the range of powers of the integral operator associated to the RKHS
[
18
, Section 4]
. Using eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of this operator hence yields many insights into interpolation spaces and turns out as very helpful in statistical learning theory.

Aside from these practical advantages of fine index
2
, considering other values turned out as beneficial. In particular, the following two cases are of special interest.

•

Fine index
1
is of interest as it is allows for certain embedding that are not possible for other fine indexes, see e.g.
[
17
, p. 4]

•

Fine index
\infty
is of interest as it allows for a precise analysis of approximation errors, see e.g.
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
.

For these reasons, we consider real interpolation spaces of the general form

\displaystyle[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu),H]_{\theta,r}\,\text{,}

(2)

that is, we allow for all possible fine indexes
1\leq r\leq\infty
.
Our aim is to present methods and results that treat such interpolation spaces for all fine indexes
r
in a joint fashion. The results, we will derive address the following main questions:

•

Do the spaces (
2
) and their norms still possess a spectral representation?

•

The interpolation spaces consist of equivalence classes of functions at first. When can we identify the spaces (
2
) with Banach spaces consisting of functions?

•

When are the spaces (
2
) continuously embedded into
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
?

The first question is clearly motivated by the practicality of spectral representations, as indicated by the papers mentioned earlier.
Positive answers to the second question have applications in all situations where a point evaluation of functions is necessary. A main example is given by the theory of Gaussian processes in the context of machine learning, as paths cannot be equivalence classes but need to be defined everywhere, see e.g.
[
12
, Assumption 1]
.
The third question will turn out to be tightly connected to the second one, but applications go beyond that. In particular, the statistical analysis of kernel-based learning algorithms relies on such embeddings, see e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 7.23]

[
5
, Section 3]

[
17
, p. 4]
.

This work is organised as follows: We start by introducing the necessary notation and fundamental definitions in Section
2
. Then the main results are provided in Section
3
. We present an exemplary application to error estimation in statistical learning theory in Section
4
. All proofs and the ideas behind the results will be given in Section
5
. Finally, some auxiliary results can be found in two appendices.

2
Preliminaries

Given normed
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, we use
E_{1}\simeq E_{2}
to denote isometric isomorphy,
E_{1}\triangleq E_{2}
for norm equivalent spaces and
E_{1}\hookrightarrow E_{2}
if
E_{1}\subset E_{2}
and the embedding is continuous.

For a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{A})
, we denote the set of measurable functions
X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
\mathcal{L}^{0}(X)
.
The set of bounded functions in
\mathcal{L}^{0}(X)
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(X)
and this space is equipped with the supremum norm.

Let us assume that additionally, a measure
\nu
is given on
X
. Then for
1\leq p<\infty
, we consider the space
\mathcal{L}^{p}(\nu)
of all functions in
\mathcal{L}^{0}(X)
with finite seminorm
\|f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}(\nu)}:=(\int_{X}|f|^{p}\mathrm{d}\nu)^{\frac{1}{p}}
.
Similarly,
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\nu)
denotes the space of essentially bounded functions in
\mathcal{L}^{0}(X)
equipped with the seminorm
\|f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\nu)}:=\essup_{x\in X}|f(x)|
.
Furthermore, we consider equivalence classes
[f]_{\sim}
of functions that are
\nu
-almost everywhere equal to
f\in\mathcal{L}^{0}(X)
.
For
E\subset\mathcal{L}^{0}(X)
, we denote the set of all equivalence classes of functions in
E
as
[E]_{\sim}
.
In particular, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\nu):=[\mathcal{L}^{p}(\nu)]_{\sim}
is equipped with the classical
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-norm inherited from
\mathcal{L}^{p}(\nu)
.
These spaces are Banach spaces.
For such indices
p
, we extend the typical arithmetic by
\frac{1}{\infty}:=0
.
If
\nu
is fixed and if
w:X\mapsto[0,\infty)
is a measurable
“weight”
function, we denote the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-space with respect to the measure
wd\nu
by
\mathrm{L}^{p}(w)
. For the counting measure
\nu
, the same applies to
\ell^{p}(w)
.

Interpolation spaces

Let us introduce some background from the theory of interpolation spaces of the real interpolation method. For more details, see
[
3
, Chapter 5]
or
[
4
, Chapter 3]
.
In the following, we only consider interpolation spaces of Banach spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
where
E_{1}\hookrightarrow E_{2}
.

To define the real interpolation spaces, we use the so-called
K
-method. To this end, for all
x\in E_{2}
and
t>0
, we define the
K
-functional as

\displaystyle K(x,t,E_{2},E_{1}):=\inf_{y\in E_{1}}\left(\|x-y\|_{E_{2}}+t\|y\|_{E_{1}}\right)\text{.}

(3)

For
x\in E_{2}
and the parameters
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r<\infty
, this enables us to set

\displaystyle\|x\|_{[E_{2},E_{1}]_{\theta,r}}:=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\theta r-1}K^{r}(x,t,E_{2},E_{1})\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}

(4)

and similarly, for
r=\infty
, we let

\displaystyle\|x\|_{[E_{2},E_{1}]_{\theta,\infty}}:=\sup_{t>0}\left(t^{-\theta}K(x,t,E_{2},E_{1})\right)\text{.}

(5)

Now the interpolation space of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
to the parameters
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
is defined as

\displaystyle[E_{2},E_{1}]_{\theta,r}:=\{x\in E_{2}\,:\,\|x\|_{[E_{2},E_{1}]_{\theta,r}}<\infty\}

(6)

and this space is equipped with the
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{[E_{2},E_{1}]_{\theta,r}}
-norm.
These spaces are again Banach spaces, see
[
3
, Proposition 1.8 in Chapter 5]
.
In fact, such interpolation spaces perform a kind of interpolation of Banach spaces, as they are
“in between”

E_{1}
and
E_{2}
in the sense that
E_{1}\hookrightarrow[E_{2},E_{1}]_{\theta,r}\hookrightarrow E_{1}
, see Lemma
A.2
.

Note that the definition of real interpolation spaces presented here is just one of several alternatives. Other ways, such as the
J
-method, yield equivalent norms.
In certain situations, such equivalent norms can be helpful to gain insight into interpolation spaces.

Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and Banach spaces of functions

Let
X
be a set and suppose that
E
is a Banach space that consists of functions
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. If for all
x\in X
, the evaluation maps

\displaystyle\delta_{x}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad f\mapsto f(x)

(7)

are bounded functionals on
E
, then
E
is called a Banach space of functions (BSF), see
[
19
]
for more details. These spaces recently attracted interest, as they yield insights into neural networks
[
2
]
.

If additionally, the norm of such a Banach space of functions
H
is induced by an inner product, for each
x\in X
, we can find a function
k(\cdot,x)\in H
that is associated to
\delta_{x}
by the Riesz representation theorem. Then
H
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) on
X
with kernel
k
. We refer to
[
16
, Chapter 4]
for fundamental results on RKHS.

Now let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space.
Suppose that
H
is an RKHS of a measurable and bounded kernel
k
on
X
such that
H
is compactly embedded into
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)
, that is the
“embedding”

I_{k}:H\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)
given by
f\mapsto[f]_{\sim}
is a compact operator.
In this case, we consider the self-adjoint, positive and compact operator
T_{k}:=I_{k}I_{k}^{*}
, called the integral operator of
k
, as it is given by

\displaystyle T_{k}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu),\quad f\mapsto\left[\int_{X}k(\cdot,y)f(y)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\right]_{\sim}\text{,}

(8)

see e.g.
[
18
, Lemma 2.2]
.
Let us denote the non-zero eigenvalues of
T_{k}
in non-increasing order by
\mu=(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
, where
I=\{1,...,n\}
or
I=\mathbb{N}
.
Due to the positivity and compactness of
T_{k}
, we know that
\mu_{i}>0
and, in case that
I
is infinite,
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{i}=0
.

As proven in
[
18
, Lemma 2.12]
, in such a situation, there are functions
e_{i}\in H
such that
([e_{i}]_{\sim})_{i\in I}\subset\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)
forms an ONS in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)
consisting of eigenvectors of the integral operator
T_{k}
, such that additionally
(\sqrt{\mu_{i}}e_{i})_{i\in I}
forms an ONS inside
H
.

The range of
I_{k}
, that is
[H]_{\sim}
, becomes a Hilbert space by using the quotient norm
\|[f]_{\sim}\|:=\inf\{\|g\|_{H}\,|\,I_{k}g=[f]_{\sim}\}
.

We summarise the above in the following assumption:

Assumption H.

Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a measure space and that
H
is an RKHS of a measurable kernel
k
on
X
such that
H
is compactly embedded into
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)
. Then, for a suitable index set
I=\{1,...,n\}
or
I=\mathbb{N}
, we denote the eigenvalues of
T_{k}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)
by
\mu_{i}
and the associated eigenfunctions by
e_{i}
for
i\in I
.

Assumption H+.

Let Assumption
H
be satisfied. In addition, let the measure space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be complete and
\mu
be
\sigma
-finite. Furthermore, assume that
k
is a bounded kernel.

Power spaces

Our goal is to describe the interpolation spaces from (
2
) together with their norms by a spectral representation, i.e. in terms of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the operator
T_{k}
.
To this end, for a
“weight function”

w:I\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, we consider the following partition of
I
:

\displaystyle M_{j}:=\left\{i\in I\,\middle|\,2^{j}<w(i)\leq 2^{j+1}\right\},\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}\text{.}

(9)

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
be a non-increasing sequence of positive numbers and
b=(b_{i})_{i\in I}
be a sequence of real numbers. Then for
\theta>0
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
, let

\displaystyle c_{j}(b):=\left(\sum_{i\in M_{j}}b_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{-\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(10)

and

\displaystyle\|b\|_{(\mu,\theta,r)}:=\|(c_{j}(b))_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{r}}\text{.}

(11)

Furthermore, let
\ell^{(\mu,\theta,r)}:=\left\{b\,:\,\|b\|_{(\mu,\theta,r)}<\infty\right\}
be equipped with the
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{(\mu,\theta,r)}
-norm.

It is not hard to see that, as the
\mu_{i}
are positive,
\|\cdot\|_{(\mu,\theta,r)}
is in fact a norm on
\ell^{(\mu,\theta,r)}
. The choice of powers of
2
in (
9
) is for notational convenience. Replacing it by any
s>1
yields equivalent norms. It will turn out that
\ell^{(\mu,\theta,r)}
is actually a Banach space, see Remark
5.8
.
Note that in the case
r=2
, one finds that
\ell^{(\mu,\theta,r)}=\ell^{2}(\mu^{-\theta})
with equal norms. In consequence, the following definition generalises the notion of a power of an RKHS from
[
18
, Section 4]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
H
,
e_{i}
and
\mu_{i}
be as introduced in Assumption
H
.
Then the power space of
H
to the parameters
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
is defined by

\displaystyle H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}:=\left\{f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)\,\middle|\,\exists b\in\ell^{(\mu,\theta,r)}\text{ with }f=\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}[e_{i}]_{\sim}\right\}

(12)

with norm given by

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}[e_{i}]_{\sim}\right\|_{H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}}:=\|b\|_{(\mu,\theta,r)}\text{.}

(13)

Note that this norm is well defined due to the uniqueness of the Fourier coefficients
b_{i}
.
Recall that in
[
18
, Section 4]
only the spaces
H_{\nu}^{\theta,2}
were introduced and investigated. Moreover, for
r\neq 2
, the spaces
H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}
are no longer Hilbert spaces.

3
Results

Our three main results are provided in the following. The first result states the norm equivalence of the power spaces
H_{\nu}^{\theta,r}
to the interpolation spaces of the form (
2
), as previously mentioned.
This is the foundation for the subsequent result, which shows that we can view these interpolation spaces as a BSF, in case they are embedded into
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)
.
The third result then analyses in which situations such an embedding exists.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
and suppose that
H
is as in Assumption
H
. Then

\displaystyle H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}=[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu),[H]_{\sim}]_{(\theta,r)}

(14)

and the norms on these spaces are equivalent.

Note that as
[H]_{\sim}
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu)
consist of equivalence classes of functions, so does the interpolation space considered in Theorem
3.1
. Not only for applications in learning theory, it is of interest whether such spaces can be viewed as actual function spaces with continuous evaluations functionals by a suitable choice of representatives.
Note that for
r\neq 2
, the spaces
[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu),[H]_{\sim}]_{(\theta,r)}
are no Hilbert spaces any more, so we have to expect BSFs instead of RKHSs.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\nu)
and
H
be as in Assumption
H+
.
Moreover, let
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
be such that the embedding
H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)
exists and is continuous.
Then there is a BSF
\overline{H}_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}
on
X
such that the operator

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}\rightarrow H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)},\quad f\mapsto[f]_{\sim}

(15)

is a well-defined isometric isomorphism with

\displaystyle\|\overline{H}_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(X)\|=\|H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)\|\text{.}

(16)

The existence of an embedding of the power space
H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}
into
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)
seems to be a crucial requirement of Theorem
3.2
.
Let us elaborate in which situations we can expect such an embedding.
For fine-index
r=2
, this question has been approached in
[
18
, Section 5]
. We extend this approach to
1\leq r\leq\infty
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\nu)
,
H
,
\mu_{i}
, and
e_{i}
be as in Assumption
H+
.
Moreover, let
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
, where
\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{r^{\prime}}=1
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

It holds that

\displaystyle\essup_{x\in X}\left\|\left(e_{i}(x)\mu_{i}^{\theta}\right)_{i\in I}\right\|_{(\mu,\theta,r^{\prime})}\leq\kappa

(17)

(ii)

The embedding
H_{\nu}^{(\theta,r)}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)
exists and is continuous with operator norm at most
\kappa
.

4
Application to statistical learning

Let us introduce some basic notions from statistical learning theory, where we refer to
[
16
]
for details. We assume that
(X,\mathcal{A})
is a measurable space, and that
Y\subset\mathbb{R}
is bounded. Suppose there is a probability measure
P
on
X\times Y
, wherefrom only some samples
(x_{1},y_{1}),...,(x_{n},y_{n})
are known.
Typically, one searches for a prediction function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that predicts an

Y
-value associated to a given
X
-value with low risk:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f):=\int_{X\times Y}L(y,f(x))dP(x,y)\text{.}

(18)

Hereby,
L:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the loss function, such as the least squares loss
L(y,t)=(y-t)^{2}
.
A function which minimises the risk over all prediction functions
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called Bayes decision function and denoted by
f_{L,P}^{*}
.

To find a good prediction function, one can choose an RKHS
H
on
X
, a
\lambda>0
and perform a Tikhonov-regularised minimisation of the risk, that is

\displaystyle f_{P,\lambda}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{f\in F}\;\lambda\|f\|_{H}^{2}+\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f)

(19)

where
P
is in practice replaced by the empirical probability measure assigned to the given data.
As done in
[
6
]
, we assume in this section that the kernel of
H
is bound by
1
.

Assuming that
L
is the least squares loss,
f_{P,\lambda}
can alternatively be found by the eigen-decomposition of
H
, see
[
18
, Theorem 5.4]
:

\displaystyle f_{P,\lambda}=\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\lambda+\mu_{i}}\left\langle f_{L,P}^{*}\,{,}\,[e_{i}]_{\sim}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(P_{X})}e_{i}

(20)

where we assume that
P_{X}
is the marginal distribution, that
Y=[-1,1]
and that
H
, the eigenvalues
\mu_{i}
and the basis
e_{i}
are as in Assumption
H
.
By this reformulation, we can to describe various other methods of regularised minimisation than (
19
) by replacing the term
\frac{\mu_{i}}{\lambda+\mu_{i}}
by a more general function
\mu_{i}g_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})
. The following conditions taken from
[
6
]
ensure that a reasonable algorithm emerges.

Definition 4.1
.

A family of functions
g_{\lambda}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for
0<\lambda\leq 1
satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\mu\leq 1}\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}|\lambda g_{\lambda}(\mu)|

\displaystyle<\infty

(21)

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\mu\leq 1}\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}|\mu g_{\lambda}(\mu)|

\displaystyle<\infty

(22)

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\mu g_{\lambda}(\mu)

\displaystyle=1

(23)

for all
0\leq\mu\leq 1
is called an admissible filter function, if there is a constant
\alpha_{0}>0
such that for all
0<\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\mu\leq 1}\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}|1-\mu g_{\lambda}(\mu)|\mu^{\alpha}\lambda^{-\alpha}<\infty\text{.}

(24)

Let us now replace the term
\frac{\mu_{i}}{\lambda+\mu_{i}}
in (
20
) by the more general
\mu_{i}g_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})
, that is

\displaystyle f_{P,\lambda,g}:=\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}g_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})\left\langle f_{L,P}^{*}\,{,}\,[e_{i}]_{\sim}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(P_{X})}e_{i}\text{.}

(25)

This converges in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(P_{X})
if
f_{L,P}^{*}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(P_{X})
, as
\mu_{i}g_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})
is bounded by (
22
).
For example, to describe the Landweber iteration, that is a form of gradient descent with early stopping, we can choose

\displaystyle g_{t}(\mu):=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}(1-\mu)^{i}

(26)

by using
\lambda:=t^{-1}
with
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
For details on the Landweber iteration and for further examples of various algorithms arising from different choices of
g_{\lambda}
, see
[
6
]
.

We can estimate the
“approximation error”
, in our case this is
f_{P,\lambda,g}-f_{L,P}^{*}
measured in interpolation space norms. This can be viewed as the distance of the optimal Bayes decision function
f_{L,P}^{*}
to the result of the regularised empirical risk minimisation
f_{P,\lambda,g}
in the case of full knowledge of the distribution
P
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X
be a measurable space,
P
a measure on
X\times[-1,1]
and
k
a measurable kernel on
X
with RKHS
H
that is compactly embedded into
\mathrm{L}^{2}(P_{X})
, where the eigenvalues of
T_{k}
are bounded by
1
.
Let
0<\beta<1
,
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
. If
f_{L,P}^{*}\in[\mathrm{L}^{2}(P_{X}),[H]_{\sim}]_{\beta,r}
, the following holds.

(i)

There is a constant
C_{1}
independent of
\lambda
and
g_{\lambda}
such that

\displaystyle\|[f_{P,\lambda,g}]_{\sim}-f_{L,P}^{*}\|_{[\mathrm{L}^{2}(X),[H]_{\sim}]_{\theta,r}}\leq C_{1}\sup_{i\in I}\left(\big|1-\mu_{i}g_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})\big|\mu_{i}^{\frac{\beta-\theta}{2}}\right)\|f_{L,P}^{*}\|_{[\mathrm{L}^{2}(X),[H]_{\sim}]_{\beta,r}}\text{.}

(27)

(ii)

There is a constant
C_{2}
independent of
\lambda
and
g_{\lambda}
such that

\displaystyle\|[f_{P,\lambda,g}]_{\sim}\|_{[\mathrm{L}^{2}(X),[H]_{\sim}]_{\theta,r}}\leq C_{2}\sup_{i\in I}\left(\big|\mu_{i}g_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})\big|\mu_{i}^{\frac{\beta-\theta}{2}}\right)\|f_{L,P}^{*}\|_{[\mathrm{L}^{2}(X),[H]_{\sim}]_{\beta,r}}\text{.}

(28)

Suppose that in addition to the requirements of Proposition
4.2
, we know that the embedding
[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\nu),[H]_{\sim}]_{\theta,r}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)
is continuous with operator norm
\kappa
, that is

\displaystyle\|[f_{P,\lambda,g}]_{\sim}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)}\leq\kappa\|[f_{P,\lambda,g}]_{\sim}\|_{[\mathrm{L}^{2}(X),[H]_{\sim}]_{\theta,r}}

(29)

which yields in combination with (
28
) that

\displaystyle\|[f_{P,\lambda,g}]_{\sim}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)}\leq C_{3}\sup_{i\in I}\left(\big|\mu_{i}g_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})\big|\mu_{i}^{\frac{\beta-\theta}{2}}\right)\|f_{L,P}^{*}\|_{[\mathrm{L}^{2}(X),[H]_{\sim}]_{\beta,r}}\text{.}

(30)

Such estimates are play an important role in the statistical analysis of kernel-based learning algorithms, see e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 7.23]
.
Note that the required existence of the embedding to
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)
may be checked via Theorem
3.3
.

Remark 4.3
.

We elaborate Proposition
4.2
for two concrete regularisation settings. To this end, we assume that
\beta>\theta
, and hence
\gamma:=\frac{\beta-\theta}{2}>0
.

(i)

Tikhonov regularisation.
Here we have
g_{\lambda}(\mu)=\frac{1}{\lambda+\mu}
. For the supremum in Proposition
4.2
, we find that

Liftings

Let us introduce a measure-theoretic technique that will play a role in the subsequent proofs.
To this end, we consider the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(X)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu),\quad f\mapsto[f]_{\sim}

(62)

which assigns to each

This mapping is surjective by definition of
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\nu)
, hence it possesses a, not necessarily unique, right inverse.
However, this reasoning does not yield further properties of such a right inverse. Nonetheless, by means of lifting theory, one can show that a bounded linear right inverse exists.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\nu
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on the measurable space
(X,\mathcal{A})

\mathcal{A}

\nu
-complete.

(63)

that satisfies

and

\displaystyle[\phi([f]_{\sim})]_{\sim}=[f]_{\sim}

(64)

for all

.

The proof is based on lifting theory as developed in
[
8
]
. For a derivation of the specific result, see
[
18

.

So in other words, for each equivalence class in

Appendix: Interpolation spaces

In this appendix, we collect some auxiliary results on interpolation spaces, which are probably known to experts.

We begin by proving that isometric isomorphisms can be carried over from Banach spaces
E_{2}\hookrightarrow E_{1}
to the interpolation spaces
[E_{1},E_{2}]_{\theta,r}
.
For a map
f:A\rightarrow B
and
A^{\prime}\subset A
,
B^{\prime}\subset B
, we use the notation
f|_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B^{\prime}}
for the map
A^{\prime}\rightarrow B^{\prime}
given by
x\mapsto f(x)
in case it is well defined.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
E_{2}\hookrightarrow E_{1}
and
F_{2}\hookrightarrow F_{1}
be Banach spaces. Suppose there is a bounded linear operator
\phi:E_{1}\rightarrow F_{1}
such that
\phi|_{E_{1}\rightarrow F_{1}}
and
\phi|_{E_{2}\rightarrow F_{2}}
are isometric isomorphisms. Then for
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
, the following map is an isometric isomorphism:

\displaystyle\phi|_{[E_{1},E_{2}]_{\theta,r}\rightarrow[F_{1},F_{2}]_{\theta,r}}

(114)

Proof.

Let us begin by showing that
\phi|_{[E_{1},E_{2}]_{\theta,r}\rightarrow[F_{1},F_{2}]_{\theta,r}}
is an isometry. To this end note that for all
x\in E_{1}
and
t>0
we have

\displaystyle K(x,t,E_{1},E_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in E_{2}}\left(\|x-y\|_{E_{1}}+t\|y\|_{E_{2}}\right)

(115)

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in E_{2}}\left(\|\phi(x-y)\|_{F_{1}}+t\|\phi(y)\|_{F_{2}}\right)

(116)

\displaystyle=\inf_{z\in F_{2}}\left(\|\phi(x)-z\|_{F_{1}}+t\|z\|_{F_{2}}\right)

(117)

\displaystyle=K(\phi(x),t,F_{1},F_{2})\text{.}

(118)

This shows that
\|\phi(x)\|_{[F_{1},F_{2}]_{\theta,r}}=\|x\|_{[E_{1},E_{2}]_{\theta,r}}
, that is
\phi|_{[E_{1},E_{2}]_{\theta,r}\rightarrow[F_{1},F_{2}]_{\theta,r}}
is isometric.
Finally, applying the same argument to
\phi^{-1}_{E_{i}\rightarrow F_{i}}
shows that
\phi^{-1}|_{[F_{1},F_{2}]_{\theta,r}\rightarrow[E_{1},E_{2}]_{\theta,r}}
is also isometric. Obviously, it is the inverse to
\phi|_{[E_{1},E_{2}]_{\theta,r}\rightarrow[F_{1},F_{2}]_{\theta,r}}
.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and let
H_{1}\subset H
be a closed subspace.
Suppose the Hilbert space
H_{2}
satisfies
H_{2}\hookrightarrow H_{1}
. Then for
0<\theta<1
and
1\leq r\leq\infty
, it holds that

\displaystyle[H,H_{2}]_{\theta,r}=[H_{1},H_{2}]_{\theta,r}

(119)

and the norms of both spaces are equal.

Proof.

Our first goal is to show

\displaystyle[H,H_{2}]_{\theta,r}\cup[H_{1},H_{2}]_{\theta,r}\subset H_{1}

(120)

To this end, we first note that
[H_{1},H_{2}]_{\theta,r}\subset H_{1}
by Lemma
A.2
.

For the proof of
[H,H_{2}]_{\theta,r}\subset H_{1}
we pick an
x\in[H,H_{2}]_{\theta,r}

by Lemma

, we consider the unique decomposition

with
x_{1}\in H_{1}
and
x_{0}\in H_{1}^{\perp}
.
It suffices to show that
x_{0}=0
. To this end, we assume the converse, that is
\|x_{0}\|_{H}>0
. Then for
t>0
, it follows that

\displaystyle K(x,t,H,H_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in H_{2}}\left(\|x-y\|_{H}+t\|y\|_{H_{2}}\right)

(121)

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in H_{2}}\left(\|x_{1}-y+x_{0}\|_{H}+t\|y\|_{H_{2}}\right)

(122)

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in H_{2}}\left(\sqrt{\|x_{1}-y\|_{H}^{2}+\|x_{0}\|_{H}^{2}}+t\|y\|_{H_{2}}\right)

(123)

\displaystyle\geq\|x_{0}\|_{H}\text{.}

(124)

, we find that

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\theta r-1}K^{r}(x,t,H,H_{2})\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}

(125)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\theta r-1}\|x_{0}\|_{H}^{r}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}

(126)

\displaystyle=\infty\text{.}

(127)

\displaystyle\|x\|_{[H,H_{2}]_{\theta,\infty}}=\sup_{t>0}\left(t^{-\theta}K(x,t,H,H_{2})\right)\geq\sup_{t>0}\left(t^{-\theta}\|x_{0}\|_{H}\right)=\infty\text{.}

(128)

and hence we find

\displaystyle K(x,t,H,H_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in H_{2}}\left(\|x-y\|_{H}+t\|y\|_{H_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in H_{2}}\left(\|x-y\|_{H_{1}}+t\|y\|_{H_{2}}\right)=K(x,t,H_{1},H_{2})\text{.}

(130)

The equality of the interpolation spaces and their norms hence follows.

Appendix: Direct sums of Banach spaces

A closer look at Definition

reveals that the
\ell^{(\mu,\theta,r)}
-norms are built up from two nested norms: The inner part (
10
) of the
(\mu,\theta,r)
-norm originates from an

-norm taken over the restricted indices in

, while the outer sum in (
11
) corresponds to the
\ell^{r}
-norm taken over the values given by the inner part.

To deal with these norms, let us introduce such nested norms in a more general setting.
For notational convenience, let us assume that
I=\{1,...,n\}
or
I=\mathbb{N}
is an index set.

Definition B.1
.

Let
I
be an index set and suppose that
E_{i}
are Banach spaces for all
i\in I
.
Then for
1\leq p\leq\infty
we define the
p
-direct sum of the
E_{i}
as

\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}:=\left\{x=(x_{i})_{i\in I}\,\middle|\,x_{i}\in E_{i}\text{ and }\|x\|_{p}<\infty\right\}\text{,}

(131)

where the norm is defined by
\|x\|_{p}:=\|(\|x_{i}\|_{E_{i}})_{i\in I}\|_{\ell^{p}}
.
We define the
0
-direct sum as

\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(0)}E_{i}:=\left\{x=(x_{i})_{i\in I}\,\middle|\,x_{i}\in E_{i}\text{ and }\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\|x_{i}\|_{E_{i}}=0\right\}

(132)

where the limit of a finite sequence is defined as
0
. We equip this space with the norm
\|x\|_{0}:=\|x\|_{\infty}
.

It is well known that such
p
-direct sums of Banach spaces
E_{i}
are again Banach spaces, see e.g.
[
1
, Section 16.11]
.

Remark B.2
.

Let
E_{i}
be Banach spaces for indexes
i\in I
, let
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
C>0
and suppose that
x_{i}^{\prime}\in E_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\in I
.
Then the following holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\text{ converges for all }x\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}\;

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;\sum_{i\in I}|x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})|\text{ converges for all }x\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}

(133)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\leq C\|x\|_{p}\text{ for all }x\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}\;

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;\sum_{i\in I}|x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})|\leq C\|x\|_{p}\text{ for all }x\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}

(134)

\displaystyle\sup_{\|x\|_{p}\leq 1}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\;

\displaystyle=\;\sup_{\|x\|_{p}\leq 1}\sum_{i\in I}|x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})|

(135)

Thereby,
“
\Leftarrow
”
and
“
\leq
”
follow directly. To see
“
\Rightarrow
”
and
“
\geq
”
, we choose
\tilde{x}_{i}:=x_{i}
if
x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\geq 0
and
\tilde{x}_{i}:=-x_{i}
otherwise. Then we find that
x_{i}^{\prime}(\tilde{x}_{i})=|x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})|
, and we still have
\|\tilde{x}\|_{p}=\|x\|_{p}
. Hence using
\tilde{x}
instead of
x
on the left hand side gives the right hand side.

In addition, note that due to

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\leq\left|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\right|\leq\sum_{i\in I}|x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})|

(136)

we might replace one of the sums by
\left|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\right|
and still obtain the equivalences.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
I
be an at most countable index set and suppose that
E_{i}
are Banach spaces for all
i\in I
.
Then the mapping

\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p^{\prime})}E_{i}^{\prime}\rightarrow\left({\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}\right)^{\prime},\quad x^{\prime}\mapsto\left(x\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\right)

(137)

is an

(i)

isometric isomorphism if
p=0
and
p^{\prime}=1
.

(ii)

isometric isomorphism if
1\leq p<\infty
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
.

(iii)

isometry if
p=\infty
and
p^{\prime}=1
.

Proof.

A proof of (i) and (ii) can be found in
[
1
, Theorem 16.49 and the following remark]
. Let us prove (iii) here.
We begin by showing that the operator is well defined in the sense that the sum converges. In fact, we even have absolute convergence, as for
x^{\prime}\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(1)}E_{i}^{\prime}
and
x\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(\infty)}E_{i}
, we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}|x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})|\leq\sum_{i\in I}\|x_{i}^{\prime}\|_{E_{i}^{\prime}}\|x_{i}\|_{E_{i}}\leq\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}\|x\|_{\infty}<\infty\text{.}

(138)

To show that (
137
) is an isometry, let
x^{\prime}\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(1)}E_{i}^{\prime}
.
We have to show that the operator norm of

\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(\infty)}E_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})

(139)

agrees with
\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}
.
To this end, note that

\displaystyle\left\|x\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\right\|=\sup_{\|x\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\left|\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|x_{i}\|_{E_{i}}\leq 1\\
\text{for all }i\in I\end{subarray}}\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})

(140)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\sup_{\|x_{i}\|_{E_{i}}\leq 1}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})

(141)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\|x_{i}^{\prime}\|

(142)

\displaystyle=\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}\text{.}

(143)

Concerning the absolute values, we used Remark
B.2
.
This shows that the mapping in question is in fact an isometry.
∎

For
p
and
p^{\prime}
as in Lemma
B.3
, we introduce for
x\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}
and
x^{\prime}\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p^{\prime})}E_{i}^{\prime}
the dual pairing

\displaystyle\left\langle x^{\prime}\,{,}\,x\right\rangle:=\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\text{.}

(144)

Note that in situations (i) and (ii), Lemma
B.3
allows us to describe the entire dual space.
However, by part (iii) we can at least understand part of the dual space in a similar fashion as we can understand part of
(\ell^{\infty}(I))^{\prime}
by the embedding
\iota:\ell^{1}(I)\rightarrow\ell^{1}(I)^{\prime\prime}\simeq(\ell^{\infty}(I))^{\prime}
.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
I
be an at most countable index set and suppose that
E_{i}
are Banach spaces for all
i\in I
. Let
x^{\prime}_{i}\in E_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\in I
and suppose that
1\leq p,p^{\prime}\leq\infty
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

The sum

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})

(145)

converges for all sequences
x\in\bigoplus_{i\in I}^{(p)}E_{i}
.

(ii)

The sum (
145
) converges absolutely for all sequences
x\in\bigoplus_{i\in I}^{(p)}E_{i}
.

(iii)

We have
x^{\prime}\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p^{\prime})}E_{i}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For finite
I
, all norms are finite sums, hence the assertion follows. So let us assume
I=\mathbb{N}
without loss of generality.

“(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)”
follows by Remark
B.2
.

“(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i)”
. Let
x^{\prime}\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p^{\prime})}E_{i}^{\prime}
. Then an application of Lemma
B.3
yields that the functional

\displaystyle\gamma:{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})

(146)

is well-defined with
\|\gamma\|=\|x^{\prime}\|_{p^{\prime}}
.
In particular, all sums in (
145
) converge.

“(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)”
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let us consider the bounded operator

\displaystyle S_{n}:{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\text{.}

(147)

Notice that for all
x\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|S_{n}(x)|=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\right|\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})|<\infty\text{,}

(148)

where we used the absolute convergence of (
145
).
Hence the uniform boundedness principle
[
13
, Theorem 4.52]
yields that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|S_{n}\|<\infty
.

Our next goal is to show

\displaystyle\|(x^{\prime}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\leq n})_{i\in I}\|_{p^{\prime}}=\|S_{n}\|\text{.}

(149)

Case
1\leq p<\infty
.
By Lemma
B.3
, we can understand
S_{n}\in\left(\bigoplus_{i\in I}^{(p)}E_{i}\right)^{\prime}
as the functional associated to the sequence
(x_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\leq n})_{i\in I}
. Hence
\|S_{n}\|=\|(x^{\prime}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\leq n})_{i\in I}\|_{p^{\prime}}
as desired.

Case
p=\infty
. Now we have
S_{n}\in\left(\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}^{(\infty)}E_{i}\right)^{\prime}
. As Lemma
B.3
does not directly cover this dual space, we argue differently here.
To this end, note that for the operator norm we find the following:

\displaystyle\|S_{n}\|=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}^{(\infty)}E_{i}\\
x\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{|S_{n}x|}{\|x\|_{\infty}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}^{(\infty)}E_{i}\\
x\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{|S_{n}((x_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\leq n})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}|}{\|x\|_{\infty}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\bigoplus_{i\leq n}^{(0)}E_{i}\\
x\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{|S_{n}x|}{\|x\|_{\infty}}\text{.}

(150)

This means that the operator norm of
S_{n}
equals the operator norm of
S_{n}
restricted to
{\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}^{(0)}E_{i}
.
This restricted operator is as an element of
({\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}^{(0)}E_{i})^{\prime}\simeq{\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}^{(1)}E_{i}^{\prime}
, where we are using the isometric isomorphism from Lemma
B.3
.
This isometric isomorphism assigns
S_{n}|_{\bigoplus_{i\leq n}^{(0)}E_{i}}
to the sequence
(x^{\prime}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\leq n})_{i\in I}\in\bigoplus_{i\leq n}^{(1)}E_{i}^{\prime}
.
We hence find the equality of norms

\displaystyle\|S_{n}\|=\left\|S_{n}|_{\bigoplus_{i\leq n}^{(0)}E_{i}}\right\|=\left\|(x^{\prime}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\leq n})_{i\in I}\right\|_{1}\text{.}

(151)

which finishes the second case.

Finally, we can put together the previous findings to prove that

\displaystyle\|x^{\prime}\|_{p^{\prime}}=\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\|x^{\prime}_{i}\|^{p^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\sum_{i\leq n}\|x^{\prime}_{i}\|^{p^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}

(152)

\displaystyle=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|(x^{\prime}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\leq n})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{p^{\prime}}

(153)

\displaystyle=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|S_{n}\|<\infty

(154)

as desired.
∎

Lemma
B.4
does not provide concrete bounds on (
145
) or on
\|x^{\prime}\|_{p^{\prime}}
. However, in combination with the previous results, we can derive a quantitative version directly from Lemma
B.4
.

Proposition B.5
.

Let
I
be an at most countable index set and suppose that
E_{i}
are Banach spaces for all
i\in I
. Let
x^{\prime}_{i}\in E_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\in I
and suppose that
1\leq p,p^{\prime}\leq\infty
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. Let
C\geq 0
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

For all sequences
x\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p)}E_{i}
, the sum
\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})
converges and it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\in I}x^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\right|\leq C\|x\|_{p}\text{.}

(155)

(ii)

It holds that
x^{\prime}\in{\bigoplus_{i\in I}}^{(p^{\prime})}E_{i}^{\prime}
with norm
\|x^{\prime}\|_{p^{\prime}}\leq C
.

Proof.

At first, let
x^{\prime}\in\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}^{(p^{\prime})}E_{i}^{\prime}
. By the isometric embeddings from Lemma
B.3
, the functional

\displaystyle\gamma_{x^{\prime}}:{\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}^{(p)}E_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})

(156)

satisfies
\|\gamma_{x^{\prime}}\|=\|x^{\prime}\|_{p^{\prime}}
.

“(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)”
. An application of Lemma
B.4
(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii) shows that
x^{\prime}\in\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}^{(p^{\prime})}E_{i}^{\prime}
. Hence using Remark
B.2
we find that

\displaystyle\|x^{\prime}\|_{p^{\prime}}=\|\gamma_{x^{\prime}}\|=\sup_{\|x\|_{p}\leq 1}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})=\sup_{\|x\|_{p}\leq 1}\left|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\right|\leq C\text{.}

(157)

“(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)”
. The convergence of the sum follows by Lemma
B.4
.
For
x=0
, the desired estimate is clear. For
x\neq 0
, we observe that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\|x\|_{p}}\right|=\left|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}\left(\frac{x_{i}}{\|x\|_{p}}\right)\right|\leq\sup_{\|\tilde{x}\|_{p}\leq 1}\left|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}^{\prime}(\tilde{x}_{i})\right|=\|\gamma_{x^{\prime}}\|=\|x^{\prime}\|_{p^{\prime}}\leq C

(158)

and note that the assertion follows by treating absolute values as explained in Remark
B.2
.
∎
