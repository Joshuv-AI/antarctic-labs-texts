Maternal Nutritional Practices and Associated Factors Among Breastfeeding Mothers in Jimma Town, Ethiopia: A Community‐Based Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Buzuneh Tasfa Marine, Yematawork Alemu Haile, Mihiret Genene Zewde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdequate maternal nutrition during lactation is essential for maintaining maternal health and supporting optimal infant growth. Evidence from Ethiopia indicates that breastfeeding women are vulnerable to poor dietary intake due to socioeconomic constraints, limited nutrition knowledge, and cultural food practices. This study aimed to assess maternal nutritional practices and associated factors among breastfeeding mothers in Jimma Town, Southwest Ethiopia.MethodsA community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from December 2023 to June 2024 among breastfeeding mothers. Data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews using a structured questionnaire, including socio‐demographic characteristics, household economic status, dietary practices, nutrition counseling, and minimum dietary diversity for women (MDD‐W). Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with good maternal nutritional practice. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.ResultsGood maternal nutritional practice was associated with higher maternal education (secondary: AOR = 5.4, 95% CI: 1.69–17.56; college and above: AOR = 11.4, 95% CI: 3.13–41.73), being married (AOR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.14–3.25), maternal age 26–35 years (AOR = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.29–7.95), higher household income ( > 6000 ETB; AOR = 3.58, 95% CI: 1.65–7.76), and husbands employed in private business (AOR = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.10–4.35). Positive dietary behaviors including consuming ≥ 3 meals per day (AOR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.33–2.97), dietary diversity ≥ 5 food groups (AOR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.16–3.35), absence of food taboos (AOR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.11–2.80), and consuming additional meals during lactation (AOR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.28–0.87) were also associated with better nutritional practices. Conversely, large family size ( ≥ 4 members), lack of nutrition counseling, and daily labor occupation were negatively associated.ConclusionThis study identified several key determinants of nutritional practices among breastfeeding mothers, including educational level, maternal occupation, maternal age, marital status, household monthly income, family size, husband's occupation, average meal frequency per day, changes in food intake during lactation, food taboos, dietary diversity score, and receipt of nutrition counseling. Strengthening nutrition education and counseling, improving household economic empowerment, and addressing cultural food taboos are essential to improve maternal dietary practices during lactation.Keywords: breastfeeding women, determinants, dietary diversity, maternal nutritional practice

Introduction

Background of the Study

Nutrition encompasses the processes involved in the intake and utilization of food by living organisms, including ingestion, digestion, absorption, transport, and metabolism of nutrients. It is a fundamental pillar of human life, health, and development throughout the life course [1]. Adequate nutrition is essential not only for survival but also for physical growth, cognitive development, productivity, and overall well‐being. However, nutritional requirements vary according to age, sex, and physiological conditions such as pregnancy and lactation.

The nutritional and health status of mothers remains a major public health concern globally. Women's multiple biological and social roles including pregnancy, breastfeeding, food preparation, and caregiving often increase their vulnerability to nutritional deficiencies. This problem is particularly pronounced in low and middle‐income countries, where cultural norms and gender‐based inequities frequently compel women to prioritize household food needs over their own nutritional requirements [2]. As a result, mothers are disproportionately affected by malnutrition due to the combined effects of biological demands and social disadvantage [3].

Adequate nutrition during lactation is critical for the health of both mothers and their infants. Breastfeeding women have increased energy and nutrient requirements to support breast milk production, typically requiring an additional 300–500 kilocalories per day depending on activity level [3]. A nutritionally adequate diet rich in proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, and minerals is essential to maintain maternal health and ensure optimal milk quantity and quality. Insufficient maternal intake during this period can compromise breast milk production and nutrient content, potentially affecting infant growth and development [4].

Poor maternal nutrition has well‐documented adverse health consequences. For mothers, it increases the risk of undernutrition, anemia, micronutrient deficiencies, impaired immunity, fatigue, and reduced work capacity [3, 4]. For infants, maternal undernutrition during lactation is associated with insadequate breast milk intake, increased susceptibility to infections, growth faltering, stunting, underweight, and delayed cognitive development [5, 6]. Furthermore, children born to malnourished mothers face elevated long‐term risks of poor linear growth, impaired neurodevelopment, reduced educational attainment, and increased vulnerability to chronic diseases later in life [6].

Maternal dietary practices during lactation are strongly influenced by socio‐economic status, cultural beliefs, food taboos, access to diverse foods, household income, education, and nutrition knowledge [3, 7, 8, 9]. In many settings, traditional beliefs restrict the consumption of nutrient‐rich foods during lactation, while poverty and food insecurity limit access to adequate diets. Although these factors have been broadly described in the literature, there is limited context‐specific evidence on how socio‐economic, cultural, and dietary factors interact to shape maternal nutritional practices among breastfeeding women in urban settings of Southwest Ethiopia, including Jimma Town.

Globally, an estimated 2 billion people suffer from micronutrient deficiencies, and approximately 793 million experience chronic energy deficiency [4]. Among women of reproductive age (15–49 years), anemia and vitamin A deficiency remain highly prevalent, particularly in low‐income regions [10, 11]. In developing countries, women often spend a substantial proportion of their reproductive years pregnant or breastfeeding, which increases their risk of nutritional depletion, especially when pregnancies are closely spaced [12]. Poor maternal nutritional status during these periods can reduce lactation performance and negatively affect infant feeding outcomes.

In Ethiopia, recurrent food insecurity and monotonous diets place breastfeeding women at high nutritional risk. Approximately five million people experience food shortages annually, and dietary intake patterns remain suboptimal, particularly among women [4]. Breastfeeding mothers are recognized as a nutritionally vulnerable population, with increased risks of maternal morbidity and adverse child health outcomes [11]. This study aims to assess maternal nutritional practices and identify their determinants among breastfeeding mothers in Jimma Town, Southwest Ethiopia.

Methodology

Description of the Study Area

The study was conducted in Jimma Town, located in Oromia Regional State, Southwest Ethiopia, about 352 km from Addis Ababa. The town has 17 kebeles and an estimated population of 195,228, with approximately 40,450 households. It is an important commercial center known for coffee production. Jimma Town has four government health centers providing primary healthcare services. The area falls within the “Weyna Dega” agro‐ecological zone and experiences moderate climatic conditions.

Study Design

A community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted to assess the nutritional practices of breastfeeding mothers and their associated factors.

Target Population

The target population consisted of breastfeeding mother residing in Jimma Town who had a child aged under 2 years and had lived in the town for at least 6 months. Both mothers practicing exclusive breastfeeding and those practicing mixed feeding (breastmilk with complementary foods) were included.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were breastfeeding mothers with children under 2 years of age, residents of Jimma Town for at least 6 months, and mothers who were practicing breastfeeding, either exclusively or alongside complementary feeding. The exclusion criteria included mothers who were severely ill or unable to respond during data collection and those who were not breastfeeding at the time of the survey.

Sampling and Sample Size Determination

Sampling Size

The sample size was calculated to estimate the prevalence of good maternal nutrition practices and to assess associated factors among breastfeeding mothers. For the prevalence estimate, a single population proportion formula was used with a 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error, and a prevalence of 33.9% from a previous study [13], resulting in 345 participants. After adding a 10% non‐response rate, the sample became 380. To ensure adequate power for identifying significant determinants, the sample size for associated factors was calculated using the double population proportion formula, yielding a larger sample of 518, which was used as the final sample. Thus, 518 breastfeeding mothers with children under 2 years were included in the study.

Sampling Method

A multi‐stage sampling method was employed to select study participants. First, kebeles (the smallest administrative units) in Jimma Town were identified, and the number of households with breastfeeding mothers in each kebele was obtained from local health extension records, which served as the sampling frame. Next, households were proportionally allocated across kebeles, and simple random sampling was used to select households from each kebele. Within each selected household, one eligible breastfeeding mother with a child under 2 years was interviewed.

Variables of the Study

Dependent Variable

Maternal nutritional practice was the dependent variable of the study and was categorized as good or poor nutritional practices.

Independent Variables

The independent variables of the study included socio‐demographic factors, maternal health and obstetric factors, maternal nutrition‐related factors during lactation, and maternal dietary diversity. Socio‐demographic factors comprised maternal age, marital status, maternal and husband's education levels, maternal and husband's occupation, family size, religion, and monthly household income in Ethiopian Birr (ETB). Maternal health, obstetric, pregnancy, and delivery‐related factors included the place of delivery of the current child, birth interval of the current child (time between the previous birth and the index child), number of antenatal care (ANC) visits, number of pregnancies, and breastfeeding practices. Maternal nutrition‐related factors during lactation encompassed receipt of nutrition information or counseling, source of nutrition information, frequency of breastfeeding per day, family encouragement to consume additional meals, additional meal consumption during lactation, number of meals consumed per day, and changes in food frequency or quantity during lactation. Maternal dietary diversity was assessed using the Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women (MDD‐W) indicator based on consumption of ten food groups within the previous 24 h: grains, white roots and tubers, and plantains, pulses (beans, peas, lentils), nuts and seeds, dairy products, meat, poultry, and fish, eggs, dark green leafy vegetables, vitamin A rich fruits and vegetables, other vegetables, and other fruits. Women who consumed foods from five or more food groups were classified as having adequate dietary diversity, while those consuming fewer than five food groups were considered dietary inadequate.

Measurement of Variables

Maternal nutritional practice (dependent variable) was assessed using seven structured questions designed to evaluate dietary behaviors during the lactation period. The questions addressed key aspects of maternal nutrition, including consumption of additional carbohydrates between meals, factors interfering with daily food intake, avoidance of specific foods or dietary patterns, number of meals consumed per day, snacking practices, monitoring of body weight during lactation, and cravings for foods not typically consumed. Respondents answered “yes” or “no” to each question, indicating whether the statements reflected their dietary practices, and were encouraged to elaborate on their responses through open‐ended questions to provide a deeper insight into their nutritional habits. To evaluate the level of nutrition practices among the respondents, scores were computed based on their answers, with each correct response related to optimal maternal nutrition receiving one point and incorrect responses receiving zero. The total score for nutritional practices ranged from 0 to a maximum of 7 points, which was then converted to a percentage out of 100. The mean score was calculated for all respondents, classifying those who scored above the mean as having good nutritional practices, while those who scored below the mean were considered to have poor nutritional practices.

Responses were recorded as “Yes” or “No,” and participants were encouraged to provide additional explanations for deeper insight into their practices. For scoring, responses indicative of optimal maternal nutrition were assigned a value of 1, while suboptimal responses were assigned 0. The total score ranged from 0 to 7 and was converted into a percentage. The mean score of all participants was calculated, and respondents scoring above the mean were classified as having good maternal nutritional practices, while those scoring below the mean were classified as having poor maternal nutritional practices.

Data Collection Tools and Procedures

Data for this study were collected using a structured, interviewer‐administered questionnaire adapted from previously published literature on maternal nutrition and dietary practices to align with the study objectives [14, 15]. The tool included components related to nutritional practices, dietary assessment, household socio‐economic status, maternal education, and environmental factors influencing maternal nutrition. Face‐to‐face interviews were conducted to collect the data. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into local languages (Afan Oromo and Amharic), and then back‐translated into English to ensure consistency. Data were collected from each randomly selected mother using a pretested structured questionnaire. Six trained data collectors and two supervisors conducted the interviews. Training was provided on the study objectives, ethical considerations, interviewing techniques, and data quality assurance procedures.

Data Processing and Analysis

Data were coded and entered into EpiData version 4.6 and analyzed using Stata version 17. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were computed for all variables and presented in tables and figures. Binary logistic regression was first conducted to examine the association between each independent variable and maternal nutritional practice. Variables with a p < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounders. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p‐values were reported to determine the strength and significance of associations. Variables with p < 0.05 in the multivariable model were considered statistically significant. To ensure the robustness of the regression model, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). The goodness of fit of the final model was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, confirming that the model adequately fitted the data.

Operational Definitions

Maternal nutritional practices: Refers to the dietary behaviors of breastfeeding mothers, including meal frequency, dietary diversity, consumption of additional meals during lactation, and avoidance of harmful food taboos. In this study, maternal nutritional practice was assessed using a structured questionnaire of seven items. Responses were scored as 1 for optimal practice and 0 for suboptimal practice. The total score ranged from 0 to 7 and was converted to a percentage. Mothers scoring above the mean were classified as having good nutritional practices, while those scoring below the mean were classified as having poor nutritional practices.

Good maternal nutritional practice: Mothers whose composite score of nutritional practices was above the mean of the study population, reflecting adherence to recommended dietary behaviors during lactation.

Poor maternal nutritional practice: Mothers whose composite score of nutritional practices was below the mean, reflecting suboptimal dietary behaviors during lactation

Meal frequency: The number of times a mother consumes meals per day. Adequate meal frequency is defined as consuming three or more meals per day during lactation.

Dietary diversity: The variety of food groups consumed by the mother over a 24‐h period. Dietary diversity was measured using the Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women (MDD‐W), which includes 10 food groups. Adequate dietary diversity was defined as consuming ≥ 5 food groups in the previous 24 h.

Result

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics of the Study Participants

Out of the 518 sampled breastfeeding women, 512 participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 99%. The mean (± SD) age of the respondents was 27.5 (± 3.5) years. The largest proportion of mothers (44.1%) were aged 20–29 years, while only 7.8% were in the 15–20‐year age group Regarding educational status, about one‐quarter of the mothers (25.6%) had no formal education, whereas the largest proportion (34.2%) had completed primary education. Nearly one‐fifth (19.3%) had secondary education, and 20.9% had attained a college diploma or higher. In terms of occupation, the majority of mothers were housewives (62.1%), while a smaller proportion were government employees (15%) or engaged in private business (10.7%). Daily laborers constituted the least common occupational group among mothers (9.8%). Most husbands were employed in government or NGO sectors (22.5%) or worked as farmers (21.9%), whereas 15.2% reported having no employment. Most respondents were married (62.1%), followed by divorced mothers (23.0%). Household economic status showed that over half of the participants (51.6%) reported a monthly household income between 3,000 and 6,000 Ethiopian Birr (ETB), Regarding family size, the majority of households (52.9%) had fewer than four members, while only 9.2% had more than six members (Table 1).

Maternal Dietary Diversity Factors Food Group Consumption Lactation

The results presented in Table 4 provide a comprehensive overview of maternal dietary diversity among breastfeeding mothers in Jimma Town. The data reveals that a significant portion of mothers (43.9%) consume starchy foods like rice, bread, and pasta several times a week, indicating a reliance on staple carbohydrates, with 25.4% consuming these daily. However, the intake of dairy products is notably low, with only 8.8% consuming them daily and 55.9% sometimes, which may limit their calcium and protein intake. Leafy green vegetables are consumed daily by just 7.4%, with 49.8% consuming them sometimes, suggesting a deficiency in vitamin A‐rich foods. Fried foods are consumed daily by 8.6% of mothers, while a significant number (46.1%) eat them sometimes, hinting at a preference for less nutritious options. The consumption of grains and tubers mirrors that of starchy foods, with 55.9% consuming them sometimes. Notably, 30.1% of mothers include meats, poultry, and fish daily, with 56.1% consuming them sometimes, reflecting better access to protein sources. Fruit intake is concerning, with only 7.2% consuming them daily and over half (53.3%) rarely consuming any, indicating a gap in essential vitamins and minerals. Egg consumption is also low, with 60.7% of mothers rarely including them in their diet. Furthermore, 51.2% reported consuming complementary foods, which is crucial for adequate nutrition during lactation.

The analysis of factors associated with maternal nutritional practices indicated several significant associations. Mothers with higher education levels were more likely to exhibit good nutritional practices, with secondary‐educated mothers being 5.4 times more likely (AOR = 5.4, 95% CI: 1.69–17.56) and college‐educated mothers 11.4 times more likely (AOR = 11.4, 95% CI: 3.13–41.73) compared to mothers with no formal education. Marital status was also associated with nutritional practices, as married mothers had higher odds of good practices (AOR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.14–3.25), whereas divorced or widowed mothers were less likely to exhibit good practices (AOR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.22–0.55). Maternal occupation showed a significant relationship, with mothers engaged in private business more likely to have good nutritional practices (AOR = 3.1, 95% CI: 1.81–5.32), while daily laborers had lower odds (AOR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.05–0.66). Similarly, mothers whose husbands were engaged in private business were more likely to have good nutritional practices (AOR = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.10–4.35). Age was also associated, with mothers aged 26–35 years showing higher odds of good practices (AOR = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.29–7.95) compared to younger mothers. Larger family size ( ≥ 4 members) was negatively associated with good practices (AOR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.41–0.94), while higher household income ( > 6000 ETB/month) was positively associated (AOR = 3.58, 95% CI: 1.65–7.76). Nutritional counseling was another important factor; mothers who did not receive counseling had lower odds of good nutritional practices (AOR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.08–0.25). Regarding dietary behaviors, consuming three or more meals per day (AOR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.33–2.97), having a dietary diversity score of five or more food groups (AOR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.16–3.35), and absence of food taboos (AOR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.11–2.80) were positively associated with good maternal nutrition, whereas not consuming additional meals during lactation was negatively associated (AOR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.28–0.87).

Discussion

Improving maternal nutrition practices during lactation is crucial for enhancing health outcomes for both mothers and their infants. This study focuses on the factors influencing maternal nutrition among breastfeeding women in Jimma Town, Southwest Ethiopia, utilizing a community‐based cross‐sectional approach. The findings reveal that 57.6% of respondents demonstrated good nutrition practices during lactation, a figure that is slightly higher than the 57% reported in a similar study conducted [13], where pregnant women exhibited good nutritional practices. This comparison raises important questions about the contextual factors that shape maternal nutrition behaviors in different regions, including cultural influences, access to resources, and educational levels. Effective maternal nutrition not only supports the health of mothers but also plays a vital role in the growth and development of infants, preventing malnutrition‐related complications.

Maternal education was significantly associated with good maternal nutritional practices. In this study, mothers with primary, secondary, and college‐level education or above had progressively higher adjusted odds of practicing good maternal nutrition compared with mothers who had no formal education. This finding suggests that educational attainment enhances women's knowledge, health awareness, decision‐making autonomy, and ability to access and effectively utilize nutrition‐related information. Similar associations have been reported in studies conducted in Southern Ethiopia [16, 17] and Pakistan [18], which also demonstrated a positive relationship between maternal educational status and improved nutrition practices. However, our findings contrast with reports from the Amhara region of Ethiopia [19, 20], where maternal education was not found to be a significant predictor of maternal nutritional practices. This discrepancy may be attributed to contextual differences between study settings, including variations in socio‐cultural norms, access to nutrition information, health service utilization, economic conditions, and study design. Educated mothers are generally better positioned to understand health messages, engage with healthcare services, and make informed dietary and childcare decisions. Education may also improve women's socioeconomic opportunities and bargaining power within households, further supporting positive nutritional behaviors. These findings underscore the importance of maternal education as a key social determinant of health. Strengthening access to female education, even at the primary level, could therefore serve as a sustainable strategy to improve maternal nutrition practices, particularly in low‐resource settings such as Jimma Town. Investing in women's education not only addresses immediate nutritional behaviors but also contributes to long‐term intergenerational health benefits, reinforcing the strong linkage between education and maternal and child health outcomes.

The findings regarding maternal occupation provide important insights into how employment status influences maternal nutrition practices. Mothers engaged in daily labor were significantly less likely to practice good maternal nutrition compared to housewives. This may be explained by the irregular and unstable income commonly associated with daily labor, which can limit household access to diverse and adequate foods. Income instability may increase food insecurity and constrain dietary choices, thereby negatively affecting maternal nutrition. Furthermore, the physically demanding nature of daily labor and long working hours may reduce the time and energy available for meal preparation, healthcare utilization, and self‐care during lactation. In contrast, mothers involved in private business had significantly higher odds of practicing good maternal nutrition. This may reflect relatively better financial autonomy, income control, and flexible working conditions, which can support improved dietary practices and access to health information. This finding is consistent with studies conducted among pregnant and breastfeeding women in other settings, which reported that formal or stable employment is associated with improved maternal nutritional status and better healthcare utilization [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. The similarity across studies may be explained by improved economic access and greater decision‐making autonomy among employed women.

The findings regarding marital status highlight the influence of family structure on maternal nutritional practices in Jimma Town. The analysis showed that married mothers were more likely to practice good maternal nutrition, whereas divorced or widowed mothers had significantly lower odds of good nutritional practices compared to single mothers. This suggests that marital stability may positively influence maternal dietary behaviors. Married mothers may benefit from shared economic resources, emotional support, and joint decision‐making within the household, which can facilitate improved access to food, healthcare services, and nutrition information. In addition, shared parenting responsibilities may reduce maternal workload and allow more time for self‐care and appropriate dietary practices. This finding is consistent with studies conducted among pregnant and breastfeeding women [25, 26, 27], where married women demonstrated better dietary diversity and maternal health practices compared to their unmarried counterparts, largely attributed to greater financial and social support. Conversely, divorced or widowed mothers may face economic hardship, reduced household income, and increased caregiving responsibilities, all of which can negatively affect their nutritional practices. Emotional distress associated with separation or bereavement may further limit attention to self‐care and dietary adequacy. Similar findings have been reported in studies among women of reproductive age in comparable contexts [17, 21, 22], where single‐headed households were more vulnerable to food insecurity and poor maternal nutrition outcomes.

The results of the study indicate a clear association between higher income levels and improved maternal nutrition. Families with greater financial resources are more capable of affording a diverse and nutritious diet, which is essential for the healthy growth and development of both mothers and their children. Adequate nutrition directly impacts physical health, cognitive function, and overall well‐being, highlighting the critical role of economic stability in promoting health outcomes. Additionally, increased income often correlates with better access to healthcare services, enabling families with monthly incomes exceeding 6000 ETB to afford medical care, vaccinations, and preventive health measures that are vital for maintaining maternal health. Moreover, higher income levels typically allow families to secure better living conditions, including adequate housing and sanitation. Improved living environments can significantly contribute to overall health and reduce the risk of illnesses that might compromise maternal nutrition. This finding is consistent with existing studies conducted in Southwest Ethiopia [17, 28], reinforcing the notion that economic factors play a crucial role in maternal health and nutrition.

However, the study also points out that low nutritional practices among participants may stem from several challenges, including relatively high family sizes, low monthly incomes, low educational attainment, and a lack of nutritional information [29]. These factors can create barriers to accessing and prioritizing nutritious food, leading to suboptimal health outcomes for mothers.

The study's findings on meal frequency underscore an important aspect of maternal nutrition during lactation. In this study, more than half of the respondents (54.5%) reported consuming three or more meals per day, indicating relatively positive dietary practices among breastfeeding mothers in Jimma Town. For comparison, a study conducted in Accra, Ghana, reported that only 37.7% of women consumed three or more meals daily during pregnancy, and even fewer (11.5%) did so before pregnancy [3, 17, 30]. While these populations and time points differ, the comparison highlights contextual differences in dietary practices among women of reproductive age. The higher meal frequency observed in Jimma Town may reflect better access to food, higher household income, or socio‐cultural practices that prioritize maternal nutrition during lactation. Local customs, food availability, and health education may also contribute to these practices. In contrast, the lower meal frequency reported in the Accra study may reflect economic constraints, food insecurity, or reduced emphasis on nutrition during pregnancy. These findings emphasize the need for context‐specific interventions that address maternal dietary practices according to population needs and stage of reproduction.

Dietary diversity emerged as a key determinant of maternal nutritional status. Mothers who consumed more than five food groups were nearly twice as likely to be well‐nourished compared with those whose intake included five or fewer groups. This emphasizes the value of consuming a wide range of foods to ensure adequate energy and nutrient intake. A varied diet supports immune competence, metabolic function, and overall health, while insufficient intake during lactation can lead to maternal depletion and increased vulnerability to undernutrition. Similar associations have been reported in studies conducted in the Jimma Zone [23, 31] and the Chiro district of eastern Ethiopia [27, 32]. Insufficient nutrient intake can hinder the body's immune function and metabolic activities; thus, a diverse diet enhances dietary quality and increases daily nutrient and energy intake. Breastfeeding women who do not receive adequate energy and nutrients risk maternal depletion, further worsening undernutrition among women.

The prevalence of food taboos among breastfeeding women in this study, reported at 45.5%, highlights significant cultural variations in dietary practices during lactation across different regions. This figure is notably lower than the 55% prevalence observed in a study conducted in India [33], suggesting that food taboos may be less entrenched or pervasive in the population studied here. Conversely, the prevalence is considerably higher than the 5.6% reported in Khartoum [23, 34], indicating a stark contrast in dietary restrictions among breastfeeding women in different cultural contexts. The differences in food taboo prevalence can be attributed to various factors, including cultural beliefs, traditions, and socioeconomic conditions. In some cultures, food taboos are deeply rooted in traditional practices and may be based on misconceptions about the effects of certain foods on maternal health or milk production. In contrast, the lower prevalence in this study may reflect a more progressive approach to nutrition among breastfeeding women, possibly influenced by increased access to nutritional education and healthcare resources.

Limitations of the Study

This study has some limitations. Its cross‐sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between variables. Data were self‐reported, which may have introduced recall and social desirability bias. Dietary diversity was assessed using a 24‐h recall, which may not represent usual intake or seasonal variation. Additionally, the findings may not be generalizable beyond the study area, particularly to rural settings.

Conclusion

This study identified key factors influencing maternal nutritional practices among breastfeeding mothers in Jimma Town. Dietary diversity, meal frequency, maternal education, marital status, occupation, household income, and adherence to cultural food taboos were found to be the most important determinants of maternal nutrition. Mothers with higher education, diverse diets, more frequent meals, and supportive socio‐economic conditions were more likely to maintain good nutritional practices, while daily laborers, mothers in larger households, and those observing restrictive food taboos were more vulnerable to poor nutrition.

Based on these findings, interventions should focus on promoting nutrition education for women of reproductive age, encouraging dietary diversity and adequate meal frequency, addressing culturally rooted food taboos, and supporting economic empowerment through vocational training or small business initiatives. Strengthening community support systems and providing culturally sensitive counseling can further enhance maternal nutrition. Implementing these measures has the potential to improve maternal health and, consequently, child health outcomes in the community.
