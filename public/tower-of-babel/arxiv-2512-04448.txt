Has ACL Lost Its Crown? A Decade-Long Quantitative Analysis of Scale and Impact Across Leading AI Conferences
Jianglin Ma, Ben Yao, Xiang Li, Yazhou Zhang

Abstract
The recent surge of language models (LMs) has rapidly expanded NLP/AI research, driving an exponential rise in submissions and acceptances at major conferences. Yet this growth has been shadowed by escalating concerns over conference quality, e.g., plagiarism, reviewer inexperience and collusive bidding. However, existing studies rely largely on qualitative accounts (e.g., expert interviews, social media discussions, etc.), lacking longitudinal empirical evidence. To fill this gap, we conduct a ten-year empirical study (2014–2024) spanning seven leading conferences. We build a four-dimensional bibliometric framework covering conference scale, core citation statistics, impact dispersion, cross-venue and journal influence, etc. Notably, we further propose a metric Quality–Quantity Elasticity (QQE), which measures the elasticity of citation growth relative to acceptance growth. We highlight two key findings: (1) conference expansion does not lead to proportional growth in scholarly impact, as QQE consistently declines over time across all venues; (2) ACL has not lost its crown, continuing to outperform other NLP conferences in median citations, milestone contributions, and citation coverage. 3.2
Multi-Dimensional Bibliometric Framework

We develop a four-dimensional bibliometric framework that enables a more holistic characterization of conference evolution, as shown in Fig.
2
.

(1) Scale Dimension.

This dimension quantifies the growth of each conference as a scholarly venue. It includes three indicators:

(i) publication count (PC)
, the total number of accepted long papers, reflecting the venue’s output capacity; and

(ii) annual paper growth rate (APGR)
, the year-over-year percentage increase in accepted papers, which captures how rapidly a conference expands in size.

(iii) annual citation growth rate (ACGR)
, the year-over-year percentage increase in total citations accumulated by papers from each conference.

(2) Influence Dimension.

To capture the magnitude and distribution of scholarly influence, we use six indicators:

(i) average citations (AC)
and
(ii) median citations (MC)
, representing central tendencies of citation performance;

(iii) highly cited ratio (HCR)
(share of papers with more than 100 citations), measuring the upper tail of impact; and

(iv) zero-cited ratio (ZCR)
, quantifying the share of papers that never attracted citations;

(v) H-index
, which reflect the concentration of highly influential papers;

(vi) milestone influence index (MII)
, defined as the proportion of papers exceeding both an absolute citation threshold (
\geq
1000 citations)
7
7

7

Scientific milestones lack a unified definition and are commonly proxied using citation-based indicators in scientometric studies.

(
15
)
and a relative threshold (top 0.1% of the annual citation distribution)
(
14
)
.

(3) Prestige Dimension.

Beyond citation volume, we analyze citation sources to situate a venue in the scholarly network. We compute:

(i) top-conference citation share (TCS)
, the fraction of citations coming from leading peer conferences;

(ii) top-journal citation share (TJS)
, the fraction coming from elite journals, reflecting broader uptake; and

(iii) top-conference citation coverage (TCC)
, the fraction of the venue’s papers that receive at least one citation from a top conference, reflecting penetration breadth.
Top conferences are defined as the union of CCF A-level and CORE A* venues, and top journals are represented by
Nature
and
Science

(
3

.

(4) Quality–Quantity Elasticity (QQE).
While static bibliometric indicators capture snapshots of scale or impact, they fail to track whether expansion is supported by a proportional diffusion of knowledge. To quantify this dynamic coupling, we propose
Quality–Quantity Elasticity (QQE)
, grounded in scientific growth theory
1
. This framework posits that an efficient venue should exhibit proportional or superlinear growth in citation-based influence relative to its paper volume, as a larger knowledge stock should generate greater spillover effects.We define
C_{t}
as the citation mass and
N_{t}
as the number of accepted long papers in year
t
. The annual elasticity is calculated as:

QQE_{t}=\frac{C_{t}/C_{t-1}}{N_{t}/N_{t-1}}.

(1)

This metric captures how scholarly influence scales relative to conference size by comparing their respective multiplicative growth rates. To distinguish between operational modes, we define the log-growth of scale as
s_{t}=\ln(N_{t}/N_{t-1})
, where
s_{t}>0
denotes
expansion
and
s_{t}<0
denotes
contraction
. This unified framework allows for a consistent interpretation of growth efficiency across diverse institutional regimes.

Impact-Scale Elasticity Regime.
The relationship between citation growth and scale expansion is characterized by
QQE_{t}
:

(i)
|QQE_{t}|>1
(Effective Elasticity).

Indicates that citation-based influence changes faster than scale. When
s_{t}>0
, this corresponds to
efficient expansion
; when
s_{t}<0
, it reflects
quality-concentrating contraction
, where impact density increases despite shrinking volume.

(ii)
|QQE_{t}|=1
(Unitary Elasticity).

Indicates proportional change, where citation mass evolves in direct alignment with conference scale.

(iii)
|QQE_{t}|<1
(Ineffective Elasticity).

Indicates lagging impact gains, corresponding to
impact dilution
during expansion (
s_{t}>0
) or
malignant contraction
during scale reduction (
s_{t}<0
).

3.3
Analytical Method

We adopt a combined cross-conference and longitudinal analytical design. At the cross-sectional level, we compute ten-year averages of thirteen bibliometric indicators for ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, AAAI, IJCAI, ICLR, and NeurIPS to characterize each venue’s overall scale, citation performance, impact distribution, citation composition, and growth efficiency (QQE). At the longitudinal level, we use a one-year sliding window to track temporal dynamics based on six core indicators—average and median citations (AC, MC), highly cited and zero-cited paper ratios (HCR, ZCR), QQE, and milestone influence (MII)—with additional within-NLP comparisons among ACL, EMNLP, and NAACL, enabling an analysis of how citation impact and growth trajectories evolve over time (Details please see App.
D
).

3.4
Normalization Controls

Because the selected conferences differ substantially in scale (with AI venues typically accepting far more papers than NLP ones), raw impact indicators can systematically favor larger venues. To make fair comparisons, we apply two normalization controls to adjust for differences in publication volume.

(1) Normalized H-index.

The conventional H-index increases with the number of publications, which means that larger conferences tend to obtain higher H-indices simply due to their scale.
To mitigate this bias, we compute a
normalized H-index
:

\text{Norm-H}=\frac{H\text{-index}}{\text{total publications}}

(2)

This ratio reflects the density of highly influential papers per accepted paper.

(2) Gini Coefficient of Citation Distribution.

Citations in NLP/AI conferences typically follow a long-tail pattern. As conference volume increases, this tail may expand, potentially inflating total citation counts without improving median quality. To measure this concentration, we compute the annual
Gini coefficient
for each venue. Let citation counts be sorted as
c_{1}\leq c_{2}\leq\dots\leq c_{n}
, where
n
is the paper count and
\mu
is the mean citation count. The Gini coefficient is computed as:

\text{Gini}=\frac{1}{n\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2i-n-1),c_{i}

(3)

A value of 0 indicates a perfectly uniform distribution, while values approaching 1 signify extreme concentration in a small subset of “blockbuster” papers.

6
Discussion

In machine learning venues (ICLR and NeurIPS), continued growth does not undermine impact: average and median citations remain high, the share of highly cited papers is stable, tails are well controlled, and citation inequality does not worsen persistently after expansion. Their QQE trajectories stay close to 1 over many years, indicating that changes in impact largely track changes in scale. These venues sustain high visibility with stable head concentration under growth.

In broader AI venues (AAAI and IJCAI), expansion is not accompanied by comparable impact concentration. Both venues show lower high-impact ratios and heavier tails, with IJCAI in particular exhibiting uniformly low citation levels rather than balanced influence. Their QQE trajectories fluctuate early and converge more quickly toward values below 1 in recent years, indicating that impact growth increasingly lags behind scale adjustment.

Within the NLP domain (ACL, NAACL, and EMNLP), expansion leads to differentiation rather than convergence. ACL consistently maintains higher medians, a larger share of highly cited papers, tighter tails, and the strongest contribution to milestone work, confirming its role as the central NLP venue. NAACL achieves the highest impact density relative to its smaller scale, while EMNLP absorbs expansion at the cost of greater dispersion, with longer tails, rising citation inequality, and QQE converging below 1 in recent years.

7
Conclusion

This work presents a decade-long empirical comparison of seven major NLP and AI conferences (2014–2024) using a four-dimensional bibliometric framework and the proposed Quality–Quantity Elasticity (QQE). We examine how conference scale, citation impact, impact dispersion, and cross-venue influence evolve under sustained expansion.
Our results show that ML venues (ICLR and NeurIPS) maintain consistently high and stable influence with tight scale–impact alignment. Within NLP, expansion leads to clear internal differentiation: ACL remains the central venue for high-impact and milestone work, NAACL achieves high impact density under limited scale, and EMNLP exhibits increasing dispersion as it absorbs growth. Broader AI venues (AAAI and IJCAI) show weaker impact concentration and lower scale–impact alignment.
This work provides the first systematic longitudinal evidence that post-expansion conference standing is determined not by size, but by how effectively scholarly impact is concentrated and sustained as scale grows.

8
Limitations

Our study has several limitations.
First, although we aggregate data from multiple high-quality sources (ACL Anthology, Semantic Scholar, and OpenAlex), citation records may still contain minor inconsistencies due to update delays or incomplete indexing. These issues are unlikely to affect cross-venue comparisons but may influence fine-grained year-to-year values.
Second, our analysis focuses exclusively on long papers; short papers, findings papers, and workshop papers are excluded due to inconsistent archival standards across venues. While this choice improves comparability, it may omit certain forms of early-stage or exploratory research.
Third, citation-based indicators inherently lag behind real-time research influence and may partially reflect field-specific citation practices or short-term topic popularity rather than long-term scientific contribution.
Finally, while our framework captures aggregate and distributional patterns, it does not explicitly model venue-specific policy changes (e.g., review procedures, submission formats, or acceptance strategies), which may also shape conference trajectories.

These limitations suggest several directions for future work, including incorporating acceptance-rate dynamics, policy changes, and field-normalized impact measures to achieve a more fine-grained understanding of conference evolution.

Appendix A
Data Collection and Processing

To ensure the comprehensiveness and accuracy of our longitudinal analysis, we curated a large-scale dataset of publications and their corresponding citation metrics from seven premier AI and NLP conferences. We restrict our analysis to main-track long papers. Findings papers, short papers, and workshop papers are excluded to ensure cross-year and cross-venue comparability. The data collection process was conducted in two primary stages: metadata extraction and citation retrieval.

Temporal Scope: 2014–2024.

Our analysis covers 2014–2024. Since ACL submission volumes were essentially stable from 2009–2014, e.g., 634 (2011), 571 (2012), 664 (2013), and 572 (2014), but began accelerating sharply in 2015 (692), 2014 provides a natural pre-expansion baseline. Due to the absence of an official Google Scholar API, we rely on Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex to obtain high-quality citation data. In our pipeline, paper titles were first harvested directly from the official websites of each conference and subsequently matched with external databases using exact string matching—augmented by venue and year constraints—to minimize ambiguity. Semantic Scholar served as the primary data source due to its superior coverage and its provision of time-sliced citation records essential for calculating dynamic metrics like Quality-Quantity Elasticity (QQE). This approach achieved a 99.3% successful match rate, with the remaining 0.7% (approximately 430 entries) supplemented via OpenAlex. Together, these sources provided granular, programmatically retrieved metadata, including annual and total citation counts, necessary for our longitudinal analysis.

The final dataset underwent a cleaning process to resolve potential title mismatches and to ensure that only main conference research papers (excluding workshops and tutorials) were included in the longitudinal study.

Appendix D
Analytical Method

We adopt a combined cross-conference and longitudinal analytical design.

(1) Cross-Conference Comparison.

We compute the ten-year averages of thirteen bibliometric indicators for ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, AAAI, IJCAI, ICLR, and NeurIPS.
This provides a macro-level snapshot of each venue’s overall scale, citation performance, impact distribution, citation composition, and growth efficiency (QQE).
The analysis characterizes the long-term static landscape of major NLP/AI conferences and their relative positions.

(2) Longitudinal Evolution Analysis.

We then track year-to-year changes using a one-year computation window, focusing on six core dynamic indicators: average and median citations (AC, MC), the proportions of highly cited and zero-cited papers (HCR, ZCR), QQE, milestone influence (MII), and within-NLP comparisons among ACL, EMNLP, and NAACL.
This longitudinal component examines the temporal evolution of citation levels, impact distribution, and growth efficiency across the seven venues over the past decade, revealing the shifts in their developmental trajectories.

Appendix G
Field-level decomposition of publication growth and QQE dynamics

Our main experiment considers all papers in aggregate and reveals a systematic decline in QQE as publication volume expands. However, these aggregate trends may potentially be influenced by changes in the mix of fields, as different research fields exhibit different growth rates and citation dynamics.

To separate these effects, we conduct a field-level analysis by first categorizing papers into major research areas using DeepSeek-V3, with the annual publication counts and QQE values shown in Tab.
5
. We then examined the relationship between annual publication counts and QQE over the period 2015–2024 within each field. Specifically, for each field, we compute the Spearman rank correlation between annual publication volume and QQE values, allowing us to assess whether the observed negative association reflects a field-specific phenomenon or a more general structural pattern.

As shown in Tab.
3
, we observe predominantly strong and statistically significant negative correlations across most research fields. In particular, Representation Learning & Optimization, Machine Learning (incl. Deep Learning), Interpretability, Fairness & Applications, Reinforcement Learning & Decision Making, and Natural Language Processing exhibit Spearman correlation coefficients below
-0.85
(p
\leq
0.01), indicating that sustained increases in publication volume are systematically associated with declining QQE. These results suggest that as these fields expand, marginal increases in publication output yield diminishing returns in scholarly impact.

An exception is AI Foundations & Theory area, where the correlation is weaker and statistically insignificant (p > 0.05), possibly reflecting the fact that publication growth in foundational research does not necessarily dilute research impact. Overall, the persistence of negative correlations within individual fields demonstrates that the decline in QQE values represents a robust, cross-fields phenomenon in these ML, AI, and NLP conferences. While spillover effects from accumulated knowledge remain present, their marginal contribution to scholarly impact appears to weaken as research fields scale up.
