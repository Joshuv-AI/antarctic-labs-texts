Spirooxindole 5‐(Trifluoromethyl)benzothiazole Hybrids: Synthesis, Crystal Structure, and Antioxidant Activity
Authors: Nida Karabag, Asu Busra Temizer‐Keser, Murat Pekmez, Elif Mertoğlu‐Kamalı, Filiz Betül Kaynak, Nurten Özsoy, Nazlı Arda, Nilgün Karalı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntioxidants neutralize free radicals to reduce oxidative stress and cellular damage, preventing various chronic diseases. In this study, the antioxidant activities of novel 5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐3H‐spiro[1,3‐benzothiazole‐2,3′‐indol]‐2′(1′H)‐ones (4a–h), along with the spirocyclic 2‐indolinones (4i–m and 5a–n) previously synthesized by our group, were investigated for the first time, to find a multi‐modal antioxidant and to understand the impact of the trifluoromethyl group located at the 5‐position of the benzothiazole on antioxidant activity. For this purpose, the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH•) free radical scavenging, superoxide anion scavenging, ABTS•+ radical cation scavenging, trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity, ferric ion reduction antioxidant potential (FRAP), and lipid peroxidation (TBARs) assays were carried out. The cytotoxic effects of compounds (4g, 4h, 4i, 4j, 4l, 5a, 5b, and 5h), which showed promising in vitro antioxidant activity in at least two in vitro tests, were investigated in healthy human fibroblast (HFF‐1) cells. The effects of non‐toxic doses of these compounds on intracellular reactive oxygen species were also evaluated. As a result, it was determined that compound 4l (R1 = 5‐F and R2 = H) exhibited greater radical scavenging activity than the other compounds tested. The IC50 of compound 4l was 74.13 µM while compound 4g (R1 = 5‐Cl‐7‐CH3 and R2 = H) was non‐toxic (IC50 > 200 μM). Compounds 4e (R1 = 5‐Cl and R2 = H) and 4f (R1 = 7‐Br and R2 = H), exhibited higher lipid peroxidation inhibitory activity than α‐tocopherol in vitro; however, the difference was not statistically significant.

Introduction

Oxidation is a fundamental biochemical process for the continuation of life. Oxidation must occur to the extent necessary under physiological conditions. This is because oxidation triggers the formation of free radicals in the body. While free radicals have positive effects in small amounts, excessive concentrations can damage cells. When free radicals accumulate uncontrollably in the body, oxidative stress occurs [1, 2, 3, 4]. This stress can cause mutations that may result in tumors by damaging biomolecules such as lipids, proteins, enzymes, and DNA in cells and tissues. As a result, they contribute to the development of a range of disorders, including cancer, inflammatory diseases, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, autoimmune diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, and aging [5].

Antioxidants neutralize free radicals by providing them with electrons. A balance between free radicals and antioxidants is necessary for optimal physiological function. Living organisms have a defense system that helps maintain oxidative stress in balance [3, 6]. In humans, indole‐3‐propionic acid (I) produced by intestinal bacteria and certain metabolites of tryptophan (e.g., serotonin (II), melatonin (III)) have been found to have free radical scavenging properties and are important indole derivatives with high antioxidant activity at low doses [7] (Figure 1). Most plants have a high natural antioxidant content, and their antioxidant effects come from enzymes, amino acids, vitamins, carotenoids, terpenoids, flavonoids, and so forth, in their structure [8]. In plants, brassinin (IV), spirobrassinin (V), and 1‐methoxyspirobrassinol (VI), which are indole derivatives of phytoalexins in the Cruciferous family, play an important role in defense against oxidative stress and pathogens, and have been proven to exhibit in vivo cytotoxic effects in solid tumors and leukemia cells (Figure 1). Gelsemin (VII), the most well‐known spirooxindole natural product belonging to the Gelsemium genus, has shown powerful antioxidant effects [9] (Figure 1).

Chemical structures of antioxidant‐active indole derivatives.

Both natural and synthetic compounds bearing a spirooxindole skeleton have attracted increasing interest as free radical scavengers and antioxidants. Several studies in the literature have attributed the antioxidant activity to the presence of NH groups [10, 11, 12]. Kaur et al. synthesized a series of spiro[oxindole‐isoxazolidine] (VIII) derivatives and evaluated their antioxidant activities using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. All compounds exhibited stronger antioxidant effects than the reference standard BHT (IC50 = 5.37 μg/mL). The ethyl‐substituted derivative showed the highest potency, with an IC50 value of 0.44 μg/mL. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) analyses indicated that electron‐donating substituents enhanced antioxidant activity. Furthermore, the presence of an enolic OH group arising from the keto–enol tautomerism between the NH and CO groups at the 1‐position of the oxindole ring was identified as a key structural feature contributing to the observed activity [13]. Shrestha et al. synthesized a series of spirooxindole derivatives (IX) and evaluated their antioxidant potential using the ferric ion reduction antioxidant potential (FRAP) assay. SAR analysis revealed that derivatives with an unsubstituted indole ring or those bearing 5‐OCH3 or 7‐Cl substituents exhibited higher activity. Compounds containing electron‐donating or electron‐withdrawing groups at the ortho or para positions relative to the spirooxindolic NH were also found to possess greater potency. Moreover, the presence of an acetyl group on the spirooxindole nitrogen contributed positively to the antioxidant activity [14]. Nishtala et al. synthesized a series of spirooxindole–carbamate derivatives (X) and assessed their antioxidant properties using the DPPH assay. The compounds featured hydrogen, alkyl, or benzyl substituents at the 1‐position of the oxindole core. Among these, benzyl‐substituted analogs exhibited the highest radical‐scavenging activity, with potencies comparable to the reference antioxidant α‐tocopherol [15]. Pourshab et al. diastereoselectively synthesized a series of spirocyclopropane oxindole derivatives (XI) and evaluated their antioxidant potential using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. The results indicated that compounds bearing an NH group at the 1‐position of the indole ring exhibited higher antioxidant activity compared to those containing a triazole substituent. The most active derivatives were further examined for their in vitro cytotoxicity against HeLa cancer cell lines using the MTT assay. Among these, the compound bearing a chloro‐substituted phenyl ring showed the strongest cytotoxicity, with an IC50 of 4.50 ± 0.3 μg/mL [16]. In a study conducted in 2014, spiro[oxindole‐thiazolidine] derivatives carrying a fluorine group at the 5‐position of the 2‐indolinone ring were synthesized, and their antioxidant potentials were tested using the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay. All compounds exhibited excellent antioxidant activity, and SAR studies revealed that the presence of the CF3 group and the spirooxindole ring, incorporated with thiazolopyrimidinethione (XII) or thiazolodiazaphosphorine (XIII), enhanced the activity [17]. Free radical scavenging potentials of spirooxindole‐dihydropyrimidinones were evaluated using DPPH and H2O2 tests. Compound (XIV), chlorine groups bearing at the positions 5‐ and 7‐ of the spirooxindole ring, was identified as the most potent compound compared to the reference ascorbic acid [18]. Figure 2 illustrates the chemical structures of the spirooxindole derivatives reported in these studies.

Chemical structures of spirooxindole derivatives.

A large number of 2‐arylbenzothiazole derivatives have been designed and synthesized due to their significant anticancer and antioxidant effects [19, 20, 21]. It is recorded that benzothiazinon and indolobenzothiazine derivatives are also obtained along with spirobenzothiazoline derivatives synthesized from the reaction of 1H‐indole‐2,3‐diones with 2‐aminobenzothiol. However, in the studies conducted by our group, the structures of the only product, spiro‐2‐indolinones, obtained by the reaction of 1H‐indole‐2,3‐diones and 2‐aminobenzothiol in ethanol were confirmed by spectral and X‐ray single crystal analyses [22, 23, 24, 25]. Its antioxidant effects were found to be superior to α‐tocopherol, and methyl and trifluoromethoxy substitutions at the 5‐position of the 2‐indolinone ring increased cytotoxicity [22]. In the study in which spiroindolinones bearing the 5‐chlorobenzothiazoline moiety were synthesized, it was noted that the fluorine‐substituted compound at the 5‐position of the 2‐indolinone ring was more effective as an antioxidant than α‐tocopherol [26].

The trifluoromethyl group is commonly used as a bioisostere in the design of new drug candidates, replacing functional groups such as methyl, chlorine, and nitro. Due to their similar dimensions, this group exhibits better compatibility with target proteins, possessing high electronegativity, strong lipophilicity, relatively low steric effects, and high permeability [27]. Furthermore, the trifluoromethyl group, which also enhances metabolic stability, modulates the binding affinity to target proteins via hydrogen bonds, thus contributing to improved pharmacological profiles. Consequently, it represents a valuable structural strategy in modern drug design [28].

In this study, the antioxidant capacities of newly synthesized spiroindolinone‐benzothiazole hybrids (4a–h) and similar spirocyclic 2‐indolinone compounds (4i–m and 5a–n) previously synthesized by our group [25] were examined in order to compare their multifaceted effects and to investigate the contribution of the trifluoromethyl group located at the 5‐position of the benzothiazole ring to the antioxidant effect. The in vitro radical‐scavenging abilities, trolox‐equivalent antioxidant capacities, ferric ion‐reducing power, lipid peroxidation‐inhibitory activity, cytotoxicity toward healthy HFF‐1 cells, and effects on cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation of these compounds were investigated. In addition, compliance with Lipinski's 5 rules (RO5) and ADME criteria of the compounds was evaluated in silico using the Qikprop module. The spirocyclic structure of compound 5h was elucidated by spectral and single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction analyses.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

In this study, 5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐3H‐spiro[1,3‐benzothiazole‐2,3′‐indol]‐2′(1′H)‐ones (4a–h) were newly synthesized by reaction of the 1H‐indole‐2,3‐diones (1a–h) with 2‐amino‐4‐(trifluoromethyl)benzenethiol (3) in ethanol [22]. The structures of the new compounds 4a–h were confirmed by analytical and spectral (IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR‐APT, HMBC‐2D, and LC‐MS) data. The general structures of compounds 4a–h and other spirooxindole‐benzothiazoles (4i–m and 5a–n) previously synthesized by our group are shown in Scheme 1 [25].

Synthesis of 5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐3H‐spiro[1,3‐benzothiazole‐2,3′‐indol]‐2′(1′H)‐ones (4a–m and 5a–n).

In the IR spectra of compounds 4a–h, indole and benzothiazole N─H stretching bands were observed in the 3369–3167 cm−1 region [29, 30, 31]. The fact that the C═O stretching band characterizing the ketone group of 1H‐indole‐2,3‐dione was not observed. The lactam group C═O stretching band, proving the accuracy of the spiroindolinone structure signaled in the region 1730–1701 cm−1 [32]. In the 1H NMR spectra of compounds 4a–h, the NH proton signals of the benzothiazoline and the indole rings were observed at δ 7.60–7.77 and 10.48–11.11 ppm regions, respectively [22].

In the 13C NMR‐APT spectra of compound 4a–h and the HMBC‐2D spectra of compound 4f, the ketone C═O (C3) carbon signals (δ 181.5–184.5 ppm) of 1H‐indole‐2,3‐dione were not detected. Observation of lactam C═O (C2) carbon signals at δ 175.16–176.48 and spiro C resonances at δ 74.49–76.02 ppm were confirmed the spiroindolinone structures of the compounds [19, 22]. In the HMBC‐2D spectra of compound 4f, the indole NH proton (δ 10.80 ppm on the 1H axis) interacted with indole C7a (δ 141.19 ppm), C3a (δ 131.71 ppm), and spiro (δ 76.02 ppm) carbons on the 13C axis. These findings further confirmed the spiroindolinone structure bearing benzothiazoline residues.

LC‐MS analyses were performed by positive (electrospray ionization [ESI]+) or negative (ESI−) ionization techniques. In the spectra of compounds 4a–g taken in the positive mode, [M + H]+ peaks were detected as the main peak (except for compound 4g). In the mass spectra of bromine‐substituted compounds 4a and 4f taken in positive mode, [M + H]+ and [M + H]+ + 2 peaks with a relative abundance ratio of approximately 1:1 arising from the 79 and 81 isotopes of the bromine atom were observed as the main peaks. In the mass spectrum of the chlorine‐substituted compound 4e taken in the positive mode, the presence of [M + H]+ + 2 peak in a relative abundance ratio of about 3:1 originating from the [M + H]+ and chlorine isotopes 35 and 37 confirms the molecular weight and is observed as the main peak. In the negative mode mass spectrum of 5,7‐dichloro‐substituted compound 4h, the [M‐H]−, [M‐H]− + 2, and [M‐H]− + 4 peaks in the isotopic ratio 9:6:1 confirm the molecular weight. The mass spectrum of the 5‐iodine substituted compound 4b, taken in positive mode, showed [M + H]+ signals and [M + Na]+ and [M + 2Li]+ peaks of varying relative abundance, confirming its molecular weight and observed as the main peak.

Although all four compounds formed suitable crystals, compound 5h was specifically chosen for structure elucidation in the present study because it demonstrated the highest antioxidant activity among them.

The asymmetric unit contains one molecule of 5′‐nitro‐1′‐methyl‐5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐3H‐spiro[1,3‐benzothiazole‐2,3′‐indol]‐2′‐on. The molecule adopts a spiro form, and it is not planar, as documented in previous studies [22, 23, 24, 25]. The indole and benzothiazole moieties are approximately perpendicular, with a dihedral angle of 90.00(3)° between them. Based on the puckering parameter values [34], the benzothiazole ring at C8 is twisted around the C8─S1 bond. All bond lengths and angles are within normal ranges and consistent with those reported in previous studies [22, 23]. The torsion angle N1─C8─C9─N2 is −135.63(13)°, indicating an anticlinal (‐ac) conformation, whereas the C11─C8─N1─C6 torsion angle is 150.20(14)°, indicating an antiperiplanar (ap) conformation. These findings are consistent with the geometry of related structures [22, 24].

The molecular structure of 5h reveals that the trifluoromethyl group is disordered over two positions, indicating rotational flexibility commonly observed in CF3‐containing aromatic systems. The C─F bond lengths for the major disordered component [1.305(6)–1.333(6) Å] are within the expected range for related CF3‐substituted spiro systems. Furthermore, the bond angles around the spiro center [102.9(9)–108.9(7)°] suggest a slightly distorted yet essentially preserved tetrahedral geometry. Although minor deviations from the ideal tetrahedral value are observed, no significant conformational distortion is evident when compared with structurally related trifluoromethyl‐substituted spirooxindole‐benzothiazoline derivatives reported previously [24].

To validate the geometric reliability of the in silico studies, a structural superposition between the experimental X‐ray crystal structure and the predicted lowest‐energy conformation of the title compound was performed using Schrödinger Maestro. The structural alignment demonstrated a high geometric correlation with an RMSD value of 1.5670 Å, as shown in Supporting Information: S1.

Antioxidant Activity

In this study, the antioxidant activities of the compounds 4a–m and 5a–n were evaluated using in vitro methods targeting different mechanisms. The free radical scavenging capacities of the compounds were determined using the DPPH•, superoxide anion, and ABTS•+ radicals. The Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC) expressed as Trolox equivalents quantifies the electron‐donating capacity of the compounds compared with that of Trolox. The FRAP assay measured the compounds' electron‐donating capacity for metal ion reduction. The anti‐lipid peroxidation assay evaluated the compounds' ability to prevent oxidative lipid degradation. Radical Scavenging Capacity

The compounds showed similar efficacy in scavenging DPPH•, ABTS•+ and superoxide anion radicals, with EC50 values of 0.43–2.38, 0.73–2.35 and 0.89–2.21 mM, respectively. The tested several compounds demonstrated comparable or higher radical scavenging capacities compared to BHT, with EC50 values of 1.20, 1.28, and 1.18 mM, respectively (p ≤ 0.05). However, the radical scavenging capacities of the compounds were lower than that of tocopherol, with EC50 values of 0.17, 0.61, and 0.49 mM, respectively (p < 0.05). It has been determined that the most effective DPPH•, ABTS•+, and superoxide anion radical scavenger compound is 4l (R1 = 5‐F and R2 = H), with EC50 values of 0.43, 0.73, and 0.89 mM, respectively. It was observed that the active oxidant activity is significantly decreased by replacing the fluorine group with halogens such as chlorine, bromine, or iodine, by replacing at the 7‐position of the indole ring, or by methyl substitution at the 1‐position of the indole ring.

When the data in Table 4 was examined, it was seen that compounds 4g, 4h, 4i, 4j, 4k, 5a, 5b, 5g, and 5h had similar effects as BHT. Test results showed that the radical scavenging capacity increased significantly with hydrogen, methyl, trifluoromethoxy, and nitro substitutions, especially fluorine substitution, at the 5‐position of the indole ring, as well as disubstituted compounds at the positions 5‐ and 7‐ of the indole ring. However, it was found that the antioxidant potency was significantly decreased by methyl or halogen substitutions at the 7‐position of the indole ring. While methyl substitution at the 1‐position of the indole ring significantly decreased the antioxidant activity of the 5‐fluorine and 5‐trifluoromethyl substituted compounds, and 5,7‐disubstituted compounds, it did not significantly change the activities of the 5‐unsubstituted and 5‐methyl substituted compounds. This substitution caused a significant increase in the antioxidant activities of all other 5‐substituted and 7‐substituted compounds.

Antioxidant Capacity via TEAC and FRAP Methods

The TEAC values of the tested compounds ranged from 0.85 to 1.94 mM, whereas the FRAP values were distributed over a broader range of 0.30–3.08 mM. Although none of the synthesized derivatives surpassed the maximum antioxidant capacity of α‐tocopherol (TEAC = 2.021 mM, FRAP = 3.75 mM), a considerable number of compounds belonging to series 4 (R2 = H) exhibited significantly higher or comparable activities relative to the synthetic reference antioxidant BHT (TEAC = 1.30 mM; FRAP = 0.98 mM).

In the TEAC assay, the most potent compounds were identified as compounds 4h (R1 = 5,7‐diCl and R2 = H) and 4g (R1 = 5‐Cl,7‐CH3 and R2 = H), with values of 1.94 and 1.90 mM, respectively. In addition, compounds 4a (R1 = 5‐Br and R2 = H) and 4b (R1 = 5‐I and R2 = H) also showed strong radical scavenging capacities with values of 1.73 and 1.80 mM, respectively. These findings indicate that halogen substitution at the 5‐position of the indole ring, particularly with heavier halogens such as bromine and iodine, enhanced radical scavenging activity. Furthermore, simultaneous substitution at the 5‐ and 7‐positions of the indole ring provides an additional favorable contribution to antioxidant capacity. In contrast, compounds of series 5 generally exhibited reduced TEAC activities, with values mainly clustered between 1.04 and 1.33 mM. Among this series, only compound 5a (R1 = H and R2 = CH3) with values of 1.33 mM retained activity comparable to BHT.

The FRAP assay results further supported the superiority of series 4 (R2 = H) compounds. Most derivatives from this series displayed ferric reducing capacities higher than BHT and approaching that of α‐tocopherol. The most active compounds in this assay were compounds 4a (R1 = 5‐Br and R2 = H), 4b (R1 = 5‐I and R2 = H), 4g (R1 = 5‐Cl,7‐CH3 and R2 = H), and 4l (R1 = 5‐F and R2 = H) with FRAP values of 3.06, 3.08, 3.03, and 3.00 mM, respectively. These results suggest that halogen substituents strongly contribute to electron‐transfer ability and ferric ion reducing potential. On the other hand, compounds belonging to series 5 (R2 = CH3) showed significantly diminished FRAP activities. Although the unsubstituted compound 5a (R1 = H and R2 = CH3) exhibited moderate activity with a FRAP value of 1.32 mM, substitution of the indole ring led to a marked decrease in reducing capacity. This effect was particularly evident in compounds 5m (R1 = 5‐Cl,7‐CH3 and R2 = CH3) and 5n (R1 = 5,7‐diCl and R2 = CH3), which displayed reduced FRAP values of 0.37 and 0.30 mM, respectively.

TEAC and FRAP results suggest that an unsubstituted indole N─H (series 4) is crucial for antioxidant activity due to its hydrogen‐donating ability, whereas N‐methylation (series 5) reduces activity by eliminating this key feature and introducing steric hindrance. It is also suggested that 5,7‐disubstitution, as well as bromine or iodine substituents, may enhance activity by providing a more favorable steric and electronic environment. Effects on Intracellular ROS Generation

To evaluate the effects of selected compounds 4g, 4h, 4i, 4j, 4l, 5a, 5b, and 5h against hydrogen peroxide–induced ROS generation in HFF‐1 cells, a spectrofluorometric 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) assay was employed. The data showing the relative fold increase or decrease compared with the control are presented in Figure 9. Compounds 4g, 4h, 4i, 4l, and 5a showed a tendency to reduce ROS levels by a range of 0.42 to 0.30 fold compared to the control group. In contrast, compounds 4j, 5b, and 5h tended to increase ROS levels within the range of 0.04–0.30 fold. While compounds 4h (0.42) and 4l (0.40) exhibited the highest numerical ROS inhibitory activity among the tested derivatives, none of the differences between compounds reached statistical significance (p > 0.05). Nevertheless, these findings point to the potential ROS‐inhibiting activity of the non‐substituted 5,7‐dichloro derivative 4h and 5‐fluoro derivative 4l at the 1‐position. Besides, the intracellular ROS‐lowering tendency of 4g, 4h, 4i, and 4l may be due to the lack of substitutions at the 1‐position of the indole ring, and/or chlorine/fluorine substitution, which may cause relatively high radical scavenging and anti‐lipid peroxidation activities. However, compound 4j, which induced ROS generation, and compound 5a, which reduced ROS generation, do not meet this criterion; this is likely due to the substitution of the CH3 group at different positions of the indole ring. Undoubtedly, to avoid speculation and elucidate this SAR, further investigation of the tested compounds at higher concentrations, as well as assessment of the activities of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase, which may help reduce ROS levels, and conversely, enzymes that generate ROS are also needed.

In Silico Physicochemical Parameters

The physicochemical properties of the compounds 4a–m and 5a–n and their compliance with Lipinski's rule of five were evaluated in silico using the QikProp module (Schrödinger LLC, 2015). The molecular weights of all compounds were determined to be within the range of 322.30–462.22 Da, thus remaining below the 500 Da threshold and meeting the related criterion. Compounds with a higher number of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, which are indicative of their potential to interact with target proteins, are predicted to exhibit stronger binding affinity and consequently higher biological activity. The calculated logP values, reflecting lipophilicity and expected to be < 5, ranged between 2.52 and 4.20, confirming compliance with the rule. Since none of the compounds violated Lipinski's rules (RO5), all derivatives were considered to possess drug‐like properties.

Additional pharmacokinetic parameters, including water solubility (logS), blood–brain barrier partition coefficient (logBB), human serum albumin binding (logKhsa), and predicted human oral absorption (HOA), were also assessed in silico. All evaluated compounds satisfied the recommended thresholds. Oral absorption in humans was defined as > 80% for high oral activity and < 25% for poor oral activity. Predicted HOA was consistently > 87.74% for all derivatives except compound 5h, which displayed an absorption rate of 75.15%, indicating moderate oral availability. The calculated logS values, indicative of aqueous solubility and expected to fall within the range of −6.5 to 0.5, were determined as −3.70 to −5.32 for all compounds. The logBB values, which estimate the ability of molecules to penetrate the blood–brain barrier and should range between −3.0 and 1.2, were found to be between −0.27 and 1.10. The logKhsa values, representing the binding affinity to human serum albumin and expected to remain within −1.5 to 1.5, ranged from 0.19 to 0.53. Taken together, these findings suggest that all tested spiroindolinone derivatives possess favorable physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties and meet the essential drug‐likeness criteria (Table S1).

Conclusion

In this study, novel spiroindolinone 5‐(trifluoromethyl)benzothiazole hybrids (4a–h), along with previously reported compounds 4i–m and 5a–n, were comprehensively evaluated for their antioxidant potential in order to investigate the contribution of the trifluoromethyl group and determine the SARs of this series of compounds. SAR studies have shown that the presence of an N–H group at the 1‐position of the indole ring is critical for strong antioxidant activity. In contrast, the strategic introduction of halogen substituents emerges as a key structural determinant that enhances overall antioxidant efficacy. Among the radical scavenging analyses, compound 4l with fluorine substitution at the 5‐position of the indole ring was identified as the most potent antioxidant. While other substituents in the indole ring caused an increase in activity in the order H > 5‐CH3 > 5,7‐diCl > 5‐Cl,7‐CH3, it was determined that the presence of methyl at the 1‐position and halogen and methyl substitutions at the 7‐position generally decreased the radical scavenging capacities. Based on the TEAC and FRAP results, most compounds exhibited higher activity than the reference antioxidant BHT. Indole N–H–containing compounds (series 4) showed significantly enhanced radical scavenging and ferric‐reducing abilities compared to the N‐methylated series. Among the series, the 5‐chloro‐7‐methyl‐substituted compound 4g and the 5,7‐dichloro‐substituted compound 4h displayed the strongest activities in the TEAC and FRAP assays, respectively. However, in lipid peroxidation inhibition assays, while 1‐methyl substitution resulted in significant inhibitory effect losses, 7‐chlorine, 7‐bromine, and 5,7‐dichlorine substituted compounds showed higher inhibitory effects than tocopherol. It has been determined that the most potent inhibitors of ROS formation are unsubstituted compounds at the 1‐position of the indole ring, especially 5,7‐dichloro and 5‐fluoro substituted compounds. The results obtained reveal that spirooxindoles 5‐(trifluoromethyl)benzothiazoline hybrids identified to have in silico drug‐like properties are promising lead compounds with their strong antioxidant capacities, lipid peroxidation inhibitory effects, and low cytotoxicity.

It is widely acknowledged that the relationship between spirooxindole structures and their antioxidant activity is intricate, involving multiple factors rather than a single mechanism. The antioxidant properties of spirooxindoles arise from a combination of factors, including their ability to scavenge free radicals, chelate metals, and suppress lipid peroxidation. Additionally, they can modulate cellular antioxidant pathways by activating endogenous defenses via transcription factors such as Nrf2 and by inhibiting ROS‐producing enzymes. The data obtained here highlight the antioxidant activity of the tested compounds for the first three mechanisms in vitro, with some showing dual effects. Overall results indicate that spirooxindoles containing the benzothiazoline moiety show considerable potential for antioxidant activity and warrant further detailed studies, particularly in cellular systems and in vivo.

X‐Ray Crystal Structure Determination and Refinement

Single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction data for compound 5h were collected on a STOE IPDS II image plate diffractometer at 293 K. Graphite‐monochromated MoKα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å), and the scan technique were used. The structure of 5h was solved by direct methods using SHELXS‐97 [36] and refined by the full‐matrix least‐squares on F 2 using SHELXL‐2014 [37], as implemented in the WinGX program suite [38]. Non‐hydrogen atoms were refined with anisotropic displacement parameters. Data collection and cell refinement for compound 5h were carried out using Stoe X‐AREA, while data reduction was performed with Stoe X‐RED [39]. The general‐purpose crystallographic program PLATON [40] was employed for structure analysis and presentation of the results. The dihedral angles were calculated using the PARST95 program [34].

Determination of Antioxidant Activity

Determination of DPPH Radical Scavenging Effect

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of the compounds was determined according to the method developed by Brand‐Williams et al. [41]. To 0.1 mL of indole derivative solution in DMSO (0.625–10 mM), or BHT (0.625–10 mM) and α‐tocopherol (0.08–0.625 mM), 3.9 mL of DPPH‐solution in methanol (6 × 10−5 M) was added, and the mixture was kept in the dark for 30 min. Absorbances were read against methanol in a spectrophotometer at 517 nm. Positive and negative controls were run in parallel, with the positive control using standard (α‐tocopherol and BHT) in place of the compounds under study and DMSO as the negative control. The experiment was repeated three times and averaged. DPPH radical scavenging activity was calculated according to the following equation: DPPHradicalscavengingactivity(%)=1−Absorbanceofsampleat517nmAbsorbanceofcontrolat517nm×100.

Determination of Superoxide Radical Scavenging Effect

The superoxide radical scavenging effect of the compounds was determined according to the method developed by Aruoma et al. [42]. The experiment is based on reading the absorbance at 560 nm of blue‐purple colored formazones formed as a result of the reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) by superoxide radicals produced by the xanthine/xanthine oxidase system. The experiment was carried out in a 3 mL reaction medium. 100 μL indole derivative (0.625–10 mM) or BHT (0.625–10 mM) and, α‐tocopherol (0.16–2.5 mM), 100 μL 30 mM ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (1 mM), 10 μL 30 mM xanthine dissolved in 50 mM KOH (0.1 mM), 100 μL 3 mM NBT (0.1 mM) and 2.59 mL 57.5 mM KH2PO4–KOH, pH 7.4 (50 mM) were mixed and the reaction was initiated by the addition of 100 μL 1U xanthine oxidase. After 5 min at 25°C, absorbances were read against the blind at 560 nm. The experiment was repeated three times and averaged and the superoxide radical scavenging activity was calculated according to the following equation: Superoxideradicalscavenging(%)=1−Absorbanceofindolederivativeat560nmAbsorbanceofcontrolat560nm×100.

TEAC Assay

The total radical antioxidant potential of the indole derivatives was measured using the Trolox equivalent antioxidant coefficient (TEAC) assay as described by Re et al. [43] with minor modifications. The TEAC value is based on the ability of the antioxidant molecules to quench the long‐lived ABTS•+, a blue‐green chromophore with characteristic absorption at 734 nm, compared with that of Trolox, a water‐soluble vitamin E analog. ABTS was dissolved in water to a 7 mM concentration. ABTS radical cation was produced by reacting ABTS•+ stock solution with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (final concentration) and allowing the mixture to stand in the dark at room temperature for 12–16 h before use. At the beginning of the analysis day, an ABTS•+ working solution was obtained by the dilution in 96% ethanol of the stock solution to an absorbance of 0.70 (± 0.02) at 734 nm. After the addition of 990 μL of ABTS•+ solution to 10 μL of indole derivatives (0.625–10 mM) in DMSO, (0.625–10 mM), α‐tocopherol (0.16–2.5 mM) or trolox standards (1.5, 1, 0.5, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.125 mM), the decrease in absorbance at 734 nm was monitored exactly 6 min after the initial mixing. Appropriate DMSO or ethanol blanks were run in each assay. All determinations were carried out in triplicate. The ability to scavenge ABTS•+ radical was calculated by the following equation: ABTSradicalscavengingactivity(%)=(1−Absorbanceofindolederivativeat734nmAbsorbanceofcontrolat734nm)×100.

The TEAC values were calculated by using a regression equation between the Trolox concentrations and the percentage of inhibition of absorbance at 734 nm at 6 min of incubation and were expressed as mM TEAC.

Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) Assay

The FRAP assay was carried out according to the procedure of Benzie and Strain [44]. The FRAP reagent contained 2.5 mL of 10 mM TPTZ solution in 40 mM HCl, plus 2.5 mL of 20 mM FeCl3.6H2O and 25 mL of 0.3 M acetate buffer, pH 3.6. FRAP reagent (900 μL), prepared freshly and incubated at 37°C, was mixed with 90 μL of DMSO and 30 μL of the tested sample in DMSO or DMSO (for the reagent blank). The increase in absorbance at 593 nm was measured at 4. min. The standard curve was constructed using iron sulfate heptahydrate solution (125–2000 μM), and the results were expressed as mM/l Fe2+. All the measurements were taken in triplicate, and the mean values were calculated.

Determination of Lipid Peroxidation Inhibitory Activity

To determine the capacity of indole derivatives to inhibit lipid peroxidation, the TBARs method recommended by Patro et al. [45] and modified by Hamad et al. [46] was applied. An artificial lipid system consisting of liposomes prepared from soy PC was used. Liposome suspension was prepared by sonication of a mixture containing 300 mg PC in 30 mL of Na‐phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.4) for 2 h on ice. Liposome suspension (250 μL), 5 mM of indole derivatives or DMSO as a control (450 μL), FeCl2, H2O2, and ascorbic acid, each in a final concentration of 125 μM (to a final volume of 1 mL), were mixed and incubated at 30°C for 4 h. α‐Tocopherol was used as a reference antioxidant. Following 4 h of incubation, malondialdehyde (MDA), one of the end products of lipid peroxidation, was detected by using the TBA reagent, containing 0.375% TBA, 15% TCA, and 0.25 M HCl. A 500 μL TBA reagent was added to a 250 μL assay mixture. MDA solutions at various concentrations were also reacted with the TBA reagent to generate a standard MDA curve. These mixtures were kept at 100°C for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature, pink colored TBA‐reactive species (TBARs), as adducts of MDA with 2‐TBA, were obtained by centrifugation (3000×g, 5 min) as a supernatant, and their absorbance at 532 nm was recorded spectrophotometrically. MDA levels in the assay mixtures were calculated using the MDA standard curve. Lipid peroxidation inhibition (%) was calculated using the following formula. Lipidperoxidationinhibition(%)=(1−MDAlevelofsampleMDAlevelofcontrol)×100.

The quantitative data were presented as mean ± SD based on at least three independent experiments. Statistical evaluation was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software with one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The criterion for statistical significance was p < 0.05.

Cell Culture Studies

Cytotoxicity Test

Human foreskin fibroblast cell line (HFF‐1, ATCC, SCRC‐1041) was used in the study. The cells were cultured in 25 cm2 culture dishes using heat inactivated 10% FBS, 1% NEAA, DMEM containing 1% antibiotic‐antimycotic solution, 1% sodium pyruvate (100 mM), 1% GlutaMAX (200 mM), and 4.5 g/L D‐glucose [47] in an incubator (37°C, 5% CO2). The cytotoxic effects of 8 compounds (4g, 4h, 4i, 4j, 4l, 5a, 5b, and 5h), which were determined to have antioxidant activity in at least two in vitro tests, were detected by using the MTT method proposed by Mosmann [48] and modified by Uçar et al [49]. For this purpose, cells were seeded into 96‐well culture dishes and incubated for 24 h at 37°C and in an incubator containing 5% CO2. At the end of this period, the medium on the cells that had adhered to the culture dish was removed, and medium containing different concentrations of compounds (only medium for the control group) was added to the experimental group cells and kept in the oven for 48 h. At the end of this period, the medium on the cells was removed, and 30 µL of MTT stock solution (5 mg/mL, in PBS) was added to each well. Followed by 4 h of incubation, 150 µL of DMSO was added to each well, and the mixture was shaken until homogeneous. Finally, the absorbance of the colored products was measured in a microplate reader (Multiplate Reader UV/Vis Reader, BioTek), and the % viability value was calculated using the following formula: Viability(%)=100×Absorbanceinexperimentalgroupat540nmAbsorbanceincontrolgroupat540nm.

The quantitative data were presented as mean ± SD based on at least three independent experiments. For statistical analysis, GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used, and nonlinear regression was applied to the sigmoidal dose‐response curve to calculate IC50 values.

Determination of Intracellular ROS

HFF‐1 cells were grown in 96‐well culture plates (black ones for fluorescence measurement) for 24 h before measurement. Oxidative stress was induced by exposing the cells to 200 µM H2O2 for 2 h. Based on the cytotoxicity results, a concentration of 15 μM was chosen, as it did not reduce cell viability below 100% for any of the tested compounds. This concentration was then used to evaluate the effects of the compounds on intracellular ROS generation. The experimental group was treated with 15 µM of each test compound for 48 h. The control group was treated with DMSO (the solvent used to dissolve the samples) instead of the samples, following H2O2 exposure. Intracellular ROS were determined spectrofluorometrically using 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA). For this purpose, the liquid media in the control and experimental groups were gently removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Then, 10 μM H2DCFDA in a balanced salt solution (HBSS) was applied to the cells at a concentration of 200 μL in each well, and the culture dishes were incubated at 37°C in an oven containing 5% CO2 for 30 min. After removing the supernatant, 100 μL HBSS was added to each well. Kinetic measurements were taken in a spectrofluorometer (FLx800 Fluorescence Microplate Reader, BioTek) at 495 nm excitation and 525 nm emission wavelengths for 2 h at 37°C. ROS levels were calculated by normalizing fluorescence measurements to the cell number in each well. The results were evaluated as a fold change in ROS level in the experimental groups compared to the control.

The quantitative data were presented as mean ± SD based on at least three independent experiments. Statistical evaluation was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software with one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The criterion for statistical significance was p < 0.05.

In Silico Determination of Physicochemical Parameters

Lipinski's rule of five (RO5), defined in 1977 by Lipinski et al. [50], is based on their study of small and lipophilic drug molecules. This study encompasses various pharmacokinetic properties of drug candidate molecules in the body, including absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. It is a set of rules commonly used to assess oral bioavailability and drug‐like properties. This set of rules does not define the pharmacologic activity of drug candidate molecules. Although it has lost some of its principles and value with the new rules developed today, it remains important as the basic criterion in the design and development of new drug molecules targeted for oral use.

Consisting of four rules, RO5 allows for a maximum of one rule not to be followed. RO5 states that a candidate molecule must meet the following criteria to be orally available and have drug‐like properties: –Hydrogen bond donor group should not be more than 5.–Hydrogen bond acceptor group should not be more than 10.–Molecular weight should be less than 500 daltons.–Octanol/water distribution coefficient (logP) must be less than 5.–Must not violate more than one rule.
